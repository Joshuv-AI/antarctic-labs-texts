Association of Prepregnancy Cardiometabolic Factors With Gestational Diabetes Among Asian Populations in the United States
Authors: Theresa Boyer, Christine Hsueh, Kevin Sun, Yaa Adoma Kwapong, Arthur Jason Vaught, Justin Echouffo Tcheugui, Elizabeth Selvin, Chiadi E Ndumele, Allison G Hays, Erin D Michos, Josef Coresh, Anum S Minhas
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAsian individuals are among the highest immigrant group to the United States and at particularly high risk of gestational diabetes, which is associated with long-term maternal cardiovascular disease. Limited data are available on the association of prepregnancy cardiometabolic risk factors with gestational diabetes among Asian American subjects.ObjectivesThis study sought to determine the association of prepregnancy hypertension and body mass index (BMI) among Asian ancestry subgroups and by maternal nativity.MethodsUsing data from the National Vital Statistics System from 2016 to 2019, the prevalence of gestational diabetes, stratified according to prepregnancy hypertension and obesity, was examined according to maternal race. Predicted probability of gestational diabetes with continuous BMI was calculated across Asian ancestry subgroups and by maternal nativity.ResultsAmong 13.3 million pregnancies, gestational diabetes occurred in 6.5%. Asian subjects had the highest prevalence (12.3%). Among those with prepregnancy hypertension, Asian subjects had the highest prevalence (29.3%). Across the range of BMI (18-42 kg/m2), Asian subjects had the highest predicted probability. There was substantial heterogeneity across Asian ancestry subgroups between the association of BMI with gestational diabetes, with highest probability among Japanese and Korean American subjects at BMI >23 kg/m2. Predicted probability of gestational diabetes was higher for mothers born outside the United States among all Asian subgroups, except for Japanese American subjects.ConclusionsAsian American individuals have the highest prevalence of gestational diabetes at any BMI and with hypertension. Substantial variation in risk is present across Asian subgroups. Findings suggest a need for more tailored screening and intervention for gestational diabetes among Asian American subjects, with a specific focus on risk for individuals born outside the United States.Key Words: cardiometabolic, gestational diabetes, obesity

Gestational diabetes mellitus, defined as glucose intolerance during pregnancy, is common, with a prevalence of 6% to 9% in the United States based on data from 2007 to 2016.1 The incidence of gestational diabetes continues to increase,2 coinciding with growing rates of obesity, metabolic syndrome, and higher maternal age.3 Gestational diabetes carries high morbidity, with individuals with gestational diabetes having elevated risks of preeclampsia and cesarian section, and neonates at risk for macrosomia, shoulder dystocia, birth trauma, and hypoglycemia.4 Long term, gestational diabetes is associated with a 4 to 10 times increased risk of type 2 diabetes and up to a 3-fold increased risk of maternal cardiovascular disease (CVD).5,6 Recent data also suggest that heightened CVD risk with gestational diabetes is likely independent of incident type 2 diabetes.7

Prior studies have reported a higher prevalence of gestational diabetes among Asian populations compared with others.2,8 Less is known about the risk association of prepregnancy cardiometabolic factors, such as hypertension and obesity, with gestational diabetes among Asian populations, especially among those who may have immigrated to the United States.9 In the last 2 decades, the Asian population in the United States has nearly doubled, and approximately 57% of Asian American individuals were born in another country.10 Importantly, Asian American subjects are a heterogeneous population, with multiple ancestry subgroups, which can have implications on risk association and prevalence. However, studies with national representation are limited.11

Asian American individuals represent a diverse group, with multiple ethnicities and varying nativity. Given the increasing diversity of ethnic groups in the United States and increasing rates of obesity nationwide, the current study aimed to: 1) characterize the prevalence and association of prepregnancy hypertension and body mass index (BMI) with gestational diabetes among Asian American subjects according to ancestry subgroups; and 2) examine these associations by nativity.

Methods

Study Population

We used data from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention National Vital Statistics System natality files from 2016 to 2019. This study was exempt from Johns Hopkins Medicine Institutional Review Board review due to the de-identified nature of the data set. The natality files contain birth certificate data that capture approximately all live births within the United States. The birth certificate tracks important indicators of maternal and neonatal health on a national level, including maternal prepregnancy risk factors, maternal morbidity, and birth outcomes. Of all live births that occurred from 2016 to 2019 (N = 15,379,982), we excluded non-singleton pregnancies, non-U.S. residents, and those who reported other race and ethnicity as well as multiple races. Also excluded were pregnancies missing the exposures and outcomes of interest as well as key covariates. Lastly, we excluded individuals with prepregnancy diabetes given that a diagnosis of gestational diabetes, by definition, would not be possible (Supplemental Figure 1).

Prepregnancy BMI was categorized as underweight (<18.5 kg/m2), normal weight (18.5 to <25.0 kg/m2), overweight (25.0 to <30.0 kg/m2), and obese (≥30.0 kg/m2). In keeping with recommendations from the World Health Organization, the following BMI cutoffs were applied for individuals who identified as Asian: underweight (<18.5 kg/m2), normal weight (18.5 to <23.0 kg/m2), overweight (23.0 to <27.5 kg/m2), and obese (≥27.5 kg/m2).12 Prepregnancy hypertension was defined as the presence of hypertension before pregnancy. Gestational diabetes was recorded as the presence of diabetes during pregnancy without evidence of pre-gestational diabetes.

Analyses were stratified according to maternal race and ethnicity, including Asian ancestry subgroups. Maternal race and ethnicity were self-reported on the birth certificate and represent the bridged maternal race and ethnicity. We considered the six largest maternal race and ethnicity groups: American Indian and Alaska Native, Asian, Black, Hispanic, Native Hawaiian and Other Pacific Islander, and White. Asian ancestry subgroups were included as follows: Chinese, Filipino, Indian, Japanese, Korean, Vietnamese and other Asian.

Statistical Analysis

Demographic and pregnancy characteristics are shown overall and according to Asian ancestry subgroups. The prevalence of gestational diabetes was calculated across maternal race and ethnicity and among individuals with obesity and prepregnancy hypertension. Characteristics are shown as mean ± SD for continuous variables and n (%) for categorical variables.

Logistic regression models were used to estimate the association (adjusted prevalence OR [aPOR]) between prepregnancy cardiometabolic risk factors (eg, prepregnancy hypertension, obesity) and gestational diabetes overall and stratified according to maternal race and ethnicity. Asian individuals were further stratified according to Asian ancestry.

First, we estimated the unadjusted association between each prepregnancy cardiometabolic risk factor and gestational diabetes. Next, we adjusted for a priori confounding demographic, biologic, and health systems-level variables, including BMI category (underweight, normal weight, overweight, obese), hypertension (yes or no), maternal age (continuous), education (did not graduate high school, graduated high school, or General Educational Development completed; some college, bachelor’s degree or higher), smoking (yes or no), parity (nulliparous or multiparous), and nativity (born inside the United States or born outside the United States), access to prenatal care (none, first-trimester, second-trimester, or third-trimester prenatal care) and insurance status (Medicaid, private, self-pay/other). As a sensitivity analysis, the analyses were performed only including nulliparous individuals to better control for risk factors such as a history of gestational diabetes that are not captured on the birth certificate.

To assess associations between maternal prepregnancy BMI and gestational diabetes across maternal race and ethnicity, BMI was modeled as a linear spline with a knot at 23 kg/m2. We selected this model because the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) recommends early screening for gestational diabetes for Asian individuals with a BMI >23 kg/m2 in the presence of one other risk factor.4 The predicted probability of gestational diabetes across the continuum of BMI was also calculated. First, we fit the model in which BMI was modeled as a linear spline with a knot at 23 kg/m2 with covariates for age, education, nativity, smoking, parity, hypertension, prenatal care, and health insurance status. Next, the “predict” function in R was used to extract the predicted probabilities conditional on the median or modal values of the data (ie, maternal age 29 years, bachelor’s degree or higher, born in the United States, nonsmoking, multiparous, without hypertension, received prenatal care in the first trimester, had private health insurance). To explore differences according to maternal nativity among Asian individuals, the predicted probability of gestational diabetes across maternal BMI was calculated for those born within the United States and for those born outside the United States.

To further characterize the burden of prepregnancy cardiometabolic risk factors and their contribution to gestational diabetes on a population level, the population attributable fraction (PAF) was also calculated for each prepregnancy cardiometabolic risk factor. To calculate the PAF, we first calculated the proportion of gestational diabetes cases exposed to the prepregnancy cardiometabolic risk factor. Next, this proportion was multiplied by (aPOR−1)/aPOR.13

All analyses were performed by using R version 4.2.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Results

Among 13,352,459 pregnancies resulting in live births, the mean maternal age was 28.9 ± 5.8 years, and 53.8% were among White individuals. Among deliveries included, 50.1% of birthing individuals had private health insurance, 33.0% attained a bachelor’s degree or higher, and 61.4% were multiparous (Table 1). Overall, 867,893 (6.5%) individuals developed gestational diabetes during pregnancy. The prevalence of gestational diabetes ranged from 5.2% among Black individuals to 12.3% among Asian American individuals (Figure 1A). Among Asian American individuals, the prevalence of gestational diabetes was highest among Indian (14.3%), Filipino (12.9%), and Vietnamese (12.8%) American individuals (Figure 1B). Asian American subjects had the highest prevalence of gestational diabetes in the presence of either prepregnancy hypertension or obesity compared with other race groups (Central Illustration). Among Asian ancestry subgroups, the prevalence of gestational diabetes in the setting of prepregnancy hypertension was highest among Vietnamese individuals at 32.5% and lowest among Japanese individuals at 22.8%. Similarly, the prevalence of gestational diabetes with prepregnancy obesity was highest among Indian individuals at 21.5% and lowest among Japanese individuals at 15.8%.

Heterogeneity was also observed in the risk associations of prepregnancy hypertension and obesity with gestational diabetes across maternal race and ethnicity groups as well as maternal Asian ancestry (Supplemental Tables 1 and 2). Prepregnancy hypertension was associated with an 84% higher prevalence of gestational diabetes, with the strongest association among Hispanic individuals (aPOR: 2.03; 95% CI: 1.98-2.09) (Central Illustration). Obesity was associated with 3.36 (95% CI: 3.34-3.38) times the prevalence of gestational diabetes, and the association ranged from 2.63 (95% CI: 2.58-2.67) in Asian individuals to 3.71 (95% CI: 3.69-3.74) in White individuals (Supplemental Figure 2). Our results were similar when the analysis was restricted to nulliparous individuals (n = 4,862,362) (Supplemental Table 3).

When looking at BMI as a continuous measure, we noted that the probability of gestational diabetes was highest among Asian American individuals and lowest among non-Hispanic Black individuals across all ranges of prepregnancy BMI (Figure 2A). Asian American individuals of Vietnamese background had the highest probability of gestational diabetes at a BMI of 23 kg/m2. Under BMI 23 kg/m2, Indian, Filipino, Korean, Vietnamese, and other Asian individuals had a greater change in gestational diabetes prevalence with increasing BMI compared with Chinese individuals. The steepest rise in gestational diabetes prevalence with BMI >23 kg/m2 was noted in Japanese and Korean individuals (Figure 2B).

Regarding nativity status, Asian American individuals born outside the United States had a higher predicted probability of developing gestational diabetes across all ranges of BMI except among those who identified as Japanese (Figure 3). Importantly, a higher proportion of Asian American individuals were born outside the United States (81.4%) compared with non-Asian American counterparts (18.6%), and Asian American individuals born outside the United States vs inside the United States were more likely to be slightly older and had a lower prevalence of prepregnancy hypertension and obesity (Supplemental Tables 4 and 5).

Discussion

In this national study of 13.3 million singleton births, 6.5% of pregnancies were affected by gestational diabetes. We found that Asian American subjects had the highest prevalence of gestational diabetes among all race or ethnicity groups, with a prevalence of 12.3% overall and reaching 29.3% among Asian American subjects with prepregnancy hypertension and 20.3% with prepregnancy obesity. BMI was also strongly associated with gestational diabetes, with the burden increasing at higher BMI values. Compared with other groups, Asian American subjects had a substantially higher probability of gestational diabetes at all BMI values, with the predicted probability of gestational diabetes approaching 5% among Asian American individuals at a BMI of 21 kg/m2; this probability was reached at a higher BMI of 25 to 31 kg/m2 among other race and ethnicity groups. Heterogeneity was noted across Asian ancestry subgroups regarding predicted probability of gestational diabetes with BMI.

Notably, the PAFs for both hypertension and obesity were much lower among the Asian American population compared with other groups. Finally, across the majority of Asian ancestry subgroups, the probability of gestational diabetes was higher among those born outside the United States.

Given the risks associated with gestational diabetes, ACOG recommends universal screening at 24 to 28 weeks of gestation, with earlier screening at the initial prenatal visit advised for individuals at risk of pregestational diabetes or early gestational diabetes.4 This recommendation includes those with prediabetes, history of gestational diabetes in a prior pregnancy, age ≥45 years, hypertension, or elevated BMI in the overweight or obese range, with one additional metabolic risk factor such as sedentary lifestyle or polycystic ovarian syndrome. Current screening guidelines for gestational diabetes recommend different BMI thresholds for screening depending on race and ethnicity. Screening is advised early for individuals of Asian descent if they have a BMI ≥23 kg/m2 along with at least one other risk factor (eg, history of gestational diabetes, sedentary lifestyle). Our findings indicate that the ACOG criteria for screening Asian American subjects may be too conservative given that at even a BMI of 20 kg/m2, the prevalence of gestational diabetes was >5%. Our findings also support the importance of clinicians appropriately counseling individuals before pregnancy regarding optimal weight and blood pressure, underscoring the importance of managing these potentially modifiable prepregnancy cardiometabolic risk factors to prevent gestational diabetes.

The current study extends prior smaller studies showing stronger associations between Asian ancestry and gestational diabetes compared with other race and ethnicity groups, regardless of prepregnancy BMI.9,11,14 This may be due to heterogeneity by ancestry in the association of insulin resistance and gestation.15 Individuals with a higher prepregnancy BMI may enter pregnancy with underlying insulin resistance and inadequate compensation by pancreatic beta cells for the metabolic demands of pregnancy.16,17 However, Asian American subjects were noted to have higher probability of gestational diabetes at any BMI. It is possible that Asian individuals have a higher percent body fat at a given BMI, raising the limitations of BMI in accurately capturing variations in fat distribution among individuals of different race and ethnicity groups.18 Ultimately, these findings highlight the importance of investigation into risk factors and causal mechanisms of gestational diabetes among Asian populations that extend beyond prepregnancy weight and its contribution to insulin resistance. We also underscore the importance of expanding gestational diabetes prevention trials beyond weight management, especially for Asian populations.19,20 Although prepregnancy BMI is a risk factor, the lower PAF for obesity for Asian American subjects compared with others suggests that alternative factors beyond maternal obesity are significant in the risk of gestational diabetes, and that future gestational diabetes prevention and management trials should consider new interventions and be inclusive of individuals of diverse backgrounds such as Asian ancestry.

Prior studies have also shown an association between prepregnancy hypertension and gestational diabetes.21,22 We find that this association is present and in fact stronger among Asian American subjects compared with other maternal race and ethnicity groups. Individuals with prepregnancy hypertension are more likely to have advanced maternal age and a higher BMI, which are established risk factors for gestational diabetes.23 Endothelial dysfunction may be a key mechanism linking prepregnancy hypertension, obesity, and gestational diabetes.24,25 In our study, on average, maternal age at first pregnancy in Asian American subjects was higher than that among other maternal race and ethnicity groups. Older maternal age at birth and increased propensity for cardiometabolic abnormalities with BMI may be potential contributors to higher prevalence of prepregnancy hypertension among Asian American subjects with gestational diabetes.

Lastly, in this nationally representative sample of all live singleton births, we confirmed a stronger association of gestational diabetes with BMI among Asian American individuals born outside the United States compared with those born inside the United States; this finding has previously been suggested by smaller prior studies.11,14 The reasons for higher prevalence of gestational diabetes by nativity likely relates to complex social and environmental contributors, epigenetic changes, or perhaps differences in genetic predisposition. For example, acculturation may lead to rapid exposure to a Westernized diet, changes in physical activity patterns, and increased stress, which could lead to insulin dysregulation.26 It is possible that intrauterine malnutrition may be implicated in the developmental origins of gestational diabetes through impaired beta-cell function, and it is possible that this may be more prevalent in countries outside the United States, reasons for which are unknown.27

Study Strengths and Limitations

Strengths of the current study are inclusion of a national sample of all singleton live births within the United States and disaggregation of Asian ancestry subgroups. Our study responds to recent calls to better capture the heterogenous experiences of Asian American individuals within the field of maternal health using individual Asian ancestry subgroups.28 We have also modeled maternal prepregnancy BMI as a continuous variable to show gestational diabetes risk across the full spectrum of BMI, thereby highlighting the risk of gestational diabetes for Asian populations.

Limitations that should be considered in this study include moderate sensitivity although high specificity of gestational diabetes on the birth certificate.29 However, this would be more likely to move associations toward the null, and we found high prevalence and association of prepregnancy cardiometabolic risk factors with gestational diabetes among Asian American subjects. In addition, it is possible that prepregnancy BMI on the birth certificates could differ from that on medical records, although the correlation coefficients between birth certificates and Women, Infants, and Children records are reported to be high at 0.95 for weight, 0.83 for height, and 0.93 for BMI,30 with good percent agreement.31 Lastly, given that the data were collected at a national level, granular data such as physical activity, dietary patterns, waist circumference, duration of stay in the United States, and glycosylated hemoglobin values are not available.

Conclusions

We observed that Asian individuals had a higher probability of gestational diabetes than any other maternal race and ethnicity groups across all ranges of prepregnancy BMI, including at a BMI <23 kg/m2, which is currently used as a threshold for clinical screening for gestational diabetes. In addition, Asian American individuals had a higher adjusted prevalence for gestational diabetes in the presence of prepregnancy chronic hypertension compared with other groups. Our findings highlight the critical role of optimizing prepregnancy cardiovascular health and mitigating risk factors as a potential mechanism to lower gestational diabetes risk. Consideration should also be given to conduct gestational diabetes screening in all Asian American individuals independent of BMI.

COMPETENCY IN MEDICAL KNOWLEDGE: Asian individuals have the highest prevalence of gestational diabetes at any given prepregnancy BMI and in the setting of hypertension.

TRANSLATIONAL OUTLOOK: Early screening for gestational diabetes should be considered for Asian individuals independent of maternal BMI and other risk factors. Risk for gestational diabetes among Asian individuals may not be as strongly associated with BMI compared with other race groups, and investigation of additional contributors to risk among Asian populations should be performed.
