Atrioesophageal Fistula: A Rare, Life-Threatening Complication of Pulmonary Vein Isolation
Authors: Katarina L Fabre, Mays T Ali, Peter A Noseworthy, John M Stulak, Sunil V Mankad
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractKeywords: Atrioesophageal fistula, Atrial fibrillation, Pericardial effusion, Pulmonary vein isolation

Introduction

Pulmonary vein isolation (PVI) is a common treatment strategy for atrial fibrillation refractory to medical therapy. Atrioesophageal fistula (AEF) is a rare complication of PVI, typically occurring 1 to 6 weeks after ablation, with an incidence between 0.1% and 0.25%.1 Symptoms are often nonspecific and include fever, chest pain, nausea, vomiting, hematemesis, dysphagia, and neurologic deficits.1 We discuss a case of AEF discovered in the context of hospitalization for symptoms of progressive dyspnea, chest pain, and fevers in a patient who underwent PVI 1 month prior. The patient subsequently developed mixed cardiogenic and septic shock and underwent computed tomography (CT) of the chest, which revealed an AEF. The standard approach to the management of AEF includes emergent surgical repair of the fistula. Complications of AEF include cerebrovascular accidents (CVAs) secondary to air emboli, food emboli, or septic emboli.2 This case illustrates the importance of keeping a high index of suspicion for AEF in patients who develop nonspecific symptoms of fevers and chest discomfort in the context of recent PVI.

Case Presentation

A 63-year-old man with a history of atrial fibrillation, heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and hypertension was admitted to our institution for acute heart failure exacerbation and atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular response. One month before admission, the patient underwent PVI for persistent atrial fibrillation at an outside hospital. Per outside records, the procedure was complicated by constrictive pericarditis, volume overload, and recurrence of atrial fibrillation.

On initial presentation to our hospital, the patient noted progressive dyspnea, chest pain, and fevers. Laboratory workup revealed hemoglobin of 12.1 g/dL, a white blood cell count of 6.7 x 109/L, and creatinine of 1.28 mg/dL. Baseline troponin T was 34 ng/L, with a 2-hour repeat value of 40 ng/L. N-terminal pro–brain natriuretic peptide was elevated at 3,641 pg/mL. C-reactive protein was elevated to 10.8 mg/L.

Electrocardiography revealed atrial fibrillation without evidence of ischemia. The patient underwent transthoracic echocardiography (TTE), which revealed a small posterior pericardial effusion (Figure 1, Video 1). There were respirophasic changes in septal motion as well as a dilated inferior vena cava with expiratory diastolic flow reversals in the hepatic veins concerning for constrictive hemodynamics (Figure 2, Video 2). Exaggerated respiratory flow variation in the peak transmitral Doppler was nondiagnostic because of the patient’s atrial fibrillation and beat-to-beat variation. Given the clinical presentation, elevated C-reactive protein, and findings on TTE of expiratory diastolic flow reversals in the hepatic veins, the leading diagnosis was pericarditis with a likely component of constriction, although etiology at the time of diagnosis was unclear. The patient was subsequently started on colchicine and broad-spectrum antibiotics as well as high-dose diuretics for mixed septic and cardiogenic shock complicated by recurrent pericarditis requiring intensive-level care.

There was initial alleviation of symptoms, and the patient was transferred to the general cardiology service. While eating dinner, the patient developed acute-onset respiratory distress and altered mental status requiring emergent intubation and transfer back to the cardiac intensive care unit. Blood cultures were obtained and positive for Candida albicans and Staphylococcus epidermitis.

The patient underwent repeat TTE, which revealed an enlarging pericardial effusion (Figure 3, Video 3) with new development of right ventricular systolic dysfunction (Video 4). Although the inferior vena cava was dilated without inspiratory collapse, there were no other echocardiographic findings to suggest cardiac tamponade. Repeat C-reactive protein was markedly elevated at 219.1 mg/L. Given the patient’s acute decompensation along with recent history of PVI, the patient underwent CT of the head, chest, abdomen, and pelvis, which revealed a small amount of nondependent air in the left atrium (LA) abutting the esophagus along with a new pericardial effusion concerning for AEF (Figures 4 and 5).

Serial two-dimensional TTE, parasternal long-axis diastolic view obtained following the aspiration event, demonstrates a larger pericardial effusion.

Nongated, noncontrast chest CT, sagittal view, demonstrates a pericardial effusion (red arrow) and nondependent air in the LA abutting the esophagus (yellow arrow), consistent with an AEF.

Nongated, noncontrast chest CT sequential axial (stack) views from superior (top left) to inferior (bottom right) demonstrates the extent of the air in the LA (arrow) as well as the pericardial effusion.

The patient was placed in the Trendelenburg position and initiated on broad-spectrum antibiotics, and he underwent emergent surgery for successful repair of the AEF related to recent PVI ablation.

Discussion

PVI is a common treatment for atrial fibrillation refractory to medical therapy; AEF is a rare complication of this treatment with an incidence rate of 0.1% to 0.25%.1 It typically occurs 1 to 6 weeks after PVI, and patients often present with nonspecific symptoms of fever, chest pain, nausea and vomiting, hematemesis, dysphagia, or neurologic deficits.1 The esophagus is most susceptible to perforation closest to areas of endocardial ablation and can often shift in position during ablation. AEF has been documented with all types of atrial fibrillation ablations. Typical mode of injury consists of damage to the anterior esophagus, creating a fistula into the pericardium or LA with bidirectional flow of esophageal contents into the pericardium or LA and blood flow into the esophagus.1

Although a rare complication of PVI, AEF throughout the literature appears to present similarly with nonspecific symptoms often attributed to infectious states, which quickly lead to the catastrophic complications such as CVA. Rapid clinical deterioration is not uncommon, and stroke symptoms may occur within the first 24 hours of fever onset.3 Our case and those throughout the literature further emphasize how critical it is that a high index of suspicion be held in the setting of atrial fibrillation ablation and the subsequent development of chest pain and constitutional symptoms.4 In our case, suspicion for AEF peaked after the episode of respiratory distress and altered mental status while eating, which was the likely timing of the patient’s CVA, which prompted chest CT. For early diagnostic studies, if AEF is suspected, a white blood cell count is a sensitive value along with blood cultures. Given presenting symptoms of chest pain and dyspnea, patients will often undergo TTE as the initial imaging study. It is important for the echocardiographer to identify findings suggestive of pericarditis, constriction, and/or tamponade. If any of these two-dimensional or Doppler echocardiographic findings are present in a patient who has recently undergone PVI, there should be high clinical suspicion for AEF, and further diagnostic testing is warranted. The reference standard for diagnosing AEF noninvasively is CT of the esophagus.1 Findings on contrast-enhanced CT consistent with AEF include free air in the LA, mediastinum, or pericardium; left atrial changes including thrombus or diverticulum; mediastinal inflammation; esophageal perforation or thickening; and the presence of a fistula tract.4 Careful review of previously obtained computed tomographic examinations or serial chest CT days later should be performed, as findings may be subtle and overlooked, or the AEF may progress. Importantly, if AEF is suspected, endoscopy should be deferred, as air insufflation with esophageal endoscopy could lead to air embolism delivered directly into the LA, resulting in stroke or death.1

Once the diagnosis is made by CT, surgery must be performed, as the mortality is 100% without surgical repair.2 Complications of AEF include CVA and can lead to death in 80% of cases.5 This case stresses the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion in patients presenting with nonspecific malaise, chest pain, bacteremia, and candidemia, and pericardial effusion in the context of recent PVI leading to AEF. Early identification via CT is imperative for early surgical repair in the hope of decreasing the risk for detrimental outcomes, including cerebrovascular events.

The likely etiology of the patient’s pericarditis and pericardial effusion was thought to be secondary to development of the AEF after PVI and the progressive inflammatory response. The patient underwent emergent surgical repair of the AEF. The clinical course was complicated by multiple ischemic strokes. The patient was subsequently transitioned to comfort measures and expired.

Conclusion

PVI is a common procedure performed for atrial fibrillation that is refractory to medical therapy. AEFs are a rare complication of these ablation procedures and have a 100% mortality rate if not treated with surgical repair. A high index of suspicion should be maintained in patients with nonspecific symptoms of fever, bacteremia, chest pain, and pericardial effusion in the context of recent ablation, and patients should undergo chest CT if clinical suspicion remains high.
