In vivo immune cell engineering from bench to clinical reality
Authors: Hui Yu, Maosen Han, Xiaotian Zhao, Chunwei Tang, Yanda Jia, Weiqiang Jing, Xinyi Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Adoptive immune cell therapies, exemplified by chimeric antigen receptor T cells, have transformed the treatment of hematological malignancies. However, their broader clinical application is limited by complex ex vivo manufacturing, high cost, and safety concerns. In vivo immune cell engineering has emerged as an alternative strategy that delivers genetic instructions directly to immune cells, thereby generating or modulating therapeutic immune cells within the body and reducing the reliance on individualized in vitro operations. These advances underscore the need for a systematic evaluation of this emerging field. Therefore, this review systematically summarizes the mechanistic principles and delivery strategies underlying in vivo immune cell engineering, with an emphasis on in vivo CAR-T cell generation and the engineering of other immune cells. We then discuss major viral and non-viral delivery platforms and clarify how these platforms influence cargo delivery, cell specificity, and functional immune-cell programming. We further discuss recent preclinical and emerging clinical advances across cancer, autoimmune diseases, and degenerative diseases, while examining key translational challenges, including delivery specificity, off-target effects, controllability, persistence, and manufacturing standardization. Overall, although the field of in vivo immune cell engineering is advancing rapidly, its clinical success will depend on coordinated improvements in delivery precision, therapeutic efficacy, safety, and controllable immune-cell programming.

Introduction

The immune system is an important defense line of the human body, capable of identifying and responding to the invasion of pathogens and tumors [1,2]. T lymphocytes, macrophages (MΦ), natural killer （NK）cells , and other subsets of immune cells play a crucial role in immune surveillance [1,3,4] and the elimination of abnormal cells [5]. In the past decade, the precise regulation of the functions of these immune cells has attracted much attention in the treatment of various major diseases such as cancer [6], autoimmune diseases [7] and degenerative diseases [8]. Traditional ex vivo immune cell therapy, such as chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR-T) therapy have achieved significant breakthroughs in hematological malignancies by enabling the tracking and specific killing of tumor cells [9,10]. However, clinical data indicate that a proportion of patients experience disease recurrence within one year after treatment [11,12]. In addition, conventional CAR-T cell manufacturing requires individualized and multi-step ex vivo procedures [13], including cell isolation, activation, genetic modification, expansion, quality control, and reinfusion [14]. These complex processes demand specialized equipment, high costs [15], and may be associated with safety risks such as cytokine release syndrome [16]. Therefore, exploring safer and more efficient immune-cell therapies has become the focus and cutting-edge direction of research in the field of disease treatment.

In recent years, the breakthrough advancements in gene delivery technology have transformed our ability to regulate the functions of immune cells [17]. Non-viral vectors [18] or viral vectors [19] have been developed to deliver genetic cargo or genome-editing tools to target areas such as the spleen or lymph nodes in the body [20]. These vectors can enter immune cells and release genetic cargo or editing components, enabling them to express key molecules such as CAR/TCR, thereby achieving in situ modification of immune cells [21,22]. As shown in Fig. 1, ex vivo immune cell engineering typically involves steps such as peripheral blood cell collection, activation and expansion, gene editing, and reinfusion. The advantage lies in strong controllability, but the manufacturing process is long and the cost and time burden are significant. In contrast, in vivo immune cell engineering delivers vectors systemically or locally to target immune-cell populations, allowing cellular uptake, intracellular release, and therapeutic genetic programming to occur directly within the patient. These engineered immune cells will be activated and proliferate abundantly within the body, demonstrating the ability to efficiently identify and eliminate tumor cells. This breakthrough has opened up new research directions for immune cell therapy. In parallel, the development of base editing [23] and prime editing [24] provide genome-editing approaches that reduce reliance on DNA double-strand breaks, thereby potentially improving the safety and clinical feasibility of in vivo immune cell editing [25]. In 2025, multiple studies reported strategies for the in situ generation of CAR-T cells [[26], [27], [28]]. This emerging strategy has potential to shorten the manufacturing process and is expected to solve the common clinical problems encountered in ex vivo immune cell engineering. Furthermore, a complete immune system can more effectively facilitate the spread of epitopes and trigger a strong anti-tumor immune response [29,30].Fig. 1Processes of ex vivo and in vivo immune cell engineering.

In vivo cell engineering

In vivo T cell engineering

In recent years, in vivo T cell engineering therapy has emerged as a frontier strategy for advancing immunotherapy [22]. Its central aim is to deliver genetic cargos directly to endogenous T cells, thereby reprogramming their phenotype, antigen specificity, or effector functions in situ to enhance, redirect, or reset immune responses in different disease contexts [39]. Among the reported treatment modalities, in vivo CAR-T cell therapy is the most widely studied form of in vivo T cell editing [40]. This technology may significantly simplify the production process, reduce costs and shorten the treatment time, addressing the challenges faced by current ex vivo CAR-T cell therapies [41].

To further enhance the safety, efficacy and precision of in vivo CAR-T therapy, the design concepts in recent years no longer merely focus on enhancing the activation effect, but rather place greater emphasis on conditional activation, in order to better distinguish tumor tissues from normal tissues. This is particularly critical in vivo, because vector biodistribution and the resulting off-target exposure are more difficult to precisely control than in ex vivo manufacturing. Therefore, researchers proposed the logic-gated CAR design, coupling the activation of T cells with combinatorial antigen sensing, in order to enhance the ability to distinguish tumor tissues from normal tissues [42]. The “AND gate” design requires T cells to simultaneously recognize two different tumor antigens in order to activate the killing function [43]. For example, the logic-gated intracellular network (LINK) CAR created by pairing LAT- and SLP-76-based receptors may reduce on-target, off-tumor toxicity caused by single-antigen expression on normal cells [44,45]. The “NOT gate” actively inhibits T cell activity by co-expressing an inhibitory CAR (iCAR) when a specific antigen of normal tissues is detected, thereby achieving the protection of healthy cells [46]. This multi-signaling activation mechanism significantly improves the safety and precision of tumor recognition [47,48]. The introduction of logical gating further optimizes the function of CAR-T cells and may enhance their therapeutic window in vivo. However, their application in vivo will require careful optimization of vector capacity, co-delivery efficiency, and the duration of each signaling module, because these factors can substantially influence both antitumor activity and safety [49,50].

Meanwhile, the tumor microenvironment (TME) is regarded as a key factor influencing the efficacy of in vivo CAR-T cells [51]. Therefore, a variety of strategies to engineer CAR-T cells to resist or remodel the TME have been explored, including switch receptors that convert inhibitory signals into activating outputs, CARs designed to reshape the TME, and synthetic receptors that couple tumor antigen recognition to conditional cytokine signaling. For instance, engineering T cells with modified TGF-β receptor signaling modules can attenuate TGF-β-mediated immunosuppression and enhance T-cell activation and function [52]. In addition, cytokine-receptor modules introduced into CAR-T cells can reshape local immune signaling and, under defined conditions, improve T-cell persistence and antitumor activity [53]. Synthetic receptor systems that link tumor antigen recognition to cytokine signaling, such as IL-15-related pathways, may further support T-cell expansion and survival within suppressive tumor environments [54]. These advances indicate that the design of in vivo CAR-T cell therapy is shifting from solely pursuing targeted killing efficiency to constructing a multifunctional integrated system [55]. However, the in vivo application of these strategies will require careful control of delivery specificity [56], expression intensity [57], and duration to balance antitumor efficacy with safety [58].

Overall, although there are still many challenges in vivo T cell engineering technology, with the progress of gene editing technology and the continuous optimization of delivery systems, this technology is expected to bring substantial progress to disease treatment [59]. In addition, expanding the application of treatment, integrating advanced technologies, addressing the mechanisms of drug resistance, and considering regulatory and ethical impacts will be critical to the future success of this field.

In vivo macrophage engineering

MΦ are a key component of the immune system, possessing multiple functions such as phagocytosis, digestion, and antigen presentation, thereby forming a crucial bridge between innate and adaptive immunity [60,61]. The diverse and highly plastic functions of MΦ make them excellent “basic cells” for immunotherapy [62]. However, MΦ are frequently driven toward an immunosuppressive, pro-tumoral state by cytokine cues such as IL-4, IL-13 [63], IL-10 [64], and M-CSF [65] within the TME, which may impair antigen presentation and anti-tumor phagocytic activity. Therefore, identifying and blocking these signals on MΦ is a strategy to enhance anti-tumor phagocytic effects. Studies have shown that CD11b through activating Src-Syk promotes the degradation of Cbl-b-mediated MyD88 and TRIF, negatively regulating TLR signals, providing a new idea for regulating the inflammatory function of MΦ [66]. In recent years, researchers from Stanford University have successively discovered MΦ phagocytosis inhibition signaling pathways such as CD47-SIRPα [67], β2M-LILRB1 [68], and CD24-Siglec -10 [69], expanding the regulatory modules for reshaping the tumor phagocytic ability of MΦ. More importantly, the understanding at the mechanism level can be further translated into specific directions for in vivo engineering. Editing or reconfiguration can be carried out around key inhibitory receptors and their intracellular signaling nodes, and regulatory elements with temporal and spatial controllability can be introduced to limit the effect to the tumor area as much as possible. At the same time, it can be combined with targeted delivery strategies for MΦ to ensure both efficacy and safety. Systematically reviewing the key nodes with editing potential and establishing precise and locally controllable delivery and expression plans is a crucial step in promoting the clinical translation of in vivo MΦ engineering.

Chen et al. reported an intracavitary in situ programming strategy in which an extracellular matrix–mimicking hydrogel enables localized gene delivery to MΦ infiltrating the resection cavity, thereby generating GSC-targeting CAR-MΦ with enhanced phagocytic and antigen-presenting capacity [70]. Meanwhile, Huang et al. used a CD206-targeted small extracellular vesicle delivery strategy that was administered by inhalation and enriched the lungs, specifically transducing MΦ to achieve efficient in situ generation of CAR-MΦ. This method enhances the phagocytic function and pro-inflammatory response of MΦ, thereby improving the anti-tumor efficacy, reducing lung metastasis, prolonging survival, and lowering the risk of recurrence [71]. These preclinical studies collectively demonstrate the feasibility and therapeutic promise of in vivo MΦ engineering. They also emphasize that future developments will depend on improving the efficiency of in vivo delivery, enhancing spatial and temporal control, and identifying measurable clinical and immunological indicators that can link the reprogramming of MΦ to patient benefits.

With the continuous development of foundational theories, technological methodologies and clinical medical exploration studies such as induced pluripotent stem cell engineering approaches, immune cell therapy, and in situ gene editing, in vivo MΦ engineering therapy is expected to bring clinical potential in the treatment of solid tumors and autoimmune diseases.

In vivo natural killer cell engineering

Natural killer (NK) cells are a type of powerful immune cell that can quickly identify and eliminate pathogens [72]. In vivo NK cell engineering refers to the strategy of directly delivering genetic cargos or genome-editing tools into the host's body to reprogram the endogenous NK cells. This approach is expected to simplify the manufacturing process and increase accessibility. However, compared to T cells, genetic manipulation of NK cells in the body remains challenging. CAR-NK cells are generally considered to have favorable safety features, including a lower risk of severe immune-related toxicity than some CAR-T-cell approaches [73]. Nevertheless, their relatively short lifespan may limit persistence and antitumor durability, indicating that future in vivo NK cell engineering strategies must carefully optimize both effector function and functional persistence [74].

Due to the limitations of delivery and in vivo persistence, current studies typically use in vitro screening to identify regulatory factors that can significantly enhance the effector function of NK cells. A research team from The University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center developed a genome-wide CRISPR knockout screen for 19,281 genes, systematically knocking out each of these target genes in primary NK cells. The engineered NK cells were tested for their ability to combat cancer cells. The research found that the absence of MED12, ARIH2 or CCNC enhances the response ability of NK cells to various human tumors with drug resistance. In vitro experiments and animal models demonstrated that the genetically edited NK cells exhibited stronger antitumor activity. Further analysis revealed that CRISPR editing enhances the innate immunity of NK cells and the effector functions mediated by CAR, which were associated with increased metabolic adaptability, elevated pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion, and the expansion of cytotoxic NK cell subpopulations. The study identified key molecules that regulate NK cell function, providing candidate targets for future in vivo NK-cell engineering and the development of more effective NK-cell therapies [75]. However, their translation into true in vivo NK-cell engineering will require delivery systems capable of achieving efficient, selective, and safe genetic modulation of endogenous NK cells.

In recent years, the clinical translation of CAR-NK-cell products has accelerated, with ongoing studies using diverse cell sources, including peripheral blood, umbilical cord blood, and iPSC-derived NK cells, as well as modular CAR designs targeting different tumor antigens [76]. At the same time, through multi-gene editing and synthetic biology strategies, the receptors and auxiliary means of in vivo CAR-NK have been continuously optimized to enhance the effector function and in vivo persistence [77]. It is worth noting that knocking out CISH in iPSC-derived NK cells (iPSC-NK) has been shown to induce metabolic reprogramming and enhance anti-tumor activity [78]. However, as of now, research in the in vivo engineering of CAR-NK cells is still at an early stage. Andorko et al. reported a proof-of-concept study showing that intravenous administration of a CD7-targeted lentiviral vector encoding an anti-CD20 CAR generated CAR-NK cells in situ and mediated antitumor activity against CD20-positive tumor cells in mice [37]. These findings suggest the feasibility of generating CAR-NK cells directly in vivo, but further optimization of NK-cell-selective targeting, vector safety, transgene persistence, and off-target transduction will be required for broader clinical application.

In vivo engineering of other immune cells

In addition to T cells, MΦ and NK cells, in vivo immune cell engineering is expanding to additional key immune cell subsets, including B cells, antigen-presenting cells that initiate adaptive immunity, and HSPCs/HSCs that enable sustained immune reconstitution. Among them, B cells are the main effector cells of humoral immunity and are involved in antigen presentation, immune regulation, the occurrence of autoimmune diseases, and the formation of B cell malignancies. In this context, B cells can either serve as therapeutic targets or be directly genetically engineered. For example, in vivo generation of CD19-targeted CAR-T cells can selectively eliminate abnormal or malignant B cells, thereby promoting immune resetting or disease control [28]. In parallel, some cutting-edge research has begun to explore the use of viral vectors [79] or LNPs [80] to directly deliver genetic cargos or genome-editing tools to B cells in vivo, for correcting genetic defects or endowing B cells with the ability to stably secrete therapeutic antibodies.

Among antigen-presenting cells, dendritic cells (DCs), especially cDC 1/cDC 2 subsets involved in antigen presentation and T-cell priming, have received extensive attention due to their crucial role in the initiation stage of the adaptive immune response. Targeted delivery mediated by receptors such as DEC-205 can enhance the loading and immune activation of DCs [80]. In addition, LNP-based systems have been explored to deliver CRISPR components to in vivo DCs. For example, PD-L1 disruption in DCs can relieve inhibitory PD-1/PD-L1 signaling and enhance DC-mediated antitumor T-cell responses [38]. The further development of this technology requires more precise targeting of DC subpopulations, fine regulation of immune activation, and strict assessment of non-target delivery, genome editing, and inflammatory toxicity issues.

Delivery system

The requirements for the delivery system for in vivo immune cell engineering are much higher than those for traditional gene delivery or vaccine applications [31]. An ideal delivery vector not only needs to achieve specific targeting of immune cells in the complex in vivo environment, but also must efficiently deliver genetic cargos or genome-editing tools, and minimize non-specific editing of non-immune tissues and systemic toxicity [81]. Therefore, the selection and optimization of the delivery system is one of the core bottlenecks for the clinical translation of in vivo immune cell engineering.

Currently, the delivery strategies for in vivo immune cell engineering can be broadly classified into viral vector delivery systems and non-viral vector delivery systems. As shown in Fig. 2, viral vectors usually rely on receptors for endocytosis and escape from endosomes and deliver genetic cargos to the nucleus to enable relatively durable transgene expression. In contrast, non-viral vectors such as LNPs mostly release RNA in the cytoplasm and mainly achieve expression through translation, with expression being more transient and easier to achieve dose regulation. This distinction is not only a technical feature of the vector, but also a key determinant of the therapeutic window. Durable expression may be advantageous when long-term immune surveillance is required, whereas transient expression may be preferable when reversible immune modulation or reduced long-term toxicity is desired. Therefore, the choice between transient and durable expression systems should be matched to the biological timescale and safety requirements of each disease context.Fig. 2Mechanisms of viral and non-viral vector-mediated immune cell engineering. However, different delivery platforms have significant differences in terms of immune cell accessibility, editing efficiency, safety, and clinical scalability [82]. Importantly, currently reported delivery efficiencies are highly context-dependent and cannot be directly compared across studies without considering species, administration route, vector dose, cargo format, target-cell definition, tissue source, and detection method. For example, antibody-targeted LNPs can induce transient CAR expression in a defined fraction of circulating T cells, whereas myeloid-cell-directed nanoparticles or extracellular vesicles often show higher uptake in MΦ and dendritic cells due to their intrinsic phagocytic activity. By contrast, efficient in vivo engineering of non-phagocytic lymphocytes, particularly NK cells, remains at an earlier stage, and quantitative transduction or editing efficiencies are less consistently reported. Therefore, in addition to describing delivery platforms qualitatively, representative quantitative benchmarks are needed to evaluate the translational maturity of different in vivo immune-cell engineering strategies.

Viral vector delivery system

Adeno-associated virus vectors

Adeno-associated virus (AAV) vectors are established gene-delivery platforms with relatively favorable safety profiles and the potential for long-term transgene expression. Nevertheless, their use in vivo immune cell engineering is limited by restricted packaging capacity [83], low targeting efficiency [84], and significant non-targeted organ enrichment [85]. These limitations have prompted the development of engineered AAV capsids with enhanced specificity and reduced transduction ability to non-target organs. Deverman et al. have developed a general machine learning method called Fit4Function for systematic design of multi-feature AAV capsids. By utilizing a library of AAV capsids with uniformly sampled manufacturable sequence spaces, this method generates reproducible screening data to train accurate sequence-to-function models, thus aiding the design of AAV capsids with various desirable characteristics, such as the ability to deliver genes to specific organs or across multiple species [86]. Lu et al. reported an AAV vector (AAV6-M2) for in vivo T cell targeting. In animal models, it demonstrated significantly improved T cell transduction efficiency compared to wild-type AAV, and reduced liver transduction [87].

Despite these advances, current AAV studies still mainly focus on transduction efficiency [88] and tissue tropism [89]. However, systematic evaluations regarding the specificity of in vivo gene editing, the risk of off-target editing, and long-term safety are still insufficient. The future development of the AAV platforms should balance the targeting specificity of immune cells, safety, and the multi-component editing system. At the same time, high-throughput sequencing, biological distribution analysis, and data-driven capsid design can help improvevector specificity and support a more comprehensive safety assessment.

Lentiviral vectors

Lentiviral vectorsare widely used in tumor immunotherapy and immune-cell engineering because of their efficient transduction capacity and ability to support durable transgene expression [79]. Compared to AAV, lentiviral vectors can integrate transgenes into the host genome, support continuous expression and are widely used in domains such as immune cell gene modification and CAR-T cell therapy [90]. These vectors are commonly derived from HIV-1 and engineered as replication-defective systems, enabling efficient target-cell transduction while minimizing the risk of productive viral replication [79]. Currently, lentiviral vectors are employed in several FDA-approved CAR-T therapies as well as in multiple ongoing in vivo CAR-T clinical trials. For example, Mei et al. from Tongji Hospital reported research demonstrating that lentiviral vectors can be used to directly infect T cells in patients, enabling the in situ generation of CAR-T cells [91].

Although lentiviral vectors have many advantages, their genomic integration property may pose an irreversible genetic toxicity risk during in vivo editing, which is particularly relevant in non-malignant diseases or reversible immune regulation applications. Therefore, although lentiviral vectors have mature technologies and clinical foundations in the field of immune cell engineering, their application to in vivo immune cell engineering still requires careful safety evaluation.

Non-viral vector delivery system

Lipid nanoparticles

Lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) have become one of the most clinically advanced non-viral nucleic acid delivery platforms with the highest degree of clinical translation at present and are gradually being explored for in vivo immune cell engineering-related applications. Due to their excellent biocompatibility [92], tunable size and surface characteristics [93], and ability to efficiently deliver mRNA [94], siRNA [95], and genetic cargos or genome-editing tools to target cells [96], LNPs play a crucial role in in vivo immune cell engineering. Although LNPs demonstrate high delivery efficiency in hepatocytes and phagocytes, there are still significant technical challenges in achieving precise delivery of specific immune cell subsets in vivo, and this highly depends on the specific application scenario.

One of the core advantages of LNPs is their tunable chemical composition. By regulating the lipid formulation, the apparent pKa of ionizable lipids, and the degree of PEGylation, the in vivo circulation time, cellular uptake efficiency, and endosomal escape efficiency of LNPs can be modulated [97]. Surface modification strategies based on antibodies or ligands can further enhance cell-type-specific binding and uptake. For example, targeting ligands can be conjugated to the surface of LNPs to selectively interact with specific cell populations, including immune-cell subsets, alveolar epithelial cells, or tumor cells [98].

Recent studies have shown that by systematically regulating the components of LNPs, it is possible to alter their biodistribution at the organ level. For instance, increasing the proportion of cationic lipids enhances membrane fusion efficiency, while incorporating polyethylene glycol (PEG) extends the drug's circulation time in vivo [99], suggesting that achieving organ-selective delivery through formulation engineering is feasible. The research team from Cornell University achieved efficient spleen-enriched mRNA delivery and expression by substituting cholesterol and PEG-lipids in traditional LNPs with zwitterionic pyridine carboxybetaine ionizable lipids[100]. Cheng et al. developed a novel biomaterial by integrating peptides with ionizable lipids, thereby enabling organ selective and adjustable mRNA delivery [101]. However, it is important to note that spleen enrichment does not equate to the specific delivery to immune cell subsets; actual uptake often mainly occurs in MΦ and dendritic cells within the reticuloendothelial system [102]. Thus, these studies highlight both the flexibility of LNPs formulation design and the persistent challenge of translating broad organ tropism into precise immune-cell targeting.

With continued advancements in LNPs technology, including improved stability [103], enhanced organ- and tissue-specific targeting [98], and controlled release mechanisms [104], the therapeutic potential will significantly increase. However, the wide application of LNPs to in vivo immune cell engineering is still limited by several key bottlenecks, including inefficient uptake by non-phagocytic lymphocytes, limited endosomal escape after immune-cell uptake, and the risk of non-specific immune activation. Therefore, the current advantages of LNPs mainly lie in the transient gene regulation of innate immune cell populations, rather than the precise and long-lasting gene editing of adaptive immune cells. In the future, breaking through these limitations will be the key to promoting the development of the next generation of LNP-based in vivo immunoengineering strategies.

Polymeric nanocarriers

Polymer nanoparticles can be chemically modified to precisely control their hydrophobic/hydrophilic balance, surface charge, and physical structure, thereby meeting the delivery requirements of editing payloads such as mRNA [105]. These nanoparticles have been explored in the field of in vivo immune cell engineering [106]. Notably, studies have reported that when cationic poly (β-amino ester) nanoparticles are administered intravenously, they can preferentially accumulate in F4/80+ MΦ and CD11c+ DCs in the liver and spleen [38]. These poly (β-amino ester) nanoparticles delivered Cas9 mRNA and PD-1-targeting sgRNA to induce PD-1 knockout-mediated antitumor activity, while showing a favorable preliminary safety profile with no detectable complement activation, hemolysis, or cytokine storm. Additionally, the ionizable PAMAM derivative can be injected subcutaneously to efficiently deliver to CD11c+ DCs locally, and deliver base editor mRNA/β2M sgRNA to the lymph nodes to achieve a 25% editing efficiency, regulating antigen presentation, showing good local tolerance and systemic safety [107]. These examples highlight the potential of polymer nanocarriers in achieving programmable release of payloads, regulating biological distribution, and facilitating in vivo degradation, thereby improving the overall efficiency and safety of in vivo delivery.

Mechanistically, cationic polymers self-assemble into nanoparticles through electrostatic interactions with negatively charged nucleic acids such as mRNA [108]. However, efficient encapsulation often requires an excess of polymer, which can yield positively charged particles and increase the risk of immunotoxicity and non-specific interactions in vivo. Therefore, the design of polymer nanocarriers for in vivo immune cell engineering should balance delivery efficiency with reduced immunotoxicity [109]. Common strategies include tuning polymer molecular weight, optimizing polymer architecture, and introducing functional group modifications [110].

Future development of polymeric nanocarriers will focus on deepening our understanding of their structure–function relationships and the mechanisms underlying their interactions with cells and tissues in physiological environments [111]. This will allow more efficient and safer systems for the delivery of mRNA to be discovered, and will subsequently facilitate the clinical translation and expanded applications of mRNA therapeutics in the future.

Other delivery systems

In addition to the above delivery systems, emerging delivery platforms such as extracellular vesicles, inorganic nanomaterials, cell-derived carriers, and injectable biomaterials provide additional options for in vivo immune cell engineering [112]. Studies have shown that genetically engineering the surface of exosomes or loading specific ligands, the affinity of exosomes to specific immune cells can be enhanced, may improve immune-cell binding and uptake [21]. Inorganic nanomaterials (such as gold nanoparticles or silica nanoparticles) can be precisely controlled in terms of size, surface chemical properties, and release behavior, enabling customized delivery of genetic cargos or editing components [113]. However, their biodegradability, long-term accumulation and inflammatory potential should be systematically evaluated. Meanwhile, cell-mediated and tissue-targeted delivery strategies, such as red blood cell-carrying delivery or injectable hydrogel reservoirs, have also been employed to improve biodistribution, prolong local exposure time, and reduce systemic toxicity. Overall, these emerging delivery systems offer a greater variety of options for in vivo immune cell engineering, demonstrating the continuous integration of materials science, immunotherapy, and translational medicine in the development of immune-cell engineering strategies.

From a translational perspective, targeted LNPs and engineered lentiviral vectors are currently the most clinically advanced delivery platforms for in vivo CAR engineering. LNPs offer scalable manufacturing, transient and dose-adjustable expression, and a relatively favorable safety profile, making them particularly attractive for applications requiring reversible immune modulation, such as autoimmune diseases. By contrast, lentiviral vectors have a stronger clinical foundation in ex vivo CAR-T manufacturing and can support more durable CAR expression, which may be advantageous for malignancies requiring sustained immune surveillance. However, their integrating nature raises concerns regarding insertional mutagenesis, off-target transduction, and long-term safety [35]. Therefore, the clinical translatability of each platform should be judged according to the target disease, required duration of CAR expression, target-cell specificity, manufacturability, and safety window.

Therapeutic applications of in vivo immune cell engineering

Neoplastic diseases

Solid tumors

Solid tumors remain one of the most challenging problems in the field of medicine, mainly due to their complex microenvironments and mechanisms of immune evasion [114]. Although surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immune checkpoint blockade have improved outcomes in selected tumor types, long-lasting responses are still limited for many advanced or refractory solid tumors. Therefore, advancements in tumor immunology, gene delivery, and synthetic receptors have facilitated the development of more precise and personalized strategies, including in vivo engineering of immune cells, to redirect or reconfigure the endogenous immune cells within the tumor lesion.

At present, CAR-based cellular immunotherapy has achieved significant clinical efficacy in hematological malignancies, thereby prompting researchers to explore further application of the CAR strategy to solid tumors [115]. However, this therapy has target deficiency in the treatment of solid tumors, and there are issues such as tumor heterogeneity and safety problems [116,117]. More importantly, solid tumors are substantially more difficult to treat than hematologic malignancies because engineered immune cells must function within a hostile tumor microenvironment rather than simply encounter malignant cells in relatively accessible blood, bone marrow, or lymphoid compartments. Abnormal vasculature and dense stromal barriers restrict immune-cell trafficking and intratumoral penetration, whereas hypoxia, immunosuppressive cytokines, inhibitory ligands, and metabolic competition for oxygen and nutrients weaken cell persistence and effector activity [118]. Therefore, effective in vivo immune cell engineering for solid tumors must overcome not only antigen heterogeneity, but also the physical, immunological, and metabolic barriers that limit immune-cell function within tumor lesions. These challenges have driven the development of safer and more effective cellular immunotherapy strategies capable of improving tumor infiltration, resisting immunosuppression, and remodeling the tumor microenvironment.

As a new form of cellular immunotherapy, CAR-MΦ may offer distinct advantages over CAR-T and CAR-NK therapies in the treatment of solid tumors [118]. From a mechanistic perspective, MΦ have the characteristics of chemotaxis and tissue residence, and are more likely to infiltrate areas with insufficient blood supply and dense stroma, improving tumor accessibility [119]. Secondly, after CAR recognizes the target antigen, CAR-MΦ can trigger clearance effects, which is different from the killing mode of CAR-T that mainly releases cytotoxic substances [120]. Moreover, MΦ as professional antigen-presenting cells, can process and present antigens through MHC-I/MHC-II, and secrete chemokines to recruit endogenous T cells, promoting epitope spreading, thereby extending the single-target effect to a broader anti-tumor immune response, which helps to alleviate antigen escape [121].

In solid tumors, the therapeutic activity of CAR-MΦ depends not only on antigen recognition and tumor infiltration, but also on their ability to maintain function within a hostile tumor microenvironment [122]. Although MΦ are adapted to inflamed and stroma-rich tissues, hypoxia, metabolic stress, and inhibitory ligands may still impair their phagocytic activity and pro-inflammatory polarization. The required magnitude and duration of CAR expression in solid tumors should be considered in relation to the hostile tumor microenvironment [36]. Therefore, in vivo engineering of MΦ should aim to maintain local anti-tumor activity while avoiding excessive immune activation and systemic toxicity. Based on the above approach, Jing et al. have developed an innovative in situ CAR-MΦ cell therapy. By in situ engineering chimeric IL-2 receptors to enhance CAR-MΦ tumor-killing activity, this approach targets renal cell carcinoma treatment. This strategy not only improved the therapeutic persistence and efficacy of CAR-MΦ, but also provided a new approach for the clinical translation of solid tumor immunotherapy [123].

As the technical pathway matures, the clinical translation of CAR-MΦ therapy is accelerating. Preclinical studies have validated the anti-tumor activity of CAR-MΦ in over ten solid tumor models, including ovarian cancer, breast cancer, and glioblastoma. Hematologic malignancies

At present, recurrence and resistance remain major clinical challenges for hematological malignancies. Autologous CAR-T therapy has transformedtreatment landscape for these patients and multiple products have been approved for various indications [124]. However, some patients are unable to benefit from these treatments due to the complexity of product preparation, high costs, or the rapid progression of their disease [125]. UB-VV111 (Umoja Biopharmaceuticals) is an example of an in vivo CAR-T-cell therapy in the clinical stage and is being evaluated for the treatment of specific relapsed/refractory B-cell malignancies. Mechanistically, UB-VV111 employs a lentiviral vector delivery system, aiming to transduce endogenous T cells in vivo, enabling the expression of functional modules such as an anti-CD19 CAR together with a rapamycin-activated cytokine receptor module. This approach is designed to transduce endogenous T cells and generate CD19-directed CAR-T cells in vivo; however, its clinical efficacy, persistence, and long-term safety remain under investigation [126]. Recent clinical advances further support the translational potential of in vivo CAR engineering for hematological malignancies. Immune-evasive lentiviral vectors and targeted LNPs represent two major platform directions for generating CAR-T cells directly within the body, thereby reducing reliance on individualized ex vivo manufacturing and potentially improving treatment accessibility. Nevertheless, successful translation will require further clarification of T-cell-selective delivery, tumor-antigen specificity, CAR expression kinetics, functional persistence, and long-term safety [127].

Autoimmune diseases

As investigations into the mechanisms of autoimmune disease pathogenesis have intensified, scientists begin to recognize that there was a strong association between abnormal B cell and plasma cell activation and autoimmune disease development [128]. The early intervention measures targeting B cells were based on the clinical experience with anti-CD20 monoclonal antibodies (such as rituximab). However, due to its inability to eliminate persistent or long-lived plasma cells, it limits its wide clinical application [129]. In this situation, in vivo CAR-T technology targeting B-cell lineage antigens (particularly CD19) has become a highly promising strategy. It can eliminate B cells expressing CD19 and specific precursor cell populations, thereby reducing the pool of self-reactive B cells and indirectly reducing the production of pathogenic antibodies. It is worth noting that although CD19-targeted strategies may not directly eliminate long-lived plasma cells, they can still promote disease control through a broader impact on B-cell antigen presentation and immune reconstitution, potentially achieving immune resetting in autoreactive immune environments [130].

This immune-resetting also implies a different requirement for CAR expression persistence. Unlike solid tumors, autoimmune diseases may not require continuous cytotoxic surveillance. Instead, transient but sufficiently strong CAR expression may be preferable if it achieves deep depletion of pathogenic or autoreactive B-cell compartments and permits subsequent immune reconstitution [131]. Excessively durable CAR expression in this setting may prolong B-cell aplasia, impair humoral immunity, increase infection risk, and convert a reversible immunomodulatory intervention into long-term immune ablation. Therefore, mRNA/LNP-based transient systems, self-limiting, or repeat-dosed systems may be particularly attractive for autoimmune indications, whereas integrating or highly durable systems should be used more cautiously in non-malignant diseases.

Based on this, Aghajanian et al. reported a targeted lipid nanoparticle for mRNA delivery, which can generate CD8+ T cells expressing CAR in vivo. In preclinical studies, this method produced functional CAR-T cells that could recognize and eliminate B cells, and achieved deep B-cell clearance in cynomolgus monkeys, accompanied by immunological features similar to immune resetting [18]. These findings support the feasibility of in vivo CAR-T generation as a translational platform for autoimmune diseases.

More recently, early clinical evidence further extended this concept to refractory systemic lupus erythematosus. In this study, LNP-mediated in vivo generation of CD19 CAR-T cells led to B-cell depletion and reduced disease activity in patients with refractory systemic lupus erythematosus, with no major toxic effects reported during early observation [28]. This clinical evidence suggests that in vivo CAR-T cell engineering may move beyond preclinical proof-of-concept toward early therapeutic application in autoimmune diseases. Nevertheless, the number of treated patients remains limited, and longer follow-up is still required to evaluate the durability of immune resetting, B-cell reconstitution, infection risk, repeat-dosing feasibility, and delayed toxicity [132]. Further studies in broader autoimmune indications will also be needed to determine whether this strategy can be generalized beyond systemic lupus erythematosus.

Degenerative diseases

In recent years, the technology of in vivo immune cell engineering has begun to emerge for the treatment of degenerative diseases. The related work includes the systematic explanation of the mechanism of action, the iterative optimization of delivery systems, as well as the construction and verification of treatment strategies for different degenerative diseases, laying a foundation for further translational applications in this field. The main direction of in vivo immune cell engineering for treating degenerative diseases is to directly adjust the function of immune cells or reduce neuronal loss in the patient's body through in vivo delivery of genetic cargos or immunomodulatory programs, in order to alleviate neuroinflammation, remove pathological proteins and promote regeneration [133].

Meanwhile, using gene editing to precisely enhance the self-repair and regulation capabilities of immune cells is becoming an important direction in the treatment of degenerative diseases [22,134]. By directly modifying immune effector cells, these approaches may improve their ability to clear pathological protein aggregates, regulate chronic inflammatory responses, and produce neuroprotective or regenerative factors in a controlled manner. Through these mechanisms, in vivo immune cell engineering may attenuate neuronal injury, improve the diseased tissue microenvironment, and exert protective or reparative effects in preclinical models of degenerative diseases. Moreover, interdisciplinary research combining immune cell engineering with nanotechnology and gene therapy provides more treatment approaches for complex degenerative lesions.

Advantages and challenges of in vivo immune cell engineering

Advantages of in vivo immune cell engineering

Continuous optimization of CAR design has generated multiple CAR architectures with improved signaling strength, persistence, and safety (Fig. 5A), making it increasingly feasible to induce therapeutic immune-cell functions directly in vivo rather than through conventional ex vivo manufacturing. In vivo immune cell engineering avoids complex steps such as cell collection, in vitro culture, expansion, and reinfusion, not only shortening the treatment time for patients from several weeks to several days, but also reducing the reliance on advanced cell preparation facilities, thereby improving the accessibility of treatment [138,139]. At the same time, these in situ edited cells show better tumor homing ability. Another important motivation for in vivo immune cell engineering lies in its potential for platform development. In principle, modular design can standardize key elements of carrier composition, formulation, manufacturing workflow and dosing strategy, while adapting the therapeutic payload or encoded sequence to different disease indications [141]. However, for each vector and target cell type, the tissue affinity, dose, and safety range may still need to be optimized [99]. Advances in materials science and formulation engineering, particularly strategies for lymphoid-tissue targeting and selective organ targeting (SORT), are reshaping the design of in vivo delivery systems. This enables the reduction of the dominant role of the liver and the increase of delivery to certain lymphoid or immune-related organs in certain systems [142,143]. This shift significantly broadens the range of targetable immune cells [144,145]. Innovative therapeutic strategies such as “in vivo antibody factories” and “in vivo programmable effector cells” are being translated from conceptual designs into early translational strategies [146]. It is worth noting that in several development processes, the establishment of standardized CMC is being actively promoted [147]. These works collectively indicate that a new paradigm for drug development, centered on genetic sequences and supported by programmable technology platforms, is emerging (Fig. 5B).

Challenges of in vivo immune cell engineering

Despite rapid progress, the clinical translation of in vivo immune cell engineering remains limited by challenges in delivery specificity, immunogenicity and safety, controllability and persistence, and CMC and regulatory challenges (Fig. 5C). First, a major bottleneck is how to precisely concentrate nucleic acids or editing tools in the body, specifically within T cells, B cells, and myeloid cells [41]. Although recent advances in formulations such as SORT and recent preclinical studies of in vivo CAR-T cell generation show that spleen/lymphatic targeting and in vivo engineering can be feasible, more consistency and reproducibility across species and indications is required [25,148].

Second, different delivery platforms present distinct safety concerns. AAV vectors are limited by pre-existing neutralizing antibodies, restricted packaging capacity, liver enrichment, long-term persistence, and barriers to repeated dosing. Lentiviral vectors can enable durable transgene expression, but their integrating nature raises concerns regarding insertional mutagenesis, clonal expansion, and irreversible genetic modification, particularly for direct in vivo use [79]. By contrast, LNPs and other non-viral carriers enable transient and more readily titratable expression, but their application is still limited by inefficient lymphocyte delivery, limited endosomal escape efficiency, innate immune activation, and immune responses associated with PEGylation or repeated dosing [99]. Thus, delivery platform selection should take these indication- and goal-dependent risks into account.

Genome-editing-related safety risks also require systematic evaluation. In vivo immune cell engineering should distinguish off-target delivery from off-target genome editing. The former refers to unintended vector distribution or uptake in non-target tissues or cell populations, whereas the latter involves unintended genomic alterations in cells exposed to the editing machinery. Unlike ex vivo engineered cell products, immune cells generated in vivo cannot be comprehensively characterized before treatment, making it necessary to monitor off-target editing, unintended modification of non-immune tissues, prolonged nuclease exposure [149]. Future studies should integrate biodistribution with cell-type-specific editing assessment and validated off-target assays. Strategies such as selective or localized delivery, transient nuclease expression, tissue-restricted promoters, high-fidelity editors, and optimized guide RNA design may help reduce unintended editing in non-target tissues [150].

Controllability and persistence represent another major translational issue. Durable CAR or transgene expression may be beneficial for hematologic malignancies, where sustained immune surveillance is often required, but excessive persistence may increase the risk of chronic immune activation, prolonged B-cell aplasia, T-cell exhaustion, or on-target/off-tumor toxicity [151]. In autoimmune diseases, transient but sufficient immune resetting may be more desirable than permanent immune ablation. In solid tumors, engineered immune cells must persist long enough to overcome stromal barriers, hypoxia, immunosuppression, and metabolic competition within the tumor microenvironment. The appropriate level and duration of expression should therefore be evaluated in a disease-specific manner.

Finally, in vivo immune cell engineering also raises CMC and regulatory questions that differ from those of conventional cell therapies. Although sequence-based platforms may facilitate payload replacement, changes in the cargo sequence, targeting ligand, vector composition, manufacturing process, or administration route may affect biodistribution, immunogenicity, and safety. Therefore, comparability assessment, release criteria, potency assays, stability testing, and long-term safety follow-up need to be defined for each platform and indication. In clinical development, strategies such as dose fractionation, safety switches, or adapter CAR designs may improve controllability, but their value will depend on the specific delivery system and disease context. These considerations indicate that future translation will require not only improved engineering efficiency, but also clearer standards for product characterization, safety evaluation, and regulatory assessment [152].

Future outlook

In the future, personalized strategies are expected to become an important direction for in vivo immune cell engineering [153]. Detailed characterization of patient-specific genomic features, antigen profiles, immune status, and disease microenvironment may guide the selection of therapeutic targets, genetic cargos, delivery platforms, and dosing strategies [154]. Such customized treatment plans, which are tailored to the specific characteristics of the disease and the individual needs of the patients, can not only enhance the therapeutic effect but also reduce off-target effects [155]. With advances in molecular diagnostics, delivery technologies and genome engineering, the personalization of these therapies will become increasingly precise and efficient, thereby better accommodating the needs of individual patients [156,157].

The discovery of new therapeutic targets will further expand the application of this field. More specific tumor antigens, disease-associated immune-cell markers and immune-regulatory molecules may provide new opportunities for selective immune-cell programming [158]. At the same time, advances in CAR, TCR and synthetic receptor design, together with CRISPR-based editing strategies, may improve immune-cell specificity, persistence and resistance to suppressive microenvironments. For CAR-T [159] and CAR-NK [160] cell therapies, identifying more specific tumor antigens is crucial for improving treatment efficacy.

The rapid development of computational science has brought new opportunities for drug discovery [161]. In recent years, the widespread availability of protein structure prediction tools has significantly lowered the threshold for obtaining target structures, enabling structure-based drug design (SBDD) to cover more targets and pathways related to immune regulation, and providing a designable space for the key molecular components required for in vivo immune cell engineering [[162], [163], [164]]. For instance, in the ligand–receptor interfaces, viral capsid–receptor interactions, delivery-associated proteins, and editing-enzyme regulatory interfaces, SBDD can be utilized to enhance binding selectivity, reduce off-target risks, and optimize in vivo stability and pharmacokinetic characteristics [165,166]. At the same time, the integration of structural information with multi-omics data helps to identify the cell-type-specific editing feasibility, accessibility and safety windows [167]. Together with automated design and screening platforms, these approaches may accelerate the development of delivery and editing systems with improved specificity, efficacy and translational feasibility [168].

In brief, with the development of more precisely targeted vectors, safer and more controllable genetic programming systems, and clearer clinical and regulatory pathways, in vivo immune cell engineering is likely to advance first in settings with well-defined targets, such as hematologic malignancies and autoimmune diseases, before expanding to broader and more complex indications (Fig. 5C).

Summary

In vivo immune cell engineering is emerging as a promising strategy for generating or modulating therapeutic immune cells directly within the body. By replacing individualized ex vivo manipulation with in situ reprogramming of endogenous immune cells, this approach may simplify manufacturing, reduce treatment costs, and improve therapeutic accessibility. Recent advances in viral and non-viral delivery platforms, together with RNA delivery and genome-editing technologies, have enabled more precise and controllable genetic programming of immune cells in vivo. These developments have extended the scope of immune-cell engineering beyond conventional ex vivo CAR-T-cell therapy, supporting the in vivo modulation of T cells, MΦ, NK cells, and other immune populations for diverse therapeutic applications.

This review summarizes the mechanistic principles, delivery strategies, immune-cell targets, and therapeutic applications of in vivo immune cell engineering. Particular emphasis is placed on in vivo CAR-T-cell generation, which provides an important proof of concept for producing therapeutic immune cells without conventional ex vivo manufacturing. Recent preclinical and early translational studies in cancer, autoimmune diseases, and degenerative disorders highlight the therapeutic potential of this approach, but also reveal key barriers to clinical translation. These include limited delivery efficiency, insufficient cell-type specificity, off-target delivery or editing, immunogenicity, safety concerns, controllability, persistence, and CMC/regulatory challenges. Future progress will depend on more precise delivery systems, safer and tunable editing tools, disease-matched expression strategies, and standardized evaluation frameworks. With continued optimization, in vivo immune cell engineering may develop into a scalable and programmable therapeutic platform for immune-mediated diseases.
