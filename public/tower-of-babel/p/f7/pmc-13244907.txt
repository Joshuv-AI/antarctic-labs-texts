The final frontier: can Kerala lay out a roadmap to a ‘zero snakebite deaths initiative’ for India?
Authors: Jaideep C. Menon, Ajan Maheswaran Jaya, Harikumar Sivanandan, Anu Suresh, M. S. Aravind, Mohammed Anvar, Rajan N. Khobragade
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
India accounts for nearly half the global mortality attributed to snakebite envenoming (SBE). In response, the Government of India launched the National Action Plan for Prevention and Control of Snakebite Envenoming in 2024, targeting a 50% reduction in deaths and disabilities by 2030. Kerala’s use of mobile apps, certification of snake rescuers, and overall health system preparedness have enabled early care and reduced mortality. Based on these success stories, we are proposing a scalable roadmap, ‘The Kerala Model,' for achieving zero snakebite deaths in India. Kerala has initiated the Zero Snakebite Deaths Initiative, building on national policy and state-level success. A multidisciplinary conclave held at Amrita Institute of Medical Sciences on February 13th, 2025, brought together stakeholders from the fields of health, forestry, basic sciences, and civil society to develop a comprehensive state action plan for the prevention of snakebite envenoming (SAPSE). The strategy includes community empowerment through school-based education, training first responders and healthcare workers, improving pre-hospital care, ensuring access to quality ASV, and establishing a digital snakebite registry integrated with ecological and surveillance data. Kerala’s evidence-informed, multi-stakeholder framework aligned to the NAP–SE offers a replicable model for high-burden states. With continued investment in digital data systems, community outreach, and healthcare training, the goal of zero snakebite deaths in India is not aspirational but achievable.

Snakebite in India

Snakebite envenoming (SBE), a neglected tropical disease (NTD), accounts for an estimated 58000 deaths annually in India [1]. The National Center for Disease Control in India released NAPSE (National Action Plan for the Prevention and Control of Snakebite Envenoming) under the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare, Government of India, in March 2024. This document provides a broad framework for management, prevention, and control of SBE in India, aligned to the broader WHO–SDG (World Health Organization–Sustainable Development Goals) of halving deaths and disabilities from SBE by 2030, adopting a ‘One Health’ approach [2].

The Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR) conducted a National Task Force project assessing the ‘Incidence, mortality, and socioeconomic burden of snakebite in India,’ covering 13 states (31 districts) in 5 zones of India [3]. Based on the learnings of that project, the ICMR sponsored an implementation research proposal titled ‘Zero Snakebite Deaths Initiative,’ studying the feasibility, acceptance, and adoption of six proven interventions in reducing mortality and morbidity across seven states of India [4]. We aim to lay out a practical, systems-oriented roadmap for “Zero snakebite death initiative” in India based on learnings from Kerala.

Snakebite epidemiology in Kerala

The tropical climate, thick vegetation and forest cover, and abundance of rivers and waterways alongside fields and grasslands bless Kerala, a state in South India, with a rich diversity of snakes, both venomous and non-venomous [5]. Despite this, the number of deaths due to SBE in the state is low as compared to most other states of India. Odisha, with a population of 45 million (2024 estimate), reports 800–900 deaths per annum as compared to Kerala (110 annual deaths before 2020) with a population of 36 million (2024) [6, 7].

This low incidence of deaths can be attributed to various socio-demographic, environmental, health system-related, and infrastructural factors in Kerala. Kerala has the highest Human Development Index (HDI) among Indian states, reflecting comparatively better literacy, healthcare access, and social development indicators relative to much of the country [8]. Illiteracy, especially in rural areas, is a known risk association for increased mortality from SBE [1]. In this context, Kerala’s high literacy rate (male: 96.1%, female: 92.1%), especially among women, has a great influence on improved health care access and thus better health outcomes [9, 10]. Snakebite risk has been linked to occupational exposure, especially in agriculture and outdoor occupations, such as farmers, pastoral activities, plantation workers, and manual laborers in rural areas [11]. While not a desirable or replicable trend, the observed decadal decline in livestock, especially bovines, has inadvertently contributed to decreased snake–human conflicts and, consequently, snakebite incidence in the state [12]. Open defecation has also been identified as a risk factor for snakebites; Kerala state achieving “open defecation free status” in 2016 could have contributed to a reduction in snake–human conflict [13].

Ensuring timely and effective management of envenomation and its complications is crucial for reducing snakebite mortality. Kerala has a dense network of health facilities—14 government medical colleges, 18 district, 18 general, 48 Taluk HQ hospitals, and 40 Taluk hospitals [14], along with an average of at least 3 private hospitals in each district with the capability of managing SBE, typically accessible within a 20–30 km radius [15]. This is further complemented by comprehensive training provided to frontline workers in the community, including ASHA personnel, on first aid and referral protocols across all districts by the state public health system [16]. Similar community-based approaches have been successfully demonstrated in the national multi-centric ICMR snakebite taskforce study, where ASHA workers were trained in snakebite identification, first-aid awareness, referral facilitation, and community-level surveillance and reporting of snakebite cases [3]. The Dahanu Snakebite Project conducted by ICMR–NIRRCH in Maharashtra further demonstrated substantial reduction in snakebite case fatality rates through community awareness initiatives and structured capacity building of healthcare providers [17]. Urbanization also has an impact on less snake–human exposure, with Kerala having an urban population twice that of the national average [18]. Transport in Kerala is also comparatively better than most other states of India; Kerala’s estimated 14 million motor vehicles for a population of 36 million ensure that most households have rapid means of transport, facilitating early access to medical care in emergencies [19].

Recent decline in snakebite incidence: the Kerala model

Kerala’s death rate due to SBE has significantly and steadily decreased recently. The National Task Force Study conducted in two districts of Kerala (Kannur and Ernakulam) recorded 6 deaths over a 1-year period (2022–23). The State Forest Department pays a compensation for each death due to SBE; official numbers reveal a decreasing trend. Deaths peaked, at over 120 per year in the late 2010 s, and decreased from 123 in 2018–19 to 52 in 2020–21. There was a small increase to 65 in 2021–22, which then dropped over the coming years to 34 reported in 2025. Over a 6-year period, this pattern shows an overall decrease of over 70–75% [6].

Coordinated, multi-sectoral action appears to be the cause of Kerala’s recent and ongoing decrease in mortality due to SBE rather than a random epidemiological fluctuation. The combined effects of legislative prioritizing, increased surveillance, community involvement, governance, and technological innovation are reflected in this shift. This developing ‘Kerala model’ shows how multi-sectoral interventions improve care pathways and outcomes after snakebite envenomation.

A major component in the Kerala model is an app, “SARPA (Snake Awareness, Rescue, and Protection App),” launched in August 2020. The app allows for communication between the community and qualified/certified snake rescuers, offers standardized, empirically supported first-aid instructions, and facilitates snake identification through photos and descriptions. SARPA has made a substantial contribution to improved care by decreasing response times, avoiding improper first-aid procedures, and facilitating prompt referrals to medical facilities [20].

Complementing SARPA, in 2021 another app called Snakepedia was launched, with both a website and a mobile application, which offers comprehensive details on both venomous and non-venomous snake species of that geography with photographs. Beyond identifying species, it provides precise, fact-based instructions for first assistance after snakebite. Along with contact information for certified and skilled snake rescuers, the platform also provides facts on medical facilities with the capability and resources to manage SBE [21].

Another critical factor for effective snakebite management in Kerala is an adequate, uninterrupted ASV supply. Procurement of antivenom for the public sector hospitals through Kerala Medical Services Corporation Limited (KMSCL) has increased the efficiency of supply. KMSCL is a centralized, transparent, and IT-enabled procurement and supply chain system, which ensures low-cost, quality-assured drugs and equipment across more than 1300 government health institutions [22].

Initiatives at the policy level have reinforced this trend. The October 2025 acceptance of the "Snakebite Death Free Kerala Initiative" demonstrates high-level political commitment to eradicating snakebite mortality. The program’s objective of halving deaths in 2 years and achieving zero mortality in five reflects a commitment toward the goal. In order to facilitate systematic reporting and real-time data collection, the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare, Government of India, has listed snakebite as a notifiable condition. The Kerala state mandated the reporting of snakebite incidents and fatalities starting November 2025 [20].

Financial protection mechanisms have also been reinforced by the Kerala government, which has increased the compensation for the kin of snakebite death victims from INR 4 lakhs–10 lakhs (INR 4 lakhs from the State Disaster Relief Fund and INR 6 lakhs from the forest department). Financial assistance is also provided for hospitalization following SBE [23].

In addition, following the NAPSE guidelines, frontline workers, especially ASHA workers in Kerala, have been engaged in community awareness on the prevention and management of snakebite. This has also contributed to reducing snakebite incidence.

Zero snakebite death initiative

A 1-day stakeholder’s meeting was held at Amrita Institute of Medical Sciences, Kochi, on the 13th of February, 2025, on the theme of ‘Zero Snakebite Deaths Initiative,’ involving representatives from the State Health Department, State Forest Department, National Centre for Disease Control (NCDC), ICMR Headquarters—New Delhi, ICMR institutes, including NIRRCH—Mumbai, NITM—Belagavi, and RMRC—Bhubaneshwar, representatives from the district health offices from each of the 14 districts of Kerala, clinicians, researchers, ophiologists, and NGOs (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Roadmap to zero snakebite death

Roadmap to zero snakebite death

The discussion led to several key recommendations and action points across 4 domains. A session on “Empowering and Engaging Communities” emphasized the importance of involving local self-government, schools, and traditional healers (rural areas) for spreading awareness.

The session on “Ensuring Safe and Effective Management” stressed timely and effective pre-hospital care, enhancing public ambulance services (e.g., 108), and implementation of a hub-and-spoke model, where CHCs (Community Health Centers) and taluk hospitals serve as hubs supporting a network of PHCs (Primary Health Centers). Clinical hesitancy in administering anti-snake venom (ASV) and quality inconsistencies in ASV production and supply were addressed as barriers. The establishment of regional venom banks would support both clinical management and research, given that species outside the Big-4 species, like the hump-nosed pit viper (Hypnale hypnale), are the offending species to a significant extent in certain geographies.

For “Health System Strengthening”, several recommendations were raised. Mapping treatment facilities based on their capacity and readiness, real-time data sharing from facilities, a robust referral system, and auditing of snakebite-related deaths were some of the strategies.

Recommendations in “Increasing Partnerships, Coordination, and Resources” include training collaborations, data sharing and integration, and research collaborations. To move forward with ASV development and snakebite management research, it is important to make it easier to get venom samples while following ethical and legal guidelines.

The state’s model offers a replicable blueprint for high-burden states. However, upscaling requires adaptive strategies to local contexts, such as addressing species-specific envenomation (e.g., hump-nosed pit viper) and strengthening interdepartmental coordination (Table 1). The goal of ‘Zero snakebite deaths’ in India would depend very much on the preventive aspects involving engaging the community, empowering it, and creating greater awareness on the issue of SBE. This, alongside the other measures listed, would help India achieve her WHO–SDG of reducing death and disability due to snakebite by 50% by 2030 (World Health Organization, 2019).
