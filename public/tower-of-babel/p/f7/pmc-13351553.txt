Caution in causal interpretation of ecological analyses of life expectancy during COVID-19 in Brazil
Authors: Marcelo Queiroga, Francisco Cardoso, Raphael Câmara Medeiros Parente
Published: 2026

The article “Burden of disease and life expectancy decomposition in Brazil and its federated units, 1990–2023”1 provides an important descriptive overview of mortality, disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), and life expectancy trends during the COVID-19 pandemic in Brazil. However, some interpretative conclusions appear to exceed the inferential capacity of the ecological and model-based design employed.

The analysis is fundamentally based on aggregated estimates derived from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) framework. Ecological analyses are valuable for identifying temporal and geographic patterns and for generating hypotheses, but they have important limitations regarding causal inference, particularly when complex political, institutional, and behavioural determinants are involved.

In this context, the interpretation that “scientific denialism” and lack of national coordination contributed to reductions in life expectancy requires greater methodological caution. The article states that “Taken together, these findings suggest that scientific denialism and lack of national coordination contributed to deleterious consequences for Brazil, including a setback in LE during the pandemic”. However, the study does not provide an operational definition of “scientific denialism”, measurable exposure indicators, or analytical strategies capable of estimating attributable effects. Without explicit causal modelling or counterfactual approaches, such interpretations risk conflating temporal association with causal inference.

Similarly, the discussion regarding Constitutional Amendment EC-95 appears to exceed the inferential scope of the ecological design employed. Although fiscal policy may plausibly influence health outcomes, the study does not include specific exposure metrics, causal modelling, or analytical strategies capable of estimating the independent effect of EC-95 on mortality or life expectancy trends. As such, these interpretations should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than causal conclusions.

Additionally, several potentially relevant confounding factors are not fully explored in the interpretative discussion, including regional heterogeneity in demographic structure, prevalence of obesity and diabetes, healthcare infrastructure, timing and intensity of epidemic waves, circulation of viral variants, socioeconomic conditions, testing capacity, and differences in state and municipal mitigation policies.

Importantly, the article itself recognises substantial heterogeneity in pandemic responses across Brazilian states and municipalities. This decentralised context complicates attempts to attribute national mortality patterns to a single explanatory factor at the federal level.

Another relevant consideration concerns the intrinsic uncertainty of GBD estimates. Although highly valuable for population health surveillance, many estimates rely on statistical modelling, redistribution of garbage codes, and correction algorithms for underreporting. These methodological features reinforce the need for caution when advancing categorical interpretations regarding causation.

Finally, reductions in life expectancy during the COVID-19 pandemic were observed across countries with markedly different political systems, public health structures, and mitigation strategies. Such international heterogeneity suggests that simplified monocausal explanations may be insufficient to explain the complexity of pandemic-related mortality outcomes.

These considerations highlight the importance of distinguishing descriptive association from causal inference in ecological analyses.
