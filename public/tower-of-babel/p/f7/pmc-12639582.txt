Tranexamic acid induced seizure during spine surgery: A case report
Authors: Thomas J. Ryan, Christopher Lucasti, Danielle E. Chipman, Emily K. Vallee, Maxwell M. Scott, Joseph M. Kowalski
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT

Despite its benefits, TXA can pose risks, including rare but severe complications such as seizures. Understanding the balance between its hemostatic advantages and potential adverse effects is critical. Introduction

Perioperative blood loss is a significant driver of morbidity in spine surgery, and reductions in perioperative blood loss represent a potential avenue for significant improvements in patient outcomes [1,2]. There are multiple strategies to reduce perioperative blood loss. One of those methods is allogenic blood transfusions; however, this comes with a surplus of complications including transfusion-associated cardiac overload and transfusion-associated lung injury [[3], [4], [5]]. These complications, along with the greater healthcare cost of blood products and conservation technologies [6], have continued to generate interest in techniques to maintain red cell mass. One of these methods is perioperative provision of antifibrinolytic agents, including the administration of tranexamic acid (TXA) [7,8].

The use of TXA in spine surgery has been noted to decrease estimated intraoperative blood loss by over 30 % [9,10] and postoperative blood loss by over 50 % [9]. More specifically, TXA is also associated with reduced perioperative blood loss in posterior spine surgery to treat spinal cord compression through laminectomy [1,11,12]. The complication rate of TXA in spine surgery has been reported as low as 3.2 % in pediatric patients and 8.2 % in adults despite using a high dosage (loading dose ≥ 30 mg/kg) [13]. Although there have been reports of thromboembolic complications of TXA in orthopedic surgeries, studies have shown that there is no significant difference in thromboembolic complications in patients receiving TXA compared to groups not receiving TXA, 2.4 % vs. 2.8 % respectively [14]. Another severe adverse effect of TXA use is seizures, yet this occurrence is very rare, especially in spine surgery.

To the authors' knowledge, there has only been one case reported by Kawagishi et al. noting seizures following TXA administration for orthopedic spine surgery [15]. The goal of this report is to describe the surgical course of a 66-year-old female who experienced multiple generalized tonic-clonic seizures after TXA administration intraoperatively. This case report has been reported in line with the SCARE checklist [16].

Case

A 66-year-old female with a past medical history significant for hypertension, diabetes, depression, anxiety, and recent T11-L3 instrumentation removal presented to the hospital after falling while getting out of bed. Upon arrival, the patient reported right lower quadrant and hip pain. She was at her neurologic baseline with no documented personal or family history of seizures. Her current relevant medications included Hydrocodone-Acetaminophen 5–375 mg as needed, Duloxetine HCl 60 mg orally, and Diazepam 5 mg orally.

After discussing the benefits are risks, the patient elected to undergo surgical intervention. She underwent T10-L5 instrumentation posterior lateral fusion and L1-L2 laminectomy under general anesthesia. She received a standard bolus of 1 g TXA. An incidental intraoperative durotomy was repaired uneventfully, and the remainder of the procedure proceeded without complications. After being flipped supine, the patient began to develop a generalized tonic-clonic seizure while she was still intubated. The seizure broke with 100 mg of propofol and 2 mg of versed. She was then transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU).

Postoperatively, a head computed tomography (CT) scan was ordered to investigate her seizure activity. Upon arriving to the CT suite, she experienced another generalized tonic-clonic seizure, followed by pulseless electrical activity which necessitated multiple rounds of cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) and 1 mg of IV epinephrine before she was successfully resuscitated. This CT scan was unremarkable with no acute pathology. The neurology team recommended treatment with Aspirin, 1000 mg of Keppra IV twice a day, and Lipitor. She was extubated on postoperative day five. On postoperative day five, she also exhibited evidence of right foot drop for which she was placed in an Ankle-Foot Orthosis (AFO).

A repeat head CT 12 days later demonstrated a small subarachnoid hemorrhage, leading to a neurosurgery consultation and a recommendation for brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). The patient refused the MRI, but a subsequent head CT the following week which revealed an interval decrease in the previously noted subarachnoid hemorrhage.

Throughout her hospital stay, her wound remained dry, and she showed mild improvement in the strength of her right lower extremity, she continued to work with physical therapy. She had no further seizures and was discharged to rehabilitation on postoperative day 29. At the time of discharge, she had established follow-up with neurology. Informed consent was obtained from the patient for publication of this report.

Discussion

The use of TXA in surgical and trauma settings has been shown to reduce bleeding and the need for transfusions. However, concerns regarding thromboembolic complications exist, and caution is advised in patients with a history of thromboembolic disease. The mechanism of tonic-clonic seizures due to TXA administration is caused by TXA binding to plasmin and plasminogen. This prevents their binding to fibrin which exerts a hemostatic effect through antifibrinolytic action. In contrast, when TXA crosses the cerebrovascular barrier, it binds to and acts antagonistically against gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) receptors in the central nervous system (CNS). These receptors have anticonvulsant effects, and TXA binding amplifies neuronal excitation without opening the chlorine channels. This hyperexcitation results in seizure [17,18]. This pathophysiology was seen in cases where accidental intrathecal injections of TXA caused seizures [18,19].

Studies investigating TXA dosing on inducing seizures in cardiac surgery showed that a high TXA bolus or loading dose of at least 30 mg/kg [17,20] had significantly greater rates of seizures than low dosing. Furthermore, the report of TXA-induced seizures is low in orthopedic surgery, as the risk for significant bleeding is much lower when compared to cardiovascular surgery. In addition, seizures are less likely to occur in orthopedic surgeries because such procedures typically have shorter durations, less extensive manipulation of bodily systems, and fewer requirements for complex anesthetic techniques compared to other surgeries [21]. Finally, intraoperative seizures are infrequent under general anesthesia, which likely contributed to the overall low risk of seizure during this procedure [22].

Drug-drug interactions were evaluated in comparison with potential adverse drug reactions associated with this seizure episode. The patient's medication regimen of duloxetine, diazepam, and hydrocodone-acetaminophen has no known pharmacokinetic or pharmacodynamic interactions with TXA. In the absence of interacting agents, it is likely this event reflects a direct adverse effect of TXA rather than a drug interaction. Additionally, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration labeling cautions that elevated plasma concentrations of TXA may cause neurologic toxicity, including myoclonus and seizures, with no specific antidote available. Supportive management is recommended in such cases [23]. This highlights the ambiguity surrounding TXA's dose-related neurotoxicity and reinforces the need for cautious perioperative use.

The incidental durotomy occurring intraoperatively presents a source of potential complication. Dural tears are not uncommon within spinal surgery, with incidence rates of 0.3 % to 17 % [24,25]. However, the complications associated with fully repaired dural tears are rare. In 2010, West et al. documented a case of a tonic-clonic seizure following an incidental durotomy. In this case, the patient was on chronic medications, including Olanzapine and Amisulpride, which have epileptogenic side effects. It was hypothesized that these medications with their seizure threshold-lowering side effects played a role in the post-durotomy seizure [26]. Our patient was not on any chronic medications that significantly lower seizure thresholds, including Diazepam, which acts on the central nervous system (CNS). This, combined with the uneventful intraoperative repair of the durotomy, uneventful seizure workup, and the resolution of the seizures in the days following the surgery, suggests the TXA bolus was the trigger of the perioperative seizures.

The only other study to report a TXA-induced seizure in an orthopedic spine surgery was published by Kawagashi et al. in 2022. Comparing our case to this report, we observe striking similarities and significant points of concern regarding the use of TXA in orthopedic surgery. In our case, a patient experienced seizures following a T10-L5 instrumentation, fusion, and L1-L2 laminectomy. Despite using a standard bolus of TXA to manage intraoperative bleeding and an incidental durotomy that was repaired uneventfully, seizures occurred. This mirrors the case reported by Kawagishi et al., where a generalized tonic-clonic seizure immediately followed placing the patient in a supine position after the administration of a normal dose bolus of TXA during spinal surgery [15].

One factor linking these cases may be the hemodynamic changes associated with shifting a patient to the supine position. Flipping a patient supine enhances cerebral arterial and venous blood flow [27]. Therefore, increased cerebral blood flow (CBF), combined with the prothrombotic potential of TXA [3], could trigger neurologic events, such as seizures. Additionally, TXA competitively binds GABA type A receptors, potentially lowering seizure threshold [28].

There was an additional consideration that the subarachnoid hemorrhage observed on postoperative imaging predisposed the patient to seizures. Subarachnoid bleeding may increase intracranial pressure (ICP) and alter neuronal excitability, but in this case, the hemorrhage was not detected on initial postoperative imaging and showed resolution on follow-up. It is more plausible that the seizure occurred due to the neuroexcitatory properties of TXA combined with the increase in ICP, while the positional changes during flipping likely did not precipitate seizures secondary to hemorrhage, as such physiologic shifts generally remain within normal autoregulatory limits and are not independently associated with seizure activity [27].

Although such events are rare, they present a critical need for increased awareness among orthopedic surgeons regarding the potential risks of TXA. Orthopedic surgeons traditionally regard TXA as safe, supported by a meta-analysis involving 10,488 patients from 1972 to 2011 [24]. This analysis, comprised of 129 studies, indicated no increased risk of myocardial infarction, cerebral infarction, or venous thrombosis with TXA use [29]. However, the potential for seizures, particularly in the context of higher doses or specific patient susceptibilities, warrants heightened attention.

This case illustrates the importance of vigilant intraoperative and postoperative monitoring for complications such as seizures and stroke, especially in patients with complex medical histories. The use of TXA, while beneficial for bleeding control, necessitates careful consideration in patients at risk for seizures. Immediate and appropriate management of seizures, including antiepileptic medication and intensive care support, is crucial for positive outcomes. Future recommendations include comprehensive preoperative assessment to identify risk factors for postoperative complications, tailored perioperative medication management to mitigate seizure risk, and multidisciplinary coordination for postoperative care. Continued follow-up with neurology specialists is essential to ensure ongoing recovery and management of potential complications.

The strengths of this paper include its clinical relevance, highlighting the rare yet serious complication of TXA administration during spine surgery. Additionally, the case benefits from the multidisciplinary approach, emphasizing the need for collaboration across specialties with complex cases. There are also multiple limitations to this case report. As a single case report, it is difficult to generalize the findings of this unique patient experience. In addition, the brief follow up period might be insufficient to assess the long term neurological outcomes of the patient. Finally, the patients complex history, including hypertension, diabetes, and previous spine surgeris, could confound the association between TXA and the tonic-clonic seizures. Despite these limitations, the authors believe this case report provides valuable information that adds to the literature.

Conclusion

Our patient presented with a rare generalized tonic-clonic seizure following a standard bolus of TXA. While generally well-tolerated, TXA has been known to cause side effects such as nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, and, less commonly, thromboembolic events. However, perioperative seizures caused by TXA administration are not well documented in the literature. It is especially rare for an orthopedic patient, with no past medical history of epilepsy, chronic medications that lower seizure threshold, or electrolyte imbalances, to experience a TXA-induced seizure. High clinical awareness for seizures caused by hemostatic agents can help prompt timely intervention. Additionally, we advocate for a comprehensive preoperative assessment to assure risk factors are minimized before administering TXA.
