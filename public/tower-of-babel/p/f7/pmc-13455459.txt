Body composition, anxiety, and fitness test performance in Chinese college students: biopsychosocial association patterns across gender-differentiated testing contexts
Authors: Taihe Liang, Qiong Xu, Junpeng Yan, Dunguo Rong, Jinghua Zhang, Yu Zhu, Bin Xu, Ziheng Ning
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPhysical and psychological factors jointly relate to physical performance, yet few studies have examined the indirect associations through which body composition and exercise behavior relate to performance via psychological factors. This study examined whether biopsychosocial association patterns with fitness test performance differ across gender-differentiated testing contexts that impose different physical demands, using China’s mandatory national fitness testing system as a naturalistic paradigm.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted with 1,916 Chinese college students (492 males, 1,424 females) recruited from universities in Shanghai during the 2025 autumn fitness testing period. Participants completed measures of fitness test anxiety (FTAS), mental toughness (MTI), and exercise behavior (PARS-3). Body mass index (BMI) and official composite fitness test scores were obtained from university records. Structural equation models were estimated separately for each testing context using maximum likelihood with robust standard errors, supplemented by path-by-path constraint tests within a multi-group framework. Sensitivity analyses re-estimated the models using a non-BMI reweighted composite score, a log-transformed exercise measure, and a specification excluding training experience; additional shared-item and raw-score sensitivity analyses evaluated whether the BMI-related contrast was also present in test components completed by both sexes. Network analysis (EBICglasso) with bridge centrality and a Network Comparison Test were conducted on 24-node networks.ResultsBMI was negatively associated with scores in both testing contexts, more strongly in the male context (β = −0.519 vs. −0.242); this contrast persisted in the full-battery sensitivity analyses (non-BMI composite and log-transformed exercise measure) but was attenuated when the outcome was restricted to shared test components and was no longer detectable when the four shared components were represented as raw, directionally aligned z scores. Exercise showed a significant direct association with scores in the male context and a weaker, less stable association in the female context. Path-by-path constraint tests indicated that context-dependent differences were concentrated in the physical-to-performance interface (BMI → score: Δχ² = 34.29, p < .001), although the exercise-related contrasts were sensitive to the scaling of the exercise measure. The psychological-to-performance interface did not differ across contexts (anxiety → score: p = .338; mental toughness → score: p = .592). Network analysis identified a specific cognitive anxiety item concerning failure to meet the passing standard as the strongest psychological bridge node across both contexts.ConclusionsThe findings indicate context-specific biopsychosocial association patterns within a gender-differentiated national fitness testing system. The BMI-related contrast appears localized to the gender-differentiated and algorithmically scored components of the official testing battery rather than being a general feature of shared raw performance; exercise-related contrasts should be interpreted cautiously given their sensitivity to measurement scaling. Because gender and task structure are institutionally confounded in this design, the findings cannot separate task demands from gender or biological sex. The cross-context stability of the psychological-to-performance paths, which was robust across all sensitivity specifications including shared-item analyses, suggests that institutional reforms reducing evaluative threat may benefit students across testing contexts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04938-z.Keywords: Anxiety, Mental toughness, Structural equation modeling, Network analysis, Biopsychosocial model, Physical fitness testing, Chinese college students

Introduction

Research on the determinants of physical performance has traditionally proceeded along two parallel tracks. Exercise and sport science has focused on physical predictors such as body composition, cardiorespiratory fitness, and training history [1, 2], while sport and exercise psychology has examined the role of psychological factors including anxiety, self-efficacy, and mental toughness [3, 4]. In practice, however, physical and psychological factors do not operate in isolation [5, 6]. Body composition may shape psychological states. For example, a student with elevated body mass index (BMI) may experience heightened anxiety in a context that publicly evaluates physical capacity, and psychological states in turn modulate how effectively physical capacities are deployed under pressure. Despite the longstanding recognition of this bidirectional interplay within biopsychosocial frameworks [5, 7], surprisingly few empirical studies have examined the indirect associations through which physical factors relate to performance via psychological factors. This gap is compounded by a sampling limitation: most research on psychological predictors of physical performance has been conducted with competitive athletes, whose physical capacities cluster within a narrow range and whose psychological resilience is subject to self-selection, restricting the variance in both domains that is needed to detect indirect associations [8].

Among the psychological factors most consistently linked to physical performance, anxiety and mental toughness occupy central roles. We focus on these two constructs because they represent the two theoretically opposing psychological processes most directly implicated in evaluative physical performance: a debilitative process and a facilitative resource. Multidimensional anxiety theory [9] distinguishes between cognitive anxiety and somatic anxiety. Cognitive anxiety involves worry, self-doubt, and negative performance expectations; somatic anxiety involves physiological activation such as elevated heart rate and muscle tension. Both have been associated with impaired sport performance through attentional disruption and effort dysregulation. Mental toughness refers to the psychological capacity to perform consistently under varying conditions of stress and adversity [10]. It has been positively associated with sport performance across diverse contexts [3], with its protective function theorized to operate through superior self-regulation and persistence under discomfort. We model anxiety and mental toughness as parallel rather than sequential linking variables, because they are conceptually distinct processes theorized to operate concurrently rather than one through the other, with anxiety serving as the debilitative process and mental toughness as the facilitative resource. Other psychological constructs such as self-efficacy, motivational regulation, and perceived competence are also relevant, and we return to them in the Discussion; the present study was designed around this debilitative–facilitative pairing rather than as a comprehensive psychological model. Three further considerations constrain the current evidence. First, anxiety and mental toughness are typically modeled as direct predictors rather than as variables through which upstream physical factors may be indirectly associated with performance. Second, few studies have examined non-athlete populations in compulsory evaluation contexts. Third, although gender differences in anxiety and body composition are well documented, the possibility that the indirect role of psychological factors depends on the physical demands of the specific performance task has received little attention.

This third point warrants emphasis. In many standardized physical assessments, males and females complete different test items that impose different biomechanical demands [7, 11]. If a male-specific item (e.g., pull-ups) creates a stronger physical constraint from body mass than a female-specific item (e.g., sit-ups), then the relative contribution of psychological factors to performance may differ not because of gender per se, but because the task ecology differs [12]. An observed “gender difference” in biopsychosocial association patterns could therefore reflect, at least in part, a task-structure-dependent difference [7, 11]. We emphasize, however, that gender and task structure are inseparable in any system that assigns test items by gender: because the same individuals who share a gender also share a test battery, the two cannot be experimentally or statistically disentangled. The most that such a context can offer is a comparison of association patterns across gender-differentiated testing contexts, with the source of any difference remaining open; this difference may reflect task structure, biological sex, or their interaction.

China’s national fitness testing system provides an informative setting for such a comparison. Under the National Student Physical Health Standards (revised 2014) [13], all enrolled college students complete an annual fitness test battery that includes shared items (BMI, vital capacity, 50-m sprint, standing long jump, sit-and-reach) and gender-specific items (males: pull-ups and 1,000-m run; females: one-minute sit-ups and 800-m run). Composite scores (0–100) carry direct academic consequences: students who fail may be denied degree conferral, and scores affect eligibility for merit-based awards. This system offers three methodological advantages: compulsory participation ensures broad variability in physical and psychological characteristics; gender-differentiated items create natural variation in task-specific physical constraint; and the high-stakes nature of the assessment generates ecologically valid evaluative pressure [14]. Because each student’s raw performances are converted into a composite score by a fixed national scoring algorithm, this setting is also an instance of algorithmically standardized performance evaluation. This aligns the present context with contemporary calls in sports science for transparency and methodological rigor in how standardized assessment systems quantify physical performance [15]. We refer to this mandatory, consequential, and structurally differentiated setting as an institutionalized evaluative physical performance context. We use this phrase as a descriptive label that extends existing evaluative-stress and high-stakes-assessment frameworks to the domain of standardized physical performance testing, rather than as a new formal theoretical construct; it has potential relevance beyond China to any standardized physical assessment that links performance to evaluative outcomes.

The present study examined biopsychosocial predictors of fitness test performance in a large sample of Chinese college students, guided by a central proposition: that body composition and exercise behavior are associated with performance not only through direct physical associations but also through psychological linking variables (fitness test anxiety and mental toughness) [16], and that the relative weight of these association patterns differs across gender-differentiated testing contexts that impose different physical demands. We pursued three specific aims. The first aim was to test whether physical factors are associated with fitness test scores both directly and indirectly through psychological linking variables (Hypothesis 1). The second aim was to compare the strength of specific path coefficients across the two gender-differentiated testing contexts, using separate structural equation models supplemented by formal path-by-path constraint tests. We hypothesized that the direct association of BMI with scores, and that of exercise with scores, would be significantly stronger in the male testing context, as indicated by significant constraint tests, reflecting the greater biomechanical penalty of the male-specific items (Hypothesis 2). We further hypothesized that the associations of anxiety and mental toughness with scores would not differ significantly across testing contexts (i.e., the corresponding constraint tests would be non-significant), because the psychological-to-performance interface is less task-dependent (Hypothesis 3). The third aim was to use network analysis to identify which specific psychological contents serve as bridge nodes connecting the physical and psychological domains [17, 18], with the prediction that cognitive anxiety items concerning evaluative consequences would emerge as primary bridges given the high-stakes nature of the assessment (Hypothesis 4) [19, 20].

Method

Participants and procedure

Prior to data collection, an a priori power analysis was conducted to determine the minimum sample size required for the planned structural equation models. Using an RMSEA-based approach [21], we estimated that N = 1,446 was needed to distinguish close fit (RMSEA ≤ 0.05) from not-close fit with 80% power at df = 3 and α = 0.05. To accommodate potential data loss from quality control screening, a recruitment target of at least 2,000 participants was set.

A total of 2,044 college students from universities in Shanghai, China, were recruited via convenience sampling during the 2025 autumn semester fitness testing period. After excluding participants who did not provide informed consent (n = 6), failed the attention check item (n = 51), had duplicate IDs (n = 14), reported ages outside the 16–30 range (n = 6), or had missing data on key variables, the final analytic sample comprised 1,916 participants (492 males, 1,424 females; Mage = 18.80, SD = 0.87). The unequal gender distribution reflects the composition of the participating universities and is considered in the Data Analytic Strategy and Limitations Sect. [22]. Participants completed an online questionnaire assessing psychological variables within one week prior to their scheduled fitness test. Fitness test scores and anthropometric data were obtained from official university records. The study was approved by the institutional ethics review board (102772025RT175), and all participants provided written informed consent.

Measures

Fitness test anxiety

Fitness test anxiety was assessed using the Physical Fitness Test Anxiety Scale (FTAS; Yin, Tang, & Gao, [23]), a 12-item self-report measure developed and validated specifically for the Chinese college fitness testing context. The scale was constructed through literature analysis, semi-structured interviews, and iterative expert review, drawing on established sport anxiety instruments (e.g., the CSAI-2 [9]), while adapting item content to the standardized fitness testing situation. The FTAS comprises two subscales: cognitive anxiety (7 items; e.g., “I am worried about failing to meet the passing standards of the fitness test”) and somatic anxiety (5 items; e.g., “Before the fitness test begins, I feel my heart racing”). Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = never to 5 = always). In the development study, the scale demonstrated good model fit (χ²/df = 2.29, RMSEA = 0.051, CFI = 0.980), with Cronbach’s α from 0.903 to 0.928 and AVE from 0.575 to 0.667 [23]. In the present study, Cronbach’s α was 0.91 for cognitive anxiety, 0.92 for somatic anxiety, and 0.94 for the total scale. CFA results supported the two-factor structure (CFI = 0.993, TLI = 0.991, SRMR = 0.059), with CR values of 0.92 (AVE = 0.69) and 0.93 (AVE = 0.79).

Mental toughness

Mental toughness was measured using the Chinese version of the Mental Toughness Index (MTI; Gucciardi et al., [10]), validated in Chinese athlete and university student samples by [24]. The MTI is an 8-item unidimensional scale (e.g., “I believe in my ability to achieve my goals”) rated on a 7-point Likert scale. Li et al. reported good factorial validity (CFI = 0.99, RMSEA = 0.039), internal consistency (α = 0.91), test-retest reliability (ICC = 0.61), and measurement invariance across gender. In the current sample, α = 0.94, ω = 0.94, CR = 0.94, AVE = 0.74.

Exercise behavior

Exercise behavior was assessed using the Physical Activity Rating Scale-3 (PARS-3) [25], which evaluates exercise volume as the product of intensity (1–5), duration (1–5), and frequency (1–5). This multiplicative composite has adequate test-retest reliability (r = .82) [25] and is widely used in Chinese exercise psychology research. The composite was positively skewed (skewness = 2.39 in the female subsample). Rather than relegating this issue to a supplementary note, we estimated the structural models using both the raw PARS-3 composite and a log-transformed version, log(PARS-3 + 1), and report both specifications in the main Results so that the robustness of every exercise-related path can be evaluated directly.

Training experience

Training experience was assessed with a single item rating prior systematic sport or exercise training (1 = no formal training to 5 = extensive competitive experience). Because this was a single-item measure, internal-consistency reliability could not be estimated. Measurement error in this variable may attenuate its own associations and may also affect the adjustment involving correlated predictors. We therefore treat training experience as a background covariate rather than as a substantive physical predictor, and we report a sensitivity analysis in which the structural models are re-estimated with training experience removed (see Results and Supplementary Materials).

Body mass index and fitness test performance

BMI (kg/m²) was calculated from measured height and weight. The dependent variable was the official composite fitness test score (0–100). Under the National Student Physical Health Standards [13], this composite is a weighted sum of item-level scores: BMI (15%), vital capacity (15%), 50-m sprint (20%), standing long jump (10%), sit-and-reach (10%), the gender-specific endurance run (1,000 m for males, 800 m for females; 20%), and the gender-specific strength item (pull-ups for males, one-minute sit-ups for females; 10%). Two features of this outcome require explicit treatment. First, because the male and female composites are built from partially different items and scoring algorithms, they are not measurement-equivalent indicators of a single latent performance construct; we therefore treat them as context-specific institutional scores and interpret the separate-group models as comparisons of association patterns rather than of a common latent outcome. Second, BMI is itself a weighted component (15%) of the official composite, creating a partial mathematical overlap between the BMI predictor and the outcome. To address this overlap, we conducted a sensitivity analysis in which the structural models were re-estimated using a non-BMI composite score, computed by reweighting the six remaining components to sum to 100%. Because the non-BMI score can be computed only for participants with complete item-level data (n = 1,712), the official composite score model was also re-estimated on this same subsample so that the two outcomes could be compared on identical cases. To further evaluate whether the BMI-related context contrast reflects the gender-specific composition of the official battery or is also present in shared test components, we constructed three additional outcomes restricted to items completed by both sexes: a 5-item shared composite using national-rubric scores for BMI, vital capacity, 50-m sprint, standing long jump, and sit-and-reach, reweighted to sum to 100%; a 4-item shared composite identical to the first but excluding BMI; and a 4-item raw z-score composite, computed by standardizing the four shared raw performances across the full sample, with 50-m time sign-flipped so that higher values indicate better performance, and averaging with equal weights, thereby reducing reliance on the national scoring algorithm. These analyses use the n = 1,894 subsample with complete shared-item data and full predictor data, and are reported in Supplementary Table S9.

Data analytic strategy

Analyses proceeded in four stages. First, measurement models for the FTAS and MTI were evaluated using confirmatory factor analysis with the WLSMV estimator. For the MTI model, we additionally examined modification indices and standardized expected parameter changes (SEPC) to probe the source of any localized misfit, reporting these as diagnostic information without modifying the validated factor structure. Measurement invariance was tested via sequential multi-group CFA (configural, metric, scalar), with ΔCFI < 0.010 as the invariance criterion [26], given the known sensitivity of the Δχ² test under WLSMV estimation [27, 28].

Second, separate structural equation models were estimated for the male and female testing contexts using maximum likelihood with robust standard errors (MLR). All continuous variables were standardized within each testing context before estimation. This placed coefficients on a common within-context standard-deviation scale, consistent with our aim of comparing association patterns rather than raw-score differences across non-equivalent testing batteries; we chose within-context standardization over pooled standardization precisely because the batteries are non-equivalent, so a pooled scale would not carry a consistent substantive meaning. These coefficients should not, however, be interpreted as evidence that the composite scores are measurement-equivalent across contexts. We also report 95% confidence intervals for the key path coefficients. To supplement the separate-group analyses with formal tests of context-dependent differences, we conducted path-by-path constraint tests within a multi-group framework: for each focal path, a model constraining that path to equality across contexts was compared to the freely estimated model using a scaled Δχ² test [29]. Because the male subsample (n = 492) is substantially smaller than the female subsample (n = 1,424), the male path estimates carry larger standard errors and lower precision; consequently, non-significant paths in the male model are ambiguous, and within-context significance comparisons are not a sound basis for inferring context differences [30, 31]. The constraint tests, which use both subsamples simultaneously, are the appropriate basis for evaluating context-dependent differences. Path directionality in all structural models reflects a theoretically motivated specification and is not interpreted as evidence of causal order.

Third, network analysis was conducted separately for each testing context using EBICglasso (γ = 0.5) [32] on 24-node networks. We focused on bridge strength centrality, computed with Physical, Psychological, and Outcome communities, because the research question concerns how the physical and psychological domains connect to each other, and bridge centrality quantifies the connectivity of a node to communities other than its own, whereas overall strength does not make this distinction. Stability was assessed via case-dropping bootstrap (CS-coefficient; 2,500 iterations) and edge weight accuracy via nonparametric bootstrap (2,500 iterations); only centrality indices that met the CS-coefficient stability threshold are reported. Both networks triggered the dense-network warning; a sensitivity analysis using threshold = TRUE verified the robustness of bridge centrality rankings.

Fourth, a Network Comparison Test [33] tested global structure and strength differences. Edge-level differences were subjected to FDR correction. All analyses used R 4.5.1 with lavaan, semTools, bootnet, qgraph, networktools, and NetworkComparisonTest.

Measurement models and invariance

CFA results supported the two-factor FTAS (CFI = 0.993, TLI = 0.991, SRMR = 0.059) and the unidimensional MTI (CFI = 0.993, TLI = 0.990, SRMR = 0.059) according to CFI, TLI, and SRMR. All loadings exceeded 0.68 (anxiety) and 0.74 (mental toughness) (Supplementary Table S3). RMSEA values were elevated (0.115 for the FTAS and 0.198 for the MTI). Although elevated RMSEA is a recognized property of WLSMV estimation with ordered-categorical data [28], an elevated RMSEA still warrants interpretive caution even when other indices are acceptable, and we therefore examined its source directly. For the MTI, all of the largest modification indices were residual covariances between items rather than cross-loadings (a unidimensional model admits no cross-loading indices), and they were concentrated in adjacent item pairs (e.g., items 3–4, 5–6, 7–8); freeing these four adjacent-pair residual covariances in a diagnostic model reduced the RMSEA from 0.198 to 0.098 (Supplementary Table S4). This pattern indicates that the misfit reflects localized residual dependence among semantically adjacent items rather than a structural problem such as substantial cross-loadings. The localized dependence is consistent with item-wording or method overlap. Because these diagnostics were post hoc and the MTI is a validated unidimensional scale, we retained the original factor structure and interpret MTI-related findings with appropriate caution; the elevated RMSEA was substantially reduced in the diagnostic model but remained above conventional thresholds. Both scales achieved scalar invariance across gender (Table 2; all ΔCFI < 0.010).

Direct associations

Path coefficients are presented in Table 3; Fig. 1. BMI was associated with lower scores in both testing contexts (males: β = −0.519; females: β = −0.242; both p < .001). Training experience was positively associated with scores in both contexts (males: β = 0.147, p = .005; females: β = 0.194, p < .001). Exercise was significantly associated with higher scores in the male context (β = 0.162, p < .001); in the female context the direct association was weaker and did not reach significance in either the primary specification (β = 0.045, p = .121) or the log-transformed specification (β = 0.053, p = .053), although it did reach significance under the non-BMI composite outcome and when training experience was excluded, and is therefore best characterized as a weaker and less stable direct association rather than as the absence of one. Anxiety was associated with lower scores in both contexts (males: β = −0.116, p = .008; females: β = −0.165, p < .001). Mental toughness was associated with higher scores in the female context (β = 0.076, p = .012) and showed a positive but non-significant coefficient of similar magnitude in the male context (β = 0.050, p = .202); as the constraint test reported below indicates, these coefficients did not differ significantly across contexts. All physical variables were significantly associated with both psychological linking variables in the expected directions (Table 3).

Path-by-path constraint tests

Constraint tests (Table 5) indicated that the association of BMI with scores differed significantly across testing contexts (Δχ²(1) = 34.29, p < .001). The associations of exercise with scores (Δχ²(1) = 5.21, p = .023) and of exercise with mental toughness (Δχ²(1) = 5.82, p = .016) also differed significantly under the primary specification; however, both of these exercise-related contrasts fell below significance when the exercise measure was log-transformed (exercise → score: Δχ²(1) = 3.11, p = .078; exercise → mental toughness: Δχ²(1) = 2.96, p = .086), indicating that they are sensitive to the scaling of the PARS-3 measure. By contrast, the BMI → score contrast remained significant under the full-battery specifications, including the log-transformed exercise measure and the non-BMI composite outcome (see below), although additional shared-item sensitivity analyses (Supplementary Table S9) indicated that the contrast was attenuated when the outcome was restricted to shared test components. The associations of anxiety with scores (Δχ²(1) = 0.92, p = .338) and of mental toughness with scores (Δχ²(1) = 0.29, p = .592) did not differ across contexts, and this non-difference was robust across all specifications. This pattern indicates that the most robust context-dependent difference lies in the BMI component of the physical-to-performance association, the exercise-related differences are less stable, and the psychological-to-performance paths do not differ across contexts.

Sensitivity analyses

Four sets of sensitivity analyses supported the robustness of the central findings while clarifying their boundaries. First, when the official composite score was replaced with a non-BMI reweighted composite (computed on the n = 1,712 subsample with complete item-level data, and compared with the official composite re-estimated on the same subsample), BMI remained significantly associated with lower scores in both contexts (males: β = −0.380; females: β = −0.153; both p < .001), with the coefficient attenuated by roughly one fifth in the male context and somewhat more in the female context; critically, the BMI → score constraint test was essentially unchanged (official-score subsample: Δχ²(1) = 18.85; non-BMI subsample: Δχ²(1) = 19.50), indicating that the context difference in the BMI–performance association is not an artifact of the BMI scoring component (Supplementary Table S6). Second, the log-transformed exercise specification left the within-context significance pattern of all path coefficients unchanged but, as noted above, rendered the exercise-related constraint tests non-significant (Supplementary Table S7). Third, excluding training experience from the models left the BMI-related contrast and the cross-context non-difference of the psychological-to-performance paths unchanged (Supplementary Table S8); The only notable change was that the direct exercise → score association reached significance in the female context, reinforcing the characterization of that association as weak and specification-sensitive rather than absent. Participants excluded from the non-BMI analysis (n = 204, with incomplete item-level data) had, on average, lower composite scores and higher BMI and anxiety than those retained, so the non-BMI analysis should be read as a sensitivity check among test completers. Fourth, to evaluate whether the BMI-related context contrast is confined to gender-specific test components or is also present in shared items, we constructed three shared-item outcomes (Supplementary Table S9). When the outcome was restricted to the five shared national-rubric components (BMI plus vital capacity, 50-m sprint, standing long jump, and sit-and-reach), the BMI → score contrast remained significant but was markedly smaller than in the full official composite (Δχ²(1) = 5.77, p = .016). When BMI was removed and only the four non-BMI shared national-rubric components were retained, the contrast was attenuated and no longer significant (Δχ²(1) = 2.80, p = .095). When these four shared components were represented as raw, directionally aligned z scores rather than national-rubric scores, the BMI contrast was absent (Δχ²(1) = 0.45, p = .503). Across all three shared-item variants, the psychological-to-performance paths remained non-different across contexts (anxiety → score and mental toughness → score: ps > 0.60) and exercise → score contrasts were non-significant (ps = 0.115 to 0.192). These analyses indicate that the official-score BMI contrast is not evident in shared raw performance components and is likely localized to the gender-differentiated and algorithmically scored components of the official testing battery.

Discussion

This study examined whether physical factors are associated with fitness test performance through psychological linking variables, and whether these biopsychosocial association patterns differ across gender-differentiated testing contexts in China’s mandatory fitness testing system. Three principal findings emerged. First, physical factors were associated with performance both directly and indirectly through anxiety, supporting the biopsychosocial indirect-association hypothesis (Hypothesis 1). Second, formal constraint tests revealed that the clearest and most robust context-dependent difference lay in the BMI → score association, whereas the psychological-to-performance paths did not differ across contexts; Hypothesis 2 was thus only partially supported, because the exercise-related contrasts, although significant under the primary specification, were sensitive to the scaling of the exercise measure, and Hypothesis 3 was supported. Third, a specific cognitive anxiety item concerning failure to meet the passing standard emerged as the strongest psychological bridge node across both contexts, supporting Hypothesis 4.

Context-dependent association patterns at the physical-to-performance interface

The path-by-path constraint tests locate the context-dependent differences in the physical-to-performance interface. The BMI → score association differed significantly across the male and female testing contexts (Δχ² = 34.29, p < .001) and this difference persisted under the full-battery sensitivity analyses, including the non-BMI composite outcome and the log-transformed exercise measure. Additional shared-item sensitivity analyses refine the interpretation of this contrast (Supplementary Table S9). The contrast persisted in both full-battery composites, indicating that it was not simply a mathematical artifact of including BMI in the official score. However, when the outcome was restricted to shared test components the contrast was attenuated, and when the four shared components were represented as raw, directionally aligned z scores rather than national-rubric scores the contrast was absent. The BMI contrast should therefore not be interpreted as a general difference across all physical-performance components; rather, it appears localized to the gender-differentiated and algorithmically scored structure of the official testing battery. This localization is consistent with a task-demand account but still cannot distinguish task demands from biological or biomechanical sex differences. The exercise → score and exercise → mental toughness contrasts were also significant under the primary specification (Δχ² = 5.21 and 5.82) but fell below significance under the log-transformed exercise measure, so they are best regarded as suggestive rather than definitive. In contrast, the psychological-to-performance paths did not differ across contexts (anxiety → score: p = .338; mental toughness → score: p = .592), a non-difference that was stable across every specification we examined. Taken together, the physical-to-performance association was context-dependent while the psychological-to-performance paths were context-general. This overall pattern is consistent with an account in which the male-specific items, namely pull-ups and the 1,000-m run, impose a stronger body-mass constraint than the female-specific items. We emphasize, however, that gender and task structure are completely confounded in this testing system: because every student who shares a gender also shares a test battery, the present design cannot establish that the difference is driven by task structure rather than by biological or biomechanical sex differences. Both explanations remain viable, and our data cannot adjudicate between them.

A task-demand account of the BMI contrast is mechanically plausible. Pull-ups require repeatedly lifting the entire body mass against gravity, so excess body mass acts as a direct load penalty; the same body-mass dependence characterizes other validated bodyweight power tasks, such as rope climbing, in which heavier individuals are systematically disadvantaged because they must displace a greater absolute mass [34], and analytical models of bodyweight movements formalize how body mass enters the mechanical work and power such tasks demand [35]. The 1,000-m run similarly penalizes excess body mass through increased metabolic cost. Sit-ups involve primarily trunk movement with a weaker dependence on whole-body mass, and the 800-m run is 20% shorter. The direction of the association between body mass and performance is itself task-dependent: in external-resistance tasks such as the bench press and squat, greater body mass predicts greater maximal strength [36], whereas in the body-mass-bearing items that dominate the male battery body mass instead acts as a load that must be overcome. The negative BMI–score association observed here is therefore consistent with the body-mass-bearing structure of the male-specific items rather than with body mass being detrimental to performance in general. Consistent with this reasoning, the psychological-to-performance associations operated with statistically indistinguishable strength across the two testing contexts [37, 38], whereas the BMI–performance association did not. We note, however, that this remains an interpretation rather than a demonstration: because the male and female batteries are completely confounded with gender, the steeper BMI–performance association in the male context is equally consistent with biomechanical sex differences, and the present cross-sectional design cannot separate the two.

The NCT results are consistent with this interpretation. Global network strength did not differ across contexts (p = .077), indicating that the total connectivity within each biopsychosocial system is comparable. Global structure, however, differed significantly (p < .001), and no individual edges survived FDR correction, suggesting that the structural difference reflects a distributed redistribution of connection weights across the physical domain rather than localized changes in specific edges [17].

BMI also showed significant indirect associations with scores through anxiety in both testing contexts, indicating that body composition is statistically linked to performance partly through anxiety-related cognitions. This is consistent with research on social physique anxiety, which suggests that individuals who perceive their bodies as discrepant from normative standards experience heightened anxiety in evaluative contexts involving physical display. These findings are compatible with the view that, even in non-competitive assessment settings, the association between body composition and performance is not purely mechanical.

Exercise benefits: direct in one context, indirect in both

Exercise was associated with performance through somewhat different patterns across the two testing contexts. In the male context, exercise showed both a significant direct association (β = 0.162) and a significant total indirect association through the two linking variables (β = 0.036). In the female context, the direct association was weaker and did not reach significance in the primary specification (β = 0.045, p = .121), although it became significant under several alternative specifications, while the indirect association was consistently significant (β = 0.028, p < .001). The constraint test indicated a stronger direct association in the male context under the primary specification (Δχ² = 5.21, p = .023), but this contrast did not remain significant when the exercise measure was log-transformed; the exercise-related context difference should therefore be treated as suggestive rather than established.

This pattern is consistent with the exercise-affect literature, which documents robust anxiolytic effects of regular physical activity [39] and positive associations between exercise and mental toughness [40]. The present findings extend this literature by linking these psychological benefits to a concrete performance outcome. A practical implication is that exercise promotion programs that emphasize only physical fitness benefits may understate the most relevant association for students in testing contexts where physical constraints are less dominant. Framing exercise as a means of psychological preparation, not only physical conditioning, may be a more complete motivational message.

Mental toughness: a small, context-general association

Mental toughness showed a positive association with scores that reached significance in the female context (β = 0.076, p = .012) and was positive but non-significant, with a similar point estimate, in the male context (β = 0.050, p = .202); the two coefficients are consistent with a small, context-general association between mental toughness and performance [40]. The appropriate conclusion is drawn from the constraint test, which indicated that these coefficients did not differ significantly across contexts (Δχ² = 0.29, p = .592): the mental-toughness-to-performance association does not significantly differ between the two testing contexts. The within-context difference in significance should not be over-interpreted, because the male estimate is less precise; the coefficient reached significance in the larger female subsample but not in the smaller male subsample. The primary inference here is the non-significant constraint test, not a claim about an effect hidden by limited power. Accordingly, the non-significant male coefficient should not be read as evidence that mental toughness is irrelevant in that testing context. In the shared-item sensitivity analyses (Supplementary Table S9), the direct mental-toughness → score association was further attenuated (males β = 0.013, females β = 0.037 under the raw z-score composite), suggesting that this small direct association is outcome-dependent and should not be over-interpreted.

The relative weight of the psychological domain in the female model warrants one further clarification. Where the female model shows a larger relative contribution of the psychological linking variables, this arises not because psychological factors are more potent in that context but because the physical-domain associations are weaker there. The constraint tests show no cross-context difference in the psychological-to-performance paths; the larger relative contribution instead reflects weaker physical-domain associations, leaving more residual variance for the psychological variables to account for. For intervention design, this implies that the scope for psychological preparation, within these gender-differentiated testing contexts, appears to depend on the strength of the physical-to-performance association; the present design, however, cannot determine whether this reflects task structure, biological sex, or their interaction.

Identifying a specific cognitive bridge: item a1

Network analysis identified item a1 (“I am worried about failing to meet the passing standards of the fitness test”) as the strongest psychological bridge node in both contexts, a finding robust across thresholded sensitivity analyses. The content of a1 is directly tied to the institutional consequence structure: under the current policy framework, students who fail the passing standard may be denied degree conferral, a consequence that elevates the fitness test from a routine health assessment to a high-stakes evaluative event. The structural centrality of this item suggests that the anxiety cognition most closely connecting the physical and psychological domains is a specific evaluative appraisal, not diffuse physiological arousal. This is consistent with broader cognitive-appraisal accounts of evaluative stress [41].

This finding should be interpreted as a statistical indicator of structural centrality within the network, not as evidence of causal transmission [17]. As a candidate target for intervention, it suggests that practitioners could focus on the specific cognition of failing the standard through cognitive restructuring techniques. At the institutional level, increasing transparency about remedial testing opportunities, providing progress-oriented feedback, and communicating the health-monitoring purpose of the assessment could reduce the evaluative threat that this cognition reflects [42, 43]. These recommendations align with broader calls for reducing the threat structure of high-stakes physical assessments [44].

Methodological complementarity

SEM and network analysis provided convergent evidence from different levels of analysis. SEM indicated that physical factors are associated with performance both directly and indirectly through psychological linking variables. Network analysis identified which specific psychological content serves as the primary bridge. The NCT showed that context differences are systemic (significant structure difference, p < .001) rather than localized to individual edges (none survived FDR correction), broadly consistent with the SEM finding that the clearest context difference was located in the physical-to-performance interface rather than the psychological-to-performance interface. Network analysis is an exploratory technique and its edges represent partial correlations, not causal effects [17, 45]; the two methods should be understood as complementary descriptions rather than as confirming each other’s causal claims.

Alternative explanations

Several alternative explanations merit consideration. First, the cross-sectional design is equally compatible with reverse or bidirectional relations [46]: students who perform well may subsequently experience reduced anxiety, and prior testing experiences may shape current psychological states, so the directionality specified in our models reflects a theoretically motivated structure rather than an established causal order. Second, the observed associations may also be shaped by processes we did not measure. These include motivational orientation, prior success or failure experiences in fitness testing, social comparison, and perceptions of the fairness of the testing system, any of which could contribute to the anxiety–performance association. Third, BMI should not be read as a purely biomechanical variable: higher BMI may also be associated with body image concerns, lower self-esteem, and exposure to weight-related social stigma, and these psychosocial correlates could contribute to both the BMI–anxiety and the BMI–performance associations. Finally, although psychological measures were self-reported while test scores were objectively recorded, which partially mitigates common method bias [47], the physical predictor variables (exercise, training experience) were also self-reported and may share method variance with the psychological linking variables [48, 49].

Practical implications

The findings suggest that differentiated preparation strategies may be more effective than uniform approaches. In testing contexts where physical constraints are more dominant (e.g., the male battery with pull-ups), body composition management and targeted physical training are likely to be priorities. In contexts where physical constraints are less dominant, psychological preparation may deserve comparatively greater emphasis. The cross-context stability of item a1 as a bridge node suggests that concern about failing the passing standard is a common psychological vulnerability regardless of testing context, pointing to institutional design as a lever for reducing assessment-related anxiety. Self-determination theory offers one framework for such institutional reform: Ahmadi et al.’s [50] classification of autonomy-, competence-, and relatedness-supportive instructional behaviors suggests concrete ways in which the testing environment could be made less threatening. Specific examples include transparent communication about remedial opportunities, progress-oriented feedback, and framing the assessment around health monitoring rather than evaluative ranking. More broadly, these findings illustrate that in an institutionalized evaluative physical performance context, the relative scope for psychological intervention should be interpreted in relation to the physical constraints of each testing battery.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations should be emphasized. First and most fundamentally, gender and task structure are completely confounded in this testing system: because the test battery is assigned by gender, the design cannot separate task-structure effects from biological or biomechanical sex differences, and the context differences we report should therefore be read as context-specific association patterns rather than as evidence that task demands operate independently of gender. Second, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference; all paths represent theoretically motivated associations, not established causal effects, and longitudinal or intervention designs are needed to test directional hypotheses [46]. Third, the outcome is a composite institutional score: the male and female composites are built from partially different items and scoring algorithms, so they are not measurement-equivalent, and the composite can obscure meaningful variability across individual test components. This is a particularly central limitation given this study’s emphasis on task structure, and item-level analyses would be needed to fully characterize the underlying differences. Fourth, BMI is itself a 15% weighted component of the official composite; although the non-BMI sensitivity analysis indicated that the BMI–performance context difference is not an artifact of this overlap, that analysis was necessarily restricted to the subsample with complete item-level data, which had higher scores and lower BMI and anxiety than the excluded participants. Fifth, exercise and training experience were self-reported; exercise was a positively skewed multiplicative composite (and the exercise-related context differences were sensitive to its scaling), and training experience was a single item without estimable reliability. Sixth, the male and female subsamples were unequal (492 vs. 1,424), so within-context significance patterns partly reflect precision differences; the constraint tests, which use both subsamples jointly, provide more reliable evidence for context-dependent differences. Seventh, convenience sampling from Shanghai universities may introduce institutional and socioeconomic biases that limit generalizability. Eighth, because psychological measures were collected within one week before testing, the temporal proximity between the anxiety assessment and the performance varied across participants, which may have added noise to the observed anxiety–performance associations. Finally, RMSEA values for the CFA models were elevated; as shown in the Results, the MTI misfit reflects localized residual dependence among adjacent items rather than a structural problem, but MTI-related findings should still be interpreted with caution.

Conclusion

This study examined biopsychosocial association patterns with fitness test performance across the two gender-differentiated testing contexts of a mandatory national testing system. Formal path-by-path constraint tests indicated that the clearest context-dependent difference in the official full-battery analyses resides in the BMI → score association, which differed across contexts under every full-battery specification examined; additional shared-item sensitivity analyses indicate that this contrast is localized to the gender-differentiated and algorithmically scored components of the official testing battery. The psychological-to-performance paths involving anxiety and mental toughness did not differ across contexts. The exercise-related contrasts were significant under the primary specification but sensitive to the scaling of the exercise measure, and are therefore reported as suggestive. This overall pattern is consistent with a task-demand account, but because gender and task structure are institutionally confounded, it cannot be taken as evidence that task demands operate independently of gender or biological sex. Network analysis identified concern about failing the passing standard as the specific cognitive content most centrally connecting the physical and psychological domains, linking the findings to the institutional design of the assessment. These results suggest that institutional reforms reducing evaluative threat may benefit students across testing contexts, while the relative role of psychological preparation should be interpreted within the physical constraints of each gender-differentiated testing battery.
