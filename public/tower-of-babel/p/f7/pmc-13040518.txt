Self‐Regulating Sodium‐Ion Battery Materials: From Phase Reconstruction to Functional Activation
Authors: Hong Gao, Dingyi Zhang, Chao Wang, Tianxiao Chen, Xingwang Peng, Yuxiu Xing, Xin Guo, Yufei Zhao, Yong Wang, Guoxiu Wang, Hao Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSodium‐ion batteries are advancing toward practical deployment, yet their long‐term durability is governed not by static material properties but by the coupled evolution of the lattice, interface, and electrolyte environments under operating conditions. However, a systematic consolidation of these dispersed mechanistic insights is still lacking. This review introduces a unified self‐regulation framework in which structural and chemical changes remain confined, reversible, and functionally aligned with electrochemical transport. Evidence is organized along three axes: lattice and phase evolution in layered oxides, where controlled slab glide, moderated Na‐vacancy ordering, and stabilized oxygen participation narrow phase windows; interphase chemistry and mechanics, emphasizing inorganic‐rich, self‐renewing interfacial architectures that sustain ion transport and limit dissolution; and electrolyte‐materials coupling, where solvation structure, concentration regimes, and targeted additives steer interphase reconstruction and near‐surface transport. The scope spans major cathode families and self‐buffering anodes, with electrolytes treated as purposeful enablers. From these insights, we distill design rules linking operando signatures to composition and processing choices and outline opportunities for model‐guided optimization and data‐driven discovery. This framework provides a materials‐first roadmap toward programmable, durable sodium‐ion batteries operating reliably under practical constraints.

Introduction

Sodium‐ion batteries (SIBs) are gaining traction for grid and cost‐sensitive storage because sodium is abundant, precursor supply chains are flexible, and established lithium‐ion manufacturing processes can be adapted with limited retooling [1]. The decisive factor for durable cells remains how active materials and their interfaces evolve during operation rather than static properties [2, 3, 4, 5]. In this review, self‐regulation denotes structural and chemical evolution that remains confined and self‐limiting during cycling so that transport pathways stay open, parasitic reactions are curtailed, and performance is sustained over long service. This review focuses on SIBs with layered oxide cathodes and self‐buffering alloy or conversion anodes. Electrolytes are considered targeted enablers used to reinforce intended material behavior.

Within this scope, we organize the field along four coupled axes. First, the lattice–phase axis in layered oxides: an O3 ↔ P3 window with O2 accessed only at deep desodiation, together with restrained Na‐vacancy ordering and controlled layer glide, enables low‐hysteresis cycling [6, 7, 8, 9], while composition, site, and entropy design steer these pathways [10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16]. Second, the interface axis, robust cathode electrolyte interphase (CEI) and solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) built on inorganic backbones capped by thin organic layers preserve charge transfer and diffusion channels and curb gas evolution and transition‐metal dissolution [17, 18, 19, 20]. Third, composition‐structure programmed intrinsic self‐healing: by jointly tuning electrode composition (including site/entropy design) [11, 21, 22, 23, 24] and self‐buffering architectures, [25, 26, 27] the electrode can confine phase evolution and interfacial/mechanical degradation into a reversible window, thereby maintaining percolating ion/electron transport during cycling without relying on external triggers. Fourth, electrolyte‐materials coupling: solvation states and concentration regimes steer interphase chemistry and near‐surface transport, amplifying the reversibility encoded by the lattice and interface [28, 29, 30, 31].

Evidence from recent studies supports the importance of self‐regulation in SIBs. For example, in layered oxide cathodes, entropy engineering [6, 11] and site design [12, 13] can stabilize lattice‐oxygen participation while minimizing hysteresis and smoothing phase evolution. On the anode side, yolk–shell Sb@Void@graphdiyne nanoboxes exemplify intrinsic self‐regulation: the internal void buffers Sb expansion, the π‐conjugated graphdiyne shell sustains electronic/ionic percolation, and the SEI remains compact during repeated reconstruction. In full cells (Sb@Void@graphdiyne ∥ Na3V2(PO4)3), capacity remains 354 mAh g−1 after 500 cycles at 1 A g−1 [32]. In addition, electrolyte additives can trigger self‐regulation at the interface [33, 34, 35]. For example, sodium difluoro(oxalato)borate (NaDFOB) reconstructs the SEI into a thin organic skin atop a compact sublayer rich in NaF and borate species that densifies during the first ∼50 cycles. It's demonstrated that this evolution seals interfacial defects, limits solvent access, and sustains ionic conduction. This behavior represents an archetype of self‐passivation and supports durable cycling in SIBs [36]. Taken together, self‐regulation functions as a design posture that aligns lattice pathways with interphase renewal, with electrolyte choices applied where they strengthen this alignment.

Despite progress, several practical issues remain. In electrodes with high areal capacity, the interphase must nucleate rapidly and stay spatially uniform across tortuous mesostructures so that charge‐transfer and diffusion pathways stay open. Crosstalk between dissolved transition‐metal species and the anode interphase should be curtailed while maintaining operation at elevated voltage and acceptable initial efficiency. Reliable service across a wide temperature range requires interphases that prevent solvent cointercalation at low temperature and suppress accelerated parasitic reactions at high temperature [159, 160]. Finally, design rules need to remain valid under scale up constraints, including binder chemistry, conductive network architecture, calendering, and stack pressure, so that behavior demonstrated in coin cells translates to industrial electrodes [161–163].

Building on this rationale, the remainder of the review assembles in‐operando evidence along four coupled axes summarized in Scheme 1: (i) programmable lattice/phase trajectories, (ii) self‐limiting interphase chemistry and mechanics, (iii) composition/structure‐guided intrinsic self‐healing (including self‐buffering anode architectures), and (iv) electrolyte‐materials coupling that reinforces intended lattice–interphase feedback. Within this map, we translate mechanistic signatures into composition, site, and entropy strategies for layered cathodes, and examine self‐buffering anodes with attention to particle size, conductive‐network architecture, and strain dissipation. Self‐Evolution Mechanism of Layered Oxide Cathodes for SIBs

Layered oxides remain the most extensively explored cathodes for SIBs, supported by mature synthesis routes and good compatibility with hard‐carbon anodes. Their practical behavior is governed less by static structure than by how the lattice and interfaces evolve under cycling. Here, self‐evolution denotes structural and chemical changes that proceed reversibly and become self‐limiting under operating stresses, thereby sustaining transport and minimizing parasitic loss. In contrast, uncontrolled evolution enlarges phase transition windows and accumulates defects, leading to rising hysteresis and accelerated degradation.

The performance of layered oxides is governed by a coupled set of processes linking stacking/phase topology, Na+/vacancy ordering, diffusion, transition‐metal migration, and lattice‐oxygen participation. Typically, the accessible O3 ↔ P3 window (O2 phase appears only under deep desodiation), and the kinetics of slab gliding determine whether the phase transitions stay narrow, repeatable, and low‐hysteresis. Na+/vacancy ordering modulates site energies and diffusion pathways, shaping voltage profiles and rate capability. Cation migration and oxygen redox set the ceiling for high‐voltage stability. Importantly, these variables are not independent: subtle changes in layer stacking reshape Na+ diffusion fields and local stress, which in turn govern transition‐metal migration and lattice‐oxygen redox activity. We distinguish two regimes of evolution: one that is reversible and spatially confined, sustaining low voltage hysteresis; the other that grows irreversibly, enlarges hysteresis, and accelerates degradation.

Phase‐Transition Window and Layer‐Sliding Kinetics

The electrochemical performance of layered sodium transition‐metal oxides (NaxTMO2) is governed by phase evolution during Na+ (de)intercalation. Transitions originate from slab glide of TMO2 layers coupled to Na+/vacancy ordering, which together modify oxygen stacking and Na+ coordination. To understand and control these transitions, it is necessary to deconstruct the mechanism into its driving forces (interlayer electrostatics and bonding), the specific degradation pathways in common host structures (O3 and P2), and the emergent behavior in high‐voltage intergrowth systems. Key parameters describing these phenomena include the interlayer spacing (c‐axis), glide vectors, stacking fault density, solid‐solution region width, and activation energy. This section reviews these mechanisms, analyzing kinetics and regulation strategies through the following sub‐aspects.

Mechanics of Slab Gliding and Interlayer Dynamics

Layered NaxTMO2 cathodes can be classified by the Na+ site geometry between adjacent TM─O slabs and the stacking periodicity of those slabs. In the O3‐type phase (space group R3m), Na+ occupies octahedral sites within ABCABC oxygen stacking, and Na+ diffusion proceeds through tetrahedral bottlenecks. In P2‐type structures (P63/mmc), Na+ resides in trigonal‐prismatic sites within a distinct stacking sequence that affords more open two‐dimensional ion diffusion pathways. The framework stability is therefore subject to both the Na+ coordination environment and the kinetic barrier for interslab sliding.

Slab glide simultaneously alters oxygen stacking and Na+ coordination, and it is governed by interlayer electrostatics and bonding rather than interlayer spacing alone. A large average interlayer spacing can lower Na+ migration barriers and may weaken the thermodynamic driving force for gliding only when coupled with changes in interlayer bonding (e.g., stronger TM─O covalency and/or increased interlayer charge density); spacing enlargement by itself does not guarantee glide suppression [37, 38, 39]. For example, Wang et al. examined P2‐Na0.7[Mn0.6Ni0.3Mg0.01]O2 and showed that Mg2+ substitutes both TM and Na sites, slightly increasing the c‐axis spacing from 5.127 Å (x = 0) to 5.140 Å (x = 0.1), mitigating slab glide and stabilizing the framework [40].

Evolution and Degradation in O3‐Type Hosts

In O3 hosts, phase evolution is predominantly controlled by a competition between reversible slab gliding and progressive strain accumulation. De‐sodiation frequently promotes glide along (1/3, 0, 0), changing oxygen stacking from ABCABC to ABCBAC and giving the O3↔P3 window. For instance, Na0.75Fe0.25Cu0.25Mn0.5O2 shows a reversible window over 3.2–4.1 V with a lattice‐volume change of about 1.4% from 127.4 to 125.99 Å3 based on ex‐situ XRD [41]. In situ XRD on NaNi0.5Mn0.5O2 revealed decreasing O3 (003) intensity together with the emergence of a P3 (003) reflection, which is consistent with a two‐phase O3 to P3 transformation in the probed range, since peak splitting rather than uniform shifting was observed [42].

However, irreversibility arises when accumulated lattice strain impedes Na+ reinsertion, effectively broadening the transition window and increasing voltage hysteresis [43]. Concomitantly, stacking‐fault buildup is commonly reflected as peak broadening in diffraction patterns [44]. At deeper de‐sodiation, Jahn–Teller‐active Mn3+ and changing electrostatics can drive monoclinic O′3 or P′3 states with concomitant changes in TM─O bond lengths and angles. Xu and colleagues investigated O3‐NaNi0.4Mn0.4Co0.2O2 and revealed that synthesis‐induced native lattice strain triggers irreversible transitions during cycling; relaxation of this strain coincided with pronounced changes in the c parameter and capacity decay, as resolved by in situ synchrotron X‐ray diffraction (SXRD) (Figure 1a) [6]. Similarly, Hong et al. reported Sc‐doped O3‐NaNi1/3Fe1/3Mn1/3O2, where Sc3+ enhances TM─O covalency and expands the Na+ diffusion layer spacing from 2.954 to 3.065 Å; slab glide and the P3 → O3′ transition were suppressed, leading to improved cycling stability (Figure 1b) [7].

Strain metrics provide a quantitative bridge between phase‐evolution pathways and practical degradation. Ding and co‐workers reported anisotropic strain in the high‐entropy NCFMS cathode (NaNi0.3Cu0.1Fe0.2Mn0.3Sn0.1O2), reaching about 0.51% along c‐axis, whereas Ti‐substituted NCFMT exhibited approximately 0.01% overall strain. The higher planar strain promoted cation displacement, elemental segregation, and crack initiation (Figure 1c), consequently deteriorating cycling performance, vice versa (Figure 1d). Likewise, geometric‐phase analysis (GPA) on cycled O3‐NaNi0.4Mn0.4Co0.2O2 located tensile fields along Sn‐rich regions, and the (003) plane was stretched by approximately 3.6% relative to the pristine lattice; strain relaxation coincided with dislocations and stacking faults that limited Na+ reinsertion and exacerbated voltage hysterisis (Figure 1e) [6].

Evolution and Degradation in P2‐Type Hosts

Distinct from the O3 systems, P2 hosts tend to become unstable at low sodium contents, where deep desodiation can trigger abrupt stacking changes and, in severe cases, structural collapse. When x < 0.3 (NaxTMO2), P2 phases frequently undergo a transformation to O2 phases through slab gliding along [1/3, 2/3, z] and [2/3, 1/3, z], changing oxygen stacking from ABBA to ABAC. The transition features a marked contraction of the c axis together with a unit‐cell volume decrease, producing anisotropic strain and intragranular cracking. Microstructural observations are consistent with this strain‐driven degradation picture. In highly strained O3‐NaNi0.4Mn0.4Co0.2O2 charged to 4.4 V, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed dislocations and stacking faults characteristics of strain‐relaxation processes (Figure 1f) [6]. Similarly, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of the NFMMT (Na[Ni0.4Fe0.1Mn0.4Mg0.05Ti0.05]O2) after 200 cycles revealed intragranular cracks that penetrate particles, attributing to stress accumulation driven by anisotropic lattice changes (Figure 1g) [9].

Complex Stacking and High‐Voltage Intergrowths

Under high‐voltage operation, particularly in Fe‐containing layered oxides, phase evolution can deviate from simple O3↔P3 or P2↔O2 pathways and instead proceed through complex stacking sequences and coherent intergrowths that determine reversibility. In this regime, ordered OP4‐type motifs and coherent Z‐type stacking have been identified as intermediate or limiting structures. This evolution is typically initiated by site‐selective Na extraction that disrupts Na ordering and oxygen stacking, followed by slab gliding and coordination reconfiguration that generate coherent P‐ and O‐type layer intergrowths, as captured by operando XRD and annular dark‐field scanning TEM (ADF‐STEM) [45]. 57Fe Mössbauer spectroscopy of post‐cycled cathodes can track charge‐compensation pathways and the reversibility of the local Fe coordination environment, providing an independent probe of high‐voltage stability [46].

Taken together, two descriptors consistently define the key design constraints for layered cathodes: the breadth of the accessible phase‐transition window and the magnitude/anisotropy of the lattice strain generated within that window. Both are governed by Na‐layer electrostatics, interslab bonding, and intrinsic strain states, meaning that interlayer spacing must be tuned in concert with TM─O covalency/charge distribution and Na‐layer field regulation rather than treated as an independent lever. Ultimately, phase‐transition reversibility is limited by the framework's mechanical tolerance to slab gliding and strain accumulation: O3 hosts tend to undergo high‐voltage shear‐driven stacking changes, whereas P2 hosts are vulnerable to deep‐desodiation‐induced collapse; in both cases, managing the glide pathway (vector and barrier) and minimizing lattice mismatch provides a more general strategy than simply enlarging spacing. Accordingly, mechanistic validation should integrate operando diffraction with quantitative strain metrics and standard electrochemical readouts to confirm narrow, repeatable transition windows with low hysteresis, thereby establishing the structural prerequisite for activating high‐capacity anionic (oxygen) redox chemistry in the next section.

Oxygen Redox and Structural Stabilization

Transition‐metal redox defines the baseline capacity of layered oxides but is intrinsically constrained by accessible electron count. Unlocking capacities beyond this ceiling, therefore require additional charge compensation, and lattice oxygen redox has emerged as a powerful route once its accompanying structural instabilities can be managed. A series of operando and ex situ evidence supports oxygen redox participation during charging/discharging in Na‐layered oxides, including in situ XANES at TM K‐edges together with 57Fe Mössbauer signatures in NMF/NMFC systems [47, 48], high‐voltage electrochemical features near 4.0‐4.3 V [49], and enhanced O K‐edge pre‐edge intensity consistent with increased TM 3d–O 2p hybridization [50]. However, oxygen redox (O‐redox) is treated as a potentially reversible contribution that becomes practically useful only when electronic and local structural conditions suppress oxygen loss and mitigate large voltage hysteresis. The following subsections thus focus on the electronic origins of this behavior, the mechanisms of hysteresis, and the chemical/structural strategies to regulate oxygen activity.

Electronic Origins and Covalent Stabilization

At the electronic‐structure level, O‐redox arises when TM 3d–O 2p hybridization brings oxygen‐derived states close to the Fermi level. Under sufficiently strong TM─O covalency, the oxidized oxygen states can be stabilized within the lattice and largely recovered upon discharge; For instance, Na3RuO4 has been demonstrated as a perfect prototype Na‐rich layered oxide, where the highly covalent Ru─O bonds enable highly reversible anionic redox activity while effectively suppressing structural degradation and irreversible oxygen gas release [51]. By contrast, weak hybridization favors non‐bonding oxygen character and promotes O–O dimer formation with attendant gas release (Figure 2a). Partial density‐of‐states (pDOS) analyses capture the bonding and antibonding manifolds near EF and provide a compact descriptor for accessibility of O‐redox states (Figure 2b) [17, 52].

Operationally, lattice‐oxygen participation can be screened using three electrochemical indicators [55, 56, 57]. First, a distinct plateau or shoulder appears above the transition‐metal redox region in galvanostatic profiles, with a corresponding differential‐capacity feature near 4.2–4.3 V (Figure 2c). Second, the feature persists under quasi‐equilibrium probing by galvanostatic intermittent titration technique (GITT) or the potentiostatic intermittent titration technique (PITT), which helps exclude a purely kinetic origin. Third, the hysteresis area between charge and discharge contracts when TM─O covalency is strengthened. In practice, reliable assignment of oxygen redox requires a multi‐technique “fingerprint”. This typically involves correlating high‐voltage electrochemical signatures with spectroscopic evidence of bulk oxygen participation from resonant inelastic X‐ray scattering (RIXS) (Figure 2d,e), surface oxygen redox via X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and Raman spectroscopy characterizations [53, 54].

Material examples show how local electronic structure can be tuned toward reversible O‐redox. In Na0.8Li0.12Ni0.22Mn0.66O1.95F0.05(NLNMOF), Li+/F− co‐doping adjusts orbital hybridization: Li substitution at TM sites introduces oxygen‐derived states near the Fermi level and activates high‐voltage O‐redox, while F− strengthens Mn–O–F covalency, raising the oxygen‐vacancy formation energy from 3.329 to 3.859 eV and lowering the Na+ diffusion barrier; the result is high O‐redox activity of about 125 mAh·g− 1 at 1C with ∼64% capacity retention after 1000 cycles at 10C [58]. A surface‐reconstruction route on O3‐NaNi1/3Fe1/3Mn1/3O2 uses ammonium tetraborate pretreatment to form Na2B4O7 and near‐surface B3+ incorporation; Rietveld refinement shows c expanding from 16.05949 to 16.1163 Å and transition metal‐oxygen (TM─O) slab thickness from 2.0281 to 2.1191 Å, consistent with widened Na‐migration layers. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations confirmed a reduced migration barrier, and O K‐edge soft X‐ray absorption spectroscopy (sXAS) displays enhanced TM 3d–O 2p hybridization; the material reaches 133.3 mAh·g− 1 at 0.1 C with 90.6% capacity retention after 200 cycles [18].

Hysteresis Mechanisms and Gas Evolution

The key limitation of O‐redox is not capacity activation itself, but the frequent emergence of voltage hysteresis and partial irreversibility caused by path‐dependent structural and electronic rearrangements. Two coupled processes are most often implicated: (i) localization of oxygen holes followed by O–O bond formation (leading to molecular O2 or lattice‐trapped O2‐like species), and (ii) TM migration/slab gliding that reshapes the redox landscape between charge and discharge. Within the Zaanen‐Sawatzky‐Allen framework, when charge‐transfer energy becomes sufficiently small, O 2p states approach the Fermi level; if hybridization remains inadequate, oxygen holes condense, and O2 release becomes favorable. Differential electrochemical mass spectrometry (DEMS) directly observes O2 during cycling of strained NaNi0.4Mn0.4Co0.2O2 between 2.0 and 4.4 V (Figure 2f) [17]. An important stabilizing behavior at the top of charge is often described as reductive coupling, where a fraction of TM centers undergoes partial reduction while oxidized oxygen states form, producing a covalent interaction that keeps charge compensation within the lattice and disfavors molecular O2 formation. Operando XANES captures this as a transient decrease in formal metal valence that coincides with growth of the O K‐edge pre‐edge (Figure 2g, h), and it typically correlates with a contracted hysteresis area and improved capacity retention during cycling [17].

Beyond electronic factors, de‐sodiation reduces interlayer electrostatic shielding and increases repulsion among oxidized oxygen species, which biases slab glide and transition‐metal migration. In the high‐entropy P′2‐Na0.59Mn0.90Ti0.02Cu0.02Ni0.02Co0.02Fe0.02O1.95F0.05 composition, in situ XRD shows a mild and reversible evolution between OP4 and P′2 and then to P″2 with a change in the c parameter of about 9.10%. Electronic‐structure analysis attributes the stabilization to strengthened Mn 3d–O 2p hybridization by multi‐cation substitution and to symmetry breaking by F−, which lowers the Mn–O anisotropy ratio from 1.099 to 1.061 and mitigates internal stress [59]. In P2‐Na2/3[(Ni0.5Zn0.5)0.3Mn0.7]O2, electrochemically inert Zn2+ replaces the P2 to O2 transition with a reversible evolution between P2 and OP4, and the associated volume change is near 5% [60]. Pair‐distribution‐function (PDF) analysis on P3‐Na0.6[Li0.2Mn0.8]O2 detects a shortened O–O distance of approximately 2.5 Å in the charged state, linking local O–O interactions to the observed macroscopic phase evolution [61]. Consistently, RIXS/DFT on Na0.6[Li0.2Mn0.8]O2 shows oxygen holes formed on charge can condense into lattice‐trapped O2; reversal then requires O–O cleavage and TM back‐migration, shifting reduction to lower voltages.

Chemical and Structural Regulation Strategies

Stabilization strategies converge on enhancing TM–O interactions and managing local fields so that oxygen is oxidized and reduced within a confined structural manifold. Elemental substitution offers three complementary routes. First, strongly covalent dopants such as Fe3+ increase 3d–2p hybridization and suppress localization of oxygen holes, which lowers irreversible capacity loss in Na2/3Fe2/9Ni2/9Mn5/9O2 from about 25% to about 4% [62]. Second, electrochemically inert, electronegative ions (e.g., Mg2+ or Zn2+) create a stable local environment that allows participation of non‐bonding O 2p states without molecular oxygen release. P2‐Na2/3[Mg0.28Mn0.72]O2 is a representative case, where mRIXS shows no signature of irreversible O2 formation [63, 64]. Third, high‐valent d0 or d10 dopants such as Sn4+ or Ti4+ increase TM–O bond covalency and redistribute O 2p electron, which inhibits Jahn‐Teller distortion and delays the O3 to P3 evolution, as shown for NaNi0.5Sn0.5O2 [65].

At the structural and electrostatic level, ordering and field control can further confine the oxygen activity and reversibility. Honeycomb‐type cation ordering raises the energy of O–O antibonding states and enhances hybridization with TM orbitals, which stabilizes lattice oxygen and improves reversibility [66, 67]. Alkali layer regulation using large‐radius, high‐electronegativity ions like K+ widens interlayer spacing, strengthens Mn–O covalency (as indicated by crystal orbital hamilton population analysis), weakens Na–O interactions, and suppresses high‐voltage structural change in P2‐Na0.612K0.056MnO2 [68]. Interfacial strategies provide an additional lever: phosphate coatings such as NaPO3 scavenge protic impurities and mitigate electrolyte‐driven oxygen loss [19], while trace Mo6+ substitution induces a layered and tunnel intergrowth that resists detrimental phase changes under cycling [69].

Overall, while activating lattice oxygen redox effectively breaks the capacity ceiling of transition‐metal limits, it introduces critical challenges regarding voltage hysteresis and gas evolution driven by metastable oxygen‐hole configurations and coupled cation migration. The transition from oxygen loss to reversible O‐redox relies on strictly confining charge compensation within a stable covalent network and restricting the structural degrees of freedom through composition, ordering, and interfacial/field control. In long‐term cycling, however, electronic stabilization must be matched by mechanical accommodation of the strain fields induced during de‐sodiation, motivating the multi‐scale defect engineering and stress‐management strategies discussed below.

Defect Engineering and Electrochemical–Mechanical Coupling

Defect engineering tunes layered oxides across point, line, and planar imperfections so that Na‐ion percolation, transition‐metal migration, and stress evolution remain within a reversible window. At the atomic scale, cation/anion vacancies and heterovalent substitution reshape local bonding and diffusion barriers; at the mesoscale, mixed‐phase architectures and coherent interfaces redistribute strain. This section classifies these interventions into defect‐level levers and their coupled electrochemical‐mechanical responses.

Defect‐Level Levers

Vacancy topology in the TM layer governs Na‐layer ordering and the reversibility of oxygen redox, as seen by the contrast between honeycomb‐ordered Na0.75[Li0.25Mn0.75]O2, which facilitates rapid vacancy clustering and promotes lattice O2 detected by high‐resolution RIXS, and stripe‐ordered Na0.6[Li0.2Mn0.8]O2, which raises the clustering barrier and stabilizes oxygen holes with reduced voltage hysteresis [70].

Substituting electrochemically inert Mg2+ into P2‐type Na0.67Ni0.33‐xMgxMn0.67O2 suppresses layer gliding and phase transitions; at x = 0.15 the material delivers 123 mAh·g− 1 at 0.1 C (2.0–4.3 V) with 92% retention after 100 cycles, and introducing Ti4+ into Na0.66Li0.22Ti0.15Mn0.63O2 strengthens TM–O covalency, shrinking oxygen‐redox hysteresis from about 280 mV to about 50 mV and reducing oxygen evolution by roughly 80% [65, 71].

Partial substitution of O2 − with F− raises the oxygen‐vacancy formation energy and reinforces TM–O bonding, exemplified by Na0.95[Ni0.5Mn0.5]O1.95F0.05, which retains 92% after 500 cycles at 1C with minimal secondary‐particle cracking and a thin protective surface layer that suppresses rock‐salt growth and TM dissolution [72]. Topologically intergrown P2/O3 domains confine bulk strain to approximately 2% and provide coherent nanoscale interfaces that act as coupled sites for stress release and ion transport, thereby improving cycling durability [73]. Multi‐component TM sublattices further homogenize stress and suppress Na+/vacancy ordering as well as Jahn–Teller‐driven parameter swings; a representative high‐entropy system remains crack‐free after cycling and raises the exothermic peak from 237 to 310°C, which here serves as supporting evidence for defect–stress coupling with broader compositional aspects deferred to Chapter 4 [74].

Coupling to Electrochemical–Mechanical Response

The coupling between defect engineering and the electrochemical–mechanical response of layered cathodes is expressed along four interrelated dimensions: regulation of Na+/vacancy ordering and Na+ transport; suppression of transition‐metal migration and associated structural degradation; management of mechanical stress to delay crack initiation and growth; and reinforcement of interfacial stability with improved charge‐transfer kinetics.

Defect control can open percolating Na networks and lower diffusion barriers, with Ca2+‐type dopants weakening Na+/vacancy ordering, widening interlayer spacing, and improving conductivity as reflected by convergent GITT‐derived diffusivity and reduced low‐frequency DRT features [75]. Strengthening local bonds such as Li–O, Ti–O, and F–O reduces the propensity for Mn3+‐related migration and stabilizes the phase window. Under specific coordination environments, pseudo‐Jahn–Teller effects can disperse electronic degeneracy and local stress, which suppresses disordered migration and narrows hysteresis [76]. At the mesoscale, multi‐cation interactions distribute lattice strain during cycling, lowering the maximum variations of the c‐ and a‐axes to about 5.5% and 3.0% (vs 6.3% and 3.6% in a control), reducing accumulated bulk microstrain from approximately 39.1% to approximately 15.7%, and sustaining 94.8% retention after 100 cycles at 1C and 83.5% after 500 cycles at 5C [20]. Combining surface Na2Ti3O7 coatings with bulk Ti4+ substitution couples interface and bulk stabilization by reducing charge‐transfer resistance, expanding lattice parameters, and suppressing the high‐voltage P2 to O2 transition and Na+/vacancy ordering, which yields nearly no decay after 200 cycles at 5C and a threefold improvement in high‐rate retention over the pristine material [77].

Defect‐level levers confine accessible evolution pathways by opening Na‐ion percolation while raising the threshold for oxygen loss, suppressing transition‐metal migration and slab glide to narrow the phase window and hysteresis, and redistributing stress at coherent boundaries to delay crack initiation. The combined effect is a more reversible bulk response under practical cutoffs, setting the stage for electrolyte‐coupled self‐regulation discussed later.

The current layered oxide stabilization strategy.

Ultimately, defect engineering serves as the unifying lever that bridges atomic‐level electronic modulation with mesoscale mechanical resilience. By precisely tailoring vacancy topology and interphase coherence, it is possible to construct a percolating yet robust network that accommodates the structural breathing inherent to Na‐ion insertion. These interventions transform the “self‐evolution” from a degradation mode into a controlled, reversible mechanism, thereby ensuring that the high‐capacity benefits of phase and oxygen‐redox chemistry can be practically realized in long‐life SIB cathodes.

Interfacial Reconstruction and SEI Evolution

Stabilizing the electrode‐electrolyte interface in SIBs extends beyond forming a passivating SEI. It requires dynamic regulation of interface chemistry, defect evolution, and phase transitions. Recent advances highlight that interfacial layers are not static but undergo reconstruction during cycling, necessitating strategies to modulate their composition and stability at both the molecular and mesoscale level [78].

Dynamic SEI Formation and Induced Evolution

Electrolyte‐derived SEI forms through coupled reduction/polymerization pathways and then evolves under repeated Na+ flux, stress, and interfacial potential gradients, so its stability is governed by the balance between continuous repair and cumulative fracture/regrowth. Using fluoroethylene carbonate (FEC) as a representative additive, this part elucidates how reaction selectivity and transport control steer SEI chemistry and morphology toward an inorganic‐rich, mechanically compliant passivation layer, thereby linking SEI composition/structure/mechanics to Coulombic efficiency, impedance growth, and cycling durability.

FEC‐Regulated SEI Structure and Electrochemical Performance Optimization

Electrolyte‐derived SEI formation is no longer viewed as a static passivation event but as a dynamic and controllable evolution process. Using fluoroethylene carbonate (FEC) as an exemplar, prioritized reduction seeds NaF‐enriched, hybrid organic‐inorganic networks that densify upon cycling, lower porosity, and slow the growth of charge‐transfer resistance [33, 34, 35].

Huang and co‐workers showed that FEC participation in the Na+ solvation sheath triggers preferential reduction and forms a thin, inorganic‐rich SEI that suppresses continuous electrolyte decomposition, delivering >95% Na plating/stripping efficiency with stabilized interfacial impedance (Na/Cu, ∼50 cycles), >500 h stable Na/Na cycling, and 500‐cycle retention in Na//NVPOF full cells [33]. Complementary computational insights [34] an optimum FEC fraction: modest additions densify the SEI and lower its cavity fraction, whereas excess intact FEC coordinates with sodium propylene carbonate (NaPC) residues to form “bridges” and, together with higher viscosity, hampers beneficial polymerization, yielding a less cohesive interphase. Consistently, Weng et al. [35] showed that in an FEC‐only electrolyte the S@MRP cathode builds a NaF‐rich “chocolate‐cookie” CEI via a two‐step route—nucleophilic reaction above ∼1.0 V followed by further electrolyte decomposition below 1.0 V—yielding a thin (∼5 nm) outer layer; by contrast, PC forms a much thicker (∼40 nm) organic CEI (Figure 3a,b).

Three‐Step Evolution Mechanism and Key Parameter Characterization of SEI

In line with the dynamic view of SEI growth outlined above, recent in situ and nanomechanical investigations have quantitatively unveiled a unified, three‐step scenario that governs how the SEI attains and sustains its protective role. First, a two‐step formation pathway is confirmed by Ji et al. They found that in both PC and PC + FEC electrolytes the SEI experiences an initial densification followed by a thickening stage, with FEC raising the modulus by an order of magnitude to about 8.5 GPa while slashing the dissolution ratio from about 55% to 8%, thereby directly linking early densification to suppressed dissolution and stable impedance (Figure 3c,d) [79]; second, Gao's two‐step AFM nano‐indentation introduces the maximum elastic deformation energy U that couples Young's modulus and the elastic strain limit and correlates positively with Coulombic efficiency and cycle life across chemistries (Figure 3e), furnishing an a‐priori metric for SEI robustness [80]; third, Bouibes and Nagaoka's simulations reveal that elastic constants and Vickers hardness peak at roughly 1 vol % FEC (Figure 3f), because low FEC yields a dense SEI with sub‐nanometer cavities that resists tension and shear, whereas excess FEC enlarges cavities and softens the film [81].

These findings delineate a consecutive mechanism in which solvation‐biased, additive‐first reduction sets the mechanical baseline defined by modulus, hardness and U, after which inorganic enrichment and pore suppression reconstruct an ion‐permeable yet electron‐blocking film that passivates the surface and halts further electrolyte decomposition.

Vacancy‐Driven Reconstruction and Self‐Passivation

Na+ extraction, oxygen loss, and transition‐metal migration generate vacancies and reactive sites that destabilize interfaces in layered cathodes and carbon anodes. The goal is to convert these defect precursors into self‐passivating motifs so that reconstruction terminates in dense, inorganic‐rich interphases rather than porous films. Three routes are covered: additive‐mediated vacancy sealing, solvation tuning coupled with vacancy control, and defect pre‐inhibition via surface engineering.

Vacancy Sealing and Interface Self‐Passivation Mediated by Electrolyte Additives

Introducing a small amount of NaDFOB (0.5 wt%) into carbonate electrolytes promotes borate‐ and fluoride‐rich SEI and CEI layers that increase interfacial energy and suppress parasitic reactions, thereby limiting vacancy accumulation at both electrodes [82]. When NaDFOB is paired with tris(trimethylsilyl)borate (TMSB), the cooperative pre‐reduction chemistry boosts full‐cell capacity retention from 17% to 83% after 200 cycles [83]. Chemo‐mechanical evidence for defect‐sealing self‐passivation comes from operando and depth‐resolved characterization of the NaDFOB‐derived interphase. Surface XPS identifies an outer organic‐rich skin (<5 nm), whereas XPS depth profiling reveals a thick, compact inorganic‐rich inner layer dominated by B/F/Na‐containing species (the study does not provide a calibrated absolute thickness) [36]. The inorganic sub‐layer densifies during early cycling and reaches its maximum density after ∼50 pre‐cycles (Figure 4a), coinciding with the most stable interfacial resistance and transport. In situ quantitative NMR supports preferential anion‐derived interphase formation with suppressed solvent consumption, and bulk solid‐state NMR further corroborates the inorganic SEI chemistry, consistent with progressive defect sealing by a densifying inorganic framework.

Dual‐Electrode Stabilization via Solvation Tuning and Vacancy Control

A complementary route couples vacancy control with solvation tuning. Using a self‐synthesized NaDFBOP additive in 1 M NaPF6 EC/EMC, Deng et al. formed dissolution‐resistant SEI and CEI on HC//NaNi1/3Fe1/3Mn1/3O2 (NFM) full cells, achieving 85.45% retention 85.45% after 1000 cycles at 30°C and 90.76% after 500 cycles at 50°C, alongside lower interfacial resistance and suppressed H2/HF signals [84]. Mechanistically, DFBOP− enters the first Na+ solvation shell and preferentially reacts to yield NaF and oxalate‐derived species that strongly bind NaF, producing compact, vacancy‐tolerant interphases that stabilize both electrodes under thermal stress (Figure 4b, c).

Defect Pre‐Inhibition via Cathode‐Targeted Additives and Surface Engineering

For layered oxide cathodes, LiDFOB offers a cathode‐targeted variant of the same idea. Adding 0.8 wt% LiDFOB to carbonate electrolytes for Na0.67Ni0.33Mn0.67O2 produces a Li‐Na‐B‐O‐F CEI that curbs transition‐metal dissolution and electrolyte oxidation; hard‐carbon full cells deliver 432.8 Wh kg− 1 and maintains 99.7% capacity after 700 cycles at 1 C, indicating practical viability for long‐life systems. Surface engineering can also pre‐empt defect nucleation. An ultrathin Al2O3 layer deposited by atomic layer deposition on hard carbon homogenizes Na+ flux, lowers local current hot spots, and guides formation of an Al‐O‐Na‐rich SEI, improving rate capability and sustaining more than 90% capacity after 200 cycles [87].

Mechanistically, these outcomes align with the two‐step, mechanics‐aware interphase picture above: early inorganic enrichment increases U and suppresses crack nucleation, while solvation control dictates which species arrive first to “cap” vacancy clusters before they percolate into porous pathways.

High‐Throughput Interphase Design Under Harsh Conditions

Under elevated temperature and high‐rate operation, conventional SEI layers are prone to structural collapse, gas evolution, and interfacial delamination. This part surveys robust interphase designs that sustain fast ion transport while improving thermal tolerance, with emphasis on liquid‐electrolyte regulation, ferroelectric composite electrolytes, and fluorinated block‐copolymer electrolytes.

High‐Throughput Interface Construction in Liquid Electrolyte Systems

In liquid systems, Yang et al. engineer a “high‐throughput” interphase by regulating the solvation structure with NaDFOB. In a PC/EMC‐based electrolyte (with FEC), NaDFOB drives anion‐rich solvation, preferentially decomposes to form NaF and B‐oxide species, and yields a thin (∼4 nm), dense CEI on NFM together with a NaF/B‐oxide‐rich SEI. Cylindrical HC||NaNi0.33Fe0.33Mn0.33O2 cells retain about 94.1% capacity after 1000 cycles at 0.5 C and operate stably from −40 to 60°C, with low and stable interfacial resistance and improved Na+ diffusion at the interface.

Interface Polarization Enhancement of Ferroelectric Composite Electrolytes

To further enhance thermal‐electrochemical coupling, Wang et al. [88] construct a ferroelectric‐engineered composite electrolyte by infiltrating NaClO4/poly(ethylene oxide) (PEO) into a porous NASICON (Na3Zr2Si2PO12) framework and applying a K0.5Na0.5NbO3 ferroelectric coating [85]. The interfacial polarization reduces charge‐transfer and SEI resistances and mitigates side reactions at the anode (Figure 4d). Symmetric Na3V2(PO4)3 (NVP)||NVP cells show 86.4% capacity retention after 650 cycles, and asymmetric NVP||Prussian‐blue cells achieve ∼9000 stable cycles at 500 mA g−1 with a fading rate near 0.005% per cycle.

Self‐Assembled Ion Channel Design of Fluorinated Block‐Copolymer Electrolytes

Complementing this, Chen et al. design fluorinated, charged block‐copolymer electrolytes that self‐assemble into well‐ordered ion‐transport channels [82]. Figure 4e,f shows a robust body‐centered cubic (BCC) window (f PFPE ≈ 0.15–0.30) persisting from 30 to 80°C, confirmed by SAXS peak ratios of 1:√2:√3, underpinning three‐dimensional (3D)‐connected Na+ pathways. The 3D BCC morphology delivers Na+ conductivity up to 1.42 × 10−4 S cm−1 at 80°C, supports dendrite‐free Na plating for >5000 h at 0.1 mA cm−2, and enables NVP full cells to retain over 91% capacity after 1000 cycles at 0.5 C. This morphology‐property linkage connects continuous nanoscale pathways and improved interfacial contact with the emergence of a fluoride‐rich, mechanically robust interphase.

These emerging approaches reflect a paradigm shift from passive SEI formation to functionally engineered, responsive interphases, incorporating surface templating, additive design, and solid‐state architecture for long‐life SIB operation.

Composition and Architecture for Intrinsic Self‐Repair

We take self‐repair to mean confined, reversible evolution that preserves percolation and recovers function without external triggers. The chapter maps how entropy/site/anion chemistry and stress‐tolerant architectures deliver that behavior across layered oxides, polyanion/PBA frameworks, conversion hosts, and self‐buffering alloy/conversion anodes, evaluated under a shared set of electrochemical and mechanical readouts.

Layered Oxides: Confining Phase Evolution by Entropy, Site, and Anion Design

In NaxTMO2, durable cycling requires that O3 ↔ P3 transformations remain reversible (with O2 only at deep desodiation) [89], Na‐vacancy ordering be restrained, and layer glide be moderated. Composition/entropy and local site/anion design act together: [11, 12, 13] configurational entropy suppresses segregation and glide‐assisted multiphase break‐up; targeted substitutions tune TM─O covalency and Na‐layer electrostatics; surface/alkali‐layer “pillars” stabilize spacing and lower diffusion barriers. The mechanistic objective is not to eliminate evolution, but to confine it within a narrow, repeatable window with low hysteresis.

Multication Entropy to Suppress Glide and Smooth O3 ↔ P3

High‐entropy layered oxides combine multiple metal elements (e.g., Na, Ni, Co, Mn, Fe) in approximately equimolar ratios, resulting in enhanced structural stability and reduced phase transitions. The layered structure provides efficient ion channels, boosting cycling stability and rate performance. By fine‐tuning elemental composition, high‐entropy oxides balance high‐entropy effects and layered structure functionality, improving electrochemical performance. One example is the P2/O3 biphasic structure developed under the octahedral radius matching principle, which allows for reversible phase transitions and improved cycling stability (Figure 5a) [14]. However, challenges remain, including lower capacity compared to single‐component cathodes and higher costs due to the multi‐element system. Despite these trade‐offs, high‐entropy layered oxides show promise for enhancing NaMO2 cycling stability, with further structural optimization needed to balance performance and scalability. Sheng et al. [15] obtained a single solid‐solution high‐entropy layered oxide with uniform elemental distribution and wider sodium channels by adjusting the Zn/Cu ratio. The entropy value of this oxide was calculated to be 1.62R (Figure 5b), and it can lower the migration barrier and smooth the phase evolution (Figure 5c). Within a NaNi0.5Mn0.5O2 framework, partial substitution of Ni by Fe, Li, Ti, and Cu produced an O3‐type high‐entropy‐doped cathode that combined bulk entropy stabilization with surface rock‐salt protection, broadening the usable voltage window [16]. Among the doped elements, Fe3+ and Cu2+ provide charge compensation through their own redox reactions to enhance capacity; Ti4+ acts as a “structural stabilizer” to fix the transition metal layer and suppress lattice defects; while Li+ triggers anionic redox to broaden the voltage window.

Dual‐Site and Anion Regulation to Stabilize TM–O and O‐Redox

Targeted multi‐cation mixing strengthens TM─O bonding and adds interlayer anchoring, which suppresses layer glide and broadens a benign O3 to P3 window. Strain and hysteresis decrease, and cycling stability improves [21, 91]. Addressing anisotropic lattice strain, Wang and colleagues [20] synthesized a Cu/Ti/Sn co‐doped series (CFTS) from NaNi0.35Mn0.35Fe0.3O2 (NFM), which demonstrated a uniform elemental distribution. This material not only lowers the sodium ion migration barrier but also facilitates a smooth phase transition from the O3 phase to P3, then to O3′, and finally back to O3. These transitions contribute to its excellent performance in full sodium‐ion batteries (Figure 5d). A composition screen across sodium contents of 0.65 to 1.00 identified Na0.80Ca0.06Ni0.48Mn0.40Cu0.06Ti0.06O2 as an optimum in which Ca2+ widened transport channels, Cu redox added capacity, Ti4+ anchored the transition‐metal layer, and activated Cu/Ni chemistry was retained while Mn remained at Mn4+ [92]. To suppress multiphase evolution linked to layer glide and sodium vacancy ordering, O3‐type NaNi0.40Mn0.40Ti0.13Fe0.06Al0.01O2 increased TMO2 interlayer spacing while reducing sodium‐layer spacing; calculations supported stronger TM─O bonding and a lower sodium ion diffusion barrier [93]. From a stress management perspective, a high‐entropy transition‐metal layer combined with Li, Mg, and Ti co‐doping produced a more homogeneous stress field and fewer intragranular cracks, improving structural retention [94].

Alkali‐Layer and Surface Pillars to Manage Slab Electrostatics

Na‐layer “pillar” site engineering introduces stabilizing species in alkali slabs, interstices, or on surfaces to provide structural support and regulate transport, thereby suppressing interlayer collapse while maintaining continuous Na+ pathways and a favorable interfacial environment [95, 96]. As a representative case, Liu et al. [22] formed deep Na+ pillars in vanadium oxides via a low‐temperature hydrothermal route, producing lattice expansion with oxygen vacancies and partial amorphization; these features lowered the Na+ migration barrier and enabled fast, reversible pseudocapacitive behavior with improved structural stability. At the interface, Hong et al. [97] achieved one‐step calcination to form a perovskite‐type CaZrO3 surface on O3‐type NFM (Figure 5e), stabilizing the CEI and limiting transition‐metal dissolution, while concurrent introduction of Ca, Zr, and F constructed a high‐voltage‐compatible interlayer environment that constrained strain and Fe migration(Figure 5f). Zhang et al. [98] created dual pillars by partial Zn2+ and Mg2+ substitution at sodium sites, strengthening O‐Na‐O interactions and adjusting interlayer spacing, which improved cycling stability, rate capability, and moisture tolerance.

Polyanionic Frameworks: Inductive Strengthening With Small‐Strain Pathways

Following layered oxides, polyanionic frameworks provide a distinct materials‐first route in which inductive tuning and rigid XO4 backbones govern stability and voltage. Corner‐sharing XO4 tetrahedra cross‐link TM–O units and limit lattice breathing during Na+ insertion and extraction, while the high electronegativity of the polyanion sets higher TM redox levels and suppresses parasitic oxygen participation, thereby raising operating voltage without compromising structural integrity [99, 100]. Design choices center on mixed‐anion chemistries, hetero‐cation selection aligned to the inductive field, and solid‐solution or defect management.

Mixed‐Anion and Inductive‐Field Tuning

Combining PO4 3− with F− or related anions adjusts local inductive fields and framework rigidity, which elevates redox potential and can lower Na+ migration barriers. As representative cases, Wang et al. [101] reported a single‐phase, low‐strain host with multi‐electron redox and a capacity of approximately 113 mAh g−1 after 150 cycles at 50 mA g−1 (Figures 6a–b). In the same study, they also created a spray‐dried Na4Fe3(PO4)2(P2O7)/Na2VTi(PO4)3 (NFPP/NVPP) heterostructure, which enhanced conductivity via Ti3+ formation and interfacial charge redistribution.

Selecting transition‐metal occupants to couple efficiently to the polyanion field tunes band positions and activates complementary redox couples while the polyanion skeleton suppresses strain. For example, Tan et al. [24] prepared IsK‐NaNLMF using sol‐gel with in situ doping, where K+/Li+/F− acted synergistically to stabilize the framework and widen Na+ channels; in P2‐type hosts, multi‐site Mg2+ placement at alkali and transition‐metal layers has been shown to stabilize stacking in a pillar‐like manner and to mitigate charge‐ordered phases as well as Na+/vacancy ordering.

Defect/Solid‐Solution Management for Contiguous Na+ Pathways

Forming homogeneous solid solutions and introducing controlled point defects stiffen TM─O networks and create contiguous Na+ pathways under the strong inductive background, enabling stable oxygen redox with reduced hysteresis. Zhang et al. [105] showed that Ti‐O bonding restricts electron delocalization and, together with strong O 2p and Cu 3d overlap, stiffens the oxygen framework, suppressing irreversible transitions and supporting reversible O‐redox. In contrast, Wu et al. [103] introduced Bi3+ into NFPP, strengthening Bi–O covalency, lowering the bandgap from 3.29 to 0.16 eV, facilitating Na+ diffusion, and creating lattice defects that provide additional storage sites (Figure 6c,d).

Prussian Blue Analogues (PBAs): Open Frameworks with Defect and Hydration Control

Within polyanionic cathodes, PBAs offer an open three‐dimensional cyanide framework AxM[M'(CN)6]1‐y□y·zH2O (where A represents alkali metal ions, M and M' are transition metals, and □ denotes [Fe(CN)6] vacancies) with approximately 4.6 Å channels that supports rapid and reversible Na+ insertion/extraction [106, 107]. The CN− ligand field raises transition‐metal redox potentials and enables high operating voltages (tunable by M/M′ selection), while theoretical capacities approach ∼170 mAh g−1 [108, 109]. Practical performance is constrained by [M′(CN)6] vacancies and coordinated water, which reduce accessible Na+ sites and may promote parasitic reactions, and by strain‐driven phase changes during deep desodiation. Current efforts, therefore, center on multi‐transition‐metal mixing to tailor redox and conductivity, and on A‐site/water and defect management to stabilize the framework [110, 111, 112].

Multi‐TM Mixing for Stepwise Redox and Conductivity

Multi‐transition‐metal mixing in the [M(CN)6] network enables stepwise redox with smoothed plateaus and improved electronic structure. Zhang et al. [23] realized a vanadium hexacyanoferrate with ruthenium oxide (VHCF/RuOx) quantum dot heterostructure through in situ co‐precipitation (Figure 6e). The d–p hybridization between the 4d orbitals of Ru and the 2p orbitals of cyano groups (C≡N) in VHCF induces π back‐donation and constructs an “electronic highway,” which enables the regulation of d‐electrons in V/Fe. By shifting their d‐orbital centers, continuous multi‐electron transfer is facilitated. The optimized d‐electron structure reduces the proportion of V5+, thereby alleviating vanadium dissolution during cycling and ultimately enhancing the material's electrochemical performance. From a compositional perspective, Xu et al. [112] compared early/late transition‐metal combinations (e.g., Fe–VO, Fe–TiO, Fe–ZrO, Co–VO, Fe–Co–VO;) and highlighted Fe–V d–d compensation that enhanced redox reversibility, kinetics, conductivity, and structural stability.

A‐Site Population and Hydration Control for Durability

Control of the A‐site population (Na+/K+/vacancies) and coordinated water is equally important for durability. Moderate K+ incorporation stiffens the framework by size matching, an appropriate vacancy fraction sustains transport without undermining support, and minimal coordinated water limits pore blockage and Fe–CN–Fe disruption. Chou et al. [113] introduced trace amounts of Zn2+ to partially substitute Fe2+, precisely modulating the Fe–N coordination environment, synergistically enhancing lattice stability, blocking water intrusion, and optimizing the electronic structure, significantly improving the diffusion kinetics and structural integrity of iron‐based PBAs. Huang et al. [114] implemented post‐synthesis or in situ vacancy repair using concentrated sodium ferrocyanide, suppressing Fe dissolution and improving electrochemical stability. In parallel, a potassium‐assisted route yielded iron‐based PBAs with low water content, fewer [Fe(CN)6]4− vacancies, preferred (220) orientation, and higher Na loading (e.g., NKPB‐3), improving intrinsic safety and integrity [115]. Introducing mixed transition metals at the B‐site further buffers anti‐site defects and hydration‐induced perturbations, maintaining structural continuity even with partial disorder and variable environmental moisture.

Conversion Oxide Anodes: Structural Retention and Entropy‐Enabled Design

Conversion‐type metal oxides offer higher theoretical capacities than practical hard‐carbon anodes, yet large volume change and pronounced voltage hysteresis limit energy efficiency and cycling under realistic conditions. The generic pathway (MaOb + 2b Na+ + 2be−⇌ aM + bNa2O) entails full structural reconstruction; durability therefore depends on moderating phase evolution and interfacial overpotentials rather than on chemistry alone [116, 117]. Overall, conversion oxides achieve self‐regulation by combining framework/entropy stabilization with conductive, strain‐buffering hosts, keeping conversion reconstruction within a more reversible, lower‐hysteresis operating window.

Stable Sub‐Lattices and Controlled Disorder for Reversible Storage

Recent studies indicate that stable sub‐structures, controlled disorder/ defect chemistry, and continuous conductive scaffolds can narrow phase windows and keep transport channels open, thereby reducing hysteresis and strain. Pinna et al. [118] demonstrated that FeNb2O6, in which local disorder of FeO6 octahedra coexists with preserved NbO6 zigzag chains, provides a stable framework that accommodates sodium‐ion insertion and resists structural degradation. Yabuuchi's team [119] prepared a vacancy‐rich P3‐type Na0.5Cr0.5Ti0.5O2 by K+/Na+ ion exchange; enhanced Na+/vacancy disorder and improved electronic conductivity increased capacity and rate performance. Wei et al. [104] observed that anatase‐phase TiO2 undergoes in situ transformation into rocksalt‐phase NaTiO2 during cycling. This phase transition echoes the multi‐step c‐a’‐RS phase transition behavior of anatase‐phase TiO2 observed in previous studies, further enriching the understanding of the diverse phase evolution of these materials during sodium storage (Figure 6f–h). The rocksalt phase features a cation‐disordered structure and optimized ion pathways, enabling solid‐solution sodium storage, a process with a volume change of approximately 2%, which is much lower than the significant volume expansion induced by electrochemically driven Na+ intercalation into the anatase phase.

Entropy‐Stabilized Oxides and Graphene/Porous Hosts for Strain Relief

High‐entropy oxides (HEOs) extend the above idea by using configurational entropy and multi‐element interactions to suppress coarsening and alleviate stress, with in situ diffraction indicating reversible phase sequences [120]. Wang et al. [121] used polyoxometalate (POM) clusters as structure‐directing agents to obtain multicomponent HEOs nanowires with sub‐nanometer features under mild conditions, enabling controlled integration of otherwise immiscible oxides. As Na‐ion anodes, these HEOs exhibited reduced sodiation/desodiation stress and improved reversibility, attributed to entropy‐driven phase stabilization augmented by POM‐mediated electronic effects. Wang et al. [122] further reported a HEO/graphene composite consisting of ∼4.57 nm spinel‐structured (FeCuCrMnNi)3O4 hollow porous microspheres uniformly anchored on reduced graphene oxide. Entropy stabilization mitigated volume strain, while oxygen vacancies lowered the Na+ diffusion barrier and facilitated reversible (de)intercalation. In situ XRD resolved a reversible phase sequence during cycling in which the structure changed from spinel to monometallic oxides and subsequently to a rocksalt arrangement. Even so, translation to high‐loading full cells remains the outstanding challenge, tied to synthesis controllability at the nanometer scale, initial coulombic efficiency, and manufacturability. Thus, conversion‐type oxides, including HEOs, are mechanistically instructive for self‐regulation, but their near‐term role is to inform design rules rather than to replace hard‐carbon anodes.

Viewed across layered oxides, polyanionic frameworks, Prussian blue analogues, and conversion‐type oxide anodes (including high‐entropy oxides), a consistent materials‐first view emerges: self‐regulation hinges on confining phase evolution while preserving percolating transport under practical loads (Table 2). High‐entropy oxides and site‐engineered layered oxides both target bulk retention but act through different dimensions: multication disorder suppresses phase separation and smooths complex transitions, whereas Na‐layer and dual‐site tuning moderate slab electrostatics and alleviate layer glide. Under high voltage or wide‐temperature service, fluorine‐containing chemistries and polyanionic frameworks add inductive strengthening and rigid XO4 backbones, raising redox potential and limiting oxygen participation, with attention to possible penalties in conductivity and Na+ mobility. For the rate–durability balance, Prussian blue analogues rely on open channels and elastic response provided by vacancies, and water is controlled, while polyanionic hosts favor small‐strain cycling through stiff covalent networks; light doping mainly repairs vacancies in PBAs but tunes Na‐site fields in polyanionic lattices. In practice, selection should match failure mode to mechanism: complex phase landscapes suit high‐entropy design; glide‐dominated behavior is addressed by site‐engineered layered oxides; extreme conditions are served by polyanionic or fluorinated hosts; and kinetic priorities favor PBAs, with all choices demonstrated in high loading full cells that show reproducible, low hysteresis operation.

Self‐Buffering Alloy/Conversion Anodes: Intrinsic Stress Accommodation

Self‐buffering alloy/conversion anodes are a key route to self‐regulation in SIBs because they accommodate large sodiation/desodiation strains through internal structural adaptation rather than external healants. In these chemistries, degradation commonly stems from stress‐induced cracking/pulverization, loss of electronic/ionic continuity, and recurrent SEI rupture/regrowth that progressively increases interfacial resistance. Here, intrinsic self‐buffering refers to stress relaxation enabled by electrode architecture together with a resilient conductive framework, so reconstruction proceeds with limited fresh‐surface exposure and a more compact, stable interphase. This definition keeps the discussion centered on self‐contained mechanisms within the electrode that can be assessed by rate durability, long‐life cycling, ICE, and full‐cell robustness [25]. Overall, this subsection focuses on how architecture‐driven stress accommodation helps sustain percolating transport and a stable SEI, thereby supporting durable high‐rate cycling and robust full‐cell performance.

Void‐Managed Yolk–Shell and Nanocage Architectures

Void‐managed scaffolds and resilient conductive networks are the two recurring levers. As a representative intrinsic strategy for alloy/conversion anodes, red phosphorus encapsulated in conductive carbon nanocages (RP@CNCs) shows that void confinement at ultrahigh active loading (∼85.3 wt%) can buffer volumetric strain while maintaining continuous ion/electron pathways. The yolk–shell/void‐confined architecture with a percolating carbon network unloads stress and limits crack propagation, delivering 1363 mAh g−1 after 150 cycles at 100 mA g−1 and >80% retention after 1300 cycles at 5 A g−1. Mechanistically, the internal void accommodates P expansion, while the conductive scaffold preserves percolation and a stable interphase during reconstruction [126]. For high‐rate alloy anodes, yolk–shell Sb@Void@graphdiyne (GDY) nanoboxes provide a representative self‐buffering architecture that can be directly connected to a self‐regulation outcome. The internal void serves as a stress‐relief reservoir that accommodates Sb expansion during sodiation, thereby reducing particle‐level pulverization and limiting shell rupture (Figure 7a). Meanwhile, the continuous and mechanically compliant GDY shell maintains an electronically percolating network and offers in‐plane subnanometer channels that function as fast, three‐dimensional Na+ transport pathways. By preserving structural integrity and minimizing repeated exposure of fresh Sb surface to the electrolyte, this cavity–shell coupling suppresses recurrent SEI breakage and uncontrolled regrowth, favoring a more stable (and effectively renewable) interphase under severe flux. Consistent with this self‐buffering → interphase‐stabilization chain, in situ TEM observations and kinetic analyses report faster Na+ transport than conventional Sb@C and durable high‐rate cycling, linking cavity engineering and shell mechanics to stabilized interfacial resistance and full‐cell robustness [32].

Bimetallic Stress‐Adaptive Motifs and Anchored Nanodots

A “breathing” Bi/N‐doped carbon nanosheet (Bi/N‐CNSs) quasi‐array offers a rate‐hard pathway [127]. A hierarchical Bi distribution, consisting of porous Bi lamellae interlaced with ultrafine Bi nanoparticles anchored on conductive N‐doped carbon, creates cooperative void space and in‐plane channels that redistribute sodiation stress, maintain electronic and ionic percolation, and preserve a compact SEI during repeated reconstruction (Figure 7b, c). The architecture sustains ultrahigh current operation (100 A g−1) and low‐temperature cycling (−40°C), with long‐life durability over 26 000 cycles, illustrating an intrinsically regulated system that reconfigures reversibly without external healants and translating cavity engineering plus network mechanics into interface stability under severe flux.

Beyond carbon hosts, intrinsic buffering can also be realized in high‐loading alloy frameworks and bimetallic architectures. Void‐confined Bi dots uniformly anchored in a KOH‐modified porous carbon scaffold (Bi@MC) exemplify intrinsic self‐buffering: nanodot anchoring suppresses agglomeration, the 3D carbon network preserves percolation, and the alloying/de‐alloying pathway remains highly reversible. The anode delivers 88.1% initial Coulombic efficiency, no decay over 10 000 cycles at 2.5 A g−1, and 345 mAh g−1 at 50 A g−1; full cells reach about 195 Wh kg−1. These data indicate that cavity engineering together with a continuous conductive network can stabilize the interphase and unload strain without external healants [26]. A bimetallic “stellar” architecture with a Bi core, Sn sheath, and carbon encapsulation achieves stress self‐adaptation: [128] finite‐element analysis shows homogenized stress during sodiation, while DFT indicates improved conductivity and a lower Na‐diffusion barrier (Figure 7d–f). The anode sustains 461 mAh g−1 (100 cycles, 0.1 A g−1) and 86.9% retention over 2000 cycles at 1 A g−1; NVP‖Sn–Bi@C full cells retain about 97% (∼251 Wh kg−1). The cooperative metal pairing and shell mechanics maintain electronic and ionic percolation and a compact SEI under severe flux.

Extrinsic Repair via Liquid‐Metal (LM) Phases

Beyond intrinsic buffering architectures, extrinsic repair relies on externally initiated responses that close cracks and re‐establish electronic pathways. Liquid metal‐assisted designs exemplify this approach by allowing mobile metal phases to flow, wet interfaces, and reconnect the conductive network as needed. Guo et al. engineered room‐temperature Ga‐In liquid‐metal nanoparticles that reliquefy after full de‐alloying, flow to close cracks, and restore electronic percolation (Figure 7g–i) [129]. In Na cells, the anode delivers 222.3 mAh g−1 at 0.1 C and ∼60 mAh g−1 at 5 C, while full cells with P2‐Na2/3[Ni1/3Mn2/3]O2 show stable cycling, directly linking phase reversion to macroscopic durability. The SEM evidence visualizes a crack‐and‐heal cycle synchronized with alloying/de‐alloying. Song et al. used CuGaS2 nanoplates where in situ liquid Ga forms under conversion/alloying and acts as a mobile self‐healant to fill gaps and stabilize the SEI across 268 to 318 K (−5–45°C); the Cu backbone ensures low resistivity, and Na half‐cells validate applicability to SIB chemistry. This embedded healant behaves functionally like an extrinsic agent while being generated internally, making it a useful bridge between intrinsic buffering and extrinsic repair paradigms [27].

These studies highlight the versatility of self‐buffering strategies in SIB anodes. Whether through intrinsic elasticity or extrinsic repair, these designs collectively embody the principle of adaptive structural evolution, a hallmark of the self‐regulating battery paradigm. Future work should continue to explore hybrid strategies that unify both internal resilience and external reconfigurability, aiming to deliver mechanically robust, interface‐stable, and high‐rate‐capable anodes across a broad range of conditions.

Electrolyte–Material Coupling and Triggered Reconstruction

Electrolyte–material coupling determines whether interphases emerge as thin, ion‐conductive, and self‐limiting layers or as sources of parasitic loss [130, 131]. In sodium‐ion cells, charging and discharging drive continuous co‐evolution at the electrode–electrolyte boundary rather than static coexistence. On the anode, reductive reactions form the solid electrolyte interphase (SEI); its inorganic richness, continuity, and renewal kinetics set Na+ transport and long‐term coulombic efficiency. On the cathode, oxidative reactions, surface reconstruction, and transition‐metal dissolution are balanced by a cathode electrolyte interphase (CEI) that must remain stable at high potentials [132]. In this chapter, we take a materials‐first view and treat the electrolyte as a targeted external trigger: by tuning solvation structure, anion identity, salt–solvent ratios, diluents, and selected additives, interfacial reconstruction can be steered toward contiguous, inorganic‐rich SEI/CEI that preserve charge transfer and diffusion while suppressing gas evolution and cross‐talk. The scope covers practical SIBs and focuses on mechanisms that link controllable electrolyte variables to lattice and interphase responses.

Solvation Engineering

Solvation engineering tunes the primary solvation shell, desolvation kinetics, and interphase formation. Here we connect solvation microstates with interfacial stability and ion‐transport performance, and summarize design guidelines with representative examples of programmed solvation systems.

Solvation Structure Regulation and Low‐Temperature Performance Optimization

Solvation engineering regulates the primary solvation shell, desolvation kinetics, and interphase‐formation pathway, thereby steering both transport and interfacial stability. In the first shell, Na+ coordinates with solvent molecules and anions (e.g., dimethyl sulfoxide, DMSO; glutaronitrile, GN; and TFSI−) through ion–dipole and hydrogen‐bond interactions [28, 29, 30]. Reported co‐solvent pairs such as DMSO with GN can reconstruct the solvation environment into a more anion‐participating sheath, which lowers interfacial barriers and sustains Na+ transfer at low temperature (down to approximately −55°C in specific formulations) [133]. Complementarily, weakly solvating electrolytes (WSEs) based on fluorinated esters (e.g., TFEP) and localized high‐concentration electrolytes (LHCEs) that employ inert diluents reduce solvent participation at the metal center while avoiding excessive viscosity, facilitating low‐temperature kinetics [134, 135]. The same solvation control dictates the film‐formation pathway. Anion‐participating shells favor preferential anion reduction/oxidation and yield inorganic‐rich SEI/CEI (e.g., NaF/Na2CO3), which combines mechanical robustness with acceptable ionic conductivity and suppresses continuous electrolyte decomposition. A representative case is NaTFSI in diglyme (G2), where the inorganic SEI remains operable across a wide temperature window (about −40–45°C).

Representative Case Studies of Programmed Solvation Strategies

Case studies further illustrate how solvation can be programmed. Li et al. [136] proposed a “hierarchically doped” solvation strategy by combining ethyl methyl carbonate (EMC) and ethoxy(pentafluoro)cyclotriphosphazene (PFPN) in a propylene carbonate (PC)‐based electrolyte, reconstructing primary and secondary shells and improving wide‐temperature behavior. Liu et al. [137] developed a ternary, weakly solvating low‐concentration salt electrolyte in which PFPN enters the secondary solvation shell, weakens Na+–propylene carbonate (PC) and Na+– anions coordination, accelerates desolvation, and enhances oxidative stability (Figure 8a). Xu et al. [138] proposed a tetrahydrofuran (THF)‐modified carbonate electrolyte, which synergistically stabilizes the cathode/anode interphases of SIBs via dual mechanisms: weak THF coordination promotes Na+– anion interaction and forms an inorganic‐dominated SEI, while trace water triggers in situ ring‐opening polymerization of THF to create a flexible polymeric film that reinforces the anode SEI and mitigates cathode transition‐metal dissolution (Figure 8b); full cells showed capacity retention increasing from 24% to 93% after 150 cycles, with up to 207 mAh g−1 at 5C (Figure 8c).

Taken together, these approaches converge on two objectives: stabilizing the electrode/electrolyte interphase and sustaining rapid Na+ transport. In practice, the most reliable gains arise from controlling solvation microstates rather than making wholesale changes to formulation. Mechanistic understanding at the solvation level should therefore inform macroscopic choices of solvents, salts, diluents, and additives for operation across a wide temperature range and at high rates.

Additive Synergy

Electrolyte additives can cooperatively regulate interphase chemistry and the solvation process. This part summarizes the key roles of typical single additives, clarifies the mechanistic basis of multi‐additive synergy, and proposes matching principles between additive combinations and the base electrolyte.

Functional Characteristics of Typical Single Additives

Electrolyte functionalization in sodium‐ion cells is most effective when additives act cooperatively on interphase chemistry and solvation. Fluoroethylene carbonate (FEC) is a widely used film‐former that is preferentially reduced at the anode to yield NaF‐rich, inorganic‐dominated SEI, which suppresses continuous solvent decomposition and mitigates abnormal Na deposition under demanding conditions [142, 143]. Its benefit is system‐dependent: in formulations with poor intrinsic film‐forming ability, FEC stabilizes the SEI; in systems that already form robust interphases, additional FEC can thicken the SEI and raise impedance [144]. Insights from Li systems (e.g., defect‐site adsorption differences between FEC and EC) suggest more uniform SEI nucleation on defect‐rich carbons (Figure 8d), but direct extrapolation to Na requires caution [139].

Difluoro(oxalato)borates (NaDFOB or LiDFOB) provide dual functions of interphase construction and solvation tuning. The DFOB− anion participates in forming B/F‐rich hybrid interphases with favorable mechanics and ionic transport, while its involvement in the Na+ shell lowers solvation binding and accelerates desolvation [145, 146, 147]. Small amounts of LiDFOB are sometimes used to modify solvation and interphase chemistry; any “Li+–Na+ synergy” should be quantified and reported for Na systems rather than presumed [148, 149]. In ether‐rich media, NaDFOB preferentially reacts to form robust interphases on both electrodes, suppressing abnormal deposition and improving cycling in FeMn‐PBA||hard‐carbon cells without detectable parasitic by‐products [150].

Synergistic Regulation Effect of Multi‐Additive Systems

To further broaden the operational voltage and temperature window, silyl‐borate and sultone additives are used as targeted stabilizers [151]. These gains should be balanced against possible penalties in impedance, viscosity, or gas evolution, emphasizing composition windows rather than single‐point recipes.

In summary, FEC establishes inorganic‐rich SEI, DFOB salts co‐optimize interfacial chemistry and solvation kinetics, and silyl‐borate/PES families extend high‐voltage and thermal robustness. Their division of roles is most effective when matched to the base electrolyte's film‐forming propensity and validated with standard electrochemical and operando readouts, with explicit reporting of additive ratios and total salt‐to‐solvent composition.

Trigger‐Induced Reconstruction

Trigger‐induced reconstruction offers an active regulation route enabled by electrolyte composition and structural design. We elucidate the coupling between trigger‐driven interphase evolution and bulk phase response, and summarize design concepts and representative functional trigger agents.

Mechanism of Trigger‐Induced Interface and Bulk Phase Reconstruction

Trigger‐induced reconstruction is the purposeful use of electrolyte composition and structure to start interfacial chemistry that subsequently couples to bulk responses. Near the ESW limits, controlled redox of solvent or salt produces intermediates that seed SEI or CEI; the resulting interphase then governs electrolyte consumption, ion transfer, and local polarization. The design goal is a thin, inorganic‐rich, ion‐conductive interphase and confined bulk evolution, rather than progressive decomposition or stress amplification [31, 152]. On practical anodes (e.g., hard carbon), insufficiently conductive or porous interphases raise interfacial impedance and promote heterogeneous Na storage; the ensuing stress can widen phase windows in the cathode or increase local disorder in the anode, which in turn exposes new reactive sites and further perturbs interfacial chemistry. The objective of trigger design is therefore to bias this cycle toward thin, inorganic‐rich, ion‐conductive layers and confined bulk evolution rather than runaway decomposition.

Typical Application Cases of Functional Triggers

Representative triggers illustrate the design space. Peng et al. [140] used 1,3,6‐hexanetricarbonitrile (HTCN) to reorganize the hydrogen‐bond network in a perchlorate‐based aqueous formulation, connecting solvents of different polarity via Na+ coordination. The electrolyte reached an ESW of ∼3.5 V, optimized Na+ solvation in the bulk phase, and formed a hybrid CEI at the interface to suppress transition metal dissolution (Figure 8e,f). For low‐temperature scenarios, Zheng et al. [141] utilized trimethylsilyl trifluoromethanesulfonate (TMSOTF), generating radicals in situ to tune solvent polarity and disrupt Diglyme(G2) aggregation at low temperatures. Meanwhile, an inorganic‐rich SEI containing NaF lowered transport and desolvation energy barriers in the bulk phase (Figure 8g), enabling ∼2400 cycles at −40°C on hard carbon. Chen et al. [153] screened tris(2,2,2‐trifluoroethyl) phosphate (TFEP) by adsorption energy, reconstructed the Helmholtz layer via an “adsorption‐dragging” effect, and obtained a fluorine‐rich SEI; the ESW extended to about 5.40 V in a low‐salt carbonate electrolyte with improved cycling stability and safety.

In sum, trigger‐induced reconstruction operates by molecular‐scale interventions that steer interphase chemistry and bulk responses toward self‐limiting regimes. Chemical triggers (additives, anion identity, diluents), together with electrical or thermal process variables (formation protocol, cutoff potentials, temperature), can be combined to confine phase evolution and preserve transport.

Interface Polarization and Channel Self‐Assembly in Solid‐State and Gel Electrolytes

Interfacial polarization and ion‐transport channel self‐assembly in solid‐state and gel electrolytes are governed by their compositions and microscale interactions, and are optimized through distinct structural designs. A comparative framework is provided by classifying polarization origins and channel‐formation mechanisms in the two systems, followed by general design principles for high‐performance electrolytes.

Interface Polarization and Channel Self‐Assembly in Solid‐State Electrolytes

For solid‐state electrolytes, their core components include oxides (e.g., Na3Zr2Si2PO12), sulfides (e.g., Na3PS4), and polymers (e.g., polyethylene oxide, PEO). Interfacial polarization mainly originates from two aspects: first, physical contact defects between the electrolyte and the electrode (e.g., gaps between ceramic particles and the electrode); second, the enrichment or depletion of Na+ induced by differences in component electronegativity, which further forms an interfacial space charge layer. In contrast, channel self‐assembly relies on the ordered arrangement of components. For instance, Chen et al. [86] demonstrated that in fluorinated charged block copolymers (CBCPs) consisting of perfluoropolyether (PFPE) segments and charged PEO blocks (Figure 8h), the thermodynamic incompatibility between PFPE and PEO enables morphological transitions from a disordered (DIS) phase to body‐centered cubic (BCC), hexagonal (HEX), and other phases by adjusting the PFPE volume fraction (fPFPE ). Among these phases, the BCC phase stands out for forming three‐dimensionally interconnected ion transport channels, which not only ensure efficient Na+ transport but also construct a low interfacial resistance (∼80 Ω) and a fluoride‐rich SEI layer (Figure 8i,j). This layer significantly suppresses dendrite growth, allowing the battery to maintain a capacity retention rate of over 91% after 1000 cycles at 0.5 C—an archetypal example of solid‐state systems optimizing channels and interfaces via ordered component arrangement.

Interface Polarization and Channel Self‐Assembly in Gel Electrolytes

Gel electrolytes are characterized by a polymer matrix (e.g., poly(vinylidene fluoride‐hexafluoropropylene), PVDF‐HFP; polyacrylonitrile, PAN) encapsulating a liquid electrolyte (a carbonate solution containing salts such as NaPF6). Their interfacial polarization is notably lower than that of solid‐state systems, benefiting from the improved interfacial wettability by the liquid component; however, side reactions between the liquid electrolyte and the electrode (e.g., solvent decomposition) may exacerbate polarization. In terms of channel self‐assembly, gel systems form porous structures through phase separation‐induced self‐assembly of the polymer matrix; after these pores are filled with the liquid electrolyte, efficient Na+ transport channels are established. Moreover, interactions between polymer chains and the liquid component (e.g., hydrogen bonding) can regulate channel properties. For example, Du et al. [154] developed a smart gel polymer electrolyte (denoted as PCIE) via the in situ polymerization of cyanoethylurea‐containing methacrylate (CM) and isocyanate‐based methacrylate (IM). The cyano, urea, and other functional groups of PCIE adsorb on the electrode surface to modulate charge distribution, which not only suppresses interfacial polarization but also promotes the formation of stable CEI/SEI layers. Polymerization at 60°C forms a gel network that self‐assembles into ion transport channels, while cross‐linking between urea groups and isocyanates at temperatures ≥120°C blocks these channels to prevent thermal runaway—enabling the HC||NaNi1/3Fe1/3Mn1/3O2 battery to retain 80% capacity after 500 cycles at 50°C. Another electrospun PLA‐NaF gel polymer electrolyte (GPE) induces membrane swelling via dipole interactions between the C═O groups of PLA and solvents [155], forming self‐assembled nanochannels. The aggregated ion pair (AGG) structures (ClO4 −/Na+ > 1) generated by NaF dissolution promote the formation of an 8.4 nm thick inorganic CEI through their highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) energy and stabilize the SEI via their lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) energy. Additionally, low interfacial tension inhibits Na dendrite growth, further mitigating interfacial polarization.

This design paradigm—“regulating microscale interactions via components and optimizing interfaces/channels via microscale interactions”—provides a universal strategy for electrolyte development (Table 3). On one hand, the advantages of different systems are complementary: for example, combining the high stability of solid state electrolytes with the excellent interfacial compatibility of gel electrolytes could open new avenues for composite electrolytes. On the other hand, practical applications must balance the feasibility of preparation processes and cost control, avoiding over reliance on complex components or precise regulation. In the future, deeper insights into the “component‐microscale interaction‐long term performance degradation” correlation and reduced damage to interfaces/channels from side reactions will more effectively advance the commercialization of energy storage technologies (e.g., sodium‐ion batteries) toward low cost, long lifespan, and wide operating conditions.

Future Directions and Outlook

Building on the framework developed above, this section summarizes the review's central message and outlines a time‐scaled roadmap for implementing self‐regulation in practice (Figure 9). The evidence base for self‐regulation is organized consistently with the four coupled paths emphasized in the Introduction: (i) lattice/phase evolution, (ii) interphase chemistry and mechanics, (iii) composition and structure for intrinsic self‐healing, and (iv) electrolyte‐materials coupling. Across these paths, the practical objective is not to expand chemical scope, but to establish comparable validation, transferable readouts, and explicit handoff rules that preserve mechanistic meaning from half cells to high‐loading full cells. Several unresolved issues continue to limit clarity and translation. Mechanistic comparability remains constrained by inconsistent definitions and reporting of reversible versus non‐reversible evolution regimes, particularly when phase trajectories, interphase renewal, and transport degradation coexist. Interphase chemo‐mechanics is still frequently described qualitatively, and the connection between interphase continuity, stress accommodation, and the persistence of percolating transport pathways is often not parameterized in a transferable manner. Electrolyte effects are not always attributed with sufficient rigor, and the respective roles of supportive coupling versus externally imposed passivation are not consistently separated by evidence chains. Half‐cell to full‐cell translation remains a major bottleneck, because signatures observed under dilute constraints often change under lean electrolyte, high areal loading, stack pressure, and thermal gradients.

These gaps motivate the short‐, mid‐, and long‐term roadmap below, which retains a fixed chemistry scope and uses electrolyte choices selectively to reinforce the coupling between lattice evolution and interphase renewal.

Short‐Term Validation and Standardization

A near‐term priority is convergence on a small set of standard readouts and transparent reporting. Formation and steady‐state coulombic efficiency, impedance and relaxation features of charge transfer and mass transport, diffusion trends from intermittent titration, and the area between charge and discharge open‐circuit voltage curves form a sufficient basis; operando gas analysis and transition‐metal crossover can be added where informative. Using existing chemistries, studies should demonstrate repeatable self‐limiting behavior and regeneration with light‐touch operando combinations and clearly specified process parameters such as formation protocol, temperature, and upper‐voltage window. Success is judged by reproducibility across laboratories rather than extremes of a single metric.

Mid‐Term Full Cell Translation and Field Coupling

The mid‐term goal is to translate high‐loading full cells evaluated under coupled thermal, mechanical, and electrochemical conditions. With chemistry held fixed, hierarchy‐aware electrode and cell design—porosity control, continuity of the conductive network, stack pressure, and thermal management—should enable rapid and spatially uniform interphase formation while maintaining open transport pathways. Compact models calibrated to the standard readouts can provide practical handoff rules from half‐cell evidence to full‐cell operation by locating instability thresholds and indicating which operating parameters restore reversible cycling.

Long‐Term Programmable Mechanism to Design Loop

Over a longer horizon, AI‐assisted inversion, prediction, and optimization can render the mechanism to design cycle programmable [156, 157, 158]. Standard readouts can be mapped to interpretable evolution parameters such as phase window width, layer glide kinetics, interphase continuity, and transport coefficients that vary in time. Physics‐informed surrogate models with quantified uncertainty then predict the response to process parameters and identify operating profiles that preserve reversible behavior under practical constraints. In parallel, multi‐scale digital twins and high‐throughput experimentation close the loop by updating the models and consolidating a reusable parameter dictionary, enabling scale‐up without changing the underlying chemistries and moving toward programmable, self‐evolving control of the materials system.
