Molecular mechanisms of EPAS1 in regulating pulmonary vascular remodeling in COPD: a review
Authors: Yunchao Wang, Xinhua Wang, Yi Zhang, Lin Lin, Xiaoming Ma, Guisen Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is an incompletely reversible heterogeneous lung disease with high morbidity and mortality. Progressive worsening of dyspnea is its core clinical manifestation, and patients often develop severe complications such as pulmonary hypertension during the advanced stages of the disease. The oxygen-sensitive gene endothelial PAS domain-containing protein 1 (EPAS1) is a core transcription factor activated under hypoxic conditions. EPAS1 acts as a key regulator of hypoxia-induced pulmonary vascular remodeling in COPD, although other hypoxia-responsive factors, including HIF-1α and NF-κB, also contribute to this complex process. This gene is involved in the pathological progression of COPD and is closely associated with pulmonary vascular remodeling. Pulmonary vascular remodeling, in turn, is a critical process in COPD pathogenesis, driven by chronic hypoxia, inflammatory responses, tobacco exposure, and cytokine dysregulation. These pathological changes can directly lead to the development of pulmonary hypertension and further exacerbate dyspnea symptoms. Under hypoxic conditions, aberrant expression of EPAS1 regulates the transcriptional activation of its target gene, vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), while also mediating the activation of the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling pathway. These molecular events are closely related to the structural remodeling of pulmonary vasculature in COPD patients. Therefore, in-depth investigation into the molecular mechanisms by which EPAS1 regulates pulmonary vascular remodeling in the hypoxic microenvironment will not only help elucidate the pathogenesis of complications in advanced COPD but also provide potential molecular targets for clinical intervention, offering significant theoretical and clinical value for the precision treatment of COPD.

Background

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a progressive respiratory disorder characterized by persistent airflow limitation and chronic respiratory symptoms, including chronic cough with sputum production, dyspnea, chest tightness, and wheezing [1]. Extensive research has established smoking, occupational exposures, and biomass fuel combustion as major risk factors [2, 3]. The disease pathogenesis involves a complex interplay of environmental and endogenous factors, including recurrent respiratory infections, genetic susceptibility, impaired lung development, and bronchial hyperresponsiveness [2].Notably, genetic susceptibility also contributes to COPD, as evidenced by familial clustering of cases [4, 5]. These environmental and genetic factors interact to drive disease progression.

Pulmonary vascular remodeling (VR) is a critical pathological process in COPD. Chronic pathological stimuli induce structural changes in pulmonary vasculature, including vascular wall thickening and luminal narrowing. These changes contribute to hypoxia, impaired gas exchange, and disease complications.Emerging evidence has highlighted the role of airway epithelial and endothelial alterations in COPD pathophysiology. For instance, studies in mice have shown that cilia function critically influences mucus hypersecretion and airway remodeling, which are central features in COPD progression [6].These findings complement investigations of hypoxia-responsive genes such as EPAS1, providing a more comprehensive understanding of disease mechanisms. Notably, these structural changes resemble those observed in populations chronically exposed to high-altitude hypoxia [7]. Chronic hypoxia enhances the contraction, migration, and proliferation of pulmonary cells. It also upregulates the production of vasoactive substances, which in turn promote the proliferation of vascular wall cells and increase the secretion of extracellular matrix proteins. Collectively, these processes drive pulmonary VR and lead to structural alterations in the pulmonary vasculature [8]. Our research group previously conducted a study on the prevalence of COPD among individuals aged 40 years and older in southern Gansu Province. The findings revealed a notably high COPD prevalence of 20.3% in the region, which substantially exceeds the national average [9]. This marked discrepancy suggests that chronic hypoxic exposure associated with high altitude may constitute a significant risk factor in the development and progression of COPD. Taken together, these findings confirm that gene-environment interactions contribute to the pathogenesis of COPD, notably to the structural remodeling of its pulmonary vasculature.

Endothelial PAS domain-containing protein 1 (EPAS1), also known as hypoxia-inducible factor-2α (HIF-2α), is a hypoxia-inducible transcription factor predominantly expressed in endothelial cells and serves as a central mediator of cellular adaptation to hypoxic stress [10]. Under hypoxic conditions, HIF-2α-encoded by the EPAS1 gene on chromosome 2-initiates the expression of multiple target genes to maintain normal physiological functions [11], leading to a marked upregulation of EPAS1 transcription and translation in lung tissue. The primary mechanisms involved in this process include pulmonary VR and vascular cell proliferation, which collectively enhance the body’s adaptability to hypoxic environments. COPD is characterized by parenchymal tissue destruction and irreversible airflow limitation resulting from chronic inflammation. As lung function deteriorates and the disease progresses, alveolar hypoxia and hypoxemia become increasingly severe. According to a study by Raguso, the symptoms exhibited by COPD patients are similar to those observed in healthy individuals chronically exposed to high-altitude hypoxia, suggesting that hypoxia plays a significant role in the initiation and progression of COPD [12]. Investigations by Takeda [13] and Zhen [14] have further revealed that systemic hypoxia is not only a hallmark of advanced COPD but also plays a critical role in the pathogenesis of its associated comorbidities. Concurrently, our research group has identified that the EPAS1 rs13419896 G > A polymorphism reduces susceptibility to COPD and may serve as a genetic marker for predicting the 10-year absolute risk of the disease [15]. In summary, EPAS1, as a key oxygen-sensitive transcriptional regulator, mediates the expression of HIF-2α under hypoxic conditions and participates in adaptive responses such as pulmonary VR and cell proliferation, thereby playing an essential role in maintaining oxygen homeostasis.

In the context of COPD, progressively worsening alveolar hypoxia further activates EPAS1-related signaling pathways, not only exacerbating pulmonary pathological changes but also potentially contributing to the development of systemic complications. Therefore, a deeper investigation into the regulatory mechanisms of EPAS1 in the hypoxic response of COPD will help elucidate its potential role in disease progression and the occurrence of comorbidities, laying a theoretical foundation for future research.

Epidemiology and pathogenesis of COPD

According to the 2022 Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD), a post-bronchodilator ratio of forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) to forced vital capacity (FVC) < 0.70 confirms the presence of persistent airflow limitation. When this spirometric criterion is combined with clinical manifestations such as dyspnea, chronic cough and sputum production, and a history of risk factor exposure, a diagnosis of COPD can be established [16]. As one of the major global public health challenges, the disease burden of COPD is substantial. It is estimated that by 2025, the total number of patients worldwide will approach 600 million, with an overall prevalence of approximately 2.7%, and a prevalence as high as 10.3% among individuals aged over 30 years [17–19]. In recent years, the prevalence of COPD has shown a sharp upward trend globally, particularly in low- and middle-income countries and among female populations. In China, the prevalence among individuals aged over 40 years rose from 8.2% in 2007 to 13.7% in 2018, reflecting a similarly critical situation [1, 20]. A contributing factor to the high prevalence of COPD is the relatively low public awareness of the disease, leading to low rates of early screening and diagnosis and, consequently, missed opportunities for timely intervention. Furthermore, as the global population ages, the health economic burden imposed by COPD is poised to become a major public health concern [22]. Among the various risk factors, tobacco exposure is considered the primary environmental contributor to the development of COPD. To address this, China has implemented prevention and control strategies such as “tobacco control” and “environmental protection,” which have, to some extent, alleviated the burden of COPD [23].

The pathophysiological mechanisms of COPD are exceedingly complex, with current consensus focusing on three core pathways: inflammation/immunity, oxidative stress injury, and protease-antiprotease imbalance. During the chronic inflammatory process, various inflammatory cells, including neutrophils, macrophages, and lymphocytes, are recruited and activated. They exacerbate pulmonary inflammation by releasing a plethora of inflammatory factors and chemokines, ultimately destroying lung parenchyma and its normal repair and defense mechanisms, leading to irreversible pathological changes such as emphysema and small airway fibrosis [24, 25]. These activated inflammatory cells are also a significant source of key cytokines such as interleukins (ILs), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β). The release of these cytokines further amplifies the inflammatory cascade, promoting the progression of pathological features including pulmonary edema, airway fibrosis, and VR [24, 26, 27]. Concurrently, oxidative stress and protease-antiprotease imbalance serve as key mechanisms of chronic hypoxia-induced lung tissue injury and are intricately intertwined with the inflammatory process [28]. On one hand, oxidative stress markers generated during the inflammatory phase of COPD actively participate in inflammation, autoimmunity, and VR, forming a vicious cycle. On the other hand, the balance between proteases and antiproteases is crucial for maintaining the structural integrity of lung tissue. When the massive release of proteases (NE, MMPs) from inflammatory cells such as neutrophils overwhelms the inhibitory capacity of antiproteases (TIMPs), it leads to direct destruction of lung tissue, resulting in declining lung function and airway remodeling, and ultimately, irreversible airflow limitation [29]. Notably, hypoxia-a core pathological feature of COPD-can directly induce protease-antiprotease imbalance by activating oxygen-sensitive signaling pathways and upregulating the expression of related genes. Simultaneously, hypoxia-driven chronic inflammation can indirectly exacerbate this imbalance by promoting a surge in NE, MMPs, and TIMPs from fibroblasts, collectively driving the progression of COPD [30].

Notably, as one of the core pathological features of COPD, hypoxia can activate oxygen-sensitive signaling pathways and upregulate the expression of related genes, thereby inducing and exacerbating protease-antiprotease imbalance, which collectively drives the progression of COPD [26]. COPD is not a disease driven by a single factor, but rather a complex pathophysiological process induced by long-term environmental exposures (such as tobacco smoke and hypoxia) on the basis of genetic susceptibility. The interaction between environmental factors and individual genetic background permeates the core mechanisms, including inflammation, oxidative stress, and protease-antiprotease imbalance, collectively determining the occurrence, development, and clinical outcomes of COPD. Therefore, continued focus on and in-depth investigation into the specific molecular mechanisms of “environment-gene interaction” in COPD is of paramount scientific importance and clinical value for understanding the nature of the disease, identifying high-risk populations, formulating precise prevention strategies, and developing novel therapeutic targets.

Overview of research on VR in COPD

Overview and triggers of VR in COPD

VR is not merely vasoconstriction, but a complex and progressive process involving various cellular and extracellular events. Under the influence of factors such as growth factors and inflammatory mediators, cells within the vascular wall-including endothelial cells, smooth muscle cells, and fibroblasts-undergo abnormal proliferation, migration, and apoptosis, accompanied by increased deposition of the extracellular matrix (ECM). These pathological changes collectively lead to vessel wall thickening, luminal stenosis, and even occlusion. This dynamic alteration in vascular wall structure, driven by changes in cell behavior and ECM synthesis/degradation, results in elevated hemodynamic resistance and consequent structural and functional abnormalities, a process defined as VR [31]. VR is a core pathological feature of numerous cardiovascular diseases and is commonly observed in conditions such as hypertension, atherosclerosis, pulmonary arterial hypertension, and cor pulmonale [32]. Its occurrence and progression are intricately linked with various vascular disorders. In COPD, pulmonary VR is particularly critical, as it is closely associated with the development of pulmonary hypertension, a severe complication during the disease course. Persistent chronic hypoxia, inflammatory responses, and tobacco exposure are considered the three major triggers driving pulmonary VR in COPD. Specifically, chronic inflammation establishes a self-perpetuating microenvironment through macrophage and lymphocyte activation, generating pro-remodeling cytokines including IL-4, IL-6, and TNF-α. These inflammatory mediators engage in complex crosstalk with endothelial growth factors and fibroblast-derived signals, ultimately driving vascular wall hypertrophy and ECM reorganization. The resultant structural compromise impairs vascular compliance and potentiates right heart dysfunction [33]. Notably, tobacco-derived compounds such as nicotine and their metabolites directly subvert vascular homeostasis through elastin network degradation and endothelial-mesenchymal transition (EndMT). This smoke-induced injury cascade exacerbates ECM dysregulation while also promoting oxidative stress-mediated matrix metalloproteinase activation, thereby accelerating VR progression. The interplay between these hypoxia-, inflammation-, and toxin-driven mechanisms is schematically summarized in Fig. 1 [34, 35].

Main mechanisms for VR in COPD

Pulmonary VR in COPD manifests through three hallmark structural alterations: muscularization of distal pulmonary arterioles resulting from aberrant smooth muscle cell migration into non-muscularized vessels; concentric thickening of arterial walls involving fibroblast-driven hypertrophy across intimal, medial, and adventitial layers [36] and ECM deposition disrupting vascular biomechanical properties [37]. These anatomical changes arise from three interconnected molecular pathways: oxidative stress dysregulation, MMP imbalance, and hypoxia-mediated vasoactive signaling. (Fig. 2). Tobacco smoke initiates a cascade of oxidative injury through sustained oxidant accumulation that depletes pulmonary antioxidant reserves. This redox imbalance potentiates protease-antiprotease system collapse, triggering parenchymal destruction and activating VR through elastolytic degradation of vascular scaffolds [38]. Simultaneously, MMPs dysregulation emerges as a central effector mechanism. Smoke-derived oxidants and chronic inflammation synergistically upregulate MMP-9/MMP-2 expression, driving vascular smooth muscle cell (VSMC) hyperproliferation and ECM disorganization. Notably, MMP-9 exhibits dual pathological roles by directly mediating vascular wall thickening while amplifying inflammatory feedback loops through cytokine crosstalk [39]. Hypoxia further exacerbates this pathogenic network through transcriptional activation of MMP-9/MMP-2 and endothelial growth factors, promoting small pulmonary arterial hyperplasia. The resultant vascular stiffening and luminal narrowing create a self-reinforcing cycle of hemodynamic stress and hypoxic signaling [40].

The core molecular mechanism underlying this pathological process can be summarized as the “hypoxia-HIFs-MMPs/VEGF-remodeling” cascade. Specifically, chronic hypoxia serves as the core upstream driver by activating EPAS1, which initiates a cascade of reactions that integrate and amplify multiple injury mechanisms, including inflammation, oxidative stress, and protease-antiprotease imbalance. Chronic hypoxia stabilizes hypoxia-inducible factors (HIFs), particularly EPAS1, which is predominantly expressed in endothelial cells. Once stabilized, EPAS1 translocates to the nucleus and binds hypoxia response elements (HREs) in the promoters of downstream target genes. This activity upregulates key mediators, including matrix metalloproteinases (MMP-2 and MMP-9) and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF).

The elevated MMP levels degrade the extracellular matrix, compromising vascular wall integrity and facilitating smooth muscle cell migration and proliferation, thereby contributing to pulmonary vascular remodeling. Concurrently, VEGF overexpression potently promotes endothelial cell proliferation, migration, and increased vascular permeability, directly contributing to intimal hyperplasia. The synergistic action of MMPs and VEGF collectively drives the remodeling of all layers of the vascular wall-including the intima, media, and adventitia-manifesting as smooth muscle cell proliferation and migration, fibroblast activation, and abnormal ECM deposition. These pathological changes ultimately result in vascular wall thickening and luminal stenosis, culminating in the characteristic pathology of pulmonary vascular remodeling.

Importantly, the hypoxia-driven transcriptional regulatory mechanism described above does not operate in isolation; rather, it is intricately intertwined and synergistically interacts with other canonical injury pathways implicated in COPD pathogenesis. On one hand, tobacco exposure and its constituent oxidants deplete pulmonary antioxidant reserves, leading to an oxidant-antioxidant imbalance and robust generation of ROS. On the other hand, tobacco components can directly stimulate MMP-mediated excessive ECM production, triggering vascular smooth muscle cell hypertrophy and aberrant migration. Furthermore, in the context of chronic inflammation triggered by hypoxia and smoke exposure, pro-inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor (TNF) and interleukins (ILs) are abundantly released. These cytokines not only directly upregulate MMP expression but also amplify protease-antiprotease imbalance by recruiting and activating inflammatory cells (e.g., macrophages, neutrophils). The triad of oxidative stress injury, inflammatory responses, and protease imbalance mutually reinforce one another, collectively exacerbating ECM degradation, alveolar structural disruption, and impaired gas exchange, thereby establishing a self-perpetuating vicious cycle of tissue destruction and functional decline.These multidimensional interactions between oxidative damage, proteolytic imbalance, and hypoxic adaptation are schematically summarized in Fig. 2.

In summary, EPAS1, as a core transcriptional regulator of the hypoxic response, directly drives pulmonary vascular remodeling by mediating the expression of target genes such as MMPs and VEGF. Simultaneously, its regulatory network integrates and interacts with classical mechanisms-including oxidative stress, inflammation, and protease-antiprotease imbalance-collectively constituting the complex molecular basis of pulmonary vascular remodeling in COPD. Therefore, in-depth investigation into the pivotal role of EPAS1 within this intricate network is of significant scientific importance for elucidating the pathogenesis of pulmonary VR in COPD and for identifying potential therapeutic targets.

Main mechanisms for VR in COPD

Having established the central role of the hypoxia-EPAS1-MMPs/VEGF axis in pulmonary vascular remodeling, the subsequent section examines the broader cytokine network that interacts with and modulates this core pathway.Aberrant cytokine signaling constitutes a pivotal driver of VR in COPD, with VEGF, ET-1, MMPs, and TGF-β which form an interconnected pathogenic network (Fig. 3). VEGF, a key transcriptional target of EPAS1, demonstrates hypoxia-independent upregulation(e.g., under normoxic conditions) mediated by tobacco exposure and inflammatory mediators. This growth factor orchestrates multifaceted vascular alterations through endothelial cell hyperproliferation, pathological angiogenesis, and microvascular permeability enhancement-processes central to COPD progression [41]. Moreover, VEGF exhibits dual regulatory capacity under varying concentration gradients: elevated levels drive endothelial expansion, while deficiency triggers apoptotic cascades. This biphasic behavior establishes serum VEGF as both a mechanistic contributor and potential biomarker for COPD severity stratification. Furthermore, VEGF potentiates VSMC migration via Mitogen-Activated Protein Kinase(MAPK)-dependent ROS signaling, establishing cross-talk between angiogenic and oxidative stress pathways [42]. Concurrently, MMPs and TNF-α engage in reciprocal activation loops that exacerbate ECM degradation. Cigarette smoke stimulates TNF-α release, which in turn induces MMP overexpression across endothelial and VSMC populations. The resultant proteolytic imbalance disrupts ECM homeostasis, fostering VSMC proliferation and inflammatory cell infiltration. These coordinated events culminate in progressive vascular wall thickening and luminal compromise, hallmarks of COPD-associated VR [43].

The oxygen-sensitive gene EPAS1

Molecular structure and oxygen-sensing regulatory mechanisms of EPAS1

The EPAS1 gene is located on chromosome 2p16-21 and encodes hypoxia-inducible factor-2α (HIF-2α), which dimerizes with HIF-1β to form the transcriptionally active HIF-2 complex [44]. Under normoxic conditions, proline residues in HIF-2α undergo hydroxylation, enabling recognition and binding by the von Hippel-Lindau protein (pVHL) and subsequent degradation via the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway. During hypoxia, the hydroxylation of HIF-2α is attenuated, preventing its binding to pVHL. This leads to HIF-2α accumulation in cells and sustained activation of the HIF signaling pathway, which regulates the expression of multiple downstream target genes involved in various physiological and biochemical processes [45]. EPAS1 is predominantly expressed in endothelial cells and is activated under hypoxic conditions [9]. As a core regulator of the hypoxic response, EPAS1 plays a pivotal role in cellular biochemical pathways and is primarily involved in oxygen sensing and response [46, 47]. In addition to directly binding to hypoxia response elements (HREs), EPAS1 can regulate downstream gene expression through interactions with other transcription factors, such as zinc finger E-box binding homeobox 1 (ZEB1), or with miRNAs, mediating hypoxia-induced changes in the expression of numerous proteins and cytokines throughout the body [48]. Tissue hypoxia is a state in which the free oxygen concentration in the body decreases to a level insufficient to maintain normal intercellular reactions and metabolism, potentially leading to functional impairment or even pathological conditions. Under such circumstances, the activation of hypoxia regulatory mechanisms becomes particularly critical [11, 49], During tissue hypoxia, signaling pathways such as AMPK, PI3K/Akt, and MAPK regulate the activity of the HIF pathway.

Population genomic studies have identified EPAS1 as a key gene involved in high-altitude hypoxia adaptation in Tibetans and Sherpas [10, 11]. Research has shown that EPAS1 expression is significantly downregulated in umbilical vein endothelial cells from Tibetan populations, and animal experiments have demonstrated that downregulating EPAS1 expression attenuates the physiological responses to chronic hypoxia [49, 50]. Yoo et al. [13] performed an integrative analysis of DNA methylation and gene expression data and found that increased methylation is associated with reduced EPAS1 gene expression, a process directly associated with decreased lung function. EPAS1 expression is regulated at multiple levels. At the transcriptional level, inflammatory signals such as IL-4 can promote HIF-2α transcription [51]. At the translational level, 15d-PGJ2 can inhibit HIF-2α translation via IRP1-IRE binding [52]. At the level of protein stability, the PTEN/PI3K/AKT pathway can influence the ubiquitination and degradation of HIF-2α by promoting VHL phosphorylation [53]. Furthermore, multiple miRNAs are involved in the post-transcriptional regulation of HIF-2α [54]. Collectively, these regulatory mechanisms may operate within the hypoxic and inflammatory microenvironment of COPD, modulating EPAS1 expression levels and thereby contributing to disease progression. In recent years, microRNAs (miRNAs) have garnered widespread attention as important post-transcriptional regulators of EPAS1 expression. Studies have shown that multiple miRNAs can directly target the 3’untranslated region (3’UTR) of EPAS1 mRNA, thereby regulating its expression levels under hypoxic conditions. For instance, miR-145 is downregulated in lung tissues of COPD patients and participates in airway remodeling and lung function decline by targeting EPAS1 [15]. Furthermore, the miR-17-92 cluster has also been confirmed to regulate HIF-2α expression, playing a significant role in angiogenesis and cell proliferation [55]. The regulatory network involving these miRNAs and EPAS1 may act synergistically within the hypoxic microenvironment of COPD, collectively influencing the process of pulmonary vascular remodeling.

Molecular structure and oxygen-sensing regulatory mechanisms of EPAS1

Motif enrichment analysis has revealed that several downstream genes of EPAS1 overlap with multiple COPD-related genes, including VEGF, AP-1, C/EBPβ, XBP-1, and miR-145 [10]. These genes have been reported to play critical roles in lung development, chronic inflammation, fibrosis, airway remodeling, and lung function decline [56–58]. A small-scale study also found increased EPAS1 expression in COPD patients [59]. Furthermore, our research group has confirmed that EPAS1 gene polymorphisms are key predictors of COPD susceptibility. Elevated levels of free iron in lung tissue disrupt reactive oxygen species homeostasis, exacerbating pulmonary inflammation and airway remodeling. Concurrently, HIF-2α-mediated pulmonary vascular remodeling in the context of pulmonary hypertension is a crucial component of COPD pathology. As the protein encoded by EPAS1, HIF-2α participates in multiple hypoxia-induced pathological processes in COPD, suggesting that EPAS1 may serve as a potential biomarker for early COPD diagnosis. Target genes of EPAS1 include VEGF, hepatocyte growth factor (HGF), and basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF). As adaptive responses to hypoxic stimulation, the products of these genes promote angiogenesis, playing an important role in vascularization under hypoxic conditions [36]. Studies have confirmed that HIF-2α, encoded by EPAS1, exhibits enhanced binding to the VEGF promoter, promoting vascular remodeling and cell proliferation, thereby contributing to pulmonary vascular wall thickening and the development of pulmonary hypertension [60]. HIF-2α is a crucial regulatory hub in hypoxic physiological mechanisms. Further research has confirmed that both HIF-2α mRNA and protein levels are significantly increased in lung tissues of COPD patients, with this upregulation involving HIF-2αin pulmonary vascular remodeling and vascular cell proliferation [61]. Hypoxic environments can induce EPAS1-mediated cellular processes including apoptosis, proliferation, and migration. A study investigating the relationship between EPAS1 and MAPK signaling pathways under hypoxic conditions confirmed that EPAS1 overexpression or silencing was accompanied by corresponding changes in MAPK/ERK pathway activity and vascular endothelial growth factor receptor (VEGFR) expression [62]. Molecular mechanisms of EPAS1-mediated pulmonary VR

Target gene regulation: VEGF-mediated endothelial cell proliferation and angiogenesis

EPAS1-encoded HIF-2α, as a core transcription factor in the hypoxic response, directly binds to hypoxia response elements (HREs) in the promoter regions of target genes, among which VEGF is one of the most critical target genes of EPAS1. Studies have confirmed that HIF-2α exhibits higher binding efficiency to the VEGF promoter than HIF-1α, suggesting that EPAS1 acts as a key regulator in hypoxia-induced angiogenesis, although other hypoxia-responsive factors may also contribute to vascular remodeling [62]. Activated EPAS1 transcriptionally upregulates VEGF expression, thereby promoting endothelial cell proliferation, migration, and increased vascular permeability, directly leading to intimal hyperplasia and vascular wall thickening. VEGF exerts its biological effects primarily by binding to vascular endothelial growth factor receptor 2 (VEGFR2) on the surface of vascular endothelial cells. VEGFR2 is the main signal transduction receptor for VEGF-mediated angiogenesis and endothelial cell survival, and its activation mediates endothelial cell proliferation, migration, and increased vascular permeability [63, 64]. Studies have shown that VEGFR2 expression is altered in pulmonary vascular endothelial cells of COPD patients, and dysregulation of its signaling pathway is closely associated with pulmonary vascular destruction and emphysema formation [64, 65]. Therefore, the EPAS1-VEGF-VEGFR2 signaling axis constitutes a key molecular pathway driving hypoxia-induced pulmonary VR. Studies in COPD animal models have demonstrated that EPAS1 overexpression in lung tissue significantly increases VEGF expression and microvascular density, while EPAS1 knockout reverses these changes, confirming the central role of the EPAS1-VEGF axis in pulmonary vascular remodeling [60]. Clinical evidence further supports the clinical relevance of the EPAS1-VEGF axis. Valipour [66] found that circulating VEGF levels in stable COPD patients were significantly positively correlated with FEV₁% predicted (r = 0.47), with VEGF levels significantly elevated during acute exacerbations and decreasing after recovery. Sun Zhina [67] confirmed that serum VEGF levels were significantly higher in COPD patients with pulmonary hypertension compared to those without, and were closely correlated with pulmonary artery systolic pressure. Yan [68] demonstrated that serum VEGF combined with other indicators achieved an area under the curve of 0.966 for the diagnosis of COPD complicated with pulmonary hypertension. These clinical findings indicate that VEGF levels are significantly correlated with lung function, disease activity, and pulmonary hypertension in COPD patients, providing important clinical evidence for the role of the EPAS1-VEGF axis in pulmonary vascular remodeling in COPD.

Signaling pathway interaction: the role of the MAPK/ERK pathway in smooth muscle cell proliferation

MAPK is a family of serine/threonine kinases most closely associated with the regulation of cell proliferation. It can be activated by various extracellular stimuli and serves as a common pathway for intracellular signal transduction and information transmission from the extracellular environment to the nucleus, widely participating in biological processes such as cell proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis [69, 70]. MAPK cascade activation is central to multiple signaling pathways, representing a class of important molecules that receive signals converted and transmitted by membrane receptors and carry them into the nucleus, playing a key role in many cell proliferation-related signaling pathways. The basic components of the MAPK pathway follow a three-tiered kinase cascade conserved from yeast to humans, consisting of MAPK kinase kinase (MKKK), MAPK kinase (MKK), and MAPK. Upon stimulation by growth factors or other factors, MAPK receives activation signals from MKKK and MKK, becoming activated through sequential phosphorylation, thereby collectively regulating cell growth, differentiation, adaptation to environmental stress, inflammatory responses, and various other important cellular physiological and pathological processes.

Under hypoxic conditions, the stable expression of EPAS1 can mediate the activation of the MAPK signaling pathway [62]. Studies have confirmed that phosphorylated MAPK protein levels in the plasma of COPD patients are significantly higher than those in normal individuals [71], and processes such as chronic hypoxia and oxidative stress can activate MAPK [72]. Meanwhile, MAPK plays an important role in pulmonary vasoconstriction: Ang II can induce MAPK activation, regulating G proteins through phosphorylation, leading to decreased cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) levels and increased pulmonary vascular resistance, which may be one of the contributing factors to pulmonary hypertension in COPD patients [73]. Additionally, the p38MAPK/ERK signaling pathway is also a pathway upon which endothelial cell proliferation and abnormal pulmonary VR depend [74]. The MAPK family is mainly composed of JNK, ERK, and p38MAPK [75], among which the ERK signaling pathway is closely associated with cell differentiation and proliferation [76], playing a key role in hypoxia-induced vascular remodeling. ERK1/2 promotes cigarette smoke extract-induced pulmonary artery smooth muscle cell proliferation and pulmonary vascular remodeling by upregulating cyclin E1 expression [77]. p38MAPK is widely present in vascular endothelial cells and pulmonary artery smooth muscle cells [78, 79], and under hypoxic conditions, it can cause endothelial cell dysfunction, promoting cell proliferation and migration by upregulating VEGF expression [80, 81]. JNK plays an important role in embryonic development, cell differentiation, and tumorigenesis [82]. The above processes are closely related to chronic hypoxia. Under hypoxic conditions, p38MAPK and ERK are phosphorylated and activated within cells [72], subsequently inducing an imbalance between cell proliferation and apoptosis, leading to pathological changes such as smooth muscle cell proliferation and vascular wall thickening. Studies have confirmed that ERK, p38MAPK, and JNK, as important members of the MAPK family, can promote the proliferation and hypertrophy of smooth muscle cells and fibroblasts by facilitating growth factor- and hormone-mediated cell proliferation [83]; simultaneously, they can exacerbate vascular smooth muscle cell proliferation, altering vascular structure and function [84]. In addition to MAPK directly affecting endothelial function, the release of inflammatory factors triggered by tobacco exposure can also mediate p38MAPK signaling pathway activation, thereby driving vascular remodeling [85]. Extracellular matrix remodeling: regulation of MMPs/TIMPs imbalance

In addition to directly regulating VEGF, EPAS1 can also upregulate the expression of MMPs and participate in ECM remodeling. Mechanistic studies have shown that HIF-2α can directly bind to the HRE in the promoter region of MMP-9, transcriptionally activating its expression [86]. Meanwhile, under hypoxic conditions, the activation of MMP-2 requires the formation of a ternary complex with MT1-MMP and TIMP-2 on the cell membrane surface, a process regulated by hypoxia [87]. Studies have confirmed that EPAS1 can upregulate MMP-2 and MMP-9 expression either directly or indirectly (through inflammatory factors) [39, 40]. Increased MMP expression leads to aberrant degradation of the ECM, compromising the structural integrity of the vascular wall and creating space for smooth muscle cell migration and proliferation. EPAS1 expression in lung tissues of COPD patients is positively correlated with MMP-9 levels, suggesting an important role for the EPAS1-MMPs axis in pulmonary vascular remodeling [39]. The imbalance between MMPs and TIMPs further exacerbates the dysregulation of ECM degradation and deposition, leading to vascular wall structural remodeling. Corresponding to the upregulation of MMPs, clinical studies have found that serum TIMP-1 levels are significantly elevated in COPD patients, while TIMP-2 levels are significantly decreased, resulting in an increased MMP-2/TIMP-2 ratio. This ratio correlates with disease stage, confirming that MMPs/TIMPs imbalance is a key determinant of vascular wall structural remodeling [88].

Synergistic role of the inflammatory microenvironment: interplay between EPAS1 and cytokines

Activation of EPAS1 promotes the release of inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α [34]. These cytokines, in turn, act on vascular endothelial cells and smooth muscle cells, further activating the expression of MMPs and growth factors, thereby establishing a positive feedback loop of “hypoxia-EPAS1-inflammation-remodeling.” In addition to EPAS1 activation promoting the release of inflammatory factors, these cytokines can also feedback-regulate HIF expression. Studies have revealed the existence of a TNFα/NF-κB/HIF/VEGF self-regulatory signaling network in endothelial cells: hypoxia induces endothelial cells to produce TNFα, which activates HIF-1 and HIF-2α through an NF-κB-dependent pathway, subsequently promoting VEGF production and angiogenesis [89]. IL-6 can upregulate the expression of monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1) and promote DNA synthesis in vascular smooth muscle cells via the JAK/STAT3 pathway, a process that can be partially blocked by the JAK2 inhibitor AG490 [90]. Inflammatory responses triggered by tobacco exposure can also mediate activation of the p38MAPK signaling pathway, thereby driving VR [85]. This study indicated that cigarette smoke-induced release of inflammatory factors can exacerbate pulmonary vascular structural changes through the p38MAPK signaling pathway. These inflammatory mechanisms intertwine with the EPAS1-driven hypoxic response pathway, collectively promoting the progression of pulmonary VR in COPD.

Conclusions

COPD is a complex heterogeneous disease characterized by persistent airflow limitation, and currently, no effective interventions can completely reverse its disease progression. Smoking and exposure to tobacco particulate matter are its most common environmental risk factors. Our research team previously conducted a pulmonary function survey among over 1,000 Tibetan and Han Chinese residents in the Gannan region of Gansu Province, located at an altitude of approximately 3,000 m. We found that the prevalence of COPD among individuals aged over 40 years in this region was 20.4%, significantly higher than the national average of 13.7%. The prevalence rates were 22.8% in the Tibetan population and 20.0% in the Han population, suggesting that high-altitude hypoxic environments may exacerbate the disease burden of COPD [8].

EPAS1 is a core transcription factor in oxygen-sensitive pathways and functions as a key regulator in the hypoxic response. Studies have shown that EPAS1 is differentially expressed in human lung tissues and closely associated with COPD, although other hypoxia-responsive pathways also contribute to pulmonary vascular remodeling.The HIF-2α protein encoded by EPAS1 is stably expressed under hypoxia and activates downstream effector molecules by binding to hypoxia response elements (HREs) in target gene promoters. This regulation includes VEGF and MMPs, directly influencing endothelial cell proliferation, vascular wall remodeling, and the progression of pulmonary hypertension.Concurrently, EPAS1 expression levels are positively correlated with MAPK signaling pathway activity. Under hypoxic conditions, stabilization of EPAS1 activates the MAPK/ERK pathway, which in turn promotes pulmonary artery smooth muscle cell proliferation and migration, mediating medial thickening and vascular structural remodeling.

Pulmonary VR is a process of progressive changes in vascular physiological function and structure, closely associated with various vascular pathologies. Persistent chronic hypoxia, inflammatory responses, and tobacco exposure are considered the primary triggers of pulmonary vascular remodeling in COPD. As the disease progresses, the pulmonary vascular structure in COPD patients undergoes irreversible remodeling, which not only exacerbates ventilatory dysfunction and inflammatory responses but also induces severe complications such as parenchymal destruction, emphysema, pulmonary hypertension, and cor pulmonale. The mechanisms underlying pulmonary vascular remodeling involve aberrant expression of various cytokines, including VEGF, ET-1, and MMPs. Through pathways such as oxidative-antioxidative imbalance, matrix metalloproteinase imbalance, and inactivation of vasoactive mediators, these mechanisms lead to proliferation and hypertrophy of fibroblasts, smooth muscle cells, and endothelial cells, ultimately resulting in vascular wall thickening and luminal stenosis. The EPAS1-MAPK signaling axis plays a central hub role in this process: on one hand, hypoxia-induced aberrant expression of EPAS1 can directly upregulate cytokines such as VEGF, exacerbating vascular remodeling and the pathological progression of COPD; on the other hand, EPAS1 can directly mediate smooth muscle cell proliferation and vascular structural changes by activating the MAPK signaling pathway.

Although the aforementioned pathological mechanisms have been extensively validated in cellular models, animal experiments, and clinical studies, systematic research on the association between EPAS1 and pulmonary VR remains relatively scarce. As a key regulatory gene aberrantly expressed under chronic hypoxic conditions, EPAS1 may participate in the regulation of critical pathological processes during the progressive exacerbation phase of COPD, particularly during periods of hypoxia. Therefore, in-depth investigation into the molecular mechanisms by which EPAS1 regulates pulmonary vascular remodeling in the hypoxic microenvironment will not only help elucidate the pathogenesis of complications such as pulmonary hypertension in advanced COPD but also provide potential molecular targets for clinical intervention, offering new theoretical foundations and strategies for the precision diagnosis and treatment of COPD patients during exacerbation.

Limitations of this review include the reliance on published studies with heterogeneous models and the lack of large-scale clinical trials validating EPAS1-targeted interventions. Future research should focus on elucidating the interplay between EPAS1 and other hypoxia-responsive factors in COPD, and on exploring therapeutic strategies targeting the EPAS1-MAPK-VEGF axis in clinical settings.

Literature screening method

To ensure comprehensive and systematic coverage of the literature, a thorough search was conducted in PubMed, Web of Science, and CNKI (China National Knowledge Infrastructure) databases up to February 2026. The search keywords included “EPAS1,” “HIF-2α,” “COPD,” and “pulmonary vascular remodeling.” Studies were included if they were in vivo or in vitro research or review articles, published in Chinese or English, focused on EPAS1-mediated molecular pathways in COPD, and provided experimental or clinical evidence supporting pulmonary vascular remodeling. Studies were excluded if they were not peer-reviewed, unrelated to pulmonary vascular remodeling, or lacked translational relevance. A total of 134 articles were initially screened, of which 90 met the inclusion criteria.

From the included studies, key findings were extracted including EPAS1 expression, VEGF and MMP activity, MAPK pathway activation, and vascular remodeling outcomes. Data were categorized by study type (in vitro, in vivo, clinical) to ensure consistency in analysis.
