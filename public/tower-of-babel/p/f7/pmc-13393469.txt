Physical activity patterns in chronic kidney disease
Authors: Demi Boom, Elife Öztürk, Monique Mullens, Jessica Noelmans, Marc H Hemmelder, Jeroen P Kooman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLow levels of physical activity and sedentary behavior are highly prevalent in dialysis patients and are associated with adverse outcomes. However, detailed data on physical activity patterns across the different stages of CKD are limited. The present study aimed to assess physical activity patterns among patients with CKD stages G3, G4, and G5, compared with control subjects.MethodsIn this prospective cross-sectional observational study, patients with CKD G3 (n = 53), patients with CKD G4-G5 (n = 72), and age-matched controls (n = 70) were included. Physical activity and sedentary behavior were measured with an accelerometer. Participants were categorized based on their moderate-to-vigorous physical activity level into physical activity intensity groups. Multiple regression analysis was conducted to assess the associations of age, gender, BMI, eGFR, marital status, education level, Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) score, and diabetes mellitus (DM) with mean daily step count and total sedentary time.ResultsDaily step count was 6396 in the CKD G3 group, 4396 in the CKD G4-5 group, and 9792 in the control group ( p <.001). Sedentary time was the highest in the CKD G4-5 group (662 min/day), followed by the CKD G3 group (618 min/day) compared to the control group (577 min/day) (p < .001). Most patients with CKD G4-5 (84%) and G3 (59%) were insufficiently active, compared to controls (33%). Multiple regression analyses demonstrated that BMI (t = -2.88, p = .005), eGFR (t = 2.41, p = .017), and CCI score (t = -2.59, p = .011) were associated with daily step count, and BMI (t = 4.32, p < .001), education level (t = -2.81, p = .006), and DM (t = 2.24, p = .027) were associated with total sedentary time.ConclusionsPatients with CKD G3 and G4-5 showed lower levels of physical activity and higher levels of sedentary behavior compared to controls. There were significant differences in physical activity between CKD G3 and CKD G4–5, but no significant differences in sedentary behavior between the CKD groups. Most patients with CKD G3 and G4-5 did not meet the international physical activity guidelines. Introduction

Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD) ranks as the 16th leading cause of years of life lost worldwide and is predicted to be the fifth leading cause by 2040 [1, 2]. Patients with kidney failure often experience decreased strength and muscle mass, along with skeletal muscle abnormalities, which may result from disease-related changes in muscle physiology and structure [3–6]. Low levels of physical activity and high levels of sedentary behavior are common in patients with end-stage kidney disease and those receiving kidney replacement treatment, and are associated with adverse outcomes [7]. Moreover, there is also a relation between physical activity, functional performance, and health-related quality of life [8]. This suggests a vicious cycle that can only be disrupted by the incorporation of exercise interventions [9].

There are limited data on detailed physical activity patterns in the earlier stages of CKD (stages G3-5). Prior research focuses on patients with end-stage kidney disease, receiving dialysis, and therefore may not accurately reflect physical activity patterns in earlier disease stages [10, 11]. Studies including the earlier stages of CKD (G2-4) have primarily relied on self-reported questionnaires to assess physical activity levels and examine the association between adherence to physical activity guidelines and sedentary time with mortality, and the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) [12, 13]. Although these studies suggest that most patients meet the physical activity guidelines, self-reported measurements are known to be less accurate and reliable than objective measurements such as accelerometry, and they do not capture detailed patterns of physical activity and sedentary behavior [12, 14]. Therefore, there is a need for studies using objective measurements to identify detailed physical activity and sedentary behavior in CKD stages G3-5, which are crucial for understanding when physical activity declines and sedentary behavior increase compared with controls. Identifying detailed patterns of physical activity and sedentary behavior in early stages of CKD may pave the way for early intervention programs that could prevent or delay the decline in physical activity and the development of comorbidities.

The present study aimed to compare the physical activity patterns between patients with CKD stages G3 to G5 with those of control subjects. We hypothesized that patients with CKD would exhibit lower levels of physical activity and higher levels of sedentary behavior, with the greatest differences observed in the more advanced stages (CKD G4-5).

Materials and methods

Study population

This is an exploratory analysis of baseline data from a prospective longitudinal observational study. Individuals aged 18 years and older with CKD stages G3 to G5 who visited the nephrology outpatient clinic were consecutively selected and enrolled in the study. Individuals were excluded if they met the following criteria: (1) inability to provide informed consent; (2) active symptomatic coronary artery disease or cardiac failure NYHA class III or IV, within three months before inclusion; (3) active malignancies, with a life expectancy of less than one year; (4) active infections, within three months before inclusion; (5) previous kidney transplantation.

Control subjects aged 18 years and older were recruited by advertising in local newspapers, through the internet, and among hospital staff and partners of the patients. Interested individuals underwent a phone screening to test if they were eligible for the study. Individuals were excluded if they met the following criteria: (1) inability to provide informed consent; (2) diabetes mellitus (DM); (3) self-reported cardiovascular disease/chronic kidney disease; (4) blood pressure higher than 160 mmHg systolic and/or higher than 100mmHg diastolic. Informed written consent was obtained from each participant.

Measurements

Baseline variables

The study included 131 patients with CKD (41% stage 3, 59% stages G4-5) and 73 control subjects. During the study visit, baseline variables were collected from the participants, including age, weight, body mass index (BMI), DM, marital status, education level, eGFR, and Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) score.

Accelerometer

Physical activity and sedentary behavior were assessed using the ActivPAL4™ physical activity monitor (PAL Technologies, Glasgow, UK). This compact device (53 × 35 × 7) weighs 15 g and features a triaxial accelerometer that captures movement across vertical, anteroposterior, and mediolateral axes. In addition, ActivPAL4™ identifies the posture, such as stepping, standing, sitting, or lying, based on acceleration data [15]. Participants were asked to wear the device for eight consecutive days, removing it only when bathing, swimming, or using a sauna. To ensure waterproofing, the device was covered in a nitrile sleeve before being attached to the skin of the participant’s right thigh. Once the device was removed, participants were asked to replace it. The data from the device was uploaded to ActivPAL™ Analysis version 9.1.165 using the CREA 1.3 software [16]. Data from the first day were excluded from the analysis, as participants performed physical function tests at the research center after the device was attached. Additionally, data from the final wear day were excluded because the device reported 14 or fewer hours of waking time data. Participants were included in the analysis if the ActivPAL™ recorded at least six valid days to assess the weekly intensity pattern [15].

Parameters obtained with the ActivPAL4™

Step counts were measured as the number of reciprocal leg movements in a day. Sedentary behavior included time spent sitting, lying, and seated transport and was measured in minutes per day [17]. Data were collected from seven complete 24-hour periods, and the average values were calculated daily for daily step count and sedentary time. The number of steps in cadence bands was drawn from the ActivPAL™ data to assess the moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) intensity per week. The MVPA was estimated as ≥ 100 steps per minute, which is the standard threshold for older adults [15, 18]. This was calculated by summing the total number of minutes spent with a cadence of ≥ 100 steps per minute for each weekday. The weekly physical activity intensity patterns were categorized based on MVPA as follows: insufficiently active (< 150 min per week of MVPA) and sufficiently active (≥ 150 min per week of MVPA), following the international physical activity guidelines of the World Health Organization (WHO) [19]. The sufficiently active category was subdivided into “Weekend Warriors” and regularly active. “Weekend Warriors” were participants who did 50% or more of the weekly MVPA intensity on only one or two days [20]. Participants who did their MVPA intensity on three or more days were defined as regularly active [20]. Furthermore, the ActivPAL™ algorithm divided the body posture into four categories: stepping, standing, sitting, and lying. “Stepping” was defined as an upright posture with continuous ambulation. “Standing” denoted an upright posture without continuous ambulation. “Sitting” denoted a non-upright posture, and lying referred to a non-upright posture with a rotated thigh [21]. Data from seven complete 24-hour periods were averaged to give the average amount of time spent walking, standing, sitting, and lying.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized as mean (standard deviation (SD)) for continuous variables or as proportions (n, %) for categorical variables and compared using t-tests or χ2 tests. Not normally distributed variables were compared using the Kruskal-Wallis test. The activity outcomes were reported as median (interquartile range (IQR)).

The differences in physical activity and sedentary behavior between CKD G3, CKD G4-5, and control subjects (primary outcome) were analyzed using a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) test, followed by a Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) test for normally distributed data. For not normally distributed data, group differences were analyzed using a Kruskal-Wallis test, followed by a Mann-Whitney Test with a Bonferroni-adjusted significance level ⍺ =0.0167. For the secondary outcome, differences in physical activity intensity patterns among patients with CKD G3, CKD G4-5, and control subjects, a Chi-square test was performed, followed by post-hoc pairwise comparisons of column proportions using a Bonferroni correction (⍺ =0.0167). Additionally, multiple linear regression analyses were conducted in the CKD population to assess the association of mean daily step count and total sedentary time with age, gender, BMI, eGFR, marital status, education level, and CCI score. An exploratory multiple linear regression analysis was subsequently performed, including DM instead of the CCI score, to examine whether DM was associated with mean daily step count and total sedentary time after adjustment for the same covariates.

Unless otherwise specified, statistical significance was set as p-value < 0.05 (two-tailed test). All data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 28 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Ethical approval

The research activities being reported are in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The Medical Ethics Committee of the MUMC + and the University of Maastricht approved the study protocol (NL68518.068.18).

Results

In total, 204 people were recruited. For the study, only those participants with at least six valid days of recording time data from the ActivPAL4™ were included. Nine participants had less than six valid days of ActivPAL™ recording data, due to non-wear time, and were excluded from the analysis. The study used the data from 53 patients with CKD G3, 72 patients with CKD G4-5, and 70 age-matched control subjects (Fig. 1). The mean age of the patients with CKD stage G3 was 69 years, with a SD of 9, 76% was male, and the mean eGFR was 40.62 ml/min/1.73m2, with a SD of 9.22. The CKD patients with stages G4-5 had a similar mean age of 68 years, with a SD of 10, 65% were male, and a mean eGFR of 20.79 ml/min/1.73m2, with a SD of 5.84. The control group had a mean age of 66 years, with a SD of 10, 74% were male, and a mean eGFR of 77.17 ml/min/1.73m2, with a SD of 10.34. There were no significant differences in age and sex between the three groups. Patients with CKD had a higher weight and BMI, lower education level, higher prevalence of DM, and more comorbidities as reflected by the CCI score compared to the control group (Table 1).

Physical activity intensity patterns

The distribution of physical activity intensity patterns was significantly different among patients with CKD G3 and CKD G4-5 compared to control subjects, χ² (2, N = 195) = 39.80, p < .001. The highest percentage of insufficiently active participants was 84% in the CKD G4-5 group, 59% in the CKD G3 group, and 33% in the control group (Table 3; Fig. 4). Post-hoc analysis with Bonferroni correction revealed significant differences in physical activity patterns among the three groups (Table 3). Individuals in the CKD G3 group who were classified as insufficiently active and “Weekend Warriors” significantly differed from the control group. Those in the CKD G 4–5 group showed significant differences across all three categories compared to the control group. Additionally, participants in the CKD G3 group who were classified as insufficiently active and regularly active were significantly different from those in the CKD G4-5 group.

Daily activities

Daily time spent walking and standing differed significantly between the groups (H(2) = 71.39, p < .001), H(2) = 53.39, p < .001) (Table 4). Post-hoc comparisons with the Mann-Whitney U test for time spent walking and standing showed significant differences between all the groups. Analysis with ANOVA showed significant differences for daily time spent sitting F(2,192) = 8.94, p < .001). Post-hoc analysis with Tukey’s HSD test revealed significant differences for daily time spent sitting in both CKD groups and the control group, but no significant difference between the two CKD groups (p = .520). Daily time spent lying did not differ between the groups (H(2) = 4.22, p = .122) (Table 4; Fig. 5).

Discussion

In this prospective cross-sectional cohort study, detailed physical activity patterns were compared across different CKD stages in comparison to control subjects using an objective measurement. The results suggest that patients with CKD, even in the moderate stage (G3), have lower levels of physical activity and greater sedentary behavior compared to control subjects with preserved renal function. While these differences were more pronounced in CKD G4-5, they were already apparent in CKD G3 patients, supporting our hypothesis that there are stage-related differences in physical activity and sedentary behavior in CKD.

The observed daily step counts in the present study align with a previous meta-analysis [22] and systematic review [23], which reported averages between 5638 steps per day (95% CI: 4052–7224) and 7404 steps per day (95% CI: 4578–10343), and that step count decreases as CKD progresses [22, 23]. While previous studies often lacked a comparative control cohort, the current study addresses this gap by directly comparing daily step counts in patients with CKD versus control subjects. This comparative study provides critical insights into the specific impact of CKD on mean daily step count. Based on an estimated 16-hour waking day, control subjects in the present study spent approximately 60% of their waking day in sedentary postures, whereas CKD patients spent 67% [16]. These results align with prior evidence indicating that individuals without CKD spent more than 50% of their awake time sedentary, while patients with CKD spent more than 67% [24]. Despite these findings, data regarding sedentary time in the earlier stages of CKD are limited, highlighting a critical need for further research to elucidate the progression of sedentary behavior in CKD [25, 26].

A notable finding was the low adherence to WHO-recommended physical activity guidelines (≥ 150 min/week MVPA [19]) in the CKD groups, especially in the CKD G4-5 group, contrasting with the high adherence observed in control subjects. Furthermore, regular MVPA was observed in only a minority of the CKD patients, and the “Weekend Warriors” pattern was even less prevalent. Although there is limited evidence on physical activity intensity patterns in CKD, it is recommended for CKD G1-5 to engage in daily physical activity [27].

The outcomes of the multiple regression analysis align with findings of the Maastricht Study, demonstrating that individuals with reduced kidney function (eGFR < 60 ml/min/1.73m2) were more sedentary and engaged in less total daily physical activity compared with participants with higher kidney function (eGFR ≥ 60 ml/min/1.73m2) [28]. Another cross-sectional observational multicenter study demonstrated that patients who have a greater number of comorbidities were associated with greater odds of being less physically active (OR 1.172, 95% CI 1.057–1.300, p = .003) [29]. Consistent with these findings, the present study shows that a higher CCI score is independently and inversely associated with mean daily step count. A cross-sectional survey study, which also used an accelerometer to measure sedentary behavior, demonstrated that a higher BMI, lower eGFR, and DM are associated with greater sedentary time in patients with CKD [30]. In addition, several studies in older adults demonstrated that lower education levels were associated with reduced sedentary time, which is in contrast with the findings of the present study [31, 32]. The underlying mechanisms explaining the association between higher education level and reduced sedentary time remain unclear; future research is needed to explore this association in patients with CKD.

These findings in this study underscore the necessity for a paradigm shift in CKD care, as the current integration and stimulation of physical activity into daily routine remains insufficient [8]. This study adds novel evidence by objective measurements of physical activity and sedentary behavior across CKD stages and compares them with a control group, highlighting clear stage-specific differences in physical activity and sedentary behavior. The results support the use of wearable devices in clinical practice for developing more personalized and accessible physical activity plans that address the physical limitations and barriers in this population. The use of wearable devices reduces reliance on self-reported measures and better captures behavioral variability across CKD stages. Future research must focus on improving these objective measurement tools within clinical care and self-management frameworks to ensure that physical activity interventions are realistic, tailored, and effective in improving long-term patient outcomes.

This study has several strengths. The primary strength of this study is the use of the ActivPAL4™, which provides measurements of sedentary postures, as it assesses posture rather than movement counts [33–36]. Further, the study compared patients with different CKD stages to age-matched control subjects. This made it possible to assess the impact of CKD in different stages on physical activity patterns. Some limitations of this study must be acknowledged. First, the “Hawthorne effect” remains a possibility; physical activity may be overestimated because participants may have altered their physical behavior by increasing their activity levels due to the awareness of being continuously monitored, potentially overestimating physical activity. Despite the use of an accelerometer to objectively measure physical activity, the possibility of measurement bias cannot be excluded. Second, the ActivPAL4™ may be less accurate than the ActiGraph for measuring physical activity due to its algorithm and thigh placement [33]. Third, due to the sample size of the CKD group, there were not enough individuals to divide patients into separate CKD G4 and CKD G5 groups, preventing us from identifying the specific point of inactivity that may occur as a patient encounters end-stage kidney disease. Finally, the cross-sectional nature of the study does not allow for causal conclusions regarding the decline of physical activity.

In conclusion, patients with CKD exhibit lower levels of physical activity and higher levels of sedentary behavior from stage G3. Given that the majority of this population is insufficiently active, there is an urgent need for clinical strategies that prioritize reducing sedentary time and supporting physical activity.
