Arm Contouring in Patients With Massive Weight Loss: A Literature Review
Authors: Atiyeh Bishara, Rawad Chalhoub, Edwin Chrabieh, Fayez Daoud, George Greige, Tarek El Hachem, Saif Emsieh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:A wide variety of brachioplasty approaches and modifications have been described to make the procedure safer and more appealing to patients and surgeons alike. There is, however, no consensus on the most appropriate surgical approach. The current report aimed at identifying the technique that yields optimal aesthetic outcomes and scar quality associated with highest patient satisfaction and lowest scar visibility and complication rates, in the growing population of patients with massive weight loss.Methods:A PICO PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science literature search was conducted to identify clinical cohort studies published since 1974 about post-bariatric patients undergoing brachioplasty.Results:Fifteen studies satisfied the inclusion criteria and were retrieved for review. Two main methods for excess skin and fat removal, en bloc sharp excision or liposuction-assisted brachioplasty, and 2 final scar placements, medial and posteromedial, with some variations, together with various axillary scars, have been reported. Higher overall complication rate was associated with excisional brachioplasty. Two main scar placements have also been reported: primarily medial, with various extensions to excise additional tissues in the axilla or the elbow region, and posteromedial.Conclusions:Each described technique has advantages and disadvantages. Although quantitative analysis was not possible to conduct, it seems logical that combining advantages of liposuction-assisted brachioplasty with reduced posteromedial scar visibility, in addition to a stepwise “cut-as-you-go” approach, would be most favored, allowing safe procedure execution with optimal aesthetic outcomes and lowest complication rates.

Takeaways

Question: What is the safest and most effective brachioplasty technique for patients with massive weight loss in terms of tissue excision and scar placement?

Findings: Through a systematic review of studies on brachioplasty after massive weight loss, the authors found that liposuction-assisted brachioplasty combined with posteromedial scar placement significantly reduces complication rates and improves patient satisfaction compared with sharp excision and medial scars. A stepwise “cut-as-you-go” approach enhances safety and contour harmony.

Meaning: For optimal results after massive weight loss brachioplasty, combining liposuction-assisted excision with posteromedial scar placement and stepwise resection is recommended.

INTRODUCTION

Brachial lipodystrophy with ptosis becomes increasingly evident with age, particularly after massive weight loss.1 Renewed interest in upper arm contouring has paralleled recent advances in bariatric surgery.2–6 In the 1930s, Thorek7 was the first to describe reduction of pendulous arm deformity by medial arm fusiform skin and fat excision. Aesthetic brachioplasty was described in 1954 by Correa-Iturraspe and Fernandez.8 Their technique yielded suboptimal results and was associated with frequent wound dehiscence, hematoma, seroma, and irregular arm contour.1,9–11 Brachioplasty following massive weight loss was described by McCraw in 1974.12

Baroudi,9 Strauch et al,13 and others have proposed various arm deformity classifications to guide in the selection of optimal treatment of excess fat and skin laxity.10,11 Coefficient of Hoyer (the ratio between proximal and distal upper arm circumference) or the ratio of height of hanging skin to its thickness can assess skin laxity.10 Bicipital groove to lowermost border of pendulous skin distance determines the grade of ptosis.1 These guidelines are helpful; nevertheless, significant operative improvization tempered with clinical judgment may be imperative to properly correct both arm “bat-wing” and armpit contour.3,10

A new classification and treatment strategy adapted from other authors1,10,14 was recently published by Miotto et al.15 Early stages with mild lipodystrophy and ptosis can be corrected by liposuction alone. Occasionally in selected patients, improved arm contour may be achieved by correcting volume deficiency in the bicipital groove with fat grafting.16 For more advanced deformities, particularly following massive weight loss, dermolipectomy extending from the elbow to chest is necessary.13,17,18 Most advanced cases with lateral thoracic laxity require extended brachioplasty to treat arms in combination with axilla, back, and chest.4,19,20

Although described techniques are aimed at making the procedure safer and more appealing to plastic surgeons and patients,2,10,11,18,21 there is no consensus on the most appropriate approach and specific indications for the use of adjuncts. Studies providing practitioners with clear evidence-based guidelines are also lacking.5,22 The current review of various brachioplasty techniques that have evolved over time is aimed to identify the safest and most appropriate approach that yields the most optimal aesthetic outcome with the highest patient satisfaction, and lowest complication rates for the growing population of patients with massive weight loss.

Two independent investigators reviewed identified studies for relevance. Initial screening involved titles and abstracts. In case abstracts lacked sufficient detail, the methods section was reviewed. Disagreements in study selection were resolved by consensus or by consulting a third independent reviewer. Full texts of eligible studies were retrieved, and relevant data extracted using a standardized form. Meta-analysis was not performed due to large study heterogeneity in addition to the small number of patients involved, particularly those undergoing posteromedial brachioplasty.

RESULTS

In total, 15 studies were identified for review. Most were from Italy, 3 were from the United States, and there was one each from France and Egypt. Though ethnicity and skin type of patients involved were not provided; judging from clinical photographs, patients were mostly White.

Two methods for excess skin and fat removal are used: sharp en bloc excision and liposuction-assisted brachioplasty (LAB). Standard wet or superwet LAB was performed in 8 studies,2,23–29 but, when there was incomplete deflation of skin, only excision was achieved. Simultaneous liposuction to the non-excised area was performed in 5 studies18,20,22,24,30 and liposuction 3–5 months before brachioplasty in 1.31 Skin avulsion or excision was performed in 5 studies.23–25,28,29 Though reporting of complications lacked consistency, higher overall complication rate was associated with excisional brachioplasty.20,22,27 LAB permitted easier flap dissection and reduced complications.18,24–26,29 In one study, monobloc resection was performed in 20 patients, and LAB in 11. Incidence of complications was significantly lower with LAB associated with higher patient satisfaction.23

Two scar placements are reported: medial (n = 732) at the bicipital groove along the medial intermuscular septum with various extensions to excise additional tissues in the axilla or the elbow region2,18,20,22,23,25–28,32–34 and posteromedial (n = 141), defined by a straight line from medial epicondyle to posterior axillary fold.24,26,30

High patient satisfaction was described in 8 studies: 5 with medial,23,25,26,29,30 and 2 with posteromedial scars.24,35 Patient-reported outcome measures were mostly about arm contour; scar quality was not a primary outcome. In 1 study, the scar was placed medially in the first 10 patients; it was constantly visible to them, friends, and family. For the next 40 patients, posteromedial scar placement was more acceptable and resulted in subjectively reported good scar quality.31 Scar quality was objectively measured by the Vancouver Scar Scale and explicitly reported in only 1 study with posteromedial scars.24

DISCUSSION

Brachioplasty for patients with massive weight loss almost invariably entails resection of excess redundant tissue. Factors determining outcomes are degree of skin and fat excision, modality of excision, and location of final scar while carefully striking a balance between resecting enough tissue for optimal contour and overresecting to avoid wound closure under tension.36

Despite numerous refinements, complications are frequent and can be as high as 56%. Major complications are particularly high in post-bariatric patients.2,16,33,37 Dissatisfaction with scar appearance is also a most common complaint.38,39 Recently, complications have been reported to be much lower5,37; nevertheless, a considerable rate with concerns regarding wound dehiscence, infection, seroma, edema, lymphocele, lymphedema, nerve injury, under-resection, banding, and residual contour deformity still persist.18,19,28,33,38

Skin excess has been described to be both circumferential and longitudinal; excisions with distal to proximal and circumferential transverse vectors of pull have been recommended.34 In fact, longitudinal skin excess has been overemphasized; it is mostly minimal and rarely a problem.40,41 Techniques with only a transverse axillary component may be adequate for only moderate proximal deformity with appropriate skin tone. They are not suitable for patients with massive weight loss, in whom skin excess is mostly circumferential.4,42–44 Apparent longitudinal excess is sagging of deflated skin envelope that can be corrected by circumferential reduction42 (Fig. 3).

Resection of redundancy for circumference reduction achieves simultaneous vertical lift.

Brachioplasty scars in most patients with massive weight loss are better when longitudinal; however, their exact position, key to patient satisfaction, remains debatable.11,18–20,22,27,36,45,46 There is also no consensus on whether scars should be straight or sinusoidal, or what should be excised and from where.45 Anthropometric studies demonstrated that the best way to determine skin excess is to project a truncated conical frustum from the axilla and deltoid to the elbow and wrist.45 Any skin that lies outside this cone can be considered for removal. With the assumption that brachioplasty scar location does not contribute to scar quality,13,47 and that scarring that never entirely fades away is inevitable, the medial straight scar popularized by Baroudi9 is mostly performed and traditionally considered to be most acceptable, though it is seldom of high quality.5,32 This scar is camouflaged in the bicipital groove shadow and is presumably well hidden, virtually imperceptible most of the time during composed posture, and acceptable during expressive conversations and daily activities.1,10,18,25

Nevertheless, wound closure along the medial intermuscular septum can lead to eye-catching scar line depression. Instead, scar placement between the bicipital groove and posterior edge of the arm is suggested.48 Regardless of variations in medial placement, such scars are constantly visible to patients.31 In contradistinction, posteriorly based scars within the brachial sulcus, a shallow oblique depression located at the center of the humerus lateral border, though not visible to the patient, are almost always visible to everybody else looking from behind.2,21,46,49

In an attempt to reduce tension and improve scar quality, Lockwood50 advised suspension of the arm fascial system to clavipectoral fascia. Goddio51 described de-epithelialization and tucking of the posterior skin flap beneath the anterior flap. Modified by Nisi et al28 as the “jaws” brachioplasty, the technique reduces tension through transfixed points. El Khatib1,52 suggested pulling down the upper excision margin and fixing it to deep fascia with permanent sutures. On the other hand, Regnault42 described the necessity to modify longitudinal excisions depending on amount of skin excess. To prevent widened or hypertrophic scars and reduce linear contracture, Strauch et al13 and De Souza Pinto et al53 suggested a sinusoidal pattern. Fantozzi45 proposed the triple-vector approach creating 2 asymmetric curves with medial and posterior flaps. S-shaped34 and kris-knife54 excision patterns have also been described to reduce scar tension. Distal curvilinear extension of medial arm incision to the anticubital surface is reported as well, to correct excessive sagging at the elbow.25 Despite all tension distribution attempts, sinusoidal scars are the most aesthetically displeasing, with high complications and scar revision rates.2,55 Moreover, deeply placed anchoring sutures may not be advisable, as vital structures particularly in the axilla may be injured.13 Finally, silk-derived surgical scaffold (SERI; Allergan, Inc., Irvine, CA) was used to support the medial arm superficial fascial system as an adjunct to conventional brachioplasty.52,56 Claims that this device results in faster maturation and better scar quality await confirmation.

Scar placement at the posteromedial aspect of the arm constitutes an important improvement in longitudinal brachioplasty. It has been reported to minimize frontal scar visibility when the arms are elevated.13,38,39,52,53,57,58 Symbas and Losken59 have reported their experience with the 2 currently most widely used approaches: the L-shaped medial resection of Hurwitz and Holland30 and the double-ellipse posteromedial technique of Aly et al.36 Even though their report is not comparative, their preference went to posteromedial scars that are not visible to patients even when looking in a mirror. After bicipital groove scar placement for their first 10 patients, Cannistra et al31 elected to place scars well posteriorly along a line between the medial axillary line and olecranon for their next 40 patients and considered this to be more acceptable.

Although patients claim preoperatively not to be concerned about scar appearance, objection to scar length and location becomes readily expressed after surgery.5,11,21,60 Unpredictable scar quality is the most common adverse outcome and remains a challenge for both surgeons and patients. Lack of skin elasticity in patients with massive weight loss is probably a major predisposing factor for pathologic scarring.13 Many other factors including wound closure under tension, wound dehiscence, infection, and Fitzpatrick skin type contribute to poor scarring as well. Combination of gravitational forces, stress, thin skin, and a superficial fascial skin system can also lead to scar widening and hypertrophic scarring.56

Scar shape and placement need not be “a matter of taste and philosophy”36; it is about understanding tension forces in dynamics and how a more even spread of forces along the arm can reduce complications and long-term scarring problems.11,45 Closer analysis of tissue displacement dynamics reveals that for wound closure in both medial and posterior linear scar placement, skin and subcutaneous tissues must be displaced and pulled. In standing position with the arms abducted for daily gesticulation, the pull is superior against gravity, resulting in high stress forces. Intensity of gravitational stress and its distribution are greatly reduced in the posteromedial scar location (Fig. 4).24,31,38

Scar location may make its presence more or less acceptable. Posteromedial scar placement is more effective in scar concealment when the arms are elevated or are resting at the side.5,13,24,56 Unfortunately, no reliable PROMs are available to confirm this observation. Though very little data are available regarding healing time comparing medial to posteromedial scar placement, medial scars are associated in general with higher complication rates, including higher incidence of wound dehiscence, 24%–40% risk of hypertrophic or wide visible scarring, and higher revision rates. They are also frequently associated with residual contour deformities and skin ptosis.1,2,10,11,18,29,36,55,56 A recent meta-analysis of the impact of incision placement on complications demonstrated that medial excisional patterns are predominantly characterized by higher complication rates relative to all other incisions (43.7% versus 9.7%). They result in higher aberrant scarring (15.6% versus 2.98%), wound dehiscence (8.7% versus 3.1%), seroma formation (8.41% versus 1.4%), ptosis (9.44% versus 5.84%), overall reoperation for aesthetic purposes (9.9% versus 3.8%), and nerve-related complications including temporary or permanent dysesthesia, paresthesia, or confirmed nerve sectioning or compression (2.89% versus 1.39%).5

With sharp en bloc excision along the bicipital groove, damage of medially located major lymphatics and sensory subcutaneous nerves can occur, even with careful dissection. Medial brachial and antebrachial cutaneous sensory nerves are at great risk. Ulnar and median nerves may also be vulnerable in cases where deep brachial fascia is violated.15,18,20,22,45,59,61–63 With sharp excision along the brachial sulcus,4,13,45,55,60,64 in addition to flattening of posterior natural rounding of the arm,54 the subcutaneous posterior branch of the medial cutaneous antebrachial nerve may be injured, resulting in painful neuroma and elbow paresthesia.15,18,23

Combined liposuction/excision brachioplasty offers a safer option.15,19 Recent evidence suggests that LAB, whether liposuction is at the excision site, adjacent to it, or circumferential, does not lead to higher overall complication rates regardless of scar placement.2,5,18,21,22,26,27,38,41,65 On the contrary, liposuction is associated with lower incidence of seroma formation (3% versus 13.53%), lymphedema (1.6% versus 6.5%), and nerve injury (1.5% versus 5.5%). It even results in lesser aberrant scarring (7.5% versus 20.08%).4 However, concomitant liposuction may cause some difficulty in wound closure without tension due to exaggerated fluid infiltration and edema formation, leading to underresection. It also increases slightly the risk of wound dehiscence, skin necrosis, and hematoma formation.4,5,21,40 Suction-assisted lipectomy a few months before brachioplasty has been suggested whenever debulking is required.31

Skin-only excision or avulsion combined with aggressive liposuction of the area to be excised (Fig. 5) has been described to significantly decrease complications and improve patient satisfaction. It preserves underlying structures and decreases bleeding compared with sharp dissection. It also allows easy wound closure.4,18,21–24,41,45,47,49,57,64,65 Worth noting is that contrary to recommendation of tapering and feathering liposculpture of adjacent areas,24 edges of the liposuctioned zone should remain very distinct, creating a dished-out deformity as described by Knotts et al57 to result in smooth contour.

Regardless of excisional technique and final scar placement, wound closure is not met infrequently with difficulty. If delayed until after complete excision, edema invariably settles complicating wound suturing, particularly if the extent of excision has been overestimated. Easy wound closure in this situation necessarily entails undercorrection.20 Moreover, uneven tension along the suture line results in distressing arm banding. Preoperatively existing banding can also be exacerbated.33 Stepwise skin excision and the “cut-as–you-go” approach with temporary staples allow correction and readjustment of skin excision and closure with controlled tension. It greatly reduces the risk of banding that is very difficult to correct subsequently.36

Correcting axilla deformity is an essential component of brachioplasty. From simple geometric analysis, it cannot be achieved by just medial elliptical excision along the bicipital groove; minimal excision is possible near the vertex angle proximally in Strauch zones III and IV where most skin excess occurs. For wider excision, a large vertex angle would be necessary; this creates stress during suturing with unfavorable “dog-ear” conical deformations. Additional excision with resultant T, L, W, and Z scars in the armpit are unavoidable for restoration of upper arm and axillary contour.11,15,30,46,66,67 However, scars across the axilla are not to be taken lightly. Despite claimed advantages, they do not yield optimal upper arm and axillary contour in many patients.11,15,30,66,68 With Z-plasty recommended to deepen the axillary dome,22 underresection is the norm. Moreover, coning deformity develops at each closed angle of the Z that does not fit well in a dynamic concave axillary dome.30 Fish tail incision extended over anterior and posterior axillary folds may be more appropriate11; however, scars across the anterior fold are highly objectionable to many patients.42 On close examination, excess tissue is actually in the posterior axillary fold in continuity with excess in the posteromedial arm. Excision is thus better centered along this fold, sparing the axillary dome and extending to the lateral chest wall whenever necessary65,69 (Fig. 6). With this approach, a normal axillary hair pattern, much appreciated by male patients, is preserved.36

LIMITATIONS

This review is not without limitations. The few studies reviewed are largely observational, providing a low level of evidence and carrying high risk of bias. Moreover, in addition to a high degree of heterogeneity and incomplete data reporting, there is large disparity across studies pertaining to what constitutes true complication or undesirable outcome, as well as in reporting scar quality and patient satisfaction. Reporting of complications lacked consistency, limiting data synthesis and interpretation. Use of standardized scales of surgical complications should be encouraged for future studies.

CONCLUSIONS

The main focus of various brachioplasty technical modifications has been to improve arm, axilla, and upper trunk contour, optimize scar placement, and minimize complications. Nevertheless, none of the described techniques is without drawbacks, complications, and need for possible revisional touch-ups. Though scar position has to be discussed critically with patients, it remains somewhat unclear which combination of excision technique and scar position offers the best outcome. It seems that posteromedial scar placement, in addition to reduced visibility to the patients as well as others during daily gesticulation, reduces tissue stress, theoretically leading to better scar quality. Furthermore, rather than sharp excision, liposuction of the area to be excised followed by skin-only excision offers added safety and greatly reduces complication rates. Finally, along with surgical and artistic judgment, “cut–as-you-go” stepwise skin excision results in optimal outcomes with smooth and harmonious contour. There is, however, a need for prospective comparative studies to validate this approach.
