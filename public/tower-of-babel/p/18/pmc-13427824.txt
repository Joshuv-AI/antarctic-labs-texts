Numerical simulation study on combustion and emissions of n-butanol/diesel blended fuel
Authors: Kun Shao, Heng Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study employed three-dimensional numerical simulation methods to investigate the combustion and emission characteristics of three fuels—B0 (pure diesel), B15 (a mixture of 15% n-butanol and 85% diesel), and B30 (a mixture of 30% n-butanol and 70% diesel)—under the coupled effects of different injection pressures and injection timings. The results indicate that an increase in injection pressure significantly improves fuel atomization, promotes the transition to premixed combustion, and shortens the ignition delay time (ID) and combustion duration (CD) by 0.25, 0.51, and 0.73°CA, and 8.13, 8.19, and 8.26°CA, respectively. As injection pressure increased, the indicated thermal efficiency improved by 0.4%, 0.7%, and 1.2%, respectively, but NOx emissions increased by 107.4%, 84.7%, and 63.7%, respectively, while soot emissions showed the opposite trend. Furthermore, injection timing has a significant impact on the combustion atmosphere within the cylinder; delayed injection leads to prolonged ID and CD, adversely affecting indicated thermal efficiency. The study found that the injection timing yielding maximum thermal efficiency for B30 fuel is 9°CA, which is slightly earlier than that for B0 fuel. When the fuel injection timing was delayed from 11°CA BTDC to 3°CA BTDC, NOx emissions for all three fuels showed a decreasing trend to varying degrees, but soot emissions increased by 45.1%, 38.3%, and 35.2%, respectively. It is worth noting that soot emissions from B30 fuel remained at a relatively low level across different injection timings. The results of this study provide a theoretical basis for optimizing the combustion performance and emission control of biodiesel-diesel blends.

Introduction

As the global energy crisis intensifies and emissions regulations for internal combustion engines become increasingly stringent, the search for clean, renewable alternative fuels has become a major research focus in the field. Due to their high thermal efficiency and reliability, diesel engines dominate the transportation and construction machinery sectors; however, the trade-off between NOx and soot emissions remains a critical issue that urgently needs to be addressed1. Among the many alternative fuels, n-butanol is considered a highly promising diesel additive due to its high energy density, good miscibility, moderate cetane number, and low hygroscopicity2,3. Compared to methanol and ethanol, n-butanol exhibits better miscibility with diesel, eliminating the need for co-solvents; simultaneously, its higher cetane number contributes to improved ignition stability in compression-ignition engines. Therefore, investigating the combustion and emission characteristics of n-butanol/diesel blends in diesel engines holds significant engineering and practical value4.

In recent years, scholars both in China and abroad have conducted extensive research on n-butanol/diesel blended fuels.Xie’s research found that advancing the injection timing of n-butanol can effectively improve the combustion and emission performance of dual-fuel direct-injection engines. Specifically, this results in a more uniform mixture and more complete combustion, leading to an approximately 3.5% increase in thermal efficiency and a reduction in soot emissions, despite a slight increase in nitrogen oxides. Therefore, under medium-load conditions, an earlier injection timing is recommended to achieve the goals of high efficiency and clean combustion5. Mingrui analyzed the effects of adding n-butanol to diesel fuel on engine combustion and emissions. The results showed that increasing the proportion of n-butanol prolongs ignition delay and shortens combustion duration; however, it promotes the complete combustion of diesel fuel, ultimately increasing in-cylinder pressure and the rate of heat release. Nevertheless, n-butanol also increases NOx and CO emissions6. Sengupta’s research demonstrated that optimizing the staged injection strategy can effectively achieve the combustion of a n-butanol/diesel blend in a stock single-cylinder diesel engine. The study found that advancing the injection timing and increasing the proportion of diesel in the mixture can significantly reduce ignition delay and lower nitrogen oxide emissions by 91%; at a 25% n-butanol energy ratio, it can simultaneously reduce emissions of soot and hydrocarbons7.

Through simulation analysis, Xie found that in a dual-fuel direct-injection engine with a high n-butanol blend (60%), the timing of the two diesel injections is a key factor influencing combustion and emissions: the timing of the first injection governs the exothermic process and thermal efficiency, and if it is too early, it can lead to wet walls; the timing of the second injection, in turn, significantly controls the combustion phase, pressure rise rate, and soot/NOx emissions. In contrast, the energy fraction of the first injection has a relatively limited impact on overall combustion and emissions8. By developing a dual-fuel direct injection system, Bai achieved direct injection and mixed combustion of diesel and n-butanol. The study found that increasing the n-butanol ratio delays combustion and reduces thermal efficiency; however, optimizing the injection timing of both fuels can effectively improve performance. Specifically, advancing the n-butanol injection timing at low loads and advancing the injection timing of both fuels simultaneously at medium loads significantly improves thermal efficiency and reduces emissions. Ultimately, the optimized dual-fuel mode increased the indicated thermal efficiency by 2.8% and reduced NOx and soot emissions by 82% and 79%, respectively9. Shao’s research revealed the mechanism of action of n-butanol/diesel blended fuel through chemical kinetics and three-dimensional simulations. The study found that increasing the n-butanol ratio prolongs ignition delay and promotes mixture homogenization, thereby significantly reducing soot emissions, though it has a minimal effect on NOx. The underlying chemical mechanism is as follows: n-butanol suppresses the low-temperature reaction chain of diesel to delay ignition, while simultaneously providing oxygen-containing radicals to enhance oxidative activity, promoting the oxidation of soot precursors and reducing soot formation at its source10.

Among engine operating parameters, fuel injection strategies play a decisive role in the combustion process. Injection pressure and injection timing directly affect fuel atomization quality, mixture formation, and the thermodynamic conditions within the cylinder. Although some studies have explored the effects of injection parameters on n-butanol/diesel blended fuels, existing research has primarily focused on analysing the impact of individual parameters, with few systematic studies on the coupling mechanisms between injection pressures and injection timings at various n-butanol blending ratios. In particular, for high-blend n-butanol fuels (such as B30), the optimal combustion phase differs from that of conventional diesel. Currently, there is a lack of specific injection timing optimisation data for the point of maximum thermal efficiency, and the trade-off between nitrogen oxide and soot emissions, as well as its underlying mechanisms, remains unclear when injection strategies are altered.

To address these research gaps, this study employs three-dimensional computational fluid dynamics (CFD) numerical simulation to investigate the combustion and emission characteristics of B0, B15, and B30 fuels under the coupled effects of varying injection pressure and timing. Compared to experimental studies, numerical simulation can provide deeper insights into the evolution of in-cylinder flow fields and the mechanisms of chemical reaction kinetics. The primary objectives of this study are: (1) to quantify the influence of injection pressure and timing on the ignition delay, combustion duration, and indicated thermal efficiency of the blended fuels; (2) to elucidate the trade-off mechanism between NOx and soot emissions under different injection strategies; (3) To determine the optimal injection timing and injection pressure control strategies for achieving maximum thermal efficiency with B30 fuel, thereby providing a theoretical basis for the practical engine optimization of n-butanol/diesel blends.

Test system and test condition

The test utilized a 2.7-liter water-cooled turbocharged four-cylinder diesel engine with a compression ratio of 17 and an output power of 84 kW; a schematic diagram of the test bench is shown in Fig. 111.

Schematic diagram of test stand.

To improve computational efficiency, a 1/6-sector combustion chamber model was constructed based on the number of injector orifices (see Fig. 2). The model boundaries were defined as follows: the cylinder head and cylinder liner were set as stationary walls, the piston as a moving wall, and the side walls as periodic boundaries. Wall temperatures were derived from in-engine measurement data, with the cylinder head, cylinder liner, and piston set at 520 K, 450 K, and 550 K, respectively. The calculation begins at intake valve closure (-135°CA) and includes initial parameters such as in-cylinder pressure, temperature, and composition at this point. To match the experimental air-fuel ratio, the initial pressure was determined to be 0.15 MPa and the temperature 350 K after iterative adjustments. Residual exhaust gases were neglected, and the initial working fluid was simplified to pure air. The default grid size is set to 1.4 mm. Two local grid control zones have been defined: one near the nozzle, where the grid is refined from 3° CA before injection to 3° CA after injection, with a scale of 2, resulting in a grid size of 0.35 mm; The other is near the piston boundary, where the geometry is more complex; here, the Scale is also set to 2, and the Embedding Level is set to 1. This grid refinement applies to the entire calculation process.

Model validation

This study employs the n-heptane mechanism, n-butanol mechanism, and simplified PAH model developed by Wang et al. to construct a three-dimensional simulation model15. n-Heptane was selected because its cetane number is compatible with that of diesel fuel and it can reproduce two-stage ignition characteristics; this was quantitatively verified under pure diesel operating conditions (with peak pressure error, ID error, and NOx emission error all less than 5%). Figure 3 shows a comparison of cylinder pressures between pure diesel and a 30% diesel/n-butanol blend at 1800 r/min and 50% load. Due to differences in the physicochemical properties between actual diesel components and the substitute n-heptane, coupled with the limitations of the reaction mechanism for oxygenated fuels, there are slight discrepancies between the simulated and experimental values. Overall, the simulation results show a high degree of agreement with the experimental trends, demonstrating the model’s validity and meeting the requirements for subsequent research.

Results and discussion

Injection pressure

Figure 4 shows the distribution of the equivalence ratio for B30 fuel during injection. As shown in the figure, as injection pressure increases, the penetration distance of the fuel jet increases, while the local rich zones decrease. This is because increasing injection pressure increases the momentum of the fuel jet, thereby enhancing the disruptive forces acting on it during its development. This accelerates processes such as fuel fragmentation, atomization, and evaporation, resulting in a finer spray. At the same time, the contact area between fuel and air increases, and the rate of fuel-air mixing increases relatively, improving the formation of the fuel-air mixture. Ultimately, this leads to a more uniform fuel-air mixture when injection pressure is increased.

Fig. 4Distribution of the air-fuel ratio at different injection pressures for B30 fuel.

Distribution of the air-fuel ratio at different injection pressures for B30 fuel.

Figure 5 shows the curves of cylinder pressure and heat release rate for the three fuels at different injection pressures, with an injection timing of 7°CA BTDC. When the injection pressure increased from 85 MPa to 145 MPa, the maximum in-cylinder detonation pressure for fuels B0, B15, and B30 increased by 14.6%, 13.9%, and 13.4%, respectively. The peak heat release rates for B0, B15, and B30 increased by 154.92%, 124.34%, and 84.96%, respectively. To highlight the differences in the physicochemical properties between oxygen-containing fuels and diesel, B0 and B30 were selected for comparative analysis. As shown in the figure, the point at which the cylinder pressure curves of B0 and B30 fuels diverge from the pure compression curve occurs slightly earlier as the injection pressure increases, meaning the ignition onset is advanced, and the peak cylinder pressure gradually increases while the peak phase gradually advances. The peak heat release rate also increases with rising injection pressure. The heat release rate curve for B0 fuel shifts from a “bimodal” curve to a “unimodal” curve, indicating that increasing injection pressure facilitates the transition toward a premixed combustion mode16.

Fig. 5Comparison of cylinder pressure and Heat release rate at different injection pressures. (a) Comparison of combustion parameters, (b) B0, (c) B30.

Comparison of cylinder pressure and Heat release rate at different injection pressures. (a) Comparison of combustion parameters, (b) B0, (c) B30.

Figure 6 shows the curves of the combustion characteristic parameters for B0, B15, and B30 fuels as a function of injection pressure. It can be observed that, for the same fuel, both the ignition delay and the combustion duration decrease gradually as the injection pressure increases. When the injection pressure increases from 85 MPa to 145 MPa, the ignition delay and combustion duration for B0 decrease by 0.25°CA and 8.13°CA, respectively, while those for B15 decrease by 0.51°CA and 8.19°CA, and for B30, they decrease by 0.73°CA and 8.26°CA, respectively. This is primarily because increasing the injection pressure results in a finer fuel spray, thereby increasing the contact area between the fuel and air17. Consequently, the volume of combustible mixture formed prior to ignition increases, and the probability of spark nucleation rises, causing the ignition onset to occur earlier. As for the combustion duration, the increased fuel injection pressure promotes the formation of the mixture, leading to a higher average reaction rate of combustion. At the same time, the proportion of premixed combustion increases while that of diffusion combustion decreases, ultimately resulting in a gradual shortening of the combustion duration18.

Fig. 6Comparison of combustion parameters at different injection pressures. (a) ID, (b) CD, (c) Indicated thermal efficiency.

Comparison of combustion parameters at different injection pressures. (a) ID, (b) CD, (c) Indicated thermal efficiency.

Figure 7 shows the variation of the in-cylinder OH radical molar fraction as a function of crankshaft angle for fuels B0, B15, and B30 as injection pressure increases. The OH radical is a key indicator of high-temperature ignition, and changes in its concentration curve reveal the microscopic mechanisms underlying the influence of injection pressure from a chemical-kinetics perspective. As injection pressure increases, the peak concentration of OH radicals rises significantly and its phase advances. This is primarily attributed to the intensification of the fuel-air mixing process caused by high-pressure injection: higher injection velocities promote droplet fragmentation, reduce the Sauter mean diameter, thereby improving the uniformity of fuel-air mixing and reducing localized areas of excess concentration. When injection pressure increases, the onset time of OH is significantly advanced, the rise slope increases, and the peak concentration rises substantially. The earlier onset of OH directly explains the shortened ignition delay under high injection pressure; the increased slope of the OH rise reflects the acceleration of high-temperature chain-branching reactions, which is the intrinsic mechanism behind the reduced combustion duration. Furthermore, B30 fuel exhibited the greatest increase in OH peak concentration under high pressure, indicating that a high proportion of n-butanol is most sensitive to injection pressure, and its oxygen-containing properties are fully realized after improved atomization.

Fig. 7Comparison of changes in OH group mass at different injection pressures. (a) B0, (b) B30.

Comparison of changes in OH group mass at different injection pressures. (a) B0, (b) B30.

Figure 8 shows the curves of cylinder temperature versus injection pressure for the two fuels. As injection pressure increases, the pre-mix combustion ratio increases, and the peak heat release rate rises; consequently, the temperature peaks for both B0 and B30 fuels show a gradual upward trend. However, the combustion duration and heat release time are shortened, making it impossible to maintain subsequent in-cylinder temperatures, resulting in a gradual decrease in temperature later on.

Comparison of temperature curves at different injection pressures. (a) B0, (b) B30.

Figure 9 shows the simulated temperature distribution inside the B30 cylinder. It can be seen that during the early stages of combustion, as the injection pressure increases, the number of high-temperature zones within the cylinder increases, and temperatures in the squirt zone and near the injector rise significantly19.

Fig. 9Cylinder temperature distribution at different injection pressures for B30.

Cylinder temperature distribution at different injection pressures for B30.

Figure 10 shows NOx emissions at different injection pressures. It can be seen that as injection pressure increases, NOx emissions for all three fuel types follow a pattern of gradual increase. However, the effect of injection pressure on the rate of NOx emission increase varies among the three fuels. When injection pressure increases from 85 MPa to 145 MPa, NOx emissions for B0, B15, and B30 increase by 107.4%, 84.7%, and 63.7%, respectively. This is because increased injection pressure gradually raises the combustion temperature inside the cylinder. Higher temperatures increase the concentration of O and OH radicals during combustion, promoting NOx formation20. Therefore, regardless of the fuel type, NOx emissions are directly proportional to injection pressure. However, at the same injection pressure, B30 has a longer ignition delay period and a more uniform mixture, which mitigates the effect of injection pressure on the fuel-air mixing process. Therefore, when increasing injection pressure to improve in-cylinder combustion, using a n-butanol-diesel blend results in a smaller increase in NOx emissions.

NOx emissions by mass at different injection pressures.

Figure 11 shows the mass distribution of NOx within the cylinder during the combustion process of B30. It can be seen that the NOx formation zone largely coincides with the high-temperature zone. Since the temperature near the injector increases with higher injection pressure, a small amount of NOx is also generated near the injector—that is, at the center of the combustion chamber. Furthermore, the concentration of NOx in the piston recess and the compression zone increases as the injection pressure rises21.

Fig. 11NOx distribution at different injection pressures for B30.

NOx distribution at different injection pressures for B30.

Figure 12 shows the variation in soot mass at different injection pressures. As can be seen from the figure, soot mass decreases gradually as injection pressure increases. When the injection pressure was increased from 85 MPa to 145 MPa, soot emissions for B0, B15, and B30 decreased by 84.8%, 89.5%, and 94.9%, respectively. This is because, although combustion temperature rises with increased injection pressure, the mixture becomes more homogeneous, reducing localized areas of excess fuel in the cylinder. Additionally, the high temperature increases the concentration of OH radicals, enhancing oxidation and thereby weakening the conditions for soot formation, thus suppressing soot generation22.

Soot emissions by mass at different injection pressures.

Figure 13 shows the soot formation process for B30 fuel. It can be seen that as the injection pressure increases, soot formation near the fuel jet decreases, with only a small amount forming on the walls of the piston recess. Once soot formation and oxidation reach equilibrium, the final soot emission within the cylinder is inversely proportional to the injection pressure; when the injection pressure is 145 MPa, virtually no soot is formed inside the cylinder23.

Injection timing

Figure 14 shows the distribution of the air-fuel ratio for B30 fuel at different injection timings during the injection process. As the injection timing is delayed, the in-cylinder temperature and pressure at the start of fuel injection gradually increase, thereby promoting the fragmentation, atomization, and evaporation of the fuel jet. This helps homogenize the mixture and reduces the size of local rich zones; However, as the piston moves downward, changes in cylinder temperature and pressure occur. Consequently, the in-cylinder conditions during fuel injection at different injection timings do not differ significantly. Furthermore, since B30 itself has good volatility, it mitigates the influence of temperature and pressure on mixture formation. This results in a relatively minor effect of different injection timings on mixture formation when using B30 fuel.

Fig. 14Distribution of the air-fuel ratio at different injection timings for B30 fuel.

Distribution of the air-fuel ratio at different injection timings for B30 fuel.

Figure 15 illustrates the curves of in-cylinder pressure and heat release rate for various fuels at different injection timings. It can be seen that, for the same fuel, the relationship between injection timing and in-cylinder pressure and heat release rate follows a similar pattern: as the injection timing is delayed, the peak in-cylinder pressure gradually decreases, and the peak phase is also delayed. The cylinder pressure curve changes from a “single-peak” form to a “double-peak” form. For fuel B0, a double-peak form appears when the injection timing is later than 5°CA BTDC, whereas for B30, due to its low cetane number and longer ignition delay, the combustion onset occurs earlier—after top dead center—so that a double-peak form in cylinder pressure is already observed when the injection timing is 7°CA BTDC. Meanwhile, the heat release rate curve shows that as the injection timing is delayed, the peak heat release rate first decreases and then increases; when the injection timing is delayed to 3°CA BTDC, the heat release rate continues to decrease24.

Fig. 15Comparison of cylinder pressure and heat release rates at different injection timings. (a) B0, (b) B30.

Comparison of cylinder pressure and heat release rates at different injection timings. (a) B0, (b) B30.

To further explain the combustion characteristics described above, Fig. 16 shows how the combustion parameters of the fuel vary with the injection timing. It can be seen that as the injection timing is delayed, the ignition delay period of B30 first decreases and then increases, while the combustion duration gradually increases. When the injection timing is 3°CA BTDC, the ignition point is 8°CA ATDC. Although the in-cylinder temperature and pressure are high at the start of fuel injection, the fuel-air mixing process primarily occurs during the expansion stroke after top dead center. At this point, the in-cylinder temperature and pressure begin to decrease, significantly extending the ignition delay period. Simultaneously, the lower temperature reduces the reaction rate of premixed combustion, resulting in a relatively low peak heat release rate. Furthermore, since the combustion process at this timing occurs during the power expansion stroke, the power output is poor, and the specific heat capacity is low, ultimately resulting in lower cylinder pressure and indicated thermal efficiency. When the fuel injection timing is gradually advanced from 4°CA BTDC to 9°CA BTDC, the cylinder temperature and pressure during the fuel-air mixing process are sufficiently high, resulting in a shorter ID. The combustion timing and the main heat release timing gradually shift toward top dead center, increasing the specific heat capacity. Consequently, combustion pressure and thermal efficiency gradually rise. When the fuel injection timing is 7°CA BTDC, the ignition delay period is the shortest, the proportion of premixed combustion is lowest, and the peak heat release rate is the lowest. When the injection timing is 11°CA BTDC, the in-cylinder temperature and pressure prior to ignition are lower, resulting in a longer ignition delay period and a higher proportion of premixed fuel. The peak heat release rate is highest. Additionally, since the combustion process occurs during the compression stroke, the in-cylinder pressure and temperature during combustion are at their highest, increasing the reaction rate and shortening the overall combustion duration. However, because combustion primarily occurs during the compression process at this point, the negative work performed by the engine increases, leading to a decrease in thermal efficiency25. Furthermore, as the injection timing is delayed, the temperature gradually decreases, reducing the average reaction rate of combustion and extending the combustion duration. For B30 fuel, the indicated thermal efficiency is highest when the injection timing is 9°CA BTDC.

For pure diesel B0 fuel, most combustion parameters follow a similar trend with respect to injection timing as B30. However, due to diesel’s high cold-start performance and short ignition delay, it adapts well to both the in-cylinder thermal environment and the activated atmosphere. At an injection timing of 3°CA BTDC, there is no significant prolongation of the ignition delay, and the indicated thermal efficiency is maximized at an injection timing of 7°CA BTDC.

Comparison of combustion parameters at different injection timings. (a) ID, (b) CD, (c) Indicated thermal efficiency.

Figure 17 shows the variation of the molar fraction of the OH radical in the cylinder for B0, B15, and B30 fuels as the injection timing is advanced from − 11°CA to -3°CA. Unlike injection pressure, which affects OH evolution by improving atomization, adjusting the injection timing primarily alters the moment of fuel injection into the cylinder and the resulting thermodynamic environment. When injection timing is advanced, fuel is injected at lower initial temperatures and pressures, extending the duration of physical evaporation and low-temperature reactions. Consequently, the onset of OH concentration shifts forward approximately linearly with advancing injection timing. More critically, advanced injection timing provides a longer “mixing time window” for fuel-air mixing, resulting in a more homogeneous premixed charge before high-temperature ignition. Consequently, once ignition occurs, the rate of rise of OH concentration increases significantly, and the peak concentration rises markedly. From a chemical kinetics perspective, this explains why, although advanced injection prolongs the ignition delay, it promotes premixed combustion and shortens the combustion duration; it also reveals the underlying mechanism by which advanced injection leads to increased NOx emissions (expansion of the high-temperature zone). Furthermore, the OH peak for B30 fuel is lower than that of B0 at all injection times, attributed to its higher latent heat of vaporization, which reduces the local reaction temperature; this is also the direct reason why the increase in NOx for B30 is smaller than that of B0 at the same injection time.

Figure 19 shows the cylinder temperature distribution for B30 fuel at various crankshaft angles corresponding to different injection timings. As shown in the cross-sectional diagrams, during the early combustion phase at 11°CA ATDC and 13°CA ATDC, the combustion duration increases as the injection timing is advanced, thereby gradually increasing the high-temperature zone above 1750 K. However, as combustion progresses, excessively early injection timing results in insufficient heat release during the late combustion phase. Combined with heat and mass transfer within the cylinder, the overall high-temperature zone remains largely unchanged as injection timing is advanced, but the local high-temperature zone above 2225 K decreases. When the injection timing is 3°CA BTDC, the combustion delay period is longer, and heat release occurs later; consequently, the high-temperature zone within the cylinder at this injection timing is slightly larger than that at 5°CA BTDC26.

Figure 20 shows NOx emission levels at different injection timings. It can be seen that NOx emissions gradually decrease as the injection timing is retarded; when the injection timing is retarded from 11°CA BTDC to 3°CA BTDC, NOx emissions decrease by 62.1%, 60.3%, and 58.7%, respectively. This reduction in NOx emissions is attributed to the gradual decrease in cylinder temperature and the shorter duration of high-temperature conditions following the delayed injection timing. Consequently, the concentration of oxidizing free radicals such as O and OH also decreases, leading to lower NOx emissions. Additionally, NOx emissions increase with higher n-butanol blending ratios. The primary cause of NOx formation is localized high-temperature oxygen enrichment. A higher n-butanol blending ratio results in a longer ID, more uniform mixing of fuel and air within the cylinder, and a broader area of high temperatures generated during combustion27. At the same time, the longer ID and the oxygen atoms inherent in n-butanol reduce oxygen deficiency in certain areas caused by uneven fuel distribution. Under the combined effect of these two factors, the area where NOx is generated becomes more extensive, leading to increased emissions28.

As shown in the cross-sectional view, when the injection timing is delayed from 11°CA BTDC to 5°CA BTDC, the area where NOx is generated within the combustion chamber pit is significantly reduced.

Figure 22 shows the changes in soot mass for three different fuels at various injection timings. It can be seen that as the injection timing is delayed, the temperature decreases, which suppresses the formation of soot precursors and soot. However, at the same time, the residence time at high temperatures is shortened and the concentration of OH radicals decreases, reducing the proportion of soot precursors that are oxidized and consequently leading to increased soot formation. When the injection timing was delayed from 11°CA BTDC to 3°CA BTDC, soot emissions for the three fuels increased by 45.1%, 38.3%, and 35.2%, respectively. Furthermore, increasing the n-butanol blending ratio significantly reduces soot emissions, a result of the combined effects of reduced soot formation and enhanced oxidation. On the one hand, the low cetane number and high latent heat of vaporization of n-butanol extend the ignition delay and lower the combustion temperature, thereby suppressing early-stage soot formation; on the other hand, its inherent oxygen content improves the combustion process and increases the oxidation rate of already formed soot. The above analysis demonstrates that burning a n-butanol/diesel blend in combination with delayed fuel injection can reduce NOx emissions without a significant increase in soot, thereby improving the “trade-off” between NOx and soot.

Conclusions

This paper primarily investigates the combustion and emission characteristics of a n-butanol-diesel blended fuel under coordinated control of boundary conditions. It also analyzes the differences in operational characteristics between the blended fuel and pure diesel (B0), as well as the potential of different boundary conditions to improve combustion and reduce emissions. The main conclusions are as follows:

Increasing injection pressure raises the premixed combustion ratio and improves indicated thermal efficiency. However, since B30 combustion is primarily premixed, pure diesel (B0) is more sensitive to injection pressure in terms of combustion characteristics. At the same time, increasing injection pressure reduces soot emissions but increases high-temperature zones within the cylinder, leading to higher NOx levels; compared to pure diesel, the increase in NOx for B30 is relatively lower.Delaying fuel injection increases the ignition delay period and reduces indicated thermal efficiency.
