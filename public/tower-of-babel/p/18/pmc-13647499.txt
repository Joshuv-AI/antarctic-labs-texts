Building social and structural connections for the prevention of opioid use among youth experiencing homelessness: study protocol for a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Jodi Ford, Natasha Slesnick, Christopher Browning, Xin Feng, Tansel Yilmazer, Bethany Boettner, Jacsen Luthy, Margaret Fitzpatrick, Lia Pinkus, Eugene Holowacz, Celina Hang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundYouth experiencing homelessness (YEH) are exposed to significant adversities while living on the street, and many experienced child abuse and neglect prior to leaving the home setting. Multiple social determinants of health (SDoH) are uniquely associated with youth homelessness, driving substance use and adverse mental health consequences. However, limited research has identified pragmatic interventions that have a long-term ameliorating impact on the complex, multi-symptomatic issues that YEH face. This study overcomes prior gaps in research through the testing of a multi-component prevention intervention targeting SDoH and their effects on biopsychosocial health indicators and longer-term health and social outcomes. In partnership with a local drop-in center for YEH, youth between the ages of 14 to 24 years are engaged and randomly assigned to conditions using a dismantling design so that essential intervention components can be efficiently identified.MethodsYEH (N = 300) are recruited through the drop-in center and randomly assigned to one of the following interventions: a) Motivational Interviewing/Community Reinforcement Approach + Services as Usual (MI/CRA + SAU, n = 80), b) Strengths-Based Outreach and Advocacy + SAU (SBOA + SAU, n = 80), c) MI/CRA + SBOA + SAU (n = 80) or d) SAU (n = 60). Interventions are delivered over six months by the drop-in center’s therapists and youth advocates to enhance sustainability. Primary outcomes include opioid use, other substance use, and mental health symptomatology. Secondary outcomes focus on education, employment, and housing stability; access to health care and met health needs; diagnostic status, and drug overdose. Biopsychosocial pathways underlying change, potential moderation of intervention response, and a cost effectiveness analysis will be examined. Youth are assessed at baseline and at 3-, 6-, 12-, 18- and 24-months post-baseline to examine longer-term prevention effects on the outcomes.DiscussionPrevention efforts addressing SDoH and embedded within systems that serve youth, such as drop-in centers, may be the most promising strategy to prevent opioid use and other substance use disorders as well as chronic homelessness. The study addresses a significant public health concern, and if our hypotheses are supported, the intervention may be readily transported to communities around the country.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov, TRN: NCT06311838, Registration date: 26 February 2024.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28177-4.

Background

Opioid use disorder and overdose remain at crisis levels in the US with unprecedented rates observed as early as adolescence [1]. Among young people, youth experiencing homelessness (YEH) carry a disproportionate burden in the opioid epidemic with rates of heroin use reported to be more than 13 times higher for YEH compared to their peers who are housed [2] Homelessness among youth is not rare as recent estimates indicate that 1 in 30 adolescents (ages 13–17) and 1 in 10 young adults [ages 18–25] in the US experience homelessness each year [3, 4]. Homelessness is associated with poor health outcomes including exacerbation or onset of mental and physical health problems, substance use, and premature mortality often due to drug overdose and/or suicide. [3] Multiple social determinants of health (SDoH) underlie the negative health outcomes among YEH [5], including a lack of housing, income, education, and access to services as well as high levels of violence exposure, discrimination/stigma, and social isolation [6]. However, despite the critical need, few studies have examined opioid use among YEH and even fewer have tested prevention interventions with this population. Engaging and intervening with YEH and addressing the SDoH associated with homelessness is essential to prevent the staggering individual and societal costs associated with substance use, death by suicide, drug overdose, and other health consequences.

In partnership with a community drop-in center serving YEH, the current randomized controlled trial (RCT) tests the unique and additive benefits of two evidenced-based interventions over a six-month period in a sample of 300 YEH aged 14 to 24 years: (1) a substance use/mental health intervention – Motivational Interviewing (MI) and the Community Reinforcement Approach (CRA) and (2) an outreach and advocacy intervention focused on linkage to community and social supports – Strengths-Based Outreach and Advocacy (SBOA). Primary outcomes include opioid use, other substance use, and mental health symptomatology. Secondary outcomes focus on education, employment, and housing stability; access to health care and met health needs; diagnostic status, and drug overdose. The two interventions address the SDoH underlying substance use and mental health risks among YEH in distinct ways. Specifically, MI/CRA targets individual change through skills-building and motivation for change, while SBOA addresses the social context in which the youth contend through community linkages and social connection with an advocate. The rigorous examination of the unique, as well as additive, benefits of these interventions on youth outcomes will provide insight on those intervention components that are essential to fully address the complicated and multiple SDoH associated with substance use and mental health among YEH.

Furthermore, to understand how, why and for whom change occurs, mediation by social stability (structural and social connections) and biopsychosocial factors (psychosocial resources: self-efficacy, coping, motivation, and resilience and stress: perceived and biological stress via cortisol and inflammation), and moderation by sociodemographic subgroups will be examined. Short and longer-term prevention effects on primary and secondary outcomes are assessed via interviews and surveys at baseline and at 3-, 6-, 12-, 18- and 24-months post-baseline. To enhance sustainability, the intervention is embedded in and delivered by the staff of the youth drop-in center, and a cost effectiveness analysis will be completed. In the present protocol description, we provide the background and rationale for the current study, an overview of our conceptual underpinnings, and a description of the study design, interventions, assessments and measures, and data analytic plan.

Need for interventions and prevention

Substance use and mental health of YEH

YEH report frequent use of opioids and other substances that often leads to devastating outcomes [7]. In prior research with service disconnected YEH [8], Slesnick et al. found 40.5% of youth reported using hard drugs (excluding marijuana) in the 30 days prior to baseline survey, and of these, nearly 27% had used heroin and 24% used other forms of opiates. Marijuana use was also high with a mean of 19 days of use in the past 30 days [9]. YEH have more mental health diagnoses, suicidal behaviors, and hospitalizations for suicide attempt than youth who have not spent time on the streets [10, 12]. Gewirtz O’Brien et al. found YEH, youth who had runaway, and youth who were both experiencing homelessness and had runaway had 2.4, 4.9, and 7.1 increased odds of a suicide attempt in the prior year, respectively, compared to youth who were stably housed [12]. Rates of mental disorders are three times higher among YEH compared to housed youth with 66% to 89% of YEH having a mental health disorder, especially depression, anxiety, and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) [13, 14]. Overdose is a significant concern. In the US, overdose deaths increased 109% from 2019–2021 among all youth 10–18 years; 90% of deaths involved opioids, and 83.9% involved illegally manufactured fentanyl [15]. Mortality rates among YEH (15–24 years) were found in one study to be 10 times higher compared to same-age youth in the general population with alcohol and drug misuse and suicide as the leading causes of death [10]. These studies underscore the significant risk of opioid misuse among YEH and highlight the importance of identifying prevention interventions to curb the epidemic among this high-risk group of youth.

Social determinants of health and youth homelessness

Social determinants of health are the social and environmental conditions, such as economic stability, education and health care access and quality, neighborhood and built environment, and social context, which shape overall health and well-being across the lifespan [16]. Gaetz et al. [5] argue that adopting a SDoH perspective for youth homelessness is critical and supporting YEH means not only access to housing but also addressing the challenges that undermine housing stability and well-being, including a lack of safe affordable housing; inadequate education, employment and income; violence exposures; social isolation and feelings of loneliness [17, 18]. Below we discuss briefly the SDoH associated with youth homelessness and relationships to youth outcomes.

Educational attainment

While unaccompanied YEH are eligible for kindergarten through grade 12 public education (upper age limit varies by state), they have lower educational attainment and achievement than stably housed youth [3], are at greater risk of dropping out of school, and they frequently face more challenges accessing adequate support systems and supplies that aid in their academic success [19, 20]. In one study of YEH 18–24 years, 33% had dropped out of high school or were currently suspended [21]. YEH are also less likely to attend college, despite a desire to do so, and when they do, they often have difficulties meeting academic responsibilities due to the many challenges they face. Support for these youth is critical for successful academic engagement and attainment [19, 20].

Economic and employment stability

A lack of income or the loss of steady employment is a central cause of homelessness and also provides challenges to meeting basic daily needs [4]. Thus, obtaining stable employment is often an important goal for YEH to improve their quality of life [22]. Slesnick et al. found employment increased housing stability and improved mental health among YEH along with a reduction of income obtained from high-risk survival behaviors (e.g. panhandling, selling blood, dealing drugs, sex work, theft, etc.) [23].

Social integration and support

Unaccompanied YEH often have a history of child abuse and neglect, disruptive family relationships, and/or familial conflict [24]. While seeking close supportive relationships is adaptive among housed youth, YEH often develop avoidant attachment styles, which can make it difficult to build and maintain supportive relationships [25]. Moreover, behaviors of youth’s social networks may present challenges as Slesnick et al. found that among YEH who were at high risk for suicide, family and friend social support was only protective if they were not engaged in substance use or criminal behaviors, respectively [26]. YEH frequently avoid supportive services that may be available to them, citing predation by older adults experiencing homelessness, lack of insurance, transportation barriers, limited knowledge of how to access services, and fear of betrayal or return to foster care or home settings [27].

Neighborhood and community context

In research with housed youth, residential neighborhood poverty, violence/crime, and social/physical disorder are associated with substance use, impaired mental health, and biological stress [28, 34]. Few studies have examined effects of community exposures on the outcomes of YEH, and due to the transient nature of homelessness, an activity space approach may be more effective at capturing their exposures than traditional neighborhood methods (e.g. residential census tract). Browning and colleagues [35, 38] have advanced activity space research with adolescents, an approach that measures all locations individuals encounter in their routine activities. Few have used this approach with persons experiencing homelessness [39], but in one small study of YEH (N = 28), the more activity locations that youth reported, the greater their psychological well-being [40]. However, for many YEH, harassment and discrimination in both public and private spaces [41, 46] is common and associated with higher levels of substance use, self-harming behaviors, and suicidality [46, 48]. Exposure to violence is also highly prevalent while living on the streets, and in one study, 52% of YEH reported being physically assaulted, 25% robbed, 21% sexually assaulted and 28% poly-victimized [49] while on the streets. Others have reported up to 83% of YEH in their sample were physically and/or sexually victimized [50]. Street victimization increases the likelihood of PTSD, depression and substance use disorder [49, 51, 53], and one study found YEH who had experienced primarily threats or witnessed violence with low levels of victimization were just as likely to have depression and PTSD as youth who reported both high levels of victimization and witnessed violence [51].

Health care access and met health needs

Although health care is not considered a SDoH, access to and utilization of treatment for substance use and mental health is critical for improving outcomes for YEH. Frequent emergency department (ED) visits are often a marker of unmet heath needs, and compared to the general U.S. population, the rates of hospitalizations and ED visits are disproportionally higher among individuals experiencing homelessness that are often triggered by a crisis event [54, 56]. YEH are more likely to receive care in the ED, and have frequent ED visits (four or more) compared to stably housed youth, particularly youth with mental health and substance use disorders leading to increased health care costs [57]. Individuals experiencing homelessness often have complex health care needs and difficulties accessing and maintaining needed healthcare services due to costs and a lack of insurance and transportation [56, 58] as well as concerns over distrust and fear of provider stigma [41, 42, 45].

Evidence on substance use and mental health interventions with YEH

A recent review identified 53 randomized intervention studies for YEH, and found many were limited by small sample sizes, short follow-ups, and low rigor [59]. Twenty-three of the studies tested various mental health or substance use/health risk interventions, but few included long-term follow-up. Motivational interviewing interventions showed short-term positive effects [60, 62], and a behavioral substance use/mental health intervention, the Community Reinforcement Approach showed positive effects [62, 63]. Similar conclusions were drawn from a review by Wang et al. [64] identifying cognitive and behavioral therapy as a promising intervention to address both depression and substance use in YEH. Morton et al. [59] identified two RCT studies that tested case management interventions that showed generally positive effects on housing, mental health, education and employment [65, 66], but two less intensive/minimal case management interventions failed to demonstrate positive effects [67, 68]. Finally, Slesnick et al. engaged service disconnected YEH using outreach and supportive advocacy, which showed positive effects on substance use, mental health and housing outcomes [9, 69]. Morton et al. [59] and Wang et al. [64] conclude that while substantial growth has occurred in the evaluative evidence base for a range of outcomes for YEH, the literature still lacks conclusive evaluative evidence to support many of the current program models. They further conclude that evaluative evidence is lacking on how interventions vary by subpopulations disproportionately impacted by homelessness and evidence on long-term outcomes is lacking.

Conceptual framework

Knowledge of how and why effective interventions work clarifies the connection between the intervention activities and the outcomes – even across multiple interventions [70]. We seek to optimize the generality of the intervention by understanding what is needed to make the intervention work, and what components must not be diluted to achieve change [70]. Bronfenbrenner [71] suggests that there are four types of social interactions – those that occur within a specific setting (micro-system interactions), those that occur between settings (meso-system interactions), those that occur between local institutions and local settings (exo-system interactions), and those that occur between the society at large and local settings (macro-system interactions). Using the Theory of Social Ecology [71] as a guide, this study attempts to change the relationship between YEH and their micro- and meso-systems. As social systems are inter-related, positive change in one system is expected to impact other social systems in the individual’s life.

Our conceptual model (see Fig. 1) illustrates our hypothesized mechanisms of change, specifically, the intervention will increase the youth’s structural and social connections to supportive resources and people, which in turn enhances youth’s own psychosocial resources and reduces their biological stress, and ultimately leads to improved mental and behavioral health outcomes. Our hypothesized mechanisms of change are supported by prior research evidence, which found service linkage and social support can mitigate the increased risks associated with street life, including direct and indirect (e.g., witness violence exposures) [9, 72]. YEH with access to services and social support report less illicit drug use and depressive symptoms [9, 72, 73]. To date, biological stress in YEH is understudied despite the high rates of adversity they face and our findings will elucidate potential new pathways for intervention.Fig. 1Conceptual model

Study design and methods

Study aims and design

The study is designed as a randomized controlled, parallel group, four-arm, superiority trial (see Fig. 2). The primary aim is to examine the singular and combined effect of two evidence-based interventions on substance use, mental health, and SDoH-related outcomes (e.g., housing, education, employment stability, health access and met health needs) among YEH. Youth experiencing homelessness are recruited from a local drop-in center (N = 300) and randomly assigned to one of four conditions, each delivered over 6 months. All youth regardless of condition will continue to receive services as usual (SAU) as provided by the drop-in center with the type and number of services received recorded. The intervention includes:Motivational Interviewing and Community Reinforcement Approach (MI/CRA) + SAU (n = 80); Strengths-Based Outreach and Advocacy (SBOA) + SAU (n = 80); (3) SBOA + MI/CRA + SAU (n = 80); and (4) SAU alone (n = 60). We hypothesize that youth assigned to both SBOA and MI/CRA (+ SAU) will show better short and long-term outcomes on opioid use and prevention, other substance use, mental health, and stability of housing, education, employment and health care access at 3-, 6-, 12-, 18- and 24-months than youth assigned to either intervention alone or to only SAU. We use an urn randomization program to balance study conditions by moderating factors (e.g., age, sex race/ethnicity, and experience of childhood abuse and neglect). Upon completion of the baseline assessment, the research staff contacts the principal investigator with randomization information, and the principal investigator randomizes the client using the computerized urn randomization program. The randomization to intervention is not blinded. An intent-to-treat design will be followed so that all youth, regardless of participation in the interventions, will be tracked.Fig. 2Study design

Study design

A secondary aim of the study is to examine whether intended mediators of change (structural and social connections, psychosocial resources, perceived and biological stress) produce the desired change on substance use, mental health, and SDoH-related outcomes. As noted, we hypothesize the interventions will increase youths’ social stability (enhanced structural and social connections and decreased violence exposures) that in turn, will lead to an increase in youths’ psychosocial resources (e.g. self-regulation, self-efficacy) and reductions in psychological and biological stress and ultimately, improve targeted outcomes. Our study will also explore how the moderators of age, sex, race/ethnicity, and experience of childhood abuse and neglect influence intervention response and conduct an evaluation of the cost effectiveness of the intervention approaches.

Participants, setting, and recruitment and retention protocol

Youth are eligible to participate in the study if they meet the following criteria: (1) aged 14–24 years; (2) meets the criteria for homelessness as defined by the McKinney-Vento Act: “children and youth who lack a fixed, regular, and adequate nighttime residence”; or “live in a welfare hotel, or place without regular sleeping accommodations”, or “live in a shared residence with other persons due to the loss of one’s housing or economic hardship”, and (3) unaccompanied by a parent or caregiver. Although the study is focused on prevention of opioid use disorder, we chose not to limit engagement by frequency of substance use, diagnosis, or type of substance used given the high rates of substance use and polydrug use among YEH. Enhancing generalizability of study findings to all YEH more broadly fits with the goals of the study to prevent initiation and progression of substance use among high-risk groups.

Youth are recruited primarily from a local drop-in center located in a large Midwestern city that serves YEH ages 14 to 24 years of age, and other local settings if needed, such as sandwich lines, homeless camps, and libraries. Research staff approach youth at the drop-in center (or other locations as outlined) to assess interest. Youth interested in participating are interviewed in a private location by research staff to verify eligibility, and if confirmed, the research staff obtains informed consent for youth 18 years and older and informed assent for youth 14 to 17 years. Youth who are ineligible for participation are referred to the drop-in center’s basic services (SAU), which provides food, showers, washer/dryers, case management, and access to other programs, such as health care.

To enhance retention into the RCT, research staff collect extensive locator information at the baseline assessment in which youth designate usual hangout-spots, phone and social media contact information, as well as collaterals such as friends or family members who may know where the youth would be if research staff lost touch with them. At each follow-up assessment, the research staff review and update the youth’s contact and collateral information. The study team has been very successful in maintaining high levels of contact with youth in prior research using multiple means including phones, email, social media, and outreach [74, 75].

Description and rationale of study interventions

The Motivational Interviewing (MI)/Community Reinforcement Approach (CRA)

Current evidence recommends integrating interventions targeting substance use and mental health disorders, especially combining MI with behavioral interventions such as CRA [76]. Enhancing intrinsic motivation for behavioral change is the central purpose of MI, a clinical method built on the insights and strategies described by Carl Rogers as client-centered therapy. However, MI is also directive in selectively eliciting and reinforcing client “change talk” [77]. Typically offered as a brief intervention of one or two sessions, MI has a strong record of efficacy in the treatment of alcohol and other drug use disorders as well as mental health disorders (e.g., Frost et al. [78]).

The Community Reinforcement Approach (CRA) offers an empirically-based multifaceted approach to substance abuse/mental health treatment that also addresses many of the clinical needs of individuals experiencing homelessness [62, 79, 80]. Adolescent CRA is identified as an evidence-based practice by the Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration’s National Registry of Evidence Based Practices and Programs [81]. CRA is also identified as an evidence-based approach by the National Institute of Drug Abuse [82]. The efficacy of CRA was first demonstrated nearly 40 years ago with a small sample of inpatient adults with alcohol use disorder [83] and 20 years later in an RCT for adult men experiencing homelessness [79]. In a recent review of behavioral therapies for adolescents with opioid use disorder, only three studies were identified which tested CRA, MI and cognitive behavioral therapy [84]. These studies used non-randomized designs but indicated initial efficacy in reducing opioid use and related problems including mental health. Adolescent CRA alone was shown to improve substance use, mental health and social outcomes in four randomized trials, and was deemed a stand-alone well-established substance use treatment intervention for youth [85, 86] while MI provides mixed evidence as a stand-alone approach for long-term effects, remaining probably efficacious.

For the current RCT, MI is conducted using an adapted Project Match manual designed for YEH and youth who have runaway that has been utilized in prior trials [63, 74]. The first session begins with a period of open-ended MI to establish therapeutic rapport and elicit client change talk. In session two, the therapist continues to focus on enhancing intrinsic motivation for change, developing discrepancy, transitioning as appropriate into the negotiation of a change plan and evoking commitment to the plan. The CRA intervention occurs after the MI sessions, and although the 12 CRA sessions are flexibly determined, there are set procedures for activities that must occur in any given session as it depends upon the youth’s goals, needs and resources. In session one, the therapist assesses the youth’s history and background, motivation for change, and conducts the CRA Functional Analysis, which examines internal and external triggers of substance using behaviors, short-term positive consequences of use, and long-term negative consequences of use [87]. In this session, the therapist focuses on establishing rapport and motivating the youth that treatment has begun and reinforcing that they can improve their life situation. In session two, the therapist and youth collaboratively develop a tentative treatment plan to target areas for change to reduce the probability of substance use and other maladaptive behavior. Areas of change include substance use, social relations, psychiatric issues (depression, anxiety), legal problems, and recreational activities which can interfere with health behaviors and stability. In sessions 3–12, the therapists follow CRA treatment strategies using both a standard set of core procedures and a menu of optional treatment modules matched to clients’ needs [87]. The core session topics include social skills training including communication and problem-solving skills, social and recreational counseling, decision-making, refusal skills training, and relapse prevention. Optional treatment modules include, but are not limited to, self-talk and self-guidance, anger management and affect regulation, relaxation and stress management, couple counseling, assertiveness training, and self-management planning. Role-play and manageable homework assignments are incorporated into the sessions to generalize and practice newly learned skills. The MI/CRA intervention consists of two MI sessions (~ one hour each) and 12 CRA sessions (~ one hour each) over six months.

Strengths-Based Outreach and Advocacy (SBOA)

Some research suggests that engagement with an advocate is key to success when linking individuals experiencing homelessness to available services and support in the community. Goering et al. [88] found that among adults experiencing homelessness, those who formed a stronger alliance with their outreach worker/advocate showed faster mental health improvement and had fewer symptoms and better social functioning at 18 months. The strengths-based model, developed at the University of Kansas School of Social Welfare, is based on the premise that the purpose of advocacy “is to assist consumers in identifying, securing, and preserving the range of resources, both external and internal, needed to live in a normal, independent way in the community” (p. 331) [89]. Strengths-based interventions focus on enhancing well-being and happiness rather than attempting to correct deficits or pathology. A meta-analysis of 45 strengths-based intervention studies reported a medium effect on improving well-being among participants [90]. In order to address social connections, SBOA has built a strong evidence base for vulnerable youth [9, 75, 91], including findings from our prior research showing increased engagement in services is associated with reduced drug use and mental health problems, greater self-efficacy, and improved social stability [8, 9, 69].

For the current RCT, the advocate secures needed services for the youth and supports them as they traverse the system of care. The focus of the first several weeks of advocacy is on obtaining identification and ensuring basic needs are met (food, safety, medical care, housing, etc.) As basic needs are addressed, youth and advocates focus on other high need areas including education, employment, mental health and substance use. Advocates provide referrals and/or transport the youth to appointments as needed. Advocates will continue to work and engage with the youth for six months regardless of the level or type of service connection. If the youth is not interested in being linked to a particular service or program, the advocate will continue to engage and meet with them and address other needs they may have. The number of SBOA intervention sessions over the six months is flexible depending on the youth’s need.

Training, supervision and treatment adherence

The intervention is embedded in and delivered by staff at the youth drop-in center. MI/CRA therapists are master’s level counselors/social workers and the advocates providing SBOA are paraprofessionals with a minimum high school diploma. Training for MI/CRA and SBOA consists of readings (CRA book [87], MI book [77] and SBOA) and at least a two-day training in each of the respective interventions, including role-play exercises and ongoing supervision. All interventions are digitally recorded and monitored by research staff for fidelity to the intervention. MI, CRA and SBOA fidelity measures have been used successfully in prior trials [8, 63, 74]. A random sample of 20% of sessions will be coded to ensure that the procedures are delivered as intended. Percent performance of prescribed behaviors and undesirable behaviors will be used as measures of adherence.

Assessments and measures

Baseline assessments are conducted with all youth and follow-up assessments occur at 3-, 6-, 12-, 18- and 24-months (see Fig. 3). The 3-month assessment examines mediators, the 6-month assessment allows examination of the immediate benefits of the intervention, and the 12-, 18- and 24-month assessment will track longer-term prevention effects. All measures are collected at every assessment timepoint unless otherwise noted. Assessments consist of interviewer-administered and self-administered surveys along with a biomarker data collection component, consisting of venipuncture and hair collection to assess biological stress. To ensure data quality, research staff receive intensive in-person training, practice sessions, and observation/supervision in the field. All assessments also receive quality assurance checks.

Primary outcomes

The primary outcomes for this study include opioid use and substance use measured as quantity and frequency of use via the interviewer-administered Form 90 Substance Use interview developed for the National Institute of Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism funded Project Match [92], which has shown excellent test–retest reliability for indices of drug use in major categories [93]. The Form 90 differentiates illicit drug use from prescribed drug use including marijuana. As further validation of self-reported drug use, urine toxicology screens are collected onsite at baseline, 12-months and 24-months using the one-step BMC ToxCup® Test Kit (Branan Medical Corp., Irvine, CA), which provides instant readings for the detection of cannabinoids, amphetamines, methamphetamines, phencyclidine, cocaine/crack, and opiates. The psychometric properties have been rigorously examined [94]. Problem consequences of substance use is measured using the Shortened Inventory of Problems – Alcohol and Drugs (SIP-AD) [95]. The SIP-AD measures consequences related to impulse control and social responsibility, as well as physical, interpersonal, and intrapersonal domains with good psychometric properties.

Mental health is also a primary outcome measured using several assessment tools to capture depressive and anxiety symptoms and overall mental health. The Beck Depression Inventory II (BDI-II) [96] is the most frequently used self-report instrument to assess mood, cognitive and somatic aspects of depression. The BDI-II has good psychometric properties [97, 98]. The Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI) [99] is used to assess current anxiety symptoms via 21 items, rated in intensity with scores ranging from 0–63. It was developed to discriminate symptoms of anxiety from depressive symptoms and has alpha coefficients ranging from 0.90—0.94. The Short Form-12 is a standardized, internationally used instrument that provides a general measure of health status [100]. The 12 items on the SF-12 are summarized in two weighted summary scales and generate a mental health and physical health score. Lower scores indicate poorer health [100] Construct validity has been evaluated with adult users of a homeless day shelter [101].

Secondary outcomes

Stability of SDoH, including education, employment, and housing, access to health care and met health care needs are assessed via Slesnick’s Homelessness Experiences Questionnaire (HEQ), which queries youth about their demographic and homelessness background, number of days youth attended school, were employed, were housed, received social and health services, and had met health care needs in the prior three months or since the last interview.

Because we intend to prevent escalation of substance use and mental health symptoms, we also query youth on experiences of drug overdose and diagnostic status. Drug overdose is measured using a series of items from the Repeated-dose Behavioral Intervention to Reduce Opioid Overdose Trial to assess overdose experiences, including number, type of drug, and treatment [102]. Diagnostic status is assessed at baseline, 12-months and 24-months via the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-5 Disorders (SCID), Short Form, which is a semi-structured diagnostic interview that is used to make DSM-5 diagnoses, including opioid and other substance use disorders [103]. The SCID has shown significantly greater validity compared to standard clinical interviews, and the substance use disorder section has shown good reliability, with kappas ranging from 0.65 to 1.0 [103]. Similarly, we will assess diagnostic status of anxiety and mood disorders (e.g., major depression, posttraumatic stress disorder).

Mediators

Structural and social connections

Structural connections consist of youth’s connections to SDoH and health care services which are assessed using a services contact log that queries youth on their frequency of contact with different service areas including: substance abuse treatment, housing, employment training, educational training, health insurance, medical care, dental care, mental health care, family services, legal issues, and identification, including birth certificate and social security card. Youth rate the perceived need for and helpfulness of each service. Structural connections also include youth’s community characteristics and exposures that they routinely encounter, also known as their activity space. The interviewer asks youth to list the locations of their routine activities (e.g., sleep, work, school) and hangouts that they go to during a typical week, including weekends. The interviewer enters the named location into an online mapping platform and confirms the location with the youth. Interviewers record each location and its longitude and latitude coordinates. Then for each named location, the interviewer queries about how often and when the youth usually spends time in the location, activities they usually engage in, presence of other people in the location and homelessness status (if known), perceptions of safety and social support, violence exposures, and their engagement in and witnessing of substance use and violent behaviors. Each of the individual-level activity space measures corresponds to the average rate of exposure across the youth’s activity space locations. The following measures have been used in prior research by the team assessing activity space of youth and found to be reliable and valid measures [104, 106]. Perceived safety is measured using one item asking youth how safe they feel in each named location (very safe, somewhat safe, not safe at all) and two additional items querying youth as to which named location do they feel the most safe and which location do they feel the least safe. Perceived social support will be measured as the mean of four items: “There is usually someone around… “that I can rely on”; “who cares about my wellbeing”, “who I can trust” and “who will help me if needed” with response options of strongly agree, agree, neither agree nor disagree, disagree or strongly disagree. The exposure to violence measure queries youth, “During the last 3 months, when you have been at [LOCATION], how often [never, once, twice, 3 ­ 5 times, or 6 or more times] did someone “disrespect you”; “verbally harass or insult you”; “threaten to physically hurt you”; “hit, slap, kick, or punch you”; “beat you up”; “attack you with a weapon”; and “sexually assault you”. Youth are also questioned on their own engagement in substance use and violent behaviors as well as that of others in each named location, “During the last 3 months, when you have been at [LOCATION], how often did you/did you see someone”…”get drunk”; “use marijuana”; “use other illegal drugs, such as heroin or cocaine”; “buy or sell illegal drugs”; “carry a gun”; “threaten to physically hurt someone else”; “hit, slap, kick, or punch someone else”; “attack someone else with a weapon”; and “sexually assault someone else” with response options of never, once, twice, 3 ­ 5 times, or 6 or more times. Objective characteristics of the census block group for each named location, such as proportion of vacant housing and violent crime rates, will also be measured using data from the American Community Survey and the Columbus Division of Police crime incident reports. These data include geocoded location and crime type (Uniform Crime Report Part 1 violent offenses – homicide, robbery, rape, and aggravated assault). Data on crime at locations that fall outside the Columbus Division of Police jurisdiction are available from the Ohio Division of Public Safety Office of Criminal Justice Services violent crime reports. The project team has used both data sources in prior research [36, 37].

Social connection measures include working alliance defined as the perceived connection and shared goals between the youth and therapists/advocates measured at 3- and 6-months using the Working Alliance Inventory [107]. The inventory yields three subscale scores (task agreement, goal agreement, and bond development) that have shown excellent psychometric properties [107, 108]. Additional social connection measures include social integration and social support using an adapted version of the Social Network Inventory (SNI) [109]. The SNI has been used in multiple studies with YEH by Slesnick et al. [26, 110] and others with high-risk adolescents [111] with test–retest reliabilities via intraclass correlation (ICC) of 0.74 to 0.82 for the key SNI variables [112]. Youth are asked to nominate up to ten network partners and a range of questions regarding each partner is asked, including type of relationship; ties among network partners [e.g. who knows who]; frequency, modes [in-person, phone/text, online] and stability of contact; perceptions of and types of social support received; perceived closeness; and substance use, homelessness status and criminal behaviors of network members. Social integration will be assessed using social network size and strength of ties (constructed from contact frequency and perceived closeness) and social support will include the construction of the mean perceived social support across all network members [26, 110]. Last we examine perceived social connectedness via the Loneliness Questionnaire-short version, which is a 9-item scale with high reliability via internal consistency (α = 0.92) and validity established via item response theory with a large diverse sample of youth [113].

Psychosocial resources: self-regulation, coping, resilience, self-efficacy and motivation

Self-regulation is measured via the Short Self-Regulation Scale [114, 115], a 31-item instrument of cognitive competence, highly correlated with the larger 63-item survey (α = 0.92) and a measure of generalized self-regulation capacity. Coping is assessed using the Coping Inventory for Stressful Situations-Short Form [116]; a 21-item measure that yields 3 subscale scores: task-, emotion-, and avoidance-oriented coping. Reliability for the three subscales is 0.90, 0.88, and 0.83, respectively [117]. Resilience is measured using the Brief Resilience Scale (BRS) [118], a Likert-type 5-item instrument measuring the ability to bounce back or recover from stress, and recommended for use in stressful contexts [119]. The BRS has shown good internal consistency (α = 0.80—0.91) [118]. Self-efficacy is assessed via two measures, including specific self-efficacy for alcohol and other drug use using the 8-item Drug-Taking Confidence Questionnaire-8 [120], developed based on Bandura’s self-efficacy theory and Marlatt and Gordon’s [121, 122] high-risk situations for relapse. The psychometric properties have been rigorously examined [94]. Personal control self-efficacy is assessed using Pearlin and Schooler’s 7-item Mastery Scale [123], which has robust psychometric properties [123] and proven validity with those experiencing homelessness [9, 124, 125]. Motivation for change is evaluated using the Stages of Change Readiness and Treatment Eagerness Scale (SOCRATES) [126]. The SOCRATES has evolved through eight revisions, with a clear factor structure reflecting three vectors: Readiness, Ambivalence, and Taking Steps [127, 128]. Test–retest correlations for subscales range from 0.83 to 0.99 (ICC = 0.82—0.94) and internal consistency from α = 0.87—0.96.

Perceived and biological stress

Perceived stress is measured using Cohen’s Perceived Stress Scale (PSS) [129], a widely used instrument for measuring an individual’s perception of their stress levels and reports both high validity and reliability [130]. The PSS consists of 10-items and higher scores indicate greater perceived stress. Biological stress is measured using two indicators, cortisol and inflammation. We examine hair cortisol concentration (HCC), a valid and reliable retrospective measure of cumulative cortisol over time [131, 132]. Each one centimeter (cm) of hair growth approximates the cumulative HCC for the corresponding month [133]. We focus on the first 1-to-3 cm of hair growth as cortisol is leached out at greater lengths [133]. We include youth with shorter hair to minimize selection bias. Trained interviewers cut 10–75 mg of hair from the posterior vertex of the scalp; hair is prepped and assayed using an adapted protocol [133, 135]. Samples are assayed in duplicate and inter- and intra-assay coefficients of variation will be calculated. Inflammation measures include systemic inflammation and pro-inflammatory cytokines collected via venipuncture by trained personnel. High-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) is an acute phase protein in the systemic inflammatory process and will be examined using clinical cut-points: normal (< 3 mg/L), low grade inflammation (3–10 mg/L), and acute inflammation (> 10 mg/L). Proinflammatory cytokines, INF-y, IL-1B, IL-6, TNF-a, are continuous measures and will be assessed via human ProInflammatory II multiplex tissue culture kits from MesoScale Discovery (Rockville, MD). HS-CRP and the cytokines were selected based on their associations with depression, traumatic exposures, and/or substance use [136, 141]. We will assay the hs-CRP and cytokines in duplicate and calculate the inter- and intra-assay coefficients of variation. We also collect hair and health-related measures that may affect the biomarker. Prior to analysis, we will examine effects of hair length, hair care practices (e.g., chemical use, washing, heated styling tools), and hair style (e.g., level of curl) on HCC and effects of season of collection, medications (e.g., steroids, non-steroidal anti-inflammatories, psychiatric medication, etc.), body mass index [height/weight objectively measured three times and mean calculated], and recent illnesses/infections to assess the need to include measures as covariates in analyses [142, 143].

Moderators

The measures for race and ethnicity, age, and biological sex at birth are consistent with the PhenX core SDoH measures and assessed using the Slesnick HEQ. History of child abuse (physical, sexual, emotional) and neglect are based on the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System [144], assessed in the Slesnick HEQ, and will be measured as total lifetime exposure.

Analytic strategy

Following the completion of each assessment, data will be screened and cleaned, and the psychometric properties and distributional characteristics of study variables will be examined. The missing data pattern and intervention effects will be examined before analysis. If data are missing at random, they will be estimated using full information maximum likelihood (FIML) or multiple imputations (MI) method. When data are missing completely at random (MCAR) or are missing at random (MAR), both FIML and MI produce unbiased results [145]. The main statistical program for this study can handle missing data by providing FIML estimation. To ensure that differences between the intervention providers are not affecting improvements in main variables of interest and engagement in treatment, analyses between interventionists will be conducted prior to the planned statistical tests. Using interventionist as the independent variable and number of completed sessions (MI/CRA) or meetings (SBOA) as the dependent variable, an analysis of variance will be conducted to examine differences.

Analyses will follow the four study aims. Latent growth models (LGM) and mediational models will be conducted and evaluated using generally accepted criteria including Chi-square, comparative fit index, root mean square error of approximation, and standardized root mean square residual model fit statistics. All analyses will be conducted first with the intent-to-treat sample and then with the treated sample, who attend 25% or more treatment sessions [74]. We expect similar results for the intent-to-treat and treated samples. Analyses will be conducted at the completion of the study; no interim analyses are planned.

Aim 1

The impact of the intervention conditions (MI/CRA, SBOA, MI/CRA + SBOA, and SAU) on the primary and secondary outcomes of the youth will be tested for Aim 1. Latent growth models will be fitted using the longitudinal data across six assessment points (baseline, 3-, 6-, 12-, 18- and 24-months follow-up) to estimate the trajectories of change over time. Differences among intervention conditions on estimated growth parameters, including intercepts (initial status) and slopes (the rates of change), will be tested. We will model linear and nonlinear (e.g., quadratic) changes in the outcomes. We hypothesize that the MI/CRA + SBOA group will have greater improvement in substance use and mental health during and after the treatment, compared to the MI/CRA, SBOA, and SAU groups alone.

Aim 2

To test whether intended change processes (e.g., structural and social connections; biopsychosocial mechanisms) associated with the intervention produce the desired change in youth outcomes, mediation analyses will be conducted within the LGM analyses. LGM is a flexible longitudinal approach that allows modeling of the growth in multiple repeatedly measured variables and the relationships among the latent growth factors. Specifically, longitudinal mediation analysis will be conducted to test whether the intervention affects the changes in the mediator variables, which in turn influences the changes in the outcome variables. A series of longitudinal mediation analyses will be conducted with the contrasts between the intervention conditions as the independent variables, the slopes of change in the proposed change mechanisms as the mediators, and the slopes of change in the primary/secondary outcomes as the dependent variables. Following MacKinnon and colleagues [146], the product of the path coefficient between the independent variable (treatment conditions) and the change in the mediator and the path coefficient between the change in mediator and the change in the outcome will be estimated as the indirect (mediation) effect between the intervention and outcome. The strength and significance of the mediation will be estimated using the bootstrap sampling method [147]. We will test the extent to which the proposed mechanisms of structural and social connections and the biopsychosocial processes mediate the effect of intervention on outcomes immediately following the intervention. Path analyses will be used to test these mediation effects, with the contrasts between intervention conditions as the independent variables, structural and social connections and the biopsychosocial mechanisms assessed at three- or six-months (i.e., during treatment) as the mediators, and primary/secondary outcomes assessed at 12-months or after (i.e., post-treatment) as the dependent variables. Consistent with the longitudinal analysis, we will estimate mediation using the bootstrap sampling method.

Aim3

The effect of the moderating variables (age, race/ethnicity, sex, history of child abuse and neglect) will be explored by examining the interactions between intervention type and each moderator in predicting outcome slopes and intercepts in the LGM models described under Aims 1 and 2. The contrasts between intervention types will be multiplied by the moderator variables to form the interaction terms. Next, the interaction terms will be included in the LGM models for Aims 1 and 2 for the primary and secondary outcomes, one at a time, and their strength of association with the outcome slope will be assessed while controlling for the main effects of the treatment and the moderator variables. A significant interaction suggests moderation.

Statistical power analysis for study aims 1–3

We conducted statistical power analyses separately for different types of analyses proposed to address the hypotheses, using the Monte Carlo simulation method [148] via Mplus. Given our prior studies typically achieved follow-up rates of 88–90% up to 18 months post-baseline, we assumed 15% overall attrition for the power calculation. Slesnick et al.’s previous clinical trial testing the effects of SBOA with YEH found a medium effect size (d = 0.47) in reducing the participants’ substance use favoring SBOA over SAU [9]. The team’s clinical trial testing the effects of CRA yielded small-to-medium effect sizes in reducing substance use (ds = 0.15—0.38) and depression (ds = 0.25—0.40), favoring CRA over SAU [62, 63].

For the latent growth analyses proposed in Aim 1, under the conditions of continuous outcome variables (e.g., substance use) and dichotomous predictors that have regression coefficients of small-to-medium effect size (0.15) for the slopes of growth factors [148], the proposed sample size (N = 300) can produce a power of 0.95 to detect intervention effects on the growth rate of outcome variables. For Aim 3, the power to detect moderating effects of youth race/ethnicity, sex, and age on the relationships between intervention and outcomes is 0.80, assuming medium effect sizes. For Aim 2, in the simulated longitudinal mediation analysis, we assumed a small-to-medium effect size for the intervention effects on the mediators. Data from our prior clinical trials showed associations between social/individual resources and outcomes were of medium-to-large effect size. Specifically, personal control was associated with depressive symptoms at r = −0.46 and with general mental health at r = 0.43. Whereas no published data are available on the effect of proposed intervention on biological mediators, recent meta-analyses reported small-to-medium sized associations (rs = 0.25—0.31) between trauma exposure and proinflammatory factors [136] and small sized associations (Hedge’s gs = 0.18—0.20) between proinflammatory markers and internalizing disorders [149]. In the simulated mediation analysis, we again assumed small-to-medium effect sizes for the path coefficients involved in the mediation analysis. Following the model specification suggested by Thoemmes et al. [150], for models with the latent growth factors as the mediator, the proposed sample size could provide a power of 0.92 to detect mediation effects; in the path model with structural/social connections and the biopsychosocial mechanisms as mediators, power to detect mediation during treatment was 0.96.

Aim 4: the economic cost of SBOA, MI/CRA and SBOA + MI/CRA

Yilmazer et al. created the instrument to collect and organize the cost of SBOA [151, 153]. The instrument utilizes the principles of activity-based costing, where relevant costs are decomposed into pre-defined activities, and the quantity and unit price of each resource used for each activity are measured. The instrument categorizes costs of intervention start-up and implementation separately. The SBOA intervention start-up comprises five mutually exclusive categories of activities that will be recorded weekly on project worksheets: (1) initial planning meetings (date, meeting length, attendees, meeting purpose), (2) training for SBOA (date, training length, preparation time, attendees), (3) development and/or revisions to policies and procedures to accommodate organizational workflow and processes (title of staff, time spent), (4) hiring activities (time spent, title of staff), and (5) management activities (time spent, title of staff). The drop-in center youth advocates received training to administer SBOA. Start-up costs of SBOA using the unit costs (e.g., salary) and the quantity of each resource (e.g., staff time) will be calculated. The SBOA intervention delivery comprises mutually exclusive and comprehensive activity categories. Two activities, including pre-contact activities (e.g., scheduling of intervention sessions) and direct intervention delivery (length, type, location), are directly linkable to participants and recorded electronically by the advocate following each engagement with the youth. Three activities including evaluation, ongoing staff training, and program management are recorded weekly on pre-created project worksheets. These three activities are not linkable to participant YEH, but the cost of these activities will be calculated by multiplying unit cost (e.g., salary) and quantity of each resource (e.g., time). Data on the unit costs of resources including national and local level wages of staff have been collected. The cost of SBOA intervention delivery will be calculated at the level of YEH for SBOA (n = 80) and one component of SBOA + MI/CRA (n = 80) groups separately by summing the per-participant cost of each of the five implementation activities.

The instrument to collect the cost of MI/CRA intervention is identical to the instrument created for SBOA. Costs of start-up and implementation of the MI/CRA intervention are categorized separately. The MI/CRA intervention start-up comprises five mutually exclusive categories of activities (same as SBOA intervention) that are recorded weekly on project worksheets. The drop-in center therapists received training on the MI/CRA intervention. The MI/CRA intervention delivery comprises mutually exclusive and comprehensive activity categories. Pre-contact and direct intervention activities will be recorded electronically by the therapist following each engagement with YEH. Supervision, ongoing staff training, and program management are recorded on pre-created project worksheets on a weekly basis. The cost of MI/CRA intervention delivery will be calculated at the level of YEH for MI/CRA (n = 80) and one component of the SBOA + MI/CRA (n = 80) group.

Cost Effectiveness Analysis (CEA)

The cost of SBOA + MI/CRA intervention delivery supports a full CEA. Incremental cost effectiveness ratios (ICER) will be calculated by dividing the per-participant cost of implementation of SBOA + MI/CRA minus per-participant cost of either intervention alone by the difference in primary outcomes among YEH in the SBOA + MI/CRA group and either intervention (or SAU) [154]. Since services received in SAU are not controlled, the cost of SAU does not enter into CEA analysis. However, clients in SBOA + MI/CRA, as well as clients in SBOA and MI/CRA conditions, can receive SAU at the drop-in center, therefore the cost of SAU cancels out. To reflect sampling variability in CEA, nonparametric bootstrapping and parametric methods will be used to characterize joint parameter uncertainty around the ICER estimates.

Ethics and human subjects review

The study protocol has been reviewed and approved by the Ohio State University’s Institutional Review Board (OSU IRB) as a minimal risk study; thus no data safety and monitoring board was established. The research team meets bi-weekly to review compliance with the study protocol and discuss trial progress. Modifications to the protocol are submitted as an amendment to the OSU IRB for approval and any deviations from the approved study protocol will be reported to the OSU IRB.

Maintaining participant confidentiality is of utmost importance. The project’s electronic data are stored on fire-wall and password protected servers. Hard copies, such as consent forms and data collection/surveys, are stored in separately keyed rooms, with separately keyed file cabinets. Biomarker samples are stored in secure, locked laboratories at OSU that are accessible only to approved personnel. The physical specimens and assay results include only numerical identifier to link to the survey data. No names will be used on the data and efforts to identify participants through deductive disclosure are prohibited. In addition, project staff are trained in procedures to maintain strict confidentiality, which includes a prohibition against removing records and specimens from the project area, and a prohibition to divulge any information or revealing the identity of participants without written consent. Any breach of confidentiality will be reported to the OSU IRB within 48 h.

Serious adverse events that are unanticipated and may be related to study procedures will be reported to the OSU IRB and sponsor (NIDA) within 48 h of learning of the event. Adverse events that are unexpected and potentially related to the study, but not meeting the definition of a serious adverse event will be reported to the IRB within 10 days. The OSU IRB reviews the adverse event report and determines if the event is a result of study procedures. If the event is considered a direct result of study procedures, the MPIs will meet within 48 h to discuss whether protocol modifications are needed. The trial will be stopped if any intervention is found to cause harm to participants.

Discussion

Multiple SDoH factors underlie the staggering negative health outcomes among youth experiencing homelessness, including a lack of stable safe housing, income, education, and health care access, as well as social isolation and exposure to violence. Prevention efforts addressing these factors that are embedded within systems that serve youth, such as drop-in centers, may be the most promising strategy to prevent opioid use and other substance use disorders as well as homelessness in adulthood. As such, this RCT addresses a significant public health concern, and if our hypotheses are supported, the intervention may be readily transported to communities around the country.
