Bioactive recombinant human elastin gel for reconstructing elastic fibers and rejuvenating endogenously aged skin
Authors: Yi Yang, Wenjie Huang, Jingjing Shi, Bei Tian, Caihong Fu, Jianxi Xiao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractEndogenous skin aging results in oxidative stress and degradation of elastic fibers, leading to reduced skin elasticity and dermal laxity. Current treatments rarely target elastic fiber regeneration. Here, we introduce a bioactive gel containing recombinant humanized elastin (RHE) that effectively restores skin elasticity and promotes extracellular matrix remodeling. RHE gel demonstrated superior efficacy in reversing UV-induced damage and oxidative stress in zebrafish models, and significantly improved skin function in aged mice, outperforming commercial vitamin E and recombinant humanized collagen (RHC) gels. Molecular analyses revealed enhanced expression of key elastic fiber and collagen components alongside reduced matrix-degrading enzymes. This study highlights RHE gel's potential as a novel therapeutic for regenerative dermatology and skin aging.Keywords: Recombinant human elastin, Skin aging, Elastic fibers, Antioxidant therapy, ECM regeneration

Introduction

Skin aging is driven by intrinsic and extrinsic factors [1]. Intrinsic aging leads to epidermal thinning, increased TEWL, reduced elasticity, and wrinkles [2]. At the molecular level, cellular senescence, mitochondrial dysfunction, and oxidative stress disrupt the extracellular matrix (ECM), especially collagen and elastin [3,4]. Among ECM components, degradation of elastic fibers critically impairs skin resilience [5]. Elastin, essential for tissue elasticity and cellular regulation, is scarcely synthesized after early development [[6], [7], [8]]. Aging further reduces elastin crosslinking enzymes like lysyl oxidase and fibrillin-1, while increasing elastin-degrading enzymes such as MMP-2 and MMP-12, causing fragmented, disorganized elastic fibers typical of aged skin [[8], [9], [10], [11]].

Although many anti-aging products focus on epidermal hydration or collagen remodeling [12,13], they often neglect the degradation of elastic fibers, which plays a crucial role in skin aging. Hydrating agents like hyaluronic acid and glycerin enhance moisture retention and strengthen the skin barrier, reducing fine lines [14]. Lipophilic antioxidants such as vitamin E and coenzyme Q10 protect against oxidative stress by scavenging reactive oxygen species and modulating inflammation [12,15]. Collagen-based formulations support dermal structure and counteract photoaging by promoting fibroblast activity and regulating ferroptosis [16]. However, these approaches rarely target elastin degradation or promote its regeneration, limiting their long-term effectiveness in restoring skin elasticity and biomechanical function [17].

Despite the promising potential of elastin-based therapies, their clinical translation faces significant challenges related to source reliability, biosafety, and bioactivity [18]. Animal-derived elastin, a common source of elastin material, suffers from batch-to-batch variability, undefined molecular composition, limited bioactivity, and risks of immunogenicity or pathogen transmission [18,19]. Synthetic elastin-like polypeptides (ELPs), composed of repetitive short motifs such as VPGXG, offer partial biomimicry but lack the full domain architecture of native elastin, limiting their ability to assemble into functional elastic fibers [20]. Additionally, recombinant human tropoelastin (rhTE) has been produced using expression systems such as Escherichia coli and Nicotiana tabacum [21]; however, its clinical application is constrained by relatively low yields and production challenges. In contrast, recombinant humanized elastin (RHE), which contains fully humanized functional sequences capable of replicating the properties of native elastin, represents a promising alternative that overcomes many limitations of previous elastin sources. Nevertheless, reports on RHE remain limited.

Herein, we have developed a novel bioactive gel dressing based on recombinant humanized elastin (RHE) that reconstructs elastic fibers and rejuvenates endogenously aged skin (Fig. 1). RHE is a recombinant protein containing the functional amino acid sequences of human elastin, produced via an Escherichia coli expression system. The therapeutic efficacy of the RHE gel was validated in vivo using both UV-induced zebrafish models and D-galactose-induced murine models of intrinsic aging. Treatment with the RHE gel significantly improved skin elasticity, reduced transepidermal water loss (TEWL), and promoted extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, demonstrated by increased deposition of collagen and elastin fibers. Histological analyses confirmed restoration of dermal architecture, while molecular assays revealed upregulated elastin synthesis and related genes (LOX and fibrillin-1), suppression of matrix metalloproteinases (MMP-2 and MMP-12), and enhanced expression of key structural ECM proteins including collagen types I and III (COL I and COL III). Collectively, these findings highlight the excellent anti-aging potential of the RHE gel and support its promising application in next-generation regenerative dermatology.

Materials and methods

Materials

Glycerol was purchased from Shandong Dexin Medical Technology Co., Ltd. Hyaluronic acid (HA, 1200–1600 kDa) was obtained from Bloomage Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Alginate, carbomer 940, arginine, 1,2-hexanediol, and p-hydroxyacetophenone were supplied by Guangzhou Jiemei Biotechnology Co., Ltd. L929 fibroblast cells were sourced from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. Fetal bovine serum (FBS), penicillin–streptomycin, and Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) were obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific. The Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8), Calcein-AM/PI live/dead staining kit, Phalloidin-TRITC, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining kit, Masson's trichrome staining kit, malondialdehyde (MDA) assay kit, superoxide dismutase (SOD) assay kit, catalase (CAT) assay kit, and reduced glutathione (GSH) assay kit were all obtained from Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd.

Preparation of recombinant humanized elastin (RHE) and RHE gel

The recombinant humanized elastin (RHE) gene was synthesized and cloned into the pCold-III vector, and expressed in Escherichia coli BL21(DE3). Briefly, cultivation was carried out and expression was induced with IPTG following a stepped temperature protocol. Cells were harvested and lysed using a high-pressure homogenizer. The soluble fraction was purified by nickel-affinity chromatography followed by ion-exchange chromatography. SDS-PAGE was performed to evaluate the purity of the RHE protein (Fig. S3), showing a single prominent band at the expected molecular weight and no detectable contaminating proteins.The purified RHE protein was lyophilized and stored at −20 °C for subsequent use.

The gel formulation (w/w%) and the function of each ingredient are summarized in Table S2. Carbomer 940 (0.5 %) was first dispersed in deionized water at 85 °C with stirring at 500 rpm to form the gel base, providing gelation and mechanical stability. Trehalose (5 %), glycerol (3 %), and sodium hyaluronate (1 %)—the latter also supporting mechanical stability—were then added as humectants to enhance biocompatibility, followed by high-speed homogenization (10 000 rpm, 3 min) and heating (85 °C, 10–15 min). Arginine (0.5 %) was incorporated as a pH adjuster to improve biocompatibility, together with 1,2-hexanediol (0.5 %) and phenoxyethanol (0.5 %) as preservatives. After cooling to 30 °C, lyophilized recombinant humanized elastin (0.1 %), the active biofunctional component, was added and thoroughly mixed to yield a clear gel. The final product was aseptically packaged into sterile 35 mL plastic dropper bottles.

Stability evaluation of RHE gel

Phase stability was evaluated by subjecting the RHE gel to three consecutive centrifugation cycles at 3000 rpm for 30 min each. Thermal stability was assessed by incubating samples at 40 °C, and cold-storage stability by storing them at 4 °C, both over a three-month period. Gels were diluted 1:10 (w/v) in distilled water and read on a calibrated pH meter. Rheological behavior was characterized on an MCR 302 rheometer (Anton Paar, Germany) at 25 °C. Viscosity was first measured under a constant shear rate of 100 s−1, then flow curves were recorded across shear rates ranging from 1 to 100 s−1 to evaluate shear-dependent viscosity.

Biocompatibility and bioactivity assessment of RHE gel

In vitro cytotoxicity

In vitro cytotoxicity of RHE gel was assessed by CCK-8 assay in L929 fibroblasts. Cells were seeded at 1 × 105 cells/mL (100 μL/well) in 96-well plates and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. The medium was then removed and replaced as follows: blank wells received 100 μL high-glucose DMEM (with cells), control wells received 100 μL DMEM without cells, and test wells received 100 μL RHE gel suspensions in DMEM at 1 × , 10 × , 20 × , and 100 × dilutions. After a further 24 h incubation, media were discarded and 100 μL CCK-8 reagent added to each well. Cell proliferation

Gel extracts were prepared by incubating 1 mL of RHE gel or commercial recombinant humanized collagen (RHC) gel in 9 mL high-glucose DMEM at 37 °C for 24 h. L929 cells were seeded at 1 × 105 cells/mL (100 μL per well) in 96-well plates and allowed to adhere for 24 h. The medium was then replaced with 100 μL of blank DMEM, RHC extract, or RHE extract. Cell proliferation was evaluated by CCK-8 assays on days 1, 3, and 5: 100 μL CCK-8 reagent was added, plates were incubated at 37 °C for 1.5 h in the dark, and absorbance at 450 nm was recorded. Proliferation rates were normalized to the blank-DMEM group on day 1.

Live/dead cell staining

L929 cells (1 × 106 cells/mL) were seeded in confocal dishes and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. The medium was then replaced with 1 mL of blank DMEM or DMEM containing RHC or RHE gel. At days 1, 3, and 5, cells were stained with Calcein-AM/PI per the manufacturer's instructions. Live (green) and dead (red) fluorescence images were acquired on an Olympus FV3000 confocal microscope, and the proportion of live cells was quantified using Image J.

Immunofluorescence staining

L929 fibroblasts (1 × 106 cells/mL) were seeded in confocal dishes and cultured at 37 °C for 24 h. The medium was then replaced with 1 mL of blank DMEM, RHC gel suspension, or RHE gel suspension, and cells were further incubated for 24 h. Following treatment, cells were fixed, permeabilized, and stained with Phalloidin-TRITC to visualize F-actin and counterstained with DAPI for nuclei, according to the manufacturer's instructions. Fluorescence images were captured on an Olympus FV3000 confocal microscope to evaluate cytoskeletal organization.

Cell migration

Fibroblast migration was evaluated by scratch assay. L929 cells (1 × 106 cells/mL) were plated in 6-well plates and cultured at 37 °C for 24 h. A sterile 100 μL pipette tip was used to create a straight scratch across the cell monolayer; debris was removed by washing three times with PBS (defined as 0 h). Wells were then treated with 1 mL blank DMEM, RHC gel suspension, or RHE gel suspension and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C. Wound closure was imaged on an Olympus IX53 inverted fluorescence microscope, and the migrated area at 24 h was quantified in Image J as the percentage reduction in wound width relative to 0 h.

Repairing effect of RHE gel on UVB-induced caudal fin damage in zebrafish larvae

Two-day-post-fertilization zebrafish larvae (n = 10 per well) were allocated to five groups in 6-well plates: blank control, UVB model, positive control (10 mg/mL tea polyphenols), 10 × diluted commercial RHC gel, and 10 × diluted RHE gel. The concentration of tea polyphenols (10 mg/mL) used as the positive control in the UVB-induced zebrafish sunburn model was selected based on the Chinese industry standard TZHCA014-2022 and previous studies [22], both of which demonstrate the effective repair of UV-induced tail fin damage by tea polyphenols in zebrafish. After removing E3 medium, 3 mL of the appropriate test solution was added and larvae were incubated at 28.5 °C for 2 h. The blank group received no treatment. All groups except blank were then exposed to UVB irradiation (three 15-min sessions, total dose 2.07 J/cm2) with 30 min intervals, and subsequently returned to fresh E3 medium at 28.5 °C for 22 h.

Evaluation of antioxidant activity of RHE gel in zebrafish

Three-day-post-fertilization zebrafish larvae (10 per well) were assigned into four groups: blank control, positive control (0.1 mg/mL dipotassium glycyrrhizinate), 10 × diluted commercial RHC gel, and 10 × diluted RHE gel. The dipotassium glycyrrhizinate concentration was chosen according to literature precedent for plant antioxidants used as positive controls in zebrafish ROS models [[23], [24], [25]]. Larvae were incubated with 3 mL of the respective test solution at 28.5 °C for 1 h. Oxidative stress was induced by treating larvae with 10 μM CuSO4 for 20 min, followed by three rinses in fresh E3 medium. Intracellular ROS levels were then detected by incubating larvae in 20 μg/mL DCFH-DA in the dark at 28.5 °C for 1 h. After washing, larvae were immobilized in 3 % low-melting-point agarose and imaged on an Olympus SZX16 fluorescence stereomicroscope. Fluorescence intensity was quantified using Image J.

The DPPH scavenging assay was conducted under light-protected conditions by dissolving 2 mg of solid DPPH in 50 mL of 95 % ethanol to prepare a 0.1 mM DPPH solution. A 0.5 mg/mL vitamin C solution served as the positive control. RHC gel and RHE gel suspensions were diluted tenfold with ultrapure water. Then, 2 mL of each sample solution was mixed with 2 mL of freshly prepared DPPH solution and incubated in the dark for 30 min. Absorbance was measured at 517 nm using a microplate reader.

D-galactose-induced aging mouse model

Female Kunming mice (≈27 g; n = 18 per group) were acclimated for one week, then had a 2 × 4 cm area of dorsal hair removed and were randomized into five groups: Blank, Model, VE (topical vitamin E lotion), RHC gel, and RHE gel. All procedures were conducted following the guidelines established by the ethics committee of the College of Chemistry and Chemical Engineering at Lanzhou University (Approval No. G20). To induce systemic aging, all groups except Blank received daily subcutaneous injections of D-galactose (500 mg/kg in 200 μL saline) for 8 weeks, while the Blank group received 200 μL saline. Beginning in week 3, 0.1 g of the assigned topical treatment (VE, RHC gel, or RHE gel) was applied once daily for 6 weeks. Body weight was measured at seven-day intervals. During each interval, the mental status, food intake, and activity levels of the mice in each group were closely monitored. Changes in body weight were recorded and visualized using graphical representations. At the end of week 8, mice were euthanized; dorsal skin was excised and divided into two portions—one snap-frozen at −80 °C for biochemical assays and the other fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde for 24 h for histological analysis.

Non-invasive anti-aging assessment

At weeks 2, 4, and 8, skin condition was non-invasively evaluated using the DermaLab®-Combo system (Cortex Technology, Denmark), which integrates multiple modalities: dermatoscopic imaging under both polarized and non-polarized white light to qualitatively assess epidermal morphology; high-frequency ultrasound to measure dermal echogenicity as a proxy for collagen density and structural integrity; electrical capacitance readings of the stratum corneum to quantify surface hydration; and an open-chamber vapor-pressure gradient method to determine TEWL. To ensure robustness, each parameter was recorded in quadruplicate at each sampling site, and mean values were used for all analyses.

Skin elasticity assessment

Skin elasticity was quantified by the pinch-release method [26]. In brief, the midline dorsal skin was gently pinched and lifted vertically with the thumb and index finger until the hindlimbs lightly touched the support surface. After holding for 1 s, the skin was released, and the time required for full recoil to its original position was recorded in real time as an index of skin elasticity.

Histological analysis

Paraffin-embedded dorsal skin samples were sectioned at 3.5 μm and mounted on poly-L-lysine–coated slides. Sections were stained with hematoxylin & eosin (H&E) to assess epidermal architecture and thickness, Masson's trichrome to evaluate collagen fiber deposition, and Verhoeff's elastic stain to visualize elastic fiber organization. Digital micrographs were acquired on an Olympus IX53 inverted fluorescence microscope under identical illumination and magnification parameters. Quantitative morphometry was performed in Image J (v1.52) using color deconvolution and thresholding to segment stain-positive areas. The collagen volume fraction (CVF) was calculated as the percentage of collagen-positive area relative to total tissue area: Collagen area fraction (%) = (collagen fiber area/total skin area) × 100 %. Similarly, the elastin area fraction (EAF) was calculated as: Elastin area fraction (%) = (elastin fiber area/total skin area) × 100 %. Analyses were conducted on three non-overlapping fields per section and averaged to yield representative values.

In vivo antioxidant activity in aging skin model

Frozen dorsal skin samples (−80 °C) were thawed, blotted dry, and precisely weighed. Tissues were homogenized in ice‐cold extraction buffer (0.1 g tissue per mL buffer) and centrifuged at 8000×g for 10 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatants were assayed for malondialdehyde (MDA) and reduced glutathione (GSH) concentrations, as well as superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT) activities, using commercial kits according to the manufacturers’ protocols. All measurements were performed on a Tecan Infinite F200/M200 multimode microplate reader (Tecan, Männedorf, Switzerland).

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from dorsal skin using a commercial kit, and RNA purity and concentration were determined spectrophotometrically (NanoDrop 2000; Thermo Fisher Scientific). RNA integrity was confirmed by visual inspection of the A260/A280 and A260/A230 ratios. First-strand cDNA synthesis was performed with a reverse transcription kit according to the manufacturer's protocol. Quantitative PCR was carried out on a real-time PCR system using TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ II (TaKaRa) in a 20 μL reaction volume under the following cycling conditions: 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 30 s. Gene-specific primers (Table 1) were synthesized by Sangon Biotechnology Co., Ltd. GAPDH served as the internal control. All reactions were performed in triplicate, and relative mRNA levels were calculated using the 2 (−ΔΔCT) method.

Immunofluorescence staining of skin tissues

Skin tissue samples from the dorsal region of mice were fixed, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned. After baking, dewaxing, and rehydration, antigen retrieval was performed to enhance antibody binding. The sections were then incubated with primary antibodies against elastin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, PA5-89281), MMP-2 (Proteintech, 10373-2-AP), TGF-β1 (Proteintech, 26155-1-AP), and Smad3 (Proteintech, 66516-1-lg), followed by fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Fluorescence images were acquired using a confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM), and fluorescence intensity was quantified semi-quantitatively using Image J software.

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). All in vitro experiments were conducted with at least three independent biological replicates, each containing three technical replicates. Statistical analyses were performed by one-way ANOVA in SPSS 24 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) with LSD's post hoc test for multiple comparisons. Significance thresholds were set at p < 0.05 (∗), p < 0.01 (∗∗), and p < 0.001 (∗∗∗). Graphical representations were generated in GraphPad Prism 8.0.2 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

Results and discussion

Preparation and physicochemical stability of RHE gels

The recombinant human elastin (RHE) gel formulation remained homogeneous and transparent after accelerated centrifugation (3000 rpm × 30 min, 3 cycles) and prolonged storage at both 4 °C and 40 °C for 12 weeks. No phase separation, protein precipitation, or odor development was observed. pH values remained within the narrow range of 6.38–6.41, and viscosity measurements (0.77–0.94 Pa ·s) exhibited minimal variation relative to baseline (Table S1). Within a shear rate range of 1–100 s−1, the viscosity of the RHE gel decreased progressively with increasing shear rate, indicating the presence of shear-thinning behaviour (Fig. S1). These results confirm that the RHE gel maintains its structural integrity and rheological properties under stress conditions, satisfying key criteria for a stable topical dressing.

In vitro biocompatibility and bioactivity of RHE gel

In vitro cytotoxicity of RHE gel was first assessed in L929 fibroblasts. CCK-8 assays using undiluted and serially diluted (1:10, 1:20, 1:100) RHE gel extracts all yielded viabilities exceeding 100 % relative to high-glucose DMEM controls (Fig. 2a), confirming excellent cytocompatibility. Cell proliferation in RHE gel was further examined by CCK-8 assay (Fig. 2b) and live/dead staining (Fig. 2c and d). Fibroblasts cultured on RHE gel exhibited significantly higher growth rates than those on commercial RHC gel, reaching 125.62 ± 3.59 %, 156.25 ± 3.20 %, and 188.53 ± 4.69 % on days 1, 3, and 5, respectively (Fig. 2b). Live/dead fluorescence imaging corroborated these results, showing predominantly viable (Calcein-positive) cells in the RHE group with no detectable cytotoxicity (Fig. 2d). Quantitative counts of live cells further confirmed superior proliferation on RHE gel (802 ± 93, 997 ± 45, and 1101 ± 87 cells on days 1, 3, and 5) versus RHC gel (671 ± 17, 743 ± 29, and 884 ± 61 cells) (Fig. 2c). These findings demonstrate that RHE gel markedly enhances fibroblast proliferation in vitro while maintaining excellent biocompatibility.

Immunofluorescence staining of the actin cytoskeleton was used to evaluate RHE gel's effect on cell-matrix interactions. Fibroblasts on the blank substrate exhibited a typical spread morphology (Fig. 2e). In contrast, cells on RHE gel developed elongated, well-aligned actin stress fibers and a pronounced spindle-shaped profile, reflecting enhanced cytoskeletal organization and spreading. These features were more prominent than in the commercial RHC gel group, indicating that RHE gel more effectively promotes cell adhesion and structural polarization (Fig. 2e). A scratch‐wound assay was performed to evaluate RHE gel's effect on fibroblast migration. Uniform scratch boundaries were created at 0 h in all groups. After 24 h, the RHE gel group achieved 76.05 ± 4.54 % wound closure, significantly higher than the RHC gel group (39.35 ± 2.29 %) and the blank control (14.83 ± 2.08 %) (Fig. 2f and g). These results highlight the potent pro-migratory activity of RHE gel in dermal fibroblasts.

These enhancements likely derive from tropoelastin, the soluble monomeric precursor of elastin. Tropoelastin has been shown to potently induce mesenchymal stem cell proliferation and to preserve phenotype stability, rivaling insulin-like growth factor-1 and basic fibroblast growth factor, and uniquely sustaining cell expansion under serum-deprived condition [27]. This intrinsic biofunctionality underpins RHE gel's ability to maintain cell viability and drive pro-proliferative and pro-migratory responses in vitro.

Therapeutic efficacy of RHE gel in a UV-induced zebrafish caudal fin model

The zebrafish caudal fin, which recapitulates key structural and functional features of human skin, was employed to evaluate RHE gel's reparative capacity following UV-induced photodamage [28]. UV irradiation reduced fin area from 366 837.48 ± 51 363.02 μm2 in the blank group to 210 534.43 ± 60 899.27 μm2 in the model group, confirming successful damage induction (Fig. 3a–c). Application of 0.01 g/mL tea polyphenols (positive control) restored fin area to 357 213.18 ± 41 470.39 μm2, corresponding to a 93.84 % inhibition of shrinkage. Notably, RHE gel (10-fold dilution) achieved a comparable recovery (358 891.30 ± 46 690.91 μm2; 94.92 % inhibition), whereas 10-fold diluted commercial RHC gel produced only partial repair (304 741.80 ± 27 919.66 μm2; 60.27 % inhibition) (Fig. 3b and c). These data demonstrate that RHE gel markedly attenuates UV-induced fin shrinkage, highlighting its promise as an anti-photoaging agent capable of promoting extracellular matrix restoration in regenerative skincare applications.

Antioxidant activity of RHE gel in a zebrafish oxidative stress model

An in vivo zebrafish larval model of CuSO4-induced oxidative stress was employed to evaluate RHE gel's ROS-scavenging efficacy (Fig. 4a) [25]. Exposure to CuSO4 elevated fluorescence intensity to 91.56 ± 20.55 A U. in the model group, confirming ROS overproduction. Treatment with 0.1 mg/mL dipotassium glycyrrhizinate (positive control) reduced the signal to 26.78 ± 6.63 A U. Notably, RHE gel (10 × dilution) effected a similar decrease (23.12 ± 6.25 A U.), whereas 10 × diluted RHC gel achieved only partial suppression (34.41 ± 7.20 A U.) (Fig. 4b and c). These results demonstrate that RHE gel possesses potent in vivo antioxidant activity, comparable to a well-established ROS scavenger. We further assessed the DPPH scavenging ability of RHE gel (Fig. S4), supporting that its capacity to alleviate oxidative stress may derive from abundant amino acid residues within elastin that readily donate electrons to neutralize free radicals [19].

Non-invasive evaluation of the anti-aging effects of RHE gel in a D-galactose-induced mouse model

Chronic D-galactose administration induces oxidative stress, tissue damage, and inflammation, recapitulating key features of natural aging [29,30]. To evaluate RHE gel's anti-aging efficacy, mice received daily subcutaneous injections of D-galactose (500 mg/kg) for eight weeks, with topical application of RHE gel, vitamin E (VE), or RHC gel beginning at week 3 (Fig. 5a). Body weight monitored weekly for all experimental groups (Fig. S2). D-galactose treatment precipitated progressive weight loss in the model group, reaching 38.00 ± 0.89 g by week 8. In contrast, RHE gel–treated mice maintained body weight comparable to the blank control (41.33 ± 1.97 g vs. 41.50 ± 1.05g), indicating mitigation of systemic aging effects (Fig. S2).

Dermatoscopic imaging (DermaLab®-Combo) revealed early signs of cutaneous aging—xerosis, loss of elasticity, and fine wrinkling—in all D-galactose–treated groups by week 2 (Fig. 5b). By week 4, the model group exhibited pronounced laxity and deepening of wrinkles, whereas RHE gel application substantially restored elasticity. By week 8, RHE gel–treated skin was virtually indistinguishable from blank controls in elasticity and wrinkle depth (Fig. 5b), demonstrating potent non-invasive anti-aging activity in vivo.

High-frequency ultrasound imaging was utilized to quantitatively assess dermal structural integrity, with echogenic signal intensity (white > yellow > red > blue > green) reflecting increasing dermal density. At both weeks 4 and 8, the RHE gel-treated group exhibited a pronounced increase in hyper-echoic regions (white/yellow) and a concomitant reduction in hypo-echoic areas (green/blue), indicative of enhanced dermal matrix density (Fig. 5c). By week 8, quantitative analysis revealed that the RHE gel group achieved a dermal density of 60.36 ± 2.45 A U., comparable to the blank control and significantly higher than both the vitamin E (VE) group (51.27 ± 1.26 A U.) and the commercial recombinant collagen gel group (51.87 ± 3.61 A U.) (Fig. 5d).

Skin barrier function was concurrently evaluated by measuring stratum corneum hydration and TEWL. By week 8, hydration levels in the RHE group were fully restored (284.22 ± 16.36 μS), statistically indistinguishable from the blank group (Fig. 5e). In contrast, both the VE and commercial product groups exhibited significantly lower hydration levels (240.62 ± 7.40 μS and 264.97 ± 5.48 μS, respectively) (Fig. 5e). TEWL values in the RHE group returned to baseline (10.79 ± 0.64 g m−2 h−1), suggesting effective reconstitution of epidermal barrier integrity (Fig. 5f).

Skin elasticity was assessed via retraction time following standardized mechanical deformation [26]. By week 4, retraction times in the RHE group were comparable to those of the blank group, indicating substantial improvement in skin viscoelasticity (Fig. 5g). Collectively, the DermaLab®-Combo assessments underscore the superior therapeutic efficacy of RHE gel compared to VE lotion and commercial recombinant collagen gel, as evidenced by its ability to enhance dermal remodeling, restore hydration balance, reduce TEWL, and improve skin elasticity. These comprehensive bioactivities support the potential of RHE gel as a non-invasive intervention for attenuating endogenous skin aging and facilitating facial rejuvenation.

Skin histological analysis

Histological analyses were conducted to evaluate the protective and restorative effects of RHE gel on D-galactose-induced aging skin (Fig. 6). Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining revealed that the blank group displayed a well-organized tissue architecture, characterized by uniform epidermal thickness, densely packed epidermal cells, intact epidermal–dermal junctions, and regularly arranged dermal fibers (Fig. 6a). In contrast, the aging model group exhibited significant epidermal thinning (10.82 ± 0.47 μm) and disorganization of dermal collagen fibers (Fig. 6aandb). Treatment with RHE gel markedly improved epidermal structure, with thickness increasing to 16.47 ± 0.94 μm and dermal fibers appearing evenly distributed and structurally comparable to the blank group. While both the VE and commercial RHC gel groups showed moderate improvement in epidermal thickness (13.80 ± 0.72 μm and 14.43 ± 0.72 μm, respectively), these values remained significantly lower than those observed in the RHE gel group (Fig. 6a–b).

Masson's trichrome staining was employed to visualize collagen fibers, which appear bright blue in the dermis. In the blank group, collagen fibers were densely bundled and well organized (Fig. 6a). The aging model group showed markedly disrupted collagen architecture, with sparse, fragmented fibers and a reduced collagen volume fraction (33.09 ± 0.95 %) (Fig. 6a andc). In contrast, the RHE gel group demonstrated significantly restored collagen density and organization, with a collagen volume fraction of 66.55 ± 0.95 %, comparable to that of the blank group. The VE and commercial RHC gel groups showed partial restoration, with collagen volume fractions of 51.15 ± 1.33 % and 53.09 ± 1.08 %, respectively, yet still significantly lower than that of the RHE gel group (Fig. 6a andc).

Verhöeff staining was employed to evaluate elastic fiber content, which appeared as black, filamentous structures. In the blank group, elastic fibers were long, continuous, and wavy in morphology (Fig. 6a). Aging markedly reduced the quantity and integrity of elastic fibers, resulting in a lower area percentage (1.55 ± 0.34 %) and a disrupted fiber network (Fig. 6a andd). Treatment with RHE gel significantly increased elastic fiber content (3.75 ± 0.25 %), restored structural alignment, and showed no significant difference compared to the blank group. Both the VE and commercial RHC gel groups exhibited lower elastic fiber percentages than the RHE gel group, confirming the superior regenerative efficacy of RHE gel (Fig. 6a andd). Furthermore, immunofluorescence analysis (Fig. 9d) provided higher-resolution visualization of elastic fibers and further corroborated these findings.

Aging-related epidermal thinning is associated with impaired keratinocyte proliferation and differentiation, fibroblast senescence, and MMP-mediated degradation of extracellular matrix components such as collagen and elastin [31]. Elastin contains the VGVAPG peptide motif, which promotes keratinocyte proliferation and migration [32]. In our study, RHE gel may thus facilitate epidermal thickening through elastin-mediated signaling, consistent with our in vitro results showing enhanced fibroblast proliferation. Moreover, previous reports indicate that co-application of elastin and hyaluronic acid enhances the integration of exogenous elastin into native elastic fibers [6], which may explain the histological improvements observed after RHE gel treatment.

Antioxidant efficacy of RHE gel in mitigating oxidative stress associated with endogenous aging

Under normal physiological conditions, oxidative homeostasis is maintained by a coordinated network of antioxidant defenses, including enzymatic antioxidants such as superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT), as well as non-enzymatic antioxidants like glutathione (GSH) [33]. However, D-galactose-induced aging disrupts this balance by promoting excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, leading to elevated levels of lipid peroxidation byproducts such as malondialdehyde (MDA), ultimately resulting in cellular dysfunction and apoptosis [34]. To investigate the antioxidant potential of RHE gel in aged skin, the activities of SOD, CAT, and GSH, as well as MDA content, were measured in skin tissue (Fig. 7). Compared to the blank control, the aging model group exhibited significantly reduced activities of SOD, CAT, and levels of GSH, accompanied by a substantial increase in MDA levels, consistent with elevated oxidative stress (Fig. 7a–d). Treatment with RHE gel significantly restored antioxidant enzyme activity and reduced MDA accumulation. Specifically, SOD, CAT, and GSH levels increased to 226.48 ± 5.35 U/g, 214.47 ± 9.88 U/g, and 230.32 ± 4.96 mg/g, respectively, while MDA levels decreased to 1.46 ± 0.21 nmol/g—values not significantly different from those of the blank group (Fig. 7a–d). In contrast, the VE and commercial RHC groups exhibited only partial recovery of antioxidant parameters and significantly lower efficacy than the RHE gel group.

The observed antioxidant effects of RHE gel may be attributed to its capacity to enhance endogenous redox defense pathways. SOD catalyzes the dismutation of superoxide anions (·O2−) into hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), which is subsequently detoxified by CAT and GSH, thereby reducing oxidative burden (Fig. 7e). The attenuation of ROS further limits lipid peroxidation of cellular membranes, as reflected by reduced MDA content. These findings suggest that RHE gel effectively restores redox homeostasis in aged skin by upregulating intrinsic antioxidant systems and suppressing oxidative damage. This antioxidant capacity likely contributes to its overall protective effects against endogenous skin aging.

Effect of RHE gel on the expression of ECM regeneration -associated genes

ECM homeostasis is essential for maintaining skin structure and function, and is tightly regulated by the balance between matrix synthesis and degradation. LOX and fibrillin-1 are critical mediators of elastic fiber assembly, facilitating the cross-linking of tropoelastin into mature elastic fibers [35]. In contrast, matrix metalloproteinases MMP-2 and MMP-12 are key enzymes involved in the proteolytic degradation of ECM components, particularly elastin [8]. To elucidate the molecular basis of RHE gel-mediated skin repair, the expression levels of ECM remodeling-related genes were assessed (Fig. 8).

(a) LOX, (b) Fibrillin-1, (c) MMP-2, (d) MMP-12, (e) Elastin, (f) Collagen I (COL I), and (g) Collagen III (COL III) mRNA expression levels in skin tissue following treatment. (h) Schematic representation of the proposed mechanism by which RHE gel promotes ECM remodeling in aged skin, highlighting enhanced synthesis and reduced degradation of structural proteins. Statistical significance: ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001.

In the RHE gel-treated group, LOX and Fibrillin-1expression was significantly upregulated compared to the model, VE, and commercial RHC gel groups (Fig. 8a and b). These results indicate enhanced elastic fiber synthesis following RHE treatment. Additionally, MMP-2 and MMP-12 expression was significantly suppressed in the RHE group, suggesting inhibition of age-associated elastin degradation (Fig. 8c and d). RHE gel also markedly upregulated the transcription of Collagen I (Col I), Collagen III (Col III), and Elastin, compared with the model group (Fig. 8e–g). The fold increases in Col I, Col III and Elastin were significantly higher than in both the VE and RHC gel groups, underscoring the superior regenerative efficacy of RHE gel. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that RHE gel restores ECM integrity through a dual mechanism: stimulation of matrix synthesis via upregulation of LOX, Fibrillin-1, Col I, Col III, and Elastin, and suppression of ECM degradation through downregulation of MMP-2 and MMP-12 (Fig. 8h). This molecular profile is consistent with histological observations and supports the conclusion that RHE gel effectively promotes ECM remodeling and structural restoration in aged skin.

RHE gel restores elastic fiber integrity by modulating TGF-β/Smad pathway and matrix degradation

TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway plays a pivotal role in elastin formation [6]. TGF-β1 promotes elastin synthesis by upregulating tropoelastin promoter activity and increasing elastin mRNA levels [36], while the expression of LOX and fibrillin-1 is also regulated by TGF-β1 [37]. Conversely, elevated MMP-2 levels accelerate elastin degradation and impair its synthesis [6].

To evaluate the effects of different treatments on TGF-β/Smad signaling, matrix degradation, and elastic fiber integrity, we performed immunofluorescence staining for TGF-β1, Smad3, MMP-2, and elastin (Fig. 9). In the Blank group, TGF-β1 expression was 50.84 ± 1.44 %, which significantly decreased to 24.24 ± 0.99 % in the Model group (Fig. 9a ande). Treatment with RHE gel markedly restored TGF-β1 expression to 46.62 ± 1.78 %, significantly higher than that observed in the VE group (33.00 ± 2.42 %) and RHC gel group (37.48 ± 2.75 %). Similarly, Smad3 expression decreased from 38.64 ± 2.39 % in the Blank group to 18.85 ± 1.48 % in the Model group (Fig. 9b andf). Post-treatment, Smad3 levels were restored, with the RHE gel group (35.28 ± 0.70 %) significantly exceeding the VE group (26.88 ± 2.14 %) and RHC gel group (28.42 ± 2.31 %).

In contrast, MMP-2 expression was markedly elevated in the Model group (15.21 ± 1.16 %) due to aging-induced activation. RHE gel treatment significantly reduced MMP-2 expression to 7.72 ± 0.83 %, a level comparable to the Blank group (7.12 ± 0.57 %) (Fig. 9c andg). Neither the VE group (9.77 ± 1.26 %) nor the RHC gel group (10.68 ± 0.54 %) restored MMP-2 expression to baseline levels.

For elastin, the Model group exhibited a pronounced reduction to 8.55 ± 2.26 % (Fig. 9d andh). RHE gel treatment significantly enhanced elastin synthesis, reaching 17.61 ± 0.84 %, which was not significantly different from the Blank group. In comparison, elastin levels in the VE (12.69 ± 0.76 %) and RHC gel (14.15 ± 0.72 %) groups remained significantly lower than in the Blank group.

Collectively, these results demonstrate that RHE gel more effectively modulates TGF-β/Smad signaling, attenuates matrix degradation, and promotes elastic fiber reconstruction than VE or commercial RHC gel. The TGF-β/Smad pathway likely represents a key mechanism underlying the upregulation of LOX and fibrillin-1 expression by RHE gel, although the involvement of MAPK signaling cannot be excluded, as it has also been implicated in extracellular matrix remodeling and elastic fiber assembly.

Conclusion

This study comprehensively evaluates a novel recombinant humanized elastin (RHE) gel designed to restore dermal elasticity and counteract key features of endogenous skin aging. The RHE mimics native tropoelastin's functional properties while ensuring biosafety, batch consistency, and scalable production via microbial fermentation—overcoming limitations of animal-derived elastin.

In vitro and in vivo results demonstrated that RHE gel enhances fibroblast activity, neutralizes oxidative stress, and promotes regeneration of dermal extracellular matrix components, including collagen and elastin fibers. These effects translate into significant improvements in skin structure and function in aging models, outperforming conventional antioxidants and collagen-based treatments.

Importantly, RHE gel addresses fundamental mechanisms of skin aging, oxidative imbalance, impaired matrix synthesis, and elastic fiber degradation, positioning it as a mechanistically rational, bioactive intervention beyond superficial hydration or collagen modulation.

From a translational perspective, RHE production is compatible with Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) standards, enabling cost-effective, large-scale manufacturing with consistent quality. The gel's formulation employs safe, well-characterized excipients, potentially facilitating regulatory approval under FDA and EMA frameworks. Although further preclinical and clinical studies are necessary, these attributes support the promising clinical development of RHE gel for regenerative dermatology and aesthetic medicine.

In summary, this work establishes RHE gel as a next-generation, non-invasive dermatological platform capable of rebuilding the dermal ECM, neutralizing oxidative stress, and rejuvenating aged skin. It holds substantial potential for integration into advanced skincare formulations for both preventive and therapeutic applications.
