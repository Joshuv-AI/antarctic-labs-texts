Co‐Designing Peer Support for Women Labelled With Intellectual Disabilities Who Have Experienced Sexual Violence
Authors: Alexis Buettgen, Tammy Bernasky, Kelly MacDougall, Ann Fudge Schormans, Jennifer Gordon, Sandra Tavares, Verónica Schiariti, Robin Mason, Janice Du Mont, Maria Huijbregts
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundWomen labelled with intellectual disabilities face high rates of social exclusion and sexual violence, with limited research and few services tailored to their needs. Peer support offers many benefits including recognition of diverse lived experiences and nonprofessional perspectives. This article highlights collaborative efforts of women labelled with intellectual disabilities, service providers and researchers in advancing peer support through an applied research project.MethodsUsing trauma‐informed, arts‐based and human‐rights‐focused co‐design approaches, we fostered inclusive research and programme development in a 3‐day virtual symposium.ResultsThe study emphasises shared control of research agendas, shaped by a multistakeholder team prioritising lived experience knowledge. Women took more control over the research through co‐creative practices to that meaningfully included them in the design of future research and programming.ConclusionThis inclusive model champions equality and equity, advocating for positive discourse around intellectual disabilities while bridging lived, academic and professional insights in the field.Keywords: arts‐based methods, experience‐based co‐design, intellectual disability, peer support, sexual violence

Introduction

We want people to know they need to accept us for who we are, instead of putting us in a corner and telling us we don't exist.

This is what co‐author, Kelly, wrote about the need for inclusion of people labelled with intellectual disabilities in research and services for women who have experienced sexual violence. The frequency and risk of violence against women with disabilities is greater than those without disabilities (García‐Cuéllar et al. 2023; Ledingham et al. 2022; Tomsa et al. 2021, 278). According to the 2018 Survey of Safety in Public and Private Spaces in Canada, 55% of women with disabilities who had ever been in an intimate partnership had experienced intimate partner violence compared to 37% of women without disabilities (Savage 2021). Women with disabilities are twice as likely to be victims of violent crimes than those without (DisAbled Women's Network of Canada 2019). Estimates suggest that between 30% and 70% of women labelled with intellectual disabilities experience sexual violence and repeat victimisation, as well as significant barriers to social inclusion 1 (Gehl and Whittington‐Walsh 2016; Tomsa et al. 2021).

Globally, women with disabilities experience barriers to reporting violence to the police (Bernasky 2022). A systemic failure by public authorities to recognise the extent and impact of violence toward people with disabilities, especially women labelled with intellectual disabilities (Byrne 2018), is evidenced by the reluctance of police to proceed with investigations due to a false perception that women labelled with intellectual disabilities are not credible witnesses or that their inability to say no is wrongly perceived as consent (McGilloway et al. 2020). If survivors do want to report their abuse, they are faced with communication barriers, lack of service provision and ongoing exclusion from decisions that directly affect their lives (McCarthy 2014; McGilloway et al. 2020).

In terms of service support, the historic exclusion of people labelled with intellectual disabilities and their lived experiences in the design and implementation of human services means that services are not inclusive at an organisation and system level (Cameron et al. 2019). This exclusion is rooted in ableist assumptions that people labelled with intellectual disabilities are not capable of having the knowledge, opinions and ideas that can improve services, nor do they have the desire to share their insights with others (Fudge Schormans and Chambon 2012). There is also a notable lack of training among service providers in disability awareness, making it difficult for providers to understand and meet the needs of women labelled with intellectual disabilities (McGilloway et al. 2020). This is compounded by a lack of accessible information, training and sexual education for people labelled with intellectual disabilities, which is a barrier to empowering women to have a valuable emotional and sexual life (e.g., Hole et al. 2022; Matin et al. 2021).

For decades, people with disabilities have argued for their inclusion in the design and implementation of human services and supports that meet their interests and needs (e.g., Abbas 2020; Charlton 2000; Jones et al. 1984). Likewise, there is a large body of literature on inclusive, participatory or collaborative research with people labelled with intellectual disabilities to increase access and power in the design, implementation and mobilisation of knowledge (e.g., Buettgen 2025; Callus 2024; García Iriarte et al. 2023). This literature argues that these inclusive research processes can and should be used in the development and implementation of services and supports (e.g., Hewitt et al. 2023; Ottmann and Laragy 2010). This argument is supported by the UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (CRPD) (UN General Assembly 2007), which emphasises the rights of persons with disabilities (including women) to full and effective participation and inclusion in society through the development of effective and appropriate measures, including through peer support, to attain and maintain maximum independence to strengthen and extend services and programmes (See Art. 3 General Principles, Art. 6 Women with disabilities and Art. 26 Habilitation and Rehabilitation).

Previous research suggests that peer support has many benefits for women with disabilities who have experienced violence (e.g., Abbas 2020; Collings et al. 2020). Peer support is shown to be effective in strengthening confidence, social support, empathy, acceptance, reducing stigma and providing hope for a better future. In general, peer support values lived experience and nonprofessional vantage points, authentic support for unique forms of empathy and validation, and practical advice (Mead and MacNeil 2006; see also Abbas 2020). For example, Millard (1994) wrote about her experience with a women's group for people with learning disabilities. While not initially formed as a sexual abuse support group, the issue emerged from the women themselves when they felt safe to disclose and discuss their experiences in a group setting, and they reported benefitting from the experience. Given the potential for peer support to benefit women labelled with intellectual disabilities who have experienced sexual violence, more research in this area is needed (Collings et al. 2020).

In this paper, we share our experience as an interdisciplinary team of women labelled with intellectual disabilities, service providers and researchers working in the fields of women's health, disability studies, medical and health sciences and social work, who sought to address this gap in research by hosting a 3‐day virtual symposium in April and May 2022 during the COVID‐19 pandemic, to co‐design research and programming on peer support for women labelled with intellectual disabilities who have experienced violence.

Our project was anchored in critical disability theory and the human rights model of disability. Critical disability theory addresses the social, cultural, political and economic factors that contribute to disability, marginalisation and dis–citizenship (Barnes and Mercer 2010; Oliver 1990; Devlin and Pothier 2006; Thomas 1999) rather than framing disability as an individual misfortune. The theory brings the social construction of disability to the forefront of thinking about ‘normal’ versus ‘abnormal’ and contends that achieving equality, well‐being and empowerment for people with disabilities is not an issue of medicine, health, rehabilitation or charity; rather it is a question of politics, power and powerlessness (Devlin and Pothier 2006).

Similarly, the human rights model of disability, which stems from critical disability theory, conceives people with disabilities as diverse rights‐bearing citizens and embraces substantive and transformative conceptions of equality that address the various barriers that undermine their rights and dignity (Degener 2016). This perspective considers the multiple intersecting identities that persons with disabilities hold and forms of oppression related to their sex, gender, age, race or other characteristics. It is based on the general principles of the CRPD, such as respect for inherent dignity, individual autonomy, participation and inclusion in society and respect for difference. The disability rights perspective focuses on the barriers that people with disabilities face in society and the ways in which they can be dismantled.

Our paper is organised into three parts. First, we outline the design of the symposium and its objective, which was to co‐create terms of reference for future research and programming to advance peer support for women labelled with intellectual disabilities who have experienced sexual violence in Canada. Then we address what we did to prepare for and implement the symposium. We conclude with critical reflections on our co‐creative process and recommendations for future research. Our hope is that other investigators will be encouraged to apply more inclusive processes in their research by learning from and building upon our experiences in Canada and beyond.

Project Background and Research Design

Our project (a 3‐day symposium) was a follow‐up of a pilot study initiated in 2017–2018 by Family Service Toronto (FST) a Toronto‐based social service organisation, in response to an expressed desire from women labelled with intellectual disabilities who experience sexual violence to develop more positive personal relationships and community connections. During the pilot study, FST adapted their sexual assault counselling services to meet the needs of women labelled with intellectual disabilities (hereinafter referred to as women with lived expertise) 2 and created a training curriculum. They trained six peer mentors with lived expertise who had experienced violence (not necessarily sexual) and were not in crisis and then matched them with a mentee. The mentors and mentees both attended counselling. Over the course of 1 year, peer mentors provided peer mentorship, received support from a social worker (every 2 weeks) and participated in five group supervision sessions.

The pilot study tested the feasibility of supplementing existing counselling and support services with peer support. The intervention was premised on the belief that support provided by trained peers (i.e., women with lived expertise who were further along in the healing journey) may be especially helpful in addressing the loneliness and isolation experienced by so many women. Participants completed a quality‐of‐life questionnaire at the beginning of their counselling, after 10 sessions, and again 3 months after. They were also interviewed twice.

The pilot study found that peer mentors with lived expertise can play an important role in supporting women labelled with intellectual disabilities who have experienced sexual violence. Participants expressed positive feelings about their experience in the study, noting the value of peer support counselling, opportunities to be heard, the sharing of lived experiences and learning healing strategies. The peer mentors also benefited from supervision, emotional support and logistical support by the service provider.

The pilot study was the motivation for our project whereby a senior leader at FST (author M.H.) co‐led the development of a 3‐day symposium to engage more women with lived expertise, service providers and researchers from across Canada in dialogue about how to promote authentic and equitable collaboration in future research and programme development.

We used a community‐based participatory research approach (MacKinnon 2018) to develop a 3‐day symposium to draft terms of reference for future research and programming to advance peer support for women with lived expertise. The development of the project involved an advisory committee comprised of women with disabilities including those with lived expertise (e.g., peer mentors from the pilot study including our co‐author K.M.) who informed the goal and objectives of the symposium, as well as FST staff, other health and social service providers, a creative arts facilitator (J.G.) and academic and community‐based researchers from Ontario, British Columbia and Nova Scotia working in the areas of disability, human rights, sexual violence, women's health and trauma counselling (including authors A.F.S., J.D.M., R.M. and V.S.). The symposium planning process began in 2021 and was delayed due to the COVID‐19 pandemic and concerns about health and safety. While originally intended to be in‐person, the symposium was held virtually in April and May 2022. 3 Three groups (women with lived expertise, service providers and researchers) were invited to bring together diverse perspectives, knowledge and experiences in relevant service provision and research to the symposium. The objectives of the symposium were to (1) disseminate knowledge about the pilot study which tested the feasibility of supplementing existing counselling and support services with peer support from women with lived expertise; (2) engage symposium participants to identify gaps and areas for improvement in the peer support program; (3) identify a multisectoral group of partners who are committed to supporting further research on how peer support counselling may improve outcomes for women with intellectual disabilities who have experienced sexual violence and (4) establish terms of reference and other activities for these partners to move this work forward in a timely manner.

Methods

To promote inclusive research during the symposium, we used an arts‐based research approach inspired by experience‐based co‐design (EBCD). ‘The concept of inclusive research epitomizes the transformation away from research on people, to research with them … the concept encapsulates the drive to involve people in the design and conduct of research about them, reach and represent their lived experience, respect them and value different ways of knowing’. (Nind 2017, 278).

Arts‐based research uses artistic forms and expressions to explore, understand, represent and challenge human experiences (Leavy 2020; Wang et al. 2017). Specifically, we co‐created visual imagery using pictures and words to capture individual and collective perspectives, experiences and ideas. We drew from Leavy's (2020) articulation of visual art as a method of ‘aesthetic intervention’ by ‘exposing and altering unequal relations of power, privilege and oppression’ (Leavy 2020, 238). Drawing from bell hooks' (1995) theoretical work, Leavy describes how visual art can not only depict social life as experienced by people from different social locations but also how those lives can be (re)imagined. We used visual art to capture how women with lived expertise, service providers and researchers conceptualise and imagine peer support in relation to mainstream sexual assault services and counselling from their respective social locations.

EBCD was used to facilitate a step‐by‐step, creative and iterative process of collaboration. We chose this approach for its participatory nature, user‐centred design and previous experience using this approach within our team (Bate and Robert 2006; Buettgen et al. 2022; Micsinszki et al. 2022). EBCD begins with a focus on the experiences of persons with lived expertise and service providers, considering the ‘touchpoints’ or memorable moments that define their experiences (Point of Care Foundation n.d.). The touchpoints are then explored in a systematic, collaborative process between researchers, persons with lived expertise and service providers to prioritise areas for improvement and then co‐create solutions (Donetto et al. 2015). Outcomes of this process can include changes to policies, processes, relationships and impacts that can range from improved service experiences to paradigm shifts in ideas and ways of working together in systems and society.

All study procedures were reviewed and approved by the Office of Research Ethics at York University and the FST Research Ethics Board in March 2022.

Participants

Participants were identified through research team networks using a criterion‐based purposeful and snowball sampling strategy. 4 Before participating in the study, all participants were asked to sign an informed consent form which we reviewed verbally and/or in writing. This included the invitation to participate in a demographic questionnaire, small and large group discussions via Zoom with permission to audio and video record all sessions. We discussed potential risks and benefits of participation, and the right to withdraw at any time and data management procedures to protect participant confidentiality and anonymity.

All participants were over the age of 18 years, identified as women, and living in Canada. Participants included three stakeholder groups: women with lived expertise (Group 1), service providers (Group 2) and researchers (Group 3). Group 1 included seven women, some of whom had peer mentorship experience and all had received trauma counselling or group support, were not currently in crisis, and could govern their own lives with support. Two women identified as racialised persons (e.g., Black identified, South Asian, Asian, Latinx). All participants were heterosexual and received disability income support. All Group 1 participants were compensated for their time in preparation and during the symposium with an honourarium above an hourly living wage rate.

Group 2 included 18 service providers (e.g., social workers, psychotherapists, other health and social service providers), three of whom identified as a person with a disability. Three providers identified as racialised persons, one as an Indigenous person and seven identified as members of the LGBTQ+ community. This group was larger in sample size due to the nature of our networks giving us greater access to this group of participants and a target group for our study as we aim to engage service providers in the co‐creation of peer support research and programming.

Group 3 included nine academic and community‐based researchers working in the areas of gender‐based violence and disability, one of whom identified as a person with a disability. Three researchers identified as racialised persons. See Table 1 for an overview of participant demographics.

All symposium sessions and group discussions were audio and video recorded. Qualitative data was collected during small and large group discussions. Each small group had two facilitators and a note taker. The recorded discussions were transcribed verbatim for analysis. Notes were used to provide a supplementary record of the discussion, to capture nonverbal cues, contextual information and nuances that may not be fully conveyed through recordings alone. Notes served as a backup in case of recording failure and used as an additional reference to identify specific comments or themes, saving time during the analysis process.

Data Analysis

Thematic content analysis (Braun and Clarke 2008) was used to analyse multiple data sources including observation notes, transcripts, Zoom Whiteboard and Google Jamboards. After Day 2, the research team (author initials anonymized for peer review) independently reviewed all sources of data and engaged in group discussion to identify key themes raised during Days 1 and 2. A written summary of overarching themes was shared with participants on Day 3 for further discussion and refinement. This member‐checking process is a crucial step in EBCD to confirm findings and interpretations with the participants who shared their experiences (Point of Care Foundation n.d.). This step ensures the accuracy, credibility and relevance of the data collected and analysed. This process is described further below. A review of the final analysis was conducted by the authors of this article, which comprises members of the research team and participants of the symposium, including women with lived expertise.

What We Did

The following sections describe what we did (methods) to reduce barriers and increase access to the symposium as well as our creative process over the course of the 3 days.

In Preparation

Prior to the 1st day of the symposium, participants were asked about any accessibility or accommodation needs to support their full participation (e.g., including personal support workers, closed captioning, sign language interpretation, etc.). All participants were mailed a package of art supplies (sketchbook, colouring pencils, markers and crayons) beforehand. Participants were invited to use the art supplies to express their thoughts during the symposium or engage in verbal, written or digital forms of expression and communication. Some participants made digital art, some used paper and we took screenshots of their work, some sourced their own imagery and others described images which facilitators helped create. Our aim was to provide several flexible options for participation to promote freedom of expression and support all participants to choose how they wanted to engage in dialogue.

To support virtual access to the symposium, all participants were invited to an information and training session to practice using Jamboard and Zoom. Only women with lived expertise attended these sessions, which were provided in plain language for 30–60 min individually or in a group depending on participant availability. Technology support was also available throughout the symposium to provide real‐time assistance. Two women with lived expertise without personal access to technology were invited to use socially distanced computers in a private office space at FST. No other participants requested this participation option.

In addition, women with peer support experience volunteered to offer emotional support to other women with lived expertise during the symposium; and participants were provided with phone numbers of at least two professional counsellors available throughout the symposium.

Day 1: Identifying Touchpoints and Experiences With Peer Support

The 1st day focused on identifying touchpoints in participants' experiences with peer support. Each small participant group (women with lived expertise, service providers, researchers) joined their own private Zoom room. The facilitators welcomed participants, provided an overview of the project and facilitated a group discussion about how to co‐create a safe space for dialogue. Participants were then asked to share an object or visual image that represented their thoughts or experiences with peer support. This activity served as an icebreaker and tool to explore what peer support meant to them. Objects and images included drawings of climbing a mountain, paper people holding hands, a rock and so on (see Figures 1 and 2). These were described as metaphors for peer support representing growth in difficult circumstances, an upward journey, the value of connecting with others, companionship, natural relationships, strength and healing. Some of these images are included in the ‘Drawer of Mementos’ co‐created in Group 1 (women with lived expertise) (Figure 3). 6

This initial activity opened the conversation that allowed facilitators to explore with participants the meaning of the metaphors and understandings of peer support in relation to their own experiences from their relative standpoint. A semi‐structured focus group guide was used to follow up on participants' memorable peer support moments, including positive and negative experiences, challenges and tensions. Participants were also asked to identify gaps and areas for improvement in peer support programming and research, as well as one or two things they wanted to discuss over the next 2 days of the symposium. These reflections were captured using Zoom Whiteboard and Google Jamboard to allow participants to contribute text or images in real time. The small groups worked together to map their reflections and experiences that emerged through discussion. Participants were invited to share their thoughts verbally or use the chat function in Zoom. Participants were also invited to contribute to the interactive whiteboards themselves or have the facilitator draw or type their ideas for them.

Day 2: Brainstorming Solutions to Advance Peer Support

The 2nd day focused on brainstorming solutions for research and programming to advance knowledge and practice around peer support. During this session, each participant group met again on Zoom with their facilitators and note taker, who reviewed the notes and touchpoints discussed during Day 1. Then participants used this data as a starting point for an in‐depth 2‐h brainstorming session. Consistent with an EBCD process, each group brainstormed and developed prototype solutions using Google Jamboard to collectively illustrate how to advance peer support. The facilitators used the boards in a way that encouraged democratic and accessible sharing between participants. The board included guiding discussion questions (e.g., How might we promote research to advance inclusive and accessible peer support for women labelled with intellectual disabilities who have experienced sexual violence?) and instructions on how to utilise the space. An inverted stoplight activity was used to visualise the progression of the EBCD process from brainstorming (green), clustering possible solutions (yellow) and then deciding on a prototype (red). For women with lived expertise, these instructions were presented as a list with reduced text, plain language and imagery that reflected the objects and images shared during session one.

To develop prototype solutions, participants were asked to ‘stick’ ideas on the Google Jamboard. Service providers and researchers brainstormed their ideas using text in a series of sticky notes. The brainstorming process for women with lived expertise included concrete questions about issues and images shared during the first session. Questions included: If peer support was accessible to you, what would that look like? What rules should be followed? What support is needed for peer support workers? This process resulted in a collage of images selected by participants to express their feelings and brainstorm ideas. Each group then clustered similar topics or concepts together on Jamboard into rankings of ‘awesome’, ‘good’ or ‘needs work’. These clusters were used to prototype solutions. Day 3: Consolidating Terms of Reference for Future Research and Programming

Day 3 brought together all stakeholder groups to learn about each other's perspectives and identify key priorities for research and programming. The research team presented the results of a preliminary thematic analysis of all data collected during Days 1 and 2 from across all groups in a draft terms of reference. These were shared with all participants in a screen reader accessible Word document prior to Day 3 and a PowerPoint presentation that was presented live in a large group plenary session.

After the presentation, each small group gathered again to reflect on and refine the draft terms of reference. These reflections were captured using a Jamboard formatted in the same way for all groups to promote synergy between groups (see Figure 6). We asked participants to describe ‘things that ground us’ by adding sticky notes in the grass, ‘things that move us’ by adding sticky notes in boats in water and ‘dreams for the future’ by adding sticky notes in the clouds. Each small group then shared their Jamboard with the large group for consensus building to finalise the terms of reference.

Final reflections by service providers.

The symposium concluded with a shared commitment to continue working on the draft Terms of Reference to reflect the contributions collected during the final day.

Since the completion of this symposium, we have engaged in various forms of advocacy to share learnings and promote peer support in service provision and research including the production of a video summarising our findings and process that has been shared with participants and our networks. We have also presented a knowledge mobilisation workshop for researchers, service providers and self‐ advocates as well as meetings with relevant service providers to campaign for funding for peer support initiatives.

Discussion and Reflections

In this section, we discuss the findings of our symposium in our terms of reference, then share our reflections of doing applied inclusive arts‐based research and co‐design online. This process takes creativity, flexibility, ongoing engagement and willingness to keep exploring until there is shared meaning found.

Terms of Reference for Future Research and Programming

The terms of reference co‐created from the symposium identified shared values and meanings of peer support. Participants indicated that peer support research and programming must be rooted in values of ‘Nothing About Us Without Us’ which resonates with the philosophy and history of the disability rights movement (Charlton 2000). This was further described in terms of mutual understanding, empowerment, respect and kindness in working together toward a common goal of supporting women with intellectual disabilities who have experienced sexual abuse.

Participants also developed a shared understanding of the meaning of peer support as a process of healing, companionship and a form of caring, sharing, listening and connection. This is particularly important because people labelled with intellectual disabilities have chronic experiences of loneliness and isolation (Schormans et al. 2021). However, participants also described the implementation challenges of providing peer support and training requirements. These were described in relation to the need for financial compensation, ongoing emotional support and assurances for peer mentors, effective matching processes, accessible communication, disability‐related accommodations and logistical support. Similar to previous research on peer support, participants also described the challenges and barriers presented by organisational cultures that perpetuate the over‐representation of nondisabled service providers and researchers in peer support (Ibrahim et al. 2020; Pfeiffer et al. 2024). As such, participants developed a series of recommendations to co‐produce an intentional and broadly inclusive approach to the co‐design of accessible peer support training, and provision of emotional and practical supports for mentors. Participants also recommended that other service providing agencies (who are interested or engaged in peer support for people with or without disabilities) should adapt the co‐design process used in our symposium to enhance services and embed peer support in the disability and sexual violence service sectors.

Thus, our research highlights the meaningful and critical contributions of all members of the larger communities of interest in the advancement of peer support for women labelled with intellectual disabilities who have experienced sexual violence. We argue that this inclusive process should bring together multiple vantage points and perspectives to collectively engage those with lived expertise and decision‐making power. To our knowledge, this is the first Canadian initiative in this area. A critical component of our contribution includes the building of partnerships within and outside of the disability service sector.

Reflections on the Symposium Process

The planning and design of the online symposium and our use of participatory visual methods were an iterative process that involved paying close attention to issues that could affect the participation of all groups, including women with lived expertise. We understood that arts‐based work in a virtual environment would be challenging, but given the ongoing effects of the COVID‐19 pandemic and the shift toward using online technology more broadly, we agreed it was important to meet these challenges.

We drew from principles of accessibility in Articles 9 and 21 of the CRPD to identify and remove barriers for women with lived expertise to access, on an equal basis with others, information and communications technologies. We used a combination of plain language materials, real‐time provision of technical and emotional supports as well as choices and opportunities for various arts‐based and other methods of communication to create a virtual space in which participants could meaningfully engage in the project.

Inclusion of people labelled with intellectual disabilities is critical for ensuring effective implementation of programming to meet their needs (Buettgen and Klassen 2020; Joffe 2010; Vanwyck 2022). It also contributes to empowerment of women labelled with intellectual disabilities, ensuring they are included in processes for decision‐making about issues that affect their lives. This is a fundamental element of realising the rights of women with disabilities in accordance with Article 6 of the CRPD and ensuring that research is conducted with them and not just about them.

With these principles in mind, we considered issues of digital equity—defined as a condition in which all individuals and communities have the information technology capacity to fully participate in society (Ragnedda 2020). Digital technologies provided us with the mediums to safely connect across geographic locations in the midst of the pandemic. To promote digital equity we provided presymposium training in plain language, physical access to technology for two women with lived expertise who did not have access to digital technologies (e.g., computers or tablets) and virtual or socially distanced in‐person technical support throughout the symposium. These supports were a particular concern given the significant barriers to digital equity that people labelled with intellectual disabilities experience. We proactively aimed to address barriers to education and training for people labelled with intellectual disabilities in the use of technology, combined with economic constraints resulting from reliance on inadequate financial supports from government which limit access to technology and the internet (Schormans et al. 2021).

We emphasised choice in how participants wanted to express themselves and engaged in an interactive flexible approach to communication and facilitation. If an activity was well received by a group, we embraced that and built on it. For example, on Day 1, women with lived expertise used mostly symbolism to create a collage (see Figure 3 above) so we offered more opportunities to create collages (see Figure 5). Conversely, service providers and researchers preferred to use text and sticky notes to express their thoughts in words. The ability to meet, discuss and create this work over several days with time in‐between gave facilitators the opportunity to learn more about their group and what methods they valued which allowed us to customise the activities to each group. When observing these differences over the first 2 days of the symposium, our creative arts facilitator made a common template for all three groups (see Figure 6) with the same imagery to create synergy between the groups and encourage coming together in development of shared terms of reference for future research and programming. All three final group scenes were unique in the text that participants added via sticky notes, but allowed our rooted ideas along the grass to grow together, our boats to float together and our clouds to define our collective dreams (see Figure 6). This approach brought these three stakeholder groups together into the same plan in the end. This activity also supported everyone to play with the imagery in a way that we feel addressed the typical power imbalances between these stakeholder groups.

The small group discussions and resulting images provided a sense of connection between participants, especially for women with lived expertise. For example, one woman with lived expertise, drew a message surrounded by coloured hearts that said, ‘We all are different, but we all need love the same way’. See Figure 7.

Drawing by a woman with lived expertise.

Reflecting on this drawing and the symposium process, our co‐author Kelly said women with lived expertise ‘were able to openly, safely express ourselves. The artwork gave us a sense of accomplishment because it was our feelings that we expressed’. Likewise, our co‐author Sandra said she was able to bring ‘so much feeling’ into the creative process and still uses art to express her feelings.

Given on our focus on women who have experienced sexual violence, we used a trauma‐informed approach to centre well‐being in all interactions, decisions and processes (Campbell et al. 2019). We proactively provided peer and professional support to all participants, informed by our understanding that too often people labelled with intellectual disabilities are silenced, dismissed and not believed when they disclose acts of violence perpetrated against them (McCarthy 2014; McGilloway et al. 2020; Plan International 2013). Similarly, the rates of criminal charges and conviction of perpetrators of abuse and assault is significantly low (Fudge Schormans and Sobsey 2020). This highlighted the need for the sharing of difficult stories in a space and manner guided by women with lived expertise.

In line with practices of inclusive research, we co‐created guidelines for facilitators to respond in situations when upset occurred. The arts‐based approach was helpful for working through difficult content by offering participants a creative outlet for sharing their perspective and collectively reimagining and re‐conceptualising mainstream sexual assault services to include peer support for women labelled with intellectual disabilities. We viewed such considerations as ‘radical acts of care—caring about, caring for, caregiving, care receiving and caring with’ (Kuri and Fudge Schormans 2022, 6)—in that we recognised and attended to the unjust exclusion, and lack of control, choices and social supports that many women with lived expertise experience in their lives.

Moreover, we recognised power differentials when people with lived expertise, service providers and researchers come together in decision making. We drew from EBCD to bring these different groups together to reflect on their experiences of peer support and sexual violence, disability or other counselling services, working together to identify priorities, design changes and then jointly reflect on their ideas. We facilitated small stakeholder groups to engage in co‐creative practices as a way to make connections, build trust and redistribute power in the decision making process to establish our terms of reference. Similar to previous EBCD research we found small groups were effective in promoting open, honest discussion between participants with similar vantage points (Donetto et al. 2015). In the large group, we intentionally shared experiences and ideas equally from each small group to develop shared understandings.

Limitations and Future Research

This work stems from an interdisciplinary team that included women with lived expertise. Invitations to participate in the symposium were limited to individuals known to the team plus some snowball recruitment (Patton 1990). It is unclear how representative our participants were of the different target groups. For example, all women with lived expertise identified as heterosexual and ‘woman’. The online symposium excluded people who did not wish to participate virtually or lacked the resources to do so.

Some women with lived expertise—including Kelly and Sandra—felt that the symposium would have been better in person. They said that it was difficult to meaningfully connect with other women with lived expertise as their peers in a virtual environment. Moreover, they said that the three half‐day sessions was not a sufficient amount of time for women with lived expertise to fully express their thoughts, ideas, feelings and spend time together. The recommended that future research and programming should adapt the symposium methodology over more days to foster greater connections and insight among women with lived expertise. These recommendations align with previous research to embed peer support in inclusive research by enhancing communication, peer assistance and sharing personal experiences in the research process. For example, Pfeiffer et al. (2024) developed a peer support toolkit with the intention to teach team members with intellectual disabilities how to serve as peer supporters on a research team. While our research included peer mentors for women labelled with intellectual disabilities who have experience sexual violence, future research could add a component of peer mentorship in the research process itself.

Moreover, future research could adapt our approach for inclusive co‐design among a more diverse group of persons with disabilities, in the broader human service sector, with consideration of other cultural diversities, as well as in other geographic locations. For example, the overrepresentation of LGBTQ+ identified service providers (7/18) in our project is striking but did not arise during group discussions or a relevant category in our thematic analysis. These demographic characteristics and positionalities are worthy of more explicit consideration in future research.

Conclusion

Our applied research model adopts an inclusive co‐design process, guided by human rights to promote equality, equity and inclusion and encourage positive language to change discourse about people labelled with intellectual disabilities and their role in research and health and social services. As sexual violence against women with disabilities is a global issue, our findings amplify the need to work with women with lived expertise. In accordance with Article 16 of the CRPD, we advocate for respectful and inclusive planning and programming to promote legislative, administrative, social, educational and other measures to protect all people from all forms of violence, abuse and exploitation.

This study also adds to knowledge of inclusive arts‐based research through an integration of EBCD and conducted virtually. In this article, we illustrate the creative and collaborative ways in which co‐design research can advance disability rights through explicit considerations of accessibility, freedom of expression and digital equity. We present visual images that illustrate co‐creative practices used to collectively work toward freedom from violence and abuse. We show how these processes can be used to authentically and meaningfully include women with lived expertise in the design of research and programming. We show how a human rights based approach orients women with lived expertise as active participants in the system of services and supports that have historically been delivered by nondisabled service providers.

There is a need to shift power and control over service design and provision to people who use services by shifting people labelled with intellectual disabilities from passive and dependent service recipients to active decision makers and collaborators. In our project, people labelled with intellectual disabilities acted as powerful collaborators and critical advisors working alongside nonlabelled academic and community‐based researchers and service providers. We believe the combination of our perspectives to be relevant to the field of research in intellectual disabilities by effectively bridging lived experience, academic and professional knowledges.
