Systematic optimization of a domestic nose-only aerosol exposure system for respiratory pathogen research in nonhuman primates
Authors: Qingni Li, Yunfeng Chen, Wenjing Zhang, Zhuo Chen, Wanlu Hua, Chunyang Li, Yuanlang Liu, Yi Tian, Kaili Sun, Ruyu Wang, Xiuli Xu, Yuwei Li, Yan Zeng, Jia Lu, Zejun Wang

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Scientific questions: Aerosol exposure provides the most physiologically relevant route for respiratory pathogen infection models in nonhuman primates (NHPs). Introduction

Epidemics caused by respiratory pathogens, such as severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus (SARS-CoV), influenza virus, respiratory syncytial virus and SARS-CoV-2, have and will continue to pose significant global threats [1]. Developing effective animal models is crucial for understanding these diseases. Based on their high similarity to humans in anatomy, physiology, and immunology, nonhuman primates (NHPs) are widely used in research on human respiratory infectious diseases [2], [3]. The data obtained to date for NHPs exposed to various respiratory pathogens confirm the relevance of NHPs as preclinical models for the evaluation of drugs and vaccines and for studies of the clinical outcome of respiratory infectious diseases [4]. When establishing animal models, different transmission routes can lead to variations in disease progression and symptoms [5]. The United States Food and Drug Administration recommends using exposure methods that closely approximate human exposure pathways to establish animal infection models [6]. Therefore, improving the pathogen exposure process to simulate natural infection processes as closely as possible is crucial [4].

Compared to commonly used nasal or intratracheal instillation of respiratory pathogens, aerosol infection can more realistically reproduce the natural exposure conditions of respiratory contact with pathogens. Systems for exposing experimental animals to aerosols have been continuously developed and improved for decades to provide controlled, characterized delivery of pathogenic aerosols to the respiratory system [7], [8]. Various systems have been developed to accommodate or restrain experimental subjects and provide controlled atmospheres for exposure. Inhalation exposure systems typically include components such as aerosol generation and delivery, exposure chambers, exposure concentration monitoring, and exhaust gas treatment [9]. Generally, based on different animal exposure methods, exposure systems can be divided into whole-body chambers, head-only exposures, nose- or mouth-only methods, lung-only exposures, and partial-lung exposures [10]. Compared to other exposure methods, nose-only exposure can concentrate exposure primarily in the respiratory tract, minimize skin or fur contamination, use less exposure material, and has good containment. Therefore, nose-only systems have emerged as particularly useful for exposing laboratory animals to hazardous airborne materials [11].

Although rodent nose-only inhalation exposure systems have a long history of use, studies using nose-only aerosol exposure systems for infectious disease experiments in NHPs are rare. S. Sharpe et al. [12], [13] used a modified Henderson device controlled by an AeroMP (Biaera) control unit to deliver Mycobacterium tuberculosis aerosols to each sedated animal's nostrils through modified veterinary anesthesia masks for related research. However, in China, there are no reports of infectious pathogen exposure in NHPs via nose-only inhalation routes. One important reason is that inhalation systems for large animals like nonhuman primates often require customization according to the laboratory, and issues such as material loss in delivery lines and concentration testing in chambers need to be evaluated before exposure begins to ensure the equipment can stably and accurately expose experimental animals to aerosols at expected concentrations [9].

As the most impactful respiratory infectious disease in recent years, coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) research helps provide experience for scientific responses to future emerging infectious diseases. This study aimed to construct a SARS-CoV-2 aerosol infection model in cynomolgus macaques (Macaca fascicularis) through nose-only exposure. However, when beginning experiments, it was found that the SARS-CoV-2 aerosol concentration at the mask could not reach the doses reported in other studies [13], [14]. Therefore, the nose-only aerosol exposure system was first optimized to enhance its spray factor (SF). The SF is defined as the ratio of the aerosol concentration (measured by an impinger) to the nebulizer concentration, which facilitates standardized comparisons between different exposure events, chambers, and environmental settings [15]. Using this optimized system, a SARS-CoV-2 inhalation infection model was preliminarily evaluated in cynomolgus macaques. The primary objectives of this study were to systematically optimize and evaluate a domestically developed nose-only aerosol exposure system and to assess its feasibility for establishing a SARS-CoV-2 aerosol infection model in cynomolgus macaques. These findings may provide a technical reference for future inhalation studies of respiratory pathogens.

Materials and methods

Biosafety and ethics statement

All SARS-CoV-2 Delta work was performed within an animal biosafety level 3 (ABSL-3) facility. Samples were collected after the animals were anesthetized with 5 mg/kg Zoletil 50 (Virbac).

Cells, viruses and infectivity assays

Cell culture and virus propagation

The SARS-CoV-2 Delta variant (B.1.617.2) used in this study was obtained from Hubei, China, and propagated in the biosafety Level 3 laboratory of Wuhan Institute of Biological Products Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). Vero cells were maintained in Dulbecco's modified Eagle medium (DMEM, Gibco) supplemented with 10% newborn calf serum, 50 IU/mL penicillin, and 50 µg/mL streptomycin at 37 °C, 5% CO2. Viral titers were determined by 50% cell-culture infectious doses (CCID50) assay. Virus was aliquoted and stored at − 80 °C, and diluted to varying concentrations for aerosol generation.

CCID50 assay

Confluent Vero cells in 96-well plates were infected with 10-fold serial dilutions of SARS-CoV-2 samples in DMEM containing 2.5% newborn calf serum. Plates were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 4 days. Cytopathic effect (CPE) was assessed microscopically compared to uninfected controls, and viral titers calculated using the Kärber method [16].

Aerosol exposure system evaluation and optimization

Design and biosafety containment of the aerosol exposure system

This study was performed based on a nonhuman primate nose-only aerosol exposure system (HRH-LMNE3986, Beijing Huironghe Technology Co., Ltd.). A schematic of the system is shown in Fig. 1. The system comprises five core components: a control module, an aerosol generation module, an animal exposure module, an environmental monitoring module, and an exhaust gas treatment module. The entire apparatus is housed within an ABSL-3 laboratory. To ensure biosafety, all infectious aerosol pathways are enclosed in a large stainless steel negative-pressure shielding cabinet.

Schematic of the original nose-only aerosol exposure system for nonhuman primates. The apparatus generally integrates a control module (flow controller), aerosol generation module (aerosol generator, cross-flow diluter, plenum chamber), animal exposure module (nose-only mask, head hood, primate restraint chair), environmental monitoring module (environmental monitoring sensor, aerosol sampler), and exhaust gas treatment module (HEPA filter). Components that handle infectious aerosols are enclosed within a stainless-steel negative-pressure shielding cabinet. HEPA-filtered compressed air drives the aerosol generator; produced aerosols are mixed in the plenum chamber and delivered to up to six restraint chairs via individual nose-only masks, each enclosed in a secondary containment head hood. All exhaust and sampling lines terminate with HEPA filters. Abbreviation: HEPA, high efficiency particulate air.

In the original configuration, high efficiency particulate air (HEPA)-filtered dry compressed air (dew point: −100 °C to −95.9 °C), equilibrated to the laboratory ambient temperature (21 °C–23 °C), drives the generator to produce aerosols, which subsequently enter a central plenum chamber for airflow distribution. The aerosols are then transported via tubing to the animals' nose-only mask. This setup supports the simultaneous exposure of up to six NHPs. During operation, the system utilizes automated components to actively maintain a three-tier negative-pressure protective gradient cascade: ranging from the plenum chamber (−100 Pa to −50 Pa) and the head hood (which encloses the nose-only mask to serve as a secondary containment layer, −20 Pa) down to the overarching shielding cabinet (−10 Pa). All exhaust air is HEPA-filtered before release into the laboratory ventilation system. At the conclusion of the exposure experiments, the system utilizes pre-installed dedicated vaporized hydrogen peroxide (VHP) interfaces to facilitate disinfection of the internal tubing pathways and filters. Detailed information regarding the system's dimensions, materials, airflow control parameters, and disinfection are illustrated in Fig. S1 to Fig. S4.

System performance assessment

Viral loss during aerosol generation

Two aerosol generators were evaluated for SARS-CoV-2 aerosol generation performance: a vibrating mesh nebulizer and a Collison 6-jet nebulizer (both from Beijing Huironghe Technology Co., Ltd.). Assessment included viral infectivity loss, nebulization efficiency, and particle size distribution. To assess the impact of aerosolization on viral infectivity, both nebulizers were operated for a 10-minute duration. For the vibrating mesh nebulizer, 8 mL of SARS-CoV-2 viral suspension was aerosolized directly into a T25 cell culture flask. Following nebulization and a 3-minute settling period, residual droplets were collected by gentle flask rotation. For the Collison 6-jet nebulizer, aerosols generated at an airflow rate of 12.5 L/min were captured using sterile collection tubes connected to the outlet. Viral titers were quantified pre-nebulization (0 min) and post-nebulization (10 min) to determine the loss of infectivity. The nebulization efficiency of both nebulizers was evaluated by measuring their weight before and after nebulization, with the weight loss calculated to determine the amount of liquid nebulized. Inactivated SARS-CoV-2 suspensions were used to characterize the optical equivalent diameters of aerosols generated by both the Collison and vibrating mesh nebulizers. Measurements were conducted approximately 20 cm from the generator outlet using a handheld optical particle counter (HANDHELD 3016-IAQ, Lighthouse Worldwide Solutions) with a sampling flow rate of 2.83 L/min. To prevent particle concentrations from exceeding the instrument's concentration limit, the aerosol stream (12.5 L/min) was mixed with approximately 70 L/min of HEPA-filtered clean air before measurement. The detection range covered six size channels from 0.3 μm to 25.0 μm.

Viral loss during aerosol transport

According to the design of the nose-only exposure system, aerosols generated by the aerosol generator were carried by HEPA-filtered clean air into the plenum chamber, where they mixed with dilution air before being transported via tubing to the nose-only masks. In this test, a single cynomolgus macaque infection scenario was assumed. A Collison 6-jet nebulizer was used for aerosol generation, with a ventilation flow rate of 12.5 L/min and a dilution flow rate set to zero. Aerosols were collected using AGI-30 impingers (Beijing Huironghe Technology Co., Ltd.) at the inlet and outlet of the plenum chamber, as well as at the nose-only mask. SARS-CoV-2 viral titers in the collected aerosols were measured to assess aerosol losses at different stages of the transport process. Viral ribonucleic acid (RNA) was quantified by quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR), and all transport loss calculations were based on RNA concentrations to minimize sampling-derived artifacts on infectivity.

Viral loss during aerosol sampling

To accurately monitor aerosol concentrations at the nose-only masks of cynomolgus macaque, the sampling performance of an all-glass impinger (AGI-30) was evaluated for collecting SARS-CoV-2 aerosols. The AGI-30 was operated at a flow rate of 12.5 L/min with 20 mL of collection medium. Two sequential experiments were conducted: First, the effect of the 10-minute sampling process on viral viability within the AGI-30 was assessed. Second, an additional AGI-30 was connected in series after the first sampler to evaluate aerosol escape by comparing viral titers recovered from both devices. This setup allowed for quantification of viral loss due to high-impact impingement and post-collection aerosol dispersion.

System optimization design and implementation

Based on the findings from Sections 2.3.2, which evaluated SARS-CoV-2 aerosol viability during generation, transport, and sampling, several modifications were implemented to optimize the domestically developed nose-only aerosol exposure system for nonhuman primates. While retaining the original air supply, control, and exhaust treatment modules, the aerosol generation and exposure modules within the negative-pressure cabinet were redesigned. To accommodate intraoperative general anesthesia, reduce per-animal handling time, improve animal welfare, mitigate biosafety risks, and minimize aerosol transmission losses, the original large-volume stainless steel plenum chamber was replaced with a compact mixing chamber containing built-in screening plates (Fig. S2). Additionally, the transfer tubing between the plenum and the mask was eliminated, and the new mixing chamber was directly connected to the nonhuman primate's mask. Consequently, the system was reconfigured for single-animal exposure.

As illustrated in Fig. 2, SARS-CoV-2 viral suspension was aerosolized using a vibrating mesh nebulizer. The generated aerosols were introduced into the compact mixing chamber and combined with 12 L/min of clean air before direct delivery to the primate's mask. The mask incorporated two dedicated ports: one for real-time internal pressure monitoring and another for aerosol sampling. Samples were collected using an AGI-30 impinger operated at a flow rate of 12.5 L/min. Following collection, viral titers were determined from the recovered samples. To minimize aerosol leakage, the sampling flow was set slightly higher than the inlet flow, maintaining a slight negative pressure throughout the exposure tubing. During system evaluation, simulated exposures using mannequin cynomolgus macaques were conducted. Viral titers were measured from both the nebulizer and the sampler to calculate aerosol concentrations and verify whether target exposure doses were achieved.

Functional validation in nonhuman primates

Aerosol exposure of animals

Three cynomolgus macaques were exposed to SARS-CoV-2 via the enhanced nose-only system. Aerosols were generated using virus concentrations of 107.25-107.50 CCID50/mL and delivered using 12 L/min airflow for 10 min. Aerosol sampling used AGI-30 impingers with 20 mL DMEM supplemented with 0.25% newborn calf serum. The virus aerosol concentration at the mask is uncorrected for potential minor perturbations arising from the mask-side sampling flow. Respiratory rate was continuously monitored by visual counting. Inhaled infectious dose (CCID50) was calculated as the product of aerosol concentration and total inhaled air volume. Due to the absence of a plethysmography chamber for direct measurement, tidal volume was estimated at 7 mL/kg body weight under anesthesia [17], though individual variations may occur due to physiological differences and anesthesia depth.

Clinical monitoring and sampling

Clinical symptoms and feeding behavior were monitored daily post-infection. In addition to nasal, pharyngeal, and rectal swab sample collections, body temperature and weight data were recorded at each time point (1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 9, 11, 14, 17, 21, 24, 28, 31, and 35 days post-exposure) to assess the health status of the animals. All swab samples were used for quantitative viral load analysis. Blood samples were obtained at 0, 3, 7, 14, 17, 21, 24, 28, 31, and 35 days post-exposure. At 35 days post-exposure, remaining animals were euthanized and necropsied. Tissue samples from all seven lung lobes were collected for viral analysis. Additionally, histopathological analysis was performed on all lung tissue samples to assess pulmonary lesions. One animal (Animal ID: 174205C) with pre-existing severe alopecia developed hypothermia and reduced activity by 11 days post-exposure and was euthanized at 14 days post-exposure in accordance with welfare protocols, and it was therefore excluded from the main comparative analyses. Virological, serological, and pulmonary pathological data collected from this animal up to the time of euthanasia are provided in Supplementary material (Table S1–S3, and Fig. S5).

Laboratory analyses

Neutralization assay

CPE-based microneutralization quantified serum neutralizing antibody titers. Two-fold serial serum dilutions were mixed with 200 CCID50 SARS-CoV-2 in DMEM with 2.5% newborn calf serum, incubated, and inoculated onto Vero cell monolayers (100 µL/well). After 4-day incubation at 37 °C with 5% CO2, CPE was assessed microscopically. Neutralization titer (NT50) was defined as the highest serum dilution completely inhibiting CPE.

Viral load quantification

Swab samples were vortexed (30 s) and centrifuged [4,000 revolutions per minute (rpm)], 10 min, 4 °C. Tissue samples (100 mg) were homogenized in 1 mL PBS and centrifuged. RNA was extracted from 200 µL of supernatant using 500 µL lysis buffer and 20 µL proteinase K, incubated at 70 °C for 15 min, then processed using an automated nucleic acid extraction system (DAAN Gene). SARS-CoV-2 RNA was detected using a commercial kit [polymerase chain reaction (PCR)-fluorescent probe method; DAAN Gene] on ABI QuantStudio™ 5 Real-Time PCR System (detection limit: 102.3 copies/mL).

Pathology examination

Post-mortem examinations were conducted on euthanized cynomolgus macaques. All seven pulmonary lobes were collected, and the tissue samples were fixed in 10% neutral formalin (Servicebio, China) for 14 days. Following fixation, tissues were embedded in paraffin, sectioned at a thickness of approximately 5 µm, and subjected to hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. Stained sections were examined and imaged under an optical microscope for histopathological analysis.

Results

Systematic evaluation of the original aerosol exposure system

Minimal viral viability loss during aerosol generation

Viral titer analysis demonstrated that the aerosolization process did not significantly compromise SARS-CoV-2 infectivity for either nebulizer during the 10-minute operation period (Fig. 3A). The Collison 6-jet nebulizer, which requires an external air flow of 12.5 L/min for aerosol generation, achieved a nebulization efficiency of 0.23 ± 0.01 mL/min. In comparison, the vibrating mesh nebulizer, which generates aerosols through vibration without additional air flow, demonstrated a comparable efficiency of 0.25 ± 0.02 mL/min (t-test, P > 0.05). As shown in Fig. 3B, under unified optical particle counting measurement, the vibrating mesh nebulizer produced a broader and more polydisperse particle size distribution compared to the Collison nebulizer. The conventional Collison nebulizer generated an aerosol profile in which the majority of particles (57.6%) resided in the fine respirable size range (1.0 µm–2.5 µm). In contrast, the aerosol produced by the vibrating mesh nebulizer was distributed across a broader range of particle sizes (0.3 µm–5.0 µm), with substantial proportions in the submicron (0.3 µm–1.0 µm, 16.4%), fine (1.0 µm–2.5 µm, 39.2%), and coarse respirable (2.5 µm–5.0 µm, 43.1%) fractions. This distribution pattern shows closer alignment with the wide size range of aerosols naturally produced during human expiratory activities (e.g., breathing, speaking, and coughing), which typically span from submicron to several micrometers [18], [19], [20]. Based on its superior ability to generate an aerosol with a polydisperse size spectrum that qualitatively aligns with that of human expiratory aerosols, the vibrating mesh nebulizer was selected for subsequent in vivo challenge studies.

Aerosol transport losses identified in the plenum chamber

Aerosolized SARS-CoV-2 generated by the nebulizer was carried by clean air into the plenum chamber for mixing, then transported through tubing to the nose-only mask. An integrated analysis of aerosol transport losses was conducted across the aerosol exposure system. Aerosols were collected at the inlet and outlet of the plenum chamber, as well as at the nose-only mask, and SARS-CoV-2 RNA was quantified by qPCR. To minimize sampling-derived artifacts, all calculations were based on RNA concentrations rather than infectious viral titers. Analysis of the SF revealed that transit through the plenum chamber resulted in a significant reduction in SF (ΔSF = 0.45, t-test, P < 0.01) (Fig. 3C). In contrast, the tubing section between the plenum chamber outlet and the exposure mask induced a more modest, non-significant reduction (ΔSF = 0.20, t-test, P > 0.05) (Fig. 3D).

Sampling-induced viral inactivation and physical escape

To quantify the previously indicated loss of viral viability during AGI-30 impinger sampling, two experiments were conducted. Initial assessment confirmed a significant reduction in viral titer, with a decrease of 2.79 log10-unit observed after sampling a solution of 103.25 CCID50/mL for 10 min (Fig. 3E). Based on previous findings that newborn calf serum preserves viral viability [21], this loss was effectively mitigated by supplementing the collection medium with 0.25% newborn calf serum in all subsequent work. The physical capture efficiency of the sampler was further evaluated using a two-stage serial configuration. Analysis of the serial samplers demonstrated that the first stage did not capture all aerosols, with a quantifiable fraction (5.82% ± 1.14%) escaping to the second stage (Fig. 3F), thus defining the escape rate of the system.

Performance enhancement achieved through system optimization

An analysis of SARS-CoV-2 infectivity loss within the domestic nose-only exposure system revealed that the sampling process was the primary contributor to viral viability reduction, accounting for 67.88% of the total loss, followed by aerosol transport through tubing at 18.44% (Table 1). Based on these systematic evaluations identifying the primary sources of viral loss, targeted optimization strategies were implemented to enhance system performance: (1) adoption of a vibrating mesh nebulizer for aerosol generation; (2) reduction of the plenum chamber volume; (3) minimization of connecting tubing length, and (4) addition of 0.25% newborn calf serum to the AGI-30 sampling medium as a protective agent.

Post-optimization, the SF increased by a mean of 2.19 log10-units (Fig. 4), corresponding to a 155-fold enhancement in overall system efficiency. Preliminary evaluation of SARS-CoV-2 infection following aerosol exposure in cynomolgus macaques

The enhanced system achieved target aerosol concentrations, with estimated inhaled doses ranging from 105.21–105.72 CCID50 per animal based on body weight-adjusted tidal volume calculations and real-time aerosol concentration monitoring via AGI-30 impinger sampling. At necropsy (35 days post-exposure), viral RNA was detected in multiple lung lobes of both animals (Fig. 5D), suggesting pulmonary viral distribution. Animal 195626C showed higher viral loads in lung tissues than animal 186193C, which was generally consistent with the higher viral RNA levels observed in its respiratory swabs.

Both animals developed detectable neutralizing antibody responses, with seroconversion occurring by 14 days post-exposure. Neutralizing antibody titers reached NT50 values of 90–256 and remained elevated through 35 days post-exposure (Fig. 5E), indicating the induction of an immune response following aerosol exposure.

Histopathological analysis of lung tissues collected at 35 days post-exposure showed chronic interstitial inflammation accompanied by structural repair/remodeling (Fig. 5F). The main findings included diffuse thickening of alveolar septa with infiltration of chronic inflammatory cells, focal residual alveolar wall injury, and the formation of fused cystic cavities. These findings are consistent with pulmonary involvement following SARS-CoV-2 exposure, although interpretation is limited by the small number of evaluable animals.

These results demonstrate that the optimized domestic nose-only aerosol exposure system achieved its primary functional objectives: stable aerosol generation and delivery, accurate dose monitoring, and the induction of measurable virological and immunological responses following respiratory exposure. The system's ability to deliver infectious doses within the target range and generate infection-associated findings supports the effectiveness of the implemented optimization strategies and its potential utility for respiratory pathogen research applications.

Discussion

The development of reliable inhalation exposure systems is fundamental to advancing respiratory pathogen research and preclinical therapeutic evaluation. This study systematically evaluated and improved a domestically produced nose-only aerosol exposure system, achieving significant improvements in aerosol generation, transmission efficiency, and overall performance through targeted optimization strategies.

Performance comparison with international commercial systems

Comparative analysis with established commercial systems reveals that the optimized domestic system achieves competitive performance metrics. The Biaera AeroMP system, considered the “gold standard” for aerosol exposure research, demonstrates a SF of approximately 10-6.35 for SARS-CoV-2 in Henderson apparatus configurations [23]. While commercial Collison nebulizers typically generate particles in the 1–3 μm range with geometric standard deviations of 1.2–1.4 [23], [24], whereas the vibrating mesh nebulizer employed in this study more closely approximates natural human respiratory aerosol characteristics. The 2.19 log10-units improvement in SF achieved through systematic optimization positions the domestic system within the operational range required for nonhuman primate studies, potentially offering superior particle size distribution for deep lung deposition.

Aerosol generator selection and optimization

The selection of aerosol generators is fundamental to achieving consistent and reproducible exposure conditions. Based on different generation principles, aerosol generators can be classified into twin-fluid atomization, centrifugal nebulization, ultrasonic nebulization, vibrating mesh, and electrospray ionization [25]. During the 10-minute nebulization process, both vibrating mesh nebulizers and Collison nebulizers maintained viral viability with minimal loss of SARS-CoV-2 activity. Generator-induced viral activity damage is related to testing conditions such as aerosol generation duration and pathogen type [26]. Therefore, testing the impact of aerosol generation on pathogen activity before conducting exposure experiments is recommended.

Comparative analysis of optical particle size distributions revealed that the vibrating mesh nebulizer generated a broader, more polydisperse aerosol profile than the conventional Collison nebulizer. In contrast to the relatively monodisperse aerosol produced by the Collison nebulizer, particles from the vibrating mesh device spanned a wider size spectrum. This polydisperse distribution more closely approximates the heterogeneous size ranges of aerosols naturally emitted during human respiratory activities (e.g., breathing, speaking, coughing) [18], [19], [20]. Given its superior ability to simulate these physiologically relevant aerosol characteristics, along with its demonstrated efficacy in achieving lower respiratory tract deposition in nonhuman primates [27], the vibrating mesh nebulizer was selected for all subsequent exposure experiments.

Aerosol transmission pathway optimization

Aerosol transmission pathways represent a critical factor influencing particle loss during delivery. To stabilize airflow pressure and uniformly distribute aerosols, the original system incorporated a plenum chamber downstream of the aerosol generator. However, the large volume and extended residence time of this plenum chamber became the primary site for particle deposition during transmission, resulting in a 0.45 log10-unit reduction in SF when aerosols passed through the chamber. This loss is primarily attributed to gravitational settling, inertial impaction, and diffusional deposition mechanisms [28].

To mitigate these losses, the original plenum chamber was removed and the transmission path length was substantially shortened. Nevertheless, an appropriately sized chamber at the aerosol generator outlet remains essential for several critical functions: diluting and mixing newly generated aerosols to ensure uniform concentration distribution, selectively depositing larger particles through gravitational settling to optimize particle size distribution, and reducing pipeline blockage risk to improve transmission stability during extended exposure periods. Consequently, the original large-volume plenum chamber was replaced with a compact deposition chamber and screening plates were incorporated to intercept oversized aerosol particles.

Exposure configuration and biosafety considerations

In specialized biosafety containment equipment, space limitations typically necessitate single-animal exposure for large animal models [27]. Despite adequate space availability, the multi-animal exposure configuration was modified to single-animal exposure. While this approach reduces experimental throughput, it provides several significant advantages, including more precise individual dose control, shorter animal handling time per experiment, and reduced animal stress responses. Additionally, aerosol exposure contaminates experimental animals' fur, posing serious biosafety risks when handling highly pathogenic agents such as SARS-CoV-2. The single-animal exposure system reduces operational complexity, effectively minimizes environmental contamination risk, and enhances biosafety protection during the exposure process.

Aerosol sampling and viability preservation

Accurate aerosol concentration monitoring is crucial for dose estimation and experimental reproducibility. The AGI-30 is commonly used as a standard sampler for bioaerosol sampling, with sampling efficiency typically related to particle size, sampling medium volume, sampling flow rate, and other factors [29]. Through cascade sampling tests, approximately 5.82% ± 1.14% of viral particles were not captured by the AGI-30 under experimental conditions, which had minimal impact on aerosol monitoring.

Of particular concern is the time-dependent decline in viral viability during the sampling process. Adding 0.25% newborn calf serum to the sampling medium proved effective in maintaining viral viability, confirming previous research on the protective role of protein additives during aerosol collection [21]. This finding emphasizes the importance of using protective media during aerosol sampling.

System performance evaluation and broader applications

Following comprehensive improvements to the aerosol exposure system, to achieve aerosol concentrations exceeding 103.50 CCID50/L at the exposure port, viral suspensions with titers of only 106.50 CCID50/mL were required. This performance level meets the requirements established in published nonhuman primate SARS-CoV-2 infection studies [14], [22]. Application of the improved system for SARS-CoV-2 inhalation infection in cynomolgus macaques resulted in observed viral shedding and seroconversion, indicating successful infection consistent with reports in the literature [30], demonstrating that the optimized aerosol exposure system can be used for inhalation infection of respiratory pathogens.

The optimization framework established in this study demonstrates broad applicability across respiratory pathogens. Successful aerosol exposure models using similar AeroMP-based systems have been developed for Mycobacterium tuberculosis [achieving ultra-low doses of < 10 colony-forming unit (CFU)], Ebola virus, Middle East respiratory syndrome coronavirus (MERS-CoV), and mpox virus in nonhuman primates [12], [31], [32], [33], [34], [35], [36]. These studies consistently utilize comparable aerosol generation principles and sampling methodologies, suggesting that the optimization strategies reported here—particularly the vibrating mesh nebulizer selection, transmission path minimization, and protective sampling medium enhancement—could be systematically applied to establish domestic models for other high-priority respiratory pathogens. The documented success of low-dose aerosol challenges (5–50 CFU) for tuberculosis research further validates the technical feasibility of extending this optimization approach to bacterial pathogens, supporting broader availability of characterized aerosol exposure platforms.

Finally, this work demonstrates the initial feasibility of developing and optimizing a domestically produced aerosol exposure system. By presenting a detailed optimization process and initial evaluation data, it contributes to the technical resources available for respiratory infectious disease research. The optimized system represents a domestically developed approach that may complement existing commercial aerosol exposure platforms.

Research limitations and technical development directions

Several limitations must be considered. First, the inability to quantify complete viral particle capture efficiency introduces uncertainty in dose calculations. Second, key aerodynamic parameters (e.g., mass median aerodynamic diameter, geometric standard deviation) were not directly measured due to technical constraints, and we assumed that the particle size distribution at the generation source equates to that at the animal mask overlooks potential aerosol aging (e.g., deposition, agglomeration, and evaporation) during transport. Third, calculating the delivered dose relied on estimated tidal volumes based on body mass. Fluctuations in anesthesia depth and individual physiological variations can significantly shift actual minute volumes, introducing inherent approximations into the dosage. Fourth, the use of non-humidified dilution air potentially altered the relative humidity of the inhaled mixture. Unquantified humidity fluctuations could potentially impact respiratory physiology and viral activity. Fifth, the limited animal sample size and the lack of sham-exposure or alternative-route control groups constrain the statistical power for assessing infection outcomes and system-specific effects. Finally, optimization focused primarily on SARS-CoV-2 in cynomolgus macaques, and different pathogens may exhibit varying aerosol characteristics.

Future technical development should focus on: (1) developing real-time aerosol and environment (temperature/humidity) monitoring technologies to improve dose accuracy; (2) incorporating direct respiratory volume measurement systems (e.g., respiratory inductive plethysmography) for anesthetized animals to refine exposure estimates; (3) expanding the pathogen validation scope and conducting comparative studies across different nonhuman primate species to establish standardized methodologies; and (4) establishing industry standards and quality control systems for aerosol exposure systems.
