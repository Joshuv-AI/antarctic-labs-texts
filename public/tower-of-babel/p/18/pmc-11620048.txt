Fabrication and comparison of interlaminar fracture toughness behaviour of glass epoxy composites with recycled milled Kevlar-carbon hybrid fillers
Authors: Indhumathi Elango, V. Arumugam, Hom N. Dhakal
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Current research uses a novel recycled milled carbon (rmCF), recycled milled Kevlar (rmKF), and innovative Hybrid fillers (rmHF) of both to increase glass/epoxy composite laminate delamination resistance. This study examines how crack propagation and fibre orientation affect laminated composite delamination fracture toughness. Recycled milled Fillers in the interlayer increase stiffness, delamination resistance, and fracture toughness by increasing the energy needed to crack the interlaminar domain. Here, Mode I, Mode II, and Mixed Mode (I/II) with GIGII = 25 %, 50 % and 75 % were studied for four different Interface fibre orientations. It appears [06/902/06] increased delamination toughness. Adding the novel recycled milled fillers improved delamination resistance. Among the three fillers, rmCF increased fracture toughness by 271 % and rmHF and rmKF composites had 220 % and 182 % higher fracture toughness. The synergy compensated for Kevlar's lower fracture toughness in the hybrid (rmHF). SEM analysis of fractured surfaces revealed crack deflection, individual debonding, and filler/matrix interlocking, all of which increase fracture toughness on different levels.

Introduction

Fibre breakage, matrix cracking, fibre/matrix debonding, fibre pull-out, plugging, and interlaminar delamination are all possible fracture modes in fibre-reinforced composites [1]. While the primary failure mode to highlight may vary depending on the nature of the damage, many modern laminated composite structures fail by delamination [2]. The critical strain energy release rate GIC, GIIC of unidirectional laminate (UD) can be identified with the aid of standardized tests such as double cantilever beam (DCB) [3] and End notch flexure(ENF) [4] test. However, most practical uses necessitate multidirectional laminates (MD), in which delamination occurs within layers with different fibre orientations, despite the convenience of UD specimens for testing needs.

It has been reported that crack branching or delamination that departs from the middle plane is seen in multidirectional layups [5]. The test would fail to characterize the interface of interest if the delamination moved away from the original defect plane. Therefore, it is likely that only the initiation phase of delamination resistance can be determined with the DCB test on multi-directional laminates [6]. Since the ENF test for Mode II can be numerically established using beam theory, it sees the most use. However, there are two shortcomings; one is the inability to prevent crack closure. Due to crack closure, frictional force is induced at the crack surface, which can affect fracture toughness [[7], [8], [9]]. The beam specimen configuration is the second issue. The ENF test's load-deflection behaviour often deviates from elementary beam theory [[10], [11], [12], [13], [14]]. Deformation around the crack tip and deflection due to the shearing force is responsible for this deviation. Although theoretically correctable, the correction procedure is often so time-consuming and laborious that using it to obtain fracture mechanics properties is impractical.

The mixed-mode bending (MMB) test combines the double cantilever beam (DCB) test, the standard for Mode I toughness, with the end notched flexure (ENF) test, the standard for Mode II toughness. Adjusting the lever loading position “c” determines the ratio of Mode I to Mode II loading during an MMB test. The MMB test has several advantages, such as the ability to test over nearly the entire range of Mode I to Mode II ratios with a single specimen configuration, the use of a closed-form model to calculate toughness values from measured quantities, and a mixed-mode ratio that remains essentially constant during delamination growth [15]. The mixed mode test (GIGII = 4/3) on carbon/epoxy UD and [45/−45] specimens was investigated by Choi et al. [16] and revealed that the intraply damage occurred in the angled specimen, and also concluded that it was possible to obtain true interlaminar values for the initiation total critical strain energy release rate GC. These values were 65 % higher than those of UD specimens. Testing glass/polyester and filament wound glass/epoxy MMB samples, Ozdil and Carlsson [17,18] obtained -increasing initiation GC. values by increasing ϑ. Blackman et al. [19]. published the findings of their end-loaded split (ELS) mode II testing on carbon fibre reinforced composite substrates that were adhesively bonded. Problems with accurately determining the fracture length arose from the presence of a microcracked area preceding the main crack in these trials.

There are various methods other than DCB, ENF and MMB are also reported. A so-called single leg bending (SLB) specimen is extremely comparable to the MMF specimen [20]. It turns out that the MMF specimen and the SLB specimen are identical. The sole distinction is that the supports in the SLB test can be of varying heights. It is possible to achieve various mode I to mode II ratios using this specimen. Mode II dominates the crack lap shear (CLS) test [21]. By manipulating a stepped thickness ratio, different GI/GII ratios can be attained for this test. The CLS test allows for semi-stable crack formation, and multiple data points can be recorded with crack growth. But as the crack length increases, there is a significant amount of scatter in the results of this test [21]. One such way to create mixed-mode loading circumstances is to use the edge delamination tension (EDT) specimen [22].

Adding fillers in polymer composites can potentially change the physical and mechanical properties of the polymers [23]. The addition of micron-sized particles is a simple way to increase stiffness, as the stiffness of rigid inorganic particles is typically much higher than that of polymer matrices [24]. The strength, however, is highly dependent on the stress transfer between the fillers and the matrix. Saravanakumar et al. [23] investigated the effects of adding milled glass fibres to glass/epoxy composite laminates and reported the inclusion of 5 % of mGF improves fracture toughness by increasing delamination resistance via a bridging effect up to 107 % and 125 % in GI and GII. Fracture toughness of fly ash filled composite is independent of initial crack length but dependent on the orientation of fibre, as shown by Srivastva et al. [25,26], who also demonstrated that toughness can be increased by adding fly ash filler to an epoxy matrix. The mechanical properties and mode-I interlaminar fracture behaviour of carbon fibre-reinforced and nano clay-filled epoxy matrix composites were investigated by Naveed et al. [27]. The results showed that the addition of 3 wt% clay increased fracture toughness by around 60 %.

On the other hand, composite industries typically send their end of life composite materials to landfills. Mechanical recycling of carbon fibres offers significant environmental and economic benefits, and the recovered fibrous/powdered products can be reincorporated either as reinforcement or filler in new composite materials, as reported by Pickering et al. [28]. If the necessary structural modifications for the practical use of these fillers do not incur excessive costs, this presents an opportunity, which is crucial for the recycling of carbon fibres [29]. The mechanical properties of polymeric materials can be improved by using recycled milled carbon fibres as an alternative to commercial particles/fillers, provided that the fibres are used as close as practicable to their as-received state after milling. The non-biodegradability and recycling difficulties of Kevlar composite waste create serious problems for the environment. Pollution and toxic waste products can persist for a long time after improper disposal. Ballistic armour, and aerospace parts are just a few examples of the many uses for Kevlar-epoxy composites due to its high strength, low weight, and impact resistance. Because of the expensive price mark up, energy-intensive manufacturing process, and ecological effect, recycling Kevlar is just as crucial as recycling carbon fibre. The strength and longevity of both materials make them valuable, and recycling them helps cut down on energy use, landfill trash, and the demand for raw materials. Recycling Kevlar for use in high-performance contexts helps the advanced composite materials sector be more environmentally friendly.

A review of the relevant literature revealed that the fracture toughness of thermoset polymers was enhanced when fibre and filler were used together. The fracture toughness is significantly improved by the presence of multidirectional fibres. However, there are only limited studies done using milled glass fillers to improve fracture toughness. So far, no work has been reported to use recycled milled carbon fibres and recycled milled Kevlar fibres as fillers to enhance the fracture toughness of the glass epoxy composites. This paper addresses the effect of interface modification by appropriate microscopic observation and links it to the toughness mechanisms of laminates, with a particular emphasis on the use of recycled milled carbon fillers (rmCF) and recycled milled Kevlar filler (rmKF) in glass/epoxy composite laminates to increase their resistance to mode I and mode II and mixed mode (I/II) delamination, respectively. Hybridizing recycled milled carbon and recycled milled Kevlar fillers(rmHF) is shown to be novel, and the synergic effect of the hybrid fillers is studied.

Mode I (DCB) and Mode II (ENF) tests, as well as Mixed Mode (I/II) tests, have been used to measure the fracture toughness of glass/epoxy composites in this work. The specific goal of this work is to shed light on how these properties of glass/epoxy composites can be enhanced by varying the fibre orientations and by using optimized amounts of the recycled milled fillers; this work also uses scanning electron microscope (SEM) micrographs to show the fracture mechanism.

Selection of interface angles

It is important to minimise the influence of elastic couplings on interlaminar fracture toughness measurements when choosing stacking sequences for multi-directional specimens. For instance, large anticlastic deformations can be caused by bending-bending couplings, leading to highly curved delamination fronts and a significant mode I/II component at the specimen edges. Starter delamination between plies with different orientations inevitably occurs in specimens, as do bending-twisting and membrane-bending couplings. These interactions can lead to the development of asymmetrical delamination fronts and substantial residual stress effects in GC measurements [39,40]. The influence of fibre orientation on interlaminar fracture toughness is studied using four distinct orientations (Fig. 2a–d). To determine the optimal orientation for studying the impact of recycled milled fillers, the DCB, mixed mode with 50 %, and ENF tests have been performed on the base samples.

According to Refs. [40,41], the couplings have the potential to induce this effect. It was found that the critical strain energy release rate for these interfaces grew in tandem with the ply angle.

The greater crack resistance observed in the 90/90 fiber orientation compared to the +45/-45 and 90/+45/-45/90 interfaces is due to differences in stress distribution and crack propagation characteristics. In the 90/90 layout, perpendicular fibers in adjacent layers provide effective stress distribution and resistance to crack growth, as they limit crack paths and offer better load transfer. Conversely, the +45/-45 and 90/+45/-45/90 interfaces experience more complex crack paths and potential delamination due to the angled fiber orientations, which create weaker interfaces and larger crack areas. This results in lower energy absorption and reduced resistance to crack propagation in these layouts. The 90/90 configuration's strong fiber bridging and improved interlaminar toughness further enhance its crack resistance, making it more effective in resisting fracture compared to the other orientations. Accordingly, the interlaminar fracture toughness, GC, is affected by the fibre orientations in the plies that are near the delamination interface. In all three modes (I, II and I/II). Therefore 90/90 interface has been chosen to study the effects of recycled milled fillers.

Crack propagation characteristics

Specimens tested in the MMB (Mixed-Mode Bending) apparatus show peculiar crack propagation behaviour when subjected to load. The interface between the mid planes (7th and 8th plies) in specimens is typically the initial location for the crack. This delamination begins at the tip of a Teflon film insert that was inserted at the time of specimen fabrication. The crack initiates in the mid-plane but frequently leaps up inside the eighth ply. The delamination fracture energy is significantly affected by this difference. Delamination occurs mainly within the 7th ply and at the interface between the 7th ply and the mismatched 8th ply; thus, the fibre direction of the 8th ply is the key variable impacting this difference [43].

Because the flexural strength of the lamina close to the mid-plane of an MMB specimen exceeds the maximum normal tensile stress developed due to flexure, the lamina will break down by starting micro-cracks when subjected to tensile loading [44]. When the adjacent plies in the midplane have highly mismatched angle, the flexural strength on these plies will be at peak and valley. As a result, matrix micro cracks propagate across these interface layers near the mid-plane. Therefore, the cracks jump to the adjacent ply through these micro cracks (Fig. 5). Mixed mode

Mode-I fracture energy is relatively invariant over a wide range of mixed-mode ratios, while mode-II fracture energy rises sharply with increasing mode-II ratios. When the mode-II loading ratio is less than about 50 %, the crack tip encounters the greatest stresses and more than half of the total fracture energy comes from mode-I loading. In the range beyond 50 % mode-II ratio, mode-II loading becomes more important than mode-I loading in determining the total fracture energy. Mode-II loading sets more emphasis on sliding than opening, and its highest stresses reside away from the crack tip. Since the mode-II plastic zone is longer than the mode-I plastic zone, more energy is dissipated during mode-II loading [43,49]. Therefore, mode-II loading results in more energy, leading to greater fracture toughness than mode-I loading. Total delamination fracture toughness is proportionally higher to mode-II ratio because of the higher mode-II fracture energy.

After a mode-II GII failure, various fracture surfaces can be seen. When compared to mode-I failure, all the systems displayed a rougher fracture surface and more hackles [53]. The formation of hackles again indicates strong resistance of the resin to delamination [54]. Saw-toothed fracture patterns with rough hackles are characteristic of mode II failure, which is caused by transverse cracks in the resin that run perpendicular to the fibres. This is consistent with the high GII results and demonstrates the material's increased energy absorption under mode-II loading. Crack deflection, debonding, and interlocking of the filler/matrix interface are all examples of toughening mechanisms that, according to Ref. [55], make it more difficult to initiate and propagate a crack, and thus contribute to increased fracture toughness. This has been established for the case of rmF introduction, including rmCF, rmKF, and rmHF, and is related to the filler's even distribution throughout the composite [56].

Conclusions

The modes I, I/II, and II interlaminar fracture toughness experiments were conducted. The effect of different orientations 0/0, 90/90, +45/-45, and 90/+45/-45/90o of fibre interface was studied. Out of which 90/90 orientation exceeds in crack resistance propagation by 172 %, 69 % and 126 % in Mode I, Mixed Mode (50 %) and Mode II respectively. Fibre orientation affects fracture energy due to stiffness of fibre and laminate. Unidirectional laminates delaminate more due to their higher flexural modulus, dissipating failure energy. Mode II has a higher bending modulus than mode I. Fibre orientation reduces laminate bending modulus and fracture energy. Also, fibre orientation affects fracture toughness due to interlaminar friction and bridging.

The influence of three types of recycled milled fillers rmCF, rmKF and rmHF were observed on 90/90 interface composites. The addition rmCF, rmKF and rmHF significantly enhance mode I (GI), Mixed mode I/II (GC), and mode II fracture toughness (GII). The fracture toughness improved by 52 %,4 %, and19 % in GI, 73 %,5.1 % & 69 % at 25 %, 271 %,182 % & 220 % at 50 %, 53 %,31 %, &32 % at 75 % of GIIGC in propagation, and 22 %, 6.3 %, and 9 % in GII for rmCF, rmKF, rmHF This is due to the strong filler/matrix interfacial and high energy dissipation mechanism, including crack deflection, debonding, and interlocking. The hybrid fillers compensated for the Kevlar fillers' inferior fracture toughness.

Recycled milled fibres significantly enhance interlaminar fracture toughness in addition to fibre orientation in glass/epoxy laminates. Despite their easy recovery from end-of-life composites, recycled milled fibres can be used as efficient fillers for composite laminates.
