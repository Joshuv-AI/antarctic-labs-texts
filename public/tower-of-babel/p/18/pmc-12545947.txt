Gaming, religiosity and mental health among Georgian students
Authors: Konstantine Chakhunashvili, Davit G. Chakhunashvili, Rushikesh Rajesh Patil, Elizabet Mchedlishvili, Shai-li Romem-Porat, Alexander Reznik, Richard Isralowitz, Ilia Nadareishvili
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe dramatic rise in high-speed internet access and smartphone usage has made multiplayer online gaming increasingly prevalent among young adults. While moderate gaming can confer cognitive benefits, excessive gaming is linked to sleep disruption, addiction, and a spectrum of adverse mental health outcomes—including depression, anxiety, stress, and impaired academic performance. In 2018, the World Health Organization formally recognized Gaming Disorder (GD) as an addictive mental health condition, before that Internet Gaming Disorder (IGD) had been added to DSM-5 in 2013. Despite growing concern, data remain scarce on IGD’s prevalence and psychological correlates in Georgian university students. This study therefore assessed the relationship between IGD, gaming duration, and key psychosocial variables (burnout, loneliness, life satisfaction, religiosity, and substance use) in a representative Georgian student sample.Materials and methodsFrom November 2024–January 2025, an anonymous online survey was administered to students at four major Georgian universities, yielding 506 responses (157 complete). Participants reported weekly gaming hours and completed validated scales: Internet Gaming Disorder Scale, Burnout Scale, Loneliness Scale (emotional/social), Satisfaction with Life Scale, and Financial Wellbeing Scale. Self-reported religiosity, substance use patterns, and demographic data were collected. Statistical analyses (SPSS v26) included Pearson correlations, independent samples t-tests, ANOVA, chi-square tests, and eta coefficients; significance was set at p < 0.05.ResultsAmong 506 respondents (mean age 22.1; 33.4% male), mean weekly gaming duration was 8.26 h and mean IGD score was 15.04. Males reported significantly longer gaming duration and higher IGD scores than females (p < 0.001). Weekly gaming duration showed a weak positive correlation with IGD scores (r = 0.31, p < 0.001), explaining ~ 10% of variance. MANOVA indicated that gaming duration retained its statistical significance inside the model (P < 0.001). ANCOVA indicated religiosity (p = 0.016, partial η2 = 0.05) and gender (p = 0.040, partial η2 = 0.03) remained significant predictors of IGD after adjusting for continuous covariates.ConclusionExcessive gaming among Georgian university students is associated with higher IGD severity, greater burnout, and reduced life satisfaction, with religiosity serving as a potential protective factor. These findings underscore the need for targeted interventions—such as psychoeducation and time management strategies—to promote balanced gaming habits. The high attrition rate is the primary limiting factor to generilizing the findings of the study.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s44192-025-00297-0.

Introduction

The worldwide web is one of the biggest technological advancements of the modern era and it is no surprise that it has become an integral part of every aspect of our daily lives. High internet speed and rapid growth of smartphones is making online gaming more and more accessible these days, which leads to the increased numbers of young people spending more time on gaming activities [1]. Multiplayer online games are the most played video games nowadays and according to recent data, men are more addicted to such games than women [1]. Online video games are also getting more and more popular amongst college students, for example in Europe 53% of population between 6 and 64 years play video games, 78% of population among 18 to 24 years old [2]. Online gaming might have some benefits, like better problem-solving skills or hand–eye coordination, but might also lead to disrupted sleep and addiction [3]. It is known that adolescents who are addicted to gaming might suffer from depression, anxiety, stress, low self-control, poor academic performance, decreased learning motivation personality disorders and other mental health conditions [4, 5]. Although psychological mechanisms are unclear, excessive time spent with online games could also disrupt academic delay of gratification (ADOG), an important predictor of student performance which was first introduced by Karabenick and Bembenutty [6].

In 2018, the World Health Organization (WHO) and its International Classification of Diseases (ICD), a medical classification used worldwide for epidemiology, health management clinical purposes, included online game addiction in the category of “Disorders due to substance use or addictive behaviours” as “Gaming Disorder” (IGD), before that it had been included in DSM-5 as “Internet Gaming Disorder” [5]. Issues related to excessive gaming and internet use have increased, raising clinical concern. IGD involves aberrant reward processing and impulsivity, executive function and decision-making, that tend to resemble substance misuse and OCD in neurobiology [7–11]. Attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) increases IGD susceptibility via executive control dysfunction [7]. Although gaming uniquely engages cognitive, motor, and sensory brain regions, excessive use can trigger mental health issues and compulsive behaviors [7].

IGD can be meaningfully interpreted through the Interaction of Person–Affect–Cognition–Execution (I-PACE) model. According to this model, excessive gaming behavior emerges from the interaction between individual core characteristics (e.g., personality traits, demographic variables, religiosity), affective responses (e.g., burnout, stress), cognitive processes (e.g., expectancies, reward valuation), and executive functioning (e.g., self-control, inhibitory control). These domains dynamically influence each other over time, reinforcing maladaptive use patterns through both positive (reward-seeking) and negative (stress-avoidance) reinforcement. From the I-PACE perspective, weekly gaming duration reflects both motivational engagement and habitual reinforcement mechanisms, making it a central behavioral marker of IGD severity. Burnout and loneliness can be conceptualized as affective risk factors that may increase the likelihood of using gaming as a coping strategy, while life satisfaction represents a broader indicator of psychological well-being potentially buffering against problematic use. Religiosity, in turn, may serve as a personal characteristic influencing cognitive control and value systems, thereby acting as a protective factor. Gender differences documented in prior research may reflect both dispositional and sociocultural variations in gaming motivations and reinforcement patterns. The present study tested whether weekly gaming duration, burnout, loneliness, satisfaction with life, religiosity, and gender are associated with IGD severity among Georgian university students. We hypothesized that higher weekly gaming duration, burnout, and loneliness, along with lower satisfaction with life, would be associated with higher IGD scores, whereas greater religiosity would be associated with lower IGD severity. Gender differences were also expected, with males showing higher IGD scores than females.

Materials and methods

Data collection

For data collection, we conducted an online survey in a nonprobability sample of local Georgian students at four universities located in the capital city of Tbilisi (Caucasus University, University of Georgia, Ilia State University, and David Tvildiani Medical University). A total of 506 responses were collected, of which 157 were fully complete [see Supplemental File 1]. Partially completed responses were also used in analyses where appropriate. Specifically, if participants provided sufficient data for individual variables or scale scores, those responses were included in analyses. If not they were excluded. For handling missing data, we applied pairwise deletion, allowing each statistical test to include all available data for the relevant variables. This approach maximized the usable sample size for specific comparisons while maintaining analytical integrity.

Among these, 169 respondents were male, 263 were female, and 74 did not specify their gender. Data were gathered from November 2024 to January 2025. The study was conducted in cooperation with Regional Alcohol and Drug Abuse Research (RADAR) center at the Ben Gurion University.

Four universities—Caucasus University, University of Georgia, Ilia State University, and David Tvildiani Medical University—were selected based on institutional access, willingness to participate, and the presence of existing academic collaborations. These universities are among the most prominent and diverse higher education institutions in Tbilisi, collectively serving a large and varied student population across different academic disciplines, including medicine, social sciences, and business. While the sample may not represent all universities in Georgia, the inclusion of both public and private institutions and the high response rate from students allowed for robust analysis of the study objectives.

Given the observational design and the aim of examining associations between IGD scores and multiple psychological and demographic variables, we conducted a post-hoc power analysis to evaluate the adequacy of our final sample size (n = 157). Using SPSS SamplePower and assuming a small-to-moderate effect size (Cohen’s f2 = 0.08), an alpha of 0.05, and a power of 0.80, the required sample size for detecting effects in a multiple linear regression model with 5 predictors is approximately N = 153. Our analytic sample of 157 slightly exceeds this threshold, indicating sufficient statistical power to detect small-to-moderate effects in multivariate models. Sensitivity analyses were conducted to evaluate the robustness of findings to missing data handling methods. Main analyses used pairwise deletion; repeating the models with listwise deletion and multiple imputation produced qualitatively similar results, with all significant associations remaining in the same direction and of similar magnitude.

Participants provided information on the time spent on online gaming per week (Valid N = 397) and completed several surveys, which had been translated into the Georgian language: the Internet Gaming Disorder (IGD) scale (Cronbach’s Alpha = 0.83) (Valid N = 230) [12], the Financial Well-Being Scale (Cronbach’s Alpha = 0.63) (Valid N = 257) [13], the Burnout Scale (Cronbach’s Alpha = 0.89) (Valid N = 253) [14], the Loneliness Score (Cronbach’s Alpha = 0.86) (Valid N = 232) [15], and the Satisfaction with Life Scale (Cronbach’s Alpha = 0.85) (Valid N = 234)[16]. All measurement tools were adapted into Georgian following established cross-cultural adaptation guidelines. The research team reviewed all discrepancies and resolved them to ensure cultural relevance and semantic accuracy. A pilot test was conducted with 20 students to assess clarity, comprehensibility, and cultural appropriateness of items, leading to minor wording adjustments. Cronbach’s alpha values for the adapted scales in this study ranged from 0.63 to 0.89, indicating acceptable internal consistency. In addition, data were collected on religiosity, religious belonging, gender, relationship status, substance use within a month, increases in substance use over the same period, and exacerbation of depression, nervousness, tiredness, loneliness, and anger.

Religiosity was measured as a categorical variable based on participants’ self-reported adherence to religious practices (e.g., attendance at services, private prayer) and the perceived importance of religion in daily life, reflecting the personal, behavioral, and cognitive dimensions of faith and indetifying themselves as not religious, somewhat religious, or religious/very religious. Religious affiliation was recorded as a categorical variable indicating membership in a specific religious group. This distinction was made to examine both the experiential and institutional aspects of religion, recognizing that they may have different relationships with psychological and behavioral outcomes.

Survey scoring and interpretation

For the Financial Wellbeing Scale, both adjusted and raw scores were used in the analyses. Similarly, the Loneliness Score was utilized as a total score as well as broken down into emotional and social loneliness components. In the case of the IGD scale, a higher score indicated more severe internet gaming disorder, whereas for the Financial Well-Being Scale, a higher score signified better financial well-being. For the Burnout Scale, higher scores reflected greater burnout, and for the Loneliness Score, higher scores denoted increased loneliness.

The Satisfaction with Life Scale was analyzed both as a numerical variable and as a categorical variable based on the following classification: 31–35: Extremely satisfied; 26–30: Satisfied; 21–25: Slightly satisfied; 20: Neutral; 15–19: Slightly dissatisfied; 10–14: Dissatisfied; 5–9: Extremely dissatisfied.

Data on substance use within a month was quantified by assigning a score of 1 for each positive response and 0 for each negative response, with the total score calculated as the sum of these responses. The same quantification method was applied to assess increases in substance use.

The study was approved by David Tvildiani Medical University Ethics Committee document number # 2-REC/24 and it was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The participants were informed about the study background, aims. Informed consent was collected from all the patients. All the data was collected anonymously, and confidentiality of the responses was guaranteed by the study team.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 26. Pearson’s correlation coefficient and linear regression were employed for analyses involving scale variables. When comparing scale and categorical variables, the eta test was applied, and chi-square tests were used for analyses involving categorical variables. Bivariate and multivariate Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) and Analysis of Covariance (ANCOVA) was conducted where it was applicable. T-test was conducted to compare the means. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05, with a 95% confidence interval.

Multivariate ANOVA

A multivariate ANOVA model including burnout, gaming duration, satisfaction with life, financial wellbeing, and total loneliness as predictors yielded a significant result, explaining 18.8% of the variance in IGD scores (R2 = 0.188, df = 151, p < 0.001). However, in the model only gaming duration stayed a statistically significant predictor of IGD (P < 0.001). Diagnostic checks for multicollinearity indicated acceptable values (VIF range: 1.06–1.55; tolerance range: 0.64–0.94), suggesting that these variables could be included in the same model without inflating standard error.

Hierarchical multiple regression

A hierarchical multiple regression was conducted to examine whether religiosity moderated the association between weekly gaming duration and IGD scores. In Step 1, main effects of gaming duration (centered) and religiosity (dummy-coded with “religious/very religious” as the reference category) were entered. This model was significant, R2 = 0.17, F(3,224) = 15.71, p < 0.001, with gaming duration positively predicting IGD scores, B = 0.14, SE = 0.03, β = 0.30,t = 4.97, p < 0.001, and both “not religious” (B = 3.23, SE = 1.20, β = 0.18, t = 2.70, p = 0.008) and “somewhat religious” participants (B = 3.41, SE = 0.82, β = 0.28,t = 4.18, p < 0.001) scoring higher on IGD than the reference group.

In Step 2, the interaction terms between gaming duration and religiosity were added. The model remained significant and explained additional variance in IGD scores, R2 = 0.19, F (2,222) = 3.35, p = 0.028. The interaction between gaming duration and “not religious” was significant (B = 0.23, SE = 0.11, β = 0.15, t = 2.03, p = 0.044, as was the interaction between gaming duration and “somewhat religious” (B = 0.13, SE = 0.06, β = 0.22, t = 2.18, p = 0.030), indicating that the slope of gaming duration predicting IGD was steeper for less religious participants. Simple slope analyses revealed that weekly gaming duration was significantly and positively associated with IGD scores for “not religious” (B = 0.28, p < 0.01) and “somewhat religious” participants (B = 0.18, p < 0.05), but not for “religious/very religious” participants (B = 0.05, p = 0.29). These findings suggest that higher religiosity may attenuate the link between gaming time and IGD severity.

Data interpretation

Our study investigated associations between weekly gaming duration and various psychological and behavioral variables, including IGD. We observed a positive correlation between weekly gaming duration and IGD, indicating that longer gaming hours are associated with more severe internet gaming disorder; and analysis of variance (ANOVA) revealed that gaming duration accounted for approximately 10% of the variance. In contrast, while there was a weak positive correlation between gaming duration and increased substance use, ANOVA indicated an effect size of only around 2%, suggesting limited practical significance. No significant correlations were found between weekly gaming hours and the other variables analyzed.

IGD and burnout scores were positively correlated with ANOVA indicating an effect size of about 5%, implying that greater IGD severity is associated with higher burnout. Additionally, IGD was negatively correlated with satisfaction with life scores; in linear analyses, the effect size was approximately 3%, whereas a non-linear analysis demonstrated η2 of 0.06, which might be deemed as moderate effect. Furthermore, IGD showed a weak correlation with the number of substances used within a month (ANOVA effect size ~ 2%), and no additional significant correlations were observed between IGD and other variables.

Multivariate ANOVA showed that, in the model including only continuous variables, gaming duration was the only statistically significant predictor. In contrast, the ANCOVA indicated that only religiosity and gender remained statistically significant predictors from categorical variables after controlling for the continuous covariates.

A weak correlation was identified between scores of burnout and financial well-being (ANOVA effect size ~ 4%), as well as burnout and loneliness (effect sizes ranging from 3 to 4%). In contrast, life satisfaction cores exhibited a moderate negative correlation with those of burnout. Linear regression analysis revealed an effect size of 17%, and the η2 value computed from comparison of categorical and scale variables was 0.18, indicating a large association between higher burnout severity and lower satisfaction with life. Additionally, higher burnout scores were weakly associated with increased substance intake (ANOVA effect sizes of 5% for the number of substances used and 2% for increased substance use).

Life satisfaction scores were negatively associated with the number of substances used per month (ANOVA effect size ~ 3%) and increased substance use in a month (ANOVA effect size ~ 2%). Finally, our analysis of social determinants revealed that individuals identifying as more religious or very religious had lower IGD scores, with a statistically significant moderate association (ANOVA effect size 8%) between religiosity and IGD scores. No significant associations were found between IGD scores and marital/relationship status or religious affiliation, and gender was weakly associated with IGD scores (ANOVA effect size 5%).

Discussion

The digitalization of everyday processes has led to increased screen time exposure, with outcomes that vary by activity and age. For example, in infancy and toddlerhood, exposure has been associated with an increased risk of expressive language delay [17], which can cause significant parental distress. Some studies also suggest that screen time habits may heighten the risk of developing gaming addiction and can predict later gaming patterns [18]. It is well established that excessive solo gaming can impair parent–child interaction, thereby hindering developmental progress [19]. However, when it comes to multiplayer games or those requiring socialization, these platforms can foster bonding and communication. Therefore, encouraging parents and children to game together may provide quality interactive time, although it remains important to maintain limits to ensure a balanced routine that includes physical activity [20].

The present survey among local students of higher educational institutions in the country of Georgia identified several factors significantly associated with elevated Internet Gaming Disorder (IGD) scores, with longer gaming duration being a notable example. These findings could inform the development of targeted recommendations and awareness programs to mitigate the risk of gaming addiction.

Because the study focused exclusively on Georgian students—a group predominantly identifying with Christianity—the sample lacked sufficient religious diversity. Consequently, the potential impact of alternative religious affiliations on IGD outcomes could not be thoroughly investigated.

Previous research has demonstrated that religiosity serves as a protective factor against substance abuse [21]. Although the evidence is less conclusive in the context of gaming, several studies have reported an association between higher religiosity and more favorable gaming disorder outcomes [22–24]. Conversely, one comparative study of students from Russia and Kazakhstan found that religiosity was not a significant predictor of IGD scores [25]. In our analysis of Georgian students, ANOVA results indicated that religiosity explained a moderate proportion of the variance (approximately 8%) in IGD scores, with lower scores observed among participants who identified as religious or very religious. These findings are not surprising in the Georgian context, where religiosity is deeply embedded in the social fabric and family life. The Georgian Orthodox Church is one of the most trusted institutions in the country, and religious values often influence moral decision-making, social norms, and lifestyle choices. While not all respondents identified as religious, those who did are likely to hold this identity sincerely, with associated beliefs and practices guiding their daily behavior. In such a context, religiosity may function as a protective factor against addictive behaviors, including problematic gaming, by promoting self-regulation, prioritization of family and community responsibilities, and engagement in non-digital social activities. This cultural dynamic may partly explain why religiosity emerged as a stronger protective factor in our sample than has been observed in studies from other societies.These discrepancies across studies suggest that socio-cultural factors may influence how individuals interpret and report religiosity, thereby affecting its relationship with gaming behaviors.

There is also a large body of evidence that shows an association between frequent substance use or addiction to gaming disorders [26–28]. Although our study did not find a strong positive correlation between such factors, we identified a statistically significant, but weak, positive correlation which can be considered aligned with the current state of the evidence.

Although religious belonging has been linked to several health‑related behaviors—most notably vaccine hesitancy [29, 30] and reduced alcohol consumption [31]—evidence suggests these relationships are generally more strongly driven by individual levels of religiosity (i.e., the degree of personal religious commitment) than by mere religious affiliation. Several large epidemiological investigations have reported no significant association between religious affiliation and the presence of DSM‑IV psychiatric diagnoses [32, 33]. In the current study, assessment of religious affiliation as an independent variable was not feasible absolute majority of the Georgian population identify as members of the Georgian Orthodox Church, resulting in insufficient variability. Future research will examine the impact of religious affiliation on mental health outcomes in more heterogeneous populations—such as international student cohorts—where religious diversity is greater.

A study in Israel detected that gaming disorder was a key predictor of burnout [34], in this study we identified that there was an association between high IGD scores and higher burnout, however, the effect size of ANOVA was 4.84%.

Although the observational nature of this study precludes definitive causal conclusions, the strongest association was observed between IGD scores and weekly gaming duration, suggesting that reducing gaming time may help lower IGD severity and improve life satisfaction. Based on our findings, we recommend targeted interventions aimed at promoting balanced gaming habits. Furthermore, the combination of burnout, gaming duration, satisfaction with life, financial wellbeing, and total loneliness may serve as a valuable framework for both risk assessment and risk mitigation strategies. Educational institutions should be equipped with the necessary tools to identify at-risk individuals early and support healthier patterns of gaming behavior, particularly in light of the observed link between excessive gaming and elevated IGD severity.

The limitation of the study is that the data are self-reported, and bias is possible. We experienced a high attrition rate. Such attrition raises the possibility of non-response bias if individuals who discontinued participation differed systematically from those who completed the survey. For example, it is plausible that students with heavier gaming involvement, lower interest in the topic, or time constraints were less likely to finish, which could influence the observed associations. Although sensitivity analyses suggested that the main results were robust across different missing data handling methods, the high dropout rate limits the generalizability of our findings and warrants caution in interpreting effect sizes. Future research should aim to reduce attrition through shorter survey formats, increased engagement strategies, and follow-up reminders Also, the observational design prevents us from claiming causal relationships between the factors studied.

Since our study focuses on Georgian students, cultural factors and the unique characteristics of a student population should be considered when generalizing these results. We recommend that future research include broader populations and adopt longitudinal designs to better understand these relationships over time.

Conclusion

Our study investigated the associations between weekly gaming duration and various psychological and behavioral variables among Georgian university students. Our findings revealed a statistically significant positive correlation between gaming hours and IGD scores, with gaming duration accounting for approximately 10% of the variance in IGD severity. Additionally, IGD was modestly linked to increased burnout and lower satisfaction with life, while other examined variables—such as financial well-being, loneliness, and substance use—showed either weak or non-significant relationships with gaming behavior. Higher religiosity emerged as a potential protective factor against IGD. Although the observational design limits causal interpretation, the strong association between prolonged gaming and higher IGD scores suggests that targeted interventions to reduce gaming time may contribute to improved psychological outcomes. Given the specific cultural and demographic characteristics of the sample, future research should consider broader, more diverse populations and employ longitudinal methods to further elucidate these relationships.
