A Case of Hidradenitis Suppurativa in a Genetically Confirmed Lowe Syndrome Patient
Authors: Jun Hyo Lee, Jiwon Lee, Dae Hun Suh
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractLowe syndrome (LS), also known as oculocerebrorenal syndrome, is an X-linked multisystemic disorder caused by mutations in OCRL1, which encodes a member of the inositol-5-phosphatase family. As implied by its name, congenital cataracts, defects in the central nervous system, and renal manifestations are the main symptoms. Early hidradenitis suppurativa (HS) occurrence in Dent disease 2 (DD2), which is a mild variant of LS and shares the OCRL1 gene mutation, has been reported, although not in LS patients. Here, we report a case of HS in a 17-year-old boy with genetically confirmed LS, which suggests that defects in the OCRL1 gene may contribute to HS pathogenesis.Keywords: Dent disease 2, Hidradenitis suppurativa, Lowe syndrome, Mutation, OCRL gene

INTRODUCTION

Hidradenitis suppurativa (HS) is an inflammatory disorder characterized by chronic deep-seated nodules, abscesses, fistulae, sinus tracts, and scars on the axilla, inguinal area, submammary folds, and perianal area. This disfiguring condition is accompanied by pain, embarrassment, and significantly decreased quality of life. Although the mechanism of HS has not been entirely elucidated, lesion formation has been reported to center around follicular hyperkeratosis within the pilosebaceous– apocrine unit1. Lowe syndrome (LS), also known as oculocerebrorenal syndrome, is an X-linked multisystemic disorder caused by mutations in the OCRL1 gene, which encodes a member of the inositol-5-phosphatase family2. Although a high prevalence of HS in LS patients has not been reported to date, Dent disease 2 (DD2), which is considered a mild variant of LS, is known to be associated with a high prevalence of HS3. Here, we report a case of HS in a patient with genetically confirmed LS treated with a local steroid injection and oral antibacterial agents.

CASE REPORT

A 17-year-old boy was referred to our department for recurrent tender subcutaneous nodules with erythematous red to brown skin surface accompanied by a discharge on the buttocks, which originally started 3 years ago (Fig. 1). A few nodules showed pus-like discharge when pressure was applied. His medical history included genetically confirmed LS of the OCRL p.S374P mutation. No family history of LS was reported. He was taking sodium bicarbonate and alfacalcidol for renal tubular acidosis and for impaired renal function owing to LS. Although no skin biopsy was performed, the patient was diagnosed as having HS with Hurley stage 1, on the basis of characteristic clinical findings. Laboratory findings, including complete blood count, liver function test results, and blood urea nitrogen and creatinine levels, were within the normal range, although urinalysis showed increased protein-to-creatinine and calcium-to-creatinine levels. Amoxicillin 500 mg three times daily was given for 1 month, and triamcinolone 10 mg/ml intralesional injection was administered twice with a treatment gap of 3 weeks. On follow-up, much improvement was observed in the skin lesions, and erythematous skin surfaces had mostly turned brown (Fig. 2). Oral medication and local steroid injection were discontinued, and no relapse was observed for 13 months since his last clinic visit.

Subcutaneous nodules with erythematous red to brown skin surface on the buttocks are observed on the first visit.

After amoxicillin administration and triamcinolone intralesional injection, reddish patches faded into brownish lesions. The patient expressed no discomfort caused by hidradenitis suppurativa lesions after the treatment.

Patient consent for publication of medical details and images was obtained.

DISCUSSION

The pathogenesis of HS is not well understood. Because the lesion forms around the pilosebaceous–apocrine unit, the primary cause is reportedly related to follicular hyperkeratosis, which leads to rupture of the hair follicle and inflammation of the apocrine glands. During this process, external factors such as friction, microbiome, and smoking may exacerbate the condition in genetically predisposed individuals. Recent investigations suggest that the interleukin-12–interleukin-23 pathway and tumor necrosis factor α (TNF-α) are involved in the pathogenesis of HS, supporting the proposition that it is an immune or inflammatory disorder4. Diseases such as metabolic syndrome and inflammatory bowel disease are risk factors for HS and are influenced by TNF-α1.

HS has familial traits. Of the patients reported as having HS, 30% to 40% had at least one affected family member5. Genes that contribute to this trend are PSENEN, PSEN1, and NCSTN, which play a role in maturating hair follicle cells and normalizing immune system functions by controlling γ-secretase production. When inherited, HS follows an autosomal dominant pattern with incomplete penetrance5, and affected members tend to have a more severe phenotype6.

LS is an X-linked multisystemic disorder caused by mutations in OCRL1, which encodes a member of the inositol-5-phosphatase family. As implied by its name, the characteristic triad includes congenital cataracts, intellectual disability, and proximal renal tubular dysfunction7. DD2, which is considered a mild variant of LS, shares the OCRL1 gene mutation with LS. A high prevalence of HS in DD2 patients has been reported, although not in LS patients. Previous cases of HS in DD2 patients showed a male predominance with earlier onset of HS than that in the general population3. Our case of HS with LS had similar characteristics.

The OCRL1 gene mutation causes loss of phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate 5-phosphatase, an enzyme located in the Golgi apparatus8, resulting in increased levels of phosphoinositol 4,5-bisphosphate in the plasma membrane. Increased phosphoinositol 4,5-bisphosphate levels in fibroblasts facilitate staphylococcal aggression9. The pathophysiological mechanism of HS is accompanied by chronic inflammation of the hair follicle, which may be aggravated by Staphylococcus. The OCRL1 gene is also known to be essential for bacterial phagocytosis, especially Yersinia pseudotuberculosis and Listeria monocytogenes. It is likely that other bacterial pathogens exploit OCRL1 during phagocytic entry into the cells10. Like DD2 patients, LS patients may be vulnerable to HS due to increased bacterial infestation caused by the OCRL1 gene mutation.

In addition to exogenous factors such as bacterial infection, endogenous factors influenced by the OCRL1 gene mutation might be the cause of HS in LS patients. The OCRL1 gene is involved in the maturation of polarizing epithelial cells and in controlling membrane traffic and the actin cytoskeleton for cell migration to recycle clathrin-coated components in endocytosis2. Cases of multiple epidermal or cutaneous cysts in LS patients have been reported11. Previous studies have suggested that cyst formation is a result of localized reaction by accumulation of phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate 5-phosphate and extracellular release of lysosomal enzymes, causing inflammation and tissue damage8. Differential diagnosis of multiple inflamed epidermal cysts was considered in this case but limited distribution of lesions in buttock and chronic disease course without tenderness favored HS. Mutation of the OCRL1 gene could have affected epithelial cell differentiation, thereby causing HS.

We report the case of a genetically confirmed LS patient with early onset HS. LS patients may be vulnerable to HS due to mutations in the OCRL1 gene.
