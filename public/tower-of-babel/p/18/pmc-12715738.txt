A Universal 19F NMR Probe for Enantiomeric Discrimination of Primary, Secondary, and Tertiary Amines
Authors: Jiajin Weng, Guangxing Gu, Wenjing Bao, Yanchuan Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Chiral amines are fundamental building blocks in pharmaceuticals and bioactive molecules, necessitating fast and reliable enantiomeric analysis. However, developing a single probe capable of effectively distinguishing primary, secondary, and particularly tertiary amines remains challenging due to their diverse steric and electronic characteristics. Herein, we report a novel 19F-labeled palladium-based probe bearing a strategically positioned trifluoromethyl group away from the coordination site to minimize steric hindrance. This structural design enables broad applicability across all three classes of amines, producing well-resolved 19F NMR signals that directly correlate with their absolute configurations. The method allows rapid, derivatization-free determination of enantiomeric excess and demonstrates a strong potential for application in pharmaceutical analysis and high-throughput screening.

Introduction

Chiral amines are key structural motifs in pharmaceuticals, agrochemicals, and bioactive natural products, with many small-molecule drugs and fine chemicals incorporating at least one chiral amine unit. , In recent years, biocatalytic strategies such as transaminase- and reductive aminase-catalyzed amination have rapidly expanded, providing efficient access to enantiopure amines. − Simultaneously, the advent of automated synthesis platforms and machine learning–based reaction prediction tools has enabled the rapid generation and evaluation of large libraries of chiral amine analogs, significantly reducing the time required for compound screening. − These advances collectively underscore the urgent need for high-throughput, accurate, and generalizable methods for determining enantiomeric excess (ee) and for achieving reliable chiral recognition across all classes of aminesincluding primary, secondary, and sterically hindered tertiary aminesto accelerate early stage drug discovery and catalyst development.

To address the limitations of conventional chromatographic techniques, such as prolonged analysis times and extensive method development, various spectroscopic approaches have been explored for chiral amine detection. − Among these, 19F NMR spectroscopy stands out as a particularly attractive tool, owing to its high sensitivity, broad chemical shift dispersion, and lack of background interference due to the scarcity of fluorinated compounds in biological matrices. − When integrated into chiral sensors, fluorine atoms enable the generation of distinct 19F signals upon binding with different enantiomers, allowing for rapid, direct, and nonseparative enantiomeric analysis. − Recent strategies for 19F NMR-based chiral analysis have utilized diverse probe architectures, including fluorinated metal complexes, chiral derivatizing agents, and host–guest systems, which have proven effective in resolving primary and secondary amines via analyte-induced changes in the 19F NMR spectrum. − For example, Su and co-workers developed a 19F-labeled chiral derivatizing reagent capable of simultaneously analyzing multiple amino acid enantiomers in complex mixtures. Huang’s group designed a trans-4-fluoro-l-proline–based chiral tag that undergoes rapid amine-aldehyde condensation, enabling analysis of 22 primary amines. Kim and co-workers introduced a two-step protocol involving derivatization with 2-fluorobenzoyl chloride followed by interaction with a chiral cobalt­(III) complex, achieving enantiomer resolution through induced shifting of the 19F NMR signal. The Song group developed a fluorine-containing chiral rhodium complex as a chiral sensor, which generated distinguishable 19F NMR signals for enantiomeric analytes, enabling the differentiation of various primary amines and amino acids. Our group previously reported a cyclopalladium-based 19F probe that facilitated efficient enantiomeric discrimination of secondary amines, providing a valuable tool for the high-throughput screening of asymmetric imine reduction reactions.

Despite substantial progress in chiral sensing, the enantiodiscrimination of tertiary amines remains a persistent challenge. Unlike primary and secondary amines, tertiary amines lack an N–H moiety, making them largely unreactive toward conventional derivatization strategies. , Their protonated forms also interact only weakly with chiral solvating agents such as crown ethers, yielding minimal spectral differentiation in NMR analyses. , Moreover, severe steric congestion around the nitrogen center often hinders effective coordination with metal-based probes, rendering stable probe–analyte complex formation energetically unfavorable. These combined steric and electronic constraints underscore the need for new sensing systems capable of universally addressing all classes of chiral amines. To this end, we developed a spatially optimized probe design to alleviate steric congestion around the metal coordination center. Specifically, the bulky side arms of the probe were replaced with flexible alkyl groups to enhance accessibility for amine tertiary binding (Scheme ). Simultaneously, the chiral fluorine-containing moiety was repositioned away from the coordination site to minimize interference during complex formation. Through this design approach, we report a 19F-labeled chiral probe that enables broad-scope enantiomeric discrimination of primary, secondary, and critically tertiary amines. The probe produces distinct 19F NMR signals that correspond precisely to the absolute configurations of a wide range of analytes, offering a powerful and versatile platform for nonderivatizing chiral analysis.

Optimizing the Previous Sterically Hindered 19F-Labeled Probe by Reducing Steric Hindrance to Enable Its Binding to Tertiary Amines

Experimental Section

Preparation of (R)-3,3,3-Trifluoro-2-hydroxy-2-methylpropanamide (2)

A solution of (R)-2-hydroxy-2-methyl-3,3,3-trifluoropropanoic acid (1.00 g, 6.33 mmol) in THF (20 mL) was cooled to 0 °C and stirred for 10 min. Carbonyldiimidazole (CDI) (1.03 g, 6.33 mmol) was added, and the mixture was stirred for 5 min before being warmed to room temperature. After being stirred for 2 h, the reaction mixture was added dropwise to a precooled (0 °C) solution of ammonia (0.4 M in 1,4-dioxane, 65 mL, 26 mmol). The resulting mixture was allowed to warm to room temperature and stirred overnight. The crude product was purified by column chromatography (dichloromethane/methyl alcohol = 98:2) to afford compound 2 (664 mg, 67% yield) as a white solid.

Preparation of (R)-3-Amino-1,1,1-trifluoro-2-methylpropan-2-ol Hydrochloride (3)

Under an inert atmosphere, compound 2 (340 mg, 2.16 mmol) was dissolved in anhydrous tetrahydrofuran (5 mL) and cooled to 0 °C. A solution of LiAlH4 in tetrahydrofuran (THF, 2.5 M, 5.1 mL, 12.75 mmol) was added dropwise, and the mixture was allowed to warm slowly to room temperature. After being stirred for 30 min at room temperature, the reaction was heated to 60 °C and stirred overnight. Upon completion (monitored by 1H NMR), the mixture was carefully quenched by sequential dropwise addition of H2O (484 μL), aqueous 15% NaOH (484 μL), and H2O (968 μL). The resulting suspension was filtered, and the solid residue was washed with THF. The filtrate was treated with 4 M HCl in THF (1 mL), concentrated under reduced pressure, and triturated with Et2O (20 mL). The precipitate was collected by filtration to afford compound 3 (264 mg, 68% yield) as a white solid.

Preparation of Ligand 4

To a solution of 3 (135 mg, 0.752 mmol) and 2,6-pyridinedicarboxylic acid monomethyl ester (150 mg, 0.827 mmol) in anhydrous dichloromethane (DCM, 10 mL) were added sequentially 1-[3-(dimethylamino)­propyl]-3-ethylcarbodiimide hydrochloride (EDCI, 158.5 mg, 0.827 mmol), 4-dimethylaminopyridine (DMAP, 9 mg, 0.075 mmol), and NEt3 (315 μL, 2.26 mmol). The reaction mixture was stirred at room temperature overnight. After the reaction had reached completion, the solvent was removed under reduced pressure. The crude product was purified by column chromatography (DCM/MeOH = 98:2) to afford the intermediate methyl ester. The purified ester was dissolved in a mixed solvent of THF/H2O (1:1, 20 mL), and NaOH (90 mg, 2.26 mmol) was added. The mixture was stirred overnight at room temperature. The reaction was then extracted with EtOAc (20 mL × 3), and the aqueous phase was acidified to pH = 1 with dilute HCl. The product was extracted with EtOAc (40 mL × 5), and the combined organic layers were dried over anhydrous Na2SO4, filtered, and concentrated under reduced pressure to yield the ligand 4 (125 mg, 57% yield) as a white solid.

Preparation of Probe 5

Under an inert atmosphere, ligand 4 (50 mg, 0.171 mmol) and Pd­(OAc)2 (42 mg, 0.188 mmol) were dissolved in a mixed solvent of CH3CN/MeOH (1:1, 20 mL). The reaction mixture was stirred at ambient temperature for 2 h. After completion, the mixture was filtered through a 0.22 μm syringe filter and the filtrate was concentrated under reduced pressure. The crude product was transferred to a filter funnel and washed extensively with diethyl ether to afford probe 5 (60.6 mg, 81% yield) as a yellow solid.

NMR Measurements

For the NMR measurements, stock solutions containing the designated probe and analytes were prepared to achieve the required concentrations. After mixing, these solutions were transferred to a 5 mm NMR tube, ready for spectral acquisition. 19F NMR spectral data were obtained at a temperature of 298 K by using a Bruker Avance NMR spectrometer (operating at 564 MHz for 19F) equipped with a 5.0 mm BBFO probe. Deuterated chloroform was used as the solvent, and spectral processing was done using the MestReNova. Standard parameters, including a relaxation delay (D1) set at 1 s and a scan count of 32, were used for the acquisition of the 19F NMR spectra.

Results and Discussion

Our structural optimization strategy leveraged an NNO pincer scaffold to create a relatively open binding site and replaced the previously employed rigid phenyl side arm with a flexible alkyl chain to effectively reduce the steric hindrance. Additionally, a trifluoromethyl group was positioned at the distal β-position of the amide moiety to further minimize steric interactions during complexation with tertiary amines. Crucially, the chiral center was relocated from the sterically congested α-position to the β-position, thereby preserving the chiral recognition capability while mitigating spatial constraints. Following this design, the key β-trifluoromethylated chiral amine intermediate was synthesized from (R)-2-hydroxy-2-methyl-3,3,3-trifluoropropanoic acid (1) via amide formation and subsequent reduction. This intermediate was then coupled with 2,6-pyridinedicarboxylic acid monomethyl ester and hydrolyzed to afford ligand 4 (Scheme a). Finally, 19F-labeled probe 5 was obtained by complexation of ligand 4 with palladium acetate (Scheme b).

Preparation of the 19F-Labeled Pd­(II) NNO Pincer Complex Probe 5

With the newly designed probe in hand, we first evaluated its recognition ability toward tertiary amines. In 19F NMR titration studies using (S)-N,N-dimethyl-1-phenylethan-1-amine (A 1S ) as a model analyte, we maintained a constant concentration of probe 5 while gradually increasing the amount of the analyte. Upon analyte addition, the 19F spectra exhibited progressively intensified complex signals alongside a corresponding decrease in the free probe signal, consistent with a slow-exchange binding regime (Figure S1, Supporting Information). At five equiv of analyte, the signal corresponding to the probe–acetonitrile complex nearly disappeared, confirming successful analyte recognition. To determine the binding stoichiometry between probe 5 and analyte A 1S , we performed 1H NMR experiments using a mixture of probe 5 with 5 equiv of A 1S . After complex formation, the integration of the triplet pyridine proton signal from probe 5 (fully bound) and the benzylic proton signal of the bound analyte appeared in a 1:1 ratio. In addition, comparison of the signals from free versus bound analyte showed a 4:1 ratio (Figure S2, Supporting Information), consistent with the presence of excess free analyte. These observations confirm that probe 5 forms a 1:1 complex with A 1S . Under optimized conditions, we were able to detect analyte A 1S at a concentration as low as 50 μM, corresponding to a total analyte mass of approximately 3.7 μg in the NMR tube, demonstrating the high sensitivity of the approach (Figure S3, Supporting Information). In contrast, previously developed probes , failed to show any interaction with A 1S , highlighting the importance of relocating the CF3-bearing chiral center away from the binding site to reduce steric hindrance (Figure S4, Supporting Information). We also synthesized a probe bearing CH3 and CF3 substituents at the α-position (Figure S5, Supporting Information). Upon addition of 5 equiv of A 1S , no changes were observed in the 19F NMR spectrum, further validating the importance of positioning the CF3-bearing chiral center away from the binding site to minimize steric hindrance. Interestingly, the analysis of A 1S or A 1R revealed that the newly formed major signals were often accompanied by two weaker peaks. We attribute these additional signals to distinct binding conformations (Figure a). Due to the substantial steric bulk of tertiary amines, once coordinated to palladium, rotation around the Pd–N bond becomes restricted, resulting in slow interconversion among the binding modes on the NMR time scale. This hypothesis is supported by DFT calculations, which identified four energetically distinguishable binding modes between probe 5 and A 1R with varying conformational arrangements. Among these, mode A was the most energetically favorable, while modes B–D were +3.18, + 1.17, and +1.62 kcal/mol higher in energy, respectively (Figure S6, Supporting Information). The absence of a detectable signal for mode B likely reflects its relatively high energy and low population under experimental conditions. The high energy of mode B originates from the proximity of the chiral carbon of A 1R to the amide group of probe 5, which induces pronounced steric repulsion and structural strain. By contrast, in mode A the chiral centers of A 1R and probe 5 are positioned on opposite sides, maximizing their separation and yielding the most favorable configuration. The energy difference between modes C and D arises from interactions between the CH3 substituent on the A 1R chiral center and the CF3 group of the probe, with repulsion between CH3 and CF3 stronger than between H and CF3 (Figure S7, Supporting Information). These multiple binding states underscore the intrinsic challenge and complexity of achieving efficient chiral discrimination for sterically hindered tertiary amines. DFT calculations indicated that in the most stable structure of the probe 5-A 1S complex, the phenyl ring is positioned closer to the trifluoromethyl group, leading to a stronger deshielding effect than in the probe 5-A 1S complex (Figure S8, Supporting Information). Consequently, probe 5-A 1S exhibits a downfield 19F NMR shift, whereas the weaker deshielding in probe 5-A 1R results in a stronger upfield resonance. Moreover, variable-temperature NMR experiments demonstrated that the chemical shifts of the complex remained unchanged even at 50 °C, confirming that the binding modes are thermally stable across the investigated temperature range (Figure S9, Supporting Information). Notably, although a given enantiomer may generate multiple signals, the broad chemical shift dispersion of 19F NMR allows these signals to be well resolved, thereby enabling clear and unambiguous chiral discrimination.

To evaluate the broad applicability of probe 5 in chiral recognition, we applied it to a structurally diverse set of primary, secondary, and tertiary amine analytes (Figure b–ab). The experimental results confirmed that probe 5 could reliably discriminate enantiomers across all three amine classes, generating distinct 19F NMR signals that corresponded accurately to their absolute configurations. Notably, the spectral patterns varied significantly, depending on the amine type. Primary amines typically produced a single pair of well-resolved enantiomeric signals with characteristic R/S doublet features. In contrast, secondary and certain tertiary amines exhibited multibinding behavior, generating additional peaks due to slow chemical exchange between distinct binding modes. Analysis across analytes A 1 –A 27 revealed considerable variation, largely dictated by structural features. Greater steric or electronic disparity between substituents generally led to higher Rs valuesfor example, A 8 (naphthyl) outperformed A 1 (phenyl), and A 4 (ester) showed superior resolution compared to A 9 (hydroxyl). A consistent trend was observed wherein S-configured analytes (e.g., A 1 , A 3 , A 8 , A 14 , and A 15 ) exhibited downfield-shifted signals relative to their R-enantiomers, facilitating straightforward assignment of absolute configurations for structurally related compounds. For cyclic tertiary amines (e.g., A 2 , A 5 , A 6 , and A 11 ), the emergence of multiple signals likely arose from alternative binding modes, enhancing recognition robustness and enabling differentiation even in the presence of overlapping peaks. Overall, tertiary amines exhibited lower Rs values than their primary and secondary counterparts, likely due to the absence of N–H-mediated polar interactions and an increased reliance on steric effects. For instance, tertiary amine A 3 displayed a lower resolution than primary amine A 20 . Probe 5 demonstrated excellent functional group tolerance, effectively resolving analytes containing hydroxyl (A 2 , A 9 , and A 17 ), ester (A 4 , A 6 , A 11 , A 24 , A 25 ), and Boc-protected (A 5 , A 19 ) groups. Importantly, it enabled the chiral recognition of analytes with remote stereocenters (A 4 , A 9 , A 16 , A 19 ) and those with poor UV absorbance (A 2 , A 13 , A 16 , A 17 ), eliminating the need for derivatization typically required in conventional HPLC analysis. Compared to chiroptical methods, − the sharp and well-resolved 19F NMR signals enable unambiguous multicomponent analysis, making this approach particularly well suited for complex real-world mixtures, including opaque or dark samples that can interfere with optical measurements. Furthermore, its capacity for multiclass recognition allowed simultaneous enantiomeric analysis of primary, secondary, and tertiary amines within complex mixtures (Figure S10, Supporting Information), underscoring its versatility and practical utility in biological and pharmaceutical research.

To evaluate the practical utility of probe 5 for quantitative determination of the ee in chiral samples, we selected model analyte A 1 for validation. Given that probe 5 may exhibit differential binding affinities toward the enantiomers of chiral tertiary amines, such disparities could introduce bias in quantitative analysis. To address this, a correction factor was introduced based on the observed 19F NMR signal intensity ratios from a racemic mixture (Figures S11 and S12, Supporting Information). This correction compensates for enantiomer-selective binding effects, enabling accurate quantification despite the inherent affinity differences. When applied to enantiomerically enriched samples, the correction factor effectively compensated for differential binding affinities, resulting in accurate signal ratio calibration and ensuring precise ee determination. As illustrated in Figure , the corrected ee values exhibited excellent agreement with the actual enantiomeric composition of the samples, thereby validating the method’s accuracy and reliability. The high efficiency and precision of this approach make it particularly well-suited for early stage screening and quantitative analysis of chiral drug development. The stereochemical configuration of chiral amine-based drugs plays a critical role in determining their pharmacological activity. For instance, baclofen, a muscle relaxant used to treat spasms, spinal cord injuries, and multiple sclerosis, exhibits greater potency in its R-enantiomer form. Similarly, the R-enantiomer of rasagiline shows enhanced efficacy in the treatment of Parkinson’s disease, whereas the S-enantiomer of rivastigmine is more effective for managing Alzheimer’s disease. These examples highlight the urgent need for universal and rapid chiral discrimination methods to support drug activity optimization and chiral pharmacokinetic studies. Using probe 5, we developed a broadly applicable detection platform capable of generating highly distinctive 19F NMR signals for the enantiomers of chiral amine-based drugs. As illustrated in Figure , the method effectively distinguishes enantiomers across a wide range of amine-containing drugsincluding primary, secondary, and tertiary aminesthus significantly extending the scope of conventional chiral detection techniques. This approach offers several key advantages, including the elimination of derivatization steps, rapid analysis, and excellent chiral resolution, providing an efficient and reliable tool for the enantiomeric analysis of pharmaceutical compounds. Its robustness and versatility position it as a promising technology for pharmaceutical quality control and regulatory compliance.

Conclusions

In summary, we have developed a spatially optimized 19F-labeled palladium-based chiral probe that enables universal enantiodiscrimination of various amines. By reducing the steric bulk of side arms and repositioning the trifluoromethyl-containing chiral center away from the metal coordination site, the probe achieves strong and selective binding, even with highly hindered tertiary amines. This design overcomes long-standing challenges associated with the chiral analysis of tertiary amines, which are often incompatible with conventional derivatization- or coordination-based strategies. The probe generates well-resolved, analyte-specific 19F NMR signals that directly reflect the absolute configuration of a broad range of amine enantiomers. Its broad substrate compatibility, derivatization-free operation, and high-resolution output make it a versatile tool for rapid ee determination. Given its robust performance across all amine classes, this method offers a powerful platform for high-throughput screening, asymmetric reaction evaluation, and pharmaceutical quality control. Future work will focus on expanding its applicability to structurally complex amine derivatives and integrating it into automated sensing workflows.
