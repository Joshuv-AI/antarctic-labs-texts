Promotional effects of aged ZnO nanoparticles on migration, invasion and metastasis of human breast cancer MCF-7 cells in vitro and in vivo
Authors: Na Yu, Jian Hu, Dixiang Kang, Jiahui He, Meimei Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe high success of ZnO nanoparticles (NPs), mainly associated with their unique physicochemical properties, biocompatibility, and cost-effective mass production, has led to an increasing application in our daily life, industry and biomedical fields. Although many studies have been carried out to detect potential toxicity of pristine ZnO NPs, most of them have been developed under acute and short-term exposure conditions. In terms of human risk, the evaluation of long-term exposures to sub-toxic doses of NPs, especially regarding environmental transformed (aged) ZnO NPs remains a challenge. Our previous findings have revealed that ZnO NPs underwent physicochemical transformations with aging and its genotoxicity/carcinogenicity change accordingly.MethodsIn the present work, we focused on to determine the cancer progression-related end-points under a scenario of four months exposure to non-cytotoxic aged ZnO NPs (1.5 μg/mL) in MCF-7 cells (a human breast cancer cell line with poor tumorigenicity in nude mice). We performed the in vitro assays regarding cancer cell progression such as plate cloning formation, soft agar colony formation, transwell migration and invasion. A mouse in situ xenograft model was constructed by intravenously injecting MCF-7 cells that had been chronically exposed to aged ZnO NPs for 4 months, to examine the effects of aged ZnO NPs on tumor metastasis in vivo. Hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining and immunohistochemistry (IHC) were used to detect proliferation and the expression of Ki67 protein for evaluation of lung/liver metastases.ResultsThe results showed that aged ZnO NPs significantly enhanced the migration and invasion ability of MCF-7 cells in vitro. Surprisingly, this cancer progression effect was also reflected by the fact that MCF-7 cells underwent specific hepatic metastasis rather than pulmonary metastasis in vivo. Mechanistically, this process was accompanied by up-regulation of Claudin-2 (CLDN2). The further in vitro experiments showed that CLDN2 knockdown significantly decreased the migration and invasion of MCF-7 cells.ConclusionThe study points out the potential cancer-promoting risks of exposure to ZnO NPs at low dose and under long-term exposure condition, and the importance of using real-life exposure scenarios to fully assess the safety of NPs.

Introduction

Globally, breast cancer represents the most common malignant tumour in women, with the highest incidence and mortality rates [1,2]. Individuals diagnosed with breast cancer often adopt a positive outlook, given that nearly 44 % of patients diagnosed in the early stages have an almost 100 % chance of surviving for five years. Nevertheless, the survival rate declines significantly, to 26 %, when the cancer has metastasized to other organs [3]. Therefore, prevention of breast cancer metastasis has always been a major clinical issue of utmost importance. Investigations aiming to identify the promoting risk factors responsible for enhancing the growth and metastasis of breast cancer may help people prevent the increased malignancy and lethality of the disease.

It is becoming increasingly evident that chemical pollutants, in addition to other risk factors, exert an influence on the signaling pathways that are involved in the development and progression of metastatic tumour cells [4]. The correlation between heavy metals such as arsenic/cadmium and breast cancer metastasis has been reported [[5], [6], [7]], but it is still unclear whether metal (oxide) nanomaterials (NMs), a new class of pollutants, are also pro-tumor metastasis, i.e., whether NMs exposure would be a risk factor for promoting cancer metastasis. A multitude of nanoparticle (NP) types, including the extensively employed zinc oxide (ZnO) NPs, have been deemed safe for use in a plethora of applications, including the cosmetic industry, as drug delivery vehicles, and as novel anticancer agents in modified photodynamic therapy [[8], [9], [10]]. However, the premise of being considered safe is based on our current judgment against safety testing of NPs in their pristine state, whereas in fact once NPs are released into the environment or enter the human body, they may undergo chemical/physical/biological transformations and exhibit partial or completely different effects, and they may become no longer safe [[11], [12], [13], [14], [15]]. For instance, the application of iron oxide nanoparticles to contaminated soil for the purpose of remediation resulted in alterations to the aforementioned parameters, including stability, transformation, and removal efficiency, bioavailability, toxicity, and impact on soil ecosystems [16]. It has been reported that ZnO NPs could be chemically transformed to amorphous material and Zn3(PO4)20.4 H2O and/or Na(ZnPO4)·H2O in simulated sweat within 168 or 24 h. Such kind of chemical change affected the antimicrobial activity of ZnO NPs against Escherichia coli, and the toxicity of NPs was reduced due to the concentration of dissolved zinc being considerably lower [17]. Numerous studies have shown notable alterations in toxicity following the treatment with sulfidized ZnO NPs on zebrafish embryos [18], microalgae Chlorella sorokiniana [19] and earthworm Eisenia fetida [20]. Previously, we discovered by chance that ZnO NPs maintained in ddH2O for dozens of days could converted into Zn2+ and hydrozincite (Zn5(CO3)2(OH)6) [21]. We thought firstly aged ZnO NPs as safer because they showed lower cytoxicity. Nevertheless, subsequent observations revealed that aged ZnO NPs exhibited a markedly elevated level of mutagenicity in human-hamster hybrid (AL) cells, accompanied by enhanced carcinogenicity in mouse embryonic fibroblast (MEF) cells and nude mice [21,22]. Indeed, both TiO2 NPs and carbon nanotubes (CNT) have been proved to be carcinogenic NMs with promoting effects on cancer progression [23,24]. Lu and her collaborates have proved that respiratory exposure to NPs in the lungs can act on non-lung tumors through the circulatory system rather than through direct contact of NPs with tumor cells, promoting the development of ectopic tumors and eventually their metastasis to distal organs [24]. Therefore, it is reasonable to believe that NMs that enter the human body through different routes of exposure may ultimately influence the final fate of cancer cells. Since aged ZnO NPs also possess similar stability and carcinogenicity as carbon NMs, and the purpose of our study is to evaluate the stimulatory effects of aged ZnO NPs on breast cancer progression both in vitro and in vivo, MCF-7, a breast cancer cell line with low metastatic potential, was chosen here as a “non-heathy” in vitro experimental model. Given that breast cancer cells primarily metastasize to distant organs such as the liver, lungs, and bones via haematogenous dissemination, the tail vein injection model is widely recognized and adopted within the field as one of the optimal experimental models. Compared to orthotopic injection into the mammary fat pad, it offers advantages including highly simulation of the haematogenous metastasis process, greater reproducibility, and a shorter experimental cycle-given that metastasis typically disseminates within a few weeks [25]. In detail, first, we examined the cytotoxicity of 0–20 μg/mL of fresh and aged ZnO NPs to MCF-7 cells by a 72 h acute exposure assay. Then a chronic exposure dose of 1.5 μg/mL was selected based on the results of the acute experiment described above. Further, after MCF-7 cells were chronically exposed to NPs for up to 16 weeks, the promotional effects of NPs on cancer progression was assessed using various parameters, including i) alterations in proliferation rate, ii) the capacity to grow without surface adherence, as tested by the soft-agar assay, iii) migration/invasion capacity. Our focus in this study on the promotion of cancer cell metastasis by aged ZnO NPs better reflects the actual exposure scenario, where most of the population is chronically exposed to low, and non-toxic pristine/fresh and transformed/aged NPs during long-term periods. In addition, the potential risk of tumor -promotion and -invasion associated with aged NMs exposure is a very important topic needing urgent investigation. All together, these types of studies can provide relevant information for comprehensive risk assessment of NMs and identification of risk factors for cancer.

Materials & methods

In line with the approach previously outlined for the study of nanoparticle toxicity [22], a concise experimental plan was devised, as illustrated in Fig. 1. This plan entailed the continuous exposure of MCF-7 cells to a selected subtoxic concentration (1.5 μg/mL) over a 16-week period (40th passage). Reagents/chemicals and NPs characterization

ZnO nanopowder (20 nm, purity 99.5 %, spherical, Specific Surface Area 40 m2/g; Nanostructured & Amorphous Materials, Katy, TX, USA) was suspended in Milli-Q water (Millipore, 18 MΩ cm) and sonicated (Shumei, KQ-800KDE, Kunshan, China) using a continuous mode. The 1 mg/mL stock suspension (referred to as fresh NPs) was sterilized by heating to 120 °C for 30 min and stored at room temperature (25 °C) for naturally aging period (60 days, referred to as aged NPs), as described previously [21]. The Dulbecco's Modified Eagle's medium (DMEM) (high glucose) and fetal bovine serum (FBS) were acquired from Gibco (Grand Island, NY, USA). Plastic culture plates and flasks used in the experiment were supplied by Corning Incorporated (Costar, Corning, NY, USA). Anti-CLDN2 was purchased from Santa Cruz Biotechnology (Shanghai, China) and anti-GAPDH was bought from Servicebio Technology (Wuhan, China).

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEOL, JEM-2010, Japan) was employed to examine the morphological alterations of 100 μg/mL of freshly prepared and aged ZnO NPs dispersed in Milli-Q water. Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS, NANOSIGHT NS300, Malvern Panalytical Ltd., Worcestershire, United Kingdom) was employed to ascertain the hydrodynamic particle size and the polydispersity index (PDI) of the sonicated NPs (50 μg/mL) in Milli-Q water at room temperature or suspended in DMEM/10 % FBS at 37 °C for 72 h. Inductively Coupled Plasma Optical Emission Spectrometry (ICP-OES, EXPEC-6500D, Focused Photonics Inc., Hangzhou, China) was used to quantitatively assess the soluble fractions (Zn ions) of fresh and aged NPs suspensions (50 μg/mL) after centrifugation at 20 000 rpm (28 000 g) for 1 h with cooling.

Cell culture and viability of MCF-7 after 72h exposure to fresh and aged ZnO NPs

Breast cancer cell line MCF-7 was derived from the Shanghai Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences. The cells were maintained in DMEM supplemented with 10 % FBS and 50 units/mL penicillin-streptomycin in a 5 % CO2 atmosphere at 37 °C. To ensure the stability of the experiment, we selected cells that have been passaged no more than five times for the assay. A total of 4 × 105 cells were seeded in triplicate into 6-well plates and incubated for 24 h to allow for attainment of a logarithmic phase of growth. Subsequently, the cells were treated with 2 mL of culture medium containing 1, 5, 10, and 20 μg/mL of freshly prepared or aged ZnO NPs for a period of 72 h. NPs suspensions were sonicated before used in cell culture. The control group was incubated with medium without NPs. The viability of the cells was evaluated using the Count Star method, as developed by Beckman Coulter (Brea, CA). The viability of the exposed cells was expressed as a percentage of the viability of the unexposed controls.

Long-term fresh and aged ZnO NPs exposure

MCF-7 cells were subjected to continuous exposure to fresh and aged NPs at a sub-toxic concentration of 1.5 μg/mL for a period of 16 weeks (40th passage). The sub-toxic dose was selected on the basis of the results of a cytotoxicity assay conducted after 72 h of acute exposure. Approximately 1 × 105 cells were distributed in 60 mm dishes and incubated overnight. The working solutions of the fresh and aged ZnO NPs were subjected to sonication for a period of 30 min at a power output of 100W at room temperature. The cells were sub-cultured at 3.5-day intervals in a fresh medium containing either fresh or aged ZnO NPs. The NP-exposed cells were compared to unexposed controls that were matched by passage, as well as the ZnCl2-exposed groups. During chronic treatment, cells were frozen after every 5 passages to ensure the reproducibility of the experiment. ZnCl2 powder (Sigma Aldrich, Shanghai, China) was suspended in Milli-Q water to prepare a 1 mg/mL stock suspension, which was sterilized by heating at 120 °C for 30 min and stored at room temperature (25 °C). The stock suspension was diluted to 1.5 μg/mL with DMEM/10 % FBS before each use for chronic exposure of MCF7 cells.

Plate colony formation assay

After 16 weeks of exposure, the influence of fresh and aged ZnO NPs on the proliferation of MCF-7 cells under the chronic exposure condition was detected by colony formation assay. 500–700 fresh or aged ZnO NP-treated, ZnCl2-treated cells and control cells without any treatment were seeded into 6-well plates per well. Replenish the culture medium regularly (every 5 days) to prevent drying out. Following a period of two weeks, the colonies were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde, after which they were washed with PBS and subsequently stained with crystal violet dye (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). The plates were treated with 100 % methanol for a period of 15 min at room temperature, in order to facilitate the fixation of the cells. The cells were subsequently stained with Giemsa for a period of 20 min at room temperature. After PBS washing and air-drying, the clone formation rate was calculated under microscope. Phase-contrast microscopic images were captured for each samples using a digital camera (AxioCam ICc 5, Zeiss, Gottingen, Germany) coupled to a microscope (Zeiss Primovert, Zeiss, Gottingen, Germany).

The soft agar colony formation assay

MCF-7 cells after chronic exposure to fresh or aged NPs at selected subtoxic concentration (1.5 μg/mL) for up to 16 weeks (40th passage), as well as ZnCl2-group and control group cells were suspended in 0.3 % low-melting agarose with complete medium (20 % FBS), and plated onto a layer of 0.6 % agarose-containing medium in 6-well plate (1000 cells per well). Colonies were counted after 2–3 weeks, and the phase-contrast microscopic pictures were taken for each samples using a digital camera (AxioCam ICc 5, Zeiss, Gottingen, Germany) coupled to a microscope (Zeiss Primovert, Zeiss, Gottingen, Germany).

Cell invasion/migration ability assay

The capacity for cell invasion and migration was evaluated following chronic treatment with either fresh or aged ZnO NPs, as well as with ZnCl2 and a control group. This was conducted using transwell chambers with 8-μm pore filters (Corning, USA) in 24-well plates. For the migration assay, cells in 200 μL serum-free medium were seeded into the upper transwell chambers, while the lower chambers were filled with 700 μL complete medium to induce chemotaxis. After incubation for 48 h, the cells were washed for three times with PBS, fixed with methylalcohol and stained with crystal violet. The invasion assay differed from the migration assay only in that the chambers were coated with Matrigel (BD Biosciences, USA). Subsequently, the migrating and invading cells were enumerated, and the images were captured with a digital camera (AxioCam ICc 5, Zeiss, Gottingen, Germany) coupled to a microscope (Zeiss Primovert, Zeiss, Gottingen, Germany).

In vivo tumorigenicity assay

Five-week-old female BALB/c nude mice were obtained from Nanjing University Model Animal Research Center and placed in an SPF environment. The animals were treated in a humane manner, with due consideration given to the alleviation of suffering. The mice were housed in an animal facility with five to six animals per cage under conditions of controlled temperature (21–25 °C), humidity (50 %–60 %), and 12/12-h cycles of light and darkness. For the xenograft study, mice (n = 5) were anesthetized with lidocaine (Beyotime, shanghai, China), and then the nude mice were inoculated with ZnO NP (fresh or aged)- or ZnCl2 -treated (16 weeks) or control MCF-7 cells (1 × 106 cells diluted in 100 μL of PBS) via the tail vein once. The injections were alternated between groups of animals in order to minimize variances during the injection. The growth of the mice was observed and weighed every 3 day. Eight weeks after cell inoculation, the mice were de-necked and killed after being deeply anesthetized. The livers and lungs of the mice were collected for subsequent examination and analysis. There were no surviving animals at the end of the experiment. All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of Anhui Medical University. The design of the animal experiment was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Anhui Medical University (LLSC 20210812).

Tissue section preparation and hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining

After sampling, the lungs and the livers were gently rinsed with PBS as soon as possible. Afterwards, the organ samples were fixed in 10 % neutral buffer formalin solution, and then the tissues were immersed in ascending grades of ethanol to extract the water from the tissue. Later, the ethanol was replaced with xylene to clear the tissue, making it miscible with the embedding medium paraffin. Moreover, the tissue embedded in paraffin wax was sectioned at a thickness of 5 μm and mounted on glass slides. Finally, after deparaffinization, hydration and stained with HE respectively, the slices were mounted for observation and analyzed with a microscope (Zeiss Primovert, Zeiss, Gottingen, Germany).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

The expression of Ki67 in the lung and liver of mice was examined through immunohistochemical staining. The organ samples were fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde for a period of 24 h. Following this, the tissues were embedded in paraffin and subsequently cut into sections with a thickness of 4 μm. The sections were dewaxed and rehydrated using a series of descending gradients of alcohol and xylene. The removal of endogenous catalase was completed by incubating the sections with 3 % hydrogen peroxide for a period of 10 min. Following a 3-min boiling period in citrate buffer, the sections were blocked for 30 min at 37 °C in 5 % normal goat serum. The primary antibody anti-Ki67 (1:100, ab15580, Abcam, Shanghai, China) was applied to the sections for a 12-h incubation at 4 °C. Subsequently, a goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (1:200, A21020, AmyJet Scientific, Wuhan, China) was added for a 30-min reaction at 37 °C. The sections were stained with a sequential application of Diaminobenzidine solution (Solarbio, Beijing, China) and hematoxylin solution (Solarbio, Beijing, China), in accordance with the instructions provided. The sections were then dehydrated through a series of ascending gradients of alcohol and xylene, after which they were sealed in neutral resin. The positive staining of Ki67 was observed and photographed using an optical microscope (CX41, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Protein extraction and western blot assay

MCF-7 cells after chronic exposure to fresh or aged NPs at selected subtoxic concentration (1.5 μg/mL) for up to 16 weeks (40th passage), as well as ZnCl2-group and control group cells were rinsed with PBS and resuspended in RIPA lysis buffer containing PMSF (ratio is 100:1) at 4 °C for 15 min. The supernatants were collected as the total cellular proteins by centrifugation at 12 000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C, and the protein concentration was determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). At last, 20 μg of protein from each sample were separated by 10 % SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. They were then blocked with 5 % no-fat milk (w/v) for 2 h with gentle shaking and subsequently incubated with primary antibodies (CLDN2, 1:500 dilution or GAPDH, 1:3000 dilution) at 4 °C overnight. Subsequently, the membrane was incubated with the secondary antibody (anti-rabbit/mouse IgG, HRP conjugate 1:5000, Elabscience, China), followed by several washes with Tris-Buffered Saline with Tween-20 (TBST). The protein signal intensity was quantified using an imaging system (Fisher Varioskan, Thermo, USA). The density of the bands was subsequently determined using Image J software, with the intensity of GAPDH taken as the standard.

Short hairpin RNA (shRNA) transfection

Knockdown of CLDN2 using short hairpin RNA (shRNA) was generated from Genechem, China. Double-strand oligonucleotides, which corresponded to the RNA sequences, were cloned into the pGV493-RNAi plasmid. Target sequences for human CLDN2 mRNA were as follows: CAAAGTCAAGAGTGAGTTCAA. Then the plasmids were transfected respectively into MCF-7 (40th passage) cells (seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 7 × 105 cells per well) using Lipo8000™ Transfection Reagent (Beyotime, China) in accordance with the manufacturer's recommended protocols. Following a 48-h transfection period, the biological behaviours of MCF-7 cells were assessed through an in vitro migration and invasion assay.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical comparisons between two groups were performed using the unpaired or paired Student's t-test, as appropriate. For comparisons among the four groups (under a single grouping factor), a one-way analysis of variance (One-way ANOVA) was employed. The generation of graphs and calculation of p-values by one-way ANOVA were performed using GraphPad Prism 8 software (GraphPad Software, Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA). A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered to indicate a statistically significant difference.

Results

Characterization of fresh and aged ZnO NPs

The dispersion and characterization of NPs has been previously documented [21]. Specifically both were performed in dilute suspensions (100 μg/mL of NPs for TEM imaging and 50 μg/mL in Mill-Q water or medium for hydration particle size measurements). TEM images showed that fresh ZnO NPs were accompanied by not particularly regular spherical shapes, but the surfaces of the regular crystal structures were still relatively clear, whereas aged NPs showed amorphous or fuzzy crystal boundaries (Fig. 2A). More details regarding the other physical and chemical characteristics of the NPs used are shown in Fig. 2B. Obviously, both fresh and aged NPs were inclined to aggregate in ultrapure water, whereas they agglomerate to a significantly lesser extent in cell culture medium. Zinc quantification performed on the supernatant after centrifugation of the suspension showed that dissolution of NPs was affected to some extent by aging, although the increase was not statistically significant (Fig. 2B). The XRD patterns of fresh and aged ZnO NPs can refer to our previous articles [21,22]. Cell viability after short-term exposure to fresh and aged ZnO NPs

To examine the effect of fresh and aged ZnO NPs on cell proliferation, MCF-7 cells were incubated with 0–20 μg/mL NPs for 72 h and assessed with cell viability assay. As can be seen in Fig. 3, neither fresh nor aged NPs showed significant cytotoxicity at treatment concentrations not greater than 10 μg/mL, and there was no significant difference in cell lethality between the two states of NPs. In contrast, at relatively high incubation concentration (20 μg/mL), fresh NPs showed significantly higher cytotoxicity (with less than 5 % cell viability) than aged NPs (about 25 % remaining viability of MCF-7 cells). In other words, for MCF cells, aged NP showed significantly lower cytotoxicity than fresh NPs. Subsequently, we considered 1.5 μg/mL as a non-toxic dose to further evaluate the long-term effects of NPs on breast cancer MCF-7 cells.Fig. 3Acute exposure to fresh and aged ZnO NPs induced cytotoxicity in MCF-7 cells. Promotional effects of fresh and aged ZnO NPs on the proliferation, migration and invasion of MCF-7 cells in vitro

Cell proliferation, migration and invasion in response to NPs’ chronic treatments were assessed. As shown in Fig. 4, the cells treated with fresh NPs for 16 weeks exhibited significantly increase in proliferation compared to control cells. In contrast, the number of colonies in MCF-7 cells treated with aged NPs was 2.2 times that of the control group and 1.66 times that of fresh NPs group, and was significantly higher than that of ZnCl2 group (p < 0.01; Fig. 4B). Compared with the cells in fresh NPs group, the cells in aged NPs group also showed a significant increase in soft agar colony formation after 16 weeks of chronic exposuree, reaching 1.77 times that of the fresh group (Fig. 5). Soft agar colonization of cancer cells is thought to reflect anchorage-dependent growth of tumor-inducing cells/cancer stem cells [26]. We observed that aged NP-treated cells formed relatively larger and more colonies compared with the other three groups of cells (Fig. 5A). Transwell assays were conducted to further assess the impact of prolonged exposure to both fresh and aged ZnO NPs on migration and invasion. As shown in Fig. 6A and B, the number of migrated cells was the highest in aged NP-treated group, which was 10.2 and 1.94 times higher than that in control and fresh groups, respectively. It was also significantly increased compared to ZnCl2 group (p < 0.01). Consistent with expectations, the aged ZnO NP-treated group demonstrated a similar trend, with the greatest number of invading cells traversing the Matrigel-2.44 times that of fresh group (Fig. 7). To determine whether fresh and aged ZnO NPs may affect tumor progression in vivo, we injected MCF-7 cells chronically treated with NPs for 16 weeks into BALB/c nude female mice via tail vein to generate a lung metastasis model. To our surprise the injected MCF-7 cells did not colonize and form tumors in the lungs, but metastasized to the liver tissues (Fig. 8). As shown in Fig. 9, the relatively most pronounced liver metastatic burden remained to be caused by the cells chronically treated with aged NPs. CLDN2 could be involved in fresh and aged ZnO NP-induced liver metastasis of MCF-7 cells

Intriguingly, the chronic administration of fresh or aged ZnO NPs to MCF-7 cells resulted in the deliberate translocation of these cells to the liver following their injection via the tail vein. In order to explore the mechanisms of this phenomenon, we read a large amount of literature and found that Claudin-2 (CLDN2), as a component of cellular tight junction, has been reported to play a role in the progression of various cancers through its aberrant expression [[27], [28], [29]]. Various studies indicated that CLDN2 is an independent prognostic factor for early liver recurrence in breast cancer [[30], [31], [32], [33]]. Therefore, we examined the expression level of CLDN2 in MCF-7 cells chronically treated with fresh or aged ZnO NPs for 4 months. As shown in Fig. 11, Western blot analysis exhibited that aged NPs’ treatment resulted in significant increase in the levels of CLDN2. Conversely, down-regulation of CLDN2 by shRNAs mitigated the migration and invasion abilities of MCF-7 cells treated with fresh and aged ZnO NPs for 40 passages (Fig. 12). Discussions

Breast cancer is the most common malignancy in women worldwide, and approximately one-third of women diagnosed with non-metastatic breast cancer at an early stage will eventually develop metastatic disease [[34], [35], [36]], which often becomes resistant to therapies [37]. The situation suggests that exploring pro-metastatic influences is becoming increasingly important for preventing breast cancer metastasis and improving the overall survival of women with breast cancer.

Given that a considerable number of tumors have an environmental aetiology, it is imperative to ascertain the potential carcinogenic risk of environmental agents [38]. Nanoparticles, as a kind of new pollutants, their toxicities have been well elucidated and extensively explored [8,39,40]. Regarding the carcinogencity of ZnO NPs, most reports focused on fresh NPs. Annangi and his team once attempted to study the carcinogenic effects of fresh ZnO NPs using wild-type mouse embryonic fibroblast (MEF) cells and its isogenic 8-oxo-guanine DNA glycosylase 1 (Ogg1) knockout cells. However, their results demonstrated that 1 μg/mL exposure over 12 weeks was was incapable of inducing cellular transformation in both cell types, as evidenced by the absence of observable morphological cellular alterations, variations in the secretion of matrix metalloproteinases, and anchorage-independent cell growth potential [41]. As to the main differences of pristine (fresh) NPs between our previous research [22] and Annangi's [41], except for the different characters of NPs themselves, were the exposure dosage and the time range. Later, the malignant transformation of colonic mucosal cells (IMCEs) was observed following the introduction of fresh ZnO NPs, which demonstrated hyperproliferation, morphological alterations, an anchorage-independent growth capacity in the soft-agar assay, and migratory potential (as evidenced by the wound-healing assay). In addition to the different biomarkers analyzed in the in vitro carcinogenesis study, the tumour phenotype of the cells after 30 passages of exposure was confirmed, given their tumourigenic potential when subcutaneously administered to nude mice [42]. However, there are no much more work have been carried out to explore the carcinogenicity of transformed NPs after they have been released into environments or used by human in the daily life or the medical area. Actually, an improved understanding of the environmental transformation processes and the corresponding changes in the toxicities (especially carcinogenicity) of NPs is imperative for risk assessment and management [43].

Our previous studies have proved that ZnO NPs underwent sophisticated physicochemical transformations in ultrapure water with aging, such as increased tendency towards agglomeration, the microstructural changes, the formation of hydrozincite (Zn5(CO3)2(OH)6) and the release of free zinc ions, regardless of original morphology (irregular vs nearly spherical) or size (90–200 nm vs 20 nm). And aged ZnO NPs resulted in relatively higher degree of mutation frequencies in human-hamster hybrid (AL) cells than fresh NPs. After separating the soluble and insoluble fractions of fresh and aged ZnO suspension by centrifugation, together with the control measurements using metal chelator and endocytosis inhibitor, the mutation induction of aged ZnO NPs was found to be closely related to the soluble fraction and partially mediated by the transformed particulates [21,44]. Later, we further demonstrated that prolonged exposure of MEF cells to aged ZnO NPs in cultures induced malignant transformation of the treated cells, which obtained the ability to form subcutaneous tumors in a xenograft model with immunodeficient mice [22]. However, the toxicological evaluation of NPs has so far been performed more on normal animals, and the possible effects of NPs on tumour growth/metastasis have not been adequately considered [24,45]. It is well known that carcinogenesis is a continuous process comprised of initiation, promotion, and progression [7]. Herein, we asked whether aged ZnO NPs with relatively high stability, compared with fresh NPs, could turned to be a kind of cancer metastasis/progression stimulator. We obtained novel evidences that low-dose chronic exposure to ZnO NPs (especially aged NPs) could enhance the cancer progression ability of breast cancer cells. Anchorage-independent growth of cells in soft agar is a defining feature of cellular transformation, with normal cells typically unable to proliferate in semi-solid matrices [26]. A significant increase in the soft agar colony formation ability was found in MCF-7 cells after 16 weeks of continuous exposure with low dose of fresh or aged ZnO NPs compared to untreated groups (Fig. 5). In particular, aged NPs always induced more colonies formation than that of fresh NPs and ZnCl2 (p < 0.01, Fig. 5). Similarly, the migration and invasion assays showed similar trends as the soft-agar cell colonies assay (Fig. 6, Fig. 7). Furthermore, the utilization of a mammary tumour xenograft model enabled the demonstration that the treatment of MCF-7 cells with 1.5 μg/mL fresh or aged ZnO NPs for 16 weeks resulted in hepatic metastasis in nude mice, with aged NPs showing the strongest enhancement (Fig. 8, Fig. 9, Fig. 10). These observations indicated that fresh ZnO NPs promote tumor metastasis in vivo, whereas aged NPs are more pro-tumor metastatic. As outlined in the report published by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC), carcinogens should possess specific characteristics, including genotoxicity, alterations to DNA repair mechanisms or genomic instability, induction of oxidative stress or chronic inflammation, immunosuppression or immortalisation, and alterations to cell proliferation and cell death pathways [46]. Thus here, combined with our earlier findings [21,22], we conclude that fresh and aged ZnO NPs promote the initiation, promotion and aggressiveness of tumorigenesis, and that such effects are stronger in aged NPs.

As stated above, in testing the pro-carcinogenic effect of aged ZnO NPs, we chose a nude mouse lung metastasis model in which MCF-7 cells (a human breast cancer cell line with poor tumorigenicity in nude mice) were injected via the tail vein, and we would have expected to observe first should be lung metastases, but in fact we observed specific liver metastases. Considering that liver metastases are associated with the worst survival compared to locoregional, bone, brain and lung metastases [47,48], thus, we aimed to delineate the mechanism underlying preferential metastasis of breast cancer to the liver induced by aged ZnO NPs. Several studies have shown an association between high expression of claudin-2 (CLDN2) and liver recurrence. Claudins (CLDNs) are scaffolding proteins of tight junction strands in epithelial and endothelial cells and play an important role in cellular adhesion, polarity, permeability and glandular differentiation [49,50]. Kimbung et al. showed that it was an attribute of primary tumors, as a significantly higher proportion of patients with liver metastases also displayed had high levels of CLDN2 in their tumors [30]. In addition, Tabaries et al. provided the functional evidence characterizing CLDN2 as a breast cancer liver metastasis virulence gene that confers on circulating breast cancer cells an enhanced ability to adhere, survive, and proliferate in the liver microenvironment [31,32]. Liu and his colleagues posited that elevated CLDN2 expression facilitates breast cancer liver metastasis by augmenting the interaction between liver cells and breast cancer cells with extracellular matrix components [48]. They further explained that breast cancer cells with elevated CLDN2 expression rely on the initial extracellular chain and CLDN2 expressed by hepatocytes to form a CLDN2-CLDN2 homologous complex, which mediates adhesion between hepatocytes and breast cancer cells. The adhesion between cancer cells and liver cells results in the destruction of the tight junctions between liver cells, which are transformed into gap junctions. This increases the permeability of the epithelium, thereby providing breast cancer cells with the conditions required for colonization, growth and proliferation in liver tissue. Our recent transcriptome sequencing results for hepatocellular carcinoma cells also showed that both short-term and long-term exposure to fresh or aged ZnO NPs induced the up-regulation of CLDN2 expression (Yu et al., unpublished results), so we here knocked down the CLDN2 gene and found that CLDN2 was indeed associated with the enhanced migration and invasion of MCF-7 cells treated with fresh or aged ZnO NPs (Fig. 12), and may also be associated with fresh or aged ZnO NP-induced specific hepatic metastasis of MCF-7 cells. Upregulation of CLDN2 may activate several downstream pathways and subsequently promote the colonization and proliferation of cancer cells in the liver. Evidence from cancer types other than breast cancer suggested that CLDN2 could suppress the expression of NDRG1 (N-myc downstream-regulated gene 1) by stabilizing the CLDN2/ZO1/ZONAB complex, thereby driving colorectal cancer progression [27]. Additionally, research by Wang et al. indicated that CLDN2 upregulation might promote colorectal cancer cell proliferation, epithelial-mesenchymal transition, and lymph node metastasis via MAPK signaling pathway [51]. Although the precise downstream signaling cascades of CLDN2 in breast cancer remain to be fully elucidated, our study suggested that CLDN2 could act as a key mediator linking chronic ZnO NPs exposure to enhanced liver metastasis. Collectively, these findings highlight the important role of CLDN2 as a prognostic biomarker, not only for predicting the likelihood of breast cancer recurrence but also as a functionally relevant biomarker for anticipating the development of NP-promoted liver metastasis in breast cancer.

While this study provides initial evidence that chronic in vitro exposure to ZnO NPs (1.5 μg/mL) enhances the metastatic potential of breast cancer cells, we acknowledge its limitations. The selected concentration, though justified as a sub-cytotic dose to model low-level chronic exposure, represents a single data point, lacking a dose-response relationship. Furthermore, the experimental tail-vein injection model, while valid for assessing extravasation and colonization, bypasses critical early metastatic steps like primary tumor invasion and intravasation, and does not account for the influence of a primary tumor microenvironment. Future work will essentialy incorporate a range of ZnO NPs concentrations to establish a dose-effect curve and employ orthotopic implantation models to study the full metastatic cascade within a physiologically relevant context. Elucidating the precise molecular mechanisms through transcriptomic/proteomic analyses and investigating the effects of direct in vivo ZnO NPs administration on spontaneous metastasis will be crucial next steps for a comprehensive risk assessment.

Conclusions

Since our previous findings have revealed that ZnO NPs underwent physicochemical transformations with aging and its genotoxicity and carcinogenicity change accordingly, here we further focused on the potential promotional effects of aged ZnO NPs on migration, invasion and metastasis of human breast cancer MCF-7 cells in vitro and in vivo. We observed that aged ZnO NPs significantly enhanced the proliferation, migration and invasion of MCF-7 cells in vitro. Surprisingly but interestingly, the cancer progression-enhancing effect was also reflected by the fact that MCF-7 cells underwent specific hepatic metastasis rather than pulmonary metastasis in vivo. Mechanistically, this process was accompanied by up-regulation of CLDN2. The further in vitro experiments showed that CLDN2 knockdown significantly decreased the proliferation and migration of MCF-7 cells. Our findings confirmed the environmental transformation of NPs with aging, and the transformed NPs could play much more advent roles in the promotion potential of NPs on cancer cell metastasis, which provides new insights into the health risk assessment on NPs. However, we have only made a preliminary attempt to unravel the underlying molecular determinants, and a great deal of further work is needed to analyse the real state, behaviour and toxicity of NPs in the environment, the carcinogenicity of pristine and transformed NPs, to elucidate the mechanism of NP-enhanced carcinogenicity.
