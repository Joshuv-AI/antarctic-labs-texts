Clonal diversity of Enterobacter cloacae complex of clinical and colonizing isolates harboring NDM-type carbapenemase genes in Cuba
Authors: Dianelys Quiñones Pérez, Meiji Soe Aung, Haiyang Yu, Yenisel Carmona, Nobumichi Kobayashi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Carbapenem-resistant Enterobacter cloacae complex (CR-ECC) in Cuba was first genetically characterized.•Among clinical isolates of ECC (2018-2023), 32% were CR and harbored blaNDM-1.•CR-ECC consisted predominantly of E. hormaechei among three Enterobacter species.•In addition to global lineages (e.g. clonal complex74), unique major sequence types (e.g. ST182) were detected.•In a carrier isolate, blaNDM-5 was identified, the first report in Cuba.

Introduction

Enterobacter cloacae complex (ECC) is among the commensal Gram-negative bacteria that inhabit the gastrointestinal tract and also colonizes the respiratory and urinary tracts [1]. Nevertheless, several ECC species are clinically relevant and cause a wide spectrum of health care-associated infections, including bloodstream and urinary tract infections, and pneumonia, which are associated with prolonged hospitalization [2]. In recent years, ECC has been recognized as an important multidrug-resistant subgroup of carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales (CRE) through the acquisition of carbapenemase genes such as blaNDM, blaKPC, and blaIMP [2]. ECC has been reported to be the third most common bacterial species of CRE, following Klebsiella pneumoniae and Escherichia coli, with a trend of global emergence and growth [2,3].

In Cuba, ECC was the second most frequently identified species among CRE (13%; 20/152) in a multicenter study from 2016 to 2021, showing an increasing trend after 2019 [4]. In a tertiary care hospital in Havana, carbapenem-resistant ECC (CR-ECC) accounted for 13% (16/124) of CRE clinical isolates, harboring only blaNDM [5]. In addition, 15.5% (46/297) of hospitalized patients (within 48 hours of admission) in eight clinical departments of a tertiary care hospital were fecal carriers of CRE, among which ECC was the third most common species and produced NDM-type carbapenemase [6]. The present study aimed to characterize the genotypes of ECC and carbapenemase genes from clinical isolates and carriers to understand the clonality of the ECC, which has shown an increasing trend in Cuba, and to provide basic information for infection control.

Methods

In this study, both clinical and colonizing ECC isolates were analyzed. The clinical isolates were collected from 37 hospitals in 12 provinces of Cuba as part of the national surveillance program conducted by the National Reference Laboratory for Health Care-Associated Infections over a 6-year period beginning January 2018. In contrast, the colonizing CR-ECC isolates were collected through an active surveillance study of hospitalized patients in a tertiary care hospital in Havana, Cuba, from 2021 to 2022 [5].

Clinical specimens were cultured on MacConkey agar, followed by incubation at 37°C for 18 to 24 hours. Bacterial species were identified using an automated analyzer (Vitek 2; bioMérieux, France). Partial 16S ribosomal ribonucleic acid and rpoB gene sequences were determined to confirm the Enterobacter species. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed using the Vitek 2 system with the VITEK® 2 AST-245 card. Susceptibility to amikacin and tigecycline was determined by Etest (bioMérieux), and to colistin was determined using the broth disk elution method. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing results were interpreted per Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute 2021 guidelines and European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing 2022 guidelines for tigecycline. E. coli American Type Culture Collection 25922 served as the quality control strain. Identification of β-lactamases (CTX-M, TEM, and SHV) and carbapenemase (IMP, NDM, VIM, OXA-48, and KPC) genes was performed by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using previously reported primers and conditions [7]. Full-length nucleotide sequences of blaTEM, blaCTX-M, and blaNDM were determined by direct sequencing from PCR products and their subtypes were identified using the Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST) search available at the National Center for Biotechnology Information website (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi). Sequence types (STs) were assigned using the web-based genotyping tool PubMLST (https://pubmlst.org/bigsdb?db=pubmlst_ecloacae_seqdef). A phylogenetic dendrogram based on concatenated ST sequences identified in this study was constructed using the maximum-likelihood method in the MEGA software package (https://www.megasoftware.net/).

Results

During the study period, a total of 176 clinical ECC isolates were collected. The main source of isolates was blood (42%), followed by respiratory specimens (13%) and urine (12.5%) (Table S1). The clinical ECC isolates showed high resistance rates to cephalosporins (55-84%), aztreonam (51%), quinolones (43-46%) (Table 1). Carbapenem resistance was detected in 56 isolates (32%). CR-ECC showed high resistance rates to ampicillin/clavulanic acid, aminoglycosides, and sulfamethoxazole/trimethoprim (89-100%), followed by piperacillin/tazobactam and ciprofloxacin (74%) (Table S2). For genetic characterization, 27 CR-ECC clinical isolates were selected from different years (2018-2023), and cities (Havana and four other cities), and patient characteristics (age, sex, etc.) and specimen types. Nine isolates from carriers and six carbapenem-susceptible isolates were also included in the genetic analysis (Figure 1, Table S2). CR-ECC was differentiated into 20 STs (a total of 25 STs, including the carbapenem-susceptible isolates), which were phylogenetically differentiated into three clusters (I-III). Eight STs (ST2852-ST2859) were novel and identified in this study. The clusters corresponded to the ECC species assigned by ST or rpoB sequence. Clusters I and II comprised E. hormaechei and contained most of the CR-ECC isolates. Cluster III comprised E. asburiae, E. kobei, and E. cloacae and contained only two CR isolates (E. asburiae and E. cloacae). The most common CR-ECC STs were ST171, ST182, ST231, and ST1833 (four or five isolates each), among which ST171 and ST182 were assigned to clonal complex (CC) 171. Furthermore, three STs (ST114, ST344, and ST1380) in cluster I and all five STs in cluster II were grouped into CC114 and CC74, respectively. Carbapenemase genes in CR-ECC belonged exclusively to the NDM type, whereas no genes encoding IMP, VIM, OXA-48, or KPC were detected. blaNDM-1 was identified in most clinical ECC isolates except for three isolates, whereas blaNDM-5 was harbored by only one colonizing isolate belonging to ST231. Most CR-ECC isolates (except those belonging to ST90 and ST2852 from carriers) harbored the extended-spectrum beta-lactamase genes blaCTX-M-3 and/or blaCTX-M-15. Discussion

In Cuba, ECC has been recognized as one of the main species of CRE, along with K. pneumoniae and E. coli, and has shown an increasing trend [4]. The present study revealed the clonal diversity of CR-ECC strains and the types of carbapenemase genes distributed within this species group. The exclusive detection of NDM-type genes (predominantly blaNDM-1) in ECC was consistent with previous findings for carbapenem-resistant E. coli, K. pneumoniae, and Acinetobacter calcoaceticus-baumannii complex from clinical specimens and carriers in Cuba, which also demonstrated the predominance of blaNDM-1 [[6], [7], [8]]. Despite the presence of other carbapenemase genes, such as those encoding KPC and VIM, NDM-1 is considered the overwhelmingly predominant carbapenemase among Enterobacterales and other Gram-negative bacteria in Cuba. Notably, blaNDM-5 was identified for the first time in Cuba in the present study. As with blaNDM-1, it was detected in a carrier (hospitalized patient). These findings suggest that carriers harboring ECC with NDM-type carbapenemase genes may serve as a reservoir for nosocomial infections, highlighting the need for enhanced CRE surveillance and control from the perspective of colonized patients.

Molecular epidemiological analysis in the present study revealed that CR-ECC comprised diverse ECC clones, most of which belonged to the species E. hormaechei, implying that blaNDM-1 may have disseminated among multiple ECC strains rather than within a single clone. Furthermore, several predominant STs/CCs, including ST171, CC74 (or CC78), and CC114, have previously been described as globally disseminated CR-ECC clones [2,[9], [10], [11]]. In our study, CC74 included three novel STs in addition to ST76 and ST78, suggesting diversification of this clonal complex, probably resulting from its spread within the population. The other common STs, ST182, ST231, and ST1833, have not been reported as globally widespread clones, suggesting that they may represent unique CR-ECC clones circulating in Cuba.

In summary, the current CR-ECC isolates in Cuba belonged predominantly to E. hormaechei and comprised diverse STs, including globally disseminated CCs as well as presumptive unique local clones, harboring blaNDM-1 as a predominant determinant of carbapenem resistance. Given the apparent dissemination of diverse CR-ECC clones, continued surveillance of CR-ECC and investigations of factors associated with infection and colonization are warranted to support infection control in Cuba.

The present study has several limitations. Because this was the first study to characterize the current molecular epidemiological status of CR-ECC based on STs in Cuba, whole-genome sequencing of the isolates, resistance determinants other than beta-lactamases (e.g., colistin resistance gene), and the association between ECC genotypes and clinical characteristics were not analyzed. In addition, the isolate selection process may have introduced selection bias, and the sample size was relatively small. Nevertheless, the initial findings of the present study would provide a foundation for further investigations to gain additional insights into ECC.
