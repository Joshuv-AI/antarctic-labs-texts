QTL mapping reveals a wild-derived segment controlling plant architecture in peanut (Arachis hypogaea L.) using a cultivar-wild hybrid population
Authors: Jiaowen Pan, Xun Zhang, Chun Fu, Fagang Yu, Guanghao Wang, Han Xia, Xiaofeng Song, Hui Yang, Qiuzhi Li, Xingjun Wang, Chuanzhi Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPlant architecture is a key agronomic trait of peanut (Arachis hypogaea L.), which is closely associated with yield, stress resistance, and suitability for mechanical harvesting. However, research on the genetics and gene mining of peanut plant architecture remains relatively limited, thereby hindering the genetic improvement of peanut plant architecture.ResultsMost cultivated peanut varieties exhibit an erect or semi-prostrate growth habit, whereas wild peanut species predominantly display a trailing growth habit. In the present study, a recombinant inbred line (RIL) population, designated as the TI population was developed by crossing the female parent Tifrunner with the male parent IpaDur, a synthetic amphidiploid derived from cross of Arachis ipaënsis × Arachis duranensis. Traits related to plant architecture, including lateral branch angle (LBA), lateral branch length (LBL), main stem height (MSH), main stem thickness (MST), lateral branch thickness (LBT), internode length (IL), number of branches (NBS), and biomass (BIO), were evaluated across three environments. Based on a high-density genetic linkage map, 20 QTLs associated with these traits were identified, which explained 6.04%-18.68% of the phenotypic variance (PVE). A locus controlling LBA, LBL, and MST was mapped to an overlapping interval (122.57-137.18 Mb) on chromosome 14. Phenotypic effect analysis revealed that this wild species-derived segment is crucial for controlling the typical morphogenesis of wild-type peanut species. In addition, we identified a set of genotypes derived from cultivated-wild hybrid population, which exhibited the convergence of one or more favorable agronomical traits.ConclusionsUsing a cultivar-wild hybrid population, 20 QTLs for plant architecture were identified in peanut. A wild-derived genomic segment was found to control typical wild-type morphogenesis. Novel germplasm pyramiding multiple agronomic favorable traits were selected. This study provides key theoretical insights and valuable resources for utilizing wild species in peanut improvement.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09283-2.Keywords: Peanut, Arachis ipaënsis, Arachis duranensis, Plant architecture, QTL mapping

Introduction

Cultivated peanut (Arachis hypogaea L.), also known as groundnut, stands as one of the world’s most important economic and oilseed legume, with a global cultivation area exceeding 31.16 million hectares annually in 2024, and in China, peanut planting has exceeded 4.87 million hectares (FAOSTAT, https://www.fao.org/faostat/en/#data/QCL). Peanut plays a pivotal role in global food security by providing high-quality edible oil and plant-based protein. Renowned for their exceptional nutritional value, peanut kernels typically contain approximately 50% oil, 25% protein, and 10% carbohydrates by weight. Beyond these macronutrients, peanuts serve as a rich source of essential micronutrients, including vitamins, minerals, and so on. In addition, peanut haulms serve as excellent green fodder for livestock [1]. Peanut production faces multiple constraints, including vulnerability to numerous foliar and soil-borne diseases and abiotic stresses. Increasing productivity and improving stress tolerance have emerged as the key objectives in modern peanut breeding programs [2]. However, genetic improvement was significantly constrained by cultivated peanut narrow genetic diversity.

Arachis hypogaea L., an allotetraploid species (2n = 4x = 40), originated from a natural hybridization event between two diploid progenitor species, A. duranensis and A. ipaënsis, followed by a spontaneous polyploidization. This recently evolved crop exhibits remarkably reduced genetic diversity, a consequence of both its recent evolutionary origin and the subsequent reproductive isolation from its wild diploid relatives [2]. The genus Arachis, comprising approximately 80 species with 31 of them classified in the section Arachis, has been extensively studied by researchers. Notably, the majority of Arachis species maintain a diploid genome constitution [2, 3]. Wild Arachis have played significant roles in human societies even prior to the domestication of cultivated peanut [2]. These wild species continue to serve multiple purposes today, including as forage crops, ornamental plants, and particularly as valuable genetic resources for peanut improvement programs. Comparative studies have revealed that wild Arachis accessions demonstrate exceptionally high levels of resistance to major peanut pathogens and insect pests when compared to cultivated A. hypogaea germplasm [4]. Furthermore, researchers have identified wild accessions possessing superior desirable agronomic traits (such as reduced days to flowering) and enhanced nutritional profiles, including higher levels of oil content, protein concentration, and sugar composition [2, 5]. These wild genetic resources hold great promise for the genetic enhancement of cultivated peanut varieties. Despite their potential value, the utilization of wild diploid species in the genetic improvement of cultivated allotetraploid peanut has been significantly constrained by several inherent challenges, including ploidy level differences, cross-compatibility barriers, undesirable agronomic traits in wild species, and longer maturity periods [2, 5]. Notwithstanding these limitations, several research groups have successfully demonstrated the feasibility of introgression of valuable resistance genes from wild diploid relatives into elite peanut cultivars. Notable examples include the transfer of resistance genes against leaf spot diseases and nematodes [3, 5, 6]. A particularly promising strategy involves the development of synthetic amphidiploids through interspecific hybridization followed by chromosomal doubling. These synthetic bridges effectively overcome ploidy barriers and have proven to be invaluable genetic resources for peanut breeding programs [5–8].

The introgression of wild-species-derived resistance into elite peanut germplasm enhances the durability of disease management in cultivated varieties. Tetraploid (2n = 4x = 40) peanut germplasm lines, GP-NC WS 16, GP-NC WS 17 and GPBD4, derived from an interspecific hybrid population derived from the diploid wild species A. cardenasii, exhibited superior resistance to multiple diseases. Using these germplasm, RIL populations were constructed, and many major QTLs for late leaf spot (LLS) and rust resistance were detected [6, 9, 10]. Wild Arachis species are primarily found in the Cerrado, a savannah-like biome, and have evolved specialized physiological and biochemical traits to endure water scarcity, thereby improving their survival in arid environments [11]. GP-NC WS17 has also demonstrated drought tolerance in both field and greenhouse studies [6]. Chromosome segment substitution lines (CSSLs) are powerful for QTL mapping and instrumental for elucidating the molecular basis of agronomically important traits in wild species. Using a CSSL population derived from the cross between ‘Fleur11’ and the synthetic allotetraploid (A. ipaënsis × A. duranensis), 24 QTLs associated with peanut morphology (including growth habit, main stem height, plant spread) were identified, which provide molecular foundation for deciphering genetic bases of agriculturally interesting traits in peanut [12, 13]. These studies on plant architecture using wild peanut germplasm were relatively preliminary.

A recombinant inbred line (RIL) population, the TI population, was generated by crossing the female parent ‘Tifrunner’ with the synthetic amphidiploid IpaDur and a high-density genetic bin map was subsequently established through whole-genome resequencing analysis. Using this population, twenty-eight QTLs for LLS disease resistance were identified [8]. In this study, using the TI population, QTLs associated with plant architecture were mapped based on phenotyping data from three environments. A total of 20 QTLs were mapped on the peanut 12 chromosomes. Our findings represent a significant breakthrough in overcoming ploidy barriers, thereby facilitating more efficient introgression of valuable wild-species traits into cultivated peanut varieties.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and phenotypic evaluation

The plant materials, A. ipaënsis, A. duranensis, synthetic amphidiploid (IpaDur), and Tifrunner were obtained from the Institute of Crop Germplasm Resources, Shandong Academy of Agricultural Sciences. Synthetic amphidiploid (IpaDur) with high level of resistance to late leaf spot was developed from cross of A. ipaëensis × A. duranensis. The TI population consisted of 410 lines was developed from a cross between ‘Tifrunner’ (female parent) and IpaDur (male parent), using single-seed-descent method [8]. The TI population and its parents were sowed in field trials in 2022 Weifang, 2023 Jiyang and 23 Weifang, ten seeds of each line were sown in a 2.0 m×0.9 m plot in a randomized complete block design with three replicates. Field management was performed according to the local cultivation practices of each location.

The plant architecture related traits, including lateral branch angle (LBA), lateral branch length (LBL), main stem height (MSH), main stem thickness (MST), lateral branch thickness (LBT), internode length (IL), number of branches (NBS), and biomass (BIO), were measured across three environments. Phenotypic data for each line were obtained by measuring nine individual plants and the mean value was used for subsequent analyses. LBA was measured as the angle between the main stem and the first lateral branch using an angulometer at 30 days after sowing. LBL, MSH, MST, LBT, IL, NBS, and BIO, were measured at mature period. MSH was measured as the length of the main stem from its base, where the primary lateral branches emerge, to the terminal meristem. LBL was defined as the length of one of the first pair of primary lateral branches, from its junction with the main stem to the terminal meristem. NBS was recorded as the total number of lateral branches, including both primary and secondary branches. IL refers to the distance between adjacent nodes on the first lateral branch. MST and LBT were measured using vernier caliper at the base of the main stem and the first lateral branch, respectively. For BIO, a nine-grade classification system was used to assess the aboveground biomass of RILs. Representative lines corresponding to each grade are presented in Figure S1.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses, including analysis of variance (ANOVA) and independent sample t-tests, were conducted using SPSS software (version 13.0). The normality of phenotypic distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Pearson’s correlation analysis was performed based on the mean value of each line across the three environments.

QTL analysis and gene identification

The previous constructed high‑density bin‑based genetic map for TI population was used for QTL mapping, and the genetic map contained 4,809 bin markers spanning a genetic distance of 2051.51 cM [8]. QTL mapping for the eight phenotypic traits was performed using IciMapping 4.2 software under the default settings of the BIP (bi-parental population QTL mapping) module. A scanning window of 1 cM was employed, and the LOD threshold was determined for each trait through 1,000 permutations at a significance level of P < 0.05. A LOD score of 2.5 was applied to identify putative QTLs associated with related trait [8]. The identified QTLs were designated with the prefix “q”, followed by the trait abbreviation (e.g., qLBA, qLBL) and the corresponding chromosome number. When multiple QTLs were detected on the same chromosome, a sequential number was appended. Sample collection, sequencing data acquisition, filtering, alignment, SNP/InDel identification, and genetic map construction were performed following the methods described in our previously published study [8]. The identified SNPs and InDels were annotated using SnpEff software (version 5.4a), based on the peanut genome annotation information available at https://www.peanutbase.org/genome/ (Arachis hypogaea cv. Tifrunner, Version 2, gnm2). Subsequently, SNPs and InDels located within the identified QTL regions that fell inside genes or in transcriptional regulatory regions were screened, followed by the selection of genes harboring non-synonymous mutations. Furthermore, based on the physical intervals of the QTL regions, genes located within these intervals were extracted using the peanut genome annotation GFF file. Gene annotations and functional descriptions for the QTL regions were retrieved from PeanutBase (https://www.peanutbase.org/search/gene). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis for each predicted gene model was conducted using the online platform available at https://geneontology.org/.

Results

Phenotypic variation of plant architecture in RILs and their parents

Tifrunner and IpaDur exhibited differences in plant architecture (Fig. 1). Eight plant architecture related traits, including LBA, LBL, MSH, MST, LBT, IL, NBS, and BIO, were investigated for the two parental lines and their recombinant inbred line (RIL) population across three environments (22weifang, 23jiyang and 23weifang). Extensive phenotypic variation and transgressive segregations were observed for all the examined traits in RILs across the three environments (Figs. 1 and 2; Table 1). Shapiro–Wilk (W) normality test demonstrated that all the measured traits conformed approximately to a normal distribution, with the exception of LBA. The coefficient of variation (CV) of these traits spanned a wide range, from 3.32% for LBA to 38.72% for LBL (Table 1; Fig. 2). The LBA of Tifrunner ranges between 77 and 83 °, while that of IpaDur is 90 °. The LBA of the RILs varied from 15 ° to 90 ° in 22weifang, from 50 ° to 90 ° in 23jiyang, and from 60 ° to 90 ° in 23weifang (Table 1). For LBL, the phenotypic values of Tifrunner ranged from 43.33 to 47.57 cm, whereas those of IpaDur varied from 108.33 to 112.78 cm. In the RIL population, the LBL exhibited broader variation, with ranges of 18.0–200.0 cm in 22weifang, 11.8–220.0 cm in 23jiyang, and 28.0–190.0 cm in 23weifang (Table 1). Across the three experimental environments, the phenotypic values of MSH for Tifrunner ranged from 31.32 to 38.20 cm, whereas those for IpaDur varied from 33.67 to 39.12 cm. In contrast, the RIL population displayed substantial phenotypic variation in MSH, with ranges of 12.0–95.0 cm in 22weifang, 12.0–80.0 cm in 23jiyang, and 13.0–140.0 cm in 23weifang (Table 1). The MST of Tifrunner ranged from 3.33 to 4.13 mm, whereas that of IpaDur varied from 3.23 to 4.90 mm in three environments. The MST variation in RILs varied from 6.4 to12.3 mm, 3 to 17.1 mm, and 3.4 to 13.8 mm in three environments, respectively (Table 1). The LBT of Tifrunner and IpaDur ranged from 3.33 to 4.36 mm and 2.67–4.43 mm, respectively. For the RIL population, the LBT exhibited notable variation across the three environments, with ranges of 3.5–9.9 mm in 22weifang, 2.6–10.1 mm in 23jiyang, and 2.5–9.5 mm in 23weifang, respectively. Across the three environments, the IL ranged from 2.50 to 2.63 cm for Tifrunner, while those for IpaDur varied from 3.17 to 3.63 cm. For the RIL population, IL exhibited considerable phenotypic variation across the three experimental environments, with ranges of 2.0–9.0 cm, 1.0–7.0 cm, and 2.0–7.5 cm (Table 1). The NBS of Tifrunner ranged between 6.4 and 7.7, while that of IpaDur ranged between 4.3 and 5.3. The NBS ranged among RILs from 2.0 to 12.0, 2.0 to 26.0, and 2.0 to 16.0 in three environments (Table 1). The BIO score of Tifrunner was 4-4.3, which is significantly higher than that of the IpaDur (2.3-3) across the three environments. The BIO varied among RILs from 1.0 to 8.0, 2.0 to 9.0, and 2.0 to 9.0 in three environments (Table 1). The broad-sense heritability ranged from 0.329 for NBS to 0.687 for LBL (Table 1), which indicated that these traits are predominantly under genetic control, a substantial proportion of the phenotypic variation is also attributable to environmental factors.

Identification and co-localization analysis of QTLs regulating plant architecture traits in peanut

Using the high-density genetic map constructed by our previous research [8], 20 QTLs associated with plant architecture were identified across three environments, distributed in 12 chromosomes, explaining 6.87%-18.68% of phenotypic variance (PVE) (Table 2). For LBA, two major-effect QTLs were mapped to chromosomes Chr14 and Chr18, with their PVE ranging from 14.39% to 18.68%. Notably, the QTL designated as qLBA18.1 was stably detected across two environments (Table 2; Fig. 4). A total of five QTLs associated with LBL were detected, distributed across five chromosomes and accounted for 7.32%–12.65% of the PVE (Table 2; Fig. 4). One QTL, qMSH19.1, was detected associated with MSH in 23weifang, with PVE of 7.89%. For MST, six QTLs were mapped onto six distinct chromosomes, with PVE ranging from 7.59% to 11.83%. Two LBT associated QTLs (qLBT1.1 and qLBT4.1) were identified, accounting for 6.87% and 7.33% PVEs, respectively. For IL, one QTL, qIL4.1, was detected on Chr 4, with the PVE of 8.28%. For Bio, one minor QTL (qBio8.1) was identified on Chr8 in 22weifang with the PVE of 9.92% (Table 2; Fig. 4). For NBS, two QTLs, qNBS13.1 and qNBS18.1, were identified on Chr13 and Chr18, explaining 7.39% and 8.67% PVE (Table 2; Fig. 4). Three QTLs, qLBL14.1, qMST14.1, and qMST14.1 regulating LBA, LBL and MST, respectively, were mapped to the same interval of 122.57 Mb to 137.18 Mb on Chr14, suggesting that this genomic region plays an important role in regulating plant architecture in peanut (Table 2; Fig. 4). Additionally, the QTLs qNBS18.1 and qLBA18.1 were co-localized with in the 122.57-137.18 Mb interval on chromosome 18, controlling LBL and NBS (Table 2; Fig. 4).

Candidate gene analysis in QTL regions

Candidate genes within the intervals of identified QTLs were predicted according to the annotation of cultivated peanut genome. Basing on whole-genome resequencing result, significant SNP/InDel variations were identified in multiple genomic regions, including the 3’ UTR, 5’ UTR, upstream and downstream of gene, coding regions, intron, or splicing region. For LBA, a total of 544 and 465 genes harboring significant sequence variations were identified within the genomic intervals of qLBA14.1 and LBA18.1, respectively. Genes encoding proteins involved in phytohormone biosynthesis, signal transduction, and cell wall modification, including protein kinases, phosphatases, and transcription factors such as auxin response factors and ethylene-responsive factors, were identified as candidate regulators of LBA morphogenesis (Table S1). The genomic intervals of the five QTLs associated with LBL contained 1385 putative candidate genes with sequence variations. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed that these genes were primarily involved in biological processes and molecular functions, including oxidation-reduction processes, metabolic processes, carbohydrate metabolic processes, as well as molecular functions such as DNA binding, protein kinase activity, structural molecule activity, protein binding, polygalacturonase activity, and acid phosphatase activity (Table S2). Functional descriptions suggested polygalacturonase, plastid-lipid associated protein (PAP), ectonucleotide pyrophosphatase, gibberellin 20 oxidase 2-like, katanin p60 ATPase-containing subunit A1, glucan endo-1,3-beta-glucosidase 8-like, and FAR1-related sequence are potential candidate genes for controlling LBL (Table S2).

For MSH, the qMSH19.1 region spanned a 61-kb genomic region and contained six putative genes (Table S3). Among them, the gene J2XU5N, which encodes a protein kinase that plays a pivotal role in regulating plant growth and environmental adaptation, could be a potential candidate gene for MSH. The stem, consisting of a vascular bundle system and a layer of mechanical tissue, plays a dominant role in determining the overall lodging resistance of the plant. Greater stem diameter and wall thickness are primary determinants of lodging resistance. Additionally, the rigidity of the basal culm is enhanced by its carbohydrate components, such as cellulose, soluble sugars, starch, and lignin [14]. Within the genomic intervals of six MST-associated QTLs (qMST6.1, qMST8.1, qMST9.1, qMST11.1, qMST12.1, and qMST14.1), a total of 9, 4, 2, 1, 21, and 544 putative candidate genes harboring sequence variations were detected, respectively (Table S4). For LBT, a total of 35 and 47 putative candidate genes harboring significant sequence variations were detected within the genomic intervals of qLBT1.1 and qLBT4.1, respectively (Table S5). GO analysis for these genes revealed that protein binding, catalytic activity, DNA binding, GTP binding, lipid transport, serine-type endopeptidase activity, and carbohydrate metabolic process were enriched (Table S5). Cell wall modification-related genes, such as non-specific lipid-transfer protein (5IM6J0), subtilisin-like serine protease (8V978B), lysosomal alpha-mannosidase-like (CX678H), rac-like GTP-binding protein 7-like (3ZR9GH), ellulase (VT1Y4S), and polygalacturonase (HI5XRQ) were identified in the candidate interval and is potentially involved in the regulation of both MST and LBT [14, 15].

Within the 348.7-kb genomic interval of qIL4.1, a total of 13 putative genes were detected (Table S6). Two candidate genes encoding Transducin/WD40 repeat-like superfamily protein and receptor-like protein kinase might be candidate genes since their homologous genes have been reported to be involved in the regulation of plant growth and development [16, 17]. Plant branching development is a critical component of plant morphogenesis and plays a pivotal role in shaping aboveground plant architecture. Branch number and branch angle are two key agronomic traits that jointly determine the overall plant structure. The number of effective branches directly affects leaf canopy distribution and pod formation, thereby exerting a decisive influence on the yield potential of crops [18]. Phytohormones, sugar, nitrogen, light, and water play a determining role in shoot branching regulation [19]. Two mapped QTL (qNBS13.1 and qNBS18.1) associated with branch number were mapped to genomic intervals overlapping those of lateral branch length. Within these co-localized candidate intervals, genes involved in phytohormone metabolism, signal transduction, and carbohydrate metabolism were detected (Table S7).

Within the genomic interval of qBio8.1, only four genes harboring sequence variations were detected, and a gene (DHT5D0) encoding nuclear factor Y subunit, which have been found involved in regulation of various plant developmental processes and stresses response, was proposed as candidate gene for BIO (Table S8).

The 14.61-Mb interval of Chr14 was identified as a locus controlling plant architecture of wild peanut

Interestingly, we found that four QTLs qLBA14.1, qLBL14.1, and qMST14.1, which regulate LBA, LBL, and MST, respectively, were all mapped to the same interval on chromosome 14 (122.57 Mb to 137.18 Mb). Previous studies revealed that nearly all bin markers within this region were inherited from the wild parent IpaDur in the 200 recombinant inbred lines (RILs) [8]. To evaluate the phenotypic effects of this wild peanut-derived genomic segment on LBA, LBL, and MST, haplotype analysis was performed using the flanking bin markers C14M65 and C14M68. The genotypes of the flanking bin markers C14M65 and C14M68 derived from Tifrunner were designated as ‘‘aa”, those from IpaDur were designated as ‘‘bb”, and the heterozygous genotypes were designated as “ab”. Across three experimental environments, the RILs carrying the ‘‘bb” genotype at both markers C14M65 and C14M68 exhibited larger lateral branch angles compared with those carrying the ‘‘aa” genotype, with the most pronounced difference observed in 22weifang (Fig. 5A, B). Across the three experimental environments, the mean lateral branch length values of RILs carrying the “aa” genotype at marker C14M65 were 45.9 cm, 48.8 cm, and 62.6 cm, respectively, while those carrying the “bb” genotype were 75.5 cm, 78.3 cm, and 81.2 cm (Fig. 5C). Across three environments, the mean MST was 4.0 mm, 8.0 mm, and 5.7 mm for the ‘‘aa” genotype, and 5.1 mm, 8.1 mm, and 7.7 mm for “bb” genotype of C14M65, respectively (Fig. 5E). The C14M68 also presents similar results (Fig. 5D and F). These findings demonstrate that this wild peanut-derived chromosomal segment confers a plant architecture characterized by larger lateral branch angles, longer lateral branches, and thicker main stems, which was the typical of wild peanut species trait.

Development of wild-species based elite peanut lines

The cultivated species A. hypogaea is most likely derived from a unique cross between the wild diploid species A. duranensis (A genome) and A. ipaënsis (B genome), followed by spontaneous chromosome duplication and successive selection, which ultimately produced a highly conserved genome. Consequently, the narrow genetic base of the primary gene pool has resulted in limited genetic diversity, remaining a major constraint for the genetic improvement of peanut [4]. The RIL population (TI population) used in this study was developed from a cross between peanut cultivar Tifrunner and IpaDur, and a set of lines containing genomic segments from the synthetic amphidiploid were obtained. Some of these germplasm resources derived from wild species exhibit the convergence of one or more agronomically favorable traits, including elevated oleic acid content, increased oil yield, enhanced disease resistance, semi-prostrate plant architecture and larger pod size. The line TI-358 is characterized by a high-oleic acid phenotype (oleic acid content > 80%), combined with resistance to late leaf spot (LLS) and a semi-prostrate growth habit. The TI-995 line exhibits a prostrate growth habit and high seed oil content (approximately 60%), but its lateral branches are relatively short. TI-673 is a semi-prostrate peanut line demonstrating moderate resistance to LLS disease and relatively large pod size. TI-385 is an erect-type peanut line (Fig. 6) with late leaf spot resistance. The lines TI-505 and TI-913 produce large pods with fabulous morphology. TI-505 exhibits an erect growth habit, whereas TI-913 is characterized by a semi-prostrate growth type (Fig. 6) with moderately resistance to peanut pod rot. The lines TI-249 and TI-91 exhibit moderate resistance to LLS with large pod size. Both TI-249 and TI-91 are characterized by a semi-prostrate growth habit and large pods (Fig. 6). These resources expand the genetic diversity of cultivated peanuts and constitute valuable germplasm for genetic improvement.

Discussion

Breaking ploidy barriers: QTL identification of plant architecture traits in peanut via a cultivar-synthetic amphidiploid hybrid population

Plant architecture refers to the three-dimensional organization of the aerial parts of a plant, encompassing traits such as plant height, branching pattern and number, and the formation of vegetative and reproductive organs. Plant type is a key determinant of crop yield, and an ideal plant architecture can effectively enhance productivity [20, 21]. Wild Arachis accessions exhibit significantly greater resistance to major pathogens and pests than cultivated peanut [2]. Additionally, they possess desirable agronomic and nutritional traits, including early flowering, enhanced nutritional profiles (higher oil and protein contents, and optimized sugar composition) [2]. Most cultivated peanuts display an erect or branching growth habit, whereas the majority of wild Arachis species exhibit a prostrate growth habit with creeping branches that can take root upon contact with the soil and a prominent main stem. Apparently, peanut plant type is a domesticated trait. Notably, some wild Arachis species exhibit extremely long lateral branches, substantial biomass accumulation, and a high number of pods per plant. It is important to note that the prostrate habit of wild Arachis is not directly targeted for peanut breeding. Instead, the underlying genetic components controlling branch angle and ground coverage, together with linked stress-adaptive alleles, represent valuable resources for reshaping plant architecture and broadening the genetic base of cultivated peanut. Therefore, wild Arachis germplasm resources hold considerable potential for the genetic improvement of cultivated peanuts, particularly in the modification of ideal plant architecture. However, their application in breeding programs faces multiple constraints, including ploidy differences, undesirable agronomic traits, and prolonged maturity periods in wild diploid species [2]. In this study, a hybrid population derived from cultivated peanut Tifrunner and a synthetic amphidiploid was utilized to map QTL associated with plant architecture related traits. In total, 20 QTLs were identified, and candidate genes in the intervals of identified QTLs were also analysed (Table 2). Using a previously reported cultivated-wild hybrid population, our study demonstrates an effective approach to overcome ploidy barriers, thereby potentially enhancing the introgression of desirable wild traits into cultivated peanut varieties.

In recent years, QTL mapping studies for peanut architecture related traits have been reported, along with the development of molecular markers. Using populations derived from crosses between spreading-type and erect-type cultivars, a major QTL controlling LBA was identified in the region of 156–159 Mb on Chr15 [22–24]. Another stable QTL, qGHA06, has also been identified in the region of 111.94-111.98 Mb on Chr 6 [22]. In this paper, two novel major QTLs were identified in Chr14 and Chr18 (qLBA14.1 and qLBA18.1). Genes encoding proteins involved in phytohormone biosynthesis, signaling, and cell wall modification were identified in the candidate interval (Table S1). Using two populations, a hot spot of QTLs for plant height were identified on Chr A09, and located at 10.23–13.63 Mb interval [25]. Using the population of Huayu36 × 6–13, three major QTLs, located on Chr7, Chr9 and Chr16, were repeatedly detected in more than one environment [26]. Li et al. detected 11 QTLs for MSH and distributed on seven chromosomes [27]. Among these QTLs, qMSHB09 was consistently detected in six environments, and localized in a region near 156.23 Mb on Chr19 [27]. In our results, only one QTL for MSH was mapped to 2.03–2.09 Mb physical region on Chr19, which have no overlap regions in the previous study (Table 2). A stable QTL for LBL was mapped to 110.49-110.54 Mb physical region on Aradu.A06 corresponding to 101.49-102.04 Mb physical region of Chr06 in Tifrunner genome, which was 4.26 Mb away from qLBL6.1 mapped in our result (Table 2) [28]. Using a RIL population developed from a cross between peanut cultivars Huayu 36 and 6–13, three major QTLs were mapped on chromosomes 7, 9, and 16 for primary lateral branch length, and nine consensus QTLs associated with branch number were identified on chromosomes 7, 9, 10, 12, 15, and 17 [26]. Li et al. detected seven QTLs for LBL distributed on chromosomes A08, B05 and B09 [27]. In this study, the identified MSH and LBL-related QTLs are novel. The primary reason is that the parental line of our population was derived from wild species, whereas those in published studies predominantly used cultivated peanut varieties; thus, these QTLs were not reported in previously published studies or overlapping with previously reported genomic regions.

Chr14 QTL hot spot for peanut plant architecture traits

Peanuts are unique plants that flower above ground but develop their pods underground, making plant architecture particularly crucial for this crop. Both branch number and angle are critical agronomic traits that shape peanut architecture, while effective branching and appropriate angle directly influences pod number and pegging, which ultimately determine peanut yield [18]. In this study, four important QTLs (qLBA14.1, qLBL14.1, and qMST14.1) associated with peanut architecture (LBA, LBL, and MST) were co-localized on Chr14 within the interval of 122.57-137.18 Mb. This finding indicates that this genomic region is a hot spot governing peanut plant architecture (Table 2). Apical dominance in plants is characterized by the inhibition of axillary bud outgrowth by the shoot apex. Removal of the apex releases bud dormancy, promoting branch formation. This process is mediated by auxin, synthesized in young apical leaves and transported basipetally to suppress bud activity along the stem. Elevating endogenous indole-3-acetic acid levels significantly reduces lateral branching [18, 29]. Dormancy is maintained by the bud’s inability to export its own auxin, while bud outgrowth requires establishment of an independent polar auxin stream [29, 30]. The establishment of non-vertical growth angles in lateral shoots is a complex process that integrates gravity perception, signal transduction, and auxin-mediated curvature responses. Within gravitropic cells, auxin transport, asymmetrical distribution, and auxin signaling play a central role in establishing branch angle [31, 32]. In rice, OsPIN1 and OsPIN2 have been reported to regulate tiller angle through modulating asymmetric auxin distribution [33]. In the interval of qLBA14.1, three genes (953E9B, BAIH62, LAPE95) encoding auxin efflux carrier family protein with sequence variations were identified (Table S1). These auxin efflux carrier proteins might affect auxin export from axillary buds and the asymmetric distribution of auxin at the base of lateral branches, ultimately influencing both branch number and branch angle formation. In addition, two genes, RE139W and TI401D, encoding “2-oxoglutarate (2OG) and Fe(II)-dependent oxygenase (2OG oxygenase)” and “F-box family protein”, respectively, were also identified in this interval. The homologs of these genes were reported to regulate plant architecture. miR394 targets the transcripts of LC4, a rice F-box protein encoding gene, which mediates auxin-regulated leaf inclination and plant architecture in rice [34]. Furthermore, 2OG oxygenases, a superfamily of enzymes involved in the metabolism of diverse phytochemicals including phytohormones, have been shown to regulate the columnar growth habit of apple [35, 36].

The plant height, internode length, stem diameter, and culm wall thickness are crucial determinants of plant architecture, and are closely related to lodging resistance of the overall plant [14]. The phytohormone ethylene regulates key developmental and growth processes in plants. Natural and engineered variations in the promoter and coding sequences of ethylene biosynthesis and signaling genes shape plant root and shoot architecture, with direct impacts on biomass production and grain yield [37]. ZmACS7, which encodes a 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylic acid (ACC) synthase, involved in the synthesis of ethylene, exhibits pleiotropic effects on maize architecture. Compared with their respective wild types, ZmACS7-overexpressing lines showed reduced plant height, increased leaf angles, and a greater number of brace root layers with reduced root angles [38]. Recently, GWAS analysis have shown that STRONG2 encodes the mannan synthase CSLA5, and MYB61-STRONG2 module enhances culm diameter and lodging resistance by coordinating mannose and cellulose accumulation to promote secondary cell wall formation in rice [39]. The receptor-like cytoplasmic kinase OsRLCK191 has been identified as a candidate gene influencing culm thickness and plays a key role in enhancing culm strength, thereby improving lodging resistance [40]. Cellulose, the primary component of the plant cell wall, contributes significantly to mechanical strength through its proportion and structural orderliness. Mutations in cellulose synthase 9 (CESA9) have been reported to impair cellulose synthase complex integrity, thereby reducing cellulose crystallinity (CrI) and enhancing lodging resistance in rice [41, 42]. The homologs of these genes related to plant architecture, such as ACC (UY7VYK), MYB transcription factor (2BK3KU), receptor-like protein kinase (DS8T8H, 122PQY, FJX8JV, S3PEXW), and Cellulase (VT1Y4S) with sequence variations, were also identified in the interval of qLBL14.1 (Table S2).

In summary, a wild-derived interval on chromosome 14 (122.57-137.18 Mb) serves as a master switch that simultaneously governs lateral branch angle (LBA), lateral branch length (LBL), and main stem thickness (MST). Hormonal signaling pathways, involving auxin, ethylene, and gibberellin, modulate branching and elongation, while cell wall remodeling acts as the core process determining stem and branch thickness. Additionally, carbohydrate metabolism and protein quality control provide essential energy and maintain the proper functioning of cell wall enzymes and signaling receptors.

Wild peanut-derived segment on chromosome 14: enrichment of genes related to plant architecture and late leaf spot resistance

In our previous study, using the same population, a major QTL qLLS14.1 conferring resistance to late leaf spot (LLS) disease was mapped to 122.57-137.18 Mb physical region on Chr14 [8]. This interval overlaps with those of qLBA14.1, qLBL14.1 and qMST14.1 identified in this study (Table 2). In this interval, a genomic segment with a low recombination rate was identified and nearly all bin markers in 0-2.58 cM genetic interval of LG14 were inherited from male parent IpaDur. A disease resistance gene enrichment zone, which contains 28 disease resistance proteins (TIR-NBS-LRR class), was found in this interval [8]. In this study, a series of genes controlling plant architecture were also identified in this region, and these genes are involved in mediating the typical morphogenesis of wild-type peanut species (Table S1 and Fig. 5). These results indicate this wild species-derived segment is crucial for regulating disease resistance and plant architecture in peanuts. Furthermore, this segment provides a genetic explanation for the robust disease resistance and the prevalent creeping phenotype observed in most wild peanut relatives.

Conclusion

Using a population derived from a cross between ‘Tifrunner’ and the synthetic amphidiploid (IpaDur), 20 QTLs associated with plant architecture traits in peanut were identified explaining 6.04%-18.68% of the PVE. There different locus controlling LBA, LBL, and MST was mapped to an overlapping interval (122.57-137.18 Mb) on chromosome 14, which also contained favorable alleles contributing to late leaf spot resistance. Phenotypic effect analysis revealed that this wild species-derived segment is crucial for controlling the typical morphogenesis of wild-type peanut species. A set of lines derived from cultivated-wild hybrid population, which exhibited the convergence of one or more agronomically favorable traits, were identified. This study lays a theoretical foundation and offers valuable germplasm resources for utilizing wild species in peanut breeding improvement programs.
