Cepharanthine alleviated chronic prostatitis/chronic pelvic pain syndrome by activating Nrf2 and inhibiting the NF-κB pathway
Authors: Hang Zhang, Dahong Zhang, Ziyi Wang, Kailong Liu, Chao Xu, Hao Sun, Zhiliang Yan, Chongbin Li, Baosai Lu, Yuewei Yin, Chenming Zhao, Yalin Niu, Wei Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChronic prostatitis/chronic pelvic pain syndrome (CP/CPPS) is a common disorder impacting the genitourinary system of adult males, primarily driven by pathogenic mechanisms involving immune and inflammatory mediators, immune cells and molecules, oxidative stress, and apoptosis. Cepharanthine (CEP), a bisbenzylisoquinoline alkaloid, exhibits properties such as immunomodulatory, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antitumor activities. Nevertheless, the effects of CEP on CP/CPPS and its underlying mechanisms remain insufficiently characterized.MethodsTwo experimental models were developed, one utilizing experimental autoimmune prostatitis (EAP) mouse models and the other employing LPS-induced RWPE-1 cells. The pivotal role of Nrf2 was investigated using Nrf2 knockout mice and the Nrf2 inhibitor ML385.ResultsTreatment with CEP reduced prostate inflammation scores, alleviated tactile sensitization of the pelvic region in EAP mice, improved pathological damage in prostate tissues, and inhibited the upregulation of systemic and localized pain and pro-inflammatory mediators in the prostate. Furthermore, CEP treatment was shown to inhibit oxidative stress, ameliorate mitochondrial dysfunction, and suppress apoptosis. In vitro studies indicate that CEP treatment stabilizes Nrf2 protein levels by binding to the Nrf2 protein and inhibiting its ubiquitination-mediated degradation. Consequently, CEP treatment activates the Nrf2 signaling pathway, facilitates its nuclear translocation, and upregulates the expression of downstream target proteins, such as HO-1 and NQO1. Concurrently, CEP suppresses the activation of the NF-κB pathway. Notably, the blockade of Nrf2 signaling negates the protective effects mediated by CEP.ConclusionsCEP inhibits the ubiquitination-mediated degradation of Nrf2 and exhibits potential to activate the Nrf2 pathway while concurrently inhibiting the NF-κB pathway, thereby alleviating CP/CPPS.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13062-026-00744-0.

Introduction

Chronic prostatitis represents a prevalent condition within the male genitourinary system and constitutes the primary reason for males under the age of 50 to seek consultation at urology clinics. Among the various classifications, chronic prostatitis/chronic pelvic pain syndrome (CP/CPPS) is the most frequently diagnosed [1]. CP/CPPS is predominantly characterized by persistent pelvic pain, lower urinary tract symptoms, sexual dysfunction, and neuropsychiatric manifestations, all of which substantially impair the quality of life of affected individuals, with pain symptoms exerting a particularly profound impact [2]. Present therapeutic approaches predominantly focus on symptomatic relief through the use of antibiotics, α-receptor blockers, and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs. However, these treatments often fail to meet the expectations of both patients and healthcare providers [3]. Consequently, there is a pressing need for the development of novel pharmacological agents and a comprehensive understanding of their therapeutic mechanisms.

The pathogenesis of CP/CPPS is characterized by a multifaceted interplay of mechanisms, including immune and inflammatory mediators, immune cells and molecules, oxidative stress, apoptosis, dysfunction of the pelvic floor muscles, and alterations in intestinal microbiota. Emerging evidence indicates that autoimmune inflammation and oxidative stress are critical contributors to the progression and pathogenesis of CP/CPPS [4–6]. Dysregulation of autoimmune function results in the aberrant infiltration of immune cells, such as CD4+ T cells and macrophages, into prostatic tissue. These cells release a variety of inflammatory and nociceptive mediators, including TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-17 A, and chemokine (C-C motif) ligand 2 (CCL2), thereby establishing a complex inflammatory network. The disruption of the equilibrium between intracellular oxidative and antioxidant systems leads to oxidative stress and the excessive accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [7]. This oxidative stress, induced by the overaccumulation of ROS, not only directly inflicts cellular damage but also precipitates mitochondrial dysfunction, disrupts cellular energy metabolism, and perpetuates a deleterious cycle with inflammatory responses. This cycle ultimately triggers cellular apoptosis or necrosis, thereby exacerbating tissue damage. Consequently, investigating therapeutic strategies that concurrently target critical pathways involved in inflammation and oxidative stress is of substantial importance for the management of CP/CPPS. This approach aims to mitigate the excessive activation of inflammatory responses at their origin.

Nuclear factor E2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) is a pivotal transcription factor that regulates oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction [8]. In response to stimuli such as oxidative stress, Nrf2 becomes activated and translocates to the nucleus, where it initiates the expression of various downstream antioxidant proteins, including Heme Oxygenase-1 (HO-1) and NAD(P)H: Quinone Oxidoreductase 1 (NQO1), to restore intracellular redox balance. Additionally, Nrf2 plays a crucial role in regulating mitochondrial dysfunction. An increasing body of research indicates that activation of the Nrf2 pathway to alleviate oxidative stress can ameliorate prostate diseases and exert analgesic effects [9–11]. The nuclear factor κB (NF-κB) signaling pathway is integral to numerous physiological and pathological processes, including immune and inflammatory responses, cell proliferation, apoptosis, and other biological functions [12]. It is particularly crucial in orchestrating immune and inflammatory responses [13]. Importantly, the activation of Nrf2 can interact with and inhibit the NF-κB pathway, a key pro-inflammatory signaling cascade, thereby mitigating excessive inflammatory responses at an upstream level. This distinctive Nrf2/NF-κB axis presents a promising therapeutic target for managing CP/CPPS.

Cepharanthine (CEP), a bisbenzylisoquinoline alkaloid derived from the plant Stephania japonica, demonstrates a range of pharmacological activities, including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and immunomodulatory effects, as well as anti-tumor properties and the prevention of thrombocytopenia [14]. Furthermore, CEP can alleviate inflammation and pain associated with snake bites [15]. In the context of gastric cancer, CEP exerts its effects by modulating Nrf2 to induce oxidative stress and alter energy metabolism [16]. The potential of CEP to ameliorate the core pathological features of CP/CPPS, its dependence on the modulation of the Nrf2/NF-κB signaling pathway, and specifically its ability to influence Nrf2 protein stability, remain uncertain. In this study, we employed a well-established CP/CPPS research model to systematically assess the impact of CEP on prostatic inflammation, pain, oxidative stress, mitochondrial function, and apoptosis. Additionally, we conducted an in-depth investigation into the molecular mechanisms underpinning its action, focusing on the novel pathway involving “inhibition of Nrf2 ubiquitin degradation - activation of Nrf2 - suppression of NF-κB.” This research seeks to provide a robust theoretical foundation and empirical support for the potential development of CEP as a therapeutic agent for CP/CPPS.

Materials and methods

Laboratory animals

In this study, male wild-type (WT) and Nrf2−/− C57BL/6J mice, aged 6–8 weeks, along with male SD rats, were utilized. The WT mice were sourced from Beijing Spibio Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The Nrf2−/− mice, which are global knockouts, were kindly provided by Professor Guangxin Chen from the School of Life Sciences at Shanxi University in Taiyuan, China. All animal protocols and procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of The Second Hospital of Hebei Medical University (Approval No. 2024-AE244). The animal experiments were conducted in compliance with the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Establishment of EAP model and drug treatment

The EAP mouse model was developed in accordance with the methodology outlined in prior research [17]. Prostate homogenates were derived from SD rats, and the supernatants were isolated to serve as prostate antigens (PAgs). These PAgs or normal saline were combined with an equal volume of complete Freund’s adjuvant (CFA, MCE) and thoroughly homogenized through repeated pipetting. Mice received intradermal injections of CFA-emulsified PAgs (300 µg per mouse) at specific anatomical sites: the shoulder (50 µL), the base of the tail (50 µL), the right callosity (25 µL), and the left callosity (25 µL) on days 0 and 28, respectively. Control mice were administered an equivalent volume of CFA-emulsified normal saline and were euthanized on day 42. WT and Nrf2−/− mice were randomly allocated into six groups, each comprising six mice: the Control group, the EAP group, and the EAP + CEP group. Based on previous studies [18], mice in the EAP + CEP group received intraperitoneal injections of CEP at a dosage of 10 mg/kg, commencing two days prior to the second immunization. The control group was administered the vehicle instead.

Behavioral tests

On day 42, prior to euthanasia, mechanical allodynia in the lower abdominal region adjacent to the prostate of mice was evaluated using Von Frey filaments with forces of 0.04, 0.16, 0.4, 1.0, and 4.0 g, across a total of 10 trials. A positive nociceptive response was characterized by the presence of any of the following behaviors: (a) jumping; (b) immediate licking or scratching of the stimulated area; (c) rapid abdominal contraction. The response rate was calculated as the number of positive responses divided by 10.

HE staining

Prostates excised from mice were fixed, dehydrated, and embedded at room temperature. Paraffin-embedded tissues were sectioned into 4 μm slices and baked at 65 °C for 2 h. Sections were then treated with xylene for 10 min, followed by alcohol solutions (100%, 95%, 75%) for 5 min each, and rinsed with distilled water. Subsequently, the inflammatory status of prostate tissues was observed via HE staining. The degree of prostatic inflammation was identified as previously described, and the criterion [19] for prostatic inflammation identification in EAP.

mice was listed in Supplementary Table S1.

ELISA

Collected blood samples were centrifuged at 2000 g/min for 10 min to remove blood cells. The levels of cytokines in mouse serum, including TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-17 A, IL-6, GM-CSF, IFN-γ, and CCL2, were detected using ELISA kits (Feiyue, China).

Immunohistochemical(IHC) staining

The prostate samples sliced were prepared as described in HE staining. After dewaxing, hydration, and antigen retrieval, the sections were treated with 3% H₂O₂ for 25 min at room temperature, washed three times with PBS, and blocked with goat serum. Subsequently, the sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: CD4 (1:500, GB15064, Servicebio, China), CD68 (1:400, GB113109, Servicebio, China), Nrf2 (1:200, 16396-1-AP, Proteintech, China), and P65 (1:200, 10745-1-AP, Proteintech, China). After another PBS washing, the sections were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody for 2 h at room temperature. Finally, the DAB chromogenic reaction was performed, followed by hematoxylin counterstaining, and images were captured under a light microscope.

Immunofluorescence(IF) staining

Slides were prepared as described in immunohistochemistry. After fixation and permeabilization of the samples, slides were incubated with the primary antibodies against CCL2 (1:50, ab214819, Abcam, UK) overnight at 4 °C. Subsequently, secondary antibodies were incubated for 2 h. After staining with DAPI, the immunofluorescent signals were captured using a confocal laser scanning microscope (ZEISS, Germany).

After fixing cell climbing slices with paraformaldehyde, the cells were permeabilized with Triton X-100. After blocking RWPE-1 cells for 1 h, they were incubated overnight at 4 °C with antibodies against Nrf2 (1:200, 16396-1-AP, Proteintech, China) and P65 (1:200, 10745-1-AP, Proteintech, China). The following day, the samples were incubated with secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature, followed by nuclear staining with DAPI and mounting. Observations were made under a fluorescence microscope.

Electron microscopy

Prostate tissues (1 × 1 × 1 mm) were fixed in 3% glutaraldehyde, after washing with PBS, tissues were fixed with 1% osmium tetroxide. Afterward, tissues were cut into 50–60 nm slices for dehydration and insertion. Finally, samples were observed and scanned by using a transmission electron microscope (Hitachi, Japan).

TUNEL staining

The apoptosis of cells/prostate tissues was determined using a TUNEL apoptosis detection kit (Beyotime, China). Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min. For prostate tissue sections, proteinase K dilution (20 µg/ml) was added and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. After washing three times with PBS, TUNEL detection solution was added and incubated at 37 °C for 60 min in the dark, followed by three additional PBS washes. Cell nuclei were stained with DAPI for 10 min. Observations were performed under a fluorescence microscope.

Cell culture and treatment

The human prostate epithelial cell line RWPE-1 (Chinese Academy of Sciences, China) was cultured in K-SFM medium (10744019, Gibco) supplemented with keratinocyte growth additive and 1% penicillin/streptomycin solution at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ atmosphere. Cells were stimulated with 2.5 µg/ml LPS (L2880, Sigma) to establish an in vitro prostatitis model [20]. Two hours prior to LPS treatment, cells were pretreated with 4 µM CEP and 5 µM ML385, respectively. LPS-pretreated RWPE-1 cells were stimulated with 10 µM MG132 and 50 nm BafA1 for 8 h, and with 100 µg/ml cycloheximide (CHX) for 6 h, 12 h, and 24 h.

Cell viability

Cell viability was determined using the CCK-8 kit (SEVEN, China). Cells were seeded into 96-well plates at the indicated density. At specified time points, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well containing 100 µL medium and incubated for 1–2 h at 37 °C. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured with a microplate reader. Each condition was tested in triplicate and repeated independently three times.

Intracellular ROS level detection

Intracellular ROS were measured using a ROS Assay Kit (Solarbio, China). DCFH-DA was diluted to a final concentration of 10 µmol/L in serum-free medium at a ratio of 1:1000. After removing the cell culture medium, the cells were washed three times with serum-free medium and incubated with the diluted DCFH-DA at 37 °C for 30 min. Intracellular ROS was immediately detected using a fluorescence microscope.

Mitochondrial membrane potential measurement

Changes in mitochondrial membrane potential were evaluated using a Mitochondrial Membrane Potential Assay Kit (JC-1) (Solarbio, China). Cells were incubated with JC-1 working solution at 37 °C for 20 min, followed by washing 3 times with PBS and imaging under a fluorescence microscope.

Mitochondrial ROS detection

Mitotracker Green (Beyotime, China) can specifically label functionally active mitochondria within cells, and we utilized it to localize mitochondria in RWPE-1 cells. Mitochondrial ROS levels were analyzed using the Mitochondrial Superoxide Detection Kit (Beyotime, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. After removing the culture medium from the 6-well plates and washing the cells with PBS, Mitotracker Green solution and MitoSOX Red solution were added to each well. Following a 30 min incubation at 37 ℃ in the incubator, cells were washed with PBS. Images were then acquired using fluorescence microscopy.

Determination of antioxidant-related enzyme activities

Mouse serum and cell supernatant samples were collected. The activities of superoxide dismutase (SOD), malondialdehyde (MDA), and glutathione (GSH) were measured using detection kits (Solarbio, China), respectively. Enzyme activity assays were performed following the protocols provided by the manufacturer. Absorbance measurements were recorded using a microplate reader, and the resulting data were systematically analyzed to determine enzyme activity levels.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

Co-IP assays were conducted using the Pierce Classic Magnetic IP/Co-IP Kit (Thermo Scientific, USA). Cells were lysed in IP lysis buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors. Lysates were incubated with specific antibodies or control IgG for 1 h at room temperature, followed by incubation with pre-washed Protein A/G magnetic beads for an additional hour. After thorough washing, immunoprecipitated proteins were eluted and analyzed byWestern blotting. Input controls were loaded to verify protein expression.

Western blot

After treatment, cells were washed three times with 1× PBS and then lysed in whole protein lysis buffer. After 15 min, the lysates on the cell plates were repeatedly scraped using a cell scraper to collect proteins. Protein concentrations were quantified, and equal amounts were separated by SDS-PAGE before transferring to PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA). After blocking with 5% skim milk for 2 h, membranes were incubated with primary antibodies, COX-2 (1:2000, 12375-1-AP, Proteintech), iNOS (1:1000, 22226-1-AP, Proteintech), IL-1β (1:1000, 16806-1-AP, Proteintech), IL-6 (1:1000, 21865-1-AP, Proteintech), CCL2 (1:1000, ab214819, Abcam), Nrf2 (1:10000, 16396-1-AP, Proteintech), HO-1 (1:3000, 10701-1-AP, Proteintech), NQO1 (1:4000, 11451-1-AP, Proteintech), P65 (1:1000, 10745-1-AP, Proteintech), P-P65 (1:5000, 82335-1-RR, Proteintech), IκBα (1:10000, 10268-1-AP, Proteintech), P-IκBα (1:2000, 82349-1-RR, Proteintech), Bax (1:10000, 50599-1-Ig, Proteintech), Bcl-2 (1:1000, 12789-1-AP, Proteintech), caspase-3 (1:500, 19677-1-AP, Proteintech), Lamin B (1:2000, 129871-AP, Proteintech), β-Tubulin (1:5000, 10094-1-AP, Proteintech), and GAPDH (1:50000, 60004-1-Ig, Proteintech), protein bands were visualized using chemiluminescent HRP substrate (Millipore, USA) and detected with the Fusion FX imaging system (Vilber Lourmat, France). Finally, the ECL chemiluminescent reagent was used for development, and band densities were quantified using Image J software.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted using an RNA extraction kit (SEVEN, China), and RNA concentration was measured using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher, USA). First-strand cDNA was synthesized using the M-MLV First Strand Kit (Thermo Fisher, USA). qRT-PCR was performed on an ABI 7500 FAST system (Applied Biosystems, USA) using Platinum SYBR Green qPCR SuperMix-UDG (Thermo Fisher, USA). Relative expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. All primers were listed in Supplementary Table S2.

ATP assay

ATP levels were measured using an ATP assay kit (Beyotime, China). ATP standard solutions were prepared according to the manufacturer’s instructions and mixed with mouse serum. Firefly luciferase catalyzes luciferin using energy provided by ATP to generate fluorescence. Cellular ATP levels were calculated by establishing a standard curve, and the results were expressed as fold changes relative to control cells. The experiment was independently repeated three times to ensure biological reproducibility.

Molecular docking

The structures of Nrf2 and its heterodimeric complex were downloaded from the PDB database (http://www.rcsb.org/). The 3D structure of CEP was downloaded from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). Molecular docking was performed using Sybyl X2.0 software, and the docking results were processed with PyMOL software.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as the mean ± SD deviation from at least three independent experiments (n ≥ 3). All collected data were analyzed using GraphPad software (Version 8.3) and R (Version 4.3). Differences between groups were assessed by two-tailed Student’s t-test or one-way analysis of variance (one-way ANOVA). A P-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

CEP alleviates prostate inflammation and pain in EAP mice

The administration protocol and experimental procedure specific to CEP are depicted in Fig. 1A. HE staining demonstrated that, in comparison to the control group, the prostate tissues of EAP mice exhibited pronounced perivascular tortuosity and extensive infiltration of inflammatory cells. Following CEP treatment, the aforementioned inflammatory tissue injury was markedly alleviated (Fig. 1B), and the inflammation score was significantly reduced (Fig. 1C). The frequency of response to Von Frey filament stimulation in the pelvic region was significantly elevated in the EAP group compared to the control group; however, CEP treatment substantially ameliorated this abnormal response (Fig. 1E). ELISA results indicated that the levels of inflammatory factors, including TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-17 A, IL-6, GM-CSF, IFN-γ, and CCL2, were significantly elevated in the serum of EAP group mice, whereas CEP treatment significantly decreased the expression of these factors (Fig. 1F). Additionally, CD4 and CD68 were selected as markers for CD4+ T cells and macrophages, respectively, for further detection. IHC analysis revealed a pronounced infiltration of CD4+ T cells and CD68+ macrophages within the prostates of mice in the EAP group. Conversely, treatment with CEP significantly diminished the infiltration of these immune cell types (Fig. 1G). Complementary IF assays further corroborated that CEP treatment markedly reduced the expression of CCL2 in the prostate tissues of EAP-afflicted mice (Fig. 1H-I). To assess the specificity of the EAP model and the safety profile of CEP administration, we analyzed changes in body weight and examined the pathological characteristics of vital organs, including the heart, liver, lungs, and kidneys, across the different experimental groups. The findings indicated no significant differences in body weight among the groups, and no discernible pathological damage was detected in the major organs (Fig. 1D, Fig. S1). These results suggest that the EAP model developed in this study exhibits high specificity, and the administered dose of CEP does not induce significant toxicity. In summary, CEP effectively mitigates the pathological progression of EAP by downregulating the expression of inflammatory and pain mediators in both systemic and local prostate tissues and by reducing immune cell infiltration.

CEP regulates redox homeostasis and maintains mitochondrial function in EAP mice

To further investigate the potential mechanisms by which CEP may treat CP/CPPS, we utilized network pharmacology to predict its therapeutic targets. GO functional enrichment analysis indicated that CEP might exert its therapeutic effects primarily through processes such as “regulation of reactive oxygen species metabolic process,” “intracellular calcium ion homeostasis,” and “mitochondrial apoptosis-related changes,” suggesting that oxidative stress and mitochondrial function are critical functional links (Fig. 2A). Additionally, transcriptome sequencing analysis of gene expression differences in prostate tissues was conducted, with volcano plots illustrating the differentially expressed genes between the control group vs. the EAP group and the EAP group vs. the EAP + CEP group (Fig. 2B).

To validate the proposed mechanism, we assessed oxidative stress markers in mouse serum. The findings indicated a reduction in SOD and GSH levels, alongside an elevation in MDA levels within the EAP group. Notably, treatment with CEP significantly counteracted these alterations (Fig. 2C). Transmission electron microscopy further revealed that prostate epithelial cells from the EAP group exhibited ultrastructural damage, including mitochondrial swelling and chromatin condensation, which were markedly ameliorated by CEP treatment(Fig. 2D). Given that mitochondrial dysfunction is frequently associated with compromised ATP synthesis, our study consistently observed a reduction in ATP levels in the prostate tissues of the EAP group, while CEP treatment effectively restored these levels (Fig. 2E). Mitochondrial dysfunction is also known to precipitate cellular apoptosis. TUNEL staining results demonstrated an increase in apoptotic cells in the EAP group, whereas CEP treatment significantly inhibited apoptosis (Fig. 2F, G). In summary, we propose that CEP may exert therapeutic effects on CP/CPPS by mitigating oxidative stress, enhancing mitochondrial function, and consequently inhibiting cellular apoptosis.

CEP ameliorates inflammatory response, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial dysfunction in LPS-induced RWPE-1 cells

To elucidate the mechanism of action of CEP, this study employed an LPS-induced RWPE-1 cell model to assess its impact on cellular inflammatory response, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial function. Initially, the CCK-8 cell viability assay identified 4 µM as the optimal concentration of CEP for subsequent experiments (Fig. 3B). In terms of inflammatory factor expression, qRT-PCR analysis revealed that CEP pretreatment significantly inhibited the LPS-induced upregulation of TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-17 A, IFN-γ, GM-CSF, IL-6, and CCL2 mRNA levels (Fig. 3C). Western blot analysis further corroborated that CEP effectively downregulated the protein expression levels of COX-2, iNOS, IL-6, IL-1β, and CCL2 (Fig. 3D, E). To examine intracellular levels of SOD, GSH, and MDA, it was observed that LPS treatment resulted in decreased activities of SOD and GSH, along with increased MDA content in RWPE-1 cells, consistent with previous animal experiment findings; however, CEP intervention significantly reversed these alterations (Fig. 3F). Detection using the DCFH-DA probe indicated that CEP effectively inhibited the LPS-induced increase in intracellular ROS levels (Fig. 3G). Further analysis of mitochondria-specific oxidative stress using the MitoSOX probe demonstrated that CEP significantly mitigated the excessive production of mitochondrial ROS induced by LPS (Fig. 3H). Subsequently, we assessed changes in mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) using the JC-1 probe. Under normal conditions, JC-1 forms aggregates within mitochondria, emitting red fluorescence; however, when ΔΨm decreases, JC-1 exists as monomers and emits green fluorescence. Experimental results showed that LPS treatment resulted in a significant reduction in the JC-1 red/green fluorescence ratio in RWPE-1 cells, whereas CEP treatment effectively restored this ratio (Fig. 3I), suggesting that CEP stabilizes mitochondrial membrane potential. TUNEL staining revealed that CEP significantly reduced LPS-induced apoptosis in RWPE-1 cells (Fig. 3J). Western blot analysis demonstrated that LPS treatment facilitated the upregulation of pro-apoptotic proteins BAX and Caspase-3, while concurrently downregulating the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2. Notably, CEP intervention was capable of reversing the aberrant expression of these apoptosis-related proteins (Fig. 3K, L). These findings suggest that LPS-induced sustained oxidative stress, particularly characterized by a mitochondrial ROS burst, leads to a reduction in mitochondrial membrane potential, thereby activating the mitochondrial-dependent apoptotic pathway. CEP mitigates LPS-induced injury in RWPE-1 cells by inhibiting ROS production, preserving mitochondrial function, and modulating the expression of apoptosis-related proteins.

CEP activates the Nrf2 pathway and inhibits the NF-κB pathway both in vivo and in vitro

To elucidate the molecular mechanism underlying CEP’s therapeutic efficacy in CP/CPPS, this study concentrated on the Nrf2/NF-κB pathway. Nrf2 serves as a pivotal transcription factor in regulating antioxidant stress, and its activity is frequently linked with IκB/NF-κB signaling, which collectively contributes to inflammatory processes and the progression of related diseases. In animal experiments, immunohistochemical analysis revealed that, relative to the control group, the expression of Nrf2 was downregulated, whereas the expression of P65 was upregulated in the prostate tissues of mice within the EAP model group. Following CEP treatment, there was a marked increase in Nrf2 expression and a corresponding decrease in P65 expression (Fig. 4A). In cellular experiments, Western blot analysis indicated that LPS stimulation resulted in a reduction of both nuclear and total Nrf2 protein levels in RWPE-1 cells, thereby suppressing the expression of its downstream target proteins, HO-1 and NQO1. The administration of CEP effectively counteracted this effect, suggesting its potential role in mitigating oxidative stress (Fig. 4B, C). Immunofluorescence staining further demonstrated that the LPS-treated group exhibited diminished Nrf2 fluorescence intensity with reduced nuclear localization. In contrast, CEP treatment not only enhanced the overall fluorescence intensity of Nrf2 but also facilitated its translocation from the cytoplasm to the nucleus (Fig. 4F, H). Additionally, LPS stimulation significantly elevated the phosphorylation levels of IκBα and P65 and promoted the nuclear translocation of P65 (Fig. 4D, E). Immunofluorescence analyses consistently revealed a substantial presence of P65-positive cells exhibiting pronounced nuclear translocation within the LPS-treated group. In contrast, intervention with CEP significantly inhibited P65 nuclear translocation (Fig. 4G, H). Collectively, both in vivo and in vitro experiments consistently indicated that CEP mediates its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects through activation of the Nrf2 pathway and inhibition of the NF-κB pathway.

CEP alleviates the inflammatory response in LPS-induced RWPE-1 cells by activating the Nrf2 signaling pathway

To elucidate the pivotal role of Nrf2 in the cytoprotective effects conferred by CEP, we conducted reverse validation using the specific Nrf2 inhibitor, ML385. Western blot analyses demonstrated that ML385 treatment not only suppressed the CEP-induced enhancement of Nrf2 nuclear translocation (Fig. 5A, B) but also negated the inhibitory impact of CEP on P65 nuclear translocation (Fig. 5C, D). IF results further substantiated that, in the presence of ML385, CEP was unable to effectively modulate the subcellular localization of Nrf2 and P65 (Fig. 5E, G). Moreover, ML385 effectively reversed the CEP-induced upregulation of Nrf2 expression and significantly diminished the protective effects of CEP on LPS-induced RWPE-1 cells. In the context of inflammatory factor expression, CEP demonstrated a significant inhibitory effect on the LPS-induced elevation of mRNA levels for TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-17 A, IFN-γ, GM-CSF, IL-6, and CCL2. However, the introduction of ML385 substantially counteracted this anti-inflammatory effect (Fig. 5H). Similarly, the suppressive impact of CEP on the protein expression levels of COX-2, iNOS, IL-6, IL-1β, and CCL2 was nullified following ML385 intervention (Fig. 5I, G). These findings collectively suggest that the inhibition of Nrf2 can negate the protective effects of CEP against LPS-induced inflammatory responses in RWPE-1 cells, thereby reinforcing the notion that the Nrf2 pathway constitutes the principal molecular mechanism underpinning the efficacy of CEP.

These findings suggest that the protective effects of CEP against LPS-induced oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and apoptosis in RWPE-1 cells are contingent upon the activation of the Nrf2 pathway.

The alleviating effects of CEP on EAP mice depend on the Nrf2 signaling pathway

To elucidate the pivotal role of Nrf2 in the mechanism by which CEP alleviates EAP, we employed Nrf2−/− mice to develop models and validate the underlying mechanism. The findings indicated that, in the absence of Nrf2, the therapeutic efficacy of CEP on EAP was entirely negated. Histopathologically, the Nrf2−/− EAP group exhibited inflammatory cell infiltration comparable to that of the WT EAP group; however, CEP did not ameliorate the pathological damage to the prostate (Fig. 7A), and there was no statistically significant difference in inflammation scores between the Nrf2−/− EAP group and the Nrf2−/− EAP + CEP group (Fig. 7B). In terms of behavioral assessment, CEP also failed to mitigate the abnormal pain responses of Nrf2−/− EAP mice to Von Frey filament stimulation (Fig. 7C). Further analysis demonstrated that Nrf2 deficiency resulted in the loss of CEP’s systemic and local anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties. Specifically, CEP was ineffective in reducing serum inflammatory factor levels in EAP mice (Fig. 7D) and was unable to reverse the aberrant changes in oxidative stress markers (SOD, GSH, MDA) (Fig. 7H). In parallel, the regulatory influence of CEP on immune cell infiltration, specifically the reduction in CD4+ T cell and CD68+ macrophage accumulation within prostate tissues (Fig. 7E), as well as the suppression of CCL2 expression (Fig. 7F, G), was absent in Nrf2−/− mice. Furthermore, the anti-apoptotic effect of CEP was nullified (Fig. 7K, M). These in vivo experiments utilizing the Nrf2 knockout model substantiate that the therapeutic efficacy of CEP on EAP is significantly contingent upon the presence of the Nrf2 pathway.

CEP increases Nrf2 expression by inhibiting its ubiquitin-dependent degradation in RWPE-1 cells

To explore whether CEP augments Nrf2 protein levels by impeding its degradation, we initially treated RWPE-1 cells with the protein synthesis inhibitor cycloheximide (CHX) and monitored the degradation kinetics of Nrf2. As depicted in Fig. 8A and B, Nrf2 protein levels progressively declined over time with CHX treatment alone. Conversely, in the cohort co-treated with CHX and CEP, the degradation rate of Nrf2 was markedly reduced. To elucidate the degradation pathway of Nrf2 further, cells were treated with the proteasome inhibitor MG132 and the autophagolysosome inhibitor BafA1, respectively. The findings indicate that MG132 markedly elevated Nrf2 protein levels, while BafA1 treatment did not produce significant alterations. Additionally, MG132 effectively counteracted the LPS-induced suppression of Nrf2 expression (Fig. 8C, D). Molecular docking analyses revealed a binding energy of -8.5 kcal/mol between CEP and the Nrf2 protein. CEP predominantly interacts with the hydrophobic pocket of Nrf2 and stabilizes this interaction through various non-covalent forces (Fig. 8E). Importantly, the force network surrounding LYS75, a critical lysine residue for Nrf2 ubiquitination, may directly impact the ubiquitination process. Co-IP experiments further demonstrated that LPS treatment enhances the association of Nrf2 with ubiquitin, thereby promoting its ubiquitin-mediated degradation; in contrast, CEP intervention significantly reduced the binding of Nrf2 to ubiquitin (Fig. 8F). In summary, CEP directly associates with the Nrf2 protein, disrupts its ubiquitination process, thereby inhibiting proteasome-mediated degradation, and ultimately elevates the expression level of Nrf2 protein.

Discussion

In this study, we conducted a systematic investigation into the therapeutic effects and molecular mechanisms of CEP on CP/CPPS utilizing integrated in vivo and in vitro experimental systems. We found that CEP significantly ameliorated pathological damage in prostate tissue, alleviated pelvic pain responses, and reduced both inflammatory cell infiltration and the expression levels of inflammatory factors in EAP model mice. Mechanistic analyses demonstrated that CEP facilitated the nuclear translocation of Nrf2 by binding to the Nrf2 protein and inhibiting its ubiquitination-mediated degradation, thereby enhancing cellular antioxidant defense mechanisms. Concurrently, CEP inhibited the activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway, thereby mitigating the inflammatory response. Furthermore, CEP conferred tissue-protective effects by enhancing mitochondrial function, reducing oxidative stress, and inhibiting apoptosis.

CEP has been shown to mitigate the production of free radicals by activating NADH oxidase [21], thereby enhancing its ability to protect nuclear DNA from damage caused by endogenous oxidants [22]. By attenuating inflammatory and oxidative responses, CEP confers protective effects against muscle and kidney injuries induced by limb ischemia-reperfusion [23]. In acute myeloid leukemia, constitutive activation of NF-κB enhances inflammatory responses in the tumor microenvironment, promotes tumor cell survival through the upregulation of anti-apoptotic genes, and reduces the sensitivity of leukemic cells to therapy [24]. In rat models, CEP has demonstrated efficacy in ameliorating diabetic nephropathy by inhibiting NF-κB and reducing the levels of IL-1β and TNF-α [25]. Current research has unequivocally identified autoimmune dysfunction as a central pathogenic mechanism in CP/CPPS. The aberrant activation and infiltration of immune cells not only contribute to the pathological progression of CP/CPPS but are also intricately linked to the manifestation of disease-related symptoms such as pain [26, 27]. CD4+ T cells play a crucial role in modulating the disease progression and pain symptoms associated with CP/CPPS, while macrophages further exacerbate the pathological development of CP/CPPS through synergistic interactions with T cells. Additionally, CEP functions as an immunomodulator by regulating multiple signaling pathways in abnormally activated T cells, including the NF-κB, cell cycle, MAPK, and PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathways [28]. CCL2, also referred to as monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1), is secreted by monocytes and has the capacity to stimulate nerve endings, thereby generating pain afferent signals. It plays a pivotal role in immune regulation, inflammatory responses, and various pathophysiological processes. Empirical studies have demonstrated that serum levels of IL-1β, IFN-γ, IL-17 A, and CCL2 are elevated in EAP mice, accompanied by immune cell infiltration within the prostate parenchyma and increased CCL2 concentrations [29]. These findings align with our research, which indicates that CEP can significantly mitigate pathological damage in prostate tissue, reduce pelvic tactile hypersensitivity, and decrease immune cell infiltration in EAP mice. Furthermore, CEP treatment is associated with a reduction in serum levels of key inflammatory and pain-related factors, including TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-17 A, and CCL2.

Oxidative stress is intricately linked to the pathogenesis of CP/CPPS, with ROS serving as pivotal endogenous signaling molecules. Empirical studies have identified aberrant levels of oxidative stress-related biomarkers, such as GSH, MDA, SOD, NADPH, and ROS, in EAP models [30, 31]. The accumulation of ROS in CP/CPPS results in deleterious effects on cellular proteins, DNA, organelles, and cell membranes via lipid peroxidation, culminating in pathological alterations including edema, congestion, epithelial hyperplasia, and fibrosis within prostate tissue. These changes facilitate the advancement of CP/CPPS [32, 33]. Studies have shown that oxidative stress mediates mitochondrial dysfunction, induces resistance to oxidative phosphorylation inhibition, and enhances glycolysis, thereby promoting the progression of glioma [34]. Mitochondrial dysfunction further exacerbates ROS production and ATP synthesis, leading to cellular energy stress, which intensifies tissue damage and inflammation in CP/CPPS. This establishes a vicious cycle that accelerates the progression of CP/CPPS and contributes to joint destruction [35]. Mitigating oxidative stress and bolstering antioxidant defenses in prostate tissue may represent a viable therapeutic strategy for individuals with CP/CPPS [36]. Nrf2 plays a critical role in maintaining intracellular redox homeostasis by regulating the expression of various antioxidant genes and enzymes [37]. Redox regulation and mitochondrial metabolism-mediated metabolic reprogramming are closely associated with immune regulation, in which Nrf2 serves as a central transcriptional hub and plays a critical role [38]. Elevated intracellular levels of ROS facilitate the nuclear translocation of Nrf2, which subsequently binds to antioxidant response element (ARE) sequences in DNA. This interaction triggers the transcription of phase II detoxifying enzymes, such as SOD and catalase (CAT), as well as antioxidant proteins, including HO-1 and NQO1, thereby contributing to the maintenance of oxidative stress homeostasis [39, 40]. Research conducted by Zhao et al. has illustrated that lycopene mitigates CP/CPPS-related oxidative stress by activating the Nrf2 signaling pathway, which results in the upregulation of CAT, glutathione peroxidase (GSH-PX), and SOD expression levels, alongside the downregulation of MDA expression levels [41]. As target genes of Nrf2, HO-1 and NQO1 are pivotal in preserving mitochondrial function, preventing oxidative stress-induced damage, and inhibiting apoptosis [42]. The current study corroborated that treatment with CEP effectively ameliorated oxidative stress in EAP mice by enhancing SOD and GSH activities and reducing MDA levels, while also improving mitochondrial function, decreasing ROS production, and restoring cellular energy homeostasis. The antioxidant mechanism of CEP primarily involves the modulation of the Nrf2 pathway. CEP facilitates the nuclear translocation of Nrf2 and enhances the expression of its downstream target genes, HO-1 and NQO1. Furthermore, upon upregulating Nrf2, CEP markedly suppresses the phosphorylation of P65 and IκBα, which are critical components of the NF-κB pathway. Notably, the anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects of CEP are entirely negated in Nrf2 knockout mice or upon administration of the Nrf2 inhibitor ML385, thereby identifying Nrf2 as an upstream regulator in the pharmacological actions of CEP.

Maintaining oxidative stress homeostasis and mitochondrial function is a critical mechanism for the regulation of apoptosis [43, 44]. Recent studies have demonstrated a close association between apoptosis and CP/CPPS, suggesting that apoptosis may be involved in the pathological mechanisms of CP/CPPS and could potentially serve as the final link in the dysregulation of signaling pathways [45]. The anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2 plays a pivotal role in preserving mitochondrial integrity and function by preventing the release of cytochrome c into the cytoplasm [46]. Conversely, under apoptotic signaling, the accumulation of Bax in the outer mitochondrial membrane compromises mitochondrial membrane integrity, leading to the activation of caspase-3 and the induction of irreversible apoptosis [47, 48]. In this study, compared to the EAP group, treatment with CEP significantly reduced the TUNEL-positive rate of prostate cells, decreased the expression levels of Bax and caspase-3, and increased the level of Bcl-2. However, the anti-apoptotic effect of CEP was reversed in Nrf2−/− EAP mice. These findings were corroborated by in vitro experiments. Consequently, CEP enhanced mitochondrial function through the Nrf2 signaling pathway, thereby inhibiting cellular apoptosis.

Currently, the precise mechanism by which CEP facilitates Nrf2 expression remains to be elucidated. Protein degradation is a fundamental process for cellular homeostasis, primarily mediated by the ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS) and lysosomal pathways. To further investigate the degradation pathway of Nrf2, we conducted experiments using RWPE-1 cells to assess the impact of CHX on these cells in the presence and absence of CEP. The results indicated that CEP reduced Nrf2 degradation and increased Nrf2 expression. Moreover, MG132 mitigated the LPS-induced suppression of Nrf2 in RWPE-1 cells, while BafA1 did not affect Nrf2 expression. Our findings suggest that Nrf2 degradation is mediated via the UPS pathway. Molecular docking analyses revealed the presence of Lys residues in the interaction between CEP and Nrf2. Given that Lys residues are the exclusive covalent attachment sites for ubiquitination, their presence is essential for the ubiquitination process to occur. Co-IP assays revealed that CEP intervention led to a reduction in the co-precipitation of intracellular Ub with Nrf2, suggesting that CEP diminishes Nrf2 ubiquitination and subsequent degradation. Furthermore, Nrf2 is known to undergo degradation via the Keap1-mediated ubiquitin-proteasome pathway. The Kelch domain of the adaptor protein Keap1 recognizes the Neh2 domain of Nrf2, facilitating its linkage to the E3 ubiquitin ligase complex and promoting its degradation through the proteasome [49]. Zhang et al. reported that SAL has a protective effect on vascular endothelial dysfunction induced by AGEs, and the mechanism is related to activation of Nrf2/HO-1, reduction of Keap1 expression, reduction of ROS formation and suppression of NF-κB pathway activation [50]. Therefore, we hypothesize that CEP might directly interferes with the Keap1–Nrf2 interaction. Our study demonstrated that CEP treatment inhibits the ubiquitination and degradation of Nrf2 in RWPE-1 cells, it confirms that the Nrf2 signaling pathway is involved in the biological effects of CEP, but the precise molecular targets upstream of Nrf2 have not yet been fully elucidated. Identifying its key upstream molecules, such as kinases, adaptor proteins, or other regulatory molecules, is of great significance for deepening mechanistic research and optimizing CEP-based therapeutic strategies.

To date, extensive studies have been conducted on the phytochemistry and pharmacological activities of CEP. However, its clinical translation still faces numerous challenges. First of all, CEP suffers from poor solubility, low oral absorption, and limited bioavailability, which severely compromise its therapeutic efficacy. To better understand the pharmacological activities of CEP, further human pharmacokinetic studies are therefore warranted. Secondly, CEP is significantly restricted by the physical blockage of the blood-prostate barrier and the active efflux of P-glycoprotein (P-gp), resulting in remarkably low actual bioavailability in prostate tissue. Insufficient drug exposure in the prostate after systemic administration represents a key factor limiting the efficacy of CEP against prostate-related diseases. Finally, comprehensive toxicity evaluations, including acute and subchronic toxicity studies, are needed to further validate the safety of CEP.

Conclusion

The study is the first to elucidate the effects of CEP on CP/CPPS, indicating that CEP can mitigate CP/CPPS by attenuating Nrf2 ubiquitination and degradation, as well as inhibiting the NF-κB signaling pathway. These findings underscore the therapeutic potential of CEP for CP/CPPS and offer novel insights into its application.
