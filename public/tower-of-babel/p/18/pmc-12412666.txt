Formation of breccia pipes associated with a hydrogen-rich hydrothermal system on the east Caroline plate in the West Pacific
Authors: Yuanyuan Xiao, Kun Wang, Yuan Wang, Shuai Yuan, Hongyun Zhang, Tianyu Zhang, Congying Li, Annan Ren, Guozhi Xie, Vadim S. Kamenetsky, Hao Xie, Fengtai Tong, Shengji Wei, Weidong Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
A large hydrothermal field at depths >4300 meters was found on the east Caroline plate in the western Pacific Ocean. Here, we show that large hydrothermal pipes with steep walls and breccia-dominated bottoms suggest explosions of billion metric tons of TNT (trinitrotoluene) equivalent. More than 800 short-duration seismic events were detected within 28 days along a 150-kilometer profile, indicating widespread ongoing explosive gas release. We suggest that billions of tons of hydrogen gas were produced through serpentinization associated with fluid infiltration via fractures formed by bending of the oceanic plate. Hydrogen was retained in fractures sealed by mud and carbonate. However, hydrogen alone cannot produce the explosive capacity responsible for the formation of pipes. Oxygen released from circulating seawater can be concentrated at crustal depths and then mixed with mantle-derived hydrogen. Explosion of the gas mixture releases large amounts of heat that triggers further explosions of compressed hydrogen gas and results in the formation of hydrothermal pipes.

INTRODUCTION

A large hydrothermal pipe swarm, named Kunlun (1), was found in the east Caroline plate near the Mussau Trench (2–4) during the National Science Foundation of China (NSFC) expedition no. 583 in 2024. Twenty large round/oval pipes cover a total area of ~11 km2. High concentrations of hydrogen were detected in hydrothermal vents using an on-site in situ laser Raman spectrometer (1). What we have found is far larger than Lost City, the estimated hydrogen flux of Kunlun accounting for 5% of the estimated global hydrogen emission (5, 6). The pipes are large, with diameters ranging from 450 to 1800 m and depths of 30 to 130 m (1). To form such large pipes through explosion requires energy of 106 to 109 metric tons of trinitrotoluene (TNT) equivalent. Most craters of this type would be volcanic (7). However, this is well removed from the incipient arc activity on the other side of the trench (Fig. 1). There is no evidence of volcanic activity in this region, and the cause of such explosions is potentially related to the release of explosive hydrogen. How did hydrogen form and concentrate into an explosive gas responsible for these pipes?

RESULTS

Hydrothermal pipes

Twenty pipes in total were found within 80 km west of the Mussau Trench (Fig. 1, A and B). Most of the pipes are distributed on a platform of ~300 m higher than the adjacent seafloor (Fig. 1, A and B) (1). Four of the pipes have been investigated in detail through diving using the Fendouzhe human-occupied vehicle (HOV).

Pipe A (“Laoshan”) and pipe B (“Zhushan”) are both ~130 m deep, with steep walls and breccias at the bottom (Fig. 2) (6), similar to those of kimberlite pipes (7). Each pipe contains several generations of smaller pockmarks inside, often with three to five bowl-shaped pockmarks of tens of meters in diameter, which in turn host groups of smaller pockmarks of meter to submeter in diameter. Active hydrothermal vents are observed at the bottoms of these pipes, and hydrothermal fluids spray out through gaps or cracks in breccia piles (Fig. 2 and movies S1 to S4) or through small tubes of centimeter to subcentimeter in diameter on walls of pockmarks in large pipes (fig. S1). Fossil hydrothermal tubes of similar sizes have been identified on walls of large hydrothermal pipes, together with the presence of gaps filled with carbonate (movie S5), indicating that similar processes have repeated several times.

Both Laoshan and Zhushan (Fig. 1B) lack any large chimney, which is commonly found in other hydrothermal systems (8). For example, the Lost City hydrothermal fields have carbonate chimneys that are tens of meters high (9, 10). One reason for the lack of chimneys might be that the water depths in the Kunlun pipe swarm range from 4300 to 4500 m, which are deeper than the carbonate compensation depth (CCD), which occurs at ~4300 m in the West Pacific Ocean (11). By definition, the CCD refers to the depth where the deposition rate of CaCO3 is balanced by the dissolution rate (12, 13). It is essentially the carbonate saturation depths, which vary with temperature and concentrations of CO2, Ca2+, and Mg2+. The water depths of the Lost City are only 800 to 850 m (8, 14), and so carbonate chimneys are well preserved.

Short-duration seismic events

Twenty-one passive source ocean bottom seismometers (OBS) (15, 16) were deployed across the Mussau Trench along a profile of ~150 km (Fig. 3), with a mean deployment period of 28.3 days. From these, 19 OBS have complete data records. One type of seismic event named as a short-duration event (SDE) was detected in all 19 OBS. The SDE has a characteristic duration of <1 s, without definition of P or S wave arrivals, and a dominant frequency between 5 and 20 Hz. The SDE is generally visible on three components of the seismometer and, sometimes, also on the hydrophone. Such SDEs have been reported over sediments covering the ocean floor and might be related to sudden energy release associated with fluid migration (17–21).

The SDEs were first identified using the automatic short-time averaging/long-time averaging algorithm and then manually confirmed. A total of 824 SDEs have been confirmed based on the continuous data of 19 OBS (Fig. 3). The observed SDEs are not recorded simultaneously by neighboring OBS, which indicates that the sources are not far away from each OBS and that their magnitudes are small, similar to previous observations of SDE in cold seeps on ocean floors (17, 18, 22, 23). Such signals are not caused by fish bumps, moving mammals, instrumental, nor current-related noise. Instead, it appears that they are related to small-scale gas release events induced by hydrothermal activities beneath sediments (17, 18, 22, 23).

In the Mussau Trench, SDE occurred more frequently in the flexure section of the subducting Caroline plate (e.g., station nos. 7, 8, and 10 have 72 to 118 SDEs; Fig. 3). Notably, station nos. 1 and 2 around Laoshan and Zhushan, well away from the flexure region, are also high with 65 and 83 SDEs, respectively. This is consistent with an inference of strong and persistent hydrogen-related activities. In this case, SDEs indicate widespread active gas leakage in association with the Mussau Trench.

Bending fractures

High-precision multibeam echo sounding shows a series of large fracture zones to the west of the Mussau Trench. These fractures are roughly parallel to each other, at an angle of ~25° to the trench (Fig. 1C). The south ends of some fractures have already been subducted through oblique subduction along the trench (3).

A diving expedition along one of the fractures has been carried out using Fendouzhe. This fracture zone is bounded by two large normal faults in the subducting plate west of the trench, which is dipping roughly southeastward. The water depths of the fracture zone are ~100 m deeper than the adjacent region to the west and ~50 m shallower than that to the east (fig. S2). To some extent, the fractures we observed are analogous to those in the outer rise region of a mature subduction system, which is caused by the upward bending of the subducting plate near the trench in response to the plate dipping (24, 25).

The Mussau Trench was formed through induced subduction initiation (2, 3), which is characterized by compression (26), although the subduction stopped at its infant stage (2–4). Numerical modeling and seismic observations show that such bending-induced fractures in the outer rise can cut into the lithospheric mantle (25, 27–30). For instance, in the Mariana outer rise, such fractures reach depths of 40 km (31, 32). In subduction initiation such as with Mussau Trench, the lithosphere was broken due to bending, such that fractures would cut through the whole lithosphere.

Mud layers and fossil pipes

We directly observed the stacked mud layers widely distributed in the east Caroline plate near the Mussau Trench while diving (movies S6 and S7). The mud is pale, and its soft and sticky nature made it difficult to pick up with the mechanical arm of Fendouzhe (movie S8). Beds of mud layers range in height from several centimeters to meters (Fig. 4). The surface of the beds is covered by a millimeter-thick black layer (Fig. 4). Samples of the mud layers were obtained with a push core device (movie S9). The mud consists of a mixture of carbonate fossil fragments, silicate minerals, and manganese-rich minerals. All the mud layers studied contain dolomite (fig. S3).

Vertical open tubes of centimeters to subcentimeters in diameter were observed in sediments on steep walls in the fracture zone (Fig. 4). Abundant tubes are found on several cliff profiles of more than 10 m high (movies S10 and S11), which is identical to those found on the walls of the Kunlun pipe swarm, indicating that the tubes are fossil hydrothermal channels. One possible explanation is that carbonate minerals in the mud layers were formed through hydrothermal activity and the mud layers were formed through mud eruptions.

Fractures that cut deeply into or even through the lithosphere mantle allow seawater penetration into the lithospheric mantle, resulting in large-scale serpentinization, similar to that observed on subducting plates (Fig. 4C) (29, 33–35). At temperatures below 700°C (36), the reaction of peridotite with hydrothermal fluids results in serpentinization that yields hydrogen gas (Eq. 1)Peridotite+water=serpentine+magnetite+H2(1)

The reaction can be expressed as Eq. 2 (37, 38)2FeO+H2O=Fe2O3+H2(2)

The density of serpentine is ~2.5 g/cm3, while that of mantle peridotite is ~3.3 g/cm3. Therefore, the volume of mantle rocks could expand by ~25% when they are fully serpentinized. Such volume expansion during serpentinization causes fractures to seal. The stress field of the subducting plate changes during the subduction initiation (39). Once subduction commenced, the compression within the subducting plate released dramatically. Subsequently, the strongly bent subducting plate, i.e., the east Caroline plate, rebounds back and the fractures tend to further shrink and close. Meanwhile, friction between the surface of the subducting plate and the base of the overriding plate also promotes the closure of fractures in the subducting plate. Serpentinite and carbonate both have weaker mechanical strength than the mantle peridotite. Therefore, these minerals are more easily deformed, crushed, and milled during the closure of fractures. Milled serpentinite with newly formed carbonate is squeezed out through consecutive eruptions of mud volcanos that formed multiple mud layers, through mud volcano eruptions (Fig. 4D). In addition, carbonate further seals cracks within mud layers.

DISCUSSION

The similar crater characteristics of the Kunlun pipe swarm compared with those of kimberlite pipes (7) suggest a similar formation mechanism. Considering that kimberlite pipes are generally attributed to explosive eruptions of CO2-rich kimberlite magmas, the Kunlun pipe swarm might have formed through explosions of compressed hydrogen.

Serpentinization is the most efficient geologic process that forms hydrogen. Ferrous iron content in peridotite, fluid-to-rock ratios, and temperatures and pressures all influence the hydrogen production rate and the potential for hydrogen emissions (38). The highest serpentinization rate is expected at ~200° to 500°C, e.g., at 300°C, the amount of H2 produced can be up to 158 mmol/kg (40–42). No serpentinization is expected at temperatures higher than 700°C (43–46). Therefore, serpentinization starts from the top of the lithospheric mantle and proceeds downward.

Molecular hydrogen has the highest diffusion capacity, and thus, it is not easily retained in geologic settings. However, mud is highly leakproof and one of the best geologic material that can seal hydrogen. Once serpentinite mud is squeezed out along fractures, it seals the escape of hydrogen. Such closed fracture systems are more or less like high-pressure cookers. The water circulation inside a sealed fracture regulates the temperature of the mantle and thus facilitates further serpentinization and formation of hydrogen. Considerable amounts of hydrogen can be accumulated in sealed fractures. Once the pressure is high enough, i.e., higher than the damage limit of covering rocks plus water pressures, explosions could occur purely due to physical expansion, forming pipes. This suggestion is consistent with the wide distribution of breccias at the bottom of all the large pipes (Fig. 2 and movies S1 and S2).

Using the empirical equation presented in Materials and Methods, to form a pipe of 450 m in diameter through one blast requires 1.86 × 106 tons of TNT equivalent on soil, and 2.5 × 107 tons on hard rocks. For the largest pipe, 1800 m in diameter on hard rocks, it requires 2.6 × 109 tons of TNT equivalent. Specifically, the adiabatic expansion of 1 ton of hydrogen from 1500 bar down to 400 bar is equivalent to 0.21 ton of TNT (Materials and Methods), i.e., 1.2 × 108 to 1.2 × 1010 tons of hydrogen is required. This requires 1.1 × 109 to 1.1 × 1011 tons of water to be fully reduced to hydrogen gas. Serpentinites have been inferred even at depths of 40 km using seismic data (31). The pipes are about 10 km apart from each other. To host the amount of water required in a closed system, the effective width of the fracture zone ranges from 2.8 to 280 m within a 10-km-wide, 40-km-deep block. However, far less hydrogen is needed if these pipes were formed through multiple explosions over a long time. Consistently, in both pipe Laoshan and pipe Zhushan, there are several generations of smaller pipes/pockmarks after the main explosions, likely due to repeated cycles of water penetration, serpentinization, sealed hydrogen gas, and explosion.

Serpentinization consumes mantle peridotite and hinders further water-rock interactions, such that the hydrogen production rate decreases dramatically after each cycle of explosions (Fig. 5). This ends up with smaller sizes of the younger generation of pockmarks and declining hydrogen hydrothermal activities in the pipe swarm. However, the smallest generation of pipes/pockmarks is shallow. It is not clear whether such shallow depths of breccias can accumulate enough hydrogen gas for explosions through pressure release.

An alternative scenario is that the pipe swarm and smaller pockmarks may be formed through blast of the hydrogen reacting with oxygen. The key factor in this model is the source of the oxygen. According to global ocean circulation models, cold surface water from the Antarctic and Greenland sinks to the ocean bottom and circulates globally (47). The Mooring array results suggest that deep water has high oxygen contents of 5 mg/liter (48, 49). The source of this oxygen is from atmospheric O2 dissolving in surface water. Gas dissolved in a hydrothermal fluid sample collected with a pressure holding sampling device shows nitrogen isotope compositions similar to that of the atmosphere (Fig. 6). The δ15N value is approximately −1.8‰, only marginally lower than that of the atmosphere, while the clumped nitrogen isotope (Δ15N15N) value is +19‰, which is identical to that of the atmosphere. This origin thus supports high potential O2 concentrations in hydrothermal fluids.

The solubility of O2 in water decreases exponentially with increasing temperatures (50). Therefore, seawater of 1.5°C near the bottom of the east Caroline plate releases oxygen once it circulates into the hot lithosphere along the fractures. Free oxygen gas will migrate upward and be trapped in the top part of sealed fractures (Fig. 5, B and D). Hydrogen formed through serpentinization also migrates upward and accumulates in the same reservoirs.

Overall, the amount of O2 gas released from seawater is several orders of magnitude less than the total of amount of hydrogen formed out of the same amount of seawater through serpentinization. Therefore, the gases sealed in the fractures should be dominated by hydrogen. However, oxygen is released at the crustal depths, whereas hydrogen is rising from the mantle depths. As such, an explosive mixture of oxygen and hydrogen can be produced in the sealed fractures. Hydrogen content in the mixture (i.e., hydrogen contents ranging from 4 to 95%) may be explosive, depending on pressures (51).

The volume of product of Eq. 3 is smaller than that of the total reactants even above the supercritical point of water, because three volumes of gas forms two volumes of water vapor, i.e., it will not explode by the reaction itself.O2+2H2=2H2O(ΔH=−572kJ/mol)(3)

Nevertheless, this reaction releases large amounts of heat. The reaction will then heat up the compressed hydrogen and trigger a much more serious explosion. The combustion of 1 ton of hydrogen releases 143 GJ of heat, which is equivalent to 34 tons of TNT. Consequently, the remnant hydrogen gas explosively expands due to dramatic increases in temperatures (fig. S4). Hydrogen may react with CO2, forming methane (43, 52), which can also be explosive in the presence of O2, especially under high temperatures and pressures.

The Kunlun pipe swarm suggests that hydrogen release through serpentinization at convergent margins may be far more intense than that occurring at continental margins and mid-ocean ridges. Billions of tons of hydrogen may be stored in sealed fractures in the deep ocean. Future prospection of hydrogen reserves should focus on major seafloor bending. Explosive hydrogen release can destroy hydrogen gas reserves, perhaps more so than the mobility of hydrogen gas. Care should be also exercised during exploration of deep-ocean hydrogen reserves.

Analytical method of nitrogen isotope

The analyses were carried out in Nanjing University. The pressure-holding sampling bottle was connected to a vacuum separation system via an additional cold trap [¼ inch (0.635 cm) in diameter]. The sealing valve was opened after the vacuum of the cold trap is better than 1 × 10−5 mbar, and the dissolved gases in the sample were absorbed into the cold trap. The valve was closed, and then the cold trap was immersed in liquid nitrogen to trap components such as carbon dioxide and water. The remaining gas was transferred to the vacuum system for further purification through a gas chromatography column to obtain pure nitrogen. The purified nitrogen gas was introduced into a high-resolution isotope ratio mass spectrometer (IRMS) (Thermo Fisher Scientific 253 Ultra High Resolution IRMS) to analyze nitrogen clumped isotopes.
