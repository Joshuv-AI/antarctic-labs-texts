Regulatory effects of winter Morchella esculenta cultivation on the summer maize phyllosphere microbiome and plant health-related traits
Authors: Gaofeng Liu, Kaifeng Zhou, Qiwei An, Chengcui Yang, Shunqiang Yang, Liuxing Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn response to the intensifying demand for reduced fertilizer use and increased climate instability, the strategic incorporation of cover crops to improve the maize (Zea mays L.) phyllosphere microenvironment and leaf functional traits has emerged as an important approach for sustaining and enhancing crop productivity. This study compared the effects of two-year (2023–2024) winter cultivation of Morchella esculenta (ME) and fallow (CK) on summer maize leaf functional traits, phyllosphere microbial community composition, and their potential implications for maize health-related traits. The results showed that the interannual variation mainly affected stomatal density (P < 0.05). ME increased net transpiration rate by 30.7% (P < 0.05) and net photosynthetic rate by 23.0% compared to those of CK. ME exhibited varying degrees of enhancement in water-holding capacity and phyllosphere nutrient levels than those for CK, except for vitamin C and inorganic phosphorus content. Consistent with these physiological shifts, the bacterial normalized stochasticity ratio (NST) decreased under ME; meanwhile, β-deviation relative to that of the CK treatment increased, which was accompanied by selective enrichment of putative plant growth-promoting genera such as Glutamicibacter and Microbacterium and inhibition of the potential pathogen Erwinia. By contrast, the fungal NST remained above 50% and further increased under ME, whereas network complexity and the proportion of persistent taxa increased. Overall, ME cover cropping modified carbon–water–defense allocation in maize leaves and altered leaf surface conditions. Bacterial communities exhibited enhanced deterministic assembly coupled with enrichment of growth-promoting taxa, whereas fungal communities were characterized by amplified stochasticity and a more consolidated network structure. Therefore, the cultivation of ME in winter is recommended as an optimal management practice.Keywords: Cover crops, Maize, Phyllosphere, Plant health-related traits, Morchella esculenta, Legacy effects

Introduction

Maize is a major global cereal crop that is a fundamental component of food production systems and supports both dietary supply and agricultural livelihoods in various regions [1]. Owing to global warming [2], limited resources [3, 4] and environmental constraints [5], conventional high-input systems that rely extensively on mineral fertilizers and pesticides to maintain high crop yields have demonstrated unsustainability [3, 6, 7]. Therefore, precise manipulation of the plant microbiome [8, 9] is required to secure yield and stability while reducing external inputs. Cover crop management can modify soil physicochemical properties [10] and microbial diversity [11], thereby imparting soil conditioning effects [12] that may enhance the nutrient use and productivity of subsequent crops. However, whether these effects propagate upward along the soil–leaf continuum to restructure the phyllosphere microbiome and, subsequently, feedback on key physiological processes, such as photosynthetic performance and leaf water use dynamics, remains poorly understood.

The phyllosphere is a key interface between aboveground plant tissues and the external environment, and hosts highly diverse microbial communities [13] that play crucial roles in nutrient assimilation [14], photosynthetic efficiency [15], and stress responses [16]. Emerging evidence indicates that healthy plants frequently harbor phyllosphere communities with greater diversity and more stable functional organization [17], characterized by the enrichment of plant growth-promoting taxa [17] and the suppression of pathogens [18], thereby enhancing nutrient acquisition [19]and immunity [20]. Phyllosphere community assembly is influenced by deterministic filters such as host traits [21], microclimatic conditions [22], and stochastic processes, including dispersal and ecological drift [23], with bacteria and fungi frequently exhibiting asymmetric assembly mechanisms [24]. Recent studies on crop rotations involving Morchella esculenta (ME) indicate that ME has a strong ecological filtering effect in the soil, altering microbial communities and improving soil microenvironmental conditions and quality [25]. Similarly, peach-ME rotation or intercropping systems optimize soil physicochemical properties and microbial community composition, thereby enhancing crop yield and quality and increasing farmers’ income [26]. Concurrently, soil microbial legacies have been demonstrated to cascade upward by altering early endophyte colonization and aboveground biotic interactions [27, 28], suggesting that the “soil–leaf axis” may represent an important upstream driver of phyllosphere assembly. However, whether ME-based cover cropping in maize systems can propagate these soil-level effects upward along the soil–leaf axis, restructuring phyllosphere microbiomes and, subsequently, shifting maize leaf photosynthetic performance, water use dynamics, and overall plant health (as an integrated state of enhanced carbon–water efficiency, reduced metabolic defense costs, and a pathogen-suppressed, growth-promoting microbiome), remains poorly understood.

Accordingly, this study aimed to elucidate the influence of prior winter ME cultivation on bacterial and fungal community structures within the summer maize phyllosphere and examine how these community shifts are associated with plant health-related traits through alterations in key leaf functional traits. We proposed the following hypotheses: (1) interannual variation and cover crop type differentially regulate the photosynthetic and structural traits of maize leaves, and (2) ME cover affects leaf-surface nutrient composition, thereby directing bacterial–fungal assembly toward beneficial enrichment and pathogen suppression, which promotes healthier, low-defense maize phenotypes. This study provides theoretical and practical evidence for the use of mushroom-based cover crops to promote crop health in sustainable agroecosystems.

Materials and methods

Study site and environmental conditions

Field experiments were conducted during 2023–2024 in Hongnizha Village, Zhaoyang District, Zhaotong City, Yunnan Province, China (27.28° N, 103.60° E; altitude 1989 m), which is characterized by a plateau montane climate. Meteorological monitoring during the maize growing seasons showed that in 2023, total rainfall and mean air temperature were 1,084 mm and 14.4 °C, respectively, whereas in 2024 they were 698 mm and 12.2 °C (Fig. 1). Long-term records for the study region indicate a mean annual temperature of 12.2 °C and mean annual precipitation of 678 mm (30-year average). Other environmental variables, such as soil physicochemical properties, were not monitored simultaneously. The local cropping system typically follows a winter–summer rotation pattern, in which edible mushrooms, forage, or vegetables are cultivated as a winter cover crop from November to April, followed by maize grown from May to October.

Experimental design and field management

This study was conducted from April 2023 to October 2024 over two consecutive field seasons to compare the effects of two rotation systems: winter fallow (CK) and ME–summer maize cultivation. A randomized block design was used, with three replicates per treatment and a plot size of 40 m2 (5 × 8 m). ME was cultivated (abundance of ME in the soil: 1.5 × 105 CFU g− 1) in the same plots following the summer maize harvest in late November each year and harvested in mid-April of the following year, after which maize was replanted in the original plots. Prior to ME cultivation, the soil was plowed and air-dried for 10 d, after which ridges were formed with a height of 15 cm, top width of 1 m, and inter-ridge furrow width of 50 cm. Commercial ME spawn (Zhaotong Longxing Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) was broken into granules of 1–1.5 cm diameter and sown in three furrows per ridge at a rate of 15–22.5 kg hm− 2. The spawn was then covered with 2–3 cm of soil and thoroughly irrigated to increase the volumetric water content of the 0–20 cm soil layer to ˃60%. On the day of inoculation, a nutritive substrate (moisture content of 56%) composed of wheat grain, rice hulls, and wheat bran in ratios of 40%, 40%, and 20% was applied to the ridge surface at a rate of 24,000 kg hm− 2, followed by plastic film mulching for moisture conservation and the installation of a shading net with 50% light transmittance. The maize cultivar (Zhaohuang 24) was sown in May and harvested in October at a planting density of 7.5 × 104 plants ha− 1. The maize growing period was 162 d in 2023 and 169 d in 2024. The detailed agronomic management practices are summarized in Table 1. No chemical fungicides were applied during the maize growing season to maintain the natural phyllosphere microbiota. In the ME plots, part of the spent ME beds and substrate remained in the field following harvest to simulate actual production conditions, whereas the CK plots were maintained as bare soil. Additionally, the CK plots were managed with the same field practices as the ME treatment, except for the absence of morel cultivation and exogenous nutrient substrate addition.

Sample collection and pre-treatment

Fifty healthy leaves (the first leaf above the ear) per plot replicate were collected between 09:00 and 11:00 under calm to light wind conditions (wind speed < grade 3) at the full flowering (Bologische Bundesanstalt, Bundessortenamt, and Chemical Industry scale: 65) [29]. Within each treatment, sampling was performed in each of the three replicate plots, and the 50 leaves collected from a given plot were pooled as one composite sample. Photosynthetic parameters were measured in situ on ear leaves, after which the samples were divided into three subsets for different analyses. The first subset, used for leaf structural and physiological measurements, was immediately placed in ice boxes after collection and rapidly transported to the laboratory for storage and analysis. The second subset, used for biochemical assays, was collected with scissors, wrapped in aluminum foil, transported on dry ice to the laboratory, and stored at − 20 °C until analysis. The third subset, used for phyllosphere microbial diversity analysis, was collected under sterile conditions, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, transported under low-temperature conditions, and stored at − 80 °C for long-term preservation to maintain nucleic acid integrity and community structure.

Measurement methods

Structural and physiological traits

Net photosynthetic rate (Pn) and transpiration rate (Tr) were measured using a portable photosynthesis system (LI-6800, LI-COR Biosciences, USA). All measurements were conducted between 09:00 and 11:00 and the data were recorded after the readings stabilized. The leaf chamber was set to a flow rate of 500 µmol s− 1, relative humidity of 50%, reference CO2 concentration of 400 ppm, fan speed of 10,000 rpm, and photosynthetic photon flux density of 1,500 µmol m− 2 s− 1.

Leaf physiological and morphological traits: Moisture retention capacity [30] was measured by gently blotting surface water from sampled leaves and immediately recording fresh weight (FW). The leaf base was then immersed in deionized water at 4 °C in the dark for 6 h until it was fully turgid, and the turgid weight (TW) was recorded after blotting. The leaves were then dried at 70 °C for 48 h to a constant weight (DW). The moisture retention capacity was calculated from FW, TW, and DW. Moisture retention capacity was calculated as follows: MRC = (FW − DW)/(TW − DW) × 100%. Stomatal density was determined from epidermal impressions prepared using clear nail polish in the middle portion of the leaf [31]. After the nail polish dried, the film was gently peeled off, mounted on a slide, and observed under a light microscope (400×). Nine random fields were counted per leaf, and stomatal density was expressed as the mean number per unit area. Trichome density was assessed by mounting segments approximately 1 cm2 from the middle of the leaf on a sample stage and examining them using a stereomicroscope. For each sample, trichomes were counted in nine random fields, and trichome density was calculated as the mean number per unit area.

The leaf wax content was determined using a rapid chloroform extraction method [32]. After extraction, samples were first air-dried in a fume hood and then oven-dried at 60 °C to constant weight, and the results were expressed on a leaf-area basis. Vitamin C content was extracted with trichloroacetic acid and determined using a microplate colorimetric assay based on the reduction of Fe3⁺ and color development with 2,2’-bipyridyl [33]. The soluble protein content was quantified using the Bradford method: Coomassie Brilliant Blue G-250 binding under acidic conditions. The absorbance was measured at 595 nm, with concentrations calculated from a standard curve [34]. The inorganic phosphorus content was determined using a molybdate-based colorimetric method under acidic conditions, and concentrations were calculated from a KH2PO4 standard curve [35]. The soluble sugar content was quantified using the phenol–sulfuric acid method, with dehydration in concentrated H₂SO₄, condensation with phenol, and absorbance measured at 480–490 nm [36]. Cuticular permeability was assessed by monitoring water loss from excised leaves. Immediately after excision, the initial fresh weight was recorded using an electronic analytical balance with a precision of 0.0001 g. The leaves were then placed in open tubes and allowed to dehydrate at 23 °C under ambient conditions, with leaf weight measured hourly over a 12-h period to track the time course of water loss. Finally, the leaves were oven-dried at 37 °C for 72 h to determine their final dry weight [37]. The reducing sugar content was measured using the DNS method; samples were reacted with the DNS reagent under heating, and the absorbance of the brown-red product was recorded at 540 nm [38].

Phyllosphere microbial community analysis

Approximately 5 g of functional maize leaves were placed in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) containing 0.05% Tween-20 and shaken to detach the epiphytic microbes [13]. The wash solution was filtered through 0.22 μm membranes to concentrate epiphytic microorganisms, and total DNA was extracted from the biomass retained on the membranes using the DNeasy Power Plant Kit (Qiagen, Germany). DNA purity and concentration were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The V3–V4 region of the bacterial 16 S rRNA gene was amplified using primers 338 F/806R, and the fungal ITS1 region was amplified using primers ITS1F/ITS2R [39, 40]. Purified PCR products were sequenced (2–300 bp) on an Illumina MiSeq platform. Raw paired-end reads were quality-filtered using fastp (v0.23.4) and merged using FLASH (v1.2.11). Reads were clustered into OTUs at 97% sequence similarity using UPARSE/USEARCH (v11), and chimeras were removed using UCHIME. Taxonomy was assigned using the RDP Classifier (v2.11) against the SILVA (v138) database for 16 S rRNA and the UNITE ITS database (v9.0; ITS_fungi) for fungi with a confidence threshold of 0.70. Sequences annotated as chloroplasts or mitochondria were removed, and the OTU table was rarefied to 55,095 reads per sample for downstream analyses. Community β-diversity (Bray–Curtis, weighted UniFrac) was calculated, and group differences were assessed by PERMANOVA (with PERMDISP/betadisper performed to check homogeneity of dispersion). Microbial co-occurrence networks were constructed based on Spearman’s correlations and visualized using Gephi 0.10.1.

Data analysis

All data were expressed as mean ± standard error (mean ± SE). Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 26.0; IBM Corp., USA). A two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to test the effects of year (Y), cropping system (C; ME vs. CK), and their interaction (Y × T), and significant differences among the means were identified using Duncan’s multiple range test at P < 0.05. The following statistical model was used:

Yijk = µ + Yi+Cj+(Y×C)ij+ɛijk, where Yijk is the response variable, µ is the overall mean, Yi is the year effect, Cj is the crop effect, (Y×C)ij is the interaction effect, and εijk is the residual error. Graphs and visualizations, including changes in physiological traits, community diversity, and principal component analysis (PCA), were generated using Origin 2022 (OriginLab Corporation, Northampton, MA, USA). Microbial co-occurrence networks were inferred from pairwise Spearman correlations of taxa relative abundances after filtering low-prevalence/low-abundance taxa to reduce sparsity. Edges were retained only when they met an absolute correlation cutoff (|ρ| ≥ [0.5]) and remained significant after Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction (q < [0.05]). Networks were analyzed and visualized in Gephi 0.10.1, and topological parameters (average degree, modularity, and clustering coefficient) were calculated using the built-in Network Analyzer. The resulting networks represent statistical co-occurrence associations rather than direct ecological interactions. Beta diversity was computed using Bray–Curtis distances and visualized by PCoA. Group differences were tested using PERMANOVA (Adonis) with 999 permutations in R. To ensure that PERMANOVA results were not driven by heterogeneity of dispersion, we additionally performed PERMDISP (vegan::betadisper). Differential taxa were identified using Kruskal–Wallis and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, and biomarker discovery was performed using LEfSe. Where applicable, P-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) (reported as P_adjust/q-values).

Results

Year and cover crop selectively affected specific physiological and structural traits

The stomatal densities on the adaxial and abaxial surfaces in 2024 were 32% and 26% lower than those in 2023, respectively (P < 0.05, Table 2). Pn and Tr were 23% and 12% higher, respectively, in 2024 than in 2023 (P > 0.05). Compared to those of CK, ME increased Tr and Pn by 30.7% and 23.0%. The adaxial and abaxial stomatal densities under ME were similar to those under the CK (P > 0.05). ME produced only minimal changes in most biochemical and structural traits compared to those of CK (Fig. 2A–H): soluble protein (Fig. 2A) and soluble sugar (Fig. 2C) contents showed a slight increase, whereas inorganic phosphorus content tended to decrease (Fig. 2B); cuticular wax, reducing sugar content, and moisture retention capacity (Fig. 2D–G) were also modestly higher under ME (P > 0.05). By contrast, ME significantly increased the cuticular permeability (P < 0.05; Fig. 2F), but significantly reduced vitamin C content (P < 0.05; Fig. 2H).

ME drives host-linked, more deterministic assembly of phyllosphere bacterial communities

At the genus level, the 10 most abundant bacterial taxa (Fig. 3A) were Pseudomonas (ME > CK), Pantoea (CK > ME), Glutamicibacter (ME > CK), Frigoribacterium (CK > ME), Microbacterium (ME > CK), Curtobacterium (CK > ME), Sphingobacterium (ME > CK), Erwinia (CK > ME), Serratia (CK > ME), and Quadrisphaera (ME > CK). CK and ME contained 11 (13.41%) and 18 (21.95%) unique OTUs, respectively, and shared 53 (64.63%) (Fig. 3B). At the genus level, apparent shifts were observed between CK and ME; Glutamicibacter, Microbacterium, Xanthomonas, Sanguibacter, Klenkia, Arthrobacter, and Paenibacillus were significantly enriched, and Erwinia was significantly reduced in ME than in CK (P < 0.05; Fig. 3C).

Co-occurrence networks (Fig. 4E) revealed that bacterial communities were highly connected and dominated by positive correlations, with multiple highly connected nodes. Notably, families such as Enterobacteriaceae, Rhizobiaceae, Nocardiaceae, and Sphingobacteriaceae occupied central positions and played prominent functional roles in the network, thereby supporting tightly coupled modular structures. Consistently, most nodes were connectors (Pi > 0.62) with high among-module connectivity, while only a few were peripherals (Pi ≤ 0.62) in Zi–Pi (Fig. 4F); no module hubs or network hubs were detected. At the compositional level, ME reduced the proportion of persistent taxa and increased the number of transient and intermittent taxa (Fig. 4G). ME significantly lowered the bacterial normalized stochasticity ratio (NST) compared to that of CK (P < 0.05, Fig. 4H). Additionally, the regression between bacterial PC1 and leaf traits (Fig. 5) showed that among the 12 variables, only the vitamin C content was significantly and negativity correlated with PC1 (P < 0.05). CK and ME shared 88 species (41.51%), and contained 90 (42.45%) and 34 (16.04%) unique species, respectively (Fig. 6B). Multivariate and environmental analyses (Fig. 7) further elucidated the ecological significance of these shifts. PCoA (Fig. 7A) showed that PC1 and PC2 explained 45.88% and 25.74% of the total variance, respectively, with the CK and ME forming completely separate clusters at a 95% confidence level. Fungal stability indices (Fig. 7B) were similar for the CK (0.757) and ME (0.756). Environmental correlation analysis (Fig. 7C) showed that vitamin C content was significantly and positively correlated with the relative abundances of Filobasidium and Bullera (P < 0.05). Cuticular wax content was positively correlated with the relative abundances of Symmetrospora (P < 0.05), whereas reducing sugars, soluble protein, and cuticular permeability were significantly associated with the relative abundances of Leptosphaerulina and Erythrobasidium (P < 0.05). The community structure in both CK and ME was significantly related to multiple environmental factors and was dominated by positive correlations (P < 0.001, Fig. 7D), with CK being negatively associated with inorganic phosphorus content and moisture retention capacity.

The fungal co-occurrence network (Fig. 7E) exhibited a pronounced modular structure, with several families occupying highly connected central positions. In particular, Kondaceae and Erythrobasidiaceae acted as major hubs, showing numerous co-occurrence links with families such as Didymellaceae, Phaeosphaeriaceae, Filobasidiaceae, and Bulleraceae, forming the foundational framework of the phyllosphere fungal network. Most fungal nodes were classified as peripherals (Zi < 2.5, Pi < 0.62) or connectors (Zi < 2.5, Pi > 0.62), and no modules or network hubs were identified (Fig. 7F). At the compositional level, ME increased the proportion of persistent taxa and reduced the number of transient taxa, with persistent taxa accounting for more than 99% of the total relative abundance in both treatments (Fig. 7G). In addition, the NST values for both CK and ME substantially exceeded 50%. ME further increased NST (P < 0.001, Fig. 7H). Moreover, the regression between fungal PC1 and leaf traits (Fig. 8A-L) showed a highly significant negative correlation with soluble sugars (P < 0.05, Fig. 8G) and a significant positive correlation with cuticular permeability (P < 0.05, Fig. 8J)

Functional implications of dominant bacterial and fungal taxa

Table 3 highlights that the most abundant phyllosphere taxa under ME and CK differed in their type as well as in their functional profiles. Under ME, several dominant bacterial genera, such as Pseudomonas, Glutamicibacter, Microbacterium, Sphingobacterium, and Quadrisphaera, were mainly associated with plant growth promotion, enhanced photosynthesis, nutrient solubilization, and improved stress tolerance. By contrast, CK-enriched bacteria such as Erwinia and certain Curtobacterium strains included typical or potential pathogens, alongside taxa with more ambiguous or context-dependent roles such as Pantoea, Frigoribacterium, and Serratia. For fungi, ME promoted taxa such as Papiliotrema, Symmetrospora, and Alternaria, which facilitate root development, act as biocontrol agents, or serve as indicators of disease-resistant germplasms. By contrast, CK harbored higher relative abundances of stress-associated or pathogenic fungi and yeasts such as Hannaella, Vishniacozyma, Epicoccum, Filobasidium, Cladosporium, and Dioszegia. Collectively, these shifts indicate that the ME altered the dominant phyllosphere microbiota toward a consortium with stronger growth-promoting and resistance-supporting potential, whereas the CK phyllosphere exhibited a greater prevalence of stress-linked and pathogenic taxa, which is consistent with a host state with a “higher defense cost.”

Discussion

Potential sources and soil–plant–phyllosphere transmission

The assembly of the phyllosphere microbiome may be linked to the soil reservoir, suggesting a soil–plant–phyllosphere transmission continuum [79]. In our system, winter ME cultivation likely shaped the summer maize phyllosphere through both direct alteration of the source pool and indirect host filtering.

First, regarding the source pool, soil and crop residues represent major reservoirs for phyllosphere microbiota [13]. The retention of ME substrate and mycelial residues in the field could plausibly enrich soil and residue-associated microbial pools, increasing the availability of saprophytic or plant-associated taxa for aboveground colonization. Soil-borne microbes can reach leaf surfaces via rain-driven splash and bioaerosolization [80], providing a stochastic dispersal route consistent with the high stochasticity inferred for our fungal community assembl, in addition to possible plant-mediated transmission through belowground compartments.

Secondly, regarding indirect reshaping, while soil provides the microbial “seed bank,” our results suggest that the plant host acts as an important deterministic filter, particularly for bacteria. Notably, ME significantly decreased the bacterial NST, indicating a shift in bacterial community assembly toward stronger deterministic processes (host/environmental filtering) rather than stochastic colonization. Moreover, aboveground–belowground interactions [81] and soil legacies [82] can cascade upward to alter leaf physiology and defense-related traits. In this study, ME significantly modified maize leaf traits—most notably increasing cuticular permeability and decreasing vitamin C—which likely reshaped the leaf-surface physicochemical microenvironment and thereby favored the colonization of specific bacterial taxa (e.g., Glutamicibacter) while suppressing others (e.g., Erwinia). Together, these patterns support the interpretation that ME indirectly restructures the phyllosphere microbiome by optimizing the leaf niche and strengthening host filtering of bacterial colonizers, in addition to any upstream shifts in the soil inoculum source.

Interannual climate and cover crops differentially regulate maize leaf structure and carbon–water–defense functions

The two-year comparison in this study indicated that year-to-year climate and cover cropping do not act on maize leaves through the same pathway but rather dominate different levels of regulation. Interannual variation primarily affects the leaf construction phase by changing anatomical traits such as stomatal number, whereas cover crops influence this preformed structural framework by reallocating water and carbon and modifying the antioxidant status, thereby altering leaf functional phenotypes. In 2024, the stomatal density on both adaxial and abaxial surfaces was notably lower than that in 2023 (P < 0.05), whereas Pn and Tr were generally higher. This appears to contradict the intuitive expectation that more stomata indicate increased gas exchange [83–85]; however, it is consistent with developmental mechanisms underlying the coordinated adjustment of stomatal density, size, and maximum stomatal conductance. Typically, stomatal density is largely fixed during leaf development; high temperature and drought frequently induce “smaller and denser” stomata, whereas cooler conditions during leaf formation promote “larger and sparser” stomata [86], and stomatal density is generally negatively correlated with stomatal size [87]. Therefore, a lower density can maintain or even increase single-stomatal conductance and maximum leaf conductance. The early season low-temperature conditions in 2024 likely slowed epidermal cell division and reduced stomatal density per unit area; however, larger stomata and a higher theoretical conductance ceiling facilitated increased Tr and Pn later in the growing season.

Compared to those of CK, ME did not notably change stomatal density within a given year, but increased Tr and Pn, significantly increased cuticular permeability (P < 0.05), and reduced vitamin C content (P < 0.05), indicating that ME primarily modulated leaf function rather than structure. Previous studies have indicated that high Tr [88] and cuticular permeability [89] are frequently interpreted as signals of water stress or membrane damage; however, in this context, a different interpretation is more plausible. By improving soil structure and water status [26, 90] and buffering temperature extremes and evaporative loss [91], ME likely positioned maize within a more optimal water microenvironment, alleviated stomatal constraints, and enabled a “growth-oriented” hydraulic strategy characterized by high Tr and higher Pn. Therefore, the increase in cuticular permeability is more likely associated with enhanced water flow to facilitate increased transpiration rates mediated by the activation of membrane water channels and related permeability [92, 93], rather than to compromised membrane integrity.

Simultaneously, ME significantly decreased the leaf vitamin C content (P < 0.05), indicating a shift from a high vitamin C content–high defense cost state to a low vitamin C content-growth-prioritized allocation pattern. Although a high vitamin C content facilitates ROS scavenging [94] and resistance to pathogens [95], its synthesis and maintenance require substantial carbon and nitrogen, contributing to a typical growth–defense trade-off under resource limitation [96, 97]. ME likely alleviates stress sufficiently for plants to downregulate costly chemical defenses and reallocate part of the carbon and energy previously invested in antioxidant protection to other vital processes [96, 98].

Therefore, the interannual climate acts mainly through temperature and water conditions during leaf development to modify the stomatal anatomy and establish the potential upper limit of gas exchange. Based on this structural foundation, ME further improved the water environment and alleviated pathogen pressure, modifying antioxidant status and leaf-surface traits, shifting maize from a “conservative” to a “growth-oriented” hydraulic strategy, and establishing a new balance among water use, carbon intake, and defense mechanisms.

ME reshapes the maize phyllosphere microbiome toward a growth-promoting, pathogen-suppressed steady state

In this study, ME altered the bacterial community composition as well as changed how communities were assembled. For example, ME significantly enriched putative PGP genera, such as Glutamicibacter, Microbacterium, and Xanthomonas, and inhibited the potential pathogen Erwinia (P < 0.05, Table 3). Microbacterium exhibits typical PGP functions, including auxin production and P solubilization, in the phyllosphere of various crops [99, 100], and Glutamicibacter can alleviate salt stress and enhance photosynthesis in halophytes and related systems [48]. In the present study, Pn under ME only showed an increasing trend, but the relative abundance of Glutamicibacter was positively correlated with Pn, suggesting a functional coupling between ME-driven PGPB enrichment and photosynthetic enhancement. Under ME, the phyllosphere environment appears more favorable for PGPB colonization and proliferation, and these taxa may, subsequently, facilitate increased Pn by improving nutrient acquisition or stress relief, forming a “host physiology–PGPB” positive feedback loop [101].

The notable decline in Erwinia (P < 0.05) provides a complementary “pathogen–defense” perspective on vitamin C content dynamics. Vitamin C is a key molecule in plant defense, and its synthesis is typically upregulated under stress [102, 103]. In CK, Erwinia abundance and vitamin C content were both higher and positively correlated, whereas in ME, both Erwinia abundance and vitamin C content decreased (P < 0.05), and bacterial PC1 showed a significant negative correlation with vitamin C conten (P < 0.05). Together, these patterns indicate that the high vitamin C levels in the CK represent a defense cost rather than a health indicator; plants experiencing increased pathogen pressure allocate additional resources to antioxidant and defense pathways. By deterministically filtering out Erwinia and other pathogen-associated taxa, ME likely reduces perceived biotic stress, enabling plants to downregulate vitamin C content-based chemical defense and reallocate part of this carbon and energy investment back to growth and metabolism, similar to findings previously reported [96]. Community persistence analysis further supported this mechanism in this study. ME reduced the proportion of persistent taxa and increased transient taxa; however, owing to the lower NST, this “core disruption” is more likely to indicate community reassembly under a new host filter rather than instability. ME effectively resets the phyllosphere environmental filter by altering the carbon–water–antioxidant interface of maize leaves. The bacterial core community previously assembled under a “high vitamin C content–high pathogen pressure” regime becomes disrupted, creating colonization opportunities for growth-promoting and opportunistic taxa adapted to a “high photosynthesis–low defense cost” environment.

Overall, ME enhances environmental filtering to establish a “growth-promoting–enriched, pathogen–suppressed” bacterial module, consistent with previous findings [104]. This process forms a positive feedback loop involving host resource reallocation from defense to growth: as pathogen pressure declines, plants can reduce costly vitamin C content-based defenses, resulting in increased Pn and water use efficiency. This physiological state subsequently promotes further PGP colonization and enrichment. Under continued ME influence, the phyllosphere bacterial community is driven toward a new stable state characterized by reduced pathogen pressure and increased growth-promoting potential. The functions of the bacteria observed in different studies and their abundances in the current study (Table 3) further support the aforementioned results. This pattern is more consistent with a stress-responsive host adjustment than with vitamin C acting as a direct regulator of community composition. Specifically, the positive association of vitamin C with potential pathogens (Erwinia and Serratia) in CK, together with the concurrent declines in pathogen abundance and vitamin C under ME, suggests that higher vitamin C levels likely reflect an elevated defense investment under greater biotic pressure [102, 103]. Meanwhile, the ME-induced increase in cuticular permeability may also shift the leaf-surface physicochemical microenvironment, which could indirectly alter colonization opportunities for epiphytic taxa [105]. Nevertheless, these relationships are correlative, and reciprocal effects remain plausible (phyllosphere microbes may also modulate host antioxidant status and leaf traits).

Fungal assembly: increased stochasticity and a persistent backbone network

In contrast to the more deterministic bacterial response, the fungal NST index was ˃50% in the CK and increased further under ME, indicating that fungal assembly in the phyllosphere is dominated by stochastic processes and that this stochastic component is enhanced by ME. This bacterial–fungal divergence in assembly has been increasingly reported [24]. Notably, this pattern differs from numerous soil and rhizosphere systems, in which fungi are frequently more deterministic, and bacteria are more stochastic. For example, fungal NST was 33.72% compared to 45.23% for bacteria in agricultural soils, indicating stronger environmental filtering by fungi [106]. Stochasticity can be substantially high in certain rare fungal endophytes or ectomycorrhizal communities, particularly in taxa strongly influenced by climate, dispersal, and local microenvironments [107, 108]. Our findings indicate that in a highly open and exposed phyllosphere niche, fungal assembly resembles the latter pattern.

ME may enhance the stochastic components of fungal assemblies through two pathways. First, by altering the soil microenvironment and fungal source pool [109, 110], ME likely increases the frequency and randomness with which soil- and surface-derived spores reach leaf surfaces through wind and rain splash [16, 111], thereby elevating the contribution of dispersal and drift. Second, ME notably increases cuticular permeability (P < 0.05), which likely enhances leaf washing and trans-cuticular diffusion, increasing exudation of soluble sugar content and amino acids to the leaf surface [112, 113] and creating highly patchy “nutrient hotspots” at the micrometer scale. Studies involving cut membranes and leaf permeability demonstrate that solute leakage is concentrated at a few structural sites, resulting in an island-like distribution of resources on the leaf surface [114]. Under these conditions, the success of an individual spore largely depends on whether it lands on these resource-rich hotspots, a strongly opportunistic and stochastic process [114]. This is consistent with our finding that fungal NST increased under ME and that fungal PC1 was negatively correlated with soluble sugar content and positively correlated with cuticular permeability. By increasing cuticular permeability, ME modifies the leaf-surface resource landscape, simultaneously enhancing the stochastic component of fungal assembly and promoting taxa with an increased capacity to exploit these heterogeneous resource patches.

Based on this highly stochastic background, ME-enriched genera included Symmetrospora, Leptosphaerulina, and Erythrobasidium (Table 3). Leptosphaerulina was positively correlated with reducing sugars and soluble protein content, consistent with studies on strong extracellular enzyme activity and the ability to access plant-derived carbon by generating soluble sugars from polymeric substrates [115]. Symmetrospora is frequently identified on leaf surfaces [65] and has been proposed as an indicator taxon for resistant materials and resistance-inducing treatments [66, 67]. In the present study, its abundance was positively associated with wax content, suggesting an adaptation to leaf surfaces with stronger physical barriers. Similar to the “PGP–pathogen antagonism” module among bacteria, ME facilitated an “opportunistic–physical synergy” module in fungal communities: certain taxa efficiently consumed leaked carbon, reducing resource access for potential pathogens or saprotrophs, whereas others were adapted to thick cuticles and high-wax backgrounds and contributed to maintaining the physical defense barrier. In this study, ME treatment for bacteria selectively enriched PGPB genera, such as Glutamicibacter and Microbacterium (P < 0.05), while significantly inhibiting the potential pathogen Erwinia as well as vitamin C content (P < 0.05). For fungi, ME notably enriched Leptosphaerulina, Symmetrospora, and Erythrobasidium (P < 0.05), and the abundance of Leptosphaerulina was positively correlated with reducing sugars and soluble proteins, whereas Symmetrospora was positively associated with wax content, collectively supporting the notion that ME promotes an “opportunistic–physical synergy” functional module in the phyllosphere.

Crucially, ME notably reduced Filobasidium and Bullera (P < 0.05), both of which were positively correlated with vitamin C content and were associated with a high defense status. Filobasidium has been linked to plant stress responses [73] and elevated antioxidant states. Several Bullera strains show strong antifungal activity and potential as biocontrol yeasts [116], indicating that these yeasts tend to occur in plants with high defense levels or under stronger stress. When combined with the findings from this study, these results suggest that under CK, a “high vitamin C content–high Filobasidium and Bullera–high pathogen pressure” configuration tends to be self-reinforcing. However, under ME conditions, the reduced vitamin C content and suppressed Erwinia coincided with a shift from a defense-associated fungal assemblage to one dominated by opportunistic taxa and a persistent backbone. In addition, ME increased the proportion of persistent taxa and the number of nodes and edges in the fungal networks, indicating that despite stronger stochastic inputs, a subset of successful colonizers was retained over time and formed a tighter interaction backbone under the new resource and defense regime.

Overall, the fungal response to ME involves a combination of “stochastic input” and “selection-shaped output”: stochastic processes predominantly determine early arrival order and initial colonization sites, whereas persistence and network complexity mainly indicate which taxa are retained and engaged in stable interactions in response to environmental filters. This trajectory differs from the bacterial pathway of “lower NST, core disruption and enhanced growth-promotion,” and follows a pattern of “higher NST, thickened persistent backbone and tighter networks.” Thus, bacteria act as the frontlines of growth promotion and pathogen antagonism, while fungi provide buffering and redundancy through stochastic reassembly and a persistent network. Together, guided by cross-kingdom host traits such as vitamin C content and cuticular permeability, they co-construct a more robust phyllosphere under ME.

This study has the following limitations. Because morel cultivation and substrate addition were implemented concurrently, their effects cannot be strictly disentangled. Our analyses primarily linked community structure to leaf-level physiological traits, without integrating soil physicochemical properties, microclimate, or functional omics, and we did not directly assess crop yield or disease incidence/severity. In addition, the co-occurrence networks were inferred from correlation analyses of relative-abundance data and may be affected by compositionality; thus, the network patterns should be interpreted as statistical associations rather than evidence of direct microbial interactions.

Conclusions

Relative to that of CK, the ME cover modified leaf carbon–water defense phenotypes, reestablished phyllosphere nutritional and defense filters, strengthened bacterial selection, and increased stochastic inputs while structuring a stable fungal backbone network. These processes form a phyllosphere that is growth-promotion-enriched, pathogen-suppressed, and structurally robust. This study provides a mechanistic basis for the use of mushroom-based cover crops as microbial management tools for sustainable maize production. Based on these results, the cultivation of ME in winter is recommended as an optimal management practice. Future work should implement decoupled controls (substrate-only, morel-only, and combined treatments) and conduct multi-site, multi-year comparisons that integrate soil, microclimate, omics, and re-inoculation, while incorporating yield measurements to quantify the respective contributions, validate the soil–leaf axis mechanism under broader conditions, and assess its coupling with yield and stress tolerance.
