Mitral Transcatheter Edge-to-Edge Repair in Severe Left Ventricular Dysfunction
Authors: Sho Shiba, Masanori Yamamoto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding Author

Weber et al1, in this issue of JACC: Case Reports, describe a clinically compelling strategy for severe mitral regurgitation (MR) in the setting of severely reduced left ventricular (LV) dysfunction. Mitral transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (M-TEER) was performed under Impella 5.0 (Abiomed Inc) support, with the device maintained after the procedure and gradually weaned over several days. This feature distinguishes the present report from prior descriptions of “protected” M-TEER.2 Rather than using mechanical circulatory support (MCS) solely to facilitate procedural safety, the authors used it to create a period of controlled ventricular adaptation after MR reduction. The result was striking, with removal from the transplant waiting list and sustained NYHA functional class I-II at 3.5-year follow-up. This is a story worth telling. But it raises a clinical question of whether we can reliably identify the right patients for this strategy before we proceed.

M-TEER Under MCS in Severe LV Dysfunction

The recent OCEAN-Mitral (Optimized Catheter Valvular Intervention-Mitral) registry3 provides important context. In this large multicenter Japanese experience of MCS-assisted M-TEER, procedural success rates were high and periprocedural complications were acceptably low across a broad range of patients with severely reduced LV function, reinforcing that the technical execution of protected M-TEER has become increasingly feasible. Improvements in M-TEER technology, imaging guidance, and periprocedural management have collectively raised the bar for what can be achieved safely in the catheterization laboratory. Afterload mismatch and hemodynamic deterioration remain important concerns,4 and the prolonged postprocedural Impella weaning strategy described by Weber et al1 represents a thoughtful solution. Of note, the authors highlight that subclavian access for Impella insertion enabled early patient mobilization throughout the support period, a practically meaningful advantage that transforms what might otherwise be a bed-bound intensive care course into an opportunity for early rehabilitation and physical recovery. But as procedural success becomes more reproducible, the harder and more consequential question shifts from “can we do this safely?” to “should we do this, and for whom?”

When to Proceed, and When to Pivot

This brings us to the most clinically challenging question this case raises, namely when protected M-TEER is the right strategy and at what point the clinician should pivot toward durable LV assist device implantation5 or heart transplantation. There is no simple answer, but several considerations may help frame the decision. Protected M-TEER seems most appropriate when secondary mitral regurgitation (SMR) is disproportionate to the degree of underlying LV dysfunction and identifiable as a primary hemodynamic driver, and when guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT) has been genuinely maximized before any structural consideration. The divergent outcomes of the MITRA-FR (Percutaneous Repair with the MitraClip Device for Severe Functional/Secondary Mitral Regurgitation) trial6 remind us that SMR is not uniformly a primary hemodynamic driver, and M-TEER without prior GDMT optimization may address the symptom while leaving the underlying substrate untouched. In addition, there should be evidence of residual contractile reserve, including preserved right ventricular function, absence of severe end-organ dysfunction, and sufficient hemodynamic stability to permit elective M-TEER. Patients in whom GDMT uptitration has been incomplete due to hypotension or poor perfusion represent a particularly important subgroup. In these cases, the protected hemodynamic environment created by Impella support may itself enable the GDMT optimization that was previously not achievable, turning MCS into a facilitator of reverse remodeling rather than merely a procedural safety net. By contrast, when LV dilation is extreme, filling pressures remain refractory, end-organ perfusion is chronically impaired, and SMR appears to be a marker rather than a driver of disease, durable MCS or transplantation deserves earlier consideration.

Unanswered Questions

Two procedural questions also warrant attention. When M-TEER is performed under active Impella unloading, both residual MR severity and transmitral gradient may not accurately reflect postweaning physiology, making it difficult to define an optimal intraprocedural endpoint. Similarly, weaning guidance remains largely empirical, and a more structured approach incorporating invasive hemodynamics, biventricular echocardiographic assessment, and markers of end-organ perfusion would help standardize this critical phase. Patient selection, ultimately, must move from retrospective recognition to prospective definition. Importantly, the relationship between M-TEER and GDMT may not be strictly sequential. Yoshida et al7 recently demonstrated that M-TEER itself creates an opportunity for early GDMT uptitration that was previously not tolerated.7 In this framework, MCS-assisted M-TEER followed by protected GDMT optimization may serve not only as a therapeutic strategy, but also as a diagnostic bridge. The true need for transplantation or durable LVAD can only be meaningfully assessed after the ventricle has been unloaded, MR corrected, and medical therapy fully maximized. The present patient, relatively young with ischemic cardiomyopathy and favorable valve anatomy, had not yet reached the ceiling of medical therapy before intervention, suggesting that the ventricle retained meaningful capacity for recovery once afterload was addressed and GDMT could be fully optimized under Impella protection. This, in retrospect, is what made him an excellent candidate for this protected M-TEER strategy rather than a more definitive approach. The next task is to define, reliably and prospectively, for whom it should be tried and for whom the clock has already run out.
