Genomic Landscape of GNAQ and GNA11 Mutations in Metastatic Solid Tumors: A Real-World Data Analysis
Authors: Minsuk Kwon, Yunjin Go, Ji Eun Shin, Sung Hee Lim, Jung Yong Hong, Se Hoon Park, Joon Oh Park, Young Suk Park, Kee-Taek Jang, Jeeyun Lee, Seung Tae Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPURPOSEActivating mutations in GNAQ and GNA11 are recognized oncogenic drivers in uveal melanoma (UM); however, their prevalence across various cancers and their genomic context remain inadequately characterized. This study aimed to elucidate the genomic landscape of these mutations across a range of metastatic solid tumors.MATERIALS AND METHODSWe conducted a pan-cancer analysis involving 5,416 patients with metastatic solid tumors who had undergone next-generation sequencing. Our evaluation encompassed mutational distribution, hotspot identification, tumor mutational burden (TMB), microsatellite instability (MSI), and coalterations.RESULTSMutations in GNAQ (n = 10) or GNA11 (n = 16) were detected in 26 patients (0.48%). Aside from UM, these mutations were most frequently observed in colorectal cancer (38.5%), melanoma (15.4%), gastric cancer (11.5%), and neuroendocrine tumors (7.7%). A notable subset exhibited an immunogenic profile, with 42.3% classified as TMB-High (≥10 Mut/Mb) and 19.2% as MSI-High. This immunogenic subgroup was primarily associated with nonhotspot mutations (eg, p.Gln88His, p.Ala231Val), suggesting that these may represent bystander events in hypermutated tumors. Conversely, canonical hotspots (Q209, R183) were predominantly identified in TMB-Low/microsatellite stable tumors, such as UM. Notable coalterations included NOTCH3 (80%), FAT1 (70%), and TP53 (37.5%-40%). Exploratory survival analysis suggested favorable OS in the immunogenic subgroup, supported by cases with durable ICI benefit including a treatment-free remission exceeding 21 months.CONCLUSIONThis real-world analysis demonstrates that GNAQ and GNA11 mutations are not exclusive to UM, occurring across diverse solid tumors, particularly colorectal cancer. The strong association between nonhotspot mutations and high TMB/MSI status defines a distinct immunogenic subgroup, underscoring the need to differentiate canonical hotspot drivers from bystander mutations when selecting targeted pathway inhibitors versus immune checkpoint blockade.

INTRODUCTION

The guanine nucleotide-binding protein G(q) subunit alpha (GNAQ) and guanine nucleotide-binding protein G(11) subunit alpha (GNA11) are highly homologous paralogs that encode the Gαq and Gα11 proteins, respectively. These proteins serve as critical signaling nodes, acting as alpha subunits of heterotrimeric G-proteins.1 Canonically, activating mutations in GNAQ and GNA11, predominantly occurring at the Q209 and R183 hotspots, result in the constitutive activation of downstream signaling pathways. This activation primarily occurs through the phospholipase C/protein kinase C (PKC) axis2 but also involves the engagement of the Hippo pathway effector YAP,3 thereby facilitating oncogenesis.

Historically, these mutations have been most prominently linked to uveal melanoma (UM), where they are regarded as founder events, present in over 80% of cases.4,5 This high prevalence has characterized UM as a distinct molecular entity, significantly driven by aberrant G-protein signaling, in contrast to the BRAF or NRAS mutations commonly associated with cutaneous melanoma.6

While the role of these mutations in UM is well-established, the broader prevalence, distribution, and clinical significance of GNAQ and GNA11 mutations across a diverse array of other solid tumors remain inadequately characterized. With the increasing implementation of comprehensive genomic profiling (CGP) in routine oncology practice, real-world evidence (RWE) now enables systematic investigation of these alterations in a pan-cancer context,7 revealing their presence in tumors such as colorectal cancer, melanoma, and neuroendocrine tumors, as indicated by our cohort data.

This investigation transcends mere academic interest. The GNAQ/GNA11 signaling pathway, once deemed undruggable, has become the subject of intense therapeutic development. Novel agents, including the potent PKC inhibitor darovasertib (IDE196), are currently under investigation in clinical trials, demonstrating potential both as monotherapy and in combination strategies.8,9 Consequently, establishing the real-world prevalence and genomic context of these mutations is a critical unmet need for identifying patient populations outside of UM who may benefit from these emerging targeted therapies.

In this study, we used a large-scale RWE cohort of metastatic solid tumors to conduct a comprehensive analysis of GNAQ and GNA11 mutations. Our objectives were as follows: (1) define the pan-cancer distribution and prevalence of these alterations, (2) characterize their specific mutational hotspots, and (3) analyze the associated genomic landscape, including tumor mutational burden (TMB), microsatellite instability (MSI) status, and key comutations, to enhance our understanding of their potential therapeutic implications.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patient Cohort and Data Collection

Patients with metastatic solid tumors who had undergone clinical genomic profiling at the Samsung Medical Center (SMC) between October 2019 and March 2025 were analyzed. All participants provided written informed consent for the research utilization of their data and specimens before testing. The study protocol received approval from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of SMC (IRB# 2019-09-052). Clinical data, including demographic information, definitive histopathologic diagnoses, and treatment histories, were systematically extracted from the institution's electronic medical records.

Specimen Preparation and Nucleic Acid Extraction

Archival formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue blocks served as the source material for all genomic analyses. To ensure sufficient tumor content (a minimum purity of 20%), each specimen was reviewed by a board-certified pathologist, who performed microdissection as necessary. Nucleic acids (both DNA and RNA) were isolated using the AllPrep DNA/RNA FFPE Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), following the manufacturer's protocol. DNA concentration was quantified using a Qubit dsDNA HS assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA), whereas DNA integrity was assessed using the Genomic DNA ScreenTape assay on an Agilent 2200 TapeStation system (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA).

Genomic Profiling and Data Analysis

CGP was performed using two distinct hybrid capture–based next-generation sequencing platforms to identify a broad spectrum of molecular aberrations. All experimental procedures, including library construction, sequencing, and preliminary bioinformatics processing, adhered to standardized clinical diagnostic protocols established by the manufacturers.

The primary evaluation of the cohort was conducted using the Illumina TruSight Oncology 500 (TSO 500) assay, which targets 523 cancer-associated genes.10 A minority of specimens were analyzed using the Oncomine Comprehensive Assay Plus (OCA Plus, Thermo Fisher Scientific),11 which encompasses over 500 genes. Detailed gene lists for both panels are available in Appendix Table A1.

For the TSO 500 pipeline, alignment was performed using BWA-MEM, single-nucleotide variant and insertion/deletion calling were executed using Pisces and Psara, copy number variation (CNV) analysis was performed using CRAFT, and fusion detection was performed using Manta.10 For the OCA Plus pipeline, data processing was conducted using Torrent Suite (v5.18.1), Ion Reporter (v5.20), and Oncomine Reporter (v5.9.2). Both analysis pipelines referenced the GRCh37/hg19 human genome. Identified genomic alterations were annotated using the Ensembl Variant Effect Predictor engine11 and cross-referenced with major public databases (eg, dbSNP, gnomAD, ClinVar, COSMIC). Clinical actionability and oncogenic potential were further evaluated using the OncoKB precision oncology knowledge base.12

TMB was calculated using proprietary algorithms specific to each panel, with TMB-Raider used for TSO 500 and reported as mutations per megabase (Mut/Mb). A TMB of ≥10 Mut/Mb was classified as TMB-High. MSI status was assessed by evaluating instability at microsatellite loci using the Hubble algorithm for TSO 500. An instability score of ≥20% was designated as MSI-High.10

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.1.2). Prevalence and other descriptive statistics were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Differences in categorical data were analyzed using Fisher's exact test or the chi-square test, as appropriate. A P value of <.05 was deemed statistically significant. For patients with available clinical follow-up data, overall survival (OS) was calculated from the date of palliative first-line treatment initiation to the date of death from any cause or last follow-up, with a data cutoff of March 31, 2025. Progression-free survival (PFS) for the melanoma subgroup receiving pembrolizumab was defined as the time from the first administration to the last administration of pembrolizumab. Patients who were alive without documented progression at the data cutoff were censored. Survival curves were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. For exploratory analyses within the GNAQ-/GNA11-mutated cohort, patients were classified into an immunogenic subgroup (TMB-High [≥10 Mut/Mb] or MSI-High) and a nonimmunogenic subgroup (TMB-Low and microsatellite stable [MSS]). Given the limited sample sizes, these survival analyses were considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating.

The genomic landscape of GNAQ-mutated tumors is illustrated in Figure 2B. Frequent coalterations were identified, particularly in NOTCH3 (80%), FAT1 (70%), BARD1 (50%), and BRCA1 (50%). Clinically significant comutations in TP53 (40%) and KRAS (30%) were also detected. In addition, concurrent MYC amplification was observed in two patients.

All 10 identified GNAQ variants were missense mutations. As depicted in the lollipop plot (Fig 2C), these mutations were predominantly located at the canonical G-protein hotspots. The Q209 hotspot (p.Q209L, p.Q209H, p.Q209P) represented the most frequent mutation site, accounting for 40% (n = 4) of all mutations, followed by a mutation at R183 (p.R183Gln). However, a significant divergence was noted when correlating mutation sites with markers of genomic instability. As outlined in Table 2, canonical hotspot mutations (eg, p.Q209) were primarily observed in TMB-Low/MSS tumors (eg, UM), whereas TMB-High/MSI-High tumors (eg, colorectal cancer, renal pelvis tumors) were associated with noncanonical, nonhotspot mutations (eg, p.Gln88His, p.Arg166Cys, p.Ala231V). This suggests that while hotspot mutations serve as primary drivers, nonhotspot variants may emerge as secondary events in hypermutated colorectal or gastric cancers.

Regarding the mutation types (Fig 3C), the spectrum predominantly comprised missense mutations (n = 17) along with a single frameshift deletion. Consistent with findings from the GNAQ cohort, a clear dichotomy was evident between UM and gastrointestinal cancers. Canonical hotspots Q209 and R183 were primarily identified in TMB-Low/MSS UM cases, thereby differentiating them from the nonhotspot mutations (eg, p.Val344Met, p.Leu288Val) observed in the TMB-High/MSI-High gastrointestinal subgroup. Notably, 66.7% of colorectal cancers with GNA11 mutations were classified as MSI-H, suggesting that alterations in GNA11 in these patients likely occur within a framework of global genomic instability rather than functioning as isolated oncogenic drivers.

Exploratory Survival Analysis and Representative Cases of Immunotherapy

To explore the clinical implications of the genomic dichotomy identified in our study, we conducted an exploratory survival analysis within the GNAQ-/GNA11-mutated cohort (Appendix Fig A1). Among 23 patients with evaluable OS data, the immunogenic subgroup (TMB-High or MSI-High; n = 9) demonstrated a numerically favorable OS trend compared with the nonimmunogenic subgroup (TMB-Low and MSS; n = 14), with a median OS of not reached versus 46.0 months (36-month OS: 76.2% v 50.6%; log-rank P = .551). Although not statistically significant, likely because of the limited sample size and low event rate, the direction of this trend is consistent with the known prognostic benefit of TMB-High/MSI-High status in the immunotherapy era.

Cancer type–specific analyses further supported this observation. In a cohort of 136 patients with melanoma receiving pembrolizumab, three patients with GNAQ/GNA11 mutations (all canonical Q209 hotspot mutations in UM, TMB-Low/MSS) showed comparable median PFS with wild-type patients (5.7 v 5.8 months; P = .520), but notably, no mutated patient achieved a durable response beyond 12 months (12-month PFS: 0% v 32.1%). Conversely, among 1,706 patients with colorectal cancer, 9 GNAQ-/GNA11-mutated cases (predominantly nonhotspot variants) showed OS that was not inferior to wild-type (median OS: not reached v 73.1 months; P = .388).

To illustrate the clinical relevance of the immunogenic subgroup, we identified representative cases of patients who received immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs). A patient with MSI-High colorectal cancer (TMB: 61.0 Mut/Mb) harboring a nonhotspot GNA11 p.Val344Met mutation with concurrent MSH2 frameshifts received pembrolizumab as second-line therapy and remained alive without progression at 30.7 months. Similarly, a patient with TMB-High gallbladder cancer (TMB: 64.2 Mut/Mb) harboring two nonhotspot GNA11 mutations (p.Asp319Gly and p.Glu245Lys) received durvalumab plus gemcitabine/cisplatin and was alive at 20.2 months. By contrast, the three UM patients with canonical GNAQ Q209 hotspot mutations all progressed on pembrolizumab (PFS: 3.4, 5.7, and 9.2 months), with none achieving a 12-month PFS. These observations reinforce the distinction between the immunogenic subgroup, where nonhotspot mutations co-occur with high genomic instability and potential ICI benefit, and the canonical hotspot subgroup, which is characterized by limited ICI efficacy.

DISCUSSION

In this comprehensive real-world analysis, we characterized the genomic landscape of GNAQ and GNA11 mutations within a large-scale, pan-cancer cohort. Our study offers two primary insights. First, while GNAQ/GNA11 mutations are established drivers in UM, our findings confirm their role as rare but recurrent oncogenes across a diverse array of solid tumors, particularly in colorectal cancer (38.5% of our cohort) and various rare histologies, such as NET and angiosarcoma. Second, and most critically, tumors harboring non-UM GNAQ/GNA11 mutations exhibited a genomic profile that was markedly different from that of UM tumors, characterized by a high prevalence of TMB-High (42.3%) and MSI-High (19.2%) status.

This immunogenic phenotype (high TMB/MSI) is a significant finding. UM is traditionally considered a cold tumor, displaying low TMB, low PD-L1 expression, and substantial resistance to ICIs.6,13 Our data strongly suggest that this immunologic coldness is not an inherent characteristic of the GNAQ/GNA11 mutation itself but rather a feature of the uveal lineage. By contrast, the GNAQ-/GNA11-mutated colorectal, gastric, and other cancers identified in our cohort, which often present as TMB-High/MSI-High, are prime candidates for immunotherapy.14,15 This underscores the need to interpret the clinical utility of GNAQ/GNA11 as a biomarker within the context of the primary tumor's genomic background, where patients without UM may access therapeutic options, such as ICI therapy, that are typically unavailable for patients with UM.

A key distinction highlighted by our data is the genomic dichotomy between canonical hotspot mutations (Q209, R183) and nonhotspot mutations. Our analysis (Table 2) demonstrates that TMB-Low/MSS tumors, such as UM, almost exclusively harbor these hotspot driver mutations, which are known to cause constitutive activation of the Gαq signaling pathway. Conversely, TMB-High/MSI-High tumors, such as those found in colorectal cancer, frequently present with non-hotspot mutations (eg, p.Gln88His, p.Ala231Val). This pattern suggests that while hotspot mutations are bona fide oncogenic drivers conferring pathway dependency, the nonhotspot mutations observed in hypermutated tumors may represent passenger events arising from global genomic instability rather than primary drivers.16 This distinction holds clinical significance as patients with canonical hotspot mutations are most likely to benefit from targeted PKC inhibitors,8,9 whereas those with nonhotspot, potentially bystander mutations may derive greater benefit from ICI.17

The identification of these mutations outside of UM is increasingly pertinent as targeted therapies, such as the PKC inhibitor darovasertib (IDE196), progress into later-phase clinical trials.8,9 Our findings broaden the potential patient population for these agents beyond UM. However, the complex comutational landscape we observed—including concurrent alterations in TP53 (37.5%-40%), KRAS (30%-31.3%), and PIK3CA (31.3%)—may present challenges. These parallel drivers in key oncogenic pathways indicate that the Gαq axis may not be the sole survival dependency in non-UM histologies. Consequently, monotherapy with Gαq-pathway inhibitors may be constrained by de novo or acquired resistance, highlighting the necessity for rational combination strategies tailored to the specific coalteration profile of each tumor.18,19

Our exploratory survival analysis provides preliminary clinical validation for the genomic dichotomy identified in this study (Appendix Fig A1). Within the GNAQ-/GNA11-mutated cohort, the immunogenic subgroup (TMB-High/MSI-High) demonstrated a numerically superior OS compared with the nonimmunogenic subgroup. Cancer type–specific analyses further supported the clinical relevance of this distinction: in melanoma, GNAQ-/GNA11-mutated patients (canonical hotspot, UM) showed no durable responses to pembrolizumab beyond 12 months, consistent with the established immunotherapy resistance of GNAQ-driven UM. Conversely, in colorectal cancer, GNAQ-/GNA11-mutated patients demonstrated OS that was not inferior to that of wild-type patients. Most compellingly, a patient with MSI-High CRC harboring a nonhotspot GNA11 mutation achieved a durable treatment-free remission after pembrolizumab (partial response over 28 cycles, followed by 21 months off therapy without progression), and a patient with TMB-High gallbladder cancer with nonhotspot GNA11 mutations continues on durvalumab maintenance therapy at 33 months. These cases illustrate the potential for substantial ICI benefit when GNAQ/GNA11 mutations represent passenger events in hypermutated tumors. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously given the small sample sizes and prospective validation in larger cohorts is warranted.

This study has inherent limitations because of its retrospective design and single-center origin. The cohort of 26 patients is relatively small, which restricts the statistical power to draw definitive conclusions regarding specific cancer types. The exploratory survival analyses presented herein are hypothesis-generating and limited by small sample sizes (n = 23 for OS), low event rates, and the absence of systematically collected PFS data for nonmelanoma tumor types. In addition, within the melanoma cohort, all GNAQ-/GNA11-mutated patients were of uveal subtype, creating an inherent confounding between mutation status and tumor lineage that cannot be disentangled in this data set. Furthermore, we lacked access to comprehensive treatment response data (eg, RECIST criteria) beyond treatment duration, which limits the depth of clinical correlation. Prospective studies with larger, multi-institutional cohorts are needed to validate the prognostic and predictive significance of the genomic subgroups identified in this study.

In conclusion, this real-world data analysis demonstrates that activating mutations in GNAQ and GNA11 are not exclusive to UM and are recurrently found in other solid tumors, particularly colorectal cancer. Importantly, we show that the genomic context—specifically the association with TMB and MSI—dictates whether these mutations function as primary drivers or secondary passengers. This underscores the critical need to differentiate canonical hotspot driver mutations from likely bystander mutations present in hypermutated tumors as this distinction has direct implications for selecting patients for targeted pathway inhibitors versus immune checkpoint blockade.20 Our findings advocate for a context-aware approach in precision oncology for patients harboring GNAQ/11 alterations.
