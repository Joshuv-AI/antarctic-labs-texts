Peripheral retinal defocus in children and adolescents with different refractive statuses and its relationship with refractive parameters
Authors: Zejiang Song, Yu Wang, Yuexuan Li, Cuicui Dong, Ying Fu, Dan Wu, Xiaoxia Ding
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWhile the role of peripheral retinal defocus in myopia progression has been established, systematic comparisons of defocus patterns across different retinal regions and refractive statuses remain limited. Furthermore, the specific defocus characteristics that can predict axial length (AL) and spherical equivalent (SE) progression warrant further investigation. This study aimed to characterize the regional distribution of peripheral retinal defocus in children and adolescents with varying refractive statuses using multispectral refractive tomography (MRT), and to evaluate its association with SE and AL.MethodsRetinal defocus values (RDVs) in different eccentricities and quadrants were measured in different refractive groups under cycloplegic conditions using Multispectral Refractive Tomography (MRT). Statistical analysis included group comparisons and correlation analyses between RDVs and SE/AL. Multiple linear regression analyses were performed to analyze the predictors of SE/AL.ResultsThere were statistically significant differences in RDVs and TRDV in various eccentricities and quadrants among the groups. The RDVs in the low and moderate myopia groups were significantly higher than those in the emmetropia group; RDV20-30 in the moderate myopia group was higher than that in the low myopia group. RDV in the vertical direction showed asymmetry in all three groups, while RDV in the horizontal direction showed asymmetry in the emmetropia and low myopia groups. SE was negatively correlated with age, flat keratometry (Kf), steep keratometry (Ks), TRDV, and RDVs in all eccentricities and quadrants. AL was positively correlated with age, TRDV, and RDVs in all eccentricities and quadrants, while being negatively correlated with Kf and Ks. Age, Kf, Ks, RDV0-10, and RDV10-20 were significant predictors of SE; age, Kf, RDV0-10, RDV10-20, and RDV20-30 were significant predictors of AL.ConclusionsRetinal defocus in the vertical direction was observed to exhibit asymmetry across all groups, while the horizontal defocus asymmetry appeared to diminish in the moderate myopia group. Furthermore, RDV0-10, RDV10-20, and RDV20-30 were identified as potential significant predictors of AL.Keywords: Multispectral refractive tomography, Peripheral retinal defocus, Children and adolescents, Myopia

Background

Myopia is a common condition that can impair the visual health of children and adolescents, and it has become a major public health issue that cannot be overlooked [1]. Based on current epidemiological trends, the prevalence rate of myopia among Chinese children and adolescents will continue to increase in the future [2]. While the pathogenesis of myopia is not entirely understood, a growing body of evidence suggests that peripheral retinal defocus is associated with its occurrence and development [3–5]. Retinal defocus values (RDVs) refer to the difference between the spherical equivalent (SE) of a certain area in the peripheral retina and that of the central macular area [6]. Hyperopic defocus in the peripheral retina can induce axial myopia; therefore, evidence suggests that manipulating peripheral retinal defocus can control eye growth and refractive status [7].

Traditionally, open-view autorefractometry has been regarded as the reference standard for measuring peripheral refraction [8]. However, its routine clinical application is limited by the time-consuming nature of point-by-point measurements and the high demand for patient fixation stability [9]. Multispectral refractive tomography (MRT) is a new method that utilizes optical imaging refractive compensation to measure peripheral retinal defocus. This method acquires a sequence of fundus images by sequentially projecting monochromatic light rays of different wavelengths. After lens aberration compensation, computer algorithms are used to perform pixel-by-pixel comparative analysis on the multispectral images, ultimately constructing a visual refractive topographic map. Notably, this examination process is rapid and imposes low requirements on the patient’s fixation stability [10]. This method can measure RDV within an eccentricity range of 0° to 53° [11].

Currently, most studies on peripheral retinal defocus have focused on RDVs at different eccentricities from the posterior pole (15°, 30°, and 45°) [12–15]. However, this approach may not fully capture the complexity of regional retinal defocus, as previous literature indicates that axial elongation induces asymmetric morphological deformation along the horizontal and vertical meridians, leading to uneven defocus patterns across the retina [3, 16, 17]. Moreover, systematic comparisons of peripheral defocus characteristics among different myopia statuses remain scarce. In particular, it is still unclear which specific regional defocus parameters could serve as effective biomarkers for predicting axial length (AL) and spherical equivalent (SE) progression in myopia.

Therefore, this study compared the RDVs of children and adolescents with emmetropia, low myopia, and moderate myopia using MRT. We examined differences in total RDV (TRDV) and its spatial distribution across eccentricities and quadrants among these refractive statuses. By analyzing the relationship between regional peripheral retinal defocus and SE/AL, this study aims to identify the specific retinal zones most closely associated with axial elongation. Ultimately, determining these critical regional targets could provide a theoretical foundation for the clinical design of targeted myopia control interventions, such as defocus-modifying lenses.

Materials and methods

Patient eligibility

The study included 207 eyes from 113 patients at Fushun Eye Hospital between February and March 2025. Inclusion criteria: (1) aged 6–15 years; (2) diopters: -6.00D < SE ≤ + 0.50D [SE = sphere (S) + cylinder (C) / 2]; (3) best corrected visual acuity (BCVA) better than 0 (logMAR); (4) astigmatism ≤ 1.50 D. Exclusion criteria: (1) history of strabismus; (2) history of congenital or acute, chronic ocular disorders; (3) history of wearing orthokeratology lenses; (4) history of ocular surgery potentially affecting refraction or AL; (5) SE > + 0.50D or SE ≤ -6.00D. The included eyes were classified into three groups based on their SE. The classification standards were adopted from the consensus guidelines defined by the International Myopia Institute (IMI) [18]: emmetropia (-0.5D < SE ≤ + 0.50D), low myopia (-3.00D < SE ≤ -0.5D), and moderate myopia (-6.00D < SE ≤ -3.00D).

Ophthalmic examination

All included participants underwent a series of ophthalmic examinations for this study, including slit-lamp microscopy, applanation tonometry, cycloplegic refraction, fundus examination, BCVA, refractive errors, AL and keratometry and MRT.

BCVA was measured using the Standard Logarithmic Visual Acuity Chart, and visual acuity was recorded as logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution (logMAR). Cycloplegia was induced using compound tropicamide eye drops (Xingqi, Shenyang, China), which were administered once every 10 min, for a total of three cycles. Under full cycloplegia, refractive error was measured using an autorefractometer (AR-360 A, NIDEK Co. Ltd., Japan), and the final results were determined by a skilled optometrist. AL and keratometry [flat keratometry (Kf) and steep keratometry (Ks)] were measured using the IOL Master Biometry (Master 500, Zeiss, Germany).

Defocus examination

Peripheral retinal defocus was measured using MRT (MSI C2008P, Shengda Tongze, Shenzhen, China). To strictly control for accommodative factors and ensure stable pupil dilation, all MRT measurements were completed within a 20-minute window immediately following the confirmation of full cycloplegia. Imaging was performed in a darkroom setting using the fast scanning mode, and images with a reliability index ≥ 90% were selected. Retinal defocus within the range of 0°-53° was recorded as TRDV. Retinal defocus values within the eccentricities of 0°-10°, 10°-20°, 20°-30°, 30°-40°, and 40°-53° were calculated by extracting the raw data from the instrument via the Mean Dloop Test system (Fig. 1A), and recorded as RDV0-10, RDV10-20, RDV20-30, RDV30-40, and RDV40-53, respectively. In contrast, the retinal defocus values with eccentricity between 15° and 53°(divided into four quadrants by the 45° and 135° axes) were derived directly from the device’s standard automated output (Fig. 1B), which were recorded as RDV-S, RDV-I, RDV-N, and RDV-T, respectively. To better understand the distribution characteristics of relative peripheral refraction, we included representative defocus topographic maps automatically generated by the MRT to visualize the retinal defocus patterns (Fig. 2). RDV for myopic defocus was negative, while that for hyperopic defocus was positive. The greater the absolute value of RDV, the greater the degree of defocus.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS (version 25.0) statistical software. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov normality test was utilized to assess whether the data were normally distributed. Normal distribution data were presented as mean ± standard deviation, while non-normal data were depicted as median and interquartile range. In the analysis of variance among the three groups, normally distributed data were compared using one-way analysis of variance, with further pairwise comparisons performed using the LSD-t test; non-normally distributed data were compared using the Kruskal-Wallis H test, with further pairwise comparisons conducted using the Mann-Whitney U test. The paired t-test was used to compare the symmetry of peripheral defocus in the vertical direction and horizontal direction within each group. The correlations between biological parameters as well as peripheral defocus and SE, AL were analyzed: Pearson’s and Spearman’s correlation coefficients were used for normally and non-normally distributed data. Multiple linear regression analyses were performed to model the relationships of biological parameters and RDV with SE and AL. A value of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

General characteristics

A total of 113 cases (207 eyes) were included in this study. 97 (46.86%) eyes were from males and 110 (53.14%) eyes from females; 107 (51.70%) eyes were right eyes and 100 (48.30%) eyes were left eyes. The age range was 6–15 years, with a mean of 10.42 ± 1.86 years. The range of SE was − 6.00D < SE ≤ + 0.50D (-1.32 ± 1.03 D). Grouping was performed according to SE, including 37 (17.87%) emmetropic eyes, 155 (74.88%) low myopic eyes, and 15 (7.25%) moderate myopic eyes. The range of AL was 22.56–25.77 mm, with a mean of 24.08 ± 0.66 mm. There were no statistically significant differences in gender, age, eye laterality, Kf, and Ks among the three groups (p > 0.05), while statistically significant differences were found in SE and AL (p < 0.05). In pairwise comparisons, the differences in SE and AL were statistically significant among the groups (p < 0.05) (Table 1).

Discussion

Myopia has become a global public health issue that cannot be ignored. By 2050, approximately 49.8% of the world’s population is projected to suffer from myopia [19]. Animal experimental studies have shown that ocular growth is associated with peripheral retinal defocus, which regulates central refractive development independently of central visual signals [20, 21]. In previous studies, instruments for measuring peripheral retinal defocus, such as autorefractors and wavefront aberrometers, have not been widely used in clinical practice due to their complex operation, labor-intensive nature, and long acquisition times [9]. In recent years, MRT has been adopted to measure peripheral retinal defocus. Compared to traditional measurement methods, MRT does not require subject cooperation with multiple fixation points during imaging. Although the repeatability and accuracy of MRT have been widely established in previous studies [11, 22, 23], we performed the measurements under full cycloplegia and in a darkroom environment to strictly eliminate potential accommodative interference [24]. Additionally, only images with a reliability index ≥ 90% were included in our study.

Benavente-Pérez et al. [7] applied hyperopic and myopic defocus to marmosets respectively and found that peripheral retinal hyperopic defocus is associated with the development of axial myopia, while myopic defocus is associated with the development of axial hyperopia. Consistently, emmetropic eyes usually have relative peripheral myopic defocus, whereas myopic eyes have relative peripheral hyperopic defocus [12, 25]. Our study observed that the TRDV of the emmetropic group showed relative myopic defocus, whereas both myopia groups exhibited relative hyperopic defocus. Wu et al. [26] utilized MRT to analyze peripheral refraction in myopic adults, similarly reporting that myopic eyes exhibit peripheral hyperopic defocus. They [26] investigated local defocus by combining quadrants with specific eccentricity rings, finding that the magnitude of relative peripheral hyperopia increased with eccentricity, and that a significant relative peripheral hyperopic defocus was first observed in the temporal region within the 40° visual field. Our study analyzed eccentricity rings and quadrants as independent variables. Among the RDVs at different eccentricities, it was found that emmetropic eyes all showed relative peripheral myopic defocus, while low myopia and moderate myopia exhibited relative peripheral hyperopic defocus initially at 30°-40° and 20°-30° in the centrifugal direction, respectively. Furthermore, previous studies have demonstrated that RDVs at different eccentricities in the peripheral retina increase with increasing myopia severity [27–29], our study found similar results.

In this study, we analyzed RDVs in different quadrants and observed that all three groups exhibited relative myopic defocus in the superior quadrant, which was consistent with the findings of Zhao et al. [29]; additionally, the magnitude of this relative myopic defocus gradually decreased with increasing myopia severity. In the inferior and nasal quadrants, all three groups exhibited hyperopic defocus, with the magnitude increasing alongside myopia severity. In the temporal quadrant, the emmetropic group showed myopic defocus, whereas the low and moderate myopia groups exhibited hyperopic defocus.

Our study found that vertical defocus showed significant asymmetry in all three groups, this aligns with Wu et al. [30], who also reported significant vertical asymmetry across hyperopic, emmetropic, and myopic populations. In contrast, asymmetry in horizontal defocus was observed in the emmetropic and low myopia groups but appeared to diminish in the moderate myopia group. This loss of asymmetry may be attributed to the temporal quadrant shifting from myopic to hyperopic defocus as myopia progresses, while the nasal quadrant remains in a state of hyperopic defocus [30].

Previous studies have established that myopia progression involves an imbalance in ocular development, exerting a more pronounced impact on peripheral defocus along the horizontal meridian than the vertical meridian [3, 17]. Our results appear to corroborate this finding; the notable loss of horizontal asymmetry in the moderate myopia group, contrasted with the persistent vertical asymmetry across all groups, suggest that the horizontal meridian might be more significantly affected by axial elongation, undergoing significant morphological deformation and hyperopic defocus shifts. The vertical meridian, by comparison, appears structurally more stable and less susceptible to deformation, potentially due to anatomical constraints, such as the mechanical pressure exerted by the eyelids and the tension of the vertical rectus muscles, which restrict ocular expansion in the vertical dimension [16].

The findings of this study align with most existing research [27, 29, 30], namely that SE exhibits a negative correlation with TRDV, while AL shows a positive correlation with TRDV. A previous study found that peripheral defocus in the horizontal direction, especially within the range of NRDV30 (0°-30° eccentricity of RDV-N), has a greater effect on the development of myopia in children [31]. We explored the correlation between RDVs at different eccentricities (0°-10°, 10°-20°, 20°-30°, 30°-40°, and 40°-53°) and quadrants (superior, inferior, nasal, and temporal) and SE/AL. Our study revealed that SE showed a significant negative correlation with all these parameters, while AL showed a significant positive correlation with all these parameters. In this study, we did not find a correlation between horizontal or vertical RDV asymmetry and SE/ AL.

Multivariate linear regression analysis in the present study revealed that RDV0-10 and RDV10-20 are significant predictors of SE and AL, while RDV20-30 is a significant predictor of AL. We rigorously controlled the influence of astigmatism on the results through both study design (excluding astigmatism > 1.50 D) and statistical analysis (including Kf and Ks as covariates). The results suggest that the influence of peripheral retinal defocus appears to be independent of astigmatic variations. Smith et al. [32] demonstrated that the imposed myopic defocus within roughly 20° of the foveal center can slow central axial elongation, and the central 15° range is optimal. Du et al. [11] demonstrated that there is a strong relationship between myopia and the 20°-30° eccentricity annular region. Given the identified predictive value of the 0°–30° eccentricity range for axial elongation, we propose that future optical designs could consider prioritizing inducing myopic defocus within this specific region. This potentially supports the development of lenses with smaller central optical zones to ensure the defocus signal reaches these critical retinal regions [33, 34].

In the schematic model eye described by Escudero-Sanz and Navarro [35], optical field curvature causes the effective focal plane to be located anteriorly in the periphery relative to the foveal area. In an eye with a spherical or oblate retinal surface, this optical phenomenon results in a relative myopic defocus. However, as myopia progresses, the eye typically undergoes axial elongation, causing the posterior retinal surface to transition from an oblate to a less oblate or prolate ellipsoidal shape [16]. This morphological change reduces the distance to the peripheral retina relative to the focal plane or places the retina anterior to the image shell, thereby creating the measured positive (hyperopic) refractive difference, Wu et al. [26] supports the theory in the study of adults with high myopia. Although our regression analysis identified significant associations between peripheral defocus and refractive parameters, the cross-sectional design limits our ability to determine the definitive temporal sequence. Numerous animal studies have confirmed that exposing the peripheral retina to relative myopic or hyperopic defocus can actively modulate eye growth [7, 20].

This study has some limitations. First, the sample sizes among the groups were unevenly distributed, the sample size of the moderate myopia group being relatively small due to the low prevalence and the exclusion of subjects resulting from early intervention, which may affect the statistical analysis, we will expand the sample size in our future study. Second, this study is a cross-sectional study, which makes it impossible to determine causal relationships. While we identified significant predictors for SE/AL, longitudinal data are required to confirm whether manipulating defocus in these specific zones actively slows myopia progression. In subsequent research, we will adopt a prospective study design, include a high myopia group, and conduct follow-up investigations to explore the relationship between peripheral retinal defocus and myopia. Finally, future studies could consider validate MRT against open-field autorefractometry to further strengthen the evidence for its broader clinical application.

Conclusion

In summary, our findings suggest that vertical defocus maintains asymmetry in the emmetropia, low myopia, and moderate myopia groups, while the asymmetry of horizontal defocus appears to diminish as the degree of myopia increases. The peripheral retinal defocus regions at eccentricities of 0°-10°, 10°-20°, and 20°-30° emerged as potential key regulatory targets for myopia progression. These findings could potentially offer a precise regional reference for the future design of defocus-controlled lenses in myopia prevention and control.
