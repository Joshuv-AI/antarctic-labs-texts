Single-nucleus RNA sequencing reveals GABAergic vulnerability and reactive gliosis driven by loss of TDP-43
Authors: Rashmi Thapa, Navin Adhikari, Sishir Gautam, Mingming Sun, Sierra McOmie, Viyaleta Davydzenka, Derek Smith, Jack Syring, Harlie Kaligis, Jade Marie Kosmicki, Rong Chen, Yun Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryTDP-43 is an RNA-binding protein important for RNA processing, whose loss of function is involved in multiple neurodegenerative disorders, including frontotemporal dementia, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, and Alzheimer’s disease (AD). We performed single-nucleus RNA-sequencing to study the convergent and divergent molecular signatures of short-term and long-term TDP-43 depletion in the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) using Tdp-43F/F mice, compared with that of 5xFAD mice, a well-established AD mouse model with β-amyloid plaque pathology. Our results demonstrated a significant loss of GABAergic neurons in the mPFC after short-term TDP-43 depletion. This was accompanied by a remarkable reactive gliosis in the mPFC. Our results revealed a strong GABAergic and glial involvement during early stages of TDP-43 loss of function, suggesting that the GABAergic system is vulnerable to TDP-43 pathology and could be considered a potential target for developing therapeutic strategies and biomarkers for early detection in TDP-43 linked AD-related dementia.Subject areas: neuroscience, transcriptomics

Introduction

TAR DNA binding protein 43 kDa (TDP-43), a highly conserved DNA/RNA binding protein, is an autoregulatory nuclear protein involved in multiple functions of RNA processing and metabolism.1,2 Mislocalization of TDP-43, marked by the appearance of cytoplasmic inclusions enriched with hyperphosphorylated and ubiquitinated TDP-43 along with its nuclear depletion, is a major pathological hallmark in up to 97% of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) cases and around 45% of frontotemporal dementia (FTD) cases.3,4,5,6 Similar TDP-43 proteinopathy has also been identified in up to 57% of cases of Alzheimer’s disease (AD), where it is associated with accelerated memory loss and brain atrophy.7,8

Both gain-of-function and loss-of-function mechanisms have been proposed to contribute to TDP-43-linked neurodegeneration.5,9 Studies from various TDP-43 knockdown and knockout animal models, including worms, flies, fish, and rodents strongly support the loss-of-function mechanism as the major driver of neurodegenerative processes.10,11,12,13,14,15 Notably, a major function of TDP-43 is to repress the incorporation of non-conserved cryptic exons into the matured mRNA, and such cryptic exons have been identified in ALS, FTD, and AD cases linked with TDP-43 proteinopathy.16,17,18,19 Currently, little is known about the convergent and divergent pathogenic roles of the proteins involved in AD-relevant proteinopathies, such as β-amyloid plaque, tauopathy, and TDP-43 proteinopathy.20,21,22

In this study, we performed single-nucleus RNA-sequencing (snRNA-seq) in the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) of Tdp-43F/F mice wherein TDP-43 was depleted for short- and long terms (i.e., 2 and 6 months, respectively), through a focal injection of adeno-associated virus (AAV) delivering Cre-recombinase. Additionally, we also compared these molecular signatures with that of 5xFAD mice, a common AD mouse model enriched with β-amyloid plaque. Our results revealed a unique feature of the vulnerability of GABAergic neurons triggered by TDP-43 loss of function in the mPFC. We also demonstrated a remarkable presence of disease-associated astrocytes and disease-associated microglia following short-term TDP-43 depletion. Together, we concluded that TDP-43 loss of function triggered an early neurodegeneration in GABAergic neurons, accompanied by drastic gliosis including reactive astrocytes, microglia, and oligodendrocytes. Our results highlight vulnerable GABAergic system as a potential biomarker and therapeutic target for early detection and intervention in TDP-43-linked AD and AD-related dementia (ADRD).

Results

Single-nucleus RNA-sequencing reveals transcriptomic profile changes in TDP-43-depleted mPFC

To unravel the molecular signatures of TDP-43 nuclear depletion relevant to neurodegeneration, we performed snRNA-seq in micro-dissected mPFC from Tdp-43F/F mice focally injected with AAV1-CamKII-Cre virus (KO group) or control virus (AAV1-CamKII-GCaMP6f, control group) for 2 months (harvested at 6 months of age) and 6 months (harvested at 13 months of age). We also performed snRNA-seq in the mPFC of 5xFAD mice (harvested at 13 months of age), a common AD mouse model with profound β-amyloid plaque pathology, to identify convergent and divergent pathogenic pathways triggered by different AD-relevant proteinopathies (Figures S1A and S1B).

On average, we captured 2,326 genes and 5,625 reads per nucleus in our samples. Using the clustering method in Seurat,23,24 a total of 37,695 nuclei were clustered into 46 clusters initially and further classified into seven major cell types, based on the expression profiles of known cell-type-specific markers (Figure 1A, also see Figure S1C; Table S1). The most abundant cell type was excitatory neurons (60.59%), followed by inhibitory neurons (15.02%), astrocytes (8.7%), oligodendrocytes (6.22%), microglia (3.39%), and oligodendrocytes precursor cells (OPC, 2.2%) (Figure 1B).

We observed the presence of Cre transcripts in 18.7% of excitatory neurons and 12.36% of inhibitory neurons, accompanied by significantly reduced TDP-43 transcripts in both excitatory and inhibitory neurons (Figure 1C, also see Figure S2A). We further confirmed efficient TDP-43 knockdown of this viral injection approach on protein levels at the mPFC via both immunostaining and western blot with an N-terminal TDP-43 antibody (Figures S2B and S2C).

Surprisingly, besides the neural presence of Cre transcripts, we also detected glial presence of Cre transcripts in 10.61% astrocytes, 9.48% microglia, 5.88% oligodendrocytes, and 8.33% OPCs, despite the fact that our AAV virus was driven by the CaMKII promoter (Figure S2A). Notably, Cre presence in these glial cells was not associated with significant changes in TDP-43 transcripts (Figure 1C). These observations indicated that in our KO mouse models, TDP-43 depletion did not occur exclusively in neurons, but also occurred non-negligibly in glial cells. Given the fact that TDP-43 proteinopathy occurs in both neurons and glial cells of human ALS, FTD, and AD cases, our KO model is a valuable tool to study molecular signatures driven by TDP-43 loss of function in neurons and glial cells.

We performed Nissl staining and set up criteria for nuclei sizes25 to differentiate between neurons and glial cells. We detected a significant neuron loss 6 months following TDP-43 depletion, but not 2 months following TDP-43 depletion (Figure 1D, also see Figure S2D). Interestingly, we detected a significant increase in the number of glial cells as early as 2 months following TDP-43 depletion, suggesting a remarkable reactive gliosis following the short-term TDP-43 depletion. In contrast, the 5xFAD model displayed a significant neuron loss without apparent glial cell proliferation.

Systematic analysis of differentially expressed genes identifies common pathogenic pathways between TDP-43 loss of function and amyloid pathology

To determine how individual cell types were affected by the loss of function of TDP-43, we identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in each cell type and found that, the DEG loads were highest in excitatory neurons, followed by inhibitory neurons in both KO group and 5xFAD mice (Figures S3A; Tables S2, S3, and S4). We observed that neurons displayed a higher proportion of downregulated DEGs, while glial cells displayed a higher proportion of upregulated DEGs (Figures S3B). We also compared our DEGs identified in excitatory neurons driven by TDP-43 loss of function, with the previously published DEG lists identified from bulk RNA-seq of various mouse models and human iPSC-derived motor neurons with dysfunctional TDP-43,1,26,27,28,29 and confirmed that these lists were highly correlated (Figures S3C–S3G).

We next performed gene enrichment analysis to study transcriptomic alterations in cellular pathways of all cell types following 2 and 6 months of TDP-43 depletion, and compared the data with those in the 5xFAD model (Figure 2A, also see Figure S4A). Our transcriptomic analysis demonstrated distinct patterns associated with pathway dysregulation in the two models. Particularly, pathways upregulated by TDP-43 short-term depletion were concentrated in glia, whereas downregulated pathways were pronounced in neurons and astrocytes. The 5xFAD model, on the other hand, displayed a widespread upregulation of pathways in all cell types while downregulation was mostly restricted to neurons.

Importantly, we observed a convergence in downregulated genes associated with both excitatory and inhibitory neurons 2 months following TDP-43 depletion, relevant to several known AD pathogenic pathways. For instance, genes involved in calcium homeostasis were downregulated, including calcium pumps such as Atp2a2 and Atp2b2-4, calcium-binding proteins such as Calm1-3, calcium-signaling downstream effectors such as Camk2a, and calcium permeable channels such as Cacna and Cacnb. We also identified downregulated genes involved in oxidative phosphorylation (i.e., electron transport chain), including various cytochrome c oxidase subunits, such as Cox7c and Cox8a, as well as NADH dehydrogenase subunits, such as Ndufa4 and Ndufb8. Additionally, we demonstrated downregulation in genes relevant to synaptic formation and function, such as Dlg4, Vamp2, Syt13, and Sv2b (Figure 2B; Tables S2, S3, and S4).

We further performed RT-qPCR with additional independent experiments and verified either significant or trajectory downregulation following 2 months of TDP-43 depletion, including genes involved in calcium homeostasis such as Cacna1b, Atp2a2, Calm3, and CamK2a, genes involved in oxidative phosphorylation, including Cox7c and Cox8a, and genes relevant to synaptic formation and function such as Dlg4, Vamp2, Syt13, and Sv2b (Figures 2C–2E). The results between snRNA-seq and RT-qPCR were well correlated (Figure S4B). Disrupted calcium signaling, compromised mitochondrial energy generation, and loss of synapses are previously identified pathogenesis pathways associated with β-amyloid plaque and tauopathy.30,31,32,33,34 Therefore, our analysis suggests that short-term TDP-43 depletion elicits several convergent pathogenic pathways commonly occurring in AD.

Since we previously observed a hyperactive mPFC network following 2 months of TDP-43 depletion and hypoactivity following 6 months of TDP-43 depletion,35 we asked if the expression of c-Fos (an immediate-early gene) changed accordingly upon TDP-43 depletion. Immunostaining of c-Fos in the mPFC following 2 months of TDP-43 depletion displayed a significantly higher expression level, whereas the mPFC from 6 months of TDP-43 depletion and 5xFAD mice showed a lower level of c-Fos (Figure S4C). Increased expression of c-Fos in general is associated with elevated neural activity36,37,38; therefore, the c-Fos staining is consistent to our previous observations of hyperactive mPFC network following 2-month TDP-43 depletion.35

Additionally, we observed a significant increase in caspase-3-positive particles in the mPFC following both 2- and 6 months of TDP-43 depletion (Figure S5A), consistent with the previous findings that TDP-43 proteinopathy led to mitochondrial dysfunction and apoptosis.39,40 Analysis of apoptosis related genes with our snRNA-seq data revealed distinct profiles between the TDP-43 KO and 5xFAD models (Figure S5B). Both excitatory and inhibitory neurons in 5xFAD samples displayed a general suppression of apoptosis signaling, with downregulation in both pro-apoptosis and anti-apoptosis genes. However, neurons from TDP-43 KO model displayed a complex profile with mixed upregulation and downregulation in pro-apoptosis and anti-apoptosis genes.

We next looked into the ubiquitin proteins whose homeostasis were often altered in ALS/FTD and AD models,6,41,42,43 and found significantly increased ubiquitin b (Ubb)-positive particles in the mPFC after 2 and 6 months of TDP-43 depletion (Figure S5C). Analysis in ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS) genes with our snRNA-seq data (Figure S5D) revealed distinct profiles between the two models. Similarly, neurons in 5xFAD samples mainly displayed downregulation of UPS genes. In contrast, inhibitory neurons in the TDP-43 KO model displayed mainly downregulated UPS genes, whereas excitatory neurons displayed a complex profile with mixed upregulation and downregulation of UPS genes. These observations likely reflected a combinatorial effect of activated pathogenic pathways and insufficient compensatory pathways in the TDP-43 KO model.

Short-term TDP-43 depletion evokes GABAergic neurodegeneration

We identified several subclasses of GABAergic neurons in our samples, including Sst, Vip, Pvalb, Sv2b, lysosome associated membrane protein 5 (Lamp5), and Sv2c-expressing inhibitory neurons (Figures S6A and S6B). Interestingly, snRNA-seq analysis demonstrated either a significant or trajectory downregulation at transcript levels of Pvalb, Sst, Vip, and Lamp5, following 2 and 6 months of TDP-43 depletion (Figure 3A). Similarly, 5xFAD also displayed significantly or trajectorially downregulated Pvalb, Sst, and VIP at transcript levels.

We then performed immunostaining, RNAscope, and RT-qPCR assays to verify these DEGs at either transcription or protein levels with additional independent experiments. Immunostaining revealed significantly lower numbers of Pvalb- and Sst-positive particles within viral infected mPFC areas, following 2 and 6 months of TDP-43 depletion (Figure 3B). In contrast, immunostaining did not reveal any changes in Pvalb- and Sst-positive particles in viral non-infected brain areas (Figures S6C and S6D). RNAscope assay demonstrated significantly reduced Gad1 (glutamic acid decarboxylase) and Lamp5 transcripts in the mPFC following both 2 and 6 months of TDP-43 depletion, when compared with controls (Figure 3C). RT-qPCR verified the reduced transcripts of VIP upon TDP-43 depletion (Figure S6E). We further performed western blots and observed significantly or trajectorially reduced Sst and Gad65 at protein levels (Figures S6F and S6G).

We next performed experiments to distinguish between the two scenarios, whether the lowered numbers of Pvalb and Sst immunostaining particles were due to the actual loss of inhibitory neurons, or due to the downregulation of these proteins below the detectable threshold. We utilized a Cre-dependent red fluorescent protein to label inhibitory neurons in the mPFC of GAD2-IRES-Cre knock-in mice (Jax #010802), where Cre expresses exclusively in GABAergic neurons,44 followed by knocking down TDP-43 in the same brain region using a CRISPR-Cas9 mediated viral tool (Figure 4A). Particularly, we first injected AAV1-CAG-DIO-NLS-tdTomato bilaterally in the mPFC of the GAD2-IRES-Cre mice to label inhibitory neurons with tdTomato. This was followed by a second viral injection 14 days later, with AAV9-hSyn-SaCas9-U6-mTDP-43-gRNA6 virus into the same region to knockdown TDP-43 in neurons, or AAV9-hSyn-SaCas9-U6-ctl-gRNA virus as a control. The efficacy of TDP-43 knockout by AAV9-hSyn-SaCas9-U6-mTDP-43-gRNA6 virus was ∼93% (Figure S6H). We next compared the densities of tdTomato-positive particles between the TDP-43-depleted and TDP-43-intact groups (Figure 4B). We demonstrated a significantly lower number of tdTomato-labeled inhibitory neurons in the TDP-43-depleted mPFC compared with that of control. We further confirmed the loss of Pvalb and Sst interneurons in these samples by immunostaining (Figures 4B and 4C). We therefore concluded that GABAergic neurons were vulnerable to TDP-43 depletion and GABAergic neurodegeneration occurred as early as 2 months after TDP-43 depletion. The early loss of GABAergic neurons might cause disinhibition of the mPFC microcircuit, potentially explaining the early hyperactivity we previously observed upon short-term loss of TDP-43.35

Importantly, immunostaining of Pvalb and Sst in the mPFC of 5xFAD mice did not detect any apparent loss of inhibitory neurons (Figure S7A). In other words, inhibitory neurons in the 5xFAD model might be dysfunctional (e.g., lower GABAergic marker genes at transcript levels), but were still alive. We confirmed that TDP-43 expression was normal in 5xFAD mice at protein levels (Figures S7B and S7C). These observations strongly indicated that GABAergic neurodegeneration was uniquely triggered by TDP-43 depletion. To examine at the molecular level how GABAergic vulnerability was associated with TDP-43 depletion, we compared DEGs in the 2-month TDP-43-depleted inhibitory neurons with those of 5xFAD, where the inhibitory neurons were spared. We compared the common and distinct upregulated and downregulated DEGs between these two samples (Figure S7D), and identified ten genes (i.e., Syt1, Erbb4, Ctnna3, Slc2a13, Meg3, Pde10a, Tenm1, Nrg3os, Alk, and Sox2ot) that were upregulated in 5xFAD but downregulated 2 months following TDP-43 depletion. Among these genes, Erbb4, Nrg3os, Alk, and Tenm1 have been reported to be positively associated with inhibitory synapse formation, pro-survival, and neuroprotective signaling.45,46,47,48,49,50 We reasoned that upregulation of these genes might be protective for GABAergic neurons in the 5xFAD model.

We further performed gene enrichment analysis for the 295 unique upregulated genes in the 5xFAD model and the 299 unique downregulated genes in the 2-month TDP-43-depleted samples and focused on their cellular pathways (Figure S7E). We noticed that many upregulated pathways in GABAergic neurons of the 5xFAD model (e.g., regulation of GABAergic synaptic transmission, synapse assembly and transmission, axon formation, and potassium and sodium ion transport) were downregulated in GABAergic neurons following 2-month TDP-43 depletion. The downregulation in these pathways could have led to the vulnerability of GABAergic neurons following TDP-43 depletion, whereas upregulation in these pathways could have rendered resistance in GABAergic neurons of the 5xFAD model.

Prominent gliosis following TDP-43 depletion

In many neurodegenerative disorders, including AD and ALS, glial cells undergo a dramatic transformation in their transcriptional program to upregulate multiple genes. Particularly, disease-associated astrocytes (DAAs) and diseases-associated microglia (DAM) are formed in an early stage of disease progression, evidenced by increased glial cell numbers, increased expression of disease-associated markers, and disrupted homeostasis.51,52,53,54,55 We noticed that most glial DEGs in TDP-43-depleted mPFC were upregulated (Figure S3B).

Gene ontology analysis of astrocytic DEGs revealed that most enriched pathways were involved in synaptic organization and signaling (Figure 2A), suggesting that reactive astrocytes may contribute to synaptic deficits in TDP-43-depleted mPFC. Reactive astrocytes in neurodegenerative disorders and central nervous system injuries can be categorized into two specific forms, A1 and A2. A1 astrocytes are destructive for synapses whereas A2 astrocytes display a protective nature.56,57 We therefore investigated the presence of A1 and A2 forms of reactive astrocytes upon TDP-43 depletion, based on the module score for inflammatory A1 and neuroprotective A2 markers. We found a significantly increased gene signature score for A1 as well as PAN-reactive astrocytes58,59 following both 2 and 6 months of TDP-43 depletion, similar to that of 5xFAD mice (Figure 5A). In contrast, the scores for A2 astrocyte gene signatures remained unchanged upon TDP-43 depletion. Additionally, astrocytes following TDP-43 depletion induced elevation in disease-associated genes, such as Gfap, Clu, Vim, Osmr, and Serpina3n (Figure S8A). Although the overall homeostasis gene score was slightly decreased following TDP-43 depletion (Figure 5A), some homeostasis genes (e.g., Nrxn1, Nrg3, Gpc5, and Erbb4) showed increased transcripts, while others (e.g., Ntl and Dclk1) showed decreased transcripts (Figure S8A, also see Tables S2 and S3).

We confirmed an increase in the number of GFAP-positive glial cells by immunostaining upon TDP-43 depletion for 2 and 6 months, similar to that of 5xFAD mice (Figure 5B, also see Figures S8B and S8C). We further verified the prevalent presence of Vimentin (Vim), a DAA and reactive astrocyte marker, in TDP-43-depleted mPFC (Figure 5B, also see Figures S8D and S8E). Increased GFAP and Vim immunostaining were also found in viral non-infected areas of TDP-43 KO model (Figures S8B and S8D), indicating the widespread of reactive astrocytes. Excessive GFAP-positive reactive astrocytes also focally appeared in the mPFC of GAD2-IRES-Cre mice injected with TDP-43 CRISPR-Cas9 virus (Figure S8F). We performed re-clustering analysis on astrocytes and obtained five subclusters (Figure S8G). We investigated the expression of A1, A2, and homeostasis astrocyte marker genes in each subcluster following 2 months of TDP-43 depletion and observed that A1 and disease-associated astrocyte markers were mostly upregulated in subcluster 2, where the homeostatic genes were largely downregulated (Figures S8H and S8I).

Similar to astrocytes, microglia can also undergo polarization into M1 and M2 states, where M1 state is pro-inflammatory (neurotoxic) and M2 state is anti-inflammatory (neuroprotective).60 We next investigated M1 classically activated and M2 alternatively activated microglial signatures61 and demonstrated a significantly increased expression in M1 microglial gene signatures in both the 2- and 6-month TDP-43-depleted groups, but not in 5xFAD mice (Figure 6A, upper panel). In contrast, M2 microglial gene signatures remained unchanged. The homeostatic genes were significantly downregulated following 6 months of TDP-43 depletion that was similar to 5xFAD, but not 2 months of TDP-43 depletion (Figure 6A, middle panel). Additionally, we explored a different spectrum of microglial activation to characterize their property.62 We found that microglial cells were adapted with a significantly elevated expression in phagocytic, interferon 1-releasing, and antigen-presenting properties just following 2 months of TDP-43 depletion (Figure 6A, bottom panel). Particularly, we detected upregulated DAM genes, including Ifi204, H2-K1, H2-D1, Oasl2, Lyz2, B2M, and Apoe; and downregulated homeostatic genes, such as P2ry12, Cx3cr1, and Tmem119 in both 2- and 6-month KO groups. The upregulation of DAM genes and downregulation of homeostatic genes seemed more prominent in the 6-month KO group compared to other experimental groups (Figure S9A).

With Iba1 immunostaining, we confirmed a significantly increased number of activated microglial cells in both the 2- and 6-month TDP-43-depleted groups (Figure 6B). Excessive microglial cells were also detected in the mPFC of GAD2-IRES-Cre injected with TDP-43 CRISPR-Cas9 virus (Figure S9B). Additionally, we performed immunostaining for MHC-I molecules (H2-D1 and H2-K1) and demonstrated significantly increased particles following 2 months of TDP-43 depletion (Figure 6C). We also observed the colocalization of MHC-I and Iba1 (Figure S9C), and concluded that microglia was the primary source of MHC-I signal upon TDP-43 depletion. We performed RT-qPCR and confirmed that the DAM marker genes such as Itgax, Tyrobp, Ctsd, Cst7, and Oasl2 were significantly increased following 2-month TDP-43 depletion (Figure 6D). Similarly, C1qa (released by M1 class of microglia) and Gbp2 (a marker of M1 and interferon producing microglia) were also confirmed to be trajectorially increased following 2-month TDP-43 depletion (Figure S9D).

Reactive astrocytes and microglia are known to secrete various cytokines, chemokines, and extracellular matrix molecules that affect neighboring astrocytes, microglia, oligodendrocytes, and other immune cells.63,64,65 We compared transcripts of secreted inflammatory molecules among Cre+ and Cre− astrocytes and microglia in 2-month TDP-43-depleted mPFC, with those of the control group. We observed that Cre+ astrocytes and microglia exhibited higher levels of transcripts for several secreted inflammatory molecules than Cre− cells and controls (Figure S9E). Particularly, Cre+ astrocytes showed highest transcripts in C1qb, Ccl5, Tgfa, C4b, C7, Il4, and Vegfb, and Cre+ microglia displayed highest transcripts in C1qa, Cxcl10, Tgfa, C4a, C4b, and C7. These observations suggest that although TDP-43-depleted astrocytes and microglia (i.e., Cre+ cells) represent only ∼10% of glial population, they could be powerful to promote exaggerated gliosis by releasing various inflammatory factors to influence their neighboring TDP-43-intact glial cells.

Oligodendrocyte is another glial cell type that is severely affected in neurodegenerative disease and displays inflammatory response.66,67 Upon comparing the DEGs in oligodendrocytes following 2 and 6 months of TDP-43 depletion, we noticed that the inflammatory response genes, such as complement component (C4b), B2M, histocompatibility 2, and D and K region locus 1 (H2-D1 and H2-K1), were more prominently upregulated following 6 months of TDP-43 depletion, than that of 2 months, reflecting an extensive inflammatory response in oligodendrocytes following 6 months of TDP-43 depletion (Figure S10A). We verified increased Olig2-positive particles following 2 and 6 months of TDP-43 depletion via immunostaining (Figure 7A), suggesting that TDP-43 depletion led to increased oligodendrogliosis. We also observed increased B2M and C4b staining following 2 and 6 months of TDP-43 depletion (Figures 7B and 7C, also see Figures S10B and S10C), consistent with the previous observations of enhanced immune signaling and neuroinflammation in AD models.68,69,70 We performed co-immunostaining of B2M and C4b with Olig2, an oligodendrocyte marker (Figures S10D and S10E). Our results suggested that oligodendrocytes were the most prominent sources of B2M and C4b 6 months following TDP-43 depletion. Therefore, oligodendrocytes were among the cell-types displaying the most exaggerated inflammatory responses upon TDP-43 depletion. We summarized the upregulation of various marker genes for neuroinflammation upon TDP-43 depletion (Figures S10F and S10G).

Pathogenic model upon TDP-43 depletion in the mPFC

To confirm the relevance of DEGs identified from TDP-43-depleted mouse models, we compared our DEGs with the published studies obtained from bulk RNA-sequencing of ALS samples71 (Figure S11A), as well as, single cell and snRNA-seq on human FTD and AD brains55,72,73 (Figures S11B–S11D). Correlation analysis supported that our TDP-43 KO mouse model recapitulated many transcriptomic alterations similar to human neurodegenerative diseases.

Based on our observations, we propose that in TDP-43 proteinopathy-linked ADRD, TDP-43 dysfunction does not act purely as a cell-autonomous process, but rather a coordinated symphony of pathogenic cascade driven by changes in different types of brain cells. We propose a three-stage pathogenic cascade upon TDP-43 nuclear depletion in the mPFC (Figure 8): (1) GABAergic collapse stage: short-term TDP-43 nuclear depletion led to selective loss of cortical inhibitory neurons, which causes disinhibition in the mPFC microcircuit and contribute to the early network hyperactivity; (2) sustained gliosis stage: both the short- and long-term TDP-43 nuclear depletion evoke cortical glial cells transition into a proliferative and inflammatory state releasing inflammatory molecules that actively prune synapses; and (3) excitatory neuron death stage: ultimately the intrinsic cellular stress, chronic neuroinflammation, and excitotoxicity led to neurodegeneration of cortical pyramidal neurons upon long-term TDP-43 depletion.

Discussion

Our snRNA-seq analysis with Tdp-43F/F mice demonstrated a three-stage pathogenic cascade triggered by TDP-43 nuclear depletion across different cell types in the mPFC. Compared to the 5xFAD mouse model with a profound β-amyloid plaque pathology, TDP-43 loss of function uniquely triggered an early loss of GABAergic neurons and a sustained glial proliferation. We propose that TDP-43 loss of function drives an initial GABAergic neurodegeneration and a sustained reactive gliosis, followed by loss of pyramidal neurons at late stages.

Vulnerable GABAergic system following short-term TDP-43 depletion

We observed an early loss of GABAergic neurons triggered by TDP-43 depletion. We reasoned that loss of GABAergic neurons would contribute to the network hyperactivity as we previously observed.35 Hyperexcitability and excitatory/inhibitory imbalance have been found as an early hallmark of neurodegenerative diseases. The primary cause of hyperexcitability has been suggested to be the sustained impairments and/or defects in GABAergic neurons.74,75,76,77 Studies in drosophila models have associated TDP-43 depletion with the reduced expression in Gad1 and excitotoxicity.78,79 Importantly, our studies with Tdp-43F/F mice demonstrated not only the reduced expression in GABAergic markers (GAD67 and Lamp5 transcripts), but also a significant loss of GABAergic neurons as early as 2 months following TDP-43 depletion, highlighting GABAergic vulnerability driven by TDP-43 loss of function. Additionally, excitatory neurons exhibited defects in calcium homeostasis (e.g., downregulated Atp2a2, a sarcoendoplasmic reticulum calcium ATPase), which could lead to the accumulation of excessive cytoplasmic free Ca2+ ions. Together, these findings support that early network hyperactivity occurs upon TDP-43 depletion,35 as a combinatorial result of both the disinhibition due to loss of GABAergic neurons and intrinsic dysfunctions in excitatory neurons.

Loss of specific inhibitory neurons (e.g., Pvalb-, Sst-, and calretinin-positive interneurons) has been previously reported in various AD animal models and AD patients.80,81,82,83,84,85,86,87,88 The vulnerability of Sst-positive interneurons in neurological disorders has been suggested to occur because of defects in protein translation process, worsening in unfolded protein response or endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress.89,90 Conversely, the vulnerability of Pvalb-positive interneurons has been explained by their unique energy requirement to support a high metabolic rate, making them more susceptible to mitochondrial dysfunctions.91 Lamp5, which supports interneuron survival and GABAergic neurotransmission, is reduced in AD mouse models.92,93 These previous reports align well with our observations that Lamp5 was reduced besides loss of Pvalb- and Sst-positive inhibitory neurons, driven by the TDP-43 loss of function.

Sustained gliosis and neuroinflammation induced by TDP-43 depletion

We observed a remarkable glial proliferation, as measured by cresyl violet staining, associated with TDP-43 nuclear depletion but not in 5xFAD mice brains. 5xFAD mouse model exhibits neuroinflammation and gliosis.94 Multiple studies suggest that in 5xFAD mice brains, β-amyloid plaque-induced changes are primarily reflected in glial morphology, phenotype, and transcriptome ultimately leading to their disease-associated states.51,52,56,95 Additionally, these disease-associated astrocytes and microglia are mostly surrounding β-amyloid plaques.51,52

Reactive astrogliosis is a well-documented hallmark in various neurodegenerative diseases.96,97,98 Astrogliosis can be triggered by TDP-43 depletion in neurons29,35 or astrocytes.99,100 Consistent with these previous reports, our TDP-43 KO mouse model exhibited a prominent gliosis and neuroinflammation, evidenced by drastically increased numbers of glial cells in TDP-43-depleted mPFC, as well as, activation of astrocytes, microglia, and oligodendrocytes. Given that our CaMKII-driven Cre recombinase had sparse (∼10%) expression in glial cells, we propose that prominent gliosis associated with TDP-43 depletion in the mPFC might be a combinatorial consequence of both the indirect effect triggered by the loss of inhibitory neurons as well as the direct response from the loss of TDP-43 in a small proportion of glial cells.

Microglia is the resident immune cells in the nervous system acting as primary responders to neural injury.101 In neurodegenerative diseases, microglial cells get activated and manifest disease-associated phenotype that further exacerbates neuroinflammation.52,102 Proinflammatory M1 Microglia is known to release cytokines such as TNF- α, IL-1α, and C1q, which are necessary and sufficient to elicit A1 or inflammatory astrocyte phenotype.56,57,103 In our study, we observed microgliosis and DAM phenotype induced by TDP-43 depletion, where these microglial responses were more aggressive after 6 months of TDP-43 depletion. A similar transition of normal microglial to DAMs has also been observed in Grn−/− mice, an FTD mouse model with TDP-43 proteinopathy.104 Additionally, we also observed increased MHC molecules in glial cells after TDP-43 depletion. Expression of MHC molecules by astrocytes and microglia is a defining feature of phenotypic states in DAA and DAM, where MHC-I expression could serve as a potential biomarker of senescence and could mediate CD8+ T cell infiltration resulting in brain atrophy and neurodegeneration.69,105,106

Oligodendrocytes, another class of glial cells, have also been shown to adopt a disease-associated phenotype in AD progression, engaging in immune response and responding to activated astrocytes and microglia.66,107 In our study, TDP-43 depletion resulted in elevated expression of immune-related genes in oligodendrocytes, an effect that became more pronounced after 6 months of TDP-43 depletion. We observed an increased expression of C4b in all the glial cells but most prominent in oligodendrocytes. This complement molecule could play a role in synaptic destruction, as many classical complement cascade components, such as C1q, C4b, and C3, are known to mediate synapse pruning and elimination in neurodegenerative diseases.68,108,109

Common pathogenic mechanisms for neurodegeneration

We observed several key disruptions in neurons upon TDP-43 depletion, including dysfunctional calcium signaling, compromised mitochondrial, and disrupted synaptic formation/function. These are common pathogenic pathways previously identified in AD models with β-amyloid plaque and tauopathy.30,31,32,33,34 Excessive levels of Ca2+ influx followed by a sustained intracellular calcium overload in neurons can lead to excitotoxicity and activation of caspases important for apoptosis and autophagy in multiple models of neurodegenerative disorders.31,110,111

Additionally, alterations in calcium binding proteins which usually act as calcium buffers and calcium sensors, such as neuronal calcium sensor-1, calretinin, calbindin D-28, parvalbumin, and calmodulin, as well as, alterations in calcium channels and calcium pumps, can compromise calcium homeostasis, synaptic plasticity, and neurotransmitter release within neurons.110,111,112,113 Mitochondria also plays an important role in calcium homeostasis in the cytosol. Mitochondrial calcium overload contributes to oxidative stress, decreased bioenergetics, and impaired oxidative phosphorylation and ATP synthesis, ultimately leading to apoptosis.114,115

Synapse damage, dysfunctions, and loss of synaptic proteins are common hallmarks of many neurodegenerative disorders, including AD, ALS, and FTD contributing to cognitive decline.33,116,117 TDP-43 proteinopathy has also been directly associated with impairment in learning, long-term potentiation, synaptic transmission, and synaptic plasticity.118,119,120 Comparison of the DEGs identified from TDP-43 depletion samples with the DEGs from previously published single-cell RNA-sequencing studies of human ALS, FTD, and AD samples revealed multiple commonly altered pathogenic pathways.26,27,55,72 The pathways disrupted in neurons were relevant to synaptic transmission, synapse assembly, organization, Golgi to ER retrograde traffic, GABA synthesis/release/reuptake/degradation, and apoptosis. In astrocytes, we observed alterations in pathways related to trans-synaptic signaling, activation of NMDA receptors, glutamate binding and activation, and Jak-stat signaling pathway. The upregulation of Jak-stat signaling genes in astrocytes might indicate astrocyte differentiation, proliferation, and reactivity.121,122 These common changes in microglia were related to immune response and cytokine production while that of oligodendrocytes were related to microtube binding, protein polymerization, and protein localization.

In summary, our results revealed that TDP-43 loss of function drives a distinct pathological cascade initiated by an early GABAergic neurodegeneration and a sustained reactive gliosis, followed by loss of pyramidal neurons at late stages. Compared with the 5xFAD mouse model, early inhibitory neuron loss coupled with proliferative gliosis is a unique signature of TDP-43-driven pathology. These observations strongly suggest that GABAergic and glial systems are not merely involved, but are primary drivers in TDP-43-linked ADRD, highlighting early therapeutic window centered on inhibitory neurotransmission and glial modulation.

Limitations of the study

Some limitations should be considered for interpretation of our results. One of the obvious caveats of 10× Genomics Chromium protocol is the strong 3′-end bias and sparse transcript coverage in the data. It is also important to note that the sequencing of nuclei, rather than cells, may hinder the ability to detect DEGs. Another factor to consider when interpreting our data is the possibility of different RNA targets for TDP-43 between human and mouse, due to interspecies variations in the transcriptomes, since TDP-43 binds to long GU-rich repeats in nuclear RNA introns to regulate the RNA splicing process. Additional limitations in this study include all snRNA-seq samples being obtained from male mice, the age differences among the short- and long-term TDP-43 KO mice, and the primary focus on the mPFC region of mouse brains that might not recapitulate pathological changes occurring in other brain regions relevant for FTD and ALS. Lastly, our study represents the pathological mechanisms for TDP-43 loss of function without considering the toxic gain-of-function mechanisms due to cytoplasmic TDP-43 aggregation. Nevertheless, our study reveals important initial cell-type-specific changes following TDP-43 loss of function and highlights the vulnerability of GABAergic neurons in TDP-43-linked neurodegenerative diseases.

Method details

Stereotaxic virus injection and tissue preparation for snRNA seq

AAV1-CaMKII-Cre, AAV1-CAG-Flex-GCaMP6f, and AAV1-CaMKII-GCaMP6f viruses (2.0–2.8 x 1013 GC/mL) were purchased from Addgene. AAV9-hSyn-SaCas9-U6-mTDP-43-gRNA6 virus (1 × 1013 GC/mL) was custom-built in PackGene. The sequence of mTDP-43-gRNA6 is CCCGTGTCTCAGTGTATGAG. AAV9-hSyn-SaCas9-U6-ctl-gRNA virus (1 × 1013 GC/mL) was purchased from PackGene.

Viral injection was performed following the previously published protocol.123 To study the short-term (i.e., 2-month duration) and long-term (i.e., 6-month duration) knockout (KO) effect of TDP-43, Tdp-43F/F male mice (3–4 months old of age) were injected with AAV1-CaMKII-Cre (1:2 diluted in saline, for short-term KO samples), and Tdp-43F/F male mice (6–7 months of age) with AAV1-CaMKII-Cre+AAV1-CAG-Flex-GCaMP6f (long-term KO sample), bilaterally in the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) (coordinates are: A/P, 1.94 mm; M/L, 0.6 mm; D/V, 1.75 mm with reference to bregma). C57BL/6J male mice aged at 3 months were injected with AAV1-CaMKII-Cre (1:2 diluted in saline) and Tdp-43F/F mice (6–7 months of age) without any viral injection served as short-term control samples. Tdp-43F/F mice injected with AAV1-CaMKII-GCaMP6f (6–7 months of age, 1:2 diluted in saline) served as long-term control sample. These mice were harvested at either 2-month (short-term) or 6-month (long-term) following viral injection. 5xFAD mice (both carrier and non-carrier) aged at 13 months were utilized in our study as additional AD model controls. The details about mice age and numbers for each experimental group can be found in Figure S1A.

For CRISPR viral injection, GAD2-IRES-Cre knock-in mice (Jax #010802) at 3 months were injected with AAV1-CAG-DIO-NLS-tdTomato bilaterally in the mPFC. Two weeks later, AAV9-hSyn-SaCas9-U6-mTDP-43-gRNA6 virus or AAV9-hSyn-SaCas9-U6-ctl-gRNA virus were injected bilaterally into the same brain region. These mice were harvested after 2-month following CRISPR viral injection.

To collect the fresh tissues for snRNA-seq, mice were euthanized in CO2 chamber and mice brains were immediately dissected out. Using a vibratome, mice brains were freshly sliced into 200–400 μm thickness and the mPFC region was dissected out utilizing fine forceps under microscope. Four to six mPFC tissues (40–60 mg) were combined and immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and shipped to SingulOmics with dry ice for snRNA-sequencing. Brain harvesting and snRNA-sequencing were performed in two batches. The first batch comprised of four samples harvested following 2-month viral injection, including 2 duplicated KO samples (4 Cre injected Tdp-43F/F mice per sample) and 2 control samples (Cre injected C57BL6J and non-viral injected Tdp-43F/F, 4 mice per sample). The second batch comprised of four samples harvested following 6-month viral injection, including KO (4 Cre injected Tdp-43F/F mice), control (4 control viral injected Tdp-43F/F mice), 5xFAD positive (5 transgene carrier mice) and 5xFAD negative (6 transgene non-carrier mice).

Single-nucleus RNA-sequencing

The frozen brain tissues that were shipped to SingulOmics were subjected to nuclei isolation and 3′ single cell gene expression libraries (Next GEM v3.1) were constructed using the 10× Genomics Chromium system. The libraries were sequenced with ∼200 million PE150 reads per sample on a Illumina NovaSeq 6000 instrument, and reads were subsequently processed using 10× Genomics Cell Ranger analytical pipeline (v6.0.1) and mouse mm10 reference with the addition of the Cre gene. Dataset aggregation was performed using the cellranger aggr function normalizing for the total number of confidently mapped reads across libraries.

Quality control and data analysis using Seurat package

We utilized the workflow described in Seurat guided analysis for integrative analysis.23,24 We used a prefilter to filter out the genes that appeared in less than 1% of nuclei in each sample. Next, we filtered out the nuclei with the lowest 10% (unhealthy nuclei), highest 1% nFeature_RNA (i.e., number of observed genes; may be doublets), and expressing a high level of mitochondrial gene (≥10%). We detected several mitochondrially encoded genes in the samples and removed them. We normalized each sample separately and then identified the highly variable features using the selection method “vst” and 2000 nFeatures. To integrate all 8 samples, we identified the anchor set among the samples using FindIntegrationAnchors function with 30 dimensions and integrated the samples using dimensions 1–30. We next scaled the integrated matrix and ran PCA in the integrated objected with 30 dimensions. We performed k-nearest neighbor clustering using FindClusters function and initially obtained 46 clusters. The markers for each cluster were determined using Seurat’s FindAllMarkers function. We assigned the cell-type identity to each cluster based on the expression of known cell-type marker genes (Table S1) and found major 6 cell types (Endothelial and unknown cells were very few in proportion and disregarded for further analysis). Using FindMarkers function, we identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs) for each cell type for 2-month TDP-43 KO, 6-month TDP-43 KO and 5xFAD groups (Tables S2, S3, and S4). We utilized AddMouduleScore function to calculate and compare the geneset expression score between two conditions (KO vs. Control) within a group. We re-clustered the inhibitory neurons and astrocytes using the resolution of 0.1 and 0.3 respectively for FindClusters function. We also performed UMAP clustering using RunUMAP function with 10 dimensions.

Pathway analysis

The gene enrichment analysis was performed using Metascape v3.5 custom analysis using Gene Ontology Biological Process (GO:BP) database.124 We used Min overlap: 3, P-Value Cutoff: 0.01, Min Enrichment: 1.5 for Metascape. All the genes with adjusted p-values less than 0.05 and log2 fold change (log2FC) > 0.1 were taken for pathway analysis for Figure 2A. For Figure S6B, all the significant genes were considered. The common DEGs were obtained by finding the overlapping significant DEGs (adjusted p-value <0.05) between the two datasets. The Venn diagram of overlapping genes was created using Venny 2.1 (https://bioinfogp.cnb.csic.es/tools/venny/index.html).

Selection of differentially expressed genes (DEGs)

For all Gene enrichment pathway analysis, we used DEGs with p-values significant genes only, with log2FC = 0.1 as a threshold. For all correlation comparisons with previously published data, we used p-value significant (i.e., adjusted p < 0.05) genes, but with no log2FC threshold. We have provided the entire transcriptomic comparison for both p-value significant and non-significant genes and with no log2FC threshold in our Tables S2, S3, and S4.

Western Blots

To harvest viral non-injected brain samples (i.e., 5xFAD and C56BL/6J mice), frontal cortical regions were separated immediately after euthanasia. To harvest viral-injected samples, mice brains were freshly sliced into 200–400 μm thickness and the mPFC region was dissected out utilizing fine forceps under microscope. Mice brain samples were collected into 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tubes and freshly frozen in liquid Nitrogen and stored at −80°C freezer. To prepare the lysates, mice brain samples were resuspended in 40× volume of NP40 cell lysis buffer (FNN0021, Fisher Scientific) with a protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche cOmplete PIC 04693116001), homogenized and incubated on ice for 1 h with vortexing every 15 min. The samples were centrifuged at 15,000g for 15 min, and supernatants were mixed with appropriate volume of 3× SDS loading buffer and stored at −80°C freezer.

Before loading onto SDS-PAGE gels, lysates were incubated at 40 °C for 1 h. The samples were resolved on 4–12% or 8–16% SDS-PAGE gels (Invitrogen Novex) and transferred onto the nitrocellulose membrane of iBlot 2 NC Mini Stacks using iBlot2 Transfer device. The membranes were incubated with Ponceau S (Thermo Scientific A40000279) for 15 min with gentle agitation and washed with distilled water (3 times, 5 min each). The blots were imaged with Azure Biosystems 280 and used as the loading control. After washing off Ponceau S, the blots were sequentially incubated with gentle agitation at room temperature with blocking buffer (StartingBlock TBS, Thermo Scientific) for 2 h, primary antibodies (anti-Sst antibody 1:500 dilution, Invitrogen PA5-85759; anti-Gad65 antibody 1:2000 dilution, Invitrogen PA5-22260; anti-TDP43 antibody 1:5000 dilution, Proteintech 10782-2-AP) at 4 °C for 18 h, TBS with Tween (1×, Thermo Scientific J77500-K8) for washing at room temperature (3 times, 10 min each), and horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (at 1:5,000 dilution in blocking buffer, Promega W4011) at room temperature for 1 h followed by washing as before. Then, the blots were developed by Immobilon Forte Western HRP Substrate (Millipore WBLUF0100) and immunoreactive bands were visualized using an Azure Biosystems 280.

Immunostaining

The Tdp-43F/F mice (3–4 months old of age for short-term study and 5–6 months old of age for long-term study) were bilaterally injected with the control virus (AAV1-CaMKII-GCaMP6f) and the TDP-43 knockout virus (AAV1-CaMKII-Cre + AAV1-CAG-Flex-GCaMP6f) in the mPFC region. The 5xFAD mice aged (12–13 months) were also utilized without any virus injection. The brain was harvested either 2-month or 6-month after the virus injection by cardiac perfusion with Phosphate buffered saline (PBS) followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). The brain was then post-fixed overnight in 4% PFA and stored at 4°C until sectioning. The fixed brain was then sectioned into 40 μm thick coronal sections using a vibratome Leica VT1200S and stored in PBS. For immunostaining, the floating sections were blocked in 1% bovine serum albumin, 4% normal donkey serum and 0.3% Triton X-100 for 2 h at room temperature (RT). The sections were incubated overnight in primary antibody solution at 4°C. Next day, after washing the sections with PBS (5 times, 5 min each), the sections were stained with secondary antibody solution for 2 h at RT. The sections were washed with PBS (5 times, 5 min each) and mounted using Antifade mounting medium with DAPI (Vector Laboratories). All the antibody solutions were made in blocking buffer. The primary antibodies used were rabbit polyclonal anti-TDP-43 C-terminal (Proteintech, 12892-1-AP, 1:400), rabbit polyclonal anti-TDP-43 N-terminal (Proteintech, 10782-2-AP, 1:500), rabbit monoclonal anti-saCas9 (Abcam, ab203933, 1:500), mouse monoclonal anti-caspase-3 (SantaCruz, sc136219, 1:500), mouse-monoclonal anti-Parvalbumin antibody (sigma, P3088, 1:3000), rabbit monoclonal anti-Somatostatin (Abcam, ab111912, 1:400), mouse monoclonal anti-GFAP (Invitrogen, 1:400, MA5-33059), mouse monoclonal anti-Iba1 (Invitrogen, MA5027726, 1:600), rabbit monoclonal anti-vimentin (Abcam, ab92547, 1:150), rat monoclonal anti-C4b(Invitrogen, MA1-40047, 1:100), rabbit monoclonal anti-B2M (Abcam, EP2978Y,1:250), rabbit monoclonal Olig2 (Abcam, ab109186, 1:100), polyclonal goat anti-Olig2 (R&D systems, AF2418,1:40), mouse monoclonal anti-Ubb (Invitrogen, 13–1600, 1:500), mouse anti-β-Amyloid (BioLegend, 803001, 1:500).

The secondary antibody used were Donkey anti-Rabbit IgG conjugated with Alexa Fluor 594, Donkey anti-Mouse IgG conjugated with Alexa Fluor 594, Donkey anti-Rabbit IgG conjugated with Cy5, Donkey anti-Mouse IgG conjugated with Cy5, Donkey anti-Rat IgG conjugated with Cy5, Donkey anti-Rabbit IgG conjugated with Alexa 488. All the images were taken using slide scanner (ZEISS Axio Scan Z.1) and confocal microscope (Zeiss LSM980) at 20× objective. The images were analyzed using ImageJ and QuPath.

Nissl staining

Mouse brain sections dried on gelation coated/Superfrost Plus slides at RT overnight. They were then equilibrated with two washed of PBS for 5 min each and rinsed with deionized water for 1 min. The slides were dipped in 1:10 diluted cresyl violet (Abcam) solution and serially dehydrated in 90%, 95% and two changes of 100% ethanol for 3 min each. The slides were then treated with two changes of xylene and mounted using Permount mounting medium (Fisher Chemical). Images were acquired at 20× objective using ZEISS Axio Scan Z.1 slide scanner.

To quantify Nissl stained images, neuronal and glial counts were determined using QuPath based on different sizes of nuclei. Nissl staining stains nuclei of both neurons and glial cells, but the neuronal and glial nuclei differ significantly in both shapes and size.25 It was determined that the sizes of neuronal nuclei fall into 40−100 μm2, and the glial nuclei fall into 5−35 μm2. These parameters were used to differentiate between neuronal and glial cells. Nuclei detection was set automatically and all counting was done blindly. Neuronal or glial densities were determined and compared between the mPFC of the KO and corresponding Ctrl groups, as well as, between the mPFC of WT and 5xFAD groups.

RNA isolation and RT-qPCR

mRNA was isolated from the mPFC cortex region of mice brain (10 mg tissue) utilizing the Monarch Total RNA Miniprep Kit. LunaScript RT SuperMix kit (New England Biolabs) was used for reverse transcription of mRNA to cDNA. qRT-PCR was performed using PowerTrack SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Life Technologies) using QuantStudio3 (Applied Biosystems) machine. The sequences for all primers used for qPCR are provided in the Table S5.

RNAscope

The TDP-43 Control and KO mice were euthanized in CO2 chamber and fresh brain was immediately harvested. The anterior region of brain possessing the mPFC region was dissected out from the whole brain and it was flash frozen in isopentane chilled in dry ice and ethanol slurry. The fresh frozen brain sample was embedded in OCT and sectioned in cryostat into 16 μm thick coronal sections and attached to Superfrost Plus slide. The slides with the sections were stored at −80°C until use. For starting RNAscope, the slides were taken out from −80°C and immediately placed in ice-cold 4% PFA prechilled at 4°C for 1 h. The in situ hybridization was performed utilizing RNAscope Multiplex Fluorescent Reagent Kit v2 and following the user manual. The sections were dehydrated using alcohol series 50%, 70% and 100% ethanol and air dried for 5 min at RT. Using a hydrophobic barrier pen (Vector laboratories) a hydrophobic barrier was drawn around the sections and the slide was allowed to dry at RT. The sections were treated with H2O2 treatment for 10 min at RT. The sections were next treated with Protease IV for 30 min at RT followed by washing. The samples were then hybridized with the probes (C1: Lamp5, C2: Gad1) for 2 h at 40 °C at humid condition in the HybEZ Oven and stored in 5× Sodium Cirate Buffer (5× SSC) overnight at RT. Next day, the sections were treated with RNAscope Multiplex FL v2 AMP 1, Amp 2 and Amp3 sequentially at 40 °C for 30 min, 30 min and 15 min respectively at humid condition. For C1 detection, the sections were sequentially treated with RNAscope Multiplex FL v2 HRP-C1, TSA Vivid Fluorophore 570 in TSA buffer followed by RNAscope Multiplex FL v2 HRP blocker. Similarly, the C2 channel was developed using TSA Vivid Fluorophore 650 in TSA buffer. The sections were counter stained with DAPI and mounted using ProLong Gold Antifade reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Washing with RNAscope wash buffer was performed between each step for 2 times, 2 min each. All the incubation was performed in HybEZ Humidity Control Tray which had filter paper soaked with distilled water inside it. The images were taken in slide scanner (ZEISS Axio Scan Z.1) at 20× objective and analyzed in QuPath.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All values in the text and Figure legends are represented as mean ± SEM. Nonparametric statistics Mann-Whitney test was used for statistical analysis between two groups. A p value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical details of experiments can be found in the figure legends, including the statistical tests used, exact value of n, and what n represents.
