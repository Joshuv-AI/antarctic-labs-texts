The human microbiome at translational crossroads: an ecological and causal perspective
Authors: Sunny H Wong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The human microbiome has emerged as a central focus of biomedical research, driven by interest in its translational potential for chronic diseases. Although compelling associations link microbial alterations to gastrointestinal, metabolic, neuropsychiatric and systemic conditions, successful clinical translation remains limited. This perspective contends that the principal barrier is not biological relevance but the application of reductionist thinking to an inherently complex ecological system, compounded by an incomplete understanding of causality. This review examines the gradient of causal confidence across gut–organ axes, from established roles in digestive disorders to less established distal associations, as well as highlights the epistemological challenges underlying microbiome research. A critical appraisal of current strategies, including probiotics, live biotherapeutics and faecal microbiota transplantation, suggests that progress requires ecological reasoning, causal rigour and systems-level integration. Moving from association to intervention demands approaches that account for host–microbiome complexity rather than oversimplified microbial targeting.

INTRODUCTION

Advances in culture-independent sequencing, experimental model systems and human studies have established the gut microbiome as an essential biological component of human life.[12] Early gnotobiotic and transplantation studies demonstrated that alterations in microbial communities could transmit host phenotypes, including metabolic traits, providing the first experimental evidence that the microbiome is not merely a passive correlate of disease but can act as a causal or contributory factor in specific biological contexts.[34] Subsequent large-scale population studies characterised the functional redundancy and inter-individual variability of the healthy microbiome.[5]

These discoveries generated substantial clinical interest because the microbiome possesses features distinct from other biological layers. Unlike host genomics, which is largely static, the microbiome is highly dynamic, responding rapidly to diet, medications, lifestyle and environmental exposures.[67] In this sense, the microbiome functions as a biological interface between the host and the external environment—a modifiable window through which lifestyle and environmental factors shape physiology. Despite this promise, clinical translation has remained limited. The clearest success is in the treatment and prevention of recurrent Clostridioides difficile infection (CDI) by faecal microbiota transplantation (FMT),[89] where ecological collapse is central to its pathogenesis.[10] This success has culminated in the regulatory approval of microbiome-derived live biotherapeutic products (LBPs), such as Rebyota and Vowst.[1112] Outside recurrent CDI, however, microbiome-based interventions remain largely investigational. In inflammatory bowel disease, metabolic syndrome and hepatic encephalopathy, therapies such as FMT have yielded heterogeneous results that are often transient or dependent on microbial engraftment.[13] Similarly, despite widespread public use, probiotics lack sufficient evidence to support routine recommendation for most medical indications.[14]

A key reason for this translational bottleneck lies in the intrinsic properties of the microbiome. It is not a collection of single microbial or molecular targets, but an ecological system composed of foreign, non-host organisms co-existing in symbiosis with the human host. Biological effects emerge through networks of metabolic cross-feeding, competition, colonisation resistance and immune interaction rather than through linear, single-direction mechanisms. Because these microbial communities are complementary and functionally redundant, they often resist simple reductionist interventions. Translation therefore requires a shift in perspective: association alone is insufficient, and causal inference is essential. In this perspective, this review first outlines the key concepts of microbiome biology—its ecological nature and environmental responsiveness. It then examines the gradient of causal confidence across gut–organ axes, analysing why causal evidence is strong in some diseases but increasingly tenuous in more distal conditions. Finally, it discusses how lifestyle factors, digital phenotyping and multi-omics approaches may enable a transition from reductionist thinking to clinically relevant systems medicine.

KEY CONCEPTS IN MICROBIOME BIOLOGY

To evaluate the translational potential of the microbiome, clinicians must first conceptualise it accurately. It is neither merely a reservoir of pathogens nor a static organ. Rather, the microbiome is a dynamic and integral component of human biology defined by several intersecting features: its nature as an ecological system, its function as an interface with the environment and the distinct character of causal inference within microbial communities.

The microbiome as an integrated ecological system

The human microbiome is best understood as a constitutive component of the human superorganism that functions through ecological networking. Trillions of microorganisms colonise body surfaces, particularly the gastrointestinal tract, encoding a gene catalogue that is several orders of magnitude larger than the human genome.[15] The microbiome ecosystem is functionally integrated with host physiology and acts as an accessory organ that complements metabolic processes the host cannot perform independently, such as the fermentation of non-digestible carbohydrates and the production of bioactive microbial metabolites.[16] Unlike a conventional organ, however, this system operates not as a single tissue but as a complex ecological network. Microbial effects emerge from interspecies interactions—including competition, syntrophy (cross-feeding) and antagonism—rather than from the activity of individual species in isolation. Another key driver of these interactions is auxotrophy, whereby certain microbial taxa cannot synthesise essential metabolites and therefore depend on cross-feeding from neighbouring species for growth and survival. At the same time, considerable functional redundancy exists, with unrelated taxa encoding similar metabolic pathways. This redundancy allows the ecosystem to maintain stable function despite substantial taxonomic variability among individuals.[1] Within such an ecological structure, dysbiosis is rarely defined by the presence of a single pathogen but rather by alterations in network architecture or ecological resilience.

Environmental responsiveness: a window and a modifier

Unlike the host genome, which remains largely static throughout life, the microbiome is highly plastic and serves as a biological interface with the environment. It responds rapidly and reproducibly to external influences, particularly diet, medications and lifestyle factors such as sleep and physical activity.[6717] This responsiveness makes the microbiome a unique biological interface to function not only as a reflection of environmental exposure but also as a potential modifier of those exposures. For instance, the gut microbiota can modify the nutritional yield of diets, thereby altering the host’s phenotypic response to the same environmental input.[18] This bidirectionality—being shaped by the environment while also shaping the host’s response to it—makes the microbiome a potent lever for personalised medicine. At the same time, it introduces substantial confounding into observational research.

Causal inferences in microbiome biology

Establishing causality remains the central challenge in translating microbiome science into clinical practice. Unlike classical infectious diseases, where Koch’s postulates apply to single pathogens, microbiome-associated conditions often involve complex microbial communities in which causality is distributed, indirect and bidirectional.[19] In this context, causal inference requires adapted criteria that move beyond association towards mechanistic proof. Strong causal evidence exists where phenotype transfer can be demonstrated. For example, FMT from diseased humans to germ-free mice has transferred phenotypes such as obesity, insulin resistance and colitis.[420] Conversely, FMT administered to human patients has shown improvement in selected conditions, including recurrent CDI, ulcerative colitis and responses to cancer immunotherapy.[8921222324] However, distinguishing true causality from reverse causation—where disease processes or treatments alter the microbiome—remains challenging, particularly outside selected randomised controlled trial settings. Biological plausibility further requires clearly defined molecular mechanisms that link microbial metabolism to host physiology. Examples include microbial metabolites such as short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO) and immune-modulating ligands that connect microbial metabolic activity to host pathology.[2526] Clinically meaningful translations, especially for preventive or therapeutic interventions, therefore depends on the rigorous identification of such causal drivers.

Implications for translation

These conceptual principles shape the logic of microbiome-based clinical translation. Because the microbiome is an integrated ecological system characterised by functional redundancy, interventions must extend beyond simple reductionist strategies; introducing a single probiotic strain may not suffice to shift a stable ecological state without addressing the broader network context.[27] Because it functions as an environmental interface, therapeutic strategies must also account for diet and lifestyle as dominant modulators rather than mere confounders. Finally, effective interventions require targeting specific molecular mechanisms that mediate microbiome–host interactions. Achieving this will require a systems-based approach in which microbiome data are integrated with host physiology to guide precision medicine.

A GRADIENT OF CAUSAL CONFIDENCE ACROSS GUT–ORGAN AXES

The translation of microbiome science into clinical practice requires navigating a steep gradient of causal confidence. This gradient ranges from selected digestive conditions, where microbial causality is mechanistically defined and therapeutically validated, to distal systemic disorders in which biological plausibility often exceeds clinical proof. Recognising this distinction is critical for evaluating the realistic potential of microbiome-based interventions.

Strong case of causality: Clostridioides difficile and colonisation resistance

Causality is most clearly established in recurrent CDI, where disease arises from the failure of ecological suppression known as colonisation resistance. In the healthy state, the commensal microbiota limits C. difficile expansion through direct nutrient competition and the metabolic conversion of primary bile acids into secondary bile acids, such as deoxycholate and lithocholate, which inhibit spore germination and vegetative growth.[28] Antibiotic exposure disrupts these metabolic networks, depleting 7α-dehydroxylating bacteria and creating a permissive environment for pathogen dominance. The causal nature of this defect is supported by the efficacy of ecological restoration. Landmark randomised controlled trials have demonstrated that FMT is significantly superior to standard vancomycin therapy for recurrent CDI, with cure rates frequently exceeding 80%–90%, achieved through restoration of microbial diversity and bile acid metabolism.[89] This level of causal certainty has led to the regulatory approval of standardised LBPs,[1112] establishing recurrent CDI as the benchmark condition for microbiome therapeutics.

Contributory but not necessary: malignancy and the confounding of drivers

In gastrointestinal malignancies such as colorectal cancer (CRC), causal evidence for specific bacterial species is supported by mechanistic studies, although interpretation is complicated by potential confounding factors. Fusobacterium nucleatum promotes tumourigenesis through several virulence factors: FadA activates β-catenin signalling via E-cadherin binding,[29] Fap2 mediates immune evasion through TIGIT-dependent natural killer cell inhibition and facilitates tumour adherence,[3031] and RadD supports biofilm formation and colonisation.[3233] Similarly, pks+ Escherichia coli produces colibactin, a genotoxin that induces double-strand DNA breaks and leaves a distinct mutational signature (SBS88) in CRC genomes,[34] with colibactin-induced genotoxicity dependent on bacterial adhesion via FimH and FmlH.[35] Enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis secretes a toxin that activates Wnt/β-catenin signalling and promotes inflammation.[36] Peptostreptococcus anaerobius modulates cholesterol metabolism via toll-like receptor 2 (TLR2)/TLR4 interaction and enhances colonic epithelial cell proliferation.[3738] Despite these mechanistic links, recent quantitative profiling studies have identified important confounders. The association between F. nucleatum and CRC weakens substantially after adjusting for intestinal transit time,[39] an important physiological parameter regulated by dietary fibre intake and bile acid turnover, both of which shape microbiological niches within the colonic environment.[4041] Moreover, F. nucleatum may function as both passenger and driver: its abundance may initially reflect ecological succession secondary to host changes, yet once established, it may actively contribute to disease progression through defined molecular mechanisms.[424344] These observations underscore the need for causal models that incorporate feedback between host physiology, microbial ecology and specific pathogenic mechanisms.

High plausibility and mechanistic depth: the gut–liver and metabolic axes

The gut–liver axis reflects the anatomical intimacy between these organs: the portal vein continuously exposes the liver to a diverse array of gut-derived microbial products. In metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease, pathogenesis has been linked to the translocation of pathogen-associated molecular patterns, such as lipopolysaccharide, across a compromised intestinal barrier. This phenomenon, often termed metabolic endotoxaemia, activates TLR4 on hepatic Kupffer cells, driving inflammation and fibrosis.[4546] Beyond endotoxaemia, the gut–liver axis is further shaped by microbial metabolic processes, including endogenous ethanol production and disruption of choline metabolism.[4647] A recent Delphi consensus study reported that 88% of experts considered the causal role of the microbiome in liver disease to be established.[48] In broader metabolic disorders such as type 2 diabetes mellitus, seminal transfer experiments have shown that microbiota from obese donors can transmit adiposity and insulin resistance to germ-free mice, mediated through mechanisms including enhanced energy harvest and altered SCFA profiles.[418]

Distal associations and modulatory roles: gut and distal organs

As the focus shifts to more distal organs, evidence increasingly moves from direct causality towards mechanistic plausibility. The gut–brain axis operates through established pathways: microbial metabolites such as serotonin, gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) and SCFAs influence enteric nervous system activity and vagal afferent signalling,[49] and specific SCFAs, including butyrate, enhance blood–brain barrier integrity by upregulating tight junction proteins.[50] Dysbiosis has been implicated in disorders of gut–brain interaction, Parkinson’s disease and major depressive disorder.[49] The gut–lung axis is supported by observations that early-life dysbiosis—characterised by reduced Bifidobacterium, Akkermansia and Faecalibacterium abundance—may predispose to childhood atopy and asthma through impaired immune tolerance and pro-inflammatory metabolite production.[51] In adults, a dysregulated gut–lung axis involving lung Pseudomonas and gut Bacteroides has been associated with more frequent exacerbations and greater disease severity in bronchiectasis.[52] In the gut–kidney axis, microbial metabolism of dietary tryptophan, tyrosine, and choline generates uraemic toxins, such as indoxyl sulfate, p-cresyl sulfate and TMAO. These metabolites promote oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction and cardiovascular calcification, thereby accelerating chronic kidney disease progression[5354]. However, interventions to reduce these toxins, including oral sorbents such as AST-120 and probiotic formulations, have yielded inconsistent clinical outcomes.[55] The gut–skin axis remains largely associative. Observational studies link reduced gut microbial diversity with atopic dermatitis, acne vulgaris and psoriasis, potentially mediated by systemic inflammation and SCFA modulation of skin barrier function.[5657] Nevertheless, direct causal evidence in humans is sparse and intervention studies have yielded inconclusive findings.[58] While specific mechanisms and contributing roles have been described across these axes, the precise magnitude of the microbiome’s contribution to disease pathophysiology—and, consequently, the degree of clinical benefit achievable through microbiome-targeted therapies—remain uncertain.

A case of ongoing contention: autistic spectrum disorder

The distinction between biological plausibility and clinical causality is particularly evident in neurodevelopmental conditions such as autism spectrum disorder (ASD). Early animal studies suggested that microbial modulation could influence ASD-related behaviours. Transplantation of microbiota from human ASD donors into animals was reported to induce autistic-like behaviours in animals.[59] Oral treatment with B. fragilis ameliorated communicative and stereotypic defects in the maternal immune activation mouse model.[60] Similarly, Lactobacillus reuteri was shown to reverse social deficits induced by maternal high-fat diet.[61] However, recent large-scale studies and methodological critiques have fundamentally challenged this narrative. A landmark study by Yap et al.[62] demonstrated that microbiome differences observed in ASD are largely attributable to restricted dietary preferences associated with the condition, such as reduced fibre intake. More recently, Mitchell et al.[63] critically reviewed the literature and questioned the proposed causal link, highlighting limitations, including underpowered studies, questionable animal models and inadequate control for confounding factors. This re-evaluation provides an important lesson for microbiome research. In complex behavioural disorders, the microbiome may often function as a readout of lifestyle and host phenotype—reflecting dietary patterns, medication exposure and gastrointestinal physiology—rather than as a sole aetiological driver.

HOST–MICROBIOME CO-EVOLUTION AND ECOLOGICAL MISMATCH

To understand why the microbiome is so deeply integrated into human physiology, it must be viewed through an evolutionary perspective. Humans are not autonomous biological units but can be conceptualised as holobionts: the host and its symbiotic microbial communities, which have co-evolved to function as a single biological system.[1964]

The holobiont: co-evolutionary entanglement

Over evolutionary time, the human host has effectively outsourced several essential physiological functions to its microbial partners. The maturation of gut-associated lymphoid tissue, differentiation of regulatory T cells and production of secretory IgA are all influenced by microbial signalling.[65] Consequently, the germ-free state is not a natural baseline but rather an abnormal condition characterised by immune immaturity, heightened susceptibility to infection, and alterations in behaviour, sociability and cognition.[6667] This interdependence also extends to metabolism. The human genome encodes a limited repertoire of carbohydrate-degrading enzymes and therefore relies on the microbial metagenome to harvest energy from dietary fibre.[318]

Disappearing microbiota and evolutionary mismatch

Industrialisation has introduced potent selection pressures, including antibiotics, chemicals, and low-fibre and high-sugar diets, that have rapidly reshaped the gut microbial ecosystem.[68] In contrast, the human genome evolves over much longer timescales and cannot adapt at comparable speed to environmental change. As a result, host physiological systems—including immune and metabolic regulatory networks shaped under ancestral conditions—may be poorly calibrated to the altered microbial landscape of modern environments. Comparative studies reveal that industrialised populations have lost ancestral microbial diversity and specific taxa, such as Treponema and certain Prevotella strains, which remain prevalent in non-industrialised hunter–gatherer communities.[6970] The ‘disappearing microbiota’ hypothesis proposes that the modern immune system receives altered or incomplete signals from a depleted microbial ecosystem, predisposing individuals to chronic inflammatory and metabolic diseases.[71] In this view, dysbiosis in chronic disease often reflects ecological displacement driven by environmental change rather than a conventional infectious process.

Integral but not deterministic: the microbiome as a risk modifier

Although integrated into host physiology, the microbiome does not exert deterministic effects. Rather, it functions as a probabilistic risk modifier that lowers the threshold for pathology in the presence of other factors such as genetic susceptibility, diet or chronic stress.[727374] This perspective explains the heterogeneity observed in microbiome intervention trials. Microbial therapies such as probiotics or FMT may benefit patients in whom microbial signals represent a limiting factor in disease activity.[75] In contrast, when pathology is driven predominantly by genetic or structural factors, microbiome modulation alone may be insufficient.[76] The microbiome is therefore a powerful lever within a multifactorial biological system, but evolutionary mismatch cannot be corrected through a single-strain, single-target intervention.

REDUCTIONISM TO SYSTEMS MEDICINE: WHY THE MICROBIOME RESISTS SIMPLE SOLUTIONS

Modern medicine is grounded in reductionism, identifying discrete molecular defects and targeting them with high-affinity agents. Host pathways often converge on dominant nodes such as receptors or enzymes.[77] The microbiome, in contrast, functions as a distributed ecological network characterised by cross-feeding, redundancy and adaptive resilience.[78] Healthy microbiome exhibits stable network architectures, whereas diseased states are characterised by network instability and variability.[79] Although microbial metabolites may act through defined host pathways, the ecosystem itself is structurally distributed and cannot be effectively modulated through single-target interventions.

Challenge of the ‘magic bullet’

This tension is reflected in the probiotic paradigm. Despite public enthusiasm, empirical probiotics lack consistent evidence of benefit across most gastrointestinal and metabolic disorders.[14] Two biological barriers limit the ‘bug-as-drug’ model: colonisation resistance and functional redundancy. Established microbial communities defend ecological niches, often preventing sustained engraftment of administered strains.[80] Moreover, overlapping metabolic functions across taxa mean that introducing a single species rarely alters overall system output. Faecal microbiota transplantation is effective in recurrent CDI because it replaces an entire ecosystem, restoring colonisation resistance and functional balance.[89]

The case of Akkermansia muciniphila illustrates both the promise and the limitations of this approach. Early proof-of-concept studies demonstrated improvements in insulin sensitivity and cardiometabolic markers in overweight individuals.[81] A subsequent randomised trial demonstrated benefit in type 2 diabetes mellitus, but only among participants with low baseline abundance of endogenous A. muciniphila.[82] These findings underscore that even a well-characterised strain is not a universal one-size-fits-all magic bullet; its therapeutic effect is contingent on the existing microbial context.[83] Precision microbial profiling and individualisation are therefore essential for meaningful clinical translation.

Systems medicine and implications for clinicians

If the microbiome is understood as an integrated regulatory system, its management requires a systems-based approach. Rather than targeting individual taxa, clinicians must recognise dysbiosis as a network state mediated through immune, metabolic and neural crosstalk.[84] Therapeutic success depends on shifting the emergent properties of the holobiont, rather than simply altering the abundance of specific microbes.

For clinicians, this conceptual shift implies that modulating disease through the microbiome may require more than a single intervention. Effective strategies are more likely to be multimodal, personalised and sustained, particularly those involving nutrition and lifestyle modifications that are capable of reshaping ecological selection pressures and downstream metabolic and immunological outputs.

TRANSLATION REVISITED: FROM PRECISION NUTRITION TO THERAPEUTICS

Translating microbiome science into clinical practice involves various approaches to modulation, ranging from broad systems-level interventions (nutrition and lifestyle) to ecological restoration (FMT), and finally to targeted, molecularly defined interventions (LBPs and synthetic biology). Each approach carries distinct advantages, biological mechanisms and regulatory challenges.

Systemic modulation: nutrition as a key ecological driver

Diet constitutes one of the most powerful environmental modulators of the human microbiome, exerting selective pressure on microbial community composition and function within days of alteration.[685] Unlike pharmacological interventions that often target specific pathways, dietary inputs reshape the entire microbial ecosystem. High-fibre dietary patterns, such as the Mediterranean diet, enrich microbiota-accessible carbohydrates, which are fermented by microbial guilds—notably Roseburia, Eubacterium and Faecalibacterium—to produce SCFAs such as butyrate. These metabolites regulate colonic regulatory T-cell differentiation and maintain epithelial barrier integrity.[8687] Conversely, Western dietary patterns characterised by high saturated fat intake and ultra-processed foods promote expansion of pro-inflammatory Proteobacteria and reduce butyrate synthesis. Crucially, the interaction between diet and the microbiome is highly individualised. Landmark studies have demonstrated that an individual’s postprandial glycaemic responses to specific foods are more accurately predicted by baseline microbiome composition than by the nutritional content of the food itself.[8889] These findings emphasise the need for precision nutrition for effective microbiome modulation.

Ecological restoration: faecal microbiota transplantation

When the microbial ecosystem is profoundly disrupted—as in recurrent CDI—broad ecological restoration is required. In this context, FMT remains the gold standard, functioning through the introduction of a diverse donor community that displaces both the pathogen and the dysbiotic state.[89] The field has evolved from crude stool preparations to standardised, regulatory-approved therapeutics. The approvals of faecal microbiota, live-jslm (Rebyota) and faecal microbiota spores, live-brpk (Vowst), represent the integration of FMT into mainstream clinical practice for recurrent CDI.[1112] However, its efficacy in chronic, polygenic conditions remains heterogeneous. In inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) and metabolic syndrome, FMT trials have yielded inconsistent outcomes influenced by donor characteristics and delivery protocols.[1390] This variability suggests that, in complex diseases, ecological replacement alone may be insufficient without addressing underlying host–microbe maladaptation.

Precision medicine: next-generation probiotics and live biotherapeutics

To overcome the variability of FMT, the field is advancing towards LBPs—pharmaceutical-grade defined bacterial strains selected for specific mechanistic functions.[91] This represents a departure from traditional ‘probiotics’ towards next-generation probiotics derived from commensal species. A. muciniphila, a mucin-degrading bacterium, has emerged as a promising candidate for metabolic disorders. Supplementation with pasteurised A. muciniphila has been shown to improve insulin sensitivity and reduce total cholesterol in obese individuals, mechanistically linked to outer membrane proteins that interact with TLR2 to enhance barrier function.[81] Similarly, Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, a major butyrate producer consistently depleted in Crohn’s disease, is being developed for IBD maintenance therapy owing to its anti-inflammatory effects.[92] In oncology, specific strains of Bifidobacterium and Bacteroides fragilis have been identified as enhancers of response to immune checkpoint inhibitors, prompting clinical trials testing these specific consortia as adjuvants to programmed cell death protein 1 blockade.[2293]

Synthetic biology: genetically engineered bacteria and phage therapy

Beyond naturally occurring microbes, synthetic biology enables the development of engineered LBPs designed to perform functions not present in native microbial communities. Strains such as E. coli Nissle 1917 have been genetically modified to act as metabolic sinks, for example, degrading phenylalanine in the gut for the treatment of phenylketonuria.[94] Engineered bacteria are also being developed to detect inflammation or deliver therapeutic payloads directly to tumour microenvironments.[9596] Additionally, bacteriophage therapy offers a precision ‘editing’ tool. Orally administered phage consortia can selectively eliminate pathogenic organisms, such as Klebsiella pneumoniae strains driving inflammation in IBD,[97] or Fusobacterium nucleatum in CRC,[98] while preserving the surrounding commensal microbiota.

CONCLUSION: REFRAMING MICROBIOME TRANSLATION

The human microbiome is neither a panacea for all chronic diseases nor a biological artefact; it is a fundamental, modifiable component of human physiology that functions as a complex adaptive system.[12] The current translational impasse arises from applying a reductionist model—one microbe, one disease—to an ecological network characterised by functional redundancy, cross-feeding and emergent properties.[99]

Moving forward, clinical translation requires three conceptual shifts. First, priority must be given to causality, distinguishing microbes that drive disease through validated mechanisms from those that merely reflect disease-associated ecological changes. This requires integrating quantitative approaches that account for confounding by host factors such as transit time, inflammation and medication use, alongside rigorously designed prospective studies in both animal models and human populations.[3941]

Second, a systems medicine framework must be adopted, in which the microbiome is understood not as an isolated organ but as an ecological system whose collective metabolic output modulates host physiology through multidirectional feedback loops.[75] This perspective enables patient stratification based on microbial community structure and function, moving beyond taxonomic descriptions towards mechanistic insight.[100] Recognition that microbial communities assemble according to ecological principles—including niche partitioning, keystone species and metabolic interdependence through auxotrophy—has important implications for therapeutic design.[101]

Third, lifestyle must be recognised as a primary therapeutic lever. Diet and lifestyle remain the most powerful and evolutionarily coherent means of modulating the microbiome,[102] shaping community structure and metabolic output in ways that single-strain interventions cannot replicate. Ultimately, the microbiome remains on a path towards clinical relevance, but this path is unlikely to be linear. Rather, progress will depend on a shift towards systems-level management that acknowledges the ecological nature of human–microbial interactions.
