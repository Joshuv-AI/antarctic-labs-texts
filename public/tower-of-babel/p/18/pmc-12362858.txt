Dysregulated lipid metabolism in kidney stone pathogenesis: a literature review of mechanistic insights and therapeutic implications
Authors: Quan Wen, Qiuyu Li, Haoji Zhou, Boyan Su, Yalin Ren, Qiqi He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractKidney stone disease (KSD) is a globally prevalent urinary system disorder whose pathogenesis involves complex interactions among metabolic, genetic, and environmental factors. Although the role of glucose metabolism in KSD has been widely studied, the impact of lipid metabolism abnormalities remains unclear. This review systematically integrates epidemiological, animal model, and molecular mechanism studies to explore the mechanisms by which lipid metabolism disorders promote KSD through key regulatory factors, such as peroxisome proliferator-activated receptors (PPARs), fatty acid-binding proteins (FABPs), and apolipoproteins, synergizing with organelle damage pathways, including mitochondrial dysfunction, endoplasmic reticulum stress (ERS), and ferroptosis.This study revealed that abnormal lipid metabolism accelerates renal papillary calcification and stone formation by inducing oxidative stress, inflammatory cascade reactions, and crystal adhesion. Additionally, small-molecule modulators targeting the PPAR/FABP pathway and novel delivery systems, such as mitochondrion-targeted liposomes, have demonstrated the potential to intervene in the lipid metabolism/organelle damage axis, although clinical translation faces challenges such as species differences (The paradoxical effects of PPARα agonists in rodents versus humans) and safety concerns.This review highlights the importance of lipid metabolism as a new target for KSD prevention and treatment, and it suggests that future research should focus on the analysis of molecular mechanisms and the clinical translation of interdisciplinary technologies (e.g., mitochondrion-targeted delivery systems).Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12894-025-01870-z.Keywords: Kidney stone disease, lipid metabolism, PPARs, FABPs, mitochondrial dysfunction, endoplasmic reticulum stress

Introduction

Kidney stone disease (KSD), one of the most common urinary system disorders worldwide, affects approximately 10% of the global population, with a recurrence rate exceeding 50%, posing a significant threat to public health [1–3]. Despite significant advancements in understanding the pathophysiology of KSD, the underlying mechanisms remain incompletely understood, particularly the role of lipid metabolism. While metabolic syndrome and abnormal glucose metabolism have been extensively linked to KSD, the contribution of lipid metabolism abnormalities has received comparatively less attention [4–6]. A strong association between metabolic syndrome (e.g., hyperglycemia and obesity) and KSD has been widely documented—diabetic patients exhibit a 2-3-fold higher risk of KSD, and 60–80% of diabetic patients have concomitant dyslipidemia (e.g., hypertriglyceridemia and elevated low-density lipoprotein). Epidemiological data from Arab countries show [7] that the incidence rate of KSD in diabetic patients with central obesity reaches 29.3%, which is significantly higher than that in nonobese diabetic patients (13.4%), suggesting that lipid metabolism regulation may represent a core link in metabolic syndrome-related KSD.

Recent research on abnormal lipid metabolism has focused primarily on nephrotic syndrome and end-stage renal disease. However, the following three critical knowledge gaps persist regarding the role of abnormal lipid metabolism in KSD:

Unclear molecular regulatory network: The mechanisms of how key factors, such as peroxisome proliferator-activated receptors (PPARs) and fatty acid binding proteins (FABPs), influence renal lipid homeostasis through transcriptional regulation are unclear.

Synergistic effects of organelle damage: The cascade effects of mitochondrial dysfunction, endoplasmic reticulum stress (ERS), and ferroptosis in the “lipid‒crystal” interaction remain systematically uncharacterized.

By synthesizing epidemiological evidence, mechanistic insights, and emerging therapeutic strategies, this review systematically examines (1) the multidimensional role of abnormal lipid metabolism in KSD pathogenesis via the molecular regulation/organelle damage/inflammatory cascade axis, (2) the translational potential of key targets (e.g., PPARs and FABPs), and (3) the clinical applicability of interdisciplinary approaches, such as mitochondrion-targeted liposomes. These efforts collectively advance the current understanding of lipid metabolism–KSD interplay and identify novel targets for therapeutic intervention.

Abnormal lipid metabolism

Abnormal lipid metabolism refers to an imbalance or dysfunction in the processes of lipid synthesis, decomposition, transport, and storage within the human body. Abnormal lipid metabolism manifests mainly as abnormal fat accumulation and distribution, as well as irregular levels of triglycerides, high-/low-density lipoproteins (HDL/LDL), and cholesterol in the blood.

The main feature of abnormal fat accumulation and distribution is significant fat accumulation around the waist, abdomen, and visceral regions, which leads to central obesity. Central obesity is a risk factor for metabolic diseases such as type 2 diabetes and hypertension. The prevalence of dyslipidemia in the general population is greater than that of central obesity, accounting for approximately 30% of the population. Many patients have lipid abnormalities prior to developing central obesity. In hemorheology, these lipid abnormalities can manifest as increased blood viscosity and plaque formation on the blood vessel wall, which may eventually lead to vascular stenosis or plaque detachment, resulting in a series of respiratory and circulatory system disorders and cardiovascular events [8, 9].

Research on the mechanism by which abnormal lipid metabolism affects kidney diseases has focused mainly on nephrotic syndrome and end-stage renal disease [10–12], whereas relatively few studies have investigated the mechanisms related to KSD development. Abnormal lipid metabolism can induce inflammatory damage and fibrosis of the glomerulus. The accumulation of free fatty acids in podocytes leads to oxidative stress, mitochondrial damage, and endoplasmic reticulum stress, causing lipotoxic damage that accelerates cell fibrosis and loss of renal function. A total of 179 and 196 different lipid metabolites have been screened in the kidney and serum, respectively. These lipid metabolites include fatty acids, glycerophospholipids, sphingolipids, glycerides, and enol lipids. In particular, oleoylethanolamide and glycerophospholipid ethanolamine plasma alcohols are closely related to oxidative stress. Among them, the inflammatory responses in the renal tubules mediated by polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), such as arachidonic acid, eicosapentaenoic acid, and docosahexaenoic acid, as well as ceramides and lysophosphatidylcholine, are also related to the formation of kidney stones [13]. PUFAs inhibit kidney stone-induced renal tubular injury through the miR-93-5p/Pknox1 axis, reducing apoptosis, glycogen deposition, and lipid accumulation [14].

Abnormal lipid metabolism has an obvious damaging effect on renal tubular epithelial cells, which manifests as renal tubular injury, lipid deposition, and fibrosis. Under normal physiological conditions, the lipid metabolism of renal tubular epithelial cells depends mainly on the β-oxidation of fatty acids, which involves several stages as follows: (1) uptake and transport, in which nonesterified fatty acids in the plasma bind to albumin and are transported into the cells by the fatty acid transporter protein family (FATP), fatty acid translocase (CD36/FAT), and fatty acid-binding proteins (FABPs); (2) oxidative decomposition, in which intracellular fatty acids are converted into acetyl-CoA by acyl-CoA synthetase 1 (ACSL1) and then transferred to the mitochondria by palmitoyltransferase 1 (PT-1) for β-oxidation to produce adenosine triphosphate (ATP) for energy supply; and (3) storage, in which excess intracellular fatty acids in the endoplasmic reticulum are catalyzed by diacylglycerol acyltransferase 1 (DGAT1) to form lipid droplets for storage. When the body lacks an energy source, lipid droplets can be decomposed again by enzymes, such as adipose triglyceride lipase (ATCL), to provide sufficient energy in the form of fatty acids. Changes in the extracellular environment, such as the presence of unbound nonesterified fatty acids, slow or lack lipid droplet mobilization, and reduced expression of key lipid metabolism enzymes and transcription factors, can lead to disruption of renal lipid metabolism. The retention of fatty acids and lipid droplets can trigger a chronic inflammatory response, causing cell damage characterized by the secretion of a large amount of proinflammatory cytokines, which triggers a cascade of inflammatory factors; this cascade recruits various immune cells, including tissue macrophages, which migrate to the renal tubular area and induce tissue inflammation [15].

The renal interstitium is usually in a state of high calcium phosphate saturation. Owing to the continuous chemotactic effect of tissue inflammation and related factors, calcium- and phosphate-rich Randall plaques are formed in the renal papilla [16]. In addition, the accumulation and release of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in renal tubular epithelial cells can induce lipid peroxidation, damage the cell membrane, and promote crystal deposition at the cell apex. When deposition is aggravated, it pierces the cell membrane and gradually aggregates to form a stone nucleus, ultimately forming a KSD [17].

Epidemiological and animal model studies have demonstrated that obesity and dyslipidemia contribute to the development of KSD. A cohort study in Saudi Arabia indicated that in KSD patients with diabetes and central obesity, serum FABP1 levels were positively correlated with urinary oxalate excretion, and PPARα activity in renal tubular epithelial cells was significantly downregulated. This suggests that the FABP1/PPARα pathway plays a crucial role in metabolic syndrome - related KSD.Additionally, diabetic patients exhibit gut microbiota dysregulation, such as an increased Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio, which increases hepatic FASN and DGAT1 gene expression to increase circulating triglyceride levels. This gut/renal axis abnormality in KSD patients is characterized by increased renal expression of short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) receptors (e.g., FFAR2), further activating the NLRP3 inflammasome and forming a vicious lipid inflammation/crystal deposition cycle. Concurrently, clinical data has revealed a positive correlation between fecal SCFA levels and urinary oxalate excretion in Arab diabetic patients (r = 0.68, P < 0.001), suggesting that modulating the gut microbiota may serve as an upstream strategy to intervene in lipid metabolism. Collectively, these findings elucidate the complex pathogenesis of diabetes-related KSD across multiple mechanistic layers [7]. A 2022 study of the U.S. NHANES, including 10,858 participants, has revealed that individuals with higher visceral fat content (central obesity) have a significantly greater incidence of KSD. Analysis of four NHANE cycles within eight years has consistently revealed that as central obesity becomes more pronounced, the incidence of kidney stones increases, indicating a significant positive correlation [18]. Another large-scale longitudinal study has also revealed a significant positive correlation among high levels of triglycerides, cholesterol, low levels of HDL, and the incidence of KSD, especially kidney stones composed of calcium oxalate [19]. Researchers have used different diets in animal studies, including regular feed, 10% fructose, and 60% fructose, to feed rats. Compared with rats fed a regular diet, those fed a high-fructose diet present the following characteristics: lower creatinine clearance rate; significantly increased insulin and triglyceride levels; and increased phosphate, urinary calcium, and urinary magnesium levels. In addition, phosphate crystal deposition is observed in renal tubules and glomeruli of rats fed a high-fructose diet [20]. Our previous study also revealed that the incidence of KSD in obese and metabolic syndrome rats is significantly greater than that in normal rats in an induced KSD rat model. Under the same stone induction conditions, metabolic syndrome rats induced by a high-fat diet (60% fat) present a more severe renal inflammatory response, resulting in the production and aggregation of more calcium oxalate crystals in the renal papilla [21].

The inflammatory response caused by abnormal lipid metabolism is highly similar to that caused by abnormal glucose metabolism, and it is closely related to the excessive release of ROS and the inflammatory cascade. In terms of glucose metabolism, early effective blood glucose intervention strategies can effectively reduce the level of renal inflammation and the formation of renal crystals. Early intervention and regulation of lipid metabolism regulators are expected to reduce this damage and the occurrence of stones.

Correlation between lipid metabolism regulators and the occurrence of KSD

Lipid metabolism regulators constitute a series of hormones, enzymes, apolipoproteins, and small-molecule substances that have an impact on the processes of lipid synthesis, decomposition, transport, and metabolism. These regulators influence the occurrence of diseases within the body mainly by modulating lipid metabolism. When the expression of key enzymes and key transcription factors in their metabolic pathways is abnormal, lipid metabolism is disrupted, which may result in the occurrence of KSD. A small number of lipid metabolism regulators are closely associated with stone formation, and some of these are described below.

Peroxisome Proliferator-Activated Receptors (PPARs)

Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptors (PPARs) are ligand-activated nuclear transcription factors containing an activation domain, DNA-binding domain, dimerization domain, and ligand-binding domain. They form heterodimers by binding to lipid ligands and co-activating proteins, thereby regulating cellular transcription. The main subtypes include PPARα, PPARδ, and PPARγ, among which PPARα and PPARγ have been more intensively studied in highly metabolic tissues such as the liver, heart, and kidney [22–28].

In an acetate-induced mouse model of nephrolithiasis, the PPARα agonist fenofibrate reduces renal crystal formation and decreases serum creatinine (SCr) and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) levels, indicating its renoprotective effect. Mechanistically, fenofibrate alleviates oxidative damage and inflammatory cascades by regulating phospholipase metabolism [23].

Another study shows that the PPARγ agonist pioglitazone significantly reduces cellular inflammation, oxidative stress, and apoptosis, and may inhibit calcium oxalate crystal formation via the PI3K/AKT pathway. Notably, combined use of PPARα agonist and pioglitazone promotes crystal formation, highlighting functional differences between subtypes [24].

Current research is predominantly based on rodent models, which have species metabolic differences (e.g., gut microbiota composition), and the acetate-induced model cannot fully recapitulate human pathological conditions. The specific regulatory pathways of PPARs in human lipid metabolism and stone formation still require validation.

Fatty Acid-Binding proteins (FABPs)

Fatty acid-binding proteins (FABPs) are intracellular proteins widely distributed in tissues, with albumin as a structural component, participating in fatty acid transport and oxidation, and possessing clinical value in the diagnosis of myocardial infarction [29]. The most intensively studied members of the family are FABP1-FABP4:

FABP1 has a relative molecular weight of 14 kDa and is primarily expressed in hepatocytes and renal tubular epithelial cells. It binds long-chain fatty acids to generate ATP, alleviating lipid toxicity under ischemic-hypoxic conditions. Early stress upregulates the FABP1 gene, activating PPARα to form a negative feedback loop that inhibits lipid peroxidation. However, in chronic diseases, the expression of FABP1 and PPARα is downregulated, accelerating tissue damage [30, 31]. FABP2 is mainly expressed in intestinal cells and is responsible for transporting free fatty acids in intestinal epithelial cells. FABP3, also named cardiac fatty acid-binding protein, is highly expressed in cardiomyocytes. FABP4, also known as adipocyte fatty acid-binding protein, shares a similar structure with FABP3 and is highly expressed in adipose tissue and macrophages, with expression decreasing gradually from the renal cortex to the papilla. FABP4 knockout mice exhibit widespread calcium oxalate crystal deposition in the renal papilla, suggesting that its deficiency promotes stone formation [32].

Elevated perioperative urinary FABP1 levels in patients with obstructive nephrolithiasis are associated with poor renal function prognosis. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) combined with preoperative renal function assessment reaches 0.969, with a sensitivity of 87.5% and specificity of 100% [33]. Genomic analysis shows that FABP4-related lipid transport genes are downregulated during nephrolithiasis progression, and reduced FABP4 expression may promote the formation of calcified plaques in the renal papilla [32].

The mechanistic studies of the FABP family in stone formation remain limited. Further translational research is required to support the use of FABP1 and FABP4 as preventive markers or therapeutic targets.

1,25-Dihydroxyvitamin D3

The main biologically active form of vitamin D, a fat-soluble vitamin, in the body is 1,25 - dihydroxyvitamin D3 [1,25(OH)2D3]. In daily life, the two primary sources of 1,25(OH)2D3 in the human body are dietary intake and exposure to sunlight. 1,25(OH)2D3 exerts its biological effects predominantly by binding to the vitamin D receptor (VDR). 1,25(OH)2D3 has immunomodulatory functions, and it mediates cell proliferation and differentiation. The association of 1,25(OH)2D3 with bone metabolism has been extensively studied.

In studies regarding kidney stones, vitamin D deficiency is frequently observed in populations with kidney stones. Supplementation with vitamin D inhibits fat production; however, it also increases the risk of excessive urinary calcium excretion [34, 35]. Therefore, there is considerable controversy about whether patients with renal calculi need vitamin D supplementation and how to supplement it. Relevant evidence from in vitro experiments and animal studies suggests that 1,25(OH)2D3 works primarily by inhibiting the activity of fatty acid synthetase (FASN), reducing the accumulation of intracellular triglycerides and reducing lipid droplet accumulation in renal tubular cells [34]. Moreover, 1,25(OH)₂D₃ plays an important protective role in renal lipid metabolism by suppressing the inflammatory damage caused by tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and it theoretically can lower the risk of stone formation. In Arab diabetic patients, the frequency of the CYP24A1*3 allele is as high as 32%, with carriers exhibiting a 28% reduction in 1,25(OH)₂D₃ levels, accompanied by increased FASN activity and lipid deposition [7]. This gene‒metabolism interaction is particularly pronounced in KSD patients, suggesting that individualized interventions targeting CYP24A1 polymorphisms (such as tailored vitamin D supplementation protocols) may emerge as novel strategies for preventing metabolic syndrome-related kidney stones.

A prospective National Health Service (NHS) phase 2 study in the UK [35], which included 193,551 participants, revealed a greater correlation (hazard ratio 1.18, P = 0.02) between vitamin D supplementation and kidney stone events. Researchers have hypothesized that this might be closely linked to mutations in the cytochrome P450 family member 24A1 (CYP24A1) and VDR genes induced during drug supplementation. However, in the other two phases of the NHS study, no significant correlation was found.

Although 1,25(OH)2D3 is commonly deficient in the majority of individuals with kidney stones and plays a crucial role in renal lipid metabolism, supplementation with bioactive vitamin D along with calcium significantly elevates the risk of hypercalciuria. Proteomic data indicate increased excretion of proteins related to lipid metabolism/transport in the urine of children with kidney stones and hypercalciuria. These proteins include apolipoprotein A-II (APOA2), apolipoprotein A-IV (APOA4), apolipoprotein C-III (APOC3), liver fatty acid-binding protein (FABPL), and adipocyte fatty acid-binding protein (FABP4). These findings suggest that abnormal lipid metabolism may contribute to the formation of kidney stones [36]. Therefore, early identification of CYP24A1 gene polymorphisms and regular monitoring of urinary calcium levels could be a strategy for preventing kidney stone formation through 1,25(OH)2D3 supplementation.

Apolipoproteins

Apolipoproteins, which are the protein constituents of plasma lipoproteins, exist in five subtypes. The fundamental functions of lipoproteins include lipid transportation and lipoprotein structure stabilization. Moreover, certain apolipoproteins are also involved in the activation of lipoprotein metabolic enzymes and receptor recognition [36].

In extant studies on kidney stones, the research and analysis of apolipoproteins focus on screening outcomes of genomics or proteomics. In a genome-related study on the high-risk factors for kidney stones, substantial differences in the gene expression of apolipoprotein A-II (APOA2), apolipoprotein A-IV (APOA4), apolipoprotein C-III (APOC3), and FABP1 were detected between the kidney stone group and the healthy control group, and these genes were significantly associated with high calcium excretion.

After collecting urine samples from 72 patients with calcium oxalate kidney stone disease (KSD) and 30 healthy controls, Zhu et al. utilized matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF-MS) for proteomic analysis, and these researchers reported that only 13 proteins/peptides exhibit statistically significant differences, among which apolipoprotein A-I (APOA1) serves as an effective biomarker for the noninvasive diagnosis of calcium oxalate KSD [37]. Wang et al. employed electron microscopy to observe the related manifestations of renal nanocrystals in patients with end-stage renal disease and discovered that APOA1 effectively suppresses the abnormal calcification of the renal interstitium, thus preventing the formation of kidney stones [38]. Scholars have also performed proteomic bioinformatics analyses of urinary calculi and stone matrices and identified a total of 1409 proteins within the stone matrix. Among them, APOA1 and apolipoprotein D (APOD) are the most prevalent protein types; however, their functions remain ambiguous [39].

Furthermore, in clinically relevant studies, neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL), a neutrophil gelatinase apolipoprotein, has been extensively investigated because of its significant predictive value for postoperative renal function impairment in patients with KSD [40].

Overall, research on apolipoproteins in the context of kidney stones is still in the exploratory phase. Although some studies have indicated a correlation between apolipoproteins and KSD, screening and identification are still needed. The specific mechanisms and pathways involved need further analysis and research.

Potential applications of liposomes in KSD

Recent advances in lipid metabolism research in tumors [41] highlight mitochondrion-targeted liposomes as an innovative drug delivery platform capable of precise mitochondrial localization in renal tubular epithelial cells through surface modification with triphenylphosphonium cations, demonstrating promising therapeutic potential for kidney stone management. Mechanistically, targeted delivery of PPARα/γ agonists (fenofibrate and pioglitazone) enhances fatty acid β-oxidation in renal tubules, significantly reducing lipid accumulation and inflammatory responses while decreasing renal crystal burden in animal models. Moreover, selective FABP4 inhibition effectively minimizes calcium salt deposition in renal papillae, as evidenced by FABP4 knockout studies showing extensive calcium oxalate crystal distribution. Furthermore, this system enables mitochondrial functional restoration through antioxidant delivery while circumventing thiazide diuretic-associated resistance, collectively offering novel therapeutic strategies for renal lithiasis by modulating lipid metabolic pathways.

Abnormal lipid metabolism leads to organelle damage and promotes stone formation

Apart from alterations in lipid regulatory factors, organelle damage resulting from lipid metabolism may also play a crucial role in stone formation. Recent evidence indicates that changes in mitochondria and the endoplasmic reticulum, as well as cell death caused by lipid metabolism, are associated with the occurrence of kidney stone disease (KSD).

Mitochondrial damage (MD) and endoplasmic reticulum stress (ERS) synergistically promote kidney stone formation. At the microscopic level, the interaction between mitochondria and the endoplasmic reticulum is a key factor in maintaining the function of all organelles and the stability of the cellular environment. Mitochondria serve as the primary energy source for cells, and they regulate oxidative stress, aging, and cell apoptosis. The endoplasmic reticulum is responsible for protein secretion and is the intracellular calcium storage site, maintaining calcium homeostasis. Approximately 20% of the outer mitochondrial membrane is connected to the endoplasmic reticulum membrane, facilitating lipid molecule exchange and calcium signal transduction, which is known as the mitochondria-associated endoplasmic reticulum membrane [42]. Under abnormal lipid metabolism conditions, the accumulation of lipid droplets leads to the production of a large amount of reactive oxygen species (ROS), causing lipid peroxidation reactions and increasing the permeability of the mitochondria-associated endoplasmic reticulum membrane. This results in a large influx of calcium ions into the mitochondria, which affects the activities of respiratory chain complexes I/III and cytochrome c, leading to MD. MD exacerbates the oxidative stress response and damages mitochondrial DNA, resulting in the accumulation of unfolded or misfolded proteins in the endoplasmic reticulum, thereby inducing ERS. Studies have shown that MD and ERS play important roles in the formation of kidney stones induced by high uric acid [43]; the use of ROS inhibitors, such as N-acetylcysteine (NAC), or ERS inhibitors, such as 4-phenylbutyric acid (4-PBA), reduces the deposition of calcium oxalate in Randall plaques, effectively improves mitochondrial function, reduces tissue inflammation and reduces oxidative damage [42, 44]. In addition, 4-phenylbutyric acid effectively reduces the expression levels of osteopontin (OPN) and ERS-related proteins, such as glucose-regulated protein 78 (GRP78) and C/EBP homologous protein, while inhibiting other ERS-related proteins and transmembrane signal molecules, such as protein kinase- like ER kinase (PERK), inositol-requiring enzyme 1 (IRE1), and activating transcription factor 6 (ATF6). This leads to a reduction in the activation of downstream signaling pathways, cell apoptosis and inflammatory pathways, ultimately reducing the formation of Randall plaques and preventing stone formation [37]. Notably, mitochondrion-targeted liposome technology provides a new tool for intervening in this pathway. By delivering mitochondrial protectants (such as MitoQ) or endoplasmic reticulum stress inhibitors (such as 4-phenylbutyric acid), this technology blocks ROS-mediated lipid peroxidation and prevents calcium homeostasis imbalance, thereby inhibiting the formation of Randall plaques. For example, 4-phenylbutyric acid reduces the protein expression of GRP78 and CHOP in renal tubular cells while decreasing crystal adhesion rates [41].

An in-depth study of the mechanism of kidney stone formation has revealed that the downstream autophagy and ferroptosis activated by MD and ERS are related to lipid metabolism, especially ferroptosis, which is closely related to the abnormal lipid metabolism mechanism of KSD. Ferroptosis, a new form of cell death, has been widely studied because of its role in promoting the formation of KSD. Hyperoxaluria is a key factor in this mechanism [45, 46]. The main characteristics of ferroptosis are iron-dependent phospholipid peroxidation, lipid peroxidation, and iron accumulation, which lead to mitochondrial dysfunction and reduced antioxidant capacity, resulting in the release and accumulation of mitochondrial ROS. Continuous high-level oxalic acid stimulation further deteriorates mitochondrial function under weak antioxidant capacity, maintaining a vicious cycle of ferroptosis. During this process, oxidative stress damage and inflammatory responses contribute to the development of KSD [47]. As the severity of ferroptosis increases, the neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL) expression levels, kidney injury molecule-1 (Kim-1) expression levels, serum creatinine levels, urea nitrogen levels, and number of TUNEL-positive renal tissue cells increase, whereas renal function significantly decreases [48]. In addition, the activity of superoxide dismutase (SOD) decreases, whereas the content of malondialdehyde (MDA) increases, reflecting the antioxidant and oxidative stress status of mice with kidney stones [13]. Lipid peroxidation is the core event of ferroptosis, which triggers membrane lipid peroxidation damage through reactive oxygen species (ROS), destroys the structure and function of normal cells, and affects the cell microenvironment and calcium homeostasis. In the ethylene glycol-induced kidney stone rat model and the oxalate-treated HK-2 cell model, the expression of lysosome-associated membrane protein 2 (LAMP2) and murine double minute 4 (MDM4) decreases, and the expression of LAMP2 and MDM4 is positively correlated with ferroptosis inhibitors [such as solute carrier family 7 member 11 (SLC7A11), glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), heat shock protein beta-1 (HSPB1), and nuclear factor erythroid 2-related Factor 2 (NFE2L2)] but negatively correlated with ferroptosis drivers [such as ChaC glutathione-specific gamma-glutamylcyclotransferase 1 (CHAC1)]. Treatment with the Fer-1 ferroptosis inhibitor partially reverses oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation while simultaneously reversing the changes in the expression of LAMP2 and MDM4. Therefore, ferroptosis may be involved in the formation of kidney stones by regulating LAMP2 and MDM4 [49].

In addition, ferroptosis regulates cell physiological processes by affecting lipid metabolism pathways, mainly by disrupting the mitochondrial membrane potential and permeability, interfering with the function of the mitochondrial respiratory chain and the oxidation of free fatty acids, promoting the formation and accumulation of intracellular lipid droplets, and thus exacerbating the stress level of subcellular organelles [50].

When the stress level reaches a certain threshold, cells initiate the autophagy process. Autophagy uses lysosomes to degrade damaged organelles and unfolded proteins to maintain cell homeostasis, and autophagy plays an important role in the growth, development, and aging of organisms. Singh et al. first reported that autophagy regulates lipid metabolism by decomposing triglycerides and preventing fatty acid degeneration [51]. Observation of lipid transport in autophagosomes under a microscope has demonstrated that the expression level of the light chain 3 (LC3) autophagosome protein is related to the reduction in lipid droplets and that the ratio of LC3-II/I can be used to estimate the level of autophagy. Studies related to KSD have reported that after the occurrence of endoplasmic reticulum stress (ERS), excessive autophagy is induced through the PERK/eIF2α pathway, regulating cell damage and apoptosis. Phenylbutyric acid, an ERS inhibitor, significantly inhibits excessive autophagy, reduces cell apoptosis, protects renal function, and prevents the formation of KSD [52, 53].

However, because the occurrence of KSD is related to multiple metabolic factors, organelle stress often triggers a cascade reaction, which should be further comprehensively observed from a macroscopic perspective. The organelle stress response related to lipid metabolism is still in the exploratory stage, and the specific coordination mechanism and interaction mode are still unclear and need further research for clarification.

Discussion

This review synthesizes compelling evidence that dysregulated lipid metabolism orchestrates KSD progression through a multilayered pathogenic network, revealing novel mechanistic insights and therapeutic opportunities. At the molecular level, the duality of PPARα/γ signaling presents a fascinating paradox—while PPARα activation attenuates renal crystal formation in rodent models via increased fatty acid β-oxidation, its synergy with PPARγ agonists unexpectedly exacerbates renal injury—highlighting species-specific metabolic adaptations, particularly in gut microbiota-mediated drug metabolism [23, 24]. Similarly, FABP1 and FABP4 play context-dependent roles, in which early-stage upregulation mitigates lipotoxicity through PPARα crosstalk, but chronic suppression—as observed in metabolic syndrome—accelerates tubular damage by impairing lipid clearance and antioxidant defenses [31–33].

The organelle stress nexus further underscores the complexity of lipid-driven KSD pathogenesis. Mitochondrial–ER miscommunication precipitates a self-amplifying triad of mitochondrial dysfunction, ERS and ferroptosis. Key findings reveal that disrupted mitochondria-associated membranes (MAMs) dysregulate calcium flux, promoting hydroxyapatite nucleation in Randall’s plaques. Additionally, impairment of the GPX4/SLC7A11 axis exacerbates iron-dependent lipid peroxidation, directly linking ferroptosis to tubular barrier rupture in calcium oxalate models [47–49]. In addition to investigating intracellular stress, clinical and preclinical data highlight gut–renal metabolic crosstalk, in which dysbiosis-driven lipid rewiring (e.g., Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes imbalance) elevates hepatic FASN/DGAT1 expression, increasing circulatory triglyceride levels. Concurrently, renal SCFA receptor (FFAR2)-mediated NLRP3 inflammasome activation establishes a feedforward loop between lipid inflammation and crystal deposition, suggesting a novel therapeutic target.

Despite these mechanistic advances, translating preclinical insights into clinical practice faces significant challenges. Pharmacological paradoxes, PPARα agonists inhibit stone formation in mice by regulating phospholipase metabolism, but phase III trials in humans have shown that they may increase the risk of cardiovascular events, underscore the complexities of PPAR subtype-specific regulation [23, 24]. Similarly, FABP4 inhibitors can reduce crystal deposition in animal models, but may interfere with adipose tissue metabolism, thereby inducing insulin resistance [32]; Drug delivery hurdles further complicate therapeutic translation.Emerging nanotechnological solutions, including TPP-conjugated liposomes for increased renal mitochondrial accumulation (3-fold greater than that of free drug) and dextran-PEG nanoparticles for the codelivery of PPARα agonists and FABP4 inhibitors, offer promising avenues.however, The glomerular filtration threshold (< 8 nm) limits carrier size. Although liposomes modified with triphenylphosphine (TPP) can increase mitochondrial accumulation, their long-term hepatic toxicity is still unclear [41, 54]. Systemic exposure to lipid modulators risks off-target effects, such as PPARγ agonist-induced fluid retention. Regulation and personalized treatment needs, while CYP24A1 polymorphisms dictate the efficacy of 1,25(OH)₂D₃-mediated FASN suppression, the associated hypercalciuria risk necessitates genotype-guided dosing protocols to balance therapeutic benefits and adverse effects [34, 35]. Biomarker development also presents gaps. For example, urinary APOA1 shows promising diagnostic potential (AUC = 0.987), yet its mechanistic role in stone matrix calcification inhibition remains unclear [37, 38]. Conversely, the prognostic utility of FABP1 in postobstructive renal recovery (sensitivity: 87.5%) warrants validation in larger cohorts to establish its clinical applicability [33].

Interdisciplinary convergence, particularly leveraging innovations from oncology, presents transformative opportunities for KSD management. Mitochondrion-targeted nanotherapeutics, repurposed from cancer research, enable precision targeting through brush border antigen-guided liposomes (e.g., megalin ligands) that achieve tubular-selective drug deposition, mirroring successes in reducing doxorubicin cardiotoxicity [41]. Additionally, pH-responsive carriers exploit acidic renal microenvironments for triggered drug release, minimizing systemic spillover. Multiomics integration—combining genomics (CYP24A1 haplotypes), proteomics (APOA1/APOC3 profiles), and microbiomics (SCFA-producing taxa)—could enable patient stratification for tailored interventions, such as probiotics in dysbiosis-predominant subgroups.

In the future, the field must prioritize mechanistic elucidation and technological innovation to transition from symptomatic stone management to pathogenesis-focused metabolic prevention. Key priorities include deciphering how CYP24A1 variants modulate FASN-dependent lipogenesis via vitamin D metabolites and mapping spatial transcriptomics of PPAR isoforms across nephron segments to refine targeting strategies. AI-driven drug design, which uses tools such as AlphaFold to predict PPARγ-ligand binding conformations, could accelerate the development of novel agonists. Organ-on-chip models emulating gut/renal axis interactions offer a high-throughput platform to screen microbiota-modulating therapies. Clinically, phase 2 trials testing fenofibrate + MitoQ combinations in metabolic syndrome-associated KSD, with endpoints including urinary oxalate and FABP1 dynamics, could validate therapeutic efficacy. Similarly, microbiome intervention studies assessing Akkermansia muciniphila supplementation for SCFA-mediated NLRP3 suppression may open new avenues for prevention (Tables 1, 2, 3 and 4).

Conclusion and future perspectives

Abnormal lipid metabolism drives KSD pathogenesis through the molecular regulatory imbalance/organelle stress/inflammatory cascade axis, with key regulators such as PPARs, FABPs, and apolipoproteins emerging as promising therapeutic targets. While preclinical studies have demonstrated the efficacy of lipid-modulating agents (e.g., PPARγ agonists and FABP4 stabilizers), clinical translation faces challenges, including species-specific discrepancies (e.g., opposing effects of PPARα agonists in rodents vs. humans) and safety concerns related to systemic drug exposure.

Mitochondrion-targeted liposomes exemplify the potential of interdisciplinary approaches, enabling precise delivery of antioxidants or lipid metabolism modulators to renal tubular cells. However, optimizing carrier biocompatibility and overcoming glomerular filtration barriers remain critical hurdles. Future research should prioritize the following: (1) multiomics-guided precision medicine to integrate genomics (e.g., CYP24A1 polymorphisms), proteomics (e.g., APOA1 as a biomarker), and gut microbiome data to stratify patients and tailor interventions (e.g., vitamin D supplementation in CYP24A1 variant carriers); (2) gut/renal axis modulation to explore probiotics or short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) therapies to correct dysbiosis-linked lipid dysregulation, thereby mitigating inflammation and crystal adhesion; and (3) advanced drug delivery systems to engineer renal tubule-specific nanocarriers (e.g., FABP1-functionalized liposomes) to enhance targeting efficiency while minimizing off-organ toxicity.

By systematically addressing these knowledge gaps, the field may evolve from a reactive approach of symptomatic stone removal to a paradigm of targeted metabolic prevention, thereby mitigating the worldwide recurrence burden of KSD.
