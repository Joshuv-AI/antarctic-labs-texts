Mental health outcomes and psychosocial correlates among adult members of business families in Germany (WIFU-GESUND): study protocol for a representative cross-sectional study
Authors: Philipp Kitta, Magdalena Wendt, Jan Josupeit, Heiko Kleve, Tom Rüsen, Christina Hunger-Schoppe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractIntroductionMental health is an urgent public health issue with social and economic relevance. Business families play a key economic role, yet their specific mental health challenges remain underexplored. High performance expectations, succession pressures and the wish for discretion may obscure psychological stress. The WIFU-GESUND study addresses this gap by conducting the first representative mental health survey among adult members of business families in Germany.Methods and analysisThis cross-sectional study targets adults with legal ownership in German family businesses. A representative sample will be drawn from the MARKUS database of Creditreform, supplemented by a voluntary sample via the WIFU Foundation. Data will be collected via computer-assisted web interviewing/computer-assisted telephone interviewing using validated brief self-report screening measures, including depression (Patient Health Questionnaire), anxiety (Generalised Anxiety Disorder), somatic complaints and selected single-item symptom indicators from the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, 10th Revision (ICD-10) Symptom Rating and alcohol use (Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test) and drug use (Drug Use Disorders Identification Test). Predictors include chronic stress (Trier Inventory for Chronic Stress-Short Screening Scale for Chronic Stress), family functioning (Experience in Social Systems), self-efficacy (ASKU), impostor feelings (Impostor Phenomenon Short Scale), physical activity and business-related contextual factors. As the study does not include diagnostic assessments, it estimates positive screening results for probable mental health problems and related self-reported mental health indicators rather than diagnostic prevalence. Analyses will include descriptive statistics, comparisons with population-based reference values, regression models and exploratory analyses. Missing data will be handled using multiple imputation.Ethics and disseminationEthics approval was granted by the Ethics Committee of Witten/Herdecke University (Application No. 26/2023). Independent data collection is ensured by USUMA GmbH. Introduction

Mental disorders have become one of the most pressing public health challenges in high-income countries.1 Globally, approximately 15% of the working-age population suffers from a mental disorder at any given time.2 In Germany, roughly 27.8% of the adult population, that is about 17.8 million individuals, are affected annually: the most prevalent diagnoses include anxiety disorders (15.4%), affective disorders (9.8%) and substance-related disorders (5.7%).3 Mental health burdens are unequally distributed along socioeconomic lines, with individuals of lower socioeconomic status being disproportionately affected, experiencing higher stress levels, reduced access to psychological and social resources and thus greater vulnerability.4 5 In fact, mental disorders cost EU (European Union) countries more than 4% of GDP (Gross Domestic Product) annually, driven by direct healthcare costs, productivity losses (eg, absenteeism and early retirement) and social expenditures.4 Studying specific population groups is therefore a central approach in public mental health research, allowing differentiated insight into psychological risks and resource patterns.6 7

One understudied but economically critical group is that of business families. While employee health has received considerable attention through corporate health initiatives,8 members of business families often remain unaddressed, despite their dual exposure to entrepreneurial responsibilities and the emotional complexities of intergenerational family life.9,11

Considering their substantial contribution to economic output and financial stability, the mental health of business families represents a relevant concern for public mental health.11 Impaired psychological functioning within owning families can undermine decision-making, delay succession processes, exacerbate conflicts and indirectly destabilise thousands of employees.9 12 13 In Germany, where the majority of firms are family-led, their mental health is therefore not merely a private or organisational concern but a matter of societal relevance.14

This relevance is not limited to business families as a specific population but also relates to the roles many adult members of business families occupy. Many hold leadership and personnel responsibilities and may therefore influence not only organisational decision-making but also how mental health is perceived, discussed and addressed in the workplace. Especially in economically challenging times, the ways in which individuals in such roles perceive stress, respond to psychological burden and evaluate or enable help-seeking may shape workplace culture, support practices and the visibility of mental health at work. The importance of this question therefore extends beyond business families in a narrow sense: the findings may also offer insights into other individuals with leadership and personnel responsibilities across different management levels. Because attitudes and behaviours in such roles can directly or indirectly affect many employees, the mental health of this population is relevant not only at the individual or organisational level but also from a public mental health perspective. The findings may help identify relevant risk and protective factors, improve understanding of help-seeking barriers and stigma in a socioeconomically influential population and inform future prevention strategies, workplace-related support approaches and subsequent public mental health research.

Mental health arises within social contexts: individuals with strong social relationships show a 50% increased likelihood of survival.15 16 In business families, high levels of family cohesion, cross-generational support and a shared vision for the future can act as protective factors.17,19 At the same time, these structures may foster specific stressors. Intergenerational loyalty dynamics, implicit role expectations and performance-driven narratives can shape family life and cause emotional strain.20 21

Family members often occupy multiple overlapping roles within the firm, acting as shareholders, executives, successors and relatives at the same time. These roles can pull against each other, creating role conflicts and chronic stress.22,24 Recent studies emphasise that emotional and relational regulation within the family system is crucial to the success of succession processes.25 Simultaneously, relational capital may buffer psychological strain and promote resilience in family businesses.26

Such dynamics can also foster psychological insecurity, such as impostor feelings, especially when responsibilities are transferred prematurely.27 28 Moreover, the high value placed on discretion and reputation in many business families may hinder open discussions of mental strain, suppress help-seeking behaviour and reinforce stigma.29 In this context, mental health must be understood as a systemic issue: shaped not only by individual resources such as self-efficacy30 but also by the family system’s openness to reflection, external support and flexible role negotiation.18 Furthermore, psychosocial factors such as mental health literacy, perceived stigma and help-seeking behaviour influence how individuals perceive and cope with psychological strain and affect symptom development.31 Physical activity has also been shown to be a protective factor.32,34

The WIFU-GESUND study addresses this gap by providing the first representative, population-based evidence on screening-based mental health outcomes and psychosocial correlates among adult members of business families in Germany. The study aims to deepen our understanding of mental health as a contextual and systemically embedded phenomenon by bridging clinical psychology, public mental health and family business research. The selection of constructs was guided by the scoping review by Peitz et al35 and refined through an interdisciplinary focus group and informal feedback from the target population. The study collects data on several psychosocial variables including chronic stress,36 substance use,37 existential anxiety,38 physical activity,32,34 self-efficacy30 39 and family cohesion.20

The present study is grounded in the dual-continua model developed in public mental health research,40 41 which differentiates between psychological well-being and diagnosable mental disorders as two distinct but interrelated dimensions. This model highlights that significant socio-psycho-physiological distress can be present even when outward functionality appears intact. The distinction is particularly relevant in the context of business families, where high performance demands, early role assumption and intergenerational loyalty norms are prominent.23 24 28 In such systems, socio-psycho-physiological distress stimulating symptoms of psychological disorders may easily be overlooked or downplayed, for example, due to stigma, familial expectations or a strong drive to protect the family image.9 11 Members of the next generation are especially likely to find themselves caught in the tension between autonomy and family obligation.22 Figure 1 illustrates the theoretical framework of the study, summarising key psychosocial predictors, the moderator and mental health-related outcomes based on the considerations outlined above.

Methods and analysis

The entire study protocol is reported in accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) statement for cross-sectional studies.

Design

This WIFU-GESUND study is a representative study to investigate population-based findings on business family members. The project is based at the Witten Institute for Family Business (WIFU), a leading academic centre for family business research. The acronym GESUND stands for ‘Gesundheit Unternehmerfamilien DACH (Germany, Austria, Switzerland).’ This study adheres to established methodological standards for sampling, recruitment and documentation to ensure the validity and generalisability of its results.42 43 A representative sample enables population-based inferences by ensuring that key characteristics of the target population are adequately reflected.44 This is particularly important for under-researched groups such as business families. The study collects data on several psychosocial variables, including chronic stress,36 substance use,37 existential anxiety,38 physical activity,32,34 self-efficacy30 39 and family cohesion.20 These constructs were selected based on theoretical relevance, previous empirical evidence and participatory refinement to ensure their contextual fit for family business systems.

Data collection will take place from 17 March 2025 to 30 June 2025 and will be conducted by the independent German research institute USUMA.

Eligibility criteria

The population of this study includes adults (aged 18 and older) who hold ownership shares in German family businesses. Eligible participants comprise members of the founding generation, their descendants and other legally entitled individuals whose shares can be transferred across generations. Employees without ownership stakes and external executives with no legal or economic ties to the business are excluded.

Sampling methods

Main sample

To ensure statistical representativeness, the company-based sampling frame was derived from the MARKUS database of Creditreform (as of 2023), one of the largest business information databases in the German-speaking region, containing current information on more than 2.2 million economically active companies in Germany, Austria and Luxembourg. For the present study, the database was used to identify companies in selected industry sectors and turnover classes, together with available ownership-related information on registered owners, shareholders and managing persons. To increase the likelihood of reaching family businesses, companies not led by individuals with at least a 25% ownership share were excluded from the sampling process. The resulting company-level sampling frame covered major industry sectors representing approximately 89% of around 280 000 economically active companies in Germany with an annual turnover of at least €2 million. Because the database was primarily structured at the company level and final eligibility had to be confirmed during the screening process, the exact number of eligible adult owners/shareholders could not be determined a priori with precision. From this sampling frame, 10 000 company addresses were selected using a disproportionately stratified random sampling approach based on industry sector and company size (annual turnover). Industry sectors were retained proportionally, whereas turnover groups were sampled disproportionately in order to oversample larger firms. This strategy was chosen to ensure sufficient coverage of analytically important but less frequent company segments, particularly larger firms, while preserving broad sectoral coverage and supporting subgroup analyses across key parts of the business population. Because this disproportional design does not precisely reflect the underlying population distribution, statistical weighting will be applied during data analysis to restore population-level proportions where appropriate. This methodological approach was intended to improve coverage of relevant business segments and to reduce potential selection bias.45,48

Supplementary sample

We also recruit participants through the WIFU Foundation’s network as an additional sample. WIFU data are flagged in the dataset and are not part of the stratified random core sample. While they do not influence the representativeness of the core sample, they offer additional analytical value: for example, for comparison of the representative data with data from the WIFU environment in which trainings and individual services have already been introduced in order to stabilise or improve the mental health of business families. If necessary, deviations from expected distributions can be statistically corrected through post-stratification weighting.

Sample size

The sample size calculation was based on the study’s primary outcome, namely, the frequency of positive depression screening results among adult members of business families compared with population-based reference values. Depressive symptoms assessed with the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-2) were used as the reference outcome because they represent one of the most prevalent and well-documented mental health indicators in the general population.3 Assuming α=0.05 and power (1–β) = 0.80, a one-sample z-test yielded a minimum required sample size of n=342. A target sample size of n=500 was defined to improve the precision of the primary outcome estimates and to support secondary and exploratory analyses with greater stability. However, these additional analyses are not considered equally powered confirmatory tests.

Survey construction

The scoping review by Peitz et al35 guided the selection of constructs for the WIFU-GESUND study survey as it identifies evidence-based indicators for multidimensional assessments of mental health. Its development was informed by international research, notably the holistic framework for mental health in business families proposed by Tetzlaff et al.11 To ensure content validity and practical relevance, an internal interdisciplinary focus group comprising professionals from psychotherapy, psychology, ethnology, sociology, mediation and family business research evaluated the survey. This process was complemented by informal feedback from business family members, the target population. Over a 6-month period, our research team conducted three structured sessions to continuously review and refine the survey. Final construct decisions were made during the third meeting on 16 December 2024.

Pre-test

A pre-test phase served the evaluation of the study survey, including the composition of the different constructs to the single items. A random sample of 500 businesses was drawn using the same sampling methodology as in the main study. Of these, 20 business family members completed the study survey. Based on their feedback, minor linguistic adjustments were made to improve item comprehensibility. We removed one vignette-based module, originally included to facilitate participant engagement with the topic of mental health, as the structured sequence of the mental health instruments (eg, PHQ-2, Generalised Anxiety Disorder (GAD-2) and ICD-10 Symptom Rating (ISR)) sufficiently supported a gradual and non-intrusive introduction to the subject matter. Throughout the main data collection, no further changes were made to the procedures, methods or design.

Patient and public involvement

Members of the target population (business families) were involved informally during questionnaire development through feedback from individuals with lived experience of family business dynamics. This input supported the contextual relevance and comprehensibility of selected survey items. However, they were not formally involved in the co-production of the study and were not involved in defining research questions, selecting outcome measures, reporting or planning dissemination.

Procedure

Data collection was designed to maximise accessibility while maintaining methodological rigour. A combination of CAWI (computer-assisted web interviewing) and CATI (computer-assisted telephone interviewing) was employed. Invitations were sent via post with individualised CAWI access links, followed by telephone outreach. This multimodal approach helps to reduce access barriers and ensures a higher response rate. To monitor participation patterns, a flow chart documenting entry, completion and dropout points will be maintained throughout the data collection process.

Reasons for non-participation

Several factors may contribute to non-participation in this representative survey among members of business families. First, not all contacted individuals may meet the inclusion criterion of holding a legally documented ownership share, for example, due to outdated address records. In some cases, the invitation may not have reached the intended member of the business family, either because it was filtered out within the company or because certain businesses had previously objected to the use of their data for specific purposes, such as participation in research studies. This could not be pre-filtered during sampling and primarily concerns larger enterprises. Second, the estimated time commitment (approximately 20–25 min) may be perceived as too demanding given ongoing entrepreneurial obligations. Third, the topic of mental health may still be regarded as sensitive or stigmatised in some family businesses, leading to hesitation or refusal to participate. In addition, unexpected business priorities may result in incomplete responses or dropouts. Such challenges are typical of population-based studies targeting high-responsibility groups. The exact number of non-participants and the reasons for non-participation will be reported after the data collection has been completed.

Measures

A detailed overview of all measures used in the study is provided in online supplemental table 1.

Demography

Personal demography

Participants will be asked to specify their status within the business: (1) member of the founding family (eg, descendant and individual entitled to shares within the family), (2) status (eg, shareholder and equity holder) (3) no affiliation with the owner or shareholder group. Personal demographic data encompass sex, year of birth, number of siblings, sibling rank, highest education, relationship status and number of children. The MacArthur Scale of (MacArthur SSS Scale)49 as a single-item measure assesses a person’s perceived rank relative to others in their group. Participants view a drawing of a ladder with 10 rungs, where those who are best off in terms of money, education and jobs are at the top and those who are worst off at the bottom.

Family business demography

Business-related demography includes the position of the participant in the company, length of time in the business position, generational affiliation of the family business, other members of the business and their generational affiliation, year the business was founded, industry affiliation, number of employees, category of annual turnover, ownership share in the company, number of individual family members with company shares, year of last leadership change, leadership logic of the business and degree of attachment to the business.

Symptoms

The PHQ-2 measures depression with two items over the last 2 weeks (4-point Likert scale: ‘Not at all’ to ‘Nearly every day’) internal consistency of α=0.83.50 The GAD-2 measures generalised anxiety with two items over the last 2 weeks (4-point Likert scale: ‘Not at all’ to ‘Nearly every day’) with internal consistency of α=0.80.51 The Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test (AUDIT-C) assesses alcohol-related problems over the past 12 months using three items on a 5-point Likert scale (‘Never’ to ‘4 times or more per week’), with internal consistency estimates ranging from α=0.75 to 0.86.52 The Drug Use Disorders Identification Test measures drug-related problems with six items over the last year (4-point Likert scale: ‘Never’ to ‘4 times or more per week’) with internal consistency of α=0.80.53 54 The ISR55 uses a 5-point Likert scale (‘Does not apply’ to ‘Applies extremely’) and includes both multi-item subscales and clinically relevant single items: The Somatoform Disorders subscale assesses medically unexplained physical complaints with three items over the past 2 weeks (α=0.78). In addition, the ISR includes single items addressing key domains such as sleep problems, suicidal ideation and contextual psychological stressors (eg, due to family or work), each referring to the past 2 weeks. While internal consistency cannot be calculated for single-item measures, prior research suggests acceptable test–retest reliability across ISR items overall.55 Self-rated mental health is assessed using a single item that captures an individual’s overall perception of their mental health over the past 30 days, rated on a 5-point Likert scale from ‘Excellent’ to ‘Poor’. Despite being a single-item measure, it has demonstrated acceptable test–retest reliability.56

Sociopsychophysiological functioning

The Experience in Social Systems assesses individuals’ experience with their key personal social system (eg, family, partner and friends) with 12 items over the last 2 weeks (6-point Likert scale: ‘Not at all’ to ‘Entirely’) with satisfactory to excellent internal consistency.17 The Trier Inventory for Chronic Stress-SSCS measures self-perceived stress with 12 items in the last 3 months (5-point Likert scale: ‘Never’ to ‘Very often’), showing good internal consistency (α=0.91).57 58 The General Self-Efficacy Scale (ASKU) assesses individuals’ belief in their ability to cope with difficult situations using three items, rated on a 5-point Likert scale (‘Does not apply at all’ to ‘Applies completely’), with internal consistency ranging from α=0.81 to 0.86.39 The Impostor Phenomenon Short Scale measures the subjective perception of lacking knowledge and skills with three items, rated on a 5-point Likert scale (‘Does not apply at all’ to ‘Applies completely’). Internal consistency has been reported as satisfactory (α=0.78).22 Existential anxiety measures one’s belief that the company is at risk of existential threat and to what extent this affects one’s mental health, with two items (binary option: ‘yes’, ‘no’; 5-point Likert scale: ‘Very low’ to ‘Very high’).59 Physical activity was assessed using two items, the first on how many days per week participants engage in physical activity that leads to sweating or shortness of breath. The second asked about the average duration of activity on those days, with options (‘less than 10 minutes’, ‘10 to less than 30 minutes’, ‘30 to less than 60 min’ and ‘60 min or more’). Based on responses, weekly physical activity was categorised into ‘no activity’, ‘up to 2 hours’ and ‘regularly ≥2 hours’, allowing estimation of adherence to WHO physical activity guidelines.27 60 As this is a derived two-item index rather than a reflective scale, internal consistency (Cronbach’s α) is not applicable. Mental health–related absenteeism was assessed with a single item referring to the number of days an individual had been absent from work or other responsibilities due to mental health conditions within a specified time frame.61 Responses were given on a 6-point scale ranging from ‘zero days’ to ‘more than 28 days or 4 weeks’. Strain at the family–business interface was assessed using a set of items specifically developed to capture perceived psychological strain arising from the intersection of family dynamics and business-related pressures. Participants rated the perceived impact of intra-family conflicts and business-related tensions on their mental well-being and professional functioning using two items (5-point Likert scale: ‘Very low’ to ‘Very high’).

Family mental health

To contextualise mental health within family system dynamics, participants were asked whether any family members had ever experienced a mental disorder, either currently or in the past. If applicable, respondents indicated the type of symptoms (open-ended) and the role of the affected person (eg, parent, sibling and child). These open-format responses provide insight into the perceived salience of mental illness in the broader family system. Due to their open-ended and retrospective nature, the items are not psychometrically scored but serve as a qualitative indicator of contextual familial burden.

Mental health literacy

Mental health literacy was assessed using a custom-developed item set that was designed to reflect the sociocultural and systemic context of business families. Items were derived from existing international instruments and refined through expert consultation. Several items were adapted from the Mental Health Literacy Scale by O’Connor and Casey,62 a validated tool designed to measure knowledge and beliefs about mental disorders that aid their recognition, management or prevention. All adaptations were conducted in accordance with academic standards for instrument modification and context-specific tailoring.

Help-seeking behaviour was measured with six binary items assessing whether participants had ever used or were currently using support services for psychological distress: (1) psychotherapy, (2) medical consultation or (3) coaching/mediation/other counselling services (response options: ‘yes’/‘no’). Barriers to help-seeking were assessed using one multiple-choice question allowing the selection of potential reasons for avoiding therapy (eg, lack of time, doubts about effectiveness, stigma concerns and uncertainty about access). Participants could select multiple options or indicate ‘no barrier’. Perceived stigma and attitudes were captured by seven items addressing concerns such as social distancing, familial devaluation and openness toward assigning responsibility or succession roles to individuals with a mental health history. All items were rated on 5- to 6-point Likert scales (eg, from ‘definitely not’ to ‘definitely yes’; one item includes the option ‘already occurred’). One additional item measured participant’s perceived preparedness to deal with mental health issues within the family: ‘How well prepared do you feel to address psychological problems of family members?’ (5-point Likert scale: ‘not at all prepared’ to ‘very well prepared’). Given the exploratory and context-specific nature of these adapted items, psychometric properties have not yet been established. However, psychometric validation is planned as part of the study, including exploratory factor analysis (EFA) and the calculation of internal consistency coefficients based on a subset of the sample (n≈200).63 This procedure is detailed in the analytic strategy section.

Adverse events

If acute suicidal thoughts are detected during the survey (eg, via the ISR),56 appropriate safeguards are activated depending on the survey mode. In CATI, trained interviewers follow a protocol-based procedure to provide help resources and, if necessary, initiate a supportive response in consultation with USUMA. In CAWI, participants are immediately shown on-screen information with mental health support contacts and emergency services. Participants are also given the opportunity to report any discomfort or negative experiences before and after the assessment. These responses will be documented and included in the final reporting of study findings.

Analytic strategy

All statistical analyses will be conducted using established software packages (eg, R or SPSS). Missing data will be assessed descriptively, including dropout rates, patterns of missingness and statistical tests for data missing completely at random. If data are missing at random, multiple imputation using chained equations (10 imputations) will be applied. For sensitivity analyses, complete-case analyses will be conducted in parallel.

Descriptive and group analyses

Data analysis will proceed in multiple steps. First, descriptive statistics will be computed for key variables, including mental health indicators (eg, depression, anxiety and somatic complaints), protective psychological resources (eg, self-efficacy) and business-related contextual factors (eg, generational affiliation, ownership roles and company size). Frequencies, means, SD and CIs will be reported as appropriate. In addition, the presence of mental illness in first-degree relatives will be included as an exploratory predictor to examine its contextual influence on symptom severity, help-seeking behaviour and attitudes toward mental illness.

Subsequently, group comparisons will be conducted using t-tests, χ2 tests or non-parametric alternatives such as the Mann-Whitney U test to identify differences in mental health outcomes between relevant subgroups (eg, participants with high vs low mental health status). To minimise potential bias resulting from disproportionate sampling, appropriate post-stratification weighting procedures will be applied during the analysis phase if sample distributions deviate significantly from population parameters (eg, industry size and ownership structure).

Regression models

To examine the associations specified in H2, regression models appropriate to the scale level of the respective outcomes will be employed. These models will include selected psychosocial predictors and business-specific variables (eg, ownership role and company size). In addition, the presence of mental illness in first-degree relatives will be included as an exploratory predictor to assess the contextual influence of family mental health burden on individual outcomes. To examine H3, regression models appropriate to the scale level of the respective outcomes will be used to test whether chronic stress moderates the associations between family functioning and depressive and anxiety symptom burden.

Exploratory analyses and psychometric validation

Beyond the pre-specified hypotheses, the richness of the dataset allows for additional exploratory analyses. For example, we may examine whether individuals with pronounced impostor feelings exhibit unique patterns of psychological distress, or whether generational differences exist in the handling of mental health issues. The analytical approach will be iteratively refined and any adaptations in response to data-driven insights or emerging research questions will be transparently reported and methodologically justified. Exploratory analyses will examine whether mental health literacy is relevant to the association between chronic stress and mental health symptom burden among adult members of business families. Given the exploratory character and the partly context-specific assessment of these constructs, these analyses are considered hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

Given the innovative and context-specific construction of the MHL (Mental Health Literacy) item set, an EFA will be conducted on a randomly selected subsample (n≈200) to identify its underlying factor structure and evaluate internal consistency. Factor extraction will follow principal axis factoring with oblique rotation. The number of factors will be determined using eigenvalues>1, scree plot inspection and theoretical interpretability. Cronbach’s alpha will be calculated for all extracted factors. Items with poor or ambiguous loadings will be considered for revision or exclusion in order to improve the psychometric evaluation of the MHL item set.

Ethics and dissemination

This study has been reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Witten/Herdecke University (Application No. 26/2023) and will be conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.64 Data protection complies with the European General Data Protection Regulation and all project members have signed confidentiality agreements. Participation is voluntary and all participants will receive detailed written information prior to enrolment, including explanations of the study purpose, data confidentiality and the right to withdraw at any time without any consequences. Informed consent will be obtained electronically before the start of the assessment.

To ensure confidentiality, participants receive a unique, pseudonymised study code. This allows them to temporarily pause and resume the survey without using personal identifiers. Once submitted, responses are no longer linkable to individual participants. The results of the WIFU-GESUND study will be disseminated through peer-reviewed journals, stakeholder reports and presentations at national and international academic conferences. The findings will be made available to business families through targeted outreach formats, including policy briefs and webinars, to promote practical implementation and support evidence-based prevention strategies.

Discussion

The present study constitutes the first representative mental health survey among adult members of business families in Germany. This sample is sufficiently powered to allow for meaningful screening-based estimates of self-reported mental health symptoms and related indicators, including depression, anxiety, somatic complaints and substance use. Selected single-item indicators will be interpreted more cautiously as supplementary or exploratory findings.

The WIFU-GESUND study addresses a critical research gap at the intersection of public mental health and family business studies. It systematically examines self-reported, screening-based mental health indicators (eg, depression, anxiety and substance use) among business family members in relation to their socio-psychological functioning, while also considering psychosocial and business-family-related contextual variables. Although mental health has received growing attention in workplace and population-based research, business families have largely remained underexamined in this context.9 11

By integrating established theoretical frameworks such as the dual-continua model of mental health,40 41 family system theory19 21 and public health indicators,27 the study applies a multidisciplinary perspective. This design allows for a differentiated assessment of both symptoms and protective psychosocial resources. It accounts for individual-level risk factors (eg, chronic stress, impostor feelings and physical inactivity) and contextual factors specific to family businesses (eg, ownership roles, succession dynamics and family functioning). The study responds to calls for greater empirical engagement with the mental health of business families and complements existing theoretical work with representative data and robust methodology.

One of the study’s key innovations lies in its representative sampling design. Whereas prior research has relied on theoretical assumptions or non-representative samples (eg, case studies or convenience samples), the present study enhances external validity and allows for population-level inference. The inclusion of validated screening instruments for both symptomatology (eg, PHQ-2, GAD-2 and AUDIT-C) and contextual predictors (eg, self-efficacy, family functioning, impostor phenomena and physical activity) facilitates the development and testing of empirically grounded hypotheses.

Moreover, the study includes additional psychosocial and context-specific variables such as mental health literacy and perceived stigma. Given the exploratory and partly context-specific nature of these measures, analyses involving these constructs are intended primarily to generate hypotheses and inform future research.

The dual-sampling approach, combining random selection via the MARKUS database of Creditreform with targeted outreach through the WIFU Foundation, adds further analytical value. While only the representative core sample forms the basis for representative inference, the comparative potential of the WIFU sample may yield supplementary insights into the effects of mental health interventions or training programmes already in use in some business families.

The scope and richness of the dataset also allow for exploratory analyses beyond the pre-specified hypotheses. For example, it may be possible to investigate whether individuals with pronounced impostor feelings show distinct profiles of psychological burden or whether generational differences exist in the willingness to address mental health issues within the family system. These additional analyses may help inform targeted support services, contribute to theory-building and inspire future research on the systemic dynamics of business families.

More broadly, findings from this representative survey are expected to highlight the multidimensional nature of mental health in business families, demonstrating that self-reported, screening-based symptoms may coexist with high functional demands and sustained occupational performance. This perspective aligns with Manwell et al’s65 argument that mental health should not be defined solely by the absence of disorder but rather by the presence of positive engagement, social integration and adaptive capacity.

Finally, the study provides a platform for future longitudinal tracking and intervention studies. By establishing an empirically validated baseline of psychological burden and protective factors in business families, it lays the groundwork for evidence-based prevention and mental health promotion in this important yet previously understudied population.

Limitations

The main limitation of this study is its cross-sectional design. This strategy increases the likelihood of accessing a typically hard-to-reach population while still allowing for statistical generalisability. Nonetheless, non-response bias remains a potential limitation, especially considering that stigma or confidentiality concerns may discourage participation among individuals experiencing high psychological strain. This limitation is acknowledged and addressed through transparency in recruitment procedures and statistical weighting during analysis.

Perspectives

In sum, the WIFU-GESUND study contributes to advancing mental health research by focusing on a socioeconomically influential yet underexplored population. Its findings will have implications not only for psychological support services and policy makers but also for the governance and sustainability of family businesses themselves. By making mental health in business families visible, the study aims to reduce stigma, promote early intervention and strengthen systemic resilience in one of the most vital sectors of the economy.
