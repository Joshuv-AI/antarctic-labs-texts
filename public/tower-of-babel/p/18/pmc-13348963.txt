Identification and profiling of HLA-A*02:01-restricted Toxoplasma gondii peptides through immunopeptidomics in HLA-A2.1 transgenic mice
Authors: Ruiying Liang, Xinru Luo, Weixiang Xia, Haotian Gu, Dan Li, Yongyu Gao, Zonghui Zuo, Fengshan Gao, Jiabo Ding, Xinming Tang, Zibin Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHLA class I presentation of pathogen-derived peptides is essential for CD8+ T-cell recognition of Toxoplasma gondii. While in vitro MHC ligands have been documented, the in vivo ligandome during infection progression remains poorly characterized. Here, we employed an MS-based immunopeptidomics approach to directly profile the T. gondii immunopeptidome presented by HLA-A *02:01 in transgenic mice. By employing a hierarchical discovery funnel, our analysis identified a comprehensive repertoire of 3,744 unique T. gondii-derived peptides. Subsequent filtering for canonical 8–12mers, matching the typical binding length for HLA-A *02:01 ligands, established a high-confidence foundational ligandome of 3,433 peptides. Source protein analysis revealed that these peptides originate from diverse parasite proteins, including a substantial proportion of previously uncharacterized hypothetical proteins. Notably, specific ligands were consistently detected across both acute and chronic stages, suggesting stable MHC-I presentation throughout the infection cycle. By integrating in silico predictions with experimental validation, we prioritized 73 high-affinity candidates, five of which exhibited robust HLA-A *02:01 binding capacity in vitro and in vivo. Specifically, we identified a novel ligand derived from glycogen synthase (PGS) and determined its co-crystal structure with HLA-A *02:01, revealing favorable binding architecture. Overall, these findings expand the known HLA-A *02:01-restricted ligand landscape of T. gondii and provide a high-priority list of candidates for future functional validation of CD8+ T-cell immunogenicity.

Introduction

Toxoplasma gondii is an obligate intracellular protozoan parasite that infects a wide range of warm-blooded hosts, including humans [1,2]. Primary infection in immunocompetent individuals is usually asymptomatic; however, the parasite undergoes systemic dissemination and differentiates into slowly replicating bradyzoites, which form dormant tissue cysts within immune-privileged sites such as the brain and skeletal muscle [3–5]. These cysts establish a lifelong chronic infection that is largely resistant to both host immune responses and current pharmacotherapies [6]. The clinical consequences of infection are most severe during pregnancy [7,8]. Vertical transmission of T. gondii across the placental barrier can lead to congenital toxoplasmosis, resulting in a spectrum of fetal and neonatal pathologies including spontaneous abortion, stillbirth, ocular lesions (potentially leading to blindness), and severe neurodevelopmental disorders [9,10]. Current therapeutic regimens, primarily targeting the acute tachyzoite stage, are ineffective against the chronic cyst stage [11,12]. With a global seroprevalence reaching nearly 33%, the ubiquity of T. gondii in both industrialized and developing regions underscores an urgent need for therapeutic innovations capable of eradicating tissue cysts and resolving chronic infection [13].

CD8+ T cells serve as essential effectors of adaptive immunity against intracellular pathogens, mediating immune control through direct cytotoxicity and the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, particularly interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) [14–18]. Extensive evidence from murine models underscores the pivotal role of CD8+ T cells in restricting T. gondii infection [18–22]. Effective activation of these cells is initiated by antigen-specific recognition, which depends on the presentation of pathogen-derived peptides (typically 8–10 amino acids) by major histocompatibility complex class I (MHC I) molecules [23,24]. This process involves the proteasomal degradation of cytosolic antigens, followed by the trimming of peptide precursors by endoplasmic reticulum-resident aminopeptidases (ERAPs) [24]. Despite the vast complexity of the T. gondii proteome, the host CD8+ T cell response is remarkably focused on a narrow repertoire of immunodominant epitopes [25,26]. Notably, the immunogenicity of these epitope peptides is characterized by a pronounced position effect, influencing their interaction within the binding groove [26–28]. This aligns with the observation that CD8+ T cells specific for certain predicted antigens often exhibit suboptimal recognition of naturally infected cells, suggesting that the host MHC-I machinery selectively presents only a discrete subset of the parasite proteome [29]. Although several targets have been identified, the specific MHC-I-restricted epitopes that elicit robust protective immunity across different infection stages remain to be fully elucidated.

In this study, we employed a mass spectrometry (MS)-based immunopeptidomics approach to directly identify T. gondii-derived ligands naturally presented by MHC-I molecules in HLA-A *02:01 transgenic mice. By employing a hierarchical discovery funnel, our analysis yielded a comprehensive repertoire of 3,744 unique pathogen-derived peptides, which as subsequently refined to 3,433 canonical 8–12mers matching the typical binding length for HLA-A *02:01 ligands. Analysis of their protein origins revealed that these ligands derive from a diverse array of parasite proteins, including a substantial proportion of previously uncharacterized hypothetical proteins. Notably, certain ligands were consistently identified across both acute and chronic infection stages, suggesting that uncharacterized functional domains may influence the MHC-I presentation landscape throughout the infection cycle. To prioritize candidates for further investigation, we integrated in silico binding predictions and identified 73 high-affinity candidates. From this selection, five ligands were subsequently validated for their binding capacity both in vitro and in vivo. Furthermore, we resolved the co-crystal structure of a high-affinity ligand derived from glycogen synthase (PGS), which exhibited a favorable binding mode within the HLA-A *02:01 groove. In conclusion, this study significantly expands the known landscape of naturally presented HLA-A *02:01 ligands of T. gondii and establishes a prioritized resource of candidates for future evaluation of CD8+ T-cell immunogenicity and vaccine potential.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

The animal experiments in this study were conducted from March 2025 to June 2025. Animal welfare and all experimental procedures were performed in strict accordance with the protocols approved by the Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of the Institute of Animal Sciences, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences (Approval number: IAS2026-5, CAAS, Beijing, China). This study was carried out in compliance with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0 (Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments). Every effort was made to minimize animal suffering.

Mice

Female HLA-A *02:01/DR1 transgenic and H-2-β2 m−/−|IAβ−/−, C57BL/6 mice aged 10 weeks were purchased from BioMice Biotech (Beijing, China). Mice were maintained at the specific pathogen-free Animal Center of Institute of Animal Sciences, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences (Beijing, China).

Infection with T. gondii

The TypeII T. gondii Prugniaud (Pru) strain was kindly provided by the National Animal Protozoa Laboratory, China Agricultural University, Tachyzoites of T. gondii were collected from invaded HFF cells in vitro as previously described [30]. A total of 18 mice were randomly divided into six groups representing different time points (n = 3 independent biological replicates per group). Each mouse was challenged intraperitoneally (i.p.) with 50 tachyzoites in 100 μL of sterile saline, while control mice received an equivalent volume of sterile saline only. Animal health and clinical signs were monitored daily. Humane endpoints were pre-defined as weight loss exceeding 20% of the initial body weight or the appearance of moribund state (e.g. hunched posture or labored breathing). No animals reached these endpoints during the study. At 1, 3, 7, 10, 28, and 42 days post-infection (dpi), the mice were euthanized by an intraperitoneal overdose of sodium pentobarbital (200 mg/kg). Death was confirmed by the cessation of heartbeat and respiration. Spleen samples were harvested under aseptic conditions and processed individually for subsequent analysis.

Splenocyte isolation and preparation

To preserve biological variance and ensure data robustness, spleens from each of the three biological replicates per time point were processed and analyzed individually throughout the entire workflow. Spleens harvested at the indicated dpi were first weighed using a precision electronic balance to quantify the degree of splenomegaly as a physiological correlate of infection progression. Subsequently, the spleens were mechanically dissociated using sterile surgical scissors and passed through a 70 μm nylon cell strainer. The resulting cell suspension was washed three times with sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 2% penicillin-streptomycin. To eliminate erythrocytes, the cell pellet was incubated with red blood cell (RBC) lysis buffer at room temperature according to the manufacturer’s instructions, followed by centrifugation at 300 × g for 5 min at 4°C. After discarding the supernatant, the cells were resuspended in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. The suspension was filtered again through a 70 μm strainer to remove any remaining cell aggregates. Finally, the cells were counted and the concentration was adjusted to 5 × 108 cells per independent sample for subsequent peptide elution.

Mild acid elution (MAE) of HLA-A *02:01-restricted ligands

Ligand elution was performed independently for each of the three biological replicates per time point (5 × 108 cells per sample). All steps from cell harvesting to ultracentrifugation were strictly conducted at 4°C or on ice. Splenocytes were washed three times with PBS via centrifugation at 300 × g for 10 min. The cell pellet was then gently resuspended in 4 mL of Mild Acid Elution (MAE) buffer (131 mM citric acid, 66 mM Na2HPO4, 150 mM NaCl, 1 μM aprotinin, and 25 mM iodoacetamide pH adjusted to 3.3 with NaOH). After a 1-min incubation in the MAE buffer, the suspension was immediately centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 min to remove the cells. The resulting supernatant was clarified through sequential centrifugation at 350 × g for 10 min and 3500 × g for 15 min. Finally, the clarified supernatant was subjected to ultracentrifugation at 250,000 × g for 1 h. The final samples were stored at −80°C for further analysis.

LC-MS/MS analysis

HLA-A *02:01-restricted peptide ligands were identified with a two-dimensional LCMS system as previously reported [28]. To ensure comprehensive coverage and confirm reproducibility, each biological replicate was analyzed via an independent LC-MS/MS run. Briefly, peptide pools were fractionated via offline high-pH reverse-phase HPLC. Each fraction was dried, resuspended in 10% (v/v) acetic acid, and analyzed using an EASY-nLC 1200 UHPLC system with an autosampler (Thermo Fisher Scientific). An aliquot (20%) of each fraction was loaded onto a nano-LC column and eluted with a linear acetonitrile-water gradient under acidic conditions (low pH). The eluate was ionized via a Nanospray Flex Ion Source ion source and analyzed with a Orbitrap Fusion Lumos (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Survey and fragment spectra for all fractions were analyzed using PEAKS Studio 11.0 (Bioinformatics Solutions Inc.). MS/MS spectra were searched against the NCBI RefSeq protein databases for Mus musculus (Assembly GRCm39, GCF_000001635.27) and T. gondii (Assembly TGGT1, GCA_000149715.2). For Mus musculus searches a 1% FDR was applied and for T. gondii a 2% FDR was used. The peptide lists identified from the three independent biological replicates per time point were statistically integrated using a union-based approach to establish the temporal ligandome landscape. To ensure the accuracy of the MS-based identifications, representative high-confidence T. gondii peptide sequences (including those selected for subsequent binding assays) were confirmed by comparing their fragmentation patterns with those of corresponding synthetic peptides.

Data analysis

Source protein accessions from T. gondii (based on NCBI Assembly GCA_000149715.2) were cross-referenced and mapped to their corresponding ToxoDB gene identifiers (www.toxodb.org). To evaluate the functional distribution of these proteins, Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed using the built-in enrichment tool on the ToxoDB [31]. Enrichment scores were calculated based on the T. gondii strain GT1. Both curated annotations and predicted GO terms were included in the analysis to maximize the functional coverage. Statistical significance for the overrepresentation of Molecular Function (MF) terms was determined using Bonferroni-adjusted p-values, with a threshold of p < 0.05 considered as significantly enriched.

In silico prediction of T. gondii

The binding affinities of all eluted peptides to HLA-A *02:01 were predicted using NetMHCpan-4.1 [32]. The percentile rank (%Rank) was employed as the primary metric to minimize bias across different MHC alleles. Following established criteria, peptides were categorized into two groups: strong binders (SB) were defined by a %Rank ≤0.5%, while weak binders (WB) were identified with a %Rank between 0.5% and 2.0%. Peptides exceeding the 2.0% threshold were initially classified as non-binders.

To screen for high-probability binders within these sequences, all overlapping 8–12mers (nested binders) within the identified ligands were further screened. A conservative threshold of %Rank ≤2.0% was applied to these nested candidates; if multiple nested binders were predicted within a single eluted peptide, the one with the highest predicted affinity (lowest %Rank) was selected for subsequent analysis. As a control, binding predictions were performed in parallel on permuted sequences generated by scrambling the amino acid order of the original eluted peptides.

T2 cell binding and HLA-A2 molecule stability assay

The human T cell line T2 (TAP-1 deficient, expression HLA-A *02:01) was purchased from Stemcell Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). To evaluate the binding affinity of HLA-A *02:01-restricted ligands, we measured the peptide-induced stabilization and subsequent surface upregulation of HLA-A *02:01 molecules. Briefly, T2 cells were incubated with individual peptides (50 μg/mL), including the five candidate HLA-A *02:01-restricted peptide ligands. We, respectively. A no-peptide control supplemented with 3 μg/mL of β2-microglobulin (β2 m) was also included. After incubation for 16 h at 37°C with 5% CO2, cells were stained with a PE-labeled anti-HLA-A2.1 antibody for 30 min at 4°C and analyzed by flow cytometry. The fluorescence index (FI) was calculated using the following formula:FI=MeanPEfluorescenewithpeptide−MeanPEfluorecencewithoutpeptideMeanPEfluorescencewithoutpeptide

Peptides were categorized based on their FI values: high affinity (FI>1.0), intermediate affinity (0.5<FI ≤ 1.0), low affinity (0.3 ≤ FI ≤ 0.5), and no binding (FI<0.3).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses in this study were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.2.3. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). For the peptide binding affinity assay, differences in FI values among groups were determined using one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s post-hoc test. p < 0.05 (reported to three decimal places) were considered statistically significant.

Protein preparation

The sequence encoding extracellular segment of HLA-A *02:01 (residues 1–275 of the mature protein) and human β2 m (residues 1–99 of the mature protein) were synthesized and cloned into pET-21a (+) vectors under the control of T7 promoter with Nde I and Xho I restriction sites. The HLA-A *02:01and β2 m recombinant proteins were in. E. coli BL21 (DE3) in the form of inclusion bodies, which were subsequently denatured using 6 M guanidine hydrochloride (Gua-HCl) for further use.

In vitro assembly of the pHLA-A *02:01 complexes

The pHLA-A *0201 complexes proteins were refolded by the gradual dilution method as previously reported [33]. Briefly, 25 mg of β2 m light chain was added to 200 mL of cold refolding buffer (0.1 M Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 2 mM EDTA, 5 mM reduced L-Glutathione, 5 mM oxidized L-Glutathione, 0.4 M L-Arginine HCl) and stirred at 4°C for 12 h. Subsequently, a mixture containing 2 mg of peptide and 45 mg of HLA-A *0201 heavy chain was added dropwise to the refolding buffer, followed by incubation at 4°C for additional 24 h. The refolded protein mixture was concentrated to a volume of 2 mL using a centrifugal concentrator (30 kDa molecular weight cutoff) and subsequently purified using a HiLoad 16/60 Superdex 200 pg column (Cytiva). The pHLA monomer fraction eluted at a volume of approximately 80 mL in chromatography buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0 and 50 mM NaCl).

Crystallization and data collection

The refolded pHLA-A *02:01-PGS (SLWQLLQAA) complex was concentrated and purified via size-exclusion chromatography using a HiLoad 16/60 Superdex 200 pg column (Cytiva), followed by anion-exchange chromatography on a Resource Q column (Cytiva). Thin, club-shaped crystals were obtained at 20°C using the sitting-drop vapor diffusion method. Specifically, 1 μL of protein solution (10 mg/mL) was mixed with 1 μL of reservoir solution consisting of 0.2 M potassium sodium tartrate tetrahydrate and 20% (w/v) PEG 3350. Crystals typically appeared within 2–4 days. Prior to data collection, crystals were harvested, cryoprotected in a mixture of reservoir solution and glycerol (3:1, v/v), and flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen. X-ray diffraction data were collected at beamline BL18U1 of the National Facility for Protein Science in Shanghai (NFPS) at the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility (SSRF). Diffraction data were indexed, integrated and scaled using HKL-3000 program suite [34]. The structure was solved by molecular replacement with Phaser within the Phenix package [35], using the coordinates of HLA-A *02:01 (PDB ID: 3MRE) as the search model [36]. This yielded unambiguous electron density for the peptide. Model building was performed manually with program COOT, followed by refinement with Refamc5 [37]. The final model was validated with PROCHECK. Structure refinement statistics were summarized in Table 1. Structural analysis and generation of illustrations

Peptide-contacting residues were identified using the CONTACT program within the CCP4 suite, with a distance cutoff of 3.3 Å between any atom of the residue and the binding partner. Solvent Accessible Surface Area (ASA), Buried Surface Area (BSA) and B-factors were calculated using the CCP4 package. All structural illustrations and electron density maps were rendered using the PyMOL Molecular Graphics System (Open-Source, Schrödinger, LLC).

Results

Identification and profiling of T. gondii- derived HLA-A *02:01 peptides

To characterize the pathogen-derived ligandome during infection, we established a comprehensive workflow integrating experimental infection with a multi-stage computational filtering pipeline (Figure 1). HLA-A2.1 transgenic mice were challenged intraperitoneally with 50 tachyzoites of the T. gondii Pru strain, and splenocytes were harvested at 6 time points chosen to cover the chronological progression of the infection (1, 3, 7, and 10 dpi, typically associated with acute responses, alongside 28 and 42 dpi representing later phases). Following the isolation of HLA-A *02:01-restricted peptides via mild acid elution (MAE) and LC-MS/MS identification, high-confidence T. gondii-derived peptides were extracted by sequentially applying sequence mapping, peptide length distribution analysis, and affinity prediction. Further, we also examined the structural characteristics of the ligands to validate their MHC-I binding specificity. The length distribution remained highly consistent across all time points, indicating stable antigen processing and presentation machinery throughout the infection (Figure 2B). Among the 3,433 unique peptides identified in 8–12 mer range (Supplementary Table S1), 8-mers (25.17%) and 9-mers (24.56%) were the most prevalent (Figure 2C). Over 70% of the sequences resided within the canonical 8–10 amino acid window. This length distribution aligns precisely with the structural constraints of the HLA-A *02:01 binding groove, thereby confirming the high specificity and quality of our immunopeptidomic data.

To evaluate the consistency of peptide presentation throughout the infection cycle, we performed a longitudinal comparison of all identified peptides across the 6 time points (1, 3, 7, 10, 28, and 42 dpi). Our analysis revealed a highly dynamic immunopeptidome characterized by remarkably limited inter-stage overlap (Figure 2D). For instance, of the 398 peptides identified at 1 dpi, only 16.3% (65) were shared with 3 dpi, and only 12.3% (49) persisted to 7 dpi. The acute peak at 7 dpi represented the most expansive phase but showed minimal carry-over at later points. Most identified peptides (3,074) were transient, appearing at only a single time point. Only 11 “persistent” peptides were consistently presented across all 6 time points (Figure 2E). This temporal profiling underscores high immunological flux in the MHC-I-restricted response, where only a minute fraction of ligand landscape remains stable during the transition from acute-associated to chronic-associated phases of infection.

Furthermore, source protein analysis revealed that T. gondii immunopeptidome originates from a broad spectrum of the parasite’s proteome (Figure 2F). Consistent with current genome annotation, a substantial portion of the peptides derive from hypothetical proteins. Among ligands with defined roles, functional annotation identified enrichment in metabolic enzymes, signal transduction (kinases and phosphatases), vesicle trafficking, and apicomplexan-specific organelle function (Figure 2F). Notably, HECT-domain ubiquitin-transferases and members of the ATPase family associated with diverse cellular activities (AAA ATPases) emerged as high-frequency sources of the immunopeptidome. Other prominent contributors included putative type I fatty acid synthase, histone lysine methyltransferase SET1, and members of the RNA pseudo uridine synthase superfamily. The overrepresentation of these sources is likely correlated with their substantial sequence lengths and high expression levels during the tachyzoite stage. Additionally, we identified diverse signaling-related proteins, such as phosphatidylinositol 3- and 4-kinases and various nonspecific serine/threonine protein kinases. Significant contributions were also observed from proteins localized to apicomplexan-specific structures, including several secreted proteins and potential antigens. These findings highlight a diverse source protein pool that likely plays a pivotal role in modulating host-parasite interactions and immune responses.

In silico screening and prioritization of high-affinity T. gondii-derived HLA-A *02:01 peptides

To further screen and prioritize high-affinity T. gondii-derived HLA-A *02:01 peptides, we utilized NetMHCpan-4.1 to predict the binding affinity of the identified ligands. Peptides were categorized as either strong binders (SBs) or weak binders (WBs) based on these predictions. Among the 73 unique peptides identified, 13 were classified as SBs and 60 as WBs (Supplementary Table S2). Temporal kinetic profiling revealed that ligand presentation was restricted during the early phase of infection (1–3 dpi), characterized predominantly by 9-mer weak binders, likely reflecting the initiation of antigen processing (Figure 3A). Conversely, the acute peak of infection (7 dpi) was marked by a significant expansion of the ligandome (n = 27). This phase featured increased length distribution, including longer 11- and 12-mer peptides, and a higher proportion of SBs (n = 6). By 10 dpi, the number of ligands contracted to 10 though four high-affinity SBs persisted (Figure 3A). Interestingly, the ligandome surged again to 24 unique peptides at 28 dpi, which may correlate with the establishment of an the early chronic stage. This expansion was transient; by 42 dpi, the breadth of the ligandome receded to levels comparable to the initial infection stage (Figure 3A). These findings suggest a dynamic and fluctuating profile in the T. gondii HLA-A *02:01-restricted ligandome, with distinct presentation peaks that potentially align with the progression from acute tachyzoite proliferation to chronic bradyzoite persistence. Notably, a subset of the identified ligands was consistently presented across multiple time points (Table 2). These persistently presented ligands primarily originated from hypothetical proteins, whose biological functions and immunogenicity remain largely uncharacterized. To gain deeper insights, we performed a comprehensive functional annotation of all source proteins, which mirrored the distribution patterns described above (Figure 3B). Specifically, hypothetical proteins and those categorized as “others” constituted the largest proportions of the source proteome, accounting for 42.47% (n = 31) and 19.18% (n = 14), respectively (Figure 3B). The remaining source proteins were identified as metabolic enzymes, transporters, and signaling kinases. Notably, our dataset included well-documented MHC-I ligand sources identified in previous cell-based studies, such as ribosomal proteins, EF1α, and HSP70 (Figure 3B). Conversely, although prior research has highlighted dense granule proteins (GRAs) as a rich source of ligands due to their secretion into the host cytosol, no GRA-derived peptides were detected in our study. This discrepancy may reflect differences in the experimental models or the specific kinetics of antigen presentation in vivo. Experimental validation of selected candidate HLA-A *02:01 ligands

To evaluate the reliability of the in silico predictions, five representative peptides derived from source proteins with previously unproven immunogenicity were selected for experimental validation. Among these, three were predicted as binders and two as non-binders (Table 3). We first employed the T2 cell stabilization assay to determine the binding affinity of these candidates. T2 cells were incubated with each peptide in the presence of β2 m for 16 h, and the peptide-induced upregulation of HLA-A *02:01 surface expression was quantified by flow cytometry using PE-labeled anti-HLA-A2.1 antibodies. The results demonstrated that three peptides (Tox-FL, FI and PGS) exhibited strong binding affinity (Figure 4A) (Table 3). Interestingly, Tox-TL and PGS, which were predicted in silico as a non-binder and a weak binder, respectively, showed high experimental affinity. In contrast, the experimental results for Tox-PGARP (weak binder) and TV (non-binder) were consistent with their computational predictions. The molecular basis for the stability and presentation of HLA-A *02:01-PGS restricted peptides

Following the preliminary peptide-MHC binding affinity assays, the PGS peptide was selected for in-depth structural characterization. This peptide is derived from glycogen synthase, a rate-limiting enzyme in the carbon metabolism of T. gondii tachyzoites, which plays a pivotal role in parasite energy homeostasis. To elucidate the molecular basis of its stability and presentation, we determined the crystal structure of the HLA-A *02:01-PGS complex. The overall structure reveals that the PGS peptide is deeply accommodated within the peptide-binding groove (PBG) of HLA-A *02:01 (Figure 5), adopting a prototypical “M-shaped” conformation. Well-defined electron density for residues P1–P3, P8, and P9 indicates high conformational stability at both termini (Figure 6A,B). In contrast, the electron density for the side chains of residues P4–P7 is largely disordered or invisible, suggesting significant intrinsic flexibility. This observation is further supported by the atomic displacement parameters (B-factors); the central P4–P7 segment exhibit markedly higher average B-factors than the N- and C-terminal regions, underscoring the dynamic nature of the central peptide segment (Figure 6C). Structural analysis reveals that the side chains of P4, P5, and P7 (but not P6) protrude solvent-ward from the PBG. These solvent-exposed, flexible residues are likely primary determinants for T-cell receptor (TCR) engagement. Conversely, residues P1–P3 and P9 function as anchoring points that stabilize the peptide within the HLA-A *0201 groove. Notably, buried surface area (BSA) calculations reveal that P3 has a higher BSA value than the canonical P1, P2, and P9 anchor positions, highlighting its indispensable role in stabilizing the HLA-A *02:01-PGS complex. Structural insights into the anchoring pockets of the HLA-A *02:01-PGS complex

To further elucidate the molecular basis of peptide binding, we analyzed the specific interactions within the six canonical pockets (A – F) of the HLA-A *02:01 groove. The A pocket, defined by residues Met5, Tyr7, Glu63, Lys66, Tyr159, Trp167, and Tyr171, stabilized PGS N-terminus through an intricate network of hydrogen bonds and van der Waals forces. While the P1 residue is often solvent-exposed in pMHC I complexes, P1-Ser in the PGS peptide formed two additional hydrogen bonds with Lys66 and Glu63 via its hydroxyl group, further enhancing N-terminal stability (Figure 7). The B and F pockets functioned as the primary specificity determinants. The side chains of P2-Leu and P9-Ala were deeply sequestered within these pockets through robust hydrophobic interactions and conserved hydrogen bonding (Figure 7). Interestingly, the D pocket emerged as a significant contributor to stabilization by accommodating P3-Trp. Consistent with its high BSA value, P3-Trp established an extensive interaction network, including hydrogen bonds with Leu156 and Tyr93, dense hydrophobic packing, and π-π stacking with His114 (Figure 7). These findings suggest that P3-Trp acts as a potent auxiliary anchor, complementing the canonical P2 and P9 anchor residues.

In contrast, the C and E pockets played auxiliary roles in peptide binding. The C pocket accommodated P6-Leu through hydrophobic contacts but lacked direct hydrogen bonding (Figure 7). The E pocket, containing P7-Gln and P8-Ala, featured relatively sparse interactions, with only a single hydrogen bond between P8-Ala and Trp147 (Figure 7). Consequently, residues P7 and P8 contributed minimally to the overall anchoring of the PGS peptide, consistent with their observed structural flexibility.

Discussion

In this study, we utilized a MS-based immunopeptidomics approach to provide a longitudinal in vivo map of the T. gondii ligandome presented by HLA-A *02:01. By employing a hierarchical discovery funnel, we initially identified a vast repertoire of 3,744 unique pathogen-derived peptides from MS data. To distinguish genuine ligands from the complex background of nonspecific fragments, this pool was refined to 3,433 canonical 8–12mers matching the typical binding length for HLA-A *02:01. Utilizing NetMHCpan-4.1 as a prioritization tool, the landscape was further narrowed to 73 high-priority candidates exhibiting high predicted biochemical affinity. Finally, five candidate peptides of interest were selected for rigorous experimental validation, confirming their robust binding capacity through in vitro and in vivo assays. These findings significantly expand the known HLA-A *02:01-restricted ligand landscape of T. gondii. It is imperative to acknowledge that while the high-confidence identification of these ligands provides a comprehensive overview of the presented immunopeptidome, biochemical presentation serves as a fundamental prerequisite for, rather than an absolute determinant of, T-cell immunogenicity. Consequently, the capacity of these candidates to elicit functional CD8+ T-cell responses and provide protective immunity remains a primary focus for future investigations. Our study thus establishes a robust molecular roadmap and a high-priority library for the subsequent functional validation of T. gondii epitopes.

The biological complexity of T. gondii is defined by its sophisticated life cycle, where the rapid proliferation of tachyzoites during acute infection transitions into the formation of latent bradyzoite cysts during the chronic phase [38]. Interestingly, the highly dynamic antigen presentation landscape identified in our in vivo model potentially reflects these underlying biological shifts, mirroring the broader systemic progression of the infection. Using HLA-A2.1 transgenic mice, we identified a repertoire of 3,744 unique HLA-A *02:01-restricted peptides, a coverage that substantially exceeds previously reported datasets from in vitro models [28] and underscores the necessity of in vivo profiling to capture the immunological complexity of an active infection. A pronounced temporal flux in antigen presentation was observed across the 6 time points, peaking at 7 days post-infection (dpi). Notably, this peak in the ligandome coincided with macroscopic physiological changes observed during tissue harvest (Suplementary Figure S1). Notably, the noticeable increase in spleen weight observed during tissue collection appeared consistent with the systemic progression of the infection. This macroscopic observation may provide a supplementary physiological context for the peak at 7 dpi, particularly where direct molecular quantification of parasite burden was not performed. The subsequent contraction at 10 dpi and the reemergence of a distinct peptide repertoire at 28 dpi suggest a regulatory landscape more intricate than a simple reflection of pathogen load. Although stage-specific molecular markers were not employed to verify the transition between parasite forms, the notable turnover and qualitative shift in the ligandome from the acute-associated phase (7–10 dpi) to the chronic-associated phase (28 dpi) potentially reflects the host–pathogen interaction during the transition from tachyzoites to bradyzoites. For instance, in the chronic stage, the periodic rupture of pseudocysts and the associated host responses might drive waves of antigen processing that differ fundamentally from the acute phase. These dynamic shifts provide a valuable consideration for vaccine strategies, suggesting that targeting epitopes from both developmental stages may be essential for eliciting the comprehensive and durable immunity required to address the complexity of the T. gondii infection cycle. Taken together, our findings establish a robust temporal map of the in vivo ligandome, which serves as a critical foundation for future efforts to correlate specific peptide signatures with definitive developmental stages of the parasite.

The identified antigen presentation landscape represents a functional manifestation of the intricate interaction between T. gondii and the host immune system. Within this context, the inherent diversity of host cell populations serves as another significant factor influencing the breadth and composition of the observed ligandome. This variety likely reflects the utilization of whole splenocytes, which provides a broad overview by incorporating the collective contributions of various cell types within the splenic microenvironment. While professional antigen-presenting cells (pAPCs), such as dendritic cells and macrophages, are the primary drivers of CD8+ T-cell priming, other infected non-APCs are also likely to contribute to the overall repertoire [39,40]. Importantly, analyzing total splenocytes allows for the inclusion of both direct presentation from infected cells and cross-presentation by pAPCs, thereby establishing a comprehensive resource of peptides available for T-cell recognition during T. gondii infection. However, we acknowledge that this approach does not permit the attribution of specific ligands to individual cell types. The observed diversity may stem from the varying proteolytic environments and MHC-I loading machinery across different splenic compartments. Future investigations employing cell-type-specific immunopeptidomics will be essential to further dissect the precise roles of distinct cell subsets in shaping the anti-T. gondii immune response.

Notably, our dataset included a substantial number of high-affinity 12-mer peptides, extending beyond the canonical 8-mer and 9-mer binders typical of the HLA-A *02:01 ligandome. While structural models traditionally depict the MHC-I binding groove as closed, previous reports indicated that in the context of T. gondii infection, longer peptides may be accommodated through mechanisms such as C-terminal extensions or central bulging conformations [26,28]. The predicted high affinity of these longer peptides implies that the in vivo processing machinery, potentially influenced by endoplasmic reticulum aminopeptidases (ERAP1/2), generates a more diverse immunopeptidome than previously recognized. This expanded repertoire could provide the T cell receptor (TCR) repertoire with a broader array of contact surfaces for recognition. Beyond length diversity, the efficiency of antigen presentation is often influenced by the “position effect” within the antigenic precursor. As previously reported, proximity to the C-terminus can facilitate more efficient proteasomal processing and protect nascent peptides from excessive cytosolic degradation, thereby enhancing their entry into the MHC-I pathway [26]. Interestingly, our experimental validation of five high-priority candidates, including the PGS ligand, revealed that these sequences are located within the internal regions of their respective proteins rather than at the C-terminus. This observation suggests that for these specific ligands, their robust presentation and binding stability are likely driven by intrinsic biochemical affinity and favorable structural complementarity with the HLA-A *02:01 groove, rather than positional advantages.

One intriguing observation was the relatively low yield of peptides derived from Dense Granule Proteins (GRAs), which are often considered dominant T. gondii antigens [41–43]. This seemingly unexpected discrepancy can be explained by several non-mutually exclusive factors that cover methodological biases as well as complex biological mechanisms. Methodologically, the under-representation of GRAs may stem from inherent biases in peptide detection and isolation. We employed mild acid elution (MAE) to isolate peptides specifically from the surface of splenocytes. While MAE is an established technique for rapid ligand discovery, we explicitly acknowledge its limitations relative to immunoaffinity purification (IP) of MHC-peptide complexes, the current gold standard. Unlike IP, which utilizes specific antibodies to capture intact MHC complexes, MAE is a nonspecific chemical process that strips all acid-soluble molecules from the cell surface. Consequently, MAE datasets often exhibit a higher background of “contaminant” peptides, which can narrow the dynamic range of mass spectrometry and mask low-abundance pathogen ligands. If GRA-derived epitopes are presented at low densities or are obscured by this high background, they may fall below the detection threshold, which might partially explain their under-representation in our results compared to studies utilizing IP-based pipelines. Furthermore, methodological biases may arise from the physicochemical properties of GRA-derived peptides; their specific hydrophobicity or charge distribution might affect their elution efficiency from the cell surface or their ionization during MS analysis, potentially leading to their exclusion from the final dataset. Biologically, alternative factors such as stage-specific expression and tissue-specific presentation must be considered. First, the stage-specific expression patterns of T. gondii may play a critical role. While many GRAs are abundant in the tachyzoite secretum, their expression is highly regulated during the transition to bradyzoites. In the splenic environment, the rapid adaptation of the parasite to host immune pressure may prioritize the presentation of structural or metabolic proteins over certain secreted effectors. Second, tissue-specific differences in antigen presentation may exist; the processing of GRAs within the spleen, a site of active systemic immune response, might differ significantly from that in the brain or muscular tissues where GRAs are traditionally studied as dominant targets [43]. Additionally, because GRAs are primarily secreted into the parasitophorous vacuole or host cytosol and may be more efficiently presented via cross-presentation by professional antigen-presenting cells rather than through direct presentation by infected splenocytes [29]. Furthermore, the dominant presentation of antigens such as SAG1 and ROPs during acute infection may competitively inhibit GRA presentation until later stages of cyst maturation.

Structural analysis of the HLA-A *02:01–PGS complex provides high-resolution validation of the identified ligandome at the 7 dpi infection peak. Our findings reveal that the PGS peptide adopts a stable “M-shaped” conformation within the binding groove, stabilized by canonical P2 and P9 anchors and significantly bolstered by an auxiliary interaction at P3-Trp. The high buried surface area of P3-Trp likely contributes substantial binding energy, while the solvent-exposed orientation of the P7-Gln side chain suggests a structural potential for receptor engagement (Figure 8). Beyond providing a molecular landscape for high-affinity ligands, the integration of in silico prediction with in vitro validation assays offered broader methodological insights. We observed that computational algorithms, although valuable, can occasionally underestimate the binding affinity of non-canonical peptides, as exemplified by Tox-TL. This finding underscores the critical role of empirical validation and structural biology in uncovering immunologically relevant “hidden” epitopes that might otherwise be overlooked by purely predictive pipelines. Notably, the biological relevance of these ligands is reinforced by the extensive sequence conservation of their source proteins across major T. gondii genotypes (Suplementary Figure S2), ensuring the structural robustness of the identified candidates across different pathogen strains. For the PGS ligand, although the sequence is identical between the GT1 and Pru strains, a single amino acid substitution occurs at the P6 position in the ME49 strain (Isoleucine to Leucine). Our structural data indicates that the P6 residue is accommodated within the hydrophobic C-pocket of the HLA-A *02:01 groove. Given that Isoleucine and Leucine are isomeric amino acids with nearly identical hydrophobic volumes, this conservative substitution is expected to be well-tolerated without significantly altering the binding affinity or complex stability. Collectively, these insights confirm that our prioritized candidates possess favorable biochemical properties that are largely conserved or structurally accommodated across major pathogen strains, reinforcing their value as high-priority resources for future functional studies.
