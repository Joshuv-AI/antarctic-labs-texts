French National Guidelines for the Appropriate Use of Opioids: Analgesia, Opioid Use Disorder, and Overdose Prevention—Part 1: Methodology and Pain Management
Authors: Célian Bertin, Julie Dupouy, Nicolas Authier, Nathalie Michenot, Sidonie Verdier, Mathieu Chappuy, Anne-Priscille Trouvin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Opioid medications play a central role in pain management but are associated with significant risks, including dependence, opioid use disorder (OUD), and overdose. In response to rising concerns, the French National Authority for Health convened a multidisciplinary working group to develop updated guidelines on the appropriate use of opioids. These recommendations, grounded in the high-level international evidence and developed through a rigorous formal consensus method, represent the most comprehensive and recent guidelines addressing the entire opioid pharmacological class, regardless of indication. The first part focuses on the clinical use of opioid analgesics in acute and chronic pain, both cancer-related and non-cancer-related. The guidelines emphasize prudent prescribing, prioritization of non-opioid therapies, and the integration of multimodal analgesia. Specific clinical situations are addressed, including management in primary care, emergency settings, postoperative care, inpatient treatment, and the perioperative management of patients already receiving opioids or opioid agonist treatment. Practical tools such as equianalgesic conversion principles, criteria for opioid rotation, and strategies to minimize adverse effects are provided to support prescribers. By considering opioids as a single pharmacological class, these guidelines enable consistent principles to be applied across diverse clinical contexts, from the initiation of short-term opioid therapy to the safe management of patients with complex dependence profiles. Although developed in France, the recommendations are intended to have international relevance, given their reliance on global evidence and their focus on balancing effective pain relief with risk reduction. Introduction

Opioids exert analgesic effects by modulating nociceptive transmission at both the spinal and supraspinal levels, significantly improving pain management. However, due to their high potential for dependence and opioid use disorder (OUD) (see definitions in Appendix 1 in Supplementary Material), opioids are associated with increased healthcare utilization (e.g., consultations, hospitalizations) and serious health consequences, including potentially fatal overdoses, particularly among patients with comorbidities. The World Health Organization (WHO) has reported a rise in opioid-related harms in recent years, largely driven by increasing use in the management of chronic non-cancer pain [1].

From an epidemiological perspective, the European situation differs markedly from the North American opioid crisis, where opioids were implicated in two-thirds of the 71,568 overdose deaths reported in the United States in 2017 [2]. However, the Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) has urged governments to take urgent action against the “opioid crisis”, which is spreading globally [3]. In its report covering 25 member countries between 2011 and 2016, the OECD reported a 20% overall increase in opioid-related deaths. While the steepest rises occurred in the United States and Canada, several European countries, including Sweden, Norway, Ireland, and England and Wales, have also reported significant increases in opioid-related mortality. These trends were largely driven by higher opioid prescribing for pain, whether appropriate or not [3].

In Europe, countries with robust drug monitoring systems, strict regulation of pharmaceutical advertising, and tightly controlled prescribing and dispensing practices appear less affected [4]. France belongs to this group, with one of the lowest opioid-related mortality rates among OECD countries: an estimated 300–400 fatal overdoses per year, primarily linked to methadone (40%) or heroin (20%), far ahead of analgesic opioids (12%) [2, 5, 6].

Nevertheless, cases of dependence and OUD have emerged, along with new patient profiles experiencing overdose who do not fit the “typical” drug user profile, such as opioid-naïve or occasional users, and patients who developed dependence following opioid analgesic treatment for non-cancer conditions [7–9]. Against this backdrop, the French National Agency for Medicines and Health Products Safety (Agence Nationale de Sécurité du Médicament et des produits de santé [ANSM]) published a national review of opioid analgesic consumption [4] and initiated comprehensive monitoring of analgesic use, notably through the French Addictovigilance Network [10].

For health authorities, the challenge is therefore to maximize the safety of opioid use while preserving access for patients with pain and/or dependence who genuinely need them, particularly in the context of an aging population where pain symptoms are increasingly prevalent.

Pharmacologically, opioids are defined as compounds with affinity for endogenous opioid receptors, and most adverse effects are receptor-mediated and common to the entire drug class. Regardless of their pharmacological potency, the benefit–risk balance of opioid analgesics is primarily dose-dependent, with risks, including the development of OUD and overdose, shared across all opioids. In this context, the classification of opioid analgesics proposed by Lussier and Beaulieu [11] is preferable to the WHO’s three-step analgesic ladder (no opioid, weak opioids, strong opioids; steps 1–3), which was validated only for cancer-related pain and may provide false reassurance to prescribers and patients [12]. The WHO classification has been debated in chronic non-cancer pain and should not be applied in this context, while it remains a reference framework in cancer pain management.

Accordingly, and with the aim of reducing overdose and dependence, these recommendations were developed using a comprehensive approach to opioid medications, encompassing both analgesics and opioid agonist treatment (OAT), in adults aged over 15 years. Principles for the prevention and management of physical dependence, OUD, and overdoses were integrated. To our knowledge, this represents the first set of cross-cutting national guidelines addressing these issues.

Method

Data Sources

The guideline development process was based on a systematic literature review conducted by the French National Authority for Health (Haute Autorité de Santé [HAS]). The search covered clinical practice guidelines, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, randomized or controlled trials, and observational studies published between January 2009 and December 2021. Literature was accessed through PubMed, Embase, the Cochrane Library, and major guideline repositories. The list of websites consulted (publishing clinical guidelines, health technology assessments, ethical or economic reports, and professional society recommendations) is provided in Table S3 (Supplementary Material). Full details of the search strategy and selection process are available in Appendix 2 (Supplementary Material) and in the HAS guidance document “Development of Clinical Practice Guidelines – Method for Clinical Practice Recommendations [13].”

Target Patient Population

These guidelines apply to patients over 15 years of age treated with opioid analgesics for acute or chronic pain, cancer-related or non-cancer-related. They also apply to patients receiving OAT for physical dependence, as well as those with physical dependence and/or OUD related to prescribed or illicit opioids.

Target Healthcare Professionals

The guidelines are intended for all healthcare professionals involved in the care of patients treated with opioid medications and/or presenting with OUD. This includes professionals working in outpatient or inpatient settings, in primary or secondary care, in social and medico-social institutions, as well as in prison healthcare units.

The HAS, an independent public body with a scientific mandate, aims to improve the quality of healthcare and social services for the benefit of patients. In this context, it was tasked with developing national guidelines to enhance professional practices concerning the appropriate use of opioid medications. This mandate encompassed both their role in pain management and the prevention and management of OUD and overdoses, through the development of evidence-based national recommendations using a formal consensus method [13].

Implementation

The detailed implementation procedures are provided in Appendix 2 (Supplementary Material).

These guidelines were developed in accordance with the methodology outlined in the HAS manual Development of Clinical Practice Guidelines [13]. This process is rigorous and transparent, ensuring independence and appropriate management of potential conflicts of interest.

A multidisciplinary working group (27 members, including healthcare professionals, patient representatives, and public agencies) conducted a systematic literature review (2009–2021, 4,453 references), resulting in the selection of 223 high-level evidence sources. Based on this review, a first draft of the guidelines was prepared and submitted to an extended reading group (49 members) for evaluation of clarity and applicability. Their feedback was incorporated into the final recommendations and summary sheets, which were validated by the HAS College and published in March 2022.

These guidelines are based on previously conducted studies and existing recommendations and do not contain any new studies involving human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

These recommendations are primarily based on high-level evidence (including systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and randomized controlled trials) when available and are complemented by expert consensus in areas where evidence is limited.

Appropriate Use of Opioid Analgesics in Acute Non-Cancer Pain

These guidelines distinguish two groups of patients: opioid-naïve patients, i.e., those receiving opioids for the first time, and patients with prior opioid treatment.

For opioid-naïve patients, the recommendations are based on studies conducted across various care settings (primary care, emergency departments, inpatient care and immediate postoperative care) and emphasize the general principles of appropriate use.

For patients already treated with opioids, although the evidence base is more limited, the recommendations provide more detailed guidance on the management of analgesics. Certain clinical situations involving severe acute pain require rapid intervention, often incompatible with a stepwise therapeutic escalation. In addition, complex clinical scenarios may necessitate individualized adjustments in opioid prescriptions, doses, and dosing intervals.

Opioid-Naive Patients

Primary Care

In primary care, opioids may be indicated for severe acute pain (e.g., crush injuries, extensive burns, fractures, or wounds with significant tissue damage) that is uncontrolled by other medications and/or causing functional impairment. Non-opioid treatments should be considered first line whenever appropriate. When opioids are required, immediate-release formulations at the lowest effective dose should be used for the shortest duration necessary [14].

However, according to the American College of Occupational and Environmental Medicine [15], clinical trial data show only limited efficacy compared with placebo for mild-to-moderate acute pain, with a notable incidence of adverse effects. Opioids are therefore not recommended for mild-to-moderate acute pain (e.g., sprains or minor injuries without tissue damage), as no high-quality evidence supports superiority over other treatments. They remain indicated for severe acute pain uncontrolled by non-opioid therapies.

In acute low back pain, systematic reviews by the American College of Physicians [16, 17] and the U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs [18] along with recommendations from Kaiser Permanente Washington [19, 20] and the HAS [21] found no evidence of superior efficacy for opioids. Their use should be restricted to second-line therapy only when Nonsteroidal Anti-Inflammatory Drugs (NSAIDs and acetaminophen [paracetamol] fail, and then for the shortest possible duration (ideally ≤3 days). HAS [21] advises against first-line opioid therapy due to the risk of OUD and recommends close monitoring of at-risk patients. In cases of confirmed OUD, collaboration with a pain management center and/or addiction specialists is advised. A low-dose opioid may be considered as a last resort, for the shortest possible duration, in refractory low back pain despite comprehensive care, including multidisciplinary rehabilitation programs. A systematic review by Tucker et al. [22] and a meta-analysis by Sanger et al. [23] highlighted the higher risks associated with opioids compared to non-opioids for subacute low back pain, including an increased risk of ongoing opioid use (RR 1.57; 95% CI [1.06–2.33]).

For acute migraine attacks and tension-type headaches, opioid use is discouraged by Kaiser Permanente Washington [24] and the French Headache Society [25].

For musculoskeletal injuries, a review by North American orthopedic specialists [26] recommended a multimodal analgesic strategy, restricting opioids to the lowest effective dose and shortest possible duration.

For dental pain, a meta-review by Moore et al. [27] and a network meta-analysis by Zanjir et al. [28] found NSAIDs, alone or combined with acetaminophen, to be more effective than opioids, particularly during the first 24 h.

Finally, a network meta-analysis by Busse et al. [29] reported significant gastrointestinal and neurological risks associated with opioids, while topical NSAIDs, followed by oral NSAIDs and acetaminophen, offered a more favorable benefit–risk profile for short-term pain relief.

Emergency Departments

The management of acute pain in emergency settings has been the focus of several French and international guidelines and systematic reviews addressing multiple key clinical questions.

Role of Opioid Analgesics

In emergency settings, the American Academy of Emergency Medicine [30] recommends a cautious approach to opioid use, prioritizing non-pharmacological measures and non-opioid medications to minimize opioid-related adverse effects. If opioids are required, immediate-release morphine sulfate is preferred over oxycodone due to its lower euphoric potential and consequently reduced risk of use disorder.

For severe acute pain (≥6 on the numerical rating scale), the French Society of Anesthesiology and Intensive Care (SFAR) and the French Society of Emergency Medicine (SFMU) [31] recommend immediate opioid use. In renal colic, however, these societies advise opioids only as second-line therapy, after failure of, or contraindication to, NSAIDs, according to the SFMU consensus conference [32].

For migraine management, the Canadian Headache Society [33] advises against opioid use when parenteral non-opioid alternatives are available, based on evidence from several low-quality trials showing no significant advantage of tramadol or morphine over non-opioid treatments.

A recent systematic review of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) comparing opioids with non-opioids across various acute pain conditions – including limb trauma, abdominal pain, and renal colic – found no superiority of opioids over non-opioid therapies in these specific conditions. These findings support reconsidering opioid use in the emergency department in favor of less risky and equally effective pain management strategies for moderate pain, while opioids remain indicated for severe, uncontrolled pain [34].

Comparison Between Opioid Analgesics

The recent systematic review of RCTs conducted in emergency department settings [34] found no significant difference in efficacy among opioid analgesics for the management of acute pain, which is predominantly nociceptive in this context. Similar conclusions were reached by another systematic review of RCTs [35] and by a review of pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) studies [36], both demonstrating no evidence of superiority of any individual opioid in this setting.

Administration of Opioid Analgesics

In emergency departments, the American Academy of Emergency Medicine [30] recommends cautious opioid titration, starting with the lowest effective dose and escalating only as needed, with regular reassessment of adverse effects to ensure patient safety, in line with more recent international recommendations such as the 2022 CDC guideline [14]. Consistent with this approach, SFAR and SFMU [32] suggest intravenous opioid titration, starting with 2–3 mg every 5 min for pain ≥6 on the Numeric Rating Scale (NRS), until adequate relief is achieved. Morphine hydrochloride is recommended due to its favorable safety profile.

A review by Broida et al. [37], summarizing 67 U.S. federal guidelines, highlighted a general trend toward limiting prescriptions to short-acting opioid formulations. The Italian Society of Anesthesia, Analgesia, Resuscitation and Intensive Care [38] recommends intravenous administration, with age-adjusted doses, titrated until at least a 50% reduction in pain or NRS <4 is achieved. For hemodynamically unstable or critically ill patients, individualized titration is advised, using low doses of intravenous morphine (1–2 mg every 5–10 min as needed) or fentanyl administered intravenously (0.5–1 µg/kg) or intranasally.

In acute abdominal conditions, opioid administration does not appear to increase the risk of diagnostic or therapeutic errors in adults, supporting their use when clinically indicated [38].

Inpatient Care

In hospitalized patients, opioids are recommended with caution for severe or moderate pain not relieved by non-opioid analgesics, or when these are contraindicated or expected to be ineffective [39, 40]. When indicated, opioids should be prescribed at the lowest effective dose and for the shortest possible duration, with preference for immediate-release formulations to avoid initiating long-acting opioids (including transdermal fentanyl) for acute pain [14]. Oral administration should be favored whenever possible; intravenous opioids are reserved for situations requiring rapid pain control or when oral administration is not feasible (e.g., malabsorption).

When switching between opioids or routes of administration, equianalgesic conversion tables should be used to account for relative potency. During opioid rotation, the new opioid dose should be reduced by 25–50% to account for interindividual variability in response and potential incomplete cross-tolerance [39].

In postoperative pain management, recommendations align with those for acute pain. The American College of Occupational and Environmental Medicine [15] recommends restricting opioids as adjuncts to other, more effective treatments, with early identification of patients likely to require opioids beyond the first two postoperative weeks, to optimize pain management plans and detect early progression toward OUD.

Multimodal analgesia is supported by various professional societies – North American [41, 42], Italian [38], and French [43], combining non-opioid analgesics, regional anesthesia techniques, and non-pharmacological measures to reduce opioid-related risks while ensuring effective pain control. The validity of multimodal analgesia protocols is supported by Nassif and Miller [44], who concluded that various opioid analgesics demonstrate comparable efficacy, as also shown in multiple systematic reviews and meta-analyses [45–47]. However, the risk of adverse events after a single oral postoperative analgesic dose is higher than with non-opioid analgesics [48], underscoring the importance of opioid-sparing strategies and optimizing pain management modalities.

Patients Receiving Prescribed Opioid Medications, OAT, or Illicit Opioids

Managing acute pain in opioid-tolerant patients, whether they are receiving prescribed opioid analgesics (short- or long-acting), OAT with methadone or buprenorphine, or using opioids outside of a medical context (prescribed or illicit), presents complex challenges. These patients often experience amplified pain due to cross-tolerance and opioid-induced hyperalgesia, further complicated by pharmacological interactions involving high-affinity partial agonists and μ-opioid receptor antagonists. Consequently, they frequently report higher-than-expected postoperative pain levels and slower recovery from acute postoperative pain.

Professional societies [26, 38, 41] emphasize the importance of ethical and respectful care, highlighting the risks of stigmatization and the need for individualized perioperative analgesia to avoid undertreatment. Barriers to adequate care may include social stigma around opioid use, concerns about misuse or relapse, and limited understanding of the pharmacokinetics of full agonists and OAT. These societies recommend avoiding abrupt discontinuation of opioids before surgery and coordinating with specialized teams (pain medicine, addiction medicine, psychiatry, or primary care) for optimal management of pain and OUD [43].

Opioid Switching

In the absence of universally accepted opioid conversion tables for acute pain management, the HAS working group recommends adjusting doses when changing the route of administration or rotating between different opioids, based on the Summary of Product Characteristics (SmPC) of each medication. The dose of the new opioid should be reduced by 25–50% relative to the initially calculated equianalgesic dose. This reduction accounts for interindividual variability in opioid response and the potential for incomplete cross-tolerance between opioids.

Appropriate use of Opioid Analgesics in Chronic Non-Cancer Pain

Benefits of Opioid Analgesic Therapy

Effects on Pain

Opioids provide modest relief for chronic non-cancer pain, benefiting approximately 25% of patients, according to the European Pain Federation, but the magnitude of benefit varies across pain syndromes, patient populations, and opioid drugs [53]. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses consistently report modest pain reductions, with small effect sizes (– 0.6 to – 0.56) in patients over 60 years [54], and mean reductions of 10–30 mm on a 0–100 mm scale [54–58].

In 2016, the European Pain Federation stated that opioids may benefit about 25% of patients with chronic non-cancer pain due to osteoarthritis, chronic low back pain, or diabetic/post-herpetic neuropathic pain [53]. However, these findings remain controversial. One meta-analysis found no significant difference in pain relief between opioids and non-opioid treatments [59]. A large systematic review and meta-analysis by Busse et al. [29], synthesizing most of the available literature, compared opioids with placebo and found some effect, but not of clinically meaningful significance, for either short-term (<3 months) or medium-/long-term (>3 months) treatment. In comparative analyses, opioids generally showed similar effects to NSAIDs and tricyclic antidepressants for chronic non-cancer pain, although the magnitude of benefit varied across pain syndromes and the certainty of evidence was low to moderate [29, 60]. Another meta-analysis of enriched enrollment randomized withdrawal (EERW) trials found superiority over placebo, but the authors judged the difference clinically irrelevant [61].

Systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and guidelines suggest a potential but variable role for opioids in conditions such as low back pain, osteoarthritis, and neuropathic pain [56]. However, their efficacy has not been demonstrated—or remains untested—for other conditions including headaches [56, 58], male chronic pelvic pain/prostatitis [62], endometriosis, and female chronic pelvic pain [63, 64]. Comparative studies have not shown consistent superiority of opioids over NSAIDs for these conditions [16, 57, 65, 66]. Fibromyalgia appears to respond poorly to most opioids, although tramadol may provide limited benefit in selected patients; however, the supporting evidence remains low quality [58, 67–70].

Effects on Function

Systematic reviews and meta-analyses suggest limited functional benefit from opioids, compared with placebo, with evidence of similar or slightly superior effects for non-opioid analgesics in some studies [29, 59]. However, results vary across pain conditions, comparators, and study durations. Reinecke et al. reported only a trend toward improved function with “weak” opioids, and no significant effect for “strong” opioids [88]. Overall, functional improvement is minimal and often clinically irrelevant, especially in patients over 60 years with chronic non-cancer pain [29, 54, 55].

For chronic low back pain, the quality of evidence is low to moderate, with small effect sizes compared with placebo [17, 65, 66, 70, 73, 76]. No significant differences were observed versus NSAIDs, except for tramadol, which again showed limited benefit based on low-quality evidence [16, 65, 70]. Some reviews found no functional improvement at all [57, 71].

For osteoarthritis, opioids may provide slight a superiority over placebo in terms of function, but the effect remains small [57, 71, 78].

In neuropathic pain, opioids appear more effective than placebo, though effect sizes are small [89].

Comparison Between Opioid Analgesics

Systematic reviews and meta-analyses have found no consistent differences in efficacy among opioid drugs for either pain relief or functional improvement. No clear superiority of “strong” over “weak” opioids has been demonstrated, although evidence remains heterogeneous across opioid drugs and pain conditions [17, 56, 82, 90, 91].

Evidence regarding tapentadol is heterogeneous: some meta-analyses report superiority over oxycodone [92, 93], others show non-inferiority [94], while one comparative analysis found it less effective than tramadol [95].

No meaningful differences have been observed between extended-release and immediate-release opioid formulations [16, 96].

Tramadol is frequently evaluated separately due to its dual opioid and monoaminergic actions. It has shown specific efficacy in osteoarthritis (for treatments >12 weeks) [97] and chronic low back pain [66, 70]. A Cochrane review reported low-quality evidence supporting its efficacy in neuropathic pain, with 50% pain reduction achieved in more than 50% of tramadol-treated patients versus 30% of those receiving placebo [85]. Accordingly, tramadol is positioned as a second-line treatment for neuropathic pain in recent French guidelines, whereas other opioids are recommended only as third-line options [81, 98]. Tramadol is also the only opioid to have demonstrated benefit in fibromyalgia [36, 79, 80, 99], likely linked to its aminergic activity.

Duration of Treatment

The scientific literature provides no definitive conclusions regarding the optimal duration of opioid therapy for chronic non-cancer pain, primarily due to the lack of robust long-term trials.

Available studies suggest limited additional benefit from opioid continuation beyond 3 months compared with the initial treatment period, although long-term data remain scarce and of low methodological quality [56, 100]. Long-term opioid therapy should therefore be reserved for carefully selected patients who demonstrate a clear early benefit.

Systematic reviews by Manchikanti et al. and Chou et al. found no convincing evidence to support treatment beyond one year for chronic non-cancer pain [101, 102]. Similarly, the 2016 Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) guidelines recommend against long-term opioid prescribing in the absence of clear evidence of sustained benefit [103]. A Cochrane review of patients treated with opioids for more than 3 months reported some benefit at 6 months, however, these findings should be interpreted with caution due to high attrition bias in long-term studies [104].

Risks of Opioid Therapy in Chronic Non-Cancer Pain

Common Adverse Effects

Frequently reported adverse effects of opioid analgesics include constipation, nausea and vomiting, somnolence, dizziness, pruritus, dry mouth and skin, and respiratory depression, all of which are well documented in the literature [17, 54, 55, 66, 68, 72, 105, 106]. Although cognitive impairment is occasionally mentioned, a systematic review found no evidence of such effects in patients receiving long-term opioid therapy [107].

Opioid-induced androgen deficiency has been reported, with prevalence ranging from 19% to 86% depending on the criteria used to define hypogonadism. Clinical consequences include sexual dysfunction, mood disturbances, reduced bone mineral density, and changes in body composition [108].

Opioid-induced hyperalgesia is increasingly recognized and may sometimes be misinterpreted as worsening of the underlying pain condition [109].

Adverse effects occur significantly more frequently in opioid-treated patients than in placebo groups [16, 70, 76, 78, 85, 86, 88, 95, 110]. Compared with non-opioid treatments, opioid-treated patients more often discontinue therapy due to adverse effects, though discontinuation rates for lack of efficacy are similar between the two groups [59].

Systematic reviews report treatment discontinuation due to adverse effects ranging from 22% to 50%, with the highest incidence observed in chronic low back pain [56, 57, 71, 104]. Häuser et al. reported that 71.5% of patients enrolled in randomized controlled trials did not continue opioid treatment in the medium to long term [100].

To prevent or relieve constipation, bulk-forming or osmotic laxatives should be systematically prescribed, alongside lifestyle and dietary advice (fiber intake, hydration, physical activity, and regular bowel habits). As second-line strategies, opioid rotation, fixed-dose combinations with naloxone or naloxegol may be considered [53, 111]. For nausea or vomiting, metoclopramide may be used, in addition to opioid dose reduction or rotation [53, 111]. For central nervous system effects, dose reduction is also recommended [53].

In trials with 12-week evaluation periods, discontinuation due to lack of efficacy was relatively low, occurring in 6.2% of patients treated with tapentadol and 3.4% with oxycodone [90]. Another systematic review reported a 10.3% discontinuation rate for lack of efficacy [71]. Notably, discontinuations for lack of efficacy were less frequent in opioid-treated patients compared with placebo, suggesting some degree of effectiveness despite dropouts [76, 78, 89].

Comparisons Between Molecules

Systematic reviews and meta-analyses have not demonstrated significant differences in the frequency of adverse events among different opioid molecules [91], with the exception for tapentadol, which has shown a lower incidence of adverse events compared with oxycodone [90]. This finding is supported by reviews from Riemsma et al. [92], the Cochrane review by Santos et al. [93], Baron et al. [94], and the meta-analysis by Mercier et al. comparing tramadol and tapentadol [95].

Nuckols et al. [112] highlighted cardiac risks associated with methadone, as well as high-dose tramadol, or when concomitantly with other medications, due to QT interval prolongation. The same review also warned of a high risk of overdose with transmucosal fentanyl [112].

Dependence and Opioid use Disorder (Misuse, Abuse, Addiction)

Definitions of the terms Addiction, Craving, Misuse, Opioid Use Disorder, and Physical Dependence are provided in the glossary in Appendix 1 (Supplementary Material).

Epidemiological studies suggest that the proportion of patients developing addiction after more than 6 months of opioid therapy is relatively low, estimated at 0.27% [71, 104]. However, misuse and abuse are more frequent, with rates ranging from 5% to 26% in the review by Sehgal et al. [60], and 5.7% in extension phases of medium- to long-term studies [100].

A systematic review by Minozzi et al., including 17 studies with 88,235 patients, reported an incidence of dependence ranging from 0% to 24% (median 0.5%) and a prevalence of 0% to 31% (median 4.5%) for treatments lasting 6–12 months [113]. According to Vowles et al., misuse prevalence ranges from 21.7% to 29.3%, while addiction prevalence ranges from 7.8% to 11.7% [114].

The 2016 CDC guidelines reported misuse risk rates between 3 and 26% in long-term opioid therapy [103]. Voon et al. noted dependence rates of 3–26% (incidence 0.5%) in patients with chronic non-cancer pain, along with “problematic use” ranging from <1% to 81%. Specific breakdowns included abuse (0.43–8%), misuse (8–29%), and addiction (0.05–14%) [115].

A meta-analysis by Higgins et al. estimated that 4.7% of patients on opioid analgesics develop new-onset dependence or abuse during treatment [116].

Identified risk factors for misuse and dependence [54, 74, 117, 118] include a history of substance use disorder, psychiatric comorbidity, daily opioid doses >120 mg morphine equivalent dose (MED), and younger age, while the risk decreases in older patients. Indeed, older adults appear less likely to misuse opioids in the context of chronic non-cancer pain management.

Finally, studies show that among patients treated with opioids for more than 3 months, abuse or dependence was observed in 0.7% of those receiving low doses (<36 mg/day MED) compared with 6.1% in those receiving high doses (>120 mg/day MED) [119]. The risk of overdose increases proportionally with the prescribed dose [103, 119, 120].

Precautions to take

International guidelines for opioid prescribing in chronic non-cancer pain emphasize the importance of a detailed clinical assessment, including comprehensive evaluation of pain characteristics and underlying diagnosis, as well as a thorough psychological and psychosocial assessment [15, 53, 68, 105, 111, 121–127]. Consultation with an appropriate specialist is recommended to ensure first-line etiological treatment [57].

Opioid use may be considered for conditions such as chronic low back pain/radiculopathy, osteoarthritis of the lower limbs (despite a recent recommendation against opioids in osteoarthritis pain [77]), chronic musculoskeletal pain, and neuropathic pain [17, 56, 58, 72, 127–129].

Conversely, opioids are not recommended for primary headaches and facial pain, chronic pelvic pain, fibromyalgia (except tramadol, as a cautious second-line option), or in patients with untreated substance use disorder or psychiatric comorbidities [58, 69, 117, 128, 130–132].

Opioid therapy should only be initiated if expected benefits for both pain and function outweigh risks, and after optimization of non-opioid strategies [14]. In practice, initiation should be considered in cases of severe pain or major functional impairment [57, 106, 122]. All indicated pharmacological and non-pharmacological treatments for the underlying condition should be attempted before resorting to opioids [15, 55, 64, 68, 72, 103, 120, 121, 123, 131, 133, 134]. Prescribing must be embedded within a multimodal approach, with realistic and relevant therapeutic goals, established in collaboration with the patient [53, 56, 57, 72, 103, 106, 111, 118, 121, 123, 124, 126, 133–136]. A 30% improvement in pain scores, function, or quality of life is commonly used as a target in guidelines [57, 58, 124, 125]. These goals may be formalized in a therapeutic agreement between patient and prescriber [55, 57, 111, 133]. Patients must be informed about potential benefits and, especially, risks [55, 105, 123, 124, 131, 133, 134], educated on proper medication use [57, 117], and, in some cases, asked to sign a written informed consent [131].

Initiation should start at the lowest possible dose, typically limited to 40–50 mg/day MED, with slow and gradual titration [53, 57, 103, 105, 124–126, 129, 131–133, 137]. Immediate-release formulations are preferred (except for transmucosal fentanyl [58]) before transitioning to extended-release formulations once the therapeutic dose is identified [14, 57, 58, 68, 103, 106, 123, 124, 132–134]. The European Pain Federation, however, supports direct initiation with extended-release formulations, citing improved adherence, better pain flare management, and reduced misuse [53]. Transdermal fentanyl is not recommended for initiation [53, 58, 111].

Before initiation and at each renewal, it is essential to screen for misuse risk factors [15, 57, 58, 74, 103, 105, 111, 117, 120–123, 126, 132–134]. Co-prescription with benzodiazepines is contraindicated in many guidelines [103, 120, 126, 132]. If co-prescription is unavoidable, benzodiazepine tapering should be pursued whenever possible [14, 53, 124].

Patients on opioids must undergo early and regular reassessment. In particular, patients should be reassessed within 1 to 4 weeks after initiation or dose escalation, and regularly thereafter to verify achievement of therapeutic goals and monitor adverse effects, particularly constipation and nausea, which should be systematically prevented with appropriate symptomatic treatment [14, 53, 55, 57, 58, 64, 68, 103, 105, 118, 120–125, 131–133, 136]. If therapeutic goals are not achieved after 3 months, discontinuation of treatment is recommended [14, 15, 53, 56–58, 103, 120, 121, 123–125, 136]. Some guidelines recommend an initial therapeutic trial lasting 3 to 6 months [53, 105, 111, 121, 131, 132].

Maximum recommended doses vary across guidelines, ranging from 50 mg/day to 200 mg/day MED [15, 68, 103, 106, 112, 120, 126, 132, 134]. A Cochrane review emphasizes that no evidence supports the use of doses above 200 mg/day MED [110].

Specialist consultation (pain medicine, addiction medicine, psychiatry) is advised for complex pain cases, particularly to ensure a comprehensive patient evaluation [15, 58, 123, 126, 133], especially in patients with a history of OUD [58, 123, 132, 134] and when prescribed doses exceed 120 mg/day MED [58, 60, 68, 120, 127, 134].

Appropriate use of Opioid Analgesics in Cancer-Related Pain

Pain is common in cancer patients, particularly in advanced stages, and is one of the four essential components of supportive oncology care to which every patient is entitled [138]. During the curative phase, 30–50% of patients experience moderate to severe pain (≥ 5/10 on a numerical rating scale), rising to 50–60% in advanced cancer and 25% in remission. A neuropathic component is present in 20–40% of patients with active cancer and 20–30% of those in remission [139].

Breakthrough cancer pain (BTcP), defined as transient, short-lasting exacerbations of moderate to severe pain, affects approximately 68% of patients, according to a landmark study conducted by the European Association for Palliative Care [140]. BTcP occurs despite a background pain being controlled with strong opioids [141].

Although cancer-related pain can generally be relieved, some syndromes remain refractory to usual treatments. An estimated 10–18% of patients with active cancer and 10–20% of those in remission experience complex pain despite first-line pharmacological management [139].

Accurate identification of pain and etiology is crucial for effective management. Multimodal analgesia combined with etiological treatment forms the cornerstone of care. Opioids are indicated for the symptomatic treatment of nociceptive cancer pain, which often presents as mixed pain with a neuropathic component [142]. However, etiological treatment should always be prioritized, without delaying symptomatic management [143].

International, European, and French guidelines provide the framework for opioid use in this context and are detailed in the following sections.

Pain Assessment and Treatment Objectives

Psychological, social, and family assessment is crucial, given the multifactorial nature of pain. Systematic screening for anxiety, depression, and cognitive disorders is recommended, notably using the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS), which has been validated in cancer patients [144, 145]. Psychological distress should be assessed and managed appropriately [143].

Pain evaluation should include an analysis of intensity, etiology, and pathophysiology, as well as the patient’s expectations regarding pain relief and functional recovery [142]. Treatment must be continuously adjusted to the patient’s clinical situation, including pain etiology, age, comorbidities, and potential adverse effects [140, 143]. Prescriptions should be understandable to patients and anticipate both predictable and unpredictable breakthrough pain. Reassessment should be aligned with the drug’s duration of action, formulation, and pain intensity [140, 143].

Implementation of Opioid Therapy

Although the WHO analgesic ladder remains validated in cancer pain [12], it is increasingly complemented or replaced by more individualized approaches, including the Lussier and Beaulieu model [11]. In current clinical practice, treatment decisions are guided not only by pain intensity but also by the underlying pain mechanisms (nociceptive, neuropathic, nociplastic, or mixed) and patient-specific factors.

In patients with cancer pain, strict adherence to a stepwise escalation is not always required, and step III opioids may be considered earlier in selected patients with severe pain when clinically justified [140, 147].

Before initiating opioid therapy, a thorough pain assessment is required, considering pain intensity, previous analgesic treatments, and the patient’s overall clinical status [142]. The starting dose, administration frequency, and titration should be individualized to optimize the benefit–risk ratio [142]. For baseline analgesia (excluding rescue doses), combining different opioids, whether strong or weak, is generally not recommended [148].

Step II Opioids

Fixed-dose combinations of codeine/acetaminophen, tramadol/acetaminophen, or opium/acetaminophen require caution to avoid excessive acetaminophen intake, either from self-medication or concomitant prescriptions, which can lead to overdose and hepatotoxicity. Recommended maximum daily doses are 360 mg for codeine, 400 mg for tramadol, and 240 mg for dihydrocodeine [140].

Step III Opioids

In some cases of mild-to-moderate pain, low doses of Step III opioids (≤ 30 mg/day oral morphine, ≤ 20 mg/day oral oxycodone) may be used instead of high doses of Step II opioids [140, 143]. The oral route is preferred [140, 143].

Initiation: Step III opioid can be started with morphine or oxycodone, either as immediate-release or extended-release formulations [140, 149]. Rescue doses of immediate-release opioids should always be prescribed, repeatable every hour if needed, with a maximum of six doses per day [140, 143]. Each rescue dose should correspond to one-tenth to one-sixth of the 24-h total dose [140, 143]. The cumulative rescue dose can then guide adjustment of the extended-release opioid dose [143].

Intravenous titration is recommended when rapid pain control is required. Patient-controlled analgesia (PCA) can be considered if the patient is capable of self-administering boluses [140].

The subcutaneous route is also an option, though effectiveness may be reduced in patients with impaired absorption (malnutrition, edema, microcirculatory disorders) [140, 143, 147].

For painful procedures, immediate-release, short half-life opioids can be administered preventively [140].

For short-lasting pain (< 1h30) with rapid onset, when background opioid therapy is unsuitable or associated with intolerable adverse effects, transmucosal fentanyl may be considered (off-label use), starting with the lowest available dose of the chosen formulation [148].

In very exceptional cases, transmucosal fentanyl may also be used in patients experiencing only breakthrough pain episodes without underlying baseline pain requiring continuous therapy [148].

Safety consideration: Transmucosal fentanyl must always be administrated under direct supervision of a healthcare professional. Close monitoring during and after administration is mandatory to ensure safe use, given the high risk of respiratory depression. Supervision time should be adequate, and populations most vulnerable to respiratory depression should not receive transmucosal fentanyl [148].

Special Populations

Older Patients

In elderly patients, opioid doses should be reduced and/or dosing intervals increased [140, 143].

Patients with Renal Impairment

Moderate renal impairment is not a contraindication to opioid therapy for cancer pain [140]. However, prolonged-release formulations should be avoided, and dosages adjusted by reducing the amount and/or extending dosing intervals [140, 143, 147].

Fentanyl and buprenorphine are preferred in this context [143]. Methadone, which has no active metabolites, can also be used safely [150, 151].

In patients with asthma or chronic respiratory insufficiency, opioids are not contraindicated but should be prescribed with caution [140, 143].

Patients Receiving OAT with Methadone or Buprenorphine

In cancer patients on buprenorphine OAT, several strategies may be considered for pain management. Buprenorphine may be continued and optimized, as no clear analgesic ceiling effect has been demonstrated at therapeutic doses (see Patients receiving prescribed opioid medications, OAT, or illicit opioids).

However, given the limited evidence-based guidance in this setting, treatment decisions should be individualized. Experts recommend avoiding the addition of full agonists for pain management. Instead, a switch to an alternative opioid may be considered, based on clinical judgment and multidisciplinary evaluation.

In patients on methadone OAT: Methadone is typically administered once daily for OAT. For adequate analgesic coverage in cancer pain, the daily dose should be divided into two or three administrations. Additional rescue doses may be required, and background doses should be adjusted accordingly.

Breakthrough Cancer Pain (BTcP)

BTcP occurs in patients whose background pain is controlled with a strong opioid. It may be spontaneous and unpredictable, without identified triggers, or associated with unpredictable factors such as coughing, sneezing, gastrointestinal or bladder spasms, and headaches [153]. BTcP can also be predictable, occurring during voluntary actions (e.g., movement, eating, defecation, urination, swallowing) or triggered by care procedures (e.g., mobilization, hygiene care) or diagnostic/therapeutic interventions [153].

If background pain is inadequately controlled, the episodes should not be classified as BTcP, but rather a sign of insufficient treatment, disease progression, or therapeutic tolerance. Likewise, end-of-dose pain is not considered BTcP and should prompt reassessment of baseline therapy [153].

Management of BTcP: primarily relies on transmucosal fentanyl, except when the desired analgesic effect must last longer than 2 h, in which case morphine or oxycodone may be preferred [153]. Most BTcP episodes, regardless of their pathophysiological mechanism, respond to transmucosal fentanyl citrate. However, for neuropathic BTcP, baseline treatment with appropriate agents (e.g., antidepressants, antiepileptics) should be reassessed to reduce both frequency and intensity of episodes while avoiding unnecessary escalation of total opioid dosage. Adjuvant measures may include antiepileptics or antidepressants for neuropathic pain, or antispasmodics for visceral pain. Additionally, complications of long-term opioid therapy such as constipation or urinary retention should be identified and managed, as they may cause acute iatrogenic pain that is often overlooked [153].

Refractory Pain

Refractory pain is defined as pain that does not adequately respond to “standard” therapies although further analgesic options may still be available [148]. In such cases, management strategies may include opioid rotation (switching to another opioid), modification of the route of administration, such as intravenous administration for rapid titration or intrathecal administration in specific circumstances [148].

When pain control is insufficient, a change in the route of administration, from oral or transdermal to intravenous, may be considered. If the patient is able to self-administer boluses, intravenous patient-controlled analgesia (PCA) can be implemented [140], either with the same opioid or as part of an opioid rotation strategy. No significant difference in efficacy has been demonstrated between these two approaches [154].

As a last resort, intravenous fentanyl or sufentanil (off-label use) may be considered, but only after consultation with a team experienced in their use [148].

Caution is required when using conversion ratios. Always use conservative (lower-bound) conversion ratios to ensure safety. When calculating the required dose of the new opioid, conversion tables [148] may be used but must always be interpreted critically in light of each clinical context. It is also recommended to prescribe rescue doses to allow therapeutic adjustment [148].

Methadone

Opioid rotation to methadone may be considered in cases of refractory cancer pain, after assessment by a specialized palliative care or pain management team, and only as a second-line option after failure of other step III opioids [155].

Treatment initiation and titration must be performed in a hospital setting under the supervision of a specialized team, due to the risk of delayed overdose, which may occur up to 7 days after initiation and requires close monitoring [148]. Once the optimal dose has been reached, treatment continuation and prescription renewal can be managed by any physician [148].

Potential drug–drug interactions should be carefully assessed at initiation, during dose adjustments, and whenever a new treatment is introduced [148].

Intrathecal Analgesia

Spinal analgesia, either intrathecal or epidural, combining opioids with local anesthetics, is recommended for palliative patients with refractory pain that remains uncontrolled despite the equivalent of approximately 300 mg/day of oral morphine, or in cases of severe adverse effects with systemic analgesics [140, 148].

Cancer Survivors

The number of cancer survivors continues to increase owing to advances in diagnosis and treatment. Although there is no universal definition of “cancer survivor” in oncology, the most widely accepted one (National Cancer Institute, National Comprehensive Cancer Network, National Coalition for Cancer Survivorship) defines a survivor as: “an individual is considered a cancer survivor from the time of diagnosis and throughout their life” [156].

Opioid prescription should be reserved for survivors with persistent pain that does not respond to standard treatments and that causes a significant physical and/or emotional burden [146]. Pain in cancer survivors is most often chronic in nature. When its intensity, impact, and underlying mechanisms warrant consideration of opioid therapy, management should follow principles applied to chronic non-cancer pain, favoring long-acting opioid formulations (see section "Appropriate Use of Opioid Analgesics in Chronic Non-Cancer Pain", Appropriate use of opioid analgesics in chronic non-cancer pain). Preventive measures should be consistent with those recommended for long-term opioid use.

Risk of Opioid use Disorder (Misuse/Addiction)

Monitoring data from the United States indicate that short-acting opioids are associated with a higher risk of OUD compared with prolonged-release opioids [157]. In France, one study highlighted a high risk of OUD related to the use of transmucosal fentanyl for chronic non-cancer pain, whereas this risk is rare when used for cancer-related pain [158].

No specific tool has been validated to assess the risk of OUD in oncology patients. Some clinicians use scales developed for non-cancer pain, such as the Prescription Opioid Misuse Index Scale (POMI) scale [159, 160].

Before initiating opioid therapy, particularly in patients likely to require long-term treatment, it is crucial to screen for major risk factors of OUD, including psychiatric and addictive comorbidities (e.g., tobacco, alcohol, and medication use) [146, 150].

For patients with a substance use disorder, whether involving licit or illicit substances, multidisciplinary management is recommended. Pain relief should be ensured while minimizing OUD risk. Weekly dispensing of opioids, rather than monthly, may be proposed [150].

Patients with a history of physical dependence and/or OUD may be reluctant to start opioid treatment due to fear of relapse. A multidisciplinary approach, including addiction medicine support, is advisable [150].

In patients presenting with OUD, it is important to rule out insufficient pain relief, cognitive impairment, or poor patient understanding leading to irregular opioid intake before concluding misuse for recreational purposes. BTcP episodes should always be systematically assessed, identified, and treated [146, 150].

OUD can also occur in patients receiving opioids for cancer-related pain, particularly when used for their anxiolytic effect. Such cases should prompt referral to a multidisciplinary team including specialists in addiction medicine and/or palliative care [146, 150].

Limitations

These recommendations have several limitations. First, the underlying literature review covered publications up to December 2021 and therefore did not formally integrate all subsequent evidence or guideline updates. Nevertheless, several major recommendations and updated versions of previously cited guidelines were added to the manuscript when deemed clinically relevant. Second, despite prioritization of high-level evidence, some recommendations rely on expert consensus or lower levels of evidence, particularly in complex clinical situations such as perioperative management in patients receiving opioid agonist treatment.

Conclusions

These national guidelines, developed under the auspices of the French National Authority for Health, provide comprehensive recommendations on the appropriate use of opioid analgesics in acute and chronic pain, both cancer-related and non-cancer-related. By considering opioids as a single pharmacological class, they emphasize prudent prescribing, prioritization of non-opioid and multimodal strategies, and systematic evaluation of the benefit–risk balance. Practical tools are provided to support prescribers in diverse clinical settings, from primary care to specialized units. These recommendations aim to promote safer and more consistent opioid prescribing practices, balancing effective pain relief with the prevention of dependence, opioid use disorder, and overdose.

A second part of the guidelines, focused on the prevention and management of opioid use disorder and overdoses, will complement the present work.
