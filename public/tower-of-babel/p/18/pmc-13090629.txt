Characterization of MACPF domain containing proteins in oyster Crassostrea gigas and their expression profile in response to immune stimulation

Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Ten CgMACPFs share highly conserved MACPF domains were identified in oyster genome.•CgMACPFs were classified into MPEG1 (Macrophage-expressed gene 1) and PRF1 (Perforin-1) subfamily.•CgMACPFs in MPEG1 subfamily functioned in embryonic development and were responsive for immune stimulation.•CgMACPFs in PRF1 subfamily (especially CgMACPF-7/8) were involved in immune priming response of oyster.Keywords: MACPF containing proteins, Oyster, Structure features, Expression pattern, Immune priming

Introduction

The immune system of organisms is a complex and sophisticated defense mechanism that has evolved to protect against a wide range of pathogens. Among the various components of the immune system, proteins containing the membrane attack complex/perforin (MACPF) domain have emerged as key players in immune responses across different species. MACPF domain containing proteins are characterized by a conserved structural motif that enables them to insert into lipid bilayers and form pores [1]. Recently, over 500 members of the MACPF protein family have been found in organisms ranging from bacteria to human beings [2,3]. Based on structure features, the MACPF protein superfamily was classified into five subfamilies: complement proteins, perforin, macrophage-expressed gene 1 (MPEG1), astrotactins (ASTN) and bone morphogenic protein/retinoic acid inducible neural-specific protein (BRINP) [3]. In detailed, members of MPEG1 and the BRINPs have relatively simple domain structures, containing a MACPF domain at the N-terminus and a C-terminal region of unknown structure and function. While, members of complement proteins and perforin subfamilies have a complex structure containing many different associated domains, except for MACPF domain. ASTN subfamily contains the largest human MACPF proteins with multiple domains [3,4]. They are composed of two N-terminal transmembrane helices, three epidermal growth factor-like domains in the central part followed by the MACPF domain and finally a fibronectin type-III domain on the C-terminus.

Proteins containing MACPF domain play diverse and critical roles in biological processes ranging from defense mechanisms—where membrane attack serves as an essential component of innate immune defense against invading microorganisms—to cellular signaling and organismal development [5]. In vertebrates, the most well-known MACPF containing proteins are perforin 1 (PRF1) and components of the membrane attack complex (MAC) of the complement system [6]. Perforin 1 is primarily expressed by cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) and natural killer (NK) cells. Upon recognition of target cells, such as virus infected or tumor cells, CTLs and NK cells release perforin 1, which polymerizes in the target cell membrane, forming pores [[7], [8], [9], [10]]. These pores then allow the entry of granzymes, proteases that initiate apoptosis of the target cell, thus eliminating the threat. The MAC, on the other hand, is assembled as part of the complement cascade. It consists of several proteins, including C6, C7, C8, and C9, all of which contain MACPF domains. The MAC inserts into the membranes of pathogens, leading to their lysis and destruction [[7], [8], [9], [10]]. In invertebrates, although the immune system lacks the complexity of the vertebrate adaptive immune system with its antigen specific receptors, MACPF containing proteins still play important roles in innate immunity. For example, in Drosophila melanogaster, the MACPF-containing protein Torso-like (Tsl) plays an important role in immunity by regulating the development of the cellular immune system, as Tsl null mutants exhibit a severely reduced number of circulating immune cells and an impaired ability to phagocytose bacterial particles [11]. In Suberites domuncula, the MPEG1 protein was identified as an executing molecule in the elimination of Gram-negative bacteria [12]. Evolutionary studies of MPEG1, have demonstrated that MPEG1 is one of the oldest eukaryotic MACPF members, present in early metazoan phyla including Porifera (sponges) [[6], [7], [8], [9]]. They are thought to contribute to the innate immune defense by either directly interacting with pathogens or by modulating the activity of other immune related cells [8,9].

Despite the growing knowledge of MACPF containing proteins in vertebrates and some invertebrates, relatively little is known about these proteins in oysters, particularly in the context of immune priming. The oyster Crassostrea gigas is an important aquaculture and widely studied bivalve species, which constantly exposed to a diverse array of pathogens in their aquatic environment [13]. In-depth study of immune defense mechanism of oyster could potentially lead to the development of strategies to improve oyster health and productivity in aquaculture. Though its genome has been sequenced [13], the detailed information of MACPF containing proteins and their possible immune functions are still waiting to be uncovered. Recently, immune priming, a phenomenon where an initial encounter with a pathogen leads to an enhanced immune response upon subsequent exposure, has been increasingly recognized in oysters C. gigas, which could potentially lead to the development of strategies to improve oyster health and productivity in aquaculture [[14], [15], [16]]. However, the underpinning mechanisms of their immune priming is still missing and whether MACPF containing proteins in oyster involved in priming response are yet not known. In the present study, the MACPF family members were identified from the Crassostrea gigas (C. gigas) genome and the molecular characterization and phylogenetic relationships were investigated through bioinformatics analysis. In addition, the MACPF family gene expression analyses were conducted in different developmental stages and different tissues, as well as the dynamic expression characteristics in response to Vibrio splendidus. The results obtained here will provide valuable information for evolutionary classification and further functional investigations of MACPF family members in invertebrates.

Materials and methods

Experimental animals and V. splendidus

Two-year-old Pacific oysters (Crassostrea gigas) with a mean shell length of 14 cm were obtained from an aquaculture facility in City Dalian, Province Liaoning, China. Oysters were acclimatized in filtered seawater at ambient temperature for 7 days prior to experimentation. During laboratory maintenance, oysters were fed daily with concentrated algal powder, and seawater was replaced every 24 h. All experimental procedures involving C. gigas were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Dalian Ocean University and conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines.

For immune stimulation experiments, bacterial pathogen Vibrio splendidus was cultured in 2216E liquid medium (Hopebio, China) at 16 °C for 24 h with shaking at 150 rpm. Bacterial cells were harvested by centrifugation (4000 × g, 10 min), washed twice, and resuspended in sterile artificial seawater to a final concentration of 2 × 10⁸ CFU/mL.

Immune challenge and tissue harvesting

One hundred and forty oysters were collected for immune stimulation experiment as previous report [10]. Those oysters were randomly divided into two groups: 1) sterile seawater control (SW), injected with 100 μL sterile artificial seawater; 2) V. splendidus challenged group (VS), injected with 100 μL V. splendidus suspension (2 × 10⁸ CFU/mL in sterile artificial seawater) as previously described. Haemolymph samples were collected from both groups at eight time points (0, 3, 6, 12, 24, 48, 72, and 96 h post-injection). Additionally, haemocytes and tissues (hepatopancreas, gill, gonad, mantle, labial palp, ganglion, and adductor muscle) were collected from unchallenged oysters. All the samples from three oysters were pooled together as one sample, and there were three replicates (n = 3). They were added with 500 μL TRIzol reagent (TransGen Biotech, China) for total RNA extraction.

Identification and analysis of CgMACPF genes in C. gigas

Putative MACPF genes were identified from the Crassostrea gigas genome (NCBI Assembly: GCF_902,806,645.1) using HMMER v3.3.2 with the PF01823 (MACPF) profile. Candidate sequences were verified by reciprocal BLASTP against the UniProt database [17]. Theoretical isoelectric points (pI) and molecular weights (MW) were calculated using the ExPASy ProtParam tool [18]. Non-redundant sequences with complete MACPF domains were retained. Domain organization was predicted using SMART v9.0 (default parameters), and detecting conserved motifs using the MEME Suite5, with a maximum motif limit set to 12 (prevent motif overlap and maintain analysis accuracy)[19]. All results were visualized using TBtools [20].

Phylogenetic analysis and chromosomal localization of CgMACPF in C. gigas

For the phylogenetic analysis, genome sequences and amino acid sequence of MACPF genes were downloaded from NCBI (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) and Uniprot (https://www.uniprot.org/) database for the following organisms: Homo sapiens, Danio rerio, Drosophila melanogaster, Mus musculus, Pomacea maculata, Strongylocentrotus purpuratus, Xenopus tropicalis, Petromyzon marinus, Mytilus galloprovincialis,Gallus gallus, Pocillopora damicornis, Haliotis rufescens, Mizuhopecten yessoensis, C. gigas. The corresponding BioProject numbers and database accession numbers for these 56 genomes, along with the retrieved MACPF amino acid sequences, are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Multiple sequence alignment was carried out by the Clustal W Multiple Alignment program, and the result was exported from the online platform (http://www.bio-soft.net/sms/). The phylogenetic tree was constructed using the neighbor-joining algorithm implemented in MEGA X software. The tree was visualized and annotated using iTOL (https://itol.embl.de/). Chromosomal locations and sizes of the MACPF genes in the oyster were derived from the oyster genome assembly (cgigas_uk_roslin_v1) and analyzed through TBtools.

Expression profiling of CgMACPF during embryonic stages and immune response in C. gigas

Gene expression analysis was performed using RPKM (Reads Per Kilobase Million) values derived from publicly available oyster RNA-seq datasets [13]. These datasets encompass multiple developmental stages and adult tissues. Expression patterns of the MACPF genes across these stages and tissues were visualized in a heatmap generated using TBtools. Expression patterns of CgMACPF post stimulations by lipopolysaccharide (LPS), glucan (GLU), peptidoglycan (PGN), and Poly(I:C) were examined using RNA-Seq datasets (project number PRJNA233528) from the NCBI database [21]. Expression patterns of CgMACPF during immune priming of V. splendidus were examined based on our previous RNA-Seq datasets [22]. HISAT2 (v2.0.5) was used for mapping data to the Pacific oyster genome, and RPKM method for estimating gene expression levels. Mfuzz package in R (version 4.2.3) was used for the analysis of gene expression trends and performed clustering.

Statistical analysis

All data were shown as mean ± S.D. The two-samples Student's test was performed for the comparisons between groups. Multiple group comparisons were executed by one-way ANOVA and followed by a Tukey multiple group comparison test using Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) 20.0 Software. The difference was considered as significant at p < 0.05.

Results

Identification and chromosomal localization of CgMACPF genes in C. gigas

A comprehensive analysis of the genomic landscape and transcriptome databases of oyster was conducted to screen all the proteins containing MACPF domains, and ten CgMACPF genes were identified and named as CgMACPF1–10. Based on the genomic data of NCBI, a varied number of MACPF genes were found in evolutionary different species, such as seven MACPFs in Amphimedon queenslandica, three in Aplysia californica, ten in Pomacea canaliculata, five in scallop Mizuhopecten yessoensis, 33 in mussel Mytilus galloprovincialis, one in fruit fly Drosophila melanogaster, 21 in sea urchin Strongylocentrotus purpuratus, ten in Zebrafish Danio rerio, 14 in mouse Mus musculus and 13 in human Homo sapiens (Table 1).

The conserved domain analysis showed that all CgMACPFs possessed a MACPF domain (Fig. 2B). Among these, CgMACPF-1–7 contained only the MACPF domain. Furthermore, CgMACPF-1/3/5/7 contained both a C-terminal transmembrane domain and a signal peptide. CgMACPF-4 and CgMACPF-2 contained a C-terminal transmembrane domain and a signal peptide, respectively. Except for MACPF domain, CgMACPF-8/9/10 all contained an additional ApeC domain and CgMACPF-6 contained three low complex regions. CgMACPF-8 and CgMACPF-9 contained an additional signal peptide, while CgMACPF-10 contained an additional C-terminal transmembrane domain.

Phylogenetic relationship and classification of CgMACPFS

Based on the structure characteristics, CgMACPFs could be classified into two subfamilies: MPEG1 subfamily with CgMACPF1–5 and Perforin-1 subfamily with CgMACPF6–10. To further confirm the result, a phylogenetic tree was constructed by comparing the full-length amino acid sequences of CgMACPF and those from other species. A total of 56 MACPF genes were assessed in the phylogenetic tree (Fig. 3). In general, those MACPF genes were mainly clustered into two branches, MPEG1 subfamily and Perforin-1 subfamily. The ten CgMACPF were classified into two distinct subfamilies, CgMACPF1–5 in MPEG1 branch and CgMACPF6–10 in Perforin-1 branch.

The mRNA expression patterns of CgMACPFS during embryonic development and different tissues

The mRNA expressions of CgMACPFs at different developmental stages were analyzed with the data deposited in the oyster genome database. RNA-seq datasets from different developmental stages and adult tissues of the oyster were used to detect the spatiotemporal expression spectrum of CgMACPFs (Fig. 4). The expression patterns of CgMACPFs can be divided into three groups across different developmental stages (Fig. 4A). The first group comprises CgMACPF-1 and CgMACPF-3, which was consistently high expressed across most developmental stages, with a transient reduction observed specifically in the d-shaped larval stage. The second group comprises three CgMACPFs that exhibit stage-specific expression during oyster embryonic development. CgMACPF-2 and CgMACPF-5 were highly expressed since d-shaped larval stage till to juvenile stage, and CgMACPF-4 was especially high expressed during the late developmental stages of spat and juvenile. In contrast, the rest five CgMACPFs (CgMACPF-6/7/8/9/10) shows lastingly low expression throughout development, with transcript levels remaining undetectable or near baseline in all examined stages. These distinct expression patterns suggested divergent functional specializations within the CgMACPF family during ontogeny.

Discussion

The MACPF containing proteins are widespread and can be found in all domains of life, with 1271 MACPF containing proteins in the SMART database (http://smart.embl-heidelberg.de), and they are involved in various aspects of biological processes. The function of MACPF family members has been widely investigated in many organisms [1–3,7]. However, no detailed analysis of the MACPF protein family in the oyster C. gigas has been reported. Consequently, the functions and characteristics of these proteins remain largely uncharacterized. In this study, a complete set of MACPF family genes was identified in the genome of the oyster C. gigas, and their protein structure, phylogenetic classification and expression patterns during developmental stages and immune response under V. splendidus stimulation were analyzed. The results provide a new perspective for a deeper understanding of the molecular evolution and functional diversity of the MACPF channel family.

In this study, a total of ten genes with conserved domain MACPF were identified and characterized in oyster C. gigas. These ten CgMACPFs all sharing highly conserved MACPF domains, highlights the evolutionary importance of this protein family across diverse taxa. The conservation of the MACPF domain, which is responsible for pore forming activity in a variety of organisms, suggests a fundamental and ancient role in immune defense. Comparing the CgMACPFs to their counterparts in vertebrates and other invertebrates reveals both conserved and divergent features. Based on structure features, the MACPF protein superfamily was classified into five subfamilies: complement proteins, perforin, MPEG1, ASTN and BRINP. And members of MPEG1 and the BRINPs have relatively simple domain structures, while those of complement proteins and perforin subfamilies have a complex structure containing many different associated domains [[1], [2], [3], [4],7]. In this study, based on the sequence similarity, gene structure, and phylogenetic relationship, CgMACPF genes were divided into two types, five MPEG1 and five PRF1. The classification of CgMACPFs into five MPEG1 like and five PRF1 like proteins based on structural characteristics provides an interesting perspective on their potential functions. In vertebrates, perforin-1 and components of the membrane attack complex are well characterized MACPF containing proteins with specific immune functions [7,8]. Perforin-1 in cytotoxic T lymphocytes and natural killer cells is essential for targeted cell killing, while the membrane attack complex lyses pathogens in the complement system [1,2]. The PRF1 like proteins in oysters, on the other hand, may potentially be associated with cytotoxic like activities, although the exact mechanisms in oysters are likely to be different from those in vertebrates due to the lack of a classical adaptive immune system.

Originally, the MPEG1 gene identified from a macrophage cDNA library showed macrophage lineage-specific and mature stage-specific expression, but it was not expressed in other tissues or cell lines [9]. Such an expression pattern suggests that the MPEG1 gene might be highly specialized and involved in the maturation or differentiation of mammalian macrophages. In invertebrates such as Drosophila, MPEG1 like proteins, also containing the MACPF domain, are involved in processes like phagocytosis and response to bacterial infections [7,8,11]. The MPEG1 like proteins in oysters may be involved in macrophage like functions, similar to their counterparts in insects. This could include processes such as engulfing pathogens or modulating the immune response through cell - cell interactions. Interestingly, while CgMACPF-1 was induced by purified PAMPs (LPS and PGN), its expression decreased upon primary and secondary Vibrio splendidus challenge. This varied response patter may reflect the different complexity between single PAMPs and pathogens, the later of which might trigger broader regulatory networks. The downregulation could also represent a negative feedback mechanism to limit potential tissue damage from this pore-forming molecule, as observed for other immune effectors in oysters [23]. It is unknown whether CgMACPF-1 participates in the differentiation of hemocytes in the Pacific oyster. However, the constitutive expression of MPEG1 in invertebrates implies that it may play a more extensive role in innate immunity or other biological processes [[24], [25], [26], [27], [28]]. The divergence in the functions of these CgMACPFs compared to their counterparts in other organisms may be a result of the unique evolutionary history of oysters. As bivalves, oysters have adapted to an aquatic environment filled with a diverse range of pathogens, and their immune system has likely evolved specific strategies to combat these threats. Understanding these evolutionary adaptations can provide valuable insights into the fundamental principles of immune system evolution and the development of novel immune defense mechanisms.

The expression patter of CgMACPFs during embryonic development or in oyster tissues were also examined to explore its possible role in immune response. CgMACPFs in MPEG1 subfamily found to highly expressed during embryonic development from d-shaped larval stage till to juvenile stage, while those in PRF1 subfamily displayed low expression throughout development. Similarly, CgMACPFs in MPEG1 subfamily were also higher expressed in the haemocytes, mantle, digestive gland, gills, and labial palp, compared with those of PRF1 subfamily. These results indicated that CgMACPFs in MPEG1 subfamily might have the potential function in both embryonic development and adult physiological processes in oyster C. gigas.

The expression patter of CgMACPFs in oyster haemocytes were also examined after PAMPs or V. splendidus stimulation. The up-regulation of CgMACPFs in MPEG1 subfamily was observed in haemocytes after immune stimulation. This result is consistent with that in abalone and sponges, where the up-regulation of MPEG1 gene was observed after challenge with bacteria or LPS [9,12,27]. In Haliotis discus, it has been described that significant up-regulation of MPEG1 transcript levels was observed in haemocytes and gill tissues following challenges with pathogens and PAMPs [29]. Previous research has confirmed that MACPF protein contains both lytic and non-lytic roles and is indispensable in innate immune defense [1,30]. The responsiveness of MPEG1 to infection suggests that MPEG1 is a positive effector in the immune defense of these marine animals.

Immune priming is a kind of innate immune memory identified in invertebrates in recent years, which enabling the body to mount more rapid and effective responses upon re-exposure to pathogens [[14], [15], [16]]. In recent years, increasing attention has been drawn to the role of MACPF domain containing proteins in this process. In the context of immune memory, several lines of research have emerged. In the adaptive immune response, some MACPF containing proteins are associated with cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) and natural killer (NK) cells [1,2]. Perforin, a well-known MACPF containing protein, is crucial for CTLs and NK cells to deliver granzymes into target cells, leading to apoptosis. This mechanism not only helps in eliminating infected or transformed cells during the initial immune response but also primes the immune system for future encounters. Memory CTLs, which are formed as a result of the initial antigen exposure, retain the ability to rapidly secrete perforin upon re-encounter with the antigen, highlighting the role of MACPF containing proteins in maintaining long term immune memory at the effector cell level. However, the exact role MACPF containing proteins in oysters is still not known. In the present study, the mRNA expression level of CgMACPFs during immune priming response induced by V. splendidus stimulation were also examined. The expressions of CgMACPF-7 and CgMACPF-8 were significantly induced by the twice V. splendidus stimulation, with a much higher expression level in the second immune response than those of the first, indicating a memory response in oysters. The detailed functions of CgMACPF-7 and CgMACPF-8 in priming response of oyster, and their interaction with other components of the immune memory related pathways remain to be fully elucidated. In contrast, CgMACPF-9, which belongs to the PRF1 subfamily, showed increased expression after primary challenge and further upregulation upon secondary challenge, suggesting that PRF1 family members may be differentially regulated during immune priming and potentially contribute to the enhanced secondary immune response. Future research is needed to precisely define these interactions and develop strategies to target or enhance their functions for therapeutic purposes.

In summary, a novel MACPF gene family have been identified in C. gigas, which comprises ten members, classified into two groups: MPEG1 and PRF1. Among these, MPEG1 members were highly expressed in haemocytes and functioned in embryonic development. CgMACPFs in PRF1 subfamily (especially CgMACPF-7/8) were involved in immune priming response of oyster. This study broadens our understanding of the MACPF family in mollusks and the function of the MACPF domain. Further studies are warranted to elucidate the detailed mechanisms by which molluscan MACPF proteins regulate immune responses.
