Analysis of the impact of irradiance and temperature on photovoltaic production: A statistical and machine learning approach
Authors: El Hadji Mbaye Ndiaye, Alphousseyni Ndiaye, Mactar Faye, Daouda Gueye, Amadou Ba, Mamadou Traore
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study explores the influence of solar irradiance (Ir) and ambient temperature (T) on photovoltaic (PV) production (P) by combining statistical analysis and deep learning techniques. A strong positive correlation was found between irradiance and PV output with Pearson coefficient (R=0.9211), while temperature exhibited a moderate effect (R=0.4477). A two-way analyze of variance (ANOVA) confirmed the statistical significance of these environmental factors. Furthermore, an autoencoder-based model was developed to capture complex nonlinear relationships and outperformed classical regression models in terms of accuracy and generalization. These results highlight the potential of machine learning methods for improving the understanding and optimization of PV systems in variable climatic conditions.Combines statistical analysis and deep learning to assess PV production factorsEmploys an autoencoder model to capture nonlinear relationships more effectivelyThe model integrates a new interaction term Ir.T that increases sensitivity to joint environmental variations.

Background

The rapid growth of renewable energy systems has elevated photovoltaic (PV) technology to a central role in the global energy transition [1]. However, the intermittent nature of solar energy production primarily driven by environmental factors such as solar irradiance and ambient temperature poses significant challenges in forecasting and optimizing PV system performance [2,3]. Understanding the extent to which these parameters influence photovoltaic output is essential for improving energy yield predictions, system reliability, and overall grid integration strategies [4].

While the impact of solar irradiance on PV production is widely recognized as positive and direct, the influence of temperature is more nuanced [5]. Higher temperatures can reduce the efficiency of PV cells, leading to a decrease in energy production, even in conditions of high irradiance [6,7]. Traditional modeling approaches, such as linear regression or empirical formulas, often fail to capture the nonlinear interactions and temporal dependencies between these environmental variables and PV output [8].

Several studies have examined how environmental conditions affect photovoltaic (PV) performance. Jatoi et al. developed an empirical model highlighting the strong influence of module temperature on efficiency [6], while Barry et al. proposed a dynamic model considering temporal atmospheric variations [9]. Petrakis et al. showed that non-uniform irradiance and temperature can cause unequal generator performance, challenging traditional models [10]. Regression approaches include Kim et al., who applied the Gompertz curve to capture non-linear irradiance–production relations with higher accuracy [11]. Other work stressed the role of tilt angle, demonstrating its optimization is crucial for grid-connected PV systems [4]. Collectively, these studies emphasize the need for accurate models to predict production under varying conditions.

The motivation behind this methodology is twofold. First, we aim to establish a robust statistical framework that quantifies the individual and combined effects of irradiance and temperature on PV production. This involves correlation analysis, multiple linear regression, and two-way ANOVA to assess the significance and strength of these relationships [12]. Second, we introduce a machine learning-based approach specifically using an autoencoder architecture to model the complex, nonlinear dynamics inherent in PV systems [13]. Autoencoders are particularly well-suited for this task as they can learn latent representations of high-dimensional data and reconstruct outputs with high fidelity, even under varying environmental conditions.

This hybrid methodology serves as both an analytical tool and a predictive framework. It offers researchers and engineers a more nuanced understanding of how environmental conditions affect PV systems, while also providing a practical machine learning solution for real-time performance forecasting. Moreover, this method is adaptable and can be extended to other types of renewable energy systems or environmental datasets.

By combining statistical rigor with the flexibility of deep learning, our methodology contributes to the development of more intelligent and adaptive energy systems. It can be particularly useful in regions with highly variable weather patterns or for PV operators seeking to implement predictive maintenance and energy dispatch strategies.

Proposed approach

Deep learning approach using autoencoders

Autoencoder architecture

To effectively capture the complex, nonlinear interactions between environmental variables (Ir, T) and, crucially, to model the temporal dependency of the Production (P), we implemented a Supervised Regression Autoencoder (AE-R) architecture. Unlike a traditional Deep Stacked Autoencoder (DAE), this model is trained in an end-to-end supervised manner for the explicit prediction task of Pt.

The core architectural decision is the use of an enriched input vector, Xt, which includes the Production measured at the previous time step (Pt−1) to better track abrupt temporal variations and system inertia. This choice is deliberate, as the primary objective of this model is Ultra-Short-Term Forecasting (Nowcasting) for the next t + 1 hour. In this operational context, the value of (Pt−1) is known and measured at the moment the prediction for Pt is required, thereby ensuring the integrity of the forecasting process and eliminating the risk of information leakage.

The resulting architecture is asymmetrical and utilizes an enriched input vector Xt (Eq. (4)), which includes the Production measured at the previous time step (Pt−1) to better track abrupt temporal variations:(4)Xt=[Irt,Tt,Pt−1]

The Hyperbolic Tangent Sigmoid (tansig) function f(⋅) was utilized for all hidden layers in the encoder. The output layer uses a linear function g(⋅) for continuous Production prediction.

Encoding and prediction mechanism

The entire network performs a sequential transformation from the enriched inputs to the final output prediction.

The transformation from the hidden layer h(l−1) to layer h(l) within the encoder is defined by Eq. (5):(5)h(l)=f(W(l)h(l−1)+b(l))

Here, W(l) and b(l) are the encoder's weight matrix and bias vectors for layer l, respectively. The input Xtis mapped to the 10-dimensional latent representation Z=h(3).

The output layer acts as the truncated decoder, transforming the latent representation Z directly into the predicted ProductionP^t (Eq. (6)).(6)P^t=g(W(out)Z+b(out))

Here, W(out) and b(out) are the parameters of the regression layer.

Method validation

Fig. 5 shows the p-values associated with the coefficients of the quadratic model relating PV production to irradiation and temperature. An Ir value with p ≈ 0 confirms that irradiation has a dominant and highly significant effect on PV production. This is consistent with physical expectations: higher irradiation generally leads to increased production. A T value with p ≈ 0.085: This is not significant at the conventional 5 % threshold (p > 0.05), but tends toward significance. It suggests that the temperature effect, while real, is not consistently strong in the sample considered. Irradiation squared (Ir²) with p ≈ 0: also very significant. This result indicates that the effect of irradiation is not purely linear: there is curvature in the relationship between Ir and production, possibly due to panel saturation effects. Temperature squared (T²) with p ≈ 0.072: marginally significant. This may indicate slight non-linearity in the temperature effect, particularly related to performance degradation at high temperatures.

These statistical results confirm the appropriateness of a nonlinear modeling approach to capture the interaction between irradiation, temperature, and production.

The R² analysis demonstrates that the non-linear, feature-extracting capabilities of the Autoencoder are essential for achieving accurate and robust predictions throughout the annual cycle, especially during periods of transition or high variability where the linear model completely fails to capture the data variance.

Our results, corroborated by the ANOVA analysis, confirm the statistical significance of temperature. However, the intensity of its impact is moderate compared to irradiance, a phenomenon also observed in other studies that included tilt angle as a study variable [4]. This modulation of the thermal effect justifies our seasonal approach and the critical days study, as the temperature's degrading effect becomes maximal only during periods of high heat and high irradiance (Hot Season and Hottest Day), a key point for the operational management of the power plant.

Limitations

While the proposed autoencoder-based model demonstrates strong performance in capturing the nonlinear interactions between irradiation and temperature on PV production, some limitations should be noted:•The model was trained and validated using data collected over a limited time frame and geographical area. Its generalizability to other locations or climates may require retraining with local data.•The autoencoder’s performance depends on the quality and representativeness of the input dataset. Missing values, sensor noise, or anomalies in the data could affect its predictive accuracy.•The method does not explicitly account for other potentially influential parameters such as wind speed, humidity, or dust accumulation on panels, which might also impact PV performance.•The interaction term Ir.T, while insightful, may not capture all higher-order effects present in real-world conditions with rapidly changing weather patterns.
