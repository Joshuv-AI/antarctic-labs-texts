Expansion of Antarctic surface melt through the 21st century
Authors: Yaowen Zheng, Nicholas R. Golledge, Alexandra Gossart, Shoujuan Shu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Climate models show that Antarctic surface melt will increase through the current century. Surface melting changes ice sheet albedo, the availability of liquid water for endemic and invasive species, and may even accelerate ice shelf collapse and global sea level rise. Here we show, using 1 km downscaled projections of potential Antarctic surface melt, that the total area experiencing surface melt will expand by more than 10% by 2100 under a Shared Socio-economic Pathway 3-7.0 scenario, with increased potential melt totals likely to threaten the viability of ice shelves mostly in the West Antarctic Peninsula and Amundsen Sea Embayment, through an elevated risk of hydrofracture. By calculating the latitudinal rate of melt migration we also find that Shared Socio-economic Pathway 1-2.6 is the only emissions scenario under which the rate of future Antarctic surface melt expansion will stabilize at present levels.

Introduction

Future warming of Antarctic air temperatures is projected to lead to a multi-fold increase in surface melt rates by mid-century1,2, as a consequence of the positive and non-linear relationship between air temperature and surface melt1,3–6. Algal supraglacial ecosystems, currently widespread in Greenland, will likely expand in Antarctica with increasing future surface melt7, darkening the surface and potentially further accelerating melt8. Expanded Antarctic surface melt may also impact ecosystems indirectly, for example, through the effects of enhanced glacial runoff on ocean temperature, salinity, and circulation9, which can impact marine productivity, or through the impact of ice shelf collapse on marine habitats7. The ability for surface melt to initiate ice shelf collapse, through a process known as hydrofracture, is highly debated. Studies differ widely in their views of how vulnerable Antarctic ice shelves are to melt-driven disintegration, from those that have forecasted widespread collapse this century10 to those that foresee widespread stability except under the highest emissions scenarios11. Recent projections of ice shelf collapse due to basal, rather than surface, melting9,12,13 imply that synergistic risks exist, in which melt at either boundary might compound the overall impact of environmental warming.

Understanding the potential ramifications of future Antarctic surface melt expansion is therefore important for correctly predicting environmental and habitat change14, ice sheet mass balance15, and ice shelf stability1,16. Yet present-day estimates of Antarctic surface melt are complex because of limited in situ observations5,17. Surface melt estimates therefore rely on surface energy balance (SEB) models, satellite-based inferences of melt occurrence, and surface climatology from reanalysis products and regional climate models18–20. Currently, Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6 (CMIP6) general circulation models (GCMs) are the main source of global climate projections that underpin international policy efforts to mitigate future environmental change21,22. These models represent surface melt at spatial resolutions of ~1°, which is too coarse to pinpoint potential zones of hydrofracture23 (Supplementary Table 1). Regional Climate Models (RCMs) have higher spatial resolutions than GCMs, but are typically still restricted to 10–50 km15,24–26, with the possibility of achieving 2 km through statistical downscaling27. Machine learning techniques that make use of satellite data and model outputs to predict melt already show great promise, but are still in their infancy16,28,29, and are yet to be applied to future scenarios for the AIS. Furthermore, machine learning regression-based approaches still rely on process-based models for training, and studies show that even regional climate models that solve the complete surface energy budget are still deficient in their estimations of Antarctic surface melt30,31 and exhibit problems of model dependency25,32.

An alternative approach that is commonly employed to calculate surface melt, for both ecological studies as well as ice sheet simulations, is the temperature-index method, or positive degree-day (PDD) approach3,5,14. PDD schemes are computationally cheaper than the surface energy balance (SEB) schemes incorporated into GCMs and RCMs. PDD models rely on the observed relationship between air temperature at the snow surface, and the amount of melting that takes place. This means that it is a statistical, rather than physics-based, approach, but the PDD approximation nonetheless employs several of the same components used in SEB schemes. PDD calculations are relatively fast, but may be affected by local-scale factors that affect how thermal energy drives snowmelt3,5. Such spatially-variable factors include, for example, the gradient of elevation33, albedo33, katabatic winds34, and the presence of local rocks and blue-ice35. Despite their widespread use, PDD models are often applied with spatially uniform, rather than spatially varying, parameters that are rarely optimised for a given area, which can make them prone to large errors5.

In this work, we contribute to the emerging picture of surface melting in Antarctica over the current century using a previously published, observationally calibrated, 1 km resolution PDD model that employs robustly tuned, spatially-varying parameters5. When compared to surface melt estimates from the SEB approach, the biases of the calibrated PDD model are significantly lower than those of PDD models that employ spatially uniform parameters5. The strength of the PDD model is that it can be applied at very high resolution over centennial-scale time periods, with much lower computational cost than more complex models. Following a sequence of discrete steps (Methods) we provide insights into future surface melting across Antarctica. We first use a statistical elevation-based method to generate high-resolution surface temperature fields for Antarctica from 2015 to 2100, under a range of greenhouse gas emissions pathways. Using our calibrated PDD model and the downscaled temperature fields, we then make a prediction of historical surface melt and validate this by comparison to regional climate model-based reanalysis predictions. We next apply the same technique to future climate scenarios, and investigate the spatial pattern, magnitude, and rate of change of our modelled surface melt. Under a high-emissions scenarios (SSP5–8.5) our results reveal a greater acceleration of potential surface melt than coarser models have shown, and even under the mid-range scenario SSP3–7.0 our model predicts a >10% expansion of potential surface melting into areas previously unaffected by this process. Considering a wide range of possible future scenarios, we find that increased potential melt may impact ice shelf viability this century, particularly around the West Antarctic Peninsula and in the Amundsen Sea Embayment.

Results

Temperature–elevation downscaling

Temperature–elevation corrections (‘lapse rate’, Supplementary Fig. S1) allow us to downscale the temperature field to force our AIS surface melt experiments at 1 km spatial resolution (Methods). This refinement allows our spatially-parameterized PDD model to capture the surface melt response to fine-scale topographic variability. Figure 1 illustrates the effect of ERA5 temperature downscaling. Exploring the cells affected by this topographic variability, we find that the largest negative and positive ERA5 surface elevation biases with respect to REMA can reach more than −2500 m and 1000 m, respectively (Fig. 1c). This effect is even more pronounced in the coarser (regridded) CMIP6 models (e.g. Supplementary Fig. S2). Grid cells over the ice shelves where most of AIS surface melt is observed from QuikSCAT satellite in the historical period4 exhibit only minor surface elevation difference, with accordingly negligible temperature correction (Fig. 1a, b). Grid cells over mountain areas, however, are far more affected by the temperature corrections because the 30 km ERA5 and coarser CMIP6 models fail to capture the topographic complexity that is present in the REMA dataset (Fig. 1d, e, and Supplementary Fig. S2). Large areas of the Antarctic Peninsula and Transantarctic Mountains show more than 500 m of elevation difference between ERA5 and REMA (Fig. 1d). The corresponding temperature corrections for these grid cells are more than 1.5 °C over extensive areas of the Antarctic Peninsula and more than 3 °C over the Transantarctic Mountains, with more than 10,000 km2 experiencing corrections of more than 6 °C. The highest of these additional warmings is of the same magnitude locally as century-end global warming under the highest emissions scenarios36. Comparison of downscaled PDD with GCM and CORDEX

Confirmation that our approach produces robust melt estimates for the historical period allows us to next calculate 1 km surface melt projections for the 21st century, using thirteen CMIP6 models. These thirteen models are the only models whose outputs are available at the daily resolution needed for our method (see Methods). For comparison, we present these projections together with i) surface melt projections derived from ten CMIP6 models that have snowmelt outputs for the AIS (see Methods) and ii) higher-resolution outputs from the COordinated Regional climate Downscaling EXperiment (CORDEX) Antarctic domain simulations (Fig. 3). We find that CMIP6 MMM annual melt totals start to diverge during the mid-century under SSP2–4.5 and SSP5–8.5 scenarios (Fig. 3a). These melt trajectories are consistent with, but of a higher magnitude than, previous AIS surface melt projections based on CMIP5 RCP4.5 and RCP8.51. Continent-wide totals from our thirteen transient CMIP6-forced 1 km PDD experiments are consistent with the surface melt CMIP6 outputs but exhibit ~40% higher acceleration in melt totals in the SSP5–8.5 scenario after mid-century (Figs. 3b and 4). Timeslice 1 km PDD experiments demonstrate noticeable differences in the magnitude of melt projected under various SSPs and RCPs by the end of the century (Fig. 3c, d). We find that the interannual surface melt variability from PDD outputs increases by the end of the century across all SSP and RCP experiments, and within each time window, it increases from low-emission to high-emission scenarios (Fig. 3c, d). Contribution of the high-resolution spatial melt field

Our 1 km surface melt results provide similar AIS total melt projections to CMIP6 MMM (Fig. 3), but our method substantially refines the spatial detail compared to the latitudinally variable native resolution of the CMIP6 GCMs. Through the elevation-based downscaling approach, we also predict a more realistic pattern of melt than simulated by GCMs (Fig. 5). With this refinement in spatial resolution, we capture a greater acceleration of melt after mid-century (Fig. 3) and enable the investigation of melt rates over regions previously unaffected by melt, as well as highly vulnerable regions such as ice shelves.

Latitudinal migration and expansion of surface melt

Having established that our downscaling approach produces high-resolution melt maps that accurately reproduce historical melt patterns, and produce continent-wide melt totals that are consistent with those from CMIP6 GCMs, we next explore the areal expansion of AIS surface melt under different SSPs. To achieve this, we first calculate the melt totals within each 0.1 degree latitudinal band from −90 °S to −60 °S. From this, we are able to determine how the southernmost limit of surface melting changes through time, a concept we call ‘latitudinal melt migration’. We find that the Ross and Filchner–Ronne ice shelves, spanning latitudes from ~−85 °S to −75 °S, are exposed to widespread increased surface melt by 2100 under SSP3–7.0 and SSP5–8.5 (Fig. 8). This indicates that the melt total over the southernmost regions by 2100 under the SSP3–7.0 and SSP5–8.5 will be 2–3 times greater than is seen in the historical period over more northerly areas such as the Antarctic Peninsula (Fig. 8). Even under the SSP2–4.5 scenario, our results indicate that by 2100 the total melt over the two largest ice shelves will be equivalent to that of the Antarctic Peninsula over the historical period (Fig. 8).Fig. 8Melt migration and trajectories.Mean annual melt totals in each 0.1 degree latitude band spanning from −90 degree South to −60 degree South under a SSP1–2.6, b SSP2–4.5, c SSP3–7.0 and d SSP5–8.5, calculated using the results from PDD CMIP6 transient experiments for the periods 1981–2010, 2021–2400, 2041–2060 and 2081–2100. e Locations of eight major ice shelf domains. f Total area of those ice shelf domains in each 0.1 degree latitude band from −90 degree south to −60 degree south. g–j Vectors of melt under SSP1–2.6, SSP2–4.5, SSP3–7.0 and SSP5–8.5 derived from (a–c) and (d). k Trajectories of potential future melt migrations compiled from panels (g–j).

We further quantify the vector of melt migration in time and space by calculating the temporal acceleration of melt and latitudinal rate of melt migration – an approach similar to the concept of ‘climate change velocity’42,43 (see Methods). We find markedly different trajectories of AIS surface melt under different SSPs (Fig. 8). These trajectories of change reveal future commitments and ongoing change both in terms of melt amount and latitudinal extent (Fig. 8k). Under SSP1–2.6 the rate of latitudinal migration and melt-rate-increase both rise during the mid-century before dropping to present-day values by 2100. In contrast, the SSP2–4.5 scenario stabilizes, but at rates of latitudinal migration and melt rate change that are higher than present. Both SSP3–7.0 and SSP5–8.5 result in southward melt migration and elevated melt rates that remain well above present by the end of the century. The divergent nature of SSP2–4.5 and SSP3–7.0 trajectories suggests that the scale of present and future climate change mitigation will determine the fate of AIS surface melt through the 21st century. The lower of the two scenarios (SSP2–4.5) shows that the latitudinal rate of melt migration will slow down, with ongoing acceleration of melt only at a low level. The higher trajectory (SSP3–7.0), however, indicates slowing latitudinal migration but still with ~2.5 times greater temporal acceleration than the SSP2–4.5 trajectory (Fig. 8h, i). These results indicate that over the remainder of the current century, Antarctic surface melt will most likely reach a condition that is unprecedented over at least the past millennium44. According to our results, SSP1–2.6 is the only emissions pathway under which future AIS surface melt can be stabilized at present levels (Fig. 8k).

Considering these results in light of our original aims, we estimate that under an SSP3–7.0 emissions scenario, the extent of Antarctic surface melt will expand by ~1.58 million km2 by 2100. This expansion will lead to sporadic surface melting in areas where it is unprecedented in at least the past three decades (Fig. 6f). Given that the last four decades have each been warmer than any preceding decade at least as far back as 1850, and were also likely warmer than any time in the last 2000 years45, we infer that these areas of new melt are without precedent in at least the last two millennia. The southward expansion of melt extent during the remainder of the current century, together with a projected increase in rainfall (Fig. 6e), will lead to steadily increasing surface water availability during summer months. As a fundamental constraint on life, the greater availability of liquid water, together with an associated expansion of snow and ice-free areas, presents new opportunities for the establishment, spread, and diversification of terrestrial life14,46, including invasive species47,48.

Significance of surface melt for ice shelf hydrofracture

Previous work has highlighted the role of ocean thermal forcing in driving ice shelf melt, which in turn impacts ice sheet discharge9,12,13,49. Other studies have emphasised the critical role of surface melt in driving ice shelf hydrofracture10,50. Our high-resolution, continent-wide, melt map builds on these insights and shows that expansion of surface melt will increasingly encroach on a) known locations of surface crevasses23 and b) areas of ice shelves previously identified as providing buttressing effects on ice sheet dynamics40. Under the SSP3–7.0 scenario at 2100 the mean annual melt rate over extensive areas of present-day fractures23 on the Filchner–Ronne Ice Shelf (Fig. 6c) remains relatively low. However, some sections of the western part of the Ross Ice Shelf show significantly increased melt compared to the historical period (Fig. 6d and Fig. 2). Ice shelves in the Bellingshausen Sea maintain relatively high surface melt rates in all four scenarios by 2100 (Figs. 6a and 7).

Under the mean scenario of strain rate change, ice shelf thinning, and potential refreezing (dark blue lines in subpanels, Fig. 9), we find >50% probability of hydrofracture by 2100 for Larsen C, WAP, Abbott, PIG, Thwaites, Crosson, Getz and Shackleton ice shelves, under SSP3–7.0. Of these eight, all but Larsen C and Getz show mean probability values of close to, or greater than, 70%. Conversely, the three largest ice shelves – Ross, Filchner–Ronne, and Amery, all have mean hydrofracture probability values that are well below 10%, suggesting that they would likely be safe from collapse this century (Fig. 9h, k, q).

Our ensemble of 60 hydrofracture calculations, using a wide range of strain rate change, ice shelf thinning, and potential refreezing values reveal additional insights into ice shelf vulnerability beyond what is indicated by the mean probabilities described above. In particular, it is readily apparent that several ice shelves are very sensitive to changes in the three parameters considered, whereas others are not. Larsen C, Abbott, Crosson, and Getz all exhibit a wide spread of hydrofracture probability values, with Shackleton exhibiting a more modest spread, and all others being much more tightly clustered. Maps of the spatial variability of hydrofracture probability are consistent with this model sensitivity – those shelves with greatest spatial variability are also typically the shelves that are most sensitive to model parameterisation (e.g. Larsen C, Abbott). Based on these considerations, we place greatest confidence on our projections of likely hydrofracture for WAP, PIG, and Thwaites ice shelves, with slightly lower confidence on Shackleton Ice Shelf. All other shelves either exhibit low probability values or a high parameter-sensitivity that diminishes our confidence in predicting collapse by 2100, at least under the SSP3–7.0 scenario.

Discussion

The model results described above imply substantial future changes in the surface environment of Antarctica, with implications for both physical and biological systems. Yet considerable uncertainties still remain.

A first consideration is that our surface melt projections are based on a stand-alone PDD model, which is not coupled with other models. As a consequence, time-evolving feedbacks between atmospheric and snow surface processes will not be represented. These limitations could include, for example, interactions between surface meltwater production and ice dynamics, or changes in surface albedo. Furthermore, the reliability of our melt projections depends on the accuracy of the forcing temperature fields, due to the temperature dependency of the PDD model5. Any error in those inputs will be propagated into our results too.

Secondly, although we consider topographic variability at a 1 km spatial resolution, any physical processes not captured by the GCMs or RCMs used for our input climate fields will still not be incorporated into our downscaled outputs. For example, air temperature changes related to vertical lapse rate effects are captured, but changes in precipitation or temperature that could result from topographically controlled differences in atmospheric circulation are not. Similarly, redistribution of precipitation by wind could be better simulated by a high-resolution RCM than by the relatively coarse models we use as inputs, which could also result in differences when compared to our downscaled product. Due to the lower number of GCM cells covering coastal regions of the ice sheet compared to inland areas, it is possible that such areas may also be associated with higher uncertainty in terms of lapse-rate calculated temperatures.

The strength of our observationally calibrated PDD approach lies in its ability to capture the pattern and amount of melt arising from the integration of all relevant processes across a wide range of climatic regimes and topographic environments. However, a third area of uncertainty lies in the range of temperatures used in the calibration of our model, which is limited to values that have occurred in the last four decades. As such, the validity of our projections for temperatures outside of this range may be reduced. By comparing our calibration data with temperatures from the GCMs used for our projection experiments, we find that this limitation may apply to areas experiencing future absolute temperatures warmer than ~+9 °C (Supplementary Fig. S4). Our calibration data do include values higher than this (Supplementary Fig. S4b, black vertical line), but they are few in number, and as such may not influence the calibration particularly strongly.

Glaciological processes represent a fourth area of potential uncertainty. Our calculations, using spatially varying hydrofracturing thresholds, incorporate the most recent continental-scale assessment of ice shelf thinning rates51. However, future thinning rates are largely unknown, because results from ice sheet models show large intermodel52 and forcing-dependent41 differences. Previous studies have highlighted that a critical global mean warming threshold of around 2 °C may mark the point beyond which the majority of Antarctic ice shelves collapse12 or become ‘non-viable’13. Given the complex interplay between sub-shelf melt, grounding line flux, and net thinning, our simulations have incorporated a range of scenarios in which thinning is either assumed to remain constant at present-day values, linearly increases by 20% or 100% by 2100, or linearly reduces to zero. Since ice shelf thickness changes may also be associated with ice velocity changes, we also explored a range of strain rate values in our hydrofracture calculation. These rates are either held constant at present-day values, or linearly increase by 20% or 100% by 2100. In our experimentation, however, the effect of these changes was found to be relatively minor (Supplementary Fig. S5).

Arguably, the greatest uncertainty in our projections surrounds the many ways that surface melt may behave once generated. In Greenland, refreezing sequesters around 50% of total surface melt, although considerable spatial and temporal uncertainty remains in how, where, and when refreezing occurs, because of sparse observations. Some studies indicate a declining capacity for refreezing under warming53–55, whereas another study found that refreezing in bare ice could in fact reduce runoff56. In Antarctica, nearly all meltwater is currently thought to refreeze in the porous firn layer57, but future changes are unknown. Because of the nature of the PDD model, our projections represent surface melt potential driven by the forcing temperature fields, and therefore, post-melting physical processes such as refreezing, as well as surface meltwater streaming4, export through river networks58, drainage and ponding59, accumulation in surface crevasses4, percolation through the snowpack4, supraglacial flow and englacial storage of meltwater60, slush ponding61, and the formation of low permeability ice layers53,62 are not simulated. Unfortunately, however, the magnitude and spatial pattern of melt ponding or refreezing within the firn layer is not known with accuracy57, confounded both by spatial variability and scenario-dependence. Studies focusing on the Amundsen Sea Embayment have found either only limited change in firn air content by the end of the current century under an SSP3–7.0 scenario63 or significant firn air depletion under a slightly warmer SSP5–8.5 scenario64. This means that whilst some ice shelves in the region may be safe from hydrofracture this century even under the warmest of these scenarios, others, such as Pine Island ice shelf are not11. Part of the problem in predicting the possible significance of refreezing is the assumption that all liquid water is retained in surface firn until available pore spaces are depleted11,24. This approach relies on modelled firn thickness distributions, and assumes that the entire firn space is permeable. However, observational evidence from both Greenland and Antarctica indicate that rising air temperatures are already leading to changes in surface properties that limit meltwater retention19,53,55,65. Acknowledging these complex and interrelated uncertainties related to refreezing, our ensemble of simulations has bracketed a range of scenarios ranging from no refreezing to high levels of widespread melt retention, based on modelled present-day firn air content51 (Methods). Our interpretations and subsequent conclusions are thus probabilistic, and are based not just on the mean of these model members, but also on their spread.

These limitations notwithstanding, our study contributes high-resolution AIS surface melt projections to the assessment of future Antarctic surface melt. Our simulations yield 1 km potential surface melt projections under SSP1–2.6, SSP2–4.5, SSP3–7.0, and SSP5–8.5, specifically focusing on areas of Antarctica where new melt could occur or where glaciological conditions suggest heightened vulnerability to hydrofracturing. We find that SSP1–2.6 is likely the only stabilization pathway for future AIS surface melt, while under the other three SSPs surface melt will accelerate to rates that are unprecedented in at least the last thousand years44. Under the moderate emissions pathway SSP3–7.0, we estimate an expansion of Antarctic surface melt area of around 1.58 million km2, ~11% of the current ice sheet/shelf area66. Constraints on hydrofracturing potential of ice shelves are sensitive to a wide range of uncertainties, but our results suggest that the Larsen C, WAP, Abbott, PIG, Thwaites, Crosson, Getz and Shackleton ice shelves may be increasingly vulnerable to collapse by the end of the century under SSP3–7.0, with other shelves also vulnerable if greenhouse gas emissions continue to follow the higher SS5–8.5 scenario.

Methods

Model setup & experiments

Consistent with prior work1 we use the term ‘melt’ throughout to mean ‘potential melt’, rather than net surface mass balance. This simplification is necessary because the complexities of snowpack processes remain poorly quantified at the whole-continent scale. Concretely, we use ‘potential melt’ to mean the quantity of liquid water that would arise by melting, for a given temperature and integrated over a given period, but excluding the unknowable portion that may be retained through refreezing processes in the firn (see ‘Discussion’ section for further discussion on this topic). Ponding60 or slush formation within firn61, for example, may temporarily delay meltwater percolation to the ice shelf surface, potentially mitigating the impacts of a short-lived, high-magnitude, melt event. These uncertainties notwithstanding, we explore four very different SSPs, from the highly-mitigated SSP1–2.6 to the unmitigated SSP5–8.5, and two intermediate scenarios of SSP2–4.5 and SSP3–7.0. Our 1 km PDD model is parameterized at the 2 km grid-cell-level using an established root-mean-square error (RMSE) minimization approach5, with improvements made by incorporating temperature–elevation corrections (see Methods) to resolve higher-resolution topographic variability. These corrections are derived from a temperature–elevation lapse rate calculated for each 1 km grid cell from 30-year (1981–2010) ERA5 reanalysis data (Supplementary Fig. S1), based on the elevation difference between ERA5 and the Reference Elevation Model of Antarctica (REMA)67.

To generate forcing climatologies, we statistically downscale temperature fields from ERA5, CMIP5 and CMIP6 to 1 km by incorporating the above temperature–elevation corrections. These downscaled temperatures are then used as inputs to the 1 km PDD model to simulate present-day (Fig. 2) and future (Fig. 3) AIS surface melting (see Methods). In total, we conduct three suites of future-scenario experiments at 1 km spatial resolution: 1) timeslice CMIP surface temperature anomaly-forced AIS surface melt simulations, 2) same as 1) but incorporating ice surface elevation changes from offline-modelled dynamic thinning, and 3) transient simulations forced by a subset of daily surface temperatures from CMIP6 model outputs.

To evaluate the quantities of surface melt totals calculated by our 1 km PDD experiments, we compare our outputs with modelled surface melt outputs from ten CMIP6 models regridded from their differing native resolutions to a common 100 km resolution for ease of use and comparison (Supplementary Table 1 & Methods). Surface melt is typically calculated from net energy balance in the land model components of GCMs. Individual GCMs and their land model components vary in terms of complexity and in their adaptation to polar regions (mono-or multi-layer schemes, snow albedo representation, etc). For example, CIESM and CMCC models use the Community land model 4.5, while CESM2-WACCM benefits from the polar adaptations included in CLM568–71 leading to improved snow density representation and reducing polar sub-snow surface melt. The implementation of multiple elevation classes within grid cells also allows melt to occur in lower-elevations columns72. This disparity in land models implies that when investigating future surface melt, using projected temperature to infer surface melt is a common approach1,24,73.

To address the potential implications of our projected melt totals for ice shelf vulnerability to hydrofracture, we implement a previously established ice shelf hydrofracturing threshold calculation10,50,74,75. This calculation accounts for a variety of glaciological conditions, including ice temperature, thickness, velocity, and strain rates (see Methods). Cognisant of the uncertainties implicit in this calculation, we explore a range of ice shelf thinning rates and strain rates, as well as different proportions of refreezing, to gauge the sensitivity of our results to the parameter values employed. From this ensemble of 60 sensitivity experiments, we gain insight into the Antarctic ice shelves that may be most vulnerable to hydrofracture this century.

Downscaling methodology

Supplementary Fig. S6a shows the workflow of the downscaling methodology. We use the Reference Elevation Model of Antarctica (REMA) surface elevation data67 as our reference elevation data. The REMA data is available with a variety of spatial resolutions, including 1 km, 100 m, and 2 m76. In order to use the 1 km REMA topography for our melt calculations, we first need to determine cell-specific elevation-dependent temperature corrections that can be used to downscale coarse-grid climate fields onto the finer-grid model domain. To do this, we first calculate the 30 km ERA5 surface elevation from surface geopotential and then, for consistency with REMA, convert it from EGM96 to WGS84 by incorporating a 30 km geoid height layer derived from BedMachine Antarctica v377,78. Then, we calculate the 30 km mean daily ERA5 2 m air temperature over the World Meteorological Organization 30-year climate-normal period from 1981 to 2010 (hereafter, baseline period) used in the IPCC AR6 Atlas79. Next, we use a 5 × 5 grid-cell box (hereafter, scanning box) to scan through the 30 km ERA5 surface elevation and 30 km mean daily ERA5 2 m air temperature synchronously. In each step of the scanning process, the scanning box contains 25 data samples from the elevation data and 25 data samples from the temperature data. Therefore, we fit an ordinary least squares regression to these samples to calculate the temperature–elevation lapse rate (°C km−1) for the central cell of the scanning box each time. Through this scanning, we generate a 30 km spatially distributed lapse rate dataset for ERA5 data during the baseline period. The size of the data sample for lapse rate calculation over the coastal AIS regions is less than 25 due to the scanning box containing ocean cells where the surface elevation data are missing. Due to this fact, the calculated lapse rates are capped within the range of [0, 15] °C km−1 in order to avoid unrealistic values.

Due to the missing values of lapse rate over the ocean cells, we need to fill some of the grid cells with interpolated values. To do this, we use the mean of all nearest neighbouring grid cells that have lapse rate values to fill areas that contain missing data. Each time this filling is conducted, the coverage of the computing domain ‘creeps’ (hereafter referred to as CREEPfill) one cell further. We conduct the CREEPfill twice to expand the computing domain by two cells, enabling us to bicubically interpolate the lapse rate from 30 km to 1 km without reducing the extent of the AIS computing domain.

Next, we interpolate the 30 km ERA5 surface elevation to 1 km and calculate the difference in surface elevations between REMA and ERA5 at 1 km resolution. Then, we multiply the 1 km surface elevation difference by the 1 km lapse rate to calculate the 1 km temperature–elevation correction for the ERA5 temperature field. We add this two-dimensional temperature–elevation correction to the 1 km daily ERA5 2 m air temperature field that is interpolated from 30 km, to derive the 1981–2010 1 km downscaled ERA5 daily T2m (°C). The 30 km ERA5 surface elevation and daily ERA5 2 m air temperature field are regridded to 1 km using bicubic interpolation without CREEPfill, because the data cover the entire domain, including the ocean cells.

DDF experiments are conducted subsequently with substituted optimal T0 (x, y) to calculate the melt rates in each grid cell to parameterize the optimal DDF (x, y) against the downscaled 2 km RACMO2.3p2 surface melt rate simulations27. CMIP6 surface melt outputs

Not all CMIP6 GCMs have snowmelt outputs available for the AIS, and not all historical simulations and SSP future projections are available for a GCM that outputs snowmelt. We searched for all available GCMs that provide melt outputs for variant “r1i1p1f1” for both historical simulations and future projections under SSP2–4.5 and SSP5–8.5 using the online CMIP6 data portal (https://esgf-data.dkrz.de/search/cmip6-dkrz/). This retrieved 11 available GCMs including: BCC-CSM2-MR, CESM2-WACCM, CIESM, CMCC-CM2-SR5, CMCC-ESM2, EC-Earth3-CC, EC-Earth3, EC-Earth3-Veg, GFDL-CM4, GFDL-ESM4 and MPI-ESM1-2-HR. Snowmelt outputs for the Antarctic domain from MPI-ESM1-2-HR are all zero, we thus remove it for our analysis and use the remaining ten GCMs. These ten GCMs are utilized to calculate the melt rate over ice shelves (Supplementary Table 1). These data are then compared with the results from transient 1 km PDD experiments for the ice shelves.

Timeslice experiments

CMIP6 and CORDEX-CMIP5 MMM temperature anomalies for near-term (2021–2040), mid-term (2041–2060) and long-term (2081–2100) relative to the baseline period (1981–2010) are bicubically interpolated to 1 km and added to the downscaled 1 km daily ERA5 surface air temperature field over the baseline period to force the PDD model (Supplementary Fig. S7). All the temperature anomalies are obtained online from the IPCC AR6 WGI Interactive Atlas (https://interactive-atlas.ipcc.ch/).

Additionally, we incorporate changes in AIS thickness to explore the effect of ice surface elevation anomaly on melt rate due to temperature–elevation correction. We utilize ice surface elevation anomaly data from refs. 9,41. The data from ref. 9 include RCP4.5 and RCP8.5 scenarios with a 5 km spatial resolution, while the data from ref. 41 comprise RCP2.6 and RCP8.5 scenarios with a 16 km spatial resolution. Transient experiments

We utilize daily temperature fields from thirteen CMIP6 GCMs to drive transient experiments (Supplementary Table 1). Shared socioeconomic pathways

In our paper, we have considered surface melt under the four most commonly used SSPs, SSP1–2.6, SSP2–4.5, SSP3–7.0, and SSP5–8.5. Which scenario is most appropriate for the future remains unknown. Whilst one study notes that SSP2–4.5 is ‘likely’, SSP3–7.0 is ‘unlikely’, and SSP5–8.5 is ‘highly unlikely’38, another shows that current cumulative emissions follow the RCP8.5 trajectory (broadly comparable to SSP5–8.5)84. Current climate policies appear to put us on a path for 2.2–3.4 °C warming by 2100, in line with SSP2–4.536. However, we choose to focus on the potential for expansion of surface melt across the Antarctic Ice Sheet under SSP3–7.0 on the basis that other studies have tended to explore worst-case scenarios in order to highlight the consequences of inadequate mitigation10.

Vector of melt migration

The vector of melt migration is similar to the concept of ‘climate change velocity’42 but with modifications. We firstly calculate the total changes in melt totals per year (Gt yr−1) within each 0.1 degree latitude band between two neighbouring time periods (e.g. 1981–2040 represents the change between 2021–2040 and 1981–2010). Then we divide these total changes by the time length between those time periods to calculate the acceleration of melt (Gt yr−2). The time length is calculated between the midpoints of time periods (e.g. the time length for 2021–2040 and 1981–2010 is 35 years, which is the length of time from 2030.5 to 1995.5). Next, we calculate the latitudinal rate of melt migration (° latitude yr−1). To do this, we divide the mean annual melt totals from 0 Gt yr−1 to 20 Gt yr−1 in increments of 0.1 Gt yr−1. This allows us to obtain a similar number of melt total bands as latitude bands and ensures coverage of the highest values of melt totals in SSP5–8.5. Then, we calculate the change in southernmost latitudes (° latitude) within each 0.1 Gt yr−1 melt band between neighbouring time periods and divide it by the time length between those periods (° latitude yr−1). For latitudes that are not covered by the melt bands, we set them to a constant value equivalent to the northernmost latitude of melt occurrence (−64.05°) in our experiments. This assumes that if a given magnitude of melt does not occur in one time period, it would instead occur at the northernmost latitude. Although this adjustment could potentially underestimate the rate of melt migration since we cannot constrain where the melt would occur north of the domain boundary, it serves to prevent unrealistic values that might arise in a scenario where a magnitude of melt occurs in one time period but not in the neighbouring time period.

Analysing the vectors of melt not only provides insight into how rapidly surface melt accelerates locally but also indicates the speed at which surface melt shifts southward. By placing these vectors from different time periods onto the same reference coordinate system, we can determine whether local melt acceleration and the velocity of latitudinal melt migration will eventually return to zero (the origin point). Illustrating these vectors as arrowed curves connected to the origin over time reveals the trajectories of Antarctic surface melt under different climate change scenarios.

To integrate this model forward in time, we annually update H to account for ice flux divergence using the most recent ice shelf thinning estimates available51. It is likely that ice shelf thinning rates will change over time, but the magnitudes and spatial patterns of such change are not at all well known52. To explore the uncertainty related to thinning rates, we use either present-day rates for the whole 86-year period of our calculation, a linear change reaching 20% or 100% increase by 2100, or a linear decrease to zero thinning by 2100. In addition, we also explore a range of strain rates, increasing them by 20% and 100% relative to their measured present-day values. Collectively, this suite of experiments helps identify how our results are affected by purely glaciological rather than climatological controls (Supplementary Fig. S5). The ensemble of 4 thinning rate scenarios combined with 3 strain rate scenarios produces 12 possible crevasse depth maps that are each then combined with five hydrofracture scenarios that employ a range of refreezing coefficients.

All calculations are performed on a 450 m grid (x, y) in order to retain the full resolution of the most detailed input data.
