Impact of Preoperative Non‐Collapse and Collapse Durations on Total Hip Arthroplasty Outcomes in Nontraumatic Osteonecrosis of the Femoral Head
Authors: Qi Chen, Kun Zhang, Yangquan Hao, Chao Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundCurrent clinical strategies emphasize delaying total hip arthroplasty (THA) in patients with nontraumatic osteonecrosis of the femoral head (NONFH). However, it remains unknown whether prolonged abnormal joint mechanics during this delayed period compromise the final surgical outcomes. Consequently, the impact of the prolonged collapsed or non‐collapsed hip joint stages on THA efficacy has not yet been evaluated. This study aimed to analyze the effect of the duration of a collapsed or non‐collapsed hip joint on the efficacy of subsequent THA.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included 111 NONFH patients who underwent primary unilateral THA between June 2022 and June 2023. Patients were grouped based on the duration of their non‐collapsed or collapsed stages (≤ 1 year, 1–3 years, and > 3 years) prior to surgery. Postoperative outcome measures, including the numerical rating scale (NRS) for pain and the Harris Hip Score (HHS) for hip function, were obtained through video‐call follow‐up. Statistical analyses included ANOVA, Kruskal‐Wallis H tests, multivariable GLM, and ROC curve analysis.ResultsA total of 111 patients were ultimately included in this study. Significant differences were found between the Joint Surface Non‐Collapse Group and the Survival with Collapse Group regarding whether they had received oral medication treatment (p < 0.05). Patients with different survival durations of collapse demonstrated significant differences in ARCO staging at the time of final THA (p < 0.05). Postoperatively, significant differences in NRS scores were observed among patients with different survival durations of collapse (p < 0.05), with the 1–3 year collapse group reporting higher pain scores compared to the > 3 year group. However, multivariable GLM revealed that preoperative duration did not independently predict the final absolute HHS (p > 0.05), indicating an equalizing trend of THA across all groups. Notably, the duration of collapse independently predicted postoperative pain, with the 1–3 year group exhibiting significantly worse NRS scores (p = 0.023).ConclusionWhile THA provides highly effective and equalized functional recovery regardless of the preoperative duration of the collapsed or non‐collapsed stages, surgical timing significantly impacts postoperative pain. Operating during the 1–3 year post‐collapse window is an independent risk factor for inferior pain relief. Given the limited subgroup sizes, these exploratory findings warrant further multicenter validation.Level of EvidenceLevel III.

Introduction

Nontraumatic osteonecrosis of the femoral head (NONFH) is a refractory orthopedic condition characterized primarily by bone loss and structural damage to the femoral head. A massive number of newly diagnosed NONFH cases are identified annually in China, posing a significant challenge to the healthcare system [1]. The etiology of this disease is complex and involves various factors, such as the use of corticosteroids and excessive alcohol consumption [2, 3], which can accelerate the necrotic process in the femoral head, ultimately leading to severe limitations in hip joint function, persistent pain, and restricted mobility. In such advanced cases, total hip arthroplasty (THA) is typically required to restore joint function. Although THA can effectively alleviate symptoms, the outcomes of functional recovery vary significantly [4], and the trajectory of recovery can differ widely between individuals.

As the disease progresses, the trabecular bone of the femoral head gradually becomes more porous, increasing the susceptibility to structural failure of the joint surface [5]. Under prolonged weight‐bearing, the joint surface may collapse, further exacerbating circulatory damage and resulting in irreversible structural destruction [6]. The collapse of the joint surface is a critical event in the progression of Osteonecrosis of the Femoral Head (ONFH), serving as the dividing point between hip‐preserving strategies and arthroplasty [7]. Therefore, determining the appropriate timing for surgery is a crucial factor influencing patient prognosis. Generally, a femoral head with an intact joint surface preoperatively predicts a better prognosis. Previous literature suggests [8, 9] that early intervention can effectively delay disease progression and maintain the non‐collapsed stage. However, once collapse occurs, alterations in joint morphology and mechanics significantly affect postoperative recovery.

In recent years, the concept of survival with collapse as a treatment approach for ONFH has gradually gained attention [10]. This approach emphasizes alleviating pain and restoring function through conservative treatments to maximize native hip joint longevity, even when collapse is irreversible. If a patient can maintain a balanced state of daily function despite collapse, they are considered to have achieved a level of clinical recovery [11]. While THA is the definitive treatment for end‐stage NONFH, delaying surgery is particularly critical for middle‐aged and younger patients to mitigate risks associated with prosthesis lifespan, aseptic loosening, and infection [12]. In clinical practice, we have observed that some patients, despite exhibiting femoral head collapse, can effectively delay THA through non‐surgical management while maintaining satisfactory function. Nevertheless, there is a lack of systematic research regarding the specific duration of the non‐collapsed and collapsed stages and their respective impacts on postoperative efficacy. Understanding how the duration of these preoperative states affects functional recovery is essential for optimizing clinical decision‐making.

This study aimed to fill this knowledge gap by retrospectively analyzing the effects of preoperative joint surface non‐collapse duration and survival with collapse duration on postoperative hip joint functional recovery in patients with NONFH undergoing THA. We hope this will provide effective and timely guidance for the prognostic assessment of THA and further promote the scientific management of ONFH.

Materials and Methods

Ethics Statement and Patient Selection

This study was approved and conducted according to the rules of the Ethics Committee of Xi'an Honghui Hospital (No. 2026‐KY‐093‐01). Written informed consent was obtained from all patients, and verbal informed consent for data utilization was acquired during the follow‐up interviews. The study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines. We reviewed data from 137 patients with NONFH who underwent primary unilateral THA at our institution between June 2022 and June 2023.

Patients

The inclusion criteria are as follows: (1) Age 18 years or older; (2) Preoperative diagnosis of NONFH staged as III or IV according to the Association Research Circulation Osseous (ARCO) [13]; (3) Clear dates for the initial diagnosis of NONFH and joint surface collapse based on imaging data, such as radiography and magnetic resonance imaging; (4) First‐time recipients of unilateral THA; (5) All patients underwent standardized surgical techniques, implants, and postoperative rehabilitation protocols to ensure consistency in treatment plans; (6) All surgeries were performed by orthopedic surgeons from the same medical team to minimize the impact of procedural variability on the outcomes.

The exclusion criteria are as follows: (1) Severe comorbidities, such as severe psychiatric disorders or acute cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events, that precluded effective rehabilitation or follow‐up assessment; (2) Loss to follow‐up or missing > 20% of study data. Notably, the dataset for the 111 included patients was complete, with no missing values for the primary variables. Therefore, the multivariable models were based on the full sample, ensuring the robustness of the statistical findings.

Study Design and Variables

This study collected the baseline characteristics of all patients from the hospital patient database. The extracted demographic and surgical parameters consisted of sex, age, body mass index, and the specific time of surgery, alongside patient contact information. For clinical evaluations, we recorded the preoperative Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) [14] for pain and the Harris Hip Score (HHS) [15] for hip function. Additionally, preoperative pharmacological management was recorded as a binary variable, defined by the continuous administration of disease‐modifying oral medications—namely anticoagulants, statins, or anti‐resorptive agents—for at least 1 month prior to surgery. This broader categorization assesses the overall impact of sustained conservative interventions rather than the specific efficacy of individual drug classes.

Data collection was conducted in two distinct phases. First, historical information was retrospectively extracted from the hospital electronic medical records and imaging systems. This extracted data included the time of initial diagnosis of ONFH based on imaging data (specific to the year), ONFH etiology (hormonal, alcoholic, or idiopathic), time of joint surface collapse (specific to the year), whether preoperative core decompression with or without bone grafting was performed as a hip‐preserving treatment, and the status of preoperative pharmacological management—defined as the administration of oral medications, such as anticoagulants, statins, or anti‐resorptive agents, for more than 1 month [8]. Specifically, femoral head collapse was strictly defined as a deviation from the spherical shape of the femoral head surface greater than 2 mm, confirmed by either anteroposterior and frog‐leg lateral radiographs or CT scans, corresponding to ARCO stage IIIA or higher.

Second, postoperative functional outcomes were evaluated through standardized video‐call follow‐ups. Two experienced physicians calculated the interval from the initial radiographic confirmation of NONFH to the first radiographic evidence of femoral head collapse. We defined this interval as the Joint Surface Non‐Collapse Duration (Figure 1). We acknowledge that the radiographic onset typically lags behind the true biological onset due to the asymptomatic “silent phase” of early‐stage NONFH [16]. Therefore, this variable represents the clinically observable duration of the non‐collapsed state rather than the absolute disease duration. Furthermore, the time from femoral head collapse to the THA surgery was defined as the Survival with Collapse Duration (Figure 1). Based on these two intervals, the overall cohort was evaluated under two corresponding grouping frameworks: the Joint Surface Non‐Collapse Group and the Survival with Collapse Group. Within each framework, patients were stratified into three sub‐cohorts (≤ 1 year, 1–3 years, and > 3 years) for comparative analysis.

Stage time diagram of the patient's articular surface.

These cut‐off points, representing the acute collapse phase (≤ 1 year), the progressive instability phase (1–3 years), and the chronic stable phase (> 3 years), were selected based on the natural history of ONFH progression described in previous literature and observed in clinical practice. Studies have indicated [17, 18] that the majority of high‐risk femoral head collapses occur within the first year following diagnosis. Hips that survive beyond 3 years often exhibit a slower progression rate or enter a clinically stable stage. The second part of the follow‐up period assessed the postoperative NRS and HHS scores of the patients. NRS score: 0, no pain; 1–3, mild pain; 4–6, moderate pain; 7–10, severe pain. HHS score: ≥ 90 is excellent, 80–90 is good, 70–80 is fair, and < 70 is poor. A postoperative HHS score of ≥ 90 at the last follow‐up (conducted at a mean of 11.78 ± 3.16 months postoperatively) was considered an indicator of successful improvement in hip joint function [19].

Any discrepancies during the study were resolved through discussion and consensus within the research team.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive and categorical data were analyzed using descriptive statistics to obtain counts and percentages. For quantitative data that followed a normal distribution, intergroup sample comparisons were conducted using independent sample t‐tests or one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA). The Kruskal‐Wallis H test was used for continuous, non‐normally distributed variables among the three groups. To determine specific differences between groups, post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using the Bonferroni correction for multiple testing. Multivariable general linear models (GLM) were employed to identify independent predictors of postoperative outcomes. Additionally, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were used to evaluate the predictive value of preoperative durations. All analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 26.0; IBM, Armonk, New York, USA). A p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient Characteristics and Follow‐Up

A total of 137 patients met the inclusion criteria, and 111 patients were successfully followed up. Nine patients refused to cooperate, and 17 were lost to follow‐up. The cohort comprised 65 males and 46 females, with a mean age of 50.41 ± 13.72 years and a mean body mass index of 24.2 ± 4.1. The mean follow‐up duration was 11.78 ± 3.16 months. In the Joint Surface Non‐Collapse Group, the number of patients for durations of ≤ 1 year, 1–3 years, and > 3 years were 82, 14, and 15, respectively. Similarly, in the Survival with Collapse Group, the number of patients for the ≤ 1 year, 1–3 years, and > 3 years intervals were 73, 16, and 22, respectively. Impact of Non‐Collapse Duration

Patients were stratified into three groups based on the duration of the non‐collapsed stage (≤ 1 year, 1–3 years, > 3 years). We observed a significant positive correlation between the duration of non‐collapse and the history of preoperative oral medication use (p = 0.039). Specifically, the proportion of patients receiving medication increased from 57.3% in the ≤ 1‐year group to 93.3% in the > 3‐year group (Table 1), suggesting a continuity of conservative management in patients with slower disease progression.

Impact of Collapse Duration

Regarding the duration of survival with collapse, significant intergroup differences were observed in preoperative medication use (p = 0.046) and ARCO staging (p = 0.015). Notably, as the collapse duration extended, the proportion of patients presenting with ARCO Stage IV advanced significantly, with 90.9% of patients in the > 3‐year group classified as Stage IV compared to 58.9% in the ≤ 1‐year group (Table 2).

Postoperative Outcomes: Pain and Function

Postoperative outcomes revealed a distinct pattern in pain relief. There was a significant difference in postoperative NRS scores among the collapse duration groups (p = 0.025). Post hoc pairwise comparisons indicated that the 1–3‐year collapse group reported significantly higher pain scores (1.19 ± 0.91) compared to both the ≤ 1‐year group (0.60 ± 0.86) and the > 3‐year group (0.68 ± 0.88), exhibiting a “U‐shaped” trend where the subacute phase correlated with the poorest pain relief.

However, no statistically significant difference was found in the final HHS scores among the three groups (p = 0.413), indicating that all groups ultimately achieved comparable functional levels.

Multivariable Analysis of Functional and Pain Outcomes

To rigorously adjust for potential confounders, multivariable GLM were employed. Based on clinical relevance and established prior literature, the a priori covariates incorporated into these models included age, body mass index, sex, etiology, ARCO staging, history of hip‐sparing surgery, and preoperative pharmacological management [20, 21]. Interestingly, the GLM revealed that neither the joint surface non‐collapse duration nor the survival with collapse duration independently predicted the final absolute HHS (p > 0.05) (Table 3). This indicates an equalizing trend of THA, where the procedure effectively restores joint biomechanics to a consistently high functional ceiling regardless of the preoperative disease duration. However, when evaluating postoperative pain relief, the multivariable model demonstrated a notable independent effect. After adjusting for the aforementioned covariates, patients with a collapse survival duration of 1–3 years exhibited a statistically significant increase in postoperative NRS scores compared to the reference group (β = 0.694, 95% CI: 0.099 to 1.289, p = 0.023) (Table 4). This suggests that operating during the 1–3 year post‐collapse window is an independent risk factor for inferior postoperative pain relief.

Predictive Value of Duration

The ROC curve analysis (Figure 3) showed that the joint surface non‐collapse duration (p < 0.05, AUC = 0.726, 95% CI: 0.592–0.860) was effective in predicting the outcomes of subsequent treatments. Survival with collapse duration (p = 0.363, AUC = 0.397, 95% CI: 0.189–0.605) showed limited predictive value for treatment outcomes. This suggests that the joint surface non‐collapse duration can be an important indicator for evaluating the treatment outcomes of THA in patients with NONFH and provides preliminary exploratory insights into the potential impact of preoperative duration on surgical outcomes.

Discussion

THA is widely recognized as the definitive salvage procedure for end‐stage femoral head necrosis, reliably restoring biomechanical function and alleviating disabling symptoms. While the surgical efficacy of THA is well established, this study offers a novel perspective by shifting the focus from the radiographic stage to the duration of the preoperative disease status. Specifically, we analyzed how the sustained duration of the “non‐collapsed” versus “collapsed” phases imprints on the postoperative functional trajectory.

Factors Influencing Joint Surface Non‐Collapse Duration

We found that patients with a prolonged non‐collapsed duration (> 3 years) exhibited a trend toward stable preoperative conditions, which may facilitate postoperative rehabilitation. This advantage appears to be driven by biomechanical and biological factors: preservation of bone stock and prevention of disuse atrophy. First, a longer non‐collapsed period typically indicates slower disease progression or effective pharmacological maintenance, resulting in better preservation of both systemic and local bone density. Superior acetabular and femoral bone stock facilitates optimal initial press‐fit stability of the prosthesis, thereby minimizing implant micromotion and reducing postoperative pain [22, 23]. Second, from a functional perspective, maintaining a non‐collapsed state allows patients to retain a better preoperative range of motion and weight‐bearing capacity. In contrast, rapid collapse often triggers severe pain and antalgic gait, leading to rapid disuse atrophy of the hip stabilizers. Therefore, the preservation of neuromuscular control in the prolonged non‐collapsed group provides a more robust physiological foundation for postoperative rehabilitation [24]. In addition to these structural advantages, we observed a significantly higher proportion of preoperative medication use in this group. Therefore, we postulate a synergistic effect: the systematic use of oral medications, such as antiresorptive agents, may have optimized the local bone metabolic microenvironment [25]. When combined with the preserved bone stock and muscle strength, this biological preconditioning appears to prime the hip for superior postoperative recovery. This suggests that extending the non‐collapsed phase—whether naturally or pharmacologically—serves not only to delay surgery but to enhance the eventual surgical outcome.

Determinants of Survival With Collapse Duration

Our multivariable analysis yielded a highly clinically relevant divergence: while THA normalizes gross motor function (HHS) across all duration groups, the survival with collapse duration remains an independent predictor of postoperative pain (NRS). The 1–3‐year collapse group experienced significantly poorer pain relief. We hypothesize that this period represents a subacute inflammatory and instability window. During this phase, the structural disintegration drastically alters load‐bearing mechanics, triggering profound secondary synovitis and bone marrow edema. Operating directly amidst this heightened secondary inflammatory response may be associated with heightened perioperative central sensitization and prolonged local tissue irritability. Conversely, rapid intervention (≤ 1 year) pre‐empts this severe inflammatory cascade, while delayed intervention (> 3 years) allows the joint to reach a chronic, sclerotic, burned‐out phase. This finding strongly suggests that surgical timing should strategically account for the inflammatory phase of NONFH to optimize subjective pain outcomes.

Functional Recovery and Clinical Implications of Total Hip Arthroplasty

Additionally, based on the baseline characteristics, we found that patients with a longer duration of collapse tended to be older, possibly because elderly patients are more likely to experience pain and have adapted to a certain degree of functional loss. According to previous reports [26], the average age of patients diagnosed with femoral head necrosis in China is approximately 50 years, which means that even when the femoral head begins to collapse, the patients are still relatively young. Furthermore, younger patients require longer treatment periods considering the lifespan, loosening, maintenance, and infection risks associated with prostheses. Thus, long‐term postoperative outcomes, particularly recovery of joint function, are crucial. In recent years, clinicians and patients have increasingly focused on postoperative satisfaction, and evaluating postoperative efficacy has become an important criterion for assessing surgical quality. Reports indicate [27, 28] that the dissatisfaction rate following primary THA ranges from 7% to 20%, with the postoperative efficacy in patients with NONFH being influenced by various factors. Therefore, clinicians should consider the factors influencing patient outcomes before and after THA. It is worth noting the nuanced relationship between preoperative non‐collapse duration and functional outcomes. In our initial univariable and exploratory ROC analysis, longer non‐collapse duration was a significant predictor of favorable THA outcomes. However, in the subsequent multivariable GLM, this duration no longer retained independent statistical significance for the final HHS. This apparent discrepancy highlights the critical importance of multivariable adjustment in retrospective studies. We hypothesize that the predictive value seen in the ROC curve is largely mediated by confounding covariates, such as continuous pharmacological interventions and less aggressive etiologies, which frequently co‐occur with extended non‐collapse periods. Once the GLM controlled for these underlying biological advantages, the chronological duration alone did not independently dictate the functional ceiling. This further reinforces our conclusion that THA provides an equalizing effect on functional restoration. Furthermore, for younger patients with ARCO IIIA stage necrosis, joint‐preserving options like rotational osteotomy should be considered to delay THA. Our findings suggest that the optimal window for such interventions is likely before the hypothesized instability phase occurs, as the biological environment and mechanical stability significantly deteriorate once advanced structural collapse is established.

Strengths and Limitations

Several strengths characterize our study design. The primary advantage lies in our rigorous stratification of disease progression into two distinct clinical phases, specifically the joint surface non‐collapse duration and the survival with collapse duration. Furthermore, the uniform application of standardized surgical protocols by a single medical team effectively minimized procedural variability. The comprehensive extraction of baseline data from objective electronic medical records also ensured the overall robustness of our dataset.

Nevertheless, our study has several limitations. First, as a single‐center retrospective study, the sample sizes in certain subgroups were relatively small, which may limit the statistical power. Additionally, we did not include patients with exceptionally long survival durations, who often exhibit worse postoperative outcomes; future multicenter studies should incorporate such extreme cohorts. Second, our follow‐up was conducted entirely via telemedicine. While this facilitated broad data collection, it inherently lacked objective clinical examinations and radiographic confirmation, introducing a potential risk of bias. Third, the determination of collapse timing relied on retrospective imaging reviews, which are inherently subject to errors related to the frequency of each patient's follow‐up X‐ray examinations. Fourth, the follow‐up period was relatively short. While this is sufficient to assess early functional recovery and pain relief, it is insufficient to evaluate long‐term implant survival or complications such as loosening. Therefore, future studies should comprehensively consider multiple factors and conduct more extensive multicenter prospective studies with larger sample sizes to enhance the power of the findings. Fifth, although we observed statistically significant differences in postoperative NRS scores among groups, the absolute difference was relatively small and did not reach the reported Minimal Clinically Important Difference (MCID) [29]. Therefore, while the statistical trend suggests a link between preoperative duration and pain perception, the clinical magnitude of this effect should be interpreted with caution. However, it is worth noting that the improvement in HHS across all groups vastly exceeded the established MCID for HHS [30]. This confirms that THA provides robust functional restoration regardless of disease duration. Finally, postoperative function is influenced by multiple factors such as surgical approach, implant positioning, and restoration of muscle strength. As a retrospective study, we could not fully control for these biomechanical variables, although all surgeries were performed by the same senior surgical team to minimize technical variability. Furthermore, predicting functional outcomes and complications following THA requires considering various patient‐specific and surgical risk factors. Recent literature, including systematic reviews on minimally invasive versus conventional THA approaches, highlights the importance of such risk‐factor‐based outcome prediction in clinical decision‐making [31]. Importantly, our conclusions are derived exclusively from patients with NONFH. As highlighted by a recent systematic review of 41 THA trials, indications are diverse—predominantly osteoarthritis, followed by fractures and osteonecrosis—with underlying diagnoses significantly influencing postoperative trajectories [31]. Given that NONFH patients are generally younger and present with distinct etiological and comorbidity profiles compared to the typical osteoarthritis demographic, our observations regarding surgical timing and pain outcomes cannot be broadly extrapolated. Therefore, these findings represent a disease‐specific pattern rather than a universal principle for all THA procedures.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the preoperative duration of the non‐collapsed stage is significantly associated with functional outcomes following THA. Strategies aimed at extending this non‐collapsed window through early diagnosis and therapeutic intervention are crucial for optimizing patient prognosis. For patients who have already progressed to structural collapse, surgical timing becomes a critical determinant of postoperative pain perception, even though THA generally provides an equalizing functional restoration. Specifically, surgical intervention within the 1–3‐year post‐collapse window serves as an independent risk factor for inferior pain relief, warranting enhanced perioperative pain management. Given the limited subgroup sizes, these clinically relevant exploratory findings require further prospective multicenter validation to provide more precise guidance for clinical practice.
