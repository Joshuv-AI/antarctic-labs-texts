Feasibility, acceptability, and usability of a novel self-monitoring program utilizing a consumer-grade wearable device paired with sleep-related patient-reported outcomes for veterans with obstructive sleep apnea
Authors: Selene Mak, Sara Ghadimi, Erin Der-McLeod, Saadia Naeem, Virginia Moore, Lizzeth Gil, Moeed Chohan, Michelle Zeidler, Garrett Ash, Constance Fung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is characterized by repeated airway obstruction and associated oxygen desaturation during sleep. Positive airway pressure remains the most common treatment, but some patients use non–positive airway pressure (non-PAP) therapies such as positional devices and oral appliances. Unlike PAP devices, these therapies do not typically provide ongoing physiologic feedback. This lack of feedback can make it difficult for patients to understand their nightly physiology between sleep studies. While consumer-grade wearables are not intended to diagnose OSA, they offer an opportunity to design patient-facing tools that support access to and interpretation of peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2) data for self-management and treatment decision-making.Methods:This clinical demonstration project developed and iteratively refined a workflow for generating a weekly report that combined smartwatch-derived SpO2 data with self-reported sleep-related outcomes for patients using non-PAP OSA therapies. Patients viewed a weekly electronic report on their own and again with a clinician at program completion. Report content and implementation procedures were adjusted through a series of Plan–Do–Study–Act cycles, with emphasis on reducing information complexity and supporting patient sensemaking. Think-aloud interviews with a subset of patients explored how they interpreted and used the report. Post-program survey data were summarized descriptively, and interview notes were reviewed to identify report-related interpretive challenges and guide iterative refinement.Results:Of the 17 patients who enrolled, 16 completed the program and 15 completed the post-program survey. At least 70% of respondents agreed that the program was feasible, acceptable, and appropriate. The System Usability Scale score was 75.8, indicating above-average usability. The Net Promoter Score was 64, suggesting willingness to recommend the program. Most respondents (≥85%) reported that “at least some effort” was made to engage them in shared decision-making during the clinician review session. Think-aloud interviews indicated that patients who were interviewed found the report helpful for making sense of their data and preferred side-by-side visualizations of higher (≥93%) and lower (<93%) SpO2 levels, which informed revisions to simplify and clarify the report.Conclusion:Incorporating consumer-grade smartwatch SpO2 data into a structured, patient-facing weekly report appears to be a feasible and acceptable way to complement OSA care for patients using non-PAP therapies.

Background

Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is characterized by repetitive upper-airway obstruction during sleep accompanied by oxygen desaturation and fragmented sleep, contributing to cardiovascular risk, impaired daytime functioning, and reduced quality of life (American Academy of Sleep Medicine, 2023; Gottlieb & Punjabi, 2020; Omachi et al., 2009; Young et al., 2002). Positive airway pressure (PAP) remains the most common therapy, but due to device recalls (Owens et al., 2021), patient preferences, and other clinical factors (Ramar et al., 2015), some patients are prescribed non-PAP treatments such as positioning devices and oral appliances. While non-PAP therapies can reduce apnea severity and improve oxygenation in appropriately selected patients (Verbraecken et al., 2022), they typically provide no ongoing physiologic feedback between sleep studies.

Although OSA requires ongoing self-management (Patil et al., 2019), this lack of feedback can make it difficult for non-PAP users to understand whether their therapy is providing benefit. In contrast to PAP devices, which provide airflow-based adherence and therapy data, most non-PAP therapies give no comparable information that might help patients observe patterns related to their condition or treatment use. Prior work shows that perceptions of treatment benefit can influence whether patients continue to use their prescribed therapy (Aardoom et al., 2020; Sedkaoui et al., 2015).

Human factors (HF) research provides a useful lens for understanding these challenges. From a sociotechnical systems perspective, healthcare work arises from interactions among people, technologies, tasks, and organizational context (Carayon, 2006). Patients managing chronic conditions often act as “knowledge workers” who gather information, interpret it, and make decisions about their care (Papautsky & Patterson, 2021; Woods & Roth, 1997). When relevant data are missing, unfamiliar, or difficult to interpret, this work can become cognitively demanding (Hollnagel & Woods, 1983). HF research on distributed cognition and cognitive artifacts further emphasizes that well-designed external representations can reduce cognitive burden, support the development of accurate mental models, and provide shared reference points that facilitate interpretation and coordination (Hutchins, 1995; Norman, 1993). Related work on visualization and representational design suggests that well-designed representations can make complex information easier to interpret and support judgment, decision-making, and sensemaking (Norman, 1993; Turchioe et al., 2019).

Consumer-grade smartwatches capable of measuring peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2) offer one potential way to give non-PAP users additional physiologic context between clinic visits. Although these devices are not intended for diagnosing OSA, they can generate night-time SpO2 values that may help patients observe patterns in their data (Ancker et al., 2015; Caban & Gotz, 2015). Recent large-scale observational work using data from wearable-equipped individuals suggests that commercial wrist-worn devices can capture SpO2 measurements under real-world conditions, supporting their potential utility for physiologic trend monitoring (Shapiro et al., 2023). However, because these data rely on averaged SpO2 values and lack sleep staging or clinical-grade validation, they are not suitable for diagnostic use (Shapiro et al., 2023).

In the broader patient-generated health data (PGHD) literature, these data have shown promise for supporting engagement, but they also pose challenges when information is unstructured, such as variable data quality, uncertain interpretation, and the cognitive effort required to make sense of unfamiliar metrics (Ancker et al., 2015; Caban & Gotz, 2015). HF literature highlights similar issues: patients may struggle when data are voluminous, difficult to contextualize, or misaligned with their existing mental models (Hollnagel & Woods, 1983). Simplifying and organizing SpO2 information may therefore help patients integrate patient-generated health data more effectively into self-management activities (Ancker et al., 2015; Norman, 1993; Turchioe et al., 2019).

Because PGHD can be unfamiliar and difficult to interpret, reviewing it with a clinician may support shared decision-making by helping patients and clinicians work from a common understanding of the data (Elwyn et al., 2012; Fung et al., 2021). Shared decision-making is defined as a process in which a healthcare provider and patient work together to make a healthcare decision that is best for the patient (Elwyn et al., 2012). HF work on cognitive artifacts and joint cognitive systems emphasizes the value of shared representations for grounding conversations and supporting coordinated sensemaking (Hutchins, 1995; Hollnagel & Woods, 1983). This need may be especially relevant for non-PAP users, who typically receive little physiologic feedback outside the initial sleep study. In line with these principles, approaches that use structured PGHD summaries benefit from iterative refinement and user-centered feedback cycles. Incorporating rapid feedback, iterative design, and evaluation of user sensemaking reflects established HF guidance on supporting comprehension and human-centered design of interactive health technologies (Dabbs et al., 2009; Norman & Draper, 1986).

This clinical demonstration project developed and refined a work-flow for collecting, processing, and presenting PGHD to patients using non-PAP therapies. Guided by HF concepts such as iterative refinement (Norman & Draper, 1986), cognitive artifacts and shared representations (Hutchins, 1995; Hollnagel & Woods, 1983), and user-centered design of patient-facing interactive health technologies (Dabbs et al., 2009), the project sought to: Explore the feasibility and acceptability of providing such data to patients, andUnderstand how patients make sense of a structured weekly report intended to support self-management and shared decision-making during discussions with their clinician.

This clinical demonstration project offers practice-based insight that may be useful for guiding future human-centered design and workflow refinement of PGHD-based tools intended to support OSA management.

Methods

Overview

We used a series of Plan-Do-Study-Act (PDSA) cycles, a four-stage iterative quality improvement method in which changes are planned, tested on a small scale, studied, and then refined based on what is learned (Langley et al., 2009). Using these cycles, we refined the workflow, report format, and implementation procedures over time. Each cycle incorporated rapid feedback, iterative design, and evaluation of user sensemaking. The duration of each cycle corresponded to the length of each patient’s participation in the program (e.g., 1 week, 2 weeks, or 4 weeks). We began with a 1-week cycle to allow rapid refinement of the program. Cycle lengths then increased over time (1 week → 2 weeks → 4 weeks) as the team assessed how patients received, understood, and used the electronic report. Cycle 4 returned to a 1-week duration to focus specifically on structured think-aloud interviews examining report interpretation following completion of the 4-week implementation period in Cycle 3. Feedback from patients informed revisions across all cycles, and Cycle 4 provided a more formal opportunity to examine how patients interpreted specific report elements. Table 1 summarizes the primary activities across cycles.

The VA Greater Los Angeles Institutional Review Board (IRB) determined that this clinical demonstration project did not constitute human subjects research and therefore did not require formal IRB review. Reporting of this project was guided by the Standards for QUality Improvement Reporting Excellence (SQUIRE 2.0) (Ogrinc et al., 2016).

Participants

We enrolled a convenience sample of patients with OSA prescribed non-PAP treatment at a Veterans Affairs sleep center in the Los Angeles area. We reviewed lists of patients who had a clinical visit in the past 6 months and were prescribed non-PAP therapy to identify potentially eligible patients. We called these patients using the phone number in their medical record and described the clinical demonstration program to them. We also introduced the program at sleep clinic staff meetings to encourage referrals. All enrolled patients provided verbal agreement to participate. They did not receive monetary compensation but were permitted to keep the smartwatch upon program completion.

Clinical demonstration program

Each patient was mailed a consumer-grade smartwatch with an accompanying user guide. During a scheduled orientation call, staff assisted with device setup, established SpO2 data-sharing through a third-party platform, and introduced how the weekly electronic report would display and summarize SpO2 data. Patients were provided with a secure link to connect their device to the platform and enable data-sharing.

Patients completed a weekly electronic survey reporting treatment use and health-related quality of life outcomes using the Symptoms, Tiredness, Alertness, Mood and Psychosocial (STAMP) questionnaire (Mehta et al., 2020). The STAMP questionnaire includes 12 items across 5 domains, rated on a 0–5 scale, with higher scores indicating greater symptom burden or impact. Smartwatch-derived SpO2 data and self-reported outcomes were visualized in a weekly electronic report designed by project team members to support patient interpretation and discussion with clinicians. The report was designed to translate raw PGHD into a concise patient-facing summary that could support sensemaking between visits and serve as a shared reference during clinician discussion.

Consistent with HF guidance to match representations to the underlying phenomena of interest, the report presented distributions of time spent in specific SpO2 ranges (i.e., minute-by-minute intervals) rather than nightly averages, since OSA events tend to cause multiple transient oxygen desaturations during a sleep period. The specific SpO2 ranges were selected by the sleep clinician based on conventions commonly used in CPAP device reporting, including the 93% threshold used to distinguish higher from lower oxygen saturation levels. This approach differs from the smartwatch’s native app, which provides averaged SpO2 values that may be less pertinent to OSA.

Because early cycles revealed areas of confusion, we added a brief structured orientation to the report beginning in Cycle 3, delivered first by phone and later via a short video. During orientation, we explained that the report reflected the data captured for each sleep period and that incomplete coverage could occur when the device did not record data for the full sleep period. Patients were then notified each week to view a personalized electronic report through a unique link. When data were unavailable for a given night, the report displayed the available data without values for that night. Interpretation support, including explanation of the displayed SpO2 ranges, was reiterated during the final clinician review session. At the end of the program, each patient reviewed the final report with a sleep clinician, and the report served as a shared reference for communication and joint interpretation, which are consistent with shared decision-making and distributed cognition in healthcare settings.

Program evaluation

A pre-program survey collected demographic information for all enrolled patients (n = 17). A post-program survey, completed by 15 patients, assessed feasibility, acceptability, appropriateness, usability, satisfaction, and perceptions of shared decision-making. These measures were used to provide a descriptive post-program assessment of early implementation outcomes, usability, satisfaction, and perceived shared decision-making at program completion. We used Proctor et al.’s framework to define implementation outcomes in this clinical demonstration project, with feasibility describing the extent to which the program could be successfully used in this setting, acceptability capturing whether patients viewed it as agreeable or satisfactory, and appropriateness reflecting perceived fit or suitability (Proctor et al., 2011; Perski & Short, 2021). Table 2 summarizes the quantitative survey instruments used across the clinical demonstration project.

Feasibility of Intervention Measure (FIM), Acceptability of Intervention Measure (AIM), and Intervention Appropriateness Measure (IAM) were included as brief validated measures of early implementation outcomes (Weiner et al., 2017). Each measure has 4 items, with questions such as, “the program meets my approval” for acceptability, “the program seems suitable” for appropriateness, and “the program is implementable” for feasibility. Each measure uses a 5-point Likert scale (1 = completely disagree to 5 = completely agree), with higher scores indicating more favorable perceptions. We tabulated and reported the proportion of respondents who answered 4 (“agree”) or 5 (“completely agree”).

Usability was assessed using the 10-item System Usability Scale (SUS) to provide a brief descriptive assessment of perceived usability of the report and workflow, which yields scores ranging from 0 to 100, with scores above 68 generally reflecting acceptable usability (Brooke, 1996). Satisfaction was assessed using an adapted Net Promoter Score (NPS) to characterize willingness to recommend the overall program, which ranges from −100 to 100, with a score of 0 considered to be a good score and higher scores indicating greater willingness to recommend the program (Reichheld, 2003). NPS is calculated by subtracting the percentage of detractors (answers 1–6) from the percentage of promoters (answers 9 or 10).

We included the three-item collaboRATE measure as an early descriptive assessment of patients’ experiences of shared decision-making during the clinician review session. CollaboRATE uses a 5-point scale for each of three items (0 = no effort to 4 = every effort), yielding total scores from 0–12, where higher scores indicate greater perceived effort toward shared decision-making (Elwyn et al., 2013). We summarized results as the proportion of respondents with total scores ≥6, indicating “at least some effort was made.” Although the developers of collaboRATE recommend calculating site- or clinician-level scores using data from at least 25 clinical encounters to ensure stability of aggregate estimates (Elwyn, n.d.), our use of the measure in this project was intended to provide an early, descriptive assessment of patients’ experiences of shared decision-making within this clinical demonstration context.

Think-aloud interviews

In Cycle 4, we invited patients sequentially to complete a phone-based think-aloud interview in which they walked the interviewer through how they read the weekly report. These interviewees were therefore a convenience subset of enrolled patients. We used these interviews to examine sensemaking, or how patients understood and extracted meaning from the information presented (Boren & Ramey, 2000; Klein et al., 2006). These interviews were used for report refinement rather than formal qualitative theme development. The team member conducting the phone interviews used a structured field note template organized by specific sections and visual features of the report to take notes (Appendix). We were interested in the sections or features of the report that patients commented on without being prompted.

Patients were also shown two versions of the report. The original version displayed both the daily number of minutes in the higher range (≥93%) and the lower range (<93%), whereas the alternate version displayed only the daily number of minutes in the higher range (≥93%). After the first two think-aloud interviews, we introduced the alternate version and showed both versions to the remaining four patients to elicit feedback on report presentation and usefulness. The alternate version was developed after a clinical staff meeting when the Principal Investigator presented updates on the clinical demonstration project.

Findings

Table 3 shows the characteristics of the 17 enrolled patients. Of these, 16 completed the program and 15 completed the post-program survey. One patient did not complete the program because of a change in schedule. Of the 16 who completed the program, one declined the post-program survey because of difficulty with the electronic survey, despite being offered the option to complete it by phone.

Post-program evaluation

Post-program survey findings were based on 15 respondents. Across FIM, AIM, and IAM, at least 70% of respondents selected “agree” or “completely agree” for each item. These findings are consistent with favorable early perceptions of program implementation. The adapted NPS was 64, indicating favorable willingness to recommend the program. The SUS score was 75.8, indicating acceptable to good usability. For shared decision-making, most respondents (≥85%) reported that “at least some effort” was made to involve them in discussions about their condition, what mattered to them, and their OSA treatment during the clinician review session.

Think-aloud interviews

Six patients completed a think-aloud interview in Cycle 4. All four patients who were shown two versions of the report preferred the version with both higher range (≥93%) and lower range (<93%) SpO2 levels visualized. Reports

Patients across all cycles identified specific report elements as confusing or in need of additional explanation (e.g., the STAMP bar chart colors, unclear icon meanings). These insights guided targeted revisions such as simplifying color palettes, adding data labels, and clarifying visual indicators. Some suggestions (e.g., adding sleep duration or awakenings) were not incorporated into the report but were flagged for clinician discussion, preserving report simplicity and avoiding feature creep that could increase cognitive burden. The final version of the report included three sections (Fig. 1): visualization of nightly SpO2 levels, self-reported treatment use, and self-reported health-related quality of life outcomes. Discussion

This clinical demonstration project suggests that a smartwatch-informed weekly report can be incorporated as a practical complement to care for patients using non-PAP OSA therapies in our sleep center. Patients generally found the report understandable and helpful for making sense of their nightly data, and several noted that it prompted reflection about treatment use and sleep patterns. Many also described the report as useful during conversations with their clinician, and the collaboRATE results suggested that patients perceived effort toward shared decision-making during the clinician review session (Elwyn et al., 2013). Patients who were presented with two versions of the report preferred the version that displayed higher-range (≥93%) and lower-range (<93%) SpO2 levels side by side, noting that this contrast made the information easier to interpret. The use of minute-by-minute data summarized into distributions may have helped patients identify meaningful patterns while keeping the report concise. These observations suggest that the workflow was workable in practice and that the report functioned as a useful shared reference during care discussions. This interpretation is also in line with the post-program evaluation findings, which indicated generally favorable perceptions of feasibility, acceptability, appropriateness, and usability among respondents.

Our experience suggests that structured PGHD summaries may offer one way to provide non-PAP users with more physiologic context between sleep studies. Patients’ comments about the value of visual contrasts, simplified summaries, and contextual cues are consistent with HF discussions of how cognitive artifacts can support the development of mental models and help users make sense of unfamiliar data (Norman, 1993). The workflow we developed integrated PGHD into a structured visualization, incorporated a report orientation component, and included a clinician review session. These workflow elements are consistent with HF guidance on iterative refinement, progressive disclosure, and user-centered design of patient-facing technologies (Dabbs et al., 2009; Norman & Draper, 1986), and they also parallel ideas from distributed cognition, which describe how shared artifacts can create common reference points that support joint interpretation and shared decision-making (Hutchins, 1995; Reddy et al., 2003). From an HF perspective, the key contribution was not simply making smartwatch data available, but translating raw PGHD into a structured external representation that patients and clinicians could interpret together. These findings illustrate how iterative changes were made to support clearer interpretation of unfamiliar PGHD within a patient-clinician workflow. In this way, the weekly report functioned as both a cognitive artifact and a practical boundary object that supported continuity of interpretation across patient and clinician work.

This project provides an early operational example of how consumer-grade smartwatch data can be incorporated into patient-facing summaries that support self-management and shared decision-making to complement clinical care (Dabbs et al., 2009). Because SpO2 data have historically been inconvenient for patients to obtain and are rarely presented in formats that support sensemaking, this approach illustrates a practical method for shaping PGHD into information that is more useful and usable for OSA management (Caban & Gotz, 2015; Mamykina et al., 2017). Given that patients perform substantial “knowledge work” as they navigate symptoms, treatment use, and clinical guidance, structured PGHD summaries can serve as cognitive artifacts that externalize information and support interpretation and coordination (Coiera, 2000; Dabbs et al., 2009; Papautsky & Patterson, 2021). In this project, the weekly report created a stable shared reference between visits rather than requiring patients to integrate multiple uncoordinated data sources on their own. While preliminary, these observations contribute to the growing conversation about how PGHD tools can be designed to better align with patient cognitive needs.

Limitations

This clinical demonstration project has several limitations. The project used a small convenience sample from a single VA sleep center, which may limit generalizability. Because the project focused on workflow development and report refinement rather than treatment effectiveness, the findings cannot be used to draw conclusions about adherence, clinical outcomes, or treatment response. Although self-reported treatment use and symptom data were collected, they were not analyzed as formal outcome measures, and the smartwatch-derived SpO2 values were not used to assess treatment response. Smartwatch data also occasionally contained missing or incomplete segments, reflecting broader limitations of consumer smartwatch SpO2 measurement (Jiang et al., 2023; Pipek et al., 2021). When data were unavailable for a given night, the report displayed available data without that night's values. We explained this to patients during orientation, clarifying that the report would present whatever data were collected rather than complete coverage of all sleep periods. In addition, the collaboRATE results should be interpreted as descriptive rather than clinic-level estimates, given the small number of clinician review sessions in this project. Finally, because staff assisted with device setup, troubleshooting, and report orientation, the level of support provided may differ from what is feasible in routine practice. Even with these limitations, the project offers early insight into the practical considerations involved in integrating PGHD into OSA self-management workflows.

Implications and applications

This clinical demonstration project offers early insight into how a smartwatch-informed weekly report can be incorporated into non-PAP OSA care to support patient sensemaking, self-management, and shared decision-making. By summarizing nightly SpO2 patterns alongside patient-reported outcomes, the report provided a structured reference that many patients found understandable and useful for reflecting on treatment use and sleep patterns. The workflow combined a weekly report, a brief report orientation, and a final clinician review session within routine care processes where patients already perform substantial “knowledge work” between visits. Structured PGHD summaries may help address the physiologic feedback gap common among non-PAP users and may support more productive clinical conversations by creating a shared point of reference. This approach may be especially valuable in asynchronous care contexts, such as for patients who live far from sleep centers or receive care in resource-constrained settings.

From a human factors and quality improvement perspective, this work illustrates how PGHD can be integrated into real clinical workflows in ways that support sociotechnical alignment and clearer representation and interpretation of patient-generated information (Carayon et al., 2014). Rather than treating wearable data as a stand-alone technical output, the workflow embedded PGHD into routine clinical activity through structured visualization, report orientation, and clinician-supported review. Viewed through a human factors lens, the contribution lies not only in collecting smartwatch data, but in translating raw device outputs into a patient-facing representation that could be interpreted, discussed, and iteratively refined within care. This integration supported shared understanding with minimal disruption to existing care processes. As consumer wearables become increasingly integrated into healthcare delivery, this project provides a practical example of how human-centered design and QI approaches can be used to shape raw device outputs into information that is usable, clinically meaningful, and supportive of coordinated chronic disease management.

Impact statement

This project provides an example of how human factors and quality improvement approaches can guide the integration of PGHD into routine clinical care in ways that support real-world use. By focusing on how data are represented, discussed, and embedded within existing workflows, the work moves beyond technology adoption to address how patients and clinicians use information to make sense of their care between visits. For human factors practitioners, the project offers a concrete example of designing for shared understanding, coordination, and sensemaking in an asynchronous care setting. At a broader systems level, our experience highlights the importance of aligning emerging digital health data streams with representation, workflow, and communication processes so that wearable device outputs become useful and usable clinical information.
