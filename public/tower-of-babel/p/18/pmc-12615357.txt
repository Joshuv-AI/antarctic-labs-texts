The relationship between sleep health characteristics and risk of multimorbidity: A cross-sectional study based on public health surveillance system in southern Tehran-Iran
Authors: Fatemeh Zarghami, Keyvan Karimi, Sarmad Salehi, Samaneh Akbarpour, Yosra Azizpour
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveSleep is essential for optimal health and is associated to development of chronic diseases. This study examines the association between sleep characteristics and multimorbidity among population in southern Tehran and its suburbs.MethodsThis is a secondary cross-sectional study conducted in January 2025. We used data from 1311 participating adults in the Integrated Repeated Public Health Surveillance System (IRPHS) in Tehran (May–September 2023). Information on sleep duration, sleep satisfaction, and chronic disease (diabetes, hypertension, cardiovascular, dyslipidemia) was collected through telephone interviews. Multimorbidity was defined as having two or more chronic conditions. Associations between sleep and multimorbidity were examined using logistic regression model.ResultsMultimorbidity was found in 78 participants (5.9 %) with more prevalence among older adults (≥65 years: 37.2 %, n = 29), females (7.6 %, n = 60), and those with high depressive mood (16.8 %, n = 19). Long sleep duration (≥9 h) was significantly associated with higher odds of multimorbidity (adjusted odds ratio (AOR): 1.94; 95 % CI: 1.04–3.65; p = 0.039). Short sleep (≤6 h) (AOR: 1.70; 95 % CI: 0.92–3.11) and poor sleep satisfaction (AOR: 1.48; 95 % CI: 0.85,2–56) showed nonsignificant associations.ConclusionLong sleep duration may indicate higher odds of multimorbidity. Routine sleep assessment could help identify high risk individuals and support preventive interventions.

Introduction

Sleep is increasingly recognized as a vital determinant of health, influencing multiple biological systems including cardiovascular, metabolic, and immune functions. Inadequate sleep is associated with adverse health outcomes such as hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), cardiovascular disease (CVD), obesity, and higher mortality risk (Avendaño and Cervantes, 2023). These associations are attributed to circadian disruption, hormonal imbalances, and inflammatory responses triggered by poor sleep (Hillman, 2021). Recognizing and addressing sleep health is crucial in chronic disease prevention, public health planning, and developing targeted interventional programs.

Increasing evidence highlights the association between sleep health characteristics and chronic conditions. The association between sleep duration and health outcomes appears complex and bidirectional. Numerous studies report a U-shaped relationship, where sleep durations below 6 h or above nine hours are associated with higher risk of CVD, hypertension, and metabolic disorders (You et al., 2024a). Insufficient sleep duration is recognized as a contributing factor to higher risk for developing conditions such as arterial hypertension, coronary artery disease, myocardial infarction (MI), and cognitive decline (You et al., 2024b). Additionally, He et al. (2022) provided evidence that short sleep duration is causally associated to a higher risk of CVD, further supporting the claim that insufficient sleep has a detrimental impact on CVD outcomes (He et al., 2022). Therefore, maintaining the recommended sleep duration—typically seven to eight hours—as advised by the National Sleep Foundation and Sleep Research Society, supports optimal health (Scott et al., 2024).

Sleep satisfaction, beyond duration alone, plays an essential role in chronic disease development. Poor sleep satisfaction has been associated with higher risks of hypertension, dyslipidemia, obesity, and T2DM (Medic et al., 2017). These effects are further exacerbated by poor psychological well-being, particularly depression, which can worsen sleep disturbances and amplify chronic disease risks (Merlo, 2019). Previous studies have primarily examined the association between poor sleep quality on the development or progression of individual chronic conditions such as hypertension, dyslipidemia, obesity, and T2DM. This body of literature firmly establishes the association between poor sleep and these individual chronic diseases. However, our study extends this focus by exploring how poor sleep quality and duration relate to multimorbidity, i.e., the coexistence of multiple chronic conditions. Multimorbidity, based on simple count of disease, is define as the presence of two or more chronic conditions (Skou et al., 2022). Some studies further categorize multimorbidity into low (2–4 conditions) and high (≥5 conditions) burden (Hu et al., 2024). In our study, we included individuals with up to four chronic conditions to reflect this commonly used threshold for multimorbidity burden in epidemiological research.

Despite international findings, data specific to Iranian populations remain limited. Local studies exploring sleep and its association with multimorbidity are scarce, particularly those incorporating mental health and sociodemographic factors. In a country like Iran, where the burden of non-communicable diseases (NCDs) is growing, identifying modifiable risk factors like sleep, is critical for disease prevention.

This research in intended to study the relationship between both sleep duration (long and short), and subjective sleep satisfaction with multimorbidity using data from Integrated Repeated Public Health Surveillance System (IRPHS) project in South Tehran. We hypothesized that poor sleep satisfaction and longer or shorter sleep duration are associated with higher odds of multimorbidity.

Methodology

Study design and population

A secondary cross-sectional study was conducted in January 2025, using data from 1311 adults participating in the IRPHS project in South Tehran that was conducted between 22-May-2023 and 24-Sep-2023. This study has obtained ethical approval from Tehran University of Medical Sciences and meets this institutions' guidelines for protection of human subjects concerning safety and privacy.

The original study (IRPHS) collected comprehensive health indicators from a representative population in the Tehran region and further information about the IRPHS and the relevant information is available elsewhere (Azizpour et al., 2025). The primary objective of the IRPHS was to assess and estimate critical health-related indicators in order to improve Iran's public health surveillance systems. By utilizing this system, the study was able to conduct a cross-sectional analysis, examining relationships between sleep satisfaction and chronic conditions such as DM, hypertension, CVD, and dyslipidemia. Participants were considered to have multimorbidity if they were affected by two or more of these four chronic diseases.

Data were gathered through structured telephone surveys. Participants were randomly selected adults aged 18 years and older residing within the Tehran University of Medical Sciences' coverage areas, including Southern Tehran, Shahr-e Rey, and Eslamshahr. The participants were selected using stratified random sampling with probability proportional to size (PPS) and provided self-reported data on sleep satisfaction and chronic diseases.

Variable measurement and definitions

Main Exposure: Sleep duration of the participants was subjectively assessed by asking them ‘How many hours do you usually sleep at night?’. Participants were categorized into three groups based on their sleep duration: insufficient or short sleep (≤6 h), normal sleep (7-8 h), and long sleep (≥9 h) (Hirshkowitz et al., 2015). Sleep satisfaction was subjectively determined by asking them ‘How satisfied are you with your sleep?’ and rated on a standardized scale from zero to 10. In this scale, 10 represented the highest satisfaction with sleep satisfaction and zero was the worst satisfaction and the value less than six is defined as poor sleep satisfaction (Badahdah et al., 2024).

Outcome: Chronic disease variables included self-reported diagnoses of DM, hypertension, CVD, and dyslipidemia by health care professional. In this study, the presence of two or more chronic health conditions at the same time in an individual is referred to as ‘multimorbidity’ (de Groot et al., 2003; Diederichs et al., 2011; Skou et al., 2022).

Covariates: After conducting comprehensive literature search on previous studies focusing on sleep health (28,29) we chose the following variable measurements tools. Referring to previous population-based studies exploring depressive mood using the Patient Health Questionnaire-2 (PHQ-2) (Bennett et al., 2025; Kroenke et al., 2003; Liao et al., 2021), IRPHS utilized the PHQ-2 to assess depressive moods among participants. This concise questionnaire consists of two items that ask respondents to rate their feelings over the past two weeks. This questionnaire was translated into Persian and adapted, demonstrating satisfactory internal consistency and validity (Mohamadian et al., 2022). Each item can be scored using a Likert scale (zero = not at all, one = on some days, two = more than half the days, three = nearly every day) and the depression mood was finally categorized as low, moderate, and high in which the higher total scores indicate more pronounced depressive symptoms.

Additionally, by referring to prior large cohort studies (Ahmadi et al., 2016) to get an insight, additional variables collected were age, sex, educational level, marital and employment status. Description of variables has been provided in the Table S1. Socioeconomic status (SES) was assessed with the question: ‘If the entire Iranian population were divided into five categories based on socioeconomic status, to which category would you assign your family? (Baigi et al., 2016) There were five categories for the SES question: ‘1-Lower’, ‘2-Low middle’, ‘3-Middle’, ‘4-Upper middle’, and ‘5-Upper’. Levels one and two were combined to “Lower” and “Upper middle” and “Upper” combined into “Upper”. Current smoking status was assessed with the question: ‘Are you currently smoking?’ and they were asked to choose ‘yes' or ‘no’.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were described in terms of frequencies and percentages, and numerical variables, including sleep duration and sleep satisfaction scores, were summarized in terms of median and interquartile range (IQR). To compare the frequency of multimorbidity across different sociodemographic factors and other variables of interest, the chi-square test was primarily used. Fisher's Exact test was applied when the two assumptions of the chi-square test were not met; specifically, when less than 80 % of the expected counts were greater than 5 or when any expected count was less than 1. The Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare continuous data, expressed as median (IQR), between groups.

For variables with missing data, case-wise deletion was initially performed. Subsequently, to ensure the robustness of the results, imputation of missing values was applied, followed by sensitivity analyses. The results indicated no substantial changes in the regression estimates, demonstrating the reliability of the analyses.

We also used univariable logistic regression analyses to explore how sleep duration and sleep satisfaction are related to the presence of multimorbidity, which was considered the outcome variable. Adjusted stepwise models were created to examine the association between multimorbidity and sleep duration and satisfaction. These models included age, sex, education, socioeconomic status, depression, and current smoking status as potential confounders, based on the available data from our study. The results of the models were presented in the form of odds ratios (OR) and the corresponding 95 % confidence interval (95 % CI). All analyses were performed using STATA software, version 17, and a p value of less than 0.05 is considered a level of statistical significance.

Ethical considerations statement and informed consent

Ethical approval for the study's design and data collection process was granted by the Research Committee of Tehran University of Medical Sciences with Ethics Code: IR.TUMS.FNM.REC.1403.113. The data utilized in this research were originally collected as part of a previous study, which had also received ethical clearance from the same committee (Ethics Code: IR.TUMS.MEDICINE.REC.1400.599). All participants provided verbal informed consent prior to participating in this study. The study meets this institutions' guidelines for protection of human subjects concerning safety and privacy.

Results

Demographic and psychosocial contributors to multimorbidity

A total of 1311 individuals participated in the surveillance study, with a nearly even sex distribution and a mean age of 40.4 and standard deviation (SD) of ±13.4 years. Out of 1311 participants, 78 (5.9 %) reported having multimorbidity.

Table 1 presents the prevalence of multimorbidity across different demographic groups. Multimorbidity was significantly more common among participants aged 65 years and older (37.2 %) compared to those aged 18 to 64 years (3.9 %), with this difference being statistically significant (p < 0.001). Additionally, females exhibited a higher prevalence of multimorbidity (7.6 %) than males (3.4 %), and this difference was also statistically significant (p = 0.002). Marital status was also associated with multimorbidity, with widowed or divorced individuals showing the highest prevalence (32.5 %, p < 0.001). Retired participants (14.7 %) and those with lower educational attainment (16.9 %) had higher prevalence compared to employed or unemployed and more educated individuals (p < 0.001 for both). The prevalence of multimorbidity increased with higher levels of depression: 4.9 % among participants with low depression, 5.1 % among those with moderate depression, and 16.8 % among participants with high depression (p < 0.001). Likewise, 19 participants with multimorbidity (24.7 %) reported long sleep duration, compared to 224 participants (18.4 %) without the condition. However, these differences were not statistically significant (p = 0.150). Poor sleep satisfaction was reported by 18 individuals with multimorbidity (23.7 %) and by 212 individuals without it (17.3 %), though this difference was also not statistically significant (p = 0.156). Discussion

Our study highlights the complex interactions between sleep health, depressive symptoms, and multimorbidity. We found that long sleep duration and higher levels of depressive symptoms were significantly associated with increased risk of multimorbidity, independent of sociodemographic factors. This study revealed that individuals who reported longer sleep durations were more likely to have multimorbidity (OR: 1.94; 95 % CI: 1.04,3.65; p = 0.039), under the confounding control afforded by the variables measured and included in this study. These variables included sociodemographic and psychological variables, including age, sex, socioeconomic status, education, and depression. This finding aligns with previous studies suggesting that excessive sleep may serve as a marker of underlying health conditions rather than a cause for it (Youngstedt and Kripke, 2004). Prolonged sleep has been associated to inflammatory processes, sedentary behavior, and depressive symptoms, all of which may contribute to chronic disease accumulation (Medic et al., 2017; Merlo, 2019).

In our study, individuals with long sleep duration had nearly twice the risk of multimorbidity, and this association remained statistically significant after adjusting for depressive symptoms and socioeconomic status. These findings suggest that long sleep may serve as an indicator of poor health and a potential risk factor for multimorbidity, particularly among older adults.

Individuals with chronic conditions or impaired physical functioning may experience longer sleep durations due to fatigue, fragmented sleep, or side effects of medication. Given the study's cross-sectional design, it remains unclear whether long sleep is a cause or a consequence of multimorbidity.

Hence, the directionality of the relationship remains unclear which requires further investigation to better understand how sleep duration and chronic diseases are related over time. The modern sleep medicine has focused more emphasis on the problems of short sleep and its association with chronic disease, little attention has been focused on long sleep duration (Youngstedt and Kripke, 2004).

The findings suggest that both short and long sleep durations may be risk factors for chronic diseases, particularly in older adults. Adhering to the recommended sleep duration could help mitigate these risks. We also observed that multimorbidity was most prevalent among participants with high levels of depression, which was significantly higher than in those with moderate or low depressive symptoms. In other words, a higher level of depression was associated with a greater odd of multimorbidity. This supports existing literature highlighting the bidirectional relationship between depression and chronic disease (Conversano, 2019; Edmealem et al., 2020; Merlo, 2019). Depression impacts health behaviors (e.g., physical inactivity, sleep disruption) and biological pathways (e.g., inflammation, cortisol dysregulation), thereby increasing the risk of multimorbidity. Depressive mood was assessed using the PHQ-2, a brief two-item screening tool (Bennett et al., 2025; Kroenke et al., 2003; Liao et al., 2021). While practical for telephone surveys, this instrument offers a relatively limited assessment and does not capture the full spectrum of depression severity. Therefore, related findings related should be interpreted with caution.

Although our findings indicated that short sleep duration and poor sleep satisfaction were associated with higher risk of multimorbidity, these associations did not reach statistical significance in our adjusted models. This contrasts with earlier studies that reported short sleep as a more consistent predictor of cardiometabolic diseases (Ai et al., 2021; Grandner et al., 2013). One possible explanation for this discrepancy is the reliance on self-reported measures in our study, which might introduce recall bias (Dalziel et al., 2018; Karimi et al., 2025). Additionally, cultural, lifestyle, geographical, and environmental variations unique to our Iranian sample may influence sleep patterns and chronic disease differently compared to western cohorts (Khazaie et al., 2024). For example, family co-sleeping is more common among Iranian households, where cultural norms often support shared sleeping arrangements (Zhang et al., 2024). In contrast, co-sleeping among Western populations is frequently discouraged due to belief that solitary sleep promotes child independence and concerns with health and disease (Owens, 2004). These factors may influence the accuracy of our findings and make it harder to detect subtle relationships.

Our analysis also revealed that poor sleep satisfaction was more common among individuals with multimorbidity (23.7 %) compared to those without it (17.3 %), although this difference was not statistically significant. Although borderline statistical significance was observed when sleep satisfaction was analyzed as a continuous variable, the discrepancy between continuous and categorical outcomes may be attributable to the choice of cut-off point or to potential information loss resulting from categorization. Previous studies have reported stronger associations between sleep disturbances and conditions such as hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia (Grandner et al., 2013; Medic et al., 2017). The lack of statistical significance in our findings may be due to the use of a single-item sleep satisfaction scale, which, while validated (Badahdah et al., 2024), may lack the level of detail needed to capture the multidimensional nature of sleep satisfaction.

The findings of this study are observational and do not imply causality. They underscore the need for future longitudinal studies that incorporate social support, healthcare access, and lifestyle factors.

This study is limited by its cross-sectional design, which precludes causal inference. Sleep and health data were self-reported, which may have introduced recall bias. The absence of objective sleep measures and the exclusion of certain confounding factors (e.g., shift work, caffeine use) may affect the accuracy of the findings. Therefore, future studies should consider including objective sleep measures to improve accuracy. Our operational definition of multimorbidity was limited to four common NCDs: diabetes, hypertension, cardiovascular disease, and dyslipidemia. This limitation may have led to an underestimation of the true prevalence of multimorbidity. Consequently, we were unable to examine complex multimorbidity, which is a better measure to identify individuals with more complex conditions and greater needs compared with a simple count of diseases (multimorbidity) (Kato et al., 2021; Ryu et al., 2023). Future research could incorporate broader disease lists or established multimorbidity indices. However, a key strength of this study is the utilization of a large, population-based sample from a public health surveillance system, which enhances the generalizability of the findings. Moreover, the findings are primarily generalizable to urban and rural residents of the southern Tehran region, rather than to the national population. Additionally, the inclusion of both sleep satisfaction and psychological factors provides a more comprehensive understanding of the contributors to multimorbidity.

Conclusion

This cross-sectional study found that long sleep duration was closely associated with a higher risk of multimorbidity. Although short sleep duration and poor sleep satisfaction showed non-significant trends. Given the cross-sectional design, longitudinal studies are needed to clarify the directionality of these relationships and to adjust for additional potential confounders, such as alcohol consumption. Future research could also apply linear trend tests across categorized sleep durations, stratify analyses by age and sex, and perform sensitivity analyses using alternative thresholds for longer sleep duration. The findings highlight the importance of incorporating sleep assessments—particularly long sleep duration—into public health surveillance to identify individuals at risk for multimorbidity.
