Cryo-EM structures of naturally occurring dimeric photosystem II complexes lacking the Mn4CaO5 cluster
Authors: Ziyu Zhao, Irene Vercellino, Julian P. Whitelegge, Karim Maghlaoui, Wojciech Białek, Peter J. Nixon, Leonid A. Sazanov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Robust oxygenic photosynthesis requires the efficient assembly and repair of the multi-subunit oxygen-evolving photosystem II (PSII) complex. Previous cryogenic electron microscopy (cryo-EM) structures of PSII assembly/disassembly intermediates have relied on the analysis of deletion mutants or removal of PSII subunits in vitro. Here we report the cryo-EM structures of naturally occurring dimeric PSII intermediates from the cyanobacterium Thermosynechococcus vestitus at a resolution of about 2.2 Å. These intermediates contain inactive dimers lacking the oxygen-evolving complex (OEC) and semi-active dimers with the OEC present in one of the two monomers. Our structural data provide a mechanism for how assembly and disassembly of the Mn4CaO5 cluster is coordinated with the binding and release of the extrinsic proteins: restructuring of the C-terminal tail of D1 subunit during assembly or disassembly of the Mn cluster triggers conformational changes in D2, CP47 and CP43 to drive the binding/release of the extrinsic proteins. A combination of structural and mass spectrometry data also suggests that the inactive PSII complexes may include damaged complexes containing oxidized D1-His332, a monodentate ligand to one of the Mn ions of the OEC.

Introduction

Photosystem II (PSII) is the light-driven multi-subunit membrane complex found in cyanobacteria and chloroplasts that catalyzes the oxidation of water and reduction of plastoquinone during oxygenic photosynthetic electron transport. PSII is the only enzyme known in nature that is able to catalyze the water-splitting reaction, making it essential for producing oxygen and supporting aerobic life1.

Assembly of PSII is a stepwise process involving multiple accessory factors, many conserved in cyanobacteria and chloroplasts, that promote and regulate biogenesis (Fig. 1)2. During the early stage of PSII assembly in cyanobacteria, the Ycf48 accessory factor binds to the lumenal side of the D1 and D2 reaction center complex (RC), preventing premature binding of ions to the Mn4CaO5 cluster binding site3–6. The PSII RC then binds the CP47 module (CP47mod) to form the RC47 complex, followed by attachment of the CP43 module (CP43mod) to create an inactive non-oxygen-evolving monomeric PSII complex7,8. D1 is synthesized as a precursor protein (pD1) with a 16-residue C-terminal extension, which is cleaved by the CtpA protease in a two-step process before attachment of CP439–12. Subsequently, fully functional PSII dimers are formed by assembly of the Mn4CaO5 cluster in a light-driven process known as photoactivation13 and by attachment of extrinsic subunits to the lumenal side of PSII to protect and stabilize the Mn4CaO5 cluster14. Light not only drives photosynthesis and photoactivation but also damages the photosynthetic complexes, leading to chronic photoinhibition15. Acceptor- and donor-side mechanisms of visible-light photodamage are still under debate15,16. The acceptor-side mechanism suggests that photodamage results from an over-reduced acceptor-side of PSII and blockage of forward electron transfer, which leads to the production of reactive oxygen species, especially singlet oxygen15,17,18. In contrast, donor-side mechanisms propose that excitation of Mn3+/Mn4+ ions by visible light or the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) by the Mn4CaO5 cluster leads to photodamage16,19–21.

Recently, cryo-EM has provided structural insights into various cyanobacterial PSII assembly intermediates, including the PSII RC6 and RC47 monomers22,23 and a Psb27/PSII dimer24. These structures were obtained from mutants with deletions of specific PSII subunits that block assembly of PSII to enhance the accumulation of low-abundance PSII intermediates6,23,24. Additionally, structures of PSII lacking the Mn4CaO5 cluster were obtained by treating oxygen-evolving PSII with NH2OH, EDTA and NaCl25,26. The PSII structure missing both the Mn4CaO5 cluster and extrinsic subunits is termed apo-PSII26 (see Supplementary Table 1 for further details of these structures).

Previous work by Nowaczyk and colleagues revealed that purification of His-tagged PSII from WT Thermosynechococcus vestitus BP-1 (T. vestitus) yielded four fractions when separated by anion-exchange chromatography27: a Psb27/PSII monomer, an oxygen-evolving PSII monomer, a fully active oxygen-evolving PSII dimer and, the fourth, a less active oxygen-evolving PSII dimer fraction, here termed PSIIpeak4, which has not been fully characterized.

Here we use cryo-EM to determine three structures of the dimeric complexes present in PSIIpeak4 at a resolution of around 2.2 Å. This pool of PSII dimers consists of active PSII dimers with extrinsic subunits (PsbO, PsbU and PsbV) bound to both monomers, semi-active PSII dimers with extrinsic subunits attached to only one monomer and inactive dimers that lack extrinsic subunits. Although lacking extrinsic subunits, the inactive PSII monomers in the semi-active and inactive dimers contain all the transmembrane subunits, including PsbJ with partial occupancy and all the key cofactors apart from the Mn4CaO5 cluster and one of the two chloride anions, termed Cl-2. Compared with fully functional PSII, the inactive PSII monomers also show structural differences in D1, D2, CP43, and CP47 around the donor side close to the binding site of the Mn4CaO5 cluster. These changes allow Mn2+ and Ca2+ ions access to and from the binding site of the Mn4CaO5 cluster. In addition, the structure of the D1-His332 residue in the inactive monomer, which is a ligand to the Mn cluster, suggests oxidation of the imidazole side chain. This oxidation could potentially reflect an early stage of photodamage to PSII on the donor-side of PSII.

Results

Purification and properties of PSIIpeak4 fraction

PSIIpeak4 was purified from T. vestitus using His-tagged CP47, as described in ref. 28. Consistent with the findings of Nowaczyk and colleagues, PSIIpeak4 is a less active PSII dimer fraction27 (Supplementary Figs. 1, 2). SDS-PAGE with Coomassie brilliant blue staining revealed reduced amounts of the three extrinsic subunits in PSIIpeak4 compared to fully functional oxygen-evolving PSII (Supplementary Figs. 1, 2), in line with the lower oxygen evolution activity of PSIIpeak4. It is unlikely that the extrinsic subunits were removed by salt during anion-exchange chromatography, as PSII retains its activity up to 90 mM MgSO429, whereas PSIIpeak4 was eluted at a MgSO4 concentration of less than 60 mM (Supplementary Fig. 1b), which is insufficient to detach extrinsic subunits. Negative staining electron microscopy confirmed that PSIIpeak4 contained dimeric PSII complexes similar in size to fully functional PSII (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Overall structures of PSII intermediates in the PSIIpeak4 mixture

The composition and structures of the PSII complexes in PSIIpeak4 were determined by cryo-EM single-particle analysis. The dataset was collected with the grid tilted 30 degrees to mitigate the effects of preferential orientation of the sample. Extensive 2D and 3D classification resulted in 1.06 million good particles, which were separated into three classes: active PSII dimer (34.5% of total particles), inactive PSII dimer (39.6% of total particles), and semi-active PSII dimer (25.8% of total particles) (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Figs. 4, 5). The presence of inactive complexes explains the reduced oxygen-evolution activity in the PSIIpeak4 mixture (Supplementary Fig. 2c). The distinction between these classes is based on the presence or absence of the extrinsic subunits (PsbO, PsbU, and PsbV) and the Mn4CaO5 cluster on the lumenal side of PSII. Active dimers contain both sets of extrinsic subunits and the Mn4CaO5 cluster; inactive dimers lack both sets of extrinsic subunits and the Mn4CaO5 cluster, and semi-active dimers have one monomer that contains extrinsic subunits and the Mn4CaO5 cluster while the other monomer lacks them. The overall resolution for all three classes is about 2.2 Å (active dimer: 2.22 Å; inactive dimer: 2.15 Å; semi-active: 2.24 Å). The PSII( + OEC) monomer in the semi-active dimer is highly similar to that in the active dimer, showing no distinct differences. Therefore, subsequent structural descriptions and comparisons are based on the PSII(-OEC) and PSII( + OEC) complexes in the semi-active dimer PSII, unless stated otherwise.

Cofactors in the PSII(-OEC) complex

In the PSII( + OEC) complex, the arrangement of key cofactors is highly similar to that of fully functional oxygen-evolving PSII determined previously (Supplementary Fig. 11). In the PSII(-OEC) complex, apart from the Mn4CaO5 cluster and Cl-2, the other key cofactors and residues in the D1/D2 heterodimer are all present at their expected positions, including PD1, PD2, ChlD1, ChlD2, PheoD1, PheoD2, QA, QB, the non-heme iron, bicarbonate, D1-β-carotene, D2-β-carotene, ChlZD1, ChlZD2, Cl-1 (Fig. 2d) as are the chlorophylls and β-carotenes in CP43 and CP47 (Supplementary Fig. 12). It is known that the Mn4CaO5 cluster is not stable without the extrinsic subunits31 and that Cl-2, proposed to be involved in a proton-exit pathway, binds to D1-N338, D1-F339 and CP43-E342 (E354 in T. vulcanus) close to the interface of D1 and the extrinsic subunits30,32,33. Redox-active tyrosines YZ (D1-Tyr161) and YD (D2-Tyr160), along with the related H-bonding histidine residues D1-H190 and D2-H189, are also located at the expected positions (Fig. 2d). The distance between Yz and His190 is crucial for rapid reduction of PD1+34. In the PSII(-OEC) complexes in the semi-active and inactive dimers, the Yz-H190 distances are all around 2.7 Å, close to the value of 2.5-2.6 Å seen in the PSII( + OEC) complexes in the semi-active and active dimers, but slightly longer than the 2.4 Å distance reported for fully functional PSII30. Additionally, water molecules, which contribute to shortening the distance between YZ and D1-H19034, are observed in similar positions in the PSII(-OEC) and PSII( + OEC) complexes (Supplementary Fig. 13). D1-N298, which is H-bonded to D1-H190 and is necessary for efficient oxidation of YZ34, is also located at the expected position (Fig. 2d, Supplementary Fig. 13).

In general, the PSII(-OEC) complex displays well-resolved cofactor density on the acceptor side, including QB (Supplementary Fig. 9), and closely resembles the structure of fully functional PSII in this region (Supplementary Fig. 14), despite lacking cofactors on the donor side (Fig. 2d).

PSII dimer is maintained in the absence of extrinsic subunits

Previous studies of PSII from the mesophilic cyanobacterium Synechocystis sp. PCC 6803 have suggested that PsbO stabilizes the dimeric state of PSII26,35–38. The lack of PsbO in our inactive PSII dimer and Psb27/PSII dimer24 would suggest that PsbO is not obligatory for maintaining the PSII dimer in the thermophiles and that attachment of extrinsic subunits to the two monomers in a dimer may occur independently.

Comparison of the PSII( + OEC) and PSII(-OEC) structures allowed us to investigate the role of the extrinsic proteins in modifying the structures of the intrinsic subunits. Interestingly, the attachment of PsbO in the PSII( + OEC) complex appears to correlate with a subtle shift in a minor loop of the CP47 subunit, spanning residues G82 to P88, compared with the PSII(-OEC) complex (Supplementary Fig. 15). Apart from this difference in the CP47 loop, no other structural changes were observed at the interface that could be attributed to the attachment of the extrinsic subunits.

We found here that a ß-sheet in CP47 (CP47-H343 to CP47-R347), previously reported to be disordered upon removal of extrinsic proteins26, remains ordered in PSII( + OEC) in the semi-active PSII dimer and identical to that of oxygen-evolving PSII, despite the absence of PsbO in the adjacent PSII(-OEC) complex (Supplementary Fig. 16). The disordered ß-sheet in this region is therefore likely caused by a loss of low-molecular-mass subunits, PsbJ, PsbY and PsbZ in apo-PSII26.

The Mn-binding site is solvent accessible from the lumenal side of PSII(-OEC)

Compared with the fully functional PSII complex, the PSII(-OEC) complex exhibits not only the absence of extrinsic subunits but also structural differences on the lumenal side. In fully functional PSII, the Mn4CaO5 cluster is not only covered by extrinsic subunits, but also by the C-terminal tail of D1 and D2 and a lumenal loop of CP43 from L312 to Y328 (Fig. 3c). In contrast, structural changes in the PSII(-OEC) complex, including alterations in the lumenal region of CP43, the C-terminal tail of D1 and D2, and a minor loop of CP47, now make the high-affinity Mn2+-binding site at D1-D170 and D1-E189 accessible from the lumen (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Fig. 17). The narrowest radius of the large channel reaching the Mn-binding site is about 4.1 Å (Supplementary Fig. 17), possibly allowing the passage of hydrated Mn2+, which has a 4.2 Å radius39. Poorly resolved regions on the lumenal side, including D1-E333 and D1-R334, suggest that this channel is dynamic and that the actual channel may be larger than observed.

CP43 displacement and flexibility prevent binding of extrinsic subunits

In the PSII(-OEC) complex, the lumenal region of CP43 from T293 to P388 shifts away from the Mn4CaO5 cluster, especially the CP43 helix that interacts with the D1 C-terminal tail (Figs. 3c, 4c). However, CP43-E342 (E354 in T. vulcanus), which acts as a ligand to the Mn4CaO5 cluster, is present at the expected position (Fig. 3c). Previous work has indicated that the CP43 soluble domain is flexible and involved in assembly of the Mn cluster14,40. An atomic force microscopy (AFM) study of T. vulcanus has suggested that assembly of the Mn4CaO5 cluster induces changes in the positions of CP43-E342 (E354 in T. vulcanus) and CP43-R345 (R357 in T. vulcanus), subsequently triggering conformational changes in the lumenal region of CP4314. While our data do support a shift in this region of CP43, the CP43-E342 and CP43-R345 residues are found at the same positions as in oxygen-evolving PSII, and the overall structural changes are of a significantly lower magnitude compared to that proposed in the AFM study (Fig. 3c).

Similar shifts in CP43 from T293 to P388 have been reported in other PSII intermediates, including apo-PSII, the Psb27/PSII dimer and Psb28/Psb27/PSII complex22,24,26. The shift in this area does not cause steric clashes with the expected positions of PsbO, PsbU, or PsbV (Supplementary Fig. 18). Instead, the displacement of the CP43 lumenal domain shifts it away from the Mn4CaO5 cluster, resulting in the disengagement of CP43 from the binding interface for the extrinsic subunits.

Additionally, the lumenal loop CP43-G313 to CP43-L325 in the PSII(-OEC) complex exhibits poor density and cannot be modeled, suggesting high flexibility in this region (Supplementary Fig. 19). Furthermore, the CP43-G326 to Y328 region shows an alternative orientation to that observed in oxygen-evolving PSII so that CP43-K327 points away from the D1 C-terminal tail (Figs. 3c, 4c). Other PSII intermediates lacking the extrinsic subunits also lack density in this area (Supplementary Fig. 20)22–24,26.

In agreement with Gisriel and colleagues26, the flexibility of CP43 is likely due to the absence of extrinsic subunits. However, we notice two key differences between CP43 in the PSII(-OEC) complex and Gisriel’s apo-PSII structure26. First, CP43-E342 (E354 in T. vulcanus), which shifts 3 Å away from its expected Mn-coordinating position in apo-PSII, remains unchanged in PSII(-OEC). Second, while the region from CP43-G389 to F404 is difficult to model in apo-PSII26, most of this region exhibits clear density in the PSII(-OEC) complex. Possibly the loss of low-molecular-mass subunits, PsbJ, PsbY, and PsbZ, in apo-PSII might increase the flexibility of the soluble domain of CP43.

Flexible D1 C-terminal tail

Although the Mn4CaO5 cluster-coordinating residues D1-D170 and D1-E189 remain at their expected positions in the inactive PSII complexes, D1-E333 and D1-R334 show ambiguous side-chain density and possibly show a different conformation to that in fully active PSII (Fig. 3c). The backbone of D1-E333 undergoes a ~ 4 Å shift, with its side chain possibly orientated toward the Mn4CaO5 cluster, in an opposite orientation to that modeled in apo-PSII26 (Supplementary Fig. 21). Density corresponding to the D1 C-terminal tail from N335 to A344 could not be observed in PSII(-OEC) likely due to the high flexibility in this region (Fig. 4a) as mass spectrometry data confirmed that the D1 C-terminus was not truncated.

Previous structures of PSII intermediates have also identified D1-D170 and D1-E189 at the Mn coordinating positions6,22–24,26. Notably, D1-D170 and D1-E189 are already positioned to bind the Mn cluster in the early reaction center assembly intermediate (RCII)6 with D1-D170, coordinating Mn4, previously identified as a high-affinity Mn2+ binding site41. Additionally, a Psb27/Psb28/PSII assembly complex22 contained an unknown metal ion density close to D1-D170. These observations further support the hypothesis that the primary high-affinity Mn2+ binding site is close to Mn4 and Ca2+26,42.

Unexpectedly, the side chain of D1-His332 in PSII(-OEC) was found to have an extra density in addition to the imidazole ring (Fig. 5a and Supplementary Fig. 22a,b). Mass spectrometry data confirmed the presence of singly oxidized D1-H332 in PSIIpeak4 (Fig. 5b, Supplementary Table 6a), so this density may arise from the oxidation of the imidazole ring to form hydroxylated histidine, similar to that previously reported for D2-H33643. To fit this density, D1-H332 needs to adopt a flipped rotamer from its Mn-coordinating conformation. The extra density could also reflect a mixture of rotamers, as D1-H332 lacks coordinating interactions in the PSII(-OEC) complex, although the fitting of two rotamers did not account for the extra density as well as an oxidized imidazole side chain. The 2.2 Å resolution of the map would be insufficient to resolve protonation as an explanation for the extra density, and the density is too close to the imidazole ring to be due to a ligated metal ion44. One possibility to explain the possible oxidation of D1-H332 is that D1-H332 undergoes photodamage due to oxidation by ROS generated during water oxidation and that the inactive complexes represent damaged complexes rather than assembly complexes. D1-H332 is close to the site of water oxidation as it ligates Mn1 of the cluster, which is thought to bind the Ox water involved in the formation of O2 through an oxo-oxyl coupling mechanism33, possibly involving a peroxide intermediate45. Although 2-oxo-histidine appears to be more commonly observed in biology under ROS-mediated conditions46,47 than hydroxylated histidine (Supplementary Fig. 22d), the specific oxidation product likely depends on the local protein environment and structure48. Early photoinduced oxidation of D1-H332 has also been observed in experiments using isolated cyanobacterial PSII complexes49 and plant thylakoid membranes50.

In addition, D1-H332 oxidation is not unique and occurs alongside oxidation of other residues, including M328, M331, and E333 (Supplementary Table 6a). Single and double methionine oxidations were detected by mass spectrometry in the D1 C-terminal tail (M328 and M331) (Fig. 5b). However, apart from a slight extra density at D1-M331 in active dimers, no clear additional density was observed on the side chains of D1-M328 or M331 in either PSII( + OEC) or PSII(-OEC) (Supplementary Fig. 23). The poor density for D1-E333 prevents distinguishing potential oxidation at this site. M328, M331, H332, and E333 have been previously reported as photodamage sites50. In our cryo-EM maps, significant extra density was observed only on the side chain of His332. The overall oxidation level of residues from A324 to R334 in PSIIPeak4 is higher than in oxygen-evolving active PSIIPeak3 (Supplementary Table 6b), suggesting that PSIIPeak4 likely contains damaged PSII complexes. Single oxidation of histidine is only observed under special conditions51,52, such as exposure to singlet oxygen53, a signature ROS generated during photodamage15, or during metal-ion-catalyzed oxidation (MCO)48. Therefore, the observed oxidation of D1-H332 is more likely a consequence of photodamage.

D1 C-terminus unfolding allows flipping of D2 C-terminus and displacement of CP47

In oxygen-evolving PSII, the D1 C-terminal tail not only provides ligands (H332, E333, D342, A344) to the Mn4CaO5 cluster (Fig. 4a), but also interacts with the C-terminal tail of D2 (Fig. 4b), the CP43 L312-Y328 loop (Fig. 4c), and a helix from T293 to G313 of CP43. The backbone oxygens of D1-R334 and D1-H337 H-bond to the backbone nitrogen and side chain of D2-N350 (Fig. 4b), respectively. The side chain of D1-N338 H-bonds to the backbone of CP43-K327. In addition, D1-F339 exhibits a hydrophobic interaction with CP43-F305 (F317 in T. vulcanus), and CP43-Q301 (Q313 in T. vulcanus) also H-bonds to D1-L341 (Fig. 4c).

In the absence of a structured D1 C-terminal tail, the C-terminal tail of D2 flips to the opposite orientation, interacting with CP47-R384 within a small loop of CP47 (Fig. 4b). This interaction induces a minor shift in the loop of CP47 and results in clashes with PsbO-G163 to D169 in the large external loop of PsbO (Fig. 3b), a crucial region for the attachment of PsbO54. Interestingly, mutation of CP47-R384 and R385 decreases the binding of extrinsic subunits55, suggesting that this interaction between the C-terminal tail of D2 and CP47 is important for assembly and possibly disassembly of PSII.

Discussion

We have determined the structures, at a resolution of about 2.2 Å, of active, semi-active and inactive dimeric PSII complexes of T. vestitus that co-purify in a single peak by anion-exchange chromatography27, here termed PSIIPeak4. PSIIPeak4 represents about 5% of total PSII (Supplementary Fig. 1) and contains the three different types of complex at similar levels, which explains the reduced oxygen-evolving activity displayed by this fraction.

The OEC-containing dimeric PSII shows no significant protein or cofactor differences compared with previous structures of dimeric oxygen-evolving PSII30. The reason why the OEC-containing PSII dimers elute in Peak 4 rather than Peak 3 remains unclear, but may be due to subtle protein variations (e.g., in the unresolved regions) or differences in associated lipid or detergent molecules. It is also conceivable that active PSII dimers observed in Peak4 are coming with the trailing edge of Peak 3, which is substantially larger than Peak 4 (Supplementary Fig. 1).

The semi-active and inactive PSII complexes may in principle represent either assembly intermediates or photodamaged disassembly complexes or a mixture of both. Mass spectrometry data together with the structural data of PSII(-OEC) raise the interesting possibility that D1-His332, a ligand to the Mn cluster, may be oxidized, although more work is needed to confirm this assignment given the resolution of the structures. However, from a physiological perspective, prompt detachment of the extrinsic proteins triggered by photodamage to D1 C-terminal residues, would allow access of reductant to the Mn cluster to promote disassembly56, thereby preventing the formation of further reactive oxygen species and further damage to PSII. A semi-active PSII dimer has also been observed after affinity purification of twin-strep tagged PsbO followed by anion-exchange chromatography. Whether it is an assembly or disassembly complex (or a mixture) remains unclear57.

A recent structure of a Psb32/PsbV/Psb27/PSII assembly intermediate has revealed the binding of the Psb27 and Psb32 assembly factors, along with the PsbV extrinsic subunit, to the lumenal side of PSII58. In this complex, the transmembrane helix of Psb32 occupies the position of PsbY and assists in binding of PsbV, suggesting that extrinsic subunits are not incorporated spontaneously, but rather in a sequential and regulated manner with the assistance of Psb32.

In contrast, the PSII(-OEC) structure shows clear density for PsbY but lacks all extrinsic subunits. Either the PSII(-OEC) complexes are derived from photodamaged PSII or they represent a late-stage assembly intermediate in a parallel pathway to that involving Psb32. Deletion mutants of cyanobacteria and plants lacking Psb32 exhibit phenotypic deficiencies only under stress conditions59,60, implying that Psb32 is not absolutely essential for PSII assembly and that alternative PSII assembly pathways exist.

A striking feature of the structures so far determined for PSII assembly intermediates lacking the OEC and PSII disassembly complexes that have had the extrinsic proteins and OEC removed is their high degree of structural similarity22–24,26. Consequently, we suggest that assembly/disassembly of PSII follow similar trajectories but proceed in opposite directions. In the case of assembly, we propose that photoactivation, which drives the assembly of the Mn cluster, is coupled to the remodeling of the C-terminal tail of D1 within the complex. The folded D1 triggers the movement of the lumenal domain of CP43 and releases the CP47 loop from the D2 C-terminal tail to allow attachment of the PsbO extrinsic subunit (Fig. 6a). The current two-quantum photoactivation model, based largely on biochemical data, fits well with our mechanism13. After the first flash oxidizing a Mn2+ ion bound to the high-affinity Mn-binding site, PSII undergoes a slow ‘dark rearrangement’ that possibly corresponds to the movement of D1, D2, CP43 and CP47 in the lumenal region. A second photon then oxidizes a second Mn2+ ion to generate the first stable intermediate in the assembly of the Mn cluster.

Our structure of the PSII(-OEC) complex revealed the presence of a putative channel for the delivery of metal ions for Mn cluster assembly (Supplementary Fig. 17). Gisriel et al. suggested that D1-E333 in the C-terminal tail could deliver Mn2+ to the Mn cluster binding site26. However, this remains an open question, as E333 adopts a different conformation in apo-PSII compared to our PSII(-OEC) structure. (Supplementary Fig. 21). It is feasible that one of the water channels, the O1 channel, which has a minimum radius exceeding 0.9-1.1 Å61,62, slightly larger than the ionic radius of Mn2+ (0.8 Å), may also serve as a Mn delivery channel, assuming Mn is dehydrated in the channel. However, mutations of O1 channel bottleneck residues do not appear to affect the assembly or structure of the Mn₄CaO₅ cluster63.

In the case of disassembly, oxidation of PSII residues close to the Mn cluster leads to destabilization of the D1 C-terminal tail, triggering the detachment of extrinsic subunits from the lumenal side and opening of the lumenal channel. Subsequently, Mn²⁺ and Ca²⁺ ions are released into the lumen, resulting in disassembly of the PSII complex (Fig. 6b). Damaged subunits, such as D1, are then degraded and replaced by newly synthesized copies. Notably, the PSII structure in which the OEC was removed after crystallization shows an empty Mn₄CaO₅ site while retaining a folded D1 C-terminal tail and all three extrinsic subunits25. This suggests that Mn and Ca ions may exit PSII via alternative pathways rather than direct lumenal release, although no fully enclosed Mn-binding site has been observed in in vivo OEC-depleted PSII intermediates.

Inactive PSII complexes show QB density (Supplementary Fig. 9), while lacking cofactors on the donor side (Fig. 2d). This observation is similar to the recently reported Psb32/Psb27/PsbV/PSII assembly intermediate58. In addition, the acceptor side of inactive PSII resembles that of the structure of fully functional PSII (Supplementary Fig. 14).

The flexible C-terminal tail of D1 is a feature of all PSII intermediates23,24,26, apart from the Psb27/Psb28/PSII complex which shows an unknown metal bound to the lumenal side of PSII22 (Supplementary Fig. 21). For all structures of PSII intermediates, the re-orientated D2 C-terminal tail is present (Supplementary Fig. 21), but density for the loop from CP43-G313 to L325 is always missing, even though in some cases this region was still built in the model (Supplementary Fig. 20)22–24.

The structure of the soluble domain of CP43 in PSII(-OEC) is highly similar to that of the T. vulcanus Psb27/PSII dimer24 and T. vestitus Psb28/Psb27/PSII monomer22 (Supplementary Fig. 24). When PSII(-OEC) is superimposed on the two Psb27-containing structures22,24, only minor clashes are observed between Psb27 and CP43 in PSII(-OEC) (Supplementary Fig. 24). Additionally, several CP43 residues previously reported to conflict with and disrupt interactions with PsbO were found in similar positions in PSII(-OEC)24 (Supplementary Fig. 25). These observations suggest that the existence of Psb27 is unlikely to induce structural changes in CP43. Instead, Psb27 likely acts as a ‘door wedge’, blocking the necessary movement of CP43 required for Mn4CaO5 cluster assembly. If the soluble domain of CP43 from the Psb27/PSII structure is moved to its position in the fully functional PSII, Psb27 sterically clashes with PsbO (Supplementary Fig. 26)24, indicating that with Psb27 bound, the soluble domain of CP43 cannot shift toward the Mn4CaO5 cluster and properly accommodate PsbO. As a result, the proper ‘dark rearrangement’ during photoactivation may be impeded.

Methods

Purification and characterization of Peak 4 PSII

His-tagged CP47 T. vestitus cells were grown under ~60 μmol photons·m−2·s−1 light intensity, and thylakoid membranes were solubilized using 1% (w/v) DDM for 10 min at 4 °C at a chlorophyll concentration of 1 mg/ml. The solubilized PSII was purified by nickel affinity chromatography followed by anion exchange chromatography according to Nowaczyk et al.27,28 with the following modifications: PSII complexes eluted from nickel affinity column were loaded directly on the Bio-Rad UNO Q-12 column using a AKTA purifier 10 system; PSII were then eluted with 5–200 mM MgSO4 gradient28. The purification methods were derived from Nowaczyk et al.27 and described in Michoux et al.28.

SDS-PAGE and CN-PAGE Electrophoresis

Protein samples containing 3 μL of chlorophyll were mixed with 2× SDS loading buffer (125 mM Tris/HCl, pH 6.8; 4% (w/v) SDS; 20% (v/v) glycerol; 0.1% (w/v) bromophenol blue; and 10% (v/v) 2-mercaptoethanol) and loaded onto a 12% Tris–Tricine gel containing 6 M urea64. Electrophoresis was performed at 5 mA for 7 hr at 4 °C. Gels were then stained with InstantBlue™ (Expedeon, UK) for 2 h.

Clear Native PAGE (CN-PAGE) was carried out using NativePAGE™ 3–12% Bis-Tris pre-cast gels (Life Technologies, UK), as previously described65. Protein samples were mixed with native loading buffer to a final composition of 50 mM Bis-Tris/HCl (pH 6.5), 7% (v/v) glycerol, and 0.01% (w/v) Ponceau S. Electrophoresis was performed at 8 mA and 4 °C using an anode buffer (50 mM Bis-Tris/HCl, pH 7.0) and a cathode buffer (50 mM Tricine, 15 mM Bis-Tris/HCl, pH 7.0, 0.05% (w/v) sodium deoxycholate, and 0.03% (w/v) β-DDM).

The Psb27 antibody was raised against the Thermosynechococcus vestitus BP-1 protein expressed in E coli28 and immunoblotting was performed as described previously66.

Measurement of oxygen evolution activity

Oxygen evolution activity was assessed using an Oxyl1-Oxylab device (Hansatech Instruments, UK). Cells were adjusted to a chlorophyll a (Chl a) concentration of 1 μg/ml. The activity was measured with 1 mM potassium ferricyanide (K3[Fe(CN)6]) and 0.1 mM 2,6-dichlorobenzoquinone (DCBQ) at a light intensity of 2000 μmol photons m⁻² s⁻¹. For PSII protein samples, 1 μg of Chl a was mixed with 1 ml of a buffer solution containing 50 mM MES-NaOH (pH 6.5), 500 mM sucrose, 30 mM CaCl₂, 10 mM MgCl₂, 1 mM K₃[Fe(CN)₆], and 0.1 mM DCBQ. The oxygen evolution activity was then measured under the same light conditions of 2000 μmol photons m⁻² s⁻¹.

Mass spectrometry

PSIIpeak4 and PSIIpeak3 protein samples were analyzed by bottom-up mass spectrometry (n = 1 for each sample). This mass spectrometry workflow and data analysis pipeline are based on extensively validated protocols routinely applied to a wide range of biological samples and species. Protein samples were heated (95 °C, 5 min.) with lysis buffer (100 μL, 12 mM sodium lauroyl sarcosine, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 50 mM triethylammonium bicarbonate (TEAB)). The samples were treated with tris(2-carboxyethyl) phosphine (10 μL, 55 mM in 50 mM TEAB, 30 min, 37 °C) followed by treatment with chloroacetamide (10 μL, 120 mM in 50 mM TEAB, 30 min., 25 °C in the dark). They were then diluted 5-fold with aqueous 50 mM TEAB, and incubated overnight with Sequencing Grade Modified Trypsin (1 μg in 10 μL of 50 mM TEAB; Promega, Madison, WI), following which an equal volume of ethyl acetate/trifluoroacetic acid (TFA, 100/1, v/v) was added. After vigorous mixing (5 min) and centrifugation (13,000 x g, 5 min), the supernatants were discarded, and the lower phases were dried in a centrifugal vacuum concentrator.

The samples were then dissolved in acetonitrile/water/TFA (solvent A, 100 μL, 2/98/0.1, v/v/v) and loaded onto a small portion of a C18-silica disk (3 M, Maplewood, MN) placed in a 200 μL pipette tip. Prior to sample loading, the C18 disk was prepared by sequential treatment with methanol (20 μL), acetonitrile/water/TFA (solvent B, 20 μL, 80/20/0.1, v/v/v), and finally with solvent A (20 μL). After loading the sample, the disc was washed with solvent A (20 μL, eluent discarded) and eluted with solvent B (40 μL)67. The collected eluent was dried in a centrifugal vacuum concentrator. The eluants were reconstituted in water/acetonitrile/FA (solvent E, 10 μL, 98/2/0.1, v/v/v), and aliquots (5 μL) were injected onto a reverse phase nanobore HPLC column (AcuTech Scientific, C18, 1.8 μm particle size, 360 μm x 20 cm, 150 μm ID), equilibrated in solvent E and eluted (500 nL/min.) with an increasing concentration of solvent F (acetonitrile/water/FA, 98/2/0.1, v/v/v: min./% F; 0/0, 5/3, 18/7, 74/12, 144/24, 153/27, 162/40, 164/80, 174/80, 176/0, 180/0) using an Eksigent NanoLC-2D system (Sciex (Framingham, MA)).

The effluent from the column was directed to a nanospray ionization source connected to a high-resolution orbitrap mass spectrometer (Q Exactive Plus, Thermo Fisher Scientific) acquiring mass spectra in a data-dependent mode alternating between a full scan (m/z 350–1700, automated gain control (AGC) target 3 ×106, 50 ms maximum injection time, FWHM resolution 70,000 at m/z 200) and up to 10 MS/MS scans (quadrupole isolation of charge states ≥ 2, isolation width 1.2 Th) with previously optimized fragmentation conditions (normalized collision energy of 32, dynamic exclusion of 30 s, AGC target 1 ×105, 100 ms maximum injection time, FWHM resolution 35,000 at m/z 200).

Raw data files were converted from .raw to.mgf format using MSConvert version 3.0 and analyzed with Mascot Server 2.8.2 and Mascot Daemon 2.8.0. Spectra were searched against the Uni-Thermoelongatus 20221202 database together with a contaminants database (20160129). Trypsin was specified as the protease with up to two missed cleavages allowed. Carbamidomethylation of cysteine was set as a fixed modification, while oxidation (A, D, E, F, H, I, K, L, M, N, P, Q, R, S, T, V, W, Y) and dioxidation of methionine were included as variable modifications. Precursor and fragment ion mass tolerances were set to 10 ppm and 50 ppm, respectively. The minimum peptide length was set to eight amino acids, and peptide identifications with Mascot scores greater than 34 were considered. Statistical significance was assessed at p < 0.05.

Negative staining with electron microscopy

PSII samples were diluted to a protein concentration of 0.01–0.04 µg/µL (estimated from chlorophyll concentration). Samples were applied to 200-mesh copper grids, washed three times with pure water, and stained with 2.5% uranyl acetate for 1 min. Images were collected using an FEI Tecnai T12 Spirit transmission electron microscope.

Grid preparation

The sample, concentrated (using Amicon 100 kDa MWCO (Millipore) concentrators) to 0.3 mg/ml in 20 mM MES pH 6.5, 2.5 mM MgCl2, 2.5 mM CaCl2, 7 mM MgSO4, 0.03% DDM, 0.05% CHAPS, was frozen on Quantifoil 0.6/1, 300 mesh copper grids manually coated with a continuous layer of carbon approximately 1 nm thick68. The grids were glow-discharged for 5 s at 25 mA prior to freezing. The sample was prepared using a Vitrobot Mark IV, set to 4 °C, 100% humidity, 2 s blot time, and 25 blot force. 3 µl of sample was applied to each grid. The sample was manipulated under green light until frozen.

Data collection

The micrographs were collected on a 300 kV Titan Krios G3i, equipped with a Gatan K3 BioQuantum direct detector. Preliminary data collection indicated that, although showing good particle spread, the sample adopts preferential orientation on continuous carbon. Therefore, the high-resolution dataset was collected with the grid tilted 30 degrees, to mitigate the effects of preferential orientation. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 6, about 9173 movies were collected in 72 h, using the software Serial-EM for 48 h and EPU for 24 hours. The grid was aligned so that rows of holes would sit along the tilt axis: this allowed us to collect nine holes with beam-image shift while keeping the beam tilt below 10 mrad69. The data collection details are summarized in Supplementary Table 1.

Data processing

The processing outline is described in Supplementary Fig. 6. After optics group assignment, the raw micrographs were aligned using their own implementation of motion correction from Relion 3.170. CTFFIND71 was used for initial CTF estimation, then Relion 3.1 was used for particle picking, 2D and 3D classification, polishing, and CTF/3D refinements, except for per-particle defoci, which were estimated using GCTF72 upon particle picking, to immediately assign the correct defoci to each particle. This step was important, since CTFFIND only estimates per-micrograph defocus, not considering the defocus change resulting from the stage tilt. Initial picking was performed with LoG in Relion, resulting in 5.9 Mln particles, then subjected to 2D classification. 3.4 M good particles were selected, pooled, globally refined, then filtered based on LogLikelihoodContribution (up to 91300), Autopick Figure of Merit (FoM) (max 0.2), CTF FOM (min 0.1), NormCorrection (0.57-0.61) to remove particles featuring strong artefacts in the micelle area. The resulting 2.65 M particles were polished and CTF-refined, then separated by 3D classification without alignment (20 Å lowpass filter of reference map, T64 and 10 classes), to resolve the compositional heterogeneity (presence vs absence of the Psb O-U-V subunits). Pre-alignment of particles via global refinement and subsequent classification without alignment resulted in better separation than 3D classification with alignment. This strategy allowed us to isolate two good classes, without artefactual streaks: a class of 636,664 particles, featuring a mixture of active (Psb O-U-V present in both PSII monomers) and semi-active (Psb O-U-V present only in one PSII monomer) particles, and one class of 420178 inactive (Psb O-U-V absent) particles. The two classes were then further classified without alignment (20 Å lowpass filter of reference map, T64 and three classes) to fully separate the particles, and this led to the three final classes: active PSII (363811 particles, 2.4 Å), semi-active PSII (272,215 particles, 2.2 Å), and inactive PSII (417537 particles, 2.5 Å). Since the active and inactive maps were perfect dimers, the maps were also postprocessed with C2 symmetry to further improve resolution, yielding 2.2 Å maps. Post-processing and local resolution estimation were performed in Relion 3.1.

Model fitting

The crystal structure of T. vestitus monomeric PSII (PDB ID: 3KZI)73 and the cryo-EM structure of T. vestitus monomeric PSII lacking PsbJ and extrinsic subunits (PDB ID: 7NHO)22 were placed into the cryo-EM density of PSII( + OEC) and PSII(-OEC) within the structures of PSII dimers using phenix.dock_in_map74,75. The model of PsbJ from monomeric PSII73 was used as the initial model for PsbJ in the PSII(-OEC). Subsequently, the structures of PSII( + OEC) and PSII(-OEC) were corrected and adjusted in COOT to match cryo-EM density76. Next, the refinement was carried out using phenix.real_space_refine with cofactor restraints generated by phenix.elbow74,75. PSII( + OEC) and PSII(-OEC) were then merged to create three PSII dimer structures, which were further refined with COOT and phenix.real_space_refine. Residues in the structures were carefully inspected in COOT, and iterative rounds of manual refinement were performed using COOT and phenix.real_space_refine until no significant errors were present in COOT and MolProbity (http://molprobity.biochem.duke.edu/)77 validation reports. Density fitting and refinement statistics are shown in Supplementary Figs 7–10 and Supplementary Tables 2–4.

Structural analysis

Figures were prepared using ChimeraX. Root-mean-square deviations (rmsd) between two structures were calculated in ChimeraX based on the Cα atoms of the backbone, representing the structural differences across all residue pairs. The channel reaching the Mn cluster was calculated by MoleOnline (https://mole.upol.cz/). Electrostatic solvent accessible surface potentials were calculated using the PyMOL APBS electrostatics Plugin (https://apbs.readthedocs.io/en/latest/) with default template settings.
