Tooth rotational control with varying attachment configurations in clear aligners under compromised periodontal ligament support: a finite element study
Authors: Yuching Huang, HiuChing Wong, Pu Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWith the high prevalence of periodontitis, tooth rotation resulting from periodontal damage can be corrected through orthodontic treatment; however, the biomechanical complexity of tooth movement in compromised periodontal tissues, combined with the inherently low rotation accuracy of clear aligners, present significant challenges to tooth rotational control. This finite element study aimed to explore appropriate attachment configurations for tooth rotation under periodontally compromised conditions.MethodsBased on cone beam computed tomography data of a patient, three-dimensional finite element models of the upper dental arch, periodontal ligament (PDL), alveolar bone, aligners, and attachments were created. The right maxillary second premolar(A5) was targeted as the rotated tooth with a 30°clockwise rotation. Simulations were conducted with varying alveolar bone loss levels (0/2/4/6 mm) and attachment configurations (none, vertical/horizontal rectangular, and vertical elliptical). A 1° aligner activation angle was applied, and finite element analysis was performed for 16 conditions to assess tooth displacement, periodontal ligament stress, and aligner condition by Ansys Workbench.ResultsAll groups achieved counterclockwise rotation of the A5, but exhibited concomitant tipping and intrusion. PDL von Mises stress peaked in the lingual cervical region and exceeded apical values. The vertical ellipsoid attachment produced the greatest rotational displacement and highest cervical periodontal ligament stress. The horizontal rectangular attachment showed a more favorable center of rotation with comparatively large rotational displacement and cervical periodontal ligament stress. The vertical rectangular attachment yielded lower cervical periodontal ligament stress and reduced anchorage loss, but smaller rotational displacement and more pronounced undesired displacement. Increasing alveolar bone loss shifted the center of rotation and increased displacement, accompanied by greater undesired biomechanical effects, anchorage loss, and periodontal ligament stress.ConclusionsThe horizontal rectangular attachment showed a condition-specific mechanical trade-off within this framework; therefore, clinical extrapolation should be cautious and supported by patient-specific in vivo validation. Treatment planning for rotational movements should consider periodontal support, with close periodontal monitoring and biomechanically informed attachment selection to reduce unintended movements and periodontal loading.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08200-1.Keywords: Tooth rotation, Clear aligners, Finite element analysis, Attachments, Periodontal damage, Alveolar bone loss

Background

Periodontitis, a chronic inflammatory disease of the periodontal tissues, affects approximately 62% of adults and continues to rise in prevalence, making it a major public health concern [1]. As periodontal damage is further exacerbated, teeth may undergo inappropriate rotation and drifting, which can lead to occlusal trauma and mobility, severely compromising both function and esthetics [2–5]. Combined periodontal–orthodontic therapy has gained increasing importance in managing periodontal lesions [5, 6]. This integrative approach controls inflammation and promotes periodontal healing while employing orthodontic intervention to reduce occlusal trauma and correct malocclusion [7, 8]. However, alveolar bone loss in periodontitis shifts the center of tooth resistance, leading to uncontrolled tipping and unintended reciprocal movements during orthodontic tooth movement. Furthermore, changes in periodontal stress distribution and compromised anchorage stability introduce additional challenges and uncertainty into orthodontic tooth movement, while also influencing the overall health and biomechanical response of the periodontal tissues [7–9].

Although no definitive evidence or guidelines currently support clear aligners as superior to fixed appliances for periodontitis patients [10, 11], their removable design facilitates periodontal maintenance. Moreover, esthetics and comfort have contributed to their rising clinical use [12–14]. Clear aligners (CA), a series of transparent thermoformed plastic appliances that guide teeth toward their planned positions, have undergone substantial technological development, extending their application from simple malocclusions to complex periodontitis cases [15, 16]. Nevertheless, constrained by material properties, patient compliance, and the types of tooth movement, clear aligners often show discrepancies between predicted and achieved outcomes, sometimes necessitating refinement aligners or adjunctive fixed appliances to meet treatment objectives [17–19]. Rotation is considered as one of the most challenging movements in clear aligners therapy [17, 19–22]. Simon et al. found that the rotational accuracy of canines and premolars ranged from 29.1% to 49.7%, with the accuracy significantly decreasing for rotations greater than 15° or when each staging of movement was designed to be greater than 1.5° per aligner [23]. Castroflorio et al. found that applying a predicted rotation of 1-degree to the round tooth resulted in an actual rotation of only 0.4 degrees [24]. The lower accuracy in the rotation of canines and premolars can be attributed to the lack of interproximal undercuts and their anatomical morphology, which makes it difficult to maintain anchorage, leading to poor fit and slippage between aligner and teeth [21].

Vitro experiments offer direct measurement and analysis of the mechanics underlying rotational movements, but they cannot account for key clinical factors such as the periodontal ligament (PDL) and alveolar bone [25]. Clinical researches are readily affected by patient compliance and other simultaneous movements, because tooth movement comprises a combination of various movement types, preventing dedicated investigation of tooth rotational movement alone [22, 24]. Finite element analysis (FEA), a novel non-invasive and quantitative method in orthodontics, enables the simulation of periodontal conditions to evaluate stress distribution and displacement patterns during tooth movement, providing detailed biomechanical insights while reducing the need for unnecessary in vivo trials and ensuring reproducibility [26, 27].

Given the biomechanical complexity of tooth movement in compromised periodontal tissues and the low rotational accuracy of clear aligners, This study aimed to explore appropriate attachment selection for tooth rotation under periodontally compromised conditions. while to provide new insights into the effects of varying alveolar bone heights on rotational movement. Finite element analysis (FEA) was conducted to evaluate the influence of differing alveolar bone loss levels and attachment configurations on rotational tooth movement and the resultant PDL equivalent stress.

Methods

A healthy 19-year-old orthodontic patient with complete permanent dentition, no obvious crowding, and healthy periodontal and mucosal conditions was selected for this study. No temporomandibular joint (TMJ) or other pathologies were observed. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee (WCHSIRB-D-2025-339), and the patient signed an informed consent form. CBCT and intraoral scan data of patient were obtained and imported into Mimics (version 23.0; Materialise, Leuven, Belgium) to construct initial 3D models of the maxillary and dentition using “threshold selection” and “Edit Mask” functions. After smoothing and mirroring the model using Geomagic Studio 2021 (Geomagic, Morrisville, NC, USA) and SolidWorks 2023 (Dassault Systèmes, Concord, MA, USA), the right maxillary second premolar (A5) was designated as the target tooth, and a 30° clockwise (mesial) rotation was applied to generate the rotated model.

The periodontal ligament was modeled by uniformly offsetting the root surface by 0.25 mm. Maxillary cortical bone thickness was set to 2 mm, and the trabecular bone was generated by a 1 mm inward offset in Boolean operations [28, 29]. Attachments were designed in three types (Fig. 1): vertical rectangular (3 mm height, 2 mm width, and 1 mm thickness), horizontal rectangular (2 mm height, 3 mm width, and 1 mm thickness), and vertical ellipsoid (3 mm height, 2 mm width, and 1 mm thickness). These attachments were conventionally placed on the buccal middle third of A5 [27, 30]. Based on the dentition and attachments, an external offset aligner of 0.5 mm thickness was constructed according to the repeated measurements. No manufacturer-specific or proprietary aligner system was involved. All finalized models of the maxillary, dentition, PDL, attachment, and aligner were imported into SolidWorks for assembly.

Horizontal rectangular attachment; VE: Vertical ellipsoid attachment

In this study, four primary groups were established based on different alveolar bone heights. Alveolar bone heights were uniformly decreased in reference to the current I-III stage periodontal disease standards [3, 31]. Using SolidWorks 2023 software, the alveolar bone height models were manually modified and filled to achieve a smooth and uniform contour, resulting in models with alveolar bone resorption heights of 0, 2, 4, and 6 mm [32, 33]. Then all components were assembled and imported into Ansys Workbench 2023 (Ansys, Pennsylvania, USA) to generate a tetrahedral-element 3D finite element model comprising the maxillary dentition, maxillary, PDL, attachment, and aligner(Figure 2). On these models, four attachment configurations (no attachment, vertical rectangular attachment, horizontal rectangular attachment, and vertical ellipsoid attachment) were separately applied in different alveolar bone resorption height(0,2,4,6 mm), resulting in sixteen experimental conditions.

Finite element models. A Different components of the model and the coordinate system; B Grouping by different alveolar bone resorption heights

Material properties (Table 1) were obtained from the literatures [30, 33–36], with teeth, maxillary, attachments, and aligners modeled as linear elastic materials and without differentiating internal tooth or bone structures. As prior research indicates that linear and nonlinear PDL setting have no significant effect on the long-term tooth movement [37], therefore, the periodontal ligament was modeled as a linear elastic, homogeneous, and isotropic material to enhance numerical stability and computational robustness, while maintaining methodological consistency with the prevailing practice in orthodontic finite element research. Furthermore, as the present simulation was confined to the initial stage of aligner activation and thus reflects a small-displacement, instantaneous mechanical response, this constitutive simplification was considered acceptable for the intended purpose of comparative evaluation across attachment configurations under identical boundary conditions [38, 39]. The model was discretized through meshing, and the number of nodes and elements for each component are provided in Table S1. To verify mesh independence, A mesh convergence analysis was performed for the baseline finite element model using seven progressively refined meshes. Peak von Mises stress was extracted and compared across mesh refinements, with convergence defined as a < 5% difference between successive refinements. The difference between the 1.0 mm and 0.5 mm meshes was 1.87%, meeting the convergence criterion.

Material properties

The upper half of the maxillary was designated as a constrained boundary region to prevent any movement, ensuring that the maxillary remained fixed when subjected to forces. Bonding contact conditions were applied between the trabecular bone and cortical bone, the inner surface of the PDL and the teeth, the outer surface of the PDL and the alveolar bone, and between the teeth and attachments. This contact configuration didn’t allow any relative motion between the contact surfaces. Additionally, slight frictionless sliding was permitted between adjacent teeth along the contact surfaces. The aligner surfaces were set for face-to-face contact with the teeth and attachments, with a Coulomb friction coefficient of µ = 0.2 [34]. The same friction coefficient was used in all groups to ensure intergroup comparability. Given the variability of intraoral lubrication, this parameter was not intended to reproduce patient-specific friction but to standardize contact behavior across groups. The contact between the teeth and aligner surfaces was considered tightly coupled, with nodes on the contact surfaces matched one-to-one.

In clear aligner systems, tooth movements are typically set to a staging of 2° rotation [16]. Studies suggested that a staging of 1–1.5.5° rotation is more appropriate [23, 40], with 1.2° considered as the average activation angle [25, 35]. Given the principle of applying light forces in orthodontic treatment for periodontitis patients, a staging of 1° rotation was selected [12, 16]. In the original tooth model, A5 as the target rotation tooth was set to undergo a 30° clockwise rotation. The aligner model was constructed with a 29° clockwise rotation of A5 as the target position, without any displacement of the other teeth. Finite element simulations were performed under static loading conditions, applying the loading force by the mismatch between the original dentition model and the aligner in the target position.

This study employed both global and local coordinate systems (Fig. 2). The global coordinate system defined the directions of the x, y, and z axes, with the x-axis representing the coronal plane (+ Left, −Right), the y-axis representing the sagittal plane (+ After, −Forward), and the z-axis representing the vertical plane (+ UP, −DOWN).For the maxillary right side teeth, a local coordinate system was defined with the origin at the incisal edge midpoint and cusp of canine or the central fossa of posterior teeth. The x-axis was set as (+ mesial, −distal), the y-axis as (+ lingual, −buccal), and the z-axis as (+ gingival, −incisal). Crown displacement was measured at the incisal center or canine cusp for anterior teeth and at the occlusal surface center for posterior teeth. Root displacement was assessed using the apical apex point as the reference.

Results

A5 tooth movement pattern

The maximum displacement occurred at A5 (Fig. 3), whereas the minimum displacement was consistently located in the root region (Table S2). When the crown minimum displacement lay near the crown center, rotation was considered more ideal and this region was considered as the “center of rotation”. Color maps showed the crown minimum displacement distribution (Fig. 4). With no bone loss, this region was near the crown center in the HR and VE groups, but closer to the buccal cusp in the no-attachment group and near the mesial marginal ridge in the VR group. As alveolar bone loss increased, the crown minimum displacement region shifted progressively mesially, away from the crown center. VE produced the greatest A5 total displacement and rotation angle, followed by HR and VR, while no attachment yielded the smallest values. Both outcomes increased with increasing alveolar bone loss (Table 2; Table S2).

Anchorage teeth movement patterns

All groups showed anchorage loss, more pronounced on the right side (Fig. 3). A4 exhibited configuration-dependent movement: in the no-attachment and VR groups, its clockwise rotational tendency decreased with increasing bone loss and was characterized by lingual crown tipping with relative extrusion, whereas HR and VE showed mesio–lingual crown tipping with relative intrusion. The right maxillary molars consistently demonstrated disto-buccal crown tipping with relative intrusion, and their total displacement followed the same intergroup trend as A5. For the anterior teeth, the right central incisor showed mesial crown tipping, whereas the right lateral incisor and canine showed lingual crown tipping.

Focusing on the right maxillary first premolar (A4) and first molar (A6), which constitute the primary posterior anchorage unit adjacent to the activated tooth, the maximum total displacement increased with increasing alveolar bone loss across all configurations (Table S3). For A4, the displacement range increased from 0.00987 to 0.02450 mm at 0 mm bone loss to 0.01502–0.03686 mm at 6 mm bone loss, with larger values in the attachment groups, particularly VE and HR. For A6, the displacement range increased from 0.00774 to 0.01478 mm at 0 mm bone loss to 0.01436–0.02108 mm at 6 mm bone loss, consistently following the pattern VE > HR > VR > no attachment at each bone-loss level (6 mm: 0.02108, 0.01977, 0.01647, and 0.01436 mm, respectively).

A5 periodontal ligament von mises stress

In the no-attachment and VR groups, PDL von Mises stress was predominantly concentrated in the lingual cervical region, whereas HR and VE showed elevated stress on both buccal and lingual cervical aspects of A5 (Fig. 6). Across all groups, the peak stress occurred in the lingual cervical region, and the mean equivalent stress in the cervical third exceeded that in the apical third (Table 3). At each bone level, local mean PDL stress ranked VE highest and no attachment lowest, consistent with the A5 maximum displacement trend. PDL equivalent stress increased with increasing alveolar bone loss within each attachment configuration.

Discussion

Tooth rotation is characterized by the displacement of the tooth along its long axis, leading to deviation from the normal arch position [41]. It may contribute to traumatic occlusion and plaque retention, potentially aggravating periodontal breakdown [42]. The relapse of rotated teeth after orthodontic treatment is linked to insufficient reorganization of periodontal fibers [43], making rotational movements inherently challenging. Despite improved predictability of clear aligner (CA) therapy with advances in materials and biomechanics [20], rotation remains among the least accurate movements, particularly in rounder teeth such as canines and premolars [44]. Proposed strategies (attachments, IPR, overcorrection, and auxiliaries) show variable effectiveness, with generalizability limited by small cohorts, heterogeneous tooth positions and activation magnitudes, material differences, patient compliance, and insufficient evaluation of unintended effects [22–24, 45–49].

Comprehensive evidence on rotational control remains limited. Three-dimensional finite element analysis (FEA) enables quantitative in vitro simulation of rotational mechanics, including displacement, center of rotation, periodontal stress, and associated undesired biomechanical effects and anchorage responses [26, 27, 36, 50, 51]. Cortona A et al. reported that effective mandibular premolar rotation could be achieved with a single attachment and 1.2° staging; but only vertical rectangular attachment was evaluated, without comparing other attachment types [35]. Sultanoğlu E et al. suggested that buccal horizontal attachments may be advantageous for mandibular premolar rotation, but did not quantify rotational undesired biomechanical effects or anchorage behavior [30]. Although maxillary premolar rotation is generally more accurate than mandibular [21, 22, 24], its lower accuracy should not be overlooked [45, 47], and the transferability of mandibular findings to the maxilla is uncertain. Accordingly, this FEA study investigated how alveolar bone height and attachment configuration influence maxillary premolar rotation, aiming to provide reproducible mechanistic comparisons under standardized periodontal-support conditions.

This study indicated that attachment groups produce greater total displacement and rotational angles than no attachment groups, consistent with previous findings [25, 30, 35, 40, 52]. VE and HR produced larger rotational displacement/angles (VE > HR) and, under no bone loss, exhibited crown minimum displacement regions closer to the crown center, indicating more favorable rotational control than VR. This aligns with previous evidence suggesting that smaller attachment improve force transmission and enhance rotational efficacy, giving ellipsoid attachments an advantage over rectangular ones [53]. Elkholy et al. also reported that vertical ellipsoid attachments increase rotational moments in mandibular canines, potentially enhancing the efficiency of rotation [52]. By contrast, Sultanoğlu et al. reported greater efficacy for HR, but their larger rotational activation (above the commonly recommended 1–1.5° per stage) may have compromised aligner fit and increased deformation, influencing the predicted displacement [30]. Clinical observations also suggest that VE attachments may be limited by fit and compliance factors in vivo [46]. Consequently, given that the mechanical forces applied in clinical clear aligners treatments differ from the instantaneous forces used in this study, the positive rotational effects observed with vertical ellipsoid attachments should be considered within the context of certain limitations.

Biomechanist Proffit have noted that pure rotation without tipping is biomechanically unattainable [19], therefore, rotational control should be evaluated not only by rotational expression but also by undesired biomechanical effects. Researches have shown that the primary lateral force component applied during the rotation of round teeth is the intrusion force [35, 52], consistent with the “watermelon seed effect” [54]. Simon et al. demonstrated that attachments increase rotational forces and moments while reducing the tendency for premolar intrusion [25]. In agreement, the no-attachment group in our model exhibited the greatest intrusion and the smallest rotational displacement, whereas attachment groups reduced intrusion and enhanced rotational expression, in line with prior observations that inadequate rotational control tends to be accompanied by intrusion [35, 55, 56]. To summarize overall undesired biomechanical effects, we quantified total displacement at the crown center and root apex; VR showed the greatest magnitude, in accordance with Ferlias et al. [40]. Notably, undesired displacements were small (≈ 10⁻² mm) with subtle intergroup differences (Fig. 5). Therefore, even if statistically detectable in the model, their clinical significance is likely limited; these values are best regarded as comparative biomechanical indices within the standardized framework rather than directly actionable clinical effects.

Our results of anchorage teeth mostly align with the findings of Cortona A et al. [35], although A4 showed a different response. With increasing alveolar bone loss, A4 exhibited reduced clockwise rotation and predominantly lingual crown tipping, whereas Cortona et al. reported lingual movement of A4 with VR attachments. This discrepancy may relate to differences in PDL material assumptions, rotation direction, and staging protocols [22]. Notably, VR had limited influence on anchorage-tooth displacement, suggesting comparatively better anchorage preservation in this model. Anchorage loss during rotational movements is inevitable, highlighting the clinical need to control lingual tipping of anterior teeth, buccal tipping with relative intrusion of molars, and to prevent anchorage loss of adjacent premolars.

The von Mises (VM) stress is recommended for quantitative assessment of PDL stress in damaged periodontal tissues [38, 57]. In the present simulations, the A5 PDL VM stress pattern broadly aligned with prior finite element observations [38, 57–60], with stress concentrating predominantly in the lingual cervical region and increasing as alveolar bone support decreased. Notably, the apical one-third exhibited lower average VM stress than the cervical one-third and, under a 1° activation step, remained numerically within the reported MHP reference interval (0.002–0.016 MPa), which may suggest a comparatively limited apical loading tendency under this standardized small-displacement input. cervical VM stress in the no-attachment and vertical rectangular setups was numerically within the MHP interval, whereas the horizontal rectangular and vertical ellipsoid groups under reduced bone support showed values closer to the upper boundary of the MTS interval (0.015–0.026 MPa), with the vertical ellipsoid group extending beyond this boundary, which may indicate a relatively higher cervical load concentration in these setups.

Importantly, VM stress in FEA should be interpreted as a biomechanical indicator of load concentration, whereas MHP and MTS should be regarded as biological reference outcomes influenced by tissue adaptation and loading duration, rather than strict mechanical thresholds within model stimulation [9, 61]. Thus, numerical proximity to or exceedance of these intervals may reflect comparatively greater instantaneous mechanical exposure rather than definitive evidence of tissue injury. This caution is particularly warranted because, in the present alveolar bone-loss model, the PDL was modeled as linear elastic, without nonlinear, viscoelastic, or time-dependent behavior, which may bias the estimated instantaneous peak stress magnitudes. Moreover, the simulations capture immediate mechanical responses rather than biologically regulated in vivo conditions [26]. Consistent with this perspective, Hohmann et al. reported less pronounced cervical resorption clinically than predicted by FEA, possibly due to dentoclast sensitivity differences and vascular modulation [62]. Collectively, these considerations indicate that the present results should be interpreted primarily as relative biomechanical comparisons among attachment configurations, and they underscore the need for further experimental and in vivo validation to enhance clinical interpretability.

Our simulations showed that, in the no-attachment and VR groups, aligner equivalent stress concentrated at the A4–A5 junction, a potential fatigue-prone region that may reduce force delivery and warrants reinforcement [17]. In the other two groups, stress localized around the A5 attachment, potentially deforming the aligner, compromising its fit, and diminishing movement efficiency. With increasing alveolar bone resorption, aligners stress decreased across all attachment groups except the vertical ellipsoid configuration, likely because greater A5 displacement brings the tooth closer to the intended position of aligner, thereby reducing stress. Whereas the rounded VE geometry concentrated stress more focally, leading to a stress increase. Stress magnitude also depended on contact conditions: at the same bone level, HR and VE produced greater A5 displacement with smaller contact areas, yielding higher stresses than the other groups. Deformation patterns further showed pronounced buccal deformation at A4 in the no-attachment and VE groups, which may support the use of a buccal attachment on A4 in these scenarios.

Overall, although the vertical ellipsoid attachment produced the greatest rotational displacement, it was accompanied by increased anchorage loss and cervical PDL stresses exceeding the MTS reference ranges, indicating a less favorable stress levels profile. The vertical rectangular attachment demonstrated comparatively lower PDL stress and improved anchorage preservation; however, its rotational efficiency was limited and associated with pronounced undesired biomechanical effects. The horizontal rectangular attachment demonstrated a relatively favorable biomechanical profile, with improved rotational expression, acceptable anchorage control, and reduced aligner deformation. However, this apparent advantage was accompanied by elevated cervical PDL stress approaching the upper bound of the MTS range. Given the modeling assumptions and biological constraints, stresses exceeding the MTS range should be regarded as reflecting a comparatively higher level of mechanical loading, rather than being taken as conclusive evidence of tissue injury. Therefore, the HR attachment is best viewed as a relative mechanical compromise within the present framework, not as a guarantee of biological safety.

From a clinical perspective, attachment selection should be individualized according to periodontal support. In mild periodontal compromise, greater rotational efficacy may be prioritized. The vertical ellipsoid attachment may improve rotational expression, but its elevated cervical stress warrants caution as periodontal support decreases. Within the modeled conditions, the horizontal rectangular attachment may provide a more favorable compromise between rotational efficiency and mechanical stress exposure. In advanced periodontal loss, treatment planning should emphasize stress mitigation, anchorage preservation, and close monitoring over maximal rotational expression; accordingly, the vertical rectangular (VR) attachment may be preferentially considered when these priorities outweigh the pursuit of peak rotational efficacy. Importantly, any “optimal” designation should remain hypothesis-generating until supported by calibrated experimental benchmarks and outcome-based in vivo validation.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. To ensure numerical stability and intergroup comparability, the model adopted idealized assumptions (linear-elastic PDL, uniform alveolar bone loss, and homogeneous rigid attachments), which do not capture the nonlinear, time-dependent behavior of periodontal tissues, the heterogeneity of clinical defects, or attachment wear/partial debonding under cyclic loading that may alter aligner–attachment contact and force transmission [36, 50, 51, 63]. In addition, only a single attachment was placed on the target tooth; clinically, multiple teeth move concurrently with coordinated attachment schemes, and rotational predictability is further influenced by material properties, aligner thickness, and staging protocols [55, 64–66]. The simulations assessed initial displacement and instantaneous stress under a static framework and therefore cannot represent long-term tooth movement, biologically mediated remodeling, or disease-related adaptation [26]. External validation or benchmarking against in vivo or experimental data was not performed; thus, the 1° activation should be regarded as a standardized small-displacement input for relative comparisons rather than a calibrated predictor of absolute clinical force levels. Finally, the use of a single patient-derived anatomy limits generalizability given inter-individual variability in maxillary second premolar morphology. Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted as comparative mechanistic insights within a controlled framework rather than deterministic predictions of biological risk or clinical outcomes, and the comparatively favorable balance observed for HR should be considered hypothesis-generating pending confirmation across diverse anatomies and defect patterns with experimental calibration and outcome-based in vivo validation.

Conclusions

Within the limitations of this study, Clear aligner–driven rotation was consistently accompanied by undesired mechanical effects, primarily intrusion and tipping, and diminished rotational control was associated with greater intrusive displacement. Among the tested configurations, the horizontal rectangular attachment represents a condition-specific mechanical trade-off within this modeling framework rather than an endorsement of biological safety; thus, clinical extrapolation should be cautious and supported by further patient-specific, biologically informed validation. Under the modeled symmetrical alveolar bone-loss condition, progressive periodontal support reduction increased displacement, shifted the center of rotation, amplified undesired biomechanical effects and posterior anchorage loss, and elevated PDL stress, indicating general biomechanical trends rather than patient-specific predictions. Clinically, treatment planning for rotational movements should account for periodontal support, with close periodontal monitoring and biomechanically informed attachment selection to mitigate unintended tooth movement and limit periodontal loading.
