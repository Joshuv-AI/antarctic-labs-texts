99mTc MAA SPECT/CT pre-treatment based surrogate dosimetry and hepatotoxicity in patients following 90Y radioembolization: association of non-tumor liver dose and clinical markers of radioembolization induced liver disease (REILD)
Authors: Ali Bassir, Casey Dang, Kenneth Chiu, Sravani Gampala, Taylor Loon, Abhijit Salaskar, Katsuhiro Kobayashi, Andrzej Krol, David J Lubin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRadiation induced liver damage can occur in healthy non-tumor liver tissue as a complication of radioembolization. In this study, we investigated the application of personalized dosimetry software with the surrogate radiotracer 99mTechnetium (99mTc) macroaggregated albumin (MAA) to maximize the radiation dose to hepatic malignant lesions while minimizing the dose to non-tumor liver. We correlated clinical outcomes with dosimetry findings to elucidate important factors in optimizing patient outcomes.MethodsIn this retrospective study, we obtained clinical follow-up in 43 patients who received radioembolization with 90Yttrium (90Y) microspheres as treatment for primary and metastatic hepatic malignancies and who had mapping with 99mTc MAA by single photon emission computed tomography. We investigated the predictive accuracy of voxel-based dosimetry from pre-treatment mapping with respect to clinical outcomes following 90Y treatment. Specifically, we analyzed correlations between pre-treatment mapping dosimetry and the incidence of radioembolization induced liver disease (REILD), a known complication from radioembolization with 90Y microspheres. Outcomes of reduced hepatic function—such as inadequate albumin production, hyperbilirubinemia, elevated international normalized ratio (INR), and new medically uncontrolled ascites were used as quantitative clinical markers of REILD.ResultsNon-tumor liver radiation dose was correlated with REILD, odds ratio (OR) =3.446 (P=0.020). Interestingly, a history of prior hepatic chemotherapy was associated with a decreased risk, OR =0.099 (P=0.046). History of cirrhosis was associated with REILD; however, this was not significant, OR of 3.495 with (P=0.156). Differences, however, between predicted and delivered absorbed radiation doses did not significantly predict the occurrence of REILD, OR =0.198 (P=0.169). Type of microsphere was also not significantly associated with REILD, OR =0.111 (P=0.079).ConclusionsThe findings of this study support the use of 99mTc MAA pre-treatment mapping as a valuable tool to predict the absorbed dose in normal liver tissue from 90Y radioembolization. This approach thus allows for a more personalized and calculated treatment plan.Keywords: 90Yttrium (90Y), macroaggregated albumin (MAA), voxel, dosimetry, radioembolization induced liver disease (REILD)

Introduction

Intravascular delivery of 90Yttrium (90Y) microspheres has been shown to be effective in treating both primary hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) (1) and metastases from other primary malignancies (e.g., colorectal) (2). By adjusting the administered activity of 90Y and delivering it to the tumor perfusion bed by intraarterial catheter, a patient-specific prescribed radiation dose can be given in each case. To predict the absorbed radiation dose to the tumor as well as to adjacent normal liver, lungs and other critical nontarget organs and to prescribe the correct dose of 90Y microspheres, a common practice is to use 99mTechnetium (99mTc) macroaggregated albumin (MAA) particles as a stand-in or “surrogate” for 90Y microspheres (2). Combining SPECT/CT with the 99mTc MAA mapping increases the sensitivity to evaluate for extrahepatic shunting and has been shown to reduce post-embolization complications significantly (3).

The purpose of this study was to assess the utility of 99mTc MAA mapping with dosimetry to predict radioembolization induced liver disease (REILD), a complication from transarterial radioembolization (TARE). Post-radioembolization liver dysfunction refers to the decline in the liver’s normal or previously established functional ability to synthesize albumin and coagulation factors, as well as decreased excretion of bilirubin following TARE. REILD is characterized by new onset or worsening of jaundice, ascites, elevated bilirubin levels (hyperbilirubinemia, bilirubin >1.2 mg/dL), and reduced albumin levels (hypoalbuminemia, <3.0 g/dL) using the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (NCI-CTCAE) v5.0 (www.cancer.gov/research/resources) (4), symptoms typically emerge between 2 weeks and 4 months post-radioembolization, provided there is no tumor growth or invasion into the biliary system (5). During TARE dose planning, accurate dosimetry is essential in order to prevent REILD (6). Three basic models have been historically utilized for dosimetry: body surface area (BSA), medical internal radiation dose (MIRD) and the partition dosimetry model (7). The BSA method is based on empiric models, leading to prescribing 90Y activity based on tumor burden, with the size of the liver assumed to follow the total BSA. We note that this assumption breaks down at the extremes of liver size and ignores the difference of tumor-to-nontumor absorbed doses (8).

The MIRD model takes into consideration nontumor tissues, however it assumes that the distribution of the radiopharmaceutical is uniform and unchanging within each tissue compartment. Thus, it is used to estimate the amount of 90Y activity which can be delivered into the tumor perfusion bed such that a given target absorbed radiation dose can be delivered to the tumor tissue compartment. Limitations of this model stem from the assumption of a uniform radiation dose within the tissue compartments. The MIRD model can be inadequate in cases of heterogeneous tumor volume which contains regions of normal as well as tumor tissue (6). To address these limitations, the commonly used partition model can be utilized (7,9). This model allows for differences in the absorbed radiation dose received by the tumor as well as that by the nontumor liver (10). One of the clinically important assumptions of the partition model is that the tumor and nontumor compartments both have their own homogenous distributions of 90Y activity and thus there is a linear relationship of the absorbed dose to the number of decays detected by imaging within each tissue compartment and thus it is possible to estimate what the activity deposited in, and hence what the absorbed dose will be, for each compartment.

If the expected ratio of the dose to the tumor to that of nontumor liver can be estimated, then the administered activity of 90Y can be more accurately prescribed to optimize treatment for each patient. To obtain this ratio for each patient, the appropriate dosimetry model in each case must be chosen. While inclusion of more tissue compartments to the model improves the accuracy of radiation dose prediction, it comes at the expense of increased model complexity. In this study, we used a multicompartment partition model to estimate the absorbed dose imparted to tissues by 90Y microspheres in cases where the tumor was multifocal (on cross-sectional imaging), whereas a MIRD-based model approach was used for tumors which were confined to be within a single hepatic segment (11). We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2025-1242/rc).

Methods

This single-center retrospective study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Internal Institutional Review Board of University Hospital, SUNY Upstate (No. 2113497), and individual consent for this retrospective analysis was waived. All patients in this study were from University Hospital, SUNY Upstate. The data collection adhered to the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) of 1996 guidelines. Detailed records of the clinical data, treatment process, imaging data, and dosimetry calculations from both the conventional model and dosimetry prediction software were maintained for comprehensive evaluation and comparison.

Patient selection

Forty-three patients who had been diagnosed with either primary HCC or had metastases to the liver from another primary malignancy and underwent percutaneous TARE with 90Y microspheres from September 2019 to June 2023 were included in this study (Table 1). Inclusion criteria included those patients who had pre-treatment 99mTc MAA SPECT/CT mapping performed less than 1 month prior to TARE. Patients were also selected if they had at least 6 months of clinical follow-up. Prior to treatment, laboratory liver function tests and other relevant clinical data were also obtained, as per our institution’s treatment protocol.

TARE technique

TARE was performed according to the protocol at our institution for treating unresectable malignant hepatic lesions. 90Y microspheres, SIR-Spheres (Sirtex Medical Limited, Sydney, Australia) or TheraSphere® (MDS Nordion, Ottawa, Canada) were prepared as per the manufacturer’s guidelines and administered via catheter under fluoroscopic guidance by the interventional radiologists. Follow-up imaging by MRI or CT was performed within 1–6 months post-procedure to evaluate the treatment response.

Pre-treatment imaging and dosimetry

99mTc MAA scintigraphy: each patient in our study underwent pre-treatment mapping by 99mTc MAA scintigraphy. This involved the selective intra-arterial injection of 99mTc MAA into the appropriate hepatic artery using the same technique as was subsequently used for delivery of 90Y microspheres during TARE. The 99mTc MAA SPECT/CT pre-treatment mapping imaging protocol was in accordance with national guidelines as well as those at our institution and was performed across all patients to ensure consistent and accurate data collection (3).

Following a hepatic arteriogram and identification of the tumor vascular bed in the Interventional Radiology suite, 10 millicurie (mCi) of 99mTc MAA was delivered via intra-arterial catheter to the tumor perfusion volume and subsequently both planar imaging and a SPECT/CT scan was performed for mapping and liver-lung shunt fraction assessment on a Siemens Symbia Intevo SPECT/CT settings: low energy high resolution (LEHR) collimator with 15 s per view, 64 views per detector. Planar imaging settings: 128×128 size matrix, 5 min/view. CT settings: CVp 130 kV, 60 mAs, 5.0 mm slice thickness (3). The acquired image data sets were subsequently sent to the picture archiving and communication system (PACS).

Dosimetry with Velocity artificial intelligence (AI) using Dose-Navigator

A retrospective analysis of 99mTc MAA SPECT/CT pre-treatment mapping scans was performed using Velocity AI V4.1 (Varian Medical Systems, Palo Alto, CA, USA). The Dose-Navigator feature of the software was utilized for dosimetry calculations using the Acuros Molecular Radiotherapy solving grid-based linear Boltzmann transport equations for radioisotope emission and radiation transport in tissues (Figure S1) (12).

Additionally, the software allows for the overlay of SPECT images with anatomical CT scans to give the distribution of 99mTc MAA localization in relation to hepatic structures and the tumor perfusion bed contours. Using a freehand drawing technique, we contoured the liver and tumor perfusion bed volumes (from the 99mTc MAA SPECT activity). Using the actual administered 90Y activity as input with the 99mTc MAA SPECT images and the Dose-Navigator feature in Velocity, we estimated the tumor perfusion bed dose and compared it to the non-tumor liver dose which was also obtained. The tumor to normal liver absorbed dose ratio (T/N), a clinically meaningful metric, often estimated based on a model, could now be estimated from actual patient-specific SPECT/CT imaging.

Using the dose operation navigator feature and the RapidSphere Tradeoff Explorer software feature from Velocity, the 99mTc MAA SPECT/CT estimated dosimetry could be calculated. Both CT and emission tomography images were analyzed for any misregistration. The tumor lesion (denoted by conspicuous 99mTc MAA activity) and the non-tumor liver (using anatomic delineation by CT) were labelled and designated by a rolling sphere method by using the threshold feature with differently colored roller-volume of interest (VOI) regions for both. Using the physical constants for 90Y of a T½ =64.08 h, and a decay constant = 0.0108 h−1. We compared the absorbed radiation dose estimated using Velocity with 99mTc MAA to the actual administered activity of 90Y to the tumor perfusion bed (assuming it received >95% of the administered 90Y activity), and subsequently, a dose volume histogram (DVH) could be calculated from the software (13).

Evaluation criteria of liver toxicity

Follow-up clinical laboratory data were obtained from the electronic medical record from 4 weeks to 6 months post-treatment to assess for REILD in patients post-radioembolization. REILD was evaluated in patients post-TARE according to the definition by Braat et al. (14). Additionally, we employed the NCI-CTCAE v5.0 definition of REILD.

Statistical analysis

We initially ran each of the clinical and imaging data variables to see if they were independently associated with the outcome of REILD, however none were independently significantly associated (using χ2 analyses, data not shown), we then proceeded to run a multivariate logistical regression. We ran linear regression analysis with the continuous variables and multiple logistic regression analysis for the categorical variables. We included the following variables for the clinical outcome of REILD: massive ascites, total bilirubin, albumin, and international normalized ratio (INR). We tested these for multicollinearity using the clinical parameters as predictors. Descriptive statistics were collected for all notable variables. Continuous variables were described as mean ± standard deviation (SD), with median and range reported. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Normality of continuous data was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. A Hosmer-Lemeshow test was utilized to assess the goodness of fit of the model. Finally, a Bland-Altman analysis was used to estimate any bias and evaluate the agreement between the predicted (by 99mTc MAA) absorbed tumor and nontumor liver dose with that to administered 90Y activity. In all cases, a P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism V.10 (GraphPad, San Diego, CA, USA) (Figure 1).

We used RStudio version 4.4.1 to run an ordinary least squares regression as well as a multivariable logistic regression using massive ascites, total bilirubin, albumin, and INR as outcomes. Multicollinearity was tested with each of the chosen predictors. Additionally, we wanted to test all the clinical variables together to determine which clinical variables were associated with REILD, to this end, we performed a random forest classifier model analysis (Python 3.11.11). We ran the model over many iterations and were able to obtain a mean value for feature importance for each of the clinical variables of REILD (hyperbilirubinemia, hypoalbuminemia, and elevated INR).

Results

This study included 43 patients (male =79%, female =21%) with a mean age of 68.9±10.5 years. The most common hepatic malignancy in this cohort presenting for treatment by TARE was HCC (51.2%). A history of cirrhosis was seen in 37.2% of patients, and 53.4% of patients had a history of prior transarterial chemoembolization (TACE)/chemotherapy. Demographic and clinical data of patients are presented in Table 1. We assessed for development of REILD using the clinical variables outlined above [defined by Braat et al. (14)]. We found that massive ascites refractory to medical therapy was seen in 10 patients (23.3%) within 6 months of TARE. Elevated total bilirubin was seen in 14 patients (32.6%) and hypoalbuminemia developed in 14 patients (32.6%) (Table 1).

Multivariable linear regression analysis was used to evaluate the impact of multiple clinical factors on the development of REILD following 90Y radioembolization. We found that the non-tumor liver absorbed dose predicted by 99mTc MAA SPECT/CT was a significant factor. For each incremental increase in the predicted radiation dose, there was a corresponding increase in the likelihood of REILD occurrence, as evidenced by an odds ratio (OR) of 3.446 (P=0.02) in Table 2. A history of prior chemotherapy or TACE actually showed a decreased risk association with massive ascites, with an OR of 0.099, which was statistically significant, P=0.046 (Table 2). The presence of cirrhosis was associated with an elevated risk of massive ascites, however this was not statistically significant, indicated by an OR of 3.495 [95% confidence interval (CI): 0.648, 22.298] with P=0.156 (15). Interestingly, relative to the predicted dose using 99mTc MAA SPECT/CT, the relative differences between predicted and actual delivered doses (from 90Y) were not significantly associated with the occurrence of REILD (P=0.169, with an OR of 0.198). For the other clinical parameters of REILD (hypoalbumin, Table 3; INR, Table 4; hyperalbuminemia, Table 5), we did not find any statistically significant association with the clinical variables of our cohort. The model’s overall fit was confirmed by the Hosmer-Lemeshow test (P=0.72), suggesting an adequate alignment of predicted probabilities with the observed frequencies. The Nagelkerke R-squared value of 0.448 highlighted a moderate explanatory power of the model.

Using both categorical and continuous data in our random forest classifier model, we found that the relative difference of the dose from the tumor perfusion bed predicted by 99mTc MAA to that of the actual delivered 90Y dose showed the greatest feature importance (feature importance frequency of 0.122 with massive ascites, 0.19 with albumin, 0.34 with elevated INR and 0.38 with hyperbilirubinemia). The predicted nontumor liver dose also showed a higher frequency of importance with respect to the outcome of REILD (0.24 with massive ascites, 0.19 with albumin, 0.15 with elevated INR and 0.16 with hyperbilirubinemia) (Table 6). These clinical feature frequencies were both almost a factor of 2 higher than the next most frequent clinical feature variable in our Random Forest Classifier Model. Similarly, from the multivariate linear regression analysis, we found that the non-tumor liver dose was associated with massive ascites (OR =3.446, P=0.020) (Table 2) but that the relative difference between the predicted dose and delivered dose was not significantly associated with REILD (OR =0.198, P=0.169) (Tables 2-5).

A Bland-Altman analysis compared the predicted tumor perfusion bed dose from 99mTc MAA SPECT/CT surrogate dose calculated by dosimetry software to the actual administered 90Y activity. This demonstrated a mean difference of 21.6 Gy, indicating that the software dosimetry program tended to slightly overestimate the absorbed dose. Most of the data were within 1.96 SD from the mean (within 95%), which ranged from −111.4 Gy to +154.7 Gy, reflecting the wide variability across patients (Figure 1). Since most of the data fell within these limits, and no proportional bias was observed, this suggests that prediction error was not dependent on dose magnitude. These broad limits of agreement, however, highlight the inherent uncertainty in some cases of surrogate-based dosimetry, and in particular, the outliers in our data set which were beyond the ±1.96 SD limits were cases with multifocal or highly heterogeneous tumor bed perfusion volumes. These findings suggest that while MAA-based predictive dosimetry provides useful estimates, caution is warranted when applying this method in complex clinical scenarios.

Discussion

The present study highlights the importance of pre-treatment mapping for 90Y radioembolization to diminish the risk of REILD. Our findings agree with the results of Sangro et al. (1) and Riveira-Martin et al. (16), who also reported a direct correlation between non-tumor liver radiation dose and subsequent post-TARE hepatotoxicity. In our study, surrogate dosimetry by 99mTc MAA mapping demonstrated utility in identifying patients who likely would have a high nontumor liver absorbed radiation dose from administered 90Y activity and thus would be at risk for developing REILD. Further, studies looking at dosimetry predicted by 99mTc MAA mapping with dosimetry measured by 90Y PET/CT have also demonstrated a relationship between the predicted dose to the non-tumor liver and adverse clinical outcomes related to REILD (17). They found that the absorbed dose predicted by 99mTc MAA mapping, when compared to the non-tumor liver absorbed dose correlated well with radiation-induced hepatotoxicity as defined by clinical and laboratory variables, whereas the absorbed dose predicted by 99mTc MAA mapping to the tumor perfusion bed did not correlate with tumor response as assessed by metrics such as Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) criteria. In comparison to the review by Sangro et al., which looked at studies using more empiric methods of assessing tumor and nontumor dose, we employed an imaging-based dosimetry method (Velocity imaging software), which provides a more accurate and personalized dosimetry measurement (18). This imaging-based method performed well at dose estimation over a wide range of the prescribed 90Y doses, as evidenced by the Bland-Altman analysis (see Results section, Figure 1). Also, in contradistinction to previous work, our study looked at clinical markers of REILD instead of imaging-based metrics as the clinical outcomes. Such clinical metrics are useful in preventing REILD and thus for 90Y dose adjustment and optimal patient treatment.

The importance of accurate dosimetry in TARE treatment of HCC and other hepatic malignant lesions has been further highlighted by Gaba et al. (19), who found that an overestimation of the tumor bed perfusion dose could lead to post-radioembolization liver failure. In our cohort, a similar pattern of dose overestimation raises concerns about potential hepatotoxicity, particularly in patients with pre-existing liver damage, due to cirrhosis, which has been shown to increase the sensitivity of nontumor liver to absorbed radiation dose toxicity (20). To prevent this, a more accurate measurement of the nontumor liver dose based on the imaging methods highlighted in this paper is proposed.

The potential effect of prior chemotherapy or TACE on the development of REILD was not seen in our study, contrary to previous observations by Iñarrairaegui et al. (20), who noted an increased risk of liver dysfunction post-90Y microsphere therapy in patients with a history of liver-directed therapies (20). Our results showed a decreased risk of ascites with a history of prior chemotherapy and TACE. This may be due to the small number of patients having a history of TACE in our study (Table 1). However, a little less than half of the patients in our study had prior chemotherapy, suggesting a more nuanced interplay between previous treatments and 90Y therapy outcomes and may be related to tissue hypoxia from microvascular damage and radio resistance (22).

Interestingly, we did not find a significant correlation between cirrhosis and hyperbilirubinemia in our analysis (Table 5). Prior work by Salem et al. (22) however reported that cirrhosis was a predictor of poorer patient outcomes post-radioembolization (22). This discrepancy between our findings and that of Salem et al. (22) could be attributed to our study’s smaller sample size and differences in patient selection criteria. Our study included only 43 participants, and prospective studies with larger patient populations are needed to validate our results and refine imaging-based dosimetry methods, which should include parameters such as baseline liver function.

Further limitations include the fact that it has been noted that even though MAA is a useful surrogate for 90Y microspheres dosimetry in metastatic liver malignancy, it is of uncertain utility for use as a surrogate for 90Y microspheres distribution and absorbed dose in HCC since MAA particles span a distribution of various sizes (20–100 µm) whereas the 90Y microspheres are more homogenous in their size distribution (~20 µm) (17,23) and this may play a role in how the tumor perfusion bed volume and vascular bed are visualized and embolized.

Considering our findings, we propose that surrogate dosimetry by 99mTc MAA mapping can be a useful tool in helping to predict the risk of developing REILD in patients, post-radioembolization. The need for individualized patient treatment planning is highlighted not only in our study but also in previous work as well, in order to reduce hepatic radiation toxicity (24). Dose prescription strategies in TARE, coupled with careful patient monitoring, remain critical for optimizing the therapeutic efficacy of 90Y radioembolization and hence strengthening the clinical utility of selective internal radiation therapy (SIRT).

Conclusions

The findings of this study support the use of 99mTc MAA pre-treatment mapping as a valuable tool to predict the normal liver absorbed dose following 90Y TARE. This allows for a personalized and more rational 90Y microspheres-based treatment plan. The implementation of imaging-based dosimetry will represent a significant advancement in the planning of 90Y radioembolization and dosimetry of tumor and non-tumor tissues. Our study reveals that while trying to maximize tumor dose and minimize non-tumor liver dose, there are certain patient clinical factors which play a critical role in the overall treatment management. There is however room for improvement, and future research should focus on enhancing the accuracy of dose predictions and understanding the multifactorial nature of REILD to reduce the incidence of post-TARE hepatotoxicity and improve clinical outcomes for patients undergoing this valuable treatment.
