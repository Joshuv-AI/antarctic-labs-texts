Autologous transplant versus matched sibling donor transplant in intermediate-risk AML in CR1 with no detectable MRD: a biological assignment comparative study
Authors: Sijian Yu, Hui Liu, Weihua Zhao, Guopan Yu, Pengcheng Shi, Danian Nie, Zhiqiang Sun, Qianwei Liu, Ruoyang Shao, Zinan Feng, Xinquan Liang, Xiong Zhang, Xin Du, Xiaojun Xu, Shunqing Wang, Qing Zhang, Yirong Jiang, Hongyu Zhang, Ziwen Guo, Min Dai, Xuejie Jiang, Dan Xu, Fen Huang, Zhiping Fan, Na Xu, Qiong Liu, Qianyi Liang, Can Liu, Meiqing Wu, Guangyang Weng, Ren Lin, Hua Jin, Jing Sun, Yu Wang, Li Xuan, Yu Zhang, Qifa Liu

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRetrospective studies suggested that autologous stem cell transplantation (auto-SCT) might achieve comparable or even better outcomes compared with allogeneic SCT for patients with genetic intermediate-risk acute myeloid leukemia (IR-AML) and no detectable measurable residual disease (MRD-). However, prospective studies are lacking comparing auto-SCT and matched sibling donor SCT (MSD-SCT).Patient and methodsWe initiated a biological assignment comparative study at fourteen institutions in China. Patients with newly diagnosed de novo IR-AML and MRD- within 3 cycles of intensive chemotherapy were eligible for the study if they were 14 to 60 years old and received auto-SCT or MSD-SCT. Patients were assigned to auto-SCT or MSD-SCT based on donor availability. The primary endpoint was the 3-year disease-free survival (DFS). The trial was registered with Clinicaltrials.gov, NCT03620955 and completed.ResultsBetween November 15, 2018, and March 31, 2022, 708 patients were screened and 518 eligible for analysis, including 259 in the auto-SCT and 259 in the MSD-SCT groups. With a median follow-up time of 55.2 (IQR 47.4–65.2) months, the 3-year DFS was 82.2% (95% CI 77.0-86.4) and 85.7% (95% CI 80.8–89.4) in auto-SCT and MSD-SCT respectively (HR, 1.24 [95% CI, 0.81–1.91]; p=.32). The treatment-adjusted difference was 3.2% (95% CI, − 3.1 to 9.4; p<.001). Because the upper bound of the 95% CI (9.4) did not exceed 10%, noninferiority was shown. The 3-year overall survival (OS) was 90.7% (95% CI 86.5–93.7) and 87.6% (95% CI 83.0-91.1) respectively (HR, 0.78 [95% CI, 0.47–1.30]; p=.34). In subgroup analyses, auto-SCT achieved similar DFS and OS to MSD-SCT among patients who reached MRD- in the first 2 cycles of chemotherapy, while auto-SCT had inferior DFS but similar OS for patients who achieved MRD- after 3 cycles.ConclusionsAuto-SCT achieved non-inferior DFS and similar OS compared with MSD-SCT, suggesting that auto-SCT is a viable and preferable first-line choice for IR-AML patients achieving MRD- within 3 cycles of chemotherapy, particularly those who reached MRD- in the first 2 cycles.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40164-026-00807-y.

Introduction

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a heterogeneous disease. The prognosis is related to factors such as genetic background and treatment strategies. More than half of younger patients with AML can achieve complete remission (CR) after induction chemotherapy. After CR, further post remission treatment (PRT) would be necessary to prevent relapse. PRT generally consists of either cytarabine-based consolidation chemotherapy (CMT) or autologous stem cell transplantation (auto-SCT) or allogeneic SCT (allo-SCT) for fit younger patients with AML. Currently, the choice of PRT for AML is mainly based on genetic stratification [1, 2]. Generally, the recommendation for AML patients in the first CR (CR1) is to utilize CMT in favorable-risk category and allo-SCT in adverse-risk category, but there is less agreement on the optimal PRT for intermediate-risk AML (IR-AML) [3–9]. Thus, a refined PRT stratification strategy integrating other prognosis-related factors is appreciated in this subset of patients.

A growing number of studies has shown the important role of measurable residual disease (MRD) in prognostic prediction [10–12], suggesting that MRD may be incorporated into treatment decision-making process in AML. Several studies showed that auto-SCT was a valid option for favorable-risk and IR AML with no detectable MRD (MRD-) [13–17]. For patients with IR-AML and MRD-, our retrospective analysis suggested that auto-SCT was associated with similar disease-free survival (DFS) and overall survival (OS) compared with allo-SCT [18]. However, the role of auto-SCT has yet been defined when compared with transplant using a matched sibling donor (MSD), which is still the first allogeneic donor choice in most cases. Furthermore, up to date, prospective studies are lacking directly comparing auto-SCT and MSD-SCT in patients with IR-AML in CR1 and MRD-. To address this issue, we initiated a biological assigned, multicenter study to compare auto-SCT with MSD-SCT in this population.

Methods

Participants and study design

This is a sub-study of a prospective multicenter cohort to compare the outcomes of auto-SCT versus MSD-SCT in patients with IR-AML in CR1 and MRD-. The trial was registered at http://www.clinicaltrials.gov with the number NCT03620955. Patients with newly diagnosed de novo IR-AML and MRD- within 3 cycles of intensive chemotherapy were eligible for the study if they were 14 to 60 years old and received auto-SCT or MSD-SCT. Patients were excluded if they were diagnosed with acute promyelocytic leukemia or AML in the absence of genetic stratification; failed to achieve CR1 after two cycles of induction; relapsed or died or experienced recurrent MRD+ before study assignment, had any conditions not suitable for the trial (investigators’ decision), or had existing contraindications for transplant (Appendix protocol). Patients with FMS-related tyrosine kinase 3 internal tandem duplication (FLT3-ITD) mutations were also excluded because they were recommended to receive allo-SCT as PRT according to our clinical practice [19]. Cytogenetic and molecular analyses were routinely performed at initial diagnosis [18]. The definition of IR-AML was based on European Leukemia Network (ELN) 2017 criteria [20].

The study protocol was approved by the institutional review boards at each participating center, and written informed consent was obtained from the patients and donors in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki before the initiation of the study.

MRD monitoring

MRD in bone marrow was assessed by eight-color multiparameter flow cytometry (MFC) after induction and each course of PRT and then every 2 months in the 1st year, 3 months in the 2st year, 4 months in the 3st year, and every 6 months from the 4st to 5st year post-treatment [18]. A locally validated panel of two antibody combinations that recognize CD34, CD117, CD33, CD13, CD56, CD15, CD19, CD7, CD38, CD45 and HLA-DR were used. A lower limit of detection of 0.01% (10− 4) was targeted. When abnormal cells were identified, the cells were quantified as a percentage of the total CD45 + white cell events. Any measurable level of MRD was considered positive [21].

Treatment protocols

Patients were scheduled for the first cycle of induction treatment consisting of daunorubicin 60 mg/m2 or IDA 12 mg/ m2 per day on days 1–3 and cytarabine 100 mg/m2 per day for 7 days. Then, the second cycle of chemotherapy named “3 + 3” regimen consisting of daunorubicin IDA 10 mg/ m2 per day on days 1–3 and high-dose cytarabine (HDAC) 2.0 g/m2 twice daily on days 1–3, or HDAC (3 g/m2 q12h, d1-3) for patients achieving CR was administered. While all patients who failed to obtain CR after the first induction were given “3 + 3” regimen as the second induction. After that, 2 cycles of HDAC followed by auto-SCT, or 1 cycle of HDAC followed by MSD-SCT was administered as PRT [19, 21]. The detailed procedures of auto-SCT or MSD-SCT were described previously [19, 22, 23]. Myeloablative conditioning regimens were used in all patients. Generally, patients in the MSD-SCT group received busulfan plus fludarabine, while those in the auto-SCT received Busulfan plus cyclophosphamide.

Outcomes

The primary endpoint was 3-year DFS. The key secondary endpoints were 3-year cumulative incidence of relapse (CIR), non-relapse mortality (NRM) and OS. Relapse was defined by morphologic evidence in the peripheral blood, marrow, or extramedullary sites. OS was evaluated from study assignment to death or loss of follow-up. DFS was evaluated from study assignment to relapse or death or loss of follow-up, whichever came first.

Statistical analysis

The sample size was calculated based on the primary end point, 3-year DFS, which was 81.3% in the auto-SCT group and 82.6% in the MSD-SCT group in our retrospective corhort [18]. The design was a noninferiority study with a 10% margin, and a minimum of 492 patients (246 in each group) were required to provide the study with a one-sided α of 0.05 and a power of 80%. After adjusting for 5% dropout, the total sample size was 518 patients (259 in each group). Sample size calculation was performed using R software (version 4.4.3). To show noninferiority, the upper limit of the 95% CI for the difference in 3-year DFS between the two groups could not exceed 10%. Patients were assigned to auto-SCT or MSD-SCT based on donor availability (biological assignment) after 3 cycles of chemotherapies. An MSD was the first choice for transplant. If an MSD was unavailable, auto-SCT was given. If one group reached the calculated sample size, the enrolment in this group was complete; and the other group continued the enrolment until the number was reached. The descriptive analysis of patient characteristics included median and interquartile range (IQR) for continuous variables, and absolute and relative frequencies for categorical variables. Variables related to patients, disease, and transplant characteristics among groups were compared using Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables and Kruskal-Wallis test for continuous variables. DFS and OS were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared by the log-rank test. Cumulative incidence curves were used in a competing risk setting with relapse treated as a competing event to calculate NRM probabilities, and NRM as competing risk to calculate relapse. The Cox proportional hazards (PH) regression model was used for the analysis of risk factors for time-to-event variables. All variables included in the Cox model were tested for PH assumption and proportionality was met for all variables. The Fine and Gray model was used for the analysis of relapse [24]. Age and WBC count at diagnosis were used as categorical variables in the univariate or multivariate analysis. The confounding factors including treatment groups, age and gender were included in the Cox or Fine-Gray model when reporting hazards ratio (HR). Other covariates (WBC count at diagnosis and cycles to achieve CR) were included in the multivariate analysis with a criterion of p < .1 in the univariate analysis. Age (grouping by median) and WBC count at diagnosis (≥ 100 × 10^9/L vs. < 100 × 10^9/L) were calculated as categorical variables in the univariate or multivariate analysis. The last follow-up time was June 30th, 2025. All tests were two-sided, with significance set at p = .05. Stata SE 12.0 and R version 4.4.3 were used for all data analysis.

Results

Patients’ characteristics

A total of 708 patients aged 14–60 with FLT3-ITD wild-type IR-AML and MRD- at 14 institutions in China between November 15, 2018, and March 31, 2022, were screened, and 545 patients were assigned to auto-SCT or MSD-SCT according to donor availability (Fig. 1). Twenty-seven patients were excluded, including 15 patients who experienced MRD recurrence or hematological relapse, 8 patients who refused treatment for other reasons, and 4 patients who failed autologous stem cell mobilization in auto-SCT. Eventually, 259 patients in the auto-SCT (between 21 November 2018 and 06 September 2021) and 259 in the MSD-SCT groups (between 15 November 2018 and 31 March 2022) were enrolled in the study and included in analysis. The median age was 38 years (IQR, 30–49), and there were 272 (52.5%) male and 246 (47.5%) female. Two hundred and fifty-four (49.0%) patients achieved MRD negative after cycle 1, 172 (33.2%) after cycle 2 and 92(17.8%) after cycle 3. The baseline characteristics of patients were comparable between the two groups (Table 1).

DFS, CIR, NRM and OS.

With a median follow-up of 55.2 (IQR 47.4–65.2) months, 84 experienced disease recurrence or died of NRM, including 46 in the auto-SCT group and 38 in the MSD-SCT group (table S1). The 3-year DFS was 82.2% (95% CI 77.0-86.4) and 85.7% (95% CI 80.8–89.4) respectively (HR, 1.24 [95% CI, 0.81–1.91]; p=.32, Fig. 2a). The treatment-adjusted difference was 3.2% (95% CI, − 3.1 to 9.4; p< .001). Because the upper bound of the 95% CI (9.4) did not exceed 10%, noninferiority was shown. An exploratory subgroup analysis showed that MSD-SCT significantly improved DFS in patients who exhibited non-CR (NR) status after the first induction (HR, 0.32 [95% CI, 0.14–0.75]; p=.009) and WBC count at diagnosis greater than 100*109/L (HR, 0.26 [95% CI, 0.09–0.74]; p=.012) compared with auto-SCT (Fig. 3).

The 3-year CIR was 15.1% (95% CI 11.0-19.7) and 8.9% (95% CI 5.8–12.7) in patients receiving auto-SCT and MSD-SCT, respectively (HR, 1.77 [95% CI, 1.06–2.96]; p=.029, Fig. 2b). The 3-year NRM was 2.7% (95% CI 1.2–5.2) and 5.4% (95% CI 3.1–8.6) respectively (HR, 0.46 [95% CI, 0.19–1.13]; p=.089, Fig. 2c).

Post-hoc multivariate analysis showed that NR status after the first induction (HR, 3.81 [95% CI, 2.38–6.10]; p<.001) and WBC count at diagnosis greater than 100*109/L (HR, 1.71 [95% CI, 1.01–2.87]; p=.045) were independently risk factors for DFS, whereas PRT choice (auto-SCT vs. MSD-SCT) did not affect DFS (HR, 1.33 [95% CI, 0.86–2.05]; p=.20). NR status after the first induction (HR, 4.61 [95% CI, 2.75–7.74]; p<.001), WBC count at diagnosis greater than 100*109/L (HR, 1.98 [95% CI, 1.13–3.47]; p=.017) and auto-SCT (HR, 1.93 [95% CI, 1.14–3.26]; p=.015) were independently risk factors for CIR (Table 2).

Thirty-nine patients experienced first hematological relapse in the auto-SCT group, in which 37 subjects received allo-SCT and 19 (51.4%) survived. Twenty-three patients experienced hematological relapse in MSD-SCT, 19 subjects received salvage treatment (7 s allo-SCT, the others donor lymphocytes infusion and/or salvage chemotherapies) and 5 survived. The 3-year OS was 90.7% (95% CI 86.5–93.7) and 87.6% (95% CI 83.0-91.1) respectively (HR, 0.78 [95% CI, 0.47–1.30]; p=.34, Fig. 2d).

Auto-SCT vs. MSD-SCT in subgroups by dynamic MRD

To compare the outcomes of auto-SCT with MSD-SCT in subgroups by dynamic MRD, exploratory analyses were performed according to the dynamics of MRD within 3 courses of chemotherapy. For patients who achieved persistent MRD negative after the first induction, the 3-year DFS was 88.5% (95% CI 81.6–92.9) and 88.7% (95% CI 81.7–93.2) in patients receiving auto-SCT and MSD-SCT, respectively (HR, 1.04 [95% CI, 0.50–2.16]; p=.91, figure S1a). The 3-year CIR, NRM and OS were comparable between the two groups (figure S1b-d). For patients who achieved MRD negative after 2 courses of chemotherapy, the 3-year DFS was 81.8% (95% CI 72.1–88.4) and 83.3% (95% CI 73.5–89.8) respectively (HR, 1.02 [95% CI, 0.51–2.07]; p=.95, figure S2a). The 3-year CIR, NRM and OS were not statistically different between the two groups (figure S2b-d). For patients who achieved MRD negative after 3 courses of chemotherapy, the 3-year DFS was 63.4% (95% CI 46.8–76.1) and 82.4% (95% CI 68.8–90.4) respectively (HR, 2.62 [95% CI, 1.13–6.09]; p=.025, figure S3a). The 3-year CIR was 31.7% (95% CI 18.1–46.2) and 11.8% (95% CI 4.7–22.3) in patients receiving auto-SCT and MSD-SCT, respectively (HR, 3.07 [95% CI, 1.18-8.00]; p=.022, figure S3b). The 3-year NRM and OS were comparable between the two groups (figure S3c-d).

Discussion

In this prospective multicenter study, the results show that auto-SCT achieves non-inferior 3-year DFS, and similar 3-year OS compared with MSD-SCT for patients with IR-AML and MRD- within 3 cycles of chemotherapy, suggesting that auto-SCT is a viable and preferable first-line choice for this subset.

Whether allo-SCT should be the first-line choice for patients with IR-AML in CR1 remains controversial [3, 4, 7, 25, 26]. Some studies showed that allo-SCT was associated with improved survival compared with non-allo-SCT [4, 5, 26], whereas other studies suggested that the outcomes of auto-SCT or chemotherapy were not inferior to allo-SCT [3, 7, 25]. To further clarify this issue, scholars in Germany initiated a randomized controlled trial (RCT), in which patients were to receive allo-SCT as first-line PRT or conventional consolidation chemotherapy with the option of salvage allo-SCT in case of relapse [9]. The results suggested that first-line allo-SCT was not associated with superior OS to chemotherapy for IR-AML in CR1 and allo-SCT provided an effective salvage for patients experiencing relapse [27]. In this study, 37 out of 39 relapsed patients in the auto-SCT group received allo-SCT as salvage and 51.4% survived, contributing to the 3-year OS of 90.7 versus 87.6% in the MSD-SCT. Nonetheless, the RCT has several major limitations. Firstly, it was terminated due to slow accrual, which might compromise the results. Secondly, 28% of patients in the chemotherapy group belonged to ELN 2017 favorable-risk category, who were more likely to respond to salvage therapy at relapse. Most importantly, MRD was not incorporated into this trial.

MRD is a powerful prognostic indicator, which has been used to optimize risk-stratification and guide decision-making in treatment of favorable-risk and IR AML [13, 28, 29]. As for IR-AML, some retrospective and prospective studies have demonstrated that patients with positive MRD are at very high risk of relapse and benefit from allo-SCT [11, 18, 30]. However, the optimal PRT choice (allo-SCT versus auto-SCT versus conventional chemotherapy) for patients with MRD- IR-AML is unclear. In GIMENA AML1310 trial [13] and HOVON-SAKK-132 trial [31], auto-SCT was delivered to MRD- patients and favorable survival outcomes were observed, suggesting that auto-SCT may be a valid option. Nevertheless, there was no comparator to auto-SCT in the two trials. More recently, a propensity score-matched analysis revealed that non-allo-SCT as first-line choice followed by allo-SCT as salvage achieved comparable OS with first-line allo-SCT in MRD- IR-AML [14]. In another registry-based comparison between auto-SCT and haploidentical SCT, auto-SCT had higher CIR but much significantly lower NRM than haploidentical SCT, resulting in better OS in FLT3-ITD wild-type MRD- IR-AML [17]. In our retrospective study, auto-SCT achieved similar DFS and OS compared with allo-SCT in MRD- IR-AML [18]. The above studies all implied that auto-SCT might be the preferred first-line choice in comparison with allo-SCT. However, so far, there are no prospective studies to define the role of auto-SCT in MRD- IR-AML, particularly when compared with transplant using an MSD, which is still the most ideal donor choice for allo-SCT in most cases. Hence, we designed the trial to compare auto-SCT with MSD-SCT by biological randomization based on donor availability. The results showed that auto-SCT had a trend of lower NRM which compensated for the higher CIR to yield a non-inferior DFS, similar OS compared with MSD-SCT, suggesting that auto-SCT is a preferable first-line choice for this population. The CIR was very low in both auto-SCT and MSD-SCT groups, suggesting the threshold of 10− 4 to define MRD- in our practice is feasible. We further explored the association of dynamic MRD with treatment and outcomes. For patients achieving persistent MRD negative after the first induction, the lowest CIR was observed. In this subgroup, auto-SCT showed similar CIR, NRM, DFS, and OS with MSD-SCT. For patients achieving MRD negative after 2 courses of chemotherapy, auto-SCT had comparable CIR, NRM and survival rates with MSD-SCT. While for patients achieving MRD negative after 3 courses of chemotherapy, despite higher CIR and lower DFS were shown in auto-SCT, the two transplant models had comparable OS. These exploratory results in subgroups suggested that auto-SCT be recommended as the first-line PRT in IR-AML patients achieving MRD- within 3 courses of chemotherapy, especially for those achieving MRD- within 2 courses.

Except for MRD, other covariates were also associated with prognosis [18]. In this study, needing 2 cycles to achieve CR and high WBC count (≥ 100 × 10^9/L) were two independent risk factors for CIR and DFS. And subgroup analysis showed that MSD-SCT improved DFS in patients with high WBC count or needing 2 cycles to achieve CR compared with auto-SCT, suggesting that MSD-SCT might partially overcome the adverse effect of both on prognosis.

We acknowledge certain limitations in the study. Firstly, we used biological randomization for treatment allocation rather than well-controlled randomization in study design. However, the baseline characteristics between treatment groups were well balanced and affecting covariates were adjusted to reduce potential bias. Secondly, patients with FLT3 mutations were excluded from the study since most of them received allo-SCT as PRT. Moreover, targeted therapies as sorafenib or gilteritinib were not widely available during the study period. Therefore, the treatment choice based on MRD for this population needs to be explored in the era of wide availability of targeted therapies.

In conclusion, auto-SCT achieved non-inferior DFS and similar OS compared with MSD-SCT, suggesting that auto-SCT is a viable and preferable first-line choice for IR-AML patients achieving MRD- within 3 cycles of chemotherapy, particularly those who reached MRD- in the first 2 cycles.
