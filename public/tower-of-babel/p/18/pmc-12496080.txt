Multivariable risk prediction models for postoperative cardiac injury in adults undergoing non-cardiac surgery: a systematic review and meta-analysis protocol
Authors: Zheng Zhang, Yi Duan, Yuze Wang, Zhifeng Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPostoperative cardiac injury, encompassing myocardial infarction (MI) and myocardial injury after non-cardiac surgery (MINS), is a major perioperative complication associated with substantial morbidity and mortality. While numerous prediction models have been developed using traditional statistical and machine learning approaches, their comparative performance, calibration quality and methodological rigour remain unclear. This protocol outlines a systematic review and meta-analysis to comprehensively evaluate multivariable risk prediction models for postoperative cardiac injury in adults undergoing non-cardiac surgery.Methods and analysisFollowing Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Protocols guidelines, this PROSPERO-registered systematic review will search PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, Scopus, grey literature and trial registries for studies developing, validating or updating multivariable prediction models for postoperative cardiac injury (MI or MINS) occurring within 72 hours of non-cardiac surgery. Two reviewers will independently extract data and assess quality using CHARMS (Critical Appraisal and Data Extraction for Systematic Reviews of Prediction Modelling Studies) and PROBAST (Prediction Model Risk of Bias Assessment Tool) tools. The primary outcome is discriminative performance (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve), with calibration metrics and diagnostic accuracy measures as secondary outcomes. Random-effects meta-analyses will pool performance estimates for models validated in multiple cohorts. Heterogeneity will be explored through subgroup analyses and meta-regression, examining factors including model methodology (regression-based vs machine learning), predictor types and validation contexts. Sensitivity analyses will test the robustness of findings.Ethics and disseminationEthical approval is not required for this study, as it is a systematic review and meta-analysis based on previously published data.PROSPERO registration numberCRD420251041628.

Introduction

Background and rationale

Postoperative cardiac injury, including myocardial infarction (MI) and myocardial injury after non-cardiac surgery (MINS), is one of the most frequent and serious complications following surgery.1 Large cohort studies such as the VISION (Vascular Events in Noncardiac Surgery Patients Cohort Evaluation) study have demonstrated that approximately 8% of patients undergoing major non-cardiac surgery experience myocardial injury within the first few days after surgery, as evidenced by elevated postoperative troponin levels, even in the absence of classical ischaemic symptoms.2 Postoperative cardiac injury is associated with a markedly increased risk of 30-day mortality, with HRs ranging from 2 to 4 compared with patients without injury.3 As such, early identification of patients at risk of postoperative cardiac injury is a critical priority to improve perioperative management and outcomes.

Traditional risk scores for perioperative cardiac complications include the widely-used Revised Cardiac Risk Index (RCRI)4 and the American College of Surgeons National Surgical Quality Improvement Program Surgical Risk Calculator.5 However, these models demonstrate modest performance in contemporary surgical populations, with c-statistics often below 0.7 in external validation studies.6 7 Furthermore, they primarily target major adverse cardiac events (MACEs) or mortality rather than specific myocardial injury.8

Recent advances in perioperative medicine have explored the use of novel biomarkers, such as N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide and high-sensitivity troponins, as independent predictors of postoperative cardiac risk.9 10 In addition, machine learning-based approaches have been increasingly applied to integrate complex clinical data and improve the predictive accuracy of perioperative risk models.1113 Several recent studies have demonstrated the feasibility of using explainable machine learning techniques, such as random forests, XGBoost and neural networks, to predict postoperative cardiac injury or troponin elevation with promising discrimination and calibration. However, the clinical utility of these newer models remains uncertain due to limited external validation, population heterogeneity and variability in outcome definitions.11 14

Although several individual models have been developed or externally validated, to our knowledge, no comprehensive systematic review has synthesised and critically appraised the predictive performance, calibration quality and risk of bias of multivariable risk prediction models specifically designed for postoperative cardiac injury. A rigorous synthesis is needed to inform clinical practice, highlight best-performing models and guide future research on perioperative cardiac risk stratification. Therefore, the aim of this systematic review and meta-analysis is to systematically identify, appraise and synthesise existing multivariable prediction models for postoperative cardiac injury.

Objectives

The primary objective of this systematic review is to identify, describe and critically evaluate all published risk prediction models for postoperative cardiac injury in adult surgical patients. In this context, ‘postoperative cardiac injury’ includes MI and related myocardial injury occurring in the postoperative period (as evidenced by clinical criteria or biomarker elevation). We aim to summarise the performance of each model (in terms of discrimination, calibration and clinical applicability) and assess the risk of bias of the model development or validation studies. Specifically, the review will address the question: ‘Which risk prediction models are available for predicting postoperative cardiac injury (eg, MI or significant troponin elevation) and how well do they perform in terms of predicting this outcome?’

A secondary objective is to quantitatively synthesise model performance and elucidate sources of heterogeneity through a two-tiered analytical approach. First, for prediction models validated in multiple independent cohorts, we will conduct random-effects meta-analyses of discrimination metrics (primarily the area under the receiver operating characteristic ROC curve (AUC)) to derive pooled performance estimates with corresponding confidence and prediction intervals. Second, to investigate determinants of performance variation across the literature, we will perform prespecified exploratory analyses including stratified meta-analysis and multivariable meta-regression. These will examine associations between model characteristics (eg, developmental methodology, predictor composition, outcome definitions) and predictive performance. This systematic synthesis aims to provide evidence-based guidance on optimal risk stratification strategies and identify methodological factors that enhance predictive accuracy in perioperative medicine.

Methods

Eligibility criteria

We will include studies that meet the following criteria:

Population

Studies involving adult patients (aged 18 years or older) undergoing non-cardiac surgery will be included.

Studies focusing on cardiac surgery procedures (eg, coronary artery bypass grafting, valve replacement surgery) will be excluded. Studies involving paediatric populations (patients under 18 years) will also be excluded.

Prediction models

We will include studies that develop, validate or update multivariable risk prediction models (or clinical risk scores) designed to predict the risk of postoperative cardiac injury. Eligible models may be based on clinical predictors (eg, logistic regression models, nomograms) or machine learning approaches. We will include models that use preoperative variables alone or in combination with intraoperative or early postoperative variables, provided the primary purpose is to predict postoperative cardiac injury.

Outcome

Primary outcome

The primary outcome will be the discriminative performance of prediction models, measured by the AUC (c-statistic).

Secondary outcomes

Calibration performance of prediction models, assessed by calibration slope, observed-to-expected (O:E) event ratio, or other reported calibration measures where available.

Sensitivity and specificity of prediction models, if reported.

Prediction target

The prediction target will be postoperative cardiac injury, defined as either: (1) postoperative MI according to the Fourth Universal Definition of MI, requiring clinical criteria such as ischaemic symptoms, ECG changes, imaging evidence and elevated cardiac biomarkers15 or (2) MINS, defined as an elevation of cardiac troponin (troponin T or troponin I) above the 99th percentile upper reference limit within 72 hours after surgery, without requiring ischaemic symptoms.2

Studies that exclusively predict long-term cardiovascular outcomes (eg, events occurring beyond 30 days postoperatively) will be excluded unless immediate postoperative cardiac injury is specifically targeted.16

Timing

Only models predicting cardiac injury occurring during the immediate postoperative period, defined as within 72 hours after surgery, will be included.17 If a study uses a slightly broader ‘in-hospital’ timeframe for cardiac injury, it may be included if the event is clearly attributable to the index surgical procedure.

Surgical types

We will include any type of non-cardiac surgery (eg, orthopaedic, vascular, abdominal, thoracic, neurosurgical, gynaecological). Cardiac surgery-specific studies will be excluded to maintain population homogeneity.

Anaesthesia type

There will be no restriction on anaesthesia type. Studies involving general anaesthesia, regional anaesthesia or local anaesthesia will all be eligible. If anaesthesia type is reported, we plan to conduct subgroup analyses comparing studies predominantly involving general anaesthesia versus those involving regional or local anaesthesia to explore its potential impact on predictive model performance.

Study designs

We will include original research articles reporting the development, external validation or updating of risk prediction models. Eligible study designs include prospective or retrospective cohort studies, and secondary analyses of clinical trial datasets. We will exclude case reports, case series, narrative reviews, systematic reviews, editorials, letters, conference abstracts without sufficient data and animal studies.

Language and publication status: no language restrictions will be applied. If feasible, non-English articles will be translated. Both published and preprint studies may be considered if sufficient methodological details and results are available.

Information sources

We will perform a comprehensive literature search across multiple databases and other sources to identify relevant studies. The following electronic databases will be searched: MEDLINE (via PubMed), Embase, Web of Science and the Cochrane Library (which includes the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews and CENTRAL). In addition, we will search Scopus to capture any literature that might not appear in the other databases, given its broad indexing of journals.

To ensure we identify all eligible studies, we will also search Google Scholar for any pertinent articles (examining at least the first 200 search results for relevant titles). We will check the reference lists of all studies that meet inclusion criteria, as well as reference lists of any relevant review articles (eg, narrative reviews on perioperative risk stratification) to find additional articles that our database search might have missed.

We will search for ongoing or unpublished studies by examining clinical trial registries (such as ClinicalTrials.gov and the WHO International Clinical Trials Registry Platform) for any registered studies on prediction of perioperative cardiac complications. If we identify conference abstracts or preprints that appear to meet inclusion criteria, we will attempt to find a full-text article or contact authors to obtain data; if this is not possible, those studies will not be included in the quantitative synthesis but may be mentioned qualitatively if relevant.

No publication date restrictions will be applied (we will cover literature from database inception to the date of the final search). The searches will be updated shortly before the final analysis to include any studies published in the interim. All references will be managed using reference management software (eg, EndNote or Mendeley), where duplicate records will be identified and removed.

Search strategy

The search strategy will be designed to capture the concepts of surgery, cardiac injury (MI) and prediction models. We will use both controlled vocabulary (eg, MeSH terms in PubMed, Emtree in Embase) and free-text keywords. The strategy will be developed in consultation with a medical librarian.

For example, the PubMed search strategy will include terms such as:

("risk prediction" OR "risk model" OR "risk score" OR "prediction model" OR "predictive model" OR "clinical prediction rule" OR "nomogram" OR "machine learning" OR "artificial intelligence" OR "logistic regression" OR "neural network" OR "random forest")

AND

("postoperative" OR "perioperative" OR "surgery" OR "surgical procedure" OR "noncardiac surgery" OR "cardiac surgery" OR "intraoperative")

AND

("myocardial infarction" OR "myocardial injury" OR "cardiac injury" OR "heart injury" OR "cardiac complication" OR "cardiac event" OR "MINS" OR "troponin elevation" OR "cardiac troponin" OR "perioperative MI" OR "postoperative MI")

This strategy will be adjusted for each database’s syntax. We will apply filters to exclude animal studies and laboratory-only studies. We will not apply an English-language filter in order to capture non-English studies. The full search strategies for all databases (PubMed, Embase, etc) will be provided in an appendix of the protocol or in the supplementary material of the published review.

After obtaining the search results, two reviewers will independently screen the titles and abstracts (as described below). We will also implement iterative searching techniques such as snowballing: scanning reference lists and using citation tracking on seminal articles (via Web of Science and Google Scholar) to ensure completeness.

Study selection

We will use a two-stage screening process (title/abstract screening followed by full-text screening) to identify studies for inclusion:

Stage 1: Title and abstract screening. Two reviewers will independently screen the titles and abstracts of all retrieved references for potential eligibility. We will use a liberal inclusion approach at this stage: if either reviewer believes a citation could be relevant (or if eligibility is uncertain from the abstract), the full text will be obtained for further assessment. We will use a screening tool or software (such as Covidence or Rayyan) to facilitate independent screening and record decisions. The reviewers will be trained on the inclusion criteria beforehand, and a pilot screening of a sample of references will be conducted to ensure agreement on the application of criteria.

Stage 2: Full-text screening. The same two reviewers will then independently assess the full-text articles of all references flagged as potentially eligible. A standardised form will be used to evaluate each study against the Eligibility criteria described above (Population, Prediction model, Outcome, etc). For each full text, the reviewers will decide to ‘include’ or ‘exclude’ and document reasons for exclusion (eg, wrong outcome, no actual model, etc).

Any disagreements between the two reviewers at either stage will be resolved through discussion and consensus. If consensus cannot be reached, a third senior reviewer will act as an arbiter to make the final decision. We will keep a record of studies excluded at the full-text stage along with the reasons for exclusion.

The study selection process will be summarised in a Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) 2020 flow diagram in the final review.18 We will report the number of studies identified, screened, assessed for eligibility and included, as well as the reasons for exclusion at the full-text stage (eg, how many were excluded for not having a relevant outcome or not presenting a model, etc). The literature search is planned to commence in October 2025, with an anticipated completion of the review by February 2026.

Data extraction and data items

Two reviewers will independently extract data using a standardised form. Prior to formal extraction, the form will be piloted on two to three studies to ensure comprehensive data capture and inter-reviewer agreement. For each included study (and for each distinct prediction model, if multiple models are reported in one study), we will extract the following information:

Study characteristics: Author(s), year of publication, journal, country and setting of the study; study design (eg, prospective vs retrospective cohort; whether it is a development study, external validation or both); single-centre or multi-centre; sample size (number of patients included); number of events (patients who experienced postoperative cardiac injury) observed in the study.

Population details: The type of surgery and patient population (eg, non-cardiac surgery patients, mixed non-cardiac surgical population, specific subgroups such as vascular surgery or orthopaedic surgery patients); inclusion and exclusion criteria of the study population; patient demographics (mean age, age range, percentage male/female); and relevant clinical background characteristics (eg, prevalence of coronary artery disease, diabetes or other comorbidities, if reported).

Outcome definition and model performance

For each included study, we will extract how postoperative cardiac injury (prediction target) was defined and measured. This includes specific criteria such as troponin cut-off values, requirements for ischaemic symptoms, ECG changes, imaging evidence and the time frame for outcome assessment (eg, within 72 hours after surgery, during index hospitalisation). We will also note whether the outcome was adjudicated or obtained from medical records, and whether it was a single event (eg, MI) or a composite (eg, MACEs).

In addition, we will extract the predictive performance metrics of the models, including measures of discrimination (AUC or c-statistic), calibration performance (eg, calibration slope, O:E event ratio, calibration plots), and sensitivity/specificity if reported.

Prediction model details: A clear description of each model or risk score evaluated. This includes the predictors (variables) used in the model (eg, age, comorbidities, lab values, intraoperative variables, etc); how the model was developed (eg, logistic regression, Cox proportional hazards if time-to-event, machine learning algorithm type, etc); any feature selection or model building strategies (eg, backward elimination of variables, or use of training and test sets); and whether the model was internally validated (and by what method—split-sample, cross-validation, bootstrap, etc). If the study is an external validation of an existing model, we will note the name of the model being validated (eg, testing RCRI in a new cohort). For models that are presented as a score, we will capture how points are assigned if available.

Performance metrics: The primary performance measures of interest are discrimination and calibration of the model. We will extract reported discrimination measures such as the c-statistic or area under the ROC curve (AUC) for both development and validation cohorts (clearly specifying which it is). If available, we will also extract other discrimination metrics like sensitivity, specificity or predictive values at certain thresholds, and likelihood ratios for defined risk categories. For calibration, we will record any reported statistics or assessments (eg, Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit p value, calibration slope, calibration intercept, observed/expected event ratios or descriptions of calibration plots). We will note if the study reported net reclassification improvement or decision curve analysis, as these speak to clinical utility. In the case of machine learning models, we will extract measures such as cross-validated AUC or out-of-sample performance metrics, and any comparison to conventional models. For each model, we will record whether performance was reported on the same dataset used for development (apparent performance), an internal validation set or an external validation set, as these distinctions are important for bias.

Risk of bias-related info: We will capture information useful for later risk of bias assessment, such as how missing data were handled (eg, were patients with missing predictors excluded, or was imputation used), and whether the outcome was assessed blinded to predictors. We will also note funding sources and any author-reported conflicts of interest in the original studies (as industry involvement might be relevant for bias).

Other relevant findings: If the study provided insights such as which predictors were most important (eg, via regression coefficients or feature importance in ML) or if it compared multiple models, we will extract that information. Authors’ recommendations for model implementation and risk thresholds will be documented.

Data extraction will be conducted in duplicate, and the two data extractors will compare their results. Any discrepancies in extracted data will be resolved by referring back to the full text and through discussion. A third reviewer will adjudicate unresolved differences if necessary. We will contact study authors for clarification or to request additional data if key information (eg, the exact AUC value or SE) is missing or unclear, to the extent that time and resources allow. All extracted data will be organised in evidence tables to facilitate comparison across studies. Data extraction items and processes will be guided by the CHARMS (Critical Appraisal and Data Extraction for Systematic Reviews of Prediction Modelling Studies) checklist to ensure comprehensive and consistent data collection across studies.19

Risk of bias in individual studies

Two reviewers will independently assess the risk of bias of each included study using the Prediction Model Risk of Bias Assessment Tool (PROBAST).20 PROBAST is a rigorously developed tool specifically designed for evaluating prediction model studies, covering both development and validation aspects. It consists of four domains: (1) Participants, (2) Predictors, (3) Outcome and (4) Analysis, each containing several signalling questions. For each domain, we will judge the risk of bias as ‘low’, ‘high’ or ‘unclear’ based on the PROBAST guidance.

Key considerations will include: whether the study population was appropriate and representative of the intended use of the model (Participants domain); whether predictors were defined and measured in a similar way for all participants and without knowledge of outcome, and whether their handling (eg, continuous vs categorical) was appropriate (Predictors domain); whether the outcome was defined appropriately and assessed without knowledge of predictors (Outcome domain); and whether the model building procedures were sound (eg, avoidance of overfitting, appropriate validation, appropriate reporting of performance and handling of overfitting, calibration, etc) (Analysis domain). For each included study, we will also assess concerns regarding applicability (ie, whether there are concerns that the study’s participants, predictors or outcome differ in a meaningful way from our review question context).

The two reviewers will first perform the PROBAST assessment independently, then compare their judgements. Discrepancies will be resolved by consensus discussion, and if needed, consultation with a third reviewer. We will present the results of the risk of bias assessment in a summary table or figure (such as a traffic light plot showing judgments for each domain per study). The overall risk of bias for each study (and for each model if multiple models are in one study) will be characterised. These assessments will inform our interpretation of results; we will perform sensitivity analyses excluding high risk of bias studies to see if findings differ. No studies will be excluded a priori based solely on quality, but the risk of bias will be taken into account when synthesising evidence and drawing conclusions.

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

We will first conduct a qualitative synthesis of all included studies and prediction models. This will involve creating summary tables that provide an overview of each model’s population characteristics, predictors, derivation and validation methods and key performance results. To aid interpretation, models will be grouped according to their intended clinical context, such as those developed for broad non-cardiac surgery versus specific surgical subspecialties.

Following this, our quantitative synthesis will adopt a hierarchical approach to rigorously manage and investigate heterogeneity, in line with current methodological guidance. The primary quantitative analysis will focus on pooling the c-statistic for the same prediction model when it has been externally validated in three or more independent cohorts. A random-effects model will be used to generate a summary c-statistic. To account for the statistical properties of the c-statistic as a proportion, a logit transformation will be applied prior to pooling, with the final summary estimate then back-transformed to the original scale for interpretation. We will report the summary c-statistic with its 95% CI, and crucially, the 95% prediction interval to quantify the degree of heterogeneity and convey the expected range of model performance in future settings.

To be clear, our primary meta-analysis is strictly confined to pooling performance estimates for the exact same model across multiple external validation studies. We will not pool performance metrics from different models, even if they are conceptually similar. The comparison between different types or classes of models (eg, machine learning vs regression) will be handled exclusively through stratified subgroup analysis and meta-regression. These comparative analyses are considered exploratory and are intended to investigate sources of heterogeneity, not to derive a single, pooled estimate of ‘state-of-the-art’ performance. For models that have not been sufficiently validated to be included in a meta-analysis, a narrative synthesis of their characteristics and reported performance will be provided.

Beyond the primary analysis of discrimination, further analyses will be conducted. Calibration performance will be synthesised qualitatively, with particular attention paid to calibration slopes and observed-to-expected ratios from external validation studies. Where studies report performance at common risk thresholds, we will consider pooling sensitivity, specificity and likelihood ratios using a bivariate random-effects model. For context, we may also pool the overall incidence of postoperative cardiac injury across studies.

Across all meta-analyses, statistical heterogeneity will be assessed using the χ2 test and quantified with the I² statistic, where a value greater than 50% will be considered indicative of substantial heterogeneity. All statistical analyses will be performed using Stata (StataCorp, College Station, Texas, USA) or the ‘metafor’ package in R.

Secondary exploratory analyses will examine geographical region and publication year to evaluate model transportability and temporal trends. All subgroup findings will be considered hypothesis-generating given their observational nature. Interaction p values and logit-transformed c-statistics will be reported following previously described statistical methods.

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses will test the robustness of findings through five approaches: (1) excluding studies rated as high risk of bias by PROBAST to assess the influence of study quality; (2) restricting analyses to external validation studies only, given that development cohort performance is typically optimistic; (3) limiting outcomes to strict MI definitions, excluding asymptomatic troponin elevation, to evaluate outcome heterogeneity effects; (4) excluding non-peer-reviewed sources if included and (5) applying alternative statistical models (eg, fixed-effects or beta-binomial approaches) for meta-analyses. Each analysis will determine whether conclusions change meaningfully, with substantial differences reported and discussed, while consistency across analyses will strengthen confidence in the findings.

Assessment of publication bias

We will assess the potential for publication bias and small-study effects for our primary quantitative outcomes, although we acknowledge that in prediction model studies this assessment has limitations. If we have pooled results from ≥10 studies (the conventional minimum number for meaningful funnel plot analysis) for a given outcome (eg, a meta-analysis of AUCs of a particular model), we will examine a funnel plot of the effect estimates against their standard errors. Visual asymmetry of the funnel plot could suggest publication bias (eg, the absence of small studies with lower AUCs).

In addition, we will employ Egger’s regression test (or an analogous test suitable for proportions/AUCs) to statistically test for small-study effect asymmetry. A p value <0.10 in Egger’s test will be considered as evidence of possible publication bias.

However, we note that funnel plots for diagnostic or prognostic accuracy measures can be hard to interpret, and heterogeneity could also cause asymmetry. Therefore, we will interpret these results with caution. If publication bias is suspected, we will discuss its potential impact on the review’s conclusions. For example, we would consider whether perhaps only the better-performing models tended to get published (or whether studies with negative results validating a model might be under-reported).

To mitigate publication bias, our search strategy includes grey literature and trial registries, as described. If we identify unpublished studies (eg., an ongoing study that has results available through a registry), we will include them if appropriate, which may help counteract publication bias towards positive findings.

Amendments

If it becomes necessary to amend this protocol, we will document the changes (with the date, description and rationale for each amendment) in the PROSPERO record and report all modifications in the final published review. Any deviations from the methods outlined here will be transparently described in the Methods section of the final paper along with an explanation.

Reporting the review findings

We will report the findings of this systematic review following the PRISMA 2020 statement for reporting systematic reviews. In the final manuscript, we will include a PRISMA flow diagram, tables summarising study characteristics and risk of bias and detailed results for each model. We will distinguish clearly between analyses that pertain to model development (apparent performance) and those that pertain to external validation. Any meta-analyses performed will be accompanied by forest plots illustrating the pooled estimates and confidence intervals for performance metrics.

We will also provide a narrative summary in the Discussion section of the strengths and limitations of the evidence base. This will include discussing the overall certainty or confidence in the evidence. While a Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluations (GRADE) assessment is not fully established for prediction model studies, we will qualitatively describe the evidence quality (taking into account risk of bias, consistency of results, directness and precision). We will identify which models seem most promising for clinical use and whether any appear ready for implementation or further prospective evaluation. Additionally, implications for practice (if any) and recommendations for future research (such as needed external validations or impact studies for certain models) will be made.

Patient and public involvement

Ethics

Ethical approval is not required for this study, as it is a systematic review and meta-analysis based on previously published data. No new data from human participants will be collected, and thus there is no direct participant involvement and no issues of privacy or informed consent. We will ensure proper attribution of all sources and will not misuse any unpublished data without permission.

Dissemination plan

The results of this systematic review will be disseminated through publication in a peer-reviewed journal. We will also consider presenting the findings at relevant scientific conferences or meetings in the fields of perioperative medicine, cardiology or anaesthesiology. The published article will follow PRISMA 2020 reporting guidelines to facilitate transparency and reproducibility. We expect that the findings will be of interest to clinicians involved in preoperative risk assessment, helping them understand the available tools for predicting cardiac risk after surgery. The results may also inform guideline developers and prompt further research (eg, if we identify certain high-performing models that merit prospective evaluation in clinical trials).

Patient and public involvement

There was no direct patient or public involvement in the design of this protocol. We did not involve patients in formulating the research question or the outcome measures, as this study is a secondary analysis of published studies. However, the topic addresses patient safety and outcomes after surgery. Once the review is completed, we aim to disseminate the findings in a manner that is accessible to stakeholders, including possibly creating summaries for a broader audience or engaging with patient safety groups to inform them of advances in risk prediction for cardiac events after surgery.
