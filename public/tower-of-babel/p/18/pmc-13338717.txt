Elongationless start–stop elements are stress-resilient translation gates that are more repressive than uTranslons
Authors: Justin Rendleman, Solomon A Haizel, Shaohuan Wu, Laurelle Lee Young, Junjie Liu, Xinyi Ge, Huijing Zou, Mahabub Pasha Mohammad, Matthew Pressler, Shuvadeep Maity, Vladislava Hronová, Zhaofeng Gao, Anna Herrmannová, Anna Smirnova, Amy Lei, Kristina Allgoewer, Daniel Sultanov, Will Edward Hinckley, Ziyue Cheng, Lauren Shelby, Tanubrata Dey, Krzysztof J Szkop, Ivan Topisirovic, Ola Larsson, Maria Hatzoglou, Leoš Shivaya Valášek, Christine Vogel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStart–stop elements are translation regulatory elements in 5′ untranslated regions (UTR) of eukaryotic transcripts, consisting of a start codon immediately followed by a stop codon. In contrast to canonical upstream Translons (uTranslons), they exclude elongation which creates unique properties. We conducted a comprehensive, carefully controlled comparison of human start–stop elements and uTranslons both at a genome-wide level and with targeted reporter assays. We found that start–stops and uTranslons were similar with respect to their presence in the 5′ UTRs of hundreds of genes, in particular transcription factors and signaling molecules, the low transcript levels of the corresponding genes, short RNA half-lives, and the negative effect on downstream translation. However, start–stop containing genes were translationally even more repressed than genes with uTranslons. Analysing the start–stop architecture and diverse ribosome footprinting datasets, we found evidence for a start–stop-specific mechanism that involves repeat cycling between initiation, termination, ribosome splitting, and 60S rejoining—a process possibly modulated by ASCC3 and eIF1. This cycling explained increased ribosome retention at start–stops and was—in contrast to ribosome retention at uTranslons—independent of the global initiation state. Finally, we showed that the start–stop element in human ATF4 augments the core regulatory model by controlling translation of the uTranslons.

Introduction

Most eukaryotic mRNA translation proceeds in a 5′ cap-dependent manner. The 43S preinitiation complex (PIC) is recruited to the 5′ cap by eIF4F and eIF3, then scans along the 5′ untranslated region (UTR) until it recognizes a start codon and initiates translation [1–3]. This start codon can be part of a uTranslon [upstream Translon; formerly known as upstream open reading frame (uORF)] or the beginning of the main translon [formerly known as coding region (CDS)] [4]. This scanning forces the 48S PIC to interpret regulatory information encoded in the 5′ UTR, which contains numerous cis-acting elements that modulate protein production, often in a condition-dependent manner [5]. Despite decades of work, we still do not fully understand this regulatory code, in part because of the large number and mechanistic diversity of such elements.

uTranslons are among the most frequent regulatory elements in 5′ UTRs and occur in approximately half of human mRNAs [6–8]. These elements are typically evolutionarily conserved, including their upstream AUG start sites [9]. Functional uTranslons undergo canonical translation, i.e. initiation, elongation, and termination. This process diverts scanning PICs and therefore usually represses translation of the downstream main translon [6]. However, some well-documented cases exist in which uTranslons promote downstream reinitiation and thereby support main translon’s translation under specific conditions [10–18].

The quantitative impact of uTranslons depends on efficiency and stringency of initiation mediated by factors like eIF1, eIF1A, eIF2, eIF3 and eIF5, and subsequently—after uTranslon translation initiation and elongation—on termination and recycling factors. These factors include eRF1/eRF3 for stop codon recognition and peptide release and ABCE1 for 80S splitting, which generates a post-termination 40S subunit still associated with the mRNA [19–21]. The fate of this post-termination 40S—clearance from the mRNA versus continued association with the mRNA, followed by reacquisition of an initiator-tRNA Met-tRNAMet, continued scanning and reinitiation at a downstream start codon—depends on several canonical eIFs, such as eIF3 [2, 14, 22, 23], as well as on specialized “reinitiation factors”, most prominently DENR–MCTS1 and eIF2D (Tma22–Tma20 and Tma64 in yeast). These factors promote eviction of deacylated tRNAs from the ribosomal P-site and thereby support reinitiation [24–27]. Prior work showed that short uTranslons and uTranslons with specific codons in the penultimate position relative to the stop codon are particularly DENR–MCTS1 dependent in mammalian cells but not so much in the budding yeast [25, 27–31].

Within this landscape, there is a special class of uTranslon-like elements in which an AUG is immediately followed by a stop codon (Fig. 1A). Because this configuration inherently lacks an elongation step, it does not represent a bona fide translon (formerly known as open reading frame); we refer to this element as a start–stop. In start–stops, initiation and termination occur at the same site without elongation, so the ribosome never “reads” downstream codons. This feature makes start–stops conceptually distinct from canonical uTranslons including the next smallest element, a 9-nucleotide uTranslon (AUG–codon–stop), in which the ribosome performs a single round of elongation.

Work in plants has established that start–stops in certain genes act as metabolite-responsive switches that change translation depending on intracellular boron levels [32–34]. In the 5′ UTR of Arabidopsis thaliana genes such as NIP5;1, which encodes a boric acid channel, the start–stop stalls 80S ribosomes in a boron-dependent manner, triggering mRNA degradation and thereby limiting gene expression. Related boron-dependent effects on translation and mRNA stability have been observed for start–stop sequences in other plant and yeast genes [35–40]. A genome-wide analysis in plants further identified boric-acid-induced ribosome stalling and highlighted regulatory behaviors of start–stops that differed from those of elongation-inclusive uTranslons [35]. Recent work suggests a highly specialized mechanism in plants in which the start–stop halts the ribosome during boron stress as boron interferes with correct termination [41]. To the best of our knowledge, a similar metabolite-driven mechanism has not yet been described for metazoa. In addition, the role of boron and response to high concentrations in metazoa is very dissimilar to that in plants; the mammalian boron transporters/channels [42, 43] do not have a start–stop in their 5′ UTRs. Therefore, the boron-induced ribosome stalling on start–stops may be a plant-specific mechanism to regulate translation and mRNA stability in response to changes in micronutrient availability.

Further, the boron-specific mechanism contrasts the classic picture of uTranslon function in metazoa where the uTranslon under normal conditions suppresses translation without the need for metabolite-based interference with termination. Similarly, start–stop elements have also been described as translation-repressive in specific metazoan genes, e.g. DROSHA, GPR37, HOXA11, and Asb8 [25, 29, 44]. For example, in the mammalian HOXA11 mRNA leader, a start–stop was proposed to cooperate with a stable stem–loop to inhibit translation of this embryogenesis-promoting transcription factor [44], although subsequent work suggested that the stem–loop alone may stall 80S ribosomes and that the start–stop may not be transcribed as part of the 5′ UTR originally analyzed [45].

A start–stop also resides upstream of the two major regulatory uTranslons in the ATF4 5′ UTR, is conserved across mammals but absent from mouse, and has been implicated in the integrative stress response [16, 25, 28, 46]. The ATF4 start–stop’s function has been proposed to depend on the non-canonical initiation factors DENR and MCTS1, because DENR knockdown partially reduced the overall stress-induced upregulation of ATF4 translation [25], and the same work suggested that DENR-MCTS1 more generally support reinitiation after start–stop elements. However, more recent work in yeast does not support this view [47].

Therefore, the general role of start–stops in mammalian genes remains unclear as well as the mechanistic implications of their elongationless modus operandi which is a fundamental difference to even the shortest canonical uTranslons (Fig. 1A). We hypothesized that the mechanism of ribosome capture on start–stops and therefore their regulatory impact might differ from that of uTranslons. Thus, we investigated the set of human start–stops in detail and compared their properties to a carefully selected set of uTranslons. We found that although start–stops and uTranslons share many characteristics, start–stops stall ribosomes and repress downstream main translon translation even more strongly than the smallest canonical uTranslon (9-nucleotide uTranslons, or ‘u9’)—through a start–stop specific mechanism. Start–stops occur in >1400 human genes, are conserved, and, unlike many plant start–stops and some uTranslons, do not trigger mRNA degradation.

Materials and methods

Identifying start–stops, uTranslons, and control genes

All genes

We downloaded GENCODE v43 human gene annotation file from the UCSC Table Browser (https://genome.ucsc.edu/cgi-bin/hgTables). When a gene had multiple principal transcripts, we selected the transcript with the longest 5′ UTR. The total number of genes was 19 514 (‘all genes’).

Core sets

To extract the 5′ UTR sequences which included start–stop sequences (“AUGUGA”, “AUGUAA”, “AUGUAG”) or 9-nucleotide uTranslons (u9; “AUGXXXUGA”, “AUGXXXUAA”, “AUGXXXUAG”), we used a customized R script. We obtained a total of 1417 and 1331 genes with start–stop and u9 sequences in their 5′ UTRs, respectively. We then removed (i) cases where the start–stop or u9 was nested within a longer uTranslon; (ii) transcripts with >2 occurrences of the either a start–stop or a u9; and (iii) genes whose 5′ UTR contained both a start–stop and u9. These filtering criteria yielded two core sets of 708 ‘start–stop genes’ and 621 ‘u9 genes’.

Control set

We constructed a control set by selecting genes with an AUG-free 5′ UTR and with a distribution of 5′ UTR lengths similar to that of the start–stop and u9 core sets using a customized R script. The control seat contained 463 ‘control genes’.

PUC_AUG set

For select analyses, we extracted from the set of ‘all genes’ the subset of genes which contained an AUG in the penultimate position of the main translon, i.e. the last codon before the stop codon (‘PUC_AUG genes’). Note that we attempted to compile a set of u9 genes with an AUG in the penultimate (or middle) position for an even more direct comparison with start–stops, but this filtering resulted in fewer than 10 genes.

Analysis of public datasets

Sequence characteristics

We downloaded basic sequence characteristics from UCSC Table Browser (https://genome.ucsc.edu/cgi-bin/hgTables) for the same GENCODE version (v43). We obtained evolutionary conservation scores—phyloP 100way, phyloP 20way (primates), phylo 7 way (vertebrates)—from the same site. We calculated the initiation context score (‘Kozak score’) for the different sets of start codons as described in [48, 49]. We retrieved data for the main translons Codon Adaptation Index from [50]. We assessed gene function enrichment using the DAVID Gene Function Enrichment tool (https://davidbioinformatics.nih.gov/gene2gene_new.jsp).

Experimental data

We downloaded RNA level, RNA half-life, and UPF1 depletion data of the respective publications as listed in the Supplementary Data S1 and Supplementary Table S1. For the analysis of protein and RNA localization data, we obtained four published datasets and databases [51–54].

When assessing translation efficiencies of main translons, we downloaded available RNA and ribosome footprint information as published in the supplementary materials for the respective publications or as available on the corresponding entry in the Gene Expression Omnibus database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) published in the publication. All references for the datasets we used are defined in Supplementary Data S1 and Supplementary Table S1. Note that unless explicitly specified, the ribosome footprinting studies typically used cycloheximide (CHX) as translation inhibitor.

Aggregate ribosome footprint data shown as screenshots for select example genes were obtained from RiboCrypt (https://ribocrypt.org/). They comprised the merged human footprint data from ∼400 studies, as listed on the RiboCrypt website.

Ribosome occupancy on different sites

To assess changes in ribosome occupancy on the respective element (start or stop codon), we downloaded the raw data for the respective publications from Gene Expression Omnibus database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). To analyze 80S ribosome occupancy in inhibitor-free datasets we retrieved data from [55, 56]. We used the same data to assess A-site vacancy [55]. To assess 40S ribosome occupancy, we retrieved data from [15, 25]. We used ribosome profiling data under normal and glutamine starvation conditions from [55] to examine 80S ribosome occupancy change under stress. These studies did not use inhibitors.

To examine the effect of translation inhibitors CHX and lactimidomycin (LTM), and no inhibitor, we used data from [55, 57, 58]. To examine the effect of DENR, eIF2D, ABCE1, ASCC3, eIF1–eIF4G1 interaction, and eIF4A1 & eIF4A2 in ribosome retention we retrieved data from [25, 59–62] as defined in Supplementary Data S1 and Supplementary Table S1. These datasets employed CHX as translation inhibitor.

We devised a pipeline using customized R scripts to analyze the downloaded raw data. First, we trimmed the raw reads with Cutadapt [63], aligned the trimmed reads to the human genome hg19 with STAR [64], extracted uniquely mapped reads with Samtools [65], and assigned these reads to their respective genes and positions. Then, we examined the reads’ distributions around start–stop, u9, and control and all genes’ main translon with a metagene analysis for each group and identified the position with the highest occupancy (peak) to determine the P- or A-site (for start or stop codons). We then counted the number of reads that fell into a 3-nucleotide window around the peak. We used the resulting counts as the 80S or 40S occupancy. From each set, we removed outlier entries if their count value was >2-fold larger than the next highest count value. Further, we removed entries that had no counts in either experimental and/or control conditions. To assess A-site vacancy, we split the read data into short (22–24 nucleotides) and long (27–30 nucleotides) reads (ribosome footprints) and calculated the ratio between these two numbers for each group. Statistical tests are specified in the figure legends.

Dual-luciferase reporter assays

Start–stop mutation constructs

To assess the impact of 5′ UTR start–stop elements on translation and messenger RNA (mRNA) stability, we used the psiCHECK-2 dual-luciferase system (Promega) in reporter assays. Wild-type (WT) 5′ UTRs from MORF4L1 (NM_006791.4), SLC39A1 (NM_001271958.2), and PSPC1 (NM_001354909.2) were inserted upstream of the Renilla luciferase (hRluc) gene at the NheI site. Start–stops located at 108 (MORF4L1), 47 (SLC39A1), and 75 (PSPC1) nucleotides relative to the main translon were disrupted using site-directed mutagenesis (AUGUAG → UUGUAG or AUUGAG). All primers are defined in Supplementary Table S2.

To construct the ‘start–stop only’ construct for ATF4, we cloned the 5′ UTR sequence of human ATF4 from the transcription start site of the main translon into the dual-luciferase psiCHECK2 plasmid, i.e. the ATF4 5′ UTR was fused to the firefly reporter gene hluc+. We then mutated the start codons of uTranslon1 and uTranslon2 to AGG using site-directed mutagenesis. We also used this ‘start–stop only’ construct and mutated the AUG start codon of the start–stop to UUG.

To construct the ‘fused uTranslon2’ construct for ATF4, we cloned the 5′ UTR sequence of human ATF4 from the transcription start site to the start codon of uTranslon2 into the dual-luciferase psiCHECK2 plasmid. This procedure ensured that uTranslon2 was in the same reading frame with the reporter gene hluc+ (firefly luciferase), allowing us to use firefly luciferase activity as a measure of translation initiation at the uTranslon2 start codon. uTranslon2 bypass under stress was measured as a decrease in translation of the uTranslon2-firefly fusion construct. We used renilla luciferase activity as a readout of translation reduction under stress. We also used the same 5′ UTR sequence, but mutated the AUG start codon of the start–stop to UUG to analyze the initiation activity at the uTranslon2 start codon in the absence of a start–stop.

Gain-of-function constructs

To examine the role of the start–stop in a gain-of-function (GOF) construct, we used the 5′ UTR of β-Actin (ACTB, NCBI Reference Sequence: NM_001101.5) which has a length of 84 nucleotides and no known translation regulatory elements. The start–stop or u9 (“AUGGCGUAG”) were positioned either 77 or 30 nucleotides upstream of the luciferase main translon to mark ‘near cap’ or ‘near main translon’ positions, respectively.

Transfection and reporter readout

HEK293T or HeLa cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (Corning) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Thermo Fisher) at 37°C and 5% CO2 until they reached ∼70% confluency. They were then transfected with 1 μg of reporter plasmid using TurboFect (Thermo Fisher). Luminescence was quantified 24–48 h post-transfection using the Dual-Glo® Luciferase Assay (Promega). Relative luciferase activity was calculated as the ratio of hRluc luminescence to the internal firefly (hluc) control luminescence. This relative luciferase activity was then normalized to the respective WT’s relative luciferase activity. All transfections were performed in triplicate as biological replicate, and both firefly and renilla luciferase activities were measured in triplicate as well. To determine bypass, we first normalized firefly luciferase and renilla luciferase activities by the transfection efficiency correction factors, and then calculated the change in normalized firefly in response to stress (i.e. bypass). We also corrected for the background change as measured by the normalized renilla luciferase activity. Statistical significance was determined by two-tailed, paired t-tests across three biological replicates.

Reporter transcript levels

To estimate mRNA stability, HEK293T or Hela cells were transfected with 1 μg of the respective plasmid. Total RNA was isolated from the cells using the RNeasy kits (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Reverse Transcription of complementary DNA (cDNA) was performed using SuperScript III Reverse Transcriptase (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Quantitative reverse transcriptase-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) was carried out to measure hluc and hRluc RNA levels using KAPA SYBR FAST quantitative PCR (qPCR) master mix (Kapa Biosystems) with the following program: preincubation at 95°C for 3 min followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 10 s, 60°C for 30 s, and 72°C for 30 s. We then analyzed the samples with the Roche LightCycler 480 system. Supplementary Table S2 lists the primer sequences for the RT-PCR.

Analysis of the endogenous start–stop element in human ATF4

Mutation of the start–stop

We used Crispr-Cas9 based genome editing to modify the start–stop in ATF4 in the human HAP1 cell line. The HAP1 cell line is near-haploid which facilitates generation of a homozygous mutant at the start–stop site. To design Cas9 guide RNA targeting this site, we identified a PAM sequence (NGG) in the ATF4 5′ UTR region surrounding the start–stop site. To assess potential target sequences, we employed the Benchling CRISPR Guide RNA Design software (https://benchling.com). We selected the guide target sequence CGCAGAGAAAACTACATCTG from the different options, which possessed an on-target score of 57.1 and an off-target score of 59.9 [66, 67].

We then used the lentiCRISPRv2 plasmid (AddGene 52 961) as a one vector system for co-delivery of the hSpCas9 protein and the single guide RNA (sgRNA); cloning of the target sequence into the plasmid was performed as previously described [67]. Two oligonucleotides with overhangs were synthesized for cloning the selected sgRNA into the lentiCRISPRv2 plasmid (Supplementary Table S2). The oligos were phosphorylated and annealed with T4 PNK (NEB) supplemented with 10 mM ATP at 37°C for 30 min, followed by 95°C for 5 min, and a ramp from 95°C to 25°C at 5°C/min. Next, the plasmid backbone was digested and dephosphorylated using BsmBI restriction enzyme (NEB) and Quick CIP (NEB), with incubation at 55°C for 1 h and heat inactivation at 80°C for 20 min. We performed gel purification using the QIAquick Gel Extraction kit (Qiagen), and ligated the linearized backbone to annealed oligos using Quick Ligase (NEB). Transformation into NEB Stable Competent E. coli bacteria (NEB) was performed according to manufacturer instructions. Single colonies were selected, and plasmid DNA was purified from overnight cultures using the QIAprep Spin Miniprep Kit (Qiagen). Proper annealing of sgRNA into the lentiCRISPRv2 backbone was confirmed by Sanger sequencing (Azenta) using the appropriate primer (Supplementary Table S2).

HAP1 cells were cultured in Iscove’s modified Dulbecco’s medium (Corning) supplemented with 10% FBS (Thermo Fisher) at 37°C and 5% CO2 until they reached ∼40% confluency. The lentiCRISPRv2 plasmid containing the start–stop target sequence was transiently transfected into HAP1 cells using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher) and incubated for 24 h. Cells were then passaged into a fresh growth medium supplemented with 0.4 μg/ml of puromycin for selection of positive transfection. After 3 days of growth in the selection medium, individual cells were isolated into separate wells of a 96-well plate using fluorescence-activated cell sorting. Clonal populations were subsequently grown out and polymerase chain reaction screening was conducted to confirm the presence of the desired band size within the ATF4 region before being sent for Sanger sequencing (Azenta) using primers as shown in Supplementary Table S2. Three lines were selected for follow up based on the sequences at the start–stop in ATF4 5′ UTR: Start–stopWT cells, which had no differences in sequence compared to the WT human genome, ΔStart–stop cells, in which the start–stop sequence was completely deleted, and Start–stopTAGTAG cells, where the start codon was mutated to a stop codon (AUG→UAG). For stress induction experiments, cells were incubated with a final concentration of 1 μM thapsigargin.

Protein levels

We used western blotting to measure ATF4 protein levels in the different HAP1 cell lines. To do so, we diluted ATF4 Rabbit monoclonal antibody (Cell Signaling) in a ratio of 1:1000 into a Clean Western Buffer with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA). Next, we washed the membranes three times in Clean Western Buffer for 5 min each and then incubated for 1 h with Digital Anti-Rabbit secondary antibody (Kindle Biosciences) at 1:2000 in Clean Western Buffer with 5% BSA. Proteins were detected with the KwikQuant Imaging System (Kindle Biosciences). As a loading control detection, we used the same Western protocol, replacing the primary antibody with the β-Actin Rabbit antibody (Cell Signaling) after incubating the blots with stripping buffer (Thermo Fisher). Western blot images were processed with the ImageJ software to quantify protein levels for ATF4 and loading controls. We subtracted background values for each sample and divided ATF4 values by loading control values to account for variability in sample loading between wells. To allow for comparisons across the three replicate blots, these normalized ATF4 values were then transformed based on the percent of the maximum value from each blot (relative % of maximum).

Transcript levels

To measure ATF4 transcript levels in the different HAP1 cell lines, we isolated total RNA from cells using the RNeasy kits (Qiagen) following the manufacturer’s protocol. cDNA was reverse-transcribed from RNA samples using SuperScript III Reverse Transcriptase (Thermo Scientific).

RNA half-lives

To estimate the decay of the ATF4 mRNA, we treated Start–stopWT and ΔStart–stop cells with either RNA polymerase II inhibitor 5,6-dichloro-1-β-ribofuranosyl-benzimidazole (+) or dimethyl sulfoxide (−) and isolated RNA after 2 h. Quantitative RT-PCR was carried out using the KAPA SYBR FAST qPCR master mix (Kapa Biosystems) with samples pre-incubated at 95°C for 3 min then 40 cycles of 95°C for 10 s, 60°C for 30 s, and 72°C for 1 s, and analyzed with the Roche LightCycler 480 system. The RT-PCR primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S2.

Results

Start–stop and 9-nucleotide uTranslons occur in hundreds of human genes

To compare start–stops and uTranslons in a controlled, comprehensive, and unbiased way, we compiled gene sets containing either a start–stop or a uTranslon as observed in the human genome. We used only one principal transcript isoform per gene, amounting to a total of ∼19 000 genes. For the uTranslon set, we chose the minimal length of a canonical uTranslon, i.e. 9 nucleotides with a single sense codon in addition to the start codon (AUG-codon-stop). Throughout this work, we refer to these 9-nucleotide uTranslons as u9s. Further, we refer to genes whose 5′ UTR contains one or the other of the elements as start–stop or u9 genes.

We predicted a total of 1417 and 1331 start–stop and u9 genes across the human genome, respectively (Fig. 1B). Of these, 369 genes had both a start–stop and a u9. Most genes had only one element per 5′ UTR; with a few genes containing up to nine and eight start–stops and u9s, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S1A). To ensure that downstream effects were attributable to a single element class, rather than multi-element interactions, we removed cases where the start–stop or u9 was nested within a longer uTranslon, and transcripts with >2 occurrences of the respective element. We also excluded genes whose 5′ UTR contained both a start–stop and u9. These filtering criteria yielded two core sets of 708 and 621 start–stop and u9 genes, respectively (Supplementary Data S1). Note that most of these genes contained other non-overlapping uTranslons in addition to the start–stop or u9, i.e. uTranslons longer than 9 nucleotides. When filtering for start–stop and u9 genes without additional uTranslons, the resulting sets were too small for meaningful analyses (172 and 130, respectively, Supplementary Fig. S1B). We also tested a filter in which we only allowed start–stop or u9 genes where the element had initiation-optimal nucleotides in the −3 and −1 position, which also resulted in very small sets (216 and 190, respectively, Supplementary Fig. S1B).

To construct a control group, we examined the distribution of the 5′ UTR lengths of the start–stop and u9 core sets. We then retrieved all human 5′ UTRs without predicted AUG while matching the length distribution of start–stop genes. This procedure yielded 463 AUG-free control genes (Supplementary Data S1). Note that we did not select more control genes to ensure a similar distribution of 5′ UTR lengths (Supplementary Fig. S1C).

We used the coresets of 708 and 621 start–stop and u9 genes (Fig. 1B) for the analyses described below. The genes showed similar stop codon usage (Supplementary Fig. S1D) and both were evolutionarily more conserved across vertebrates than surrounding nucleotides (Supplementary Fig. S1E). For both elements, the 5 nucleotides immediately upstream were also more conserved than nucleotides further upstream. In some cases, start–stops are also conserved across metazoa in general. One example is SMAD7, an oncogene with a start–stop in one transcript isoform that is conserved in the Drosophila melanogaster homolog Mothers against dpp. When expressed in S2 cells, the start–stop-containing isoform was occupied by ribosomes [68] supporting the likely functionality of the element.

Figure 1C–E shows three examples of start–stop genes, along with snapshots of the distribution of ribosome footprints along their 5′ UTR. The ribosome footprint data was aggregate information from ∼400 datasets as defined in ribocrypt.org and serves to illustrate ‘average’ occupancy on the examples. SLC39A1 is a zinc uptake transporter, MORF4L1 is a chromatin associated protein regulating gene expression, and PSPC1 is a component of paraspeckles and also involved in regulating gene expression [69]. The start–stops in the three example genes were conserved across primates, but only MORF4L1 and SLC39A1 showed conservation across vertebrates in general (Supplementary Fig. S1F).

The start–stop was the only canonical element predicted for SLC39A1’s and MORF4L1’s 5′ UTR. In comparison, PSPC1’s 5′ UTR contained a longer uTranslon in addition to the start–stop. For all examples, the ribosome occupancy on the start–stop element was higher than anywhere else in the 5′ UTR and also higher than that on the AUG of the main translon. This ribosome occupancy pattern suggested that the start–stop lowered the main translon’s translation by stalling the ribosome on the site. Indeed, when mutating the start–stop element in reporter constructs of the example 5′ UTRs, the luciferase signal increased significantly for all three genes (P-value <.001; Fig. 1C–E). This effect was independent of the cell type used, as we showed for MORF4L1 (Fig. 1D). This increase was also independent of changes in the RNA level or the type of mutation disrupting the start–stop (Supplementary Fig. S1G).

A fourth example of a start–stop repressing a gene’s expression arises from literature and involves MAP2K6, encoding a kinase in the p38 MAP kinase signal transduction pathway and as such involved in regulation of stress-induced cell cycle arrest, transcription activation and apoptosis [69]. The transcript contains a start–stop and an analysis of cancer patient data identified a mutation in the start–stop correlating with upregulation of the gene in a patient sample [70]. Indeed, controlled luciferase reporter assays confirmed that the start–stop repressed MAP2K6’s translation [70].

Both start–stop and uTranslon genes are optimized for low expression levels

Next, we examined diverse characteristics of the core set of start–stop genes, comparing them to the core set of u9 genes, the control, and all genes. Start–stop genes were similar to the three examples shown in Fig. 1C–E, significantly enriched in transcription factors and other regulators, e.g. ATF4, MAP2K6, GPR37, CDC7, NFIA, JARID2, and SMAD7 (P-value <.05; Supplementary Data S1). These genes are linked to stress and developmental signaling, consistent with tight regulation of their expression [71]. The function enrichment for u9 genes was similar.

Consistent with their regulatory roles, mRNA levels [72] of both start–stop and u9 genes were significantly lower than those of the control or all genes (P-value <.001; Fig. 2A); the same applied to the RNA half-lives as measured in diverse studies (P-value <.001; Fig. 2B and Supplementary Data S1). This lower half-life was not due to RNA degradation being triggered by ribosome stalling on the element and subsequent nonsense mediated decay (NMD): depletion of UPF1 [73], a key enzyme in NMD, did not have an effect on start–stop or u9 genes any different from that on all genes (Supplementary Fig. S2A).

Proteins encoded by both start–stop and u9 genes were depleted from most subcellular compartments, as measured in large-scale proteomics screens (Supplementary Data S1 and Supplementary Table S1) [51–54]. This depletion was likely due to the generally low levels of transcription (see above, Fig. 2A) and translation (see below) and the limits of detection of the experimental methods. The only two larger compartments in which start–stop proteins were not depleted were the endoplasmic reticulum and the nucleus (Supplementary Fig. S2B). Indeed, both PSPC1 and MORF4L1 shown in Fig. 1C and D function in the nucleus [69]. Further, proteins encoded by both start–stop and u9 genes were strongly depleted from stress granules and P-bodies (Supplementary Fig. S2B), but in contrast, their RNA levels in granules were significantly higher than RNA levels of all or control genes (Supplementary Data S1 and Supplementary Fig. S2C). This finding was consistent with a possible role of start–stops and uTranslons in repressing mRNA translation while preventing mRNA degradation through continued presence of non-colliding ribosomes on their sequence.

Next, we set out to examine the effect of start–stops and u9s on translation of the main translon. Prior to doing so, we closely examined the RNA-ribosome architecture of start–stops and uTranslons (Fig. 1A). As can be seen in Fig. 1A, the ribosome on a start–stop is simultaneously positioned to the initiation and termination sites (in the P- and A-site, respectively), omitting an elongation step and its respective ribosome configurations. We hypothesized that this positioning might still allow the binding of commonly used translation inhibitors, such as the initiation inhibitor LTM or elongation inhibitor CHX. LTM binds to an empty E-site, as is present in the initiating ribosome [76]. The binding blocks the ribosome from transitioning into elongation, i.e. the first elongation cycle. In comparison, CHX can bind to an E-site even if occupied by deacylated tRNA [76]. The binding therefore blocks the last step of elongation. However, while both inhibitors could bind to the ribosome positioned to a start–stop, we hypothesized that this binding would have no effect on ribosome retention on the element, as the ribosome on a start–stop does neither transition into elongation (LTM) nor undergo elongation (CHX). Therefore, ribosome occupancy on start–stops should be insensitive to inhibitors, while ribosome occupancy on uTranslons and main translons would be affected by both CHX and LTM (Fig. 1A).

Indeed, ribosome footprints on start codons of all genes, control genes and canonical u9s increased significantly in a dataset employing CHX compared to data from an inhibitor-free experiment (P-value <.001; Fig. 2C) [55]. In contrast, no such difference was observed in the case of start–stops in the same dataset. Similarly, occupancy on the start–stop element also was insensitive to LTM, while occupancy on the u9 and main translon start sites changed (Supplementary Fig. S2D) [58]. Therefore we concluded that indeed, start–stops are insensitive to translation inhibitors and any experiment using CHX or LTM would bias observed ribosome footprint counts against start–stop elements. The most prudent step would be to restrict the analyses to inhibitor-free ribosome profiling datasets as much as possible.

We identified three ribosome profiling datasets collected without the use of traditional translation inhibitors [15, 55, 56]. These inhibitor-free datasets had much lower coverage than conventional datasets from experiments that employed inhibitors and were therefore much noisier. However, we observed consistent trends across all three. First, we assessed the levels of initiation at the main translon of the different gene sets, as estimated by ribosome occupancy on the main translon’s start codon. We found that both start–stop and u9 genes showed main translon initiation significantly lower than that of all genes in all three inhibitor-free datasets (P-value <.01, Fig. 2D and Supplementary Fig. S2E), consistent with fewer ribosomes reaching the main translon due to retention on the upstream element(s). When examining overall ribosome occupancy (translation) of the main translon instead of translation initiation, the effect was not as pronounced which might have been due to codon composition, translon length, or other confounding factors affecting overall translation levels. Therefore, we sought ways to compare the impact of start–stops and u9s on main translon translation while accounting for these factors.

First, we confirmed the trend observed in Fig. 2D in large-scale reporter-based screens, which used randomized, same-length 5′ UTR sequences to assess their effect on translation levels (Fig. 2E and Supplementary Fig. S2G) [74, 75]. With this setup, the screens removed gene-specific differences in the main translon’s initiation context or Codon Adaptation Index which also affect translation levels. Both screens used CHX which favored ribosome retention on uTranslons, biasing against the start–stops. Nevertheless, start–stops reduced translation more so than u9s across both datasets from the large-scale screens (P-value <.01, Fig. 2E and Supplementary Fig. S2G) [74, 75].

Second, to confirm the start–stop’s ability to repress downstream translation more so than u9s while controlling for the main translon’s properties, we designed and tested the elements in a GOF reporter construct (Fig. 2F). Specifically, we used the 5′ UTR of ACTB (84 nucleotides), which is not known to have any translation regulatory elements, and introduced either a start–stop or a 9-nucleotide uTranslon (u9) as the sole upstream regulatory element. The start–stop/u9 were positioned either near the 5′ cap (position 30) or near the main translon (position 77), i.e. the AUG start codon of the luciferase reporter. To maximize initiation at the u9, we chose the middle codon to be GCG, which creates a highly favorable initiation context. Therefore, the u9’s initiation context was much stronger than that of the start–stop, as the start–stop had, by definition, an unfavorable U in the +4 position.

Similar to the genome-wide and screening data (Fig. 2D and E), both the start–stop or u9 decreased translation of the reporter when introduced into the ACTB 5′ UTR compared to translation levels of the WT ACTB sequence (Fig. 2F). Further, again consistent with the above results (Fig. 2D and E), for both positions (near the cap or near the main translon), start–stops lowered translation more so than the u9 (P-value <.001; Fig. 2F)—despite their lower initiation capability. The stronger repression by the start–stop was not due to a loss in RNA stability: on the contrary, the start–stop seemed to stabilize the transcript slightly (Fig. 2F). Consistent with other reports [77, 78], longer uTranslons repressed reporter translation even less than the minimal, 9-nucleotide uTranslon (Supplementary Fig. S2H). The effects were also independent of the choice of stop codon in the start–stop (Supplementary Fig. S2I).

We also used the results from the GOF constructs to test if the probability of reinitiating after a start–stop is different from that after a uTranslon. It is generally thought that uTranslons (and start–stops) reduce main translon initiation due to (i) lowering the number of ribosomes reaching the main translon’s AUG per time unit, as ribosomes initiate first at the upstream elements which reduces flux of the subsequent PICs along the 5′ UTR, and (ii) the need to reacquire initiation factors to the post-termination 40S that leaves the element and continues downstream, i.e. re-initiation. This reacquisition is time-dependent, therefore the distance between the element and the next downstream AUG, e.g. of the main translon, affects the probability of factor reacquisition and subsequent 60S rejoining to allow reinitiation.

Consistent with this model, both start–stops and u9s positioned closer to the 5′ end of the ACTB leader were less repressive than elements positioned near the main translon’s AUG, as the former allowed more time than the latter to reacquire the initiation factors for reinitiation at the main translon (Fig. 2F). Importantly, however, the ability to reacquire factors after termination from the elements was similar for both start–stops and u9s, as for both elements, the ‘drop’ in translation between the 5′ position of an element and the near-main translon position was about 50% (red brackets in Fig. 2F). Therefore, the probability of reinitiation after a start–stop was similar to that of reinitiation after a uTranslon, as tested in our GOF construct. This observation is significant as it provides the first direct evidence that the reinitiation efficiency is not affected by the mechanism of ribosome retention which is different between elongationless start–stops and u9s.

Ribosome retention on start–stops exceeds that on uTranslons

Given that reinitiation capability after a start–stop and after a u9 appeared to be similar (red brackets in Fig. 2F), we hypothesized that the start–stops’ greater ability to repress downstream translation might arise from stronger ribosome retention on the element itself. We tested this hypothesis for 80S and 40S footprints separately. For the 80S footprints, we focussed on the three inhibitor-free datasets described [15, 55, 56]; for the 40S footprints, we included one dataset that employed CHX [15, 25]. CHX should not affect binding of the 40S to the mRNA.

We found that in all datasets [15, 55, 56, 79], start–stop elements showed about 50% higher occupancy for both the 40S and the 80S footprints compared to the footprints on the start and stop of the 9-nucleotide uTranslon combined (P-value <.05; Fig. 3A and B, and Supplementary Fig. S3A and B). Therefore, start–stops seemed to repress downstream translation more than u9s due to stronger ribosome retention on the element. This stronger retention would reduce ribosome flux along the 5′ UTR causing fewer ribosomes to reach the main translon’s start.

Next, we tested how this observation on higher 80S and 40S occupancies on start–stops relates to the initiation potential of start–stops and u9s. As discussed above, start–stops, due to the presence of U in the +4 position, had by definition a suboptimal initiation context compared to u9s for which much of the initiation context is determined by the middle codon. Consistent with this argument, start–stops had overall a significantly lower translation initiation score [48] than u9s, as predicted from the sequence (P-value <.001; Fig. 3C). Suboptimal initiation context is thought to increase start-codon skipping, i.e. leaky scanning by the PIC, and results in lower numbers of 80S ribosomes on a start–stop. Indeed, a smaller fraction of start–stops was occupied by ribosomes than of u9s across all three inhibitor-free ribosome footprinting datasets [15, 55, 56] indicating more leaky scanning in the start–stop case (P-value <.05; Supplementary Fig. S3C). This fraction refers to the fraction of different start–stop or u9 genes which have at least one footprint on the respective element’s start codon. In comparison, the ‘occupancy’ refers to the number (or fraction) of footprints on the same sequence (gene).

Further, we examined the nucleotide sequence surrounding start–stops and u9s more closely (Fig. 3D and Supplementary Fig. S3D). We compared both start–stops and u9s, as well as ribosome occupied elements to all from the coresets (Supplementary Fig. S3D) [15, 55, 56]. The trends were consistent between the ribosome occupied and all start–stop or u9 genes. Both start–stops and u9s had a C and G as the most frequent nucleotide in the −1 and −3 position, respectively, supporting initiation at the respective AUG start codon (Fig. 3D). The nucleotide sequence upstream and downstream of start–stops had significantly higher C and GC content than the nucleotide sequence surrounding u9s (P-value <.01; Fig. 3D), suggesting that perhaps the RNA secondary structure might support ribosome positioning to the start–stop.

Finally, as initiation inhibitors, such as LTM, do not affect ribosomes on start–stops as explained above (Fig. 2C and Supplementary Fig. S2D), we thought of indirect ways to probe initiation dependence of start–stop genes compared to others. To do so, we examined conditions that change translation initiation, i.e. during the acute response to stress which lowers availability of the ternary complex [46, 80–82]. We hypothesized that if ribosome retention on start–stops was truly less dependent on initiation rates as suggested by lower initiation site scores (Fig. 3C), translation of start–stop genes should be less sensitive to stress than other genes and change less.

First, we examined the GOF reporter under tunicamycin-induced protein misfolding stress. As predicted by the hypothesis, the start–stop containing construct decreased in translation less than the control and u9-containing construct, albeit not significantly (P-value >.05; Fig. 3E). Second, we examined an inhibitor-free, large-scale ribosome footprinting dataset that measured the translation responses to cellular stress induced by amino acid starvation [55]. Translation of start–stop genes did not change significantly, while u9, control and all genes decreased as expected (P-value >.05; Fig. 3F). Surprisingly, 80S occupancy on the start–stop increased under stress while occupancy on the u9’s start codon did not change (P-value <.05; Fig. 3F). This observation might be in part a normalization effect: under stress, overall occupancy on the u9’s and other start codons would decrease due to lower global initiation, and occupancy on the start–stop may remain neutral, due to its lower initiation-dependence. Depending on the normalization procedure used in the datasets, this relationship then shifted to show an increase on the start–stop relative to the neutral u9 (Fig. 3F).

Combined, the results supported the idea that initiation at start–stops is lower than initiation on u9s and cannot explain the higher 80S and 40S retention on the start–stop element. Therefore, we tested if differences in termination efficiency could. Single molecule experiments in in vitro translation systems suggested that this is unlikely, as eRF1 recruitment to the start–stop A-site was similar to that for ribosomes terminating at short uTranslons [83]. To probe for any unexpected events in eRF1 recruitment in the large-scale datasets, we examined A-site vacancy as a proxy for termination efficiency in the inhibitor-free data [57]. We argued that an empty A-site on a start codon indicates the elongation competent ribosome waiting for an acylated tRNA, while an empty A-site on a stop codon indicates the ribosome waiting for release factors, in particular eRF1.

We found that the start–stop’s A-site vacancy was in between the A-site vacancy of the start and stop codons of canonical translons and the u9 (P-value <.001; Fig. 3G). The result indicated that at the start–stop, wait time for eRF1 was similar to that for other stop codons, consistent with the results of the single-molecule studies conducted in yeast cell lysate [83]. Therefore, inefficient termination did not seem to be a main factor contributing to the observed higher ribosome retention on start–stops.

Various translation factors underlie ribosome retention on start–stops

As the 80S and 40S enrichment on the start–stops (Fig. 3A and B) could not be explained by evidence for excessive initiation or defective termination, we hypothesized that perhaps differences in the kinetics of recycling may affect the higher ribosome retention on start–stops. Prior work had suggested ribosome recycling from start–stops to be dependent on the DENR-MCTS1 complex, but the precise differences to minimal uTranslons remained unclear [25, 28–31].

To conduct a comprehensive analysis of the comparative role of DENR-MCTS1 and other translation factors potentially affecting 80S and 40S retention on start–stops, we screened published genetic perturbation datasets targeting >20 regulators of scanning, initiation, and recycling for their differential effect on start–stop genes (Supplementary Data S1). The ribosome footprinting datasets had typically been prepared with translation inhibitors (i.e. CHX) which—as explained above—biases the data against start–stops. To remove this bias to the largest extent possible, we only examined the data with respect to translation fold changes between condition and control, but not absolute values in a single condition. We first examined all factors for changes in the overall translation efficiency of the main translon upon factor depletion i.e. inhibition or knockdown, to assess potential effects in the 5′ UTR (Supplementary Data S1). For factors that showed a significant difference in translation of start–stop genes compared to u9 genes, we examined the data more closely i.e. with respect to ribosome footprints on the elements and other data types if available. When possible, we examined these factors in additional datasets (see Supplementary Figs S4 and S5). We also examined the subset of start–stop and u9 genes with optimal nucleotides in the −3 and −1 position (blue rectangle in Supplementary Fig. S1B). Note that these sets (n = 216, n = 190, respectively) were often too small to receive meaningful coverage in riboseq experiments—in which case we omitted the data. The subset of sequences with optimal −3/−1 positions produced results similar to those discussed below and shown in Fig. 4.

We first examined data for factors related to PIC scanning and translation initiation (eIF1, eIF2A, eIF2D, eIF2S1, eIF3B, eIF3D, eIF3E, eIF3F, eIF3G, eIF4A1, eIF4A2, eIF4E, eIF4EBP1, eIF4G1, eIF4G2, DDX6; Supplementary Data S1). Of these, only eIF1/eIF4G1 had a notably different effect on start–stop genes compared to u9 genes. eIF4G1 is the central eIF4F scaffold, which in concert with eIF4E, eIF4A, eIF3, and PABP promotes loading of the 43S PIC onto the capped mRNA, to allow scanning of thus formed 48S PIC into the 5′ UTR [84, 85]. eIF1 is essential for initiating protein synthesis in eukaryotes through its role in accurate start codon recognition [86].

We found that chemical inhibition of the proposed eIF4G1 interaction with eIF1 [72] decreased translation of start–stop genes significantly while ‘all’ or the control set did not change (P-value <.001; Fig. 4A). The effect was similar, but not as pronounced, for the u9 genes when examining the subset with optimal nucleotides in the −3/−1 position (Supplementary Fig. S4A). Accordingly, ribosome occupancy on the start–stop increased under inhibition of the eIF1/eIF4G1 interaction [72] more so than on any of the other sets, albeit not significantly (P-value >.05; Fig. 4A). This result suggested a non-processive build-up of 80S ribosomes on start–stops under inhibition of the eIF1/eIF4G1 interaction.

In comparison, selective translation complex profiling (Sel-TCP-seq) of eIF4G1 bound to the ribosome [79] showed a depletion of start–stop and u9 genes compared to all genes, with a stronger depletion of start–stop genes (P-value <.001; Fig. 4A), suggesting that start–stops may depend less on eIF4G1 than u9s and other genes. The depletion of start–stop genes—and therefore perhaps eIF4G1 independence—was significantly different from that of u9 genes for the 40S Sel-TCP-seq data which is enriched for scanning PICs (P-value <.001; Fig. 4A). Supplementary Fig. S4A showed the same trend for an alternative eIF4G1 Sel-TCP-seq dataset. In comparison, the eIF1 Sel-TCP-seq dataset [87] demonstrated similar dependence of both start–stops and u9s on eIF1 (Supplementary Fig. S4A). Knockdown of eIF1 [58] reduced translation initiation for all sets, but not differently for start–stop genes (Supplementary Fig. S4A).

Collectively, eIF1, but not the eIF1/eIF4G1 contact might be the primary contributor to the effect of the eIF1/eIF4G1 interaction inhibitor on start–stops, considering that eIF1 is essential while its contact with eIF4G is not. Therefore, eIF1 depletion results in a major defect on translation in general that most likely masks the minor effect the drug has on the start–stops when inhibiting the proposed interaction between eIF1 and eIF4G1. Further mechanistic understanding of the relationship between eIF1, eIF4G1, and start–stops require more detailed analyses.

Next, we examined the recycling factors ABCE1, DENR, and eIF2D (Supplementary Data S1). Depletion of the DENR analog eIF2D [61] had no differential effect on start–stop genes (Supplementary Fig. S4B). ABCE1 catalyzes the first step of recycling, i.e. the ATP-dependent splitting of terminating 80S into 60S and a post-termination 40S which is still bound to the mRNA [69, 88, 89]. Reacquisition of initiation factors would then allow for 60S rejoining in the case of start–stop, or resumption of downstream traversal with reinitiation at the next start codon in the canonical case. After subunit splitting, ABCE1 can remain associated with the post-termination 40S and 43S/48S PICs, positioning it to influence clearance and handoff to initiation factors [21, 69, 88].

In the ABCE1 knockdown [90], all genes increased translation of the main translon, but start–stop genes more so than the other sets (P-value <.05 for start–stop versus all genes; P-value >.05 for start–stop versus u9 genes; Fig. 4B). Supplementary Fig. S5B shows the same trend for an alternative dataset [62]. For both datasets, the effect was slightly more pronounced for the subset of start–stop and u9 genes with optimal nucleotides in the −3/−1 position (Supplementary Fig. S5B). This increased translation of the main translon upon ABCE1 depletion was unexpected, in particular in start–stop genes, as failure to recycle should decrease translation of the main translon as ribosomes would ‘get stuck’ at the elements in the 5′ UTR and not proceed downstream. Consistently, ribosome occupancy on all stop codons including the start–stop increased upon ABCE1 knockdown (Fig. 4B). We describe possible mechanistic interpretations in the ‘Discussion’ section.

Next, we re-examined data for DENR-MCST1 which had been suggested to foster reinitiation after start–stops and short uTranslons, i.e. u9s and slightly longer uTranslons [28–31], although the findings could not be confirmed for yeast [27]. DENR-MCTS1 catalyzes the second step of recycling which involves clearance of the tRNA from the post-termination 40S [7, 24, 25, 28–30, 91, 92]. It has been suggested that both the penultimate codon (PUC) and uTranslon length impact DENR-MCTS1 dependence, with AUG being one of the DENR-MCTS1 dependent PUCs [25, 28, 30, 92]. Therefore, as by definition start–stops contain an AUG in the penultimate position, recycling from start–stops was expected to be strongly DENR-dependent. Due to this PUC dependence of DENR-MCTS1 function, we included in our analyses in Fig. 4C a set of genes in which AUG was the PUC before the stop codon of the main translon (‘PUC AUG stops’).

Indeed, we found that DENR depletion [25] decreased translation of the main translon of start–stop genes, while all other sets increased, creating a significant difference in the DENR effect between start–stop and u9 genes (P-value <.01; Fig. 4C). However, as u9s are also very short, they should have also been strongly DENR-dependent, in particular if they contained an AUG in the penultimate position. However, u9 genes had unexpectedly low DENR-dependence (Fig. 4C). One reason may be that u9s are strongly depleted for AUG as the middle (penultimate) codon (<10 genes), likely due to its adverse effect on the initiation context by placing an unfavorable A into the +4 position. Indeed, u9 genes were the exception with respect to DENR-dependence, as longer, but still short uTranslons showed the expected trends, in particular with an AUG in the penultimate position (Supplementary Fig. S5B). In the case of u9s, selection of the middle codon is therefore impacted by both selection of DENR optimal penultimate codons and codons optimizing the initiation context.

As expected from DENR-MCTS1 catalyzing 40S recycling [7, 24, 25, 28–31, 91, 92], DENR depletion [25] significantly increased 40S ribosome occupancy on the start–stop and the stop codons from all sets of genes (P-value <.001; Fig. 4C). However, in contrast to prior expectations on the strong DENR dependence for start–stops, the increase was the highest for the PUC AUG stops, and the lowest for start–stops and u9 stops (P-value <.001; Fig. 4C). While this result could be explained for u9s due to their depletion in DENR-dependent PUCs such as AUG (see above), it was unexpected for start–stops, as they should have had the highest increase in 40S occupancy upon DENR depletion. Similarly unexpectedly, 80S footprints increased the most for start–stops while they barely changed for PUC AUG stops (P-value <.001; Fig. 4C).

Lastly, we examined other translation factors that are known to impact 80S and 40S occupancy in the 5′ UTR (Supplementary Data S1). Amongst these, we observed small, but consistent effects for an ASCC3 knockdown dataset (Fig. 4D) [60]. ASCC3 is mainly known for its role in the trigger (RQT/hRQT/ASC-1) complex where it recognizes K63-ubiquitinated collided ribosomes and splits them, therefore reducing the number of 80S footprints at collision sites [93]. In addition, ASCC3 (RQT) might also bind to the scanning PICs and recognize pseudo-disomes in which a PIC collides with a stalled 80S or other PICs [60]. This scenario could take place in the case of start–stops.

Unexpectedly, ASCC3 depletion [60] increased translation more so in start–stop genes compared to other gene sets (P-value <.01; Fig. 4D), suggesting that ASCC3 somehow supported ribosome retention on start–stops. The trend was similar in another dataset (Supplementary Fig. S5C). This observation was paired with the smallest increase in 40S footprints upon ASCC3 depletion on the start–stop and the largest decrease in 80S footprints compared to all other groups (P-value <.05; Fig. 4D) [61, 94]. This result paradoxically suggested that 40S and 80S occupancy on start–stops was the least ASCC3 dependent or that ASCC3 perhaps positively contributes to ribosome retention on start–stops—through an unknown mechanism.

The start–stop in human ATF4 plays a subtle, but crucial role

Finally, we investigated the role of the start–stop element in the 5′ UTR of human ATF4 (Fig. 5A). The start–stop is located directly upstream of uTranslon 1 (u1) which—together with uTranslon 2 (u2)—forms the classic model of uTranslon-based translation induction under stress, discovered over 20 years ago and recently revisited [12, 16]. In this dual-translon model, the PIC initiates at the most upstream uTranslon (u1) and then, after termination from u1, re-initiates at u2 which is overlapping and out of frame with the main translon’s AUG. Therefore, under normal conditions, little ATF4 protein is made at the expense of u1 and u2 translation [12]. Under stress, availability of the ternary complex decreases which lowers the ability to re-initiate at u2 and therefore increases initiation at the main ATF4 translon.

The ATF4’s start–stop in the human gene shows a high ribosome occupancy (Fig. 5A) and is conserved across placental mammals, but not in rodents (Fig. 5B and Supplementary Fig. S6A). The start–stop is absent from mouse Atf4; the rat Atf4 retains the start but not the stop codon (Fig. 5B and Supplementary Fig. S6A). In chimpanzee and gorilla, for which footprint data is available, the start–stop is also occupied by ribosomes, albeit not as prominently as in the human sequence (Supplementary Fig. S6B). Notably, the element is also present in species more distantly related to humans than mouse or rat, e.g. in dog (as well as horse and killer whale), indicating that the start–stop was present in the common ancestor and was subsequently lost in the rodent lineage. The original Atf4 dual-uTranslon model had been discovered in the mouse gene [12]

To investigate the start–stop’s role, we first examined levels of the endogenous ATF4 protein with and without the start–stop present in the 5′ UTR (Fig. 5C). We found that upon deleting the endogenous ATF4’s start–stop via CRISPR-Cas9, baseline translation levels remained basically undetectable (time point 0; Fig. 5C), contrasting our results for other genes where mutating the start–stop, as the only upstream regulatory element, increased translation (Fig. 1C–E). The result was independent of changes at the transcript level or in RNA stability (Supplementary Fig. S6C); it was also similar when mutating the start–stop to a stop–stop (Supplementary Fig. S6D). The result confirmed that translation repression under normal conditions is fully functional due to the u1/u2 action.

However, when examining the translation response to stress, we observed a marked difference between the WT and the start–stop mutant gene: at both the 2- and 4-h time points, ATF4 induction was significantly lower compared to WT (P-value <.001; Fig. 5C). This result suggested that the start–stop impacted ATF4 inducibility more than its baseline translation levels.

Indeed, the absence of the start–stop and altered ATF4 induction impacted the cells’ stress response: in both the start–stop mutant and in the deletion, survival of treatment with different thapsigargin concentrations was lower than in cells with the WT gene (Supplementary Fig. S7A). This higher stress sensitivity of the start–stop mutant/deletion cells may have been due to lower induction of ATF4 transcription targets, such as ATF3, DDIT3, and ASNS (Supplementary Fig. S7B).

Next, we examined different reporter constructs of the ATF4 5′ UTR to investigate the start–stop’s mechanism of action in the gene. First, we hypothesized the start–stop reduced overall ribosome flux in the 5′ UTR and therefore wasteful production of the u1 and (very long) u2 peptide, without affecting the core regulatory model connecting u1, u2, and the main translon. Indeed, when examining a reporter construct in which the start codons of both u1 and u2 were mutated and the start–stop was the only element in the 5′ UTR, mutating the start–stop as well increased baseline translation (P-value <.001; Fig. 5D), similar to what we had observed for other genes (Fig. 1C–E). Further, this start–stop-only construct showed a minimal response to stress (P-value >.05; Fig. 5D) indicating a situation similar to what we had demonstrated above (Fig. 3C–F): start–stops have low initiation rates and therefore lower sensitivity to low PIC availability under stress.

Second, we examined another construct in which the start–stop and u1 were intact, but u2 was fused in frame to the firefly luciferase reporter (Fig. 5E). The reporter also included a renilla luciferase expressed from the same plasmid as firefly luciferase, which served as a normalization factor to account for the stress-associated translation changes (Supplementary Fig. S7C). Using this construct, we noted another role of the start–stop in modulating the dual-uTranslon system: the start–stop increased bypass of u2 under both normal and stress conditions (Fig. 5E). We estimated u2 bypass as [1-firefly reporter signal], i.e. the lack of reporter translation, and found that it was significantly larger in the presence rather than in the absence of the start–stop (P-value <.001; Fig. 5E). This result was independent of the overall decrease in translation (Supplementary Fig. S7C): the renilla luciferase control decreased due to the global decline in initiation during the stress response, but was not affected by the presence or absence of the start–stop.

Finally, we tested whether this interpretation of the experimental observations could be matched by a simple mathematical model that reflected different regulatory scenarios (Supplementary Fig. S5D). To do so, we incorporated the quantitative measurements into the model and conducted deterministic simulations to examine the impact of the start–stop on ATF4 translation. In the model, we based a sigmoidal dependence of reinitiation at u2 on the levels of phosphorylated eIF2α, according to a Hill–Langmuir relationship of cooperative binding. We then examined the increases in reinitiation at the main translon as a result from lowering translation of u2. The simulation showed that even only 10% less overall reinitiation at u2 due to a start–stop reducing ribosome flux—but at the same overall availability of the ternary complex—would increase ATF4 translation under stress several fold compared to a model without the start–stop (Supplementary Fig. S5D). The model also confirmed that within biologically relevant ranges of phospho-eIF2α and ATF4 mRNA levels, the start–stop is capable of mitigating the energy-demanding translation of u2, while ensuring even higher ATF4’s induction rate under stress. These results highlight how the start–stop fine-tunes the uTranslon-based regulation of this key stress factor.

Discussion

We present the first comprehensive and controlled analysis of the molecular and functional properties of human start–stop elements whose elongationless character places them into a distinct and unique position compared to canonical, elongation-capable uTranslons. Specifically, we compare start–stop elements to the smallest possible uTranslon which involves a single elongation step after initiation (u9s) (Fig. 1A and B). We demonstrate that the impact of start–stops goes beyond what has been described for individual start–stop genes [25, 44] or for a specialized case in plants [32, 41] and show that the lack of elongation results in subtle but functionally important differences in the way start–stops impact translation of the main translon (Fig. 5F).

While both start–stops and uTranslons had many similarities—e.g. low transcription, low RNA half-lives, low translation, frequent occurrence with other elements to form complex regulatory models, and prevalence in transcription regulators and signaling genes—the start–stop specific characteristics lead to higher retention of both 40S and 80S ribosomes on the element (Fig. 3A and B) and subsequently lower translation of the main translon even when compared to the shortest possible canonical uTranslon with optimal initiation context (Fig. 2D–F). We note that this ‘retention’ does not refer to changes in the affinity of the RNA to the ribosome nor does it directly report on changes in ribosome dwell time—higher retention in our interpretation simply means that more footprints have been observed.

One key mechanism underlying higher ribosome retention on start–stops might lie in the possibility that due to the elongationless character of start–stops, Met-tRNAMet may remain present in the P-site even after termination and 80S splitting or be re-recruited after splitting, which would allow for rejoining of the 60S subunit to form an initiation-competent 80S ribosome (Fig. 5G). This 60S subunit rejoining would be a start–stop specific mechanism as it is structurally and mechanistically impossible after termination from a canonical translon following even just one elongation cycle. Through this 60S reattachment, start–stops may undergo repeated ITSR cycles (Fig. 5G) without the 40S subunit moving, which would then cause higher 40S and 80S retention on the element as we observed (Fig. 3A and B).

One option for the presence of the Met-tRNAMet in the P-site arises from delayed/incomplete aminoacyl–tRNA hydrolysis upon termination (Fig. 5G). While single molecule analysis in vitro reported that eRF1 recruitment and peptide hydrolysis kinetics are largely similar at a start–stop compared to uTranslons, closer examination of the data suggested that hydrolysis times at start–stops and u9s are >1.5-fold longer than those at longer uTranslons (see Supplementary Fig. S1 in [83]). Assuming that the presence of the nascent polypeptide in the tunnel stabilizes the Peptidyl Transfer Center, then hydrolysis of aminoacyl- or dipeptidyl-tRNA at start–stops and u9s, respectively, might be slower than at longer translons as the single amino acid or dipeptide barely reach into the peptide tunnel. Further, subsequent 80S splitting by ABCE1 would still be possible even if the tRNA remains aminoacylated, as the connection between the two ribosome subunits would be less stable without a longer polypeptide and more conducive to subunit splitting. Continued presence of Met-tRNAMet in the P-site would then allow subunit re-joining and ITSR cycling, as aforementioned.

Another option for the presence of Met-tRNAMet on the 40S subunit is that it is re-recruited after 80S splitting, possibly enabled by DENR-MCTS1/eIF2 (Fig. 5G). While DENR-MCTS1 has not been shown to evict unhydrolyzed tRNA, it has been proposed to catalyze the “reverse reaction", i.e. bind Met-tRNAMet [30] and recruit it to the 40S subunit in a non-canonical, eIF2-independent manner [92]. This Met-tRNAMet re-recruitment has been demonstrated in vitro for certain viral mRNAs that position the start codon directly in the P-site without any scanning [92]. If such a mechanism exists for start–stops in mammalian mRNAs in vivo, it would set the stage for 60S subunit re-joining and ITSR cycling as illustrated in Fig. 5G.

The decision for DENR-MCTS1 to either evict the hydrolyzed tRNA, recycle the 40S and leave or to promote de novo recruitment of Met-tRNAMet might be a function of the condition-specific equilibrium established by DENR-MCTS1 and other factors. For example, DENR-MCTS1 might interact with initiation factors such as eIF3 which persist after initiation [2, 23, 79, 95] and will therefore still be present at the start–stop. eIF3 is known to promote reinitiation [15, 22, 23, 96–98] and therefore antagonizes dissociation of the 40S from the mRNA. In the case of start–stops, this inhibition and prolonged association of the 40S with the mRNA would allow DENR-MCTS1 to replace the P-site deacylated tRNA with Met-tRNAMet and enable ITSR cycling.

The 60S rejoining and the ITSR cycle (Fig. 5G) can explain the unexpected increase in 80S footprints on start–stops under DENR depletion which we observed (Fig. 4C). Under limiting DENR, the equilibrium would shift towards unrecycled 40S subunits, a fraction of which may contain unhydrolyzed Met-tRNAMet at the P-site. The 60S subunit could then rejoin and explain why DENR depletion causes 80S footprint increases on start–stops (Fig. 5G). In contrast, 60S rejoining would be impossible under limiting DENR for PUC_AUG stops, as there is no Met-tRNAMet at the P-site. The energetic cost of the ITSR cycle compared to a simple delay of the post-termination ribosome at canonical uTranslons is difficult to estimate. We speculate that if Met-tRNAMet remains on the 40S, the cost of 60S re-joining is low, virtually only one molecule of GTP consumed during this eIF5B-mediated event, compared to two molecules of GTP consumed during the only elongation cycle at u9s.

The ITSR cycle, enabled by the proposed presence of Met-tRNAMet on the 40S, is also consistent with the unexpected ABCE1 knockdown results in which start–stop genes had the highest increase in translation efficiency of the main translon (Fig. 4B). The result was counterintuitive, as unsplit, post-termination ribosomes should accumulate at stop codons, repressing downstream translation. However, since ABCE1 also promotes eRF1-mediated peptidyl-tRNA hydrolysis in the P site [99, 100], its depletion may increase the fraction of un-hydrolyzed tRNAs present in the unsplit 80S ribosome which—in the case of start–stops—could fuel the ITSR cycle. Further, as ABCE1 can remain associated with the post-termination 40S and PICs [88], it could hinder 60S subunit rejoining on the start–stop during the ITSR cycle under normal conditions. In fact, this inhibitory role of ABCE1 has previously been proposed as part of canonical initiation [88]. Therefore, ABCE1 depletion may produce even more 80S ribosomes via uninhibited 60S rejoining on the start–stop, in addition to the unsplit 80S ribosomes. However, in the case of start–stops, a large fraction of these 80S could still contain the Met-tRNAMet, some of which could continue moving downstream, as has been proposed [101–103]. These noncanonical initiation-competent 80S ribosomes could then translate the main translon which would explain the comparatively large translation increase of start–stop genes despite unchanged 80S occupancy on the element under ABCE1 depletion (Fig. 4B).

In addition to the proposed ITSR cycle, we provided evidence that two other factors may modify the function of start–stops (Fig. 5G). First, start–stop genes showed some dependence on the initiation factor eIF1, as the drug-mediated interference of its proposed contact with eIF4G led to accumulation of the 80S ribosome on the start–stop. As there is no detailed knowledge of the molecular action of the respective drug, it is hard to speculate what happens on start–stops when the eIF4G1–eIF1 interaction is inhibited.

Further, ribosome retention on start–stops appeared to be negatively regulated by ASCC3: its knockdown led to the strongest decrease in 80S ribosome occupancy on the start–stops and subsequently the highest translation increase of the main translon compared to other sets (Fig. 4D). Based on earlier work on ASCC3, we speculate that it might bind to and resolve pseudo-collisions formed between the ribosomal species sitting on the start–stop and incoming PICs [104], therefore preventing their interference with the ITSR cycle. This interpretation would be consistent with the fact that ribosome stalling on a start–stop does not appear to trigger NMD (Supplementary Fig. S2A) but seems to slightly stabilize the transcript (Fig. 2H). This feature is another difference to uTranslons where a delayed ribosome can drastically lower transcript half-life, and NMD is thought to be at least in part responsible for the translation repressive effect of uTranslons [105].

Finally, we showed that three canonical pathways could not explain the higher translation repressive function of start–stops. First, our GOF construct showed that reinitiation after a start–stop was similar to that after a canonical uTranslon (Fig. 2F). Second, confirming findings from single-molecule studies [83], termination at start–stops appeared to be normal with respect to eRF1 recruitment (Fig. 3G). Third, we showed that—in contrast to canonical uTranslons—initiation at start–stops was intrinsically suboptimal and could not explain the higher ribosome retention at the element.

Importantly though, we showed that relative initiation-independence of start–stops had subtle but important implications for the element’s function under initiation-limited conditions. While start–stops might have comparatively low initiation rates the first time the scanning PIC encounters the AUG (and high leaky scanning, Supplementary Fig. S3C), the subsequent ribosome retention through the ITSR cycle would be independent of initiation factors and would not be reduced under conditions of lowered availability of the ternary complex, e.g. under stress. Therefore, a unique feature of start–stops is that they retain the ribosome regardless of overall translation conditions. We demonstrated that this might indeed be true for the GOF construct (Fig. 3E), genome-wide data (Fig. 3F), and at the example of ATF4 (Fig. 5D). As an analogy, both the start–stop and uTranslons function as ‘gates’, i.e. negative modifiers of ribosome flux along the 5′ UTR (Fig. 5H). In the case of stress, the uTranslon ‘gate’ widens and becomes less restrictive, while the start–stop ‘gate’ remains comparatively unaffected.

Superficially, the ribosome residence on the start–stop, e.g. through the ITSR cycle, and their translation inhibitory effect appear to contrast findings in plants [41]. In plants, it was proposed that ribosome retention at start–stops was specific to high boron levels which interfered with eRF1 binding and termination [41]. However, even under normal conditions the plant start–stop shows high ribosome occupancy (see Fig. 1A in [41]) suggesting that the fundamental mechanism of ribosome retention, i.e. the ITSR cycle, on start–stops is conserved. In plants, high boron levels may amplify ribosome retention even further.

As start–stop elements occur in the 5′ UTRs of transcription factors and signaling molecules which are often dosage-sensitive, one function of the start–stop might be to prevent harmful overproduction of these genes, or changes in expression levels across various conditions. As biological reasoning is often centered on the analysis of gene expression changes, the concept of ‘buffered expression levels’ enabled via start–stops is distinct and novel: start–stops enable low, but constant expression levels that are comparatively robust to translation-altering conditions.

Further, translation repression while stabilizing the transcript also allows for precise subcellular localization of the mRNA prior to translation, as well as an increased speed of expression induction. Low translation but stable mRNA also enables storage of the mRNA molecules in granules, such as stress granules and P bodies. The enrichment of transcripts from start–stop genes in granules supports this interpretation (Supplementary Fig. S2C). At the example of ATF4, we demonstrated that start–stops may also play other, more complex roles, i.e. when combined with other uTranslons. For ATF4, the start–stop’s primary role was to reduce u1 and u2 translation while still ensuring ATF4 translation induction under stress, through increasing uTranslon2 bypass (Fig. 5C–F).

In sum, the elongationless character of start–stops enables high ribosome retention through a start–stop-specific cycle involving initiation, termination, 80S splitting, and rejoining of the 60S subunit (Fig. 5G). This ITSR cycle is comparatively initiation-independent and therefore active under conditions of reduced global translation—as is the case in many human diseases. Therefore, start–stops are highly specialized regulatory elements that ensure increased ribosome retention under varying conditions to fine-tune translation levels. For many regulators, such as transcription factors, these low translation levels are essential for the proper function and regulation of the gene.
