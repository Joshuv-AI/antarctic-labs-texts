Effects of different levels of alcohol consumption on cognitive function in older adults: A cross-sectional study using 2011 to 2014 NHANES data
Authors: Bin Zhang, Dapeng Bao, Ying Ma, Miao Zhang, Yang Cao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Older adults (≥60 years) are at high risk for cognitive impairment, yet the impact of varying levels of alcohol consumption on cognitive function in this population remains unclear. This study aims to evaluate the effect of alcohol consumption on cognitive function among older adults in the United States. Researchers obtained data from 3632 participants aged 60 years and older from the 2011 to 2014 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey. Alcohol consumption was assessed using a questionnaire, while cognitive function was evaluated using the Word Learning and Recall module from the Consortium to Establish a Registry for Alzheimer Disease, the Animal Fluency Test, and the Digit Symbol Substitution Test. The relationship between alcohol consumption and cognitive function was analyzed using weighted multivariable linear regression and stratified analysis based on key covariates. Fully adjusted Model 3 indicated that, compared to nondrinkers, light drinkers had an increase in cognitive function scores by 0.44 (β = 0.44, 95% confidence intervals [CI]: 0.20–0.67; P < .001), while moderate and heavy drinkers experienced a decline in cognitive function scores by 0.62 (β = −0.62, 95% CI: −0.89 to −0.34; P < .001) and 0.97 (β = −0.97, 95% CI: −1.23 to −0.62; P < .001), respectively. Stratified analysis revealed that heavy drinking had a more detrimental effect on cognition among individuals aged 70 and older, women, those with less than a high school education, and those with diabetes, hypertension, or a history of stroke. In older adults (≥60 years) in the United States, heavy alcohol consumption is associated with a decline in cognitive scores, particularly among those aged 70 and older, women, individuals with less than a high school education, and those with diabetes, hypertension, or a history of stroke.

1. Introduction

Cognitive decline is strongly associated with aging, and as the global population continues to age, cognitive impairment and dementia (conditions that worsen as cognitive decline progresses) have emerged as significant global health challenges.[1,2] In the United States, where the aging population is rapidly increasing, the number of individuals living with cognitive impairment is projected to reach 21.55 million by 2060, a 1.7-fold increase from 2020.[3,4] Given that the progression from cognitive impairment to dementia is irreversible and that there is currently no effective cure for dementia, preventing and slowing cognitive decline is crucial to controlling the onset of cognitive impairment and dementia.[5]Diet plays a vital role in human health, and previous studies have shown that healthy dietary choices (such as the Mediterranean diet, the DASH diet, the MIND diet, and increased intake of dietary carotenoids, antioxidant vitamins, and vitamins B-6, B-9, and B-12) can reduce the risk of cognitive impairment.[6–9] Coffee, one of the most commonly consumed beverages, contains caffeine, which has been shown to protect cognitive function.[10] However, the impact of alcohol consumption, another widely consumed beverage, on cognitive function remains less clear. This study aims to explore the effects of alcohol intake on cognitive function in older adults.

Chronic excessive alcohol consumption has been linked to a range of cognitive impairments, from mild to severe, affecting executive function, memory, and metacognitive abilities.[11] Studies have shown that excessive drinking doubles the likelihood of developing severe memory disorders, suggesting that reducing heavy alcohol consumption could be an effective strategy for preventing dementia.[12,13] Among adolescents, prolonged heavy drinking is strongly associated with poorer cognitive performance, potentially leading to long-term neural and behavioral consequences in adulthood due in part to heightened reward responses in the dopamine system and the potential disruption of neurogenesis caused by neuroinflammation, which escalates the risk of alcohol use disorder and abnormal brain development as drinking severity increases.[14–16] For middle-aged adults, light to moderate alcohol consumption has been associated with better cognitive performance,[17] whereas excessive drinking increases the risk of cognitive impairment.[18] One of the mechanisms through which excessive alcohol consumption leads to cognitive decline is by affecting the structure and function of the dentate gyrus, thereby impairing hippocampal function.[19] Some studies suggest that alcohol consumption might be linked to improved cognitive abilities in older adults, though the precise quantity of alcohol that confers such benefits requires further investigation.[20] Overall, excessive alcohol consumption increases the risk of cognitive impairment compared to nondrinkers, while the potential cognitive health benefits of moderate drinking remain to be further evaluated, particularly with regard to defining what constitutes “moderate” drinking.[21]

Given the limited research on the effects of alcohol consumption on cognitive function in older adults, particularly as most cognitive-related studies have focused on individuals aged ≥ 60 years, this study analyzed a nationally representative sample of older adults aged 60 years and older from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), categorizing alcohol intake into 5 levels (never drinking, abstaining, light drinking, moderate drinking, and heavy drinking) to investigate the associations between these levels of alcohol consumption and cognitive performance.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Date source and participants

NHANES is a national health and nutrition survey program jointly conducted by the National Institutes of Health and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. This study protocol was reviewed and approved by the research ethics review board of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and the National Center for Health Statistics. The program aims to assess the health status and nutritional intake of the American population, providing a scientific basis for public health policy development and health interventions.[22] NHANES collects comprehensive data on participants’ health, lifestyle, nutritional intake, and biomarkers through a randomized sample of the U.S. population. These data are utilized to monitor epidemiological trends in chronic diseases, evaluate the effectiveness of public health policies, and offer guidance for individual health management. All participant data are gathered through home interviews, physical examinations at the Mobile Examination Center, and laboratory tests conducted by highly qualified medical professionals. For more detailed information about NHANES, please visit the official website (https://wwwn.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/default.aspx).

The study initially included 3632 participants aged ≥ 60 who underwent cognitive function assessments in the NHANES database between 2011 and 2014. After excluding participants with incomplete cognitive function assessments, missing alcohol consumption data, or other missing covariates, the final sample consisted of 2454 individuals. The participant screening process is detailed in Figure 1. The detailed flowchart illustrating the participant screening process after complete imputation is provided in Figure S1, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q185. Additionally, the study analyzed a broader cohort of 42,716 participants aged 20 years or older with complete alcohol consumption records from the NHANES database, covering the period from 1999 to 2016, to examine the demographic and statistical characteristics of different alcohol-consuming groups.

2.2. Cognitive function assessment

Participants’ cognitive function was tested at the Mobile Examination Center using a series of assessments, including the word learning and recall modules from the Consortium to Establish a Registry for Alzheimer Disease (CERAD), the Animal Fluency Test (AFT), and the Digit Symbol Substitution Test (DSST). The CERAD is a neuropsychological test designed to assess cognitive functioning in patients with Alzheimer disease (AD), capable of detecting and evaluating the progression of AD and even screening for early signs of the disease.[23,24] During the CERAD test, the examiner reads aloud a list of 10 words to the participant, who is then asked to recall as many words as possible immediately. This process is repeated for 3 learning trials. After completing the AFT and DSST, participants are asked to recall the words again to assess long-term memory retention. Scores for each trial range from 0 to 10, and the total CERAD score is the sum of the scores from the 3 learning trials and 1 delayed recall trial.

The AFT is a neuropsychological assessment tool used to evaluate speech production and cognitive functioning. During the test, participants are asked to name as many different animals as possible within 1 minute, with 1 point awarded for each correctly named animal; the total score is the sum of the correct responses. While educational level can influence AFT results, the test is widely applicable across various cultural and linguistic contexts. The AFT is not only used to assess cognitive functioning but also serves as a predictor of the sensitivity and specificity of postoperative delirium and as a diagnostic tool for mild cognitive impairment and AD.[25–27] The DSST evaluates information processing speed, sustained attention, and working memory. Participants are given a sheet with a number-symbol key and are required to copy the corresponding symbols into boxes adjacent to 133 digits within a 2-minute period, measuring visuomotor speed and cognitive flexibility. Scores are based on the number of correctly matched digit–symbol pairs. The DSST is primarily used to identify and monitor changes in cognitive function, particularly in diagnosing and tracking the progression of neurodegenerative diseases, and it can also assess various brain dysfunctions such as schizophrenia and depression.[28,29]

Following similar studies,[30] this study defines “Z” as the composite cognitive score for participants. This score integrates results from the CERAD, the AFT, and DSST. This approach minimizes variability in cognitive ability assessments across different scoring systems and controls for extreme values, thereby avoiding cognitive assessment biases due to floor or ceiling effects. Here, x represents the participant’s score on a given test, while mean and SD denote the test’s overall mean score and standard deviation, respectively.

2.3. Alcohol consumption assessment

Data on participants’ alcohol consumption were obtained from the NHANES questionnaire, where 1 drink was standardized as the equivalent of 12 ounces of beer, 4 ounces of wine, or 1.5 ounces of liquor. Based on previous studies,[31] participants were categorized into 5 levels of alcohol intake: never drinking (<12 drinks in a lifetime); abstaining (≥12 drinks in a lifetime but no alcohol in the past year); light drinking (in the past year, women averaged 1 drink per day, and men averaged 1 to 2 drinks per day); moderate drinking (in the past year, women averaged 2 drinks per day, and men averaged 3 drinks per day); heavy drinking (in the past year, women averaged ≥ 3 drinks per day, and men averaged ≥ 4 drinks per day).

2.4. Covariates

The covariates included in this study include gender (male and female), age, race (Mexican American, other Hispanic, Non-Hispanic White, Non-Hispanic Black, and other races), educational level (below high school, high school or GED, and above high school), poverty–income ratio, body mass index (normal: <25 kg/m2, overweight: 25 to < 30 kg/m2, obese: ≥30 kg/m2), information on hypertension, diabetes, and history of stroke was obtained from participants’ self-reports, specifically indicating whether they had been diagnosed by a doctor or other healthcare professional.

2.5. Statistical analysis

The study categorized participants’ alcohol consumption into 5 distinct groups. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation, while categorical variables are expressed as counts (%). Weighted chi-square tests and Kruskal–Wallis rank-sum tests were employed to calculate P-values for categorical and continuous variables, respectively. To ensure that the results accurately reflect the U.S. population, all analyses were weighted using NHANES-provided survey weights, which account for the complex survey design, including oversampling, non-response, and post-stratification adjustments. Three weighted multivariable linear regression models were constructed to explore the association between alcohol consumption and cognitive function. Model 1 was unadjusted, Model 2 adjusted for gender, age, and race, and Model 3 adjusted for all potential covariates. Furthermore, subgroup analyses were performed to investigate the relationship between alcohol consumption and cognitive function across different strata, including age, gender, education level, body mass index, hypertension, diabetes, and stroke.

All statistical analyses were conducted using EmpowerStats (version 4.1) and R software (version 4.3.3). The relationship between alcohol consumption and cognitive function was assessed using β and 95% confidence intervals (CI), with statistical significance defined as P < .05.

4. Discussion

This study, conducted among 2454 American older adults (aged ≥ 60 years), investigated the relationship between alcohol consumption and cognitive function, revealing varied associations between alcohol intake and cognitive performance. In Model 3, compared to nondrinkers, light drinking was associated with improved cognitive function scores and cognitive protection, while moderate and excessive drinking were linked to declines in cognitive function scores. Stratified analysis results indicated that excessive drinking led to decreased cognitive function scores across all subgroups. Even moderate drinking was associated with cognitive function score declines in participants who were overweight or had hypertension, diabetes, or a history of stroke.

Excessive alcohol consumption is a significant global public health issue and one of the most severe substance abuse problems worldwide. Excessive alcohol consumption can lead to diseases in multiple organs and systems, such as endocrine system disruptions, imbalances in intestinal flora, cardiovascular diseases including hypertension and stroke, liver diseases, malabsorption of nutrients, and nutrient deficiencies; additionally, it increases oxidative stress and inflammation, thereby affecting the normal functioning of the immune system.[32–35] Alcohol also damages the nervous system, as alcohol and its metabolites affect neuroplasticity and synaptic functioning, impacting brain regions such as the prefrontal cortex and thereby interfering with cognitive functioning.[36,37] Studies have shown that excessive alcohol consumption is a risk factor for cognitive decline and even dementia in older adults.[38] As a modifiable risk factor for dementia, reducing alcohol intake can protect cognitive function.[39] Since cognitive impairment is highly prevalent among older adults (age ≥ 60), assessing reasonable levels and frequency of alcohol consumption is crucial for reducing the risk of cognitive impairment associated with excessive alcohol use in this population.

In this study, we defined one drink as 12 ounces of beer, 4 ounces of wine, or 1 ounce of spirits. The results of our research indicate that, compared to nondrinkers, light alcohol consumption has a protective effect on cognitive function, while moderate and heavy drinking are associated with cognitive impairment. Notably, heavy drinking consistently demonstrated significant cognitive damage across all stratifications. These findings align with previous studies.[40]A cohort study conducted on older adults in the United States (age ≥ 72) found that excessive alcohol consumption, defined as more than 14 drinks per week (with 1 drink equivalent to a 12-ounce can or bottle of beer, a 6-ounce glass of wine, or 1 ounce of spirits), increases the risk of cognitive impairment, particularly among those with preexisting mild cognitive impairment.[41] Two cohort studies of older adults in the UK demonstrated that excessive alcohol consumption, defined as more than 14 units per week (equivalent to 4 pints of 5.2% beer or cider, or 5 glasses of 175 ml 14% wine), significantly increases the risk of dementia.[42,43] Despite the different definitions of excessive alcohol consumption in the above studies, they all confirm that excessive alcohol consumption increases the risk of cognitive impairment in older adults.

In the stratified analysis, we observed that heavy alcohol consumption was associated with cognitive impairment across all subgroups, particularly among women aged ≥ 70 years, those with less than a high school education, and those with hypertension, stroke, or diabetes. This could be attributed to the fact that cognitive decline tends to worsen with advancing age, with women experiencing a faster rate of decline compared to men, higher education is a known protective factor for cognitive function; individuals with lower educational attainment are at an increased risk of cognitive deterioration.[44] Studies have shown that excessive alcohol consumption raises the risk of hypertension in both men and women, and elevated blood pressure in middle and old age is associated with an increased risk of dementia.[45,46] Stroke is a significant risk factor for cognitive impairment, with approximately 70% of stroke patients developing cognitive deficits within the first year post-stroke.[47] Diabetes is also linked to an increased risk of cognitive impairment, with an elevated risk ranging from 1.25 to 1.91 times that of the general population.[48] Furthermore, research indicates that excessive alcohol consumption increases the risk of both diabetes and stroke.[49,50] Therefore, it is particularly important for older adults with hypertension, stroke, or diabetes to avoid excessive alcohol consumption to mitigate the risk of cognitive decline.

This study comprehensively assessed participants’ cognitive status by combining 3 different cognitive function tests and categorized participants’ alcohol consumption into 5 levels, thereby enhancing the comprehensiveness of the findings. The study was based on the NHANES, utilizing a nationally representative sample, which increases the generalizability and credibility of the results. Weighted multivariable linear regression analysis was employed, adjusting for multiple covariates to improve the accuracy of the findings. Stratification was also performed on predefined subgroups to identify vulnerable populations most susceptible to the adverse effects of excessive alcohol consumption. However, this study has certain limitations. This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, due to the cross-sectional nature of the NHANES data, the study cannot establish causality or determine the temporal relationship between alcohol consumption and cognitive decline. Second, the reliance on self-reported alcohol consumption introduces potential recall and reporting biases, which may affect the accuracy of the findings. Third, the study primarily focuses on recent alcohol consumption, reflecting only the average intake over the past year, and does not address long-term drinking patterns or cumulative effects over a lifetime. This limitation restricts the ability to investigate how changes in drinking duration or patterns impact cognitive outcomes over time. Additionally, the categorization of alcohol consumption levels (e.g., light, moderate, heavy) is based on average intake and may fail to capture variability in drinking behaviors, such as binge drinking or episodic heavy drinking. Moreover, the definition of “excessive” drinking used in this study may require further clarification, as inconsistencies in defining a “single” drinking occasion across studies could affect the generalizability and comparability of the results. Additionally, although this study incorporated several important demographic and health covariates, key lifestyle factors known to influence both alcohol consumption and cognitive health, such as smoking status, physical activity, sleep quality, and diet quality beyond alcohol, were not considered. The omission of these factors may lead to biased estimates. Future studies should consider including these critical lifestyle factors in the modeling analysis.

In conclusion, although this study provides valuable insights into the relationship between alcohol consumption and cognitive function in older adults, future research should address these limitations by employing longitudinal study designs, incorporating objective measures of alcohol consumption, considering lifetime cumulative exposure, and using more detailed and standardized alcohol consumption classifications. Additionally, future studies should also consider cultural differences and validate the findings in different cultural contexts in other countries to further ensure the generalizability of the results.

5. Conclusion

The findings demonstrate that excessive alcohol consumption (≥4 drinks per day for men and ≥ 3 drinks per day for women) is associated with cognitive decline, particularly in individuals aged 70 and older, women, those with lower educational attainment, and those with a history of stroke, hypertension, or diabetes. These results highlight the importance of moderating alcohol intake to protect cognitive health, especially in these vulnerable groups. Healthcare providers should focus on identifying and reducing excessive alcohol consumption in at-risk populations to mitigate cognitive impairment.
