Drought stress differentially influences growth, physiology, and metabolite accumulation in Triticum aestivum (C3) and Amaranthus caudatus (C4) plants
Authors: Galal Khamis, Emad A Alsherif, Shereen Magdy Korany, Danyah A Aldailami, Abeer S Aloufi, Maha H Khalaf, Seham M Hamed, Maria Gabriela Maridueña-Zavala, Soad K Al Jaouni, Samy Selim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDrought stress affects plant growth and production. To cope with drought stress, plants induced physiological and metabolic changes, serving as a protective approach under drought-stress conditions. The response to drought can vary based on plant type (C3 vs. C4) and the intensity of the stress. Therefore, here we aimed to investigate the different responses of wheat C3-Triticum aestivum and C4-Amaranthus caudatus plants to drought stress. To this end, the growth, photosynthetic parameters, oxidative stress, total antioxidant capacity, primary metabolites (amino acids and organic and fatty acids) and secondary metabolites (polyamines) were analyzed. Drought stress reduced growth, biomass, relative water content, water potential, and photosynthesis in both plants, with more severe effects observed in wheat. Drought-induced reduction in photosynthesis was linked to lower stomatal conductance, reduced photosynthetic enzyme activity, and decreased Fv/Fm, indicating impaired PSII function, effects that were more pronounced in wheat than in amaranth. This was accompanied by increased oxidative damage, as indicated by elevated levels of lipid peroxidation. To cope with drought stress, both plants accumulated metabolites involved in antioxidant defense and osmoregulation, including total antioxidant capacity, soluble sugars, proline, polyamines, organic acids, and fatty acids. This response was more pronounced in wheat, indicating its active deployment of defenses to cope with significant stress, in contrast to Amaranthus' greater physiological resilience.Keywords: Drought stress, Wheat, Amaranthus, Metabolites, Amino acids, Sucrose, And organic acids

Introduction

Globally, drought stress is one of the most important environmental factors affecting plant development and productivity [23, 43, 47]. It disrupts several essential elements of photosynthesis, by inducing stomatal closure, which suppresses the Rubisco-catalyzed carboxylation pathway and increases photorespiration [54]. Decreases in the photosynthetic fixation of CO2, which leads to over-reduction in the photosynthetic electron transport chain resulting in reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation [45]. According to Peltzer et al. [35], drought-stressed plants produce ROS in chloroplasts. When plants experience stress, reduced activity of Calvin cycle enzymes hampers NADPH utilization and NADP⁺ regeneration. This imbalance can overburden the linear electron flow, generating ROS that impaired both PSI and PSII [48]. These species can be potentially hazardous because they consume excess light energy. According to Johnson et al. [20], biological macromolecules and membranes that are highly susceptible to attack by ROS cannot function properly. Plants use different strategies to cope with drought stress, including avoidance and tolerance systems that allow for a high degree of developmental flexibility.

To maintain water balance, preserve cellular integrity, and prevent dehydration in drought-stress conditions the antioxidant defense and osmotic adjustment mechanisms are induced [24]. For instance, osmotic solutes and antioxidants are critical for plant adaptation to drought, mitigating damage imposed by ROS [45]. Amino acids, including proline, are efficient osmotic metabolites that mitigate drought stress impact [60]. In addition to osmoregulation, several studies reported an increase in antioxidant defense [24], beta-carotene [17], and ascorbate contents in tomatoes [40]. Drought can also affect the quality of plant tissue. For instance, it negatively impacts the protein content of buckwheat [40] as well as the vitamin C, zinc, calcium, and iron content in both types of plants [17].

Plant type (C3 or C4) differentially affects their response to drought stress [30, 45]. C3 plants thrive in temperate regions and C4 plants thrive in warm, arid environments. Consequently, C4 plants are generally more drought-tolerant [30]. Their dual enzyme system reduces photorespiration and enhances photosynthetic efficiency, resulting in better CO2 fixation rates compared to C3 plants [22, 45, 60]. Drought stress disrupted chlorophyll synthesis and breakdown in both wheat and sorghum (C4). However, its inhibitory impact on chlorophyll biosynthesis was significantly stronger in wheat (C3) than in sorghum (C4), highlighting their differing capacities to withstand photoinhibition [49]. This adjustment may be related to the greater production of ROS during stress, which is more pronounced in C3 plants than in C4 plants due to their elevated photorespiration rate and decreased stress tolerance [49]. However, there is a limited understanding of how C3 and C4 plants respond differently to water stress, especially at the metabolite level. Therefore, it is important to evaluate their responses to drought and to explore their metabolic responses. Cereal grains are grown around the world in mild climates with sizable agricultural lands. Amaranth (C4) plant is a green vegetable that has an effective drought tolerance with multiple uses. They have agricultural significance as herbicide-resistant weeds or as vegetable crops [36]. Due to its third-place global output ranking, wheat has more global trade than any other cereal crop [6].

Drought stress responses between C3 and C4 plant species were previously studied [33, 45, 46]. For instance, the differences in photosynthesis levels between C3 and C4 grass species [33, 46], photoinhibition [14], and the induction of antioxidant enzymes [54]. Thus, there is a need to understand how drought impacts crop growth and productivity, particularly at their metabolite level. This study aims to integrate growth measurements, detailed gas‐exchange analyses (stomatal conductance, intercellular CO₂, water‐use efficiency), water‐relation parameters (leaf water potential, relative water content), and oxidative stress markers (ROS levels, antioxidant enzyme activities) with comprehensive metabolite profiling (osmolytes, organic acids, fatty acids, antioxidants) in C3 wheat and C4 Amaranthus under drought stress to identify key physiological and metabolic mechanisms that underpin differential drought resilience.

Material and methods

Experimental design and plant growth analysis

A homogeneous seed of wheat (Triticum aestivum L, cv Giza 118) and amaranth (Amaranthus caudatus L. cv Rawa) were sourced from the Agricultural Research Center in Giza, Egypt. These varieties were chosen for their resilience and ability to sustain growth under water stress. The seeds were surface sterilized by immersing it in Na- hypochlorite (35%, v/v) for 20 min. Subsequently, the seeds were germinated in Petri plates and cultivated in pots measuring 25 × 18 cm, holding soil (40% dry wt sand, 18% dry clay, and 35% silt). The soil initially contained 13.2 mg carbon (C), 15.6 mg nitrate-nitrogen (N), 2.04 mg ammonium-nitrogen (N), and 7.8 mg phosphorus (P) per gram of air-dried soil at a moisture content of 0.33 g water per gram of dry soil. The soil had a pH of 6.87, an electrical conductivity (EC) of 3.7 dS/m, and a potassium (K) concentration of 3.14 mmol/L. Then, two seedlings were transplanted into pots (25 cm high and 18 cm in diameter). The plants were grown for 3 weeks in a growth champers (16 h day/8 h night, 24 °C/19 °C, day/night, 400 μE m−2 s−1 PAR). Control pots were watered daily to maintain a soil water content (SWC) of 54% throughout the experiments. Control pots were watered daily to maintain a soil water content (SWC) of 54% throughout the experiments. SWC was measured by weighing soil before and after drying at 105 °C until constant weight, calculating moisture as a percentage of dry soil weight to monitor and control soil moisture accurately. For the drought treatment, irrigation was withheld after sowing, allowing the SWC to gradually decline (for 2 weeks) to approximately 32% SWC, a level that induced visible wilting and triggered a growth reduction of more than 50%. This SWC threshold was maintained to simulate drought stress conditions effectively. The drought stress was applied as a progressive decline in soil moisture after sowing, rather than an abrupt water deficit, to better simulate natural drought conditions. This approach allowed us to observe gradual plant responses to increasing drought stress. Plants were harvested during early to mid-vegetative stage. Harvested samples were stored at −80°C for additional biochemical analyses. There were five independently repeatable drought experiments carried out, with a batch of at least twenty plants under each condition. After the experiment, the soil was oven-dried for 24 h at 110 °C, and the change in soil weight was used to determine the water content. A plant moisture tensiometer (DIK-PC-40, Daiki Rika Kogyo Co., Ltd.) was used to measure the leaf water potential using the pressure chamber method [38]. Relative water content was calculated according to RWC = (FW‐DW)/(TW‐DW) * 100. Where FD, DW, and TW are referred to as fresh weight, dry weight, and turgid weight, respectively.

Determination of photosynthesis rate, stomata conductance, and transpiration rate

In the growth chamber, a portable photosynthesis measurement device (LI-6400, LI-COR, Inc.) was used to assess the rates of stomatal conductance, photosynthesis, and transpiration in leaves. Leaf chamber conditions were controlled at 400 ppm CO2 and 23.5 ◦C (block temperature), at saturating photon flux density (1500 mol m − 2 s − 1) and ambient relative humidity (VPD of leaves of 0.98 kPa, SD = 0.28). Measurements were conducted on the abaxial (lower) surface of fully expanded leaf, which were selected at the mid-vegetative stage and all measurements were conducted between 09:00 and 11:30 AM to minimize diurnal variability and ensure consistency. Chlorophyll a fluorescence transients were measured in the morning (7–9 h) on dark acclimated (at least 30 min) leaves with a plant efficiency analyzer (PEA, Hansatech Ltd., King’s Lynn, Norfolk, UK). From this chlorophyll a fluorescence transient the maximum quantum yield of primary photochemistry of photosystem II (Fv/Fm) was calculated. In the growing chamber, a metal halide lamp was used to irradiate the leaves. Using a plant efficacy analyzer (PEA, Hansatech, Ltd.), the amount of chlorophyll fluorescence in primary leaves was assessed. For 30 min before the measurement, the sample leaf is dark-adapted using a lightweight (6.2 g) plastic leaf clip when utilizing PEA. The shutter is opened while the PEA sensor unit is held above the clip for the measurement. The sensor head's LED array can produce light up to 3000 mol/m2/s with just one button click. Fv/Fm, the ratio of variable fluorescence (Fv) to maximum fluorescence (Fm), was automatically computed to determine the maximal PS II photochemical efficiency. Respiration rates were measured using the LI-COR gas exchange system (LI-COR LI-6400, Lincoln, NE, USA). Measurements were conducted in the dark by placing fully expanded leaves in the leaf chamber after dark adaptation to minimize photosynthetic activity. The system recorded CO₂ exchange rates, which were used to estimate leaf respiration.

Rubisco activity was analyzed according to Sulpice et al. [44]. It was expressed as the conversion rate of glycerate kinase (3-PGA) of extracted leaf samples (µmol 3-PGA m−2 min−1). The activity of rubisco was determined directly (“direct rubisco”), without incubation of the extract in the presence of 10 mM HCO3− and 20 mM Mg2+ to convert the non-carbamylated rubisco into the carbamylated form. The assay of phosphoenolpyruvate carboxylase (PEPC) was coupled with the malate dehydrogenase reaction, and the resulting rate of PEPC activity was expressed in µmol HCO3 m−2 min−1.

Markers of oxidative stress and amino acids evaluation

Lipid peroxidation, a technique developed by Hodges et al. [19], was used to assess the amount of malondialdehyde (MDA). After homogenizing 100 mg of frozen tissue in 80% ethanol, the samples were centrifuged for 15 min at 14,000xg. Using a micro-plate reader, the absorbances at 440, 532, and 600 nm were determined. Lipid peroxidation content was calculated as nmol g−1 DW. The frozen tissues'total antioxidant capacity (TAC) was measured according to Benzie and Strain's [8]. For amino acids evaluation, ethanol (80% (v/v)) was used to homogenize 100 mg of leaf samples in mortar. The extracted materials were centrifuged at 20,000 rpm for 15 min, with Norva-line serving as the internal standard. Following sample centrifugation and clean supernatant mixing with chloroform, the plant residue was once again dissolved in deionized water. According to Mohammed et al. (2020), After being dissolved in chloroform once again, the pellet was filtered using 0.2 µm-pore Millipore microfilters. Amino acids were separated using a Waters Acquity UPLC system hyphenated to a Waters Acquity TQD (tandem quadrupole) mass spectrometer. Chromatographic separation was performed at 37 °C on a (BEH amide column (2.1 mm × 50 mm)) with a mobile phase consisting of a 60/40 acetonitrile/water ratio mobile phase ratio and a measurement wavelength of 254 nm. Detection was primarily performed by electrospray ionization (ESI) mass spectrometry in positive ion mode, using Multiple Reaction Monitoring (MRM) for sensitive amino acid quantification. A Photodiode Array (PDA) detector was also connected in-line, providing complementary UV detection at 254 nm for derivatized amino acids or other compounds. The result was expressed as mg/g dry weight.

Primary metabolites analysis

The concentrations of sugars, organic acids, and fatty acids were assessed using chromatographic techniques [16, 21]. Using an HPLC system with a SUPELCOGEL C-610H column (300 mm × 7.8 mm, Supelco, Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA) and a UV detection system set at 210 nm (LaChrom L-7455 diode array, LaChrom, Tokyo, Japan), organic acids were obtained. At a flow rate of 0.45 mL·min − 1, 0.1% phosphoric acid served as the mobile phase. Using a calibration curve created with the appropriate standards, organic acids were measured. Fatty acid identification was done using the Golm Metabolome Database (http://gmd.mpimp-golm.mpg.de) and the NIST 05 database. Following Nelson's et al., [31], Folin–Ciocalteu, and AlCl3 calorimetric assays, respectively, total sugars, phenolics, and flavonoids were analyzed, according to AbdElgawad et al. [2].

Secondary (polyamines) metabolites analysis

Free polyamines were extracted using 5% perchloric acid, followed by centrifugation at 14,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C to separate the supernatant from the pellet [9]. The pellet was then resuspended in the same perchloric acid solution to ensure complete extraction. Polyamines were extracted in 5% perchloric acid, followed by centrifugation (14000 g, 25 min) to separate the supernatant from the pellet, which was then resuspended in the same solution. Both extractions underwent hydrolysis in 6 M HCl at 110 °C for 18 h to convert conjugated and bound polyamines into free forms. The resulting hydrolyzates were dried, dissolved in 5% perchloric acid, and quantified by HPLC using a µ-Bondapack C18 column (15–20 µm, Waters, USA), with 1,6-hexanediamine as an internal standard [1]. The total polyamine content was calculated as the sum of the individual free polyamines detected. This approach allowed for precise determination of the free polyamine pool present in the samples, which is critical for understanding their role in plant stress responses.

Statistical analysis and heat map

One-way ANOVA in SPSS 22 (Tukey test, p ≤ 0.05) was used for statistical analysis, and Levene's test was used to ensure that the data was normal. In the meantime, the R stat software package was used to perform the hierarchical cluster analysis Euclidean distance.

Results and discussion

Drought-induced differential growth decline: unraveling the roles of water relations and photosynthetic dynamics

Drought tolerance is the ability of plants to maintain growth, physiology and metabolism under frequent water deficits [24]. C3 and C4 plants deploy distinct strategies under environmental stress, thus this study compared the drought stress responses of C3 (T. aestivum) and C4 (A. caudatus) by evaluating the differential growth, physiological and biochemical responses. Under control conditions, drought stress induced a great reduction in both wheat and Amaranthus as indicated by fresh weight and dry weight (p ≤ 0.05) (Fig. 1A and B). However, the decrease in fresh biomass in Amaranthus and wheat represented 31.75 and 36.41% compared to the corresponding control, respectively (Fig. 1A). Furthermore, the decrease in dry biomass in wheat and Amaranthus under drought treatment was 41.66 and 32.36% compared to the corresponding control, respectively (Fig. 1B). The reduction in dry biomass under drought condition was combined with a significant (p ≤ 0.05) reduction in relative water content by 34.21 and 46.40% in amaranths and wheat, respectively, ((Fig. 1C). In contrast the levels of water potential were significantly increased (p ≤ 0.05) in amarantus and wheat by 280.48 and 456.06%, respectively, compared to the corresponding control (Fig. 1D). Overall, the reduction in dry biomass and relative water content were significantly higher in wheat (C3) compared to Amaranthus (C4), that indicates the drought impact on growth parameters was more representative on wheat (C3) compared to Amaranthus (C4). In line with our results, Ward et al. [56] reported that C4 plant species (Amaranthus reteroflexus) had higher leaf growth than C3 plant species (Abutilon theophrasti) under water stress. Leaf growth in both C. spinosas and C. gynandra (C3 and drought‐sensitive species) declines sharply under stress, leading to a reduced relative shoot growth rate. Notably, under identical drought conditions, the impact on C. spinosas was approximately twice as severe as on C. gynandra [54]. Similarly, at moderate and severe drought stress, wheat undergoes more damage from the stress and experiences a greater decrease in growth compared to maize plants [30]. The stress-induced variance in this plant species growth may be caused by several factors at different organizational levels [50]. Lu and Neumann [28] found that water stress inhibits leaf growth differently in C3 (barley, rice) and C4 (maize) plants, affecting leaf cell production and the size of mature epidermal cells. Furthermore, the differential responses of C4 plants underlies the superior water‐use efficiency of C4 plants compared to C3 species, enhancing their survival in arid environments. Furthermore, this improved drought resilience in C4 species is corroborated by significantly greater declines in leaf water potential and relative water content observed in C3 plants compared to C4 under identical stress conditions.

To validate the drought impact on Amaranthus C4 and wheat C3, several growth and physiological parameters were examined such as photosynthesis rate, efficiency (fv/Fm), Rubisco activity, stomatal conductance, and transpiration rate. For instance, Mostly, the reduction of growth parameters negatively affects the photosynthesis rate of plants [4]. Where, plants use photosynthesis to produce carbohydrates [22].

Herein, our results revealed that both plants Amaranthus and wheat under drought stress showed a significant reduction in photosynthesis by 38.87 and 53.28% compared to the corresponding control, respectively, (Fig. 2A). Furthermore, Rubisco enzyme activities were significantly decreased under drought stress by 38.71 and 53.76% in Amaranthus and wheat compared to the corresponding control, respectively, (Fig. 2B). The reduction in photosynthesis rate and efficiency and rubisco activity were significantly higher in wheat C3 compared to Amaranthus C4, that indicates the drought impact on photosynthesis efficiency was more representative on wheat C3 compared to Amaranthus C4. Several studies investigate the superiority of C4 plants in growth and photosynthesis compared to C3 plants under stress conditions. For instance, the higher photosynthetic rate of maize (C4) in comparison to sunflower (C3) allowed for greater development of the former under water stress [12]. When plants are exposed to drought stress, photosynthetic electron transfer Ferredoxin (FD) helps to prevent photo-oxidative damage to the photosynthetic system by taking part in the reduced ascorbate role in ROS scavenging, in addition to its electron transfer function during photosynthesis [32]. Drought stress has also been observed to cause FD gene expression to be down-regulated in Arabidopsis and up-regulated in sorghum and maize [26, 58]. Furthermore, in C4 species, photosynthesis processes are divided between mesophyll and bundle sheath cells, which have different morphological and metabolic components [14]. Recent work suggests that C3 and C4 species respond differently to drought at the cellular level [43, 47], where drought suppresses growth and stomatal development more severely in C3 crops. These contrasting responses may stem from differences in SPEECHLESS gene expression. A decline in guard‐cell turgor pressure triggers stomatal closure as both a rapid and sustained response to drought stress [43]. While closing stomata conserves water, it also limits CO₂ uptake. This imbalance leads to over‐reduction of the photosynthetic electron transport chain, reducing the effective use of absorbed light and causing losses in photochemical efficiency and growth [24]. Consequently, biomass accumulation suffers when photochemical efficiency declines. In our study, Amaranthus and wheat under well‐watered conditions exhibited high stomatal conductance, transpiration, and Fv/Fm (Fig. 2C–E).

Under drought, however, stomatal conductance decreased by 43.9% in Amaranthus and 55.5% in wheat; transpiration rates fell by 40.1% and 60.4%, respectively; and Fv/Fm dropped by 26.4% in Amaranthus versus 52.4% in wheat (Fig. 2C–E). These results indicate that wheat enacts stronger stomatal and photochemical restrictions than Amaranthus as a drought‐avoidance strategy. Moreover, C4 plants benefit from a CO₂‐concentrating mechanism that reduces photorespiration, thereby enhancing photosynthetic efficiency and productivity compared to C3 species [14, 49, 52].

Overall, drought stress more severely reduced biomass, water status, photosynthesis (including Rubisco activity), stomatal conductance, transpiration, and Fv/Fm in C3 wheat than in C4 Amaranthus. C3 plants respond by rapidly modulating non-photochemical quenching, conductance, and Rubisco activation, while C4 species rely on their CO₂-concentrating cycle but face coordination challenges with the Calvin, Benson, Bassham pathway. Targeting faster Rubisco and pyruvate phosphate dikinase activation and greater electron-transport flexibility could boost photosynthetic efficiency under drought [55].

Drought-induced oxidative stress and antioxidant responses in C3 wheat and C4 amaranthus

Drought stress triggers stomatal closure, which limits CO₂ uptake and leads to an over-reduction of the photosynthetic electron transport chain. As the Calvin cycle becomes less effective as an electron sink, the excess energy results in the accumulation of singlet oxygen and the generation of ROS within the leaves [7], Posch & Bennett, 2009). To assess how C3 wheat and C4 Amaranthus cope with this oxidative stress, we analyzed lipid peroxidation levels (MDA). Under drought conditions, MDA levels significantly increased (p ≤ 0.05) by 47.95% in Amaranthus and by 80.97% in wheat compared to their respective controls (Fig. 3A). These findings suggest that drought stress induces greater oxidative damage in wheat, as indicated by higher MDA. On the other hand, the superior resilience in Amaranthus was also correlated with reduced oxidative stress damage.

To understand the stress mitigation, we measure the total antioxidant capacity (TAC). Likewise, TAC levels were increased significantly (p ≤ 0.05) by 45.73% in Amaranthus and 30.19% in wheat (Fig. 3B). In contrast, Amaranthus showed a stronger antioxidant response, producing higher TAC levels, which may help mitigate oxidative stress more effectively. As reported by Khamis et al. [24], ROS generation under drought can cause significant oxidative damage, with lipid peroxidation serving as a key biomarker of cellular injury and potential DNA damage. Plants employ antioxidant defenses as a tolerance strategy to counteract ROS damage and adjust physiological parameters as an avoidance mechanism. High TAC levels help reduce lipid peroxidation and prevent ROS accumulation under severe stress. According to Munne-Bosch and Peñuelas [29], Šircelj et al. [42], elevated antioxidant activity can exceed ROS production, providing lasting protection against photooxidative damage to pigments and thylakoid membranes. Similarly, Uzilday et al. [54] reported that under drought, the C3 plant Cleome spinosa exhibited significantly higher H₂O₂ levels than the C4 plant C. gynandra, consistent with observed increases in MDA. These findings imply that C. spinosa's antioxidant defenses were insufficient to counter the elevated ROS, whereas the C4 species C. gynandra could effectively manage oxidative stress despite having a less pronounced antioxidant induction.

Drought stress altered primary metabolic accumulation in wheat and Amaranthus plants

Photosynthesis is a central physiological process that directly influences primary metabolism. Under drought stress, alterations in photosynthetic activity can disrupt carbon assimilation and lead to the accumulation of various primary metabolites. Therefore, to better understand the metabolic adjustments under stress conditions, we assessed the levels of key primary metabolites. For instance, under stress conditions, plants accumulate soluble sugars to mitigate drought stress by acting as osmoprotectants and their aggregation might increase the plant stress tolerance [4]. To preserve leaf cell turgor, preserve membrane integrity, and stop protein denaturation, these soluble carbohydrates, and other suitable solutes are utilized as osmolytes. When carbon fixation is decreased, the accumulation of these osmolytes indicates carbon direction for stress mitigation [57].

Under control conditions, both wheat and Amaranthus exhibited higher glucose levels than under drought, with wheat showing the highest levels in both treatments (Fig. 4). Sucrose was the dominant sugar in both species. Notably, drought significantly increased (p ≤ 0.05) sucrose levels in wheat, where 1.73-fold higher than its control and approximately 2.32-fold more than in Amaranthus under both conditions (Fig. 4C). We assessed the accumulation of sucrose, glucose, fructose, and total sugars in both species under control and drought (Fig. 3). Sugars play critical roles in energy supply, carbon skeleton formation, osmotic adjustment, signaling, and antioxidative defense [41]. Drought-induced stomatal closure limits carbon uptake and reduces carbohydrate levels, but efficient sugar production and transport can enhance tolerance [22]. Moreover, sucrose, glucose, and fructose function as signaling molecules regulating metabolic gene expression under abiotic stress [57]. Under control conditions, Amaranthus leaves had the highest carbohydrate levels, which declined with increasing drought stress; insoluble carbohydrates significantly decreased under drought [37]. Conversely, drought stress significantly increased total sugars in both cultivars, with wheat showing the highest levels (Fig. 4D). In wheat, total sugars increased by 1.47-, 1.11-, and 1.55-fold compared to its control, drought-stressed Amaranthus, and Amaranthus control, respectively (Fig. 4D). Similar increases in total and soluble sugars under stress have been observed in various C3 and C4 plants [3, 41]. Wheat (C3) accumulates more sugars than maize (C4) under drought [3, 41]. Selim et al. [41] also reported increased sucrose accumulation in stressed C3 (Hordeum vulgare) and C4 (Zea mays) plants. Although drought typically reduces carbon fixation, soluble sugars like glucose, fructose, and sucrose can still accumulate [57]. This accumulation is often considered a stress response mechanism, as these sugars can act as osmoprotectants, signaling molecules, or provide energy reserves during adverse conditions.

The accumulation of sugars under drought stress provides essential carbon skeletons and energy for the biosynthesis of bioactive metabolites, such as organic acids and amino acids. Therefore, we further examined the levels of primary nitrogen-containing compounds including amino acids and polyamines. The amino acid biosynthesis pathway was one of the significant pathways with a significant number of meta-differentially expressed genes [22]. Herein, we measured the profile of several amino acids (Fig. 5 and Table 1). Generally, after drought stress treatment, amino acids were significantly (p < 0.05) increased in control Amaranthus compared to drought stress, except for glycine, alanine, glutamine, asparagine, serine, and phenylalanine were increased significantly (p < 0.05) under drought in Amaranthus.

Our results showed significantly increased amino acid levels in drought-stressed wheat compared to controls, except for arginine, ornithine, glutamine, and methionine, which were reduced (Fig. 5, Table 1). These stress-responsive amino acids act as signaling molecules, osmolytes, ROS scavengers, and metabolic precursors [22, 41]. Notably, proline, synthesized via glutamate and ornithine pathways, contributes to ROS scavenging, signaling, and protein stabilization, which may explain its role in Amaranthus drought tolerance [41]. Wheat under drought showed higher levels of leucine, glycine, alanine, glutamic acid, aspartate, and cystine, 1.24, 1.13, 1.66, 1.54, 1.41, and 1.23-fold more than drought-stressed Amaranthus, respectively (Table 1). Similarly, Guo et al. [15] reported increased amino acids under drought in wheat genotypes, while Ullah et al. [53] observed elevated BCAAs in Triticeae. Less and Galili [27] found drought rapidly induces amino acid catabolism. Soluble sugars like sucrose and mannitol also aid protein protection under drought.

Drought stress induces the levels of organic acids in wheat and Amaranthus under drought stress compared to the control. The highest levels of oxalic acid were recorded in Amaranthus (Fig. 5A), as they were 1.13, 1.49, and 1.46-fold more than corresponding drought wheat and both control Amaranthus and wheat, respectively, (Fig. 6A). Malic acid showed the highest levels in wheat under drought stress (Fig. 5B and Fig. 7). Similarly, the evaluations of organic acids levels in several wheat genotypes shown the fumaric and malic acid demonstrated a considerable increase to maintain ionic balance and nutrient absorption under drought stress [15]. According to AbdElgawad et al. [3], organic acids have been shown to enhance stress mitigation against heavy metal stress by acting as chelating agents and osmoprotectants. Moreover, the levels of citric acid were significantly increased in drought-stressed Amaranthus, and wheat compared to the control treatment. The highest value of citric acid was obtained with drought Amaranthus as they were 1.1, 1.41, and 1.30-fold more than corresponding drought wheat and both control Amaranthus and wheat, respectively, (Fig. 5C). The accumulation of high levels of succinic acid in both Amaranthus and wheat under drought stress suggests its crucial role in the plants'stress response and mitigation efforts. Succinic acid is a key intermediate in the TCA cycle, a central pathway for ATP production [10], indicating its importance in maintaining energy supply during adverse conditions.

Under drought stress, the overabundance of NADH inhibits all dehydrogenases. Mitochondria accumulate additional ATP for adverse situations by over-synthesizing succinate [51]. The high amount of succinate in TR39477 samples of Triticeae species, which has previously been noted for its resilience to drought stress may be associated with the plant's effective utilization of the TCA cycle, which increases energy production in water-limited environments [53].

Plants'membrane lipid composition changes in response to drought, but whether lipid remodeling aids in drought adaptation and recovery, especially in crops, is not fully understood [59]. Fatty acids, crucial for plasma membrane integrity, vary in response to abiotic stressors like drought and temperature [39]. This study measured fatty acid profiles in Amaranthus and wheat under both control and drought conditions. Drought stress significantly increased fatty acid levels in both plants (p ≤ 0.05) compared to controls (Table 2 and Figs. 6 and 7). Lipid remodeling plays a role in drought recovery and tolerance, with different fatty acids contributing uniquely. For instance, hexadecadienoic and palmitic acids are crucial for drought recovery in maize. Lipids enhance membrane fluidity and reduce damage under drought stress [59]. Under drought conditions, oleic (C18:1), palmitic (C16:0), and stearic (C18:0) acids significantly increased (p ≤ 0.05) in both wheat and Amaranthus compared to controls (Table 2, Fig. 6). Under control conditions, the levels of oleic, palmitic, stearic, myristic (C14:0), and arachidic (C20:0) acids were 83.3%, 59.8%, 45.4%, 35.7%, and 90.2% in wheat, and 78.8%, 80.6%, 73.8%, 46.6%, and 97.8% in Amaranthus, respectively (Table 2). Oleic, linoleic (18:2), and α-linolenic (18:3) acids are vital for membrane integrity, energy storage, and stress signaling but can also induce oxidative stress [18]. Similarly, drought-stressed maize showed increased palmitic, oleic, and linoleic acid levels, with genotype-specific responses [59].

Overall, the extensive metabolic change in wheat, indicating a robust stress response to drought. Its significant buildup of protective biochemicals suggests it was actively struggling to maintain normal function, deploying strong defenses to mitigate stress. Meanwhile, despite their usual water-use efficiency, C4 plants like Amaranthus can face metabolic limitations during drought, likely due to reduced photorespiration and fewer alternative electron sinks compared to C3 plants [13, 60].

Primary metabolic reprogramming under drought: elevated polyamines as plant survival strategies

The accumulation of amino acids under drought stress is closely linked to sugar-derived primary and secondary metabolite production via the TCA cycle [4, 22]. Polyamines are powerful ROS scavengers that improve plant resistance to abiotic stress [22]. We found increased polyamines under drought stress in C3 wheat but decreased in C4 Amaranthus, except for a slight increase in spermidine in Amaranthus (Table 3, Fig. 5). Karami et al. [22] also reported that spermidine, putrescine, and spermine are induced under stress. Putrescine, spermine, and total polyamines in Amaranthus were 1.09, 1.03, and 1.01 times higher under control conditions than under drought. In wheat, these levels were 1.28, 1.27, 1.41, and 1.31 times higher under drought stress. Thus, polyamine induction is higher in wheat than in Amaranthus under drought, despite no significant differences among treatments.

Both Amaranthus and wheat under control conditions had higher levels of ornithine and arginine, precursors to polyamines [11]. High levels of arginine increased polyamines to improve plant stress resistance [22]. Accumulation of polyamine by PEG stress, enhancing polyamine oxidase (PAO) and spermidine synthase (SPDS) activities [11] and expression in C4 plants [22]. PAO activity plays a role in converting spermidine and spermine to putrescine, protecting the photosynthetic apparatus [34].

Overall, drought stress induced marked physiological and metabolic changes in both wheat (C3) and Amaranthus (C4). While wheat exhibited a significant stress response, Amaranthus demonstrated greater physiological resilience under drought conditions, maintaining higher photosynthetic rates, Rubisco activity, stomatal conductance, and transpiration efficiency compared to wheat.

Both species accumulated higher levels of total sugars, sucrose, branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs), organic acids (e.g., succinic, malic), polyamines, and fatty acids (oleic, palmitic, linoleic), which are linked to osmotic balance, ROS scavenging, and energy supply under stress [4, 5, 22, 25, 39, 41]. Wheat enhanced Rubisco levels and greater accumulation of metabolites suggest stronger metabolic adaptability than Amaranthus under drought. Although C4 plants like Amaranthus are typically more water-use efficient, they may be more metabolically limited during drought due to reduced photorespiration and alternative electron sinks [13, 60]. These findings highlight the role of metabolite reprogramming in drought resilience and confirm wheat’s superior biochemical capacity to cope with water-limited environments.

Conclusion

Amaranthus (C4) exhibited higher biomass, photosynthetic rates,x and elevated metabolite levels compared to the wheat (C3), reflecting its inherent efficiency under optimal conditions. Therefore, Amaranthus had greater physiological resilience to drought than wheat. This superior resilience in Amaranthus was also correlated with reduced oxidative stress damage. In contrast, wheat displayed a robust drought stress response, characterized by a significant accumulation of protective compounds such as sucrose, essential amino acids, polyamines, and various organic (succinic/malic) and fatty acids. This extensive biochemical accumulation suggests wheat was actively struggling to maintain normal physiological function under drought, vigorously deploying its defenses to mitigate stress. On the other hand, Amaranthus can metabolically limit them due to reduced photorespiration and fewer alternative electron sinks compared to C3 plants. These findings highlight distinct adaptive strategies between C3 and C4 plants under drought stress. Future research should aim to elucidate the specific metabolic pathways and regulatory networks that underpin drought tolerance in wheat, as well as investigate genetic and agronomic approaches to enhance these mechanisms. Such efforts could lead to the development of crops with improved resilience and nutritional quality, addressing the challenges posed by climate change and water scarcity.
