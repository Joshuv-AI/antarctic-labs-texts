Integrative diagnostic approach in a rare case of primary thyroid leiomyosarcoma with distant metastasis: a case report and literature review
Authors: Jiafeng Li, Bin Wu, Zhuoting Chen, Baibei Li, Muge Liu, Xiongsheng Xiao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrimary thyroid leiomyosarcoma (TLS) is an exceptionally rare malignant tumor, with fewer than 50 cases reported in the literature worldwide. In this case, the thyroid lesion was initially misdiagnosed as a benign nodule based on ultrasound and fine-needle aspiration (FNA), and treated with microwave ablation. However, the mass continued to enlarge.Case DescriptionA 69-year-old female patient was re-evaluated due to progressive enlargement of the thyroid lesion. Core needle biopsy revealed a spindle cell neoplasm. Immunohistochemistry showed positivity for smooth muscle actin (SMA), desmin, calponin, and p53. Given the presence of airway compression symptoms and the patient’s strong desire for surgery, the clinical team developed an active surgical plan and performed resection of the thyroid and involved adjacent tissues to relieve local compression and control disease progression. Postoperative histopathological examination confirmed high-grade TLS, with evidence of invasion into the recurrent laryngeal nerve and internal jugular vein. High-throughput sequencing identified coexisting mutations in neurofibromin 1 (NF1) and BCL6 corepressor like 1 (BCORL1), along with alterations in tumor protein p53 (TP53) and the telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) promoter region. Two months after R0 resection, the patient developed cervical recurrence and distant metastases involving the lungs, liver, bones, and mediastinum. In May 2025, the patient initiated intravenous chemotherapy consisting of pegylated liposomal doxorubicin and ifosfamide. The treatment response is currently being monitored during follow-up.ConclusionsWhen TLS initially presents with benign-appearing ultrasound and FNA findings, misdiagnosis may easily occur. This case highlights the importance of early integration of histopathological and molecular evaluation for progressive thyroid lesions. The co-occurrence of NF1 and BCORL1 mutations, along with TP53 and TERT promoter alterations, may underlie the tumor’s aggressive biological behavior and supports the incorporation of molecular subtyping strategies in TLS management.Keywords: Primary thyroid leiomyosarcoma (primary TLS), benign-mimicking lesion, misdiagnosis, multimodal therapy, case report

Highlight box

Key findings

• We report a rare case of primary thyroid leiomyosarcoma (TLS) in a 69-year-old woman that was initially misdiagnosed as a benign thyroid nodule and treated with microwave ablation.

• The final diagnosis of high-grade TLS was established based on core needle biopsy, immunohistochemistry, and postoperative pathology.

• Next-generation sequencing revealed concurrent mutations in neurofibromin 1 (NF1) and BCL6 corepressor like 1 (BCORL1), along with alterations in tumor protein p53 and the telomerase reverse transcriptase promoter.

What is known and what is new?

• TLS is an extremely rare thyroid malignancy that may present with benign features on imaging and fine-needle aspiration (FNA), leading to misdiagnosis and treatment delay.

• To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of TLS harboring coexisting mutations in both NF1 and BCORL1. This case also emphasizes the diagnostic value of combining core needle biopsy and molecular profiling in patients with progressive thyroid lesions and discordant imaging-FNA findings.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Early histopathological and molecular assessment should be considered for thyroid lesions with atypical clinical behavior, especially when imaging and FNA suggest benignity.

• Incorporating molecular subtyping into TLS evaluation may improve diagnostic accuracy, explain tumor aggressiveness, and guide individualized treatment strategies.

Introduction

Leiomyosarcoma (LMS) is a rare malignant tumor originating from mesenchymal tissue, most commonly found in the retroperitoneum, gastrointestinal tract, and soft tissues of the trunk and extremities, accounting for less than 1% of all adult malignancies (1). Unlike papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC), which arises from follicular epithelial cells and generally carries a favorable prognosis (2,3), primary thyroid leiomyosarcoma (TLS) is exceedingly rare, representing approximately 0.014% of all thyroid malignancies (4,5). TLS is thought to originate from the smooth muscle cells of intrathyroidal blood vessels (6), and typically demonstrates aggressive biological behavior, rapid clinical progression, and a poor prognosis.

Clinically, TLS often presents as a painless, rapidly enlarging neck mass. However, its imaging and cytological features are nonspecific, frequently leading to initial misdiagnosis as a benign nodule or another tumor type. On ultrasound, TLS typically presents as a hypoechoic, ill-defined solid lesion, often lacking high-risk features defined by the Thyroid Imaging Reporting and Data System (TI-RADS), such as microcalcifications or infiltrative margins. Consequently, some lesions may be incorrectly classified as TI-RADS category 3–4, potentially mimicking benign or low-grade malignancies and increasing the risk of early misdiagnosis (7,8). In addition, due to tumor heterogeneity and sampling limitations, fine-needle aspiration (FNA) often fails to yield a definitive diagnosis, contributing to a high false-negative rate (9). Delayed diagnosis may result in local invasion into critical adjacent structures such as the recurrent laryngeal nerve, trachea, esophagus, or major cervical vessels.

Given the extreme rarity of primary TLS, no standardized diagnostic or therapeutic guidelines have been established to date. Its management typically depends on multidisciplinary team (MDT) decision-making, while early diagnosis remains particularly challenging due to its nonspecific clinical and radiologic features. We report a case of TLS initially misdiagnosed as a benign thyroid nodule and treated with microwave ablation, after which the lesion showed progressive growth. A definitive diagnosis was eventually achieved through a comprehensive approach combining imaging, core needle biopsy, immunohistochemistry, and molecular profiling. Following MDT evaluation, an individualized surgical plan was developed, and the patient underwent resection of the primary tumor. Although the surgery was technically successful, the patient experienced rapid local recurrence and distant metastases within a short postoperative period, suggesting poor responsiveness to conventional surgical management. This case highlights the highly aggressive behavior of TLS and underscores the limitations of surgery-based strategies. It also emphasizes the need to explore the potential value of neoadjuvant and multimodal therapeutic approaches in managing atypical thyroid malignancies, with the ultimate goal of delaying disease progression and improving patient outcomes. Case presentation

A 69-year-old female patient presented with a progressive 3-month history of bilateral neck swelling noted during routine examination. She had a medical history of hypertension for 30 years and type 2 diabetes mellitus for over 20 years, both well controlled with long-term medication. Three months prior, a thyroid nodule was incidentally detected in the left lobe during a routine health examination. Ultrasonography at an outside hospital identified a thyroid nodule, and FNA cytology was reported as Bethesda category II, consistent with a benign nodular goiter. The patient subsequently underwent microwave ablation of the left thyroid nodule.

During postoperative follow-up, the local mass showed no significant reduction in size and instead gradually increased, accompanied by a mild pulling sensation in the neck, without skin erythema or ulceration. She denied any family history of malignancy or occupational exposure to radiation. Physical examination revealed a firm, ill-defined mass measuring approximately 6.0 cm × 5.0 cm in the left neck region and a 2.0 cm × 1.0 cm rubbery, poorly mobile nodule in the right lobe, both moving with swallowing. No tenderness or overlying skin changes were noted. Palpable cervical lymphadenopathy was absent bilaterally (Figure 1).

Visual inspection of the patient’s neck showing a prominent mass in the left cervical region. The overlying skin appears intact, without erythema or ulceration.

Flexible laryngoscopy demonstrated limited mobility of the left vocal cord, raising concern for involvement of the left recurrent laryngeal nerve.

Ultrasonography revealed a solid, hypoechoic nodule in the left thyroid lobe, measuring approximately 6.83 cm × 3.77 cm × 5.21 cm. The lesion exhibited an irregular shape, heterogeneous internal echotexture, and centripetal enhancement on contrast-enhanced ultrasound, with intralesional non-enhancing areas. It was categorized as TI-RADS 5 (Figure 2). Multiple mixed cystic-solid nodules were noted in the right lobe, the largest measuring approximately 1.46 cm × 1.24 cm, without definitive malignant features.

Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) demonstrated marked enlargement of the left thyroid lobe with poorly defined margins. The lesion contained low-density necrotic areas and punctate calcifications, causing rightward tracheal deviation. The left internal jugular vein was compressed and deformed, and a low-density intraluminal filling defect approximately 21 mm in size was noted, suggesting possible thrombosis or tumor thrombus. No significant space-occupying lesion was detected in the right thyroid lobe. Chest CT revealed multiple small pulmonary nodules, with the largest solid nodule located in the anterior segment of the left upper lobe, measuring approximately 6 mm. Distant metastasis could not be excluded and required further clinical correlation and follow-up.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed heterogeneous signal intensity within the left thyroid mass. The lesion appeared hyperintense on T2-weighted imaging and demonstrated restricted diffusion on diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI). Contrast-enhanced MRI revealed heterogeneous enhancement with ill-defined margins. The lesion was indistinct from the adjacent left internal jugular vein, with an intraluminal filling defect observed. The right thyroid lobe showed no abnormal enhancement or focal signal changes. Multiple enlarged lymph nodes were present bilaterally in the cervical region, suggestive of nodal metastasis. The mass also compressed the trachea and surrounding soft tissues. Both vocal cords appeared thickened, and the glottic chink was mildly narrowed.

An initial FNA performed at the referring hospital indicated a benign lesion (Bethesda category II). However, repeat core needle biopsy at our institution revealed bundles of spindle cells with marked nuclear atypia and frequent mitotic figures, suggestive of a malignant mesenchymal tumor (Figure 3). Immunohistochemical staining showed positive expression for smooth muscle actin (SMA), desmin, calponin, vimentin, and myoglobin. The Ki-67 proliferation index was approximately 30%, and P53 showed strong nuclear positivity in about 90% of tumor cells. Thyroglobulin (TG) and thyroid transcription factor-1 (TTF-1) were negative, supporting smooth muscle origin and excluding thyroid follicular epithelial lineage.

Core needle biopsy of the left thyroid lobe. (A,B) Histopathological examination revealed a densely cellular lesion composed of spindle-shaped cells with marked nuclear atypia and abundant mitotic figures, indicative of high-grade malignancy. Immunohistochemical findings supported a diagnosis consistent with leiomyosarcoma (hematoxylin and eosin staining; A, ×200; B, ×400).

Based on MDT recommendations, and considering the preoperative imaging, core needle biopsy findings, and clinical presentation, the tumor was assessed to be highly aggressive with rapid progression. A comprehensive surgical strategy with curative intent was subsequently formulated as the core component of treatment. Under general anesthesia, the patient underwent total thyroidectomy, functional neck dissection involving left cervical levels II–V and bilateral central compartment (level VI), and ligation of the left internal jugular vein due to tumor invasion. In this context, “functional neck dissection” refers to the en bloc removal of lymphatic tissues from specified levels while preserving the spinal accessory nerve, sternocleidomastoid muscle, and internal jugular vein—except where oncologically compromised, as in this case.

Intraoperative exploration revealed large solid masses in both thyroid lobes. The tumors were firm in texture, poorly demarcated, and extensively adherent to surrounding tissues. No direct invasion of the trachea or esophagus was observed. The left-sided tumor measured approximately 18 cm × 15 cm × 12 cm, and the right-sided mass measured approximately 12 cm × 10 cm × 10 cm. Both lesions were massive, ill-defined, and showed infiltrative growth patterns, suggesting high local aggressiveness and potential for extension.

The left recurrent laryngeal nerve was tightly adherent to and encased by the tumor and could not be preserved, necessitating resection. A tumor thrombus was identified within the lumen of the left internal jugular vein, with disruption of the vascular wall and obscured anatomical planes, leading to intraoperative vascular resection and ligation. These findings indicated extensive tumor invasion into critical anatomical structures. Therefore, complete resection (R0 resection) was set as the surgical goal.

The procedure was completed successfully, including total thyroidectomy and functional lymph node dissection of the planned regions. Intraoperative frozen section pathology revealed a spindle cell sarcoma, initially suggestive of a high-grade mesenchymal malignancy (Figure 4).

Intraoperative and gross pathological features of the thyroid tumor. (A) Intraoperatively, the thyroid tumor appeared markedly enlarged, firm in texture, with ill-defined borders, and extensively adherent to surrounding tissues. (B) The tumor was resected en bloc, including the left thyroid mass and a segment of the left internal jugular vein. A tumor thrombus was visible within the venous lumen.

Histopathological examination of the resected specimen confirmed a high-grade malignant spindle cell tumor. Microscopically, the tumor was composed of intersecting fascicles of spindle-shaped cells with marked nuclear atypia, brisk mitotic activity, and extensive geographic necrosis (Figure 5). According to the French Federation of Cancer Centers Sarcoma Group grading system (FNCLCC) histological grading system, the tumor was classified as Grade III, indicative of poor differentiation and aggressive biological behavior.

The cut surface showed disrupted architecture, poorly defined borders, and focal necrosis, suggestive of an infiltrative growth pattern. (C,D) Histologically, the tumor was composed of densely interlacing fascicles of spindle cells exhibiting marked cytologic atypia, abundant mitotic figures, and extensive necrosis. FNCLCC, French Federation of Cancer Centers Sarcoma Group grading system.

Immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated diffuse positivity for multiple smooth muscle differentiation markers, including SMA, desmin, calponin, vimentin, myoglobin, and IMP3, supporting smooth muscle origin. Additionally, strong nuclear positivity for p53 was observed in approximately 90% of tumor cells, and the Ki-67 proliferation index reached 30%, indicating a high proliferative rate.

In contrast, immunostaining for follicular epithelial-derived markers, such as TG and TTF-1, was negative, further excluding a follicular epithelial origin. Integrating histological morphology, immunophenotypic profile, intraoperative findings, and next-generation sequencing (NGS) results, a definitive diagnosis of primary TLS was established. This entity represents a rare, mesenchymal-derived thyroid malignancy characterized by high invasiveness and rapid clinical progression.

To further characterize the tumor’s molecular profile, high-throughput DNA sequencing was performed on formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tumor tissue obtained postoperatively. The analysis identified multiple alterations in key cancer-related genes, including a missense mutation in tumor protein p53 (TP53) missense mutation p.R282W, a promoter mutation in the telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) gene, specifically C228T, and potentially pathogenic variants in neurofibromin 1 (NF1), cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor 2A (CDKN2A), and BCL6 corepressor like 1 (BCORL1) (Table 1). These mutations are associated with dysregulation of several oncogenic pathways—namely, cell cycle control, evasion of apoptosis, chromatin remodeling, and telomere maintenance—suggesting a possible molecular basis for the tumor’s highly aggressive clinical behavior.

Despite negative surgical margins confirmed by postoperative histopathology and stable thyroid function maintained with hormone replacement therapy, the patient developed locoregional recurrence within 2 months, initially involving the left cervical lymph nodes, followed by new metastatic lesions in the right level III neck region. Imaging studies revealed rapid disease progression, with confirmed involvement of the lungs, liver, mediastinal lymph nodes, and skeletal system, indicating a highly aggressive tumor with systemic dissemination potential.

To control disease progression, intravenous chemotherapy with liposomal doxorubicin and ifosfamide was initiated on May 14, 2025. To prevent chemotherapy-induced myelosuppression, pegylated recombinant human granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (PEG-rhG-CSF) was administered on May 17. Given the risk of immunosuppression during treatment, adjunctive use of thymosin was considered based on a comprehensive assessment of the patient’s clinical condition and financial capacity.

The patient is currently undergoing the first cycle of chemotherapy, and both therapeutic efficacy and tolerability are under ongoing evaluation. At the time of this report, the patient remains alive and under close clinical follow-up.

Discussion

TLS is an exceptionally rare primary malignancy of the thyroid gland, with fewer than 50 confirmed cases reported worldwide, accounting for only approximately 0.014% of all thyroid cancers (4,5). The clinicopathological features of earlier cases have been systematically summarized by Asiri et al. and Bashir et al. (1,4). Together with the recently reported cases listed in Table 2, these studies formed the basis of our pooled analysis, which also includes the present case (see Table S1 for additional references). Notably, Tulbah et al. described a TLS case in a 6-year-old boy with Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) infection, raising the possibility of viral association in immunocompromised hosts (12). In clinical settings, limited awareness and the lack of specific early symptoms often lead to misdiagnosis as benign thyroid nodules or low-grade malignancies. At present, no standardized diagnostic criteria or established treatment guidelines exist; management is largely guided by case reports and retrospective analyses. Consequently, further investigation into the clinical features and diagnostic pitfalls of TLS is essential to facilitate earlier recognition and optimize therapeutic approaches.

The typical clinical presentation of TLS is a rapidly enlarging neck mass that is firm, poorly demarcated, and usually involves a single thyroid lobe. No consistent site of origin has been identified. In our case, the tumor also demonstrated an aggressive growth pattern. According to Asiri et al., the average tumor diameter in TLS is approximately 5.88 cm, with the largest previously reported reaching up to 13.5 cm (1). Interestingly, one patient was recorded with tumor sizes of 9 and 16 cm in different studies (1,4), likely reflecting discrepancies between preoperative imaging and postoperative pathological measurements. If the 16 cm measurement based on intraoperative or pathological findings is considered, it may represent one of the largest TLS tumors reported to date. This highlights the potential for significant local expansion in select cases of TLS.

Approximately 45% of patients with TLS present with hoarseness, dysphagia, or respiratory discomfort, suggesting potential invasion of adjacent critical structures such as the recurrent laryngeal nerve, trachea, or esophagus. Additional symptoms may include progressive weight loss, localized pain, or cervical lymphadenopathy, which can lead to misdiagnosis as benign or low-grade malignant lesions. In rare cases, tumor progression results in skin ulceration and fistula formation, likely due to central necrosis and breakdown of overlying soft tissues (13). Furthermore, two patients with large tumors reported unilateral arm pain, potentially attributable to brachial plexus involvement or infiltration of deep cervical structures (14,15). Metastatic spread is also common in TLS. Pulmonary metastasis has been observed in approximately 43% of patients, and lymph node involvement is reported in about 10% (1,4). Other metastatic sites include the liver, bones, peritoneum, and brain, with cardiac involvement being exceptionally rare (16). This aggressive disseminative behavior may be linked to the tumor’s rich vascular supply and the expression of myogenic markers such as SMA and desmin, which are associated with increased cellular motility and adhesion. These characteristics underscore TLS’s high potential for both hematogenous and lymphatic spread.

Given the nonspecific clinical presentation and high metastatic potential of TLS, early diagnosis relies heavily on the combined use of imaging modalities and immunohistochemical markers (10,17). Radiologically, TLS typically manifests as a solid thyroid mass of variable size with ill-defined margins and heterogeneous enhancement. However, these features lack specificity and may mimic other aggressive thyroid malignancies, such as papillary carcinoma, anaplastic carcinoma, or poorly differentiated thyroid cancer. In contrast, immunohistochemistry provides greater diagnostic specificity. TLS characteristically shows positivity for SMA, desmin, calponin, and vimentin, and negativity for epithelial and neurogenic markers such as cytokeratin (CK), TG, TTF-1, and S-100, as reported in previous studies (1,4,10,18). This immunophenotypic profile supports a smooth muscle origin, distinguishing it from tumors of epithelial or neurogenic lineage. Given the histologic resemblance of TLS to other spindle cell tumors, accurate differential diagnosis is essential. Key entities to exclude include anaplastic thyroid carcinoma, medullary carcinoma, and malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumor (MPNST) (1,4,17). A systematic approach integrating imaging characteristics with immunophenotypic evaluation is critical to minimize diagnostic errors, clarify tumor origin, and ensure accurate histopathological classification.

Most patients underwent surgical treatment, which remains the primary therapeutic approach. Approximately 75% of patients received either radical or palliative resection (1,4,7,10,19). Although surgery may offer some survival benefit, the aggressive nature of TLS—characterized by local invasion, recurrence, and distant metastasis—renders postoperative management critical for improving long-term outcomes. Some studies have suggested that adjuvant chemotherapy or radiotherapy may help alleviate local symptoms, though their impact on overall survival appears limited (5,7,18,20,21).

The role of surgery as the standard first-line therapy for TLS remains a matter of debate, particularly in patients with highly invasive disease or identifiable molecular targets. In such cases, targeted therapy and immunotherapy may offer greater potential for prolonging disease-free survival and improving long-term prognosis. Against this background, neoadjuvant therapy—defined as systemic treatment administered prior to surgery—may offer several advantages. Preoperative chemotherapy or targeted agents could reduce tumor burden, facilitating more complete and safer surgical resection, especially for tumors encasing vital structures such as the recurrent laryngeal nerve or major cervical vessels. In some cases, this approach may convert an inoperable or borderline-resectable tumor into one amenable to curative surgery. Furthermore, neoadjuvant therapy may help delay clinical progression by suppressing aggressive tumor biology at an early stage and addressing micrometastatic disease that often escapes detection but contributes to early relapse. Although formal evidence in TLS is lacking due to its extreme rarity, the rationale for neoadjuvant therapy is supported by experience in other high-risk soft tissue sarcomas (STS). Evidence from a phase III trial in high-risk STS supports neoadjuvant anthracycline-ifosfamide chemotherapy as a survival-prolonging, standard approach—suggesting potential applicability in aggressive TLS (22). These findings suggest that similar preoperative strategies may hold promise for patients with aggressive or borderline-resectable TLS. Future studies are warranted to evaluate the safety, optimal timing, and clinical utility of neoadjuvant regimens in TLS. Given the limited success of conventional treatment strategies, ongoing investigations into targeted therapies, immunotherapeutics, and radiotherapy offer promising avenues for the development of more effective multimodal management approaches.

Case reports have described temporary remission following combination chemotherapy with doxorubicin and ifosfamide (17). In another instance, an immune-based regimen demonstrated some degree of local control: dendritic cells (DCs) loaded with autologous tumor lysate (>1×108) were injected subcutaneously, followed 14 days later by intravenous infusion of DC-activated T lymphocytes (>1×1010) derived from peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) (23). Additionally, overexpression of the proto-oncogene c-Kit—a receptor tyrosine kinase—has been implicated as a potential oncogenic driver in uterine and TLS, offering a rationale for targeted inhibition. Imatinib, a tyrosine kinase inhibitor, has been trialed in selected cases (4,17,24,25). However, its efficacy in TLS remains inconclusive, as available data do not demonstrate a significant survival benefit. Large-scale prospective studies are needed to validate its role. Currently, most evidence is derived from isolated case reports, making it difficult to establish standardized treatment guidelines. Optimizing individualized treatment strategies for patients with TLS remains a significant clinical challenge.

Despite the widespread use of aggressive surgical interventions, the overall prognosis of TLS remains poor. A review of the literature indicates that approximately 55% of patients died during follow-up, primarily due to tumor progression or treatment-related complications (4,10,24,26,27). Although isolated reports have documented survival durations of up to 60 months (15), the majority of patients experience significantly shorter survival times, underscoring the aggressive biological behavior and dismal prognosis associated with this malignancy. Large tumor size and the presence of distant metastases at the time of surgery have been identified as adverse prognostic factors in several studies (6,11,16,18,28,29). Even when complete surgical resection is achieved, the tumor’s inherent invasiveness and strong metastatic potential often result in postoperative recurrence. These observations highlight the urgent need for early diagnosis, precise staging, and individualized postoperative strategies to improve long-term outcomes in patients with TLS.

In this case, initial imaging revealed a thyroid nodule classified as TI-RADS 3–4. FNA cytology was reported as Bethesda category II, favoring a benign diagnosis, and the patient subsequently underwent microwave ablation. However, follow-up imaging showed an increase in nodule size, which was inconsistent with the initial assessment and suggested that the lesion’s malignant potential may have been underestimated. To further evaluate the lesion, core-needle biopsy was performed, and a diagnosis of TLS was ultimately confirmed based on histopathology, immunohistochemistry, and high-throughput sequencing. In this case, the tumor’s spindle-cell morphology, minimal cytologic atypia, and deep location may have limited the diagnostic accuracy of FNA. When imaging findings suggest progressive disease despite benign cytology, clinicians should remain vigilant for low-grade malignancy and pursue core biopsy or excisional sampling for definitive diagnosis.

Molecular profiling in this patient identified concurrent truncating mutations in NF1 (p.I1803Yfs*38) and BCORL1 (p.H1144Tfs*78), a co-mutation pattern not previously reported in TLS, raising the possibility of a novel synergistic oncogenic mechanism. NF1 loss may enhance RAS/MAPK signaling, while BCORL1 inactivation could disrupt PRC1-mediated chromatin repression. Additional alterations in TP53 (p.R282W) and the TERT promoter (C228T) suggest multi-pathway disruption involving cell cycle regulation, apoptosis, and telomere maintenance. This molecular profile may be associated with the aggressive clinical course observed, including early recurrence and widespread metastases to the lungs, liver, bones, and mediastinal lymph nodes, despite R0 resection. Although surgery remains the mainstay of TLS management, this case suggests that surgery alone may be insufficient in cases with aggressive molecular features. While no approved targeted therapies exist for these mutations, the involved pathways are under active investigation in other tumor types, offering a rationale for future personalized strategies. This case underscores the diagnostic and prognostic value of comprehensive genomic profiling in rare thyroid malignancies and highlights the need for TLS to be included in rare tumor registries and multi-institutional studies to guide evidence-based treatment and improve outcomes.

Conclusions

Primary TLS is an exceptionally rare malignancy, with early imaging and cytological features often resembling benign lesions, leading to frequent misdiagnosis and delayed treatment. In this case, the lesion was initially diagnosed as a benign nodule and treated with microwave ablation, but subsequently showed continuous progression. A final diagnosis of high-grade TLS was established through histopathological, immunophenotypic, and molecular analyses. Despite achieving an R0 resection, the patient experienced rapid postoperative recurrence and widespread metastases, indicating the tumor’ s aggressive nature and limited responsiveness to conventional surgical intervention. Notably, this case identified concurrent NF1 and BCORL1 mutations, along with TP53 and TERT promoter alterations—an uncommon mutational profile not clearly reported in the current literature—which may underlie the tumor’s malignant behavior. This case underscores the importance of early histological and molecular evaluation in thyroid lesions with rapid progression and discordant imaging-cytology findings. Comprehensive integration of real-world clinical data and molecular subtyping is urgently needed to improve the diagnostic precision, risk stratification, and development of personalized therapeutic strategies for TLS.
