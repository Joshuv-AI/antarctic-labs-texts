Multiomic data integration unveils molecular mechanisms underlying chestnut and skin tissue differentiation in Dezhou donkeys
Authors: Wenting Chen, Junjie Liu, Abd Ullah, Manna Dou, Yongdong Peng, Muhammad Zahoor Khan, Chunming Wang, Changfa Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDonkey chestnuts are hairless, darkly pigmented keratinized structures located on the medial forelimbs, anatomically distinct from surrounding skin. Despite their unique morphology, the molecular mechanisms underlying their formation and function remain poorly understood.MethodsAn integrated multi-omics approach was applied to chestnut and skin tissues collected from four Dezhou donkeys. Bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) was performed on six samples to assess transcriptomic differences, followed by single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) to resolve cellular composition, and proteomics to characterize protein expression patterns.ResultsChestnut tissue displayed exceptionally high keratin expression, including KRT1, KRT5, KRT10, and KRT14 (TPM > 13,000), and was enriched in endothelial cells, pericytes, T cells, and mast cells. Proteomic analysis revealed significant upregulation of collagen I (COL1A1/COL1A2), indicative of active extracellular matrix remodeling. Pathway analysis identified enrichment in peroxisome activity, fatty acid metabolism, PPAR signaling, and PI3K-Akt networks.ConclusionThese findings collectively demonstrate that donkey chestnut is a metabolically active, specialized barrier tissue with a distinctive cellular and molecular profile. Its unique signatures related to keratinization, pigmentation, and immune cell enrichment suggest potential roles in barrier defense and anti-inflammatory regulation, providing a foundation for further investigation into the biological significance of this understudied structure.

Introduction

The Dezhou donkey (Equus asinus), a prestigious Chinese breed renowned for its superior meat quality and distinctive coat coloration, represents a valuable genetic resource with significant economic and cultural importance in livestock production [1, 2]. Characterized by their robust constitution, exceptional adaptability, and unique phenotypic traits, Dezhou donkeys have garnered increasing attention in both agricultural practices and scientific research [3]. Among the most notable characteristics observed is the distinctive chestnut coloration pattern, which exhibits regional variation across different anatomical sites, particularly when examining the morphological differences between specialized tissues such as chestnuts and adjacent skin [4]. Previously our research team reported the diverse morphological configurations of forelimb chestnuts. Comprehensive morphological and histological analyses also confirmed prominent structural differences between chestnut and adjacent skin tissues [4]. These observed phenotypic differences suggest that elucidating the underlying molecular mechanisms responsible for such tissue-specific variations would provide valuable insights into the developmental and functional characteristics of these specialized structures.

Multi-omics approach has revolutionized our understanding of complex biological systems by integrating data from multiple molecular levels, including genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics [5, 6]. This comprehensive analytical framework enables researchers to decipher the intricate molecular mechanisms underlying phenotypic variations and tissue-specific characteristics [7]. In livestock species, multi-omics studies have provided unprecedented insights into production traits, disease resistance, and adaptive mechanisms [8, 9].

Tissue-specific gene expression and molecular profiles play crucial roles in determining morphological and functional characteristics in mammals [10]. The chestnut, a specialized keratinized structure found on the inner legs of equids, represents a unique evolutionary vestige with distinct molecular signatures compared to conventional skin tissue [11]. Understanding the molecular differences between these tissues is essential for elucidating the genetic basis of coat color determination and tissue differentiation in donkeys [12].

Previous studies in equine species have identified key genes and pathways involved in coat color determination, including melanocortin 1 receptor (MC1R), agouti signaling protein (ASIP), and tyrosinase-related proteins [13, 14]. However, comprehensive multi-omics comparisons between specialized tissues like chestnuts and skin in donkeys remain limited. The integration of transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic data can provide a holistic view of the molecular landscape governing tissue-specific characteristics and color phenotypes [15].

Advances in high-throughput sequencing technologies and mass spectrometry have made large-scale multi-omics studies increasingly feasible and cost-effective [16]. RNA-sequencing enables comprehensive transcriptome profiling, while proteomics and metabolomics provide insights into functional molecular products and biochemical pathways [17]. The integration of these complementary approaches allows for the identification of key regulatory networks and biomarkers associated with specific phenotypic traits [18].

The aim of this study was to perform a preliminary multi-omics comparative analysis of chestnut tissue and skin tissue in Dezhou donkeys, to explore the molecular mechanisms underlying their tissue-specific characteristics, and to identify key genes, proteins and cells associated with the two distinct tissue types. Through integrated transcriptomic and proteomic analyses, we aimed to provide novel insights into the genetic basis of tissue differentiation and contribute to the broader understanding of equine biology and breeding applications.

Materials and methods

Ethical statement

The study protocol was approved by the institutional Special Committee of Scientific Research Ethic of Liaocheng University (AP2025031726).

Experimental animals and management

Two provincial breeding farms for Dezhou donkeys, both authorized by Shandong Province and located in northwest Shandong, were selected for this study. Both facilities employed similar management protocols, with animals fed twice daily and provided ad libitum access to water as described in previous study [19].

Sample collection and processing

The donkeys involved in this study were processed at a licensed local commercial abattoir for non-research purposes, in accordance with standard commercial livestock slaughter practices. Prior to slaughter, all animals were subjected to a 12-hour fasting period to reduce physiological stress and ensure animal welfare. Each animal was rendered fully and verifiably unconscious by electrical stunning at 220 V applied continuously for 20 s; complete loss of consciousness was confirmed before any subsequent procedure was initiated. The animals were then humanely slaughtered by exsanguination in strict compliance with standard humane slaughter protocols established for livestock. Immediately following humane slaughter at the licensed commercial abattoir, post-mortem tissue samples were collected from the left forelimbs of four female Dezhou donkeys of similar age and body weight. Using a sterile biopsy punch as a standardized post-mortem tissue collection tool, approximately 1 cm³ of tissue was excised from the chestnut region and from the normal skin located 5 cm superior to the chestnut, with dimensions confirmed using digital calipers. A total of seven samples were obtained and designated as follows: chestnut samples (A1–A4) and skin samples (B1–B3). For bulk transcriptome and proteome sequencing, samples A1–A3 and B1–B3 were used, following the principle of three biological replicates per group. For single-cell transcriptome sequencing, sample A2 was initially planned for library construction; however, it was excluded due to unsatisfactory quality control resulting from cell dissociation failure. Sample A4, collected from a fourth Dezhou donkey of identical age and management conditions, was obtained as a necessary replacement to ensure data integrity. Accordingly, single-cell sequencing was performed on samples A1, A4, B1, and B2. The skin samples were collected from exactly the same anatomical sites as those for histological analysis, ensuring consistency and comparability between histological characterization and subsequent transcriptomic and proteomic analyses. Following collection, all tissue samples were immediately rinsed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and sectioned into appropriate sizes for downstream analyses. For transcriptome and proteome sequencing, samples A1–A3 and B1–B3 were placed in sterile, RNase-free cryotubes, immediately frozen on dry ice, and transported to Glbizzia Biosciences Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). For single-cell transcriptome sequencing, samples A1, A4, B1, and B2 were preserved in cryotubes containing tissue preservation solution and transported to the same facility under 4 °C conditions.

RNA sequencing and bioinformatics analysis

Total RNA was extracted from chestnut tissue samples (chestnut tissue group, n = 3) and skin tissue samples (normal skin tissue group, n = 3) using the RNeasy Kit (Qiagen N. V, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. In this study, we collected chestnut tissue and adjacent normal skin tissue from Dezhou donkeys for multi-omics and phenotypic comparison. It should be noted that the two types of samples are anatomically and functionally distinct specialized tissues. This work is designed for inter-tissue comparative analysis, rather than a typical intervention experiment with treatment and blank control groups.

RNA integrity and genomic DNA contamination were assessed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. RNA purity and concentration were determined using a NanoPhotometer spectrophotometer, while RNA integrity was evaluated using the Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent Technologies Inc.,USA). Sequencing libraries were prepared from high-quality total RNA using the NEBNext® Ultra™ RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina® (New England Biolabs, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Library quality was validated, and libraries were pooled based on their effective concentrations and target sequencing depth requirements. Sequencing was performed on the Illumina platform to generate 150-bp paired-end reads. Raw sequencing images were converted to sequence data (reads) using CASAVA base calling software (version 1.6, Illumina Inc., San Diego, USA) with Illumina sequencing identifiers.

Raw data underwent quality filtering according to the following criteria: reads containing more than 15 ambiguous bases (N) were removed; reads in which more than 50% of bases had a Phred quality score ≤ 5 were removed; reads shorter than 2 bases were removed; and adapter sequences were automatically detected and removed. Clean reads were aligned to the donkey reference genome (Equus asinus, Ensembl release 110, assembly ASM1607732v2). Raw data underwent quality filtering and sequencing error assessment to obtain clean reads suitable for downstream analysis. Clean reads were aligned to the reference genome using HISAT2 (version 2.2.1) software [20]. Variant site analysis was performed using GATK (version 4.2.2.0) [21], with each variant site statistically characterized based on SnpEff annotation information, focusing on functional, regional, and impact statistics. StringTie software (version 2.1.6) [22] was used for novel gene assembly and structural annotation, followed by functional annotation using the NR database. Gene expression quantification was performed using StringTie to count reads mapping to each gene, including newly predicted genes [23]. Differential expression Genes (DEGs) analysis was conducted using DESeq2 software (version1.34.0) [24] on raw read counts generated from STAR alignment (quantMode GeneCounts), following the standard DESeq2 workflow. Prior to testing, genes with zero expression across all samples were excluded. DESeq2 applies independent filtering by default to remove genes with little chance of showing significant differences, and potential outlier counts are handled using Cook’s distance. P-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. DEGs were identified using the criteria: |log₂(fold change) | > 1 and adjusted p-value < 0.05. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were performed using clusterProfiler (version 4.2.2) software [25].

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and bioinformatics analysis

Tissue samples (~ 0.5 cm³; A1, A4, B1, B2) were removed from MACS® Tissue Storage Solution (130-100-008; Miltenyi Biotec B.V. & Co. KG, Germany) and washed three times in Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (DPBS, 14190144; Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Samples were minced into ~ 1 mm³ pieces using sterile scissors and transferred to 15 mL centrifuge tubes (Corning Incorporated, USA) containing 1 mg/mL collagenase D (11088866001, Merck, Germany) in Hanks’ balanced salt solution (HBSS, 14185052; Gibco, USA), and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Digestion was terminated by adding 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Grand Island Biological Company, USA), and the cell suspension was filtered through a 40 μm cell strainer (Falcon®, Corning, USA). Cells were pelleted by centrifugation at 500 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was discarded. The cell pellet was resuspended in 1× DPBS containing 0.04% bovine serum albumin (BSA, Gibco, USA). Cell viability was assessed using acridine orange/propidium iodide (AO/PI, ApexBio, USA) staining and quantified using the Luna FL cell counter (Aligned Genetics, South Korea).

Single-cell libraries were prepared using the Chromium Single Cell 3’ Reagent Kit v3 (10× Genomics) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Complementary DNA (cDNA) quality was evaluated using fragment analysis (Qsep100; Bioptic Inc, Jiang Su, China). Following library construction, library conversion was performed using the MGIEasy Universal DNA Library Preparation Kit (Shenzhen BGI Intelligent Manufacturing Technology Co., Ltd, Shenzhen, China) for platform compatibility, followed by sequencing on the DNBSEQ-T7 platform (MGI Tech Co., Ltd, ShenZhen, China).

Raw sequencing data were processed using Cell Ranger software 7.2.0 (10× Genomics) with scStar alignment, mapping reads to the reference genome (Equus asinus, Ensembl release 110, assembly ASM1607732v2) and refining annotations using GTF files. Genomic regions were classified as exonic, intronic, or intergenic. Gene annotation was obtained from Ensembl release 110. Quality control metrics, including the number of high-quality cells, median genes per cell, and sequencing saturation, were assessed using Cell Ranger software [26]. The resulting gene count matrix was imported into Seurat v4.0.4 [27] for downstream analysis. Following initial filtering using Cellranger/DNBC4 tools software (version 2.1.2), low-quality cells were further removed using Seurat and DoubletFinder packages. Exclusion criteria included: (1) multiplets identified by DoubletFinder; (2) cells expressing fewer than 200 genes or more than the 95th percentile of gene counts (to exclude potential doublets or cell aggregates); (3) cells with mitochondrial gene expression exceeding 10% of total counts, which are indicative of stressed, damaged, or dying cells; (4) cells with excessive expression of erythrocyte-related genes (in animal samples only), which were minimized by ambient RNA correction using SoupX.

Highly variable genes (HVGs) were identified using the FindVariableFeatures function in Seurat with variance stabilizing transformation (VST) method, selecting the top 2,000 features. The principal component analysis (PCA) was performed for linear dimensionality reduction, and the most significant principal components were evaluated based on their contribution to data variance. By default, the first 50 principal components were selected for downstream analysis. Graph-based clustering was performed using a K-nearest neighbors (KNN) graph constructed based on Euclidean distances in PCA-reduced space. The Louvain algorithm was applied for community detection and cell partitioning into distinct clusters (resolution = 0.4). Cellular distribution was visualized in two-dimensional space using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP, dims = 1:30, min.dist = 0.1) [28] and t-Distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE, dims = 1:30) [29].

The DEGs were identified using the FindAllMarkers function in Seurat (Wilcoxon rank-sum test, p ≤ 0.01; log₂ fold change ≥ 0.25; expressed in ≥ 25% of cells). Cell type annotation was performed through a multi-step approach that integrated multiple complementary methodologies to ensure accurate cellular identification. Initially, literature-based marker genes from relevant single-cell studies were systematically collected, followed by cross-species homologous alignment to identify conserved markers that could reliably distinguish different cell populations. Subsequently, cell types were annotated based on classical anatomical and morphological studies of the target species, leveraging established knowledge of tissue organization and cellular characteristics. Finally, functional annotation was conducted through GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses to determine the biological functions and regulatory pathways associated with DEGs, providing additional validation for the assigned cell type identities and ensuring that the molecular signatures aligned with expected cellular functions.

For functional enrichment analysis, DEGs were identified using the FindMarkers function in Seurat with |log₂FC| ≥ 0.25 threshold. Up-regulated genes were defined as having adjusted p-value < 0.05 and log₂FC ≥ 0.5, while down-regulated genes were defined as having adjusted p-value < 0.05 and log₂FC ≤ -0.5. DEG intersections were visualized using UpSetR. Functional enrichment analysis, including GO biological processes and KEGG pathway enrichment, was performed using clusterProfiler [25].

Proteomics Analysis

Tissue samples (A1, A2, A3, B1, B2, B3) were retrieved from − 80 °C storage and homogenized in SDT lysis buffer (4% SDS, Sinopharm Group Co., Ltd, Beijing, China; 100 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.6, Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd, Beijing, China; 10 mM DTT, Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd; 1 mM PMSF, Shandong Xiya Chemical Industry Co., Ltd, Shandong, China; 2 mM EDTA, Sinopharm Group Co., Ltd, Beijing, China) for protein extraction. Total protein concentration was determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). Proteins were digested with trypsin (Shanghai Promega Biological Products Ltd, Shanghai, China) and desalted using standard protocols. Resulting peptides were acidified to pH 2–3 with 20% trifluoroacetic acid (TFA, Sinopharm Group Co., Ltd, Beijing, China) and purified using C18 columns (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). Peptide concentration was quantified using the Pierce™ Quantitative Peptide Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

Peptide samples were separated using a high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) nanoflow system. Mobile phase A consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water (Sinopharm Group Co., Ltd, Beijing, China), while mobile phase B contained 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (Sinopharm Group Co., Ltd, Beijing, China). A dual-column trap-analytical setup was employed, utilizing a trap column (PepMap Neo Trap Cartridge, 300 μm × 5 mm, 5 μm, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and an analytical column (Easy-Spray™ PepMap™ Neo UHPLC column, 50 μm × 15 cm, 2 μm, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The flow rate was maintained at 2.5 µL/min. Following chromatographic separation, samples were analyzed using an Orbitrap Astral mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

MS raw data were analyzed using DIA-NN (version 1.8.1) [30] with a library-free approach using the MaxLFQ algorithm for protein quantification. The UniProtKB proteome database UP000694387 (containing 48,973 sequences) was used to create a spectral library using deep learning neural network algorithms. Match Between Runs (MBR) functionality was employed to create a spectral library from DIA data, followed by reanalysis using this library. False discovery rate (FDR) was set to < 1% at both protein and precursor ion levels. Differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) were identified using fold change (FC) ≥ 1.5 or FC ≤ 0.6667 and p-value ≤ 0.05. The use of FC ≥ 1.5 as the threshold for proteomics (versus |log₂FC| > 1 for RNA-seq) reflects the inherent differences between the two technologies in quantitative accuracy and noise levels; enforcing identical thresholds would either inflate false positives or lose true biological differences in one dataset.

Differential expression gene (DEG) analysis of chestnut and skin

Employing the stringent criteria of |log2(Fold Change) | > 1 and adjusted p-value (padj) < 0.05, DEGs analysis was conducted using the DESeq2 package. The analysis revealed a total of 5,295 DEGs between the chestnut and donkey skin, with 1,884 exhibiting up-regulation and 3,411 showing down-regulation (Fig. 4A). The final result identified a total of 15,823 expressed genes. Among them, 13,114 genes were expressed in both chestnuts and skin, 326 genes were preferentially expressed in the chestnut, and 2,383 genes were preferentially expressed in the skin (Fig. 4B). It should be noted that ‘preferentially expressed’ refers to genes meeting our differential expression criteria with TPM < 1 in the other tissue type; we cannot exclude low-level expression below the detection threshold of our RNA-seq analysis, and future qRT-PCR validation of tissue-specific markers is warranted.

GO enrichment analysis of DEGs

Through the statistical analysis of GO terms, the enrichment of DEGs between donkey skin and chestnut was annotated. Based on cellular components, molecular functions, and biological processes, these DEGs were further annotated and classified. Utilizing a False Discovery Rate (FDR) threshold for the p-value ≤ 0.05, the most significantly associated GO terms among the up-regulated DEGs were identified (Fig. 5). In the cellular component ontology, significant enrichment was observed in 13 GO terms, including cornified envelope, peroxisome, microbody, microbody lumen, and peroxisomal matrix. In the molecular function ontology, significant enrichment was noted in sterol esterase activity, triglyceride lipase activity, CoA hydrolase activity, acyl-CoA hydrolase activity, and structural constituent of skin epidermis. In the biological process ontology, significant enrichment was found in cornification, obsolete coenzyme metabolic process, fatty acid catabolic process, monocarboxylic acid catabolic process, and small molecule catabolic process.

KEGG enrichment analysis of DEGs

As shown in Table 4; Fig. 6, the pathway functional annotation of up-regulated genes in the DEGs of chestnut and skin reveals that these genes are enriched in metabolic pathways such as peroxidase, fatty acid metabolism, steroid biosynthesis, Propanoate metabolism, and Cholesterol synthesis, as well as in the PPAR signaling pathway. The PPAR signaling pathway plays a crucial role in lipid metabolism, inflammatory response [31], gut microbiota, and hepatocyte proliferation [32]. The enrichment of coronavirus disease (COVID-19) pathway reflects the database structure of KEGG, which assembled this pathway during the pandemic to represent broad-spectrum innate immune and inflammatory response modules. In the context of donkey chestnut and skin—as surface barrier tissues representing the host’s first line of defense—enrichment of this pathway should be interpreted as activation of general host defense mechanisms rather than a specific response to SARS-CoV-2.

Cell filtration and quality assessment

Following initial data processing using Cell Ranger and subsequent ambient RNA correction with soupX, stringent quality control filters were applied using Seurat to identify high-confidence single cells. Cell filtration criteria included gene detection thresholds (minimum 200 genes per cell, maximum at 95th percentile) and mitochondrial gene expression cutoffs to exclude compromised or dying cells.

Post-filtration quality assessment demonstrated robust data integrity across all samples. The distribution of detected genes per cell was predominantly concentrated above 1,000 genes, while total unique molecular identifier (UMI) counts per cell exhibited a characteristic distribution centered within the 0–2,000 range (Fig. 8). These metrics collectively indicate exceptional single-cell transcriptome capture efficiency and minimal doublet contamination, confirming the suitability of the filtered dataset for downstream computational analyses. The stringent quality control pipeline successfully retained cells with optimal transcriptional complexity while effectively removing low-quality cells, ambient RNA contamination, and potential multiplets, thereby ensuring high data fidelity for subsequent clustering and differential expression analyses.

Differential expression analysis and functional annotation

Comparative transcriptomic analysis between chestnut and skin tissue samples was performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test with stringent statistical criteria (log₂ fold change > 0.5 or < -0.5, adjusted p-value < 0.05) to identify DEGs. This analysis revealed 7,733 DEGs across all cell types, comprising 3,018 up-regulated and 4,715 down-regulated genes, indicating substantial transcriptional remodeling between tissue types.

Cell type-specific analysis demonstrated pronounced differential gene expression patterns, with the most substantial numbers of up-regulated DEGs observed in endothelial cells, fibroblasts, and keratinocytes (Fig. 11A-E). These findings suggest that these cellular populations undergo significant transcriptional reprogramming in response to tissue-specific microenvironmental demands and functional specialization requirements.

GO functional classification revealed that proteins are most enriched in cellular process and metabolic process (biological process), cellular anatomical entity and protein-containing complex (cellular component), and binding and catalytic activity (molecular function) (Fig. 17A).

KEGG functional annotation revealed that the most represented pathway categories among DEPs were cytoskeletal organization in muscle cells (8.03%) and focal adhesion complexes (4.47%) (Fig. 17B). The cytoskeleton functions as a critical regulator of cellular signaling cascades and controls specific signal transduction pathways [33], and in muscle tissue, cytoskeletal components maintain structural integrity, provide essential intracellular support, and facilitate contractile mechanisms [34], potentially contributing to the enhanced mechanical properties and firmer texture characteristic of chestnut tissue. Focal adhesion complexes mediate effective cell–matrix interactions through transmembrane receptors that connect the actin cytoskeleton to extracellular matrix (ECM) components [35, 36], and their formation and dynamics play essential roles in regulating apoptosis, proliferation, migration, and differentiation [37].

Comparative pathway analysis of up-regulated versus down-regulated DEPs demonstrated predominant up-regulation within the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway (24 up, 15 down), endocytosis pathway (23 up, 10 down), and proteasome pathway (32 up) (Fig. 17C). PI3K-Akt represents a critical regulatory network controlling cell survival, proliferation, and growth responses while preventing inflammatory responses through modulation of innate immune cell recruitment [38–40]. The endocytosis pathway facilitates cellular internalization of extracellular materials and participates in cell migration and signal transduction [41–43], while the proteasome is involved in degrading misfolded proteins and modulating diverse cellular functions [44, 45].

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis further identified significant enrichment of the proteasome and alpha-linolenic acid metabolism pathways among DEPs (Fig. 17D). The co-enrichment of the hypertrophic cardiomyopathy pathway is attributable to shared cytoskeletal and sarcomeric protein components rather than true cardiac pathology, reflecting the highly organized keratin and cytoskeletal architecture characteristic of chestnut tissue.

Discussion

Donkey chestnut tissue is morphologically distinct from adjacent skin, featuring a smooth, hairless, and highly pigmented surface. Building on our prior histological characterization of these differences [4], the present multi-omics study (scRNA-seq, RNA-seq, proteomics) provides unprecedented molecular insights into the biological.

nature of this specialized structure—revealing it as an evolutionarily conserved tissue with unique metabolic, cellular, and functional adaptations, rather than a vestigial remnant.

This study adopted single-cell RNA sequencing to systematically characterize the cellular heterogeneity between chestnut tissue and normal skin tissue of Dezhou donkeys at the single-cell resolution for the first time [46], overcoming the limitations of bulk sequencing which fails to identify cell subpopulations and cell-type-specific gene expression patterns. The core advantages of single-cell RNA sequencing lie in its capacity to resolve cellular heterogeneity in complex tissues, identify rare cell subsets, and capture cell-type-specific transcriptional signatures [47]. This technology has been widely applied in major livestock species including pigs, cattle and chickens, and has achieved substantial progress in research on embryonic development, organogenesis, immune system profiling and identification of functional cells associated with economic traits [48, 49].

In pigs, single-cell transcriptome analysis has been used to construct cell atlases across the entire embryonic development period and clarify the cell differentiation trajectories during early organogenesis [50]. In cattle, it has helped decipher cellular heterogeneity in mammary tissues and identify rare epithelial cell subsets linked to lactation performance [51, 52]. In chickens, relevant studies have revealed that Sertoli cells originate from mesenchymal stem cells during gonadal differentiation of chicken embryos [53]. Nevertheless, the application of single-cell RNA sequencing remains limited in donkeys, an important equine species. Particularly, systematic investigations into the cellular composition and functional characteristics of specialized skin structures such as chestnuts have not been reported to date.

Integrated evidence from three complementary omics approaches preliminarily constructs a molecular framework for the chestnut tissue of Dezhou donkeys, which helps to improve our understanding of this unique anatomical feature in equine animals.

The exceptional enrichment of keratin genes in chestnut tissue points to enhanced structural integrity compared to conventional skin [11]. At the transcript level, KRT1, KRT5, KRT10, and KRT14 reach TPM values exceeding 13,000, while at the protein level, Keratin type II cytoskeletal proteins and KRT10, KRT1, KRT14, and KRT5 dominate the proteomic profile with markedly higher abundance in chestnut samples relative to skin. This convergent evidence across both transcriptomic and proteomic analyses strongly corroborates the structural keratinization hypothesis for chestnut tissue. This hyperkeratinization likely confers superior mechanical resilience, serving as a protective adaptation during locomotion or environmental stress.

Complementing this structural specialization, single-cell analysis revealed that chestnut tissue is enriched in endothelial cells, pericytes, and immune cells, indicating enhanced vascularization and immunosurveillance. This cellular enrichment is consistent with the bulk RNA-seq findings of enhanced immune and vascular gene signatures, and the proteomic detection of elevated actin and structural proteins. The alignment of all three omics layers in highlighting vascular and immune features provides robust, multi-level evidence for these functional characteristics of chestnut tissue.

A key finding is the pronounced up-regulation of peroxisome-related pathways, coupled with activated PPAR signaling. Peroxisomes are critical for fatty acid β-oxidation, cholesterol biosynthesis, and reactive oxygen species detoxification [54], and their enhanced activity in chestnut tissue implies multiple functional benefits. For instance, melanogenesis is an energy-intensive process [55], and the observed metabolic up-regulation (including carbohydrate, carbon, and pyruvate metabolism) likely provides the biochemical support for sustained melanin production—underpinning the tissue’s distinctive pigmentation and potential photoprotective properties. Additionally, cholesterol biosynthesis gene enrichment reinforces epidermal lipid architecture, correlating with chestnut’s thicker epidermis [56]. PPAR signaling also mediates anti-inflammatory responses [57], suggesting chestnut tissue has enhanced capacity for inflammation resolution and homeostasis. This peroxisome/PPAR metabolic signature is evident in both the bulk RNA-seq KEGG enrichment and the proteomic pathway data, suggesting that the transcriptional upregulation translates to functional protein-level changes, reinforcing the biological validity of these findings.

Proteomic evidence of enhanced cytoskeletal organization further supports chestnut’s superior mechanical properties [33], regulation of focal adhesion pathways suggests specialized cell-matrix relationships that contribute to the tissue’s resilience and elasticity [35].

Intriguingly, both proteomic and single-cell analyses identified enrichment of coronavirus disease-related pathway annotations and mast cell/T lymphocyte populations, respectively. As discussed above, the COVID-19 pathway enrichment in KEGG should be interpreted as activation of broad innate immune and inflammatory modules, reflecting the exposed anatomical position of chestnut tissue rather than SARS-CoV-2-specific biology. Mast cells act as innate immunity sentinels, orchestrating inflammatory responses and tissue remodeling, further supporting an integrated defense system that combines structural, metabolic, and immunological specializations.

Taken together, the convergence of transcriptomic, single-cell, and proteomic data across three independent experimental platforms provides mutually corroborating evidence: (1) the structural keratinization signature is supported by elevated keratin gene expression in bulk RNA-seq, keratinocyte-specific transcriptional profiles in scRNA-seq, and high keratin protein abundance in proteomics; (2) the enhanced metabolic activity (peroxisome/PPAR) observed in bulk RNA-seq is echoed in proteomic pathway enrichment, with single-cell data further clarifying that these signals originate from coordinated activity in keratinocytes and endothelial cells; (3) immune and vascular features identified in bulk RNA-seq are precisely localized to mast cells, T lymphocytes, and endothelial cells via single-cell analysis, with proteomic upregulation of PI3K-Akt and endocytosis pathways providing functional validation. These inherent consistencies across omics layers constitute a robust, multi-level framework supporting our conclusions on chestnut tissue specialization.

Limitations and future directions

Although this study preliminarily analyzed the molecular characteristics of chestnut and skin tissues, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the number of experimental animals and samples was relatively limited. Only four Dezhou donkeys were included for multi-omics analysis with a small scale of biological replicates, which may affect the population representativeness and generalizability of the results. Notably, three biological replicates per group represents the minimum threshold for statistical analysis, and no a priori statistical power analysis was conducted to determine the adequacy of this sample size. This constitutes a critical methodological limitation, and the omics findings reported here should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive. Future work should expand the sample size and include individuals of different ages and feeding groups for further validation. Second, this is a preliminary exploratory study. We only compared molecular differences between chestnut and normal skin at the transcriptomic, proteomic, and single-cell transcriptomic levels, without independent external sample validation or in vitro functional verification of key candidate genes and proteins. The reliability of identified molecular pathways still needs further experimental confirmation, such as qRT-PCR and Western blot verification in subsequent molecular biology studies. Third, the present study only focused on Dezhou donkeys, and comparative analysis across different equine breeds was not performed. The breed-specific differences in chestnut tissue characteristics remain unexplored.

Future research directions are proposed as follows: to investigate the temporal and spatial expression patterns of key regulatory genes during embryonic development and reveal the developmental basis of chestnut formation; to carry out comparative studies among different equine breeds and species to clarify the evolutionary conservation of chestnut structures; to evaluate the mechanical properties, wound healing capacity and antibacterial activity of chestnut tissues, so as to provide functional evidence for the potential advantages predicted by omics data; and to conduct systematic multi-omics integration using professional bioinformatics tools for deeper mechanistic interpretation.

Conclusion

This preliminary multi-omics analysis revealed that donkey chestnuts represent highly specialized keratinized tissues with distinctive molecular, cellular, and functional characteristics that extend far beyond their morphological uniqueness. The integration of transcriptomic, single-cell, and proteomic data provides convergent evidence supporting enhanced metabolic activity through coordinated up-regulation of peroxisome, fatty acid metabolism, and PPAR signaling pathways, coupled with specialized cellular composition featuring enriched endothelial, immune, and structural cell populations.

The molecular evidence suggests that chestnut tissue functions as an evolutionarily adapted structure with enhanced barrier function, metabolic capacity, and potential anti-inflammatory properties. The exceptional keratin expression levels and modified cytoskeletal organization provide molecular foundations for the observed superior mechanical properties, while the enriched metabolic pathways are consistent with enhanced pigmentation and cellular homeostasis. These findings advance our understanding of equine biology and provide a molecular framework for future investigations into tissue specialization and evolutionary adaptation. The identified molecular signatures and cellular characteristics establish chestnut tissue as a unique biological system worthy of continued scientific investigation, with potential applications in veterinary medicine, comparative biology, and biotechnology.
