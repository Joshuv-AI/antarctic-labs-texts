Clinical Impact of Rapid Recurrence in Oral Tongue Squamous Cell Carcinoma and a Nomogram for Early Identification of High-Risk Patients
Authors: Younghac Kim, Eun-Hye Kim, Man Ki Chung, Han-Sin Jeong, Young-Ik Son, Jinyong Kim, Nayeon Choi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectives. This study investigated the effects of clinicopathological variables on rapid recurrence (defined as recurrence within 3 months) in oral tongue squamous cell carcinoma (OTSCC) and developed prediction models using multiple data combinations.Methods. A total of 944 patients with OTSCC were divided into two groups according to recurrence timing: the rapid recurrence group (RRG, n=89) and the non-rapid or no-recurrence group (n=855). Treatment-related variables were collected and analyzed to identify factors associated with overall survival (OS) and rapid recurrence. A logistic regression–based prediction model was developed to estimate the probability of rapid recurrence. Model performance was evaluated using 10-fold cross-validation, leave-group-out cross-validation, and calibration analysis.Results. Lower OS rates were observed with increasing age, advanced T (T3–4) and N (N1–3) stages, higher maximum preoperative positron emission tomography standardized uptake value (PET SUVmax), and rapid recurrence. In multivariable logistic regression analysis, advanced T (T2–T4) and N3 stages, positive resection margins, and higher PET SUVmax were associated with rapid recurrence, whereas postoperative chemoradiation demonstrated a protective effect. A nomogram was constructed to facilitate individualized risk estimation by integrating these clinical variables.Conclusion. Rapid recurrence was strongly associated with poor oncological outcomes in OTSCC. The validated prediction model, incorporating imaging and pathological variables, may help identify high-risk patients and support tailored treatment and surveillance strategies.Keywords: Tongue Neoplasms, Recurrence, Early Detection, Nomograms, Survival Analysis

INTRODUCTION

Oral tongue squamous cell carcinoma (OTSCC), the most common subtype of oral cavity squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC), is characterized by a high recurrence rate despite advances in multimodal treatment [1]. Although surgical resection with or without adjuvant therapy remains the standard of care [2,3], 20%–40% of patients experience recurrence, which is associated with poor survival outcomes [4,5]. Most current guidelines focus on pathological risk factors for recurrence [6]; however, they do not address the prognostic significance of recurrence timing, particularly rapid recurrence, which is typically defined as recurrence within 3–6 months after surgery [7].

Emerging evidence suggests that patients who experience rapid recurrence after curative-intent surgery represent a distinct high-risk subgroup with poor prognoses [8,9]. This pattern may reflect biologically aggressive tumor behavior or inadequate initial disease control [10]. Nevertheless, current National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines and standard adjuvant protocols do not provide stratified recommendations based on recurrence timing [6]. Therefore, there is a need to better characterize the clinicopathological features associated with rapid recurrence and to develop predictive tools for early identification of high-risk patients. Predictive modeling in head and neck cancer has recently gained increasing attention [11].

Recent studies have identified potential predictors of rapid recurrence, including advanced T and N stages, positive resection margins (RMs), and biological indicators such as elevated maximum standardized uptake value (SUVmax) on preoperative positron emission tomography-computed tomography (PET-CT) [10,12,13]. These variables may reflect tumor aggressiveness and residual disease burden [14]. In addition, histological grading systems, such as the Bryne [15] and Anneroth [16] systems, provide insights into tumor biology that may contribute to early relapse [17], although their role in predicting rapid recurrence remains unclear.

In this study, we evaluated clinicopathological factors associated with rapid recurrence in patients with OTSCC and developed and validated a predictive model to identify high-risk individuals. By analyzing a large cohort and constructing a nomogram based on identified risk factors, we aimed to provide a clinically applicable tool to support individualized management and surveillance strategies.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The study was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board at Samsung Medical Center (No. 2024-12-147). This study was conducted under the guidelines established by the Declaration of Helsinki. Because the risk to subjects in this study was minimal, the requirement for written informed consent was waived, and all patient data were de-identified for the analyses.

Patients

This retrospective study was conducted at our institution and included patients diagnosed with OTSCC who underwent curative-intent surgery between January 1995 and March 2023. Only patients with primary tongue cancers were included; those with primary tumors in the retromolar trigone or buccal mucosa were excluded due to anatomical differences. All patients underwent comprehensive endoscopic examinations, neck CT, and PET-CT prior to surgery. Magnetic resonance imaging was performed in cases involving extensive lesions. Fine-needle aspiration was used to confirm tissue diagnosis when cervical lymph node metastasis was suspected. Given the long study period, Tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) staging was harmonized according to the 8th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer Staging Manual to ensure consistent staging definitions across eras [18].

All patients underwent curative-intent resection of the primary tumor with a 1.0–1.5 cm surgical margin, and those with clinically evident nodal metastasis underwent comprehensive neck dissection. Patients with a positive final RM on intraoperative frozen-section biopsy were considered to have gross residual disease and were excluded from the study. In contrast, patients with a negative intraoperative frozen section but a positive RM on permanent pathology were considered to have microscopic residual disease and were included in the study.

Treatment and follow-up protocol

After surgery, the need for adjuvant therapy was determined through a multidisciplinary tumor board review involving specialists in head and neck surgery, medical oncology, radiation oncology, and pathology. Decisions were based on the presence of established high-risk features, including advanced tumor stage (T3 or T4), regional lymph node metastasis, a positive or close surgical RM, lymphovascular invasion (LVI), perineural invasion (PNI), depth of invasion (DOI), and extranodal extension (ENE). When indicated, postoperative radiotherapy (RT) or concurrent chemoradiotherapy was initiated within 6 weeks of surgery, in accordance with current guidelines [6,19].

The follow-up protocol adhered to the recommendations of the NCCN guideline [6]. The follow-up period commenced at the completion of definitive treatment (i.e., surgery±adjuvant therapy), and patients were monitored regularly for signs of recurrence or metastasis. In addition to regular clinical examinations and endoscopic evaluation as appropriate, CT imaging was routinely performed before radiation treatment to check tumor status and radiation fields. In addition, we performed CT scan at approximately 3 months after completion of definitive treatment. Imaging studies (CT and/or PET-CT) were also obtained earlier whenever clinically indicated, such as when recurrence was suspected based on symptoms or physical examination findings.

Data collection and grouping

Patients were categorized into two groups based on the timing of recurrence following curative-intent surgery: the rapid recurrence group (RRG), defined as recurrence within three months after the completion of definitive treatment (surgery±adjuvant therapy, consistent with the follow-up protocol below), and the non-rapid or no-recurrence group (NRRG). A comprehensive set of clinicopathologic and treatment-related variables was collected for analysis: initial T and N stages, overall clinical stage, initial treatment modality (surgery alone, surgery with RT, or surgery with concurrent chemoradiation therapy (CCRT), RM status, LVI, PNI, DOI, ENE, histologic grade (classified according to the Bryne grading system), and SUVmax on preoperative PET-CT.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software version 4.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Differences in clinical characteristics between the RRG and NRRG were analyzed using Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables and the Mann-Whitney U-test for continuous variables.

To identify prognostic factors for overall survival (OS), univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were used. Variables showing clinical relevance or statistical significance were included in the multivariable models. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors, and highly correlated variables such as T stage, DOI, N stage, and ENE were excluded from the same multivariable model to prevent multicollinearity. PET SUVmax was excluded from the multivariable survival analysis due to a large proportion of missing values (n=551/944).

Logistic regression analyses were used to identify predictors of rapid recurrence and construct a predictive model. Model performance was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve. Internal validation was performed using the 10-fold cross-validation and leave-group-out cross-validation methods. The model’s discriminative ability and generalizability were evaluated using ROC curves. Calibration plots were generated to assess agreement between the predicted and observed probabilities of rapid recurrence. A nomogram was developed based on the final logistic regression model to facilitate clinical application by visually quantifying individual risk contributions.

Predictive model for rapid recurrence and validation

To develop a logistic regression–based model for identifying patients at high risk of rapid recurrence, we used data from 393 patients with complete information for the independent variables identified in the multivariable analysis: T stage, N stage, RM status, PNI, initial treatment modality, and positron emission tomography standardized uptake value (PET SUVmax). Each variable was assigned a weighted coefficient based on its OR, and a nomogram was constructed to provide a visual tool for estimating the individualized probability of rapid recurrence (Fig. 2). In the nomogram, higher T and N stages, positive RMs, PNI, and elevated PET SUVmax were associated with an increased predicted risk of rapid recurrence, whereas postoperative CCRT was associated with a reduced predicted risk. This model may help clinicians quantify the probability of rapid recurrence at the individual patient level and support personalized decision-making and early intervention strategies.

The predictive model was internally validated using two cross-validation methods: 10-fold cross-validation and leave-group-out cross-validation. The area under the ROC curve was 0.715 for 10-fold cross-validation and 0.736 for leave-group-out cross-validation, indicating acceptable discriminatory performance (Fig. 3A and B). Model calibration was assessed using a calibration plot comparing predicted and observed probabilities of rapid recurrence (Fig. 3C). The calibration curve closely aligned with the 45° diagonal, suggesting good agreement between predicted and observed probabilities. Together, these validation results indicate that the model may have clinical utility for identifying high-risk patients with OTSCC who could benefit from intensified surveillance or tailored adjuvant therapy.

DISCUSSION

Among patients with OSCC receiving standard treatment, the reported 5-year OS rate is 65%–80% [20-22]. In our study, however, the 5-year OS rate was markedly lower in the RRG than in the NRRG (46.1% vs. 88.5%). This finding highlights the substantial prognostic impact of rapid recurrence in OTSCC and underscores the need for early identification of high-risk patients and implementation of risk-adapted management strategies. The RRG also had substantially worse oncological outcomes than the NRRG, with local recurrence in 49.4% of patients, regional recurrence in 69.7%, and distant metastasis in 23.6%. In the multivariable analysis, rapid recurrence was associated with a 9-fold higher mortality risk (HR=9.04, 95% CI=5.63–14.51, P<0.001), making it one of the strongest independent prognostic factors identified in this cohort. This pronounced survival disadvantage may reflect intrinsically aggressive tumor biology, limited salvage options after maximal initial therapy, and the presence of treatment-resistant clones [8-10,23]. Given that outcomes after locoregional recurrence are generally poor because of the aggressive nature of recurrent OSCC and the limited range of available therapeutic options [23], strategies beyond current guideline-based approaches may be required during treatment planning for patients at high risk of rapid recurrence.

We identified several independent predictors associated with rapid recurrence, including advanced T2–T4 stage (OR=2.94–5.97), N3 stage (OR=5.39), positive RMs (OR=5.22), PNI (OR=2.52), and elevated preoperative PET SUVmax (OR=1.14 per unit). These variables likely reflect both aggressive tumor behavior and inadequate locoregional control, consistent with prior studies linking adverse pathological features and increased metabolic activity [24-26] to early treatment failure. Among these factors, positive RMs showed one of the strongest associations with rapid recurrence (OR=5.22). Importantly, all patients with positive margins in our cohort had microscopic residual disease, defined as a negative intraoperative frozen section but a positive margin on permanent pathology, because patients with gross residual disease were excluded. This scenario is particularly challenging because microscopic residual disease is inherently undetectable during surgery and may not be apparent on immediate clinical assessment. Unlike gross residual disease, which can be managed with immediate re-resection, microscopic disease may remain occult until recurrence becomes clinically evident [27], typically within weeks to months, consistent with the observed mean time to recurrence of 3.20 months in the RRG. These findings highlight the importance of enhanced surveillance and treatment intensification in patients with microscopically positive margins.

PNI emerged as an independent predictor (OR=2.52), reflecting a specialized mode of tumor spread that may facilitate rapid locoregional dissemination along nerve pathways beyond conventional surgical boundaries [28]. Recent spatial and transcriptomic analyses have demonstrated complex tumor-neural microenvironment interactions with upregulation of neurotropic factors that may promote aggressive behavior [28]. The significant association between elevated preoperative PET SUVmax and rapid recurrence (OR=1.14 per unit, P=0.003) provides a potentially useful preoperative biomarker for risk stratification. High SUVmax reflects increased glucose metabolism associated with hypoxia, rapid proliferation, and treatment resistance and may identify tumors harboring aggressive cell populations capable of rapid regrowth from minimal residual disease [24-26].

An important finding was the protective association of postoperative CCRT with both reduced rapid recurrence (OR=0.36) and improved OS (HR=0.34), supporting early treatment escalation in high-risk patients. The apparent superiority of CCRT may reflect radiosensitization, improved control of micrometastatic disease, and partial mitigation of radioresistance mechanisms such as tumor hypoxia [29] and accelerated repopulation [30]. Current NCCN guidelines recommend CCRT for patients with positive margins or ENE [6], but they do not incorporate predictive models. Our findings suggest that high-risk patients identified by the nomogram, even in the absence of traditional “very high-risk” features, may derive benefit from CCRT rather than RT alone. However, treatment intensification must be balanced against increased toxicity [31], emphasizing the need for individualized decision-making.

A major strength of this study is the development of a clinically applicable nomogram integrating these high-risk features, with acceptable internal performance as reflected by area under the curve values of 0.715 and 0.736 and good calibration. This tool may support real-time decision-making during multidisciplinary discussions by informing treatment intensity, clinical trial enrollment [32], surveillance intervals, and early imaging strategies. In this study, the RRG was defined using a 3-month cutoff to capture failures arising during the adjuvant therapy planning period, which may be particularly informative for guiding early surveillance intensity and timely treatment escalation. Based on our findings, we propose risk-stratified management in which low-risk patients (<20% predicted probability) undergo standard surveillance [6], intermediate-risk patients (20%–50%) receive enhanced surveillance with 3-month imaging intervals and strong consideration of CCRT, and high-risk patients (>50%) follow intensive protocols that include baseline PET-CT within 4–6 weeks postoperatively, CCRT, and imaging every 6–8 weeks during the first 6 months.

Given the apparent role of microscopic residual disease in rapid recurrence, current standard surveillance protocols, which recommend first postoperative imaging at 3–6 months after completion of adjuvant therapy [6], may be insufficient for high-risk patients. For patients with predicted probabilities exceeding 50%, we propose baseline PET-CT within 4–6 weeks postoperatively, before initiation of adjuvant therapy. Early imaging may provide a metabolic baseline for distinguishing postoperative inflammatory change from viable tumor [12,14] and may enable treatment modification before tumor burden increases. Studies have shown that patients who develop recurrence before planned adjuvant RT have substantially worse outcomes [9], suggesting that earlier detection could allow more effective salvage strategies. Potential treatment modifications include expansion of the radiation field, dose escalation [14,33], earlier systemic therapy, or surgical re-intervention when feasible. However, false-positive findings related to postoperative inflammation require experienced interpretation [12,34].

A better understanding of the biological mechanisms underlying rapid recurrence may facilitate development of targeted preventive strategies. The association between SUVmax and rapid recurrence suggests that metabolic reprogramming may represent a therapeutic target, with emerging inhibitors of glycolysis and mitochondrial function potentially preferentially targeting metabolically active cells and radiosensitizing hypoxic tumor regions [29,35]. Given the significant role of PNI, disruption of nerve-tumor interactions represents another promising avenue, and recent analyses have identified specific molecular pathways and potential therapeutic targets [29]. Integration of circulating tumor DNA monitoring may further improve predictive accuracy and enable real-time assessment of minimal residual disease [36,37], as circulating tumor DNA detection can precede clinical recurrence by weeks to months [37]. The rapid recurrence phenotype may also reflect immune evasion mechanisms that could be addressed through checkpoint inhibition [32], and incorporation of radiomic features and machine-learning approaches may further improve prediction [11,38]. Nevertheless, recurrence is inherently a time-dependent event, and modeling rapid recurrence status as a fixed covariate in a Cox regression framework may introduce time-dependent bias if interpreted causally. Therefore, survival analyses involving rapid recurrence should be interpreted as describing a prognostic association, and future studies should evaluate this relationship using landmark or time-dependent Cox approaches.

This study has several limitations, including its retrospective design, the potential for selection bias, and the substantial proportion of missing PET SUVmax data (58.4%), which restricted nomogram development to 393 patients. In addition, PET-CT was not performed according to a fully standardized protocol across the long study period, and variability in imaging indications, timing, and acquisition-related factors may have influenced SUVmax values. Therefore, SUVmax should be interpreted cautiously, as it may reflect differences in staging practice in addition to underlying tumor biology. Smoking status, alcohol consumption, and baseline comorbidities were also not consistently available. External validation in independent cohorts is required to assess generalizability, as the limited number of events available for nomogram development relative to model complexity (events-per-variable ratio, 3.4) suggests some degree of overfitting despite internal validation [39]. The 3-month definition of rapid recurrence was based on emerging literature [7-9] but remains somewhat arbitrary. Although this study excluded cases consistent with gross residual disease on the basis of intraoperative frozen-section margins, distinguishing true rapid recurrence from occult residual disease remains challenging in retrospective cohorts. Future model refinement should incorporate emerging biomarkers, including genomic classifiers and advanced radiomic features [11,38,40].

In conclusion, rapid recurrence was associated with a 9-fold higher mortality risk and a 5-year survival rate of only 46.1% in OTSCC. Independent predictors included advanced stage, positive RMs indicating microscopic residual disease, PNI, and elevated PET SUVmax. These factors were integrated into a nomogram with acceptable discrimination and calibration. Our findings support a personalized, risk-adapted management approach. High-risk patients may benefit from intensified CCRT, enhanced surveillance with early baseline PET-CT within 4–6 weeks postoperatively, and shortened follow-up intervals. In particular, for patients with microscopic residual disease, early metabolic imaging may enable detection before clinical manifestation and allow proactive treatment modification. By translating statistical risk factors into a clinically usable tool, this study advances efforts toward more personalized care for patients at greatest risk of early treatment failure. Future prospective, multi-institutional studies with external validation are needed to confirm generalizability and determine whether implementation of this approach improves patient outcomes.

HIGHLIGHTS

▪ Rapid recurrence was associated with a 9-fold higher mortality risk (hazard ratio [HR], 9.04) in oral tongue squamous cell carcinoma.

▪ The nomogram integrates T and N stage, resection margin status, perineural invasion, treatment modality, and preoperative PET SUVmax.

▪ Microscopic positive margins and perineural invasion were associated with increased rapid recurrence risk.

▪ The 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography standardized uptake value was associated with rapid recurrence (odds ratio, 1.14 per unit increase).

▪ Chemoradiation was associated with reduced rapid recurrence and improved survival (HR, 0.34).
