Association of IL-10 promoter and IL-12 gene polymorphisms with the risk of symptomatic Helicobacter pylori infection
Authors: Pezhman Karami, Sama Mokari, Mohammad Yousef Alikhani, Sima Kazemi, Mina Mirzaei, Alireza Soltanian, Mohammad Ahmadyousefi, Fereshteh Dehdar, Alireza Rastgoo Haghi, Ebrahim Azizi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe host’s immune response to Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) infection is largely determined by its cytokine profile. Genetic variations within crucial immunomodulatory genes, including those for interleukin-10 (IL-10) and interleukin-12 (IL-12), are thought to influence an individual’s vulnerability to the infection and its clinical consequences by modifying cytokine production. Nonetheless, research data derived from diverse human populations continue to show inconsistent results.AimThis case-control analysis sought to examine a potential link between symptomatic H. pylori infection susceptibility in an Iranian population and specific genetic variants in the IL-10 (-1082G > A, -819 C > T) and IL-12 (+ 1188 A > C) genes.MethodsIn this investigation, 68 individuals with confirmed symptomatic H. pylori infection diagnosed by a positive rapid urease test and elevated anti-H. pylori IgG levels exceeding 90 ng/ml via ELISA were enrolled alongside 68 healthy controls. The control group was carefully matched to the patient group based on age, sex, and ethnic background. Genotyping for the IL-10 (-1082G > A, -819 C > T) and IL-12 (+ 1188 A > C) polymorphisms was conducted using the Amplification Refractory Mutation System-PCR (ARMS-PCR) method. To evaluate associations, the distribution of genotypes and alleles between the groups was contrasted using logistic regression, applying additive, dominant, and recessive inheritance models. The strength of any association was expressed as odds ratios (ORs) accompanied by 95% confidence intervals (CIs).ResultsThe analysis revealed no statistically significant correlations linking the investigated IL-10 and IL-12 gene variants to an increased predisposition for H. pylori infection. A notable methodological observation was the deviation from Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (HWE) across all studied polymorphisms in the control group. Regarding the IL-10 -1082G > A locus, the AA genotype was associated with a marginally elevated risk estimate; however, this finding was not statistically significant (OR = 3.45, 95% CI: 0.29–41.36; p = 0.327). Likewise, for the IL-12 + 1188 A > C polymorphism, the CC genotype, while more prevalent in the patient cohort, also demonstrated no significant association with infection risk (OR = 1.43, 95% CI: 0.42–4.87; p = 0.567).ConclusionThis investigation did not establish a significant link between the specific IL-10 and IL-12 gene variants analyzed and susceptibility to symptomatic H. pylori infection in the studied population. Although minor genetic associations were noted, they lacked statistical significance. Future research with larger sample sizes is required to validate these results and to investigate additional genetic determinants that may affect infection risk.

Introduction

Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) is a spiral-shaped, microaerophilic pathogen with a distinct affinity for colonizing the human gastric mucosal lining [1]. This bacterium is an established causative agent for a range of upper gastrointestinal disorders, from chronic gastritis and peptic ulcers to more severe outcomes such as gastric cancer and mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue (MALT) lymphoma [2]. The colonization and long-term survival of H. pylori within the stomach triggers a persistent inflammatory state, which is characterized histopathologically by the infiltration of neutrophils, lymphocytes, and plasma cells into the gastric mucosa [3].

A significant diagnostic challenge arises from the fact that a majority of infected individuals remain asymptomatic carriers [3]. Global seroprevalence studies indicate that infection rates vary substantially, being particularly high in many developing nations (often exceeding 70–90%) and lower, though still significant, in developed countries, where rates of approximately 30–50% are common in adult populations [4, 5]. Notably, regional disparities in prevalence are also observed within individual countries, suggesting the influence of geographic and socioeconomic factors.

The pathogenesis of H. pylori is multifactorial, mediated through an array of virulence mechanisms. These include the modulation of gastric acid secretion, induction of epithelial cell hyperproliferation and elongation, disruption of intercellular junctions, and the elicitation of both systemic and mucosal antibody responses (IgG and IgA) [6, 7]. The ensuing immune activation involves a complex cytokine network orchestrated by cells of both the innate and adaptive immune systems. Evidence underscores that dysregulated cytokine responses can drive the progression from chronic inflammation to precancerous lesions and ultimately to gastric malignancy [8–11].

Upon H. pylori infection, activated dendritic cells in the gastric mucosa release interleukin-12 (IL-12). IL-12, in turn, drives the differentiation of naïve CD4⁺ T cells into a T helper 1 (Th1) phenotype.

These newly polarized Th1 cells are defined by their secretion of specific pro-inflammatory cytokines, including interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), interleukin-1 (IL-1), and interleukin-6 (IL-6) [11, 12]. This Th1-dominant response, governed by the transcription factors T-bet and STAT4, is primarily aimed at bacterial clearance. However, in a subset of individuals, this robust inflammatory response becomes chronic and contributes to tissue damage and carcinogenesis [13].

The involvement of the Th2 immune response in H. pylori infection remains poorly understood. Although this pathway is typically essential for eliminating extracellular pathogens, its contribution in this particular context appears to be limited. Th2 activity is associated with the generation of IgG1 antibodies, whereas the Th1 response augments overall IgG2 levels via IL-12 and IFN-γ [14].

Functioning as a critical regulator of immunity, Interleukin-10 (IL-10) exerts powerful anti-inflammatory effects. It is synthesized by numerous immune cell types such as macrophages, lymphocytes, dendritic cells, and mast cells. The cytokine operates by inhibiting macrophage activation, restraining the release of pro-inflammatory signals from Th1 cells, and fine-tuning the functions of NK cells, antigen-presenting cells (APCs), and mast cells [15]. The gene encoding IL-10 is located on chromosome 1, and functional polymorphisms within its promoter region (notably at positions − 1082 G > A, -819 C > T [16, 17]) have been linked to altered cytokine expression levels and an increased risk of gastric cancer. Interindividual variation in IL-10 production capacity following immunogenic stimuli (e.g., LPS) is well-documented [18].

Similarly, IL-12 is a key pro-inflammatory cytokine central to protective immunity against intracellular pathogens, yet it also influences infection persistence and disease severity [19].

APCs, such as dendritic cells and macrophages, recognize microbial components through Toll-like receptors (TLRs), leading to the induction and secretion of IL-12. IL-12 promotes Th1 differentiation and enhances IFN-γ production, activating pro-inflammatory immune responses. In contrast, IL-10, produced by APCs, regulatory T cells, and other immune cells acts as an anti-inflammatory cytokine that suppresses macrophage activation, limits Th1-derived inflammatory cytokines, and provides negative feedback to control excessive IL-12 mediated responses. The balance between IL-12-mediated Th1 activation and IL-10-mediated regulation determines the overall inflammatory outcome [19]. (Fig. 1.) [19].

Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in cytokine genes are among the most common sources of genetic variation in humans and can lead to significant differences in cytokine production capacity across different ethnic and racial groups [20]. These genetic variations may predispose individuals to inflammatory conditions, including those related to H. pylori [21].

This research seeks to determine if a correlation exists between specific IL-10 and IL-12 gene variants and an individual’s susceptibility to H. pylori infection, by analyzing and contrasting genotype and allele frequencies in infected individuals versus a healthy control group.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This case-control investigation enrolled 68 individuals (51 male, 17 female) with a confirmed H. pylori infection. Participants, who had a mean age of 51.96 ± 12.28 years (range: 19–75), were recruited from the gastroenterology departments of Shahid Beheshti Hospital and Imam Reza Hospital in Kabadrahang, Iran, between December 2023 and August 2024. A specialist physician established the diagnosis based on clinical presentation and positive laboratory tests. Individuals with pre-existing inflammatory, autoimmune, metabolic or chronic infectious diseases were excluded from the study.

The control cohort for this study comprised 68 healthy individuals (48 males, 20 females; aged 20–78 years), who were meticulously matched to the patient group based on key demographic factors including age, sex, ethnicity, and geographical residence. All control subjects were screened to ensure an absence of any personal or familial history of H. pylori infection or associated gastric disorders. All control participants were confirmed to be free of infection via negative pathological biopsy and serological testing.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for this research was granted by the Research Ethics Committee of Hamadan University of Medical Sciences (Ethics Code: IR.UMSHA.REC.1402.774). Prior to enrollment, every participant provided written informed consent following a comprehensive briefing on the study’s objectives and methodology. The anonymity and confidentiality of all personal and clinical data were rigorously upheld for the duration of the research.

Determination of H. pylori infection status

A multi-method diagnostic approach, incorporating both invasive and serological techniques, was employed to accurately identify H. pylori infection. As part of this protocol, two biopsy samples were obtained from the gastric antrum of each subject during upper gastrointestinal endoscopy. One of these specimens was promptly analyzed using a rapid urease test (CLOtest®, Delta West, Australia) for the preliminary detection of bacterial urease activity. A positive result was defined by the development of a pink or red coloration within a 24-hour observation period. For patients, the presence of H. pylori was definitively confirmed by histopathological examination performed by an experienced pathologist.

Simultaneously, venous blood samples (approximately 2 ml) were drawn from all participants both the patient and the meticulously matched control groups into EDTA-containing vacuum tubes for subsequent serological and molecular analyses. Serological confirmation was achieved using a commercial ELISA kit (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. no. RAB0242) designed to detect IgG antibodies against a mixture of H. pylori-specific membrane and surface antigens. The manufacturer’s recommended cut-off value of 90 ng/ml for seropositivity, which is established based on internal validation data to optimize sensitivity and specificity, was used to define active infection. To guarantee analytical reproducibility, every sample was tested in duplicate. Conversely, individuals in the control group were required to yield negative results from both the rapid urease test and the ELISA (titer < 90 ng/ml), and histopathological assessment ensuring they were unequivocally infection-free.

DNA extraction and genotyping

Total genomic DNA was isolated from the anti-coagulated whole blood of all study subjects employing a commercial extraction kit (Sina-Clon, Iran) per the supplier’s guidelines. The resulting DNA was then assessed for concentration and purity using a NanoDrop™ 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Following quantification, all samples were standardized to a uniform working concentration of 50 ng/µL to facilitate downstream genetic analyses.

The IL-10 (-1082G > A, -819 C > T) and IL-12 (+ 1188 A/C) polymorphisms were genotyped employing the Amplification Refractory Mutation System (ARMS)-PCR technique.

The primer sequences used for ARMS-PCR amplification were as follows: IL-10 (-1082G > A): Outer Forward: 5’-CTCGCTGCAACCCAACTGGC-3’, Outer Reverse: 5’- TCTTACCTATCCCTACTTCC-3’ G Allele-Specific Inner: 5’-CTCACCTGCTCCACGGCCTT-3’, A Allele-Specific Inner: 5’-CTCACCTGCTCCACGGCCTA-3’;

IL-10 (-819C > T): Outer Forward: 5’-TCATTCTATGTGCTGGAGATGG-3’, Outer Reverse: 5’-TGGGGGAAGTGGGTAAGAGT-3’,C Allele-Specific Inner: 5’- AAGAGAGTGAGACTCCCTGC-3’, T Allele-Specific Inner: 5’-AAGAGAGTGAGACTCCCTGT-3’; IL-12 (+ 1188A/C): Outer Forward: 5’ GTCCTCAGAAGCTAACCATCTC-3’ Outer Reverse: 5’-CCTCTGCCCTGAGAAAGATAC-3’ A Allele-Specific Inner: 5’-GTGAAGAGGGCAGAGGAAGA-3’ C Allele-Specific Inner: 5’-GTGAAGAGGGCAGAGGAAGC-3’. Primer sequences were derived from established protocols [22, 23].

Genotyping for the IL-10 promoter polymorphisms (-1082G > A and − 819 C > T) was performed using established primer sequences and cycling conditions, as previously described by reference [22]. The polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was carried out using a commercial pre-mixed master mix (PCR 2X Taq Premix Mastermix, Pars Tous Co., Iran). Each 20 µL reaction mixture contained approximately 100 ng of genomic DNA template, along with 1 µL of each forward and reverse primer (10 pmol/µL). Amplification was executed on a C 1000 Touch Thermal Cycler (Bio-Rad, USA) with the following profile: an initial denaturation step at 95 °C for 4 min; 25 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 20 s, annealing at 56 °C for the − 1082G > A and 58 °C for the − 819 C > T variant for 20 s, and extension at 72 °C for 30 s; culminating in a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. Concurrently, the Beta-2 Microglobulin (B2M) gene was co-amplified as an internal control under identical conditions, utilizing a specific annealing temperature of 66 °C. To ensure the thorough elongation of all PCR products, a final synthesis phase was implemented at 72 °C for a duration of 10 min.

The amplification of the IL-12 (+ 1188 A/C) locus was performed using a thermal cycling protocol initiated by a 3-minute denaturation at 94 °C. This was followed by 35 cycles, each comprising denaturation at 94 °C for 30 s, primer annealing at 65 °C for 45 s, and strand extension at 72 °C for 45 s. The process concluded with a final elongation step at 72 °C for 5 min to ensure the complete amplification of all target fragments, as per the established methodology [23].

For each ARMS-PCR run, both negative controls (no-template DNA, containing only master mix and water) and positive controls (previously genotyped samples representing known homozygous and heterozygous genotypes for each polymorphism) were included to monitor for contamination and ensure genotyping accuracy. Subsequently, the PCR amplicons were resolved by electrophoresis on a 2% agarose gel, which was pre-stained with SYBR Safe DNA gel stain at a concentration of 0.5 µg/mL. The resulting DNA bands were then visualized and documented under ultraviolet light using a UV transillumination system.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics for categorical data are presented as counts and percentages, while continuous data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Intergroup comparisons for age, which followed a normal distribution, were conducted using an independent samples t-test. For continuous variables deviating from normality, the non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test was applied. Differences in genotype and allele distributions between the case and control cohorts were evaluated by applying multivariate logistic regression analysis. This analysis generated odds ratios (ORs) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) across additive, dominant, and recessive inheritance models. A post-hoc power analysis was performed using PS: Power and Sample Size Calculation software (version 3.1.2). Given the sample size of 68 participants per group, a two-sided alpha of 0.05, and the observed allele frequencies in the control population, the study had 80% power to detect a minimum OR of approximately 2.5 under an allelic model. To control for potential confounding, multivariate logistic regression models were employed to calculate adjusted odds ratios (AORs) and 95% CIs for the genetic polymorphisms. The genotype distribution within the control group was tested for conformity to Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (HWE) using a Chi-square (χ²) goodness-of-fit test. The entire statistical analysis was executed with SPSS software (Version 16), and statistical significance was defined a priori as a two-tailed p-value of less than 0.05.

Statistical evaluation of lifestyle factors identified significant risk associations. The prevalence of smoking was markedly higher in patients (57.4%) compared to controls (25.0%), a difference that was highly statistically significant (p < 0.001). Similarly, a positive family history was exclusively reported within the patient group, affecting 32.4% of individuals (p < 0.001). In contrast, no significant differences were observed between the groups for the variables of education level (p = 0.421) or alcohol consumption (p = 0.211) (Table 1).

Genetic association analysis

This case-control investigation was designed to evaluate the potential relationship between susceptibility to the disease and specific genetic variations in the IL-10 (-1082 G/A, -819 C/T) and IL-12 (+ 1188 A/C) genes. The analysis, which included 68 matched patient-control pairs, yielded the following detailed statistical outcomes.

IL-10 (-1082 G/A) polymorphism

Genetic analysis of the IL-10 (-1082 G/A) polymorphism revealed no significant associations with disease susceptibility. Although the A/A genotype demonstrated a higher frequency in patients (32.4%) versus controls (20.6%), this difference was not statistically significant (recessive model: OR = 1.81, 95% CI: 0.85–3.87, p = 0.122). Similarly, allele frequency distributions showed no significant deviation between groups (p = 0.232). The wide confidence intervals for the A/A genotype in codominant analysis (OR = 3.45, 95% CI: 0.29–41.36) reflect limited precision due to sample size constraints (Table 2).

IL-10 (-819 C/T) polymorphism

Analysis of the IL-10 (-819 C/T) polymorphism revealed no significant associations with disease susceptibility. The genotype and allele distributions showed no statistically significant differences between patients and controls (p > 0.05 for all comparisons). The C/T genotype was predominant in both groups, while the C/C genotype was rare in patients (2.9%) and absent in controls, preventing meaningful statistical assessment of this specific genotype. All genetic models (codominant, recessive, and allelic) consistently demonstrated null findings, indicating this polymorphism does not significantly influence disease risk in the studied population (Table 2).

IL-12 (1188 A/C) polymorphism

Analysis of the IL-12 (+ 1188 A/C) polymorphism showed no significant association with disease susceptibility. Genotype and allele distributions were similar between patients and controls (p > 0.05 for all comparisons). Although the C/C genotype showed a non-significant increase in patients (10.3% vs. 7.4%; recessive model OR = 1.43, 95% CI: 0.42–4.87, p = 0.567), the wide confidence intervals and lack of statistical significance across all genetic models indicate this finding should be interpreted with caution. The equal allele distribution between groups further supports the absence of a significant genetic effect. (Table 2)

None of the polymorphisms demonstrated a statistically significant association with symptomatic H. pylori infection, as all CIs encompassed the null value (OR = 1).

Discussion

This investigation assessed the relationship between specific functional gene variants IL-10 (-1082G > A, -819 C > T) and IL-12B (+ 1188 A > C) and the predisposition to H. pylori infection within a case-control population from Iran. The core outcome of our analysis was the absence of a statistically significant link between the tested genetic variants and susceptibility to H. pylori. A non-significant trend was noted for the IL-10 (-1082) A/A genotype, which was more common in patients (32.4%) than controls (20.6%), yielding an odds ratio of 3.45 (95% CI: 0.29–41.36). A subsequent multivariate logistic regression analysis, adjusting for the significant confounding effects of smoking and family history, yielded results consistent with the unadjusted models, confirming the lack of significant independent association between the studied polymorphisms and infection risk.

Our findings add a nuanced layer to the intricate tapestry of immunogenetic research concerning H. pylori. The data reveal a pattern of both concordance and discordance with the existing literature. Specifically, the lack of a significant correlation between IL-12 genetic variants and susceptibility to infection corroborates the null findings of Petkevicius et al., [24] regarding the IL-12 p40 polymorphism and peptic ulcer disease. This collective evidence posits that variations in the IL-12B gene are unlikely to be a major determinant in the initial pathogenic cascade following H. pylori exposure.

Nevertheless, our findings diverge from a segment of the international literature. For example, research conducted by Örenay et al. within an Indian population established a significant association of the IL-12B rs3212227 polymorphism with gastric carcinoma, the premalignant condition of intestinal metaplasia, and H. pylori infection [25]. Correspondingly, research by Miley Cárdenas et al. identified statistically significant links between specific IL-10 gene variants and the development of gastric carcinoma in a Brazilian population [26]. In an Iraqi cohort, the research of Al-Shuwaikh et al. established a significant correlation between specific polymorphisms in the IL-10 promoter region and the development of gastric pathology subsequent to H. pylori infection [27]. These discrepancies highlight the substantial ethnic-specific variations in genetic susceptibility to H. pylori-related diseases and suggest that these polymorphisms may exert more pronounced effects in the carcinogenic evolution of infection rather than in initial susceptibility.

An important consideration in interpreting our findings is that our case group represents patients with symptomatic H. pylori infection requiring endoscopic evaluation. Therefore, our results pertain to genetic susceptibility to clinically apparent disease rather than the initial acquisition or asymptomatic carriage of the bacterium. This distinction may explain discrepancies with studies that included asymptomatic infected individuals as cases, as different genetic factors might govern disease manifestation versus infection acquisition. The progression from colonization to symptomatic disease involves complex host-pathogen interactions where cytokine polymorphisms may play a more substantial role in determining inflammatory response and symptom development.

The biological plausibility of our observations is supported by functional studies. Martínez-Campos et al. [28] revealed that certain inherited variations in the IL-10 gene are associated with a concomitant rise in constitutive interleukin-10 expression and a greater propensity for H. pylori colonization. Although we did not measure cytokine levels, the absence of significant genetic association in our cohort may suggest that the functional impact of these specific SNPs is modulated by other genetic or environmental factors within our population.

Although our overall finding of no significant association aligns with some previous reports, a closer examination of the allele frequencies in our Iranian control cohort reveals distinct population-specific patterns. For instance, the frequency of the IL-10 -1082A allele in our controls (58.8%) is notably higher than frequencies reported in many European populations (often 20–40%) but aligns more closely with some reports from other Middle Eastern and Asian groups .Similarly, the distribution of the IL-12 + 1188 genotypes in our population differs from that seen in Caucasian cohorts. This divergence in baseline genetic background underscores the importance of population-specific studies. It suggests that while the net effect of these variants on susceptibility may be null in both populations, the underlying genetic and potentially epistatic interactions contributing to disease risk could be fundamentally different. Therefore, our ‘confirmatory’ finding in this distinct genetic context adds a crucial layer of evidence, reinforcing that the lack of association is not an artifact of a specific population but may be a more generalizable phenomenon, albeit one arising from diverse genetic landscapes.

Despite the biological plausibility, data on the association between IL-10/IL-12 polymorphisms and H. pylori susceptibility remain inconsistent across different ethnic groups. These discrepancies may be attributed to ethnic variations in allele frequencies, linkage disequilibrium patterns, and gene-environment interactions. Iran possesses a unique genetic structure distinct from both European and Arab populations, coupled with a high reported prevalence of H. pylori infection. No prior study has comprehensively investigated these specific cytokine gene variants in relation to H. pylori susceptibility in Western Iran. The observed deviation from HWE across all polymorphisms in our control group warrants careful consideration of its potential causes and implications. While genotyping error represents a primary concern in such scenarios, we have systematically excluded this possibility through rigorous validation measures. Alternative explanations must therefore be considered. Population stratification, though minimized by our careful matching of cases and controls for ethnic and geographic background, cannot be entirely ruled out. More likely, the observed deviation may reflect our exceptionally stringent control selection criteria, requiring negative results from both histopathological examination and serological testing. This approach, while methodologically rigorous for establishing a genuine infection-free control group, may have selected for a biologically distinct subpopulation with atypical genotype distributions at these immunologically relevant loci. The relatively modest sample size, particularly for rare homozygous genotypes, may have further amplified this effect. Importantly, this deviation does not necessarily invalidate our case-control comparisons but does highlight that our controls may not be fully representative of the general population. This limitation necessitates caution in generalizing our findings while simultaneously strengthening the internal validity of our comparisons against a rigorously defined uninfected cohort.

Our focus on cytokine genes lies downstream of the initial pathogen recognition by TLRs. Recent evidence demonstrates that polymorphisms in TLRs (e.g., TLR1, TLR2) significantly influence H. pylori susceptibility [19]. Our null findings for IL-10 and IL-12 polymorphisms suggest that in our population, variations in these downstream cytokine genes may be less critical for susceptibility than variations in the upstream pathogen-sensing machinery, a hypothesis that warrants future investigation into the entire TLR-to-cytokine signaling axis.

Another factor that may modulate the host genetic response is the substantial regional variability in H. pylori virulence factors and antibiotic resistance patterns. Notably, high resistance rates to clarithromycin and metronidazole have been documented in our geographic region [29]. Such factors could influence the chronicity and intensity of the inflammatory stimulus, potentially overwhelming or altering the subtle effects of host genetic variants on cytokine production. This interplay between host genetics and bacterial heterogeneity represents a critical and complex dimension that future studies should aim to dissect.

Collectively, these contrasting findings suggest a paradigm shift in understanding the role of cytokine polymorphisms in H. pylori pathogenesis. Rather than determining initial susceptibility, variants in IL-10 and IL-12 may predominantly influence disease progression trajectories. This explains the dissociation between studies examining infection status versus those investigating severe endpoints like gastric cancer .Future longitudinal studies are needed to directly test this hypothesis.

Several constraints warrant consideration in the evaluation of our findings. The most significant limitation of this study is its modest sample size, which constrains its statistical power. As quantified by our post-hoc power analysis, the study was only powered to detect large genetic effects (OR > 2.5). Consequently, while our findings suggest that IL-10 and IL-12 polymorphisms are not major risk factors for H. pylori infection in our population, we cannot rule out more modest associations. The wide confidence intervals observed for some effect estimates, such as that for the IL-10 -1082 AA genotype (OR = 3.45, 95% CI: 0.29–41.36), reflect this uncertainty and caution against over interpretation of point estimates. The genotype distributions in our control group deviated from HWE for all polymorphisms. While we verified our genotyping accuracy through replication, this deviation may reflect the stringent selection of our controls individuals definitively confirmed as infection-free. This selection potentially identified a genetically distinct subpopulation, which may limit generalizability but enhances internal validity for assessing genetic susceptibility in a truly infection-free cohort. We did not measure actual cytokine expression levels, which would have provided valuable functional validation of the genetic associations. We did not analyze H. pylori virulence factors (such as CagA and VacA status), which might interact with host genetic factors in determining infection outcomes. The geographically and ethnically defined cohort from Hamadan, Iran, central to this investigation, means that the extrapolation of our results to other populations should be approached with caution.

Additionally, we did not apply corrections for multiple comparisons (e.g., Bonferroni or False Discovery Rate) to our genetic association analyses. While such adjustments are recommended to control for the increased risk of Type I error when testing multiple hypotheses, our primary findings of non-significance remain robust, as none of the observed associations approached the conventional significance threshold. The unadjusted p-values are presented to facilitate direct comparison with the broader literature on these polymorphisms.

Notwithstanding its limitations, this research offers crucial insights by providing the first carefully phenotyped genetic dataset on these cytokines from a distinct Iranian population. In the context of globally inconsistent findings, our results contribute to understanding the population-specific nature of genetic susceptibility to H. pylori and underscore the critical importance of considering ethnic genetic backgrounds when evaluating immunogenetic factors. This work thereby lays the essential groundwork for future larger, multi-center studies aimed at unraveling the complex interplay between host genetics and infection outcomes in diverse populations.

Conclusion

This investigation concludes that the analyzed polymorphisms in the IL-10 and IL-12 genes are not significantly associated with an increased or decreased risk of acquiring symptomatic H. pylori infection in our specific participant sample from Iran. The divergent findings between studies highlight the ethnic-specific nature of genetic associations and suggest that these polymorphisms may be more relevant for disease progression than initial infection susceptibility. Future research should include larger, multi-center studies with adequate power to detect modest genetic effects, incorporate functional measures of cytokine expression, analyze bacterial virulence factors, and employ haplotype-based approaches rather than single SNP analyses. Research of this breadth is crucial for unraveling the intricate molecular and immunological dynamics that govern the diverse clinical manifestations of H. pylori pathogenesis in heterogeneous human groups.
