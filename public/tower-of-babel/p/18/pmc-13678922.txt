3Rs principles for biomedical research in Africa: Evidence, challenges, early career researchers’ perspectives, and pathways for One Health research governance
Authors: Olawale F Olaniyan, Modou Dumbuya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe 3Rs principles (Replacement, Reduction, and Refinement) are globally recognized as a cornerstone of ethical animal research and an essential component of responsible biomedical research systems. Nonetheless, evidence on the awareness, reporting, and implementation in African research settings remain limited. This study therefore examined integration of 3Rs principles in African biomedical research by assessing existing evidence, key challenges, and pathways forward. It also explored awareness of early career researchers concerning ethical animal use and the 3Rs principles, with a particular focus on The Gambia.MethodsA two-pronged approach was used. A structured literature search of the PubMed Central and EBSCOhost databases (2001–2022) was conducted using predefined search terms and Boolean operators to identify peer-reviewed studies from African countries that explicitly reported the 3Rs principles. Also, a cross-sectional survey was administered to 80 purposively selected early career researchers and professionals in The Gambia to assess awareness, training, and institutional support for ethical use of animals in research. Data were analysed using Fisher’s exact test (p < 0.05) and the phi coefficient (Φ) to examine associations between selected demographic and training-related variables.ResultsSeven eligible studies explicitly mentioning the 3Rs were identified in six African countries. They mainly involved rodent models and focused on evaluating drug and nutrient efficacy, plant-based mitigation of toxicity, behavioural effects, antivenom evaluation, and environmental health risks. Nearly half involved transcontinental collaborations with researchers from Europe and the Americas. Survey findings showed important gaps in research ethics awareness, training, and institutional support. Most respondents (86%) were unfamiliar with the 3Rs principles, over 65% had not received formal training in research sample collection, and 63% reported absence of institutional protocols or policies governing ethical animal experimentation. Education level was significantly associated with prior training on sample collection from animals (p = 0.01; Φ = 0.341), underscoring the importance of strengthening capacity development, institutional oversight, and research governance within health research systems.ConclusionLimited awareness, reporting, and implementation of the 3Rs principles in African biomedical research point to important gaps in research capacity, governance, and ethical oversight. Strengthening institutional support, training, and regulatory frameworks are essential for advancing responsible research practices and strengthening One Health research systems. Findings from The Gambia provide valuable insights into broader African challenges and opportunities for improvement.Keywords: 3Rs principles, Africa, Animal experimentation, Animal welfare, Biomedical research, Humane treatment, Research ethics

Background of the study

Across various disciplines in life sciences, including public health, nutrition, clinical, and veterinary medicine, interactions among humans, animals, and their shared environments are increasingly recognized through a One Health approach as central to scientific discovery and health system advancement [1–3]. While human–animal relationships have contributed substantially to advances in health and well-being, they have also sparked ethical debates about the use of animals in research.

Historically, animals have been used as experimental models for understanding anatomy, physiology, and disease processes since antiquity, with practices such as vivisection reflecting early efforts to explore biological systems through direct experimentation on live animals [4]. Comparative medicine, based on similarities in physiology and pathology between humans and animals, further strengthens the scientific rationale for use of animal experimentation, thereby contributing to major advances in biomedical knowledge [5]. However, the use of animals in research has never been without controversy.

Scientific arguments supporting animal models have historically coexisted with concerns regarding animal suffering, welfare, and moral status. Although developments such as anaesthesia and Darwin’s theory of evolution have reinforced the biological continuity between humans and animals and increased the scientific value of animal models [6], the growing recognition of animal sentience and ethical concerns over time, particularly during the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, began to challenge the dominant anthropocentric view of animals as mere tools for human health benefits [4, 7]. This ethical shift, alongside the emergence of pharmacology, toxicology, immunology, and other biomedical sciences during the twentieth century, was accompanied by growing calls for stronger ethical oversight, accountability, and careful use of animal models in biomedical research [8]. While research involving animal models has long advanced health research systems, including pivotal scientific discoveries that have been awarded the Nobel Prizes [9, 10], tension between scientific necessity and ethical responsibility of researchers nonetheless continues to shape contemporary debates on the role of animals in biomedical research and innovation.

Importantly, articulation of the 3Rs principles (Replacement, Reduction, and Refinement) by Russell and Burch in 1959 marked a significant turning point in these debates. These principles now underpin the responsible use of animals in health research globally. Specifically, Replacement encourages non-animal alternatives, Reduction seeks to minimize the number of animals used, and Refinement promotes modifications to reduce animal pain and distress [11–13]. These principles have become the cornerstone of animal experimentation and are enforced through funding requirements, ethical review boards, and international standards set by institutions such as the World Health Organization (WHO), World Organisation for Animal Health (WOAH), and Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD). Nonetheless, implementation of the 3Rs remains uneven globally. While proponents regard the 3Rs as a practical and scientifically robust framework for improving both animal welfare and research quality, critics argue that progress has been slow regarding replacement strategies because of technological limitations and infrastructural and financial constraints, particularly in the low- and middle-income countries [12].

From a One Health perspective, the responsible use of animals in research extends beyond animal welfare considerations. Arguably, ethical and scientifically rigorous animal research can contribute to generation of evidence that informs human health, animal health, environmental sustainability, and responses to emerging challenges such as zoonotic diseases, antimicrobial resistance, and food safety. Therefore, effective implementation of the 3Rs has implications not only for research ethics [5, 13] but also for the quality, credibility, and public acceptability of biomedical and health research.

Globally, institutionalization of the 3Rs is also supported through regulatory frameworks and ethical review mechanisms. However, substantial regional differences remain. In Africa, progress has been heterogeneous rather than uniform. For instance, some countries, including South Africa (South African National Standard–SANS 10386: 2008), Kenya (National Guidelines for Ethical Conduct of Biomedical Research Involving Human Participants–2020), Nigeria (National Code of Health Research Ethics–2007), and Egypt (university-based committees, for example, the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Cairo University–2012), have particularly made significant progress in ethical conduct of biomedical research involving animals. These include the development of national guidelines, mandatory animal ethics committees, and responsible integration of animal welfare standards into health research governance. However, a few other African countries, like Gambia, have no dedicated legislation or coordinated national systems governing animal use in biomedical research. These differences across African countries reflect variations in research investment, institutional capacity, regulatory maturity, and national priorities rather than a simple absence of commitment to research ethics.

Also noteworthy is that some African countries still lack formal policies or legislation that specifically address responsible animal use ethics in biomedical research. Where such ethics committees or institutional review boards exist, they often operate with inadequate resources, limited training, and insufficient infrastructure to ensure effective adherence to the 3Rs principles [14]. Implementation challenges are further compounded by limited access to validated alternatives to animal experimentation, including organ-on-chip technologies, in vitro systems, in silico modelling, and other digital simulation tools. In resource-limited settings, the adoption of replacement strategies, for instance, remains constrained by limited access to alternative research technologies, while broader challenges, including economic limitations, inadequate policy harmonization, and the absence of coordinated regional frameworks, further impede progress toward ethical and innovative health research practices [15]. While biomedical and public health research, particularly in infectious diseases, zoonoses, pharmacology, and vaccine development, is seemingly growing in Africa, there remains limited empirical evidence on awareness, institutional capacity, and the implementation of the 3Rs principles within health research systems. Addressing this evidence gap is important not only to strengthen research ethics but also to advance resilient and accountable health research systems. Therefore, this study assesses the literature and also explores context-specific awareness, institutional practices, and ethical provisions concerning the 3Rs principles, with the aim to inform dialogue, capacity enhancement, and integration of ethical principles within One Health-related research systems in African countries.

Methods

For the review, a structured protocol informed by the PRISMA principles was developed to define the research objectives, questions, search strategy, and eligibility criteria. Search terms were derived from these elements and applied in PubMed, a major biomedical and life sciences database. To improve comprehensiveness and maximize retrieval of relevant studies, the search was also supplemented with EBSCOhost.

The search terms were developed around the three components of 3Rs (Replacement, Reduction, and Refinement), animal research, and African countries. Boolean operators (AND, OR) and truncation using an asterisk (*) were used to capture alternative variations in relevant terminology. A typical search string applied across both bibliographic databases was: (replace*OR substitut* OR surrogate* OR sub OR reserve* OR fill-in* OR stand-in) AND (refin* OR alleviat* OR welfare) AND (reduc* OR limit* OR minimiz* OR minimis* OR abat* or decreas* OR diminish* OR dwindle* OR lessen*) AND (Africa OR Algeria OR Angola OR Benin OR Botswana OR Burkina Faso OR Burundi OR Cabo Verde OR Cameroon OR Central African Republic (CAR) OR Chad OR Comoros OR Congo OR Democratic Republic of Congo OR Cote d’Ivoire OR Djibouti OR Egypt OR Equatorial Guinea OR Eritrea OR Eswatini OR Ethiopia OR Gabon OR Gambia OR Ghana OR Guinea OR Guinea-Bissau OR Kenya OR Lesotho OR Liberia OR Libya OR Madagascar OR Malawi OR Mali OR Mauritania OR Mauritius OR Morocco OR Mozambique OR Namibia OR Niger OR Nigeria OR Rwanda OR Sao Tome and Principe OR Senegal OR Seychelles OR Sierra Leone OR Somalia OR South Africa OR South Sudan OR Sudan OR Tanzania OR Togo OR Tunisia OR Uganda OR Zambia OR Zimbabwe) AND (animal OR pet OR creature OR vertebrate) AND (research OR investigat* OR study OR inquir* OR examin* OR scrutinize* OR test* OR model* OR trial) AND (animal OR pet OR creature OR vertebrate) AND (Laboratory OR lab OR testing room).

Studies included were those conducted in one or more African countries; published in peer-reviewed journals between 2001 (start of a new millennium) and 2022 (COVID-19 recovery phase); involved animal-based biomedical, laboratory, pharmacological, toxicological, or related life sciences research; and explicitly applied one or more of the 3Rs principles. Studies were excluded if they were conducted outside Africa; did not involve research-related animal use; made no explicit reference to the 3Rs; were conference abstracts, editorials, commentaries, theses, or reports; or were not available in English.

A cross-sectional survey was also conducted from late 2019 to early 2020 in Gambia, a country hosting numerous clinical trials and animal studies, often in partnership with international health research institutions. The questionnaire, administered in English, primarily included closed-ended questions, with some open-ended items, to explore participants’ awareness and perceptions of the 3Rs principles and ethical considerations related to animal use in health research. The survey also collected basic sociodemographic information, including sex, profession, and education level. Participants were recruited purposively, with most respondents drawn from a training programme for early career researchers, including paraveterinarians, laboratory technicians, and animal welfare advocates. Additional participants were selected from relevant institutions and professional networks to ensure a diverse range of perspectives. Because the study did not involve clinical interventions or collection of personal identifiers, participation was completely voluntary. Of the 109 questionnaires initially received, 80 were retained for final analysis. Questionnaires with substantial missing or ambiguous data, particularly those lacking key demographic information, were excluded to ensure data integrity and analytical validity. This approach aligned with STROBE guidelines [16] on transparent inclusion criteria and missing data management. Data were entered and cleaned in Microsoft Excel, then analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 22.0). Categorical variables were summarized using frequency distributions, and associations were assessed with Fisher’s exact test, suitable for small or sparse samples. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05, with values between 0.05 and 0.10 noted for further consideration.

Results

A total of 82 peer-reviewed articles were initially identified through searches of PubMed and EBSCOhost, after removing the duplicates. Following title and abstract screening, 64 articles were excluded for not meeting the eligibility criteria. Such studies were unrelated to animal research, conducted outside Africa, or not addressing the 3Rs principles. The full texts of the remaining 18 articles were then assessed for eligibility, resulting in the exclusion of a further 11 studies for focusing on human disease models, environmental contamination, animal research without explicit reference to the 3Rs, or non-African settings. Consequently, seven articles met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final review (Fig. 1).

Table 1 summarizes the features of the studies included in this review. Peer-reviewed articles explicitly reporting the 3Rs principles in African biomedical research was limited, with the identified seven eligible studies originating from six countries: Cameroon, Egypt, Kenya, Morocco, Nigeria, and Tunisia. Across these studies, rodents (rats and mice) were the most commonly used research models. The identified research addressed diverse biomedical and public health topics, including nutrition and drug efficacy, plant-derived therapeutic interventions, reproductive and immunological toxicology, behavioural pharmacology, antivenom development, and environmental health risks. Approximately 43% of the studies involved international collaborations with researchers and institutions from North America, Latin America, and Europe.

Sociodemographic profile of respondents

Table 2 presents the demographic, educational, and professional characteristics of the survey respondents. Most were male (66.3%), and some reported affiliations with multiple institutions. Nearly 90% had not completed a bachelor's degree, although many were either enrolled in higher education programmes or held alternative qualifications such as diplomas. Furthermore, 40% had fewer than 5 years of professional experience, reflecting the early career profile of the study population.

Approximately 64% of respondents reported awareness of ethical principles related to the humane treatment and handling of animals in research, while over 60% indicated familiarity with animal welfare ethics or relevant regulations. Although awareness appeared higher among respondents with higher educational attainment and among females, no statistically significant associations were observed for education level (p = 0.26) or sex (p = 0.99).

Although 78% of respondents recognized the importance of humane animal handling in research, more than 60% reported that their institutions lacked formal protocols or guidelines governing ethical animal use (Fig. 2). Awareness of the 3Rs principles was generally low, with over 80% of respondents indicating unfamiliarity with the concept. Among those who were aware of the 3Rs, knowledge was acquired primarily through formal education or research experience. Nevertheless, nearly all respondents expressed an interest in learning more about the 3Rs principles.

Discussion

Evidence from this study suggests that the explicit reporting and implementation of the 3Rs principles remain limited within African biomedical research. Nevertheless, the predominance of studies published from 2015 onward points to increasing recognition of ethical animal use and the need to strengthen governance of animal-based research across research institutions in Africa. The findings further point to broader challenges in research governance, institutional capacity, and reporting practices. Addressing such gaps is essential to strengthen health research systems through effective institutions, policies, human resources, and governance mechanisms that can generate and translate evidence into improved health outcomes [24, 25].

The 3Rs principles have evolved from being an initial framework for ethical animal research and welfare into an important component of research quality assurance. Available evidence suggests that improved welfare standards and refinement of experimental procedures can enhance reproducibility, reduce unnecessary variability, and increase the reliability of scientific findings [26, 27]. Consequently, implementation of the 3Rs should be viewed not only as an ethical obligation but also as a strategy to strengthen the quality and efficiency of health research systems.

Meanwhile, the limited number of studies identified across Africa also raises concerns regarding institutional support for ethical research governance. Such concerns have been documented concerning low- and middle-income settings, implying that research governance structures often face challenges related to inadequate funding, limited expertise, and weak regulatory frameworks [14, 15]. Those constraints may affect both the conduct and reporting of animal-based biomedical research. In addition, incomplete reporting of ethical considerations contributes to broader concerns regarding research waste and reduced value of scientific investments [28]. By strengthening the integration of the 3Rs within research governance systems, not only will animal welfare improve, but so too will transparency, accountability, and the usefulness of research evidence for policy and practice.

The survey findings in The Gambia point to broader challenges in research governance, capacity development, and institutional support for ethical research practices across Africa. Elsewhere on the continent, limited access to specialized training and ethical oversight mechanisms have also been reported [15, 29]. Strengthening the implementation of the 3Rs will therefore require not only capacity building for health-related professionals but also stronger institutional frameworks, governance mechanisms, and sustained investment in research systems. Importantly, the present findings suggest that awareness alone is insufficient to ensure implementation; rather, effective application of the 3Rs will depend on institutional culture, access to continuous capacity enhancement, availability of resources, and supportive governance structures.

The observed differences in ethical awareness between male and female respondents were not statistically significant and should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Nevertheless, previous studies have reported variations in ethical attitudes across demographic groups, including sex and educational background [30]. Future studies involving larger and more diverse populations may help clarify the factors influencing ethical awareness and implementation across African biomedical research settings. Accordingly, interventions aimed at improving ethical research practices should prioritize systems-level approaches rather than relying solely on individual awareness raising.

Institutional and regulatory gaps emerged as recurring themes across both the literature review and survey findings of this study. While several African countries have established animal ethics committees and developed frameworks for ethical oversight [14, 15], evidence from this study indicated that implementation remains uneven. In contrast, the European Union have established regulatory and institutional mechanisms that require ethical review, justification of animal use, and systematic application of the 3Rs while linking animal welfare standards to research quality and accountability [31, 32]. Although regulatory models may not be transferable across diverse contexts, the aforementioned mechanisms offer valuable lessons for strengthening governance arrangements within African health research systems. As highlighted earlier in the literature [24, 25], effective governance requires legislation, institutional accountability, ethics review processes, monitoring mechanisms, and sustained investment in strenghtening research capacity.

The predominance of rodent models among the reviewed studies mirrors global trends in biomedical research. While animal models continue to contribute significantly to advances in human and veterinary medicine [5, 10], increasing attention is being directed toward ensuring that such research is ethically justified, scientifically rigorous, and socially accountable. Particularly, rodent models remain widely used because of their practical advantages and well-characterized biological systems [32, 33]. However, no animal model can fully replicate human disease complexity, and translational limitations remain an important scientific concern [34, 35]. Consequently, efforts to improve model selection, encourage replacement alternatives, and refine experimental procedures should be viewed as investments in research quality rather than solely as welfare measures. In this regard, expanding access to alternative approaches such as computational modelling, advanced cell culture systems, and organoid technologies may help African research institutions align more closely with international best practices.

The limited number of publications explicitly referencing the 3Rs seems to also reflect broader weaknesses in reporting practices, as some African researchers may apply aspects of replacement, reduction, or refinement without explicitly documenting them. Similar concerns regarding transparency and reporting quality have been highlighted internationally [36]. Improved reporting is particularly important in resource-constrained settings where maximizing the value of research investments is a strategic priority [28]. Therefore, strengthening of reporting standards in Africa is required to improve reproducibility, facilitate evidence synthesis, and enable policymakers and funding agencies to better assess progress in implementing ethical research practices.

The strong interest expressed by almost all the respondents in learning more about the 3Rs presents an important opportunity for capacity strengthening. In this regard, integrating the 3Rs into university curricula (undergraduate and postgraduate), professional training programmes, and continuing education initiatives could possibly improve awareness and implementation. Meanwhile, emerging educational approaches, including simulation-based learning and virtual reality technologies, offer additional opportunities to strengthen skills while reducing reliance on live animals for training purposes [37, 38]. Such interventions should be accompanied by institutional investments in infrastructure, ethics review systems, mentorship, and access to validated alternatives.

Sustainable progress in advancing the 3Rs in Africa will require embedding the 3Rs principles within broader One Health and health research systems strategies. It will also require strengthening research governance, institutional capacity, regulatory frameworks, and multidisciplinary collaboration. Doing so can help ensure that biomedical research is ethically justified, scientifically robust, socially accountable, responsive to public health needs, and contributes to effective health policies across the continent.

From a One Health perspective, responsible animal use in biomedical research has implications that extend beyond laboratory settings. For instance, the interconnectedness of human, animal, and environmental health requires research on systems that can generate evidence while maintaining high ethical and scientific standards [39]. Integrating the 3Rs into biomedical research governance can contribute to broader One Health objectives by promoting responsible evidence generation and supporting coordinated responses to health challenges at the human-animal-environment interface [39, 40]. This approach is particularly relevant in Africa, where public health outcomes remain closely linked to zoonotic diseases, food systems, environmental change, and livestock-dependent livelihoods.

Study limitations

This study has a few limitations. The literature review was restricted to PubMed and EBSCOhost and focused on studies explicitly referencing the 3Rs principles, which may have resulted in the omission of relevantarticles and an underestimation of 3Rs implementation in Africa. The survey was based on a purposively selected sample in The Gambia and relied on self-reported responses, making the findings susceptible to selection, recall, and social desirability biases. In addition, although the study followed a structured protocol informed by the PRISMA principles, a formal quality or risk-of-bias assessment of the reviewed literature was not conducted. Despite these limitations, the study provides valuable baseline evidence on the awareness and implementation of the 3Rs in Africa and identifies priorities for strengthening research governance, ethical oversight, and capacity development.

Conclusion

This study highlights limited awareness, reporting, and implementation of the 3Rs principles within African biomedical research, thereby reflecting broader gaps in research governance, ethical oversight, and institutional capacity. While evidence from this study suggests growing recognition of the 3Rs principles, their integration into health research systems remains inconsistent. Strengthening regulatory frameworks, ethics review processes, researcher training, and institutional support will be critical to advancing responsible and high-quality research practices. Embedding the 3Rs within One Health and health research system approaches can enhance research quality, accountability, and evidence-informed decision-making, ultimately contributing to stronger and more sustainable biomedical research systems across Africa.
