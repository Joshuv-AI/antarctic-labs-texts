Slab stagnation sustained by wet harzburgite and dry basaltic slab assemblages
Authors: Danian Shi, Yang Shen, Rongtao Zhao, Jianyu Shi, Changxin Chen, Xiaoming Xu, Yongqian Zhang, Yao Xu, Hua Xiang, Yan Liu, Yan Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Slab stagnation in the mantle transition zone is critical for the mantle dynamics and evolution of Earth’s interior but remains poorly understood. Here we present seismic images that show two key features near Eurasia’s northeastern margin: 1) a 700-km-deep, 400-km-wide discontinuity within the slab stagnation zone, matching the akimotoite-bridgmanite transition in an anhydrous basaltic assemblage at adiabatic temperature of ~ 1000 K, and 2) the 660-km discontinuity depressed below the Clapeyron slope of the post-spinel phase transition by ~ 15 and ~ 10 km, likely induced by water, respectively in front of the Pacific plate and at the center of the slab stagnation zone. Our mineral physics modeling indicates that slab stagnation is initiated and sustained predominantly by buoyancy from the excess depressions in the pyrolite and harzburgite mantle and aided by buoyancy of the dry basaltic assemblage in the uppermost lower mantle.

Introduction

Subducting plates stagnant in the mantle transition zone (MTZ) have been widely observed by seismic tomography studies1,2. This phenomenon has long been attributed to the negative Clapeyron slopes of the endothermic post-spinel phase transition, which leads to a depression of the 660-km discontinuity and local buoyancy within cold slabs3. Yet, there are also observations of subducted slabs descending straight into the lower mantle without stagnation1,2. Adding to this paradox are recent high-resolution in-situ X-ray diffraction studies using multi-anvil apparatus, which show that the Clapeyron slopes of the post-spinel reaction are too gentle ( − 1.3 to −0.5 Mpa/K, in the temperature range of ambient mantle and slabs) to generate sufficient depression at the 660-km discontinuity and buoyancy required for slab stagnation4. Because of this, slab stagnation has also been attributed to either akimotoite-to-bridgmanite5 or garnet-to-bridgmanite+corundum phase transition6, which possess steeper negative Clapeyron slopes. Currently, it is debated whether slab stagnation is indeed induced by the post-garnet phase transitions5,6, or by factors that affect the post-spinel phase transition, such as water, which may lead to downward displacement of the post-spinel phase-change boundary7. Other factors that may contribute to slab stagnation include upper-to-lower-mantle viscosity jumps in the ambient mantle8 and significant trench retreat at surface9,10. However, the former are presumably global features and do not explain why some slabs are stagnant while others penetrate to the lower mantle. The latter does not impose direct constraints on the forces that drive slab flattening in the MTZ, nor does it explain why slabs remain in the MTZ for tens of millions of years.

The basaltic crust of a subducted oceanic slab possesses negative buoyancy in the MTZ. It becomes buoyant from the 660-km discontinuity to depths of ~720 km11, or ~800 km12, and may be gravitationally trapped at the top of the lower mantle to form a garnetite layer, which also possibly impedes penetration of the subducted slab to the lower mantle and hinders whole-mantle convection at some locations13. Such a scenario depends largely on whether the basaltic crust can be efficiently separated from the cold and stiff subducted slab14,15 to form a garnetite layer.

The northeast margin of the Eurasian continent offers an excellent setting for seismic investigation of the stagnation of subducted slabs in the MTZ. Various seismic studies suggest that the modern Pacific plate has subducted from the Japan trench down to, and commenced stagnation, near the base of the MTZ1,2,16,17. To the southwest of the Pacific subduction zone, tomographic studies also showed conspicuous high-wave-speed anomalies near the base of the MTZ, extending thousands of kilometers from the vicinity of North China to the western Yangtze1,2 (Fig. 1). Due to their fragmented nature and lack of connection with current subduction of the Philippine Sea plate, these high-wave-speed anomalies beneath Yangtze are considered the remnants of stagnant slabs formed during the late Mesozoic subduction, between South and North China or the paleo-Pacific (Izanagi) plate18. The northeast-to-southwest variation of the slabs, therefore, contains information about the evolution of stagnant slabs over tens of millions of years. This region also has a dense collection of broadband seismic stations available from the China Digital Seismic Network and various temporary arrays (Fig. 1). Here, we present the high-resolution seismic imaging and mineral physics modeling of the stagnant slabs in the region. We observe significant excess depressions (~10–15 km) of the post-spinel phase boundary, likely induced by water, and identify a deep discontinuity (~695 km) consistent with the akimotoite-bridgmanite transition in a dry basaltic assemblage. Our results suggest that slab stagnation is primarily initiated and sustained by buoyancy generated from these water-induced excess depressions, with an additional contribution from dry basaltic assemblages localized at the center of the stagnation zone.

Results

The 410-km discontinuity is relatively simple, with an average depth of ~409 km in the region, close to the global average20 (Fig. 2). In contrast, the 660-km discontinuity is more complicated, with significant deepening near the center of stagnant slabs (for example, at locations indicated by ‘5’ and ‘6’ in Fig. 2) and where the subducting Pacific slab appears to be flattened (indicated by ‘4’ in Fig. 2). Outside of the areas with stagnate slabs as defined by high-velocity anomalies in the MTZ, the average depth of the 660-km discontinuity is ~659 km. Generally, a subduction process may lead to shallowing and deepening of the top and bottom boundaries of the MTZ, because the phase changes of the 410- and 660-km discontinuities have positive and negative Clapeyron slopes21, respectively. In addition, the topography of the 660-km discontinuity should be much more subdued than that of the 410-km discontinuity for the same thermal anomaly, because the former has a gentler Clapeyron slope5. Our observations of a nearly flat 410-km discontinuity at normal depth and obvious deepening of the 660-km discontinuity (Fig. 2) suggest that the thermal (and chemical) perturbations caused by stagnant slabs are significant only in the lower half of the MTZ in the region.

Basaltic assemblage concentrated at the center of the stagnation zone

Double seismic phases are observed at the upper- and lower-mantle boundary near the center of the stagnation zone (indicated by ‘1’ and ‘2’ in Fig. 2). Phase ‘1’ appears at a depth of ~670 km. Phase ‘2’ is observed at ~695 km depth (Fig. 2a), beyond the depth range allowed for the post-spinel phase transition5, but consistent with the occurrence of the transition from akimotoite to bridgmanite in a relatively cold region5,22. Both phases can be simultaneously and consistently observed on the non-stacked receiver functions derived from earthquakes at various epicentral distances and back-azimuths (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. 2). They have moveout curves similar to that of P660S, but different from those of major known body-wave-generated noise, involving phases PcP, PP, pP and sP. These observations indicate that the double phases are unlikely to be an artifact of stacking derived from the effects of unaccounted seismic anisotropy, inhomogeneity along the ray paths, or the contamination of these other-body-wave-generated noises. Theoretically, an increase in the basalt fraction leads to a decrease in the RF amplitudes from the 660-km discontinuity25 and an increase in the RF amplitudes from the akimotoite-to-bridgmanite phase-change boundary (Supplementary Figs. 5 and 6). Indeed, phase ‘1’ has lower amplitude than the 660-km discontinuity in the rest of the study region and phase ‘2’ (Fig. 2). Following ref. 25, we used the RF amplitude ratios of phase ‘2’ to phase ‘1’, which eliminate the effects of ray parameters (Supplementary Fig. 6), to estimate basalt fractions (f), and obtained f = ~0.6 to ~0.9, which suggests a high concentration of basaltic contents in the site of the double phases (Fig. 4b). Such a high concentration is likely formed from, 1) the results of basaltic crust detached from16, or peeling off26, the top of the plate, or 2) the results of subducted basaltic ridge trapped at the top of the lower mantle27. Although geodynamic modeling has not demonstrated an easy separation of the basaltic crust at MTZ depths14,15, the first mechanism cannot be ruled out. The stagnant cold slabs may become substantially weaker due to grain-size reduction associated with the phase transformations in the MTZ28, and cold, wet slabs are most susceptible to transition-induced weakening, with a transient weakening of up to three orders of magnitude during the olivine to ringwoodite phase transitions29. Such an interpretation is also consistent with the observation of an aseismic feature in the MTZ near the center of the stagnation zone (Figs. 1, 2). This place is also near the proposed location of the extinct Izanagi-Pacific spreading ridge that merged with the trench of the long-lasting subduction zone along the eastern margin of Asia from ~60 to 50 Ma30. Therefore, the second interpretation cannot be ruled out either. Nevertheless, the lack of observation of the akimotoite-to-bridgmanite phase boundary elsewhere suggests that the basaltic contents are relatively low under most of the 660-km discontinuity (f < 0.4 and Supplementary Fig. 6).

Excess depressions imaged in the post-spinel transitions

Some of the observed depths of the 660-km discontinuity near the center of the stagnation zone (for example, ~678 km, at the place indicated by ‘5’ or ‘6’ in Fig. 2) exceed the maximum depth limit (~668 km) of the Clapeyron slope curve for that phase change by ~10 km (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. 3a) and cannot be accounted for by under-correction of velocity inhomogeneity (under-correction of upper-mantle low-velocity anomalies may contribute to deepening of both the 410- and the 660-km discontinuities, while under-correction of high-velocity anomalies in the MTZ may contribute to an apparent shallowing rather than deepening of the 660-km discontinuity; see Methods). Similarly, at a depth of ~683 km, phase ‘4’ appears deeper than the limit expected for the normal post-spinel phase change by ~15 km (Fig. 4a). Similar depressions (~20–30 km) were observed previously in a narrow region along the strike in the front of the Pacific subducting plate hundred(s) kilometers north of our profiles B and C31,32. One interpretation is to associate this phase with the akimotoite-to-bridgmanite phase change at a mantle temperature of ~50 K higher than that of phase ‘2’ (Supplementary Fig. 3a), and attribute the disappearance of the 660-km discontinuity to an extreme concentration of basaltic component (f ≈ 1.0; cf. Supplementary Fig. 6a), or a coincidence of the negative phase ‘X2’, at the location ‘3’ (Fig. 2a). The second interpretation is to associate this phase with the post-spinel phase change and attribute the observed deepening of the boundary to a delay in the phase transition due to kinetics of the mantle under the inflection point of the subducting Pacific slab. This requires downwelling of the mantle beneath the slab deflection point and a dry mantle condition that maintains slow reaction rates, both of which are unlikely in this region. The third and our preferred interpretation is to associate this phase with the influence of minor elements (e.g., Fe and Al)21,33 or volatiles (e.g., water)7,34. In contrast to Fe, which may shift the post-spinel transition boundary to lower pressure21, an increase in Al content may shift the post-spinel transition to higher pressure33. However, the depression effects of Al may be largely counteracted by Fe34, due to the strong coupling between Fe and Al in perovskite35, resulting in no depression of the post-spinel discontinuity as observed at location ‘1’, where the basaltic slab assemblage (enriched with Fe and Al) dominates. Therefore, the minor elements Fe and Al may not be the primary factors for the excess depression. Association of the depression with water is consistent with recent experimental studies that suggest hydrous conditions are necessary for the observation of phase ‘X2'16. Previous seismic waveform modeling36 and electromagnetic studies37 suggest that the western Pacific subduction zone is particularly wet, with water contents as high as ~0.5–1.0 wt% in the MTZ37. Petrological experiments7 indicate that ~2.0 wt% water in ringwoodite may shift the phase change boundary downwards by ~15 km at 1473 K, and ~30 km at a cold mantle temperature of ~1000 K. Therefore, the excess depression of ~15 km at the location ‘4’ can be explained by the existence of ~2.0 wt% water near the bottom of the MTZ. Similarly, the extra deepening of ~10 km at the location ‘5’ or ‘6’ can be explained by the existence of ~0.7 wt% water, given the nearly linear relationship between excess depression of the post-spinel phase transition and water content38.

We note that no significant excess depression of the 660-km discontinuity at the location ‘1’ (Fig. 4a) suggests that the water content is low near the center of the basaltic mantle assemblage. This may be due to a relatively low water solubility of majorite garnet in the MTZ39 and the transition-zone water filter40. The basaltic mantle assemblage in the uppermost lower mantle is depleted of water due to the transition zone filter effect and buoyant (see Fig. 5b). Slab stagnation explained by excess depressions of the transition

To understand the dynamics of slab stagnation in the region, we calculated the density contrasts of a cold slab with respect to the pyrolitic surrounding mantle (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Fig. 7). Our results show that buoyancy due to density contrasts derived from thermal and petrological difference (including the normal anhydrous post-spinel and post-garnet phase transitions) is relatively small, and present only between ~660 and ~720 km depths (indicated by brown area in Fig. 5b), no matter whether basalt fraction f is 0 or 1 (that is, no matter whether the akimotoite-to-bridgmanite or garnet-out phase boundaries is visible or not). We also modeled the contribution to the buoyancy from the (excess) depression of the post-spinel phase-transition boundary beneath the center of the stagnation zone (Fig. 5d) and at the front of the subducting Pacific plate (Fig. 5e). Our results show that the buoyancy increases drastically with the excess depression (cf. Supplementary Fig. 7c–e), and the ~10 km excess depression near the center of the stagnation zone can generate a significant increment of buoyancy (see brown areas in Fig. 5c, d). Without the contribution from this excess depression, the thermo-chemical buoyancy of the slab with respect to the pyrolitic surrounding mantle (indicated by brown area in Fig. 5c), is much lower than the negative buoyancy of the slabs in the MTZ (indicated by cyan area in Fig. 5c), with a positive-to-negative buoyancy ratio (R) of only ~27.5%, assuming the slabs above the 660-km discontinuity have an average thickness of ~100 km as estimated from the distance between phases ‘X1’ and ‘X2’. The excess depression of 10 km increases the ratio R to ~97.5%. Therefore, together the buoyancy from the excess depression of the 660-km discontinuity and the normal thermo-chemical buoyancy of the slabs are approximately in balance of the downward force of the slabs in the MTZ, maintaining slab stagnation (Fig. 5d). In the front end of the subduction plate, the buoyancy derived from the total depression of ~25 km (relative to the normal mantle outside of the slab stagnation and subduction zone, including the excess depression of ~15 km) of the post-spinel phase boundary (proportional to the brown shaded area in Fig. 5e) is, however, much smaller compared to the overriding burden of the subducting Pacific plate that possesses large positive density contrasts above the 660-km discontinuity (denoted by the cyan shaded area in Fig. 5b, assuming the plate is composed of 10% basaltic crust and 90% harzburgite mantle lithosphere). In light of the observed bending of the plate, the gentle dip (~25°), and rheological weakness of the cold slabs due to small spinel grain-size28, we infer that the upward phase buoyancy, together with the mantle viscous resistance8, provides the bending moment to deflect the subducting plate to commence stagnation in the MTZ (Supplementary Fig. 8).

Discussion

Our findings suggest that the excess depression of the post-spinel phase-change boundary, most likely induced by water, plays a dominant role in slab flattening and stagnation in the MTZ. In comparison, the normal thermally induced post-spinel and akimotoite-to-bridgmanite5 phase transitions combined contribute no more than half of the buoyancy required for the stagnation (cf. Fig. 5c, d). Our results suggest only a localized high (f = 0.6–0.9) concentration of basaltic content in the center of the stagnation zone, within a 400-wide zone near the upper-lower mantle boundary. Our observations do not support the presence of a widely distributed garnetite layer that plays a major role in slab stagnation13 in this region.

Our observations provide an explanation for the long-term (likely >tens Ma) presence of the slab stagnation zone in the region. With a higher water solubility than the upper and lower mantle41,42, the MTZ is capable of preserving water40 and thus the excess depressions of the 660-km discontinuity, for an exceptionally long (>1Gyr) period43. Although tectonic reconstruction and numerical modeling show that the history of the Izu-Bonin trench is dominated by fast, episodic retreat from 40 to 6 Ma44, which may have resulted in the gentle subduction that facilitated the stagnation10 of the Pacific plate in the MTZ in the earlier Cenozoic, trench retreat, by itself, does not explain the forces required for the tens-of-millions-of-years stagnation of the Mesozoic slabs thousands of kilometers away to the southwest, nor the forces at the inflection point of the Pacific slab where it is deflected horizontally.

Methods

Seismic data and construction of receiver-function cross-sections

We collected seismic data from broadband seismic stations in China and adjacent regions, including permanent stations from the China Earthquake Administration45, temporary stations from ChinArray46, NECsaids (the Northeast China Seismic Array to Investigate Deep Subduction), NECESSArray (the Northeast China Extended Seismic Array)16 and NETS (NorthEastern Tibet Seismic) experiments47. We processed waveform data from teleseismic events with magnitudes larger than 5.0 and epicentral distances ranging from 30 to 90°. We used the time-domain iterative deconvolution technique48 in the calculation of the RFs, in which the Gaussian filter factor was set to 2 to suppress high-frequency ( ≥ 1 Hz) noise. We visually examined all waveform data, corrected for sensor misorientation and discarded data with abnormal waveforms and low coherence. We finally obtained a total of 65,655 high-quality RFs from 1339 seismic stations.

P-wave receiver function (RF) technique is an effective tool for imaging slab interfaces49 and mantle discontinuities down to the MTZ depths16. We implemented a procedure similar to the common-conversion-point method but without the plane-wave assumption; RFs were migrated from time to space in a 3-D volume via dynamic ray tracing50 from the sources to the seismic stations according to the Earth reference model AK135 (cf. Supplementary Fig. 1c) and then corrected for the Earth’s crust, ellipticity and station elevation. To improve depth accuracy, we used seismic tomographic models to correct for 3-D velocity inhomogeneity along the ray paths.

For each segment, let Δt denote the reference-model travel time through that segment, and let δm denote the relative velocity perturbation in the tomographic model (δVp/Vp for P wave, δVs/Vs for converted S wave). Validation of the excess depressions by different tomographic models

We used three different tomographic models, TX2019slab51, UU-P0752 and FWEA2317, to correct for the 3-D velocity inhomogeneity and validate the observation of the excess depressions. TX2019slab is a global travel-time tomographic model that incorporates a 3-D thermal model of subducting slabs in the inversion. Since the crustal anomalies were removed as station statics and not included in TX2019slab, we performed a crustal correction to the RFs when using this model. FWEA23 is a regional full-wave tomographic model for East Asia. It is worth noting that the delay-time anomalies derived from excess depression of the discontinuity may be partly explained by the velocity perturbations above the discontinuity in the inversion of full-waveforms that contain converted waves (such as P410S and P660S), especially in places that lack crossing rays, such as the east margins of our profiles. In addition, FWEA23 has better resolution for the S-wave than for the P-wave. All these may lead to an over-correction and a more flattened discontinuity observed in the RF profiles, though whether padded with a global model or not does not have an observable influence on the results. In contrast, UU-P07 is a global high-resolution P-wave tomographic model. Previous tomographic models17,51 show that the S-wave velocity anomalies are similar in pattern to, but about 1.5 ± 0.5 times of, the P-wave anomalies (with correlation coefficients of about 92% and 87% between the P- and S-wave anomalies in FWEA23 and TX2019slab, respectively). Therefore, it is possible to use UU-P07 to correct for the 3-D velocity inhomogeneity by assuming a scale factor between the P- and S-wave velocity anomalies. Indeed, assuming a larger scale factor may lead to an overcorrection, and a discontinuity to be imaged shallower than its true position, due to the fact that the tomographic model is dominated by large-scale low-velocity anomalies in the upper mantle and some relatively small high-velocity anomalies in the MTZ17,51(Supplementary Fig. 3a).

Our sensitivity tests using the three models show that our key results for the excess depressions of the 660-km discontinuity remain unchanged when corrected with different models (Supplementary Fig. 3a). For example, phases ‘1’ and ‘2’ appear at similar depths. Though the maximum depression center changes from location ‘5’ to ‘6’, the maximum depression magnitude remains unchanged when we corrected with FWEA23 instead of TX2019slab and UU-P07. A notable difference is observed for phase ‘4’, which appears at much shallower depths (though still much below the depth limit of anhydrous post-spinel Clapeyron curve) if corrected with FWEA23 and possibly a consequence of over-correction. In short, using different tomographic models did not alter our core conclusions. The receiver functions shown in Fig. 2 were corrected using TX2019slab, which is independent of the converted waves.

Mineral physics modeling of post-spinel phase transition

We computed the effects of temperature and composition on mineral phase equilibria, and the depth variation of seismic velocities and densities with the Perple X code23 and the elastic parameter database of ref. 24. We consider the subducted slab to be either an equilibrium assemblage (EA) or a mechanical mixture (MM) of basalt (i.e., oceanic crust) and harzburgite (i.e., oceanic mantle lithosphere). The mechanical mixture is a good approximation when the re-equilibration rate of basalt and harzburgite in the slab is considered to be slow53, and is more convenient to be used for comparison under the first-order approximation. The end member compositions basalt and harzburgite are from ref. 53 (see Supplementary Table 1).

Based on thermodynamic relations, water incorporation into ringwoodite can expand its stability field to higher pressures relative to anhydrous system7. We modeled such delays in the phase transitions by shifting the relevant velocity and density curves downwards by the corresponding depths, irrespective of which minor element indeed caused the additional (or excess) depression of the discontinuity.
