Energetic value and digestibility of kikuyu grass and annual ryegrass with the inclusion of exogenous enzymes for laying hens
Authors: Marcelo S. Suyama, Marcos J. Pinheiro, Loirana L. Rosa, Lays M.O. Ferreira, Clóvis E. Gewehr
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The objective of the study was to determine the energy value and apparent digestibility of kikuyu grass and annual ryegrass using exogenous digestive enzymes (EDE), and to propose prediction equations for apparent metabolizable energy (AME) to estimate the energy values of grasses for laying hens. A total of 144 Hisex Brown layers were allocated in a completely randomized design within a 4 × 2 factorial arrangement, comprising four levels of kikuyu grass or annual ryegrass substitution: 0, 30, 60, and 90 g/kg, with or without EDE. The birds were 72 weeks old in the kikuyu grass experiment and 95 weeks old in the annual ryegrass experiment. A linear decrease was observed in the values of AME, apparent digestibility of dry matter (DM), energy, and neutral detergent fiber (NDF) of the diets as the level of grass substitution increased (P < 0.05). The digesta passage time reduced linearly with the increase in the level of grass substitution (P < 0.05). The apparent digestibility of the ashes increased in the treatments that received kikuyu grass and at the level of 60 g/kg of ryegrass (P < 0.05). The apparent digestibility of crude protein (CP) improved in diets containing ryegrass (P < 0.05). The EDE improved the apparent digestibility of NDF and CP in kikuyu grass diets and CP in ryegrass diets (P < 0.05). The prediction equations utilizing two predictors, formed by gross energy (GE) and acid detergent fiber (ADF), or digested GE and DM, increased R2. Ether extract shows a strong correlation with the prediction of AME content in grasses. The values of nitrogen corrected AME (AMEn) for kikuyu were 261.5 kcal/g, and 339.3 kcal/kg for ryegrass, in natural matter (NM). The standardized AME (AMEs) of kikuyu was 267.6 kcal/kg, and 341.1 kcal/kg for ryegrass, in NM. The energy value and digestibility of ryegrass were higher than kikuyu grass, due to the lower amount of NDF and lignin in its composition. The use of exogenous digestive enzymes did not increase the AME value of the grasses. The prediction equations of AME can be used to estimate the energy value of grasses for laying hens.

Introduction

Grass consumption can increase egg weight, improve yolk pigmentation (Iqbal et al., 2018), and elevate polyunsaturated n-3 fatty acid levels, thereby improving the lipid profile of the eggs by decreasing the n-6/n-3 ratio (Mugnai et al., 2014; Kop-Bozbay et al., 2021). Despite this, the nutritional impact of consuming grasses is still largely unexplored due to the limited information currently available on the digestibility and energy value of grasses for poultry. However, given that their nutritional value is inferior compared to the feed, the ingestion of grasses may lead to a dilution of energy and nutrients in the diet (Singh et al., 2016).

There are few studies available in the literature evaluating the digestibility of grasses, comprising experiments with broilers utilizing white clover and alfalfa (Pleger et al., 2021) or pasture consisting of grasses and legumes in roosters (Buchanan et al., 2007). Iqbal et al. (2019) evaluated the digestibility in laying hens of diets with access to fescue, but they did not investigate the nutritional value of the grass.

The inclusion of EDE, including xylanase, β-glucanase, and protease, enhances nutrient digestibility and intestinal absorption by mitigating the adverse effects of elevated fiber consumption in laying hens with access to vegetated areas (Iqbal et al., 2019). Additionally, the utilization of energy from forages in roosters can be enhanced by carbohydrases (Buchanan et al., 2007).

Annual ryegrass (Lolium multiflorum L.) is a predominant grass species in temperate climates, recognized for its rapid growth, high productivity, and palatability (Carrasco-Chilón et al., 2024). Kikuyu grass (Pennisetum clandestinum Hochst. ex Chiov.) is one of the main forage species for grazing in tropical and subtropical regions, and highly resistant to grazing due to the presence of underground rhizomes (Benvenutti et al., 2020). Temperate and tropical grasses have significant differences in chemical composition, which can also vary depending on soil characteristics and defoliation height, making prediction equations useful for estimating energy value based on chemical composition or digested nutrients (Cerrate et al., 2019).

The lack of studies on the digestibility of grasses can be attributed to their chemical composition, characterized by low concentrations of starch and lipids, with a high proportion of NDF and ADF, making them a feedstuff with low nutritional potential for poultry. The consumption of grasses can negatively affect the voluntary feed intake, resulting in a reduction in energy and nutrient intake, which can impact the productive performance of laying hens (Iqbal et al., 2018). Thus, determining the nutritional value of grasses will contribute to the formulation of feeds and prevent diet dilution. It is hypothesized in this study that the energy value and digestibility of grasses for laying hens are inversely related to their NDF and ADF content; the inclusion of EDE can improve the utilization of energy and nutrients from grasses by improving fiber digestibility; increasing substitution levels of grasses will reduce the digestibility of diets due to the increased fiber intake; and reliable prediction equations can be developed to estimate the AME of grasses for laying hens.

The objective of the study was to determine the energy value and apparent digestibility of annual ryegrass and kikuyu grass using exogenous digestive enzymes through regression method and to propose prediction equations for AME to estimate the energy values of the grasses for laying hens.

Materials and methods

The experiment was conducted in Lages, Santa Catarina, Brazil, Latitude: 27° 47′ 19.975″ S, Longitude: 50° 18′ 14.105″ W and elevation of 930 m, carried out in accordance with the regulations issued by the National Council for Animal Experimentation and approved by the Ethics Committee on Animal Use of the State University of Santa Catarina (UDESC), protocol number 5750250424.

Experimental design

A total of 144 Hisex Brown layers, with a 5 % variation in the average weight (95 % uniformity), were allocated in a completely randomized design within a 4 × 2 factorial arrangement. This arrangement comprised four levels of grass substitution: 0, 30, 60, and 90 g/kg, with or without the addition of EDE, resulting in 8 treatments, each replicated 6 times with 3 birds per cage (experimental unit). In the kikuyu grass experiment, the birds were 72 weeks old and had an average body weight of 1.850 kg and an average egg production of 93.62 %. In the annual ryegrass experiment, the birds were 95 weeks old and had an average body weight of 1.925 kg and an average egg production of 84.09 %.

Both experiments lasted 8 days, with the first four days for the adaptation of the birds to the experimental diets and the following four days for the collection of excreta and measurement of feed intake. The birds were housed in an experimental aviary with open-air ventilation, in metabolic cages (50 cm x 50 cm x 45 cm) with an excreta collection tray and equipped with a trough feeder and a nipple drinker. During the experimental period, the birds received mash feed and water ad libitum, with a continuous light program of 16 hours light and 8 hours dark.

Birds that received diets with grasses were pre-adapted for a period of four weeks, preceding experiments with kikuyu grass or annual ryegrass, for the adaptation of the gastrointestinal tract (GIT) through the addition of 30 g/kg of the respective grasses, dried at 60°C for 72 h and grounded.

Digesta Passage Time

Prior to the start of the excreta collection period, the birds were fasted for 3 h, and ferric oxide (1 g/kg) was added to the feed. The duration of digesta passage was documented from the provision of feed until the appearance of red-stained excreta (Abdollahi et al., 2019).

Sample collection and analysis

The beginning of the excreta collection period was marked by the provision of feed with the addition of ferric oxide, and at the end of the collection period, feed consumption was determined. Stainless steel trays covered with plastic were used and placed under the cages. The collections were carried out twice a day at 8:00 a.m. and 4:00 p.m. to avoid fermentation and nutrient loss. During the four days of the collection period, the excreta samples from each cage were weighed and stored in a freezer at a temperature of −20°C. The excreta samples were thawed at room temperature, homogenized, and a 300 g sample from each experimental unit was separated for pre-drying in a forced ventilation oven at 60°C for 72 h. The excreta samples, experimental diets, and test foods were ground in a Wiley mill with a 1.0 mm sieve and kept refrigerated at 4°C until laboratory analyses were performed.

The kikuyu grass, annual ryegrass, excreta samples, and experimental diets from the experiments were analyzed (Table 3) using methodologies described by AOAC (Latimer, 2023). The DM was determined in an oven at 105°C for 14 h (Latimer, 2023: method 930.15). Nitrogen (N) was determined by the combustion method using the Leco AC500 analyzer (Leco Corp., St. Joseph, MI) with EDTA for calibration. The CP was obtained by multiplying the nitrogen content by 6.25. The GE was determined using a calorimetric bomb (IKA C200, IKA-Werke GmbH & Co. KG, Staufen, BW, Germany) calibrated with benzoic acid. NDF and ADF were determined using the ANKOM200 fiber analyzer (ANKOM Technology, Macedon, NY) (Latimer, 2023: method 973.18). The samples were incinerated in a muffle at 550°C for 4 h to obtain the ashes (Latimer, 2023: method 923.03). The analysis of ether extract (EE) was performed on kikuyu grass and annual ryegrass through extraction with petroleum ether in the Soxhlet extractor (Latimer, 2023: method 920.39). Calculations of metabolizable energy and digestibility coefficients

The AME of the experimental diets was calculated using the equation proposed by Potter and Matterson (1960), where: AME (kcal/kg, DM) = (GEing – GEexcr) ÷ DMing, with GEing being the product of the dry matter ingested (DMing) by the GE of the diet, and GEexcr being the product of the amount of excreta produced by the GE of the excreta. The AME value was adjusted for zero nitrogen retention utilizing a correction factor of 8.22 kcal/kg (Hill and Anderson, 1958), through the equation: AMEn = AME – (8.22 x Nret), where Nret represents the nitrogen retained, calculated by subtracting the nitrogen ingested from the diet (Ning) from the nitrogen content in the excreta. The AME can be standardized for positive nitrogen balance, since layers retain 50 % of the ingested nitrogen (Cozannet et al., 2010), with: AMEs = AME – [8.22 x (Nret – 0.5 x Ning)].

The TD was composed of the RD and the inclusion of the grasses in place of the energy-providing ingredients, so that the GE of the TD is equivalent to the sum of the GE of RD and the test feedstuffs (GEtd = GErd + GEtf), with Prd and Ptf being the proportion of GE in the TD coming from the RD and the test feedstuff, respectively, such that Prd + Ptf = 1 (Zhang and Adeola, 2017). To calculate the AME of the test feedstuff (AMEtf), it is assumed that: AMEtd = (AMErd x Prd) + (AMEtf x Ptf) making Prd = 1 - Ptf, we obtain: AMEtf = AMErd + [(AMEtd – AMErd) ÷ Ptf]. Ptf was obtained by: Ptf = [(Feedstuff substitution level x DMtf ÷ 1000) x GEtf] ÷ GEtd. DMtf is the dry matter of the feedstuff, GEtf and GEtd are the GE of feedstuff and test diet, respectively.

The apparent digested nutrients were calculated using the following equation (Potter et al., 1962): DN (g, DM) = (Nuting – Nutexcr) ÷ DMing, Nuting refers to the nutrient ingested through the diet and Nutexcr to the amount of the nutrient excreted, the digested nutrient from the test feedstuff (DNtf) was obtained by: DNtf = DNrd + [(DNtd – DNrd) ÷ Pnut], with Pnut being the proportion of the nutrient derived from the inclusion of the feedstuff in the respective TD. The apparent digestibility coefficients (DC) of the nutrients or energy were calculated as follows: DC (%, DM) = (Ad ÷ Ac) × 100. Where Ad is the portion of the nutrient digested or energy obtained from the diets or the feedstuff, and Ac refers to the composition of the nutrient or GE of the diets or the feedstuff.

Statistical analysis

The results of AME, AMEn, AMEs, the DC of the experimental diets, the evaluated grasses, and the digesta passage time were analyzed by two-factor ANOVA, with the levels of grass substitution and the EDE as factors. The means were compared using Tukey's HSD test. The level of substitution was analyzed by polynomial regression to determine if the increasing inclusion of grasses has a linear or quadratic effect. The residuals were tested for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test.

The values of AMEn, AMEs, and the DC of the grasses with and without EDE were obtained using a linear regression procedure and refer to the slope of the equations. The amount of AMEn and AMEs consumed from the evaluated gasses (kcal) was regressed against the amount of grass consumed (kg) in each experimental unit. The DC were obtained by regressing the digested nutrient (g) against the amount of nutrient consumed (g) in each experimental unit. Terms whose residuals showed 3 standard deviations were removed from the linear regression procedure. The prediction equations for AMEn and AMEs for the combination of grasses were determined by multiple linear regression using the stepwise method, with the predictor factors of the equations being nutrients and energy consumed or digested or both from the grasses. Terms whose residuals showed 3 standard deviations were removed from the linear regression procedure. Only the regression equations that showed a significant level were represented. Collinearity was inspected using the variance inflation factor (VIF) for the predictors. A VIF > 10.0 was considered indicative of potential multicollinearity among the model predictors.

The statistical procedures were carried out using IBM SPSS Statistics Software (Version 27.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY), considering a significance level of P < 0.05.

Results

Kikuyu grass

The increasing substitution of kikuyu grass in the TD caused a linear reduction in the values of AME, AMEn and AMEs (Table 4) and there was a difference between the four inclusion levels (0, 30, 60 and 90 g/kg) (P < 0.001). However, the EDE did not show a significant effect on the metabolizable energy value of diets (P > 0.05). The digestibility coefficients of kikuyu grass and annual ryegrass were determined through linear regression equations, represented by the slope (Table 10). Discussion

Effects of fiber on the digestibility of energy and nutrients in diets

Due to the effects caused by fiber on the birds' GIT, a pre-adaptation of the layers with 30 g/kg of dried grasses was carried out for four weeks before the start of the experiments. Feedstuffs rich in insoluble fibers mainly cause the adaptation of the gizzard (Abdollahi et al., 2019), the increase of digestive secretions (Svihus et al., 2004; Yokhana et al., 2016), and the adaptation of the caecum in size and fermentative activity by the caecal microbiota (Röhe et al., 2020). Thus, the development of GIT can prevent the underestimation of the nutritional values of grasses.

The results demonstrate the effects of increasing levels of substitution with insoluble fiber rich feedstuffs on the utilization of energy and nutrients in the diets of laying hens (Table 4, Table 7). A linear decrease in the values of AME and the digestibility of DM, energy, and NDF was observed as the level of grass substitution increased. This occurred due to the higher composition of NDF and ADF in the experimental diets, derived from kikuyu grass (593.7 g/kg of NDF and 223.5 g/kg of ADF) and annual ryegrass (512.8 g/kg of NDF and 224.4 g/kg of ADF). These results highlight the antinutritional effects of dietary fiber in the diets of laying hens, as the birds have a limited capacity to digest non-starch polysaccharides (NSP) due to the lack of endogenous digestive enzymes to hydrolyzing the β-bonds present in carbohydrate chains (Raza et al., 2019), combined with a reduced digesta passage time and a relatively short caecum, which limits the digestion capacity of NSP by the caecal microbiota (de Vries et al., 2012).

The passage time of the digesta decreased linearly with the increase in the level of substitution of the grasses, with a shorter passage time at the level of 90 g/kg compared to the RD in both grasses. This result is directly related to the lower values of AME and digestibility of energy and DM at the level of 90 g/kg, since the digesta had a shorter passage time through GIT, interfering with the action of digestive enzymes secreted by the birds and intestinal absorption. Moreover, the inclusion level can interfere with the passage time of the digesta, as the levels of 30 and 60 g/kg did not differ from the RD. As observed by Abdollahi et al. (2019), the inclusion of 30 g/kg of insoluble fiber-rich feedstuff for broilers did not alter the passage time.

The digestibility of NDF was not affected by the substitution level in the experiment with ryegrass, and the reduction in energy digestibility between 0 and 90 g/kg was 4.8 %. In the experiment with kikuyu grass, this reduction was greater at 7.4 %, with a linear decrease in NDF digestibility. The values of the standard error of the mean for AME, digestibility of DM, energy, NDF, and ADF were higher in the experiment with kikuyu grass. These results may indicate that fiber digestibility and energy utilization could be related to the higher NDF composition of kikuyu grass compared to ryegrass, suggesting that diet energy utilization is negatively influenced by the NDF content of the feedstuff and the diet (Liang et al., 2023). However, other factors related to birds can alter the efficiency in the utilization of energy and nutrients from the diets. Body weight (Cozannet et al., 2010) or the development of the GIT and, consequently, with the age of the bird (Mateos et al., 2019) can alter efficiency in the utilization of energy and nutrients from the diets, especially in ingredients rich in NDF and ADF. Considering that the birds in the experiment with ryegrass had a higher average age and weight compared to those with kikuyu grass.

Despite the increase in the level of substitution of grasses reducing energy utilization and DM digestibility, there was an increase in the digestibility of CP and ashes in the diets. The increase in digestibility may be mainly due to the development of the gizzard and the increased secretion of hydrochloric acid and pepsinogen by the proventriculus. Iqbal et al. (2018) demonstrated that the consumption of forages by laying hens can increase the weight of the empty gizzard, indicating the adaptation of gizzard due to the increased intake of insoluble fibers, which can enhance the secretion of hydrochloric acid and pepsinogen by the proventriculus (Abdollahi et al., 2019). The gizzard plays an important role in regulating motility and controlling the flow of digesta to the duodenum, so the consumption of insoluble fibers results in an increase in the retention time of feed in the gizzard for grinding (Mateos et al., 2012). The longer retention time favors the development of the gizzard and the action of hydrochloric acid and pepsinogen secreted by the proventriculus. The reduction in pH increases the solubilization of minerals, which facilitates intestinal absorption (Guinotte et al., 1995), as demonstrated by the increase in ashes digestibility in treatments that received kikuyu grass and at the level of 60 g/kg of ryegrass. Moreover, the reduction in pH and the increase in pepsin activity can improve protein utilization by enhancing denaturation and proteolysis through the consumption of insoluble fibers (Li et al., 2017).

The addition of low levels of ingredients rich in insoluble fibers can improve the digestibility of diets. The inclusion of oat hulls with 671 g/kg of NDF and 320 g/kg of ADF in the diet of broilers at a concentration of 30 g/kg improved the utilization of DM, organic matter, N, energy, and ashes of the diet as observed by (González-Alvarado et al., 2010). Abdollahi et al. (2021) did not find a significant difference in the utilization of DM, organic matter, CP, and ash with the inclusion of wheat bran with 593 g/kg of NDF and 119 g/kg of ADF, at levels of 30 and 60 g/Kg compared to the control in diets for laying hens at 99 weeks of age. In the present study, only the utilization of CP in the experiment with ryegrass and ash in the experiment with kikuyu increased with the substitution of 30 g/kg, indicating that the utilization of energy and nutrients in the diets may be related to the species, age, and composition of the fiber used.

In formulating poultry diets, minimal inclusion of feedstuffs rich in fiber is prioritized to prevent the occurrence of their antinutritional effects. Nonetheless, as previously mentioned, the GIT can adapt to increased intake of NSP. Elevating NDF concentration from 145 to 185 g/kg during the growth phase (7 to 12 weeks of age) did not influence carcass composition or weight gain in laying hens (Freitas et al., 2014), and NDF levels between 90.1 and 134.3 g/kg in 75-week-old laying hens do not affect productive performance (Han et al., 2023). The NDF content of the diets in the present study ranged from 114.5 to 153.6 g/kg and the ADF content from 39.3 to 54.6 g/kg, and despite the linear reduction in AME with the increase in fiber in the diets, the AME value was higher than that recommended by Rostagno et al. (2017). According to these findings, birds can adapt their GIT to digest diets that contain higher levels of NDF and ADF and absorb the nutrients and energy that are essential for their growth and production. This allows for a wider range of fiber content in their diet formulation. However, conclusions cannot be made since the objective of the research was to evaluate the digestibility and energy value of grasses in such a way that long-term evaluations are more appropriate for assessing the effects of grass consumption by laying hens on productive parameters and egg quality (Mugnai et al., 2014; Iqbal et al., 2018; Kop-Bozbay et al., 2021).

Effects of EDE on the digestibility of energy and nutrients in diets

The effect caused by the level of substitution of grasses was more significant than the addition of EDE in relation to the values of AME and the digestibility of energy and nutrients in diets. Through the addition of EDE, an improvement in the energy utilization of the diets was expected due to the greater degradation of NSP by the action of EDE's carbohydrases. However, there was no increase in the digestibility of energy and DM. Grasses have a higher proportion of insoluble NSP, and carbohydrases are mainly utilized for the degradation of soluble NSP, which can reduce the viscosity of the digesta and bacterial fermentation in the jejunum and ileum, thereby improving diet digestibility (Bedford and Apajalahti, 2022).

The increase in dietary fiber consumption in diets with grasses and the use of EDE at the recommended levels may not be sufficient to degrade the β-bonds of NSP into simple sugars (Iqbal et al., 2019). Thus, the antinutritional effects caused by NSP can be mitigated by using EDE in doses higher than recommended, improving the energy utilization of the diets (Kocher et al., 2002)

The EDE increased the digestibility of NDF and CP in the diets with kikuyu grass and CP in the diets with ryegrass. The increase in the digestibility of NDF can be attributed to the action of xylanase and cellulase, and of CP to the action of proteases. It should be emphasized that the combination of carbohydrases with proteases can improve protein digestibility, as the antinutritional effects of NSP can be mitigated, associated with a greater release of nutrients from the interior of the plant cell through the degradation of the cell wall (Olukosi et al., 2020). Such a mechanism of action of the EDE can explain the increase in protein digestibility and be associated with the increase in ash digestibility.

N-corrected and standardized AME

The AME can be corrected by the retention of N, with the N from the feed being deposited by the bird in the form of protein tissue or catabolized for energy generation. Part of the catabolized nitrogenous compounds are excreted in the form of uric acid. The correction of AME for N retention becomes important, as the utilization of dietary energy is influenced by factors such as age, lineage, and the quality of protein in the diet (Bolarinwa and Adeola, 2012), which can alter the proportion of N retained or excreted in the form of uric acid. However, the correction of AME for N retention equal to zero has some criticisms, as the value of AMEn varies depending on the bird's N balance. In this way, the correction of AME for N = 0 retention does not represent real and practical values of the AME corrected for N retention, considering that broilers, turkeys, and layers retain about 50 % of the N ingested in balanced diets (Cozannet et al., 2010). The AMEs showed higher values compared to the AMEn, as a retention of 50 % of the N ingested from the diets was considered.

Energy value and digestibility of kikuyu grass and annual ryegrass

The experiments were conducted with dried and ground grasses, as the use of grasses in NM basis would reduce the amount of DM ingested by the layers, since the total DM of kikuyu grass and annual ryegrass was only 178.4 g/kg and 148.2 g/kg, respectively. Thus, pre-drying at 60°C allowed for an increase in DM content, and the high moisture content in NM would not interfere in feed intake.

The calculated values of the energy value and digestibility of kikuyu grass (Table 5) and annual ryegrass (Table 8) demonstrate that the substitution level had a significant effect on the obtained values. In this way, the use of a single substitution level may not be the most appropriate method for evaluating the nutritional value of poultry feed, especially fiber-rich feedstuffs. Although it is a methodology that requires a larger number of birds, obtaining the energy value of foods using the regression method is more precise and robust than substituting with a single level (Bolarinwa and Adeola, 2012; Zhang et al., 2022).

Both grasses showed similar GE, with 4,500 kcal/kg. However, the energy utilization of the ryegrass was numerically superior to that of the kikuyu. The AMEn and AMEs values for kikuyu and ryegrass exhibited a linear decrease, with the lowest values recorded at the 90 g/kg level in comparison to the 30 g/kg level. The reduction is due to the high composition of NDF and ADF in the grasses, which reduced the digestibility of the diets and, consequently, their energy value. Kikuyu showed a greater difference in the AMEn value obtained between 30 and 90 g/kg compared to ryegrass. This difference in the reduction of AME between the two grasses is due to the higher concentration of NDF in kikuyu compared to ryegrass (593.7 vs. 512.8 g/kg). Despite the similar ADF composition of kikuyu and ryegrass, at 223.5 and 224.4 g/kg respectively, it is established that grasses from tropical climates have a greater lignin content in their cell walls than those from temperate climates (Chapman et al., 2014). Lignin has a more complex molecular structure than cellulose and remains practically indigestible (Raza et al., 2019). Thus, the higher lignin content of kikuyu grass compared to ryegrass (26.2 vs. 22.7 g/kg) may be related to the reduction in the AME values.

Another factor that may have improved the energy utilization of ryegrass relative to kikuyu is the higher age of the laying hens used in the ryegrass experiment, since energy utilization improves with the age of the chickens (Liu et al., 2023; Jiang et al., 2024), attributed to the development of the GIT (Mateos et al., 2019). This may be related to the smaller reduction in the digestibility of ryegrass energy and test diets with the increase in the substitution level. As a result of the improvement in energy utilization and the reduction in egg production with the increase in the age of laying hens, there is a retention of energy intake as body fat since the energy required for egg production decreases (Barzegar et al., 2020). However, the experimental design of the present study and the fact that different grasses were used do not allow for a direct comparison of the effect of age and between the grasses.

The use of EDE improved the energy digestibility of kikuyu from 36.35 % to 41.25 %. This result can be attributed to the increased digestibility of NDF with the use of EDE, reducing the antinutritional effects caused by fiber in the birds' GIT. The use of EDE improved only the AMEs of the ryegrass, possibly due to the increased digestibility of CP in the experimental diets, which caused an increase in uric acid excretion, reducing the difference between the ingested and excreted N.

The carbohydrases that constitute the EDE used, which act on the hydrolysis of hemicellulose and cellulose, are xylanase and cellulase, respectively. However, these carbohydrases are not capable of completely degrading the NSP into simple sugars, so the enzyme complex used was not efficient in reducing the antinutritional effects generated by the NDF content of the grasses (Raza et al., 2019). Consequently, employing an EDE complex with an elevated concentration of xylanase and cellulase can increase the hydrolysis of insoluble NSP from grasses and improve their digestibility.

Buchanan et al. (2007) conducted an evaluation of a mix of forages composed of Kentucky bluegrass, fescue, red and white clover, with a composition of 4,263.6 kcal/kg of GE, NDF with 311.7 g/kg, and ADF of 182.2 g/kg in cectomized roosters. The AMEn obtained was 365 and 285 kcal/kg with and without the use of EDE (β-glucanase, pentosanase, and hemicellulase), respectively, in the NM. The values obtained by the authors in DM are, respectively, 2,085.7 and 1,634.3 kcal/kg. The AMEn values that were determined by the authors are comparable to those that were obtained in our research and demonstrate high values despite the levels of NDF and ADF. However, it is important to consider the high GE value of the grasses in DM, and the value as-fed basis (i.e., NM) significantly reduces due to the high moisture content of the grasses. Arruda and Fernandes (2014) determined the energy value of leucaena leaf hay (Leucaena leucocephala) in pullets, with an AMEn value of 1676.9 kcal/kg in DM. The leucena leaf hay contained 896.5 g/kg of DM, 4892 kcal/kg of GE, 490.5 g/kg of NDF, and 264.9 g/kg of ADF. The AMEn value was determined using a single substitution level of 20 % in pullets at 15 weeks of age. The AMEn value reported by the authors, despite a greater substitution level than that employed in our research and in younger birds, demonstrates that poultry can utilize energy from fiber-rich forages relatively well.

The values of AMEn for kikuyu with and without EDE in NM were 281.1 and 237.9 kcal/kg, and for ryegrass the values were 328.3 and 352.3 kcal/kg. The values of AMEs for kikuyu with and without EDE in NM were 292.3 and 237.7 kcal/kg, and for ryegrass the values were 335.5 and 347.1 kcal/kg.

The difference obtained by Buchanan et al. (2007) using EDE was greater than that obtained with both kikuyu and ryegrass. This difference may be due to the methodology used by the authors. The insoluble fiber portion remains practically undigested until it reaches the caecum, where fermentation by the caecal microbiota occurs (JøRgensen et al., 1996). When the caecum of the birds are removed, the digestion of fiber by caecal bacteria will not occur, so the EDE used will have a more pronounced effect.

Despite the methodological differences and the composition of the forages, the values obtained by Buchanan et al. (2007) are similar to those of the present study. The results indicate that the energy value of grasses for birds is reduced when converted to NM. Considering that laying hens have a limited capacity for forage consumption, being able to consume up to about 59 g of forages in the NM per bird per day, as demonstrated by Mugnai et al. (2014), in laying hens with access to pasture and ad libitum feed. The contribution of energy from the consumption of grasses is low, and the EDE utilized in our study does not improve the energy utilization of the grasses. Therefore, the combination of results without and with EDE was carried out to obtain the values of AMEn and AMEs. Only AMEs of ryegrass showed the effect of EDE. Kikuyu has an AMEn value of 261.5 kcal/kg and 339.3 kcal/kg for ryegrass, the AMEs of kikuyu was 267.6 kcal/kg, and 341.1 kcal/kg for ryegrass, in NM.

Digestibility coefficients of grasses

The values obtained for the digestibility of DM, energy, CP, ash, and NDF showed a significant difference between the levels of substitution of kikuyu grass and annual ryegrass. With the aim of obtaining the digestibility coefficients for tropical and temperate climate grasses, a combination of the evaluated grasses and the EDE pool was carried out, resulting in the following digestibility coefficients: 0.459 for energy, 0.453 for DM, 0.518 for CP, 0.356 for NDF, 0.054 for ADF, and 0.579 for ashes (Table 10). Even with the pool of grasses and the EDE, only equation 12 for NDF showed a reduced value of the coefficient of determination (R² = 0.599).

Pleger et al. (2021) evaluated the digestibility of the protein in red clover and alfalfa leaves for broiler chickens using the regression method. The ileal digestibility of crude protein from red clover and alfalfa leaves was 50 % and 51 %, respectively. The digestibility of CP and amino acids can be determined using the linear regression method without correcting for basal endogenous loss, as the slope of the equation represents only ingredient-specific effects (Rodehutscord et al., 2004). A similar value was obtained for the digestibility of CP in the evaluated grasses, with 51.8 %. However, the measurement of protein digestibility was carried out through excreta collection, amino acids can be catabolized by bacteria in the large intestine, so ileal evaluation is more suitable for measuring protein digestibility than through excreta (Kadim et al., 2002).

Prediction models to estimate AME of grasses

Grasses have a great variation in their chemical composition. Tropical climate grasses have a higher proportion of NDF and ADF than temperate climate grasses (Chapman et al., 2014), and the height and age of defoliation exert a significant influence on the content of CP, DM, minerals, NDF, and ADF in grasses (Benvenutti et al., 2020). Thus, the value of AME may vary depending on the climatic type, soil quality, and defoliation.

The prediction equations for AMEn and AMEs for the combination of the two grasses were proposed to cover the use in different classes of grasses, and the EDE pool was conducted, since only AMEs of ryegrass show a significant effect for EDE. The equations with more predictor terms presented higher R2 values (Alvarenga et al., 2011), the ADF showed a negative correlation in predicting the energy content (Liang et al., 2023) of the grasses, and the GE and EE content showed a high correlation, being the best predictors in the equations (Xie et al., 2021).

The equations that used a predictor with GE or dGE (eq. 19.1, 20.1, 22.1, and 24.1) showed a low R2 value. However, the equations with two predictors, formed by GE and ADF (eq. 19.2 and 20.2) or dGE and dDM (eq. 22.2 and 24.2), increased the R2 value. The VIF in these equations was higher than 10.0, indicating potential multicollinearity among the predictors, thereby limiting the applicability of these regression models. Equations 21.1 and 23 showed R2 values of 0.926 and 0.917, respectively, using only EE as a predictor, indicating that the composition of EE has a high correlation in predicting the energy content of grasses.

It should be emphasized that these equations, despite estimating the energy value of grasses, should be used with caution, as the results were obtained under specific experimental conditions and with two types of grasses. Moreover, the regression equations generated with a reduced number of samples prove to be relatively accurate (Ning et al., 2022). In addition, the analysis of EE using the Soxhlet extractor method is not a simple methodology to perform, and the use of different laboratory procedures can alter the results obtained. Thus, the equations with GE or dGE as predictors are more suitable for practical use. However, the equations derived from the chemical composition assume a constant digestibility of the feed and possible interactions between nutrients are not considered, so predictive equations that use the digestibility coefficient (eq. 20.1 and 24.1), especially for feedstuffs with low digestibility, can generate equations with greater predictive power (Cerrate et al., 2019).

Few comparable studies are available to validate the proposed regression models. In a similar study that determined the AMEn value of grasses and legumes for roosters, the GE was 4,263.6 kcal/kg DM, and the experimentally obtained AMEn value was 1,634.3 kcal/kg DM (Buchanan et al., 2007). The values calculated using equation 20.1 was 1,690.8 kcal/kg DM, which is comparable with those reported by the authors. The results show that the developed prediction equations are useful tools for estimating the AME value of grasses for layers.

Conclusion

The findings of this research indicate that the energy value and apparent digestibility of grasses for laying hens are negatively associated with their insoluble fiber content. Kikuyu grass exhibited higher NDF and lignin content compared to annual ryegrass, limiting its energy value and digestibility. The AMEn for kikuyu were 261.5 kcal/g, and 339.3 kcal/kg for ryegrass, in NM. The AMEs of kikuyu were 267.6 kcal/kg, and 341.1 kcal/kg for ryegrass, in NM. Although the inclusion of EDE improved the apparent digestibility of CP and NDF, the concentration utilized was insufficient to mitigate the antinutritional effects of high insoluble fiber and did not result in significant improvements in the AME of grasses. The predictive equations developed, particularly those that use gross energy, can serve as useful tools for estimating the AME of grasses for laying hens. The increase in the level of grass substitution led to a linear reduction in the digestibility of AME, DM, energy, and NDF in the diet. However, the apparent digestibility of ash and CP increased, possibly due to the development of the gizzard and the increase digestive secretions caused by increased fiber intake.
