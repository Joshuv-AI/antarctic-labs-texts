Biodiversity conservation depends on the expansion of taxonomy and systematics research
Authors: Luis A Ruedas, Scott L Gardner
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite mammals constituting fewer than 0.3% of all described species, their conservation is compelling for a number of reasons. They contribute to biodiversity and are important in maintaining and regulating ecological communities. Many mammals have significant cultural and economic value, serving as sources of food, medicine, and tourism revenue. Furthermore, the conservation of mammals often leads to the protection of entire habitats, benefiting numerous other species and preserving ecosystem services that are critical for human well-­being. Humans share a unique evolutionary history with other mammal species, making their preservation important for scientific research, education, and understanding of our own biology and evolution. In spite of this, mammalian biodiversity is at severe risk, with 26% of all mammal species threatened with extinction. Here, we propose to use a 4-step framework with which to approach conservation strategy for mammalian biodiversity. The framework is structured and based on a protocol initially established from the standpoint of parasites by Daniel R. Brooks and collaborators in 2014. The 4 key phases are documentation (species discovery and specimen collection); assessment (species relationships, genetic diversity, and climate change vulnerability); monitoring (tracking populations and habitats over time); and action (addressing the taxonomic impediment—the lack of human and financial resources to undertake taxonomy, as well as the discrepancy between real number of existing species and human knowledge of biodiversity—and expanding protected areas). The successful integration of politics, politicians, and stakeholders into the process of conservation is critical to the success of the protocol because of the requirement to enact policy. And the urgency is now, because nothing is more vital to the human condition than preservation of biodiversity.Keywords: action, conservation, inventory, mammals, nature, natural areas

Early assessments of global biodiversity, based on the potential capacity of Noah’s ark, resulted in the estimate that 300 to 2,000 species of animals lived on Earth (Roberts 2024). Admittedly, mammals constitute a negligible part of that global biodiversity. A more recent estimate by Chapman (2009) suggested that mammals constituted 0.288% of all described species (5,487 of 1,899,587 total; down from 0.303% in Chapman 2005). Other estimates of global biodiversity might indicate that in terms of the number of species, mammals are even more inconsequential: May (1988) noted that there might be as many as 10 to 50 million species of eukaryotes, an estimate potentially somewhat lower than an earlier hypothesis by Erwin (1982) of 30 million arthropod species alone. A more recent and widely accepted review and assessment by Mora et al. (2011) proposed that there may exist ca. 8.7 million (SE ± 1.3 million) species of eukaryotes globally. However, recent work exploring parasitoid wasps (Ulrich 1999; Aguiar et al. 2013; Forbes et al. 2018; Sheik et al. 2022; Polaszek and Vilhemsen 2023; Burke and Sharanowski 2024; Cruaud et al. 2024) and arthropods in general (Stork et al. 2015) strongly suggests that the earlier estimates of Erwin (1982) and May (1988) may be closer to reality than that of Mora et al. (2011). Indeed, including microorganisms results in predictions as high as 1012 (one trillion) species (Locey and Lennon 2016). Whatever the real number, “The more taxonomists have progressed, the greater their estimates of the total number of living species. Today, estimates of ten million species seem conservative, since some estimates are as high as one hundred million” (Wheeler 1995:476). There appears to be a consensus among scientists who study biodiversity that the earth still has from 50% to 90% of all taxa yet to be discovered and described (González-Oreja 2008; Engel et al. 2021).

With particular reference to mammals as a subset of the numbers alluded to above, Chapman (2009) estimated that there eventually would be described some 5,500 species of mammals. That figure easily was put to the sword by Burgin et al. (2018), who at the time ­tabulated 6,495 species of extant mammals in the inaugural Mammal Diversity Database. At last count (Mammal Diversity Database 2024), the database listed 6,801 species (6,689 extant), with no empirical evidence that we may be close to an asymptote (correlation with year: R2 = 0.976, P = 1.95 × 10−9; Fig. 1).

At the same time, the human population also has been increasing from 1950 to present, by ca. 80,011,935 per year (R2 = 0.998, P = 2.5 × 10−92; Fig. 2), resulting in a density increase from ∼16.7 humans • km2 in 1952 to ∼54.8 humans • km2 in 2024. And, during the same period covered by the preceding datasets, there has been—not unexpectedly—no substantive change in the dry land area of the Earth (ca. 1.49 × 108 km2; United Nations 2024), which, if anything, is decreasing as a result of global warming and sea level increases.

This conjunction of interconnected numbers and trends shows that humanity is causing a massive extinction crisis (Dirzo et al. 2014; Ceballos et al. 2015, 2017; Raven and Miller 2020), not only acutely affecting the global remnant megafauna (Ripple et al. 2014, 2015; McPhee 2018; Boscaini et al. 2025) but also down to the smaller size classes of life (Hallmann et al. 2017) and everything in between (Ceballos et al. 2020), with an emphatically unnatural distribution of species numbers and mass vastly favoring domestic animals (Bar-On et al. 2018; Greenspoon et al. 2023) and an enormous loss of functional diversity (Hedberg et al. 2022; Shupinski et al. 2024), resulting in severely compromised ecosystem function (Fricke et al. 2022). Human geographic range expansion and the environmental impacts that come with human presence—for example, transition from small bands of hunter-gatherers to increasingly larger agricultural-based communities; increased human population densities in urban centers, and so on—have been the leading drivers of animal extinctions: the current iteration of the International Union for the Conservation of Nature (IUCN) Red List enumerates 789 animal species as having gone extinct since the year 1500 (https://www.iucnredlist.org/search; accessed 9 April 2025). Of those, 85 are mammal species; an additional 286 are Critically Endangered, and these numbers surely are underestimates.

The data we present above also point to a vast chasm that exists between the number of species known and the number of species hypothesized as remaining to be discovered/described: The Linnean shortfall (Raven and Wilson 1992; Hortal et al. 2015). In developing models for incidence and range sizes of known and potential unknown species, Bini et al. (2006) hypothesized that the probability of discovering new species decreases linearly with the increase of knowledge level. While those authors were referring to discovering a new species in a defined geographic area or cell based on number of inventories undertaken in that cell, the method can be extrapolated to the taxonomic data presented above to further underscore the fact that we still do not know even the basic number of species of mammals: data from MDD (Mammal Diversity Database 2024) list 43 new species discovered in 2023, and 42 in 2024, an increase in the mean rate: the rate of species description remains far from steady and appears instead to be increasing. It was estimated by Scheffers et al. (2012; based on the data from Chapman [2009]) that—at the time—97% of all mammals were known. That would have resulted in 165 additional species, to a total of 5,652. But—we are at 6,801 (and counting). The 2012 data, therefore, only represented ∼80% of the currently known mammal fauna; the 165 “missing species” from 2012 represent accumulation over a mere 4 to 5 yr at current rates of discovery, which—we hasten to reiterate—show no signs of slowing down. Finally, even though the data show an increase in discovery and description of mammals, the parasite faunas both of known and these new species of mammals have been largely ignored.

In the face of these crises, in a world on the verge of ecological collapse, what is one to do, short of despair? Here we propose a multipronged framework with which to approach conservation strategy.

The framework is structured and based on the Documentation, Assessment, Monitor, and Act (DAMA) protocol proposed by Brooks et al. (2014; see also Hoberg et al. 2022; Gardner et al. 2023a). Although the DAMA protocol initially was established to provide information about emerging infectious diseases from the standpoint of parasitism, DAMA applies eminently well to biodiversity conservation and preservation. Indeed, the recent increase in consideration of human disease as applied to conservation (Macdonald and Laurenson 2006; Hoberg and Brooks 2015; Gupta et al. 2020) has resulted in outcomes jointly beneficial both to humans and wildlife (Mackenzie and Jeggo 2019; Földvári et al. 2024) and has demonstrated that biodiversity and human health are inextricably linked (Dizney and Ruedas 2009; Keesing et al. 2010; Civitello et al. 2015; Bartlow et al. 2021).

The initial demonstration of this latter phenomenon was made by Ostfeld and Keesing (2000), who coined the term Dilution Effect in assessing the interactions between risk to humans from disease and pathogens, vectors, and vertebrate hosts in the Lyme Disease system. Those authors argued that vertebrate communities with high species diversity contain a greater proportion of incompetent reservoir hosts, thereby reducing infection prevalence and disease risk. In contrast, depauperate communities would be dominated by competent hosts, resulting in greater transmission of pathogens (Keesing and Ostfeld 2021a,b). Biodiversity poor communities tend to be ecoanthropogenically disturbed, human dominated ecosystems, where the danger of disease transmission is compounded by increases in zoonotic hosts, which tend to have more generalized feeding habits and ecological requirements (Gibb et al. 2020; Ruedas et al. 2004; de Thoisy et al. 2024). Ecological disturbances engendered by climate change and changing seasonality likewise amplify this phenomenon, particularly in socioeconomically distressed human systems (Wilcox and Gubler 2005; Parham et al. 2015; Gupta et al. 2021; Tazerji et al. 2022; Hernández-Carrasco et al. 2025; Pei et al. 2025). The association between biodiversity and human disease has been demonstrated both in directly transmitted and particularly in vectored zoonoses (Ross 1909, 1910; Audy 1947; Keesing and Ostfeld 2021a).

In the original formulation of their DAMA protocol, Brooks et al. (2014) sought to unite different knowledge streams around the common goal of responding to the outcomes of accelerating environmental change and expanding environmental perturbation. Environmental change and perturbation are human induced; it therefore stands to reason that humans should contribute to their resolution. Here, we advance a research agenda based on the protocol proposed by Brooks et al. (2014), including mammalian hosts and their parasites, and applied to mammalian biology sensu lato, with the ultimate goal of strengthening biodiversity research, resulting in enhanced conservation outcomes.

The protocol

Documentation

“The need of thoroughly skilled collectors is increasing every hour […] Now is the time to collect. A little later it will cost a great deal more, and the collector will get a great deal less. […] it is my firm belief that the time will come when the majority of the vertebrate species now inhabiting the earth in a wild state will be either totally exterminated, or exist under protection” (Hornaday 1905:1–2).

The data we presented above on mammalian species discovery covered the entire period of the modern era of taxonomy, which started with the publication of the 10th edition of Linnaeus’ Systema Naturae (Linnaeus 1758; Ruedas 2020; Ruedas et al. 2025). The rate of descriptions of new species (Fig. 1) is significant (P = 1.95 × 10−9; R2 = 0.976). In more recent times, and in spite of pronouncements to the effect that we know all there is to know about mammals, the rate of species-discovery and descriptions of new species has increased: over the past 2 decades (2000 to 2023), the average number of mammal species described has increased from a global mean of 27 per year to 36.42 ± 10.93 (SD; range = 15 to 63 [within those 2 decades plus, the annus mirabilis was 2021]; P = 2.10 × 10−27, R2 = 0.996). A similar rate of increase has occurred across all domains of life, from ca. 3,000 species a year in the second half of the 18th century, through 10,000 yr−1 in the 20th century, to ca. 15,000 to 20,000 species annually today (Singh 2025). Notwithstanding these positive directions, 2 concurrent trends argue for caution in interpreting these data.

First, the rate of deposition of specimens (number of specimens deposited per year) in natural history collections is declining. Data from over 245 natural history collections available online from the Global Biodiversity Information Facility (https://www.gbif.org) were analyzed by Rohwer et al. (2022). These authors found that addition of new specimens to collections had declined by 54% to 76% across 4 vertebrate groups from 1965 to 2018. The data for mammals only are shown in Fig. 3 (data in litt. courtesy of V. G. Rohwer, Cornell University, 2 December 2024). Specimens accessioned into collections dropped from the peak year, 1967, with 104,646, to 2021—the most recent year for which data were available—with 8,784 specimens deposited: a massive drop of 91.6%. Overall, the number of specimens deposited in the 245 collections whose data are web-enabled dropped during those years by ca. 985 specimens year−1 (P = 3.05 × 10−21; R2 = 0.778). A major concern, however, is the tail end of the curve: from 2015 to 2021, specimen accessions into museum collections dropped by ca. 4,872 specimens year−1 (P = 0.0002; R2 = 0.946). However, collection of specimens across geographic space is critical to the undertaking of the modern scientific enterprise and understanding local and deep historical biodiversity, as demonstrated by Gardner and Campbell (1992a) and Nachman et al. (2023). It remains unclear whether the phenomenon documented by Rohwer et al. (2022) is a result merely of just fewer specimens being deposited or instead whether it is due to smaller series being collected on account of increasing restrictions on specimen collection overall, and large-scale specimen collection in particular. Anecdotally, we have heard increasing reports of administrative impediments in the home institutions of numerous field workers. It is clear, however, that institutions need not only skilled collectors, but collectors period, to contribute to existing collections, and that this dearth of collectors and increase in bureaucratic demands are having an effect on aspects of the Taxonomic Impediment (below).

The second—not necessarily unlinked—factor is the Taxonomic Impediment (also taxonomic deficit; Anderson and Majka 2009). The causes of the decline in taxonomists—human resources are a critical aspect of the Taxonomic Impediment—are multiple, and have been explored elsewhere (Kim and Byrne 2006; Löbl et al. 2023; Novaes et al. 2023). We touch on this issue below (vide Action, below). The ­Taxonomic Impediment also relates to gaps in taxonomic knowledge, which—as demonstrated by the data above—is a growing concern.

The bureaucratic demands on collectors only will increase, particularly in light of the disconnection between field researchers working on animals in the wild and institutional committees and agencies regulating that research using considerations primarily based on domestic and laboratory animals (Sikes and Paul 2013). In addition, while the “3 R’s” (Reduction, Replacement, and Refinement) of Rusell and Burch (1959) remain paramount both in wild and laboratory animal research, there are challenges in applying to wild animals each of the criteria (Soulsbury et al. 2020). For example, with respect to Reduction, those authors point out that the greater magnitude of genetic variation in wild animals requires a greater sample size. Similarly, Replacement is impossible when testing taxonomic hypotheses focused on particular taxa in the wild. Fraser (2012) distinguished among 4 broad categories of how human activities affect animals; often overlooked is that scientific collecting (category 2: causing intentional harm to animals) has as a goal reducing the much greater harm caused by category 4 (harming animals indirectly by disturbing life-sustaining processes and balances of nature, for example, by habitat destruction and climate change). As population numbers decline, there may come more restrictions on large scale collecting (e.g., surveys) in favor of more targeted collecting. A case likewise has been made to scale back collecting in favor of more potentially benign alternatives such as photographs (Marshall and Evenhuis 2015), and an increased deployment of non-destructive capture-release for genetic sampling by means of plucked hair, skin snip, and so on. We emphatically decry this perspective: collecting remains of paramount importance in illuminating the true scope of biological diversity (Ceríaco et al. 2016; Gutiérrez and Pine 2017; Pine and Gutiérrez 2018), and good data on biodiversity patterns has been identified as critical in conservation planning and central to the establishment of conservation area networks (Margules and Sarkar 2007).

Nigh on a quasquicentennial removed, Hornaday’s words ring more true than ever: collection remains an integral and indispensable component of the documentation element of this protocol (Rocha et al. 2014; Krell and Wheeler 2014; Nachman et al. 2023).

Assessment

What’s in a name? Here, we interpret the original formulation of assessment, following Brooks et al. (2014) and Hoberg et al. (2022), as the step subsequent to documentation: once the collections (Documentation phase) have been undertaken, the identification phase begins, and species are assessed in an evolutionary and ecological context. How are the organisms related to each other that are found in the area subject to documentation? Are there sister taxa relationships among the local taxa; are the species locally endemic, or instead are they members of a widespread species, or related to spatially distant species? An issue in assessment—prior even to species identification—is the application of a consistent species concept in species delimitation (Karbstein et al. 2024), which, if ignored at the beginning, may lead to confusion as to the end products of the documentation phase. In addition, as in the lag time between discovery and description, there is a lag between documentation and assessment, although at times, if assessment goes hand in hand with documentation or, for particular projects, there may be a reduced time between documentation and assessment. For example, an assessment of bats (Mammalia: Chiroptera) of the genus Hipposideros (Hipposideridae) by Lavery et al. (2024) followed upon the steps of a documentation phase undertaken in 2018. Those authors found that 2 species, H. diadema (É. Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire 1813) and H. dinops Andersen, 1905, apparently widespread in mainland and archipelagic Southeast Asia, instead constituted examples of parallel evolution in the Solomon Islands: the smaller H. “diadema” from Guadalcanal was more closely related to the larger H. “dinops” from Guadalcanal than to its putative conspecific on nearby Santa Isabel Island. Similar phylogenetic discordances occurred in the same region, albeit at the genus level, in cerobatrachid frogs (Brown et al. 2015).

Genetic diversity plays a crucial role in the long-term viability and adaptability of populations, providing the foundation for survival of species in changing environments (Reusch et al. 2005; Kardos et al. 2021). Assessing population genetic diversity offers valuable insights into health of populations and their capacity to potentially withstand environmental challenges, including population bottlenecks and local extinction events (Fagan and Holmes 2006; Kardos et al. 2023; Robinson et al. 2023). Populations of organisms that have high levels of genetic variability generally are better equipped to evolve and adapt to future environmental changes, resist diseases, and avoid the detrimental effects of inbreeding depression (Lacy 1997; Hughes et al. 2008).

Conservation strategies that prioritize the preservation of genetic diversity have shown significant ecological benefits (Hughes et al. 2008). These approaches include protecting and promoting connectivity among small populations (Hilty et al. 2020; Liang et al. 2023), restoring habitats to allow population growth (Newmark et al. 2017; Hale et al. 2019), and reintroducing species to their former habitats (Love Stowell et al. 2017; Ralls et al. 2020). By implementing such measures, conservationists can enhance resilience of a population to environmental stressors and improve its chances of successfully navigating population bottlenecks (Bortoluzzi et al. 2020). Furthermore, higher levels of genetic diversity have been shown to contribute to the overall health of ecosystems and enable support of the intricate web of interactions among species (DeWoody et al. 2021; Stange et al. 2021; Hoban et al. 2022).

The assessment both of phylogenetic as well as population genetic diversity has become an essential tool in conservation biology, enabling researchers and managers to make informed decisions about conservation priorities and strategies that can be implemented that will maintain diversity (Hoban et al. 2022). Advanced genomic technologies have made it possible to quickly and cost-effectively analyze genetic variation within and among populations, providing valuable data for conservation planning (Kardos et al. 2021). By incorporating genetic diversity assessments into conservation efforts, practitioners can better identify at-risk populations, design effective management interventions, and monitor the success of conservation actions over time (Reusch et al. 2005; Margules and Sarkar 2007).

A pressing issue of our time is adaptation to climate change: are species able to adapt to climate change while remaining in situ, or are range shifts necessary for survival of the species (Chen et al. 2011)? Climate is the most important predictor of mammal abundance (Kays et al. 2024); and life histories of mammals will affect population responses to climatic events (Jackson et al. 2022; Russo et al. 2025). Thus, changing climate, including changes both in air-temperature variation as well as precipitation patterns, constitutes an existential threat to species survival (Schlaepfer and Lawler 2023). Assessment plays a crucial role in understanding and predicting the effects of climate change on mammal populations: long-term abundance records and demographic data have begun to reveal how climate change may lead to population declines in mammals (Pacifici et al. 2017). These assessments are vital for identifying vulnerable species and understanding the mechanisms underpinning population changes in response to climate change. In this regard, absolute rates of climatic evolution were estimated for 540 species in 17 clades of terrestrial vertebrates including groups of mammals, birds, lizards, snakes, turtles, crocodilians, salamanders, and frogs, by Quintero and Wiens (2013). Those authors found that climate change projected for the year 2100 (demonstrably worse currently than at time of their publication; Lyon et al. 2022; Allen et al. 2024) would require rates of niche evolution that are over 10,000 times faster than rates typically observed among species. To avoid extinction, the required rates of adaptation to projected changes accordingly would need to be “largely unprecedented” (Quintero and Wiens 2013:1095). A follow-up study found that even under intermediate climate change scenarios, between 14% and 32% of macroscopic species will be lost to climate change in the coming ∼50 yr (Wiens and Zelinka 2024). Susceptibility to the effects of climate change “depends on the species’ exposure and sensitivity to environmental change, its resilience to perturbations and its potential to adapt to change” (Huey et al. 2012:1665). Determining capabilities of organisms to adapt to climate change scenarios, as well as changing seasonality, therefore clearly fall within the purview of assessment (Foden et al. 2019; Thurman et al. 2020; Hernández-Carrasco et al. 2025). In that respect, some large mammals already have been assessed and found particularly wanting in terms of their potential adaptations to climate change, hence probability of survival (Hetem et al. 2014; Fuller et al. 2016), and thus would be candidates for further monitoring. Climate change furthermore can cause shifts in the ranges of mammal species (particularly larger species); as a result, intact dispersal corridors must be retained to maintain the integrity of species in the face of climate change (Schloss et al. 2012; Alagador et al. 2016; Williams and Blois 2018).

An attendant consideration is the need to undertake assessments in a community framework: while individual species may be partially resilient to environmental stressors, the broader ecological community in contrast may not have the same level of resilience because the synergistic effects of community relationships are magnified and the causal sequence effects in turn may affect species more severely than in isolation. A paradigmatic example may be that of Common Murres (Uria aalge Pontoppidan, 1763:621; Charadriiformes: Alcidae). While there is evidence to suggest that these birds can adapt to environmental stressors, not all populations appear to be able to do so, for example, by increasing selection to shift egg-laying date in response to temperature changes (Häkkinen et al. 2023). To some extent, however, those population responses to climate change are unidimensional, that is, restricted to a single population and a single abiotic effect, for example, wind (Louzao et al. 2019). At the community level in contrast, Renner et al. (2024) reported that a marine heat wave from 2014 to 2016 led to a persistent mass mortality event in murres, ongoing to the present and resulting in a loss of 52% to 78% of individuals in sampled populations. Rather than a direct response by murre to a climatic event, this was a response to massive declines in relative abundances of forage fish, which fell to less than 50% of normal (Arimitsu et al. 2021), resulting in a starvation event that led to the high murre mortality documented by Renner et al. (2024). A simultaneous 20% decline in North Pacific humpback whales (Megaptera novaeangliae (Borowski, 1781); Artiodactyla: Balaenopteridae) also was ascribed to loss of prey resources exacerbated by the marine heatwave (Cheeseman et al. 2024). Similarly, a massive mortality event occurred in 2015 in Saiga Antelope, Saiga tatarica (Linnaeus, 1766; Artiodactyla: Bovidae) in western Mongolia and eastern Kazakhstan; while this event was associated with unusual temperatures and humidity anomalies, the proximate cause of death was determined to be hemorrhagic septicemia caused by pathogenic Pasteurella multocida type B (Kock et al. 2018). It is becoming abundantly clear that assessment not just of species traits and characters—but also of ecological community interactions, such as including in studies parasites that have complex life cycles—therefore will play an increasingly important role in conservation (Gardner and Campbell 1992a; Brooks and McLennan 1993). These assessments are vital for identifying vulnerable species and understanding the mechanisms underpinning population changes in response to climate change. The importance of assessment further is emphasized by the fact that climate change has already caused substantial damages and increasingly irreversible losses in terrestrial ecosystems (IPCC 2023). As a result, conservation objectives are in dire need of expansion and modification to account for increasing temperatures and climate change (Gardner and Bullock 2021; Schlaepfer and Lawler 2023; Wilkening et al. 2023).

Populations of mammals and species also face increasingly significant threats from parasites and pathogens, which can cause population declines and increase extinction risk (Pedersen et al. 2007; Fisher et al. 2012; Tompkins et al. 2015; Cunningham et al. 2017). These threats may be amplified in carnivores and artiodactyls, 2 clades that include many domesticated animals, potentially suggesting that evolutionary similarity to domesticated species may be a key factor in parasite-mediated declines (Pedersen et al. 2007; Woodroffe et al. 2012). The vulnerability of mammalian populations to parasites and pathogens is influenced by various factors, including population size, genetic diversity, and life history traits (Härkönen et al. 2006; Haislip et al. 2011; Bradley et al. 2015), and because parasites and pathogens affect populations and population size, can be used as part of Criterion A in IUCN Red List assessments (IUCN 2012, 2024). Human activities such as habitat alteration, wildlife trade, and the introduction of nonnative species can exacerbate these threats to wildlife by facilitating ecological fitting (Janzen 1980; Agosta 2022) of previously unknown pathogens into naïve species or populations (Karesh et al. 2005; Almberg et al. 2011; Woodroffe et al. 2012; Gardner and Gardner 2024; Gardner et al. 2023a).

To combat these challenges, mammals have evolved various behaviors that maintain their health in natural environments, including specific grooming techniques and foraging on medicinal plants (Hart and Hart 2018; Freymann et al. 2024, 2025). The age structure of a population also can contribute to its ability to persist in the face of an emerging pathogen when susceptibility to pathogens varies by life stage (Härkönen et al. 2007; Haislip et al. 2011; Bradley et al. 2015). The impacts of pathogens on host populations depend on several factors, including pathogen virulence and immune response of the host (Anderson and May 1978, 1979; May and Anderson 1978; Tompkins et al. 2011). Understanding these complex interactions is crucial for developing effective conservation strategies because emerging infectious diseases in humans are frequently caused by pathogens originating from animal hosts (Jones et al. 2008; Cunningham et al. 2017). Assessment can and must play a role in the determination of population and species health and risk of threat.

It must be emphasized that during field research, collection of definitive hosts (= those hosts harboring an adult, sexually reproducing parasite)—which in the present instance are mammals—that are infected with parasites known to use arthropods as intermediate hosts provides much more information relative to contemporary ecological associations than if only the host were considered without regard to the parasite fauna. The presence of a tapeworm in a mammal instantly shows that the mammal is eating arthropods (or other mammals, depending on the taxon under consideration) in the particular environment where it was collected. Thus, collecting a mammal infected with parasites with heteroxenous life cycles provides data on both the definitive host and components of the host–parasite community (Gardner and Campbell 1992a; Gardner and Gardner 2024).

Assessment also has a meaning of conservation import distinct from that explicated above: assessments of extinction risk routinely are undertaken by the International Union for the Conservation of Nature (IUCN) and others (e.g., Brum et al. 2017; Davidson et al. 2009, 2017; Luedtke et al. 2023). These assessments summarize the potential risk of extinction for species and effects of recovery efforts at the time of the assessment based on a number of objective criteria (IUCN 2012, 2021, 2024; Robert et al. 2015) and are undertaken on numerous taxa, from individual species such as Ammotragus lervia (Pallas, 1777) in Tunisia (DGF; IUCN 2017) or Gazella cuvieri (W. Ogilby, 1841) in North Africa (IUCN 2018); to orders (Lagomorpha; Smith et al. 2018); Classes, such as Mammalia; (Schipper et al. 2008), Reptilia (Böhm et al. 2013; Cox et al. 2022), or Amphibia (Stuart et al. 2004; Re:Wild et al. 2023); geographic areas (Rondinini et al. 2014; Woinarski et al. 2015; WWF 2024); functional groups, for example, freshwater mollusks (Böhm et al. 2021); trees (BGCI 2021); and all life (Hilton-Taylor et al. 2008; Butchart et al. 2010; Díaz et al. 2019). It is important to underscore that all data gathered in all steps of the DAMA protocol constitute an integral part of this concept of assessment, including the data critical to undertaking IUCN Red List assessments (IUCN 2012), thereby linking the DAMA protocol to global conservation efforts. It is equally important to emphasize that the assessment and documentation steps of this protocol are inextricably linked: basic and fundamental research in taxonomy (documentation) lead to conclusions that result in conservation outcomes. Conservation outcomes of any consequence are impossible without a strong fundamental basis in taxonomy (McNeely 2002; Dubois 2003; Vogel et al. 2017; Thomson et al. 2018).

Because of the potential double meaning of assessment, we also stress that we are not here proposing any particular stepwise assessment protocol. Such protocols already exist, for example, amphibians (Heyer et al. 1994), fungi (Mueller et al. 2004), mammals (Wilson et al. 1996), reptiles (McDiarmid et al. 2012); and others and vary extensively even within the few broad taxa just cited, depending on the particular subgroup of interest. Stepwise protocols therefore would be pointless. Rather, we emphasize the necessity of undertaking such assessments.

Monitoring

Monitoring of mammal populations is a cornerstone of ecosystem conservation, particularly in the face of environmental change, and in the context of current rates of biodiversity loss (Magurran et al. 2010; Péron and Altwegg 2015; Hughes et al. 2017; Gardner et al. 2019; Raven and Miller 2020). Long-term monitoring efforts and data sets are essential for assessing the effectiveness of conservation interventions, provide crucial data on population trends (Gil-Sánchez et al. 2017), identify crucial habitat requirements, detect early warning signs of population declines or changing species distributions, and ecosystem health (Orgeret et al. 2022; Gardner et al. 2023a), thereby enabling one to make informed decisions and implement effective management strategies (Lacher et al. 2019; O'Brien et al. 2020; Nuttall et al. 2022; Li et al. 2024).

Repeated surveys allow researchers to detect changes both in numerical density of populations as well as their geographic distributions over time, which is essential for identifying species at risk and evaluating the success or failure of any conservation interventions (White 2019; Gardner et al. 2019; Fidino et al. 2021). By consistently monitoring populations of various species of mammals, scientists can track the impacts of human activities, climate change, changing seasonality, and other environmental factors abundance and behavior of species (Mayer et al. 2023; Sun 2024; Hernández-Carrasco et al. 2025). Changes in one area can alert scientists to investigate other areas to examine the potential large-scale correlations that may be driven by similar biotic or abiotic perturbations. Importantly, presence-absence data on species also can be tracked over time to assess the completeness of faunal inventories; Voss and Emmons (1996) and Voss et al. (2001) provided summaries of the accretion over time of mammal species to their own (local) and broader regional (South America) faunal studies of mammals. In particular, Voss and Emmons (1996) showed that there were numerous missing species in mammal surveys in South America—species that were expected to be present but not observed or collected during surveys—but that missing species are negatively correlated with inventory duration (R = −0.76, df = 6, P = 0.03). Omitting 1 outlier (Barro Colorado Island, with a depauperate insular fauna) resulted in an even stronger negative correlation (R = −0.90, df = 5, P < 0.01), demonstrating that long-term and repeated inventories were necessary to reveal the true diversity of a given location. No endoparasites were collected in any of these collection events; thus, from a parasitological perspective, all the data are missing. Failure to include information on parasites constitutes a critical missing conservation datum because of parasites’ potential importance in the development of the host’s immune system and the consequent effects on host survival (Spencer and Zuk 2016).

Biodiversity surveys of extensive geographic areas that include numerous ecological zones have been shown to be essential in understanding host–parasite systems. For instance, a wide-ranging collection of marsupials and their endoparasites (Gardner and Campbell 1992a) that was obtained as part of a survey of the mammals of Bolivia, enabled Gardner and Campbell (1992b) to conduct a phylogenetic analysis of the cestodes showing that collections of small marsupials over a wide geographic area—Bolivia—made possible the discovery of a zone of ancient ecological connections that otherwise would have gone unnoticed (Gardner and Campbell 1992a,b; Hoberg 1997). For another Tropical location—Costa Rica—a strong statistical correlation was found between species tabulated as present in the country and year of compendium (R2 = 0.96, df = 7, P = 1.62 × 10−5; Mora and Ruedas 2023). The necessity of ongoing and repeated assessment was underscored by Valdecasas and Camacho (2003:1113), who noted that, “How many species on a global list are actually living in an area can only be answered by updating inventories.”

Long-term monitoring programs are particularly valuable for understanding the complex dynamics of ecosystems and the interrelationships and biotic interactions between and among species (Brown et al. 2001; Lindenmayer et al. 2012; Lindenmayer and Likens 2018; Shackleton et al. 2020). These studies can reveal patterns of interspecific associations and how they change in response to environmental pressures, providing insights into community structure and ecosystem resilience (Gardner et al. 2019; Mazza et al. 2020; Fidino et al. 2021; Sun et al. 2024).

The use of advanced technologies, such as camera traps, has revolutionized mammal monitoring, allowing researchers to collect vast amounts of data on species occurrence and behavior in a noninvasive manner (Burton et al. 2015; Kays et al. 2020, 2022). These methods are particularly useful for studying elusive or rare species, providing valuable information on their ecology (Jiménez et al. 2010; Brassine and Parker 2015; but see cautionary note by Mills et al. 2019). Also important is the fact that a large amount of data can never be collected using this remote sensing method; for example, changes in prevalence of pathogens, the appearance of new parasites, or the disappearance of parasites that may be the harbingers of rapid but as yet undetected ecological change (Gardner et al. 2023a).

Long-term monitoring programs also play a crucial role in identifying and tracking indicator species, which can serve as early warning systems for ecosystem changes (Vaughan et al. 2001; Aguirre and Tabor 2004; Bossart 2011). These sentinel species can provide valuable insights into the overall health of ecosystems and help detect potential threats to both wildlife and human populations (Aguirre and Tabor 2004; Bossart 2011). Repeated surveys also are important for conducting demographic studies, which provide detailed information on population structure, reproductive rates, and survival (Péron and Altwegg 2015; Rotella 2023). These data can then be used to develop robust population models and predict future trends, allowing for the implementation of proactive management strategies (Gardner et al. 2019; Bradford et al. 2020).

The importance of repeated and long-term monitoring extends beyond individual species because repeated sampling over broad geographic areas can enable secondary large-scale studies, contributing to our broader understanding of biodiversity patterns and ecosystem functioning (Myers et al. 2000; Ceballos and Ehrlich 2006; Schrader et al. 2024). Repeated and long-term monitoring of mammal populations is an indispensable tool in conservation biology, providing the foundation for evidence-based management decisions and policy-making (Ferreira et al. 2021; Tinker et al. 2021). Robust ecological niche-based methods of predicting distribution of mammals over geographic space relative to conservation areas and climate change were undertaken on rodents of the family Ctenomyidae (Rodentia) in the southern Neotropics by Gardner et al. (2021) and subsequently more extensively by Caraballo et al. (2023). Human and nonhuman impacts on natural ecosystems continue to intensify the need for comprehensive and sustained monitoring, and assessment efforts therefore are becoming increasingly critical to safeguard biodiversity and ecosystem health for future generations.

Action

Although a number of interpretations have been applied to the Taxonomic Impediment, it is used here in the sense of the secondary meaning coined by Systematics Agenda 2000 (1994): the widening chasm between existing biodiversity and the number of practicing taxonomists (Kim and Byrne 2006), resulting in a deficit to our most basic knowledge (taxonomy) of that very diversity, an inevitable consequence of the primary challenge identified by Systematics Agenda 2000 (1994): a stagnation in the “expansion and improvement of systematic research infrastructure.” One raison d’être for this chasm is the widely held perception that the “goal of discovering, describing, and classifying the species of our planet” (Wheeler et al. 2004:285), or natural history broadly writ, is absent hypotheses and hypothesis testing, and as such, constitutes mere stamp collecting (Godfray 2002; Schmidly 2005; Johnson 2007; Christenhusz 2020; Löbl et al. 2023). That perception has the consequence of leading students not into taxonomy, but rather into other fields of biological endeavor that may be seen as more rigorous or otherwise attractive, such as molecular biology or, increasingly, bioinformatics (Pimm and Lawton 1998). The end result, beyond a taxonomic impediment, in fact is a taxonomic apocalypse: a rapidly diminishing population of experts in the taxonomy of most organisms.

Using membership in the American Society of Mammalogists as a proxy for taxonomists with expertise in mammals (granted a fraught leap for a taxon-based multidisciplinary society), one should be concerned by the significant negative correlation between the increasing number of described species and the diminishing number of members of the society (Pearson correlation coefficient, −0.816, R2 = 0.667, P = 0.000012; not shown), and similarly, by the tragic correlation between the coincident decline in members and deposition of specimens in museum collections (ρ = 0.768, R2 = 0.591, P = 0.000076). A side effect of the decline in taxonomic expertise is the increase in collaborations in taxonomy, as evidenced by the changing patterns in authorship: for the first 2 centuries since Linnaeus, the taxonomic enterprise primarily was a solitary endeavor (1758 to 1958, 5,200 recognized species described, mean authors per description 1.045 ± 0.207 [SD], min = 1, max = 2). More recently, that has changed: in this century, the number of authors per paper describing a species has steadily increased (Fig. 4). In particular, there is a marked and significant jump from the 2001 to 2005 and prior quinquennia to the 2006 to 2010 and subsequent quinquennia (P = 1.07 × 10−87). The number of expert taxonomists is decreasing and, as a potential consequence, the number of authors required to publish a species description is increasing. While some have argued that there is a positive effect in collaborations insofar as resulting in more comprehensive and higher quality integrative approaches (Karbstein et al. 2024), we in contrast view the phenomenon as inevitably resulting in a narrowing gyre with tragic consequences for taxonomy and biodiversity research at large, and unforeseen consequences to all biological research as taxonomic expertise vanishes (Hopkins and Freckleton 2002; Bartolus 2008; Pearson et al. 2011). In 1 particularly significant example of lack of expertise to identify material, the Swedish Malaise Trap Project collected ca. 20 million specimens of insects over 2003 to 2006; while much has been sorted into major taxonomic bins, only 1% had been processed and identified 15 yr later (Karlsson et al. 2020).

We have argued above that the number of species on faunal inventory lists must remain updated. Far from mindless drudgery, such lists are important for numerous purposes including research throughout biological domains, and particularly so in conservation and nature-based economic areas of activity (Cracraft 2002; Pearson et al. 2011; Tilman et al. 2017; Conix et al. 2024). But—they only can be undertaken by expert taxonomists (Weeks and Gaston 1997) who, as noted above, are disappearing at an alarming rate (Gaston and May 1992). It should be clear that there will be downstream effects from the devaluation and consequent loss of taxonomists; Valdecasas and Camacho (2003) argued that the end result of such a loss would lead to specialists in other fields working with largely meaningless taxonomic data. A similar argument was made in paleontology: fieldwork is expensive and risky, hence goes unfunded, and taxonomy lacks impact. The net result is an “extinction of experience,” with researchers inexperienced in basic field research and specimen-based taxonomic research (Maidment and Butler 2025). Thus, one of the first steps in an action plan for conservation of biodiversity needs be the recovery of the taxonomic profession, across all levels: from the amateur enthusiast, through parataxonomists, to trained professionals, while prizing rigor and quality over speed derived by automation (Wheeler et al. 2004; de Carvalho et al. 2007, 2008) and covering a broad taxonomic base (Stuart et al. 2010). Conservation is existentially dependent upon taxonomy and all the corollary data involved in the taxonomic enterprise.

Another leg of the taxonomic impediment (e.g., Coleman 2015) is the lack of taxonomic infrastructure to meet current demands. In infrastructure, we here include protected areas set aside for conservation, a critical component of protecting biodiversity (Margules and Sarkar 2007; Leclerc et al. 2022). A proposal was put forth 20 yr ago that ca. 11% of the Earth’s surface should be set aside to preserve at least 10% of the geographic ranges of terrestrial mammals (Ceballos et al. 2005). Ongoing efforts suggest that conservation of 30% to 50% of the Earth’s surface constitutes a more realistic scenario to both decrease the rate of global warming as well as to more effectively conserve biodiversity (Noss et al. 2012; Dinerstein et al. 2017, 2019), or even as much as 75% of the Earth’s land area and 93% of Tropical rainforests (Rodrigues and Gaston 2001). Clear markers toward that goal were suggested by Nicholson et al. (2021), and the “30 by 30” goal (conservation of 30% of the Earth’s land and ocean protected by the year 2030) constituted an explicit target (“Target 3”) of the Kunming–Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework (KMGBF), adopted as Decision 15/4 by over 190 countries at the 15th Conference of the Parties (Montreal, Canada, 7 to 19 December 2022). We hasten to add that Target 2 constituted an assurance that “at least 30 percent of areas of degraded terrestrial, inland water, and marine and coastal ecosystems are under effective restoration” by 2030, which would add considerable area to Target 3. Unfortunately, despite being signed by government representatives, Decision 15/4 is not legally binding, and furthermore only “urges” and “invites” signatory parties to implement the KMGBF.

In the haste to potentially conserve land, it is important to consider that not all land is equal in terms of its conservation value. A recent analysis of insular flora found that 31% of the plant species sampled were native to islands (Schrader et al. 2024), whose total areas jointly constitute only 5.3% of the Earth’s terrestrial surface area (Weigelt et al. 2013). Schrader et al. (2024) further noted that 55% of all documented plant extinctions had occurred on islands. With respect to mammals, it has been estimated that ca. 1,100 mammal species were endemic to 1 or multiple islands (1,799 islands in all), of which ca. 37% were in a threat category (Leclerc et al. 2020, 2022). These numbers almost certainly are overly conservative and underscore that islands harbor inordinate levels of endemism, and that conservation of islands and island habitats should be prioritized in protected area selection. Wallacea, Madagascar, and Indian Ocean Islands in particular have been identified as having particularly low protected area coverage (<8%; Leclerc et al. 2022). We also stress that documentation, assessment, monitoring, and action are inextricably linked: to identify priority areas for conservation (action), we first must know where species live (assessment, monitoring; Margules and Sarkar 2007). Once such areas have been identified on the basis of the previous steps of our program, they then can come under official protection regimes. At the same time, ongoing assessment efforts should be a priority because differences in initial documentation and successive monitoring efforts may lead to evolving differences in the identification and priority level of areas of conservation (Jenkins et al. 2013).

The previous steps in this protocol likewise lead into additional action streams. We have extensively cited above the IUCN Red List (https://www.iucnredlist.org; accessed 23 May 2025) and its distinct components (IUCN 2012, 2021, 2024) to assist in conservation and management efforts. The Red List is one of the more visible such endeavors but not the only one. Members of the World Commission on Protected Areas bridge the gap between science and politics in providing advice to policymakers to promote the establishment and management of protected areas. Similar interactions between putatively dissimilar and unrelated fields as are policy and biology are apparent in the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC; https://www.ipcc.ch/ accessed 24 May 2025), Intergovernmental Science-Policy Platform on Biodiversity and Ecosystem Services (IPBES; https://www.ipbes.net/ accessed 24 May 2025), the Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD; https://www.cbd.int/ accessed 24 May 2025), National Biodiversity Strategy and Action Plan (NBSAPs) developed by countries to implement the CBD at the national level (https://www.cbd.int/nbsap accessed 24 May 2025), the Key Biodiversity Area program (https://www.keybiodiversityareas.org/ accessed 24 May 2025), Alliance for Zero Extinction (https://zeroextinction.org/ accessed 24 May 2025), and many other similar endeavors and initiatives. The underlying theme here is that taxonomists are highly relevant in modern conservation and should become even more so by having the agency to interact with the conservation community, share data, explicitly link their research to conservation, and engage in committees, commissions, and dialogues whenever possible. In that vein, we reiterate the importance of including a section on conservation in any new species description (Ruedas et al. 2024) to assist in any subsequent Red List assessments. In addition, every paper in taxonomy, systematics, or phylogeography could substantially contribute to conservation by having a section in the discussion on the application of the data collected to on-going or future conservation planning and action (Ruedas et al. 2024).

Discussion

Dogmatic adherence to hypothesis-based research does no favors to biodiversity research, which often is geographically based and thus partly undirected and unstructured. When one goes into an area to ascertain biodiversity, at any level—that is, area-based collecting—one is discovering and documenting what lives there before it is obliterated by human activities, for example, Centinela Ridge (Dodson and Gentry 1991; Wilson 1992); Centinelan extinctions (Winchester and Ring 1996). What are the biotic interactions that are going on in that area? What are the interactions between biotic and abiotic components of the ecosystem? Life must be documented before hypothesis testing is undertaken, or indeed before it even is possible to undertake robust hypothesis testing. There often may be a substantial gap between discovery and formal description (Fontaine et al. 2012; Kemp 2017; Gardner et al. 2023b), or even between description and assignment to taxonomic Domain, let alone lower category, for example, Frankia, a root nodule symbiont of alders (Fagales: Betulaceae; Alnus P. Miller, 1754) was described by Norwegian naturalist (and politician and diplomat) Jørgen Brunchorst as a fungus in 1886 but not assigned to a (then) novel family of bacteria until 1970 (Becking 1970). Actinomycetota (or Actinobacteria) in general likewise long waffled between fungi and bacteria (Stackebrandt and Schumann 2006). Relegating the taxonomic enterprise to the dustbin of stamp collecting obscurity has real life consequences in this particular instance: rather than fungi, Frankia are nitrogen-fixing bacteria; and the Alnus–Frankia symbiosis is a major source of nitrogen entering the soil (12 to 200 kg N ha−1 yr−1; Baker and Mullin 1992) to up to 350 kg N ha−1 yr−1 (Wall 2000). Identifying the alder symbiont to the correct domain of life therefore has important implications for soil fertility and the resultant forest community composition, as well as limits to productivity and other processes in a number of ecosystems (Vitousek et al. 2002). Is determining the Domain of Frankia within the realm of hypothesis testing? Yes, insofar as it constitutes testing a taxonomic hypothesis or, more broadly, testing a phylogenetic hypothesis. But these are precisely the sorts of activities deemed stamp collecting, despite the patently obvious ecological implications of the work.

We presented the protocol for mammal conservation and conservation in general, with 4 key phases: documentation (species discovery and specimen collection); assessment (species relationships, genetic and phylogenetic diversity, and climate change vulnerability); monitoring (tracking populations and habitats over time); and action (addressing the taxonomic impediment, expanding protected areas, and setting the political agenda). We highlight the urgent need for increased specimen collection despite (and on account of) declining rates of collection: continued specimen collection is crucial for understanding and conserving mammal biodiversity; the decline in specimen deposition rate cannot but hinder future research. We also emphasize the crucial role of ongoing assessments in detecting genetic diversity and adaptation to environmental conditions, and stress the importance of long-term population monitoring. Genetic diversity plays a crucial role in species and population viability and adaptability, particularly in the face of climate change and emerging infectious diseases. Assessments in addition are vital for identifying vulnerable species and developing effective conservation strategies, and incorporating genetic diversity and disease considerations is crucial for long-term conservation success. Continued monitoring will provide valuable data on population trends, identify at-risk species, and assess the effectiveness of conservation interventions, while identifying temporal trends in community composition: monitoring is critical for understanding broader biodiversity patterns and informing conservation policy. We further advocate for bolstering the taxonomic profession and expanding protected areas, particularly on islands, to effectively conserve mammal biodiversity. A significant concern is the decreasing number of taxonomists alongside the increasing number of species to be documented, which ineluctably will lead to a growing crisis not just in the narrow field of taxonomy, but across numerous biological disciplines potentially critical to human well-being: effective conservation planning will be the inevitable product resulting from the interconnectedness of documentation, assessment, monitoring, and action and applying each of those steps of our protocol not just singularly but in synergistic combination.

In the Nicomachean Ethics, Aristotle (ca. 350 BC) suggested that politics was the most sovereign and most comprehensive master ­science, because politics uses the rest of the sciences, and because politics legislates what people are to do and what they are not to do. Thus, for Aristotle, it followed that the end of politics is the good of man (eudamonia, εὐδαιμονία: the good composed of all goods); we argue that eudamonia rests on effective conservation, which in turn rests on effective and continuing survey and inventory work underlain by basic taxonomy. The principal points of the DAMA protocol are not necessarily novel: some have been brought up repeatedly over the past few years or decades. However, besides the effective work by Földvári et al. (2024), DAMA largely has yet to be put in effect. One reason for this shortcoming may be that while the threats long have been understood by biologists, naturalists, and individuals in the temporally unconstrained environmental arena, actual policy instead is crafted and put into effect in the temporally constrained (by election cycles) politico-legal sphere. Calls to action from biologists, therefore must be translated to the political arena if conservation and the full implementation of DAMA is to be effected. The successful integration of politics and politicians into the process of conservation is thus critical to the success of our protocol because nothing is more vital to the human condition than preservation of biodiversity.
