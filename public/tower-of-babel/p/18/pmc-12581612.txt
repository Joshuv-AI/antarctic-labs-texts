Accurate structure-activity relationship prediction of antioxidant peptides using a multimodal deep learning framework
Authors: Huynh Anh Duy, Tarapong Srisongkram
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Antioxidant peptides (AOPs) have emerged as promising peptide agents due to their efficacy in counteracting oxidative stress-related diseases and their applicability in functional food and cosmetic industries. In this study, we developed a comprehensive quantitative structure-activity relationship (QSAR) utilizing a multimodal deep learning framework that integrates 6 sequence-based structure representations with stacking ensemble neural architectures–convolutional neural networks, bidirectional long short-term memory, and Transformer—to enhance predictive accuracy. Additionally, we employed a generative model to design novel AOP candidates, which were subsequently evaluated using the best-performing QSAR model. Remarkably, the stacking models using one-hot encoding achieved outstanding predictive metrics, with accuracy, AUROC, and AUPRC values surpassing 0.90, and the MCC above 0.80, demonstrating a highly accurate and robust QSAR model. SHAP analysis highlighted that proline, leucine, alanine, tyrosine, and glycine are the top five residues that positively influence antioxidant activity, whereas methionine, cysteine, tryptophan, asparagine, and threonine negatively impact antioxidant activity. Finally, 604 high-confidence AOPs were computationally identified. This study demonstrates that the multimodal framework improves the prediction accuracy, robustness, and interpretability of the AOP. It also enables the efficient discovery of high-potential AOPs, thereby offering a powerful pipeline for accelerating peptide discovery in pharmaceutical and functional applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13321-025-01102-4.

Introduction

Oxidative stress arises from an imbalance between the excessive generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and the limited capacity of biological systems to eliminate them through antioxidant defenses. The accumulation of ROS can trigger a cascade of deleterious effects, leading to oxidative damages of cellular components such as lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids. This process is a key contributor to the onset and progression of various chronic diseases, including cardiovascular disorders, neurodegenerative conditions, diabetes, and cancer [1]. To counteract these harmful effects, organisms have evolved a complex antioxidant defense system comprising both enzymatic and non-enzymatic components [2]. Among the non-enzymatic antioxidants, bioactive peptides have attracted growing attention due to their favorable safety profiles, high bioavailability, and multifunctional bioactivities [3]. These peptides exert antioxidant effects by scavenging free radicals, chelating pro-oxidant metal ions, and inhibiting lipid peroxidation, thereby playing a protective role against oxidative stress-induced cellular damage [4]. Peptides comprising 2–20 amino acid residues, typically with molecular weights below 3 kDa, have demonstrated significant antioxidant activity, particularly when enriched with hydrophobic residues such as proline, valine, tryptophan, and phenylalanine [3, 5].

Antioxidant peptides (AOPs) have emerged as a prominent focus in the fields of food science, cosmetics and pharmaceuticals, owing to their health-promoting properties. AOPs have been considered as promising candidates for incorporation into health-promoting food products. Their application in functional foods has gained increasing attention, owing to their dual role in enhancing nutritional content and conferring therapeutic benefits. Characterized by low molecular weight, these peptides exhibit favorable physicochemical properties, including high solubility under acidic conditions and efficient gastrointestinal absorption. These attributes contribute to their excellent bioavailability, making them particularly well-suited for inclusion in functional beverages and other nutraceutical formulations [6]. The World Health Organization highlights the importance of antioxidant-rich diets, recognizing food as the primary and most accessible source of dietary antioxidants [7]. AOPs exhibit the ability to scavenge free radicals while maintaining favorable characteristics such as rapid absorption and high stability. These properties contribute to their efficacy in delaying skin aging, forming the basis for their growing application in the cosmetic industry [6]. Beyond their antioxidant capacity, bioactive peptides have also been shown to possess antimicrobial activity against skin-associated pathogenic bacteria, along with anti-aging and anti-inflammatory effects. Taken together, these multifunctional properties have positioned bioactive peptides as promising agents in cosmetic formulations aimed at improving skin health and aesthetics [8, 9].

According to recent data, the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has approved approximately 102 therapeutic peptides for a wide range of clinical applications. These approvals encompass treatments for cardiovascular diseases, human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection, and central nervous system disorders, as well as other notable conditions such as osteoporosis, thrombocytopenia, Cushing’s disease, and hypoglycemia [10]. The increasing number of approved peptide-based therapeutics highlights the growing recognition of peptides as a promising class of bioactive molecules in modern pharmacotherapy. Among them, AOPs–either naturally occurring or synthetically engineered–have attracted significant attention due to their potent free radical scavenging capabilities. AOPs have demonstrated considerable therapeutic potential in mitigating oxidative stress-related pathologies, including neurodegenerative diseases [11], cardiovascular disorders [12, 13], and renal dysfunction [14]. The discovery of novel AOPs represent a viable direction for advancements in functional foods, cosmetics, and therapeutic applications.

Recently, deep learning demonstrates an innovative tool for understanding the quantitative structure-activity relationship (QSAR) of chemicals and peptides [15–17]. Convolutional neural network (CNN) have achieved widespread success across various domains, particularly in image processing, owing to their capability to capture and encode local spatial features [18, 19]. Additionally, a long short-term memory (LSTM) network is highly effective for modeling sequential data, as it can learn long-range dependencies and contextual semantics from temporal or text-based inputs [20, 21]. An extension of this architecture, bidirectional LSTM (BiLSTM), further enhances its performance by processing sequence information in both forward and backward directions, thereby capturing a more comprehensive contextual representation [22–24]. Transformer models demonstrate strong learning potential by effectively capturing complex relationships within data using attention mechanism. These models learn intricate sequence patterns while enabling parallel processing for faster and more efficient computation [25]. In this context, these architectures have shown great potential for classifying multi-functional peptides derived from primary protein sequences [20, 26, 27], paving the way for advanced peptide characterization and the acceleration of therapeutic discovery.

Generative models have shown significant potential in the de novo discovery of therapeutic peptides, particularly generative adversarial network (GAN) and their variants [28]. Recent studies have showcased their successful application in identifying antimicrobial [29, 30], antiviral [31, 32], and anticancer peptides [33], underscoring their capacity to model complex biological sequence spaces and generate functionally relevant candidates. However, the application of GAN-based approaches for the discovery of AOPs remains unexplored, presenting a novel and prospective direction in bioactive peptide research.

To advance antioxidant research, this study aims to develop QSAR models using CNN, BiLSTM, and Transformer networks to elucidate the structure–activity relationships of AOPs. Furthermore, a stacking neural network framework was constructed by integrating these three models as base learners to enhance predictive performance through ensemble learning. Appropriate sequence representation techniques were employed to capture the intrinsic properties of peptide sequences for each model. In parallel, a Wasserstein GAN with gradient penalty (WGAN-GP) architecture was implemented to generate novel peptide sequences. These newly generated candidates were subsequently evaluated using the best-performing predictive model. By employing this multimodal framework, our study seeks to accelerate the identification of promising AOP candidates with high predictive confidence. Methods

Data set preparation

A comprehensive open-source AOP database (AODB) [34] was utilized as a primary resource in our study. The original AODB comprised of 1517 sequences with antioxidant property. Following rigorous data preprocessing and the removal of 50 redundant entries, a total of 1467 unique AOPs were curated. We conducted a case-by-case review to ensure that the exclusion process were applied appropriately. Upon examination, we found that the redundant entries resulted from multiple independent reports of the same antioxidant activity across different published sources. These positive samples were annotated with the label “1” for the development of the predictive model and simultaneously employed as the “original sequences” input for the generative model. In parallel, non-AOPs were systematically collected from previously published studies [35]. The initial data set comprised 1511 negative sequences. Subsequently, duplicate entries potentially overlapping with the established positive data set were systematically removed to ensure data integrity and eliminate redundancy. Consequently, 1501 negative samples were identified and labeled as “0” to support the construction of the classification model. This comprehensive data set forms a robust foundation for both predictive and generative tasks within our research framework.

To the best of our knowledge, no validated database of non-AOPs currently exists. It should be emphasized that the negative samples employed in this study represent putative negatives, as their non-bioactive nature has not been experimentally verified. Following the procedure described by Li et al. [35], the negative data set was generated using an in-house Python script to produce peptide sequences with lengths identical to those of the positive data set. To reduce the risk of data leakage, sequences sharing more than 90% similarity with any positive sample were identified and removed using the Cluster Database at High Identity with Tolerance (CD-HIT) algorithm [36]. This process ensured that the resulting negative data set was length-matched, non-redundant, and exhibited minimal sequence similarity to experimentally validated AOPs. Based on these criteria, the generated putative negatives were adopted for use in this study. Such putative negatives have proven valuable in numerous computational prediction studies across various biological endpoints [37, 38].

In total, the predictive model was developed using a data set of 2,968 samples. The data set is nearly balanced, with a ratio of 49.4% positive to 50.6% negative, minimizing the risk of bias toward one class and reducing the need for class weighting during model training. The data were divided into a training set (2077 samples) and a test set (891 samples) with a 70:30 split ratio. To further enhance the reliability of the model and minimize the likelihood of over-fitting, the training set was subsequently divided into dedicated sub-training and validation subsets [39]. These subgroups were also split with the 70:30 ratio.

Sequence representations

We systematically investigated eight distinct sequence representation strategies to serve as inputs for the predictive models. The sequence-level encodings encompassed amino acid composition (AAC), dipeptide composition (DPC), and pseudo-amino acid composition (pseAAC). At the amino acid level, representations captured physicochemical properties (Phychem) and one-hot encoding (One-hot) schemes [40]. Additionally, we incorporated advanced representations, including evolutionary scale modeling (ESM), sequence-based tokenization methods (Sequence tokens), and k-mers (k = 3) feature extraction techniques [41], to comprehensively characterize the peptide sequences.

In this study, the AAC was calculated to capture the frequency of individual residues, thereby extracting global compositional patterns using pandas and native Python functions. The DPC algorithm efficiently computes the frequencies of all possible pairs of consecutive amino acids within each sequence, enabling the capture of both global patterns and local residue correlation factors [42]. The pseAAC extends the conventional AAC by integrating sequence order information, allowing for a more nuanced, discrete representation of protein sequences without fully discarding their sequential characteristics [43]. The Phychem incorporates a broad spectrum of essential sequence descriptors, including sequence length, hydrophobicity, molecular weight, isoelectric point, aromaticity, instability index, flexibility, grand average of hydropathicity (GRAVY), net charge at pH 7.4, secondary structure content (helix, turn, sheet), cysteine content, and disulfide bridge counts. The DPC features were derived using the itertools module in Python, while the ProteinAnalysis module was employed for the computation of both pseAAC and Phychem properties.

One-hot encoding, a widely adopted binary encoding strategy, represents peptide sequences in an n-dimensional vector space, where each dimension corresponds to the presence (denoted as “1”) or absence (“0”) of a specific amino acid at a given position in the sequence [44]. This encoding was efficiently implemented using NumPy (v1.26.4). ESM refers to pre-trained, unsupervised language models that generate low-dimensional embeddings, which have been demonstrated to enhance performance across a range of downstream biological tasks [45]. In this study, we employed the ESM-2 model to derive informative sequence representations. For sequence-based tokenization, we utilized the Tokenizer module from TensorFlow Keras (v2.17.0), which transforms amino acid sequences into structured numerical formats through character-level tokenization, assigning unique tokens to each residue. Additionally, k-mers were extracted as fixed-length contiguous substrings from the peptide sequences [41]. We generated binary fingerprints of k-mer features using the CountVectorizer module to capture sequence composition patterns effectively.

Convolutional neural network for predictive modeling

A 1D-CNN was designed to classify peptide sequences. The architecture comprised an input layer matching the input data shape, followed by a convolutional layer with 64 filters (kernel size: 3) and ReLU activation to extract local sequence features. A max-pooling layer (pool size: 2) was applied to reduce feature dimensions, followed by a dropout layer (rate: 0.3) to prevent overfitting. The feature maps were then flattened and passed to a fully connected neural network (FCNN) layer with 100 rectified linear unit (ReLU)-activated neurons, followed by an additional dropout layer (rate: 0.2). A final output neuron with sigmoid activation produced probabilities for binary classification. The model was trained using the Adam optimizer (learning rate: 0.001) with binary cross-entropy loss and evaluated primarily using accuracy. The model was trained over 30 epochs and batch sizes of 32. BiLSTM for predictive modeling

The BiLSTM neural network architecture was constructed using the Keras Sequential API to classify peptide sequences. The architecture comprised two stacked BiLSTM layers. The first BiLSTM layer included 64 units, followed by a dropout layer with a rate of 0.5 to reduce the risk of overfitting. The second BiLSTM layer consisted of 32 units and was also followed by a dropout layer with a rate of 0.5. After the recurrent layers, the FCNN layer with 100 neurons and the ReLU activation function was incorporated to learn complex feature representations. The final output layer contained a single neuron with a sigmoid activation function, producing probability estimates for binary classification. Training was performed for 30 epochs with a batch size of 32 using the Adam optimizer (learning rate = 0.001) with binary cross-entropy loss, and accuracy served as the principal evaluation metric. The architecture of the BiLSTM model is depicted in Fig. 2b.

Transformer neural network for predictive modeling

Stacking neural networks for predictive modeling

Stacking neural networks with fully connected neural networks as a meta-model

To integrate the predictive outputs from multiple base classifiers, a meta-model was constructed using the FCNN architecture. The meta-model was designed to process the probability outputs from the three base networks. Specifically, the meta-model architecture consisted of an input layer corresponding to the number of base models, followed by two FCNN layers with 64 and 32 neurons, respectively, each employing the ReLU activation function to capture complex nonlinear relationships among the base model outputs. The final output layer comprised a single neuron with a sigmoid activation function, suitable for binary classification tasks. The model was compiled using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.001, binary cross-entropy as the loss function, and accuracy as the evaluation metric. The training of the base models and the meta-model was conducted over 30 epochs and 50 epochs, respectively. A batch size of 32 was consistently applied to both the base models and the meta-model throughout the training process. This architecture aimed to leverage the complementary strengths of the individual base classifiers, thereby improving the overall predictive performance through stacked generalization. The schematic representation of the stacking neural network is illustrated in Fig. 2d.

Stacking neural networks without a meta-model

We developed a stacking neural network architecture that omits the use of a meta-model, instead leveraging a direct ensemble of base learners for peptide sequence classification. Specifically, each individual model–CNN, BiLSTM, and Transformer–independently produced probabilistic predictions, which were subsequently consolidated through arithmetic averaging to yield the final ensemble output, thereby eliminating the need for an additional FCNN layer for decision fusion.

Hyperparameter tuning

Hyperparameters were optimized manually through an iterative refinement process. Initial settings were informed by prior studies and domain expertise. In each iteration, one or two parameters were adjusted while keeping the remaining parameters fixed, followed by model retraining to assess changes in validation performance. This progressive adjustment continued until no further improvement in validation accuracy or loss was observed, at which point the final configuration was determined.

Hyperparameter tuning was conducted separately for each model architecture–CNN, BiLSTM, and Transformer–to achieve optimal predictive performance and ensure reproducibility. The stacking ensemble was then constructed using the optimized hyperparameters of the individual base models. For each architecture, we systematically explored both general hyperparameters (e.g., learning rate (LR), batch size, number of epochs, dropout rate) and architecture-specific hyperparameters (e.g., number of layers and units, feed-forward network dimension or FF_dim). SHapley additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis

To gain insights into the influence of AAC features on model predictions, we applied SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP), a game-theoretic approach for interpreting complex chemical and peptide modeling tasks [46, 47], in the context of the Transformer-based model. Specifically, we employed the GradientExplainer variant of SHAP, which is well-suited for deep learning models by leveraging the model’s internal gradients to estimate feature attributions efficiently. Using the training data set encoded by AAC as the background reference, we computed SHAP values for the test set to quantify the individual contribution of each AAC feature toward the model’s prediction. The mean predicted probability of the background samples was used as the expected value for interpretation. To ensure robustness, the SHAP analysis was repeated across three independent training runs, and the resulting SHAP values and feature importance rankings were aggregated by computing their mean values. This process enabled the identification of consistently influential features across runs, providing insights into the most significant amino acid compositions contributing to the classification of AOPs.

Performance assessment from generative model using WGAN-GP

To quantitatively assess the diversity of peptide sequences, we employed two complementary methodologies: Shannon entropy analysis and normalized Hamming distance calculation. These combined approaches enable a comprehensive evaluation of sequence diversity by capturing both the intrinsic sequence complexity at the individual peptide level and the overall variability across the entire original and generated data sets.

Shannon entropy

In the context of biological sequences, Shannon entropy serves as a valuable metric for assessing the compositional diversity within individual sequences [51]. Specifically, the frequency distribution of amino acids within each peptide was determined, and entropy values were calculated using the Shannon entropy formula. Higher entropy values indicate a more balanced and diverse usage of amino acids within the sequence. The mean entropy across all sequences in both the original and generated data sets was subsequently reported to provide a summary measure of intra-sequence compositional diversity [52]. All entropy calculations were conducted using custom Python scripts.

Normalized hamming pairwise distance

To quantitatively assess and compare the internal sequence diversity of both the original and generated peptides, the average pairwise diversity was calculated separately for each data set using a normalized Hamming distance approach [53, 54]. For each pair of peptide sequences, positional mismatches were determined by comparing corresponding amino acids, with any unmatched residues in longer sequences also considered as mismatches. The total number of differences was then normalized by the length of the longer sequence, resulting in a diversity score ranging from 0 (identical sequences) to 1 (completely dissimilar sequences) [55]. Pairwise distances were computed for all unique sequence pairs, and the resulting symmetric distance matrix was used to calculate the average diversity. To avoid redundant counting, only the upper triangular portion of the matrix was considered. This approach was applied consistently to both the original and generated peptide data sets to enable an objective comparison of their sequence diversities.

Wasserstein distance and kernel density estimation

To quantitatively assess the compositional similarity between the original and WGAN-generated peptide sequences, we performed a systematic amino acid composition analysis that integrated both statistical comparison and distributional visualization. Specifically, the Wasserstein distance was calculated between the distributions of amino acid compositions obtained from the original and generated peptide data sets [29, 56], offering a robust quantitative measure of compositional divergence, where lower values indicate higher compositional similarity. In parallel, kernel density estimation (KDE) was applied to model the probability density functions of the mean amino acid compositions, enabling visual examination of distributional differences between the two data sets [57]. By combining the interpretability of KDE with the statistical rigor of the Wasserstein distance, this approach provided a comprehensive evaluation of the fidelity between the original and generated peptide sequences.

AAC calculation

The AAC analysis was undertaken to rigorously compare the compositional properties of the generated sequences with those of the original data set. The relative frequency of each of the 20 canonical amino acids was computed for both data sets, providing a quantitative framework for evaluating compositional fidelity. This analysis serves to ascertain the extent to which the generated sequences replicate the amino acid distribution inherent to the original data, thereby validating the quality of sequence generation. Custom Python scripts were utilized, featuring a dedicated function, calculate_amino_acid_composition, specifically designed to facilitate a comprehensive comparative assessment.

Multimodal framework

Following an evaluation of the predictive performance across all developed models, we strategically selected the top-performing models to ensure robust and reliable identification of potential AOPs. These high-performing models were then employed to screen the novel amino acid sequences generated by the WGAN-GP model. To maximize the likelihood of discovering promising candidates, each newly generated sequence was assessed by the selected predictive models, and sequences with the highest predicted probabilities were shortlisted. To maintain a high standard of selection accuracy, we applied a stringent probability cutoff, considering only sequences with prediction scores exceeding 0.9 as viable candidates. This integrative approach, which seamlessly combines generative modeling with high-confidence predictive analytics, provides a powerful multimodal framework for accelerating the discovery of bioactive peptides.

Statistical analysis

A rigorous statistical protocol was employed to assess model performance and to determine the most suitable predictor of antioxidant activity. The same procedures were applied to time-related metrics in order to evaluate computational efficiency across models. Normality of the data was examined using the Shapiro-Wilk test. The complete data set of these newly generated sequences is provided in the Data availability section.

To further investigate the structural and distributional relationships within both the original data set (1467 sequences) and the generated data set (856 sequences), we applied advanced dimensionality reduction techniques, including uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) and t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE). A wealth of benchmarking studies has been undertaken to evaluate dimensionality reduction (DR) techniques, both within specialized domains and across diverse research areas. These investigations consistently identify non-linear DR approaches, notably t-SNE and UMAP, as the most effective methods for capturing complex data structures [59]. UMAP has demonstrated strong effectiveness in handling biological data, particularly in providing high reproducibility and meaningful organization of complex structures, such as accurately delineating cell populations in single-cell RNA sequencing studies [60]. Performance assessment of peptide generation using WGAN-GP architecture

To assess the diversity and similarity between the original and generated peptide sequences, we utilized two complementary metrics: Shannon entropy and normalized pairwise Hamming distance. Shannon entropy quantifies the variability of amino acid distribution within each sequence, capturing intra-sequence diversity and providing insight into the randomness of AAC [62]. In contrast, the normalized pairwise Hamming distance measures inter-sequence diversity by comparing sequences in a position-specific manner, calculating the proportion of mismatched residues at corresponding positions relative to sequence length. This metric reflects the overall structural similarity or dissimilarity between sequences. Collectively, these metrics offer a comprehensive evaluation of whether the generated peptides effectively preserve both the sequence diversity and structural features of the original data set.

As shown in Fig. 8a, the generated peptides exhibited a slightly higher Shannon entropy (mean = 2.18) compared to the original peptides (mean = 2.04), indicating increased variability in AAC within individual sequences. Similarly, the distribution of normalized Hamming pairwise distances revealed comparable diversity levels between the two data sets, with both the original and generated peptides showing a mean distance of 0.96 (Fig. 8b). These results suggest that the generative model successfully captures and preserves the sequence diversity observed in the original peptide data set, both in terms of intra-sequence variability and inter-sequence structural diversity.Fig. 8A Shannon entropy distribution and B Hamming pairwise distance measures of both original and generated peptide sequences

Identification of high-potential AOP candidates using a multimodal strategy

A total of 856 newly generated peptide sequences were subjected to screening for potential antioxidant activity, employing our two most effective predictive frameworks: a stacking neural network architecture, both with and without an integrated meta-model. Prior to prediction, all sequences were systematically transformed into one-hot encoded formats using the standardized preprocessing pipeline established in this study to ensure data consistency. The models subsequently assigned probability scores to each candidate sequence, and peptides exhibiting predicted probabilities greater than 0.9 were designated as potential antioxidant candidates for further consideration.

Using the stacking model incorporating a meta-model, we successfully classified 710 peptides as predicted antioxidants and 146 peptides as predicted non-antioxidants. In contrast, the stacking approach without a meta-model–based solely on the averaged probabilities from the base models–identified 616 AOPs and 240 non-AOPs. To refine the candidate selection, we performed an intersection analysis between the predicted antioxidant sets from both approaches, which resulted in the extraction of 604 peptide sequences. These overlapping sequences were designated as high-potential AOP candidates. A summary of these observations is presented in Fig. 10.

Comprehensive information regarding the sequences, individual prediction probabilities from both models, and their averaged confidence scores for the high-potential AOP candidates is provided in the Supporting Information. As the generated AOPs are de novo and thus absent from existing experimental databases, cross-referencing is not applicable. To prioritize candidates for future experimental validation, we employed a ranking strategy based on their predicted probability scores, with top-scoring peptides considered primary targets for subsequent testing. In addition, high-confidence candidates can be further evaluated using established in silico tools to assess potential toxicity [63] and hemolytic propensity [64]. AOP candidates exhibiting high confidence scores, low predicted toxicity, and minimal similarity to known hemolytic peptides are deemed the most promising for experimental follow-up. The results clearly demonstrate that the predictive performance of the individual baseline models is consistently inferior to that of the stacking approaches combining two or three baseline models, a trend observed across all four evaluation metrics. As summarized in Table 7, the relative performance drop (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta$$\end{document}Δ) for each ablation configuration and individual baseline model was computed as the difference between the metric value of that configuration and its corresponding full-stack baseline (i.e., \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta = \text {ablation/individual} - \text {full-stack baseline}$$\end{document}Δ=ablation/individual-full-stack baseline). Interestingly, the relative importance of individual modules varied considerably between the two fusion strategies. Within the stacking framework (A-series), the removal of the Transformer (A3) led to the most substantial decline in performance across all evaluation metrics, underscoring its pivotal role in the ensemble. This finding suggests that the Transformer contributes rich contextualized representations, which are effectively exploited by the meta-learner to enhance predictive performance.

In contrast, under the average fusion strategy (B-series), the exclusion of the BiLSTM module (B2) consistently resulted in a marked decline in overall performance, particularly in terms of accuracy and MCC. This highlights the pivotal role of BiLSTM in modeling sequential dependencies within the input data. Unlike the stacking approach, where a meta-learner can integrate and recover complementary signals from other base models, the averaging strategy lacks such compensatory mechanisms. Consequently, the BiLSTM becomes especially important for preserving temporal information and maintaining predictive performance in the absence of higher-level fusion. Additionally, removing the CNN module (B1) led to modest reductions in AUROC and AUPRC, reflecting its auxiliary contribution to discriminative feature extraction. Taken together, these findings emphasize the need for careful alignment between architectural components and fusion strategies when designing robust ensemble models. The individual models (I1–I3) exhibited a more pronounced reduction in predictive performance compared to the full stacking models (A0 and B0), highlighting the added value of the integrated architectures. The ablation results indicate that when used in isolation, each individual component (CNN, BiLSTM, or Transformer) yields substantially weaker predictive performance, underscoring their limited capacity to capture the full spectrum of sequence features on their own. In contrast, combining two components consistently improves performance, and the full integration of all three modules achieves the best results. The synergy among these components appears critical, as their joint utilization enables a richer and more comprehensive representation of peptide sequences than any single module can provide.

Computational time evaluation

To assess the computational efficiency of the two best performing models, the stacking configurations with and without a meta-model (A0 and B0), three timing metrics were recorded across multiple experimental runs. The training time (s) was measured as the cumulative duration required to train all base learners (CNN, BiLSTM, and Transformer) and, where applicable, the meta-model. The inference time (s) referred to the total time taken to generate predictions on the test set using the trained models. The total time (s) represented the sum of training and inference durations, reflecting the overall computational cost of each modeling cycle. Moreover, to provide a comparative foundation, three individual baseline models were assessed: CNN (I1), BiLSTM (I2), and Transformer (I3). All time measurements were collected in seconds using Python’s time.time() function and averaged over three independent repeats to ensure robustness. All experiments were conducted on a CPU-only machine equipped with an 11th Gen Intel Core i3-1125G4 processor (4 cores, 8 threads, 2.00 GHz base frequency), 8 GB of RAM, and Intel UHD integrated graphics. No dedicated GPU was available during training or inference, and all computations were performed under consistent conditions to ensure reproducibility and fairness in runtime evaluation. Table 8 summarizes the computational time performance across all experimental runs for both model configurations, with and without meta-model integration. All results were obtained using a data set consisting of 2077 training samples and 891 test samples. Meanwhile, the two state-of-the-art tools used in this study (AOPP and AnOxPP) did not report any measurements related to computational time, such as training or inference duration. The results indicate that CNN (I1) achieved the shortest training time (11.14s) and the fastest inference time (0.29s), demonstrating its computational efficiency and lightweight architecture. BiLSTM (I2) required a longer training time (24.09s) and a higher inference time (1.16s), reflecting a more complex sequential structure compared to CNN. Among all individual models, Transformer (I3) incurred the highest computational cost, with a training time reaching 114.84s, approximately 10 times that of CNN and 5 times that of BiLSTM, highlighting its substantial resource demands. Consequently, the total computational time follows a descending trend from Transformer to BiLSTM and then to CNN. Statistical analyses indicated that the time metrics of the individual baseline models did not follow a normal distribution, with all p-values being less than 0.05. Consequently, a non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn’s post-hoc test was performed. The results demonstrated statistically significant differences in training time and total time across the methods, whereas no significant difference was observed in inference time. Specifically, the individual Transformer model showed statistically significant differences compared to both CNN and BiLSTM in terms of training time and total time. This observation aligns with the computational characteristics of the examined models: Transformers incorporate resource-intensive mechanisms such as multi-head attention, resulting in significantly longer training times. BiLSTMs, while less demanding than Transformers, still involve sequential processing across time steps, which limits parallelization and increases inference time. In constract, CNNs employ simpler operations that can be executed efficiently across many processors at the same time.

Interestingly, the stacking configuration employing a meta-model (A0) exhibited a slightly shorter training time (264.35s) and total runtime (269.65s) compared to the simple averaging method (B0), which recorded 270.58s for training and 275.52s in total. This result is somewhat unexpected, given that the inclusion of a meta-model introduces an additional training step. A possible explanation lies in the lightweight architecture of the meta-model, as well as minor variations in runtime that may have occurred during the averaging process or due to fluctuations in the experimental environment. However, statistical analysis revealed no significant evidence to suggest a difference between the stacking approach and the simple averaging method. Specifically, the normality of all time metrics was confirmed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, with all p-values exceeding 0.05. Subsequently, one-way ANOVA tests were performed to evaluate the impact of model configuration on each time metric individually. Discussion

Recent advances have demonstrated the remarkable efficacy of neural network architectures in various classification tasks related to therapeutic peptides [65]. Specifically, in the domain of AOP prediction, individual BiLSTM models have exhibited strong predictive capabilities, offering an efficient and interpretable framework for peptide-based antioxidant discovery [35, 58]. In parallel, CNN-based architectures have also emerged as reliable baselines, delivering competitive performance in AOP prediction tasks [66]. Transformer-based models, such as PeptideBERT, further extend this progress by achieving state-of-the-art results in general peptide property prediction through their ability to model complex sequence representations [67]. Complementing these neural approaches, ensemble learning strategies have also proven promising. Notably, StackedEnC-AOP, an ensemble framework built upon traditional machine learning algorithms including XGBoost, Decision Trees, Random Forests, and SVMs, has demonstrated considerable success in AOP prediction, highlighting the potential of stacking-based methodologies in this domain [68]. Motivated by these advancements, we developed a deep stacked neural network architecture that strategically integrates the complementary strengths of CNN, BiLSTM, and Transformer models. This multimodal framework was designed to harness the unique representational capabilities of each architecture–CNN for local motif extraction, BiLSTM for sequential dependency modeling, and Transformer for capturing global contextual patterns. By unifying these distinct perspectives, our model offers a robust and comprehensive approach to AOP prediction, pushing the boundaries of current computational methodologies in peptide-based therapeutics.

Physicochemical attributes such as molecular weight, amino acid composition, hydrophobicity, and amphipathicity have been recognized as critical determinants of the antioxidant activity of peptides [69]. In alignment with these established associations, the high-potential AOPs identified in this study displayed similar physicochemical patterns, thereby reinforcing the validity and robustness of our predictive model. Specifically, low molecular weight has been recognized as a critical factor influencing the antioxidant properties of peptides. Specifically, peptides with molecular weights below 3000 Da have been reported to display superior antioxidant activity [70], while those below 1000 Da exhibit even greater bioavailability and reactivity toward free radicals [71]. Supporting these observations, all of the high-potential peptides identified in our study possess molecular weights below 3000 Da, with 72% of them falling under the 1000 Da threshold. These results further underscore the promising potential of the selected candidates as effective AOPs.

The influence of AAC on the antioxidant activity of peptides has been well-documented in the literature. In particular, a previous study identified proline (P), leucine (L), and glycine (G) as the predominant residues in AOPs, representing the highest proportion among total amino acids [69]. Glutamic acid (E) and histidine (H) have also been recognized as prevalent residues in AOPs [72]. This finding is consistent with our SHAP analysis, which demonstrated that these residues contributed positively to the prediction of AOPs in our data set. Furthermore, it has been reported that peptides with antioxidant activity mostly contain amino acids with a hydrophobic nature [73]. For example, peptide fractions containing a substantial proportion of hydrophobic amino acids such as proline (P), leucine (L), and alanine (A) have been associated with the highest levels of superoxide radical scavenging and reducing power activities [74, 75]. Our findings align with these observations, as the presence of these amino acid residues appeared to positively contribute to the predicted antioxidant potential of the peptides. This evidence further strengthen the reliability of our predictive models and underscore the biological relevance of the identified peptide candidates.

The GRAVY index reflects the overall hydrophobicity of a peptide by calculating the average hydropathy value across all amino acid residues in the sequence [76]. Previous studies have suggested that GRAVY scores within the range of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-0.5$$\end{document}-0.5 to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$+0.5$$\end{document}+0.5 are characteristic structural features associated with AOPs [72, 77]. In this study, the high-potential AOP candidates exhibited both mean and median GRAVY values falling within this optimal range, with no statistically significant differences observed when compared to the original AOP data set. These findings further support the structural suitability of the identified candidates for potential antioxidant activity.

The interactions among amino acid residues are recognized as critical factors influencing the antioxidant mechanisms of peptides. Beyond the well-recognized impact of hydrophobicity, the amphiphilic nature significantly contributes to their antioxidant potency. A well-balanced distribution of hydrophobic and hydrophilic residues improves peptide solubility and facilitates effective interactions with radical species, thereby enhancing overall antioxidant efficacy [69, 78]. Notably, the amphiphilic character of the high-potential peptides identified in this study did not significantly differ from that of the original AOPs, further supporting the functional potential of these novel candidates as promising antioxidant agents. In addition, a peptide with an instability index below 40 is generally considered stable, whereas values exceeding 40 indicate potential instability [79, 80]. In the present study, approximately 61% of the identified AOP candidates exhibited instability index values within the stable range. Moreover, no significant difference in the instability index was observed between the candidate data set and the original AOP set, suggesting that the predicted peptides possess comparable stability profiles to the reference sequences.

We adopted the WGAN-GP owing to its 1-Lipschitz-constrained critic, enforced via a gradient penalty term, which is theoretically grounded to improve convergence stability and alleviate mode collapse. This formulation has been widely recognized as a robust alternative to the original GAN objective, particularly in generative design tasks where preserving distributional fidelity is critical. Its effectiveness has been demonstrated in multiple peptide discovery studies, including the de novo generation of antimicrobial peptides [81, 82]. In WGAN-GP, we set \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n_{\textrm{critic}} = 5$$\end{document}ncritic=5, meaning the critic was updated five times for each generator update. This strategy ensures the critic reliably estimates the Wasserstein distance at each iteration, providing stable and informative gradients to the generator, thereby improving training stability and sample quality. These dynamics align with empirical observations from our previous work on generative frameworks [83, 84].

Advantanges and challenges

Based on the findings of this study, several noteworthy contributions are highlighted. Both predictive models, utilizing stacking neural networks with or without the integration of a FCNN as a meta-model, demonstrated excellent predictive performance. Notably, the models achieved outstanding predictive performance, with accuracy, AUROC, and AUPRC all exceeding 0.9, and an MCC above 0.8. In addition, the performance of our models was competitive with that of existing state-of-the-art tools. These observations position our models as reliable and effective computational tools for the identification of AOPs. Furthermore, the analysis of feature importance revealed several amino acid characteristics that are strongly associated with the classification of AOPs. These insights align well with previous studies, enhancing the interpretability of our model and reinforcing the biological relevance of the identified features. Finally, our study established a stringent classification threshold (greater than 0.9), which contributed to the robust identification of high-confidence AOP candidates.

Despite achieving strong and competitive performance relative to two state-of-the-art methods, our model does not yield notably significant improvements in predictive accuracy over them. Its main advantages, however, lie in other aspects. First, we introduced a generative model capable of producing sequences that share key characteristics with active AOPs. Beyond replicating known patterns, the proposed framework can be extended to generate entirely novel peptide candidates, thereby facilitating a more rational and targeted approach to peptide design. Importantly, this generative capacity represents a key distinction over existing models, which are predominantly limited to classification or predictive tasks. Second, by leveraging a consensus strategy across the two top-performing models, we were able to identify high-potential AOP candidates with improved reliability, thereby providing advantages for AOP discovery. Third, integrating SHAP analysis improves model interpretability by highlighting structural features that influence biological activity, thereby supporting a deeper understanding of SAR.

Nonetheless, several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the feature analysis primarily focused on physicochemical and sequence-based characteristics, without considering higher-order structural features such as secondary or tertiary structures, which may affect antioxidant activity. Second, although the models exhibited excellent predictive performance in silico, experimental validation of the predicted AOPs was not performed. Wet-lab studies remain essential to confirm the biological activity of the identified candidates.

Future perspectives

Several avenues for future research are proposed based on the findings of this study. In the current work, we employed binary-encoded k-mers and character-level tokens to represent peptide sequences. Future research could investigate more advanced sequence representation techniques, such as k-mer-based embeddings and token embedding strategies, to further improve model performance. Regarding the stacking neural network architecture, alternative meta-models beyond the FCNN could be investigated to further improve predictive capability. Notably, the stacking neural network without a meta-model, which aggregates predictions through averaging base model probabilities, demonstrated competitive predictive performance in this study. This approach may warrant broader application in future research, offering a more computationally efficient alternative for model development and deployment.

Conclusion

This study presents a comprehensive deep multimodal framework for the prediction and generation of AOPs, integrating diverse sequence representations with advanced neural network architectures. By integrating CNN, BiLSTM, and Transformer models within a stacking architecture utilizing one-hot encoding, the proposed framework demonstrated outstanding predictive performance, with accuracy, AUROC, and AUPRC values all exceeding 0.90, and a MCC surpassing 0.80. Additionally, SHAP analysis revealed that proline, leucine, alanine, tyrosine, and glycine were the most influential amino acid residues contributing positively to model predictions. Furthermore, the application of a generative WGAN-GP model enabled the creation of novel peptide candidates, yielding sequences with physicochemical characteristics closely aligned with known AOPs. Using this integrative framework, a total of 604 high-potential AOP candidates were identified. Beyond enhancing peptide discovery, the proposed approach improves the reliability and interpretability of peptide screening. Overall, these findings underscore the potential of deep learning–based strategies in advancing AOP discovery, marking a significant step toward the integration of AI-driven approaches in peptide-based innovation.
