Substance involvement profiles and mental health correlates in emerging adults: a person-centred cluster analysis
Authors: Ana Maria Chamorro Coneo, Loraine Bruges Martínez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInvolvement with psychoactive substances among emerging adults varies considerably in its frequency, severity, and co-occurrence. Person-centred approaches, which identify groups of individuals with shared patterns of substance involvement, may provide a more nuanced understanding of psychosocial risk than traditional substance-specific analyses. This study aimed to identify distinct substance involvement profiles among Colombian university students and examine differences in depression, anxiety, and suicide risk across these profiles, as well as sociodemographic predictors of profile membership.MethodsA cross-sectional multicentre study was conducted with 1,491 university students aged 18–26 years from the four largest cities of Colombia. Self-reported substance involvement across tobacco, alcohol, cannabis, cocaine, amphetamines, and sedatives was assessed. A two-step clustering approach using hierarchical and k-means cluster analyses was employed to identify substance involvement profiles. Differences in depression, anxiety, and suicide risk across profiles were examined using one-way analyses of variance, and multinomial logistic regression was used to identify sociodemographic predictors of profile membership.ResultsA three-cluster solution identified low- (80.5%), moderate- (14.6%), and high-involvement (4.9%) profiles. Profiles differed significantly in depression (F = 6.99, p = 0.001), anxiety (F = 5.51, p = 0.004), and suicide risk (F = 6.49, p = 0.002), with moderate- and high-involvement profiles reporting higher levels of depression, anxiety, and suicide risk. Men (OR = 1.64, p = 0.002) and students from higher socioeconomic backgrounds (OR = 2.45–2.83, p ≤ 0.001) were more likely to belong to the moderate- profile, while students from the Central region showed greater odds of membership in both the moderate- and high-involvement profiles than those from other regions (OR = 2.49–2.57, p ≤ 0.009).ConclusionsDistinct substance involvement profiles among Colombian university students were associated with meaningful differences in mental health outcomes. High involvement with cocaine, amphetamines, and sedatives may represent useful indicators of risk for poorer mental health outcomes and provide opportunities for earlier screening and intervention. These findings support integrated approaches to substance use and mental health assessment, alongside more targeted prevention efforts among young adults.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28371-4.

Background

Alcohol and tobacco remain the most widely used substances globally (18.3% and 15.2%, respectively), followed by cannabis (3.8%), amphetamines (0.77%), opioids (0.37%), and cocaine (0.35%). In Latin America, alcohol use ranges from 16.0% to 23.8% across subregions, tobacco smoking prevalence ranges from 9.4% to 19.9%, while cannabis and cocaine are the most commonly used illicit substances (2.9% and 0.88%, respectively) [1]. These estimates are likely conservative, as many low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) have weak health surveillance systems and rely heavily on modelled rather than primary epidemiological data [1, 2]. Evidence on the health and social correlates of substance use is similarly limited, with most of the literature based on samples from high-income countries [3], while Latin America remains particularly underrepresented even within the broader evidence base from LMICs [4].

The burden of frequent and problematic substance use, particularly when escalating into substance use disorders (SUDs), is associated with substantial health comorbidities (e.g., cardiovascular disease, liver cirrhosis, respiratory complications, and infectious diseases) and excess mortality [2, 3, 5]. In 2015, tobacco use accounted for 110.7 deaths per 100,000 globally, while in 2016 alcohol contributed to approximately 2.8 million deaths, and illicit drug use to more than 450,000 deaths [4]. Across substances, men consistently report higher prevalence, incidence, and mortality related to alcohol and drug use disorders than women, which may be linked to earlier initiation, greater risk-taking tendencies, and sociocultural norms that encourage substance use [2, 6]. Prospective evidence suggests that the absolute burden of SUD will continue to rise over the next two decades, driven by population growth, population aging, and widening health inequalities [2]. Consequently, strengthening public health policies, prevention efforts, and early screening initiatives remains critical to reducing substance-related morbidity and mortality [5, 6].

Emerging adulthood, typically spanning the late teens to the mid-twenties (approximately the transition into the third decade of life), represents a critical developmental period for substance use initiation and escalation, with incidence rates of SUDs peaking among young adults aged 20–24 years [1, 7]. This period coincides with neurobiological maturation, social transitions to adulthood, and increased exposure to novel risk environments [6, 8]. Indeed, for youth accessing higher education, vulnerability may be increased by academic demands, reduced parental monitoring, greater social autonomy, and exposure to new peer networks. Furthermore, students from wealthier socioeconomic backgrounds report greater substance involvement, including alcohol consumption and the nonmedical use of prescription stimulants, likely reflecting increased access and financial availability [5, 9].

Substance use is closely linked to poorer mental health outcomes, although the strength and nature of these associations vary across substances. Alcohol has the strongest and most consistent associations with intentional self-harm and suicide among psychoactive substances. It is also strongly linked with anxiety and depression; approximately 67% of university students who binge drink also report mental health problems [5, 9]. Nicotine dependence and regular cannabis use have both been associated with increased depressive symptoms, anxiety and suicidality [3, 10]. While opioids and stimulants are less prevalent among psychoactive substances, they are associated with increased risk of severe psychiatric comorbidity, including suicide attempts, panic symptoms, and psychotic experiences [3, 9].

It is worth noting that associations between substance involvement and poorer mental health outcomes are often bidirectional [11]. University students could use substances to cope with emotional distress or academic strain [9], while repeated usage may further increase vulnerability to mental health problems during a critical period of neurodevelopment [3, 8].

The reciprocal relationship between substance involvement and mental health is further complicated by the fact that young adults frequently use multiple substances simultaneously or sequentially. Longitudinal studies suggest that substance use patterns appear to follow an additive progression, commonly initiating with alcohol, tobacco and cannabis, later expanding to include stimulants, opioids and sedatives in severe cases [12–14]. Similarly, psychological distress systematically increases with the number of drugs used, a trend observed in both clinical and community samples [15]. Most reports of substance use prevalence adopt a variable-centred approach that examines individual substances in isolation. While useful for estimating prevalence, such approaches may overlook the fact that many young adults engage in multiple substances simultaneously and may fail to capture meaningful patterns of co-occurring substance involvement associated with different levels of risk [13, 16].

In contrast to substance-centred approaches, person-centred ones aim to identify naturally occurring patterns of substance use behaviour (e.g., using cluster or latent content analysis) [17–20]. Grouping individuals according to co-occurring substance use may help identify meaningful risk typologies, distinguishing between experimental use and more severe patterns of involvement. Recent Colombian evidence using cluster-based approaches has identified distinct substance use risk profiles, with higher-risk groups characterised predominantly by men, younger adults (24–40 years old), and lower socioeconomic status. However, these profiles were derived from binary indicators of substance use in a general population sample and did not examine mental health correlates [21].

Another constraint in substance use research concerns how involvement or use is measured. Much of the literature relies on simplified indicators such as “any use” within a specified period (e.g., past 12 months) or standard DSM-derived diagnoses. Such restrictive conceptualisations of substance use may overlook the complexity of individuals’ relationships with psychoactive substances, which can range from occasional experimentation to greater involvement, characterised by increased frequency of use, stronger urges to consume, perceived problems, and unsuccessful attempts to stop [13]. To date, few studies have examined multidimensional substance involvement profiles among Latin American university students, particularly in relation to mental health outcomes.

The aim of the present study was to identify naturally occurring profiles of involvement with licit and illicit substances in young adults using a person-centred approach. A secondary aim was to examine differences in depression, anxiety, and suicide risk across these profiles, and to identify sociodemographic and contextual predictors of profile membership. In doing so, the study contributes to a growing body of evidence that may help inform public health policy and university-based prevention and intervention strategies.

Methods

Design and procedure

This study used a cross-sectional design with a convenience sample of undergraduate students recruited from four universities located in the major urban centres of Bogotá, Medellín, Barranquilla, and Cali, representing three main regions of Colombia (Central, Atlantic, and Pacific). The survey was hosted on Qualtrics and distributed through institutional communication channels between August and September 2023. After providing informed consent, participants completed a battery of sociodemographic questions, substance use items and validated self-report measures of mental health outcomes. Participants did not receive financial compensation or any other incentive for participation. The study was approved by the Institutional Research Ethics Board at Universidad del Norte (Ref. 2303–5100) prior to data collection.

Participants

Participants were eligible if they were undergraduate students aged between 18 and 26 years enrolled at one of the participating universities and provided informed consent. Of the 1,756 participants, 265 (15.1%) did not provide complete substance use information and were excluded from the cluster analyses. Comparisons between included and excluded participants indicated that those with missing substance use data were more likely to be women, χ²(1) = 13.85, p < 0.001, from lower socioeconomic backgrounds, χ²(2) = 32.33, p < 0.001, and from the Atlantic region, χ²(2) = 19.35, p < 0.001. No significant differences were observed for age group, χ²(1) = 2.39, p = 0.122, depression, χ²(4) = 4.09, p = 0.394, or anxiety, χ²(3) = 0.61, p = 0.893. See Supplementary Table S1 for details.

The remaining analytic sample (n = 1,491) ranged in age from 18 to 26 years (M = 20.3, SD = 1.3), with most participants (93.3%) aged between 18 and 22 years. Further sample characteristics are presented in Table 1.

Measures

Sociodemographic data

Participants reported their age, gender and city of residence. Socioeconomic status was assessed using Colombia’s official residential socioeconomic stratification system, administered by the National Administrative Department of Statistics (DANE). The system classifies households into six categories based on residential and neighbourhood characteristics: low-low (stratum 1), low (stratum 2), middle-low (stratum 3), middle (stratum 4), middle-high (stratum 5), and high (stratum 6). For analytical purposes, strata were grouped into low (1–2), middle (3–4), and high (5–6) socioeconomic categories.

Substance involvement

Degrees of engagement with each substance (tobacco, alcohol, cannabis, cocaine, amphetamines, and sedatives) were assessed across six categories using an adapted version in Spanish [22] of the three-month substance involvement items from the Alcohol, Smoking and Substance Involvement Screening Test (ASSIST) [23]. Participants responded on a 5-point Likert-type scale ranging from 0 (never) to 4 (daily or almost daily) to five items assessing their level of involvement with each substance during the past three months: frequency of use (“How often have you used [substance] in the past three months?”), urges or cravings (“How often have you had a strong desire or urge to use [substance]?”), perceived problems (“How often has your use of [substance] led to health, social, legal, or financial problems?”), concern about use (“How often have you worried about your use of [substance]?”), and unsuccessful attempts to reduce or stop (“How often have you failed to control, cut down, or stop using [substance]?”). Responses were summed to generate a substance-specific involvement score, with higher scores indicating greater problematic involvement. The substance use scale demonstrated good internal consistency in the present sample (α = 0.86).

Depression

The Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) [24] is a widely used 9-item self-report measure based on DSM-IV diagnostic criteria for major depression and has been validated in Spanish-speaking populations [25]. Participants rate how often they have experienced each symptom over the past two weeks on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day). Total scores range from 0 to 27, with higher scores reflecting greater depressive symptom severity. Standard severity thresholds classify scores as minimal (0–4), mild (5–9), moderate (10–14), moderately severe (15–19), and severe (20–27). In the present study, the scale demonstrated good internal consistency (α = 0.84).

Anxiety

The Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) [26] assesses anxiety severity with 7 items rated from 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day). Scores range from 0 to 21, classified as minimal (0–4), mild (5–9), moderate (10–14), and severe (15–21). The Spanish version of the GAD-7 [27] demonstrated good internal consistency in the present study (α = 0.88).

Suicide risk

The Plutchik Suicide Risk Scale (PSRS) [28] is a 15-item self-report instrument. It screens suicide risk through dichotomous responses (yes = 1, no = 0) to 15 items. Item scores are summed to produce a total score ranging from 0 to 15, with higher scores indicating greater suicide risk [29]. In the present study, the Spanish translation of this scale demonstrated questionable internal consistency (α = 0.67).

Sample size calculation

Although no formal a priori power analysis was conducted for the cluster analysis due to its exploratory person-centred nature, the final analytic sample (n = 1,491) exceeded commonly recommended sample size guidelines for cluster analysis, including maintaining a large cases-to-variables ratio and adequate cluster sizes for interpretation. The smallest identified cluster represented 4.9% of the sample, meeting conventional thresholds for meaningful subgroup interpretation [30].

An a priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power to estimate the sample required for the planned one-way ANOVAs comparing mental health outcomes across the three substance involvement profiles. Given previous evidence showing moderate to large associations between polysubstance use and mental distress [15], a conservative small effect size (f = 0.12) was specified. Assuming α = 0.05, power = 0.90, and three comparison groups, the estimated minimum required sample size was 882 participants. The final analytic sample (n = 1,491) exceeded this threshold, indicating sufficient statistical power to detect between-group differences. The final sample also exceeded conventional recommendations for multinomial logistic regression, providing adequate numbers of cases within each profile to estimate associations with sociodemographic predictors, although the relatively small size of the high-involvement profile should be considered when interpreting these results [31].

Data analysis strategy

To aid interpretation of the identified profiles, descriptive analyses examined patterns of past 3-month substance use across tobacco, alcohol, cannabis, cocaine, amphetamines, and sedatives. For these analyses, the ASSIST frequency-of-use items were recoded into binary indicators, with “never” coded as 0 and any reported use coded as 1. The resulting indicators were then used to examine substance use patterns according to the total number of substances used (0, 1, 2, 3, 4, or 5 + substances). These analyses were based solely on reported use during the previous three months and did not incorporate ASSIST involvement scores, which assess multiple dimensions of substance involvement (e.g., craving, problems related to use or attempts to reduce use).

To identify risk profiles of substance involvement, a two-step clustering strategy was used. The six continuous substance involvement scores were standardised (z-scores) and entered into a cluster analysis to identify naturally occurring substance use profiles reflecting both breadth and severity of substance involvement. First, a hierarchical cluster analysis was conducted using Ward’s minimum variance method with squared Euclidean distances to determine the optimal number of clusters. The agglomeration schedule and dendrogram were examined to identify discontinuities in cluster fusion. Subsequently, k-means cluster analysis was performed specifying the number of clusters identified in the hierarchical solution. To evaluate the robustness of the resulting cluster structure, a supplementary Two-step cluster analysis was conducted using the same six substance involvement scores.

To evaluate differences in mental health outcomes across substance use profiles, one-way analyses of variance (ANOVAs) were conducted for depression, anxiety, and suicide risk. Assumptions underlying the analyses were assessed prior to testing, including homogeneity of variances using Levene’s test. Given the large sample size, ANOVA was considered robust to moderate departures from normality [32]. Where homogeneity of variances was violated, Welch’s robust test was conducted to assess the stability of the findings.

To examine sociodemographic predictors of substance involvement profile membership, a multinomial logistic regression analysis was conducted. Substance involvement profile (low-, moderate-, and high) was entered as the dependent variable, with the low-involvement profile serving as the reference category. Gender (women, men), socioeconomic status (low, middle, high), and region (Atlantic, Pacific, and Central) were entered as categorical predictors. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated to assess the strength and direction of associations. Model fit was evaluated using likelihood ratio chi-square tests, and parameter estimates were examined to identify predictors that differentiated the moderate- and high-involvement profiles from the low-involvement profile. All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Descriptive analyses of substance use in the past three months showed that tobacco and cannabis were most commonly used in combination with one or two other substances (83.1% and 80.4%, respectively). In contrast, 78.0% of participants reporting cocaine use did so in combination with three or more substances, a pattern that was also observed for amphetamine use (63.4%). After alcohol (62.0%), sedatives were the second most common substance used in isolation (8.9%). Although 26.0% of sedative users reported use in combination with three or more substances, most reported using sedatives alongside only one or two other substances (64.3%). Depression, anxiety and suicide risk across substance involvement profiles

Levene’s tests supported the assumption of homogeneity of variances for depression, F(2, 1488) = 2.70, p = 0.067, and anxiety, F(2, 1488) = 1.91, p = 0.149, but not for suicide risk, F(2, 1488) = 16.91, p < 0.001. A Welch ANOVA for suicide risk remained statistically significant, Welch’s F(2, 159.12) = 4.45, p = 0.013, indicating that the findings were robust to this violation.

Significant differences across substance involvement profiles emerged for depression, F(2, 1488) = 6.99, p = 0.001, ηp² = 0.009, anxiety, F(2, 1488) = 5.51, p = 0.004, ηp² = 0.007, and suicide risk, F(2, 1488) = 6.49, p = 0.002, ηp² = 0.005. Bonferroni-adjusted comparisons showed that both the moderate (p = 0.016) and high involvement (p = 0.018) profiles reported significantly higher depressive symptoms than the low-involvement one, with no differences between the moderate- and high-involvement groups (p = 1.00).

For anxiety, the moderate-involvement profile reported significantly higher scores than the low involvement profile (p = 0.017), whereas differences between the high- and low-involvement profiles did not survive correction (p = 0.110). For suicide risk, the high-involvement profile reported significantly greater suicide risk than the low-involvement profile (p = 0.007), while comparisons with the moderate profile were not statistically significant. Predictors of substance involvement profile membership

Multinomial logistic regression analyses revealed that sociodemographic factors significantly predicted profile membership, χ²(10) = 132.15, p < 0.001. Compared with the low-involvement profile, students in the moderate-involvement profile were more likely to be men (OR = 1.64, 95% CI [1.20, 2.25], p = 0.002), belong to higher socioeconomic strata, and reside in the Central region of Colombia (OR = 2.49, 95% CI [1.67, 3.71], p < 0.001). Students in the high-involvement profile were also more likely to reside in the Central region than in the Pacific region (OR = 2.57, 95% CI [1.27, 5.20], p = 0.009). Gender and socioeconomic status did not significantly differentiate the high and low-involvement profiles. No significant differences were observed between the Atlantic and Pacific regions for either the moderate- or high-involvement profiles (p > 0.05). See details in Table 4.

Discussion

The present study identified distinct substance involvement profiles (i.e., low-, moderate-, and high-involvement) among Colombian university students and found poorer mental health outcomes among students in the high involvement profile. Descriptive analyses further indicated that alcohol was the most prevalent single substance use category, whereas tobacco and cannabis use were most commonly observed among participants reporting two or three substances. Men and students from wealthier socioeconomic backgrounds were more likely to belong to the moderate involvement profile (high tobacco, cannabis and alcohol involvement scores), whereas membership in the high involvement profile (high cocaine, amphetamines and sedatives involvement scores) was distinguished primarily by geographical location.

The moderate involvement profile was characterised primarily by alcohol, tobacco, and cannabis involvement, consistent with previous research showing that these substances commonly co-occur among university students and represent a relatively normative pattern of substance involvement during emerging adulthood [12, 13]. In contrast, cocaine, amphetamine, and sedative involvement contributed most strongly to cluster differentiation, suggesting that they may represent key indicators of more severe patterns of substance involvement among university students. Indeed, previous research has linked cocaine and amphetamine use to impulsivity, panic symptoms, and psychotic experiences, while sedative misuse has been associated with emotional dysregulation and maladaptive coping [9, 14, 15]. Involvement with these substances may represent a useful marker of increased risk, helping university health services identify students who may benefit from more intensive assessment, monitoring, and support.

Interestingly, sedatives were the second most prevalent single-substance use category after alcohol and its use did not peak among users of four or more substances, as was the case for cocaine and amphetamines. This finding contrasts with previous studies in which sedative use was largely concentrated within severe polysubstance profiles and often interpreted as a marker of advanced substance involvement [13–15, 18].

Sedatives originate within healthcare systems and are commonly prescribed for anxiety, panic symptoms, sleep disturbances, and other internalising difficulties [14]. While university students often obtain these medications through peers and social networks rather than formal illicit drug markets, sedatives nevertheless belong to a class of culturally accepted and medically regulated substances, which may shape distinct patterns of access and use. Consequently, sedative involvement may reflect a unique form of substance involvement linked not only to recreational use but also to the management of psychological distress [9, 15]. However, the present study did not assess prescription status, reasons for use, or routes of access, and further research is needed to clarify the mechanisms underlying this pattern.

Greater depression and suicide risk were observed among students exhibiting elevated patterns of substance involvement, regardless of the specific substances characterising the profile. Although the pattern was less consistent for anxiety, the findings broadly align with previous evidence linking more severe substance involvement to increased psychiatric vulnerability [13–15, 20]. While the magnitude of these differences was small, they may be meaningful when observed across large university populations [3, 9, 15]. From a public health perspective, substance involvement and mental health should be assessed in an integrated manner rather than as separate concerns. Early identification of students exhibiting moderate to high involvement with psychoactive substances may provide opportunities for targeted intervention before more severe health, academic, and psychosocial consequences emerge [2, 3, 5].

The present findings also revealed important sociodemographic differences in substance involvement profiles. Students from higher socioeconomic backgrounds were more likely to belong to the moderate-involvement profile, whereas students residing in the Central region were more likely to belong to both the moderate- and high-involvement profiles. One possible explanation is that, within university settings, access and opportunity may play a greater role than socioeconomic disadvantage, as greater financial resources and residence in more affluent urban environments may increase exposure to recreational substance use and facilitate access to recreational psychoactive substances [1, 5, 6, 9]. The persistence of geographical differences after accounting for socioeconomic status further suggests that contextual factors, such as urban drug markets, nightlife economies, and local social norms, may also contribute to substance-related risk [1, 2, 4, 6]. However, these mechanisms were not directly assessed in the present study and should therefore be interpreted cautiously.

Taken together, these findings have several implications for public policy and university mental health and prevention services. First, the prominent involvement of cocaine, amphetamines, and sedatives within high-involvement profiles suggests that screening efforts may benefit from paying particular attention to these substances. Their presence may help identify students at greater risk of both substance-related and mental health difficulties, creating opportunities for earlier assessment, intervention, and referral to appropriate support services. Second, prevention strategies may benefit from recognising that problematic involvement with psychoactive substances can emerge through different social and economic pathways. Interventions should therefore be tailored to address risks associated with socioeconomic disadvantage as well as those linked to greater access, opportunity, and exposure to substance-use environments. Third, prevention programmes may benefit from considering gender differences in substance involvement. Embedding prevention activities within settings and programmes that attract large numbers of male students, while addressing norms surrounding substance use, risk-taking, and help-seeking, may improve the reach and effectiveness of these initiatives.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design prevents causal inference regarding the directionality of associations between substance use and mental health. As previous work suggests, these relationships are likely bidirectional, with distress increasing substance use and repeated substance use exacerbating emotional vulnerability [3, 9, 11, 15]. Second, all measures relied on self-report, which may introduce recall bias and social desirability effects, particularly for illicit substance use. Notably, 15% of participants did not provide complete substance use information. Missing data were more common among women, participants from lower socioeconomic backgrounds, and those from the Atlantic region, suggesting that substance involvement may have been somewhat underestimated in these groups. Third, although the sample included multiple sites across major Colombian regions, the convenience sampling strategy limits generalisability beyond this urban student population. Fourth, the relatively small size of the high-involvement profile may have reduced statistical power for some comparisons, particularly anxiety differences. Finally, cluster solutions are inherently sample-dependent and require replication in independent Latin American cohorts to establish stability and broader applicability.

Conclusions

Given that a substantial proportion of students reported involvement with multiple substances, prevention efforts should address patterns of co-use rather than individual substances alone. Beyond binary use/no use conceptualisations, patterns of substance involvement clustered into distinct profiles characterised by different levels of psychological vulnerability and sociodemographic risk. Future research should continue to examine the distinct contexts and motivations underlying different patterns of substance involvement, particularly those involving sedatives and other substances associated with mental health risk.
