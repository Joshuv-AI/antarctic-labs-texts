Optimizing participation in digital health studies: understanding appointment attendance
Authors: Rebecca Schnall, Hui Lin, Maeve Brin, Jean Jimenez, Amy K Johnson, Mirjam-Colette Kempf, Nan Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study examined whether attendance at online digital health research appointments in the American Women Assessing Risk Epidemiologically (AWARE) study was associated with (1) participant age, (2) scheduling factors (time of day, day of week, month), (3) appointment confirmation, and (4) HIV behavioral risk factors.Materials and MethodsWe analyzed scheduling and eligibility screening data from AWARE, a 24-month U.S.-based longitudinal digital cohort of cisgender women at elevated likelihood of HIV seroconversion. Participant demographic and behavioral data were merged with the study team’s Outlook calendar. Chi-square tests and logistic regression models assessed associations between appointment attendance and participant characteristics and scheduling factors.ResultsWomen aged ≥50 years had higher odds of missing baseline visits compared to those aged 20-29 years (44.7% vs 32.3%). Appointments scheduled at 2:00 pm (45.7%), 4:00 pm (45.2%), and 8:00 am (40.2%) had higher no-show rates than other times. No-show rates were lowest on Fridays (30.2%) and during March (27.7%) and June (25.2%). Confirming appointments 24 hours in advance significantly reduced no-shows compared to no confirmation (19.0% vs 51.6%). Histories of having been physically hurt (44.2% vs 32.1%), forced to have sexual activities (41.8% vs 34.1%) and incarcerated (39.3% vs 33.4%) were also associated with higher no-show rates. Similar patterns were observed for rescheduled visits.ConclusionAttendance in digital research was influenced by age, scheduling, and structural vulnerabilities. Incorporating digital access support into study design and grant budgets may reduce disparities, improve retention, and enhance efficiency.Keywords: HIV, digital health research, no-show rates, visit attendance

Background and signficance

Digital health research, defined as participating in study activities normally conducted in person through remote digital venues, is rapidly expanding,1 but remains a methodological frontier. The pivot to remote study designs—accelerated by the COVID-19 pandemic and supported by early guidance from the United States (U.S.) Food and Drug Administration (FDA)2—has transformed how researchers recruit, enroll, follow and retain study participants. While adoption of remote trials was initially modest—ClinicalTrials.gov data show only a 1.65 percentage point increase in remote trials during the first 10 months of the pandemic,3 the pace has now rapidly accelerated, paralleling the expansion of telehealth.4–6

Digital health research offers several potential advantages. It can reduce operational and economic barriers such as transportation, childcare, and work absences,7,8 while enabling recruitment of diverse and geographically dispersed populations, including those in rural or underserved areas.5 These features enable digital studies to improve inclusivity and reach individuals who might otherwise be excluded from research. Yet, the shift to remote designs introduces a distinct set of challenges: unequal internet access, variability in digital literacy, and privacy or data security concerns.6,7 These issues may disproportionately affect certain demographic groups, potentially reinforcing existing health disparities.

Unlike traditional in-person trials, where decades of refinement have yielded standardized workflows for recruitment,9 enrollment, and retention, methods for fully remote research remain underdeveloped. Examination of fraudulent behavior by potential participants in digital health research, with potential participants misrepresenting themselves to receive financial incentives has been examined.10–15 Yet, critical uncertainties persist regarding optimal approaches to facilitate trial participation, including engaging participants, structuring enrollment for study visit attendance, ensuring timely completion of study visits, and sustaining retention when all study contact occurs remotely.16

Research has been conducted on no-show rates in clinical care, generating robust evidence on factors influencing appointment attendance and trial participation.17–20 One study found that patients with one or more unmet social needs (eg, housing or food insecurity, healthcare costs, legal issues, safety concerns) had a significantly higher no-show rate (31.5%) when compared to those without unmet social needs (26.3% no-show rate).17 Another found that preferred language and insurance type were associated with appointment no-shows.18 In this study, Spanish speakers had a 14.2% no-show rate and English-speakers had a much lower no-show rate (7.8%), and no-show rates were higher for those without Medicare insurance (8.5%) than those with Medicare (5.1%) and higher for those with Medicaid (13.6%) compared to those without Medicaid (7.5%). Further, longer lead times between scheduling and the appointment date have been associated with higher no-show rates, with one study finding visits scheduled within 0 to 30 days had a 23% no-show rate and those scheduled with 30 days or more had a 47% no-show rate.19 Additionally, a telephone survey found that 37.6% of patients surveyed about missing their appointments reported that they did not know they had a scheduled appointment.20 An additional 16.1% cited personal issues as the reason for missing their appointment.20 Interventions such as automated reminder systems21 and patient navigation programs have been used to improve attendance in clinical settings.22 However, far less is known about whether these same insights apply in digital research studies, where the absence of physical travel and in-person interactions could alter both the barriers and facilitators to participation. Specifically, little is known about scheduling patterns, and participant follow-through in fully remote studies. There is scant evidence on the operational and behavioral factors influencing whether individuals who pass eligibility screening attend their digital research appointments. Without this data, remote studies risk inefficiencies in recruitment, inflated costs, and missed opportunities to engage participants.

The American Women Assessing Risk Epidemiologically (AWARE) study, which aims to use innovative electronic methods to recruit and retain a large diverse national sample of women at higher risk for HIV seroconversion,23 offers a unique opportunity to address these knowledge gaps. The AWARE study was originally conceived of and implemented as a remote study. Using a fully remote design, AWARE recruited, enrolled, and retained a nationwide cohort of 1,673 women from all 50 U.S. states, Puerto Rico, the U.S. Virgin Islands, and Guam who were behaviorally vulnerable to HIV acquisition.23 Women who were interested in the AWARE study filled out an online survey linked to ads posted on social media to screen for eligibility. Eligible participants were contacted through text by research staff to schedule a time for their baseline visit. Participants who showed up to their baseline visit Zoom call were then consented by research staff and enrolled into the study. Then, participants completed a survey, finger-stick blood collection, and vaginal and rectal swabs off-camera. At the end of the visit, participants were instructed on how to send their blood, rectal, and vaginal swab samples back to the research lab.

Women account for approximately 18% of new HIV diagnoses in the U.S., with heterosexual contact accounting for the majority (83%) of transmissions,24 and harmful alcohol or drug use as major risk factors for HIV acquisition among women.25 Additional risk factors include sex work, interpersonal violence (IPV), and incarceration history.26,27 Eligibility criteria for AWARE were therefore designed to enroll women with these discrete risk factors. These same factors, along with related challenges such as mental health concerns, homelessness, poverty, and social isolation, may complicate engagement in research,28 particularly when participation occurs fully remotely.

Objective

Understanding the characteristics and circumstances that influence whether eligible women proceed from screening to scheduled appointments, and from scheduling to attendance, is essential to refining recruitment and retention strategies for digital health research and is one of the goals of this manuscript. By examining both operational variables, including time and date, and individual factors, including demographics and HIV risk factors, our goal was to generate evidence to inform best practices for designing, implementing, and sustaining equitable participation in digital health research. The purpose of this study was to examine whether digital research appointment no-show rates for the AWARE study were associated with (1) participant age, (2) time of day, day of the week, month of the year of the scheduled visit, (3) appointment confirmation and (4) HIV behavioral risk factors including drug and alcohol use, sex work, and history of incarceration, IPV, or sextually transmitted infections (STIs).

Materials and methods

Ethics

This study was approved by the Columbia University Institutional Review Board under protocol number AAAU2650.

Recruitment and consent

AWARE study cohort participants were English or Spanish-speaking, HIV-negative cisgender females over the age of 14 years old from the U.S. or its territories. To be eligible, participants also had to report condomless vaginal or anal sex within the prior 6 months and one or more behavioral risk factor including drug or alcohol use (within the prior 6 months), incarceration (within the prior 5 years), history of STIs, or a sexual partner with any of these reported behaviors, exchange of sex for commodities (within the prior 6 months), or interpersonal violence (IPV) (within the prior year). Further details are published elsewhere.23

To participate in the AWARE cohort, women first completed a screening form for eligibility via REDCap. The screening form was linked to online web ads posted through Meta (ie, Facebook and Instagram), as well as embedded within a QR code in printed flyers which were posted at Community Based Organizations throughout the US. Prior to filling out screening questions, participants were provided with a full description of longitudinal study procedures and had to select “yes” to indicate interest.

The study manager reviewed new screening data daily, removing duplicates and ineligible respondents from the contact list. Once eligibility was confirmed, study staff contacted the participant to confirm their interest in participating, collected their address via an online address survey, and scheduled their baseline visit.

During the AWARE study visit, participants self-collected vaginal and rectal swabs and finger-stick blood samples that were sent to a lab to be tested for chlamydia and gonorrhea (vaginal and rectal swab), trichomoniasis (vaginal swab), HIV and syphilis (blood sample). Thus, prior to their scheduled baseline appointment, all eligible and interested participants were sent a package that included swabs, a blood collection kit, a hot compress, printed instructions, and a return envelope and airbill to send the samples to our partnering lab. The package was sent through FedEx and took up to a week to arrive to the participant’s address, depending on where in the U.S. or territories the package was sent.

After participants expressed interest in scheduling a baseline visit to enroll into the study, research staff scheduled their baseline visits for at least 1 week later than the date of scheduling. This minimum 7-day lead time between scheduling and completing the research visit gave the research staff enough time to send participants the package they needed for the participants’ baseline visit and to avoid the cost of expedited shipping service. Once the staff gathered the participant’s availability, a text message with the appointment time (in the participant’s time zone), date, and Zoom link, code and Meeting ID were sent to the participant. Twenty-four hours prior to the scheduled research visit, a study team member sent the participant a text reminder to confirm their visit date and time. The text message read, “Hi this is the AWARE Study.” Your visit is scheduled for [date] and [time]. Does this appointment still work for you?

On the day of the scheduled visit, the staff member running the visit sat in the Zoom meeting until the participant entered the waiting room. If the participant did not show up to the appointment 5 minutes after the start time, the staff sent a text inquiring if the visit time still worked for the participant and to let them know they were waiting on the call. If the participant did not show up to the appointment 15 minutes after the start time, the staff informed the participant that they missed their appointment and that they would have to reschedule. Participants were allowed a total of 3 visit reschedules. If the participant did not enroll after 3 scheduled visits, they were informed that they could no longer participate in the study.

Data cleaning

We merged participant-level screening data from a REDCap database containing demographic and behavioral information, and data from our study team’s shared Outlook calendar. Outlook calendar data included visit time, date, whether it was confirmed, completed, canceled and/or rescheduled. REDCap data were exported on March 26, 2025, and cleaned by first converting all empty strings to “NA” across all variables. Records were then deduplicated by retaining the first non-missing value per field for each record ID. Categorical variables were created to capture the time of day, day of the week, and month for which appointments were scheduled. All appointment visit times were adjusted to reflect the local time of the participants. The final merged dataset includes all participant appointments, some of which are the rescheduled appointments following no-shows at the first appointments. We divided the final merged dataset into 2 subsets, one including only the first-scheduled baseline appointments (n = 1996) and the other including only the rescheduled appointments (n = 472).

Data analysis

We conducted chi-square tests to examine the relationship between patient attendance at baseline appointments and multi-category factors including participant profile (ie, age, risk factors for HIV seroconversion) and scheduling characteristics (ie, time of day, day of week, month of year of scheduled appointment). Given that appointment reminders are a common strategy to improve attendance, we also assessed its effect on attendance at both first scheduled baseline and rescheduled appointments. For each factor with multiple categories, we conducted a single chi-square test to assess whether attendance rates differed across all categories.

We then performed a multivariate logistic regression analysis to address potential confounding variables and to further characterize the association between appointment attendance and each factor. The regression model estimated the direction and magnitude of the association between individual categories of each factor and the likelihood of no-shows. We included the following covariates: scheduling characteristics, participant profile, partner behavior, appointment confirmation status, and appointment delay (for rescheduled visits).

No-show rates also vary significantly by time of day (P < .05). Considering the participant’s local time, those scheduled at 2:00 pm (45.7% no-show) or 4:00 pm (45.2% no-show) were most likely to miss their baseline appointments. Participants who were scheduled for an appointment at 5:00 pm or later (29.1% no-show) were least likely to miss their appointment.

The day that they were most likely to miss their visit (44.6% no-show) was Saturday and the day that people were least likely to miss their visit (30.2% no-show) was Friday.

Finally, no-show rates differ significantly across months of the year (P < .05). The highest no-show rate occurred in April (43.4%), whereas the lowest no-show rate was observed in March (27.7%) and June (25.2%).

Women who were eligible for the study because they had been physically hurt by a partner, friend, or family member (ie, “Within the last year, have you been hit, slapped, kicked, or otherwise physically hurt by a partner or someone important to you?”), forced to participate in sexual activities, or incarcerated for 24 hours or more were significantly (P < .05) more likely to miss their study visits than those who were not. Women who had exchanged sex for commodities or had male partners with a history of drinking were also more likely to miss their study visits (P < .1) than those who had not (see Table 2).

We did not find significant differences in no-show rates between women with and without male partners who were living with HIV, had a history of STIs or a history of drug abuse.

Effect of appointment delay and reminders on attendance

People who missed their baseline visits and had to schedule a second visit were the least likely to miss their second visit if it was scheduled for the same day (3.3% no-show). No-show rates increased substantially when the second visit was scheduled more than 7 days later (20.0% no-show) and reached their highest level (38.6% no-show) when scheduled 29 days or more after the first scheduled visit. Lastly, people who did not confirm their appointments with the staff 24 hours prior were far more likely to miss their appointments than those who did confirm (51.6% vs.19.0% no-show, P < .001). Visit confirmations were similarly predictive of attendance at rescheduled visits: 11.4% of the participants who confirmed did not attend their reschedule visits, whereas this percentage increased to 37.5% among those who did not confirm (P < .001).

Women aged 50 years or older demonstrated a significantly higher no-show rate compared to those between 20 and 29 years of age (P < .05). Similarly, participants with baseline appointments scheduled at 2:00 pm (P < .01) or 4:00 pm (P < .1) were significantly more likely to miss their appointments. The multivariate analysis also revealed that appointments scheduled at 8:00 am were associated with a significantly higher no-show probability (P < .05) after controlling for other factors.

Regarding the day of the week and appointment month, the multivariate analysis corroborates the Chi-square analysis results: appointments scheduled on Fridays (P < .05), or in March (P < .05) or June (P < .01), were associated with significantly lower no-show rates. Additionally, August appointments showed a significantly lower no-show probability (P < .05).

In terms of individual behaviors, women who reported alcohol dependence (P < .1), experiences of physical violence (P < .01), forced sexual activity (P < .1), or incarceration for 24 hours or more (P < .1) were significantly more likely to miss appointments compared to those without such experiences. Appointment confirmation was associated with a substantially higher likelihood of attendance at both baseline and rescheduled visits compared with no confirmation (P < .01).

Finally, the multivariate analysis confirmed the Chi-square finding that longer appointment delays (>1 day) were associated with a significantly higher no-show rate for rescheduled appointments (P < .05).

Discussion

The objective of this study was to identify individual- and study-level factors associated with appointment attendance in a fully remote, national digital health trial of women at risk for HIV (the AWARE study). We found that more than one-third of women missed their first scheduled baseline appointment, though no-show rates decreased for rescheduled visits. Attendance was strongly associated with both operational factors—such as time of day, day of week, and month of the year—and participant-level characteristics, including age and selected HIV risk factors. Women who reported experiences of interpersonal violence, forced sex, or incarceration were significantly more likely to miss appointments. Conversely, simple operational strategies, such as confirming visits 24 hours in advance, were highly effective in improving attendance. These findings provide critical insight into how digital health research can optimize recruitment and retention in fully remote study designs.

This study is among the first to examine appointment attendance in a fully remote, national digital health study. While no-show rates have been extensively documented in clinical settings,29 little is known about these dynamics in digital research contexts. The goal of this study was to better understand both the individual level factors (eg, age, HIV risk factors) and the operational factors (eg, time of day, day of the week, month of year) that were associated with attendance at study visits. Our findings highlight both parallels and divergences from clinical literature, offering important implications for the design and implementation of remote trials. There is strong evidence that participants who failed to attend scheduled appointments often faced structural and operational barriers. In healthcare settings, such barriers include transportation, work conflicts, and competing caregiving responsibilities.30 Work and caregiving conflicts likely contributed to missed visits, though barriers unique to digital research, such as limited technology access, may also have played a role. On the other hand, barriers to fully remote research may include limited technology access, unstable internet connections, and unfamiliarity with virtual platforms.31,32 This aligns with our findings that women aged 50 years or older had the highest no-show rates, possibly because older participants were less comfortable with technology.33,34 However, these behaviors cannot be fully confirmed because participants who missed their appointments did not provide a reason for their absence and many did not ultimately enroll in our research study.

Importantly, higher no-show rates at 8:00 am and 2:00 pm may be partially explained by competing caregiving responsibilities. These appointment times coincide with typical school drop-off and pick-up periods, which may present logistical challenges for women with childcare obligations. Because caregiving data were only collected for participants who enrolled in the study, this variable was not available for the full analytical sample and could not be examined; future studies should consider incorporating these measures to better understand appointment attendance patterns.

Findings from this study suggest that women who have experienced IPV (ie, physically hurt or forced to have sex by a partner) or incarceration are less likely to attend their remote study visit which aligns with findings from research in clinical settings.35–37 A shift to digital platforms during the COVID-19 pandemic, in particular, introduced the risk of violence from a perpetrator when having to conduct a clinical visit online or over the telephone.37 Additionally, a systematic review identified ongoing challenges among women when transitioning from incarceration to the community such as age and chronic disease, disrupted medical care, health literacy and perceived discrimination which in turn contributed to missed appointments.36

Our results also underscore the need for tailored reminders and engagement strategies in remote research. The strong association between confirmation of study visits and attendance mirrors patterns in clinical care.38 Interventions such as text message reminders and telephone outreach, have shown measurable improvements in appointment adherence.38 Translating these approaches to digital trials may require adaptation, such as integrating reminders with online consent portals, embedding them into mobile study apps, or using multiple reminder channels to accommodate participant preferences. Future analyses should compare women who enrolled versus those who did not to further assess potential selection bias.

It is also important to acknowledge characteristics of the AWARE study population that may limit the generalizability of our findings. By design, eligibility criteria focused on women who were behaviorally vulnerable to HIV, including those reporting recent condomless sex and additional risk factors such as substance use, incarceration, sex exchange, or experiences of interpersonal violence. As a result, the sample reflects a population with high levels of structural and social vulnerability that may not mirror the broader population of women or participants in other types of digital health studies. While this focus strengthens the relevance of our findings for HIV prevention research, it also means that patterns of attendance—and barriers such as competing priorities, housing instability, or safety concerns—may differ in studies enrolling lower-risk or more socioeconomically advantaged populations. In addition, recruitment strategies relied on online advertisements and community-based outreach, which may have excluded women with limited internet access or those not engaged with social service organizations. These characteristics should be considered when interpreting our results and when adapting these findings to other digital research contexts.

There is limited published research examining appointment attendance patterns by time of day among women participating in sexual health or HIV-related research studies. Prior literature on missed appointments has largely focused on patient-level factors such as socioeconomic barriers, transportation challenges, and competing responsibilities, rather than the influence of appointment scheduling patterns. Our findings suggest that appointment timing itself may represent an important and understudied structural factor influencing research visit attendance. Understanding how specific timepoints may align with participants’ daily responsibilities could help inform scheduling strategies that reduce missed visits and improve retention in clinical research. Implications of missed virtual appointments are twofold. First, they increase the cost and duration of recruitment, threatening the feasibility of large-scale digital trials. Second, they may bias samples if missed study visits disproportionately occur among participants from certain sub-groups.39 Without deliberate design to minimize digital attrition, remote studies may inadvertently reinforce existing inequities in research representation.40

Future research should extend beyond attendance to examine upstream processes such as who completes initial screening and who is never offered an appointment. Qualitative research could elucidate the participant experience of digital scheduling and attendance, identifying both structural and motivational factors. Additionally, comparative studies between remote, hybrid and in person research studies could help determine whether certain populations are better served by mixed approaches that combine virtual and in-person touchpoints.

Conclusion

Overall, our findings suggest that missed appointments in digital health research are not simply a scaled-down version of clinical no-shows. They reflect a distinct set of factors which should be considered in the implementation of digital health research. Central to the findings of this study is that the timing of appointment—the time of day, day of the week, and month—has a strong influence on no-show rates. This information is foundational to the study of future digital health research studies and has implications for reducing overall cost of running studies. Patterns observed here have both policy and practice implications for digital health research. Findings from this study have the potential to extend beyond digital research into broader health service delivery. Policies that incentivize flexible scheduling and invest in broadband expansion could improve research participation. Incorporating resources for digital access support (eg, loaner devices, tech literacy assistance) into grant budgets may help mitigate attendance disparities. Embedding these approaches in both research and service delivery models can enhance operational efficiency, reduce attrition, and ensure benefits of leveraging digital health platforms for research. As health services researchers increasingly leverage remote study designs, understanding and addressing these challenges will be essential to ensuring both the efficiency and inclusivity of digital health research.
