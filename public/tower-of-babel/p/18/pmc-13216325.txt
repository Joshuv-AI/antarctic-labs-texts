Data-driven wear performance optimization of WAAM aluminum 5356 via intuitionistic fuzzy decision modeling
Authors: S. P. Sundar Singh Sivam, Adebayo Samuel Olabisi, Stalin Kesavan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aims to enhance the tribological performance of Wire Arc Additive Manufacturing (WAAM)-fabricated Aluminum 5356 by optimizing key operating parameters using an Intuitionistic Fuzzy-based Multi-Criteria Decision-Making (MCDM) framework. The objective was to minimize wear-related degradation and frictional losses under severe loading conditions relevant to high-performance engineering applications. Tribological experiments were conducted by varying load (400–800 N), rotational speed (2–6 rpm), and sliding distance (20–60 m) under different lubrication regimes. The measured responses included wear rate, coefficient of friction, mass loss, frictional force, wear scar diameter, and temperature rise. ANOVA and R2 analysis were employed to validate the experimental trends, while Intuitionistic Fuzzy TOPSIS (IF-TOPSIS) was used to rank the alternatives and identify the optimum condition; ranking robustness was further examined through sensitivity analysis. The results revealed that the most favourable tribological condition was obtained at a load of 400 N, speed of 6 rpm, and sliding distance of 20 m under solid lubrication, yielding the lowest wear rate (0.0535 mm/h) and coefficient of friction (0.3614). Compared with grease lubrication under the same operating parameters, the optimized condition reduced wear rate by 6.14%, coefficient of friction by 4.87%, wear scar diameter by 14.01%, and frictional force by 7.06%, confirming the superior protective action of the solid lubricant film. The findings demonstrate that the proposed IF-TOPSIS framework provides a robust and systematic route for tribological optimization of WAAM-fabricated Aluminum alloys. However, the conclusions are specific to WAAM-fabricated Aluminum 5356 under controlled laboratory conditions and should not be generalized to other alloy systems without further validation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46152-2.

Introduction

Wire Arc Additive Manufacturing (WAAM) has emerged as an advanced metal deposition process capable of producing large, near-net-shape components with superior material utilization and reduced lead time. The process employs an electric arc as a heat source to deposit successive layers of metal wire, offering distinct advantages such as high deposition rates, cost-effectiveness, and compatibility with conventional welding systems1. Over the past decade, WAAM has been increasingly adopted for structural applications in aerospace, marine, and automotive sectors due to its potential to replace conventional subtractive processes with sustainable manufacturing solutions2,3. Nevertheless, the quality, consistency, and mechanical integrity of WAAM-fabricated components depend significantly on the selection and optimization of process parameters, which govern heat input, solidification rate, and microstructural evolution4,5.

Several studies have explored the metallurgical and mechanical aspects of WAAM components across different materials and deposition strategies. Inconel 718 parts produced by WAAM showed that optimized arc energy significantly improved microhardness and density1. A three-dimensional numerical model of Cold Metal Transfer (CMT)-based WAAM demonstrated the influence of current and wire feed rate on layer uniformity2. Coupled electromagnetic and thermo-fluidic models were established to analyse droplet transfer mechanisms and temperature gradients during CMT-WAAM3. Studies on bead formation and layer height control in Aluminum alloys revealed that lower travel speed and moderate current yielded superior surface finish and reduced porosity4,5. The microstructural refinement of Al-Mg alloys produced via WAAM showed that controlled interpass temperatures enhanced the formation of equiaxed grains6. Heat flow and residual stress analysis in Inconel 625 builds highlighted the role of interlayer cooling in minimizing distortion7. Optimization of Aluminum wire feed speed and torch angle improved mechanical strength and reduced anisotropy8. These investigations collectively emphasize that WAAM performance is strongly governed by the dynamic balance between thermal input, deposition rate, and material composition9,10.

Optimizing WAAM parameters has been a major focus for researchers aiming to achieve consistent quality and mechanical performance. Regression-based models have been developed to link voltage, current, and travel speed with bead geometry and hardness11. Multi-response optimization approaches such as Taguchi–Grey relational analysis have been used to improve tensile strength and hardness in Aluminum WAAM components12. The desirability function method has also been implemented for optimizing deposition rate and surface roughness, achieving improved bead uniformity13. Correlations between cooling rate and mechanical anisotropy revealed that reduced interpass dwell time leads to stronger interlayer bonding14. Investigations on the influence of process parameters on layer morphology and porosity distribution concluded that balanced arc stability and wire feed rate are critical for minimizing defects15,16. Despite these advances, the majority of optimization frameworks have been limited to mechanical or geometrical responses, with comparatively less attention on tribological behaviour, which governs the functional performance of WAAM components under service conditions17–19.

Understanding the wear and frictional behaviour of WAAM-deposited materials is essential for applications where surface durability is critical. Studies on the tribological properties of WAAM-produced NiTi alloys found that optimized process parameters reduced wear rate through fine-grained microstructures20. Investigations on the sliding wear resistance of Inconel 625 fabricated by WAAM observed that lubrication conditions and heat input significantly affected wear mechanisms21. The friction and wear characteristics of aluminum composites indicated that higher load and sliding distance increased material removal but could be mitigated by solid lubrication22. It was further reported that wear rate and temperature rise are strongly correlated with microstructural density and surface hardness in additive-manufactured parts23. However, the literature still lacks systematic studies linking tribological performance with parameter optimization in WAAM-fabricated aluminum alloys, particularly under different lubrication regimes24,25. This gap necessitates an integrated approach combining experimental analysis with decision-making tools to evaluate multiple conflicting responses such as wear rate, coefficient of friction, and temperature rise simultaneously26–28.

Multi-Criteria Decision-Making (MCDM) methods have gained importance for handling complex optimization problems involving multiple conflicting objectives. Conventional models such as TOPSIS, VIKOR, ELECTRE, and GRA have been successfully applied in materials and manufacturing research29–31. However, these classical models often assume deterministic or fuzzy-based weights that fail to represent uncertainty in experimental data. The Intuitionistic Fuzzy (IF) extension addresses this limitation by incorporating degrees of membership, non-membership, and hesitation, thereby improving decision accuracy32,33. The IF-TOPSIS approach has been applied to evaluate machining parameters, demonstrating superior ranking stability under uncertain conditions34. IF-GRA has been utilized to optimize surface finish and tool wear35, while IF-VIKOR has been integrated for multi-objective optimization in welding processes36. Recent works have extended IF-based methods to manufacturing environments, confirming their robustness through sensitivity and comparative analysis37,38. In the context of additive manufacturing, IF-MCDM methods have been rarely applied to tribological performance evaluation. Therefore, integrating Intuitionistic Fuzzy Decision-Making with tribological experimentation offers a novel pathway for comprehensive optimization in WAAM systems39–43.

From the extensive literature reviewed, it is evident that WAAM has achieved significant progress in structural and mechanical optimization; however, its tribological behaviour under realistic operational conditions remains inadequately addressed. Previous studies have explored parameter effects on geometry, porosity, and mechanical strength but have seldom incorporated surface performance responses in a systematic optimization framework17,19,2544. examined the optimization of Inconel 690 machining through NSGA-II and TLBO, with emphasis on tribology-governed responses such as tool wear and surface integrity; future research should strengthen the findings through wider experimental validation across broader cutting domains45. investigated the machining of Hastelloy X under cryogenic and MQL environments by varying key cutting parameters, demonstrating that cryogenic assistance markedly improved tribological and techno-economic performance through reduced wear, surface roughness, energy consumption, and cost; future work may focus on hybrid cooling strategies46. Employed AI-based wear monitoring during Hastelloy C276 machining and identified cutting speed as a dominant factor affecting flank wear progression; future studies should address real-time adaptive control.

Recent studies on additively manufactured metallic alloys have shown that tribological performance is strongly influenced by process-induced microstructural heterogeneity, surface condition, porosity, and lubrication regime. In WAAM-fabricated aluminum alloys in particular, layer-wise solidification, thermal cycling, and interlayer bonding can significantly affect wear resistance, frictional response, and temperature evolution during sliding contact. Although several studies have examined mechanical behaviour and process optimization in WAAM materials, comparatively limited attention has been devoted to systematic multi-response tribological optimization of WAAM-fabricated aluminum alloys under varying operating conditions and lubrication environments. Although the present study provides a structured optimization framework for WAAM-fabricated Aluminum 5356, the tribological behaviour of other aluminum alloys may differ significantly due to variations in alloy composition, microstructural evolution, and defect sensitivity during deposition. Therefore, the current work is positioned as an alloy-specific investigation that can serve as a basis for future comparative studies across different WAAM material systems. Moreover, while fuzzy and hybrid optimization models have been widely adopted for machining and welding, their application to WAAM tribology—particularly for aluminum alloys—remains underexplored. This study addresses this gap by integrating experimental tribological testing with an Intuitionistic Fuzzy-Based Multi-Criteria Decision-Making (IF-MCDM) approach to optimize the process parameters of WAAM-fabricated Aluminum 5356. The methodology involves varying load, speed, and sliding distance under different lubrication conditions (oil, grease, and solid lubricant) and evaluating tribological responses such as wear rate, coefficient of friction, material loss, frictional force, wear scar diameter, and temperature rise. Statistical analyses, including ANOVA and R2 evaluation, validate the influence and predictive capability of the experimental model. Furthermore, sensitivity analysis under varying weight conditions and model comparisons (IF-MCDA) confirm the robustness of the rankings. The results are expected to contribute a validated decision-making framework for optimizing the tribological performance of WAAM components, offering valuable insights for high-performance sectors such as aerospace, marine, and automotive engineering. Moreover, limited comparative information is available on how different lubrication combinations influence the tribological optimization of WAAM-fabricated aluminum alloys, which highlights the need for broader lubrication-based investigations in future work.

Materials and methods

Materials

The study utilized Aluminum 5356, a widely adopted alloy in welding applications due to its excellent mechanical properties, including high strength, corrosion resistance, and good weldability. Commonly used in the aerospace, automotive, and marine industries, the alloy comprises 4.5–5.5 wt% Magnesium (Mg), 0.05–0.20 wt% Manganese (Mn), 0.05–0.20 wt% Chromium (Cr), with the balance being Aluminum (Al). The components were fabricated using Wire Arc Additive Manufacturing (WAAM), a welding-based additive manufacturing method that offers high deposition rates and cost-effective production of metal components. This technique is particularly suitable for producing durable, lightweight parts with minimal material waste.

Experimental setup and component fabrication using WAAM

The tribological samples for this study were fabricated using Wire Arc Additive Manufacturing (WAAM) on an Aluminum 5356 substrate plate. The cylindrical samples, with a diameter of 50 mm and a thickness of 10 mm, were EDM cut from the WAAM components to the standard dimensions required for tribological testing. The fabrication process involved the deposition of layers onto the base plate, with each layer being carefully deposited using optimized process parameters in Fig. 1a. The wire feed rate was set at 4.9 m/min, with a welding speed of 4.2 m/min, an average current of 97 A, and a voltage of 19 V. The shielding gas used was pure argon, ensuring effective shielding throughout the process. The components were fabricated with a Kemppi double pulse X8 MIG Welder integrated with a Yasaka 6-axis robotic arm. The welding process was controlled by Wise Fusion software, which helped achieve optimal short-circuit characteristics during pulsed MIG welding. In addition, Weld Eye Welding Management Software was employed to maintain consistent shielding gas flow rates and ensure optimal welding conditions. The filler metal used was a 1.2 mm diameter Al5356 wire, which was melted by the X8 MIG Welder and deposited onto the base metal in a controlled manner.

The base plate, made of pure aluminum with dimensions 125 mm × 100 mm × 6 mm, served as the foundation for the layer deposition. A total of 80 passes were performed to build up the component, resulting in a square wall structure with dimensions 90 mm × 70 mm × 8 mm. To prevent distortion, cracking, or other defects, inter-pass cooling was employed, with a 1-minute cooling time between layers to ensure that the sample reached near room temperature, preventing negative effects on the microstructure and mechanical properties. The inferred cooling rate was approximately 11 °C/s, and the welding process was monitored using a thermal imaging camera to adjust the cooling rate as necessary. Figure 1a shows the WAAM setup, including key components such as the wire feeder, electrode holder, regulator, argon cylinder, and the CMT welding power source used to fabricate the components. The deposition torch tip was positioned 15 mm from the workpiece, and the contact angle was maintained at 90° for all welding passes. These parameters were selected based on their influence on the mechanical properties and the overall quality of the fabricated components. The CMT machine, known for its versatility and intelligent communication capabilities, supported the welding process by facilitating seamless coordination between power sources and torches, in line with the demands of modern Industry 4.0 manufacturing.

Tribological testing

Tribological testing was conducted using a tribometer in accordance with the ASTM G99 standard. The wear disc had a diameter of 100 mm and a thickness ranging from 6 to 8 mm, while the pin diameter varied between 4 and 8 mm in 2 mm steps, with a length of 20–30 mm. The frictional force capacity was 100 N, and the wear range extended from 0 to 2000 microns. Several tribological performance responses were measured, including Wear Rate (WR), Coefficient of Friction (COF), Material Loss (ML), Frictional Force (FL), Wear Scar Diameter (WSD), and Temperature Rise (TR). WR was determined by the weight loss method to quantify material removal, while COF was continuously measured during the sliding process. ML was assessed by the weight change of the sample post-test, and FL was measured using a force sensor during sliding contact. Post-test, WSD was evaluated using a scanning electron microscope (SEM), and TR was monitored in real-time using an infrared thermal camera to observe frictional heating effects.

The present experiments were conducted under predefined operating conditions without real-time monitoring or adaptive feedback control, in order to isolate the effects of the selected tribological parameters within a controlled laboratory framework. The selected ranges of load, speed, and sliding distance were chosen to provide a controlled and statistically balanced experimental window for evaluating the tribological behaviour of WAAM-fabricated Aluminum 5356; however, these ranges do not cover the full operational spectrum that may be encountered in broader engineering applications. The selected ranges of load (400–800 N), rotational speed (2–6 rpm), and sliding distance (20–60 m) were chosen to provide a practically relevant and statistically balanced experimental domain for evaluating the tribological behavior of WAAM-fabricated Aluminum 5356. These levels represent low, intermediate, and high operating conditions within the intended testing window, allowing systematic assessment of wear and friction response under progressively increasing mechanical and sliding severity. The ranges were selected based on experimental feasibility, the need to produce measurable response variation without causing unstable test conditions, and their relevance to tribological contact situations encountered in engineering applications. In addition, the chosen parameter window was considered appropriate for structured optimization using the adopted experimental design framework. The experimental plan was developed using Response Surface Methodology (RSM), resulting in 15 structured runs designed to efficiently estimate the relevant response behavior within the selected factor space. Such a design enables statistically balanced assessment of the main and interaction effects while maintaining experimental economy. The experimental conditions included three levels of process parameters: load (400 N, 600 N, 800 N), speed (2 rpm, 4 rpm, 6 rpm), sliding distance (20 m, 40 m, 60 m), and lubrication type (oil, grease, solid lubricant). These parameters were selected to simulate industrial conditions involving high loads, rotational speeds, and sliding distances. The lubrication conditions considered in this study, namely oil, grease, and solid lubricant, were selected to represent commonly applied tribological regimes under controlled laboratory conditions; however, they do not encompass the full range of lubrication possibilities encountered in practical applications. Although tribological performance is a critical functional requirement, the long-term applicability of WAAM-fabricated aluminum alloys also depends on additional properties such as fatigue resistance and corrosion behavior, which remain insufficiently integrated into current optimization-based assessments.

The selected tribological responses provided a comprehensive evaluation of material behavior under frictional contact. WR and ML quantify material removal, with WR indicating wear resistance and ML reflecting mass-based degradation. COF and FL describe the resistance to sliding and mechanical interaction at the contact interface, impacting energy efficiency and heat generation. WSD visually indicates wear severity, while TR highlights the effects of frictional heating on material stability and performance.

Additionally, mechanical and microstructural properties were characterized using standardized techniques. Ultimate Tensile Strength (UTS) and Total Elongation (TE) were determined through transverse orientation tensile testing on a Tinius Olsen H50KS machine (Japan) at 2.5 mm/min, per ASTM E8 standards. Microhardness (MH) measurements were performed using a Struers Duramin-40 Micro Vickers Hardness tester (USA) with a 500 g load and a 10 s dwell time, following ASTM E384 standards. Compressive Residual Stresses (CRS) were evaluated using an X-ray diffractometer (XRD) by BRUKER USA D8 Advance, according to ASTM E2860 standards. Differential Scanning Calorimetry (DSC) was conducted with a PerkinElmer Paris Diamond DSC instrument at SRM IST, heating samples at 5 °C/min in nitrogen up to 550 °C to determine stored energy (J/g), in compliance with ASTM D3418 standards. These tests offer a comprehensive understanding of the material’s tribological performance, mechanical properties, and microstructural integrity. All tribological experiments were conducted under controlled laboratory conditions to ensure repeatability and to isolate the individual and interactive effects of the selected process parameters; however, these conditions do not fully reproduce the complexity of real service environments.

Optimization methodology

The tribological evaluation in the present study was performed on WAAM-fabricated Aluminum 5356 in the as-fabricated condition, without incorporating additional post-processing or surface treatment operations, so that the intrinsic response of the deposited material could be examined under controlled conditions. The Intuitionistic Fuzzy TOPSIS (IF-TOPSIS) method was applied to optimize the tribological behavior of the samples. The approach involves the following steps:

Data collection

Tribological responses, including wear rate (WR), coefficient of friction (COF), and temperature rise (TR), were systematically recorded for each set of experimental conditions. The experimental design was based on a response surface methodology with a D-optimal design subtype and randomized point exchange, encompassing a total of 15 experimental runs. The design model employed was a reduced quadratic, which is well-suited for exploring the relationship between the selected factors and the tribological performance of the material. The key factors influencing the tribological responses were defined as follows: Load (A), Speed (B), and Sliding Distance (C). Load was varied between 400 N and 800 N, Speed ranged from 2 rpm to 6 rpm, and Sliding Distance varied from 20 m to 60 m. The samples used in the study were categorized as nominal (O) under Factor D, with each sample representing a unique tribological test condition. The experimental setup was designed with continuous, numeric factors for Load, Speed, and Sliding Distance, ensuring a range of conditions for robust data collection. Each factor was carefully varied to assess its individual and interactive effects on the tribological responses. The experimental data was collected following precise measurement protocols, allowing for the evaluation of material behavior under the defined testing parameters. This approach allows for the determination of the optimal conditions for minimizing wear and friction while maintaining thermal stability, providing insights into the efficiency and durability of the material under varying operational conditions.

Intuitionistic fuzzy -TOPSIS

Intuitionistic fuzzy logic builds on classical fuzzy logic by adding a third component to handle uncertainty better. In classical fuzzy logic, elements are assigned degrees of membership (belonging) and non-membership (not belonging) to fuzzy sets. However, it lacks a way to represent hesitation or uncertainty in these assignments. Intuitionistic fuzzy logic introduces the “degree of hesitation” or “indeterminacy,” which represents the uncertainty about an element’s membership. This approach uses three parameters: membership (µ), non-membership (ν), and hesitation (λ), allowing for a more complete description of uncertain information. This framework is particularly useful in decision-making, pattern recognition, and expert systems where data may be incomplete or unclear. The Intuitionistic Fuzzy TOPSIS (IF-TOPSIS) method further refines decision-making by considering both positive and negative ideal solutions, making it valuable for situations with uncertainty.

(a) Experimental design, (b) IF-TOPSIS methodology.

Figure 2 presents the complete workflow of the study and is divided into two complementary parts. Figure 2a illustrates the experimental design and statistical optimization procedure, while Fig. 2b presents the Intuitionistic Fuzzy TOPSIS (IF-TOPSIS) decision-making framework used for multi-response ranking and optimization.

In Fig. 2a, the workflow begins with the selection of process parameters and their operating ranges. In the present study, the principal tribological control variables were load, rotational speed, sliding distance, and lubrication condition. After identifying these factors, the face-centered design values are determined so that a statistically structured experimental plan can be established. This is followed by response selection, where the relevant tribological outputs—such as wear rate, coefficient of friction, material loss, frictional force, wear scar diameter, and temperature rise—are defined according to the objectives of the study. Based on these inputs, the experimental matrix is generated and the corresponding tests are carried out, represented in the figure as conducting virtual experiments. The resulting data are then subjected to model validation and ANOVA, which provide the statistical basis for assessing factor significance, model adequacy, and response reliability. The ANOVA stage further includes checks such as the F-test, lack-of-fit analysis, adjusted and predicted R2 values, and adequate precision, all of which help confirm whether the model can represent the response behavior satisfactorily. After statistical confirmation, model equations are obtained and used to compare predicted and actual values. The validated models are then visualized through 2D and 3D surface plots, which help explain the influence of process variables and identify possible interactions among them. If the optimization objective is not achieved, the model is re-examined and the influencing factors are reassessed; otherwise, the study proceeds to optimization and finally to experimental confirmation, where the predicted optimum condition is verified through confirmation trials.

In Fig. 2b, the second stage of the methodology begins with the formulation of the decision matrix, in which all experimentally obtained response values are arranged in a structured tabular form. Since the selected tribological responses are expressed in different units and scales, the matrix is then normalized so that all criteria can be compared on a common basis. The next step is to determine the weights of the criteria, which represent the relative importance of each response in the multi-criteria decision-making process. After weighting, the normalized matrix is transformed into an intuitionistic fuzzy decision matrix, where each alternative is characterized using membership, non-membership, and hesitation information, allowing uncertainty and ambiguity in the evaluation process to be represented more effectively than in classical crisp decision models. Once the intuitionistic fuzzy matrix is established, the positive ideal solution (PIS) and negative ideal solution (NIS) are calculated. These represent the most desirable and least desirable decision states, respectively. The separation measures are then obtained to quantify how far each alternative lies from the PIS and NIS. On the basis of these distances, the relative closeness coefficient of each alternative is calculated. This coefficient serves as the final ranking index, where alternatives with higher closeness values are considered more preferable. Finally, the ranked alternatives are subjected to sensitivity analysis and supporting decision interpretation, which help assess whether the ranking remains stable under changes in criteria weights or under comparison with other decision models. Thus, Fig. 2b provides a complete visual summary of how the raw tribological response data are converted into a robust ranking and decision-making outcome.

Taken together, Fig. 2 shows that the study does not rely solely on either statistical modeling or decision-making analysis in isolation. Instead, it combines a structured experimental–statistical framework with an intuitionistic fuzzy multi-criteria ranking procedure, thereby enabling both scientific interpretation of tribological behavior and robust identification of the optimum operating condition.

Pseudo code

Intuitionistic fuzzy sets

The Intuitionistic Fuzzy Set (IFS) expands the conventional fuzzy set. It was implemented to address ambiguous scenarios regarding the extent to which elements belong to a set and the extent to which they do not belong. The IF-TOPSIS approach encompasses three crucial parameters in its study: (1) Degree of Membership (µ): This metric indicates the extent to which an element is part of a particular set, with values ranging from 0 (indicating no membership) to 1 (indicating complete membership). (2) Non-Membership Degree (v): This measures the extent to which an element does not belong to a set, ranging from 0 (no non-membership) to 1 (complete non-membership). (3) Degree of Hesitancy (λ): This parameter quantifies the level of uncertainty in determining the values of µ and ν, and it is computed as λ = 1 - µ - ν. It effectively represents the difference between being a member and not a member. To avoid duplication, it is crucial that the total of µ and ν, along with the level of hesitation, does not surpass 1. The IF-TOPSIS approach uses Intuitionistic Fuzzy Sets (IFS) to depict the options according to different criteria. The IFS expressions, incorporating µ and ν values, delineate the performance of each alternative for every criterion. The approach subsequently uses these IFS representations to make determinations based on many criteria, evaluating alternatives by their closeness to the optimal and suboptimal solutions. The optimal solution reflects the highest values for each criterion, whereas the non-optimal solution shows the lowest values. Proximity metrics are utilized to ascertain the degree of closeness between each alternative and these solutions.

Sensitivity analysis

To verify the robustness and stability of the obtained ranking results, a sensitivity analysis was conducted by systematically varying the criteria weights in the decision matrix. The weights assigned to the evaluation criteria were perturbed by ± 10% and ± 20%, while maintaining the total normalized weight constant. This variation allowed assessment of how minor or moderate deviations in weight distribution influence the final ranking of alternatives. Furthermore, the consistency of the ranking pattern was evaluated using different multi-criteria decision-making (MCDM) models, namely Intuitionistic Fuzzy MARCOS (IF-MARCOS), Intuitionistic Fuzzy Grey Relational Analysis (IF-GRA), Intuitionistic Fuzzy VIKOR (IF-VIKOR), and Intuitionistic Fuzzy ELECTRE (IF-ELECTRE). By comparing the ranking orders generated from these models under different weighting scenarios, the sensitivity analysis confirmed whether the top-performing alternative remained stable across models and weight variations. The minimal deviation in ranking outcomes demonstrated the robustness and reliability of the proposed decision-making approach, ensuring that the optimized parameters are not sensitive to minor changes in the weighting structure or decision model selection.

ANOVA and R2 analysis

The Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) was employed to statistically evaluate the influence and significance of key process parameters—load, speed, and sliding distance—on the measured tribological responses such as wear rate, coefficient of friction, and frictional force. ANOVA helps to quantify the contribution of each parameter to the overall variation in the responses by comparing the mean square values and corresponding p-values. Parameters with a p-value less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant, indicating that changes in these factors have a meaningful impact on the tribological behavior. This analysis thus establishes the relative dominance of process parameters in governing system performance.

Additionally, the R2 (coefficient of determination) analysis was performed to assess the goodness-of-fit between the experimental data and the developed predictive models. A higher R2 value (closer to 1) indicates strong model accuracy and reliability, confirming that the model can effectively predict the experimental outcomes with minimal error. Together, the ANOVA and R2 analyses validate both the statistical significance of the process parameters and the predictive adequacy of the developed model for tribological performance evaluation.

Microstructural and mechanical testing

In this study, the microstructure of the WAAM-fabricated components was analyzed using Optical Microscopy (OM). The cross-sectional samples were polished and etched with Keller’s reagent as per ASTM E3, which helped reveal the grain structure and phases in the material. For more detailed analysis, we used Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) (Model: JEOL JSM-7000) to examine the wear scars and surface morphology. This high-resolution imaging, as per ASTM E1508, enabled us to identify any cracks, microstructural defects, and the material removal patterns caused by the tribological process. We also employed X-ray Diffraction (XRD) (Model: Bruker D8 Advance) to identify the crystalline phases present in the material. The XRD analysis was done between 20° and 80° (2θ) with a scan rate of 2°/min, following the guidelines of ASTM E1471, to check for any phase changes during the WAAM process and tribological testing. For microhardness testing, we used a Vickers microhardness tester (Model: Zwick Roell 3212), applying a load of 100 g for 10 s as per ASTM E384. Hardness measurements were taken at several points, including the wear track, to assess variations across the sample.

Validation of results

To validate the optimal conditions identified by the IF-TOPSIS model by best and worse conditions, additional experiments were performed. The results were compared with the predicted outcomes to confirm improvements in wear resistance, material loss, and temperature rise.

Results and discussion

Microstructure

Microstructure: (a) As-received wire, (b) fabricated structure.

Figure 3 compares the microstructures of a wire before and after being subjected to WAAM. In the as-received wire Fig. 3a, the microstructure is characterized by uniform, fine, and mostly equiaxed grains, indicating a homogeneous structure typically produced by processes like cold drawing or extrusion. This uniformity suggests the wire is ready for further manufacturing, such as welding or additive processes, with minimal defects or dislocation accumulation. In contrast, the fabricated structure Fig. 3b reveals a significantly different microstructure, typical of WAAM, where rapid melting and cooling during the additive manufacturing process lead to elongated, columnar grains aligned along the thermal gradient. This directional grain structure, along with distinct phases or precipitates formed during solidification, can influence mechanical properties like strength, hardness, and ductility. Additionally, residual stresses, porosity, or inclusions may be present due to the thermal cycling inherent in WAAM, which could degrade the material’s properties, especially under fatigue. The differences in these microstructures underscore the importance of considering the thermal effects of WAAM, as they can result in anisotropic material behavior that may require post-processing, like heat treatment, to optimize properties for specific applications, particularly for structural integrity and durability.

Mechanical property

Fig. 4Mechanical Property: (a) As-received wire, (b) fabricated structure.

Mechanical Property: (a) As-received wire, (b) fabricated structure.

The mechanical properties of the as-received wire and WAAM-fabricated structure were compared to assess their performance under various loading conditions in Fig. 4a As-received wire, and Fig. 4b WAAM Fabricated Stucture. The yield strength of the WAAM-fabricated structure was significantly higher than that of the as-received wire, indicating improved resistance to plastic deformation. This increase can be attributed to the controlled deposition process during Wire Arc Additive Manufacturing (WAAM), which results in a more uniform microstructure and better bonding between layers. Similarly, the ultimate tensile strength (UTS) of the WAAM structure was also superior, reaching over 200 MPa, compared to the 150 MPa in the as-received wire. This improvement is due to the stronger inter-layer bonds and refined grain structure produced during the additive manufacturing process. However, the modulus of elasticity of the WAAM-fabricated structure was lower than that of the as-received wire, indicating a reduction in stiffness. This is likely due to the residual stresses and microstructural variations introduced during WAAM, which can reduce the material’s resistance to deformation. Additionally, the elongation at break of the WAAM structure was lower, indicating more brittle behavior compared to the as-received wire, which exhibited higher ductility. This reduced ductility in the WAAM components is a result of the intrinsic defects and residual stresses from the additive manufacturing process, making it more prone to brittle fracture. In summary, while the WAAM-fabricated components demonstrated improved strength, they exhibited lower ductility and stiffness, which are critical factors to consider depending on the intended application. The findings highlight the trade-off between strength and ductility in WAAM-produced materials, emphasizing the need for careful consideration when selecting materials for applications that require both high strength and flexibility.

Define the intuitionistic fuzzy decision matrix (IFDM)

Converting a normalized DM into an IFDM, representing elements as Intuitionistic Fuzzy Numbers (IFNs) consisting of membership and non-membership degrees. The procedure involved in converting the normalized decision matrix into an Intuitionistic Fuzzy Decision Matrix (IFDM) consisted of two main steps. First, the decision matrix was normalized, which involved scaling the values of each criterion between 0 and 1 to remove the influence of different measurement units. Then, each normalized value was transformed into an Intuitionistic Fuzzy Number (IFN) by calculating the membership degree (1-µ) as the normalized value itself and the non-membership degree (1-ν) as 1 − 1 − µ. A degree of hesitation (1-λ) of 0.5 was set for all elements to represent uncertainty or hesitation in the decision-making process. The resulting IFDM reflects the decision-makers’ uncertainty and hesitation regarding each criterion’s importance and provides a more nuanced representation of decision-making scenarios, allowing for more robust analyses and decision-making processes.

Consequently, WIFDM serves as a critical foundation for subsequent steps in the Intuitionistic Fuzzy Multi-Criteria Decision-Making (IF-MCDM) process, such as distance measurement from positive and negative ideal solutions and ranking determination.

To develop the Weighted Intuitionistic Fuzzy Decision Matrix (WIFDM), six critical tribological performance criteria—Wear Rate (WR), Coefficient of Friction (CoF), Material Loss (ML), Frictional Force (FF), Wear Scar Diameter (WSD), and Temperature Rise (TR)—were selected based on their relevance in evaluating the tribological behavior of WAAM-fabricated Aluminum 5356 components. Each criterion was assigned a specific weight according to its relative importance, as shown in Table 5 (WR = 0.20, CoF = 0.20, ML = 0.15, FF = 0.15, WSD = 0.15, TR = 0.15), ensuring that the sum of all weights equals one, thereby maintaining proportional significance in the decision model. The experimental results for each alternative were normalized and multiplied by the respective weights to derive the weighted normalized values, which were expressed as Intuitionistic Fuzzy Numbers (IFNs) encompassing membership (µ) and non-membership (ν) degrees. This approach captures the uncertainty and imprecision inherent in real-world tribological measurements by incorporating hesitation degrees (π = 1 – µ – ν). The resulting WIFDM provided a comprehensive overview of all 17 experimental runs, allowing a detailed comparison of alternatives across the selected criteria. Subsequently, the Positive Ideal Solution (PIS) and Negative Ideal Solution (NIS) were determined to establish benchmark reference points for ranking. The PIS corresponds to the most desirable performance values (maximum membership and minimum non-membership), while the NIS represents the least desirable conditions (minimum membership and maximum non-membership) in Table 5. The calculated PIS values were µ⁺ = {0.20, 0.20, 0.15, 0.15, 0.15, 0.15} and ν⁺ = {0, 0.0006, 0, 0, 0, 0}, whereas the NIS values were µ− = {0.1023, 0.1621, 0.086, 0.1011, 0.0764, 0.1137} and ν⁻ = {0.0977, 0.0379, 0.064, 0.0489, 0.0736, 0.0363} in Table 6. These reference solutions serve as ideal and least ideal benchmarks for evaluating the relative performance of each alternative in the Intuitionistic Fuzzy Multi-Criteria Decision-Making (IF-MCDM) process47–49. By integrating weighted fuzzy logic with experimental data, this approach provides a robust and realistic framework for assessing tribological performance, accounting for both the quantitative significance of each criterion and the inherent uncertainty of measurement data.

Optimized parameter and results interpretations

The optimization results revealed that the most favorable combination of parameters for superior tribological performance was obtained at a load of 400 N, speed of 6 rpm, and sliding distance of 20 m using a solid lubricant (SL). Under these conditions, the system exhibited the lowest wear rate (0.0535 mm/h) and coefficient of friction (0.3614), with moderate mass loss (3.6972 mg), frictional force (12.5 N), and wear scar diameter (0.3014 mm), while maintaining a stable temperature rise of 44.07 °C. The enhanced performance of the solid lubricant is attributed to the formation of a uniform and adherent tribo-film that minimizes metal-to-metal contact, reduces frictional heating, and improves load-carrying capacity. Hence, this parameter combination is considered optimized, ensuring improved wear resistance, lower friction, and overall better lubrication efficiency compared to the grease-lubricated condition.

The tribological performance of the two lubrication conditions—solid lubricant (SL) and grease (G)—was evaluated under identical operating parameters of 400 N load, 6 rpm speed, and 20 m sliding distance. Based on the ranking and performance parameters, the solid lubricant condition exhibited superior behavior compared to grease. The solid lubricant (SL) achieved the best rank owing to its lower wear rate (WR = 0.0535 mm/h) and coefficient of friction (COF = 0.3614), indicating effective formation of a lubricating film that minimized direct asperity contact between the sliding surfaces. The lower wear scar diameter (WSD = 0.3014 mm) further confirms reduced surface damage and better load-bearing capability. Although the mass loss (ML = 3.6972 mg) was slightly higher than that of grease, this variation is within acceptable experimental deviation and does not significantly impact overall wear resistance.

In contrast, the grease-lubricated (G) condition showed comparatively higher friction (COF = 0.3799) and wear rate (WR = 0.057 mm/h), suggesting inadequate film stability under the applied load and speed. The larger WSD (0.3505 mm) and higher frictional force (FF = 13.45 N) indicate increased asperity interactions and possibly thermal softening effects. Interestingly, the temperature rise (TR) for SL (44.07 °C) was marginally higher than for G (43.55 °C), which can be attributed to the solid lubricant’s better surface adherence that maintains frictional contact stability and dissipates heat more uniformly. Overall, the solid lubricant provided superior tribological performance due to the formation of a durable protective film, lower frictional losses, and improved wear resistance—hence classified as the best lubrication condition under the studied parameters.

The sensitivity analysis indicates that the effect of weight variation on the final ranking is not uniform across all alternatives. Alternatives that exhibit consistently favorable performance across several tribological responses remain relatively stable even when the criteria weights are altered, because their ranking is supported by a balanced multi-response profile rather than by dominance in a single criterion. In contrast, alternatives that perform strongly in only one or two responses but less favorably in others are more sensitive to weight changes, since their relative position depends more directly on the importance assigned to specific criteria.

Wear mechanism classification as per the alternatives

This study examines the wear mechanisms and performance indicators under various lubrication conditions, including oil, grease, and solid lubricant (SL). In high load and dry conditions, severe adhesive wear and oxidation occur, characterized by a high wear rate (WR) of 0.0979–0.1046 mm/h, a coefficient of friction (COF) greater than 0.42, material loss (ML) exceeding 5 mg, frictional force (FF) greater than 15 N, and temperature rise (TR) above 50 °C, indicating metal-to-metal contact and oxidation. In contrast, low/medium load with solid lubricant results in mild adhesive wear and boundary lubrication, with reduced WR of 0.0535–0.0679 mm/h, COF around 0.36, ML ranging from 3.3 to 3.8 mg, FF between 12 and 13 N, and TR under 45 °C, indicating effective lubrication and reduced metal contact. High load with SL shows slightly higher WR (around 0.072 mm/h) than low load SL, moderate COF of 0.367, ML of approximately 3.8 mg, and TR around 41 °C, suggesting partial lubricant breakdown. Grease lubrication demonstrates moderate WR of 0.072–0.088 mm/h, COF between 0.396 and 0.414, ML from 4.2 to 4.8 mg, FF around 13–15.6 N, and TR around 45 °C, suggesting that grease reduces adhesion and promotes sliding. Medium load in dry conditions results in moderate adhesive wear and oxidation, with WR around 0.0942 mm/h, COF around 0.4235, ML greater than 5.6 mg, FF around 15.5 N, and TR above 52 °C. Overall, solid lubricant and grease lubrication offer the best performance by reducing wear and friction, particularly under low and medium loads, while grease lubrication effectively reduces wear through reduced adhesion. These findings emphasize the role of lubrication in controlling wear and highlight the potential for optimizing solid lubricant and grease for improved performance in high-performance applications.

Severe Wear Zone (Pink): Situated in the upper-right section of the map, this zone occurs at higher sliding speeds (above 3 m/s) and higher normal loads (above 600 N). Conclusion

This study demonstrated that the tribological performance of WAAM-fabricated Aluminum 5356 can be effectively optimized through the integration of experimental investigation, statistical validation, and an Intuitionistic Fuzzy TOPSIS (IF-TOPSIS)-based multi-criteria decision-making framework. The results confirmed that load, rotational speed, sliding distance, and lubrication condition significantly affect the key tribological responses, namely wear rate, coefficient of friction, mass loss, frictional force, wear scar diameter, and temperature rise. Among the investigated conditions, the optimum performance was obtained at a load of 400 N, speed of 6 rpm, and sliding distance of 20 m under solid lubrication, yielding the lowest wear rate (0.0535 mm/h) and coefficient of friction (0.3614). Compared with grease lubrication under identical operating conditions, the solid-lubricant regime reduced wear rate by 6.14%, coefficient of friction by 4.87%, wear scar diameter by 14.01%, and frictional force by 7.06%, confirming the superior tribological effectiveness of the protective solid tribo-film. Scientifically, the study contributes a robust optimization framework that combines tribological experimentation, ANOVA/R2-based validation, and IF-MCDM ranking for multi-response decision-making in WAAM-produced alloys. The findings also indicate that the tribological behavior is influenced by WAAM-induced microstructural characteristics, including columnar grains, porosity, and residual stresses, which affect wear mechanisms and frictional stability. Practically, the identified optimum condition offers useful guidance for improving the durability and lubrication efficiency of lightweight components in engineering applications. However, the conclusions remain limited to WAAM-fabricated Aluminum 5356 under controlled laboratory conditions. Future work should extend the framework to other alloys, post-processed conditions, and machine-learning-assisted predictive wear analysis.
