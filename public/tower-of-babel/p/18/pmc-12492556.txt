Decoding the ubiquitin network: molecular mechanisms and therapeutic vulnerabilities for precision radio-sensitization in cancer
Authors: Fu-Ying Zhao, Li-Ye Shi, Lu-Jiao Yu, Si-Qi Wang, Chu-Yao Sun, Na Zhang, Hua-Qin Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRadiotherapy resistance remains a major clinical challenge, largely driven by tumors’ ability to dynamically adapt through complex molecular networks. Critically, the ubiquitin system has emerged as a critical regulator of this resistance. This review examines how the ubiquitin system orchestrates radiotherapy resistance through spatiotemporal control of DNA repair fidelity, metabolic reprogramming, and immune evasion. We explore how the ubiquitin code, defined by its chain topology diversity (such as K48-linked proteolysis versus K63-mediated signaling) and crosstalk with phosphorylation, SUMOylation, and acetylation, generates diverse resistance mechanisms. These mechanisms, however, also present vulnerabilities exploitable for radio-sensitization. Notably, monoubiquitylation of both histone and non-histone protein collaboratively modulates chromatin dynamics and DNA damage responses to maintain genome integrity during radiation. Furthermore, ubiquitination critically regulates caner metabolism, reprogramming processes such as ferroptosis susceptibility, hypoxia adaptation, and nutrient flux, thereby creating targetable vulnerabilities for radio-sensitization. While targeting key E3 ligases and deubiquitinases (DUBs) shows preclinical promise, clinical translation faces obstacles including functional redundancy, unintended on-target toxicity, and adaptive tumor responses. Distinct from other post-translational modifications (PTMs), the ubiquitin system offers unique clinical advantages: its dynamic reversibility, chain topology diversity, and recent breakthroughs in targeted degradation (e.g., PROTACs) enable precise disruption of radioresistance networks. By integrating these mechanistic insights with biomarker-guided therapeutic strategies, ubiquitin-targeting agents are emerging as fundamental components of next-generation radiotherapy protocols.Keywords: Ubiquitination modification, Radiotherapy resistance, DNA damage repair, Deubiquitinases (DUBs), Ferroptosis, Metabolic reprogramming, Targeted therapy

Introduction

Radiotherapy continues to serve as a foundational treatment for solid tumors, exerting its effects through direct DNA damage and indirect immune activation [1]. Despite its widespread application, radiation resistance remains a significant clinical challenge, frequently leading to local recurrence, metastasis, and stagnant survival rates across numerous cancer types [2, 3]. Tumors employ diverse strategies to evade radiation-induced damage, including rewiring DNA repair pathways, circumventing cell cycle checkpoints, inhibiting apoptosis, and fostering immunosuppressive microenvironments [4].

Mounting clinical evidence firmly establishes the ubiquitin system as a master regulator orchestrating radioresistance. This regulation occurs through its manipulation of DNA repair fidelity [5, 6], metabolic reprogramming [7–11], and promotion of immune evasion [12]. Recent technological advances further illuminate this complexity: single-cell transcriptomics has revealed profound intratumoral heterogeneity in the expression of ubiquitin ligases and deubiquitinases (DUBs), uncovering distinct therapy-resistance subpopulations [13]. CRISPR-based functional screens have concurrently identified novel radiosensitization targets, such as TRIM21, whose inhibition synergizes effectively with radiotherapy [12].

Critically, this deepening mechanistic understanding is now driving the development of targeted therapeutic strategies. Capitalizing on the druggable nature of the ubiquitin system, significant progress has been made, particularly with proteolysis-targeting chimeras (PROTACs), which demonstrate compelling radiosensitizing effects [14, 15]. The specificity of these agents is exemplified by EGFR-directed PROTACs that selectively degrade β-TrCP substrates in EGFR-dependent tumors (e.g., lung and head/neck squamous cell carcinomas), suppressing DNA repair while minimizing impact on normal tissues [16]. Furthermore, innovative radiation-responsive PROTAC platforms are emerging to overcome radioresistance. These include radiotherapy-triggered PROTAC (RT-PROTAC) prodrugs activated by tumor-localized X-rays to degrade BRD4/2, synergizing with radiotherapy in breast cancer models [17], and X-ray-responsive nanomicelles (e.g., diselenide-bridged RCNprotac) that selectively release PROTACs (such as MZ1 targeting BRD4) within irradiated tumors [18].

While the involvement of the ubiquitin system in DNA repair is well-documented [5, 19, 20], this review focuses on its dynamic reprogramming in therapy-resistant tumors and the emerging strategies designed to exploit these alterations for radiosensitization. We synthesize current understanding of how E3 ligases, DUBs, and ubiquitin chain plasticity generate actionable vulnerabilities and propose biomarker-guided combination approaches to enhance radiotherapy efficacy (Table 1).

Tissue-Specific roles of key ubiquitin enzymes in radioresistance and therapeutic vulnerabilities

Ubiquitin chain hierarchies in radiation resistance

Ubiquitin chain architectures, including K48-linked polyubiquitylation, K63-linked signaling scaffolds, and monoubiquitylation, constitute a sophisticated regulatory system governing tumor radioresistance through distinct spatial and functional mechanisms [5, 21–23]. K48-linked polyubiquitin chains primarily target proteins for proteasomal degradation, while K63 linkages facilitate the assembly of non-proteolytic signaling complexes [19]. Tumors exploit these canonical functions to orchestrate radioresistance, modulating DNA repair, metabolic reprograming and immune evasion (Fig. 1).

Ubiquitination cascade and functional outcomes of ubiquitin chain architectures. The enzymatic cascade involves ubiquitin (Ub) activation by E1 (ATP-dependent), E2-bound Ub carriage, and E3-mediated substrate conjugation. Substrates undergo mono-ubiquitination or polyubiquitination with distinct chain topologies (e.g., K48-linked or K63-linked), directing divergent cellular fates: K48 chains target substrates for proteasomal degradation, K63 chains enable signal transduction, and mono-ubiquitination facilitates epigenetic adaptation

K48 ubiquitination: proteostatic gatekeeper with contextual duality

The impact of K48-linked ubiquitination on radiation response are highly context-dependency. FBXW7 exemplifies this duality: in p53-wild type colorectal tumors, it promotes radioresistance by degrading p53 and inhibiting apoptosis [21]. Conversely, in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) with SOX9 overexpression, FBXW7 enhances radiosensitivity by destabilizing SOX9 and alleviating p21 repression [24]. This functional switch hinges on tumor-specific genetic backgrounds, as FBXW7 preferentially degrades substrates bearing phosphorylated degrons (e.g., p53-S33/37 phosphorylation facilitates recognition) [21]. Notably, while Zhu et al. did not explicitly report p53 status in their NSCLC models, the cell line used (NCI-H1299) is p53-null [24]. This implies that the observed FBXW7-mediated radiosensitization operates independently of p53, driven instead by the SOX9/CDKN1A axis [24]. These contrasting outcomes underscore the critical influence of tumor genetics (particularly p53 status) and signaling microenvironment (e.g., SOX9/CDKN1A in NSCLC) on FBXW7’s role in radiotherapy response.

This contextual duality extends to metabolic adaptation. SMURF2-mediated HIF1α degradation compromises hypoxic survival [25], while SOCS2/Elongin B/C-driven SLC7A11 destruction increases ferroptosis sensitivity in liver cancer [11]. Adding further complexity, TRIM21 utilizes K48 ubiquitination to degrade VDAC2 in nasopharyngeal carcinoma, suppressing cGAS/STING-mediated immune surveillance [12].

Tissue-specific enzyme activity presents another therapeutic challenge. USP14 stabilizes ALKBH5 to maintain glioblastoma stemness [26] but degrades IκBα to activate NF-κB in head/neck cancers [27]. Such opposing functions highlight the critical need for precision therapies that selectively disrupt detrimental ubiquitination events while preserving protective pathways (Fig. 2).

K48 Ubiquitination: Proteostatic Gatekeeper with Contextual Duality. K48-mediated polyubiquitination acts as a proteostatic gatekeeper with opposing roles in irradiated cells. While promoting tumor suppression through degradation of substrates like SOX9 and HIF1α, it also drives radioresistance in nasopharyngeal carcinoma by degrading pro-apoptotic factors (e.g., p53) and stabilizing NF-κB. Key regulators including E3 ligases (FBXW7, TRIM21, SMURF2), deubiquitinases (USP14, USP44), and tissue-specific enzymes, collectively influence radiotherapeutic outcomes

K63 ubiquitination: the architect of adaptive survival

Beyond structural roles, K63-linked ubiquitin chains directly orchestrate cell survival pathways. For instance, FBXW7 employs K63 chains to modify XRCC4, enhancing the accuracy of non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) repair [5]. Similarly, TRAF4 utilizes K63 modifications to activate the JNK/c-Jun pathway, driving overexpression of anti-apoptotic Bcl-xL in colorectal cancer and MCL-1 in oral cancers [28, 29]. Intriguingly, these chains can even repurpose tumor suppressors; TRAF6 modifies p53 with K63 linkages, converting it into a pro-survival mitochondrial factor [22]. K63 chains also integrate metabolic and immune regulation: TRIM26 stabilizes GPX4 via K63 ubiquitination to prevent ferroptosis in gliomas [10], while inhibiting USP14 leads to accumulation of K63-modified IRF3, triggering STING-dependent antitumor immunity [30]. This functional diversity establishes K63 networks as central hubs coordinating DNA repair suppression and immune modulation simultaneously (Fig. 3).

K63-linked ubiquitination orchestrates adaptive survival networks in irradiated cells. Radiation and genotoxic stimuli trigger K63-polyubiquitin-dependent signaling cascades that coordinate survival and death decisions. TRAF4-mediated K63 chains drive radioresistance by activating AKT/JNKs to stabilize anti-apoptotic proteins (MCL-1, Bcl-xL), while the p53-TRAF6-p300 axis regulates cell death by modulating apoptosis (via p21/Gadd45) and ferroptosis (via GPX4/TRIM26). Concurrently, K63-linked ubiquitination activates STING-IRF3 signaling to prime adaptive antitumor immunity (CD8⁺ T-cell responses)

Monoubiquitylation: guardian of chromatin and genome stability

Monoubiquitylation of both histones and non-histone proteins critically regulates radiation adaptation. For histones, UBE2T/RNF8-mediated H2AX monoubiquitylation accelerates damage detection in hepatocellular carcinoma [23], while RNF40-genearted H2Bub1 recruits the FACT complex (SUPT16H) to relax nucleosomes, a process reinforced by the linc-RA1/USP44 complex [31, 32]. For non-histone targets, FANCD2 monoubiquitylation specifically resolves carbon ion-induced DNA crosslinks [33, 34], and γ-tubulin monoubiquitylation maintains centrosome integrity, whose disruption by high-LET radiation triggers mitotic catastrophe [35]. Collectively, these functions position monoubiquitylation as a key coordinator of DNA repair and genomic integrity (Fig. 4).

Monoubiquitination coordinates chromatin remodeling, DNA repair, and genomic stability in radiation response. High-LET radiation initiates UBE2T/RNF8-mediated monoubiquitination of H2AX and FANCD2 to activate CHK1-dependent DNA repair and radioresistance at double-strand breaks. Subsequent recruitment of H2Bub1 facilitates chromatin relaxation for DSB repair while simultaneously engages USP44 to sustain genomic integrity. The USP44-linc-RA1-H2Bub1 axis further enforces genomic surveillance by preventing centrosome overduplication and balancing radioresistance with autophagy, with NBS1/BRCA1 and γ-tubulin reinforcing replication fidelity throughout the cascade

Ubiquitination orchestrates epigenetic remodeling, autophagy, and immune evasion

Ubiquitination critically reprograms epigenetic landscapes and immune responses post-irradiation. Histone ubiquitination directly regulates chromatin accessibility at DNA damage sites. For example, RNF40-mediated H2Bub1 recruits the FACT complex to relax nucleosome packing, facilitating homologous recombination repair (HRR) while simultaneously suppressing immunogenic cell death [32, 36]. Radiation-induced RNF168 activation, amplified by ZNF451-dependent SUMOylation, further ubiquitinates H2A/H2AX to open chromatin and recruit BRCA1-A complexes, enhancing DNA repair fidelity but potentially promoting radioresistance [37].

Ubiquitination also bridges autophagy and radiation adaptation. SUMOylated SH3GLB1 activates PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy, clearing radiation-damaged mitochondria to sustain tumor cell survival [38]. Conversely, destabilizing mitophagy regulators (e.g., via Parkin inhibition by dihydroartemisinin) sensitizes cancer cells to irradiation [39].

Crucially, ubiquitin signaling modulates tumor-immune crosstalk. TRIM21 suppresses antitumor immunity by promoting K48-linked degradation of VDAC2, inhibiting mitochondrial DNA release and cGAS/STING activation in nasopharyngeal carcinoma [12]. Conversely, blocking USP14 stabilizes K63-ubiquitinated IRF3, amplifying STING-dependent type I interferon responses and synergizing with radiotherapy to overcome immune evasion [30]. These immune-editing functions position ubiquitin enzymes as pivotal regulators of tumor microenvironments (“cold” and “hot”), suggesting combinatory strategies with immune checkpoint inhibitors.

Ubiquitin code plasticity: an adaptive mechanism

Radiation dynamically reprograms ubiquitin signaling by altering chain formation, creating vulnerabilities exploited by tumors. Cancer cells strategically manipulate K63-linked chains to stabilize DNA repair factors (e.g., BRCA1) while concurrently inhibiting K48-mediated degradation of survival proteins like GPX4 [5, 10]. This ubiquitin signaling rewiring manifests through distinct mechanisms, prominently including DNA repair manipulation and metabolic adaptation.

Within the DNA repair pathway, RNF126-mediated K27/K29 Polyubiquitin of MRE11 activates the ATM-CHK1 axis in TNBC, thereby promoting error-prone repair [40]. Structural studies further illuminating this process, demonstrating that radiation-induced ATM phosphorylation induces conformational changes in RNF168. This facilitates K63-chain assembly at DNA damage sites, ultimately modulating repair fidelity [41].

In addition, ubiquitin rewiring also underpins metabolic adaptation to radition stress. For instance, TRIM26 stabilizes GPX4 via K63 chains to suppress ferroptosis in gliomas [10], whereas lung cancer utilizes β-TrCP-mediated K48 ubiquitination to degrade radioprotective proteins like LZTS3 [16].

These opposing mechanisms reveal that chain-editing enzymes spatially regulate therapeutic vulnerability. Critically, DUBs fine-tune this balance: OTUB1 stabilizes CHK1 by removing K48 chains in lung cancer, prolonging cell cycle arrest [42], while USP7 counteracts ubiquitination of DNA-PKcs in HPV+ tumors to maintain repair competence [43].

Recent technological advances map these adaptations: Single-cell transcriptomics identifies radiation-induced K63 ubiquitination signatures in USP9X-high lung cancer stem cells, correlating with REV1-mediated translesion synthesis [44]. Structural biology approaches, particularly Cryo-EM, elucidate how radiation remodels CRL complexes, including neddylation-dependent COP9 activation and CRL2/β-TrCP conformational shifts that expose PROTAC-targetable interfaces [45, 46].

Therapeutic implications arise from this plasticity. While H2Bub1 modifications enhance homologous recombination, their mechanism underlying suppressing immunogenic cell death remains incompletely defined [31]. Developing agents targeting context-specific chain configurations, particularly PROTACs exploiting newly identified E3 ligase interfaces, could transform ubiquitin adaptability from a resistance mechanism into an actionable therapeutic vulnerability.

Crosstalk between ubiquitination and other post-translational modifications

Ubiquitination collaborates with phosphorylation, SUMOylation, acetylation, and neddylation to form an integrated stress-response network during radiotherapy. This system directly dictates cellular fate under radiation by regulating protein stability and functional states [47, 48]. For instance, TRAF6-mediated K63 ubiquitination of mitochondrial p53 blocks BAX activation in colorectal cancer, promoting survival [22]. Concurrent ATM phosphorylation and MDM2-mediated K48 ubiquitination of p21 override G1 arrest in esophageal SCC, enabling post-irradiation proliferation [49]. Blockade of USP14 stabilizes K63-linked ubiquitination on IRF3, promoting its phosphorylation/activation and downstream type I IFN signaling in dendritic cells, which synergizes with radiation to enhance systemic antitumor immunity [30].

Ubiquitination-phosphorylation circuitry in radioresistance

Bidirectional ubiquitination-phosphorylation crosstalk drives radioresistance by coordinating DNA repair fidelity, survival signaling, and metabolic adaptation. These modifications form a self-reinforcing regulatory cycle: phosphorylation activates E3 ligases, while ubiquitination reciprocally regulates kinase signaling, ultimately shaping context-dependent therapeutic responses (Fig. 5a).

Ubiquitination orchestrates crosstalk with (a) phosphorylation, (b) SUMOylation, (c) acetylation, and (d) neddylation to govern DNA damage response and therapeutic resistance. Radiation-triggered ubiquitination (TRIM26, RNF8, FBXW7, UBR5) dynamically intersects with phosphorylation (ATM/ATR-CHK1, PLK1, p38/MAPK), SUMOylation (PIAS2a-CBX4 on Ku80/RAD51), acetylation (TIP60-H3K56ac/β-catenin), and neddylation (Cullin-RBX1 complexes), collectively steering DNA repair (NHEJ via DNA-PKcs/RAD18; HR via γH2AX/Rad51), cell fate decisions (apoptosis/ferroptosis via p53/GPX4; radioresistance via Bcl-xL/NF-κB), and stress adaptation (mitophagy, senescence)

This synergy exhibits notable tissue specificity. In glioma, PLK1 phosphorylates TRIM26, enhancing its E3 ligase activity to promote K63-linked GPX4 ubiquitination and ferroptosis suppression [10]. However, FBXW7 deficiency in breast cancer impairs K48-linked p53 ubiquitination, increasing radiosensitivity [9]. Tissue-specific adaptations further shape these interactions: TRIM32 maintains STAT3 phosphorylation through a feedforward loop that amplifies its E3 activity to promote resistance [50], while esophageal squamous cancer exploits RAD18-mediated DNA-PKcs phosphorylation to enhance error-prone NHEJ repair [51]. Non-coding RNAs and metabolic pathways contribute additional regulatory compextity: ASB16-AS1 recruits ATM to modify TRIM37, activating NF-κB survival signals [52], whereas UBR5 cooperates with p38/MAPK to stabilize anti-apoptotic Bcl-2 [53].

Temporal dynamics critically influence functional outcomes. In nasopharyngeal carcinoma, CDC6 phosphorylation concurrently induces G0/G1 arrest and activates epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) pathways, with net biological effects determined by temporal context [49]. Wee1 inhibition in oral cancers disrupts Chk1-mediated G2/M checkpoints, restoring radiosensitivity by bypassing these timing mechanisms [54]. Such observations underscore the therapeutic relevance of chronotherapy.

Kinase-ubiquitin feedback loops further refine cellular regulation. TRAF4-mediated K63 ubiquitination of JNK activates Bcl-xL to inhibit apoptosis in colorectal cancer [28], while RNF26 ubiquitinates MRE11 in breast tumors to amplify ATR-CHK1 damage responses [40]. Counterregulatory mechanism prevent hyperactivation: MAP4K4/6/7 phosphorylate ubiquitin to impede RNF8-H2A interactions and delay DNA repair [55]. DUBs also contribute, as USP28 stabilizes c-Myc to suppress ATM/ATR checkpoints [56], while OTUD5 deubiquitinates Akt to inhibit mTOR/NF-κB in cervical cancer [57]. These regulatory principles extend to metabolism-epigenetic crosstalk, where NEK phosphorylation protects EZH2 from degradation to maintain H3K27me3 silencing in gliomas [58], and SDH5 loss stabilizes p53 by preventing its phosphorylation, restoring radiosensitivity in NSCLC [59].

Therapeutic targeting of this crosstalk shows compelling radiosensitizing effects, particularly in aggressive subtypes. Combined RNF126 and ATM inhibition in TNBC blocks K63-linked MRE11 ubiquitination, disrupting the ATM-CHK1 axis and increasing radiation-induced apoptosis [40]. Similarly, gp78 inhibition stabilizes KAP1 to suppress ATM activity, synergizing with ATR inhibitors [60]. Reactivation of tumor suppression pathways represents another strategy: Ulixertinib inhibits ERK2-driven MDM2 activation, while NSC59984 restores wild-type p53 function [61]. CDK16 inhibitors preserve functional p53 in tumors with intact alleles [62].

Clinical translation requires temporally optimized interventions: initial DNA repair inhibition (e.g., RNF126 and ATM blockade) followed by p53 reactivation maximizes tumor radiosensitization while minimizing normal tissue toxicity [26, 40]. This sequential approach exploits phosphorylation-dependent ubiquitination dynamics, demonstrating how temporal control mechanisms can convert radioresistance into therapeutic opportunities.

Ubiquitination-SUMOylation crosstalk in radioresistance

Ubiquitination-SUMOylation coordination underpins radioresistance through amplified DNA damage signaling, adaptive survival mechanisms, and targetable vulnerabilities. This interaction allows tumors to withstand radiation by synchronizing DNA repair fidelity, mitochondrial homeostasis, and transcriptional reprogramming (Fig. 5b).

At DNA damage sites, SUMOylation scaffolds enhance ubiquitin-dependent repair. ZNF451 attaches SUMO2 to RNF168, strengthening RNF8 interactions to amplify H2A/H2AX ubiquitination and recruit BRCA1-A repair complexes [37]. Similarly, PIAS2α and SENP2 fine-tune hSSB1 SUMOylation to enable rapid NBS1 recruitment to double-strand breaks [63]. Radiation redirects repair choices via SUMO modifications: ionizing stress triggers CBX4-mediated Ku80 SUMOylation, which replaces butyrylation marks to promote error-prone NHEJ via DNA-PK assembly [64]. Meanwhile, SUMOylated NPM1 stabilizes RAD51-DNA interactions, steering repair toward precise homologous recombination [65].

Beyond DNA repair, this crosstalk orchestrates mitochondrial and transcriptional radioresitance pathways, creating actionable therapeutic vulnerabilities. SUMO2-mediated SH3GLB1 SUMOylation hyperactivates PINK1/Parkin mitophagy, enabling tumor cells to eliminate radiation-damaged mitochondria and evade apoptosis. Targeting this axis with SUMO2 inhibitors (e.g., TAK-981) blocks mitochondrial clearance and synergizes with radiotherapy in sarcoma models [38, 66]. USP38 SUMOylation stabilizes KLF5 by blocking K48 ubiquitination, amplifying KLF5-mediated pro-survival transcription in lung adenocarcinoma [67]. Since KLF5 knockdown accelerates ferroptosis and increases radiosensitivity [8], targeting USP38 SUMOylation represents a promising radiosensitization strategy.

The SENP protease family calibrates this system by regulating DNA repair kinetics while preserving mitochondrial integrity and transcriptional fitness [68]. notably, SENP2 depletion radiosensitizes 3q-amplified cancers [69, 70], highlighting its therapeutic relevance.

Ubiquitination-acetylation networks in radioresistance

Ubiquitination and acetylation cooperatively drive radioresistance through epigenetic reprogramming and transcriptional control. These modifications jointly remodel chromatin to influence DNA repair pathways: RNF40-mediated H2Bub1 recruits the FACT complex to enhance H3K56 acetylation (H3K56ac), promoting precise homologous recombination repair [31]. Meanwhile, radiation-induced DTL-mediated RUVBL1 ubiquitination facilitates its assembly with β-catenin, suppressing TIP60-mediated H4K16 acetylation while activating error-prone NHEJ genes, creating an epigenetic switch under radiation stress [71] (Fig. 5c).

Ubiquitination further regulates acetylation enzymes. USP38 stabilizes HDAC1 via deubiquitylation, reducing H3K56ac and impairing NHEJ efficiency [6]. Conversely, pharmacological HDAC4 inhibition restores Rad51 nuclear localization in liver cancer by altering Ubc9 acetylation patterns [72]. This regulatory network extends to non-histone proteins: TRAF6-mediated K63 ubiquitination enables p53 acetylation, shifting its transcriptional activity toward pro-survival pathways [22].

These mechanistic discoveries reveal promising therapeutic opportunities for overcoming radioresistance. Disrupting HDAC4-Ubc9 interactions restores homologous recombination proficiency in hepatocellular carcinoma [72], while targeting USP38-HDAC1 stabilization compromises NHEJ efficiency by suppressing chromatin compaction [6]. Inhibiting the FACT complex rebalances HRR/NHEJ choices by modulating RNF40/FACT-driven H3K56ac dynamics [31]. Strategic combinations, such as TIP60 acetyltransferase inhibitors with β-catenin or p53 activators, may disrupt the epigenetic switching mechanism that promotes radioresistance [71] (Fig. 2).

Ubiquitination-neddylation crosstalk in radioresistance

Ubiquitination-neddylation interplay primarily modulates radioresistance through Cullin-RING E3 ligases (CRLs) and tumor microenvironment adaptation. Neddylation activates CRL complexes, enabling targeted ubiquitination and degradation of DNA damage regulators (e.g., CDC6, WEE1 and p21), thereby accelertating cell cycle progression and enhancing DNA repair [20]. Therapeutic agents targeting this axis include T-Norcantharidin (NCTD), which triggers Cullin1-dependent CDC6 neddylation and ubiquitination to halt DNA replication [20], and the NEDD8-activating enzyme inhibitor MLN4924, which restores radiosensitivity by stabilizing CRL substrates including WEE1 and p21 [73–75] (Fig. 5 d).

CRL-independent roles also exist: reduced NAE1/APP-BP1 activity in HPV+ head and neck cancers decreases p53 neddylation, stabilizing its tumor-suppressor functions [76]. HA-9104 selectively blocks UBE2F-mediated Cullin5 neddylation to prevent NOXA degradation, sensitizing lung tumors to radiation [77]. CRLs additionally influence microenvironmental dynamics; CRL2-mediated EGFR/HIF-1α degradation reduces hypoxia-driven proliferation in glioblastomas [78].

Clinical challenges arise from functional duality: MLN4924 stabilizes pro-apoptotic NOXA but simultaneously accumulates HIF-1α, causing toxicities [73, 77]. HA-9104 shows superior selectivity by specifically disrupting UBE2F-Cullin5 interactions, targeting tumor vulnerabilities while preserving healthy tissues [77]. These complexities highlight that biomarker-guided approaches, such as leveraging HPV-associated p53 stabilization or targeting HIF-1α in hypoxic tumors, might unlock this pathway’s therapeutic potential.

Ubiquitination-metabolism interplay in radioresistance

Ubiquitination dynamically orchestrates metabolic reprogramming to sustain tumor radioresistance, extending beyond ferroptosis and hypoxia to regulate glycolysis, nucleotide synthesis, and redox homeostasis [19]. This metabolic plasticity represents a key therapeutic leverage point (Fig. 6).

Ubiquitination-metabolism interplay controls radiation response. Ferroptosis regulation involves TRIM26-mediated K63-linked GPX4 stabilization (glioma radioresistance), OTUB1-driven GPX4 degradation via K48 chains (gastric cancer radiosensitivity), and HSP90-WWP1/2-SLC7A11/SOCS2-SLC7A11 axes (cervical/liver cancer ferroptosis modulation). Hypoxic adaptation hinges on HIF-1a ubiquitination: UCHL1 targeting in liver cancer, USP28-c-Myc-HIF-1 a in esophageal cancer, and CRL2/FBXW7-mediated HIF-1a control in glioma, with adenosines modulating sensitivity. Amino acid reprogramming includes FBXW7/GSK3b-IGF2BP2-driven methionine cycle disruption (lung cancer radiosensitivity) and USP9X-REV1-mediated glycine/serine flux alterations (lung cancer radioresistance), collectively defining ubiquitin ligases/DUBs as metabolic switches

Redox homeostasis and ferroptosis regulation

GPX4 and SLC7A11 Ubiquitination integrates with broader redox metabolism by regulating glutathione synthesis and NADPDH regeneration [11]. Tissue-specific regulatory patterns emerge: In gliomas, TRIM26 stabilizes GPX4 through PLK1-enhanced K63-linked ubiquitination, suppressing ferroptosis [10]. Conversely, β-caryophyllene disrupts OTUB1-GPX4 binding in gastric cancer, triggering K48-linked GPX4 degradation to induce ferroptosis [79]. SLC7A11 regulation exhibits similar heterogeneity: SOCS2 recruits Elongin B/C to degrade this transporter in liver cancer [11], while colorectal tumors employ CHI3L1 to stabilize SLC7A11 via p53 ubiquitination [80]. Hormonal context further modulates control; estrogen receptor α (ESR1) fluctuations regulate NEDD4L-mediated SLC7A11 degradation during breast radiotherapy [81], whereas cervical cancers counteract degradation via HSP90-WWP1/2 complexes [82]. Emerging strategies like the FBXW7 DNAzyme-Fe platform exploit this axis by stabilizing p53 to suppress SLC7A11 and glutathione production [9].

Hypoxic adaptation via HIF-1α switching

Context-dependent HIF-1α stability enables ubiquitin-mediated hypoxic adaptation. Breast cancers utilize UCHL1 to stabilize HIF-1α, activating the pentose phosphate pathway and enhancing antioxidant defenses against radiation [83], while esophageal tumors depend on USP28-mediated c-Myc stabilization to prolong HIF-1α activity [56]. In contrast, glioblastomas employ CRL2 to degrade HIF-1α/EGFR, suppressing VEGF -driven angiogenesis. Meanwhile, FBXW7-mediated HIF-1α turnover creates additional vulnerabilities, targeted by adenosine derivatives in combination therapies [78, 84]. These opposing mechanisms highlight opportunities to inhibit UCHL1/USP28 or activate CRL2/FBXW7 in hypoxic niches.

Amino acid metabolic reprogramming

Ubiquitination actively redirects metabolic flux in radioresistant tumors. In lung cancer, FBXW7 partners with GSK3β to degrade IGF2BP2, disrupting methionine cycle-driven methylation and homologous recombination repair [85]. Simultaneously, USP9X stabilizes REV1 in radioresistant lung cancer stem cells, promoting cystathionase (CTH) degradation and altering glycine/serine metabolism [44]. Such metabolic vulnerabilities suggest synergistic potential between ubiquitin-targeted drugs and dietary interventions like methionine restriction or serine/glycine deprivation.

Clinical translation challenges

Harnessing these insights faces practical hurdles: Phase-dependent SLC7A11 regulation (e.g., ESR1-NEDD4L interactions) requires temporally precise interventions [81], while USP9X-mediated metabolic plasticity demands cancer stem cell subtype-specific targeting [44]. Though preclinical agents like MLN4924 and β-caryophyllene show promise, maintaining metabolic homeostasis during treatment remains crucial. Advances in multi-omics profiling and dynamic control of ubiquitin networks may ultimately bridge these mechanistic insights to clinical practice.

Functional complexity and therapeutic precision of dubs in radioresistance

DUBs coordinate multiple facets of tumor radioresistance, including DNA repair, metabolic plasticity, immune evasion, and stem cell maintenance. Their context-dependent functions necessitate highly precise therapeutic approaches (Table 2).

Functional complexity and precision therapeutic challenges of dubs in tumor radioresistance

Tissue-Specific functional landscapes of DUB families

The USP family exemplifies functional duality across tumor types: USP5 stabilizes EphA2 to promote resistance in nasopharyngeal carcinoma [86, 87]. USP7 maintains genomic stability via CHK1 stabilization in breast cancer [88] but enhances radiosensitivity in HPV+ tumors [43]. USP9X drives metabolic adaptation in lung cancer [44] while preserving KDM4C-mediated TGF-β2 signaling [89]. USP11 enhances homologous recombination repair [90] but exacerbates radiation-induced lung damage [91]. USP14 temporally regulates repair pathways, promoting early NHEJ through 53BP1 [92] and late HR via ALKBH5 [26], while concurrently suppressing ferroptosis [7]. USP28 stabilizes c-Myc/HIF-1α in esophageal cancer [56] but ΔNp63 in squamous carcinomas [93, 94], demanding molecular subtyping for therapeutic targeting.

OTU family members utilize distinct resistance mechanisms: OTUB1 stabilizes CHK1 to maintain repair fidelity [42] interacts with SLC7A11 to inhibit ferroptosis [95], while OTUD4 triggers pyroptosis [96] despite impairing ATM signaling in NSCLC [97]. OTUD5 suppresses Akt phosphorylation to radiosensitize cervical tumors [57].

UCHL1 and BAP1 remodel stemness and microenvironment: UCHL1 stabilizes HIF-1α/PPP pathways in breast cancer [83] and sustains Notch1-driven stemness in lung cancers [98]. Conversely, UCHL3 promotes DNA repair [99] and immune evasion [100], while BAP1 stabilizes BRCA1-BARD1 complexes in colorectal cancer [101] and predicts metastasis resistance in uveal melanoma [102].

Overcoming therapeutic barriers

Temporal specificity (e.g. phase-dependent roles of USP14 [26, 92]), molecular subtyping requirements (e.g., USP7 efficacy limited to HPV+/p53-WT tumors [43, 103]), and microenvironment integration (e.g., UCHL3-mediated dual regulation of AhR/immune crosstalk [100]), complicate DUB targeting. Current strategies address these through catalytic inhibition (e.g., MLN4924 targeting NEDD8-activation [73]), allosteric modulation (e.g., β-elemene disrupting OTUB1-GPX4 binding [79]), and epigenetic reprogramming (e.g., OTUD4 demethylation to restore ATM signaling [97]). Nanodelivery systems improve precision by targeting specific cancer stem cells, with USP14 inhibitors for glioma stem cells [26] or miR339-USP8 approaches for esophageal CSCs [104]. Synergy with PARP inhibitors [105], STING activators [106], and ferroptosis inducers [79] further expands therapeutic opportunities.

Navigating functional plasticity

DUBs exhibit marked functional plasticity: USP28 acts as a tumor suppressor in breast/liver cancers [107] but promotes radioresistance in lung/esophageal malignancies [56, 108]. Similarly, UCHL1 drives resistance in breast cancer [83] yet sensitizes head/neck tumors [109]. This context-dependency underscores the need for predictive biomarkers and microenvironment-aware strategies. Emerging strategies bypass plasticity through synthetic lethality (e.g., USP1 inhibition activating STING [106]) and epigenetic targeting (e.g., linc-RA1/USP44 axis modulation [32]).

Combinatorial therapeutic synergy

Strategic combinations overcome radioresistance through complementary molecular mechanisms. USP1 inhibitors/STING agonists combinations stabilize oligo-ubiquitinated SAR1A, promoting STING trafficking to enhance radiation-induced IFN production and CD8+ T-cell infiltration in BRCA-mutant tumors [106]. OTUB1 inhibitors/ferroptosis inducers combinations exploit radiation-induced lipid peroxidation while disrupting GPX4 stabilization, creating synthetic lethality in gastric cancer [79].

Clinical validation emerges from phase I trials: Pevonedistat (MLN4924)/belinostat combinations in AML upregulate ferroptosis genes (SLC7A11, GSR) and oxidative stress markers (NQO1), correlating with prolonged stable disease in refractory cases [110]. CRISPR screens confirm that USP44 knockout sensitizes tumors to STING agonist/radiation combinations by impairing homologous recombination [111]. Collectively, these strategies simultaneously disrupt three core resistance mechanisms: (1) DNA damage tolerance (via CRL-mediated CDC6/WEE1 degradation); (2) redox homeostasis (via E3 ligase regulation of GPX4/SLC7A11); and (3) immunosuppressive checkpoints (via DUB inhibition-enhanced cytokine signaling).

Targeting E3 ubiquitin ligases to overcome radioresistance

DNA repair and cell fate regulation by RING family ligases

RING family ligases balance DNA repair and cell death to confer radioresistance. MDM2 inhibitors (e.g., Nutlin-3) reactivate p53-dependent apoptosis, radiosensitizing esophageal and prostate cancers [112–116]. A phase I trial combining sorafenib/Nutlin-3 in acute myeloid leukemia showed synergistic cytotoxicity irrespective of FLT3/p53 status, with maximal activity in FLT3-mutated cells [117]. However, dose-limiting hematologic toxicities (neutropenia, anemia) necessitate TP53-mutation-guided patient stratification.

β-TrCP exacerbates radiation-induced lung inflammation [47], while promotes lung cancer resistance through repair protein degradation [16, 118]. SKP2 inhibition enhances radiation-induced cell death in breast/rectal cancers [119–121]. FBXW7 presents therapeutic complexity: degrading MCL1/IGF2BP2 to sensitize tumors [85, 122] but stabilizing XRCC4 to boost NHEJ in repair-competent cells [5].

CRLs: gatekeepers of microenvironment adaptation

Cullin-RING complexes (CRLs) regulate radioresistance through DNA repair and microenvironment adaptation. The Neddylation inhibitor MLN4924 destabilizes CDC6/WEE1 to radiosensitize multiple cancers [20, 73], though phase I trials reveal schedule-dependent toxicities: hepatotoxicity (50–278 mg/m²) and renal impairment (including one grade 5 event) in solid tumors [123, 124]. In hematologic malignancies, MLN4924/azacitidine combinations achieved 50% overall response in AML patients, but caused grade 3/4 anemia in 39% of patients [125]. Pharmacodynamic biomarkers (MLN4924-NEDD8 adducts, CDT1/NRF2 accumulation) confirm target engagement [124, 126].

CUL2 degrades HIF-1α/VEGF to suppress hypoxia-driven radioresistance in glioblastoma [78]. Conversely, XIAP stabilizes RAD51 to maintain resistance, targeted by SMAC mimetics (e.g., Xevinapant) in ongoing trials [127–129]. RNF8/RNF168 knockdown increases radiosensitivity [130–132], while TRIM21 suppresses antitumor immunity via VDAC2 degradation in nasopharyngeal carcinoma [12] but stabilizes MLPH in gliomas, necessitating combined immunotherapy [133].

Metabolic and antioxidant control by HECT/U-box/RBR ligases

HECT family ligase NEDD4L promotes ferroptosis by degrading SLC7A11 in breast cancer [81], countered by HSP90-mediated redox balance in cervical tumors [82]. HECTD3 supports glioma resistance through degrading tumor suppressor LKB1 while activating PRDX1-mediated antioxidant pathways [134, 135]. Radiation stress further increases SMURF1/2 in lung/Glioma, promoting survival via TGF-β signaling and glycolytic reprogramming [136, 137].

U-box/RBR ligase CHIP exhibits context-dependent functions in lung cancer, promoting senescence via p21 degradation [138] while suppressing oncogenic PBK [139]. The RBR ligase Parkin enhances mitophagy to support survival in liver/nasopharyngeal cancers [140, 141], though dihydroartemisinin blocks this effects in lung/glioma models [39, 142].

Immune modulation via TRAF ligases

TRAF ligases critically modulate immune-cell death crosstalk. TRAF4 stabilzies MCL-1/surviving via Akt activation to drive oral cancer resistance [29, 143], whereas TRAF6 promotes colorectal cancer survival via K63-linked p53 ubiquitination that inhibits apoptosis [22]. Notably, TRAF6-mediated resistance is selectively disrupted by gold nanoparticles or ATR inhibitors [144, 145], underscoring its potential for context-specific targeting.

Translational challenges and future research directions in ubiquitination-targeted radiotherapy

The clinical translation of ubiquitin-targeted radiotherapy faces significant hurdles that demand integrated solutions spanning mechanistic research, technological innovation, and biomarker-driven clinical validation. Below we outline key challenges and strategic priorities.

Decoding ubiquitin networks: technological imperatives

Current mass spectrometry lacks temporal resolution to capture rapid ubiquitin chain dynamics (e.g., post-irradiation K48/K63 flux) [19]. While cryo-EM has resolved radiation-relevant conformation changes, such as USP16’s acidic patch-independent nucleosome engagement regulating H2AK119ub accessibility [146], critical knowledge gap persist. Combining cryo-EM with live-cell imaging could reveal how radiation remodels E3 ligase/DUB complexes (e.g., ATM-mediated MDM2 activation [26]). Advanced proteomics should map understudied linkages (K11/K29) and hybrid chain behaviors [19]. A critical priority is to develop time-resolved ubiquitinomics platforms integrated with structural biology approaches, enabling identification of radiation- specific conformational vulnerabilities in ubiquitin-regulating complexes.

Precision targeting: overcoming redundancy

Functional redundancy remains a fundamental barrier. USP28 exemplifies this challenge, driving duality esophageal cancer progression via c-Myc stabilization while suppressing EMT in breast tumors [56, 107]. Off-target toxicity is equally limiting, as evidenced by MLN492 caused grade ≥ 3 Hepatotoxicity in 23% of solid tumor patients, with dose-limiting renal failure reported in melanoma trials [123, 124]. To address this, we propose a biomarker framework for precision patient stratification: nuclear CDT1/NRF2 accumulation predicts MLN4924 sensitivity [124], while GPX4-K63 ubiquitination identifies TRIM26-dependent ferroptosis resistance [10], H2Bub1 depletion correlates with PARP inhibitor susceptibility [111].

To overcome on-target toxicity and compensatory resistance mechanism, several innovative strategies are emerging. Tissue-targeted agents (e.g., EGFR-directed PROTACs) achieve tumor-specific degradation of β-TrCP substrates in lung tumors while sparing normal tissues [16]. Allosteric modulators like β-elemene selectively disrupt OTUB1-GPX4 interactions to induce ferroptosis in gastric cancer without systemic toxicity [79]. CRISPR-based synthetic lethality screens identify therapeutic combinations: USP1 inhibition synergizes with STING activation in BRCA-mutant tumors [106], while co-inhibition of MDM2 and FBXW7 prevents compensatory resistance through parallel degradation pathways [9, 21].

Advancing these strategies requires accelerated development of three enabling technologies: PROTACs for spatial control of protein degradation, CRISPR-based synthetic lethality screens for identifying context-dependent vulnerabilities, and nanoparticle delivery systems (exemplified by bismuth sulfide nanoflowers) for temporal precision in targeting cancer stem cells [104]. This integrated approach will establish a new paradigm for overcoming the dual challenges of tumor specificity and evolutionary adaptation in ubiquitin-targeted radiotherapy.

Exploiting ubiquitin-PTM crosstalk

Ubiquitination’s interplay with other modifications offers untapped potential. USP14 inhition stabilize IRF3, converting immunologically “cold” tumors into radiation-responsive environments when combined with checkpoint inhibitors [30]. Simultaneously, SUMOylation inhibitors (ML-792) paired with PARP blockers disrupt NPM1-RAD51-mediated repair in BRCA-mutant tumors [65].

These approaches share a core principle, simultaneously targeting PTM hubs and immune checkpoints overwhelms adaptive survival pathways. Sequential strategies, ubiquitin inhibition followed by DNA repair/immune activation, may optimize efficacy.

Next-generation radiotherapy models

Current models inadequately recapitulate human radiotherapy responses. Animal xenografts lack mitophagy and immune microenvironment dynamics [38, 91], while patient-derived organoids (PDOs) faces significant practical, ethical, and standardization challenges. The absence of immune and stromal components in PDO overestimate radiation efficacy, and 2–4 week culture delays impede timely clinical application [147]. Biopsy risks outweigh benefits in metastatic disease, and palliative care complicates serial sampling consent. Furthermore, inconsistent radiation dosing protocols (2 Gy vs. 5 Gy/fraction) and biomarker validation require multi-center studies of > 1,000 PDOs according to EUROPDX consortium guidelines [148].

To bridge the critical gap between conventional models and human radiotherapy responses, we propose a dual approach utilizing advanced molecular profiling and physiologically relevant systems. Spatial transcriptomic analysis of radiotherapy-resistant PDOs enables identification of biomarkers, such as hypoxia-driven USP44 methylation patterns predictive of DNA repair competency [114]. Complementarily, humanized mouse models incorporating patient-derived cytokines and immune checkpoints tissue-specific radiation responses, as demonstrated by their accurate recapitulation of tumor-selective effects in therapeutic validation studies [101]. This integrated strategy concurrently addresses tumor-intrinsic epigenetic adaptations and microenvironmental crosstalk, establishing a clinically relevant platform for biomarker discovery and therapy optimization.

AI-driven precision radiotherapy integration

Artificial intelligence enables real-time radiotherapy personalization through multi-scale data integration. We envision a closed-loop clinical system initiated by pre-treatment ubiquitin mapping via rapid mass spectrometry of liquid biopsies to connect metabolic vulnerabilities (e.g., SLC7A11-dependent ferroptosis) with epigenetic states (H3K56ac levels), predicting therapeutic outcomes [149]. During fractionated radiotherapy persistent SLC7A11 ubiquitination signals ferroptosis vulnerability for dose de-escalation [149]. Deep learning analyzes radiation-induced K63/K48 ratios [59], while cryo-EM structures (e.g., UBR5 [150]) enable AI-guided PROTAC design.

The convergence of spatial transcriptomics in USP44-methylated tumors [111] with real-time ubiquitinomics could dynamically silence resistance pathways (TRIM26-GPX4) [10] and activate cell death (STING-IRF3) [145].

Conclusion

The ubiquitin system critically regulates tumor radioresistance by dynamically controlling DNA repair, metabolic reprogramming, immune evasion, and stress responses through precise regulation of protein stability and signaling cascade. Key ubiquitin-dependent mechanisms, including K48-linked degradation, K63-mediated signaling, and monoubiquitylation, collectively balance proteasomal targeting, DNA repair complex assembly, and chromatin remodeling in response to therapeutic radiation [5, 19, 31]. This regulatory network is further amplified by extensive crosstalk with phosphorylation, SUMOylation, and acetylation, creating adaptive survival networks that enhance tumor survival under radiation stress [37, 60, 151].

Preclinical studies show promise in targeting E3 ligases (MDM2, TRIM26) or DUBs (USP14, OTUB1) to destabilize critical radioresistance mediators like GPX4, HIF-1α, and PARP1 [10, 56, 152]. However, clinical translation faces substantial hurdles. Functional redundancy within ubiquitin machinery often activates compensatory pathways [153], while broad-spectrum agents like MLN4924 induce toxicity in healthy tissues [73]. Moreover, therapy-induced rewiring of ubiquitination networks, driven by dynamic interactions with other PTMs, actively fuels acquired resistances [19, 71].

Overcoming these limitations requires precision strategies targeting tumor-specific weaknesses. Tissue-selective approaches, exemplified by EGFR-directed β-TrCP degraders (PROTACs) could maximize antitumor efficacy while minimize off-target effects [16, 47]. Integrating multi-omics profiling with AI-driven network analyses may identify predictive biomarkers (USP44 methylation, SLC7A11 expression) for patient stratification [111, 149]. Rational combination therapies, such as coupling USP14 inhibitors with STING agonists or ferroptosis inducers, could overwhelm resistance by simultaneously targeting ubiquitin-dependent DNA repair and immune evasion [30, 79]. Validating these advances will require physiologically relevant models, including radiotherapy-resistant PDOs and humanized immune platforms [114].

In conclusion, converting radioresistance into a therapeutic vulnerability demands shifting from empiricism to mechanism-based approaches. Deciphering the plasticity of ubiquitin signaling through cryo-EM structural insights and adaptive AI technologies will be pivotal in developing truly personalized radiotherapy regimens, ultimately advancing a new paradigm in precision oncology.
