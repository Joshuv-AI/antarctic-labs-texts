Somatic structural variants drive upper tract urothelial carcinoma muscle invasiveness via activation of TPX2 transcription
Authors: Zhe Xu, Wei Lv, Rongzhang He, Yue Li, Xi Peng, Chunxiao He, Ling Lin, Linxiang Lan, Xiaolong Sui, Xin Liu, Wei Dong, Huanming Yang, Zhennan Yuan, Guangyi Fan, Chunhua Lin, Xi Xiang, Peng Han

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundUpper tract urothelial carcinoma (UTUC) is an uncommon but aggressive malignancy. Muscle invasion is strongly associated with poor prognosis and limited treatment options. Revealing the molecular basis of muscle invasiveness in UTUC is crucial.MethodsWe characterised somatic structural variants (SSVs) from 162 patients with UTUC and integrated these data with bulk RNA-seq, single cell RNA-seq and spatial transcriptomics, with selected SSVs validated by single-molecule sequencing. Furthermore, functional validation experiments, including dual-luciferase reporter assays, wound healing, and invasion/migration assays, were conducted on both the SSV region upstream of TPX2 and the TPX2 gene itself in the 5637 cell line.FindingsWe found that SSVs were enriched in muscle-invasive (MI) -UTUC compared with non-muscle-invasive (NMI)-UTUC, and were associated with gene expression changes independent of copy-number variation. Research in context

Evidence before this study

Upper tract urothelial carcinoma (UTUC) is an uncommon but aggressive malignancy, and muscle-invasive disease is associated with poor prognosis and limited treatment options. Recent genomic and transcriptomic profiling has clarified recurrent driver alterations and proposed molecular subtypes in UTUC. However, the somatic structural variant (SSV) landscape of UTUC remains poorly characterised. In particular, how SSVs contribute to muscle invasion, transcriptional dysregulation beyond copy-number change, and tumour microenvironment remodelling is not well understood.

Added value of this study

We systematically profiled SSVs in 162 treatment-naïve UTUC with matched normal tissues and integrated whole-genome sequencing with bulk RNA-seq, single-cell RNA-seq, and spatial transcriptomics. Using multi-caller consensus, we derived 26,812 high-confidence SSV calls and found that SSV burden and structural complexity were increased in muscle-invasive UTUC. Based on SSV patterns, we defined five rearrangement stability programs that were linked to distinct genomic features and clinical outcomes. We further demonstrated that SSVs can associate with gene expression changes independent of copy-number variation. Mechanistically, we prioritised TPX2 and showed that a recurrent upstream SSV interval has enhancer-like activity and promotes invasive phenotypes in vitro. Single-cell and spatial analyses placed TPX2-positive cycling tumour cells in EGFR/EPHA2-active, EMT-enriched microenvironments that were expanded in muscle-invasive UTUC.

Implications of all the available evidence

These findings indicate that structural variation is a clinically relevant driver of aggressive behaviour in UTUC. The rearrangement stability programs provide a framework to interpret heterogeneous SSV profiles and may stratify patients beyond conventional staging by identifying tumours with high-risk instability patterns. SSV–expression associations independent of copy number suggest regulatory SSVs are functional alterations that should be incorporated into UTUC profiling. TPX2-associated SSVs and the linked EGFR/EPHA2-active, EMT-enriched microenvironment highlight testable mechanisms and potential intervention points as biomarkers and therapeutic candidates.

Introduction

Upper tract urothelial carcinoma (UTUC) accounts for 5%–10% of all urothelial carcinomas (UCs)1 and is associated with a 5-year mortality rate exceeding 50%. For instance, the 5-year mortality rate for UTUC of the renal pelvis also surpasses 50%, representing a markedly poorer prognosis than that of urothelial bladder cancer, which has a 5-year mortality rate of less than 25%.2 The systemic management of UTUC has long been modelled after urothelial bladder cancer.1 However, emerging data suggest that UTUC is more aggressive and presents at a later stage than urothelial bladder cancer.2 Furthermore, UTUC is classified into muscle-invasive (MI) and non-muscle-invasive (NMI) subtypes, which exhibit markedly different clinical outcomes. Non-muscle-invasive UTUC (NMI-UTUC) was defined as tumours confined to the urothelium, including carcinoma in situ, or limited to the lamina propria. And muscle-invasive UTUC (MI-UTUC) was defined as tumours with invasion into the muscularis propria or beyond.3, 4, 5, 6 MI-UTUC is associated with a higher risk of metastasis and mortality, while NMI-UTUC tends to have a more favourable prognosis.7 These factors underscore the critical need for molecular characterisation and genome-informed precision medicine approaches in UTUC. Although advances in surgical and systemic therapies have been achieved, the molecular mechanisms underlying progression and heterogeneity in UTUC remain incompletely elucidated.8 The recent implementation of whole genome sequencing (WGS) and whole exome sequencing (WES) has significantly expanded our understanding of driver mutations in UTUC, identifying alterations in the signal transduction gene FGFR3; chromatin remodelling genes KMT2D and KDM6A; regulators of the cell cycle and apoptosis, including the tumour suppressor gene TP53 and its negative regulator MDM2; and other tumour suppressor genes and oncogenes such as CDKN2A and RAS.8, 9, 10, 11 While molecular subtyping studies based on transcriptomic and genomic profiling have been proposed,12 the somatic structural variant (SSV) landscape, an essential contributor to genomic instability and tumour evolution, remains poorly characterised.

Somatic structural variants (SSVs) represent a diverse class of genomic alterations, including deletions, duplications, inversions, translocations, and insertions. These mutations can exert profound effects on gene expression. Unlike single nucleotide variants (SNVs), which typically induce more subtle changes, SSVs often disrupt large genomic regions, reshaping regulatory landscapes, modifying gene dosage, and generating oncogenic fusion transcripts. In UTUC, emerging evidence suggests a widespread presence of SSVs. Furthermore, complex SSVs involving clustered breakpoints and chromosomal rearrangements may play a unique role in driving transcriptional dysregulation and disease progression. Despite advances in genomic profiling, the interplay between SSVs, gene expression programmes, and other genomic aberrations in UTUC has yet to be comprehensively elucidated.

We hypothesised that SSVs induced genomic instability is a driving force of muscle invasive transformation in UTUC, ultimately contributing to poor clinical outcomes. To test this, we analysed SSV features in a cohort of treatment-naïve patients with UTUC with a balanced number of MI and NMI cases. We defined the SSV driven UTUC signatures, and further explored the interactions between SSVs, genomic mutations, transcriptomic features. We profiled the complex SSVs, and explored the molecular mechanisms underlying SSVs driven progression, exemplified by SSVs affecting the TPX2 gene, with supporting evidence of scRNA-seq, spatial transcriptomic data and in vitro validation. Our findings underscore the importance of SSVs in shaping the genomic landscape of UTUC and highlight their potential as biomarkers for prognosis and therapeutic targeting.

Methods

Sample cohort

Surgeons at Yantai Yuhuangding Hospital resected a total of 162 fresh UTUC tumour tissues and their matched adjacent normal tissues (with matched adjacent normal tissues obtained at least 2 cm away from the tumour margin) from patients with UTUC. Two expert genitourinary pathologists independently evaluated each tissue sample for histopathological analysis. Detailed clinical features of the enrolled patients with UTUC are summarised in Supplementary Table S1. Sex (female/male) was extracted from the EHR and confirmed by the treating physicians during surgical admission/enrollment, and sex distribution is presented in baseline characteristics. Ethnicity was obtained from the electronic health record (EHR) demographic fields; all participants in this UTUC cohort were recorded as Han Chinese. All biopsies with high-quality DNA were submitted for WGS (BGI, China), and those with high-quality RNA were used for bulk transcriptome sequencing (BGI, China). Sequencing quality criteria are listed in Supplementary Table S2 and Supplementary Table S3.

Ethics

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Yantai Yuhuangding Hospital in Shandong, China (IRB 2021-456; 2022-399; 2022-401; 2022-402), and written informed consent was obtained from all patients. Each enrolled patient was assigned an anonymous sample ID to ensure privacy, with all personal identifiers such as names and addresses removed from the data.

Single nucleotide variation analysis

The quality of the raw WGS sequencing reads was initially assessed using FastQC v0.11.3 (https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc). MultiQC v1.10.113 was subsequently used to aggregate quality control metrics across all samples. Fastp v0.21.014 was employed to filter the FASTQ data that passed quality control, primarily removing low quality reads, short reads, and reads with excessive ambiguous bases, as well as performing adaptor trimming and quality-based read trimming. BWA v0.7.1215 was used to build an index of the reference genome (GRCh38.p13), and BWA-MEM was used to align the clean reads to the reference genome. Samtools v1.1716 was used to sort the BAM files after alignment and to generate indices. Somatic SNVs were identified using Mutect2 (4.2.5.0)17 in tumour-normal paired mode with default parameters. Somatic insertions and deletions (indels) were also detected using Mutect2 with default settings. Variant call format (VCF) files were subsequently processed using GATK v4.2.5.018 for variant normalisation, including splitting multiallelic variants and left trimming. VCF file annotation was performed using ANNOVAR.19,20

Copy number variation (CNV) analysis

Somatic CNVs were detected from WGS data using CNVkit v0.9.921 in tumour-normal paired mode. The CNV status of genes was assessed using the Genomic Identification of Significant Targets in Cancer (GISTIC) algorithm v2.0.22 Genes were categorised according to GISTIC scores of −2, −1, 0, 1, and 2, corresponding to deletion, loss, diploidy, gain, or amplification, respectively. Using the GISTIC score, we calculated the intratumour heterogeneity index for each patient, defined as the proportion of genes exhibiting non-diploid CNV states within each tumour sample. The intratumour heterogeneity index was calculated for each tumour sample and summarised at the patient level.

Significantly mutated gene analysis

Significantly mutated genes affected by SNVs/indels were identified using a consensus approach. The tools employed were dndscv23 and MutSigCV,24 both run with default parameters. A q-value threshold of <0.1 was applied for both dndscv and MutSigCV. Genes identified by both tools were considered significant (Supplementary Table S6, Supplementary Table S7).

Mutation signature analysis

Using the R package Sigminer,25 all mutations were classified into 96 mutation types based on the 3-nucleotide context (the mutated base and the 5′ and 3′ adjacent bases) and the base complementary pairing principle. Classification was based on six substitution subtypes: C > A, C > G, C > T, T > A, T > C, and T > G. The components of each sample were counted, and a component matrix was generated for each sample. Mutation signatures in the component matrix were identified using the non-negative matrix factorisation26 method and were categorised into four groups based on the identified mutation signatures. These four mutation signatures were then compared with known COSMIC single base substitution (SBS) signatures using cosine similarity (http://cancer.sanger.ac.uk/cosmic/signatures). The contribution of each feature in the sample was estimated using the quadratic programming method in SignatureEstimation. To avoid overfitting, features with <10% representation in each sample were omitted, and mutations were reassigned to the remaining features.

SSV analysis

Several general strategies are commonly employed to extract SSV information from genomic data: read pair and read depth methods, which leverage inconsistent alignment patterns and read depth deviations of paired-end reads that contain or span SSVs, respectively; split read methods, which utilise single-end reads that span SSV breakpoints; analysis of soft-clipped alignments from paired-end reads; and assembly-based methods, which detect SSVs by aligning contigs—assembled from entire or unmapped sequencing reads—to a reference genome.27 The outputs of multiple SSV-calling algorithms were merged to improve both precision and recall.27

In this approach, SSVs were categorised into four types: deletions (DEL), duplications (DUP), inversions (INV), and translocations (TRA), with DEL and DUP further subdivided based on SV length.27 Following classification, eight algorithms were employed for SSV detection: Manta,28 Lumpy,29 Wham,30 Tiddit,31 Svaba,32 ERDS,33 GRIDSS34 and Delly.35 Based on a predefined strategy (Supplementary Table S7), SSV calls supported multiple algorithm were extracted, intersected, and subsequently merged into a single file using bedtools.36

SSV feature extraction

A method analogous to mutation feature analysis was employed to identify the characteristics of SSVs.25 For rearrangement clustering within samples, the approach described by Nik-Zainal et al. was followed.37 In each sample, both breakpoints of each rearrangement were treated independently and sorted according to their chromosomal positions. The inter-rearrangement distance was then calculated, defined as the number of base pairs between a given rearrangement breakpoint and the immediately preceding breakpoint on the same chromosome. The average inter-rearrangement distance was subsequently computed for each sample. Breakpoints separated by a distance less than 10 times the sample-specific average inter-rearrangement distance were considered clustered.37

In addition to clustering, SSVs were also classified based on event length. For all types except translocations, events were grouped into five size categories: 1–10 kb, 10–100 kb, 100 kb–1 Mb, 1–10 Mb, and >10 Mb. SSVs were initially categorised as deletions, inversions, duplications, or inter-chromosomal translocations and were subsequently annotated according to their size and clustering status (clustered or non-clustered) to generate an SSV feature matrix. This matrix was then analysed using the Sigminer package, and the derived rearrangement signatures were compared to six previously defined rearrangement signatures using cosine similarity.25

Analysis of complex SSVs

A high-confidence consensus call set for each sample was generated by retaining SSVs detected by at least two independent algorithms. These consensus calls were subsequently standardised into BEDPE format. Concurrently, read depth coverage was calculated in 1000 bp bins using fragCounter, and profiles were corrected for GC content and mappability biases using hg38 reference files (gc1000.rds and map1000.rds from https://mskilab.s3.amazonaws.com/hg38/GCMAP/gcmap.38/).

Junction Balance Analysis (JaBbA) was employed to integrate the consensus SSV junctions (BEDPE files) with the corrected coverage profiles for each sample. This process generated balanced genome graphs that model the rearranged cancer genome by reconciling copy number segments with SSV junctions. These genome graphs were subsequently interrogated using the gGnome R package to identify and classify complex rearrangement events, including signatures such as chromothripsis, chromoplexy, pyrgo, rigma, and distinct circular topologies. Within the gGnome framework, structures classified as double minutes (DM), tyfonas events, and those indicative of breakage–fusion–bridge (BFB) cycles were specifically annotated as computational evidence supporting the presence of extrachromosomal circular DNA (ecDNA). These findings served as complementary validation for ecDNA elements potentially characterised by other methods such as AmpliconArchitect. Finally, to validate the computational predictions, the classifications of complex SVs were compared against available long-read sequencing data, confirming the accuracy of the approach.

SSVs and gene expression association analysis

A gene-centric analytical framework was implemented, similar to the method described in SVExpress.38 For each gene, a genomic window was defined that included the gene body as well as 100 kb upstream and downstream flanking regions. A binary gene–sample SSV breakpoint matrix was generated, where each entry was assigned a value of 1 if an SSV breakpoint was located within the defined region for a given sample, and 0 otherwise. In parallel, a gene–sample SSV relative distance matrix was constructed by identifying the nearest SSV breakpoint within ±1 Mb of each gene's transcription start site. The matrix entries represent the linear distance from the closest breakpoint to the gene start site; if no breakpoint was identified within this window, a default value of 1 Mb was assigned.

Gene expression levels were quantified using reads per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads. Linear regression analyses were performed to assess the association between SSV breakpoints and gene expression. Two models were applied: one without covariate adjustment and another controlling for gene-level CNV. Regression analyses were carried out using both the breakpoint presence/absence matrix and the relative distance matrix. In total, 18,077 protein-coding genes with unique gene symbols were included in the analysis. Genes were considered significantly associated with SSVs if the spatial distribution of SSV breakpoints and gene expression levels across samples exhibited statistically non-random patterns. This approach does not assume a predefined regulatory mechanism and treats SSV breakpoints as categorical features representing deletions, duplications, inversions, or translocations.

To control the false discovery rate (FDR), only genes with SSVs present in ≥13 samples (corresponding to at least 10% of tumours with available expression data) within a specific region (intragenic, upstream, or downstream) were included in the statistical testing. All genomic coordinates were aligned to the human hg38 reference genome. This gene-centric strategy enabled the integration of multiple SSV events within a genomic region and allowed assessment of their collective influence on expression. It is particularly suitable for SSVs, which may not be recurrent at the exact breakpoint level but can affect extended genomic domains relevant to gene regulation. Observed differences in association profiles may reflect sample-specific regulatory disturbances in highly dedifferentiated tumour contexts.39

Cell culture and DNA transfection

The human bladder cancer-derived 5637 cells (ATCC) were cultured in RPMI 1640 culture medium supplemented with 10% foetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Invitrogen) at 37 °C with 5% CO2 and maximum humidity. Transfections were carried out in 24-well plates using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Briefly, 60,000 cells per well were plated 24 h prior to transfection. For each well, a total transfection volume of 50 μL was prepared, containing 500 ng of DNA and 0.75 μL of Lipofectamine 2000 diluted in Opti-MEM (Invitrogen). In this study, the pLV3-CMV-TPX2 (human)-3 × FLAG-CopGFP-Puro plasmid was purchased from the SynBio company (Suzhou, China), and used for TPX2 overexpression by transient transfection. The PCR-derived TPX2 upstream SSV fragment (PCR products with tandem repeats of varying sizes and a 1025 bp main band) was used for the SSV overexpression in cultured cells. Empty vector–transfected cells were used as controls. Each experimental condition was performed with at least three independent replicates. Cells were collected for downstream analyses 48 h post-transfection.

CRISPR/Cas9 mediated knockout

SgRNAs used in this study were designed through CRISPOR (http://crispor.gi.ucsc.edu/) with the high on-target score and the minimised off-target score. Two sgRNAs targeting the chr20:31000852–31001508 interval were: TPX2-upST-sgR1: GAGAGCTTTGAGACGTACGGTGG and TPX2-upST-sgR2: GAAGGCATTGAGGTCTTCGTTGG, which were supposed to produce a 683 bp deletion within the region. For TPX2 gene knockout (KO), the sgRNAs used were: hTPX2-KO-sgR1: TTCGCTCATCTGTGCGGAAGTGG and hTPX2-KO-sgR2: TTACAGGCCCGAGTGACTAAGGG, targeting the exon 9 and 10 of TPX2 gene, respectively. The protospacer adjacent motif (PAM) sequences (NGG) are located in the genomic target sites but are not included in the synthesised sgRNA sequences. These sgRNAs were synthesised in the Genscript company (without the PAM sequence). The CRISPR/Cas9 knockout was conducted by Ribonucleoprotein (RNP) nucleofection using a Lonza 4D nucleofector, which the method was previously described.40 Nucleofection of sgRNAs without the spCas9 protein was treated as the negative control group. The cells were harvested two days after RNP nucleofection and were subjected to PCR genotyping. The primers to amplify the chr20:31000852–31001508 interval were: TPX2-upSTKO-scrF: 5′-GCAAGCGTCACAAAGTAGTTTC-3′ and TPX2-upSTKO-scrR: 5′-GATGTGTGCATTTCACTCACAG-3′; The primers to amplify the genomic region harbouring TPX2 exon 10 and exon 11 were: hTPX2-KO-scrF: 5′-GCAAAGTTAGATCCATGGTGACAG-3′ and hTPX2-KO-scrR: 5′-TGCAAGGGGCACATATGTAG-3′.

Real-time quantitative PCR

Total RNA was purified using an RNA extraction reagent (SeviceBio) following the manufacturer's instructions. For reverse transcription (RT), 1 μg of total RNA was used to synthesise the cDNA with oligo dT and random hexamer primers using the HiScript III First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (+gDNA wiper) (Vazyme). Then the qPCR was subsequently carried out in replicates with the ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme). Primers for the TPX2 mRNA qPCR were hTPX2-qP-F: 5′- CTGTGCCTTGTATGCCACCTG-3′ and hTPX2-qP-R: 5′-GGTGACCTGGCTCACTGATTTC-3′. β-actin was used as internal reference by using the primers of β-actin-qP-F: 5′-CTCACCATGGATGATGATATCGC-3′ and β-actin-qP-R: 5′-AGGAATCCTTCTGACCCATGC-3′. The relative expression levels of mRNA were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Western blot assay

TPX2 primary antibody (HUABIO, #ER65201) was used at a 1:1000 dilution. Total protein was extracted with RIPA lysis buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Beyotime). Protein concentrations were measured using a BCA assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Equal amounts of protein (20–30 μg per lane) were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore). After blocking with 5% non-fat milk or 5% BSA in TBST for 1 h at room temperature, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies. Following TBST washes, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were detected using an ECL system (Bio-Rad) and quantified with ImageJ (NIH), with β-actin (RRID tag: AB_2892540) as the loading control.

Dual-luciferase reporter gene assay

The 2331 bp TPX2 promoter region (including exon 1) was amplified by PCR from genomic DNA of 5637 cells using the primers TPX2-promoter-F: 5′-GAACTTGGTTAGGTACCgccttccaagtagcaccag-3′ and TPX2-promoter-R: 5′-AGCCATGGTGGCTAGCcgttattagccgacagcctc-3′. The resulting 2331 bp amplicon contained KpnI and NheI restriction sites at its termini. This fragment was then cloned upstream of the Renilla luciferase (Rluc) gene in the psiCHECK-2 dual-luciferase reporter vector (Promega). Successful plasmid construction was verified by Sanger sequencing. For functional assay, the PCR-derived TPX2 promoter fragment was co-transfected with the reconstructed psiCHECK-2 plasmid into 5637 cells. A co-transfection containing a 1 kb random DNA fragment and the same plasmid served as the negative control. Then the effects of TPX2 upstream SSV region on the relative expression level of Renilla luciferase were detected by the Dual-luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega) following the manufacturer's instruction.

Wound healing, transwell invasion and migration assays

For wound healing assay, the 5637 cells were seeded in six-well plates. Following a 48-h incubation, a scratch was introduced into the confluent cell monolayer. Cells were subsequently maintained in serum-free medium. Scratch images were acquired at 0 and 6 h post-wounding using an inverted microscope at 100 × magnification. The scratch width was measured and analysed with ImageJ software.

For the migration assay, about 5 × 104 transfected cells were suspended in 200 μl of serum-free medium and placed in the upper chamber, while 600 μl of 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS was added to the lower chamber. Following 12–24 h of incubation, cells that migrated through the membrane were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with crystal violet. Migration was quantified by counting cells in four randomly selected fields per well across four replicate wells at 200 × magnification. For the invasion assay, chambers were pre-coated with Matrigel (BioCoat) for 2 h prior to seeding cells; all subsequent steps were identical to the migration protocol.

Reagent validation of 5637 cell line and TPX2 antibody

The 5637 cell line (RRID CVCL_0126) was authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling (ABI 3730xl; GeneMapper ID-X) and matched the reference 5637 STR profiles reported in major databases (ATCC/DSMZ/JCRB/ExPASy), with no evidence of cross-contamination on 29 Apr 2024. 5637 cells were confirmed mycoplasma-negative by qPCR testing on 7 May 2024 and sterility testing was negative.

For immunohistochemistry, TPX2 was detected using a rabbit polyclonal anti-TPX2 antibody (HUABIO, ER65201; RRID Tags AB_3731329), generated against a synthesised peptide derived from human TPX2 (aa 301–350), and recommended for IHC-P (1:100–1:300). Antibody specifications and vendor-provided validation information are provided in the Supplementary Data.

Single cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data processing

The scRNA-seq data were processed using the Seurat R package v5.1.0.41 Each dataset was first normalised independently, and integration across libraries was performed using the FindIntegrationAnchors and IntegrateData functions. Following integration, gene expression values for 2000 highly variable genes were scaled and centred using the ScaleData function. Dimensionality reduction was conducted via principal component analysis based on the scaled data. Cell clustering was performed using the FindNeighbors and FindClusters functions, and visualisation was carried out using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) via the RunUMAP function. Cell type annotation was subsequently conducted by referencing canonical marker genes corresponding to known cell populations.

Spatial transcriptomics data generation and analysis

Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) urothelial carcinoma tissue sections were profiled using the Stereo-seq spatial transcriptomics platform (MGI, China) according to the manufacturer's protocols. Briefly, 5-μm FFPE tissue sections were mounted onto Stereo-seq chips, followed by deparaffinization, in situ RNA capture, library preparation, and sequencing on a DNBSEQ platform.

Raw sequencing data were processed using the Spatial Analysis Workflow (SAW, BGI) for spatial barcode decoding, read alignment to the human reference genome (GRCh38.p13), unique molecular identifier (UMI) counting, and generation of spot-level gene expression matrices with corresponding spatial coordinates.

Downstream analyses were conducted using Scanpy (v1.9.8) in Python. Low-quality spatial spots were filtered based on total UMI counts, detected gene numbers, and mitochondrial gene proportions. Data were log-normalised and scaled, followed by identification of highly variable genes, principal component analysis, construction of a neighbourhood graph, and UMAP visualisation.

Spatial spot annotation was performed using a marker gene-based strategy, rather than single cell reference deconvolution. Cell-type specific marker genes were curated based on established markers from our single cell RNA-sequencing analyses and published urothelial carcinoma datasets. For each spatial cluster, the top 50 differentially expressed genes were identified and manually inspected.

TPX2 positive spots were defined as epithelial tumour associated spots with detectable TPX2 expression above background levels. Spatial neighbourhood analyses were performed by identifying adjacent spots within a predefined spatial radius around TPX2 positive or TPX2 negative epithelial tumour spots. Gene set scoring was conducted using curated pathway gene sets, including EMT and EGFR/MAPK-related signatures, to assess functional enrichment within local microenvironments.

Statistics

Statistical analyses were performed using R v4.4.0. Unless otherwise stated, p-values for pairwise comparisons between two groups were calculated using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. All boxplots show the median, interquartile range (lower and upper hinge), and ±1.5 times the interquartile range for the first and third quartiles (lower whisker and upper whisker). Comparisons of categorical variables were performed using Fisher's exact test or the chi-square test, as appropriate. Linear regression models were used for analyses of continuous variables, including associations between gene expression and nearby SV breakpoints or structural variant burdens, while survival was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method. The one-sided Fisher's exact test or the chi-square test was used to determine the significance of overlap between two given feature lists. The FDR of significant genes was estimated using the Benjamini-Hochberg method. A 5% FDR cutoff was typically used to define the top molecular features; however, in cases involving fewer samples or where overlap between result sets was examined, more relaxed statistical cutoffs were used (e.g., FDR <10%). Heatmap visualisation was performed using the ComplexHeatmap package v2.20.42

Funders had no role in study design, data collection, data analyses, interpretation, or writing of this report.

Results

Characteristics of somatic structural variants reveal five rearrangement signatures distinguishing NMI-UTUC from MI-UTUC

To delineate the molecular differences between NMI-UTUC and MI-UTUC, we recruited a cohort of 162 patients with treatment-naïve UTUC, comprising a precisely balanced distribution of 81 NMI-UTUC and 81 MI-UTUC (Fig. 1A, Supplementary Table S1). Among these patients, 23 (14.2%) were diagnosed with low-grade UTUC, and 139 (85.8%) had high-grade tumours. The primary tumour sites were renal pelvis in 90 cases (55.6%) and ureter in 72 cases (44.4%). Additionally, 52 patients (32.1%) had a history of cigarette smoking, and 37 patients (22.8%) reported alcohol consumption (Fig. 1C, Supplementary Table S1). All patients with UTUC (n = 162) in this cohort included matched adjacent non-cancerous tissues (n = 162), with WGS (n = 324) and transcriptomic profiling (RNA-seq, n = 255) performed (Fig. 1C). The WGS data from tumour biopsies yielded an average genome coverage of 26.7 × , providing sufficient depth for high-confidence detection of both structural variations and SNVs across the genome (Supplementary Table S2).

To comprehensively characterise the precise SSV profiles from WGS data (n = 324), we employed eight independent SSV-calling algorithms and integrated their outputs to derive 26,812 high-confidence SSV calls. Across the cohort, the total number of SSVs per patient was not significantly associated with age, sex, smoking status, or alcohol consumption. However, a marked increase in SSVs number was observed in patients with MI-UTUC compared to those with NMI-UTUC (P = 0.0484, Wilcoxon test) (Fig. 1B). Meanwhile, we observed significantly worse overall survival in MI-UTUC than in NMI-UTUC (Fig. 1D, p < 0.0001, log-rank test, Supplementary Figure S1A, Supplementary Figure S1B). The median number of SSVs per patient was 156 (standard deviation = 51.6), with deletions accounting for the majority (54.8%, n = 91), followed by duplications (27.0%, n = 45) and translocations (12.7%, n = 21) (Fig. 1E). Most SSVs ranged from 100 bp to 10 kb in size, and we also observed a substantial fraction of ultra-long INVs longer than 10 Mb, comprising an average of 24.7% of all inversions (Fig. 1F). The observed difference in SSVs burden between MI-UTUC and NMI-UTUC raises the possibility that SSVs may be involved in MI progression in UTUC. This finding prompted us to further investigate whether distinct SSV patterns could serve as a basis for molecular subtyping of UTUC, with the goal of identifying biologically and clinically relevant subgroups.

To address this question, we clustered the patients with UTUC into five groups based on the size and genomic region clustering of SSVs (Fig. 1G, Supplementary Figure S1C, Supplementary Table S4).43 These clusters were defined by features such as clustered translocations, non-clustered translocations, proportion of tandem repeats, inversions, and deletions, and demonstrated their correlations with clinical significance (Table 1). Specifically, UTUC_RS1 shared high similarity (95%) with RS2_Nik_lab (Supplementary Figure S1D). Ninety percent of patients without copy number amplification (CNA) exhibited UTUC_RS1, UTUC_RS2, or UTUC_RS5 signatures. Patients characterised by UTUC_RS3 and UTUC_RS4 predominantly exhibited MI-UTUC, with significantly poorer survival than other patients (Table 1, Fig. 1H). Patients with non-clustered deletions and inversions in UTUC_RS3 (hazard ratio [HR]: 2.62, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.0867–6.332) and UTUC_RS4 (HR: 2.88, 95% CI: 1.0722–7.733) were approximately 2.6 times more likely to experience adverse outcomes relative to patients in other SSV-cluster types (HR: 2.87, 95% CI: 1.0722–7.733) (Table 1). Conclusively, we profiled SSVs in 162 patients with UTUC, and classified the UTUC cohort into five clusters based on SSVs characteristics.

Multi-omic and clinical characterisation of rearrangement stability groups

To elucidate the biological significance of rearrangement stability (RS) programs defined by SSV patterns, we systematically integrated genomic, mutational, copy number, and clinical features across the five RS groups. TMB differences across RS programs revealed a pronounced divergence in DNA damage accumulation. RS4 exhibited the highest TMB among all subtypes (Fig. 2A, Wilcoxon test, p < 0.05), consistent with a high mutational burden phenotype. In contrast, RS1, RS2, RS3, and RS5 remained within a low-TMB spectrum, highlighting overall limited point mutation pressure outside the hyperinstability axis. Intratumour heterogeneity also significantly differed among RS programs. RS3 demonstrated the highest MATH scores (Fig. 2B, Wilcoxon test, p < 0.05), indicating extensive subclonal diversification.

We next examined mutational signatures and known oncogenic pathways (Fig. 2C). RS1 and RS5 preferentially harboured SBS1/SBS5 ageing-related signatures and were strongly associated with driver gene FGFR3-altered, NMI-UTUC (Supplementary Figure S1E, Supplementary Table S5). In contrast, RS3 and RS4 were linked to TP53/MDM2 pathway alteration, RAS activation and the hypermutation signature cluster, parallelling their increased aggressiveness and progression risk (Supplementary Figure S2A–C). MI-UTUC were enriched in RS3 and RS4, whereas NMI-UTUC dominated RS1 and RS5 (Fisher's exact test, P < 0.05). Somatic copy number analysis revealed strongly RS specific CNV hotspot patterns (Fig. 2D). RS4 displayed the most extensive focal amplifications, particularly at 9p24.3 (JAK2/PD-L1), 5p15.33 (TERT), 3q29, 18p11.32 and 6p22.3, highlighting its hallmark status as a genome-unstable subtype. RS3 was characterised by replication-stress associated amplifications at 6p22.3 (E2F3/SOX4) and 8q22.3 (RAD21), whereas RS2 was enriched for pericentromeric CNVs (4p11, 6p11.1, 9q11), indicative of intermediate chromosomal fragility. RS1 and RS5 remained largely copy number stable with no major hotspot events.

Importantly, quantitative integration of RS programs with complex structural variation reinforced the concept of a genomic stability continuum (Fig. 2E, Supplementary Figure S3A, Supplementary Table S8). RS4 tumours exhibited the highest burden of complex SSVs, with the majority of samples harbouring one or more catastrophic rearrangement types, including breakage-fusion-bridge cycles (BFB), chromoplexy, chromothripsis, and tyfonas. By contrast, RS3 tumours showed a significantly increased frequency of replication-stress-associated rearrangements and extrachromosomal DNA (ecDNA), while displaying fewer catastrophic events than RS4. In comparison, RS1 and RS5 tumours rarely harboured complex SSVs, with most samples showing no detectable catastrophic rearrangements, consistent with a stable genomic architecture. Concordantly, complex SSVs were significantly more prevalent in MI-UTUC than in NMI-UTUC, supporting a strong association between structural complexity and clinical aggressiveness (Fig. 2F, Supplementary Figure S3).

Collectively, we profiled the distrubtion of complex SSVs in this cohort, and further delineated the correlation of five types of RS with mutational signature, and complex SSVs.

SSV-driven transcriptional upregulation reveals TPX2-SSVs associated with poor outcome

To investigate how RS-associated genomic instability affects functional and clinical consequences, we systematically characterised the transcriptional and phenotypic impact of SSVs in UTUC. Genome-wide analysis of SSV breakpoints revealed widespread associations between breakpoint proximity and gene expression changes (Fig. 3A). Across the cohort, numerous genes exhibited expression levels significantly correlated with nearby SSV breakpoints. After adjusting for copy number variation (CNV) effects, the majority of these associations remained significant, indicating that SVs frequently exert direct regulatory effects on gene expression independent of CNV dosage (Fig. 3B).

Characterisation of SSV classes underlying expression altering breakpoints showed that deletions constituted the predominant SSV type, followed by duplications, translocations, and inversions (Fig. 3C). Notably, intronic deletions accounted for the largest fraction of expression associated SSVs, whereas exonic or untranslated region disruptions were comparatively rare, highlighting intronic SSVs as a major mechanism of transcriptional dysregulation in UTUC. Although complex SSVs represented a minority of all detected SSV events, a substantial proportion of expression altering breakpoints originated from complex rearrangements (Fig. 3D).

We next assessed the clinical relevance of SSV-mediated transcriptional perturbations. Fourteen genes exhibited significantly higher frequencies of structural variants in MI-UTUC compared with NMI-UTUC (Fig. 3E, Fisher's exact test, p < 0.05). Among these, TPX2 emerged as a prominent candidate, showing both frequent structural disruption in MI-UTUC and marked transcriptional upregulation. TPX2 expression was significantly elevated in MI-UTUC relative to NMI-UTUC within the cohort (Fig. 3E, Wilcoxon test, p = 2.9e−11), a pattern that was consistently observed across three independent public UTUC expression datasets (BCM, MDA, WCM, Fig. 3G).

Survival analysis further demonstrated the clinical significance of TPX2 associated structural variation. Within the same UTUC cohort, patients harbouring TPX2 structural variants exhibited significantly poorer progression-free survival compared with those without TPX2 SVs (Fig. 3F, log-rank test, p = 0.014). Survival analyses using alternative stratifications of the same cohort yielded consistent trends, supporting the robustness of this association (Supplementary Figure S4A). At the protein level, immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis revealed significantly higher TPX2 expression in UTUC with TPX2 structural variants compared with TPX2 SSV-negative tumours (Fig. 3H, Supplementary Figure S4C). Representative IHC images further illustrated strong nuclear TPX2 staining in TPX2 SSV-positive biopsy, whereas weak or absent staining was observed in tumours lacking TPX2 SSVs (Supplementary Figure S4C). In addition to direct structural disruption, amplification events involving the upstream regulatory region of TPX2 were observed across RS programs. Although infrequent, these upstream amplification events were numerically enriched in genomically unstable RS states, supporting a context-dependent activation of TPX2 in unstable tumour genomes (Supplementary Figure S4B). Collectively, complex SSVs exert substantial regulatory effects on gene expression, and TPX2 represents a clinically and functionally relevant target of SSV-mediated transcriptional dysregulation.

The upstream SSV enhances TPX2 promoter activity thereby promoting invasiveness of bladder cancer cells

To further explore the function of TPX2-SSV in UTUC, we analysed the SSVs surrounding the TPX2 locus and found that the predominant SSV type was duplication, localised within the chr20:31000852–31001508 interval, approximately 600 kb upstream of the TPX2 gene (Fig. 4A). This interval contains eight tandem repeats of a 171 bp sequence (Fig. 4A), suggesting a potential regulatory function. To examine its role in TPX2 regulation, we designed two unique sgRNAs targeting this region and deleted a 683 bp fragment using CRISPR ribonucleoprotein (RNP) nucleofection in 5637 bladder cancer cell line. PCR and gel electrophoresis confirmed efficient deletion, as evidenced by attenuation of the 1025 bp band in RNP-treated cells (Fig. 4B). Subsequent quantitative PCR and Western blot analyses demonstrated that deletion of this interval significantly reduced TPX2 mRNA and protein expression, whereas transfection of a PCR-derived fragment corresponding to this region markedly increased TPX2 expression (Fig. 4C–F). These results indicate that the chr20:31000852–31001508 interval positively regulates TPX2 expression.

We further hypothesised that this SSV-containing interval functions as an enhancer acting on the TPX2 promoter. To test this, we cloned the TPX2 promoter region (2331 bp, including exon 1) upstream of the Renilla luciferase gene in the psiCHECK-2 dual-luciferase reporter vector (Fig. 4G). Co-transfection of this reporter with PCR-derived fragments from the chr20:31000852–31001508 interval significantly increased Renilla luciferase activity compared with a random 1 kb DNA fragment control (Fig. 4H), supporting its enhancer-like activity. Functionally, TPX2 knockout and over-expression 5637 cell lines were generated (Fig. 4I and J), and cell motility and invasiveness were assessed. TPX2 knockout significantly impaired wound healing, migration, and invasion abilities, whereas TPX2 overexpression markedly enhanced these invasive properties (Fig. 4K–N), demonstrating that TPX2 is a critical driver of bladder cancer cell invasiveness.

To further delineate the transcriptional programs regulated by TPX2, we performed RNA sequencing following TPX2 perturbation. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of genes downregulated upon TPX2 knockout (TPX2_KO vs. NC_KO) revealed significant suppression of multiple pathways involved in cell-cycle progression and mitotic regulation, including MAPK signalling and other proliferation associated pathways (Fig. 4O). These transcriptional changes were consistent with the observed impairment of proliferative and migratory phenotypes following TPX2 loss. In parallel, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) of transcriptomic changes induced by amplification of the TPX2 upstream interval (Ups_OE vs. NC_OE) demonstrated significant enrichment of pathways related to EMT, cell adhesion, and extracellular matrix remodelling (Fig. 4P). Together, these results indicate that the upstream structural variant enhances TPX2 expression and activates transcriptional programs that promote invasive and migratory behaviour in urothelial carcinoma cells.

TPX2 increases cell aggressiveness via enhanced communication with EGFR and EPHA2

To further explore the relationship between TPX2 alterations and the tumour microenvironment in UTUC, we first performed cell-type deconvolution on bulk RNA-seq data using established immune/stromal reference signatures. This analysis revealed that TPX2 structural variation was associated with distinct tumour microenvironmental features, suggesting a potential link between TPX2 dysregulation and immune-stromal context (Supplementary Figure S5A). Motivated by this bulk-RNA observation, we then conducted scRNA-seq profiling of 14 UTUC biopsies, yielding 141,707 cells. UMAP-based dimensionality reduction revealed distinct cellular clusters, including epithelial cells, cycling epithelial cells, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and multiple immune cell types, highlighting the cellular heterogeneity within the tumour microenvironment (Fig. 5A). Further analysis identified three cell populations strongly correlated with TPX2 expression, based on comparisons across cell types (Supplementary Figure 5B). Quantitative analysis revealed a significantly higher proportion of cycling epithelial cells among TPX2 positive cells (Fig. 5C). To further dissect the functional role of TPX2 expressing epithelial cells in tumour microenvironment, we conducted pathway enrichment and cell–cell communication analyses. Pathway enrichment analysis of genes highly expressed in TPX2-positive cycling epithelial cells (c_epi-TPX2+) uncovered significant enrichment in tumour-associated pathways, including cell cycle regulation, cell adhesion, ECM-receptor interaction, and cytoskeletal remodelling, indicating that TPX2 positive cells may exhibit enhanced invasive and proliferative potential (Fig. 5D). Subsequent intercellular communication analysis demonstrated distinct signalling interaction patterns between TPX2-positive (c_epi-TPX2+) and TPX2-negative (c_epi-TPX2−) epithelial cells (Fig. 5E). Notably, c_epi-TPX2+ cells exhibited enhanced outgoing signalling activities compared with c_epi-TPX2− cells, particularly through tumour associated ligands such as JAM, EPHA, CDH, CDH1, and IGF, indicating their active role in modulating the tumour microenvironment (Fig. 5F). By contrast, c_epi-TPX2− cells demonstrated relatively weaker interaction strengths. On the receiving end, c_epi-TPX2+ cells also exhibited elevated incoming signals mediated by collagen, fibronectin (FN1), JAM, CDH, and IGF pathways (Fig. 5G), highlighting their engagement with extracellular matrix remodelling and stromal interactions. Notably, TPX2-positive epithelial cells exhibited strong incoming signals via the EGF pathway (Fig. 5G), suggesting potential activation of EGFR-mediated signalling cascades that may contribute to their enhanced proliferative capacity. In both incoming and outgoing communication networks, the EPHA signalling pathway was prominently active in TPX2 positive epithelial cells (Fig. 5F and G), indicating that these cells may modulate tumour progression through EPHA mediated cell–cell interactions and motility regulation. STRING-based protein–protein interaction analysis showed that TPX2, EGFR, and EPHA2 were closely connected within a shared signalling network, alongside EFNA1 and SEMA6A (Supplementary Figure 5C). These interactions support the involvement of TPX2 positive cells in EGFR and EPHA2 signalling, consistent with transcriptomic and cell–cell communication analyses (Supplementary Figure 5C). Furthermore, biopsies with TPX2 SSVs were correlated with significantly higher EGFR expression than those without TPX2 SSVs (Supplementary Figure 5D). These results suggest that TPX2 positive epithelial cells are involved in EGFR and EPHA2 signalling. Elevated EGF input may activate EGFR-driven proliferation, while enhanced EPHA activity likely facilitates cell–cell interaction and invasion.

Using a marker gene based annotation strategy, we assigned cell-type identities to spatial spots and systematically mapped the spatial architecture of tumour epithelial cells and multiple microenvironmental components across tissue sections (Fig. 6A).

In the MI specimen, TPX2 positive spots were broadly distributed within tumour regions, accounting for approximately 3.3% of all spatial spots. In contrast, TPX2 positive spots were exceedingly rare in the NMI specimen (∼0.01%) and appeared only sporadically (Supplementary Figure S6A and B). This marked difference suggests a substantial expansion of TPX2 positive tumour cells in muscle invasive disease, accompanied by a wider tissue-level spatial occupancy. Comparison of the proportion of TPX2 positive spots across spatially defined cell-type compartments further showed that, in the MI specimen, TPX2 positivity was significantly increased in multiple compartments associated with invasive phenotypes, including tumour epithelium–enriched regions, mixed immune–stromal regions, and selected immune cell–enriched regions. In the NMI biopsy, the proportion of TPX2 positive spots in these compartments remained uniformly low (Fig. 6C). Notably, although a relatively high fraction of TPX2 positive signals was detected in some spots annotated as B cell related or immune associated in the MI specimen, given that TPX2 is a canonical cell-cycle–related gene, this pattern more likely reflects spatial co-localisation of TPX2 positive tumour epithelial cells with immune-enriched regions, rather than TPX2 expression by immune cells per se.

Consistently, evaluation of TPX2 expression across spatial cell-type compartments indicated that TPX2 was predominantly enriched in tumour epithelium–associated populations, with markedly higher expression in basal-like and selected urothelial tumour subpopulations than in immune or stromal compartments (Fig. 6B). While TPX2 signals were also detected in a subset of immune-annotated spots in the MI specimen, this observation is most plausibly explained by spatial co-localisation between proliferative TPX2 positive tumour cells and immune-enriched neighbourhoods, rather than genuine TPX2 expression by immune cells. To characterise the local microenvironment surrounding TPX2 positive tumour cells, we further conducted spatial neighbourhood analyses. Compared with TPX2 negative tumour cells, the neighbourhoods of TPX2 positive tumour cells were significantly enriched for EMT related transcriptional programs (Fig. 6D, Wilcoxon rank-sum test, p = 3.33 × 10−3), indicating that TPX2 positive cells preferentially reside in an invasive microenvironmental niche. At the pathway level, ligand expression analysis within the spatial neighbourhoods of TPX2 positive tumour cells revealed significantly elevated expression of ligands related to EGFR and EPHA2 signalling, including AREG, EREG, EFNA3, and EFNA4 (Fig. 6E).

Collectively, spatial transcriptomics links TPX2 positive tumour cell expansion in MI-UTUC to EMT-enriched microenvironments with increased EGFR/EPHA2-associated ligands. These findings support a spatial association between TPX2 positivity and invasion-related tumour ecology.

Discussion

In this study, we present the most comprehensive multi-omics dataset to date for UTUC, integrating WGS, RNA-seq, and scRNA-seq data to systematically characterise the SSV landscape. Our analysis reveals the functional contributions of SSVs to UTUC progression and identifies TPX2 as a recurrently rearranged, SSV-associated gene that promotes UTUC development. To improve the accuracy of SSV analysis from short reads high-throughput sequencing data, we applied eight analytical algorithms to our dataset to obtain precise SV calls. Although overlap-based consensus can increase confidence, benchmarking shows a strong precision/recall tradeoff.27 To balance this, we merged type aware and size aware SSV calls for global analyses while defining a high-confidence subset (≥2 callers) for locus-specific validation. Thus, we established the foundation of the SSVs study based on the precise identification of 26,812 high-confidence SSV calls in the 324 biopsies.

One of the key contributions of our study is the classification of SSVs into five distinct clusters, using a methodology originally developed in a comprehensive breast cancer study, which classified tumours based on the proportion of deletions, inversions, and tandem duplications.43 This classification, applied for the first time to UTUC, underscores its molecular heterogeneity and provides a new lens through which to interpret the biological and clinical relevance of SSV patterns. Notably, two clusters (UTUC_RS3 and UTUC_RS4) were associated with aggressive MI-UTUC and poor survival. UTUC_RS4 was enriched for TP53 mutations and characterised by hypermutation and an excess of short deletions. Consistent with previous studies across multiple cancer types,43, 44 our findings highlight TP53 mutations as central drivers of genomic instability and structural complexity in UTUC, particularly in MI-UTUC. By contrast, FGFR3 mutations, which are more common in NMI-UTUC, are associated with lower structural complexity, suggesting a fundamentally different path of tumourigenesis characterised by greater genomic stability. The inverse relationship between TP53 and FGFR3 further supports the notion of distinct evolutionary routes in UTUC subtypes.8 Together, these insights underscore a multi-layered interplay between tumour suppressor loss, mutagenic activity, and structural genome remodelling. This model offers a framework for understanding intratumoural heterogeneity and may inform subtype-specific therapeutic strategies targeting genome instability in UTUC.

Our integrative analysis reveals that SSVs are not only a hallmark of genomic instability in UTUC but also play a direct role in modulating gene expression and driving tumour progression. Beyond their association with copy number alterations, SSVs independently influenced gene expression. Hundreds of genes exhibited expression changes linked to nearby SSV breakpoints, even after adjusting for CNV effects. Many of these breakpoints, particularly deletions, were located in intronic regions and were paradoxically associated with gene upregulation. These findings suggest that structural rearrangements may reshape the regulatory architecture, potentially enhancing transcriptional output. Notably, a subset of SSVs involved key oncogenes or gave rise to complex SSVs, exemplified by recurrent circular amplification of CCND1, a known driver of aggressive tumour behaviour. Our analysis further revealed that the progression of MI-UTUC is significantly enriched with complex SSVs such as templated insertion chains (TICs), chromoplexy, double minutes (DMs), pyrgo, BFB, and chromothripsis, which greatly enhance intratumoural heterogeneity and are more prevalent than in NMI-UTUC, suggesting that an escalation in genomic complexity is closely associated with tumour invasiveness.

We confirmed the structures of these complex SSVs using long-read sequencing data. For instance, we reconstructed an ecDNA in the UTUC-82 sample, in which TERT and TBX3 were co-amplified through a complex fusion of segments from chromosomes 3, 5, and 12. Similarly, a pyrgo event identified in UTUC-164 biopsy led to the co-amplification of a known oncogenic cluster (CCND1, FGF3, FGF4) on chromosome 11 via stepwise segmental duplications. This result suggests that in UTUC, CCND1 amplification may proceed through intermediate stages involving other complex SSVs before the eventual generation of ecDNA. Our findings suggest that SSVs drive genomic and transcriptional reprogramming in UTUC, which contribute to tumour heterogeneity.

Our findings highlight the functional and clinical significance of SSV-associated genes in shaping the malignant phenotype of UTUC. By comparing structural variant profiles between MI-UTUC and NMI-UTUC, we identified a subset of genes, exemplified by TPX2, which is not only more frequently altered in MI-UTUC but also shows significantly elevated expression. This finding suggested that these SSVs contribute to transcriptional activation and disease progression. The strong predictive performance of TPX2, particularly in combination, underscores their potential as biomarkers for stratifying invasive UTUC. Importantly, SSVs affecting TPX2 was associated with poorer clinical outcomes, including shorter progression-free and overall survival, reinforcing the prognostic relevance of SSV-mediated gene deregulation. Elevated expression of these genes in SSV-positive samples further supports a model in which structural variants enhance oncogenic potential by driving overexpression of key effectors. Together, these results suggest that specific SSV events not only mark aggressive disease but also functionally contribute to tumour progression through gene activation.

Targeting protein for Xklp2 (TPX2) is a microtubule-associated protein that plays a critical role in mitotic spindle assembly and activation of Aurora A kinase during cell division.45 TPX2 has also been implicated in phosphorylation of the AURKA–PI3K–AKT signalling axis, leading to increased vascular endothelial growth factor A expression and enhanced angiogenesis.46 Our single-cell transcriptomic and deconvolution analyses provide mechanistic insight into the functional role of TPX2-overexpressing epithelial cells in UTUC progression. TPX2 expression was preferentially enriched in cycling epithelial cells, suggesting a cell-type-specific contribution to tumour proliferation. These TPX2-positive cells exhibited transcriptomic features consistent with elevated proliferative and invasive capacity, including activation of cell cycle, ECM–receptor interaction, and cytoskeletal remodelling pathways. This phenotype aligns with their increased abundance in MI tumours and association with adverse clinical outcomes. Importantly, TPX2-positive epithelial cells displayed distinct intercellular communication patterns characterised by enhanced outgoing and incoming signalling activities, particularly via EGF, EPHA, IGF, and CDH-family ligands. These signalling axes are known to regulate cell proliferation, adhesion, and motility, supporting a model in which TPX2+ cells not only proliferate autonomously but also actively shape a pro-tumourigenic microenvironment. Notably, EGFR and EPHA2, two key signalling hubs in cancer biology, were implicated in TPX2-mediated communication networks, both through transcriptional co-expression and protein–protein interaction analysis. The engagement of TPX2+ cells in EGFR signalling suggests that EGF-driven activation may further amplify their proliferative behaviour, while EPHA2-mediated interactions likely facilitate tumour cell dissemination and spatial organisation. Together, these findings point to TPX2-positive epithelial cells as functional drivers of tumour aggressiveness in UTUC, exerting both cell-intrinsic effects and microenvironmental influence. Targeting TPX2-associated signalling networks, particularly those involving EGFR and EPHA2, may offer novel therapeutic opportunities for patients with TPX2-high tumours.

Consistent with the single-cell analyses, spatial transcriptomics further contextualised the tissue-level distribution of TPX2-positive tumour cells in UTUC. In muscle-invasive UTUC, TPX2-positive cells showed a marked expansion and were spatially enriched within tumour regions, whereas such cells were exceedingly rare in non–muscle-invasive specimens. Importantly, TPX2-positive tumour cells were not randomly distributed but preferentially localised to spatial neighbourhoods characterised by enrichment of EMT–related transcriptional programs and elevated expression of EGFR-associated and EPHA2-associated ligands. These spatial patterns provide in situ support for the cell–cell communication features identified in scRNA-seq analyses, suggesting that TPX2-associated transcriptional states are executed within defined microenvironmental niches in muscle-invasive disease. Although spatial transcriptomics does not establish causality, the convergence of single-cell and spatial evidence highlights a coordinated relationship between TPX2 positivity, invasion-associated pathways, and the local tumour microenvironment in UTUC.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. On one hand, because of the use of bulk transcriptomic data, the precise impact of SSVs on gene expression within specific cellular subpopulations may be underestimated, despite partial resolution through scRNA-seq deconvolution. On the other hand, while we identified ecDNA and other complex SSV structures using short-read WGS and confirmed selected events with long-read sequencing, the full structural complexity and dynamic behaviour of these rearrangements over time remains to be elucidated. Future work using lineage tracing, as well as in vitro and in vivo models, will be essential to validate the causal roles of these SSVs in promoting invasiveness and shaping the tumour microenvironment.
