Agrobacterium virulence factors induce the expression of host DNA repair-related genes without promoting major genomic damage
Authors: Benoît Lacroix, Anna Fratta, Hagit Hak, Yufei Hu, Vitaly Citovsky
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to investigate whether the plant DNA damage levels and DNA damage response (DDR) are regulated during Agrobacterium infection and potentially manipulated by Agrobacterium to facilitate T-DNA integration. We investigated the plant genomic response to Agrobacterium infection by measuring gamma H2AX levels, which reflect the levels of double-strand DNA breaks (DSBs), and by characterizing transcription of three major DNA repair marker genes NAC82, KU70, and AGO2. These experiments revealed that, globally, Agrobacterium infection did not result in a major increase in DSB content in the host genome. The transcription of the DNA damage repair genes, on the other hand, was elevated upon the wild-type Agrobacterium infection. This transcriptional outcome was largely negated by a mutation in the bacterial virB5 gene which encodes the virulence (Vir) protein B5, a minor component of Agrobacterium pilus necessary for the translocation of Vir effector proteins into the host cell, suggesting that the transcriptional activation of the cellular DNA damage repair machinery requires the transport into the host cell of the Agrobacterium effectors, i.e., the VirD2, VirD5, VirE2, VirE3, and VirF proteins. Most likely, a combination of several of these Vir effectors is required to activate the host DNA repair as their individual loss- or gain-of-function mutants did not significantly affect this process.

Introduction

Agrobacterium tumefaciens and closely related species have the unique ability to transfer and integrate a segment of their own plasmid DNA (T-DNA, transferred DNA) into the genome of the host plants, resulting in the genetic transformation of the target plant1,2. Agrobacterium T-DNA reaches the host cell nucleus as a single-stranded DNA segment covalently attached to a single molecule of the VirD2 protein and likely interacting with several bacterial effectors and plant proteins. The last step of this genetic transformation process, i.e., integration of the T-DNA into the host genomic DNA, is not completely understood but is thought to depend mostly on the host factors. Indeed, DSBs (double-strand breaks) in the host genome represent preferential sites for T-DNA integration3–5, and several studies using Agrobacterium-mediated transformation of different yeast mutants have shown that the outcome of integration, which results from homologous recombination or non-homologous end joining (NHEJ), relies on the host DNA repair factors6,7. In plants, homologous recombination is very rare, and it is generally accepted that the integration of T-DNA is mediated by NHEJ. NHEJ comprises two major pathways with distinct mechanisms that involve different sets of proteins. In the classical NHEJ pathway, the broken DNA ends are protected by a heterodimer composed of Ku70 and Ku80, which is also required to recruit several repair factors (such as XRCC4, XLS, and DNA ligase IV). The microhomology-mediated end joining (MMEJ), or alternative NHEJ, pathway depends on microhomologies (2–20 bp) in the proximity of the break area, the initial binding protein is PARP1 and the repair is mediated by the DNA polymerase θ (or POLQ)8,9. Regardless of the nature of the host DNA repair pathway, its components are crucial for the integration of the T-DNA into the plant genome, yet the precise roles of these components in the T-DNA integration process are not fully understood. Indeed, conflicting results were obtained with mutants in different DNA repair pathways10. Therefore, similarly to the multiple redundant pathways involved in the “housekeeping” DNA damage repair11,12, T-DNA integration could occur by several concurrent mechanisms rather than via a unique pathway13. Recently, the role of polymerase θ mediated DNA repair (MMEJ) was suggested as one of the major T-DNA integration pathways in plant14.

Furthermore, some pathogenic bacteria are known to promote damage to the DNA of their eukaryotic host cells15,16; however, it remains unclear whether this damage results from the action of specific virulence factors or from the general stress induced by the infection. In the case of Agrobacterium, its infection of plants causes an increase in the expression of several genes diagnostic for the cellular response to DSBs and genotoxic stress17,18. Interestingly, this transcriptional effect was largely dependent on the presence of the virulence (vir) region—encoding most of the bacterial genes required for the Agrobacterium-mediated T-DNA transfer—but did not require the T-DNA itself17.

We endeavored to investigate the Agrobacterium-induced DNA damage and regulation of DNA repair genes in a plant host to gain insights into how Agrobacterium may manipulate its host to mediate the T-DNA integration. Specifically, we addressed two questions: does Agrobacterium directly promote the formation of DSBs in the host genome? Which bacterial factors are required for the increase in the DNA repair-related gene expression observed upon Agrobacterium inoculation? To assess the ability of Agrobacterium to induce and repair DSBs in the host plant genome, we monitored the levels of the phosphorylated variant of histone 2AX (gamma-H2AX) and transcriptional activation of several components of DNA repair machinery. H2AX is a marker of DNA breakage, and its phosphorylation represents a very early response to the induction of DNA damage, crucial for DNA damage signaling and for the assembly of the DNA repair foci19–21. Previously, we showed that Agrobacterium infection transcriptionally activates several host genes involved in DNA repair that represent reliable genetic markers of DNA damage response17. Among these genes, we selected three, NAC82, KU70, and AGO2, because their expression was consistently induced upon the wild-type Agrobacterium infection17,18, and used them as biological reporters to examine whether the export of Vir proteins from Agrobacterium to the plant cell may be involved in activation of these genes. NAC82 (known as NAC103 in Arabidopsis) is involved in the signaling of DNA damage response, acting as a transcription factor that induces the expression of several other DNA repair genes22. Ku70 is involved in the classical NHEJ pathway and is crucial to protect DNA breaks and recruit repair factors23. AGO2 is induced upon DNA damage and plays a role in DNA repair by recruiting RAD51; it is also involved in defense reaction against pathogenic viruses and bacteria, including Agrobacterium24–26. To examine whether the transcriptional response was caused by the translocation of Agrobacterium effector proteins into the host cells, we utilized an Agrobacterium virB5 mutant strain, C58-virB5. VirB5 is a minor component of the bacterial T-pilus, an essential part of the type 4 secretion system (T4SS) composed mostly of VirB2 molecules and responsible for the export of macromolecules, i.e. the VirD2-T-DNA complex as well as several effector proteins, from Agrobacterium to its host cell. The virB5 mutant is specifically disabled in the transfer of T-DNA and protein effectors from the bacterial to the host cell during infection and, thus, is avirulent27,28. The resulting data suggest that (i) Agrobacterium infection does not induce a major increase in DSB content in the host genome, (ii) the virulence (Vir) B5 protein, a minor component of the bacterial type 4 secretion system (T4SS) pilus—required for translocation of Agrobacterium effector proteins into the host cell—was necessary for induction of the expression of the host DNA repair-related genes, and (iii) transient over-expression of the bacterial VirD5 effector protein resulted in necrosis of the expressing plant tissues albeit we could not establish whether or not this effect was linked to an increase in DNA damage.

We infiltrated tobacco (Nicotiana tabacum) leaves with each of the following three Agrobacterium strains: C58, a wild-type strain that contains both the vir region and the T-DNA; C58.C1, a cured C58 strain without a Ti-plasmid, i.e., no vir region and no T-DNA; and C58.C1-pMP90, a C58.C1 strain with the helper plasmid pMP90 that contains the vir region but no T-DNA. For Agrobacterium-independent controls, the same leaves were mock-infiltrated with buffer. The infection process was terminated at different time points, i.e., 6, 24, 48, and 72 h post infiltration (hpi), by freezing the infiltrated tissues in liquid nitrogen, nuclear proteins were extracted and analyzed by western blotting. Figure 1B shows that a basal level of gamma-H2AX was readily detected in Agrobacterium-infiltrated as well as in mock-infiltrated tissues at all tested time points (top panels, lanes Ag and Mo, respectively); again, equal sample loading was confirmed by Coomassie blue staining (bottom panels). Interestingly, we did not detect clear differences in the amounts of the gamma-H2AX mark between all three strains of Agrobacterium and between the Agrobacterium-infiltrated and mock-infiltrated tissues. Thus, inoculation of tobacco plants with Agrobacterium most likely does not elicit significant global damage to the host genomic DNA.

Agrobacterium-to-plant cell translocation of Vir effector proteins is involved in increased expression of the host DNA repair-related genes

The RT-qPCR analysis showed that the infiltration of tobacco leaves with the wild-type, infectious C58 strain indeed promoted a statistically significant transcriptional activation of the NAC82, KU70, and AGO2 genes, compared to the mock-infiltrated tissues, i.e., an approximately two-fold increase in the NAC82 and KU70 expression and a tenfold increase in the expression of AGO2 (Fig. 2). These increases in expression largely did not occur when the plants were infiltrated with the C58-virB5 mutant; specifically, the differences in the NAC82, KU70, and AGO2 gene expression elicited by C58-virB5 as compared to C58 were statistically significant whereas the slight increase in gene expression relative to the mock infiltration controls was not statistically significant (Fig. 2). Transient expression of VirE2, VirE3, VirF, and VirD2 effectors does not significantly affect DNA repair-related gene expression and global levels of gamma-H2AX

We investigated whether transient expression of individual A. tumefaciens effector proteins, i.e., the Vir proteins exported into the host cell, could induce DNA breaks in the infected plant cells. For that purpose, we infiltrated tobacco leaves with a disarmed Agrobacterium strain EHA105 carrying binary plasmids with expression cassettes for each of the exported bacterial Vir effectors, i.e., VirE2, VirE3, VirD2, and VirF, and analyzed the expressing tissues at 72 hpi, when we usually observe the highest levels of Agrobacterium-mediated transient gene expression in tobacco. These infiltrations were performed in parallel with infiltrations of EHA105 carrying a GFP expression construct as a negative control for potential effects of all the components of the expression vector except the tested vir gene.

The expression of the virE2, virE3, virD2, and virF genes in the corresponding infiltrated tissues was verified by RT-PCR, with no detectable signal in plant tissues infiltrated with the control strain carrying a GFP expression construct (Fig. 4A, bottom panel); as expected, no differences in expression were observed with the constitutive control actin gene (ACT9) (Fig. 4A, top panel). The RT-qPCR experiments showed that expression of these four vir effector genes did not significantly activate the expression of NAC82, KU70, and AGO2 and even caused a slight decrease in expression in some instances (Fig. 4B). Finally, nuclear proteins were extracted from the infiltrated areas and analyzed by western blotting using anti-gamma-H2AX antibodies. Two negative controls were used for these experiments: mock infiltration or infiltration with EHA105 carrying a binary plasmid with a GFP expression cassette. Discussion

While the roles of the different pathways of DNA repair in plants in Agrobacterium T-DNA integration is still debated10, we focused on a different aspect of the integration process, i.e., the interrelationship between Agrobacterium infection and the potential induction of DNA damage and activation of DNA repair machinery and the possible roles of bacterial Vir effectors in these processes. Thus, we monitored the phosphorylation of H2AX, i.e., the generation of the gamma-H2AX mark of the cellular DNA damage, and expression of three DNA repair-related gene markers after agroinfiltration of tobacco leaves, a system in which the Agrobacterium T-DNA is efficiently transferred to the host cells without the overwhelming stress for the host plant.

Our western blot analyses of the total nuclear gamma-H2AX content in the infiltrated tissues detected no significant changes upon infiltration of the wild-type Agrobacterium strains as compared to the same strains that have been disarmed or cured of their T-DNA or the entire Ti plasmid, respectively. These findings suggest that Agrobacterium infiltration does not elicit a major amount of DNA damage irrespective of the presence of the vir region and/or the T-DNA. It is important to note that this lack of change in the global amounts of gamma-H2AX may mask more targeted and/or very transient changes in the infected cells. Thus, it cannot be excluded that a small number of DNA breaks, such as DSBs, was caused in the infected cells in a very targeted manner, that would not be detected by western blotting of the infiltrated tissues. Such a minor induction of DNA breaks would be consistent with the induction of the DNA repair-related gene expression consistently observed in response to Agrobacterium inoculation17.

To assess the potential role of translocated Agrobacterium effector proteins, we investigated the requirement for the bacterial T-pilus—which mediates the Agrobacterium-to-plant cell macromolecular transport—for the Agrobacterium-induced activation of the DNA repair-related genes. The involvement of the bacterial pilus is evidenced by our observations of the substantially reduced ability of the virB5 mutant Agrobacterium strain to activate the expression of the DNA repair gene markers NAC82, KU70, and AGO2 as compared to its parental, wild-type strain. Indeed, VirB5, an essential Vir protein, is a minor component of the T-pilus37, which is required for the Vir effector export from the bacterial cell38. VirB5 interacts with VirB2 at the tip of the pilus and participates in its biogenesis and stabilization39. This VirB5 function appears conserved in different T4SS systems because the Helicobacter pylori VirB5 homolog, CagL, also interacts with the T-pilus and facilitates the translocation of effector proteins40. In our hypothesis, mutation of any of the essential genes forming T4SS, i.e., the virB operon and virD4, would exert a similar effect on the marker gene expression by blocking the export of effector protein from Agrobacterium to the plant cell. We chose to use the virB5 mutant because, as a minor component of the T-pilus, VirB5 has minimal effect on the T4SS structure.

The absence of T-DNA does not impair the activation of expression of the DNA repair genes by disarmed Agrobacterium strains17. Thus, most likely it is the effector proteins translocated to the host cell that mediate activation of expression of these genes. Here, we focused on understanding whether the translocated Agrobacterium Vir effectors can promote, individually and independently of each other, activation of the host DNA repair-related genes. Because no such independent effector activity was observed, at least some of the tested Vir effectors may be functionally redundant in their ability to activate the host DNA repair machinery. Alternatively, we cannot rule out the existence of another, as yet unknown, bacterial effector translocated through the T-pilus.

Transient expression of the individual Vir effectors was also useful to examine whether or not the lack of apparent DNA damage may be due to insufficient amounts of the effector proteins translocated during infiltration. However, none of the known Vir effectors, i.e., VirD2, VirD5, VirE2, VirE3, and VirF, individually over-expressed in plant tissues promoted substantial changes in the total levels of the gamma-H2AX mark. In the case of VirD5, necrosis of the infiltrated plant tissues was observed at 72 hpi, and it was correlated with higher levels of virD5 expression. This is consistent with previous studies showing the toxicity of VirD5 for yeast and plant cells41,42. For example, the over-expression of VirD5 inhibits the growth of Arabidopsis seedlings, and it causes replication stress and DNA damage in yeast41,42. Yet, in our experiments, we did not detect elevated gamma-H2AX levels and observed only a very limited increase in DNA damage-related gene transcription in virD5 over-expressing tissues in comparison to the infiltration control. Thus, the effects of Vir effectors on the host genome in plants may differ from those in yeast. Because of the necrosis occurring at 72 hpi in the virD5 over-expressing tissues, these tissues are not amenable to meaningful analyses; on the other hand, at 48 hpi, the results obtained might represent only the initial stages of the plant responses to the VirD5 effector.

In conclusion, our data suggest that Agrobacterium activates the expression of DNA repair-related genes in the host cells and that this ability involves one or more of the Vir effector proteins exported into the host cells through the T-pilus. Although the activation of the DNA repair machinery suggests concomitant DNA damage, this damage is not sufficient to alter the homeostasis of the nuclear gamma-H2AX histone, a marker of DNA damage. Thus, it is also possible that the transcriptional activation of the DNA repair genes by Agrobacterium may represent an aspect of the host–pathogen interaction unrelated to the generation of direct damage to the host genome.

Materials and methods

Agrobacterium strains

The wild-type and mutant strains of A. tumefaciens used in our experiments are listed in Table S1. Mutants in virE2, virD2, and virD5 in the A348 background were generated by the suicide vector method, as described31,43. The suicide plasmid pEX18Km44 was obtained from Dr. Shen Q. Pan (National University of Singapore); the sequences of the primers used to insert these three genes into pEX18Km are listed in Table S3. For verification of the mutations, plasmid DNA was extracted from wild-type A348 and the three mutant strains and analyzed by PCR using ExTAq polymerase (Takara) and gene-specific primers (Table S2). The virE3 mutant and its parental R10 strain were a kind gift from Dr. Stephen Winans (Cornell University, Ithaca, NY).

Binary plasmids for Vir protein over-expression in planta

For transient expression of Agrobacterium pTiC58 virE2 and pTiA6 virE3 and virF, the following binary plasmids were used: pBAR-GFP as negative control, pBAR-virE2, pBAR-virE3, and pBAR-virF, carrying expression cassettes for the EGFP, virE2, virE3, virF genes, respectively, under the control of the CaMV 35S promoter and terminator. These expression cassettes were inserted into the pBAR plasmid as a HindIII segment from the plasmids pRTL2-GFP generated by inserting EGFP as NcoI-BamHI PCR segment into pRTL2-GUS45 instead of GUS; pRTL2-virE2 generated by inserting virE2 as NcoI-BglII PCR segment into pRTL2-GUS instead of GUS. The plasmids pRTL2-virE346, and pRTL2-virF47 have been described.

For transient expression of Agrobacterium pTiA6 virD2 and virD5, the following binary plasmids were used: pRCS2-GFP28, pRCS2-virD2 and pRCS2-virD5, carrying expression cassettes for DsRed2, virD2, and virD5 genes, under the control of the CaMV 35S promoter and terminator. Expression cassettes from pSAT6-GFP48, pSAT1A-virD2 generated by inserting virD2 as BamH1 PCR-amplified segment into pSAT1A49, and pSAT5-virD5 generated by inserting virD5 as NcoI-KpnI PCR-amplified segment into pSAT548 were cloned into the binary vector pRCS248 as Pi-PSPI, AscI and I-CeuI segments, respectively.

Tobacco leaf infiltration

All infiltrations were performed in equivalent areas on both sides of the central vein and between secondary veins (see Fig. S2) of fully developed, ca. 15–20-cm long, leaves of 4 to 6-week-old tobacco plants (Nicotiana tabacum cv. Turk) maintained in a controlled environment growth chamber at 23˚C with relative humidity of 50% to 60% under a 16:8-h ratio of light to dark photoperiod with the light intensity of 100 μmol photons m−2 s−1. For bleomycin infiltration, bleomycin (Bleocin, Millipore 203408) stock solution was diluted in MES buffer (10 mM MgCl2, 10 mM MES, pH 5.6) to a final concentration of 20 ng l−1. For Agrobacterium infiltration, bacterial strains were grown overnight at 28 °C, 240 rpm in LB supplemented with the appropriate antibiotics. The bacterial suspension (1.0 ml) was diluted in 4 ml of LB and incubated for 1 h at 28 °C, 250 rpm, centrifuged, and the bacterial pellet was resuspended in MES to OD600nm = 0.5 and incubated for 2 h, 200 rpm, at room temperature. For the Agrobacterium strain carrying the virD5 expression construct, OD600nm was adjusted at either 0.5 or 0.025. For negative, mock controls, the infiltrations were performed using the MES buffer. The locations of the infiltration sites on a leaf for each experiment are illustrated in Fig. S1. The tested solutions or bacterial cultures were infiltrated into tobacco leaves using a needleless 1-ml syringe. The infiltrated tissue areas were harvested at the indicated periods post-infiltration (24, 48, or 72 hpi), snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 °C.

Antibodies

Antibody against gamma-H2AX was custom-made (Genscript). Rabbits were immunized with a 10-amino-acid-long phospho-S139 peptide that includes the phosphorylated Ser139 residue, CGDIGSA{pSer}QEF. This sequence corresponds to the 10 carboxyl-terminal amino acid residues of H2AX and is identical in Arabidopsis and tobacco species. Antibodies were purified on an affinity column with the immobilized phospho-S139 peptide, and non-phospho-specific antibodies were removed by a passage through an affinity column with the immobilized unphosphorylated S139 peptide.

Western blot analysis

Frozen tobacco leaf tissue (200 mg) was ground in liquid nitrogen, using a mortar and pestle, and crude nuclear protein extract was prepared as described50. The supernatant, containing nuclear proteins, was aliquoted and stored at − 20 °C. Extract samples (20 μl/lane) were resolved on a 15% SDS polyacrylamide gel at 80 V for ca. 2 h, and proteins were either stained with Coomassie blue or transferred onto a nylon membrane by semi-dry transfer at 25 V for 40 min. After blocking for 1 h in TBST supplemented with 5% BSA, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-gamma-H2AX diluted 1/2000 in TBST with 1% BSA. Following incubation for 6 h at 4 °C with anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody coupled to horseradish peroxidase (HRP, Sigma H31440) diluted 1/2500 in TBST with 1% BSA, the membrane was treated with a chemiluminescent HRP substrate (Millipore Immobilon, WBKLS0050), and analyzed by autoradiography (Thomas Scientific, 1141J52). All western blot experiments were done in at least two biological replicates.

RNA extraction and cDNA synthesis

Total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy plant mini Kit (Qiagen, 79504) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The concentration and purity of total RNA were verified by spectrophotometry. DNase treatment was done by incubating 2 μg of total RNA in a total 20 μl volume, containing 2 μl DNase I buffer and 0.4 μl DNase I (NEB, M0303) for 30 min at 37 °C, after which DNase was inactivated by addition of 2 μl of 50 mM EDTA followed by a 10-min incubation at 75 °C. RNA preparation was aliquoted and stored at − 80 °C. Reverse transcription was performed using the RevertAid RT Kit (Thermo Scientific, K1691) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

RT-PCR

For verification of transient expression of the vir genes in planta, cDNA samples were analyzed by PCR using ExTAq polymerase (Takara), primers specific for each tested gene, or for tobacco actin (ACT9) as validated constitutive control (Table S2). The PCR products were resolved by agarose gel electrophoresis.

Quantitative PCR (qPCR)

The reaction mixture was composed of 1 μl of 4-times diluted cDNA (actin control) or non-diluted cDNA (other tested genes), 10 μl of 2 × Power SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Thermo Scientific, 4368706), 0.4 μl each of forward and reverse primers (Table S2), and 8.2 μl H2O. qPCR was performed with a QuantStudio 3 thermocycler (Thermo Scientific), with one cycle of 10 min at 95 °C, 40 cycles of 30 s at 95 °C, and 1 min at 60 °C. The specificity of amplification using these primers was verified previously by agarose gel electrophoresis and melting curves17. Each sample was analyzed in two biological replicates, or three biological replicates for the virD5 over-expression experiments, each consisting of three technical replicates, using the validated constitutive reference actin gene ACT9 to normalize qPCR data by the comparative Ct method; the relative transcript levels were calculated by the cycle threshold (CT) 2−ΔΔCt method51.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative data are presented as averages with standard deviation values. Statistical significance was calculated by the paired t-test method using GraphPad Prism 7, with p-values < 0.05 or 0.01 corresponding to the statistical probability of 95% or 99%, respectively, considered statistically significant.
