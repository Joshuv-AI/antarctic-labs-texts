Adaptor-mediated interaction between Kv1.3 and Nedd4-2 E3 ubiquitin ligase
Authors: Irene Estadella, Anna Benavente-Garcia, Jesusa Capera, Judith Robles-Prieto, Antonio Felipe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The voltage-gated potassium channel Kv1.3 is crucial for immune responses. During proinflammatory stimulation, Kv1.3 is upregulated, enhancing Ca²⁺ signaling and leukocyte activation. To prevent prolonged lymphocyte activity, excess Kv1.3 must be removed from the plasma membrane, as elevated levels are linked to chronic inflammation. The ubiquitin E3 ligase Nedd4-2 is a key negative regulator that promotes Kv1.3 degradation through ubiquitination and lysosomal targeting. Because Kv1.3 lacks canonical PY motifs required for Nedd4-2 binding, adaptor proteins are necessary. Our findings show that Ndfip1 and specific 14-3-3 proteins facilitate the Nedd4-2–Kv1.3 interaction. Following PKC activation, a rapid, transient association between Kv1.3 and Nedd4-2 occurs near the membrane, initiating ubiquitination, vesicular internalization, and lysosomal degradation. This work identifies Nedd4-2 adaptors that mediate Kv1.3 regulation, highlighting Ndfip1 as a key factor while the roles of individual 14-3-3 isoforms remain to be clarified.

Introduction

The ubiquitin (UBQ) system modulates various cellular processes, including the ubiquitin-mediated degradation of ion channels1. This process requires the sequential action of three enzymes: E1 (UBQ-activating enzyme), E2 (UBQ-conjugating enzyme) and E3 (UBQ-ligases). E3 UBQ-ligases specifically recognize the target protein for ubiquitination. The main E3 UBQ ligase groups are (i) HECT-type E3 ligases, which contain a HECT (homologous to the E6-AP COOH terminus) domain that forms a thioester bond with UBQ to ubiquitinate the target protein, and (ii) RING (really interesting new gene) finger E3 ligases, which contain a RING finger domain, acting as adaptor proteins for E2 UBQ-conjugating enzymes and facilitating the transfer of UBQ from E2 to the target. The Nedd4-like family, belonging to the first group, has a catalytic HECT domain at the C-terminus, which binds to phospholipids in a calcium-dependent manner, and 2–4 tandem WW (tryptophan) domains, which facilitate binding to specific substrates. WW domains interact with conserved PY motifs (PPXY or LPXY sequences) or proline-rich regions in target proteins1.

Nedd4/Nedd4-like protein family members are major negative regulators of ion channels1. Ion channels such as ENaC (epithelial sodium channel)2, CLC-2 (chloride voltage-gated channel 2)3, some Nav (voltage-gated sodium channels) and various Kv (voltage-gated potassium channels), including Kv7.1, Kv7.2/Kv7.3, Kv11.1, Kv1.5 and Kv1.3, are regulated by Nedd4-21,4–7. An altered function of Nedd4-2 is implicated in hypertensive disorders such as Liddle’s syndrome, epilepsy, neuropathic pain and long QT syndrome8–10.

ENaC is the best-studied example of an ion channel ubiquitinated by Nedd4-22. The C-terminal region of the channel contains a PY motif (PPXYXXФ), which overlaps with a tyrosine-based motif (YXXФ) related to clathrin-mediated endocytosis. Mutation of the ENaC PY motif causes Liddle’s syndrome, an inherited hypertension disorder triggered by increased channel activity8. The PY motif of ENaC recruits Nedd4-2 and downregulates channel surface expression via ubiquitination. The loss of the PY-binding motif reduces the ubiquitination of ENaC and increases the number of channels at the plasma membrane. Interestingly, serum and glucocorticoid-regulated kinase (SGK), a downstream mediator of aldosterone, reduces the binding of Nedd4-2 to ENaC by phosphorylating Nedd4-2, increasing ENaC cell surface expression and regulating epithelial Na+ absorption2. In this sense, many Navs and some Kvs contain PY motifs in the C-terminus. Nedd4-2, by binding to these PY motifs, reduces their cell-surface density and hence their function1,8.

The voltage-gated Kv1.3 channel, which is essential for leukocyte physiology, is negatively regulated by Nedd4-2 but contains no PY motifs6,7. Evidence has demonstrated that PKC- and EGFR-dependent activation triggers UBQ-dependent Kv1.3 endocytosis via Nedd4-211,12. The results map two UBQ-target lysine clusters at the N- and C-terminal domains of the channel13. Accordingly, Nedd4-2 silencing impairs PKC-mediated Kv1.3 endocytosis12. In this context, the coexpression of Kv1.3 with the ubiquitin E3 ligase Nedd4-2 decreases the Kv1.3 current, suggesting a reduction in membrane channel abundance7. Furthermore, SGK1 upregulates Kv1.3, inactivating Nedd4-2 through phosphorylation6.

Nedd4-2 activity is related to numerous human pathologies, including cancer14. In addition, an aberrant function of Kv1.3 has been documented in autoimmune diseases and tumor progression15. Thus, once the immune response is initiated, the removal of active Kv1.3 channels from the cell surface is essential. Impairments in this mechanism cause the persistence of Kv1.3 at the plasma membrane, triggering a sustained response and promoting a proinflammatory scenario13. Nevertheless, Kv1.3 lacks PY motifs, and information about how the interaction with Nedd4-2 takes place is lacking. Thus far, evidence suggests an alternative binding mechanism or the presence of unknown intermediate adaptors facilitating the interaction between both proteins7. In the present work, we elucidated the mechanism of ubiquitination and subsequent degradation of Kv1.3 by Nedd4-2. Using Ndfip1 (Nedd4 family interacting protein 1) as an intermediate adaptor, Nedd4-2 ubiquitinates Kv1.3 indirectly. Additionally, adaptor proteins, such as the 14-3-3 family, are also identified. Evidence supports that both groups of proteins, which participate in several channel ubiquitination mechanisms, are involved in the physiological immune response by controlling leukocyte physiology. Our work highlights these proteins as new therapeutic targets for the treatment of autoimmune diseases and cancer.

Results

PKC activation triggers ubiquitin-dependent Kv1.3 endocytosis mediated by Nedd4-2 ligase

Nedd4-2 regulates Kv1.3, and PKC activation triggers Kv1.3 endocytosis through a mechanism involving Nedd4-26,7,12. Therefore, we first analyzed whether PKC-induced Kv1.3 internalization occurred through a ubiquitin-dependent mechanism (Fig. 1). HEK293 cells expressing Kv1.3 exhibited voltage-gated K⁺ currents that were significantly reduced following a 30-min incubation with 1 µM PMA. As previously reported, this PMA-induced decrease in K⁺ currents was dependent on protein kinase C (PKC) activation, since co-incubation with bisindolylmaleimide (BIM) effectively prevented the effect (Fig. 1A). In parallel, PKC activation markedly altered the subcellular distribution of Kv1.3. Whereas Kv1.3 was predominantly localized at the plasma membrane, as indicated by wheat germ agglutinin (WGA) staining, PMA treatment promoted robust colocalization of Kv1.3 with the early endosomal marker EEA1 (Supplementary Fig. 1). After incubation with PMA, biotinylation assays (Fig. 1 B, C) and confocal microscopy (Fig. 1 D, E) revealed that although the abundance of Kv1.3 at the plasma membrane decreased, the number of channels situated in intracellular endocytic vesicles increased. The phosphorylation of CREB (p-CREB) demonstrated the PMA-induced PKC activation. PMA-induced Kv1.3 internalization correlated with a 4-fold increase in channel ubiquitination (Fig. 1 F, G). PKC activation induces a transient increase in the Kv1.3–Nedd4-2 interaction

Kv1.3 and Nedd4-2 and E3 ligase mediate ubiquitination-dependent channel endocytosis. Therefore, we wondered whether PKC activation affects this interaction. Coimmunoprecipitation experiments with Kv1.3 and Nedd4-2 revealed that after 15 min of incubation with 1 µM PMA, Nedd4-2 interacted less strongly with Kv1.3 (Fig. 3A). Further colocalization studies between Kv1.3 and Nedd4-2 were performed under a PMA time course (Fig. 3B). In the absence of insult, Kv1.3 was efficiently distributed at the plasma membrane (Fig. 3Ba), whereas Nedd4-2 was mostly intracellular (Fig. 3Bb). PMA incubation steadily activated Kv1.3 endocytosis. Intracellular vesicles, which were stained with Kv1.3, were apparent after 15 min of PMA incubation (Fig. 3Bi–l). No changes were observed in the Nedd4-2 pattern, which remained cytoplasmic throughout the entire PMA time course. Further analysis revealed that after 5 min of PMA, an early but transitory increase in Kv1.3–Nedd4-2 colocalization was apparent (Fig. 3C). Nedd4-2 interacts via the N- and C-terminal domains of Kv1.3 but requires adaptors

Nedd4-2 interacts with targets via PY motifs exposed either at the N- or C-terminal domains of the channels. However, Kv1.3 has no apparent PY motifs7. Therefore, we wanted to decipher the molecular determinants involved in the Nedd4-2 interaction. For that purpose, some Kv1.3 mutants lacking the amino (ΔN)- and carboxy (ΔC)-terminal domains were used (Fig. 4A). Interestingly, coimmunoprecipitation and FRET experiments clearly yield discrepant results. Therefore, to further characterize the physical interaction between Kv1.3 and Nedd4-2, a PLA was performed (Fig. 5C). Positive PLA staining revealed that the molecular proximity of Kv1.3 and Nedd4-2 was < 40 nm17, even in the absence of PMA. Discrete PLA signals were detected in the cytoplasm in the absence of PMA. However, after 5 min of PMA incubation, the PLA spots were closer to the cell surface, suggesting that the interaction between Kv1.3 and Nedd4-2, induced by PMA, occurred mostly near the plasma membrane (Fig. 5Cf–j). This pattern was transient and disappeared after 15 min of PMA incubation when the PLA signal returned to the cytoplasm (Fig. 5 Ck–o). These results are in accordance with early and transient approximation between Kv1.3 and Nedd4-2 upon stimulation with PMA (Fig. 3). Our evidence suggested the absence of a physical interaction between Kv1.3–Nedd4-2. Although positive FRET indicated distances < 10 nm18,19, the physical proximity between these proteins was between 10 and 40 nm, which suggests the involvement of adaptor proteins.

Members of the 14-3-3 family participate in the ubiquitination of several ion channels24,28. Various consensus motifs bind 14-3-3 proteins to phosphorylated targets29,30. Thus, RSXpS/TXP (where X is not proline and pS/T represents phosphorylated Ser or Thr) is present at the C-terminal domain of Kv1.3 (469RSNSTLS475) (Supplementary Fig. 2A). To ascertain whether this motif mediated the 14-3-3 binding to Kv1.3, and in turn, was involved in Kv1.3 endocytosis, we mutated arginine and serine residues to alanine (R469A, S470,472,475 A; R/S → A). No differences between Kv1.3 WT and the R/S → A mutant were observed in the cellular staining, and both channels were similarly internalized after 30 min of PMA incubation (Supplementary Fig. 2B, C). These results indicate that the Kv1.3 469RSNSTLS475 sequence is not responsible for the 14-3-3 interaction with the channel. Moreover, the R/S → A mutant coimmunoprecipitated with 14-3-3 (Supplementary Fig. 2D). Therefore, other domains must be involved in this interaction. In this sense, coimmunoprecipitations of Kv1.3 WT and the ΔN and ΔC Kv1.3 mutants with 14-3-3 were performed (Supplementary Fig. 2E, F). Similar to what observed with Nedd4-2 and Ndfip1, 14-3-3 proteins coimmunoprecipitated with both Kv1.3 ΔN and ΔC, suggesting that the Kv1.3 association to 14-3-3 would be via interaction with either the C- or the N-terminal tail. Like Ndfip1, the absence of the N- or C-terminus of Kv1.3 increased 14-3-3 coimmunoprecipitation with the channel, which suggests that a compensatory effect drives defective mutant channels to degrade more efficiently.

Proteomic evidence highlights 14-3-3γ as a major, but not unique, interacting isoform with Kv1.320–22. Thus, to study the possible role of 14-3-3γ in PKC-dependent endocytosis, specific 14-3-3γ silencing by siRNA was performed (Supplementary Fig. 2G). However, no differences were observed by confocal microscopy when 14-3-3γ was silenced (Supplementary Fig. 2 H). Seven 14-3-3 isoforms are expressed in mammals, and 6 out of these 7 are present in the Kv1.3 interactome20–22. Therefore, the complementary roles of other isoforms, plus the endogenous Ndfip1 function, must be contemplated.

Discussion

The number of Kv1.3 channels at the cell surface is essential for an accurate immune response. Upon insult, the channel initiates a physiological trigger. However, anomalous persistent signals promote proinflammatory inputs, often culminating in inflammatory diseases15,31. Therefore, orchestrated channel removal from the plasma membrane is essential. In such a scenario, the role of the Nedd4-2 E3 ligase, which mediates ubiquitination-dependent Kv1.3 endocytosis, is crucial. Our results highlight the association between Nedd4-2 and Kv1.3. The channel did not physically interact with Nedd4-2, but considering their molecular proximity ( < 40 nm), the presence of adaptor proteins was apparent. Our results suggested that both the N- and C-termini of the channel were involved in such interactions. The Kv1.3 interactome indicated that the Ndfip1 and 14-3-3 proteins are strong adaptor candidates. Both 14-3-3 and Nfdip1 interacted with the amino- and carboxy-terminal domains of Kv1.3. However, although the roles of the entire 14-3-3 family remain uncertain, partial elimination of Ndfip1 decreased Kv1.3 endocytosis, emerging as a partner functioning in channel degradation. Our evidence demonstrated that Nedd4-2 uses adaptor proteins for Kv1.3 ubiquitination and endocytosis.

The ubiquitin E3 ligase Nedd4-2 is a major negative regulator of ion channels32,33. Nedd4-2 ubiquitinates targets via recognition of the PY motif (PPXY or LPXY) or proline-rich regions33. ENaC is the best-studied example of an ion channel ubiquitinated by Nedd4-2. The C-terminus of the channel contains a PY motif. Mutation of this motif causes Liddle’s syndrome, which is characterized by increased channel activity8. The loss of the PY-binding motif reduces the ubiquitination of ENaC and increases the number of channels at the plasma membrane. Using these PY-binding motifs, Nedd4-2 also ubiquitinates Nav, Kv7.1 and hERG channels1,4,26,32,34,35. However, the presence of a PY motif is not mandatory for Nedd4-2 activity and Kv1.36,7, Kv1.536, Kir1.137, CLC-23 and TRPV538 channels are some examples. Kv1.3 is downregulated by Nedd4-27. Our data suggest that Nedd4-2 is involved in PKC-induced Kv1.3 endocytosis. Specific Nedd4-2 siRNA silencing decreased the number of Kv1.3 intracellular vesicles upon PMA incubation. PMA incubation triggered an early and transitory increase in Kv1.3–Nedd4-2 association near the membrane surface. Longer PMA treatments ( > 15 min) triggered weaker associations. Concomitantly, PKC-mediated Kv1.3 ubiquitination and PLA staining followed a similar pattern. Therefore, Kv1.3 transiently associated with Nedd4-2, initiating the endocytic signaling pathway. In this scenario, FRET analysis ruled out direct molecular interactions ( < 10 nm) between Kv1.3 and Nedd4-2; however, PLA revealed that both proteins were in close proximity ( < 40 nm). The combination of negative FRET efficiency and positive PLA signal indicated the presence of adaptor proteins mediating the interaction. Kv1.3 interactome analysis revealed 14-3-3 peptides and Nedd4 family-interacting protein 1 (Ndfip1) as major candidates. Our data further supported that, similar to other ion channels, either the N- or C-terminal domain of Kv1.3 would be involved in the association with Nedd4-2 (Fig. 8)7,34,39,40. Although a minor participation of intracellular loops of the channel should not be discounted, the molecular mass of Nedd4-2/adaptors complex plus the accessibility of these motifs to protein‒protein interactions in the architecture functional tetramer yield a very unlikely scenario41. Moreover, Kv1.3 contains two lysine clusters at the N- and C-terminal domains, which would facilitate an effective ubiquitination of the channel13. Kv1.3 ubiquitination and endocytosis are highly related because the annihilation of those motifs13 clearly affects the endocytosis of the channel (Supplementary Movie 2). Interestingly, at each end of the channel, there is also a proline-rich domain (PRD) motif, which recognizes SH3 domains. Nevertheless, mutation of the PRD motifs did not impair either EGF-mediated channel endocytosis or the Kv1.3–Nedd4-2 interaction7,11. Ndfip1, which contains two PY motifs at the cytoplasmic N-terminal end, facilitates the binding of Nedd4-2 ligase to its target protein27. Ndfip1 recruits Nedd4-2 for the ubiquitination of divalent metal transporter 1 (DMT1), resulting in reduced metal entry in human neurons42. Similarly, Ndfip1 recruits the TrkB receptor for Nedd4-2 ubiquitination, resulting in its lysosomal degradation43. In addition, Ndfip1, together with Ndfip2, specifically interacts with aquaporin 2 (AQP2), enabling its ubiquitination and degradation by Nedd4-1 and Nedd4-2 ligases27. Moreover, although Nedd4-2 directly ubiquitinates hERG via a PY motif located in the C-terminus of the channel, Ndfip1 and 2 recruit and activate Nedd4-2 in the Golgi apparatus, mediating the degradation of hERG proteins during channel trafficking to the plasma membrane26. Moreover, Ndfip1 plays an essential role in immune cell function. Ndfip1-KO mice exhibit complex dysregulation of T-cell activation, with severe inflammatory skin and lung phenotypes44. Evidence reveals that Ndfip1 is required for the regulation of T-cell activation through the regulation of IL-2 and CD25 levels45. In addition, Ndfip1 regulates T-cell activation by exiting CD4+ T cells from their cell cycle46. In this scenario, we demonstrated that Ndfip1 interacts with both Kv1.3 and Nedd4-2. Through Ndfip1-mediated Nedd4-2-dependent Kv1.3 ubiquitination, persistent inflammatory signaling is terminated to fine-tune the immune response.

Annihilation of Ndfip1 only partially decreased the number of Kv1.3 intracellular vesicles, highlighting the contribution of alternative adaptors. 14-3-3 proteins are a ubiquitous family involved in several signaling pathways and function as adaptors, scaffolds and chaperones for more than 200 targets47. Kv1.3 KO mice have increased expression levels of 14-3-3 proteins48. 14-3-3 interacted with both Kv1.3 and Nedd4-249. Indeed, 14-3-3 promotes Nedd4-2-mediated ubiquitination of the Kir4.1 channel28. In this sense, 14-3-3 proteins bind to certain pSer/pThr-containing motifs50, but annihilation of such motifs in the C-terminus of Kv1.3 counteracted neither the PKC-mediated endocytosis of the channel nor the 14-3-3 interaction. However, similar to Ndfip1, the absence of either the N or C terminus of the channel increased 14-3-3 coimmunoprecipitation. In fact, both truncated channels, which are aberrant in function, exhibited reduced expression. It is tempting to speculate that the number of isoforms (6 out of 7 found in the Kv1.3 interactome) increased the difficulty of the study, and although we silenced the gamma isoform (14-3-3γ, which was one of the isoforms with more unique peptides in the proteomics analysis), we did not observe any changes in Kv1.3 endocytosis because not only the other 14-3-3 isoforms but also Ndfip1 would still be functional.

Overall, we demonstrated that PKC activation induced Nedd4-2-dependent adaptor-mediated Kv1.3 ubiquitination and degradation. Kv1.3 and Nedd4-2 are not directly associated, and Ndfip1 has emerged as an adaptor. Although Ndfip1 clearly mediates Nedd4-2-dependent Kv1.3 ubiquitination, the specific role of 14-3-3 proteins in Kv1.3 turnover has not been fully deciphered. Moreover, our work also demonstrated that the interaction between Kv1.3, which has two lysine clusters at either the N- or C-terminal domain13, and Nedd4-2 adaptors also occurs at both ends of the channel. Such associations affect other protein interactions, such as forward trafficking, targeting and membrane stabilization, ensuring efficient turnover of the channel and fine-tuning the physiological Kv1.3-associated trigger during the immune response.

Materials And Methods

Expression plasmids and multisite-directed mutagenesis

Rat Kv1.3 was inserted into pEYFP-C1 (Clontech) by using BglII and HinDIII restriction sites. The N- (lacking the first 184 residues) and C-terminal (lacking the last 97 residues) Kv1.3-YFP truncations were inserted between BglII and EcoRI of pEYFP-C1 (Clontech), and the Kv1.3 (R469A, S470,472,475 A) R/S → A mutant was generated using a QuikChange Multi Site-Directed Mutagenesis Kit (Agilent Technologies). Nedd4-2, obtained from A. Sorkin (University of Pittsburg), was subcloned and inserted into pECFP-C1 (Clontech) by using BglII and HindIII restriction sites. Mouse Kvβ2.1 was provided by M.M. Tamkun (Colorado State University) and subcloned and inserted into SacII of pECFP-N1 (Clontech). All the mutants and constructs were verified by sequencing.

Cell culture and transfections

The HEK293 cell line (ATCC CRL-1573) was cultured in DMEM (Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium, Gibco) supplemented with 4.5 g/L glucose, L-glutamine, 10% FBS (fetal bovine serum, Gibco) and antibiotics (10,000 U/mL penicillin G and 10 mg/mL streptomycin, Gibco). HEK293 cells were transfected with lipotransfectin (AttendBio Research) at approximately 80% confluence. For confocal analyses, cells were plated on poly-D-lysine-coated coverslips. When mentioned, the cells were incubated with 1 μM PMA (Alomone) in DMEM for the specified times.

Protein extraction, immunoprecipitation, ubiquitination assay, membrane protein biotinylation and western blotting

Cells were washed twice with cold PBS and scraped in 0.5 mL of protein lysis buffer (150 mM NaCl, 50 mM Tris-HCl, 1 mM EDTA, and 1% Triton X-100, pH 7.5) supplemented with protease inhibitors (1 µg/mL aprotinin, 2 µM leupeptin, 1 µM pepstatin, and 1 mM PMSF). Lysates were homogenized on an orbital shaker at 4 °C for 10 min and centrifuged for 15 min at 15,000 × g. The supernatant was collected, and the protein concentration was measured using a Bio-Rad protein assay (Bio-Rad).

To study the ubiquitination of Kv1.3, cells were washed twice with ice-cold PBS and frozen O/N. Next, the cells were incubated with lysis buffer (150 mM NaCl, 50 mM HEPES, 0.1% glycerol, 0.1% Triton X-100 [pH 7.4]) supplemented with 10 nM N-ethylmaleimide (NEM), 0.2 mM MG-132, 1 mM EGTA, 1 mM EDTA, 20 mM NaF, 1 mM Na3VO4, 2 mM DTT and 1% Triton X-100 for 20 min. Next, 1–2 mg of protein was precleaned with 30 µL of protein A-Sepharose™ 4 Fast Flow beads (GE Healthcare) for 1 h at 4 °C with gentle mixing. The beads were removed by centrifugation at 1000 × g for 30 s. The samples were incubated O/N in Micro Bio-Spin™ Chromatography Columns (Bio-Rad) with 50 µL of protein A-Sepharose beads and anti-GFP (4 ng of antibody/μg of protein) (GenScript) at 4 °C with gentle agitation. Next, the columns were centrifuged at 1000 × g for 30 s, and the supernatant was collected. After 5 washes with washing solution (150 mM NaCl, 50 mM HEPES, 10% glycerol, 0.1% Triton X-100; pH 7.5), the immunoprecipitates (IPs) were collected in 60 µL of 0.2 M glycine (pH 2.5). Loading buffer containing 10% β-mercaptoethanol was added, and the samples were boiled for 7 min. Two additional controls were performed for the co-immunoprecipitation assays. First, the absence of nonspecific interactions was thoroughly verified by omitting the primary antibodies in all assays. Second, YFP was co-transfected in all co-IP combinations to evaluate potential interactions between the target proteins and GFP-derived tags (either YFP or CFP). The results of these last assays are shown in Supplementary Fig. 3. Neither negative control produced a positive signal.

Protein samples (50–100 μg) and IPs were boiled for 10 min in Laemmli SDS loading buffer and separated by 10% SDS‒PAGE. The proteins were transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore) and blocked with PBS supplemented with 5% dry nonfat milk. The membranes were immunoblotted with antibodies against GFP (1/200; 11814460001, Roche), β-actin (1/50,000; AC-15, Sigma), calnexin (1/500; 610523, BD Transduction), total CREB (1/500; D-12, Santa Cruz), phospho-CREB (1/500; 10E9, Santa Cruz), Nedd4-2 (1/200; ab245522, Abcam), Ndfip1 (1/500; D-4, Santa Cruz), pan-14-3-3 (1/100, K-19, Santa Cruz), 14-3-3γ (1/100, D-6, Santa Cruz) and ubiquitin (1/500; P4D1, Santa Cruz).

A Pierce Cell Surface Isolation Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used to biotinylate and isolate the cell surface proteins. Briefly, cells, which were plated in 100 mm dishes, were washed twice with cold PBS and labeled with 6 mL of EZ-Link-Sulfo-NHS biotin (0.25 mg/mL in PBS) for 30 min at 4 °C with gentle agitation. Next, 350 µL of quenching solution (included in the kit) was added to each dish. Next, the cells were washed twice with TBS (150 mM NaCl, 25 mM Tris; pH 7.4) and lysed for 10 min with 200 µL of lysis solution (150 mM NaCl, 50 mM HEPES, 10% glycerol, 0.1% Triton X-100; pH 7.5) supplemented with 1 μg/µL aprotinin, 1 μg/µL leupeptin, 1 μg/µL pepstatin and 1 mM PMSF as protease inhibitors. Total lysates were centrifuged at 16,200 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. Next, 2 mg of protein sample was incubated O/N in columns with 167 µL of NeutrAvidin agarose beads at 4 °C with gentle agitation. The samples were subsequently centrifuged at 1000 × g for 1 min, after which the eluted supernatant was collected. After 4 washes with washing solution (provided with the kit), the samples were resuspended in 100 µL of loading buffer (1 M DTT, Laemmli buffer supplemented with 2% β-mercaptoethanol and H2O up to 100 µL) and boiled for 5 min. The centrifuged supernatant was then subjected to SDS‒PAGE.

Protein digestion, liquid chromatography, and mass spectrometry

For the proteomic analysis, 25–30 IPs were performed for each condition. The IP eluates were concentrated by methanol/chloroform precipitation, and the protein pellet was dried at 37 °C and resuspended in 35 µL of resuspension buffer (50 mM Tris, 4% SDS; pH 7.4) and Laemmli buffer without β-mercaptoethanol. The samples were subjected to 10% SDS‒PAGE without boiling. The polyacrylamide gel was stained O/N at room temperature (RT) with bromophenol blue. To remove excess stain, several aqueous washes were performed, and the gel was then processed for mass spectrometry (MS). The selected gel bands were cut into small pieces, washed with 50 mM NH2HCO3 (ABC) and acetonitrile (ACN), reduced with 10 mM dithiothreitol (DTT) and alkylated with 50 mM iodoacetamide (IAA). Next, the samples were digested with trypsin (Sequencing Grade Modified Trypsin, Promega), and the peptides were extracted using 100% ACN and 5% formic acid. Tryptic peptide solutions were dried in a SpeedVac, reconstituted in 30 µL of 50 mM ABC and redigested with chymotrypsin for 6 h at 37 °C. Digestion was stopped with 1% formic acid, and the mixture was dried in a SpeedVac. The final redigested samples were reconstituted in 20 µL of 3% ACN and 1% formic acid in aqueous solution for MS analysis.

Confocal microscopy and image analysis

For confocal microscopy, HEK293 cells were seeded on polylysine-coated coverslips. Twenty-four hours after transfection, the cells were washed twice with 1 mL of prewarmed PBS K+-free (PBS-PF), fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, and washed three times for 5 min with PBS-K+. Finally, the coverslips were mounted on microscope slides (Acefesa) with house Mowiol mounting medium and dried at RT before imaging. To visualize Kv1.3 intracellular-containing vesicles, the cells were incubated with 1 μM PMA for different durations. Images were acquired with an inverted Zeiss LSM880 laser scanning confocal microscope equipped with an argon multiline laser (458, 488, 514 nm), a DPSS 561 nm laser and a helium-neon laser (633 nm) and a 63 × Plan Apo PH NA 1.4 oil immersion objective. All analyses were controlled using Zen Black 2.3 SP1 FP3 software. Offline image analyses were performed using the FIJI distribution of ImageJ freeware. The number of Kv1.3 intracellular vesicles was quantified using the Feature J Laplacian plugin with a fixed threshold (yen). Only vesicles between 20 and 200 nm in diameter were counted. Colocalization analysis was carried out using Manders’ overlapping coefficient (MOC).

The fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET), using the acceptor photobleaching configuration, was measured in discrete regions of interest (ROIs). Fluorescent proteins were excited at either 458 nm or 514 nm using low excitation intensities. Next, 475–495 nm bandpass and >530 nm longpass emission filters were applied. YFP was bleached using maximum laser power with more than 80% acceptor bleaching. After acceptor photobleaching, donor and acceptor images were captured. The FRET efficiency was calculated using the equation [(FCFPafter – FCFPbefore)/FCFPafter]*100, where FCFPafter and FCFPbefore are the fluorescence of the donor after and before bleaching, respectively. The loss of fluorescence as a result of the scans was corrected by measuring the CFP intensity in an unbleached part of the cell. All the images were acquired with a Leica TCS SL laser scanning confocal spectral microscope (Leica Microsystems) equipped with argon and helium–neon lasers. Experiments were performed with a 63 × oil-immersion objective lens (NA 1.32). Offline analyses were performed using ImageJ software.

Proximity ligation assay (PLA)

HEK293 cells were transfected with Kv1.3-YFP WT. The next day, the cells were washed three times with PBS-K+ and fixed with 2% PFA for 10 min at RT. After 3 new washes with 20 mM Gly in PBS-K+, the cells were permeabilized for 5 min with 0.1% Triton X-100 and 20 mM Gly in PBS-K+. Washes were repeated, and then the cells were blocked for 1 h at RT with blocking solution (5% BSA + 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS-K+). After 3 washes with 20 mM in PBS (K+ free), the cells were incubated O/N at 4 °C with monoclonal anti-GFP (1/100, Roche) and polyclonal anti-Nedd4-2 (1/100, Abcam) primary antibodies diluted in Duolink blocking solution 1x (Sigma‒Aldrich). Next, after 3 quick washes, the cells were incubated for 1 h at 37 °C with secondary PLA antibodies (for 100 µL: 20 µL Duolink in situ PLA probe anti-Mouse PLUS, 20 µL Duolink in situ PLA probe anti-Rabbit MINUS and 60 µL Duolink antibodies diluent 1 × , Sigma‒Aldrich). Afterward, the cells were washed five times and incubated for 30 min at 37 °C with ligation-PLA solution (100 µL of 20 µL of 5 × ligation buffer, 2.5 µL of ligase and 77.5 µL H2O; Duolink Ligase, Sigma‒Aldrich). After 5 washes, the cells were incubated for 3 h at 37 °C in the dark with amplification-PLA solution (100 µL of 20 µL of amplification buffer 5 × , 1.25 µL of polymerase and 78.75 µL of H2O; Duolink Far Red Amplification, Sigma‒Aldrich). Next, the cells were washed 4 more times and incubated for 2 h at RT with the fluorescent secondary antibody goat anti-rabbit Alexa 568 (1/500, Invitrogen) diluted in 1% BSA and 20 mM glycine in PBS (K+ free) to label endogenous Nedd4-2. Finally, after 3 washes, the coverslips were mounted over the slides with Mowiol mounting solution.

siRNA transfection for gene silencing

Synthetic siRNA for 14-3-3γ and mock siRNA (scrambled sequence control siRNA-A) were purchased from Santa Cruz. Synthetic siRNA for human Nedd4-2, siRNA for human Ndfip1, and nontargeting control siRNA Pool #2 were obtained from Dharmacon™ Reagents. The transfection reagents used were Lipotransfectin for 14-3-3 γ siRNA and DarmaFECT™1 for Nedd4-2 and Ndfip1 siRNAs.

Lyophilized 14-3-3γ and mock siRNAs were resuspended in RNase-free H2O to obtain 10 μM stock solutions. A 60 nM siRNA working concentration was used. siRNAs and siRNA Transfection Medium (Santa Cruz) were mixed and incubated for 5 min. Next, the siRNA mixture was added dropwise to the Lipotransfectin® mixture and incubated for 45 min. Finally, siRNA transfection medium was added to the mixture, mixed gently, and overlaid onto the cells. After 7 h of incubation, without removing the transfection mixture, supplemented medium was added, and experiments were performed 24–36 h after transfection. For Nedd4-2 and Ndfip1, lyophilized siRNAs were resuspended in RNase-free H2O to obtain a 50 μM stock solution. In 60 mm dishes, 25 nM siRNA was used as the working concentration. siRNA/NTsiRNA was prepared separately with DarmaFECT™1 reagent and further mixed with OPTIMEM for 5 min. Then, the siRNA/NTsiRNA mixture was incubated for 20 min with DarmaFECT™1 mix. Finally, antibiotic-free complete medium (DMEM + 10% FBS) was added. The next day, the cells were transfected with Kv1.3-YFP, and the experiment was performed 24 h after transfection. The knockdown efficiency was evaluated by western blotting. Mock- and siRNA-transfected cells were processed for immunofluorescence as described above.

Electrophysiology

Twenty-four hours after transfection, HEK293 cells were detached using trypsin and reseeded onto 35-mm culture dishes. For PMA stimulation, cells were preincubated at 37 °C for 30 min with PMA, either in the presence or absence of 1 µM bisindolylmaleimide (BIM), and subsequently transferred to fresh dishes. After 15 min, cells were thoroughly rinsed with an extracellular solution containing 145 mM NaCl, 4 mM KCl, 1 mM MgCl₂, 1.8 mM CaCl₂, 10 mM HEPES, and 10 mM glucose (pH adjusted to 7.4 with NaOH). Patch-clamp electrodes were fabricated from borosilicate glass capillaries (1.2 mm outer diameter × 0.944 mm inner diameter × 100 mm length; Harvard Apparatus) using a P-97 puller (Sutter Instruments) and fire-polished with an MF-830 microforge (Narishige) to obtain resistances of 2–5 MΩ. The pipette internal solution consisted of 80 mM potassium aspartate, 42 mM KCl, 10 mM KH₂PO₄, 5 mM EGTA-K, 5 mM HEPES-K, 3 mM phosphocreatine, and 3 mM Mg-ATP, with pH adjusted to 7.25 using KOH. Cells were voltage-clamped at a holding potential of −80 mV, and whole-cell currents were recorded at room temperature using a HEKA EPC-10 amplifier. Voltage-dependent currents were evoked by applying 250-ms depolarizing pulses to +60 mV.

P < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant. For data visualization, dot-blots are provided when the number of points were < 15 and box and whisker plots when the amount exceeded 15 points to improve visibility.
