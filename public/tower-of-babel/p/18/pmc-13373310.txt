Temporal Trends in Cause‐Specific Cardiovascular Disease Mortality Following Cancer Diagnosis
Authors: Ken Kurisu, Maiko Fujimori, Saki Harashima, Tatsuo Akechi, Kazuhiro Yoshiuchi, Yosuke Uchitomi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThis study analyzed temporal trends in cardiovascular disease (CVD) mortality, stratified by disease type, cancer type, and demographic characteristics.MethodsIndividuals diagnosed with cancer between January 2016 and June 2020 were analyzed using data from the National Cancer Registry in Japan. Standardized mortality ratios (SMRs) were calculated by comparing observed CVD deaths among individuals with cancer to expected deaths in the age‐ and sex‐matched general population. Outcomes included myocardial infarction, other heart diseases, ischemic stroke, hemorrhagic stroke, and all‐cause CVD mortality. Multivariable Cox models were developed to estimate hazard ratios for the covariates.ResultsThe analysis included 4,711,540 individuals and 70,630 CVD deaths during the follow‐up period. Across all outcomes, SMRs peaked 0–1 month after diagnosis (e.g., 2.35 for all CVDs) and declined sharply by 6 months (e.g., 1.18 for all CVDs). For several CVD outcomes, a secondary increase in SMRs was observed at approximately 37–48 months (e.g., 1.30 for all CVDs). Although the number of deaths for each CVD was higher among older individuals, SMRs were greater in younger age groups. The five most common cancers accounted for approximately half of all deaths and showed similar temporal trends. Cox models achieved moderate predictive accuracy, and adjusted hazard ratios were higher for regional metastasis, distant metastasis, and unknown/other categories than for localized tumors.ConclusionsThis study revealed detailed temporal patterns of CVD mortality following cancer diagnosis, particularly during acute phases.Keywords: cancer, cardiovascular mortality, myocardial infarction, National Cancer Registry, population‐based study, stroke

Introduction

Population‐based studies have demonstrated that individuals diagnosed with cancer have an elevated risk of cardiovascular mortality worldwide [1]. This risk is highest during the first month after diagnosis [2, 3, 4]. Following this initial period, the risk declines rapidly [2, 3, 4], before rising again several years later [5, 6, 7]. Cardiotoxicity from cancer treatment, especially chemotherapy, has been identified as a contributing mechanism for cardiovascular mortality among individuals with cancer [8], and targeted interventions have been specifically investigated [9].

However, the mechanisms underlying cardiovascular mortality during the first month after diagnosis, a critical period of highest risk, remain unclear. Moreover, preventive countermeasures for this period remain unexplored. As one clue to a possible mechanism, the temporal trend, characterized by a sharp increase within the first month of diagnosis followed by a steep decline, mirrors that of depression and suicide risk [2, 3, 4, 10, 11]. Studies have also reported elevated cardiovascular risk during the diagnostic workup for suspected cancer [12, 13, 14]. These findings may imply the involvement of psychological distress due to cancer diagnosis, along with treatment‐related effects, in the onset of cardiovascular diseases. Nevertheless, further studies are needed to assess the mechanisms underlying this early increase in cardiovascular mortality.

Japan's National Cancer Registry, a nationwide population‐based registry launched in 2016, enables a comprehensive investigation into cardiovascular mortality risk among people with cancer in Japan [2, 3]. A recent study using this database has provided detailed epidemiological insights into specific types of cardiovascular disease [15]. However, this research has not sufficiently examined time‐specific risks, particularly within the first month after diagnosis [15]. To understand the mechanisms shortly after diagnosis and to inform preventive strategies, detailed analyses stratified by disease type and demographic characteristics during this period are warranted. Therefore, the present study aimed to quantify temporal trends in cardiovascular mortality stratified by disease type, cancer type, and demographic characteristics.

Materials and Methods

Study Participants and Outcomes

This study used data from the National Cancer Registry, which covers all individuals diagnosed with cancer in Japan since January 2016 [2, 3, 16]. Those diagnosed between January 2016 and June 2020 were included. Following previous studies [2, 3, 16], we excluded individuals diagnosed by autopsy or death certificate, those residing outside Japan, and those with missing data on age, sex, address, or date of diagnosis.

Cardiovascular mortality outcomes were defined based on ICD‐10 codes, including myocardial infarction (I21–I24), other heart diseases (I10–I13, I71, and I72), ischemic stroke (I63), hemorrhagic stroke (I60–I62), and all cardiovascular mortality (I00–I99).

Standardized Mortality Ratios

Standardized mortality ratios (SMRs) were calculated following our previous studies and protocol [2, 3, 16]. The observed number of cardiovascular deaths in each period after diagnosis was calculated using data from the National Cancer Registry. For each individual with cancer, the expected cardiovascular mortality rate during the corresponding survival period was estimated using data from the general population, matched by age, sex, and prefecture of residence, obtained from the Vital Statistics and Population Estimates. Summing these rates yielded the expected number of deaths. Patients registered with zero months of survival in the dataset were assigned a value of 0.5 months. The SMR was defined as the ratio of observed to expected deaths. Confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using Byar's method.

The SMRs were calculated by dividing the acute phase into 0–1, 2–3, and 4–6 months, in contrast to a previous study that grouped this acute phase into a single period [15]. The period was further categorized into 7–12, 13–24, 25–36, and 37–48 months. In the current dataset, individuals diagnosed in 2016 could be followed for up to 4 years, those diagnosed in 2017 for 3 years, in 2018 for 2 years, in 2019 for 1 year, and in the first half of 2020 for 0.5 years. Accordingly, SMRs were calculated using the available data for each follow‐up period. For example, the SMRs for 0–6 months were calculated using all available data, whereas those for 37–48 months were based solely on individuals diagnosed in 2016.

These analyses were conducted using R (version 4.4.2) with the epiR package (version 2.0.77).

Survival Analysis

Multivariable survival analysis was conducted to evaluate the association between covariates and cardiovascular mortality. The observation period was defined as either the maximum available follow‐up period or until death. The dataset was randomly divided into training (70%) and test (30%) sets. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were trained on the training dataset, and predictive performance was evaluated on the test dataset using the concordance index (C‐index) [17]. All variables of interest were included in the models simultaneously. The adjusted hazard ratios were quantified using the developed models. Random survival forest (RSF), a machine learning–based survival analysis method [18, 19, 20, 21], was also developed as a benchmark for predictive performance.

These survival analyses were conducted using the survival package (version 3.8‐3) and the randomForestSRC package (version 3.3.1) in R.

Across all outcomes, age showed a wide range of hazard ratios (e.g., ranging from 0.17 to 15.89 for all cardiovascular diseases), consistent with the age‐stratified mortality counts shown in Figure 1. Hazard ratios were generally higher among men, aligning with the larger absolute death count observed among male individuals, as shown in Figure 2. Hazard ratios were higher for individuals with regional metastasis, distant metastasis, and unknown/other classifications than for those with localized tumors.

Discussion

Using data from Japan's National Cancer Registry, a comprehensive population‐based survey, this study extends previous findings by analyzing cardiovascular mortality by subtype and examining temporal risk trends across cancer types and demographic groups, with a focus on the acute phase. Although the elevated SMR at 0–1 month after cancer diagnosis observed in the present study was consistent with previous studies [2, 3], this study provides further detail by visualizing temporal trends by cardiovascular disease subtype, age group, and cancer type. Although a previous study grouped acute‐phase periods into a single category of 0–6 months [15], our analysis contributes to the literature by examining more detailed intervals (i.e., 0–1, 2–3, and 4–6 months).

Several factors may explain the increase in cardiovascular mortality immediately following diagnosis. The parallel temporal patterns of cardiovascular mortality and suicide risk [2, 3, 4, 10, 11], together with the increased risk during diagnostic workup [12, 13, 14], might imply the involvement of diagnosis‐related psychological distress. Nevertheless, the small sex difference in SMR (Figure 2) and the lower absolute risk among women (Table 2) do not strongly support this hypothesis, given that women have a higher prevalence of stress‐related disorders, such as depression, anxiety disorders, and trauma‐related disorders [22, 23]. Another possible explanation is that some cancers were detected incidentally during hospitalization for cardiovascular disease. Moreover, shared risk factors for cancer and cardiovascular disease, such as smoking, hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes, and obesity [24], may also explain the observed association. Further research is warranted to clarify the mechanisms underlying the acute‐phase cardiovascular mortality.

In addition to the acute‐phase increase, cardiovascular mortality rose again at 37–48 months post‐diagnosis. This pattern is consistent with previous studies [5, 6, 7], and as noted in the literature, several mechanisms may contribute to this pattern, including cardiotoxic effects of chemotherapy and radiotherapy, cancer‐related hypercoagulability, increased inflammatory cytokines, and concerns about recurrence. Further studies are needed to better understand the mechanisms underlying this later‐phase increase and to evaluate temporal trends beyond 48 months.

When analyzed by cardiovascular disease subtype, myocardial infarction accounted for a large number of deaths, whereas ischemic stroke showed a high SMR. Both conditions may represent primary targets for intervention. Age‐stratified analysis revealed that while the number of deaths was higher among older adults, SMRs were greater among younger individuals. This implies that, while older adults may be more accessible as participants in intervention trials, the impact of cancer on cardiovascular mortality may be larger in younger individuals, potentially leading to greater intervention effects. Given that fatal outcomes are less likely in younger individuals with fewer comorbidities, future studies should consider a broader range of outcomes, including disease onset, hospitalization, and long‐term complications.

The five most common cancers account for approximately half of all deaths due to cardiovascular diseases. Among them, people with breast cancer had lower SMRs shortly after diagnosis, whereas both the SMRs and absolute mortality increased gradually over time. This trend aligns with previous studies showing that breast cancer survivors face elevated risks of noncancerous physical conditions and suicide in the long term [25, 26]. Colorectal, gastric, and lung cancers may be suitable targets for interventions shortly after diagnosis, whereas breast cancer may require attention in the survivorship phase, several years after diagnosis.

Multivariable Cox regression analysis showed that advanced cancer stages were associated with higher cardiovascular mortality risk across all disease types, consistent with previous findings [2]. These findings imply that patients with advanced‐stage cancer may warrant closer cardiovascular monitoring. Nevertheless, the hazard ratio for metastatic cancer was 1.35 for overall cardiovascular mortality, which appears modest compared with the wide range observed for age (0.17–15.89) or with the SMRs for the entire cancer population compared to the general population (> 2.00 during the 0–1 month after diagnosis). In addition, although the classification of cancer stage and its associated prognosis or severity varies by cancer type, the current analysis grouped them into a common classification. Therefore, the role of cancer stage in prioritizing interventions remains unclear, and further stratified analyses by cancer type are necessary to examine the impact of cancer stage once additional data from the National Cancer Registry are available.

This study has several limitations. First, Japan's National Cancer Registry includes only mortality data. The small number of deaths and the low hazard ratios among younger individuals may have resulted from this limitation. Future studies should consider nonfatal outcomes, such as disease onset, hospitalization, and long‐term complications. Second, we were unable to account for major cardiovascular risk factors such as smoking, hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes, and obesity [24], because these variables were not included in Japan's National Cancer Registry. For instance, obesity is associated with both colorectal and breast cancers, including in Japan [27, 28, 29], and is also critical for predicting cardiovascular outcomes [30], thereby potentially confounding the observed association between cancer diagnosis and cardiovascular mortality. Integrated analyses incorporating these co‐existing conditions are needed to better understand the underlying relationships. Third, as one of the most aged and longest‐living societies in the world [31], Japan may have a population structure and clinical trajectory that differ from those of other countries. These contextual differences were not examined in this study. Finally, we were unable to examine cardiovascular mortality within the first week after diagnosis, as investigated by Fang et al. [4]. Such analyses are ideally desired to determine the mechanism or appropriate interventions.

In conclusion, using data from Japan's National Cancer Registry, this study identified temporal trends in cardiovascular mortality risk by disease subtype, focusing on the period immediately after cancer diagnosis. These findings provide insights for future research examining the underlying mechanisms in the acute phase and for designing preventive interventions.
