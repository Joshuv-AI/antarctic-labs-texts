The impact of trait impulsivity on suicidal behavior in adolescents with depression: the mediating and moderating role of depression severity, suicidal ideation intensity and age
Authors: Xinglian Wang, Liuyi Ran, Hao Ren, Yue Gao, Guixia Liu, Hong Luo, Bin Peng, Haitang Qiu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aims to investigate whether and how trait impulsivity differentiates suicidal ideation (SI) from suicide attempts (SA) in adolescents with Major Depressive Disorder (MDD), and the mechanisms through which impulsivity increases suicide attempt risk.MethodsA total of 185 adolescents diagnosed with MDD and suicidal ideation were categorized into SI (n = 118) and SA (n = 67) groups based on lifetime suicide attempt history. Study variables included impulsivity, depression severity, suicidal ideation intensity, and demographic and clinical characteristics. Group difference analysis compared variables between the two groups. Multiple logistic regression and moderated mediation models were applied to analyze the relationships between impulsivity and suicidal behavior.ResultsThe SA group exhibited younger age, higher depression severity, higher impulsivity, and higher suicidal ideation intensity compared to the SI group. Unadjusted logistic regression revealed that higher impulsivity was associated with higher odds of suicide attempt risk (OR = 1.03, p = 0.030); however, this association became nonsignificant after adjusting for covariates. Mediation analysis revealed a pattern consistent with an indirect association: trait impulsivity was statistically associated with higher suicide attempt risk via depression severity and suicidal ideation intensity, whereas its direct association was not significant. Age moderated the relationship between impulsivity and suicidal ideation intensity (B = -0.05, p = 0.039), showing stronger associations in younger adolescents.ConclusionTrait impulsivity alone does not directly differentiate suicidal ideation from suicide attempts. It is indirectly linked to suicidal behavior through the severity of depression and the intensity of suicidal ideation. The correlation between impulsivity and suicidal ideation intensity diminishes as age increases.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-026-08208-5.Keywords: Adolescents, Major depressive disorder, Impulsivity, Suicidal behavior

Background

According to a report by the World Health Organization (WHO), suicide ranks as the fourth leading cause of death among individuals aged 15–29 [1]. Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) significantly increases suicide risk, with patients being 20 times more likely to attempt suicide [2]. Suicidal behavior progresses through stages, including suicidal ideation, planning, attempts, and completion [3]. Numerous studies have identified various risk factors for suicide, including depression [4], any other psychiatric disorders [5], drug abuse [6], hopelessness [7], stressful life events [8], and impulsivity [9]. However, few studies have examined the distinctive risk factors associated with different types of suicidal behavior. In practice, a majority of individuals with suicidal ideation do not proceed to attempt suicide. Globally, the prevalence of suicidal ideation among children, adolescents, and young adults ranges from approximately 15.1% to 22.3%, while the prevalence of suicide attempts ranges from 3.2% to 6.2% [10–12]. Among patients with depression in China, the prevalence of suicidal ideation is 51.3%, and the prevalence of suicide attempts is 23.7% [13]. Thus, identifying predictive factors that drive the transition from suicidal ideation to suicide attempts is critical for clinical practice.

Impulsivity refers to the tendency to react quickly without considering the broader consequences of one’s actions [14]. Trait impulsivity, a stable and enduring personality characteristic, is a specific dimension of impulsivity [15]. In the ideation-to-action frameworks for suicide, multiple theories highlight the facilitating role of impulsivity. Within the interpersonal theory, impulsivity contributes to the “acquired capability for suicide” by habituating individuals to fear and pain, thus increasing the risk of suicide [16]. The Integrated Motivational-Volitional (IMV) Model posits that impulsivity plays a critical role in the volitional phase, making individuals more likely to quickly engage in suicidal behavior when experiencing perceptions of defeat and entrapment [17]. In the Three-Step Theory (3ST), suicidal capability is a key factor in the transition from suicidal ideation to suicide attempts, with impulsivity contributing to increased acquired capability for suicide [18].

Trait impulsivity has been positively correlated with the risk of suicidal ideation [19–21] and suicide attempts [20, 22, 23]. However, research examining the relationship between suicide and trait impulsivity has rarely distinguished between suicidal ideation and suicide attempts, and findings have been inconsistent. Some studies suggest that impulsivity may help distinguish between suicidal ideation and suicide attempts. For instance, a study among Chinese university students found that high motor impulsivity was a predictive factor distinguishing suicidal ideation from suicide attempts [24]. Korean researchers found that among patients with mental disorders, levels of trait impulsivity, attentional impulsivity, and behavioral impulsivity displayed a linear trend across groups, increasing progressively from those with only suicidal ideation to those with a single suicide attempt and then to those with multiple suicide attempts [25]. In contrast, American researchers found no significant difference in trait impulsivity between individuals with suicidal ideation and those with a history of suicide attempts in adult psychiatric patients [26].

Furthermore, trait impulsivity has been shown to be associated with higher suicide risk in adolescents [8]. Adolescence is a period marked by a high incidence of impulsive behaviors [27, 28], with individual impulsivity gradually increasing from childhood, peaking during adolescence, and then gradually declining into adulthood [29, 30]. Similarly, the risk of suicide among certain adolescents increases gradually during puberty, reaches its peak at the ages of 14 to 15, and then progressively declines until adulthood [31, 32]. In the adolescent depression population, there is limited research examining whether trait impulsivity is associated with the transition from suicidal ideation to suicide attempts. A cohort study in the United Kingdom found that adolescents with high impulsivity levels had a higher risk of suicide attempts compared to those with suicidal ideation alone, though the effect size was modest [33].

Based on the aforementioned background, this study aims to investigate the relationship between trait impulsivity and suicidal behavior in adolescents with depression, and to identify predictive factors that differentiate suicide attempts from suicidal ideation alone. We hypothesize that among adolescents with depressive disorders: (1) higher trait impulsivity will be observed in individuals with a history of suicide attempts compared to those with suicidal ideation only, with high impulsivity serving as an independent risk factor distinguishing suicide attempts from suicidal ideation, (2) the relationship between impulsivity and suicide attempts will be mediated by the level of suicidal ideation intensity, and (3) the relationship between impulsivity and suicidal ideation intensity will be moderated by age.

Methods

Study population and recruitment

Participants were recruited from adolescent patients who attended the outpatient and inpatient services of the Department of Psychiatry at the First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University during the period from 2020 to 2022. Eligible participants were identified by treating psychiatrists. After consent, participants completed self-report questionnaires in a quiet clinical setting under the supervision of trained research assistants. Clinical interviews were conducted by licensed psychiatrists trained in standardized assessment procedures.

The inclusion criteria for this study were: (1) age between 12 and 19 years; (2) meeting the DSM-IV diagnostic criteria for MDD; (3) currently or previously experiencing suicidal ideation or a suicide attempt; and (4) agreeing to participate in the study. Exclusion criteria included: (1) Diagnosis of any of the following conditions: (a) Neurodevelopmental Disorder; (b) Schizophrenia Spectrum and Other Psychotic Disorders; (c) Bipolar and Related Disorder; (2) Presence of missing values.

Of 202 initially eligible participants, 185 completed all assessments and were included. Exclusions (n = 17) were due to missing data on key variables.

Informed consent was obtained from all participants and their guardians after a clear explanation of the study’s purpose, procedures, and confidentiality protocols. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Human Research and Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (Approval NO: 2020-97-2).

Variables

Suicidal behavior

The Columbia Suicide Severity Rating Scale (C-SSRS) was used to assess lifetime suicidal behavior. A suicide attempt was defined as a potentially self-injurious act committed with at least some intent to die, as per the C-SSRS “actual attempt” category. Aborted or interrupted attempts and preparatory acts were not classified as attempts. Participants were categorized into the Suicide Attempt (SA) group if they reported one or more such attempts during their lifetime; those who reported only suicidal ideation (without any attempt) formed the Suicidal Ideation (SI) group.

Impulsivity

The Chinese version of the Barratt Impulsiveness Scale (BIS-11) was used to assess trait impulsivity [34]. The BIS-11 was originally developed by Barratt [35] and consists of 30 self-report items divided into three dimensions (each with 10 items): non-planning impulsivity, cognitive impulsivity, and motor impulsivity. Each item is rated on a 5-point scale ranging from 1 (never) to 5 (always). Items of the non-planning and cognitive impulsiveness subscales are reverse-scored. When calculating, each subscale score is transformed to a 0–100 range using the formula: (raw score – 10) / 40 × 100. The total scale score is calculated as the average of the three transformed subscale scores, with higher scores indicating higher levels of impulsivity. The Chinese version has shown robust reliability and validity, with Cronbach’s α ranging from 0.77 to 0.89 across subscales and test-retest reliability ranging from 0.68 to 0.89 [34]. In the present sample, Cronbach’s α for the BIS-11 total scale was 0.85; subscales ranged from 0.80 to 0.91.

Depression severity

The Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) was used to assess the severity of depressive symptoms over the past two weeks. The PHQ-9 consists of nine self-reported items scored from 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day), with total scores ranging from 0 to 27. Higher PHQ-9 scores indicate more severe depressive symptoms. The Chinese version of the PHQ-9 has also demonstrated good reliability and validity, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.86 [36]. The Cronbach’s α for the PHQ-9 was 0.77 in the present sample.

Suicidal ideation intensity

The Beck Scale for Suicide Ideation — Chinese Version (BSI-CV) was used to assess the severity of the most severe suicidal ideation ever experienced by the participant (i.e., worst-point lifetime suicidal ideation) [37]. The BSI-CV is a 19-item self-reported questionnaire, rating on a scale of 0 to 2, with total scores ranging from 0 to 38, where higher scores indicate more severe suicidal ideation. The BSI-CV demonstrates strong reliability and validity in the Chinese population, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.88 [37]. In this study, the Cronbach’s α for the BSI-CV was 0.86.

Demographic and clinical variables

This study also included demographic variables (age, sex, family history of psychiatric disorders, smoking history, and alcohol use history) and clinical variables (whether it was the first episode and total duration of illness).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (Mean ± SD) or Median (IQR), based on conformity with normal distribution, while categorical variables were described using counts and percentages (N (%)). Chi-square tests, independent samples t-tests, and Mann–Whitney U tests were applied to compare group differences. Logistic regression was used to examine the relationship between each variable and suicide attempt. Model 1 was unadjusted, testing the relationship between trait impulsivity and suicide attempts; Model 2 adjusted for age, gender, and family history of psychiatric disorders; Model 3 built on Model 2 by adding clinical variables (whether it was the first episode, duration of illness, and level of depression); Model 4 further added the intensity of suicidal ideation based on Model 3.

A serial mediation model was tested with depression severity as mediator 1 and suicidal ideation intensity as mediator 2, based on the hypothesis that impulsivity is associated with more severe depression, which in turn is associated with more intense suicidal ideation, and thereby with higher risk of suicide attempts. Additionally, a moderation model was employed to examine whether age moderates the relationships between impulsivity and depression severity, as well as between impulsivity and suicidal ideation intensity. The moderated mediation analysis was conducted using SPSS PROCESS 4.1 (Model 84), adjusting for variables showing group differences. All statistical analyses were conducted using R and SPSS 26, with statistical significance set at a p-value of less than 0.05 (two-tailed).

Results

Demographic characteristics

This study included 185 adolescents with depression and suicidal ideation, among whom 118 had suicidal ideation only (SI group) and 67 had a history of suicide attempts (SA group). There were no significant differences between the two groups in terms of family history of psychiatric disorders, smoking history, or alcohol use history.

Clinical and impulsivity characteristics

As shown in Table 1, the SA group exhibited a longer duration of illness [24 (12,35) vs. 12 (5,24), p = 0.002], higher levels of depression (21.13 ± 4.30 vs. 19.02 ± 4.92, p = 0.002), and greater intensity of suicidal ideation (28.24 ± 7.00 vs. 21.11 ± 8.27, p < 0.001) compared to the SI group. Additionally, the SA group exhibited higher total impulsivity (61.17 ± 12.29 vs. 56.00 ± 13.77, p = 0.030), non-planning impulsivity (70.46 ± 17.07 vs. 63.32 ± 19.21, p = 0.037), and cognitive impulsivity (58.93 ± 14.88 vs. 53.07 ± 16.30, p = 0.030) scores compared to the SI group. No significant differences were found between the groups in terms of first episode occurrence and motor impulsivity.

Logistic regression models

As shown in Table 2, in Model 1, where no covariates were adjusted, higher total impulsivity level was associated with higher odds of suicide attempts (OR = 1.03, 95%CI: 1.00–1.06, p = 0.030). However, in Models 2–4, as additional covariates were incrementally included, the relationship between impulsivity and the risk of suicide attempts became nonsignificant. Notably, across Models 2–4, being female was consistently associated with a higher risk of suicide attempts (p < 0.05 in all cases). In both Models 3 and 4, longer illness duration was significantly associated with a higher risk of suicide attempts (p < 0.05 in both models). Model 4 indicated that, after adjusting for all other covariates, greater intensity of suicidal ideation was significantly associated with an increased risk of suicide attempts (OR = 1.18, 95% CI: 1.09–1.28, p < 0.001).

Moderated mediation analysis

A mediation regression analysis was conducted using impulsivity as the independent variable, suicide attempts as the dependent variable, and depression severity and suicidal ideation intensity as mediators, with age, gender, and illness duration as covariates. The mediation model analysis (see sTable 4 in the supplement) revealed that the direct effect of impulsivity on suicidal behavior was not significant (Effect = 0.01, 95% CI: -0.03–0.04). Impulsivity exerted a significant indirect effect on suicide attempts through the intensity of suicidal ideation (Effect = 0.02, 95% CI: 0.01–0.44), accounting for 60.13% of the total effect. Furthermore, impulsivity exerted a significant indirect effect on suicide attempts via the sequential pathway from depression severity to suicidal ideation intensity (Effect = 0.01, 95% CI: 0.01–0.31), which explained 39.87% of the total effect.

As shown in Table 3; Fig. 1, the interaction between impulsivity and age significantly affected suicidal ideation intensity (B = -0.05, p = 0.039), indicating that age moderated the relationship between impulsivity and suicidal ideation intensity. A simple slope analysis (see Fig. 2) demonstrated that in the lower age group, the relationship between impulsivity and suicidal ideation intensity was stronger (B = 0.18, 95% CI: 0.07–0.30, p = 0.002). In contrast, in the higher age group, the effect of impulsivity on suicidal ideation intensity diminished and became statistically nonsignificant (B = -0.02, 95% CI: -0.01–0.14, p = 0.972).

Additionally, we conducted mediation analyses on the individual dimensions of trait impulsivity. The results indicated that non-planning impulsivity, cognitive impulsivity, and motor impulsivity each demonstrated similar mediation effects. However, the moderating effect of age was not significant across these dimensions (see sFigs. 1–3 in the supplement).

Given the cross-sectional design, the terms “indirect effect” refer to statistical associations consistent with a mediational hypothesis, and do not imply causal relationships.

Discussion

This study investigated the relationship between trait impulsivity and suicide attempts among adolescents with depression by comparing patients with suicidal ideation only to those with a history of suicide attempts. Using a moderated mediation model, we examined the roles of depression severity and suicidal ideation intensity in this relationship. Our findings indicate that trait impulsivity is not directly associated with suicide attempts in this sample. Instead, the data are consistent with an indirect pathway in which trait impulsivity is linked to suicide attempt risk through its association with greater depression severity and more intense suicidal ideation. Additionally, older age mitigates the relationship between impulsivity and suicidal ideation intensity. Although impulsivity was associated with suicide attempts in unadjusted models, this association became nonsignificant after covariate adjustment, suggesting that impulsivity does not independently differentiate attempters from ideators.

The study results indicate that trait impulsivity was higher in the SA group compared to the SI group, consistent with previous findings. Brokke and his colleagues found significantly higher impulsivity scores among suicide attempters than ideators in a clinical sample [38]. Moore and his colleagues confirmed a robust association between trait impulsivity and suicidal behaviors [20]. In our multiple regression analyses, impulsivity was positively associated with the risk of suicide attempts when other covariates were not included. However, after adjusting for additional variables, the relationship between impulsivity and suicide attempts became nonsignificant, suggesting that impulsivity does not have a direct effect on suicide attempts. Similarly, a survey of high school students in Taiwan found that impulsivity was associated with suicide attempts, but this predictive role of impulsivity disappeared after adjusting for demographic variables, substance use, social support, and depression symptoms [39]. Italian researchers observed a similar pattern among male prisoners; although high impulsivity was associated with increased suicidal behavior, impulsivity no longer predicted suicidal behavior when factors such as a history of aggression, childhood trauma, personality traits, hostility, and depression were considered [40]. A recent study conducted in Brazil on university students [41] found that trait impulsivity was correlated with suicide attempts, even after adjusting for trauma history, psychological neglect, and PTSD—factors that could also influence suicidal behavior. However, with the inclusion of these variables, the effect size of impulsivity on suicide attempts decreased, though it remained statistically significant.

Contrary to some previous studies [24, 25], we did not find a significant difference in motor impulsivity between suicide attempters and ideators. One possible explanation is that our sample consisted of adolescents with MDD, whereas the positive findings were observed in college students and mixed psychiatric patients. The association between motor impulsivity and suicidal behavior may be attenuated in the context of severe depression, where psychomotor retardation is common. Moreover, motor impulsivity, as measured by the BIS-11, reflects acting on the spur of the moment; this facet might be less specific to suicide attempts than cognitive or non-planning impulsivity in depressed adolescents. Future research employing behavioral tasks (e.g., stop-signal task) may further clarify the role of motor impulsivity.

In the multivariate regression analysis, after including variables such as age, gender, family history of psychiatric disorders, illness duration, first episode occurrence, and depression level, we found a strong association between suicidal ideation intensity and the risk of suicide attempts. This suggests that suicidal ideation intensity is a significant predictor of suicide attempts, consistent with findings from previous longitudinal studies on adolescents [42]. The mediation model analysis indicated that depression severity accounts for the relationship between impulsivity and suicidal ideation, which aligns with prior research [21]. Individuals with high impulsivity are more likely to experience emotional dysregulation [43], which often manifests as an inability to effectively regulate negative emotions, thereby being associated with more severe depressive symptoms, more intense suicidal ideation, and ultimately higher odds of engaging in suicide attempts [44, 45]. On the other hand, the mediation model also showed that impulsivity remains a significant predictor directly associated with higher suicidal ideation. Previous studies indicate that individuals with high impulsivity in MDD are more likely to experience suicidal ideation, even when their level of depression is relatively low [45]. According to the catalyst theory of suicide [46], suicidal ideation acts as a catalyst in the sequence of suicidal behaviors (ideation, planning, attempts, and completion), with reactions such as impulsivity, hopelessness, and perceived burdensomeness influencing the catalyst’s activity. This ideation catalyzes the transition through various stages of suicidal behavior. Within the ideation-to-action framework, theories such as the interpersonal theory, the IMV Model, and the 3ST all consider suicidal capability a crucial factor in the progression from suicidal ideation to action. Individuals with high impulsivity are more prone to engaging in painful or provocative behaviors (such as self-harm or other harmful actions), gradually acquiring a reduced fear of death and an increased tolerance for pain, leading to a higher acquired capability for suicide [47, 48]. This acquired capability is associated with a stronger link between impulsivity and suicidal ideation, and cross-sectional studies have found that impulsive individuals are more likely to report acting on suicidal thoughts once they arise. These findings indicate that impulsivity serves as a distal risk factor for suicide attempts, indirectly associated with suicidal behavior through multiple pathways.

The moderated mediation model in this study further indicates that age moderates the relationship between impulsivity and suicidal ideation intensity, with a weaker association observed among older adolescents. This finding aligns with the developmental trajectory of impulsivity, which tends to peak during adolescence but significantly declines as individuals age [30]. Research in social neurodevelopment suggests that from adolescence into adulthood, the brain’s cognitive control system matures, enhancing decision-making and coping abilities. The increasing coordination between cortical and subcortical functions supports better integration of cognition and emotion, thereby reducing the role of impulsivity in the formation of suicidal ideation [49].

In all adjusted models, female participants consistently exhibited a higher risk of suicide attempts. Previous meta-analyses have shown that females exhibit a higher rate of suicide attempts, while males have a higher rate of suicide completion [50, 51]. From the perspective of social and emotional development theories, adolescent females are more susceptible to social and emotional stressors, which may increase their risk of suicide when under pressure [52]. This phenomenon may also be attributed to survivor bias: females are more likely to seek help and openly discuss emotional issues, which may lead them to engage in less lethal suicide attempts. In contrast, males often adopt avoidance strategies for emotional issues and tend to use more lethal methods, contributing to higher suicide mortality rates among males [52].

Moreover, in Model 3, after adjusting for demographic and clinical variables, longer illness duration was associated with a higher risk of suicide attempts. The chronic progression of depression is closely linked to suicide attempts [53]. A prolonged course of illness often means that individuals are continuously exposed to negative emotions and life stressors, which may erode their emotional regulation capacities [54], thereby potentially increasing the risk of suicide. Longer illness duration also provides more opportunities and triggers for attempting suicide.

From a clinical perspective, our findings suggest several actionable strategies. First, screening for trait impulsivity in adolescents with depressive symptoms could help identify those at heightened risk of developing severe suicidal ideation. Second, interventions targeting emotion regulation and distress tolerance—such as dialectical behavior therapy for adolescents or cognitive-behavioral therapy enhanced with impulsivity management modules—may disrupt the statistical pathway observed in this study from impulsivity to depression and suicidal ideation. Third, because age attenuates the impulsivity-ideation link, early intervention in early-to-mid adolescence may yield greater preventive benefits. Finally, comprehensive suicide prevention programs should integrate impulsivity awareness into school-based mental health curricula and family psychoeducation.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, as a cross-sectional study, it cannot establish causal relationships among impulsivity, depression severity, suicidal ideation intensity, and suicide attempts. Future research should consider a longitudinal approach to track changes in impulsivity, depression severity, and suicidal ideation over time, allowing for a clearer determination of causal relationships among these variables. Second, there may be survivor bias in this study, as only patients who survived suicide attempts were included. Patients who died by suicide were not included, and there may be significant differences between these two groups, at least in terms of the lethality of suicidal behaviors. Demographic information, clinical characteristics, and traits like impulsivity may also differ between survivors and non-survivors. Future research should aim to include individuals who died by suicide to enhance the comprehensiveness of the study population. Third, the assessment of impulsivity was somewhat limited, as impulsivity is a multidimensional construct. This study focused only on trait impulsivity. Future studies could incorporate multidimensional measurements of impulsivity, including emotional impulsivity, state impulsivity, and cognitive control tasks in laboratory settings, to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the role of impulsivity in suicidal behavior. Fourth, this study did not assess social support, a robust protective factor against suicidal behavior [39]. Future studies should incorporate multidimensional measures of social support (family, peers, significant others) to examine whether it buffers the indirect pathways identified in our model. Fifth, the BSI-CV assessed the severity of the most severe suicidal ideation ever experienced by participants (i.e., worst-point lifetime suicidal ideation), whereas depression severity was measured as a current state (past two weeks). Consequently, for individuals with a history of suicide attempts, the recalled worst-point suicidal ideation may have occurred prior to, concurrent with, or even following the attempt. This temporal variability is inherent in retrospective assessments of lifetime peak symptoms and should be considered when interpreting the indirect pathways. Nonetheless, the consistency of our mediation findings with theoretical models (e.g., ideation-to-action frameworks) suggests that the observed associations are clinically meaningful, while longitudinal designs would be better suited to clarify temporal sequencing.

Conclusion

In adolescents with depression, there is a significant association among impulsivity, depression, suicidal ideation intensity, and suicide attempts. In this cross-sectional sample of depressed adolescents, trait impulsivity was not directly associated with the transition from suicidal ideation to suicide attempts; rather, it was indirectly associated with suicide attempts through depression severity and suicidal ideation intensity. These findings are consistent with the view that impulsivity may function as a distal correlate rather than a direct predictor. The correlation between impulsivity and suicidal ideation intensity weakens with increasing age.
