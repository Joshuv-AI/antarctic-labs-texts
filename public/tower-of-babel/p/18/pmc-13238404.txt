Integrated Reaction Monitoring and In Situ Structural Elucidation of Reactive Intermediates With Time‐Resolved NOAH NMR Supersequences
Authors: Sara M. Guzman, Yael Ben‐Tal, Jiayu Zhang, Mark Dixon, Jason E. Hein
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTStructural elucidation of reaction intermediates and side products is often key to mechanistic understanding. However, individual isolation of these species is time‐consuming, labor‐intensive, and often technically challenging, if not impossible. Here, we present an NMR experimental workflow involving the time‐resolved acquisition and processing of multiple types of two‐dimensional NMR spectra acquired in tandem using NOAH supersequences on an active reaction. This time‐resolved NOAH nonuniform sampling method (TR‐NOAH‐NUS) enables the generation of robust concentration–time plots for kinetic analysis in parallel to structural characterization of reaction components. Here, we demonstrate the application of TR‐NOAH‐NUS on an organo‐catalyzed acylation of alcohols. Despite the short (30 min) timespan of the reaction, we are able to obtain high‐quality HSQC, HMBC, and COSY spectra with excellent (less than 15 s) temporal resolution. From these data, we can observe both an on‐cycle catalytic intermediate and an off‐cycle catalyst degradation product and can directly assign the structures of both without need for further isolation.

Introduction

Structural characterization of reactive intermediates and side products is often key to fully understanding reactions and to allow for different potential reaction pathways to be distinguished. Resultantly, NMR is a powerful reaction‐monitoring technique as it can provide quantitative kinetic information with good temporal resolution as well as nuanced structural information [1, 2]. Most reaction monitoring with NMR utilizes one‐dimensional (1D) techniques, typically 1H and 19F monitoring. Although these methods provide excellent kinetic information, they often provide insufficient structural details (due to spectral overlap of reagents, products, and intermediates with a common structural core in the 1H spectrum or due to insufficient nuclei of interest in the 19F spectra). Although some side products can be isolated and characterized independently, this process is often time‐consuming and not always straightforward; in the case of reactive intermediates, isolation is often impossible.

Two‐dimensional (2D) NMR is the gold standard technique for structural characterization [3]. However, 2D experiments are significantly more time‐consuming than 1D NMR methodologies. Several innovations over the years have enabled a reduction in this experiment time, from the original development of pulsed‐field gradient methodologies to the implementation of nonuniform sampling (NUS) [4, 5, 6] to Frydman's more recent ultrafast 2D techniques [7, 8, 9]. One important advance is the recent development of the NMR by ordered acquisition using 1H detection (NOAH) supersequences by Kupče and coworkers [10, 11, 12]. This has enabled a dramatic reduction in the time required to perform a full characterization suite through magnetization sharing between the individual spectral modules within the supersequence, resulting in the collection of up to five separate spectra in the time it usually would have taken to acquire one.

Despite their additional analytical power, implementation of 2D techniques on in situ reactions remains uncommon due to the longer timescales required for acquisition, alongside related sensitivity challenges. In 2014, Mayzel and coworkers first reported the application of time‐resolved 2D NMR to monitor dynamic systems using multidimensional decomposition (mdd) coprocessing [13]. Soon after, in 2015 Dass and coworkers reported a similar approach using compressed sensing for individual processing of multiple overlapping spectra [14]. Both these methodologies take advantage of NUS to capture a series of 2D spectra in a time‐resolved rolling‐average fashion (TR‐NUS) [13, 14, 15]. By acquiring a single 2D spectrum utilizing NUS during the entire course of a reaction and then processing a subset of the acquired data, changing the position of the processing window each time, they were able to collect a series of time‐resolved high‐resolution 2D spectra over the course of the reaction. Subsequently, a handful of mechanistic studies [16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21] have utilized the TR‐NUS technique, for example to characterize photopolymerizations [22].

Existing reported applications of the TR‐NUS method, including novel uses of NMR supersequences in reaction monitoring, have only utilized one 2D technique at a time [23]. However, in many cases, full structural elucidation requires a combination of the information obtained from multiple 2D experiments. As there exist variants of the NOAH supersequences that are compatible with NUS [24], we reasoned that it should be possible to apply the TR‐NUS processing technique onto the data that had been collected using NOAH‐NUS to gain a series of several different types of 2D spectra, each resolved in time as the reaction progresses. Here, we demonstrate both the first application of and the utility of this combined TR‐NOAH‐NUS technique by elucidating the identity of intermediates within an organo‐catalyzed acylation reaction.

Results and Discussion

Enantioselective Acylation—Identification of Intermediates and Off‐Cycle Species

The enantioselective acylation of alcohols catalyzed by (−)‐benzotetramisole (BTM, 4) has been an ongoing object of study in our lab [25, 26]. Online kinetic monitoring of this reaction by HPLC‐MS revealed the formation of several distinct catalytically active (5) and inhibitory species (6), which appeared by mass balance to be associated with the starting BTM catalyst. However, we had little structural information about the species beyond what was provided by mass spectrometry, and isolation of any of these species proved nontrivial. Therefore, we turned to NMR to aid with structural characterization. Analysis of the one‐dimensional spectra and associated reaction time courses provided insufficient and ambiguous structural information. We envisioned that time‐resolved 2D data, highlighting multiple couplings, would allow us to identify these species.

Considerations for Implementation of NOAH in Reaction Monitoring

NOAH enables the simultaneous acquisition of multiple 1H‐detected 2D NMR experiments as a series of “modules” acquired using a shared recovery delay (D1), with careful preservation of magnetization and unused coherences between each module [12]. This dramatically reduces total experiment time while maintaining compatibility with conventional NMR instrumentation and processing methods, including NUS techniques. To get meaningful information from the TR‐NOAH‐NUS data, we carefully selected the modules within our NOAH experiment, as well as the order in which the modules were acquired. For full structural elucidation, we required at a minimum the connectivity information provided by both HSQC and HMBC, as well as COSY or TOCSY to allow us to differentiate between spin systems that could not be distinguished on the basis of kinetic behavior. Furthermore, we wanted to be able to plot accurate concentration–time data. We did this by taking integrals from the HSQC spectra; therefore, the NOAH sequence was initially designed to ensure that the HSQC was the first module to avoid the potential for uneven consumption of magnetization by other modules, and 1JHC was carefully chosen (145 Hz) by inspection of those 1JCH values measurable a priori to ensure it would be semiquantitative for this system (see Supporting Information Section S2, Figure S4). This assumption was later validated by mass balance and inspection of the 2D spectra acquired during the reaction.

Considerations for Implementation of TR‐NUS for Reaction Monitoring

Traditional 2D NMR acquires a uniform array of datasets along the indirect dimension, with the total number of sampled points (n = t 1 increments) determining the overall acquisition time, digital resolution, and sensitivity. These are collected sequentially and are limited by the maximum number of points to acquire (t 1max). However, if instead of a stepwise linear acquisition of t 1 increments, a sparse random subset of those points is acquired, the missing data can later be reconstructed to generate a full spectrum that has the same t 1max, and therefore digital resolution, but which was acquired in a fraction of the time. This technique is commonly known as NUS and is widely implemented for conventional NMR experiments [4, 5, 6, 27].

Time‐resolved NUS methods arise directly from this ability to reconstruct a full spectrum from a limited and unevenly distributed dataset. Any subset of points from a large randomly ordered dataset (e.g., one acquired over the course of an entire reaction) can be treated as an independent smaller sparsely distributed dataset instead (e.g., a specific time window within the reaction) and thus be reconstructed into its own spectrum, assuming Nyquist–Shannon sampling requirements are met for each subset [14]. This generates a series of time‐resolved 2D spectra reconstructed from the same number of points throughout a single reaction, each temporally resolved by the time required to acquire each t 1 datapoint [13, 14].

A number of different methods exist for determining both the NUS sampling schedule and the resultant reconstruction methods, each with different strengths and weaknesses [6, 13, 27]. Most conventional methods for creating this list generate a random subset consisting of any t 1 increment within the range provided by the number of points defined in the indirect dimension (t 1max). Since the experiment acquisition time is dependent on the total number of t 1 increments sampled, in the case of TR‐NUS performed on a dynamically changing sample where the 2D spectral acquisition must continue over the entire time course of the reaction, the t 1max increases far beyond what would be required for a static 2D spectrum. Furthermore, the acquired data are later processed as a series of separate spectra, each of which only uses a subset of the acquired data. An increase of t 1max increases the size of the total Nyquist grid and thus also the minimum number of NUS points required to reconstruct the space, limiting the temporal resolution [14]. Additionally, signal‐to‐noise ratio (SNR) is significantly higher for data points collected with smaller t 1 values. Thus, in order to balance signal and resolution, an artificial limit on the value of t 1max was imposed while keeping the number of acquired points high [14]. This resulted in a long NUS list containing multiple repetitions that allowed time‐resolved reconstruction of short, overlapping time windows. Indeed, we were able to observe a notable difference in SNR between different time courses recorded with different values of t 1max but otherwise processed identically (see Supporting Information Section S3.1, Figure S5).

Although some thought must be put into selecting the NUS sampling schedule ahead of time, TR‐NUS is primarily a postprocessing method, which enables the optimization of analysis without the requirement to perform additional experiments. A key feature here is in the selection of processing window size; i.e., how many NUS points are used to reconstruct each time‐resolved 2D spectrum. Although larger window sizes can increase the spectral resolution and SNR, this comes at the cost of temporal resolution of the kinetic profile [14]. In particular, the measurement “dead time”—the time at the beginning of the reaction which cannot be observed—varies dramatically with window size. As the first points in a reaction are typically those containing the most meaningful kinetic data, a delicate balance must be struck between SNR and temporal resolution. Additionally, exceedingly large window sizes may also lead to overaveraging, which can result in inaccurate representation of the reaction kinetics (see Supporting Information Section S3.2, Figure S6). Just as each reconstruction of the 2D spectra require an appropriate number of datapoints (dependent on the number of peaks and size of the Nyquist grid), defining the upper limit for the window size is essential to obtain reliable kinetic information.

Kinetic Monitoring by TR‐NOAH‐NUS and Elimination of Reaction Monitoring “Dead Time”

The specific reaction and conditions studied are illustrated in Scheme 1. Although this reaction is much shorter than any on which the TR‐NUS concept has previously been demonstrated (previous reactions have been in the order of hours to days) [13, 14, 18, 20, 28], we reasoned that we could nonetheless take advantage of the much shorter acquisition times required for quantitative HSQC relative to 1D acquisition [29] to enable collection of sufficiently high‐quality data.

Acylation of chiral alcohols catalyzed by BTM (4), alongside the specific conditions used in this study.

We initially collected a two‐module NOAH (13C‐1H HSQC‐HMBC) supersequence utilizing NUS. After optimizing the postprocessing parameters, we were able to collect a series of 771 HSQC spectra, with an average SNR of 284 in the first 10 timepoints (calculated from starting Material 1) and an average SNR of 490 at the end of the reaction (calculated from Product 3, last 10 timepoints). The concentration–time plot obtained by integrating selected peaks from the HSQC showed excellent agreement with that obtained by conventional 1D kinetic monitoring of a separate reaction with the same conditions (Figure 1), including the ability to observe all three catalyst‐linked species (see Supporting Information Section 4.1, Figure S8).

Overlaid concentration–time plots of two separate reactions monitored with either conventional 1D NMR (dark circles) or the TR‐NOAH‐NUS technique (light circles; concentration values calculated by integration of HSQC spectra).

However, even with these optimized conditions, the initial moments of the reaction, lost to both reaction assembly and to the time required to collect sufficient transients to reconstruct a 2D spectrum, are on the order of 5 min (c.f., the 2 min of “dead time” arising solely from reaction assembly in the single‐scan 1D case). As the reaction has reached completion within 40 min, this amounts to missing the first eighth of the reaction entirely.

To minimize this monitoring “dead time” we proposed to incorporate timepoints from prior to reaction initiation into our NUS spectral reconstruction (Figure 2). We acquired a 25‐min, two‐module NOAH‐NUS experiment (128 points in the indirect dimension) on a sample of the reaction solution without propionic anhydride 2; following the addition of 2, we obtained a second two‐module NOAH‐NUS experiment with 1600 points in the indirect dimension, utilizing otherwise identical parameters. These experiments (and NUS lists) were then combined to create a single 2D dataset, which could then be analyzed in the same TR‐NUS fashion as previously (Figure 3a). This novel reconstruction procedure provided a drastically decreased reaction‐monitoring dead time without sacrificing SNR or deviating time‐course “ground truth,” which improved the quality of the kinetic trends for the transient catalytic species (Figure 3b). A similar approach, utilizing signal averaging [30] rather than NUS reconstruction, should also be applicable to reduce effective dead time in 1D kinetics experiments (see Supporting Information Section S4.3, Figure S10).

As this methodology allowed for improvements in both SNR and temporal resolution, we were able to switch the order of the modules in the NOAH sequence and increase the number of scans to ensure the highest possible spectral resolution for the HMBC, which was necessary for the structural elucidation of the intermediate species (Figure 3c; Section 2.5).

It is important to note that this is a data reconstruction method; although we anticipate that it will provide excellent early‐reaction data in most instances, it is important to remember that it will be unable to provide insight into processes that begin upon reaction initiation but are complete prior to the acquisition of the first data point in the 2D FID, such as catalyst activation processes. Thus, caution and good judgment must be used when implementing this technique.

Structural Elucidation of Catalyst‐Linked Species

After determining from the HSQC that we were able to monitor all three previously observed catalyst‐linked species by 2D NMR, we turned to the HMBC module to aid us in determining the structure of all three species. Kinetic monitoring shows an initial rapid conversion of BTM Catalyst 4 to an Intermediate 5, which slowly reverts back to 4 as the reaction progresses. In tandem, we observed formation of a related off‐cycle Species 6 (Figure 3b).

Analysis of the time‐resolved HMBC spectra allowed for structural elucidation of Species 5 and 6. The resonances corresponding to each species could be distinguished from each other not only by following coupling correlations but also by each species' distinct time‐course profile. From the HSQC, HMBC, and m/z data, Species 5 was quickly identified as the acylated form of 4 (see Figure 3c, Figure 4, and Supporting Information Section S5.1, Figure S11), which is in line with previous mechanistic studies conducted on this and similar reactions [31, 32, 33].

Proposed simplified mechanistic cycle, including the observed intermediate Species 5 and the off‐cycle Species 6. The persistence of 5 is in line with previous kinetic studies on this system [31].

Analysis of the HMBC data corresponding to off‐cycle Species 6 revealed the presence of three distinct carbonyl groups, including one at 201 ppm. Although unusual for species in this catalytic system, a peak at this chemical shift is extremely diagnostic of a thioester [34]. Further analysis of a more‐resolved spectrum obtained through the combined processing of all regions of the acquired data containing a large amount of 6 allowed for further correlations to be drawn. Final confirmation of the structure of 6 (see Figure 3c, and Supporting Information Section S5) was provided by performing a three‐module NOAH experiment containing an additional COSY module. Previous work had indicated reaction inhibition in the presence of water [33, 35]; indeed, addition of water to a 1:3 mixture of BTM (3) and propionic anhydride (2) allowed us to clearly track formation of 6 and to unambiguously assign its structure using a combination of HSQC, HMBC, and COSY (see Supporting Information Section S5.2 for full details). These results are in line with similar observations previously reported by Li and coworkers [33].

Conclusions

We here present the first application of the TR‐NOAH‐NUS technique, allowing HSQC, HMBC, and COSY spectra to be acquired in tandem, with good temporal resolution throughout the course of a live reaction. Analysis of these resultant spectra allowed for the identification and full structural elucidation of a true acylated intermediate species, as well as the catalyst decomposition species, without further isolation or the need for further experimentation. This structural analysis, particularly in the latter case, required the combination of different 2D experiments obtained by this method. In this case study, careful choice of 1JCH value enabled NOAH‐HSQC spectra to be acquired in a semiquantitative value, enabling the generation of time‐course data showing excellent agreement with kinetic data acquired using conventional 1D measurement. Recent work has demonstrated the development of fully quantitative HSQC spectra [36], and work is currently ongoing in our group to incorporate qHSQC techniques into the TR‐NOAH‐NUS workflow.

Experimental

(S)‐1‐phenylethan‐1‐ol (1) (124 μmol, 15 μL) was added to a solution of (−)‐benzotetramisole (BTM, 4) (21.4 μmol, 5.4 mg) in CDCl3 (0.5 mL). The homogenous solution was transferred to a 5 mm NMR tube. A 1H spectrum was acquired after locking and shimming, followed by a 25‐min, two‐module NOAH‐NUS (HMBC, HSQC) experiment. The sample was ejected from the spectrometer, and the reaction was initiated by addition of propionic anhydride (2) (116 μmol, 15 μL). The tube was mixed well and immediately returned to the spectrometer, where a 7‐h, two‐module NOAH‐NUS (HMBC, HSQC) experiment was started without further shimming. The NUS list was generated using mddNMR 2.7 software [6], with NImax = 200 for both experiments and NI = 32 for the first NOAH experiment and NI = 400 for the second. All TR‐NUS processing was performed using the mdd‐NMR software. Further details can be found in the Supporting Information (see Section S1, Figure S1).
