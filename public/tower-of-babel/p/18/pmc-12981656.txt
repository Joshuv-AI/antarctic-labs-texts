Inference of Tumor Progression Patterns in Colon Cancer Using Optimal Cell Order Analysis in Single Cell Resolution
Authors: Marmar R. Moussa, Charles H. Street
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Accurate determination of lineage or evolutionary pathways is critical for understanding the dynamic developmental and temporal progression patterns observed in single cell omics data. In this study, we present a computational approach designed to infer progression patterns in cell populations that are actively evolving, differentiating or progressing along dynamic pathways. Our method works at single cell resolution using single cell RNA-Seq data. We determine the optimal cell order based on the cellular transcriptional profiles to uncover the progression of cell populations along differentiation, signaling, or tumor evolution trajectories. To achieve this, we developed a seriation-based method for progression pattern inference, leveraging optimally reordered hierarchies. We provide advanced visualization tools using principal curves to represent the inferred pathways in a three-dimensional latent space derived from scRNA-Seq data. Furthermore, we propose new metrics for evaluating the accuracy of the reconstructed order and identified pathways. The effectiveness of our approach is demonstrated through applying the analysis to real human colon samples transcriptomics data that we generated specifically for this study.

Introduction and Background

COMPUTATIONAL methods for modeling or reconstruction of biological processes dynamics from single cell omics data share a common goal to infer a cellular order or pseudo-time order of the cells that would accurately capture a developmental or evolutionary process. Different methods focus on various strategies; for instance, first, methods aiming to infer RNA dynamics from splicing, e.g. from [1], have been used for modeling dynamics of cell states leveraging intronic reads information, however such methods rely on assumptions such as a constant splicing rate.

Other strategies involve modeling of gene regulation in either direction (up or down regulation) and utilize highly parameterized statistical models hindering the interpretability of the results and preventing meaningful insights. Furthermore, often expensive and signal-altering manipulations and pre-processing of the data is performed, preventing such methods from being widely used. Nevertheless, performing a trajectory or pseudo-time analysis for single cell RNA-Seq analyses has become a de-facto step in the recommended scRNA-Seq analysis workflows, whether RNA dynamics or differentiation processes are expected to be manifested in the data or not. This can limit the dynamics or trajectory inference analysis to an afterthought in the analysis rather than an important tool for discovering new biological insights.

Importantly, most of the trajectory inference or pseudo-time determination algorithms that are currently available utilize Minimum Spanning Tree (MST) algorithms for creating a pseudo-timeline that aims to reflect cellular differentiation or development. Additionally, most MST-based methods exclusively operate in latent space for practicality. Popular scRNA-Seq analysis packages typically include an implementation of MST-based pseudotime inference, nevertheless, trajectory analysis based on MST face various limitations associated with their practical application.

MST algorithms can be useful for trajectory inference in single cell analysis workflows, but they come with several limitations. A key issue is their assumption of linearity, which fails to fully capture the nonlinear dynamics of biological processes such as cell differentiation, tumor microenvironment (TME) modulation, signaling pathways, or the progression from pre-cancerous states to advanced cancer and metastasis. Additionally, these methods are highly sensitive to noise and outliers in scRNA-Seq data, often requiring meticulous parameter tuning, which can be computationally demanding for large datasets. They also perform poorly with sparse sampling, especially in early developmental stages or rare cell populations, and struggle to model branching trajectories, necessitating more sophisticated approaches.

As a result, the inferred trajectories often lack biological interpretability, requiring further domain expertise and experimental validation to ensure accuracy. A detailed benchmarking study of these methods can be found in [2]. Most importantly, existing methods make the assumption that all cells within a dataset as well as all genes detected in a sample or dataset, contribute to mainly one single pattern or trajectory describing progression or dynamics. This assumption prevents cells from being labeled as active in more than one signaling pathway or process. As an example, immune cells can very well be active along multiple processes such as in both proliferation and maturation pathways or other metabolic pathways, tumor cells can also be activated along the cell cycle pathway, but simultaneously active in a cancer-related signaling pathway.

Given these limitations, especially the assumption of linearity and the assumption that a cell population can only be active in a single pathway, we developed a novel computational approach that utilities the gene expression profiles to achieve seriation-based optimal ordering for the reconstruction of cell order. The reconstructed order helps to identify relationships of cell populations, such as precursor-successor or activated-inactivated, within and across various samples. In turn, this approach helps uncover underlying longitudinal dynamics in transcriptomics data.

Our method aims at reconstructing the progression order unique to a given specific biological pathway one at a time, but allows for multiple pathways activation patterns to be examined, identified and scored within the same dataset of single cells.

We present an interactive framework with novel visualization techniques, including a three-dimensional projection of the inferred cellular trajectory in latent space.

Furthermore, we propose two novel metrics, Progression Pattern Index (PPI), and Label Change Index (LCI), which are used for identifying activated pathways as well as evaluating the reconstructed order and identified pathways.

Methods

Cell Order Reconstruction

The presented algorithm for cell order reconstruction is based on optimal leaf order identification in a hierarchy or dendrogram. The leafs represent the set of cells with latent dynamics or progression whether temporal or evolutionary. Previously, we successfully applied an optimal leaf order algorithm for cell cycle order inference in [3] and recently we presented a version of this approach in [4]. In summary, first cells’ representation in latent space is obtained by generating principal components followed by projection in low dimensional t-SNE space, ensuring variability from principal components as well as locality from t-SNE representation are preserved [3], [5].The order reconstruction step is then performed in the latent space representation to infer an optimal leaf ordering for n cells by first producing a dendrogram using any appropriate rooted binary tree algorithm (e.g. agglomorative hierarchical clustering or graph-based clustering) and second, reordering this dendrogram. It is important to note here that such a tree or dendrogram would have n−1 internal nodes and hence 2n−1 possible leaf orderings to be considered and therefore, performing additional leaf-node reordering is equivalent to minimizing the length of a Hamiltonian path [6]. Particularly for our method, a much lower complexity is achieved by using the Optimal Leaf Ordering (OLO) algorithm from [6] which produces a leaf cell ordering that minimizes the sum of distances between adjacent leaves in On3. Further improved complexity is achieved in the practical implementation of our method since the cells’ pairwise distances On2 are typically already pre-computed for upstream clustering steps, typically preceding progression analysis or trajectory inference. A toy example of a simple Dendrogram reordering is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Cells pairwise distances are calculated using those gene modules only implicated in a selected differentiation or signaling process to be examined (i.e. not all genes detected in the dataset are used but only those from the pathway annotation with detectable expression). Gene modules activated in specific pathways are defined by dedicated knowledge-bases and can be obtained as annotated gene lists (e.g. from KEGG Pathways [7] or Gene Ontology (GO) databases which are used throughout this manuscript). Consequently, the reconstructed order is a representation of the dynamics captured or activation of the progression mechanism of any considered pathway. This strategy can clearly accommodate multiple pathways to be considered for analysis and does not limit the cells to one pathway or one activation pattern.

By ordering the expression values of any individual gene from the selected pathway according to the inferred cell order, we clearly observe a distinct pattern revealing its transcriptional dynamics. This effect is illustrated in Fig. 2. Here, we consider gene EREG; the red panel represents the inferred cellular order as opposed to a randomized order in the black panel. We note here, that there are no prior assumptions as to which pattern is expected and no assumption of a certain model that the gene expression has to follow. For instance, several methods require a reconstructed order to show a sinusoidal patterns [8] for the gene expression, whereas our approach allows for the pattern to be freely discovered from the data. This non-parametric data-driven approach allows for patterns to be learned from the dataset with no prior contrainsts, as opposed to assuming a certain pre-defined model of expression that needs to fit all datasets regardless of the actual underlying dynamics.

All genes of given pathway (all genes from the gene list representing the pathway) are considered when reconstructing the sequence or cell order. Various levels of activity can be detected in various cell populations or progression clusters within one dataset. It can be assumed that typically three activation levels can exist, where one cell group is highly active given a certain pathway (for instance a cell group is highly proliferating when considering the cell cycle pathway, where other cell groups could be moderately proliferating or quiescent). Fig. 3 illustrates in detail the results of applying our ordering method to the Human Colon Cancer single cell RNA dataset [9] from 10x Genomics [10] against the ‘Colon Cancer Pathway’ as defined in KEGG Pathways Database [7]. Insights from the inferred reconstructed order is further discussed in the Discussion Section III and throughout the manuscript.

Progression Pattern Index (PPI) Metric

To evaluate the progression patterns (cell order + gene contribution to given order) inferred by our algorithm, we propose two novel metrics based on serial-correlation analysis.

Serial- or auto-correlation (can also be referred to as ‘lagged’ correlation), is defined in (1) and refers to the relationship between the current value of a variable and its past values over time: (1) Sxy[k]=∑t=0N−1x[t]⋅y[t−k] where x represents the gene expression values vector, t represents the order index or time-point and y is a lagged (by k) version of the gene ordered values.

A high serial correlation is often observed in temporal or sequential data where data points exhibit a pattern or dependency based on their temporal order. In Fig. 4 we visualize this relationship when applied to gene expression data.

We define Pathway Progression Index (PPI) metric for a pathway with N genes and given a pre-determined order as in the following (2): (2) Medsordgi−1R∑j=1Rsrandjgi:i=1,…,N where N is number of genes annotated relevant to the current pathway, sordgi denotes the first-order serial correlation score of gene i with respect to the given cell order, and srandjgi, j=1,…,R, denotes the score of gene i with respect to boot-stapping a number of R randomized cell orders. In other words, this metric quantifies the significance of an emerging pattern under order conditions versus under randomized version of the data. Clearly a ‘good’ order will result in higher PPI score as calculated over a random order lack of ubiquitous pattern. If the majority of genes are not active (i.e. the majority of genes have no significance/less than 0.05 PPI score) in the currently considered pathway, the cell population is deemed inactive for this pathway and not considered any further.

Fig. 5 show boxplots representing the PPI analysis of three progression groups from the Colon Cancer dataset. The boxplots aggregate the individual gene scores and illustrate the PPI score per group (the median line for each of the boxplots). Three cell populations or progession groups are considered: Group 1 (red) has the lowest PPI score (0.0415) which indicates that the majority of gene expression values only have a random pattern after the proposed order is applied and therefore is classified not-active, however, and in contrast, Group 3 scores highest, with PPI score of 0.3238 and significance (p-value) of 1.045e-09 as compared to the rest of the groups, indicating the majority of genes manifest a clear ‘smooth’ pattern under the given order.

From an individual gene perspective, this score denotes whether the gene displays a pattern or not (without assuming a specific distribution model or pattern) given the ordered cell population. Each gene can play different roles given different pathways and this approach indeed enables this flexibility as shown for instance in Fig. (6).

GMM-based multi-model analysis of PPI score:

The PPI score can be further extended to describe the pathway activity as a whole when there is considerable high activity for the majority of the genes of the examined pathway in a cell population. This can be achieved by fine tuning the PPI into a pathway activity score to be calculated as in (3), (3) MedGMHigh∣sordgi−1R∑j=1Rsrandjgi∣:i=1,…,N where GMHigh denotes only genes with ‘high’ first-order serial correlation are included in the pathway as classified by unsupervised classification step using generalized gaussian mixture model algorithm.

The gene scores contributing to the PPI index are classified using Gaussian mixed models, where the model best fitting with the maximum Baysian Information Criterion(BIC) is selected to predict the genes’ classes into at least one non-active gene class (lowest mean class) and one or more active gene classes (moderate to high activity classes).

The pathway activity is then calculated using all except the non-active genes class making the PPI more precise per pathway and per active cell population. Fig. 7 shows the detected classes or mixture models when several progression groups are analyzed.

It is important to note that since the gene lists obtained from public databases are based on general prior knowledge not dataset-specific, this data-driven strategy ensures tailoring the gene lists to the data and allows for precise but also adaptive pathway gene detection specific to each individual dataset.

Inferred Order Correctness: Label Change Index

To further assess the inferred order, especially when a ‘ground truth’ cell order is known, for instance known cells’ annotations or labels that correspond to progression phases or processes within a pathway, the labeled datasets can be assessed not only via the PPI score but also with a ‘Label Change Index’ or LCI. A ground truth can be established in datasets where for instance cell cycle phases within the cell cycle pathway can be experimentally identified, or when various known stages in cancer progression are samples separately. A metric adapted from [8] can be used, which measures how frequent an experimentally determined single cell labels changes along the identified progression order. Ideally, a perfectly ordered set would change labels only between the phases or processes. Using cell cycle phases of proliferation as example, an ordered set of G1, S and G2M cell cycle phases would include only two (n-1 labels) label changes along the pathway progression. Thus, we define the Label Change Index per progression group as: (4) LCI=1−∑LC−(n−1)/(N−n) where LC means the sum of the label changes between two adjacent cells in the given order, n in the number of identified groups and N is the total number of ordered cells. A perfectly correct series or order would have change index value of 1, while the most incorrect where ∑LC=N−1 (i.e. all labels were changed) would have a value of 0.

Results and Discussion

Our proposed approach presents a comprehensive analysis method for progression order reconstruction and pathway activity quantification that utilizes optimal leaf ordering algorithm and identifies clusters of progression learned from single cell RNA-Seq data. Indeed, the proposed method is able to first, infer a cell order along a progression pathway defined by gene modules from prior knowledge bases. We identify clusters of progressions, or progression ordered groups of cells where the detected gene signals and the PPI scores indicate how much activity each cell group or cluster of Progression is contributing.

Principal Curves - 3-D order visualization in latent space:

A principal curve is a smooth curve passing through the middle of a multidimensional dataset, providing a (visual) non-linear summary of the data. The algorithm for constructing principal curves utilizes a prior (ordered) summary of the data [13] to construct the curve. While we provided several options for visualizing the inferred order, e.g. through color coded bars representing cell labels as previously shown in Figs. 3 and 8, we also utilize the principal curves algorithm to fitting a principal curve to our results in three dimensions given the inferred order. Fig. 9 shows the learned orders of the hESC data in (a) and the combined colon cancer data in (b) fitted into a principal curve visualization in three dimensional t-SNE space. This approach provides an elegant visualization of the inferred order showing the succession of the progression groups for each of the examined datasets.

Insights from order and Progression Pattern inference:

As presented in Section II-A Fig. 3, the inferred order starts by mostly cells from cell type cluster 5 (yellow, second color bar), enriched in extracellular matrix gene modules which indeed plays a significant role in the colon cancer pathway situating this cell type/population as the most active group of the identified path (corresponding to Progression Group 3 (orange in top color bar)), followed by mostly goblet cells population coded by light blue (Cell type Cluster 2) cells in the second color bar. This matches known activation patterns of colon cancer progression where strong evidence suggests the involvement of ECM remodeling and Goblet Cells enrichment in pre-cancerous states progression [14].

This non-parametric data-driven approach allows for patterns to be freely learned from the dataset as opposed to assuming a certain pre-defined model of gene expression (e.g. some methods [8] assume sinusoidal patterns of gene expression) that needs to fit all genes in all datasets regardless of the underlying dynamics.

Inference of active progression pathways:

Not only can our method identify cell populations that are actively progressing along a given pathway, but we can use the PPI score to examine and quantify the activity for each potential pathway in a dataset. We show this use-case in Fig. 10. Here, two datasets are tested, first, the Human Colon Cancer adenocarcinoma dataset in the left panel, and second are Covid19 immune(T) cells [12] downloaded from [15]. The bar plots indicate the most active pathways in each dataset sorted by PPI scores (maximum per pathway over all groups). The relevance of the identified pathways using our method is clear from the pathways description active in each dataset. Per group active pathways are given in Fig. 10(c)) panel.

Typically, for basic science application, only relevant pathways need to be tested in each dataset; it is expected that pathways with mostly overlapping gene lists would produce similar PPI scores. In Fig. 11 we show the Jaccard similarity coefficient -a statistic used for gauging the similarity/diversity of sample sets-of a selected number of cancer related pathways. For the cancer types pathways, we observe a higher similarity than in cancer processes pathways. When testing pathways with lower similarities, the PPI score is particularly useful for picking out the most active pathway of the pathway set being tested.

Application in Human Colon Cancer:

Finally, we apply our method towards analyzing and inferring the progression mechanisms of cells along the colorectal cancer pathway using a combined dataset consisting of our new experimental data samples generated in our lab, 3 human colon tissue samples, where two are pre-cancerous serrated lesions (HP/Hyperplastic Polyp and SSL/Sessile Serrated Lesion), and one is a normal (N) but tumor-adjacent tissue sample that we sequenced specifically for this study. We further combine our samples’ transcriptomics data with the public advanced adenocarcinoma (Ad) Human Colon Cancer dataset to form a 4-samples/libraries combined set of real samples data simulating the progression pathways and dynamics from normal to CRC. Since Normal colon tissue can potentially advance to precancerous lesions on the way to becoming cancerous tissue, we can construct the ground truth order to go from N (Normal) to HP (benign early lesions) followed by SSL (also benign lesions that can arise from Normal or HP lesions [16] [17]) to cancerous adenocarcinoma (Ad). This analysis, presented in Figs. 12 and 13 allows to showcase our approach. Fig. 12(b)) shows nine cell types annotated in the combined set via typical single cell RNA-Seq cell type analysis upstream from progression order inference. Two epithelial cell clusters and one tumor cells’ cluster are enriched in progression group 3 (orange) in the heatmap from (c) showing the reconstructed order and progression groups. The PPI scores of the three progression groups, identify group 3 (orange) as most active with CTNNB1, HYC, AXIN2, CCND1, RHOA and RAC1 as the top genes with highest autocorrelation scores under the inferred cell order. The composition of cell types distribution within each of the progression groups in (f) indicate that Squamous Epithelial cells, SATB2-high epithelial cells and the tumor cells contribute the highest in cells activated along the colorectal cancer pathways. From Fig. 12(f)), it is interesting to observe the contribution of the Normal (tumor adjacent) sample to group 3 (most active group for colorectal cancer pathway) is minimal yet non-trivial (14.84% ) while the Ad sample has indeed the highest share in group 3. Since this analysis indicates that 14% or more of the normal adjacent tissue is active along the colorectal cancer progression pathway, we further examined the genes of that pathway that are commonly abundant in both samples N and Ad. Indeed, this revealed several genes that are expressed in both samples, including PIK3CA, MLH1, MSH6, PMS2, FGFBR2 and CEACAM5, all classical markers of colorectal cancer progression pathway. This shows the strength of the proposed approach in identifying potential early marker genes that can be identified via this tumor progression pattern inference analysis.
