Effect of combustion temperature on the unburnt carbon during ammonia co-firing with coal combustion
Authors: Machodi Mathaba
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Ammonia co-firing with coal seems to be the preferred approach to reduce unburnt carbon in existing pulverized coal-fired power plants. However, some operational conditions such as combustion temperature need to be carefully considered. As such, this study investigated the effect of combustion temperature on the unburnt carbon during ammonia co-firing with pulverized coal. Horizontal tube furnace with ammonia injection was employed and operating at different combustion temperature (800 to 1200 oC). Proximate analysis results showed that the raw coal sample contained 64.2 wt% fixed carbon. Additionally, based on the several analyses employed to assess the effect of combustion temperature efficacy on the unburnt carbon reduction, an effective combustion efficiency was achieved with ammonia co-firing with coal. X-ray fluorescence results revealed that SiO2 for the co-firing experiment decreased from 56.7476 wt% (800 oC) to 45.9423 wt% (1200 oC) while carbon decreased from 20.06 to 18.32 wt%, at the same combustion temperature respectively. X-ray diffraction also revealed a well-defined peak at 2θ = 25o, a graphite characteristic, which decreased significantly with increasing temperature, signifying unburnt carbon reduction. Thus, it can be confirmed that ammonia co-firing with coal can be used to improve the combustion efficiency coal fired power plants.

Introduction

The sustained global dependence and continual reliance on fossil fuels as the primary energy source is facing a substantial setback, especially in electricity generation. Following developments and decisions which took place at COP26 in Scotland, countries were urged to produce 2030 emission reduction targets that align with reaching net zero by 2050 and keep global warming below 1.5 oC as per the Paris Agreement1. Currently, 87% of the global energy demand is supplied by coal-fired power plants2. Coal-fired boilers in thermal power plants generate more CO2 emissions than any other means of power generation3. Therefore, it’s extremely crucial to reduce CO2 emissions from coal fired power stations4. Various technologies such as using renewable energies, increasing energy efficiency, nuclear power, fuel switching and/or co-firing with carbon-free fuel, and carbon capture storage are measures to be considered to inhibit global warming caused by greenhouse gases5. Renewable energy and fuel switching have gained significant attention to reduce emission and hydrogen which is a carbon free fuel, is expected to play an important role in achieving a low carbon economy. Although the storage and transportation of hydrogen are still challenging, ammonia (NH3) which has a very high volumetric hydrogen density compared to liquid nitrogen and organic hydride has proven to be an effective candidate for hydrogen carrier6. Various literature studies have pointed researchers globally to look closely at hydrogen and NH3 as unsurpassed carbon-free fuels that emit no harmful or toxic substances when burnt7. NH3 can be used as a combustion fuel without a hydrogen extraction process, and it is expected that it will have high potential as a fuel in thermal power plants. As such, to realize decarbonization in the next 2–4 decades, research has directed attention to co-firing with a carbon-free fuel in coal combustion chambers, among others, as a mitigation measure to meet the three pillars of sustainability3. Co-firing is simply the combustion of at least two different types of combustible fuels simultaneously and has been identified to have several key advantages over conventional strategies8. Moreover, literature studies on utilizing ammonia as a carbon free fuel in thermal power plants are well-developed and established8,4,9.

Generally, coal-burning systems are the critical sources of GHGs emissions. About 31.5 Gt of CO2 emissions emanate from coal-fired power plants; thus, more attention is being paid to the possibility of reducing the emissions by co-firing coal with ammonia3. The combustion process is sometimes incomplete due to improper burning efficiency. Hence, it results in a portion of the coal body lacking chemical energy conversion called unburned carbon (UBC); the higher the concentration, the lower the efficiency10. The high content of UBC represents an environmental problem, including an economic loss in energy that could have been obtained with optimal conditions11.Tan et al.7 demonstrated a decrease in unburnt carbon from 52 to 38% for coal combustion and coal with ammonia co-firing, respectively. The study further investigated the influence of combustion temperature on NOx emissions but not on unburnt carbon. The influence of combustion temperature on the unburnt carbon during coal with ammonia co-firing is an untapped subject with potential for further investigations. With the rapidly growing technology encompassing quantum chemistry to study coal combustion deeply, this research investigated the effect of combustion temperature on the unburned carbon during ammonia co-firing with coal.

Experimental procedure

The experimental system is composed of high-temperature horizontal tube furnace, gas transmission system and ceramic crucibles. The gas transmission system consists of gas cylinders, pressure reducing valves, flow meter, flexible rubber hoses and sealing rubber plugs as shown in Fig. 1. Argon, ammonia, and oxygen used in the experiment were supplied in pressured cylinders. The gases were directed to the tubular furnace via the flexible rubber hoses with rubber plugs ensuring a tight seal to preventing any gas leakages. Coal used in the study was sourced from eMalahleni, a coal dominant town in the Mpumalanga Province of South Africa. The coal sample was pulverized and about 15 g was used in each experimental run. Firstly, pure coal combustion runs were conducted to benchmark and then ammonia co-firing with coal combustion followed. Malvern Mastersizer 2000 instrument using laser diffraction technique was used to estimate the milled sample particle size distribution with a target size between 75 and 105 μm. Table 1 shows the proximate and ultimate analysis results of the pulverized coal. The determined calorific value of the pulverized samples was determined to be 20.0 MJ/kg higher heating value (HHV). When combusted, South African coals expend an average energy of around 25.1 MJ/kg12. This value does not deviate much from the one obtained in the literature study in which an analysis of eight coal samples averaged around 20.4 MJ/kg13. Thus, it was concluded that this coal sample strongly correlates with the most utilized bituminous coal in South Africa14.

The coal sample was pulverized and sieved to achieve a particle size below 75 μm. 15 g coal samples were loaded inside a rectangular crucible boat and placed in the middle of the tube furnace on the heating zone. Both raw coal and ammonia co-firing experiments were conducted at different temperatures in the range of 80°C – 120°C for 30 min after the desired temperature has been reached8. Both oxygen and ammonia were maintained at 100 mL/min for pure coal and co-firing tests with argon as a carrier gas set at 75 mL/min15. Ash/char samples were collected after each run for further analysis. Argon was used as a carrier gas to ensure uniform carrier gas to ensure uniform ammonia distribution around the furnace and as a diluent gas to control flame behaviour during the co-firing process16,17.

Results and discussions

Table 1 shows the proximate and ultimate analyses of the coal sample before combustion. Generally, the proximate analysis represents the physical properties of the coal body. From the table above, fixed carbon at 78.3% constituted the highest composition, followed by volatile matter. Ash and moisture content comprise the remaining volume at 7.9 and 4.5%, respectively. However, in this research study, only C, H, and O have been considered to play a vital role compared to other elements13. Their compositions were determined to be 78.3% (C), 5.81% (H) and 13.53% (O). Figure 2 shows particle size of the pulverized coal analysed using Malvern Mastersizer. Spontaneous combustion is a major problem in the coal value chain21,20. Thus, the knowledge of particle size distribution is a crucial physical attribute within coal and related processing. Its measurement plays an important role in these wide-range applications. For instance, the size of coal particles determines heat and mass transfer characteristics23.Studies have reported coal particle sizes ideal for efficient combustion to be in the range of 5–105 µm24,25.

Table 2 report on the results of X-ray Fluorescence (XRF) results of raw coal samples and residual ash samples collected after coal and ammonia co-firing combustion. The results revealed that the raw coal sample contained alumina, silica, calcium, and iron oxides amongst. The residual ash post coal combustion presented slightly higher quantities of the oxides signalling comparatively high un-combusted carbon content against the ammonia-cofiring combustion tests. Furthermore, the results showed decreasing weight for most of the oxides with increasing temperature. The most significant decrease is highlighted by silica which decreased from 56.7476 wt% at 800 °C to 45.9423 wt% at 1200 °C. Reduction in weight% of these oxides at higher combustion temperatures imply consumption or transformation which indicate efficient combustion process26. Although the XRF analysis did not account for any carbonaceous compounds, Kohse-Höinghaus26 revealed that the decrease in weight percentages of oxides can correlate with a reduction in the amount of unburned carbon due to more carbon in the fuel being converted to CO2 or CO.

Figures 3 and 4 shows the morphology of the raw coal sample, pure coal combustion and ammonia co-firing with coal combustion residuals collected at various temperatures (800–1200) °C studied using the SEM-EDX method. As seen from the figures, the general prominent spherical shape is not exhibited in most of the samples which can be attributed to the relatively higher amount of the unburnt carbon particularly for the pure coal combustion tests27. Furthermore, it is postulated that subsequent cooling rates primarily controlled the shape of residual ash particles. Generally, the analysis revealed that most of the ashes consisted of solid irregular white particles, hollow spheres (cenospheres), and irregular black porous un-combusted carbon as visualized from the micrographs and the residue ashes28. The irregular shapes and rough surfaces of the ash particles indicated incomplete combustion. These observations provided crucial insights into the influence of ammonia cofiring on the morphology of ash particles and emphasize its role in shaping the combustion behaviour. The observed reduction in unburned carbon content with increasing ammonia co-firing rates aligns with previous studies on co-firing technologies28.

Even though the SEM analysis was sufficient to justify qualitatively un-combusted carbon in the ashes at each corresponding combustion temperature, the carbon element was quantified using energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) analysis. This analysis was employed to determine it separately as it was of particular interest and the recorded spectra is presented in Table 3. From the elemental compositional analysis, greater amount of carbon could be observed in the pure coal combustion residual at 22.58 wt% compared to the ammonia co-firing with coal combustion residual at 20.06 wt% at a combustion temperature of 800 °C. The residual ash particles are represented by slightly changed, semi-coked, or coked particles and Blissett and Rowson28 alleges that the coked and semi-coked particles are produced from incomplete combustion, while the slightly changed particles are those that are exposed to heat and transformed through partial melting of the various organic components. Thus, what is noticeable from the SEM micrographs is that the larger fraction is more irregular in shape than the fine fraction, which is predominantly unburnt carbon. The difference in the surfaces of the residual ashes obtained in these experiments indicates the influence of temperature since other conditions, i.e., air flow and ammonia flow rate, were kept constant. Ultimately, coal combustion from (800–1200 °C) in the ammonia co-firing with coal experiment caused more rigorous coal burning, which resulted in pores in the collected ashes.

Most South African bituminous coals are classified as coal with higher carbon content29. The higher the carbon content, the lower the oxygen content. Comparing the pure coal and ammonia co-firing with coal combustion experiments, it can be observed that during the co-firing experiment, the oxygen content decreases significantly with increasing temperature. This comparison can be further be extended to the carbon content. For ammonia co-firing with coal experiments, carbon content decreases from 20.06 wt% (800 °C) to 18.31 wt% (1200 °C). A similar trend for pure coal firing experiment can also be observed; however, at 800 and 1200 °C, the carbon content is slightly higher. Relatively lower oxygen content in the co-firing experiment indicates that more oxygen was consumed. This is excepted as some might have reacted with ammonia during combustion, producing nitrogen gas and water vapor (Eq. 2). Notably, as the combustion temperature increased, there was a decrease in unburnt carbon in the ash sample. The raw coal sample depicts sharp peaks overlapping between (20–30) o 2θ, which has been quoted to indicate amorphous carbon and some crystalline carbon termed graphite32,33. Quartz seems to be a major mineral phase in the raw coal sample with sharp peaks around 21o, 27o, and 37o 2θ and kaolinite with peaks at (12o, 25o, and 36o 2θ) and other minor phases such as calcite and dolomite. Most literature studies claim these as the most prominent phases in coal34. As such, from this work, an analysis of the coal sample, as observed in the XRD spectrum revealed that graphite was also present in the sample, which can be attributed to the greater carbon content, as also seen from the EDX results. This was anticipated, given that it is in its raw form. In addition, a relatively large quantity of graphite was confirmed to be present through the visualization of coal’s dark colour. Generally, the presence of graphite is indicated by a sharp peak in the region around 25o 2θ, corresponding to the crystalline phase27,35. According to Chancey et al.36 graphite is classified as crystalline owing to spherical morphology indicative of unburnt carbon. Moreso, the peaks confirm the presence of a crystalline material as broad peaks represent an amorphous material and sharp peaks crystalline materials37,38.

The peaks depicted in Fig. 5 for the pure coal combustion residual ashes at different temperatures show a gradual inverse relationship with temperature. This relationship agrees with much of the literature studies, which revealed rapid carbon oxidation at higher temperatures39. Generally, at higher combustion temperatures, there is a greater degree of coal oxidation, leading to low un-combusted carbon. In contrast, at low combustion temperatures, more carbon and coke are formed; hence, less carbon is burnt39. This phenomenon is evident at 1100 °C and 1200 °C combustion temperatures, where the peak intensity decreases significantly, exhibiting that most carbon is oxidized at these high temperatures. Comparatively with peaks depicted in ammonia co-firing with coal residual ashes at various temperatures, not many variations in the peaks could be observed on the combustion temperature effect on the unburnt carbon. Liu et al.40 alleged that co-firing ammonia with coal generally reduces the amount of unburned carbon compared to pure coal combustion because ammonia gas increases the rate at which carbon burns by supplying more oxygen and nitrogen radicals, which react with carbon. This hypothesis was proved at the industrial trial conducted at Mizushima Power Station when 0.6–0.8% of ammonia was added to a 156 MW coal-fired furnace. Remarkably, Tamura et al.17 presumed a similar conclusion with ammonia regarding its flame temperature, which enhances combustion performance. Ali et al.36 further reported that any shift or reduction in peak position in the lattice or mineral phase can be identified while the sample is heated. Consequently, in this work, changes in diffraction patterns corresponding to phase presence and phase boundary are significant for a given temperature due to ammonia’s inherent heat content compared to sole coal firing. Raising the temperature from 800 °C to 1200 °C resulted in peak disappearance at about 20o 2θ for the ammonia co-firing experiment at 1100 °C. This indicates that the residual ashes obtained from the ammonia co-firing with coal experiment contained less unburnt carbon at the same combustion temperature.

The Loss on Ignition results illustrated in Fig. 7 were conducted to determine the weight loss due to un-combustible carbon in the residue ashes for both pure coal and ammonia co-firing with coal combustion experiments collected at various combustion temperatures (800–1200 °C). Even though this method tends to underestimate the amount of unburnt carbon in the residual ash due to other reactions apart from the oxidation of organic carbon resulting in weight loss, it was assumed that unburned carbon is the main component that volatilized during heating. It was deduced that the residual ashes obtained from the pure coal firing combustion experiment exhibited the greatest weight loss, indicative of the high un-combusted carbon content after combustion comparatively to the ammonia co-firing with coal combustion low weight loss. As previously mentioned, the pure coal combustion experiments were incorporated to mimic the real-world combustion conditions in coal-fired boilers. It can further be deduced that the weight loss of the residual ashes decreased significantly with increasing combustion temperature for the pure coal combustion experiment compared to the ammonia co-firing with coal experiments. The weight loss percentage for residual ashes during pure coal combustion decreased from 16.92 to 7.67 wt% at 800 °C and 1200 °C, respectively. Consequently, a similar trend is observed for the ammonia co-firing with coal combustion experiment, which decreased from 19.20 to 11.47 wt% at 800 °C and 1200 °C, respectively. This indicate that residual ashes during the pure coal combustion contained more un-combusted carbon than during ammonia co-firing with coal combustion. Notably, this observation concurs with the work of Guo et al.41 which claimed an inverse relationship between un-combusted carbon and temperature. Tan et al.7 further argued that co-firing coal with ammonia directly led to CO2 emissions and the UC was smaller than that of pure coal combustion.

Conclusions

The study aimed to investigate the effect of combustion temperature on the unburnt carbon during ammonia co-firing with coal combustion. Pure coal combustion and ammonia co-firing combustion experiments were conducted at various combustion temperatures (800–1200 °C). The results show that replacing coal with ammonia directly contribute to the reduction of unburnt carbon leading to more efficient combustion process. The decrease in residual ash’s chemical constituents, indicative of combustion efficiency (reduction in unburnt carbon), was revealed from chemical compositional analysis, particularly for the ammonia co-firing with coal combustion experiments. Additionally, the macromorphological analysis findings supplemented the assertion by further providing insights into the physical structures of the collected residual ashes for both pure coal and ammonia co-firing with coal combustion. The residual ashes from the pure coal combustion experiments depicted mostly irregular, darker particles (indicative of higher unburnt carbon). Moreover, the EDX analysis showed decreasing unburnt carbon with increasing combustion temperature. Similar trends were observed for the ammonia co-firing with coal combustion experiments. Thus, it can be confirmed that ammonia co-firing with coal can be used to improve the combustion efficiency of thermal power stations by minimizing unburnt carbon in the ashes.
