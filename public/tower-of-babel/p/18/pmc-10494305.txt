Development and delivery of a higher diploma in emergency medicine and critical care for clinical officers in Kenya
Authors: Peter Halestrap, David Aliba, George Otieno, B Jason Brotherton, Hannah W Gitura, Jonathan E Matson, Burton W Lee, Evelyn Mbugua
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe provision of emergency medicine and critical care in a cost-efficient manner has the potential to address many preventable deaths in low- and middle-income countries. Here, utilising Kern's framework for curriculum development, we describe the origins, development and implementation of the Emergency Medicine and Critical Care Clinical Officer training program; Kenya's first training programme for clinical officers in emergency medicine and critical care. Graduates are scattered across the country in diverse settings, ranging from national referral hospitals in the capital, Nairobi, to rural hospitals in northern Kenya. In these locations, they provide clinical care, leadership, and teaching. Similar programmes could be replicated in other locations to help plug the gap in critical care provision in Sub-Saharan Africa.Keywords: Emergency medicine, Critical care, Medical education, Health policy

Introduction

Critical illness has been broadly defined as ‘any immediately life-threatening, reversible condition’ [1]. Such an all-encompassing term naturally crosses all demographic and medical specialities and it should be no surprise that critical illness is responsible for several million deaths each year globally [2]. Specifically, in low and middle-income countries (LMIC), it has been estimated that timely emergency care has the potential to address 45% of deaths and 36% of disabilities [3].

In this context, we started the Emergency Medicine and Critical Care Clinical Officer (ECCCO) training program to help bridge the gap between the high burden of critical illness and the scarcity of trained clinicians [4]. This is in keeping with the current situation of emergency care in Kenya as outlined by a recent paper by Lee et all [5] in which they suggested the need to develop new methods for emergency medicine education that lie beyond the scope of short courses but are less burdensome than the rigorous masters in medicine (MMed) programmes. In this paper we utilise Kern's six steps of curriculum development[6] to highlight the process of development, delivery and evaluation of this programme.

Problem identification and general needs assessment

Broadly speaking, the challenge to enhancing emergency and critical care can be divided into improving infrastructure and training personnel. Kenya is located in East Africa and has a population of approximately 55 million people. Health care is provided by a combination of Public (45%), Private (37%), Faith Based (11%) and Other (8%) providers [7], with approximately 24% of the population covered by the National Health Insurance Fund (NHIF)[8] . Several studies in Kenya have highlighted the chronic lack of infrastructure for emergency medicine and critical care [5,9]. In the whole of Kenya in early 2020 there were estimated to be around 1 functional intensive care unit (ICU) bed for every 350,000 people compared to 1 in 3500 in the United States. [10,11]. The recent COVID-19 pandemic has made the international community acutely aware of the critical care challenges in Sub-Saharan Africa [12,13]

The greater challenge to improving critical care though, lies not in equipment, but in appropriately trained personnel [14]. Kenya has only two doctors for every 10,000 people [15], just 10% of the World Health Organisation recommendation. There are few formally trained critical care physicians in the country and most of these individuals are concentrated in the urban capital, Nairobi. Similarly, there are very few doctors who have completed residency level training in emergency medicine. While Emergency medicine was recognised as a distinct medical speciality in Kenya in 2017, and several institutions are working on a formal curriculum, there are currently no full postgraduate residency level speciality training programmes available. Physicians can undertake an 18-month diploma in primary emergency care at Aga Khan University Hospital in Nairobi [16]. This programme is accredited by the College of Emergency Medicine of South Africa. Since 2021, there is also an 18-month fellowship programme in Paediatric Emergency Medicine and Critical Care based out of the University of Nairobi.

Kenya has sought to tackle national staff shortages and unequal distribution of personnel by utilising non-physician providers known as clinical officers. These clinicians are analogous to advanced practice providers, such as physician assistants or nurse practitioners in the United States and are the frontline providers in many Sub-Saharan African nations [17]. Clinical officers undergo a minimum of three years undergraduate training to achieve a Diploma in Clinical Medicine followed by a compulsory one-year internship. Once they have completed the internship, they are able to work as independent prescribing practitioners. There is approximately one clinical officer for every four thousand people. As such, they provide a large proportion of all healthcare in Kenya. Historically, there have been few higher diploma programmes aimed at the clinical officer cadre and none in emergency medicine or critical care. However, this situation is rapidly changing as their potential to impact clinical care and outcomes is realised.

Needs assessment of targeted learners

In 2013 our own hospital, a 350-bed tertiary teaching and referral hospital based 65Km northwest of Nairobi in Kenya, reflected the national challenge in appropriately trained emergency medicine and critical care clinicians. The Emergency Department was attended by a rotating pool of non-specialised clinical officers during the day while medical officer interns covered the department at night. A consultant physician was on call at all times but was not physically present in the department for the majority of the week. The ICU was similarly managed by rotating interns alongside 24-hour consultant oversight.

Hospital mortality audits [18] helped identify system challenges, in particular with regards to inconsistency of staffing and lack of specific training in managing critically ill patients. Informal interviews with interns further supported the observation that they had little prior training in critical care. For example, none of the interns had previous practical exposure in the use of ventilators nor did they rotate in any ICU during their undergraduate training.

In 2014, to address some of these institutional challenges, we employed fully trained clinical officers (post internship) to provide around the clock coverage in the emergency department and subsequently the intensive care unit. To enhance the expertise of the personnel there was a desire to create an ‘in-house’ continuing medical education programme (CME) and thus improve the quality of patient care.

Goals and objectives

To determine the goals and objectives of the training a development team was formed. This consisted of hospital consultant specialists in Family Medicine, Internal Medicine, Surgery, Orthopaedics, Paediatrics, and Obstetrics and Gynaecology. One of the paediatricians was fellowship trained in emergency medicine and the team was also greatly assisted by a visiting Emergency Medicine physician from the United States (US). One of the internists was fellowship trained in pulmonary critical care medicine and had served as a fellowship program director in the US. The team utilised several modalities as they sought to create a relevant curriculum. These included reviewing international curriculum for emergency care, such as the Royal College of Emergency Medicine in the UK [19], as well as internal audit data to understand local pathology and demographics. The team employed the Delphi technique [20] to enable the collection of expert opinion, recognising this was a new programme and thus some hard data would be difficult to obtain. Numerous informal focus groups discussions were held with stakeholders as well as sequential reviews of various iterations of the curriculum.

This process resulted in the following stated broad goal for the programme:

‘To create clinicians who are able to take care of patients with a wide range of pathologies and differentiate those who require urgent intervention. Graduates will become experts in resuscitation and advanced life support management. They will rapidly form differential diagnoses and safely initiative both investigative and treatment plans for patients under their care.’

It was envisioned by the development team that the graduates of this course would be adequately prepared to carry out the following roles and functions;

Education strategies

The development team felt that a presentation-based curriculum [21] would be the most relevant for our context. For example, teaching around the approach to chest pain in the emergency department rather than teaching individually on myocardial infarction and pulmonary embolism. The investigative and treatment approach for patients was contextualised to the resources available in Kenya, with strong emphasis placed on clinical skills. A spiral curriculum approach was utilised to ensure that key clinical concepts and management were reviewed on numerous occasions throughout the course [22].

To balance the desire for an apprenticeship approach with protected educational time, a structure of one week of formal classroom teaching followed by 3 weeks of clinical placement was adopted. Prior to each week of teaching, the students were given extensive readings, which were then discussed in the educational weeks. The programme utilised online resources and readings enabling the course to be rapidly adapted and updated as evidence-based practice changed. Formal assessments were given at the end of each 4-week block to ensure understanding of the major concepts.

The educational weeks also provided the opportunity to teach foundational skills in an interactive, hands-on approach such as during the point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) and mechanical ventilation modules in addition to utilising the high fidelity simulation laboratory for practicing resuscitation scenarios. [23] The knowledge and skills obtained during each educational week were reinforced during the subsequent clinical weeks, during which time they worked one-to-one with supervising faculty.

The first cohort of 4 students in the ‘inhouse’ course commenced in 2014 working predominately in the emergency department. Their exposure, performance and feedback led to a desire to expand the locations for their work to include the other critical care areas of the hospital. While in many context, emergency medicine and critical care would be separate specialities, in our context we determined there was a large overlap of the skills and expertise required. With both departments requiring individuals who could appropriately identify and manage the stabilisation of critically unwell patients, and who had the requisite procedural skills in these scenarios. Therefore, in 2015 the number of trainees was increased and the curriculum enhanced to increase the amount of teaching and exposure to critical care medicine. This cohort of Clinical Officers rapidly became known as Emergency medicine and Critical Care Clinical Officers (ECCCOs) [24].

A total of 9 trainees were in the 2015 class of the ECCCO programme, a number which enabled students to cover all emergency and critical care areas of the hospital while also ensuring appropriate consultant supervision.

As the teaching faculty reflected and reviewed the results of the ‘in house’ programme, they believed that other hospitals might also benefit from such ECCCO-trained clinicians. They contacted the Kijabe College of Health Sciences and the Clinical Officer Council of Kenya to see if the curriculum could be transformed into a formal national higher diploma and offered to other institutions and individuals across the country. The College and Council carefully reviewed the curriculum during a series of stakeholder meetings and noted areas requiring harmonisation to conform to other similar national higher diplomas. They also highlighted a few areas which had hitherto been neglected, in particular research, evidence-based medicine, and health systems management. The curriculum was reviewed, and the programme was extended to 18-months to enable the coverage of these additional areas. In 2017, the programme was granted formal approval as a National Higher Diploma and the pioneer class was admitted to the course. The level of Higher National Diploma was consistent with other post-graduate programmes in Kenya and reflected an additional skill set beyond the Diploma that most clinical officers had gained at an undergraduate level.

Not surprisingly, given the limited critical care expertise in Kenya, many of the early graduates found themselves taking on teaching and leadership roles, either formal or informal immediately after graduating. While there had been a component in the curriculum on teaching and education from the beginning, this was enhanced, with students expected to participate in peer-to-peer teaching right from the start [25]. One of the goals of the programme was to enable graduates to be the key owners and stakeholders of the programme. The ECCCO graduates are heavily involved with teaching, both in the classroom and at the bedside. They are also involved in much of the programme coordination and development.

The ECCCO programme has also been replicated by a former graduate at another institution of higher learning, the Kenya Medical Training College (KMTC) [26]with its first intake commencing in 2019. The students at this programme spend much of their time rotating at the National Kenyatta University Teaching, Referral, and Research Hospital located in Nairobi. This is the highest level referral hospital within the Kenya health care system.

Utilizing the same curriculum design process and teaching model used in the ECCCO programme, a higher diploma in Paediatric Emergency Medicine and Critical Care Clinical Officer programme (PECCO) was created. Following extensive consultations and revisions, this was formally granted national approval in 2020 with the first graduates completing in 2022.

The finances for the programme, in terms of resources and trainee stipends, were initially generously provided by both the hospital and international donors. Following the formal recognition of the programme as a higher diploma, tuition fees were charged, thus ensuring the long-term financial sustainability of the programme. Participating students are now either self-funded, sponsored by local hospitals, or in a few cases sponsored by international donors.

Since the ECCCO programme was formally approved in 2017, it has had 7 intakes and has graduated 35 clinicians as of 2022. In keeping with the aims of the programme, the graduates are working in multiple environments: in emergency departments as the primary caregiver; in the critical care areas of hospitals as part of multidisciplinary teams; or, as teachers and educators in emergency medicine and critical care.

Currently, the graduates are dispersed in fourteen different facilities throughout Kenya. (See Fig 1). All but two of these sites are either rural hospitals or government facilities that had minimal to no formally trained clinicians in emergency medicine or critical care prior to the ECCCO-trained clinician's arrival. The experiences of some of the graduates have been highlighted in the national press [27] and their impact on clinical care, especially during the COVID 19 pandemic has been noted in the medical literature.[28]

Evaluation and feedback

The evaluation and feedback can be considered at an individual student level and at a programme or curriculum level.

Evaluation of the individual

The programme consists of twelve 4-week-long educational blocks as described above. At the end of each of these blocks, there is an assessment to ensure the students have gathered the requisite skills and knowledge for that component of the curriculum. The result of each of these exams contributes 5% of the students’ final marks, i.e. a total of 60%. The remaining 40% is obtained during a final assessment period comprising of both written and practical examinations. These final assessments are done in conjunction with the leadership at the other similar programme done at KMTC to ensure uniformity and standards across programmes.

The students maintain a logbook throughout the programme to ensure they have been exposed to a sufficient diversity of cases as well as performing enough relevant procedures. Students are also matched with a graduate mentor to facilitate their learning and provide another avenue for individual feedback.

Evaluation of the programme

The development team has utilised several modalities to assess the curriculum and make modifications as required. These have included:

Student feedback

At the end of blocks as well as at the end of the programme, the students can provide anonymous written feedback to the programme leadership. This has helped improve some teaching formats, for example the location and timing of airway management training in the programme.

Graduate feedback

There have been several surveys of the graduates of the programme, looking at additional skills or knowledge that could have enhanced their training. For example, all graduates have been surveyed recently about whether their exposure to paediatric intensive care was sufficient for their current practice environment.

Site visits

Faculty have visited graduates in their places of work to assess whether they feel they have the requisite skills and knowledge to operate in that environment. In many of these places they have less resources and supervision than in the teaching hospital, and thus it has reaffirmed the importance of focusing on producing graduates who can work relatively autonomously and who rely on clinical acumen rather than investigations or modalities which are unavailable at their site.

Audit

The hospital continues to place a strong emphasis on audit to drive learning and system improvement. Initial data after only one year of this programme, follow-up audit showed a strong positive improvement in clinical care and staff satisfaction in the emergency department. The Emergency Department had a mortality rate of 0.5%, which is a quarter of that published in other tertiary hospitals in Kenya [29]. Similarly, the mortality in the ICU dropped from 39% in 2014 to 30% in 2018 after the introduction of the ECCCO graduates. This data can continue to drive change in the curriculum, for example, as we see the increasing growth of critical care patients undergoing renal replacement therapy at our institution.

Future directions

Locally, the programme in Kijabe hopes to be able to expand the number of students it trains each year by increasing the number of satellite placement options at other hospitals. This is a gradual process as it is dependent on staffing those sites with our own graduates to provide the appropriate level of faculty oversight.

Ongoing CME is a priority for the increasing number of graduates. In 2019 the first Kenya National ECCCO Conference took place. It was organized entirely by graduates and had over 100 attendees. The keynote speaker for the event was the former president of the African Federation of Emergency Medicine. As time goes on, more structured and regular continuing education resources are likely to be needed. An online platform for clinical officer CME already exists; [30] and this platform could be utilized to provide ongoing education for graduates.

Another area of needed development is research. It would be beneficial to gather data to quantify the impact of ECCCO graduates on staff augmentation and patient outcomes, as well as provide feedback on areas of development for the programme. The lack of preprogramme data, and in most cases electronic medical records has made this a challenge and creative solutions will be required to address this issue [14]. We would also like to work with graduates to generate more general research into emergency medicine and critical care in LMICs. For example, recent research describes the global burden of sepsis, responsible for one in five deaths worldwide, with Africa being one of the most severely affected regions [31]. However, there remains a paucity of studies regarding the best screening and treatment strategies for sepsis from LMICs. Therefore, we are working with international collaborators to create mentored research opportunities, develop local data bases, and offer CME courses in biostatistics and research methology.

Conclusion

Internationally, there has been a call for a focus on essential emergency medicine and critical care that is appropriate and universally available for all countries in the world. The challenge many countries face in implementing such a goal is the availability of appropriately qualified clinicians. The ECCCO programme highlighted above has sought to create a team of clinicians well-suited to fit that niche. So far it has graduated 35 clinicians and has been recognized at a national level. It has spawned a second programme at another higher educational institution and inspired the creation of a paediatric equivalent. As it continues to expand locally, it could also act as a model for other countries in the region to quickly expand their own provision of emergency medicine and critical care.

Dissemination of results

The programme was approved by the Clinical Officer Council of Kenya. It has now been replicated at another site in Kenya. The concept of the programme was also presented at the Emergency Medicine Foundation Kenya annual meeting and the African Conference on Emergency Medicine.
