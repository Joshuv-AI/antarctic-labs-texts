Training of trainers on medical education reforms: a prospective multicenter French survey among intensivists
Authors: Bertrand Hermann, Jennifer Truchot, Alain Cariou, Sarah Benghanem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThe recent reform of the second cycle of medical education in France introduced competency-based medical education (CBME) with the incorporation of several pedagogical modalities into undergraduate training and assessment, including objective structured clinical examinations (OSCEs), simulation-based teaching (SBT), and clinical reasoning (CR) instruction throughout the academic year. While these tools are central to preparing competent practitioners, their effective implementation depends on adequately trained educators. We hypothesized that training would be insufficient, notably in the recently introduced pedagogical modalities.MethodsWe conducted a national, cross-sectional survey (June–September 2023) to assess the level and adequacy of training of board-certified physicians practicing in critical care and involved in undergraduate teaching. An anonymous online questionnaire collected demographic data, teaching frequency, training characteristics, perceived adequacy and usefulness of training, preparedness, and barriers. The responses were analyzed descriptively and comparatively.ResultsAmong the 151 eligible respondents (72% male, median age 41 years, 70% working in teaching hospitals, and 44% holding an academic position), lectures remained the most common teaching modality despite CBME implementation. Only 60% of the respondents reported involvement in OSCEs. Teachers’ training rates across pedagogical modalities were low and heterogeneous: 32% for OSCEs, 30% for SBT, 29% for written assessment design, 24% for lectures, and 12% for CR. Training in the SBT was more often practical, longer in duration, and rated as more useful and adequate than training in other modalities. Overall, the respondents reported moderate preparedness and frequent teaching difficulties. Prior training was associated with higher perceived preparedness and fewer difficulties when teaching students. Lack of dedicated time was the most frequently reported barrier to training (69%).ConclusionThis national survey revealed insufficient and heterogeneous training of intensivist educators in all pedagogical modalities of the undergraduate curriculum. Trainers report limited preparedness and frequent challenges. Standardized, accessible, and time-protected faculty development programs are urgently needed to support CBME education.

Introduction

Training future generations of medical practitioners is a major challenge faced by both hospitals and universities. This takes on urgency considering the profound upheavals our healthcare system is experiencing. This is one of the main reasons that led to a comprehensive reform of the medical studies curriculum in France focusing on undergraduates. This reform, called the ‘Reform of the second cycle of medical education’ (R2C), adopted in 2020 and implemented in the academic year 2023, was designed to shift from the traditional knowledge-based learning to a competency-based learning [1] to prepare students for the six core inter-related competencies defined by the Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education: medical knowledge, patient care, interpersonal and communication skills, professionalism, practice-based learning and improvement, and systems-based practice [2]. This reform has introduced various undergraduate teaching and assessment modalities, including objective structured clinical examinations (OSCEs), simulation-based teaching (SBT), clinical reasoning (CR) training sessions, and new standards for structured written assessment items. More specifically, it institutionalized CR teaching through script concordance tests, SBT sessions in dedicated simulation centers (including high-fidelity mannequin-based simulations for complex clinical scenarios, standardized patient encounters involving actors or expert patients to train physician‒patient communication, and low-fidelity simulations for the practice of technical procedures) and OSCEs as a component of the sixth-year final graduation national exam [3]. The latter conducted universities to implement OSCE-based teaching activities with structured feedback to prepare students for this assessment, although OSCEs are primarily effective for assessment purposes [1,4] and may not be the optimal modality to develop workplace specific skills.

There is a general consensus that these new training and assessment methods, striving towards a competency-based medical education (CBME), undoubtedly offer a way to achieve the goal of training more competent practitioners [5–9]. Indeed, they are focused on the students’ ability to demonstrate ‘show how’ and ‘does’ and not only ‘knows’ and ‘knows how’ according to the Millers’s Pyramid [10]. They also allow training and assessment of relational and emotional skills and communication skills beyond the mere medical knowledge usually taught during lecture courses. CBME-aligned pedagogical modalities with SBT, CR, and OSCEs are consistent with contemporary learning theories, such as social constructivism and situated learning. These perspectives emphasize the central role of educators in facilitating guided and bidirectional interactions with learners, as well as the importance of authentic clinical environments in supporting effective learning [11]. However, these modalities are not immune to criticism, and their implementation in practice may be challenging [12–15].

One of the main challenges in their implementation is the competency of the medical teachers, as these new tools must be thoroughly mastered to provide quality training to students [16–18]. As they have not experienced these teaching tools as students themselves, educators require considerable effort to learn these training modalities and become competent teachers. Mastery of these modalities, that is, the operational competence in facilitating, assessing, and structuring learning environments across teaching modalities, requires initial training of trainers, as well as repeated and regular education, owing to the frequent changes in teaching over time. Indeed, according to the teachers’ own perspective, the top three desirable qualities of an effective teacher are knowledge of the subject, enthusiasm, and communication skills [19]. One can indeed imagine a virtuous ‘teacher-student’ cycle, where a trainer familiar with the teaching modalities of R2C reform provides higher-quality education to the student. Finally, it can also be assumed that a qualified instructor is better equipped to assess the student accurately. However, this could only be achieved provided substantial adaptation and training from all the educators involved in undergraduate teaching within a relatively short timeframe. The large-scale implementation of CBME-aligned teaching and assessment modalities by the reform provided a unique opportunity to assess teachers’ level of training and perceived preparedness. We hypothesized that the training of trainers would be insufficient and heterogeneous in the context of such major educational changes in students’ curriculum.

The objective of this study was to assess the training of teachers according to the different undergraduate teaching modalities in the advent of the R2C implementation. The secondary objectives were to describe the factors associated with training perceived as adequate or inadequate, and to outline potential avenues for improving educator’s training.

Methods

Study design

We conducted an epidemiological survey targeting both academic and non-academic board-certified licensed physicians practicing in critical care (hereafter referred as intensivists) involved in the supervision of undergraduate medical students in France. Intensivists were chosen for several reasons. First, intensive care medicine is a cross-disciplinary specialty for managing acute and complex conditions requiring rapid clinical decision-making. This environment exposes students to a wide range of critical clinical scenarios and therefore provides rich opportunities for the development of clinical reasoning and competency-based learning [20–22]. Second, intensivists are frequently involved in undergraduate teaching through both bedside supervision during clinical rotations and formal academic activities. Finally, the ICU commonly integrates multiple pedagogical modalities emphasized in the recent French reforms, including SBT, structured CR sessions, and participation in competency-based assessment activities [23].

The survey consisted of a voluntary, anonymous, self-administered online questionnaire distributed via email to a list of 497 physicians practicing in intensive care medicine (ICM) compiled through the personal networks of the investigators. Additionally, the survey was distributed through the mailing list of the Intensive Care Medicine College of Teachers and made available on the website of the French Intensive Care Society (FICS). No incentives were offered, and the investigators had no supervisory authority over the respondents. The survey was open for approximately 10 weeks, with a single reminder, immediately before the start of the academic year during which the R2C reform was nationally implemented, to capture teachers’ level of training and perceived preparedness on the eve of this transition. Residents were excluded from participation because they do not hold curricular responsibility for teaching medical students in France.

Ethics

According to French regulations, as the study was a survey of healthcare professionals and did not involve patients, biological samples, nor identifiable personal data, approval from an institutional review board was not required. The participants were informed about the purpose of the research and the anonymous nature of the data collection. Participation was voluntary and completion of the questionnaire was considered as informed consent. All data were collected and managed in accordance with the prevailing legislation, that is, the Declaration of Helsinki and General Data Protection Regulation (European Union Regulation 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council of 27 April 2016 on the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and on the free movement of such data, and repealing Directive 95/46/EC).

Data collection

Demographic data collected from respondents included age, gender, type of institution (teaching hospital, general hospital, and private institution), hospital and department, affiliated university faculty, years of experience in intensive care (excluding internship), current position (university-affiliated: assistant lecturer/associate professor, hospital-based lecturer, senior consultant or non-university-affiliated: clinical associate, clinical hospital practitioner, hospital practitioner) and specialty registered with the French medical council (as many physicians practicing in critical care are dual trained in France).

The survey targeted various teaching modalities, including formal lectures and tutorials (LT), design of written assessment questions following new standards of the R2C (called ‘R2C docimology’) comprising knowledge about how to conceive pertinent ‘Multiple-choice question’, ‘Single-choice question’, ‘Short-answer question’, ‘Pointing area’, ‘Key future points’, …), OSCEs, SBT, and CR. Statistical analyses

For the primary objective, we conducted a descriptive analysis of the overall respondent population and subgroups who had received training for these different modalities. We also compared responses across the different pedagogical modalities. Furthermore, we compared the results of this survey between physicians with a university position and those without (importantly, all respondents worked daily in hospital departments hosting undergraduate student internships) and between respondents who reported having received training in at least one of the pedagogical modalities and those who had never received training.

Qualitative data are presented as frequencies and percentages, while quantitative data are presented as median and interquartile range. Categorical data were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate, and qualitative variables were compared using the Mann‒Whitney Wilcoxon test or Kruskal‒Wallis test. Post-hoc analyses were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the false discovery rate method. A significance level of 5% was considered. Statistical analyses were conducted using R software version 3.6.3 (2020-02-29) (R Core Team, 2020).

Description of respondent population

Response rate

Between June 29, 2023, and September 4, 2023, following the distribution of the questionnaire to a list of 497 intensivists, 170 responses were received, for a response rate of 34.2%. Nineteen respondents were excluded: 3 because they were residents in training and 16 because they were intensivists working in departments not accommodating hospital students daily. A total of 151 responses from senior physicians responsible for teaching undergraduate hospital students were further analyzed.

Respondent demographics

Thirty-three French faculties of medicine (out of the 39) were represented (Supplementary Figure 1). The respondents were predominantly male (72%), with a median age of 41 [35–50] years, and a median of 11 [5–20] years of experience in ICM, including 46% senior consultants, 24% professors, and 20% assistant lecturers/associate professors. 70% percent of the respondents worked in teaching hospitals (Table 1).

Training rate

Out of the 151 respondents, only 24% were holders of the national certification for OSCEs and 48% for ‘R2C docimology’. Ninety-three (61.5%) reported having received specific training in at least one of the evaluated pedagogical modalities. However, the training rate significantly varied across modalities (χ2 p < 0.001) and was generally low, with 32% trained in OSCEs, 30% in SBT, 29% in ‘R2C docimology’, 24% in LT, and only 12% in CR (Figure 2A). When considering only respondents teaching regularly (at least once per semester), the training rate increases significantly, up to 63% for OSCEs, 68% for SBT, and even 70% for ‘R2C docimology’, but remaining very low for CR (21%) (Fisher's exact tests according to the frequency of training, p = 0.025 for LT and p < 0.001 for R2C, OSCEs, SBT and CR) (Figure 2B).

Description of training programs

We then investigated the characteristics of the training programs reported by the respondents. Among the respondents who reported receiving training, the training mainly occurred before the first teaching session, especially for OSCEs (71%), SBT (72%), and CR (83%), but less so for ‘R2C docimology’ (57%) and even less for LT (22%, Figure 3A).

Training programs for OSCEs and ‘R2C docimology’ were predominantly theoretical (64% and 64%, respectively), often conducted remotely (41% and 40%, respectively), while training for SBT and CR were primarily conducted in-person (79% and 71%), comprising both theoretical and practical components (37% and 42% for SBT, 33% and 38% for CR, Figure 3B).

Training programs for SBT and LT were characterized by durations exceeding one day in 85% and 69%, respectively, whereas training programs for other pedagogical modalities were much shorter, typically lasting for 2 h (Figure 3C).

For the majority of pedagogical modalities, the usefulness of the training programs was rated 4 in median on a scale ranging from 1 (not useful at all) to 5 (extremely useful). Training in SBT was deemed significantly more useful than others (median of 5 [4–5], p < 0.05 adjusted for all pairwise comparisons with SBT) (Figure 4A). Similarly, training in the SBT was perceived as more sufficient than training in other modalities (median of 4 [4–4], p < 0.01 adjusted for all pairwise comparisons with SBT except for the SBT/CR comparison) (Figure 4B).

Perceived level of preparedness and experienced difficulties

Subsequently, we examined respondents' overall perception of their preparedness to be educators. On a scale ranging from 0 (not prepared at all) to 10 (very well prepared), respondents reported a median level of preparedness of 5 [3–6]. They also indicated having experienced difficulties due to a lack of training, with a median of 5 [2–6], on a scale from 0 (never experiencing difficulties) to 10 (constantly experiencing difficulties). There was a significant negative correlation between the reported level of preparedness and the perceived difficulties (Spearman's rho coefficient = −0.33, p < 0.001).

Respondents who received training in at least one pedagogical modality perceived themselves as better prepared than those who never received training (6 [5–7] vs. 2.5 [1–5], p < 0.001) and reported experiencing difficulties less often (3 [2–6] vs. 5 [3–7], p = 0.004) (Figure 5A). Similarly, respondents with a university position reported greater preparedness (5 [4–7] vs. 4 [2–6], p < 0.001) and lower perceived difficulty compared to respondents without a university position (3 [2–5] vs. 5 [3–7], p = 0.004) (Figure 5B).

Knowledge of faculty training and barriers to training

Most respondents answered they did not know if the faculty provided training programs for trainers (40%) or a teaching referential (68%), with higher rates among physicians without faculty position (54% vs. 21%, p < 0.001, 80% vs 52%, p < 0.001, respectively). The primary reason cited as a barrier to trainer training was the lack of dedicated time, reported by 69% of the respondents, followed by a lack of funding for 11%, poor availability of training opportunities for 9%, lack of interest for 7%, and unspecified for 5%. There was no significant difference based on whether respondents received training or not, nor based on faculty position.

A brief qualitative analysis of the answers to the open question on how teachers’ training could be improved revealed four main themes. The first, as previously reported, was the need to reduce time constraints to allow educators adequate time for both training and teaching activities. The second was related to training content, with respondents emphasizing the value of practical workshops and a clearer understanding of the pedagogical objectives expected by universities (e.g., ‘Better understanding of the university’s expectations’, ‘Clearly defined educational objectives’). The third theme concerned the timing of training, with participants suggesting that pedagogical training should begin earlier in their professional trajectory, starting before entering clinical academic positions, and be complemented by continuing education to keep pace with evolving curricula and educational practices. The last theme highlighted the importance of mentorship and peer collaboration, including opportunities for co-teaching, supervision by experienced educators, and joint preparation of teaching activities (e.g., ‘More opportunities to teach alongside an experienced educator’, ‘Theoretical guidance combined with paired teaching’, ‘Review of teaching materials by university faculty members’).

Discussion

In a survey of 151 intensive care medicine trainers, we observed a low uptake of all teaching modalities included in the undergraduate medical curriculum, with substantial variation between modalities, and a reported sense of unpreparedness and difficulties in delivering teaching. The primary barrier identified was the lack of time dedicated to training.

A generally insufficient level of training

The first finding from this survey is the low training rate among hospital trainers (approximately 25%), although the training rate is higher for OSCEs and SBT than for the design of written assessment. This low rate is close to the 35% training rate of teachers in education found in a recent survey conducted at several medical schools in Israel [24,25]. However, this low rate should be tempered by a higher level of training among educators who teach very regularly. Nevertheless, it is not uncommon for training to occur after the initial teaching. Consequently, our survey shows that many students face teachers who are not systematically trained and are therefore not qualified. While this is not unexpected, our study highlights that large-scale educational reforms require careful anticipation of faculty development needs, targeting all physicians involved in undergraduate teaching. Indeed, our survey also pinpoints a significant involvement from non-academic teachers in the training of hospital students. Therefore, training programs should also encompass this category of educators, as workplace-based assessment is a key educational tool [26] and contemporary learning theories emphasize the benefits of situated learning and authenticity in clinical environments [11].

Heterogeneous training leading to challenges for trainers

In addition to the varying levels of training across pedagogical modalities, the characteristics of the training are also heterogeneous among different instructional modalities. Thus, training for SBT appears to be predominantly face-to-face, both theoretical and practical, and often of long duration (more than one day in over 80% of cases). The increased familiarity with SBT might be due to its earlier introduction and its presence in the training curriculum of residents [27,28]. Indeed, in contrast, training for OSCEs or ‘R2C docimology’ is mainly conducted online and for shorter durations. This difference is likely explained by the recent introduction of OSCEs and ‘R2C docimology’ (notably during the COVID pandemic), whereas SBT has been established for a longer time in France in intensive care and emergency departments [29,30]. By contrast, teaching in OSCEs is less formalized, except for the national French certification (UNESS training program, https://portail.uness.fr/), where the examiner pathway had only been validated by 24% of trainers in our cohort. Interestingly, the survey was distributed during the summer of 2023, that is, one year and a half after the reform of the second cycle of medical education implementation, which took place at the beginning of the academic year 2021–2022. It would be interesting to conduct a new assessment of trainers’ training in a few years to have the necessary hindsight from the implementation of this reform. It is likely that such study would show a significant improvement in OSCEs and ‘R2C docimology’ training.

Finally, regardless of the pedagogical modalities considered in isolation, a significant proportion of respondents felt inadequately prepared and expressed frequently feeling challenged when teaching students. Although rarely assessed from the perspective of trainers, both were heavily correlated. Indeed, these feelings, more often observed among non-academic staff, which are often underrepresented in educational research, were significantly associated with the lack of prior training. For clinicians, teaching can indeed be associated with an increase in stress, which underscores the importance of prior training for the well-being of educators, in addition to the pedagogical interest for students [31]. Although student well-being is often rightly emphasized, the physical and mental health of teachers is often overlooked, while they experience a significant level of stress and burnout [32]. The implementation of CBME is a known challenge, potentially increasing the burden on teachers and physicians [1,33]. While it was recently found to be associated with a higher job satisfaction and motivation in physicians, it had little to no impact on stress level and burnout [34]. The lack of educator training when implementing new pedagogical modalities and methods might partly explain this sustained level of stress. Our study calls for a careful anticipation of educational changes by extending faculty development initiatives beyond academic staff to the benefit of both teachers and students alike.

A lack of awareness of pedagogical resources and time dedicated to pedagogy

Several potential barriers to training were identified in this questionnaire: foremost among them was the lack of time dedicated to training. This already recognized issue [35] has become even more pressing since the COVID pandemic, exacerbating the crisis in public hospitals and the lack of time for healthcare workers [36]. It is thus crucial to restore time for healthcare providers, but also to allocate time for teachers. Other mentioned barriers were the lack of access to available tools and resources [24] and insufficient knowledge of the faculty and students’ expectations. While specific faculty training programs seem effective [37,38], we found that the existing frameworks available in France were not sufficiently well known among professionals. Thus, improved communication about them could help promote their wider adoption. Importantly, training programs and funding should be made available even for physicians without academic positions, who seem to suffer more from the lack of training, as evidenced in the survey, with an emphasis on mentorship and collaborative learning between experienced and junior teachers.

Strength & limitations of the study

This study is the first national, prospective, multicentric survey to evaluate the training of trainers working in ICM. We evaluated the training after the implementation of the R2C reform, intended to improve medical education. We obtained a relatively substantial number of responses, and we covered a wide range of pedagogical areas.

However, our study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. The first is the nonresponse rate, which may have resulted in a population that is not fully representative of the demographics of trainers in the ICM. However, response rates slightly above 30% are common for healthcare professional surveys without incentives [39], particularity with relatively short survey period [40,41]. In our study, the survey period was intentionally limited and conducted immediately before the national implementation of the reform to capture teachers’ level of training and perceived preparedness at the outset of major educational change in students’ curriculum. Moreover, the wide national geographic coverage (33 of the 39 French medical faculties represented) supports the representativeness of the participating institutions. Although we included only senior physicians currently practicing in intensive care and actively implicated in undergraduate teaching, we cannot rule out a substantial heterogeneity in both medical (primary specialty for dual-trained physician) even if the registered specialty was not associated with perceived preparedness and difficulties. Moreover, as we focused on ICM physicians, our results may not fully apply to other specialties, notably regarding SBT as discussed earlier. Furthermore, although we attempted to describe the characteristics of the training attended (duration, type, timing, etc.), these questions likely do not cover the heterogeneity of the training offered, which varies greatly depending on the type of teaching and faculty. Although we found a significant association with prior training and the perceived level of preparedness and difficulties, our study cannot establish that training indeed leads to better teaching. The absence of an in-depth qualitative component in our study may limit our understanding of the educators’ experiences, including their perceived (un)preparedness difficulties encountered in teaching, and their need to develop as more competent and confident teachers. Finally, our study assessed training only from the teachers’ and not the students’ perspective, nor did it evaluate how the implementation of CBME may influence reciprocal learning between educators and students. Although a virtuous effect of training of instructor on student education is very likely, future studies using mixed-methods approaches are needed to better evaluate the impact of trainer’ training on student competencies and to inform the financial, material, and human investments required for the large-scale implementation of faculty development program fostering high-quality educational skills [42–45].

Conclusion

This survey highlights the real need to train the trainers for each pedagogical modality and especially the more recent ones introduced by the French reform of medical studies. This underscores the necessity for organization, standardization, and communication around trainer education, allowing for optimized support for future teachers. It is commonly said for a medical student, ‘Practice first, never the first time on a patient’. We strongly believe that the same should apply for trainers: ‘Learn to train first, never the first time in front of a student’.
