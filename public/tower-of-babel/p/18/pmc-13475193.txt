Pre-operative injectable testosterone therapy is associated with increased five-year revision risk after total shoulder arthroplasty: a TriNetX database analysis
Authors: Farhad A. Marzook, Reyhan Meetheen, Malek Itani, Tyler K. Williamson, Michael Osmun, Jake X. Checketts
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTestosterone replacement therapy (TRT) has become increasingly prevalent among men and influences bone metabolism, muscle strength, inflammatory signaling, and pain perception. These factors may affect implant durability and long-term outcomes after primary total shoulder arthroplasty (TSA). While perioperative risks of hormonal therapies have been evaluated in other orthopedic populations, the relationship between pre-operative TRT exposure and long-term TSA outcomes remains poorly understood. The purpose of this study was to evaluate the association between pre-operative injectable TRT use and long-term post-operative outcomes following primary TSA.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted using the TriNetX US Collaborative Network to identify male patients aged ≥18 years who underwent primary TSA between 2005 and 2020, identified by Current Procedural Terminology code 23472. Patients with documented injectable TRT use within one year before surgery were compared with nonusers, and patients with a history of prostate or male breast cancer were excluded. Current Procedural Terminology 23472 captures primary TSA and includes both anatomic and reverse procedures; revision shoulder arthroplasties were not included. One-to-one propensity score matching was performed based on demographics and comorbidities. Five-year post-operative outcomes were assessed using risk ratios (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The Benjamini-Hochberg procedure was applied to control the false discovery rate across outcomes, with significance set at an adjusted P < .05.ResultsBefore matching, 36,084 patients met the inclusion criteria; 903 were assigned to the pre-operative injectable TRT cohort and 35,181 to the no-TRT cohort. After 1:1 propensity score matching, 901 patients remained in each cohort. At 5 years, after correction for multiple comparisons, TRT use was associated with higher rates of revision surgery (3.46% vs. 1.23%; RR: 2.82; 95% CI: 1.43–5.57; adjusted P = .006), mechanical complications (8.01% vs. 4.83%; RR: 1.66; 95% CI: 1.14–2.42; adjusted P = .018), and shoulder pain (67.4% vs. 60.0%; RR: 1.12; 95% CI: 1.05–1.20; adjusted P = .006). Opioid use disorder was more frequent among TRT users (2.99% vs. 1.50%; RR: 1.99; 95% CI: 1.02–3.86) but did not remain significant after correction (adjusted P = .067).ConclusionPre-operative injectable TRT use in men undergoing primary TSA was associated with higher 5-year risks of revision surgery, mechanical complications, and shoulder pain. These associations do not establish causation and should be regarded as hypothesis-generating; they support the need for long-term outcome surveillance and perioperative counseling for TSA patients receiving TRT.

Total shoulder arthroplasty (TSA) is an established treatment for degenerative glenohumeral conditions and has been shown to provide reliable pain relief and functional improvement.9 Over the past 2 decades, the prevalence of TSA in the United States has increased substantially, with more than 800,000 individuals currently living with a shoulder prosthesis.13 This growing surgical volume underscores the importance of identifying patient-level factors that may influence post-operative complications and long-term outcomes.

Surgical technique, implant design, perioperative physiologic factors, and patient characteristics have all been shown to influence outcomes following TSA.1 This underscores the importance of understanding how systemic conditions and adjunctive therapies may affect post-operative risk. One such therapy is testosterone replacement therapy (TRT), which has become increasingly prevalent in recent years for the management of symptomatic hypogonadism and as part of gender-affirming care.23,24,36 Testosterone use among US men increased approximately fourfold between 2003 and 2013.23 National prescribing data show a further 27% increase between 2018 and 2022.26 As TRT use becomes more common among men, a growing number of TSA candidates may have a history of pre-operative testosterone exposure, yet its influence on surgical outcomes remains poorly characterized.

Testosterone has been shown to influence musculoskeletal biology and immune regulation.2,10 Prior investigations have demonstrated an association between serum testosterone levels and Cutibacterium acnes skin colonization, a pathogen implicated in periprosthetic joint infection (PJI) following TSA.25 Additionally, abnormal serum testosterone levels have been associated with an increased risk of PJI after TSA, suggesting a potential influence of the perioperative hormonal milieu on post-operative infectious risk.28 A recent study reported that pre-operative TRT use was associated with higher rates of post-operative complications following TSA,34 whereas a separate database study focused on reverse TSA did not demonstrate an association between TRT use and post-operative complications.22 Collectively, the existing literature remains limited and heterogeneous. Our study addresses this gap by isolating injectable TRT formulations, a route not specifically examined in prior shoulder arthroplasty studies.

Given the increasing prevalence of TRT and the conflicting evidence regarding its association with outcomes following shoulder arthroplasty, further clarification is warranted. Accordingly, the purpose of this study was to evaluate the association between pre-operative injectable TRT use and post-operative outcomes in male patients undergoing TSA. We hypothesized that pre-operative injectable TRT exposure would be associated with higher rates of adverse 5-year outcomes, including revision surgery and mechanical complications, in male patients undergoing TSA.

Methods

Study design and data source

This retrospective cohort study was conducted using the TriNetX US Collaborative Network and was reported in accordance with the REporting of studies Conducted using Observational Routinely-collected health Data statement.5 This federated research platform provides access to de-identified electronic medical records from more than 160 million patients across multiple healthcare organizations. TriNetX enables real-time aggregation and analysis of demographic, diagnostic, procedural, and medication data using standardized Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) and International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes. This study used de-identified data from the TriNetX US Collaborative Network and was determined to be exempt from institutional review board review and the requirement for informed consent by the University of Texas Medical Branch IRB under 45 CFR 46.104(d), exemption Category 4 (research involving existing, de-identified data; UTMB IRB #20-0085). All data were compliant with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act.

Cohort selection

The TriNetX US Collaborative Network was queried in 2025 to identify male patients aged ≥18 years who underwent primary TSA between 2005 and 2020, using CPT code 23472. Eligibility was restricted to procedures performed on or before December 31, 2020, ensuring a minimum of five years of potential follow-up for all patients. This code captures both anatomic and reverse TSA procedures, which cannot be distinguished within the dataset; TSA therefore refers to shoulder arthroplasty of either design throughout. Revision procedures were not included. TRT exposure was defined as documentation of injectable testosterone therapy within 12 months before the index TSA procedure, identified using RxNorm code 10379 (testosterone) and Healthcare Common Procedure Coding System codes J3145 (testosterone undecanoate), J1071 (testosterone cypionate), and J3121 (testosterone enanthate). Non-injectable testosterone formulations were excluded to isolate the effect of injectable TRT. Injectable formulations are typically administered at regular intervals (weekly to monthly), and prescriptions within this window likely represent ongoing therapy rather than isolated exposure. Patients were categorized into 2 cohorts based on pre-operative TRT exposure: those who received injectable TRT within 1 year before surgery and those without any documented testosterone use during the same period. Patients with a history of malignant neoplasm of the prostate (ICD-10 C61) or male breast cancer (ICD-10 C50.92) were excluded (Fig. 1; Supplemental Table S1). Outcomes of interest

Long-term post-operative outcomes were evaluated at 5 years following primary TSA and included revision surgery, shoulder pain, mechanical complications, opioid use disorder, PJI, dislocation, and periprosthetic fracture. All outcomes were identified through ICD-10-CM or CPT codes (Supplemental Table S1).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed within the TriNetX platform. Comparisons between matched cohorts were assessed using risk ratios (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). To account for multiple comparisons across the 5-year outcomes, the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure was applied to control the false discovery rate. A 2-tailed adjusted P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics

A total of 36,084 male patients aged ≥18 years underwent primary TSA during the study period. Before matching, 903 patients had documented pre-operative injectable TRT, and 35,181 had no TRT exposure. After 1:1 propensity score matching, 901 patients remained in each cohort. Baseline demographic characteristics and comorbidities were well balanced after matching, with all standardized mean differences below 0.1, indicating adequate comparability between cohorts.

The mean age at index surgery was 65.8 ± 8.7 years in the injectable TRT cohort and 65.0 ± 10.1 years in the control cohort. Both cohorts were predominantly White, comprising 90.8% of the injectable TRT cohort and 87.9% of the control cohort after matching. As specified in the inclusion criteria, the cohort comprised male patients only; a sex distribution is therefore not reported. The prevalence of major comorbidities was similar between groups, including hypertension (72.3% vs. 70.4%), diabetes mellitus (28.4% vs. 26.4%), obesity (31.0% vs. 28.5%), chronic kidney disease (15.9% vs. 14.0%), and mood disorders (34.6% vs. 36.3%). Additional comorbidities, including sleep apnea (46.6% vs. 44.4%), ischemic heart disease (30.1% vs. 29.0%), and nicotine dependence (14.3% vs. 14.5%), were also comparable between cohorts following matching (Table I).

Five-year post-operative outcomes

At 5 years post-operatively, after correction for multiple comparisons, pre-operative injectable TRT use was associated with higher rates of several adverse outcomes compared with controls. Revision surgery occurred more frequently among TRT users (3.46% vs. 1.23%, RR: 2.82; 95% CI [1.43–5.57], adjusted P = .006). Mechanical complications were also more common (8.01% vs. 4.83%, RR: 1.66; 95% CI [1.14–2.42], adjusted P = .018), as was shoulder pain (67.4% vs. 60.0%; RR: 1.12; 95% CI [1.05–1.20], adjusted P = .006), although the latter reflected a small effect size. Opioid use disorder was more frequent among TRT users (2.99% vs. 1.50%; RR: 1.99; 95% CI [1.02–3.86]) but did not remain significant after correction (adjusted P = .067). Discussion

This retrospective cohort study evaluated 5-year outcomes following TSA while limiting exposure to injectable TRT to within one year before surgery, allowing assessment of patients with active systemic androgen exposure during the perioperative period. This study suggests that pre-operative injectable TRT is associated with higher 5-year risks of adverse outcomes following primary TSA. A moderately strong association was observed for revision surgery (RR: 2.82), and a moderate association for mechanical complications (RR: 1.66). A weak but statistically significant association of uncertain clinical importance was observed for shoulder pain (RR: 1.12).

Revision surgery was more frequent among TRT users at 5 years following TSA. The 5-year revision rate observed in our control cohort (1.23%) is consistent with revision and implant-survivorship rates reported following primary TSA in US populations. A US Medicare analysis reported 5-year implant survivorship of 96.1% following TSA,30 and a large US health-system analysis of 44,952 primary TSA cases reported a 1.3% revision rate within 2 years.20 The concordance of our control rate with these published figures supports the validity of the database and matched sample, against which the elevated revision rate in the TRT cohort (3.46%) stands out. Prior studies on shoulder arthroplasty have demonstrated associations between TRT exposure and increased post-operative complications, including PJI. White et al reported a higher 5-year risk of PJI following TSA in patients receiving TRT (RR = 1.74), and other cohorts have reported increased infection rates when testosterone was administered within 6 months of surgery.28,34 Testosterone supplementation has also been associated with increased dermal Cutibacterium burden at the time of shoulder surgery, a pathogen frequently implicated in shoulder PJI.25 Although PJI remains a devastating complication requiring aggressive surgical management, infection rates were not significantly different in the present cohort, suggesting infection alone does not explain the increased revision risk and androgen exposure may contribute to an environment favorable for infection and implant failure.21 Experimental evidence also suggests that androgen exposure may impair tendon integrity and collagen homeostasis, potentially compromising the rotator cuff and subscapularis tendons that maintain shoulder stability and implant biomechanics.11,17,19 Compromising these structures may alter glenohumeral force coupling and increase eccentric loading across the implant interface, predisposing to mechanical failure and revision surgery. Notably, the individual complications evaluated that may contribute to revision, including PJI, dislocation, and periprosthetic fracture, were not individually significant, which may indicate that the elevated revision risk is not attributable to a single dominant indication and could instead reflect the aggregate contribution of mechanical and soft tissue mechanisms. The specific driver of revision cannot be definitively identified from coded data.

A moderate association with mechanical complications and a weak association with shoulder pain coding were observed in TRT users. Both are biologically plausible when considering the effects of androgens on tendon biology and shoulder biomechanics, though the weak shoulder pain association is of uncertain clinical importance.27 Pain after shoulder arthroplasty is often multifactorial and may arise from subtle mechanical abnormalities, altered load transfer, or soft tissue pathology involving the rotator cuff.8,14,35 TRT exposure has been associated with increased rates of rotator cuff pathology and tendon complications, which may contribute to these pain-generating pathways.29,31 Waters et al reported that TRT use was associated with higher rates of tendinous tears, including rotator cuff tears, and increased rates of tendon repair surgery.31 Mechanistically, dihydrotestosterone has been shown to alter cultured supraspinatus tenocytes, resulting in changes in cellular phenotype and proliferation that may influence tendon healing and tissue behavior.11 Additionally, a large database study reported increased rates of Achilles and quadriceps tendon injuries among TRT users, suggesting a systemic tendon risk phenotype.3 In TSA, compromised periarticular tendon integrity may alter glenohumeral biomechanics, producing eccentric loading and abnormal stress at the implant interface. Disruption of the rotator cuff-deltoid force couple leads to abnormal joint translation and altered load distribution across the shoulder.6 Similarly, disruption of the rotator cable-crescent complex can impair force transmission and shoulder stability.7 These biomechanical principles offer a plausible pathway by which TRT-associated alterations in tendon biology may contribute to abnormal shoulder mechanics and increased risk of mechanical complications following TSA.

Although the association with opioid use disorder did not remain statistically significant after correction for multiple comparisons, the near-twofold point estimate (RR: 1.99) may warrant further investigation. Testosterone interacts with pathways involved in pain perception, reward processing, and opioid-induced hypogonadism, providing biologically plausible mechanisms through which androgen exposure could influence post-operative opioid-related outcomes.4,12,15,18,32,33 Future studies are needed to determine whether this observation represents a true association or a chance finding.

These findings have several potential clinical implications. TRT use may represent a potential perioperative risk marker in patients undergoing TSA and may assist with pre-operative risk stratification and counseling. Prior joint replacement cohorts have reported higher revision rates among patients receiving intramuscular testosterone than among those receiving topical formulations after total knee arthroplasty.16 Because the present study defined exposure as any injectable TRT within one year before surgery, these findings highlight the importance of future research examining the role of formulation, dosing, and duration of TRT exposure in arthroplasty outcomes. Finer stratification of exposure by timing within this window was limited by sample size in the present cohort and represents an important direction for future research.

From a clinical perspective, identifying TRT use before TSA may allow for individualized perioperative counseling regarding the risks of revision surgery and mechanical complications. Multidisciplinary collaboration among orthopedic surgeons, primary care physicians, and endocrinologists may also be beneficial when evaluating the necessity and timing of androgen therapy in the perioperative setting. Future prospective studies incorporating implant-specific data, imaging findings, and validated patient-reported outcomes are needed to better characterize the relationship between androgen exposure and shoulder arthroplasty outcomes.

This study has limitations inherent to its retrospective design and reliance on the clinical database. Important clinical variables, including implant type, surgical approach, fixation strategy, radiographic findings, and intraoperative complications, were not available within the TriNetX database. CPT code 23472 includes both anatomic and reverse TSA, making reliable stratification by implant type difficult. Additional pre-operative data, including rotator cuff integrity, glenoid morphology, activity level, and functional scores, were unavailable. Outcomes were identified by diagnosis and procedure codes rather than clinical adjudication, and these code-based definitions have not been independently validated against medical records in this dataset; misclassification of exposures or outcomes is therefore possible. Shoulder pain was identified through diagnostic coding rather than validated patient-reported outcome measures, and laterality could not be confirmed; therefore, symptoms may reflect contralateral pathology. Although propensity score matching was performed, residual confounding and misclassification bias remain possible. Mechanical complications could not be attributed to specific etiologies such as instability, component malposition, polyethylene wear, or aseptic loosening. Furthermore, TRT-specific variables, including the indication for therapy, dosage, therapy duration, adherence, and baseline testosterone levels, were unavailable, precluding any assessment of a dose-response relationship. The TriNetX platform could not provide a reliable measure of median or mean follow-up duration for the analytic cohort, so although every patient had at least 5 years of potential follow-up within the network, the precise follow-up distribution could not be characterized.

Conclusion

Pre-operative injectable TRT was associated with higher 5-year rates of revision surgery, mechanical complications, and shoulder pain following primary TSA, with revision surgery showing the most pronounced association. These findings do not establish causation or define the overall risk-benefit balance of TRT in patients undergoing TSA but underscore the complex interplay among hormonal, biomechanical, and patient-specific factors that influence post-operative outcomes. Recognition of TRT use as a potential risk marker may support informed perioperative decision-making, individualized counseling, opioid stewardship, and closer longitudinal follow-up in patients undergoing TSA.
