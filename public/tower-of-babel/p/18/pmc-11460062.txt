Spatiotemporal distribution and ecological factors of brucellosis among children from 2016 to 2020 in Inner Mongolia, China
Authors: Yideng Wang, Huiqiu Zheng, Yanling Wang, Xinyan Wang, Xuemei Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveIn recent years, the increasing incidence of brucellosis in children has become more serious. However, relatively few studies have been conducted to characterize the spatialtemporal distribution of brucellosis in children. This study aimed to analyze the spatiotemporal distribution characteristics and ecological influencing factors of brucellosis incidence among children in Inner Mongolia.MethodsThis study used data on brucellosis incidence in children aged 0–14 years reported in Inner Mongolia from 2016 to 2020. A Bayesian model was used to analyze the spatial and temporal distribution of brucellosis in children from 2016 to 2020 in Inner Mongolia. Geographical weighted regression model was used to analyze the ecological factors related to the incidence of brucellosis in children.ResultBayesian spatiotemporal analysis indicated that the highest brucellosis risk and increased disease incidence were observed in Hinggan, Inner Mongolia, in children aged 0–14 years. Alxa had the lowest risk but the incidence rate increased rapidly. The incidence of childhood brucellosis was positively associated with the number of sheep at the year-end (β: 2.5909 ~ 2.5926, P < 0.01), average temperature (β: 2.8978 ~ 2.9030, P < 0.05), and precipitation level (β: 3.3261 ~ 3.3268, P < 0.01).ConclusionFrom 2016 to 2020, the overall incidence of brucellosis in children in Inner Mongolia showed an upward trend, with cases exhibiting spatial aggregation. We should focus on areas where the incidence of brucellosis in children is rising rapidly. The incidence of childhood brucellosis was associated with the number of sheep at the year-end, average temperature and precipitation level.Implications and contributionThe findings suggest that brucellosis in children is not to be taken lightly. For children should also focus on protection, take corresponding protective measures. While we focus on high-risk areas, we must also monitor areas where the risk of disease is low, but the incidence is rising fast, to prevent outbreaks in low-risk areas from becoming high-risk areas.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-024-09992-9.Keywords: Childhood, Brucellosis, Incidence, Spatiotemporal distribution, Risk factors

Introduction

Brucellosis is a widespread zoonotic disease that is endemic worldwide [1]. Brucella mainly affects livestock and wildlife, and humans are mainly infected by eating contaminated animal products or interacting with infected animals. In recent years, the global incidence of brucellosis has risen annually, with 1.6 to 2.1 million new cases of human brucellosis being reported each year [2]. Brucellosis pose a serious threat to human and animal health and was a widespread problem in some developing countries [3]. In 2014, the reported incidence rate of brucellosis was 4.22/100,000 in China, marking the highest rate ever recorded [4]. In 2016, an outbreak of brucellosis in China led to a rapid increase in incidence. In 2018, 40,328 cases were reported, representing an increase of more than 120 times compared with 2000 [5].

Brucellosis can occur in people of all ages, such as children, young adults, middle age, and the elderly. At present, there are more studies on brucellosis in young, middle-aged and old people in the world, and most of them are occupational population, and relatively few studies on spatiotemporal distribution of brucellosis in children. It was previously believed that children were rarely affected by brucellosis, and relatively few studies on spatiotemporal distribution of brucellosis in children [6]. However, a recent study conducted in India has shown that the incidence of brucellosis in children was usually underestimated, and was higher in endemic areas. In these areas, the incidence of brucellosis in children ranged from 11.0–56.0% [7]. The results of a study conducted in China showed that among children under 14 years of age, the proportion of children with brucellosis was 17.0% [8]. Brucellosis in children lacked specificity, symptoms were predominantly tachyphylactic fever, and complaints of osteoarthropathy were more common in children [9]. A study reported that the incidence of arthralgia was as high as 77.4% in children with brucellosis, and it would still recur after treatment. Arthralgia of brucella was often misdiagnosed and underdiagnosed due to the inability of infants and children to provide detailed complaints, which brought difficulties in clinical diagnosis and treatment, seriously affecting children’s growth and development, and even causing irreversible damage, such as joint and muscle deformity [10]. The potential threat should therefore be taken seriously, and it is particularly important to pay attention to brucellosis in children.

Inner Mongolia, located in the northern border of China, was a historical brucellosis infected area and is currently the area with the most severe brucellosis outbreak in China [4]. Inner Mongolia has 12 cities, about 2,400 km long from east to west, with a maximum span of more than 1,700 km from north to south. The economic development gap between cities in Inner Mongolia is large, the number of hospitals varies widely, and the overall efficiency of medical and health resource allocation is low [11]. Inner Mongolia is the largest grassland pastoral area in China [12], the geography and climate are favorable for the survival of brucellosis. During years of high brucellosis incidence, Inner Mongolia accounted for 40 to 50% of reported brucellosis cases, becoming the top-ranking region for reported cases in China [13]. A previous study showed that cases of brucellosis in children were reported annually in Inner Mongolia from 2013 to 2017. It was revealed that primary and secondary schoolchildren in the region often came into contact with sheep and had a low level of awareness regarding brucellosis. They often become infected with Brucella through activities such as handling abortions, milking, consumption of undercooked meat products and fresh milk, and vaccinating sheep [14, 15]. This paper aims to study the spatiotemporal distribution and ecological factors in Inner Mongolia, to provide scientific basis for the reduction of the occurrence of childhood brucellosis as well as its transmission.

Methods

Study design

Data sources

Data on the incidence of brucellosis among children aged 0–14 years were obtained from the direct reporting system of the infectious disease network of the Center for Disease Control and Prevention in Inner Mongolia. Brucellosis incidence rate = number of cases of brucellosis in a given year/population at the end of the same year × 100,000/100,000 [1].

Population, socio-economic, and meteorological data for cities in Inner Mongolia from 2016 to 2020 were obtained from the Statistical Yearbook of Inner Mongolia. The number of medical and health institutions, number of sheep at the end of the year, average temperature (℃), precipitation level (mm), humidity (%), and number of hours of sunshine were calculated every month, and all data were standardized using the following equation:, “X” is the observed value, “” is the mean, “S” is the standard deviation [16].

Diagnostic criteria for brucellosis

The diagnosis was based on the “Diagnostic Criteria for Brucellosis” issued by the National Health Commission of the People’s Republic of China (WS2697-2019) [17]. Epidemiological history: whether the patient has had close contact with livestock (cattle, sheep) suspected to be infected with brucellosis before the onset of illness, whether he or she has eaten undercooked meat and dairy products from livestock, whether he or she lives in an area with a high incidence of brucellosis, or whether he or she works in a job prone to brucellosis. Clinical features: Whether the patient has symptoms associated with brucellosis, such as lethargy, sweating, muscle pain, and joint pain. Laboratory examination: including laboratory preliminary screening test and laboratory confirmation test.

Definition of variables

Age

Children were classified into the following four age groups: 0 to 2 years old, 3 to 5 years old, 6 to 9 years old, and 10 to 14 years old. This classification was based on a combination of the direct reporting system of the infectious disease network of the Center for Disease Control and Prevention of Inner Mongolia and the “China’s Child Population Situation in 2020: Facts and Figures” published by the National Bureau of Statistics.

Spatial auto-correlation

When an infectious disease breaks out or becomes prevalent, the number of cases will increase abnormally in some areas within a short period of time, which usually occurs within a specific range. The incidence of the disease in these ranges is different from that in other areas, with significant differences. This phenomenon is called “clustering” phenomenon. The global autocorrelation and local autocorrelation are analyzed comprehensively. Global autocorrelation first analyzes whether there is aggregation in the study area, and analyzes whether the aggregation is statistically significant. In general, global autocorrelation alone can not determine the specific location of the aggregation region, but also need to conduct local autocorrelation analysis on this basis, so as to accurately analyze the specific region with aggregation [20]. In this study, the spatial location and attribute values of the incidence of brucellosis in children in Inner Mongolia were analyzed through global autocorrelation, and the spatial autocorrelation coefficient was calculated. The coefficient of spatial autocorrelation, Moran’s I, ranges from − 1 to 1. When the Moran’s I of a region is greater than 0, the incidence of brucellosis in children is clustered in that region, and there is a positive spatial correlation. When the Moran’s I tends to be 1, the distribution is more clustered. When the Moran’s I for a region is less than 0, the incidence of brucellosis in children is discrete, and there is a negative spatial correlation. When Moran’s I tends to be -1, the distribution is more discrete [22].

Results

Temporal trends in the incidence of brucellosis among children in Inner Mongolia

A total of 703 cases of brucellosis were reported in children aged 0–14 years in Inner Mongolia from 2016 to 2020, with an average incidence rate of 4.32/100,000. The incidence rate has shown a rising trend (AAPC = 36.95%, P < 0.05, Fig. S1). From 2016 to 2020, the incidence rate of brucellosis among children aged 0–14 years in Inner Mongolia was 2.69/100,000, 2.18/100,000, 3.61/100,000, 5.40/100,000, and 7.73/100,000, respectively.

Temporal trends in the incidence of childhood brucellosis according to sex

There were 425 cases of brucellosis in boys (60.46%) and 278 cases in girls (30.54%) in Inner Mongolia from 2016 to 2020. In the same year, the incidence rate in boys was higher than that of girls. From 2016 to 2020, the incidence rates for boys were 3.04/100,000, 2.35/100,000, 3.98/100,000, 7.15/100,000, and 8.94/100,000, respectively, and showed a trend of increase (AAPCboys=39.5%, P < 0.05) (Fig. S1). From 2016 to 2020, the incidence rates for girls were 2.32/100,000, 2.01/100,000, 3.21/100,000, 3.54/100,000, and 6.47/100,000, respectively, and showed an increasing trend (AAPCgirls=33.6%, P < 0.05) (Fig. S1). A trend of a more rapid increase in the incidence rate was observed for boys compared to girls (Fig. S1).

Temporal trends in the incidence of childhood brucellosis according to months

The incidence of brucellosis in children in Inner Mongolia was notably seasonal from 2016 to 2020, peaking mainly in May, June, and July, which accounted for 14.22%, 12.09%, and 15.50% of the total number of cases, respectively (Fig. 1).

Spatial distribution of the incidence of brucellosis in children

From 2016 to 2020, all the allied cities of Inner Mongolia except for Wuhai had reported cases of childhood brucellosis, and these cases were mainly concentrated in the Central and Eastern regions of Inner Mongolia. The highest and lowest average incidences of childhood brucellosis in Inner Mongolia were observed in Hinggan (10.05/100,000) and Alxa (1.15/100,000), respectively, from 2016 to 2020 (Table S1).

Spatial autocorrelation of childhood brucellosis

The incidence of brucellosis among children in Inner Mongolia in 2016–2020 showed significant geographic clustering (P < 0.05) (Table 1). Moran’s I values for global spatial auto-correlation were − 0.014, 0.131, 0.422, 0.728, and 0.426 from 2016 to 2020, respectively. Hinggan had “high - high” aggregation for 3 out of 5 years, Tongliao had “high - high” aggregation for 2 out of 5 years, and Ordos had “low - low” aggregation in 2 out of 5 years. Over five years, the types of regional agglomerations increased, and the number of cities with agglomerations also gradually increased (Fig. 2).

Spatiotemporal trends in the incidence of childhood brucellosis

An upward trend was commonly observed as a temporal trend for childhood brucellosis incidence from 2016 to 2020 (Fig. 3b). The highest risk of childhood brucellosis and the most rapidly rising incidence rate was observed in Hinggan. The most rapidly rising incidence rate was observed in Chifeng and Tongliao, although the highest risk of childhood brucellosis was not observed in these regions. The highest risk of childhood brucellosis but a slower rising incidence rate were observed in XilinGol and HulunBuir. The lowest risk of childhood brucellosis was observed in Alxa, but the incidence rate in this region increased more rapidly than in Ordos and all the other cities in the central region (Fig. 3a and c).

Ecological factors associated with the incidence of brucellosis in children

A GWR model was established based on the incidence rates of childhood brucellosis in each city of Inner Mongolia as the dependent variables, and the number of sheep at year-end, number of medical and health institutions, average temperature, precipitation, humidity, and number of hours of sunshine in each city as the independent variables. The results showed that the R2 value of the GWR model was 0.837, and the residuals in most areas were distributed between − 1.5 and 0.5 and 0.5 to 1.5. The fit of this GWR model was good (Fig. S2).

The incidence of childhood brucellosis was positively associated with the number of sheep at year-end (β: 2.5909 ~ 2.5926, P < 0.01) (Fig. 4a), average temperature (β: 2.8978 ~ 2.9030, P < 0.05) (Fig. 4c), and precipitation (β: 3.3261 ~ 3.3268, P < 0.01) (Fig. 4d). There was no correlation between the number of medical and health institutions (β: -1.5231~ -1.5220, P > 0.05) (Fig. 4b), humidity (β: 2.1407 ~ 2.1487, P > 0.05) (Fig. 4e), number of hours of sunshine (β: -1.6358~ -1.6319, P > 0.05) (Fig. 4f), and brucellosis in children.

Discussion

From 2016 to 2020, the incidence of brucellosis in children aged 0 to 14 years in Inner Mongolia showed an overall trend of increase, and the incidence of brucellosis in boys is higher every year than in girls. Boys had a lively and active personality, which led them to interact more with animals and have a higher chance of contracting infections than girls. Children do not always have sufficient knowledge regarding brucellosis and adequate protective measures to be taken while playing with animals, thus increasing the chances of acquiring a brucellosis infection [28].

Our results showed that the highest number of cases of brucellosis in children were mainly reported in May, June, and, July. The increase in the temperature during spring marks the start of the breeding season for cattle and sheep. Farmers and workers start shearing the sheep, and children come into close contact with sheep out of curiosity, increasing the risk of brucellosis infection [29]. Children with low knowledge of brucellosis helped to dispose of lambs and feces at home without wearing protective gear and helped to clean the sheep pen. Most farmers typically graze their livestock more frequently in summer than in winter. Children are often in close proximity to assist in feeding these animals, increasing the risk of brucellosis infection [30].

The results of Bayesian spatio-temporal analysis showed that a higher risk of childhood brucellosis and a rapid increase in the incidence rate were observed in the Hinggan, Tongliao, and Chifeng regions. The results of GWR analysis in this study showed that the incidence of childhood brucellosis was associated with the number of sheep at the year-end, average temperature and precipitation level. The number of sheep at the end of the year was positively correlated with the incidence of childhood brucellosis. The Inner Mongolia Statistical Yearbook showed that the highest number of sheep were observed at the end of the year in Chifeng and Hinggan, with these areas ranking among the top two in Inner Mongolia. These areas also had a higher risk of childhood brucellosis and rapidly increasing incidence rates. Chifeng and Hinggan have a large number of forests and pastures, which serve as important bases for cattle and sheep breeding in Inner Mongolia. In recent years, the price of beef and mutton has risen, the breeding industry has developed rapidly, and the number of livestock has increased. Consequently, the increased frequency of contact between children and livestock has led to an increased risk of brucellosis infection.

The average temperature was positively correlated with childhood brucellosis incidence. The Inner Mongolia Statistical Yearbook showed that the average temperatures in Hinggan, Tongliao, and Chifeng, where the risk of childhood brucellosis was higher, with rapidly increasing incidence rates, had the top three average temperatures in the Eastern region of Inner Mongolia. The warm environment created a good living environment for Brucellosis, which was conducive to the production and spread of bacteria [31]. Children were more likely to play outside when the temperature was relatively warm than in cold weather. Their close contact with lambs, sheep, and dogs, while playing and feeding the animals, increased the risk of infection with Brucellosis. Compared with winter, people were more willing to take children out to eat barbecues in summer and autumn [32].

There was a positive correlation between the precipitation level and the incidence of childhood brucellosis. The Inner Mongolia Statistical Yearbook showed that Hinggan, Tongliao, and Chifeng, where the risk of childhood brucellosis was higher and the incidence rate increased rapidly, had the top three precipitation levels in Inner Mongolia. The environment was humid due to heavy rainfall, which provided a suitable environment for the survival and reproduction of Brucella [33]. Brucella occurred in cattle and sheep feces and proliferated in a humid environment, thus increasing the risk of brucella infection in children [34]. During the rainy season, the birth rate of livestock tends to increase. However, livestock miscarriages are prone to occur during this season, which makes Brucella infection and transmission easier among animals.

Interestingly, the risk of childhood brucellosis in Alxa was the lowest among all cities in Inner Mongolia, but the increase in disease incidence was more rapid than that in Ordos and all the cities in the central region. The Inner Mongolia Statistical Yearbook showed a low year-end sheep population and low average precipitation in Alxa, which might contribute to the low risk of brucellosis in children. In Alxa, where animal husbandry is the main source of income, brucellosis is usually detected using serological techniques. The city had inadequate health facilities and a shortage of health care providers. In recent years, the diagnostic ability of brucellosis in Alxa Central Hospital has been improved, and more children with brucellosis have been detected, resulting in a rapid increase in the incidence of brucellosis in children in this area [35]. This suggests that increased attention to childhood brucellosis is particularly important. In addition to focusing on areas at high risk of childhood brucellosis, priority should be given to areas where the incidence of the disease is growing rapidly in order to prevent the emergence of new high-risk areas.

In the past, people paid more attention to the high-risk areas of brucellosis, thus neglecting the low-risk areas. Livestock farming exists in both high-risk and low-risk areas, and because of the lack of information sources, farmers and herders lack knowledge about brucellosis prevention and control, so they cannot teach children about brucellosis. Children are the group that families, schools and society pay attention to, and they are also the key group that spread brucellosis knowledge [14]. Local governments should make greater efforts to publicize brucellosis prevention and control knowledge, enhance children’s awareness of brucellosis prevention, improve lifestyle and hygiene habits, and enhance farmers’ and herdsmen’s understanding of the disease through children, so as to take proactive protective measures [36]. While focusing on high-risk areas, monitoring of low-risk areas should not be ignored to prevent outbreaks in low-risk areas from becoming high-risk areas.

Our study has several limitations. First, we did not further investigate the relationship between children’s cognitive level, protective measures and the incidence of brucellosis in children. Second, due to the limited data collected, rural children could not be separated from urban children for further research. In the future, we will conduct on-site investigations, collect survey questionnaires, and conduct other influencing factor studies and spatiotemporal analysis on brucellosis in urban children and rural children.

Conclusions

In 2016–2020, the overall incidence of childhood brucellosis in Inner Mongolia is on the rise, with spatial clustering of cases. We should focus on areas where the incidence of childhood brucellosis is rapidly increasing. The incidence of childhood brucellosis is related to the number of sheep at the year-end, average temperature and precipitation level.
