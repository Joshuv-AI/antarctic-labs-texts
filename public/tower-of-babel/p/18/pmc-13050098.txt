A study on egg sex-related associations based on PP-HGNet and morphological parameters
Authors: Yingjie Kuang, Jinrong Jiang, Cheng Tan, Dan Wu, Guiping Liu, Dehao Shi, Haoyuan Liu, Jinfa Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•A large, industry-relevant dataset was constructed using 1008 eggs from two commercial laying hen breeds, Xiaoyou 818 and Datu2.•Egg sex identification was investigated using a dual-validation framework, combining statistical analysis with PP-HGNet–based deep learning on morphological features.•Demonstrated that neither single nor composite morphological parameters (e.g., EI, L) can reliably determine egg sex in commercial laying hens, defining clear limits for industrial application.

Introduction

In the modern layer industry, the large number of male chicks produced during incubation has long posed a significant challenge. Globally, billions of eggs are incubated each year, approximately half of which hatch as male chicks (Krautwald-Junghanns et al., 2018; Giersberg et al., 2021). Because males neither lay eggs nor possess the rapid growth and feed conversion advantages of broiler breeds, their economic value is substantially lower than that of females (Smith et al., 2001; Rutt et al., 2023; De Haas et al., 2021; Jiang et al., 1998). In commercial egg production, billions of day-old male chicks are culled annually, posing ethical concerns and increasing operational costs; industry assessments estimate that culling each male chick costs about $1, highlighting the potential economic benefit of early in-ovo sexing (Stuff et al., 2026; Reithmayer et al., 2019). With rising public awareness and sustainability demands, rapid, accurate, and non-invasive early sex identification of eggs has become a key research focus in poultry farming and breeding (Khamoun et al., 2024; Mizuno et al., 2003; Xu et al., 2025).

To reduce male chick culling, various invasive methods have been developed, mainly involving molecular detection or analysis of embryonic fluids and hormones, which require direct sampling of eggs or embryos. Studies show that combining terahertz time-domain spectroscopy (THz-TDS) with metamaterial sensors can detect multiple sex-related hormonal signals (Zhao et al., 2025). At the molecular level, DNA hybridization and polymerase chain reaction (PCR) techniques allow high-accuracy sex identification by targeting the W chromosome or specific DNA markers and can be applied at early embryonic stages (Minematsu et al., 2004). In addition, analysis of estrogen or testosterone levels in the embryonic allantoic fluid can also differentiate embryo sex, with female embryos typically exhibiting higher estrogen levels during the mid-incubation period (Petitte et al., 1995; Weissmann et al., 2013). Although invasive methods are accurate and technically mature, they require breaking the eggshell or embryo, which can reduce hatchability and egg value. Their reliance on complex equipment and high costs also limits large-scale industrial use (Xiang et al., 2022).

Non-invasive methods are gaining attention because they avoid embryo damage, maintain hatchability, and can be automated (Jia et al., 2023). These methods primarily include spectroscopic analysis, machine vision, image processing, Raman spectroscopy, and integrated analyses based on eggshell morphology and patterns of embryonic development (Xie et al., 2023; Galli et al., 2018; Ahmed et al., 2025).

Non-invasive methods, including Vis–NIR and SW-NIR spectroscopy, machine vision, and Raman spectroscopy, determine embryonic sex from optical or vascular signals in fertilized eggs, with typical accuracies of 78–90% during days 3–7 of incubation (Zhu et al., 2021; Galli et al., 2016). Although non-destructive and suitable for automated, high-throughput screening, their high cost and complex data processing limit their application in large-scale hatcheries (Schreuder et al., 2024). In addition, high accuracy is usually achieved only at relatively late incubation stages (days 4–7 or later), beyond the window in which eggs can be marketed as commercial products (Förster et al., 2022; Burke et al., 1989). Variations in eggshell thickness, color, size, and embryonic spatial position can further affect signal quality and reduce result stability (Alin et al., 2019; Ye et al., 2022). Emerging non-invasive techniques, such as acoustic detection and volatile organic compound (VOC) analysis, show potential but remain sensitive to environmental noise, feed composition, and breed differences, and their reliability requires further validation (Corion et al., 2024, Corion et al., 2024b; Jia et al., 2023b).

Among various non-invasive methods, egg shape index (EI) and its related morphological traits have attracted attention due to their simplicity, non-destructiveness, and low cost (Kayadan et al., 2023). EI can be obtained through simple measurements without the need for expensive equipment, causing no harm to the embryo and not affecting hatchability. This method is fast, economical, easily standardized, suitable for early incubation stages, and has high potential for large-scale application. Existing studies are mostly based on non-commercial populations or experiments with limited sample sizes. Research on Lhasa White chickens shows that egg weight and EI affect hatchability, fertilization rate, and chick birth weight (Feng et al., 2018; Feng et al., 2017, Feng et al., 2018b), but Lhasa White chickens are not commercial egg-laying breeds. They are mainly used for local farming or as ornamental chickens, with limited economic value compared to mainstream commercial egg-laying breeds, so their practical application in commercial egg production is relatively low. Research on Three-Flower Geese found that egg weight and EI within certain ranges can achieve higher fertilization rates and hatchability (Liu et al., 2021), but the breed used in this study is not a commercial egg-laying breed, and thus the findings are not directly applicable to sex identification in chicken egg production. Studies on Beijing ducks and Qiandongnan Xiaoxiang chickens have shown a significant correlation between EI and sex (Zhang et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2011), but these breeds are also not commercial egg-laying breeds, and their sample sizes are small, limiting the broader applicability and commercial value of the findings. Research using smartphone imaging and machine learning has achieved an 88.9% accuracy in pre-hatch sex identification in egg-laying chickens (Indarsih et al., 2021), and deep learning methods can process more phenotypic features (Narushin et al., 2025; Wilson et al., 2017). A study on Super Nick White Layer eggs further supports that EI and other morphological traits may influence chick sex in a commercial breed, but the sample size was relatively small (Yılmaz-Dıkmen et al., 2013).

Although previous studies suggest a potential link between egg shape index and embryonic sex, they often suffer from limitations such as small sample sizes, non-commercial populations, and insufficient validation. While some research has proposed a relationship between egg morphology and embryonic development, the specific correlation between EI and embryonic sex in commercial egg-laying chickens remains unverified in a systematic manner (Bergoug et al., 2015). Moreover, from a biological standpoint, the theoretical basis connecting external egg morphology to embryonic sex is inherently weak, as earlier studies predominantly rely on speculative hypotheses, such as variations in maternal physiological status, hormonal environments during egg formation, or subtle differences in egg formation dynamics, which lack clear support from developmental biology. Nevertheless, we hypothesize that, in commercial egg-laying chicken populations, the egg shape index and related phenotypic characteristics may exhibit sex-associated patterns with embryonic sex. Therefore, this study focuses on two commercial egg-laying chicken breeds, Datu2 and Xiaoyou 818, combining large-sample statistical analysis with deep learning models to systematically assess the reliability and practical application potential of egg shape index and related phenotypic features for early embryonic sex identification. This study aims to further validate the relationship between egg shape index and phenotypic traits with embryonic sex in commercial egg-laying chickens, with the goal of providing an efficient, low-cost, and non-invasive method for sex identification in large-scale hatching systems.

To clarify the objectives of this study, the present work focuses on three main aspects:(1)It examines whether egg morphological parameters, including the egg shape index (EI) and related derived features, exhibit statistically significant differences between male and female embryos in commercial layer breeds.(2)It evaluates the ability of deep learning–based models to identify subtle sex-related patterns within these morphological parameters beyond those detectable by conventional statistical analyses.(3)It assesses the practical feasibility of applying these findings to morphology-based approaches for early sex identification in industrial incubation systems.

Materials and methods

In this study, eggs were first manually measured using a vernier caliper, and the resulting data were subsequently analyzed through statistical methods and deep learning approaches to investigate the relationship between the egg shape index, derived morphological parameters, and sex. This section describes the collection and measurement of the dataset, followed by a detailed explanation of the data analysis methods employed.

In this study, for clarity and convenience, the following abbreviations were used for egg morphological parameters: L for egg length (in millimeters), W for egg width (in millimeters), C for curvature (in pixel units), and EI for the egg shape index, calculated as the ratio of egg length to egg width (EI = L/W, dimensionless, Feng et al. 2018). The composite parameters L+EI, W+EI, and C+EI are defined as simple sums of each individual parameter with the EI.

Data collection and measurement

In this study, a total of 1008 eggs were collected from two commercial layer breeds at the Yong’an and Langen breeding farms, including 504 eggs from 24-week-old Xiaoyou 818 hens at 2 days of age and 504 eggs from 7-week-old Datu2 hens at 2 days of age. Among these, 494 were male embryos, 472 were female embryos, and 42 were unfertilized. The eggs were collected and incubated between October 9 and October 31, 2024. Before measurement, the eggs were stored under controlled conditions at 20 °C and 70% relative humidity. The eggs were then numbered and measured. Photographs of the eggs were taken using a smartphone for subsequent extraction and validation of morphological parameters; in this study, only the 504 Datu2 eggs were photographed. Image acquisition was performed using a Xiaomi 14 smartphone under indoor lighting conditions in the egg storage room. Eggs were placed on a uniform background within designated rectangular areas on the table, and the camera was positioned within a designated rectangular area on the floor to maintain relative positioning as consistently as possible. Images were captured at an approximate distance of 30 cm from the egg surface. All images were recorded at 4 K resolution to ensure sufficient detail for subsequent morphological analysis. All 1008 eggs were manually measured to obtain detailed morphological data. For each egg, L and W parameters measurements were independently performed by three operators, and the average values were used in subsequent analyses. As shown in Fig. 1. Each sample was assigned a unique id from 1 to 1008 prior to incubation to ensure precise correspondence between morphological features and sex after hatching. Prior to incubation, morphological parameters for each egg were obtained through manual measurement to ensure accuracy and reproducibility. L and W parameters were measured using a vernier caliper with a precision of 0.01 mm, following standardized procedures. Each egg was placed with its longitudinal axis horizontal, and L parameter was measured between the two farthest points along this axis, while W parameter was measured at the widest point along the transverse axis. Based on the measured L and W parameters, the EI parameter and combined parameters such as L + EI and W + EI parameters were calculated to describe the overall egg morphology.

The C parameter at the egg ends was determined using OpenCV. First, each egg image was converted to grayscale, and the initial binarization threshold was automatically determined using Otsu’s method. To optimize contour segmentation, the threshold was adjusted by ±30. The Canny edge detection method was then applied to extract the contour and obtain clear edge information. The distances between all possible point pairs on the contour were calculated, and the two points with the maximum Euclidean distance on the contour were selected as A and B. Then, 50 points were chosen on each side of A and B along the contour segment for curvature analysis. As shown in the red and blue regions in Fig. 2. To reduce noise from discrete contour points, cubic spline interpolation was applied for smoothing. The average curvature of the 50 points in each endpoint region was calculated to identify the egg tip, and the maximum curvature from the side with the larger average was taken as the egg’s C parameter. To comprehensively describe the morphology of eggs, the C parameter was combined with the EI parameter to obtain a weighted composite curvature parameter (C + EI), which was later used as a complex morphological feature to evaluate the sex of the eggs. Data analysis methods

Statistical analysis methods

During the data analysis phase, traditional statistical methods were employed to examine the relationship between morphological parameters and sex. Descriptive statistical analyses were first conducted to provide an overview of the overall distribution characteristics of the samples. For eggs classified as male or female, key morphological parameters, including L, W, EI, C and combined parameters measures were summarized by calculating the mean, standard deviation, range, and percentile values (25%, 50%, and 75%) to reflect central tendency and dispersion, providing a basis for subsequent comparative analyses. Independent sample t-tests and analysis of variance were then used to assess the significance of differences in morphological parameters between sexes. Additionally, multivariate logistic regression analysis was conducted to investigate the associations between each parameter and sex, with the significance level set uniformly at P < 0.05.

Deep learning methods

To further explore potential nonlinear relationships between egg morphological features and sex, we employed the lightweight deep learning network PP-HGNet for sex classification. PP-HGNet, proposed by Baidu’s PaddlePaddle team, is a hierarchical convolutional architecture that balances representational capacity and computational efficiency. This makes it particularly suitable for analyzing high-dimensional morphological data, capturing subtle interactions among multiple egg parameters without excessive model complexity.

Compared with conventional machine learning methods and simpler neural network architectures, PP-HGNet provides stronger feature extraction and multi-scale information integration, allowing it to effectively handle both structured numerical features and image-based inputs. In this study, two input modes were used: (1) seven manually measured morphological features, including L, W, EI, C, L+EI, W+EI, and C+EI; (2) egg appearance images, enabling the network to automatically extract spatial, textural, and curvature cues. PP-HGNet employs a lightweight Stem module as the front-end feature mapping unit for normalization and preliminary feature extraction. Multiple stages of HG-Blocks and lightweight transition layers are stacked to progressively extract and fuse multi-scale information from low-level geometric variations to high-level feature relationships. Each HG-Block integrates a hybrid multi-scale convolution structure and an enhanced channel attention mechanism to model nonlinear coupling among morphological features across different receptive fields, while residual connections ensure stable gradient propagation and effective feature reuse. Finally, global average pooling and feature fusion layers produce the sex identification output. This design allows PP-HGNet to learn the latent mapping between morphological features and sex labels, providing a basis for evaluating the discriminative potential of these features. To properly evaluate the model's predictive performance and ensure reproducibility, all experiments in this study were conducted under consistent and well-defined settings. The dataset was divided into training and test sets, with 8 parts used for training and 2 parts used for testing. Within the training set, the numbers of male and female samples were balanced to avoid class bias, while the test set retained the original distribution. Although the overall dataset exhibited a slight class imbalance, the imbalance was minimal, and no additional resampling or class reweighting strategies were applied, in order to maintain data representativeness during performance evaluation. The model was trained for 500 epochs with an early stopping strategy, using a batch size of 8. The Adam optimizer was employed with an initial learning rate of 0.001, and a learning rate scheduler reduced the learning rate by a factor of 0.1 every 150 epochs. To enhance model generalization, data augmentation techniques including random horizontal flips as well as brightness and contrast adjustments were applied. Training was performed on a computer equipped with an NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU and 24 GB of system RAM. The implementation was based on Python 3.9.23, PyTorch 2.7.1, and OpenCV 4.8. A random seed of 42 was set for all relevant libraries to ensure reproducibility.

Results

This section provides a systematic analysis of the performance of two types of methods for egg sex identification, including traditional statistical approaches and deep learning models. Statistical methods primarily explore potential differences in egg morphological parameters between male and female groups through descriptive statistics, boxplots, and t-tests or analysis of variance. The performance of deep learning models is mainly evaluated based on classification accuracy, providing a quantitative assessment of the discriminative power of the morphological features. By comparing the results of these approaches, this study investigates the relationship between egg morphological characteristics and sex, and provides a foundation for evaluating the potential application of different methods for early sex identification in industrial incubation settings.

Results of statistical analysis

To systematically assess the differences in morphological parameters for sex identification, this study conducted multi-level statistical analyses on the experimental data. Descriptive statistics were first used to quantify central tendency and dispersion for key morphological parameters, including L, W, EI, and C parameters, providing an overview of the overall distribution, and measurement consistency. Boxplots were then employed for visual comparison of the distribution patterns and potential outliers between male and female eggs, offering an intuitive and direct way to observe differences and guide further analyses. Finally, independent sample t-tests and one-way analysis of variance were performed to determine whether the morphological differences between sexes were statistically significant, thereby providing rigorous evidence for evaluating the discriminative potential of each parameter in early sex identification.

To further evaluate the statistical reliability of the observed differences between female and male eggs, a post-hoc power analysis was performed. Assumption checking analysis

In this study, assumption checks were conducted for the egg morphological features of both Datu2 and Xiaoyou 818 breeds. Since Xiaoyou 818 eggs lack the C parameter, all features related to C were excluded from the assumption checks, and only the remaining available morphological features were analyzed. To ensure appropriate statistical methods were applied for comparing morphological features between sexes, assumption checks were conducted, including tests for normality (Shapiro-Wilk) and homogeneity of variances (Levene’s test). The results indicated that for L, W, and the composite feature L+EI, some sex groups did not meet the normality assumption (Shapiro-Wilk p < 0.05), suggesting a deviation from normal distribution. In contrast, the EI satisfied normality in both sexes (p = 0.522 and 0.924). Levene’s test showed that variances between male and female groups were generally similar (Levene p > 0.05), indicating that variance differences were unlikely to meaningfully affect group comparisons. Even for features that met the normality assumption, Welch's t-test could be applied without violating robustness assumptions.

Based on these assumption checks, appropriate statistical tests were selected for each feature: non-parametric Mann-Whitney U tests were used for features that violated normality (L, W, L+EI, W+EI), whereas Welch's t-test was applied for EI, which satisfied both normality and equal variance assumptions. The actual test results showed that none of the features exhibited statistically significant differences between sexes (p > 0.05), indicating that these individual morphological traits provide limited discriminatory power for early sex identification. This assumption checking and method selection strategy ensured that comparisons for each feature were based on appropriate statistical assumptions, thereby enhancing the robustness of the analysis. Discussion

Biological basis for null findings

The results of this study indicate that intrinsic biological constraints limit the feasibility of pre-hatching sex identification based solely on external egg morphology in commercial layer chickens. Egg formation is primarily governed by maternal physiological processes, including the structure of the reproductive tract and eggshell deposition occurring in the isthmus and uterine segments of the oviduct. Consequently, eggshell thickness, curvature, and overall shape are mainly influenced by the hen’s genetic background, nutritional status, and health condition (Sah et al., 2018; Wilson et al., 2017). In contrast, avian sex is genetically determined at fertilization, whereas eggshell formation occurs subsequently and is entirely mediated by maternal tissues, resulting in no direct physiological mechanism by which embryonic sex information can be translated into external egg morphology. Based on these biological constraints, the conclusions of this study are drawn from analyses of pre-hatch eggs from commercial layer chickens. Under other conditions, such as stored eggs, different stages of incubation, or heritage and unselected breeds with greater genetic and morphological variability, the potential relationship between external egg morphology and sex may manifest differently and warrants further investigation in future studies.

Moreover, it should be acknowledged that the present study focused exclusively on eggs at the pre-incubation stage. Although genetic sex is determined at fertilization, embryos at this stage have not yet undergone substantial morphological differentiation or sex-related developmental processes. If sex-specific differences emerge primarily during early embryonic development rather than at the stage of egg formation, such differences may not yet be detectable through external morphological characteristics prior to incubation. Therefore, the selected temporal window may have further limited the possibility of identifying sex-related variation, which is consistent with the near-random predictive performance observed in this study.

Comparison with previous studies

Despite these biological constraints, the potential relationship between egg shape and sex has attracted sustained research interest. Some previous studies, particularly those based on local breeds or limited sample sizes, have reported a strong correlation between EI or related morphological parameters and sex (Zhang et al., 2025), while others have observed weak or inconsistent relationships (Peşmen et al., 2025). These conflicting findings constitute the primary motivation for the present study, highlighting the need for a systematic and reproducible evaluation of this relationship under industry-relevant conditions to determine whether such associations persist in commercial layer breeds characterized by highly standardized egg morphology. It is important to note that this study was not conducted under the a priori assumption that sex-related signals cannot be reflected in egg morphology. Rather, we objectively assessed the feasibility of morphology-based pre-hatching sex identification using a large sample size, standardized measurement procedures, and representative commercial breeds. The results demonstrate that male and female eggs exhibit highly overlapping distributions across all morphological traits, with no differences of statistical or practical significance, indicating that previously reported associations are difficult to reproducibly detect under industrial production conditions.

Methodological considerations

Although neither statistical analyses nor deep learning approaches revealed significant sex-discriminative signals, alternative explanations should be considered. If extremely subtle sex-related differences exist, their magnitude may fall below the resolution of the two-dimensional morphological measurements or visible-light imaging employed in this study. Higher-resolution or three-dimensional imaging techniques may be capable of detecting such minute variations, and sex-related morphological differences may also emerge at developmental stages not examined here. However, even if such differences could be detected under specific conditions, their practical applicability in industrial settings would likely be constrained by considerations of cost, throughput, and robustness.

The results from the deep learning model indicate that, even when using PP-HGNet to capture complex nonlinear features, relying solely on external morphological parameters or images is insufficient for achieving stable sex classification. The overall classification accuracy remained close to random levels, suggesting that the amount of sex-related information in external morphology is limited and that subtle texture or shape variations are difficult to translate into reproducible discriminative patterns. Differences observed between male and female recognition rates were minor and likely reflect random variation or class imbalance rather than genuine model sensitivity. This further indicates that when input features lack sufficient discriminative information, even powerful deep learning architectures cannot compensate, and nonlinear mappings and attention mechanisms do not significantly improve performance in this scenario. Moreover, single-parameter inputs provide almost no effective information, and the overall performance of multi-parameter combinations is also unstable. Although image inputs can capture texture, illumination distribution, and fine morphological details, early sex-related signals are difficult to detect under visible light conditions, highlighting the inherent limitations of constructing a pre-hatch sex identification system solely based on external phenotypic traits. It also indicates that even though the PP-HGNet network can fully capture complex nonlinear features, no sex-related patterns were observed, thereby reinforcing the robustness of the conclusions.

In addition, a small proportion of eggs, 12 samples, exhibited abnormal curvature estimation, indicating that two-dimensional image-based curvature extraction is sensitive to factors such as boundary segmentation, noise, illumination, and egg orientation. Although these samples were identified and excluded without materially affecting the overall results, their occurrence highlights inherent limitations in the robustness of computer vision–based approaches. Furthermore, image-based and deep learning analyses were conducted using eggs from a single commercial breed (Datu2), representing only half of the total sample, which constrains cross-breed generalizability. Even within this morphologically homogeneous and well-controlled dataset, stable classification performance was not achieved, suggesting that external egg images alone contain limited sex-discriminative information. Finally, this study adopted a binary classification framework. Although more sophisticated modeling strategies, such as probabilistic modeling or uncertainty quantification, may provide additional information regarding prediction confidence, they are unlikely to substantially improve performance when the input features themselves lack stable discriminative signals. Given that the overall model accuracy was close to random levels, these results indicate that the primary limitation arises from the intrinsic paucity of sex-related information in external egg morphology, rather than the choice of classification strategy.

Practical and research implications

This study demonstrates that relying solely on external egg morphological features is insufficient for reliable pre-hatching sex identification in commercial layer chickens, and that such an approach alone cannot meet the accuracy and robustness requirements of industrial applications. The findings provide clear guidance for the poultry industry, highlighting the limitations of morphology-based sex identification and helping to avoid the expenditure of time and resources on ineffective methods. Future research should explore multimodal, integrative strategies that combine complementary non-invasive technologies to capture subtle sex-related information not reflected in external morphology. Potential directions include machine vision, optical spectroscopy, hyperspectral imaging, and physiological signal analysis, which may further evaluate the potential role of morphological features in pre-hatching sex identification.

Conclusion

This study systematically evaluated the feasibility of using external egg morphological features for pre-hatching sex identification in commercial layer chickens. Analysis of 1008 fertilized eggs from two commercial breeds (Xiaoyou 818 and Datu2) showed no statistically or practically significant association between embryo sex and the EI or other single or combined morphological parameters. Both conventional statistical analyses and deep learning-based classification methods revealed that male and female eggs exhibit highly overlapping morphological distributions, with prediction accuracy close to random levels, indicating that external egg morphology alone is insufficient for reliable early sex identification.

For populations with greater phenotypic diversity, such as heritage breeds, bantams, or wild fowl, morphology-based sex identification may yield different results and warrants further investigation. Although egg morphology measurements offer advantages such as being non-destructive, low-cost, and easy to implement, they are unreliable as a standalone indicator for industrial-scale early sex identification. This negative finding provides practical guidance for the field and helps prevent other researchers from pursuing ineffective approaches. Future advances in early sex identification are more likely to arise from integrative, multimodal strategies that combine complementary non-invasive technologies, such as machine vision, optical spectroscopy, or physiological signal analysis, rather than relying solely on external morphological features.
