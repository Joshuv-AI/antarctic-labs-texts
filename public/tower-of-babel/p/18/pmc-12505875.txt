A predictive tool for early identification of moderate-to-severe pain following open colorectal surgery in older adults: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Yan Jin, Rongrong Feng, Hui Wang, Jianhui Huo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundModerate-to-severe pain is a common but often under-recognized complication after open colorectal surgery in older adults, leading to delayed recovery and extended hospitalization. Early identification of high-risk patients is essential for timely pain management. The objective of this study was to develop and internally validate a predictive model, presented as a nomogram, for estimating the risk of moderate-to-severe postoperative pain within 24 h among elderly patients undergoing open colorectal surgery.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study of 300 patients aged ≥ 60 years who underwent elective open colorectal surgery. Postoperative pain within 24 h was assessed using the Numerical Rating Scale (NRS); NRS ≥ 4 was defined as moderate-to-severe pain. Preoperative psychosocial, cognitive, inflammatory, and perioperative factors were evaluated. Multivariable logistic regression with stepwise AIC selection identified independent predictors. Model performance was assessed using ROC curves, calibration plots, the Hosmer–Lemeshow test, and decision curve analysis (DCA). A nomogram was developed for clinical use.ResultsOf the 300 patients, 120 (40.0%) experienced moderate-to-severe pain. These patients were older and had higher preoperative NRS and CRP levels, along with worse psychosocial and cognitive scores (P < 0.01). Seven variables independently predicted pain severity: GAD-7, PHQ-9, MMSE, MOS-SSS, CRP, operative duration, and undergoing a Miles procedure (P < 0.05). The model showed good discrimination (AUC = 0.79 in training; 0.77 in validation) and calibration. DCA demonstrated net clinical benefit across a range of thresholds.ConclusionWe developed and validated a nomogram incorporating psychosocial, inflammatory, and procedural factors to predict moderate-to-severe postoperative pain. This tool may enable early risk stratification and guide individualized analgesic strategies in elderly patients.Keywords: Postoperative pain, Risk prediction model, Nomogram, Psychological assessment, Abdominal surgery

Introduction

Postoperative pain remains one of the most common and distressing complications following major open colorectal surgery, particularly in elderly patients undergoing colorectal cancer resection (Huang et al. 2024). Despite advances in analgesic techniques, studies have shown that up to 40–60% of patients still experience moderate-to-severe pain within the first 24 to 72 h after surgery (Lindberg et al. 2020). Such inadequately controlled pain is not only associated with delayed mobilization, prolonged hospitalization, and increased healthcare costs, but also predisposes patients to long-term complications including chronic pain syndromes, psychological distress, and impaired functional recovery (Brown and McCormack 2006; Chang et al. 2019). These risks are especially pronounced in older adults with multiple comorbidities, underscoring the urgent need for proactive and individualized pain management strategies in perioperative care (Wang et al. 2024; Zhu et al. 2024).

However, current approaches to postoperative pain management often rely on reactive strategies or subjective clinician assessments, which may fail to anticipate high-risk individuals before pain becomes unmanageable. (Lindberg et al. 2020). Despite increasing awareness of postoperative pain burden, most existing prediction models are either generic or derived from heterogeneous surgical populations, limiting their applicability to specific procedures (Chang et al. 2019). In particular, few studies have specifically addressed elderly patients undergoing open colorectal surgery, a group characterized by high physiological vulnerability, complex perioperative needs, and diverse psychosocial backgrounds (Parnasa et al. 2024). Furthermore, widely used models such as the APS-POQ-R and PROSPECT guidelines rarely incorporate preoperative psychological distress, cognitive impairment, or social support levels—factors increasingly recognized as critical determinants of pain perception and reporting in older adults (Zhu et al. 2024; Mokutani et al. 2016). Therefore, there remains a critical need for a predictive tool specifically tailored to this high-risk surgical population.

To date, few studies have focused specifically on open colorectal procedures colorectal cancer surgery, a population with heterogeneous pain responses and evolving perioperative practices (Samuelsson 2017). Importantly, no validated model has been developed to predict moderate-to-severe acute pain based on routinely collected preoperative and intraoperative factors in this setting (Li and Lu 2024). Addressing this gap, the present study seeks to provide evidence for a tailored risk prediction approach in elderly patients undergoing open colorectal surgery. This study aimed to develop and internally validate a clinically applicable nomogram to predict the risk of moderate-to-severe postoperative pain within 24 h among elderly patients undergoing open colorectal surgery. The ultimate goal was to provide a practical tool to support early risk stratification and inform personalized perioperative analgesia strategies.

Methods

Study design

A retrospective, observational cohort framework was employed for this study, which was undertaken at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Naval Medical University, a tertiary medical center in China. The cohort included individuals aged 60 years and above who had undergone planned open colorectal surgical procedures under general anesthesia between January 1, 2021, and December 31, 2023. All procedures were performed by board-certified surgeons specializing in colorectal oncology, following standardized perioperative protocols. The current study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Naval Medical University (approval number SNH202504121). Written informed consents from all patients were obtained in any experimental work with humans. To safeguard patient confidentiality, all identifying information was irreversibly anonymized before any analytic procedures were undertaken.

To facilitate internal model validation, the total cohort was randomly partitioned at a 7:3 ratio into training (n = 210) and temporal validation (n = 90) subsets, ensuring comparable distributions of key demographic and clinical characteristics. Two independent reviewers systematically abstracted baseline and perioperative variables from the electronic health record system utilizing a pre-defined, standardized data collection instrument. Any inconsistencies identified between reviewers were resolved through consensus adjudication by a third investigator.

The principal objective of this study was to develop a clinically applicable predictive model for moderate-to-severe postoperative pain based on multidimensional perioperative factors. The model’s performance was evaluated according to established criteria for diagnostic accuracy studies, including discrimination, calibration, and net clinical benefit.

Patient eligibility criteria

Patients were deemed eligible for inclusion if they met all of the following criteria: (1) age ≥ 60 years at the time of surgery; (2) undergoing elective open colorectal resection for pathologically confirmed malignant or premalignant lesions; (3) receipt of general anesthesia with endotracheal intubation; and (4) completion of standardized perioperative assessments, including validated preoperative psychological and cognitive evaluations. Eligible surgical procedures included Miles’ abdominoperineal resection and Dixon anterior resection, provided they were performed via open laparotomy rather than minimally invasive approaches.

Exclusion criteria were pre-specified to minimize confounding and enhance cohort homogeneity. Patients were excluded if they (1) underwent laparoscopic, robotic, or emergency surgery; (2) had a history of chronic opioid use (defined as daily use > 30 days preoperatively); these patients were excluded to minimize the confounding effects of opioid tolerance and opioid-induced hyperalgesia, which could obscure the predictive value of other clinical variables); (3) had intraoperative conversion to palliative resection or ostomy creation unrelated to the original plan; (4) had documented diagnoses of major psychiatric conditions—such as schizophrenia or bipolar affective disorder—or progressive neurodegenerative illnesses, including but not limited to Alzheimer’s disease, that may compromise the reliability of patient-reported pain assessments; (5) exhibited major perioperative complications requiring reoperation within 24 h; or (6) had missing key variables, including postoperative pain scores or essential preoperative assessments.

To ensure data reliability, all inclusion and exclusion determinations were independently reviewed by two attending anesthesiologists and one surgical investigator. Discrepant cases were resolved by consensus discussion. The final study population was strictly limited to individuals with complete 24-h postoperative pain documentation and fully recorded covariates for model derivation and validation.

Data collection and perioperative variables

Comprehensive perioperative data were meticulously extracted from the hospital’s integrated electronic health record system, encompassing demographic characteristics, psychosocial profiles, cognitive status, inflammatory markers, anesthetic techniques, and surgical parameters.

Baseline demographic characteristics encompassed patient age, sex, body mass index (BMI), tobacco use history, and clinically established comorbid conditions, including hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, and ischemic heart disease. Preoperative psychological status was assessed using the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) and the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9)—instruments with demonstrated psychometric validity in Chinese surgical cohorts. Cognitive capacity was appraised through administration of the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), while perceived social support was quantified using the Medical Outcomes Study–Social Support Survey (MOS-SSS), delivered during the preadmission counseling encounter.

Intraoperative data included type of surgical procedure (Miles vs. Dixon resection), ASA physical status classification, operative duration, and estimated blood loss. The use of intraoperative analgesia was standardized according to institutional protocol, typically comprising fentanyl (2–4 µg/kg) or sufentanil (0.3–0.5 µg/kg) administered intravenously in divided doses, with supplementary remifentanil infusion at 0.1–0.2 µg/kg/min as indicated by hemodynamic fluctuations. Neuromuscular blockade and volatile anesthetics were used per standard protocol but not analyzed in this study.

Analgesic intervention commenced in the post-anesthesia care unit (PACU), where most patients were administered intravenous patient-controlled analgesia (IV-PCA) employing a morphine-based protocol. The standard regimen consisted of a 1 mg/mL concentration, with a 1 mL demand bolus, an 8-min lockout interval, and no continuous background infusion. Patients who received epidural analgesia were excluded from this analysis to ensure treatment homogeneity. Alternative analgesic strategies, such as NSAIDs or regional techniques (e.g., epidural analgesia), were permitted but applied infrequently in this cohort and therefore not stratified in analysis. Opioid consumption during the first 24 h post-surgery was recorded and standardized into intravenous morphine equivalent doses for exploratory subgroup analysis but not included in the final predictive model.

All variables were selected based on biological plausibility and literature precedence, and were available for at least 95% of the study population, thus minimizing the risk of bias due to missing data.

Pain outcome definition and grouping

The principal outcome measure was the incidence of moderate-to-severe postoperative pain during the first 24 h following open colorectal surgery. Pain intensity was quantified via the 11-point Numeric Rating Scale (NRS), a validated tool in surgical settings, where 0 denoted the absence of pain and 10 represented the most severe pain conceivable. As part of routine nursing care in the postoperative ward, trained nurses recorded patients’ pain intensity at rest every 6 h using standardized electronic medical records.

For analytic purposes, patients were categorized based on their maximum NRS score recorded during the first 24 h after surgery. Individuals who reported at least one NRS score of ≥ 4 at rest during this period were classified into the “Moderate-to-severe pain group,” consistent with established thresholds for clinically meaningful pain requiring intervention. Conversely, those whose pain scores remained below this threshold throughout the 24-h period were assigned to the “Mild pain group.”

This binary grouping served as the dependent variable in subsequent univariable and multivariable analyses. To ensure consistency and minimize subjective reporting bias, only rest pain was considered in the primary outcome definition, while dynamic pain (e.g., during mobilization or coughing) was not included. This decision was made to reduce heterogeneity and maintain measurement standardization across the cohort. This standardized classification allowed for robust model development and internal validation across both training and validation cohorts.

Psychosocial and functional scales

Preoperative psychological and cognitive assessments were conducted using four validated instruments that have been culturally and linguistically adapted for Chinese clinical populations. All scales were administered by trained nursing staff within 24 h prior to surgery, following standardized operating procedures to ensure consistency and minimize measurement bias.

Generalized anxiety symptoms were assessed utilizing the culturally validated Chinese adaptation of the 7-item Generalized Anxiety Disorder Scale (GAD-7), a widely used screening instrument in clinical and perioperative research settings, which quantifies anxiety symptoms on a 0–21 scale. A score ≥ 10 was indicative of clinically relevant anxiety symptoms. Depressive symptomatology was evaluated using the 9-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9), a psychometrically validated instrument in Chinese populations, yielding total scores from 0 to 27. Elevated scores on both the GAD-7 and PHQ-9 scales may indicate a heightened psychological burden in the perioperative context.

Cognitive function was assessed using the culturally adapted Chinese version of the MMSE, a standardized screening tool that evaluates five cognitive domains: orientation, registration, attention and calculation, recall, and language. Total scores range from 0 to 30, with higher values indicative of greater cognitive integrity. Mild cognitive impairment was considered at scores < 24, although the total score was treated as a continuous variable in modeling.

Perceived social support was assessed using the culturally validated Chinese version of the MOS-SSS, a 19-item instrument encompassing five distinct dimensions: emotional support, informational support, tangible assistance, affectionate support, and positive social interaction. Scores were transformed into a standardized scale (0–100), with lower scores denoting reduced perceived social support.

All assessors completed a structured training session conducted by a senior clinical psychologist prior to study initiation. Inter-rater reliability was periodically assessed throughout the study period to maintain scoring consistency. Scale completion occurred in a quiet preoperative setting, either through self-report or guided interview for patients with reading or visual limitations.

Model development and validation

To construct a clinically applicable predictive model for early identification of moderate-to-severe postoperative pain, the full cohort (N = 300) was randomly partitioned in a 7:3 ratio into a training set (n = 210) and a temporal validation set (n = 90). Randomization was stratified to preserve the proportion of patients experiencing the primary outcome in both subsets.

Initial candidate variables were screened in the training cohort through univariable logistic regression analyses. Variables associated with the primary outcome at a liberal threshold of P < 0.10 were considered eligible for multivariable analysis. Subsequently, backward stepwise logistic regression using the Akaike information criterion (AIC) was employed to optimize model parsimony and prevent overfitting. Collinearity was assessed via variance inflation factors, and clinically redundant variables were excluded to enhance interpretability.

The final multivariable model retained seven independent predictors, all of which were entered as continuous or binary variables as appropriate. Discriminative performance of the predictive model was evaluated by calculating the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). Model calibration was examined through graphical calibration plots in conjunction with the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit statistic. To assess clinical utility across varying decision thresholds, decision curve analysis (DCA) was employed to estimate net benefit.

External temporal validation was performed in the independent validation set without any retraining or reparameterization of the model. The AUC and calibration performance were re-evaluated in this cohort, and a DeLong’s test was applied to assess statistical differences in discrimination performance between training and validation datasets.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). For continuous variables not adhering to a normal distribution, descriptive data were summarized as medians and interquartile ranges, whereas categorical variables were reported as absolute frequencies with corresponding percentages. Between-group differences were evaluated using either the Mann–Whitney U test or Student’s t test for continuous outcomes, and the chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, contingent upon data distribution and underlying assumptions regarding sample size.

The discriminative capacity of the multivariable logistic regression model was assessed by calculating the AUC, with 95% confidence intervals derived via the nonparametric DeLong approach. AUC comparisons between training and validation sets were performed using the two-sided DeLong’s test.

Calibration was visually assessed through calibration plots comparing predicted versus observed outcome probabilities across deciles of risk. Formal goodness-of-fit was tested using the Hosmer–Lemeshow chi-squared test, with non-significant P values indicating adequate calibration. The model’s clinical utility was further evaluated by DCA, which estimated the net benefit across a range of threshold probabilities relevant to clinical decision-making.

All P values were two-tailed, with statistical significance defined as P < 0.05. No imputation was performed for missing data, and all analyses were based on complete case observations.

Result

Baseline characteristics and group stratification

A total of 300 eligible patients were included in the final analysis. Based on their worst Numerical Rating Scale (NRS) score within the first 24 postoperative hours, patients were stratified into two distinct groups: those experiencing moderate-to-severe pain (NRS ≥ 4; n = 120, 40.0%) and those reporting only mild pain (NRS < 4; n = 180, 60.0%). The allocation of participants and their subsequent division into training (n = 210) and validation (n = 90) cohorts are illustrated in the study flow diagram (Fig. 1), which also outlines key exclusion criteria.

Study flowchart. A total of 382 patients were assessed for eligibility. After exclusion of 82 individuals based on predefined criteria, 300 patients were included in the final cohort. Participants were randomly assigned to either the training cohort (n = 210) or validation cohort (n = 90). Based on postoperative numeric rating scale (NRS) scores within 24 h, patients were further stratified into the moderate-to-severe pain group (NRS ≥ 4, n = 120) or the Mild pain group (NRS < 4, n = 180). Event proportions were preserved across both cohorts to ensure consistent outcome representation for model development and validation

Compared to individuals in the mild pain group, those with moderate-to-severe postoperative pain were significantly older (median age 71.0 vs. 68.0 years, P = 0.012) and more likely to present with higher baseline systolic blood pressure (132 vs. 128 mmHg, P = 0.039). Although sex distribution, BMI, and most comorbidities were comparable between the two groups, a higher proportion of patients in the moderate-to-severe pain group were classified as ASA physical status III (45.8% vs. 28.9%, P = 0.003).

Several preoperative pain-related variables also showed substantial between-group differences. The moderate-to-severe pain group had significantly higher baseline NRS scores (3.0 vs. 1.0, P < 0.001) and a greater prevalence of chronic pain history (22.5% vs. 10.6%, P = 0.003). Psychosocial vulnerability was more pronounced among these patients, as reflected by elevated anxiety and depression scores (GAD-7 and PHQ-9, P < 0.001 for both), reduced cognitive performance (MMSE, P < 0.001), and diminished perceived social support (MOS-SSS score, P = 0.003).

Laboratory indicators of systemic inflammation, such as C-reactive protein (CRP), were also elevated in the moderate-to-severe pain group (10.1 vs. 7.4 mg/L, P = 0.008), whereas serum creatinine levels remained statistically indistinguishable.

Regarding intraoperative variables, patients with moderate-to-severe pain were more likely to undergo a Miles procedure (35.8% vs. 21.1%, P = 0.007) and had longer operative durations (189 vs. 172 min, P = 0.019). Intraoperative blood loss trended higher in this group, although the difference did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.084).

To ensure model robustness and temporal validity, stratified randomization preserved event proportions across cohorts. Specifically, 84 of the 120 patients with moderate-to-severe pain were included in the training set and 36 in the validation set, while 126 and 54 patients with mild pain were assigned to the training and validation cohorts, respectively (Table 1).

To explore potential contributors to postoperative pain severity, we conducted a focused comparative analysis of candidate predictors previously associated with pain vulnerability. Patients in the moderate-to-severe pain group consistently exhibited more adverse psychosocial and inflammatory profiles. Specifically, their median GAD-7 and PHQ-9 scores were markedly higher than those in the mild pain group, suggesting a greater burden of psychological distress (P < 0.001 for both). Conversely, MMSE scores were significantly lower, indicating reduced cognitive function in this subgroup (P < 0.001). In parallel, perceived social support—as measured by the MOS-SSS scale—was significantly diminished (P = 0.003), reflecting heightened social vulnerability.

Biochemical markers echoed this trend. Median preoperative CRP levels were significantly elevated in patients experiencing moderate-to-severe pain compared to those with mild symptoms (10.1 vs. 7.4 mg/L, P = 0.008), implicating heightened systemic inflammation as a potential mechanistic link.

These findings underscore the relevance of psychosocial and inflammatory markers in stratifying postoperative pain risk and provided a rational basis for variable inclusion in subsequent multivariable model development.

Multivariable analysis and predictor selection

Variables demonstrating either statistical significance in univariate analyses or deemed clinically pertinent based on prior literature or expert judgment were subsequently included in a multivariable logistic regression model to explore independent associations with moderate-to-severe postoperative pain. A stepwise backward elimination strategy, based on Akaike’s information criterion (AIC), was employed to achieve a parsimonious model with optimal explanatory power.

Seven covariates emerged as independently associated with an increased likelihood of experiencing moderate-to-severe pain within 24 h postoperatively. Significant psychological correlates of moderate-to-severe postoperative pain included higher scores on both the Generalized Anxiety Disorder Scale (GAD-7; adjusted odds ratio [aOR] 1.13, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.05–1.22, P = 0.002) and the PHQ-9 (aOR 1.10, 95% CI 1.03–1.18, P = 0.005), each suggestive of increased psychological distress. Cognitive vulnerability, indexed by decremental changes in MMSE scores, was similarly associated with elevated pain risk (aOR 1.10 per point decrease, 95% CI 1.03–1.18, P = 0.005). In addition, diminished perceived social support—quantified via the MOS-SSS—was independently linked to greater pain likelihood (aOR 1.08 per 5-point decrease, 95% CI 1.02–1.15, P = 0.008).

Among perioperative parameters, preoperative systemic inflammation, indexed by CRP levels, was independently predictive of worse pain outcomes (OR 1.05 per mg/L increase, 95% CI 1.01–1.10, P = 0.025). Procedural variables also played a role: patients undergoing Miles procedures demonstrated over twice the odds of significant postoperative pain relative to those receiving Dixon procedures (OR 2.28, 95% CI 1.27–4.08, P = 0.004), and prolonged operative duration was incrementally associated with heightened pain risk (OR 1.07 per 10-min increment, 95% CI 1.02–1.13, P = 0.004).

Model performance and validation

The predictive model incorporating the seven independently associated variables demonstrated satisfactory discriminative ability. In the training cohort (n = 210), the AUC was 0.79 (95% CI: 0.72–0.85), indicating strong capacity to distinguish between patients with and without moderate-to-severe postoperative pain. Temporal validation in an independent cohort (n = 90) yielded a consistent AUC of 0.77 (95% CI: 0.68–0.85), confirming the robustness of model performance (Fig. 3A). DeLong’s test showed no statistically significant difference between the two curves (P = 0.39), further supporting the model's generalizability across distinct patient subsets (Table 3).

Model calibration was evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit statistic, which produced a non-significant result in the validation cohort (P = 0.43), indicating no substantial deviation between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes. The calibration plot also visually demonstrated the close alignment of predicted probabilities with actual event rates across deciles of predicted risk (Fig. 3B).

Collectively, these findings underscore the model’s validity in both discrimination and calibration, supporting its applicability for risk stratification in routine clinical settings.

Clinical utility assessment

To assess the model’s potential clinical utility across a spectrum of risk thresholds, DCA was conducted separately within the training and validation cohorts. As illustrated in Fig. 4, the model consistently demonstrated superior net benefit relative to both the “treat-all” and “treat-none” approaches, particularly within the clinically pertinent threshold range of 20% to 60%. This range reflects the decision-making uncertainty zone in which clinicians are likely to consider escalating analgesic interventions.

The consistency of DCA curves across datasets reinforces the clinical applicability of the model for real-world perioperative pain risk stratification. Notably, the net benefit remained stable across this range, indicating that the model can inform treatment decisions without increasing the risk of overtreatment or undertreatment.

Nomogram construction

To facilitate individualized risk estimation and clinical application, a nomogram was developed based on the seven independent predictors identified in the multivariable logistic regression model. These variables included psychological vulnerability (GAD-7 and PHQ-9 scores), cognitive status (MMSE score), social support (MOS-SSS score), systemic inflammation (CRP levels), procedural type (Miles versus Dixon procedure), and operative duration (Fig. 5).

Each predictor was allocated a specific point value proportional to its β coefficient in the final model. Continuous variables (e.g., CRP levels, GAD-7, PHQ-9, MMSE, MOS-SSS, operative duration) were mapped directly onto their respective scales, while categorical variables (e.g., Miles vs. Dixon procedure) were assigned discrete point values. Among them, the presence of a Miles procedure and higher GAD-7 scores contributed the greatest weights to total risk estimation, while lower MMSE and MOS-SSS scores—indicative of cognitive impairment and social vulnerability—were also associated with a higher probability of moderate-to-severe postoperative pain.

The scores of all predictors are then summed to yield a total score, which is projected downward to the bottom probability scale to estimate the individualized risk of experiencing at least one episode of NRS ≥ 4 pain within the first 24 h postoperatively.

This graphical tool provides a straightforward method to support bedside decision-making and enhance individualized analgesic planning.

Subgroup discrimination analysis

To further assess the generalizability and robustness of the prediction model, subgroup analyses were conducted across clinically relevant strata, including sex (male vs. female), age (< 70 vs. ≥ 70 years), ASA physical status (I–II vs. III), preoperative CRP level (< 10 vs. ≥ 10 mg/L), and cognitive function (MMSE ≥ 24 vs. < 24).

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were constructed for individual subgroups within the validation cohort, with the AUC values computed to evaluate the model’s discriminatory capacity across strata. As illustrated in Fig. 6, the model preserved moderate-to-good discrimination across all subgroups, with AUCs ranging from 0.72 to 0.80. Notably, predictive performance remained stable regardless of sex or ASA class, and the model retained accuracy even in older patients and those with mild cognitive impairment.

These findings support the clinical utility of the proposed model across a wide spectrum of surgical patients and reinforce its applicability to diverse perioperative profiles. Nevertheless, performance variation in borderline subgroups warrants cautious interpretation and further validation in larger prospective cohorts.

Discussion

This retrospective cohort study, involving 300 elderly patients who underwent open colorectal surgery, aimed to develop and internally validate a streamlined predictive model for estimating the likelihood of moderate-to-severe postoperative pain—operationally defined as any single NRS score of ≥ 4 during the first 24 postoperative hours (Mogianos et al. 2025). Using a combination of demographic, psychological, inflammatory, and surgical variables, the final logistic regression model demonstrated robust discrimination (AUC = 0.79 in the training cohort and 0.77 in the validation cohort), good calibration, and favorable clinical utility across multiple decision thresholds (Mogianos et al. 2025; Armstrong et al. 2023). In this retrospective cohort study, we developed and validated a 7-variable prediction model for early identification of moderate-to-severe postoperative pain following open colorectal surgery. The model included validated psychological, cognitive, inflammatory, and surgical indicators (GAD-7, PHQ-9, MMSE, MOS-SSS, CRP, Miles procedure, and operative time), and demonstrated favorable discrimination and calibration across both cohorts (Han et al. 2021). These findings highlight the multidimensional nature of postoperative pain and support individualized risk stratification. Importantly, key predictors such as elevated GAD-7 and PHQ-9 scores, increased preoperative CRP levels, prolonged operative time, and lower MMSE and MOS-SSS scores emerged as independent risk factors, consistent with clinical and biological plausibility (Han et al. 2021; Caumo et al. 2002). The resulting nomogram facilitates individualized risk stratification and supports early identification of high-risk patients who may benefit from intensified analgesic strategies. A standardized PCA-based multimodal analgesia protocol was uniformly applied, reducing heterogeneity in postoperative pain control and enhancing the validity of outcome assessment.

Our findings are broadly aligned with previous reports emphasizing the multifactorial nature of postoperative pain. Prior studies have consistently shown that preoperative psychological distress, particularly anxiety and depression, is associated with heightened pain sensitivity and suboptimal analgesic response (Tadesse et al. 2021). The inclusion of the GAD-7 and PHQ-9 scores in our model reinforces this link and demonstrates the value of routine psychological screening (Zhu et al. 2024). Additionally, systemic inflammation, as reflected by elevated preoperative CRP, has been identified as a potential biomarker for postoperative pain exacerbation due to its role in peripheral and central sensitization (Zhu et al. 2024). Notably, the operative time—a surrogate for surgical invasiveness—has also been associated with increased nociceptive input and delayed recovery in colorectal procedures (Zhu et al. 2024). While previous pain prediction models have incorporated individual factors such as age, sex, or surgery type, few have integrated cognitive status (e.g., MMSE score), social support (MOS-SSS), and surgical subtype (Miles procedure), which our model demonstrated to be clinically relevant (Mokutani et al. 2016; Tran et al. 2024). The present study therefore extends prior work by incorporating a broader and more clinically interpretable set of predictors, enabling practical translation in perioperative settings. Our findings extend beyond existing pain prediction frameworks such as the APS-POQ-R and PROSPECT, which, although valuable, do not specifically target older surgical patients or incorporate multidimensional constructs such as preoperative anxiety, cognitive function, or perceived social support. By integrating these domains, our model addresses a critical gap and provides a more personalized risk stratification tool for early postoperative pain in elderly patients undergoing open colorectal surgery. However, the overall net benefit observed in DCA was modest, underscoring both the need for cautious interpretation of its real-world impact and the necessity of external validation in other surgical populations before broader application.

Several of the identified predictors offer mechanistic insights into the pathophysiology of postoperative pain and hold practical relevance for perioperative risk stratification. Elevated GAD-7 and PHQ-9 scores reflect heightened baseline anxiety and depression, which have been consistently linked to hyperalgesic responses via central sensitization and impaired endogenous pain modulation (Manganotti and Tamburin 2019). The inclusion of lower MMSE scores suggests that impaired cognitive reserve may disrupt pain-coping mechanisms and self-regulation, contributing to greater subjective pain experience despite the traditional view that cognitive impairment might attenuate pain reporting (Pires and Costa 2016). This finding may be more reflective of mild-to-moderate cognitive decline rather than advanced dementia and should be interpreted within the clinical context of an elective surgery population.

The prognostic contribution of low perceived social support (MOS-SSS) underscores the biopsychosocial nature of pain, wherein limited emotional buffering and decreased help-seeking behavior may exacerbate pain-related distress (Sun 2015). CRP, as an acute-phase reactant, likely captures underlying subclinical inflammation, which may prime nociceptive pathways (Ueda 1999). Moreover, the Miles procedure and longer operative duration serve as surgical proxies for higher tissue trauma and prolonged nociceptive stimulation, both known risk factors for intensified postoperative pain (Kumakura and Ishiyama 2011). Together, these seven predictors reflect an integrative model incorporating psychological, cognitive, biological, and procedural domains, facilitating more personalized and multidimensional perioperative care. Interestingly, lower MMSE scores were independently associated with higher risk of postoperative pain, which appears to contradict the conventional understanding that cognitive impairment may blunt pain perception or hinder accurate self-reporting. One plausible explanation is that our cohort primarily included patients with mild cognitive impairment, who retain the ability to report pain but may experience heightened anxiety, sensory amplification, or impaired coping mechanisms. Prior research has indicated that early-stage cognitive decline may paradoxically increase pain sensitivity due to diminished executive regulation and altered neuroinflammatory responses. This reinforces the importance of routinely assessing cognitive function preoperatively, even in mildly impaired patients, to better anticipate pain-related vulnerabilities.

Several methodological and contextual limitations should be acknowledged. First, its single-center, retrospective nature introduces inherent selection and information biases, including potential variability in perioperative documentation and psychological assessments (Davoudi et al. 2022). Second, although the model integrated seven clinically and psychosocially relevant predictors, other potentially important variables—such as pre-existing opioid use, pain catastrophizing, or detailed inflammatory cytokine profiles (e.g., IL-6, TNF-α)—were not captured, which may restrict the model’s comprehensiveness. Third, while cognitive impairment was included as a significant predictor, the paradox of higher reported pain in patients with lower MMSE scores should be cautiously interpreted and warrants further prospective neuropsychological validation (Zhao and Jiang 2017; Yang et al. 2010). Fourth, the clinical utility of the model was modest, as reflected by an AUC below the conventional threshold for strong discrimination (i.e., < 0.80) and limited net benefit observed in DCA analyses at common clinical decision thresholds. Standardized opioid-based PCA may have attenuated pain scores, potentially reducing model sensitivity to detect true high-risk patients.

Lastly, external validation in diverse populations, particularly in multicenter or international settings, is required to enhance generalizability. Future work should aim to incorporate dynamic perioperative variables, explore mechanistic biomarkers, and assess the impact of model-guided interventions on actual pain outcomes (Schug and Bruce 2017).

In conclusion, this study established a clinically applicable predictive model for estimating the risk of moderate-to-severe postoperative pain among older adults undergoing open colorectal surgery, offering potential utility for preoperative risk stratification. By integrating readily accessible perioperative variables—including psychosocial assessments, inflammatory biomarkers, and procedural characteristics—our model offers a personalized tool for early identification of high-risk individuals. The nomogram, together with its corresponding decision-support indicators, demonstrated adequate discriminative ability, good calibration, and practical clinical utility in both the development and validation cohorts (Topolovec-Vranic et al. 2013). This work fills a critical gap in perioperative pain risk stratification and provides a foundation for targeted analgesic strategies and multidisciplinary intervention planning. Importantly, the model was developed and validated in elderly patients undergoing open colorectal procedures, and its generalizability to other abdominal surgical populations remains to be established. With further external validation in larger, multicenter cohorts, the model may support evidence-based pain management pathways and contribute to the advancement of precision perioperative care in aging surgical populations.

Conclusion

A practical multivariable prediction model was developed and internally validated to identify elderly patients at elevated risk of experiencing moderate-to-severe postoperative pain following open colorectal surgery. By incorporating both psychological and physiological factors readily available in routine clinical practice, the model offers robust predictive accuracy and demonstrated clinical utility across risk thresholds. The visualized nomogram enhances interpretability and facilitates personalized perioperative pain management planning. Future multicenter prospective studies are warranted to externally validate the model in broader surgical populations and to evaluate its impact on pain outcomes and resource optimization in real-world clinical environments.
