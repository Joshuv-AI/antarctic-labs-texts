Supplemental Oxygen Payments and Equipment Utilization in a Low-Income Population With Interstitial Lung Disease or COPD
Authors: Kristopher P Clark, Daniel J Kass, Howard B Degenholtz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSupplemental oxygen is often prescribed to patients with interstitial lung disease (ILD) and COPD, and oxygen services are often paid through Medicare or Medicaid benefits. The specific costs to Centers for Medicaid and Medicare Services for oxygen therapy in these diseases have not been fully described.Research QuestionWhat are the estimated payments by Medicare and Medicaid for supplemental oxygen therapy, and do differences in oxygen equipment utilization or other factors impact these payments in low-income individuals with ILD or COPD?Study Design and MethodsWe reviewed claims data for years 2016 to 2020 for Pennsylvania residents with ILD or COPD who were dually eligible for Medicaid and Medicare and enrolled in traditional fee-for-service Medicare. A multilevel mixed-effects generalized linear model was used to identify variables associated with annual oxygen payments made by Medicare or Medicaid.ResultsCompared with COPD, a greater proportion of paid claims in ILD were for oxygen services (40% vs 22%) and for high flow oxygen (4.2% vs 2.2%); however, ILD represented the minority of paid oxygen claims (5.2%). The median payment for oxygen was similar at $64.53 (interquartile range, $35.04-$94.56) for ILD and $65.84 (interquartile range, $33.63-$96.70) for COPD. Most claims (≥ 94%) were for stationary concentrators. Liquid devices and stationary gas were the least used equipment. Lower payments were associated with living in a competitive bidding area and with markers of advanced age and worse health status. Higher payments were associated with living in nonmetropolitan areas, having equipment delivering high oxygen flow, and receiving liquid oxygen or a portable oxygen concentrator in addition to a stationary concentrator.InterpretationOxygen payments and equipment utilization were similar between patients with ILD and COPD who were dually eligible for Medicare and Medicaid services; however, liquid oxygen claims were rare even among patients requiring high flow.Key Words: interstitial lung disease, health care costs, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, oxygen equipment, supplemental oxygen therapy

Take-home Point

Study Question: What are the estimated Medicare and Medicaid payments for supplemental oxygen therapy, and do differences in oxygen equipment utilization or other factors impact these costs in low-income individuals with interstitial lung disease (ILD) or COPD?

Results: COPD represented most oxygen claims, whereas individuals with ILD were more likely to have an oxygen claim; however, oxygen spending, type of equipment, and other payment factors were similar in both groups.

Interpretation: Our results indicate that payments for supplemental oxygen are similar in low-income individuals with ILD and COPD and are associated with comorbidities, age, living in a rural noncompetitive bidding area, and type of equipment. Most individuals in both groups primarily used stationary oxygen concentrators and portable gas tanks.

In the United States, supplemental oxygen therapy is recommended for patients with COPD and interstitial lung disease (ILD) who have hypoxemia at rest or with exertion.1,2 Oxygen therapy is common in both diseases3, 4, 5, 6 and is associated with increased health care costs and resource utilization5,7, 8, 9, 10, 11; however, limited data exist on the specific costs associated with oxygen therapy in these diseases, and little is known about how these patients use oxygen equipment or what factors contribute to oxygen costs.

Supplemental oxygen is delivered from stationary or portable compressed gas tanks, liquid oxygen reservoirs/canisters, or oxygen concentrators. Supplemental oxygen requires a prescription from a treating provider which must include the indication, results of oxygen testing, and prescribed flow rates. Patients with ILD and COPD are often eligible for Medicare, which provides coverage for durable medical equipment (DME) services including supplemental oxygen. The Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) provide coverage for rented oxygen equipment and supplies including a 50% payment increase for equipment delivering > 4 L/min.2,12 Although providers determine oxygen prescriptions and CMS covers oxygen services, the type of equipment a patient receives is often at the discretion of the locally contracted DME supplier. Oxygen equipment is included in the CMS Competitive Bidding Program (CBP) instituted in 2011 to lower costs of highly used DME. Although the CBP reduced DME spending,13, 14, 15 it may have also resulted in a decreased number of DME providers and may have limited patients’ access to some oxygen services.16, 17, 18, 19 This has resulted in the drafting of the Supplemental Oxygen Access Reform Act currently before Congress, which aims to improve oxygen access and reimbursement policies.20

We chose to study oxygen payments and utilization in individuals who were dually eligible for and receiving both Medicaid and Medicare fee-for-service benefits because this represents vulnerable, low-income people likely to have high disease burden. This also allowed us to focus on current CMS policy regarding supplemental oxygen payments. We conducted this study of oxygen claims in dually eligible Pennsylvania residents with a diagnosis of ILD or COPD. Our objectives were (1) to estimate CMS payments for supplemental oxygen, (2) to characterize differences in oxygen equipment utilization, and (3) to identify factors associated with increased payments of supplemental oxygen therapy in these diseases.

Study Design and Methods

Data Source and Study Population

We reviewed Pennsylvania Medicare and Medicaid claims paid between January 1, 2016, and December 31, 2020. These years were chosen because they represented the most recent years for which data were available. The data include beneficiary demographics and line-item DME claim payment amounts for oxygen services. We restricted our study to DME claims only. Competitive bidding areas (CBAs) were determined by matching beneficiary data to 2021 CBA zip codes,21 which were stable when compared with zip code data from earlier years (e-Appendix 1).

Participants were dually eligible to receive both Medicaid and Medicare benefits for ≥ 1 month per calendar year. Individuals enrolled in Medicare Advantage or Special Needs Plans were excluded because encounter data were not available. Participants were required to have ≥ 2 outpatient or ≥ 1 inpatient claims associated with an ILD or COPD diagnosis based on International Classification of Diseases (ICD), 10th Revision, coding (e-Appendix 2). ILD was defined by ICD J84 series, which represents fibrotic and interstitial diseases including idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF). COPD was defined using a validated coding algorithm developed by the Chronic Condition Data Warehouse.22 Individuals were excluded if they received nursing home care or were non-Pennsylvania residents (e-Fig 1).

Supplemental Oxygen Use

Participants were identified as oxygen users if they had ≥ 1 paid claim in a calendar year associated with a Healthcare Common Procedure Coding System code for oxygen equipment (e-Appendix 3). Claims were analyzed based on equipment type (compressed gas, liquid, or concentrator), whether equipment was stationary or portable, and low vs high flow delivery. High flow was defined as stationary or portable equipment coded for providing > 4 L/min to align with CMS reimbursement policy.2

Comorbidities

Comorbid conditions expected to be associated with supplemental oxygen use (tobacco use, heart failure, and pulmonary hypertension [PH]) were included. To control for underlying health status, we constructed a count of 27 other comorbid conditions based on the validated set of algorithms (1999-2021) available from the Chronic Conditions Data Warehouse.22 See e-Table 1 for definitions and prevalence rates of comorbidities.

Statistical Analyses

The primary outcome was total CMS payments for supplemental oxygen. The χ2 test at α = .05 was used to compare median payment amounts. The total monthly payment was calculated for each beneficiary using the total annual spending by both Medicaid and Medicare divided by the number of months of dual eligibility that year. Annualized payments were adjusted for month of patient death, if applicable, and estimated by multiplying the total monthly payment by 12. Annual payments were top/bottom coded at the 95th percentile to remove outliers. Subcategories with < 10 observations are redacted.

A multilevel mixed-effects generalized linear model (MEGLM) with likelihood ratio testing was performed to identify variables associated with annual oxygen payments among those with paid claims using the number of months of dual eligibility per year as an offset term. Because nearly all claims included stationary oxygen concentrators, we restricted the analysis to only these patients and defined the dependent variable as the additional payment above and beyond that for a stationary concentrator. The model was adjusted for age, sex, race, residence in a nonmetropolitan area or CBA, long-term services and support use, diagnoses that may impact oxygen use (tobacco use, heart failure, and PH), count of other comorbidities, whether the claim was coded for high flow, and primary diagnosis (ILD vs COPD). Because of small sample sizes, we combined stationary and portable services for gas and liquid oxygen (eg, gas stationary or portable, liquid stationary or portable).

Participants with dual ILD and COPD diagnoses were included in the ILD cohort for all analyses. A sensitivity analysis was performed with these individuals included in the COPD cohort. A supplemental analysis was also performed calculating oxygen costs based on months of paid oxygen claims per year. All analyses were performed using StataSE version 18.

This study was determined to be exempt from human subjects research by the institutional review board at the University of Pittsburgh because data are from publicly available files recorded such that individuals cannot be identified.

Results

A total of 2,297 unique individuals with ILD and 72,412 individuals with COPD were included. Baseline characteristics are reported using the first year each participant appeared in the data (Table 1). Most participants were female, non-Hispanic White, and residents in a CBA and/or nonrural area. Most participants in both cohorts had ≥ 4 comorbidities. In the first year of study inclusion, a greater proportion of participants with ILD compared with COPD had claims paid for any oxygen equipment (35% vs 18%). Of these, a greater proportion of ILD claims were for high flow oxygen (4.7% vs 2.5% for COPD).

The 2,297 participants with ILD accounted for 4,344 person-years during the study period (ie, unique beneficiary who met the inclusion criteria could appear in > 1 calendar year); 40% of person-years (n = 1,752) had ≥ 1 claim for oxygen services. The 72,412 participants with COPD accounted for 144,816 person-years; 22% (n = 31,864) had ≥ 1 claim for oxygen services. Participants with ILD had a greater proportion of oxygen claims across all 5 study years (e-Table 2). Of all oxygen claims, a greater proportion for ILD was coded for high flow (4.2% vs 2.2% for COPD). Despite having a greater proportion of claims, ILD represented only 5.2% of all oxygen claims and 9.7% of all high flow claims. Because CMS policy only mandated the use of some high flow coding in 2018, we conducted a post hoc analysis on high flow coding before and after 2018 and did not find a significant difference.

The total amount of oxygen payments during the study period was $22,216,939; of this, 77% ($16,994,808) were paid by Medicare. Of the total combined Medicare and Medicaid payment amount, 5% ($1,107,372) was for ILD and 95% ($21,109,567) was for COPD. The median monthly payment for all oxygen claims was $64.63 (interquartile range, $35.04-$94.56) for ILD and $65.84 (interquartile range, $33.63-$96.70) for COPD (P = .99). Median monthly claim payments by calendar year ranged from $59.57 to $74.11 for ILD and $56.57 to $78.39 for COPD (Fig 1).

Table 5 shows the results of the MEGLM model. Of participants with payments for stationary concentrators, variables significantly associated with higher annual claim payments (P < .05) included living in a nonmetropolitan location, receiving gas or liquid oxygen or receiving portable concentrators in addition to a stationary concentrator, and receiving high flow equipment. Increasing age, Hispanic race, residing in a CBA, current or prior tobacco use, and increasing number of comorbidities were associated with lower annual claim payments. In post hoc analysis, increasing age and number of comorbidities were inversely related to payments for additional portable oxygen equipment. Post hoc analysis also found higher payments in non-CBA nonmetropolitan areas compared with CBAs in nonmetropolitan areas. An ILD diagnosis was not significantly associated with annual claim payments. The annual payment for oxygen varied based on the combination of equipment patients received, with the highest payments made for the combination of stationary and portable oxygen concentrators (Table 6).

In a sensitivity analysis where participants with dual ILD and COPD diagnoses were included in the COPD cohort, baseline characteristics and findings from the final MEGLM model were similar; however, there was no longer a significant difference in the proportion of participants with oxygen claims at baseline (P = .75) (e-Table 3). Discussion

The results of our study demonstrate that among Pennsylvania residents dually eligible to receive Medicare and Medicaid, oxygen payments were similar for individuals with ILD and COPD. A greater proportion with ILD had paid oxygen claims including claims for high flow delivery; however, participants with COPD represented most (95%) of all paid claims. The utilization of equipment other than concentrators was low in both groups, even among those receiving high flow rates. The addition of a portable concentrator to a stationary concentrator was associated with the highest payments.

ILD and COPD are associated with high health care costs and resource utilization.5,7, 8, 9, 10, 11,23 DME use, including supplemental oxygen, is also associated with increased costs.5,10,11,24 Limited studies have analyzed oxygen costs and utilization in the United States. COPD is a much more common diagnosis than ILD and represents significantly more oxygen users. An estimated 705,310 Pennsylvanians were diagnosed with COPD in 2021 at a prevalence rate of 6.8% with national COPD prevalence estimated at approximately 6%.25,26 Less is known about prevalence of ILDs in the United States; however, in 2019, it was estimated at 179.9 (male individuals) to 218.9 (female individuals) per 100,000.27 The prevalence of IPF ranges from 0.46 to 11.2 per 10,00028,29 and increases with age. A recent study of the epidemiology of IPF in Pennsylvania estimated an annual incidence of 8.42 per 100,000.30

Oxygen utilization is common in both diseases.3, 4, 5, 6 Prior studies on ambulatory oxygen assessments found that patients with ILD have greater desaturation on 6-minute walk tests31,32 and have a greater proportion who need oxygen to correct exertional desaturation, and at higher flow rates, than COPD.33 Our findings add to the existing literature by demonstrating a higher proportion of individuals with ILD have oxygen claims paid by CMS. Patients with ILD may therefore be disproportionately affected by oxygen access issues and policy changes. Our study provides important data on oxygen payments and utilization within current CMS policies.

We found that payments for specific types of oxygen equipment did not vary by diagnosis. Most paid oxygen claims (≥ 94%) included stationary concentrators, which were associated with the highest median monthly payment. We found low utilization rates for other equipment including liquid oxygen and a decrease in nearly all equipment utilization over the study period, which align with a prior CMS DME claims study.19 Liquid oxygen only accounted for 2.9% of all high flow portable claims despite guidelines recommending this therapy for ambulatory patients requiring > 3 L/min.1 Changes in equipment utilization may have been due to increased enrollment in Medicare Advantage plans, which led to a decrease in the number of dually eligible patients over our study period. Other possibilities include changes in oxygen assessments, prescribing practices, or device utilization over time. Concerns have also been raised that the CBP may have led to a decrease in DME suppliers and impaired access to some oxygen equipment.16, 17, 18,34 Our study was not designed to assess a causal relationship between oxygen payments and the CBP, nor between oxygen payments, equipment utilization, or other testing or prescribing practices.

Our model found that living in a CBA was associated with lower oxygen payments, consistent with reports by CMS, other government agencies, and prior claims reviews.15,35, 36, 37 Advanced age and increased number of comorbidities were also associated with reduced oxygen payments, specifically portable equipment. These factors are commonly associated with increased health care costs and resource utilization.38,39 Whether patients who are older and sicker were less mobile or less likely to undergo ambulatory oxygen assessments is an important avenue for further research. Tobacco use was associated with decreased oxygen payments, possibly due to concerns about fire risks with patients with active tobacco use. Neither heart failure nor PH were associated with annual payments. Hispanic race/ethnicity was also associated with lower payments; however, this represented < 5% of all patients. It is possible that our study was underpowered to detect the true effect of heart failure, PH, and race/ethnicity.

Increased payments were associated with living in a nonmetropolitan area; > 98% of nonmetropolitan claims were excluded from a CBA. Starting in 2016, CBP pricing was applied to non-CBP areas, allowing for adjustments to costs for DME delivery in these areas.40 Our post hoc analysis found payments were higher in non-CBA nonmetropolitan areas. Whether this results in a financial disincentive to provide oxygen therapy to people living in these areas is unknown. We also found that, in multivariate analysis, high flow equipment was associated with increased annual payments, consistent with CMS reimbursement policy.12 This difference was not observed in univariate analysis of low vs high flow claims by equipment type and diagnosis. Our study may have been underpowered to detect true differences given the low number of high flow claims.

Our study has several strengths. To our knowledge, we are the first to quantify oxygen spending in ILD in the United States and compare this with COPD, and the first to quantify oxygen spending in a dually eligible population. We were able to maximize our sample size by using claims data across 5 years. By focusing the study on months of dual eligibility, we were able to estimate actual CMS expenses for oxygen equipment for a vulnerable and policy-relevant population.

Our study also has several limitations. Our results may not be generalizable to patients receiving other Medicare or Medicaid services, those with substantial out-of-pocket expenses, or those who have oxygen services paid through private insurance. Results may not be generalizable to patients living in other states; however, Pennsylvania is a large state with both metropolitan and rural areas and is demographically similar to the US average. Nevertheless, data from our center suggest that regional differences in air quality may adversely impact the prognosis of pulmonary fibrosis41; therefore, oxygen utilization may have regional variation due to these exacerbating factors. Due to increased enrollment in Medicare Advantage plans over the study period, we had a decreasing number of dually eligible individuals over time. Because we limited our study to DME claims only, we were unable to calculate how oxygen payments compare with total CMS spending. We were unable to verify the accuracy of ICD codes used to identify ILD and COPD diagnoses and other comorbidities; however, our approach is similar to other claims analyses and included previously validated coding algorithms. We were unable to determine what effect, if any, the COVID-19 pandemic had on oxygen payments during the final year of our study. Future studies should examine the cost of oxygen in other populations including privately insured individuals and in national claims databases, and examine the effect the COVID-19 pandemic may have had on oxygen access and costs.

Although our study identified payments made for oxygen claims, we were unable to determine whether oxygen prescriptions were appropriate or whether beneficiaries used these oxygen services. We also did not focus our study on duration of oxygen use (eg, short-term vs long-term oxygen therapy); however, CMS payment policies do not differentiate between the 2.

Interpretation

Our results show that most supplemental oxygen claims for Pennsylvania residents dually eligible for Medicaid and Medicare fee-for-service benefits with diagnoses of ILD or COPD were for stationary oxygen concentrators. Individuals with ILD had a greater proportion of paid oxygen claims but represented only a small portion of all oxygen claims. Among users of oxygen concentrators, the addition of a stationary concentrator was associated with the highest cost. Paid claims for liquid oxygen were low in both diseases.
