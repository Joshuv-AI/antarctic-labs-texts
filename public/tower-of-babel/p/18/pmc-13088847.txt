Persian translation, cross-cultural adaptation, reliability, and validity of the updated Oslo sports trauma research centre questionnaire on health problems
Authors: Mohammad Alimoradi, Serkan Kabak, Tayfun Kara, Yasar Barut, Hassan Daneshmandi, Mohammad Alghosi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Oslo Sports Trauma Research Centre Questionnaire on Health Problems (OSTRC-H2) is a validated tool in its original English version for monitoring the full spectrum of health issues in athletes, but no Persian version exists. This study aimed to translate, culturally adapt, and evaluate the reliability and measurement properties, including internal consistency, test-retest reliability, and construct validity, of the Persian version of the updated OSTRC-H2 questionnaire for use with Persian-speaking elite athletes.MethodsThis methodological study employed a cross-sectional and longitudinal design. The OSTRC-H2 was translated into Persian following international guidelines. A total of 207 elite athletes from various sports completed the questionnaire at baseline and again after 72 h for test-retest reliability. To evaluate feasibility and describe the prevalence of health problems in this cohort, defined as any reported issue according to the OSTRC-H2 case definition, athletes also completed the questionnaire weekly for 12 weeks. Measurement properties evaluated included internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha), test-retest reliability (intraclass correlation coefficient, ICC(2,1)), construct validity (Spearman correlations with pain, days lost, and training load), responsiveness (standardized response mean [SRM], effect size [ES]), and floor/ceiling effects.ResultsThe response rate was 98% (2435/2484 weekly questionnaires). The mean weekly prevalence of any health problems was 25.3% (95% CI: 23.6–27.0). Of the 628 health problems reported, acute injuries were most common (59.5%), followed by overuse injuries (28.3%) and illnesses (12.1%). Test-retest reliability was strong (ICC range: 0.82–1.00), and internal consistency was high (Cronbach’s alpha range: 0.81–1.00). The severity score showed strong positive correlations with pain intensity (rₛ range: 0.703–0.812) and days lost (rₛ range: 0.752–0.881), and moderate negative correlations with weekly training load. The questionnaire demonstrated large responsiveness to changes in health status (SRM range: 1.58–1.92; ES range: 1.83–2.21). No significant floor or ceiling effects were detected.ConclusionsThe Persian OSTRC-H2 is a feasible, reliable, valid, and responsive instrument for comprehensive health problem surveillance in Persian-speaking elite athletes. It is recommended for use in sports surveillance and monitoring programs to track the burden of injury and illness in this population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13102-026-01647-3.Keywords: OSTRC-H2, Athletic injuries, Health problem monitoring, Epidemiology, Training load

Introduction

Injury and illness prevention is a cornerstone of sports medicine, directly influencing athletes’ health, performance, and achievements [1–3]. To create effective injury prevention programs, the initial step involves assessing the scope of the issue. For this purpose, injury surveillance systems play a crucial role in monitoring and recording physical symptoms and injuries [2, 4, 5]. Numerous global sports organizations have implemented injury surveillance programs using their own tracking systems. Examples include the National Football League [6], the National Collegiate Athletic Association [7], Fédération Internationale de Football Association [8], the Union of European Football Associations Champions League Study [9], the International Ski Federation [10], and others [11]. However, these monitoring tools often lacked standardized methodologies, particularly regarding data collection, injury definitions, and reporting practices. Additionally, applying these methods proved challenging for individual athletes or those outside structured team environments. As a result, such systems frequently missed certain health issues, including overuse injuries [12]. In 2013 and 2014, experts from the Oslo Sports Trauma Research Center (OSTRC) introduced two innovative injury surveillance methods: the OSTRC Overuse Injury Questionnaire (OSTRC-O) and the more comprehensive OSTRC Questionnaire on Health Problems (OSTRC-H) [13, 14], which was later updated in 2020 as OSTRC-H2. The OSTRC-H2 questionnaire consists of four core questions that help determine a severity score. Additionally, it gathers information on injury mechanisms, affected activities, and whether the health issue is a recurrence, exacerbation, or a new problem [15]. Furthermore, the OSTRC-H and OSTRC-H2 shifted away from the conventional time-loss definition, which only accounts for health issues that force athletes to miss training or competition, to a more inclusive definition of health problems. This broader approach encompasses any physical issue that hinders an athlete’s normal training or performance, even if it doesn’t result in missed sessions. As a result, the OSTRC-H2 captures a wider range of overuse injuries that might otherwise go unnoticed. The questionnaire tracks not only time lost but also persistent health problems, with its severity score reflecting changes in performance, intensity, or pain levels. While these tools show promise, further validation across diverse populations and settings is still needed to confirm their generalizability and measurement properties [15]. The OSTRC-O questionnaire has been translated and validated for use across multiple languages and athletic populations, demonstrating its cross-cultural applicability. Originally developed for Norwegian athletes [13], the instrument has since been adapted for German Paralympians [16], Spanish youth and adult competitors [17, 18], as well as athletes from Denmark, Sweden, Brazil, Japan, and Thailand [19–23]. However, the OSTRC-H2 has been translated and validated only in Japanese, Spanish, Slovenian and French [4, 12, 17, 23]. Despite its widespread application, no validated version of the OSTRC-H2 currently exists for Persian-speaking athletes. Given the growing engagement of Iranian athletes in professional and recreational sports, a culturally adapted and psychometrically validated tool is essential for the accurate monitoring of health problems in this group. Linguistic nuances and cultural perceptions of injury and pain can significantly influence how athletes report their health status. Therefore, proper translation and validation are critical to ensure conceptual equivalence and measurement accuracy across different cultures. This study aimed to translate, culturally adapt, and evaluate the reliability and measurement properties, including internal consistency, test-retest reliability, and construct validity, of the Persian version of the updated OSTRC-H2 questionnaire.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study employed a cross-sectional and longitudinal design to translate, culturally adapt, and evaluate the measurement properties of the Persian OSTRC-H2. The study was conducted from August to December 2025 and, for the longitudinal monitoring component, adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines [24]. This study was assessed and approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the University of Guilan (IR.GUILAN.REC.1404.090) on August 18, 2025. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study consisted of 2 phases: First, the questionnaire was translated and culturally adapted from English to Persian and pretested on a sample of 10 elite athletes to assess face validity and comprehensibility. Second, to evaluate the questionnaire’s validity and reliability, elite athletes completed the Persian OSTRC-H2 at baseline and again 72 h later. Furthermore, to assess feasibility and describe the prevalence of health problems over the follow-up period, all participants were asked to complete the OSTRC-H2 questionnaire weekly for three months. We contacted the sports federation and team coaches to invite their participation in the study. Interested teams received detailed information about the study objectives, and athletes and staff who agreed to take part provided written consent. A total of 230 elite athletes from 15 sports clubs met the inclusion criteria, which required participants to be at least 18 years old and proficient in the Persian language. For the initial test-retest reliability assessment, athletes who had an injury that completely restricted sports participation at the time of baseline testing were excluded, as they would be unable to provide valid responses to questionnaire items concerning training modification and performance. However, these athletes were eligible to join the subsequent 12-week longitudinal monitoring phase once they resumed training.

OSTRC-H2 questionnaire

The OSTRC-H2 includes four key items designed to achieve two main objectives: (1) to identify the presence of physical or health issues, and (2) to assess the severity of these issues using a scale from 0 to 100, where 0 indicates no impact, and 100 indicates the greatest possible impact on sports participation. Item 1 offers four response options, which are scored as 0, 8, 17, and 100. Items 2, 3, and 4 also have four response options, scored as 0, 8, 17, and 25. Athletes who selected the highest response option in item 1 (“Could not participate due to a health problem”) were not required to answer items 2, 3, and 4. An athlete was considered to have a substantial health problem if they chose either the third or fourth option in item 2 or 3, or the fourth option in item 1 [4].

Procedures

Persian translation, cultural adaptation, and pretest of the OSTRC-H2

Following authorization from the original authors of the OSTRC-H2 questionnaire [14], two native Persian speakers fluent in English independently translated the questionnaire from English into Persian. The translators then met to reconcile any discrepancies and develop a consensus version of the Persian translation. This version was subsequently back-translated into English by 10 independent translators who were unfamiliar with the original version and unaware of the questionnaire’s underlying concepts. A committee of experts, including the forward and back translators, as well as a methodologist, reviewed and compared the translations against the original version to ensure semantic and conceptual equivalence. The final Persian version was established through consensus. The prefinal version was then pilot-tested on a sample of 10 elite athletes to assess face validity and cultural relevance. Athletes were asked to provide feedback on the clarity of each item and to describe, in their own words, their understanding of each question. These responses were analyzed to ensure consistency with the intended meaning and to confirm that the items were interpreted as intended in the target context.

Investigation of the measurement properties of the OSTRC-H2

To evaluate the psychometric properties of the Persian version of the OSTRC-H2 questionnaire, we assessed its internal consistency, test-retest reliability, construct validity, and responsiveness. Internal consistency was examined using Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the four core items of the OSTRC-H2. A Cronbach’s alpha value of ≥ 0.70 was considered acceptable, indicating satisfactory inter-item correlation [25]. Test-retest reliability was assessed by administering the OSTRC-H2 twice to the same athletes, with a 72-hour interval between administrations, during which participants were instructed not to alter their training routines. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was used to determine the degree of agreement between the two scores. A two-way random-effects model for absolute agreement (ICC(2,1)) was selected, as it is appropriate for assessing test-retest reliability when both subjects and time points are considered random effects from a larger population. An ICC value ≥ 0.70 was considered indicative of acceptable reliability [4]. The standard error of measurement (SEM) and minimal detectable change (MDC) were calculated according to Eq. (1) and Eq. (2), respectively. The MDC is the smallest amount of change in the scores that is not due to error in measurements [26].

Construct validity was assessed by examining the association between OSTRC-H2 severity scores and related constructs using the Spearman rank correlation coefficient (rₛ) for continuous variables. A priori hypotheses predicted that the severity score would have strong positive correlations (rₛ > 0.6) with concurrent pain intensity and the number of days of training lost, and moderate negative correlations (rₛ between − 0.3 and − 0.5) with total weekly training load. The expectation of moderate, rather than strong, negative correlations reflects that training load is multifactorial; it is influenced not only by health status but also by training plans, competition schedules, and coach decisions, making a near-perfect inverse relationship unlikely. Responsiveness, the ability of the questionnaire to detect meaningful change over time, was assessed by analyzing longitudinal changes in severity scores over the 12-week follow-up period. A change in the total severity score exceeding the MDC at the 95% confidence level (MDC95%) was defined as a true change beyond measurement error. We hypothesized that the severity score would show high responsiveness: scores would increase by at least the MDC95% value when athletes reported a new or significantly worsening health problem and decrease by a similar amount when they reported recovery. This expected change was validated against an external anchor: the athlete’s weekly self-reported change in health status (categorized as new problem, worsening, unchanged, improving, or resolved). The standardized response mean (SRM) and effect size (ES) were calculated for these groups, with values > 0.80 considered indicative of large responsiveness [27]. In addition, floor and ceiling effects were evaluated by calculating the proportion of respondents who scored the lowest (0) or highest (100) possible total score. If more than 15% of participants achieved the lowest or highest score, a floor or ceiling effect was considered present [28].

Other measures

In addition to the OSTRC-H2 responses, demographic and training-related data were collected, including athletes’ age, height, mass, and years of sports participation (used to define training experience). Weekly training load was recorded separately for sport-specific sessions and strength and conditioning activities. Competition load, measured in minutes of game time, was converted into hours and combined with the training load to calculate a single variable referred to as total load.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were computed to summarize the participants’ characteristics. To confirm that the data satisfied the normality assumption for parametric tests used in specific analyses, the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was conducted. A significance level of < 0.05 was predetermined for statistical analysis. All data were analyzed using SPSS (Version 27.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Participant characteristics

Out of 230 eligible athletes, 207 (90%) completed the OSTRC-H2 and visual analogue scale (VAS) at baseline, including 127 males (61.4%) and 80 females (38.6%). The mean age was 27.7 years, with a range of 18 to 36 years. The overall training time for elite athletes was 8.5 h per week. The majority of participants (n = 59, 28.5%) were soccer players. Persian translation and cultural adaptation of the OSTRC-H2

The OSTRC-H2 was translated into Persian without encountering significant challenges. The prefinal version underwent pretesting on a sample of 10 elite Persian-speaking athletes from Iran. Since none of the athletes reported any difficulties in comprehending the questionnaire, and it was subsequently validated by the expert committee, the pre-final version was finalized as the definitive version (Supplementary File 1).

Feasibility of OSTRC-H2’s measurement properties

All athletes (n = 207) completed both the test-retest phase. They completed the questionnaires twice over a 72-hour period. During the 12-week period, 2484 questionnaires were distributed, with 2435 returned, resulting in a high response rate of 98%. Athletes reported 628 total health incidents, comprising 374 acute injuries (59.5%), 178 overuse injuries (28.3%), and 76 illnesses (12.1%) (Fig. 1). Among these, 39% were first-time occurrences, and 61% were recurrences. The mean weekly prevalence of health problems was 25.3% (95% CI, 23.6–27.0). These issues resulted in a total of 1725 lost training days, averaging 3.2 ± 2.1 days per affected athlete. Acute injuries accounted for 1250 lost days, overuse injuries for 299 days, and illnesses for 176 days. The median time to complete a questionnaire was 63 s (range: 26–174 s). In terms of weekly severity, measured on a scale of 0 to 100, illnesses were the most severe, with a mean weekly severity score of 78.2 ± 16.1 per reported incident. These illnesses were reported in 10 of the 12 weeks, peaking in weeks 5, 8, and 12. Acute injuries showed consistent weekly reporting with a mean weekly severity score of 68.5 ± 30.8. Overuse injuries had the lowest mean weekly severity score at 46.8 ± 27.9, with a notable increase observed in week 12.

Internal consistency and test-retest reliability

The test-retest reliability of the questionnaire, analyzed using a two-way random-effects ICC model (ICC(2,1)), was strong, with values ranging from substantial to almost perfect agreement (0.82 to 1.00). Question 1 (participation) demonstrated near-perfect reliability (ICC = 1.00, 95% CI: 0.99–1.00). Internal consistency, measured by Cronbach’s alpha, was also high across all items, ranging from good (α = 0.81 for symptoms) to excellent (α = 1.00 for participation and the severity score). Absolute variability was minimal, with coefficients of variation (CV) below 3.2% for all items, indicating highly stable repeated measurements (Table 2).

Construct validity (pain, severity score, days lost, and total load)

Strong positive correlations were observed between the weekly severity score and concurrent pain ratings, measured via VAS, for acute injuries (rₛ = 0.812), overuse injuries (rₛ = 0.791), and illnesses (rₛ = 0.703) (P < 0.001 for all). As expected, severity scores demonstrated very strong positive correlations with days lost for all health problem types: acute injuries (rₛ = 0.768), overuse injuries (rₛ = 0.752), and illnesses (rₛ = 0.881) (P < 0.001). Conversely, moderate negative correlations were found between total weekly training load and both severity scores and the number of days lost. For acute injuries, total load correlated negatively with severity score (rₛ = −0.452) and the number of days lost (rₛ = −0.501). Similar relationships were observed for overuse injuries (severity: rₛ = −0.398; days lost: rₛ = −0.467). The strongest negative associations were seen for illnesses, with total load showing a moderate correlation with severity (rₛ = −0.521) and a strong correlation with days lost (rₛ = −0.803) (P < 0.001 for all). These findings support the construct validity of the severity score, demonstrating its logical convergence with pain and its expected inverse relationship with training exposure.

Responsiveness

The responsiveness of the Persian OSTRC-H2 was evaluated by examining longitudinal changes in severity scores over the 12-week follow-up period. Among athletes who transitioned from a healthy state to reporting a new health problem (n = 124, based on 25.3% weekly prevalence over 12 weeks), the mean severity score increased from 11.9 ± 30.9 at baseline to 64.5 ± 28.4 at the time of reporting, representing a mean change of 52.6 (95% CI: 47.3–57.9). This change substantially exceeded the MDC95% value of 3.01, with 96.8% of athletes showing true change beyond measurement error. The SRM was 1.84 (95% CI: 1.62–2.06), and the ES was 2.13, indicating large responsiveness. Conversely, among athletes who reported recovery from a health problem (n = 98), the mean severity score decreased from 58.7 ± 29.5 to 10.2 ± 28.6, with a mean change of −48.5 (95% CI: −54.2 to −42.8). Of these athletes, 94.9% demonstrated changes exceeding the MDC95%. The SRM was − 1.71 (95% CI: −1.95 to −1.47), and the ES was − 1.98, also demonstrating large responsiveness. When analyzed by health problem type, responsiveness remained large for acute injuries (SRM = 1.92, ES = 2.21), overuse injuries (SRM = 1.58, ES = 1.83), and illnesses (SRM = 1.76, ES = 2.04). These findings confirm that the Persian OSTRC-H2 is highly sensitive to meaningful changes in health status over time (Table 3).

Floor and ceiling effects

An analysis of floor and ceiling effects was conducted using all weekly severity scores (on a 0–100 scale) reported during the 12-week follow-up. The observed scores for all health problems ranged from 8 to 92. Across the entire dataset, the minimum score (0) was not recorded, and the maximum score (100) was reported in only 0.9% of all weekly entries. For each of the 12 weekly data collection points, the proportion of responses at the ceiling (score = 100) never exceeded the 5% threshold for any health problem category (acute injury, overuse injury, or illness). These findings confirm the absence of significant floor or ceiling effects, indicating that the Persian OSTRC-H2 is suitable for measuring the full spectrum of health problem severity in this athletic population.

Discussion

The purpose of this study was to translate, culturally adapt, and evaluate the reliability and validity of the Persian version of the updated OSTRC-H2 questionnaire in elite Iranian athletes. The results show that the Persian OSTRC-H2 is a practical and reliable tool that demonstrates acceptable construct validity for health monitoring. The translation process was straightforward, and the questionnaire was well accepted, as indicated by a very high weekly response rate of 98% and a short median completion time. Psychometric tests indicated strong test-retest reliability and internal consistency. Additionally, the construct validity of the severity score was supported through its logical, strong positive correlations with concurrent pain reports and days of training lost, as well as its expected negative correlations with weekly training load.

These findings support and enhance the growing international evidence on the OSTRC-H2. The excellent reliability metrics (ICC(2,1) = 0.82–1.00) are consistent with those reported in the Slovenian version, which also found near-perfect reliability for core items and the overall severity score [12]. Moreover, the pattern of construct validity is strongly backed by Drole et al. [12], who similarly reported strong positive correlations between the severity score and days lost due to acute injuries, overuse injuries, and illnesses (rₛ = 0.754 to 0.894), confirming that the severity score accurately reflects the impact of health problems. The successful cultural adaptation process, resulting in a final version that was easily understood by athletes, aligns with the experience described in the Malay translation study [29], where cognitive interviews ensured conceptual equivalence and face validity.

However, some aspects of our findings differ from other validation studies, which can likely be attributed to methodological and sample differences. For example, the mean weekly prevalence of health problems in our study (25.3%) was higher than the 19.6% reported in the French validation study [4]. This difference may be due to the broader range of sports included in our study and the potentially different training cultures and competition schedules, which can affect injury and illness rates. Another difference involves the construct validity analysis. While our study showed strong agreement between the OSTRC-H2 severity score and a VAS for pain, Beaudart et al. [4] reported low agreement between the OSTRC-H2 and the Société Française de Médecine du Sport overtraining questionnaire. This is not necessarily contradictory, but it highlights the specificity of the constructs measured. The OSTRC-H2 aims to assess the functional impact of any health problem, whereas the Société Française de Médecine du Sport may focus on a more specific syndrome, such as overtraining. The absence of a validated Persian-language instrument measuring an identical construct limited our options for convergent validation, a challenge also noted in the Malay adaptation [29].

The findings emphasize the robustness of the OSTRC-H2’s core methodology across different languages and cultures. The high response rate and quick completion time demonstrate its practicality for routine athlete monitoring, a crucial step in early intervention and load management. The strong correlation between the severity score and external metrics, such as pain and training load loss, offers empirical support for using the score as a meaningful indicator of health issues, beyond merely recording the presence of injury. This is especially useful for identifying overuse injuries, which often do not result in full-time loss but still impact performance and require management.

Several limitations of this study should be recognized. First, although the sample was diverse across sports, it consisted solely of elite adult athletes. The validity and reliability of the Persian OSTRC-H2 for adolescent, amateur, or female-only athletic populations still need to be confirmed. Second, while we report internal consistency following conventions in validation studies, this metric may be of limited relevance for the OSTRC-H2, since its four items are intended to capture different aspects of health problems (participation, modification, performance, symptoms) rather than a unidimensional construct. Third, evaluating criterion-related validity was not feasible due to the lack of a gold-standard method for measuring health problem severity in sports settings, a common limitation in questionnaire validation noted by others [29]. Fourth, the short test-retest interval (72 h) was chosen to minimize recall bias and clinical changes; however, it may have led to inflated reliability estimates. Fifth, while the questionnaire was culturally adapted for use in Iran, the generalizability of these specific psychometric properties to Persian-speaking athletes in other countries (e.g., Afghanistan, Tajikistan) has not been established. Differences in sporting cultures, infrastructure, and health systems may influence how athletes interpret and respond to the questionnaire, and future research should explore its applicability in these contexts. Finally, the observational nature of the study means that, although associations are evident, causal relationships between training load and severity scores cannot be established.

Despite these limitations, the study has meaningful implications. The availability of a validated Persian OSTRC-H2 offers clinicians, coaches, and researchers in Iran and other Persian-speaking regions a standardized tool to prospectively track athlete health. This can support epidemiological research, evaluate prevention strategies, and enable personalized athlete care. The tool allows a shift from a solely time-loss surveillance model to one that captures the full extent of health issues. Future research should focus on confirming the Persian OSTRC-H2’s responsiveness to clinically significant changes over time. Investigating its use in broader groups, including younger athletes and various sports, is also necessary. Additionally, longitudinal studies using this translated instrument could examine sport-specific risk factors and the predictive validity of the severity score and its components for future injuries or illnesses.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study successfully translated, cross-culturally adapted, and psychometrically evaluated the OSTRC-H2 questionnaire for use with Persian-speaking elite athletes. The Persian version demonstrated high feasibility, strong reliability, and acceptable construct validity. It is therefore a suitable and recommended tool for monitoring the full range of health problems in this population, enhancing the global toolkit for athlete health surveillance.
