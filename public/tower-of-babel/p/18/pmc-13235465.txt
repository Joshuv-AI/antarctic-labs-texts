Small bone replacement leads to greater gait asymmetry, cartilage damage, and osteophyte formation than ligament injury in a porcine wrist model
Authors: Quianna M. Vaughan, Amy M. Morton, Douglas C. Moore, Janine Molino, Edward Akelman, Joseph J. Crisco
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study evaluated the Yucatan minipig (YMP) as a translational large-animal model of wrist osteoarthritis (OA) following ligament injury (LI) or small bone replacement (SBR) in a 12-week in vivo study.MethodEight YMPs were assigned to LI or SBR groups (n = 4/group). Gait data were collected preoperatively and at 3, 6, 9, and 12 weeks postoperatively. Ratios of surgical to contralateral limbs were calculated for maximum force, impulse, stance time, and stride velocity and compared within and between groups. At study completion, bilateral forelimbs were analyzed to quantify osteophyte volume, joint space, and cartilage health using OARSI histological scoring. Generalized estimating equations were used to evaluate differences in the outcome metrics between the LI and SBR groups.ResultsThe SBR group demonstrated significant gait asymmetry at week 3, with reduced maximum force, impulse, and stance time relative to preoperative values (p < 0.05), followed by partial recovery. Compared to the LI group whose symmetry was consistent over time, the SBR group exhibited greater gait asymmetry (p < 0.05) at all postoperative timepoints, while stride velocity remained unchanged. Osteophyte volume was greater in SBR than LI for seven of twelve bones (p < 0.05), whereas joint space did not differ among limbs or groups. Histological analysis revealed significantly higher total OARSI scores in SBR operated limbs compared to contralateral limbs, with greater cartilage degeneration than LI (p < 0.05).ConclusionThese findings characterize changes in gait and joint health that demonstrate the feasibility of the YMP as a translational preclinical model of the human wrist for assessing OA progression and SBR.

Introduction

The human wrist is a complex joint comprised of multiple bones and ligaments that function collectively to facilitate complex motions while providing stability. The scapholunate interosseous ligament (SLIL) serves as a key primary stabilizer between the scaphoid and lunate bones [1]. Injury to the SLIL is common. SLIL injury combined with disruption of the dorsal intercarpal ligament (DIC) leads to wrist instability and altered biomechanics [2], which can progress to degenerative joint disease, including osteoarthritis (OA). Osteophyte formation and joint space narrowing are reliable indicators of OA severity [3].

Surgical options for advanced wrist OA include wrist fusion (arthrodesis), proximal row carpectomy, and total wrist replacement (arthroplasty). However, for patients with viable articular cartilage on an opposing joint surface, replacing an individual carpal bone offers a potential motion-preserving alternative to fusion or arthroplasty with minimal hardware implantation and bone resection [4,5]. Carpal bone replacements reduce pain and improve function relative to preoperative results [[6], [7], [8]], though long-term follow-up has revealed mild progression of OA, development of osteolysis, and radiographic changes [9,10].

Animal models play a critical role in advancing our understanding of OA severity and in the development of effective treatment options. Animal models have been widely used to study OA progression in large joints, particularly the knee, using chemical, mechanical, or surgical induction methods [11,12]. Small animals such as mice, rats, guinea pigs, and rabbits are commonly used for proof-of-concept testing due to their rapid disease progression, lower cost, and ease of housing [13,14]. In contrast, larger animals such as sheep and pigs are often preferred for their anatomical, biomechanical, and physiological similarities to humans [11,14,15]. Despite the extensive use of these models to study OA in large joints, there is currently no established animal model for studying OA in smaller, more complex joints, such as those in the human wrist, and few studies have focused on OA progression or treatment optimization in the carpus.

Recently, the Yucatan minipig (YMP) has been proposed as an animal model for the human wrist [[16], [17], [18]], due to similarities in bone composition [15] and ligamentous anatomy [18], making it a potential model for preclinical testing of implants and treatments for wrist OA. The YMP distal forelimb consists of a radius, ulna, eight carpal bones, and four metacarpals, all connected by ligaments, with an overall structure homologous to the human forelimb. The YMP carpus has been used to model scaphoid nonunion [16], biologic joint replacement for trapeziometacarpal OA [17], and to assess YMP carpus biomechanics after radial intermediate ligament (RIL) and DIC transection [18]. Characterizing the skeletal response of the YMP to ligament injury and small bone replacement is important to support the use of the YMP carpus as a preclinical model of the human wrist, yet this has not been previously reported.

The purpose of this study was to evaluate the Yucatan minipig (YMP) as a preclinical animal model for assessing OA severity and progression in the carpus following either ligament injury (LI) or small bone replacement (SBR). OA progression was monitored over a 12-week in vivo period with serial gait assessments and quantified structurally at study completion using osteophyte volume and joint space measurements, with microscopic histological evaluation performed to characterize OA severity. Based on previous clinical reports of OA development following ligament injury [[19], [20], [21]], we hypothesized that the ligament injury group would exhibit greater OA severity than the group treated with a small bone replacement, as evidenced by increased gait asymmetry, greater osteophyte volume, reduced joint space, and more severe histological degeneration of cartilage.

Methods

Animal acquisition

Following IACUC review and approval, eight YMPs were acquired from accredited laboratory animal suppliers (Sinclair Bio-Resources, Windham, ME and Premier BioSource, Ramona, CA). The cohort included four females and four males, ranging in age from 24.4 to 29.8 months (mean: 25.7 ± 1.8 months) and weight from 56.9 to 101.2 kg (mean: 84.2 ± 13.6 kg). The animals were assigned to undergo LI alone (2 M/2F) or SBR (2 M/2F).

Surgical procedure

Both the LI and SBR surgeries were performed on the right forelimb to ensure procedural consistency across animals, as the YMP exhibits equal forelimb loading under normal conditions [22,23] with no evidence of inherent side dominance that would bias the surgical side. Exposure was via a ∼3 cm capsular incision over the medial aspect of the dorsal carpus, centered on the proximal row. A second incision (∼5 cm) was made on the medial aspect of the volar carpus to access the Deep Radial Carpal Flexor Tendon, which was transected at the musculotendinous junction.

For each animal in the LI group, the deep and superficial attachments of the RIL and DIC were transected between the radial carpal and intermediate carpal bones (ICB) (Fig. 1). Once the ligaments were cut, the joint capsule, fascia, and skin were closed. For each animal in the SBR group, the radial carpal bone (RCB) was removed and replaced with a comparably sized cobalt-chrome (CoCr) implant with articular surfaces polished to a mirror-like finish (∼0.5 μm) [24]. The implant was fixed via a suture anchor (AR-1318FT, Arthrex, Inc., Naples, FL) placed into the medial side of the ICB, and suturing of the Deep Radial Carpal Flexor Tendon to the dorsal ICB after passage through a dorsal-volar hole in the implant. Once the implant was fixed in place the joint capsule, fascia, and skin were closed. Gait measurement

Forelimb gait was evaluated using a digital pressure mat (HRV6 Walkway System; Tekscan Inc.), with video recorded contemporaneously to verify hoof strikes. Gait measurements were made prior to surgery (pre-op) and at 3, 6, 9, and 12 weeks postoperatively. Commercial software (Walkway 7.0; Tekscan Inc) was used for calibration, data collection, and analysis. A minimum of five successful trials were recorded per animal at each timepoint. Trial success was defined as the animal walking continuously across the mat without stopping or stepping off. Hoof strikes were visualized in the software and labeled automatically (left forelimb; right forelimb; left hindlimb; right hindlimb). The raw gait data was reduced to yield maximum force (kg) and impulse (kg-s) for each hoof strike, each normalized as a percentage of body weight, as well as stance time (sec) and stride velocity (cm/sec). For each gait metric, the ratio of the operated to contralateral limb was calculated and averaged across trials for each animal.

Imaging and bone model generation

At euthanasia, both wrists from each animal were harvested by transverse sectioning of the distal radius and ulna and the proximal metacarpals. The CoCr implants were removed from the SBR wrists via a small arthrotomy. The wrists were fixed in 10% formalin and high-resolution images (0.1205 mm isometric voxel size) were generated using a SKYSCAN 1276 micro-computed tomography (μCT) scanner (Bruker, Billerica, MA). The carpal bones, radius, ulna, and metacarpals were segmented in MIMICS v22 (Materialise, Leuven, Belgium) using density-based thresholding and manual editing. Three-dimensional (3D) models were exported as closed triangular meshes with no triangle reduction and minimal smoothing (iterations: 1; smooth factor: 0.030). All left bone models were mathematically mirrored to the right for analysis. Coordinate systems for each carpal bone were constructed using their geometric centroids as the origins and the principal inertial axes as the coordinate axes. The coordinate systems for the metacarpals and the combined radius and ulna were defined using manually identified anatomical landmarks on the bone models [25]. The metacarpal landmarks included the lateral base of MC3, medial base of MC4, and four points around the midshaft of MC3 and MC4; the radius and ulna landmarks included the radial and ulnar styloid, and four midshaft points around the radius and ulna. The directions of the coordinate system axes were defined as X in the volar-dorsal direction, Y in the proximal-distal direction, and Z in the radial-ulnar direction. The volume of each bone was calculated using custom-written code.

Osteophyte volume calculations

Osteophyte volume was calculated by Boolean subtraction of the contralateral bone model from the experimental bone model using a Dissimilarity-Excluding Procrustes (DEP) registration approach [26], with all portions of experimental bones falling outside of the registered contralateral bones considered to be osteophytes. Previous validation of DEP registration method demonstrated high accuracy for computing osteophyte volume when compared to synthetic ground truth models (β = 0.987, R2 = 0.99, intercept = 0.31 mm3) [26]. For the radius, ulna, and metacarpals, the osteophyte volume calculations were limited to the distal epiphysis of the radius and ulna, and the proximal metaphysis of the metacarpals. The radius and ulna bone models were digitally trimmed by a transverse plane through the growth plate, and the metacarpals were digitally trimmed 16 mm from the proximal articular surface, the shortest length at which osteophytes were observed. All trims were consistent across the experimental and contralateral bone models for the LI and SBR groups. The volume of each osteophyte model was calculated using open-source code that utilized a discrete form of the divergence theorem to convert volume integration to an area integral over the boundary of the triangular surface [27,28]. To account for differences in bone size across animals, osteophyte volumes were normalized by the total volume of the contralateral bones and reported as a percentage (%) of total bone volume.

Joint space measurements

Joint space was computed by calculating the inter-bone spacing between 19 sets of mating bone surfaces for the LI group and 17 sets of surfaces for the SBR group (Fig. 2). Each articular surface within the carpus was segmented by manual outlining of the joint margins on the subchondral bone models using the boundary feature in Geomagic Wrap 2021 (3D Systems, SC). The distances between the mating surface models were computed by calculating the distance between nearest neighbor nodes on paired surface models along their corresponding node surface normal (dist2surf.m; iso2mesh package, http://iso2mesh.sourceforge.net/). Previous studies have demonstrated that cartilage thickness measurements of carpal bones imaged at 10 μm resolution have a mean error of 0.009 mm [29], while bone-to-bone distance measurements imaged at 34 μm resolution achieve an accuracy of ∼0.02 mm [30], with both approaches incorporating segmentation and surface-to-surface comparisons. Based on these studies, methodological uncertainty at the present voxel resolution (120.5 μm) was estimated to be ∼0.108 mm, indicating that differences approaching this magnitude may reflect methodological variability rather than true anatomical change. This surface-to-surface approach makes the measurement independent of the overall orientation of the bones. Microscopic articular cartilage analysis

Microscopic analysis of RCB articulations was performed on sagittal sections cut through the radial side of the carpus, and frontal sections cut through the middle of the remaining ulnar side (Fig. 3). Each wrist was grossly cut to yield a radial block, obtained by making a sagittal cut at the midpoint of the radial-ulnar width of the RCB, and dorsal and volar blocks obtained by making a frontal cut at the midpoint of the volar-dorsal depth of the RCB from the remaining ulnar portion of the wrist. After gross sectioning, the radial and dorsal blocks were decalcified, embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and stained with Safranin-O (Saf-O) and fast green. The cartilage surfaces at the articulations of the RCB and radius and the RCB and CB2/CB3 (Fig. 3) were scored by the consensus of 3 reviewers (Q.M.V., D.C.M., and B.C.F.), following OARSI guidelines [31]. Statistical analysis

Generalized estimating equations (GEE, SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC) were used to evaluate differences in gait metrics between the LI and SBR groups, with fixed effects for experimental group, time point, and group-timepoint interactions, and to compare osteophyte volume and joint space across groups. Model residuals and outcome distributions were assessed to determine the appropriate distribution and link functions; a binomial distribution with a logit link was used for osteophyte volume, and a Gaussian distribution with an identity link was applied for joint space. GEEs were selected to appropriately account for repeated measurements and maximize the use of data from animals with missing observations, and classical sandwich estimation was applied in all models to protect against potential model misspecification. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

For the microscopic cartilage analysis, mixed-effects models with a binomial distribution and logit link function, Laplace approximations, and a random intercept per specimen were fitted to assess whether OARSI scores differed between operated and contralateral limbs, within and across experimental groups. Mixed-effects modeling was chosen to accommodate repeated measures and retain specimens with incomplete observations. Post hoc comparisons were conducted within models using orthogonal contrasts, and the Holm procedure was applied where appropriate to maintain a two-tailed familywise alpha of 0.05.

Results

All animals in the LI and SBR groups completed the 12-week postoperative period without major complications. Mild carpal swelling was observed for an average of 4 days postoperatively in the operated limb. One animal exhibited implant subluxation, confirmed by radiography, during immediate post-op recovery; the implant was manually repositioned, and proper placement confirmed radiographically. One animal experienced wound dehiscence one day postoperatively; sutures were repaired under sedation without implant subluxation. In the SBR group, implant placement was confirmed by radiography immediately after surgery and at 6 and 12 weeks postoperatively.

Gait analysis

Gait analysis revealed that the SBR animals reduced weightbearing on their operated forelimbs 3 weeks postoperatively, with gradual return to near normal by week 9. Weight bearing was not altered in the LI animals at any time point. At 3 weeks post-op the maximum force borne by the operated limb in the SBR animals decreased by 31.4% to a ratio of 0.72 compared to pre-op values (CI: 0.65–0.78; p < 0.001) (Fig. 4A), impulse decreased by 43.9% to 0.60 (CI: 0.52–0.68; p < 0.001) (Fig. 4B), and stance time decreased by 13.9% to 0.87 (CI: 0.84–0.90; p < 0.0001) (Fig. 4C). The asymmetry in maximum force and impulse decreased substantially after post-op week 3 but persisted at a low level when compared to pre-op throughout the study in the SBR group. At 6 weeks postoperative, impulse and stance time were less than pre-op by 21.5% (ratio: 0.84, CI: 0.80–0.89; p < 0.0001) and 8.9% (ratio: 0.92, CI: 0.90–0.95; p < 0.0001), respectively. At weeks 9 and 12, impulse remained less by 14.0% (ratio: 0.92, CI: 0.88–0.96; p < 0.0001) and 15.9% (ratio: 0.90, CI: 0.81–0.98; p = 0.008), respectively. Osteophyte volume

On average, osteophyte volumes at 12 weeks in the carpus of the SBR and LI groups were 15.9% and 7.2% of total bone volume, respectively. Osteophyte volumes were greater on the bones of the SBR group than the LI group, most notably in the carpal bones: by 20.1% for CB2 (p < 0.0001), 10.4% for CB3 (p = 0.0005), 12.1% for CB4 (p < 0.0001), and 14.4% for ICB (p = 0.002) (Fig. 5). On MC5 (p = 0.047) and the radius (p = 0.03) there was greater osteophyte formation for the SBR animals than the LI animals, 6.8% and 4.1%, respectively (Fig. 5). Discussion

The aim of this study was to evaluate the YMP as a preclinical model of wrist OA following LI and SBR. We assessed gait, osteophyte volume, joint space, and cartilage degeneration over 12 weeks after transection of the RIL and DIC. We hypothesized that the LI group would demonstrate greater gait asymmetry, greater osteophyte volume, and narrower joint space than the SBR group.

Contrary to our hypotheses, ligament transection did not lead to significant gait alterations, changes in joint spacing, or histological cartilage degeneration within 12 weeks, and resulted in 9% lower osteophyte volume than the SBR group. These findings are consistent with Altieri et al. who reported minor kinematic changes (∼3° in extension) of the YMP carpus following transection of both the RIL and DIC [18]. The minimal changes observed in the LI group contrasts with what is known about injuries to the SLIL and DIC, after which the LI group was modeled, causing wrist instability and altered biomechanics [2] that can lead to OA. This suggests that the modeled ligament transection did not induce sufficient carpal instability to drive OA progression during the study period.

In contrast, the SBR group exhibited functional and structural changes. Gait asymmetry occurred at 3 weeks postoperatively. Although there was partial recovery, symmetry did not return to baseline within the 12 weeks of this study. Structurally, osteophyte volume was significantly greater in the SBR than LI group, with osteophytes present on all carpal bones rather than limited to those articulating with the RCB. Osteophytes were primarily located in the volar and dorsal regions of the metacarpals and carpal bones, suggesting a biological response to surgical trauma, as formation was not confined to bones surrounding the RCB; however, the mechanism underlying new bone formation remains uncertain. Despite osteophyte formation in both groups at 12 weeks, joint space narrowing was not detected. Histologically, severe cartilage degeneration was observed in SBR operated limbs, with total OARSI scores approximately four times higher than contralateral limbs and substantially greater than LI limbs. Together, this data suggests that SBR induced more structural degeneration, while both models may represent early-stage OA, as osteophyte formation preceded joint space narrowing, which was not observed in either group.

Gait analysis using pressure mat systems is an established method for detecting OA-related functional changes in animal models that most commonly report offloading of the affected limb [[32], [33], [34], [35]]. LaVallee et al. observed no gait differences following monosodium iodoacetate-induced knee OA over 12 weeks [35], whereas Karamchedu et al. reported greater hindlimb asymmetry at 4 weeks in a treatment group for ACL repair with a tissue-engineered implant compared to ACL transection [32]. Consistent with Karamchedu et al., SBR animals exhibited early and persistent gait asymmetry at 3 weeks postoperatively, whereas LI animals showed minimal functional change, similar to the limited gait alterations reported by Karamchedu et al. after ACL transection at 12 weeks postoperatively, which remained unchanged through 52 weeks in their study [32].

Osteophyte formation and joint space narrowing are also established features of OA in the human wrist [[36], [37], [38], [39], [40], [41], [42], [43], [44]]. In rodent knee models, osteophytes develop within weeks of destabilization, as demonstrated by Das Neves Borges et al. following medial meniscectomy [45] and by Hayami et al. after ACL transection and meniscal resection [46]. In the YMP carpus, osteophytes were present at 12 weeks in both groups, though onset was not recorded. The absence of joint space narrowing contrasts with clinical wrist OA findings in the trapeziometacarpal [36,42], carpometacarpal [37], and pisotriquetral [44] joints. Ten Berg et al. reported a median pisotriquetral joint space of 0.8 mm in healthy joints versus 0.1 mm in osteoarthritic joints [44], suggesting that the mean joint distance of 0.9 mm measured in the YMP carpus in both the LI and SBR groups is consistent with a healthy joint. However, osteophytes may precede joint space narrowing, as reflected in the Kellgren-Lawrence grading scale [47], suggesting that the YMP carpus may have been in an early OA stage at 12 weeks.

Histopathological findings in the SBR group align with prior OA models demonstrating elevated OARSI scores following joint destabilization or reconstruction. Zhao et al. demonstrated higher scores after ACL reconstruction in a porcine model [33]. In contrast, Karamchedu et al. found no significant differences in OARSI scores between ACL transection, ACL reconstruction, and ACL repair with a tissue-engineered implant [32]. Although outcomes vary by species and injury model, cartilage degeneration characterized by high OARSI scores in the SBR operated limbs demonstrate that the YMP carpus can develop moderate-to-severe OA cartilage pathology.

Several limitations warrant consideration. The YMP is a quadruped; however, approximately 60% of body weight is placed on the forelimbs [48], representing a high-loading or worst-case condition relative to humans. The small sample size limited exploring comparisons such as potential sex-based differences. Nevertheless, the model could detect significant differences in gait, osteophyte volume, and histopathological outcomes. Preoperative gait measurements were not symmetric (surgical/contralateral = 1), potentially due to clockwise ambulation across the pressure mat during turning, which may alter forelimb loading relative to head orientation. Quantification of osteophyte volume and joint space narrowing were limited to a single time point, preventing assessment of the initiation and progression. Quantification of osteophyte formation using DEP registration and Boolean subtraction may misidentify inherent shape differences between contralateral and experimental bone models as osteophytes, which was not histologically confirmed leaving the underlying mechanism unknown. Manual surface delineation of the articular surfaces for calculating joint distance may have introduced operator-dependent variation, although this was minimized by using a single trained reviewer and a standardized protocol across all specimens. The 12-week study duration may have been insufficient to detect joint space narrowing.

In summary, RIL and DIC ligament transection did not induce significant OA within 12 weeks in the YMP carpus, whereas CoCr RCB replacement resulted in persistent gait asymmetry, greater osteophyte formation, and marked cartilage degeneration. Although joint space narrowing was not detected, the functional and structural changes support the feasibility and translational potential of the YMP as a preclinical model for wrist OA. Longer follow-up periods, additional animals, refined instability models, and alternative implant materials will further clarify the YMPs utility for modeling wrist pathologies and treatments.
