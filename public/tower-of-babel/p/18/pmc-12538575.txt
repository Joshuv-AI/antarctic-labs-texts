Illuminating the druggability of the Giardia duodenalis kinome through reannotation and high-throughput screens
Authors: Alexander Y.F. Lam, Isabelle S. Lucet, Aaron R. Jex, Samantha J. Emery-Corbin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Giardia duodenalis (syn Giardia intestinalis, Giardia lamblia) is a neglected, microaerophilic gastrointestinal parasite reliant on broad spectrum anti-microaerophilic/-anaerobic nitroheterocyclic antibiotics (metronidazole) which have been in use for over 70 years. New drugs which avoid their predecessor’s shortfalls of toxic and adverse effects, as well as circumvent its increasing treatment failure, are urgently required to lower global rates of up to 200 million symptomatic cases annually. Kinases are essential regulatory enzymes that primarily catalyse the phosphorylation post-translational modification involved in dynamic cellular processes. Kinases are well-validated and attractive drug targets, with many kinase inhibitors demonstrating great success in the clinic as anticancer therapeutics. In G. duodenalis, its intriguing set of minimal “core” protein kinases and the highly expanded Giardia-specific Never-in-Mitosis-A related kinases (Neks) emerge as a novel druggable space. We propose this kinome as an understudied and underutilised space to explore novel antigiardial targets. Intriguingly, despite over 15 years of advances in kinase biology and new annotation tools, there are limited functional evidence on the existence of ‘Neks’ in G. duodenalis. To incentivise new efforts, we provide an updated kinome reannotation and examination of the giardial core and specific sub-kinomes using novel bioinformatic tools, suggesting a nomenclature and providing insights in a drug-discovery context. Lastly, we have conducted a high-throughput screening of 430 compounds, covering 53 kinase targets and 51 chemical scaffolds, identifying 83/430 antigiardial kinase inhibitors of which 33 true positives could be validated in a subset subjected to drug-susceptibility testing, highlighting intriguing spaces for further development and molecular probes to further explore kinase regulatory pathways in this parasite.

Introduction

Protein kinases (PKs) are essential enzymes which regulate protein phosphorylation, a post-translation modification (PTM) central to nearly all eukaryotic cell processes, including metabolism, division and signal transduction (Cohen, 2001; Hunter, 2012; Ardito et al., 2017). Dysregulation of PKs and their pathways are often associated with human diseases (Paul et al., 1997; Johnson and Nakamura, 2007; Malemud and Pearlman, 2009; Kim and Choi, 2010). To date, there are 231 small molecule PK inhibitors that have been approved by the FDA, with a corresponding druggable PK target (Anderson et al., 2023); PK-targeting small molecule screens have been performed for over 20 years (Gray et al., 1998; Goldstein et al., 2008; Cohen et al., 2021). The core structure of a PK domain has been conserved across all domains of life, defined by their five β-strands and an α-helix in the N-termini lobe, and six α-helices in the C-termini lobe (Hanks and Hunter, 1995; Cheek et al., 2002; Kornev and Taylor, 2010; McClendon et al., 2014; Kanev et al., 2019; Modi and Dunbrack Jr, 2019a). Linking PK structure and function is central to developing selective inhibitors (Hanks and Hunter, 1995; McClendon et al., 2014; Modi and Dunbrack Jr, 2019a). Further, bona fide PKs are characterised by a “catalytic triad” of protein residues, consisting of an ATP-binding lysine, substrate-interacting aspartate, and another magnesium-binding aspartate (Modi and Dunbrack Jr, 2019a); all three residues are required (by the current nomenclature) to perform phosphorylation (Jacobsen and Murphy, 2017; Kwon et al., 2019). Additional PK/substrate sequence motifs and co-occurring protein domains divide hundreds of these orthologous core enzymes into eight major groups: the AGC kinases, which contain PKs A, G, and C, the casein kinases 1 (CK1); the sterile 20 kinases (STE); the tyrosine kinases (TK) and the tyrosine kinase-like (TKL), the guanylate cyclases (GYC), the calcium/calmodulin-dependent kinases (CAMK) and the group of cyclin-dependent kinases, mitogen-activated kinases, glycogen synthase kinases and casein kinases (CMGC), a “core” set of PKs (Hanks and Hunter, 1995; Cheek et al., 2002; Scheeff and Bourne, 2005; Modi and Dunbrack Jr, 2019b).

Many parasitic PKs are validated druggable targets (Doerig, 2004; Ward et al., 2004; Rotella, 2012; Cestari et al., 2016; Paquet et al., 2017; Wyllie et al., 2018; Mahindra et al., 2020; Rice et al., 2020). The push to explore PKs in parasites largely coincided with the launch of many protist genome sequencing projects and the availability of sequence databases (Aurrecoechea et al., 2009; Alvarez-Jarreta et al., 2024), which supported efforts to identify novel antiparasitic chemotherapeutics. In this vein, the first genome of Giardia duodenalis assemblage A WB genome was published in 2007 (McArthur et al., 2000; Morrison et al., 2007) with a 93 % chromosomally mapped genome a few years later (Upcroft et al., 2010). In 2011, Manning and colleagues (Manning et al., 2011) performed the first comprehensive analysis of the G. duodenalis kinome, discovering a minimal core kinome relative to higher eukaryotes, highlighting an intriguing druggable space in a parasite where drug-discovery efforts have traditionally been underserved.

Giardia duodenalis (syn Giardia intestinalis, Giardia lamblia) is a microaerophilic gastrointestinal parasite causing giardiasis and burdens which disproportionately affect children from lower socioeconomic tiers, which in turn causes developmental and nutritional disorders (Adam, 2001; Berkman et al., 2002; Feng and Xiao, 2011; Torgerson et al., 2015; Lalle and Hanevik, 2018). Further, giardiasis has been associated with lifelong post-infectious disorders, such as irritable bowel syndrome and chronic fatigue (Litleskare et al., 2018). In the two-stage life cycle of G. duodenalis, chemotherapeutic treatments mainly target the virulent trophozoite stage (Adam, 2001), and remain the gold standard in clearing existing infections, given limited development on vaccine candidates (Lee et al., 2011). The infective cyst stage also poses public health challenges, as these cysts are resilient to traditional water disinfection strategies (Ongerth, 1990; Cacciò et al., 2003; Nasser et al., 2012).

The call for new antigiardial chemotherapeutics is neither new nor unexpected. For the past seven decades, the primary drug has been the broad-spectrum anti-microaerophilic/-anaerobic nitroheterocyclic antibiotic metronidazole (Gardner and Hill, 2001; Raether and Hanel, 2003). Whilst nitroheterocyclics selectively target the microaerophilic metabolism of G. duodenalis, they also target the anaerobic gut microbiome, causing dysbiosis of the microflora and bacterial infection sequelae (Samuelson, 1999). Moreover, due in part to continued and non-compliant usage, treatment failure attributed to drug resistance is common (Loesche et al., 1993; Upcroft et al., 2006; Lalle and Hanevik, 2018). Common adverse effects of metronidazole include nausea and vomiting, and whilst rare, severe toxic effects can include transient leukopenia, neutropenia and neurotoxicities (McKendrick and Geddes, 1979; Kim et al., 2004). Therefore, with an unmet need to develop new chemotherapeutics with novel mechanisms, the understudied kinome of G. duodenalis represents an underutilised opportunity to exploit a variety of parasite-essential signalling and regulatory pathways to break infection and transmission cycles.

In humans, the core kinome is estimated to include ∼500 PKs, covering eight major groups (Manning et al., 2002). In comparison, the first annotation of G. duodenalis PKs (Manning et al., 2011) estimated 80 core PKs in G. duodenalis, one of the smallest known core kinome in any eukaryote at the time of its publication (Kwon et al., 2019). Intriguingly, they highlighted a large, expanded group more than twice the size of the core kinome (198/278) (Manning et al., 2011). This was proposed as an expanded, highly divergent, single PK group, based on sequence homology of a few to the Never-in-Mitosis-A (NIMA)-related kinases (Neks) orthologs, but primarily based on in-paralogs within sub-species (Manning et al., 2011). Further, this kinome hosts one of the largest proportions of pseudokinases relative to the size of its complete kinome (Manning et al., 2011; Kwon et al., 2019). These PKs lack the complete catalytic triad and are considered catalytically inactive (Jacobsen and Murphy, 2017; Kwon et al., 2019; Mace and Murphy, 2021). Consequently, based on a limited number of publications (Abel et al., 2001; Ellis et al., 2003; Kim et al., 2005; Cox et al., 2006; Hernandez et al., 2007; Davids et al., 2008; Hennessey et al., 2016, 2020), nearly all of these Giardia-specific Neks have no known functions, no known substrates, no sequence motifs – more than meeting definitions used in model species of ‘understudied’ PKs (Gomez et al., 2024). Notably, most of these Neks share little sequence homology with Neks in higher eukaryotes, as well as limited homology within the parasite (Manning et al., 2002, 2011), such that they could represent parasite-unique targets that could exploit divergent chemical scaffolds or substituents for Giardia-specific chemotherapeutics.

We credit the rationale that both core and expanded branches of the kinome in G. duodenalis represent interesting drug-discovery spaces: the minimal core kinome containing essential PKs with little biological redundancy and the highly divergent Nek kinome containing Giardia-specific PKs (Manning et al., 2011). However, these spaces have remained mostly unexplored over the last decade, and herein, we have taken several steps to review, update current annotations and incentivise PK research in this parasite. Firstly, we propose a reannotation of PKs in the most recent genome of G. duodenalis (Xu et al., 2020), noting that the initial annotation of this unique kinome in 2011 was built upon a fragmented genome (McArthur et al., 2000; Manning et al., 2011). To facilitate this, we look to new bioinformatic tools at both the protein sequence and structural level (Rozewicki et al., 2019; Edgar, 2022), specifically noting the state-of-the-art AlphaFold2 protein structure prediction algorithm (Jumper et al., 2021; Abramson et al., 2024). Secondly, PKs are demonstrated druggable enzymes primarily as targets in tumours (Johnson, 2009), partly due to the well-conserved PK domain structure throughout evolution (Hanks and Hunter, 1995). Thus, we probe the core kinome in G. duodenalis with designed small molecule PK inhibitors from these screens to discover novel antigiardial compounds, thus highlighting novel druggable opportunities within existing molecular spaces.

Herein, we stringently re-annotated a portion of the new G. duodenalis assemblage A kinome, highlighting new sequence- and structure-based annotations that should be adjusted in silico based on the newer, high-quality genome assembly of G. duodenalis. Further, we performed a high-throughput screen of a curated library of small molecule PK inhibitors and identified molecular probes to probe G. duodenalis PK regulation. Crucially, we shed light on the “Nek kinome”, providing a revised perspective on these likely Giardia-specific PKs, including a suggestion for their annotation, providing an incentive to functionally validate the biological roles of these PKs and pseudokinases.

Materials and methods

Query and classification of kinase-like proteins in the G. duodenalis genome

InterProScan v5.61 (Jones et al., 2014) was used to extract PK domain boundaries (under the superfamily accession for the PK domain IPR011009) from the predicted protein sequences from the G. duodenalis genome (WBC6 2019 (Xu et al., 2020)). Identified PK domain boundaries were queried against a curated list of Hidden Markov Models (HMMs) using HMMsearch (Eddy, 2023); the complete list of HMMs was retrieved from the Conserved Domain Database (CDD (Wang et al., 2023)), from Kwon et al. (2019) (for a confident set of defined and clustered pseudokinases) and Tyagi et al. (2010) (for a set of clustered prokaryotic PKs) (Supplementary Data S1). HMMbuild generated pseudokinase and prokaryotic PK HMMs based on their respective sequence alignments. Each identified PK domain was assigned numerical suffixes based on the location of the PK domain in the full-length protein; 293 PK domains were retrieved (Fig. 1, Supplementary Tables S1, S2, S3). All 293 PK domain boundaries were queried on AlphaFold2 and AlphaFold3 for predicted protein structures (Jumper et al., 2021; Abramson et al., 2024). Domains were aligned to representative mammalian PK crystal structures cyclin dependent kinase 16, B-Raf kinase and phosphatidylinositol-4-phosphate 5 kinase (PDB: 3MTL and 5G6V, 5CSW and 9BFB, and 5E3S) using TM-align to generate TM-scores normalised to representative structures (max TM-scores to each template was taken as the representative value) (Zhang and Skolnick, 2005). All folded PK domains were manually verified using ChimeraX-1.8 (Meng et al., 2023); structures with unique/lack of secondary structures were noted, and those which did not resemble the canonical PK domain were excluded from further analyses (Hanks and Hunter, 1995) (Fig. 1A).

Identified PK domains were filtered for more stringent, “confident” HMM assignments based on either of the following two criteria: 1) the difference between the best HMM domain match score and the second best HMM domain match score must be greater than 50 (ΔHMMscore >50), or, 2) the best HMM domain match and second best HMM domain match belong to the same “group” of PKs (e.g. best match CDKL5 is from the CMGC group, and second best match CDKL2 is also from the CMGC group). Confident atypical PKs (aPKs) were assigned if HMM assignments were not part of the “PKc” group from CDD (CDD: cd00180). A total of 64 “confident” core HMM assignments were made (discussed below), 9 were aPKs, and 55 were part of the core kinome (Fig. 1C).

In silico query of kinase-domain containing proteins in G. duodenalis

For all identified PK and pseudokinase domains, common PK domain motifs and key catalytic residues (Hanks and Hunter, 1995) were identified with XSTREME (version 5.5.5) with default settings (Supplementary Table S4) (Grant and Bailey, 2021). Those PK domains with no XSTREME-identified catalytic triads were manually queried using a structural-based sequence alignment through AlphaFold-generated structures (Jumper et al., 2021; Abramson et al., 2024). Briefly, structures were individually aligned to the crystal structure PDB: 5CSW, and Seed Extension (SE) was used to extract pairwise alignments (Tai et al., 2009). Pairwise alignments were then aligned using MAFFT (--merge option) to create a structural-based, transitive multiple sequence alignment of PK domains (Katoh and Standley, 2016). Those PK domains with no XSTREME-identified catalytic triads were then manually identified based on this alignment (Supplementary Table S5). InterProScan v5.61 was used to identify co-occurring protein domains (Jones et al., 2014) (Fig. 1D). All PK domain-containing proteins were queried on the DeepTMHMM web server to predict the presence of transmembrane domains (Hallgren et al., 2022).

Structural phylogeny of kinase-domain containing proteins

A structural phylogenetic tree was generated for the complete set of initial 284 PK domains (excluding aPKs), and another tree was generated for the “confident” 55 core PK domains using FoldTree (Moi et al., 2023). Predicted PK domain structures (provided in Supplementary Data S2) were grouped based on previous HMM assignments (Supplementary Tables S1, S2, S3) and visualised using ggtree in RStudio (Yu et al., 2017).

Classification and evidence of ncPK expression throughout G. duodenalis lifecycle

The non-core protein kinases (ncPKs) were sub-classified by the presence of the catalytic triad and co-occurring domains. Evidence of ncPK expression in the trophozoite stage of G. duodenalis (WB-1B isolate (Upcroft et al., 1995)) was identified from a previous mass spectrometry dataset (Balan et al., 2021). Peptides with pep scores <0.01 were mapped to the new G. duodenalis proteome (UniProt UP000001548) and used as a proxy for ncPK expression evidence in the trophozoite stage during WB growth. Previously identified significant (previously annotated) Neks during the G. duodenalis lifecycle (Balan et al., 2021) were cross-referenced to this in silico ncPK dataset.

Cultivation of parasitic protists in vitro

Giardia duodenalis trophozoites (WB-1B) were maintained in 10 mL of TYI-S-33 supplemented with 10 % heat-inactivated bovine serum in flat-sided tubes as previously described, with subculturing performed twice a week (Emery-Corbin et al., 2020).

Compounds and compound libraries

The SelleckChem PK inhibitor (n = 423) and the epigenetic inhibitor (n = 7) libraries were obtained through Compounds Australia, Griffith University. 10 mM stock solutions of compounds were made in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, Cell Signalling Technology) and stored indefinitely at – 20 °C. Compounds Australia provided the SelleckChem compounds and their PK targets. Torkinib (PP242) was sourced from Sapphire Biosciences (#13643-1 MG), and stocks made in 10 mM DMSO were stored indefinitely at – 20 °C.

Cell viability assays with CellTiter-Glo

High-throughput screening of SelleckChem inhibitor libraries against G. duodenalis trophozoites

Single replicate, single dose (10 μM) Cell viability assays were performed in a 384-well plate (Greiner Bio-One Microplate: 781098) format against G. duodenalis trophozoites in incubation volumes of 25 μLs (Ansell et al., 2016; Emery-Corbin et al., 2020). Briefly, 500 trophozoites were seeded into 384-well plates and incubated anaerobically for 48 h (BD GasPak EZ: 260683) before the addition of 10 μL of CellTiter-Glo reagent to each well; firefly luminescence was measured on microplate readers with the emission spectra set to 580-80 nm.

Drug-susceptibility testing of PK inhibitors against G. duodenalis

Drug-susceptibility testing of a subset of compounds (n = 57) was performed once in technical duplicates or quadruplicates in a 10-point serial dilution series from 30 μM in 384-well plate formats as above. For torkinib, drug-susceptibility testing was performed in technical triplicates in biological quadruplicates (n = 4) at an 8-point serial dilution series from 5 μM in 96-well plate formats (Corning NBS Microplate: CLS3995) (Ansell et al., 2016; Briefly, 2000 trophozoites were seeded into 96-well plates in 50 μL incubation volumes and incubated anaerobically as above for 48 h before the addition of 30 μL of CellTiter-Glo reagent to each well and measured on microplate readers as above.

Normalisation and data processing of CellTiter-Glo assays

Luminescent readouts were normalised to in-plate controls for a percentage viability. Within each 384-well microplate, the median ATP-luminescence values of at least eight wells containing 20 μM metronidazole were used as a positive control of complete inhibition of trophozoite growth (‘kill value’), and the median ATP-luminescence values of at least 16 wells containing 0.5 % DMSO were used as a negative control of uninhibited trophozoite growth (‘live value’). The kill value was subtracted from each luminescent value in each plate before these values were divided by the dynamic range (live value – kill value) to obtain a normalised luminescent value for each well, which were then used as a proxy for percentage viability of trophozoites (Supplementary Table S6).

The median normalised trophozoite viability value for the overall screen was calculated to be 90.16 % based on 462 wells containing 0.5 % DMSO. This was used to establish a dynamic cut-off for trophozoite viability using the median absolute deviation method (2x MAD); compounds which reduced trophozoite viability below “Median – 2x MAD” (Akinshin, 2020) were considered “hits”. Single-dose screening data were visualised with ggplot2 in RStudio (Wickham, 2016).

For drug-susceptibility assays, normalised parasite viability was visualised on GraphPad Prism 10 and dose-response profiles were fitted with the log[compound] vs normalised response, variable slope model with robust regression fitting, and curve constraints at – 25 % as a minima and 125 % as a maxima as previously described (Lam et al., 2021).

Clustering of chemical analogues across PK inhibitor libraries

Available SMILES were provided by SelleckChem (through Compounds Australia). Open Babel (v3.1.0) was used to convert the retrieved SMILES to 2D mol2 structures, and a pairwise comparison between 430 unique, small molecules was performed using the FP2 path-based fingerprint format to generate Tanimoto coefficients (O’Boyle et al., 2011; Reinecke et al., 2023). The output identity matrix of Tanimoto coefficients was clustered using the cluster_matrix function with the ward.D2 method with the RStudio package “textshape”. Using a Tanimoto coefficient cut-off at 0.7, the hierarchical clustered matrix guided the manual assignment of compound clusters.

Compound set enrichment analysis of identified chemical clusters for antigiardial activity

Enrichment analyses of identified chemical structure clusters (n = 51, from 430 unique compounds) were performed with multiple two-sided Fisher’s Exact Tests and p-values adjusted with the Benjamini-Hochberg method; adjusted p-values <0.05 were used as cut-offs. Within each cluster, targets were assigned based on a concatenation of PKs from each cluster member.

Results

Re-annotation of the G. duodenalis PK domains with HMMs

Since the initial annotations of the highly divergent G. duodenalis kinome by Manning and colleagues in 2011 (Manning et al., 2011), a chromosomal-scale genome assembly was made available for the reference G. duodenalis assemblage A in 2020 (Xu et al., 2020), providing better gene models and hence more accurate predicted protein sequences. For example, the number of genes almost halved in this new contiguous genome compared to the previous assembly (McArthur et al., 2000; Xu et al., 2020), prompting investigations into possible erroneous kinase-coding genes in the previous genome but also the possibility of new kinase-coding genes in this new genome; we show in this work 31 deprecated kinase-coding genes in the old genome, and six new kinase-coding genes in this new assembly (Supplementary Tables S1 and S7). Further, recent advances in accurate protein structure prediction algorithms (Yang et al., 2015; Jumper et al., 2021; Wheeler, 2021; Abramson et al., 2024) allowed for high-throughput structural comparisons with resolved crystal structures of PKs. Together, these provided the incentive to re-explore and re-annotate this protist’s intriguing kinome, particularly to attempt to resolve the low-confidence PK assignments in this parasite. Within this new G. duodenalis proteome (UniProt UP000001548), we took a stringent HMM- and structure-based approach to confidently identifying PK domains.

Initially, we used a sequence-based HMM approach which catalogued a combined 293 PK domains (284 PK domains and 9 aPK domains) within the most recent proteome of G. duodenalis assemblage A, strain WB (Fig. 1, Supplementary Tables S1, S2, S3). Here, 31 previous PK sequences in the 2011 study were not found in this new proteome (Supplementary Table S7); these were classified previously as ‘Nek-GL4’ or ‘Nek-Unclassified’ (Manning et al., 2011). Further, whilst pilot sequence-based phylogenetic and HMM analyses for these PK domains confirmed the expanded Nek-kinome as demonstrated previously (Manning et al., 2011; Kwon et al., 2019), we also observed a novel category of numerous PK domains annotated as “PknB” (protein kinase B (Young et al., 2003; Mieczkowski et al., 2008; Rakette et al., 2012)) or “Ciliate-A5” (Kwon et al., 2019). These annotations clustered ambiguously in the initial phylogeny with Neks (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Table S1) rather than in discrete novel subgroups. These results agree with Manning and colleagues’ previous hypothesis that Neks are derived from early-branching eukaryotes or prokaryotes, as the PknB group are bacterial eukaryotic-type PKs (Young et al., 2003; Mieczkowski et al., 2008; Rakette et al., 2012). However, these may also be novel eukaryotic-emerged PKs based on annotations to the Ciliate-A5 group, where the HMM models were constructed from protist-specific PKs (Kwon et al., 2019). Together, these could indicate that these G. duodenalis PKs occurred at the midpoint of evolution between bacterial and eukaryotic PKs but were nonetheless highly divergent from either of these groups. Hereinafter, we broadly group these ambiguously annotated PKs (with low confidence assignments, see Methods 2.1) which matched to these HMMs as “non-core protein kinases” (ncPKs), and the remaining were designated as the “core” kinome as previously published (Manning et al., 2011).Fig. 2Structure-based phylogenies of PK domains in the most recent G. duodenalis assemblage A (WBC6) predicted proteome (Xu et al., 2020). Given that these equivocal annotations were based only on sequence homology, we stringently filtered initial HMM results (Methods 2.1) and used a structure-guided approach as an orthogonal method to annotate the core kinome (Zhang and Skolnick, 2005; Jumper et al., 2021). Our combined approach was used to conservatively filter for a set of “confident” PK domains, which are more likely to resemble the canonical PK domain based on predictive structures and with accurate HMM assignments. Here, we excluded 29 PK domains as their predicted protein structures either lacked confidence or did not closely resemble the conserved PK domain (Fig. 1B). Based on ΔHMMscores >50 or a broad group match of best and second-best HMM assignments (See Methods 2.1), we identified 9 confident aPK domains and 55 core PK domains (Fig. 1C).

Validation of 55 core PKs in the G. duodenalis WBC6 predicted proteome

Using our stringent HMM-assigned, structure- and phylogeny-supported core kinome, we assigned 55 PK domains into four major groups: 24 belonging to the CMGC, 7 to the STE, 7 to the AGC and 5 to the AMPK-like group, with the remaining 12 classified as others (Fig. 2B–Supplementary Table S1). Within this list of 55 core PKs, 49 concurred with the previous annotations proposed in 2011 (Manning et al., 2011); those with UniProt identifiers: A8B7V0, E2RTY0, A8BQY4, A8BLF9, A8B8B7 and A0A644FBG0 did not concur with previous annotations (Supplementary Table S1). However, we note that the previous annotation for five of these PK domains was also noted to have weak homology based on their classification searches and were likely tentative assignments at the time (Manning et al., 2011); only A8B7V0 was previously unannotated and herein annotated as part of the CMGC group. Further, when we cross-referenced the remaining 23 non-confident core PKs (a total of 78 core PKs annotated herein) with previous annotation notes, we found that a majority of these again were tentative assignments in 2011, where sequence homology was noted as “too divergent” to known relative PK domains in higher eukaryotes (based on high E-values in either BLASTP and HMM search methods). Some were also noted to contain large inserts, which we confirmed through predicted PK structures and noted similar instances of inserts with the PK domain in other parasitic protists, such as Plasmodium falciparum (Kappes et al., 1995).

Ten of the 55 core PKs identified contained unusual predicted secondary structures (such as additional α-helices, β-strands and disordered inserts), which we believe confounded the generated structural phylogeny (Fig. 2B–Supplementary Table S1). Nonetheless, we also noted that seven of these 55 core PKs contained one or more accessory domains (Supplementary Tables S1 and S3), which are common in PKs, affecting their functionality and cellular localisation. Two annotated “AMPK-alpha” PKs (A8BIZ4 and A8BU43) contained a C-termini KA1 domain, which is a typical accessory domain of orthologous PKs in higher eukaryotes (Tassan and Le Goff, 2004; Elbert et al., 2005; Amodeo et al., 2007; Crozet et al., 2014). Two contained C-termini armadillo (ARM)-like repeat folds (A8BGL6 and A8BQY4), which are proposed to mediate protein-protein interactions as with other “repeat-like” folds (Andrade et al., 2001). One labelled as a Rho-associated coiled-coil containing kinase (ROCK) contained a C-termini pleckstrin homology (PH) domain (A8B1S2) (Julian and Olson, 2014). PH domains are known for low-affinity binding to phosphoinositides (Lemmon et al., 2002). One contained an N-termini CRIB domain (A8B738) and was classified here and previously as a p21-activated kinase (PAK) (Supplementary Table S1). Finally, we noted another PK (A8BKE0) with an unusual insert that bisected the PK domain; this was annotated as a papain-like cysteine peptidase domain with an E-value at ∼10−6 (Supplementary Table S1). However, the pLDDT scores (as a measure of prediction confidence on a protein structure) within this insert were low (data not shown). Hence, we believe this is a PK domain split by an insert rather than another protein domain.

Our stringent annotation has further condensed the core kinome in G. duodenalis assemblage A to an even smaller complement, from 78 to 55 PKs. The 23 PK discrepancy between ours and the previous annotations is not an explicit disagreement; whilst we still classify these as core PKs, we could not confidently subclassify these into the 7 core PK families, as these have secondary HMM assignments which were not confidently mapped (Methods 2.1).

An abundance of ncPKs in the G. duodenalis WBC6 predicted proteome

When the initial structural-phylogeny of 284 PK domains was constructed, we observed several instances of ambiguous clustering of domains assigned Nek, PknB and Ciliate-A5 (Fig. 2A). We have termed these “ncPKs” (non-core protein kinases). While Nek annotations were expected, the presence of two additional novel HMM assignments, which notably also did not have strict phylogenetic clusters between each other, brought into question the reliability of homology-based models and the downstream complication of extrapolating both relationships and function through assigning PK annotations (i.e. assignments to a specific PK group). Based on their unique and divergent sequences, we believe previous attempts at classifying ncPKs through HMM assignments and BLASTP searches either did not include enough species and/or diverse PK groups outside the model opisthokonts. We hypothesised that these ncPKs comprised a larger supergroup of Giardia-specific PK domains (currently without an HMM profile), which led to the ambiguous clustering of these ncPKs in G. duodenalis. Such an expansion of a family of kinases was also noted in other parasitic protists, namely the apicomplexan-specific FIKK kinases (Ward et al., 2004; Schneider and Mercereau-Puijalon, 2005). In total, we identified 177 ncPK domains in the new G. duodenalis proteome (reinforced by AlphaFold predicted structure validation, i.e. folds like a predicted PK domain), four of which are novel as they were missing in the previous genome assembly (Supplementary Table S1). We noted that most of these 177 ncPKs contained disordered amino acid inserts (mainly at the PK activation loop but also in the N- and C-lobes, Fig. 1D).

Subsequently, we devised a sequence/structure/functionality-driven classification of all ncPKs in the G. duodenalis WBC6 (assemblage A) proteome (Fig. 1D). The previous nomenclature of this group was based on a combination of sequence similarity within the PK domains and three-way inter-assemblage orthology between GS, WB and P15 strains of G. duodenalis (Manning et al., 2011). We believe this was problematic given the complexity of the Giardia species assemblage (Emery-Corbin et al., 2018; Seabolt et al., 2022; Wielinga et al., 2023), and the inevitability of taxonomic revisions and renewed genome assemblies. We therefore suggest an alternative nomenclature which highlights functional information. This proposed system is intended to classify ncPKs with confident PK features related to functionality in G. duodenalis. More specifically, our nomenclature explicitly highlights the presence of key residues and domains, which we hypothesise are evidence of functional roles rather than relying solely on sequence similarities between PK domains (Fig. 1D).

Firstly, the nomenclature captured the presence of the canonical ‘KDD’ triad, which confers catalytic activity. Protein kinases which lack these three residues, by the current nomenclature, are termed pseudokinases (Jacobsen and Murphy, 2017; Mace and Murphy, 2021). As such, the first two categories of ncPKs were termed ncPKDDs (ncPKs containing the catalytic triad), whereas PKs without the ‘KDD’ triad were herein termed ncPsKs (non-core pseudokinases). We defined 58 ncPKDDs and 119 ncPsKs. A second categorical annotation was added based on the existence of co-occurring domains. Within this category, 111/177 ncPK domains also contained co-occurring C-termini ANKs and/or RING/U-box domains (Supplementary Tables S1 and S8), which are broadly considered facilitators/mediators of protein-protein interactions (PPIs) (Aravind and Koonin, 2000; Li et al., 2006; Cunha and Mohler, 2009), often mediating substrate-catalysis. Consequently, ncPKs with co-occurring PPI domains were given the prefix “Int-“ (“Interacting”). Based on these four categories, we now highlight 26 ncPKDDs and 40 ncPsKs, which lacked additional domains, compared to 32 Int-ncPKDDs and 79 Int-ncPsKs (Fig. 1D–Supplementary Tables S1 and S8).

High-throughput phenotypic screening of SelleckChem PK inhibitors

While the extent of the ncPKs in G. duodenalis is abundant, our further reduced annotation of its core kinome highlighted the lack of redundancy, which may prove to be druggable (i.e. minimal core kinome susceptible to small molecule-mediated perturbations). To probe this minimal core kinome of G. duodenalis, we used the SelleckChem curated library of small molecule inhibitors (n = 430 small molecule inhibitors mainly targeting the mammalian core PKs) to perform a high-throughput screen against G. duodenalis trophozoite viability (Promega: CellTiter-Glo). Within this small library, we sub-classified 126 compounds into 51 “chemotypes” clusters based on the presence of at least two chemical analogues. We noted some limitations in this method, as most of the resultant clusters were small, these were unlikely to achieve enrichment significance (discussed below); only three clusters contained a maximum of five chemical analogues per cluster, whilst most clusters had only two analogues per cluster (Supplementary Tables S9 and S10). In this single-dose (10 μM) single-replicate screen, we selected a ‘hit’ cut-off at 47.3 % trophozoite viability relative to uninhibited growth (trophozoites grown in 0.5 % DMSO). This cut-off was selected based on twice the median absolute deviation (MAD) below the median trophozoite viability (median viability: 90.16 %, from n = 462 wells containing 0.5 % DMSO) (Fig. 3Aa). Using this cut-off, 83 compounds were deemed antigiardial hits at 10 μM, consequently indicating a hit rate of 19.3 % (Supplementary Table S9). Within these 83 compounds, 22 compounds were sub-classified into 14 chemotype clusters (Supplementary Tables S9 and S10), and six clusters harboured more than one active antigiardial analogue (Fig. 3Aa). Among these 83 hits, we noted some inhibitors which canonically target mammalian tyrosine kinases (TKs) which theoretically should not be present in G. duodenalis (Supplementary Table S1). With an unexpectedly high hit-rate of ∼20 % relative to previous antigiardial high- and medium-throughput screening studies (Bonilla-Santiago et al., 2008; Chen et al., 2011), we performed multiple Fisher’s Exact Tests on identified chemotype clusters to determine whether inhibitors with a particular chemical scaffold (pharmacophores) were enriched for antigiardial activity. In total, 51 tests were performed, and when adjusted for false-discovery at 5 % with the Benjamini-Hochberg method, no clusters were significantly enriched for antigiardial activity (Supplementary Table S10). This was likely due to the lack of sample size within each cluster, i.e. the high prevalence of small cluster sizes (Supplementary Table S10). Nonetheless, clusters 2, 16, 17, 25, 27 and 28 contained a higher proportion of active to inactive analogues (>66 %), whereby targeted screens with more chemical analogues could be more conclusive about the potential of the scaffold for targeting G. duodenalis.

Five of these six clusters with a high proportion of antigiardial analogues were reported to target the aPKs (Fig. 3Aa), particularly mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) kinase. These PKs share structural similarities to a canonical PK domain but have notable divergences in their underlying sequence (Kanev et al., 2019). Our review of this protist’s kinome revealed nine confident aPKs (Supplementary Table S1). The highest ratio of active:inactive chemical analogues in this single-dose screen (four antigiardials and one inactive) were those compounds from cluster 16 which were proposed to target the ATP-binding site of the mTOR PK (Feldman et al., 2009; Sun, 2013; Borsari et al., 2021). Consequently, we selected the representative compound torkinib for dose-dependent drug-susceptibility testing to determine its potency. This confirmed the activity of torkinib at > 5 μM doses (Fig. 3Ab). However, these in vitro IC50 values were not as potent as other antigiardials in other studies, with only an average IC50 value of 2.23 μM relative to other sub-micromolar antigiardial candidates (Chen et al., 2011; Kulakova et al., 2014; Emery-Corbin et al., 2021), possibly alluding to some key structural differences despite moderate sequence similarity of the PK domain to other mammalian TOR kinases (Muñoz-Muñoz et al., 2023).

Lastly, we validated our single-dose screen via drug-susceptibility testing of a subset of 56 PK inhibitors (47 antigiardial compounds and 9 inactive compounds in the single-dose screen). We confirmed the antigiardial activity of 33 compounds based on quantified IC50 values < 10 μM and confirmed the inactivity of the 9 inactive compounds based on IC50 values > 10 μM; 14 compounds which were active in the initial screen at a 10 μM dose were validated as false positives with IC50 values > 10 μM (Fig. 3B). In this dose-response validation, four compounds from clusters 2, 27 and 28 were active, particularly AZD8055 (cluster 2) and apitolisib (cluster 27), which had demonstrated sub-micromolar IC50 values (Fig. 3B). Again, both these compounds target the mTOR PK, with apitolisib also demonstrating activity against the lipid kinase phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K) (Powles et al., 2016). Other notable compounds with sub-micromolar IC50 values did not cluster within any chemotypes in our analysis, these were the glycogen synthase kinase 3β inhibitor AR-A014418, the glycogen synthase kinase 3 and cyclin dependent kinase inhibitor R547, the Janus kinase inhibitor AZ960, and the PI3K/mTOR inhibitor omipalisib.

Discussion

In the 15 years since Giardia’s first assembled genome (McArthur et al., 2000) and the 10 years since its kinome’s original annotation (Manning et al., 2011), its PK complement has become no less intriguing. Specifically, neither improved sequence models nor the addition of structural tools have resolved conclusive homologies to existing PK groups/families for 177 G. duodenalis ncPKs annotated herein. Furthermore, of the previous complement of core PKs (PKs which belong to the eight canonical eukaryote PK groups), we emphasise that only 55 of these have confident assignments, where a further 23 do not have sufficient homology (based on our models) to attribute them to one of these eight core groups. Based on this, we have opted for a conservative nomenclature, particularly for the ncPKs in G. duodenalis, which likely contain bona fide Neks but also more divergent PKs which clustered in our analysis with the Nek, PknB and Ciliate-A5 PKs. This highlights the presence or absence of functional features (catalytic triad) and additional domains. In the case of the former, this more explicitly highlights the larger proportion of what should be considered pseudokinases in the G. duodenalis kinome, which may not play a role in enzymatic phosphorylation but in the broader signalling cascade which are likely localisation-dependent (Siddiqui et al., 2020), such as scaffolding of protein complexes or interactors to other proteins (Mace and Murphy, 2021). While we believe this adds clarity to the ncPKs in this parasite, we note that these 177 ncPKs likely do not belong to a single PK group (that is, all Neks are now ncPKs). Instead, functional characterisation of these ncPKs should be prioritised to further sub-classify within this supergroup of ncPKs, deriving function-driven and cellular localisation niches rather than homology-based PK annotations in this parasite.

Our work did not significantly change the number or broader family classifications of previous annotations, except for a change in nomenclature for potentially Giardia-specific ncPKs (Manning et al., 2011). Primarily, based on our annotations, none of the initial 293 sequence-identified PK domains were classified as tyrosine kinase (TK) or tyrosine kinase-like (TKL) proteins, concurring with previous observations that some parasitic protists completely lack this group of kinases, and TKs/TKLs may have evolved later throughout eukaryotic life (Ward et al., 2004; Parsons et al., 2005; Wei et al., 2013; Kwon et al., 2019). However, phosphorylated tyrosine (pY) residues exist in G. duodenalis, suggesting that TKs evolved from earlier in eukaryotic life from other PKs to gain TK activity (Manning et al., 2011; Emery et al., 2018). However, which of these G. duodenalis PKs (or other G. duodenalis enzymes) harbour TK functions to perform phosphorylation on tyrosine residues is still unknown. Similar to the TKs, exemplars from the TKL group are not found in early eukaryotes such as G. duodenalis (Manning et al., 2002, 2011; Ward et al., 2004; Martin et al., 2009; Judelson and Ah-Fong, 2010; Solyakov et al., 2011; Shen et al., 2019); our models concur with these observations as we found no additional evidence of these TKLs within G. duodenalis in our annotation.

Our data here, along with others (Diaz-Gonzalez et al., 2011; Phan et al., 2020), demonstrated success in perturbing PI3K/mTOR pathways as an approach for yielding potent antiparasitic properties and may hold potential for drug development as an antiparasitic target (Fig. 3A). Elsewhere, genetic approaches to perturb the PI3K/mTOR pathway have demonstrated their essentiality in parasite growth, with the same phenocopy observed after perturbation with small molecule inhibitors (Barquilla et al., 2008; Paquet et al., 2017). In G. duodenalis, whilst small molecule perturbation of the orthologous PI3K/GTOR (in G. duodenalis, mTOR is termed ‘GTOR’ for Giardia target of rapamycin) has been studied, genetic methods to demonstrate this phenocopy has not yet been shown (Herabutya, 2015; Muñoz-Muñoz et al., 2023). If available genetic methods (Wampfler et al., 2014; Marcial-Quino et al., 2017; McInally et al., 2019; Horáčková et al., 2022) can demonstrate the essentiality of this PI3K/GTOR pathway, it should incentivise PI3K/GTOR-driven drug-design of inhibitors as novel antigiardials. Furthermore, we observed higher-than-expected inhibitory doses of compounds such as torkinib against G. duodenalis compared to its potent activity in mammalian counterparts (Sun, 2013; Borsari et al., 2021). This, along with GTOR’s sequence divergence from human and yeast mTOR (Muñoz-Muñoz et al., 2023), suggests GTOR may have minute differences within the PK domain. These differences may be exploited with small molecule inhibitors, providing an incentive for refining GTOR-specific scaffolds, which could provide efficacy and selectivity against the parasite.

Tyrosine kinases primarily relay extracellular signals into the cell, where more than half of the current defined TKs are classified as receptor-TKs (Robinson et al., 2000). Paradoxically, TKs are absent (by current definition) in early-branching eukaryotes and even in some plants, even though these cells are constantly exposed to dynamic and external stimuli to which they must adapt for survival (Luan, 2002; Ward et al., 2004; van Bentem and Hirt, 2009; Ghelis, 2011; Manning et al., 2011; Kwon et al., 2019). For example, in G. duodenalis, the parasite must constantly adapt to changing physiological conditions throughout the host’s gastrointestinal tract (Gillin et al., 1989). Despite the absence of TK annotations in the G. duodenalis kinome, we discovered TK inhibitors which were active in the initial high-throughput screen against G. duodenalis (e.g. EGFR PK inhibitors), but by current convention, such inhibitors should not be active. Interestingly, this phenomenon has been previously observed in another kinase unique to apicomplexan parasites (FIKK kinase), where some TK inhibitors were shown to bind with high affinity to inhibit FIKK kinase function (Lin et al., 2017). Further, tyrosine phosphorylation events have been demonstrated in G. duodenalis (Manning et al., 2011; Emery et al., 2018) and are present in other early eukaryotes as well (Sharma, 2000; Solyakov et al., 2011; Kesely et al., 2020). These observations, along with our findings of seemingly specific TK inhibitors with antigiardial activity, further credit the presence of these signalling pathways in early eukaryotes, which may still be evolving into the canonical TK protein structure by current nomenclature. Hence, TK inhibitors may target the earlier features of a typical TK to exert its antigiardial properties. Therefore, studies identifying the ancestral or early diverging TKs sequences in G. duodenalis could streamline SAR studies, which may guide the development of early eukaryotic TK inhibitors with specific antigiardial activity.

Genetically validated drug targets are more attractive from a drug-discovery perspective, as it is more likely to guarantee a phenotype from small molecule-directed perturbation. Our work here provides an interesting prospect in developing novel inhibitors against potentially Giardia-specific ncPKs. Given the size and sequence diversity of these ncPKs (Manning et al., 2011), sequence homology and structural identity to other empirically-validated PKs are insufficient in informing the true (biochemical and genetic) function(s) of these ncPKs; only few ncPKs were probed for their function, where some were correlated to regulation of microtubule dynamics (Hennessey et al., 2016, 2020). However, implementing functional genetic tools from model eukaryotes to G. duodenalis to probe the biological functions of ncPKs is not straightforward, as these parasites are less genetically tractable and significantly divergent from the closest model eukaryotes (e.g. yeast). Hence, from a global health (macro) perspective, we propose prioritising the characterisation and druggability of ncPKs, which are implicated in stage-transition from G. duodenalis trophozoites to its infective cyst form (Balan et al., 2021), under the assumption that inhibiting such ncPK function would block host-to-host transmission.

For example, based on this we could propose the prioritisation of one ncPKDD (A8BUD9) and three Int-ncPsKs (A8BFA4, A8BP90 and A8BJ13), which are implicated in stage-transition from G. duodenalis trophozoites to their infective cyst form, given their significant up-regulation in encysting cells at the protein level (Table 1 (Balan et al., 2021)). If biological consequences of these proteins can be proven through reverse genetic methods (i.e. perturbation of these proteins leads to an inability for trophozoites to encyst), numerous target-based drug discovery/design methods could be employed in lieu of viability screens against these genetically validated targets. In all four cases, crystallographic methods to resolve protein structures are also key in informing rational drug design (Farber, 1999). Against a catalytically capable ncPKDD, discovering/designing orthosteric inhibitors (i.e. antagonists) at the ATP-binding site is common, as they directly inhibit enzymatic function (Neubig et al., 2003). However, orthosteric inhibitors towards pseudokinases (the three Int-ncPsKs above) do not guarantee a biological effect; drugging pseudokinases commonly involve perturbing the interaction of the pseudokinase domain with interacting protein or nucleic acid partners (Wrobleski et al., 2019; Mace and Murphy, 2021). Therefore, targeted inhibitors against pseudokinases often require an in-depth analysis of their interactome (scaffolding partners) before designing allosteric inhibitors to perturb these scaffolds (Wrobleski et al., 2019), leading to a phenotypic effect. Whilst more rigorous, drugging these Int-ncPsKs often leads to higher selectivity towards the pathway/organism of interest, particularly for inhibitors which bind at the interface between proteins (Wyllie et al., 2018; Kathman et al., 2023; Won et al., 2024).

Overall, our work here shows the potential of PK inhibitors as attractive antigiardials but also highlights the gaps for in silico annotation methods, which should only be empirically validated by functional characterisation, especially in non-model organisms such as G. duodenalis. When functional and genetically validated PK targets can be shown, a shift to target-based or rational drug development of antigiardial PK inhibitors may offer better selectivity and potency for new chemotherapeutic options.
