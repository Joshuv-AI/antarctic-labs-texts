An integrative analysis of transcriptome, methylome and single-cell RNA sequencing data identifies UBE2H as a marker of oxaliplatin resistance in colorectal cancer
Authors: Jinman Fang, Qizhi Zhu, Bo Hong, Xueling Li, Hongzhi Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Metastatic colorectal cancer is commonly treated with oxaliplatin. However, patients may develop resistance to treatment over time, and currently, there are no validated predictive biomarkers for this resistance. A differential analysis of the transcriptome and DNA methylome of colorectal cancer cell lines, classified by their varying IC50 values for oxaliplatin, revealed that genes associated with resistance were enriched for interferon regulatory factor pathways. In contrast, sensitive genes showed enrichment for transcription, amino acid metabolism, development, and binding motifs of c-MyC:Max, AP1 and others. In univariate Cox’s proportional hazard model analysis, it was found that UBE2H expression is linked to shorter survival time in the TCGA dataset and was further validated across five GEO datasets of colorectal cancer. The transcription of UBE2H, along with its gene body methylation, and copy number variation was found to be higher in resistant cell lines compared to sensitive ones. Additionally, UBE2H levels were higher in cancer samples than in control samples, while the promoter methylation is lower in cancer samples than in control samples. In groups with high UBE2H, there was an increased infiltration of eight cell types, including CD8+ T cells and type 2 T helper cells. Conversely, only T helper 17 cells showed reduced infiltration. Moreover, UBE2H expression was positively correlated with checkpoint inhibitors, CTLA4 and PDCD1, along with immune regulatory genes, such as FOXP3, IL10, IGFB1, CD274, and LAG3, etc. Analysis of single cell RNA-sequencing data revealed that UBE2H expression is elevated in undifferentiated and proliferative cells located at the base of intestinal crypts in normal colon tissue. Our findings suggest that UBE2H could serve as a resistance marker to oxaliplatin, as it is associated with methylation, the presence of proliferative and undifferentiated cancer cells, and immune exhaustion. The proposed analytical pipeline may also be applicable to other cancers and diseases.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12935-025-03996-4.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) ranks third in mortality worldwide and accounted for 9.6% of the estimated 20 million new colorectal cancer cases in 2022, with a 2nd mortality rate of 9.3% among approximately 9.7 million new cancer deaths [1].

Oxaliplatin, used in combination with infusional 5-fluorouracil and leucovorin (FOLFOX), is commonly prescribed for treating metastatic colorectal cancer, as well as for adjuvant treatment of CRC.However, as treatment duration increases, drug resistance develops [2, 3]. Furthermore, myelosuppression [4, 5] and other toxicity associated with various administration methods and schedules of oxaliplatin [6] represent significant dose-limiting factors for its treatment.

Approximately 15% of early-stage and 5% advanced advanced colorectal cancers (CRCs) are characterized as deficient in mismatch repair or exhibit microsatellite instability (dMMR/MSI) [7]. The status of microsatellite instability has been shown to correlate with resistance to 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) when combined with oxaliplatin [8]. Patients with stage III mismatch repair deficient colon are reported to benefit significantly from initiating oxaliplatin treatment earlier [9]. Furthermore, colorectal cancer organoid models have identified genes associated with oxaliplatin resistance, such as STMN, VEGFA and NDRG1, as well as transcription factors including E2F1, BRCA1, MYBL2, CDX2 and CDX1 [10].The activation of ABCG2, which alleviates endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress-induced apoptosis, has been implicated in mediating oxaliplatin resistance in colorectal cancer cells [11]. While the mechanisms of oxaliplatin-resistance have been documented, the role of the epigenetic factors, particularly DNA methylation, which can be more easily modified than DNA mutations, remains underexplored. Additionally, multi-omics approaches hold promise for identifying more reproducible and reliable markers. Studies indicate that treatment resistance is influenced not only by intrinsic factors, such as the tumor cells themselves, but also by extrinsic factors within the tumor microenvironment [12]. Nevertheless, the effects of the cellular heterogeneity in the tumor microenvironment on oxaliplatin resistance are still not fully understood.

In this study, we integrated transcriptome and DNA methylome data from the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) database [13], TCGA and scRNA-Seq data from colorectal cancers. By performing differential analysis between resistant and sensitive cell lines and examining their association with overall patient survival across multiple independent datasets, we identified differentially expressed genes linked to the response to oxaliplatin treatment. From this analysis, we discovered the predictive marker UBE2H, which may serve as a prognostic indicator for colorectal cancer.

Additionally, we conducted a Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCAN) of checkpoint inhibitors and immune regulatory molecules. We also performed cell type-specific differential gene expression analysis and examined immune infiltration within the bulk tissue transcriptome. Furhtermore, we conducted a pseudobulk analysis of 19 cell types derived from single cell transcriptomics, allowing us to characterize the immune regulatory molecules associated with the newly found resistant marker, UBE2H, in both high and low expression groups.

Methods and materials

Bulk data collection

We downloaded the gene expression and DNA CpG methylation data for COAD cell lines, along with their IC50 values for cisplatin from the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) database [13] (see Supplementary Data File 1, Sheets 1–3), We also downloaded TCGA COAD data, including gene expression and patient clinical survival. For validation, we downloaded the following datasets from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database: GSE17536 [14], a five-year recurrence-free survival dataset of colon cancer from Moffitt Cancer Center; GSE17537 [14], a five-year recurrence-free survival dataset of colon cancer from Vanderbilt Cancer Center; GSE38832 [15], a dataset of metastatic colorectal cancer with disease-free and specific survival time, and molecular subtypes treated with FOLFOX and targeted therapies; [16] and GSE39582 a dataset of colon cancer molecular classification and prognosis [17].

We downloaded microarray datasets of GSE19860, GSE28702, GSE72970, and GSE69657 from GEO and integrated them for cell-type-specific DEG estimation [18] by high-resolution deconvolution with CIBERSORTx [19], after removing the batch effect with comBat [20] and excluding patients who had not received oxaliplatin treatment.

Single cell data and cellular location analysis

The colorectal cancer scRNA-Seq dataset, GSE178341 [21], was collected from the GEO database. We compared the expression level of UBE2H in different cell types, and analyzed the differentially expressed genes in the UBE2H highly and lowly expressed groups in 19 cell types including epithelial and non-epithelial cells based on the scRNA-Seq data from GSE178341 using the Seurat package and pseudo- bulk DESeq2 differential analysis, respectively. As stated in our previous pipeline [22], a cell-type gene signature matrix was constructed using the CIBERSORTx web tool with randomly sampled 5000 cells from the GSE132465 scRNA-Seq data. This matrix was then used for cell-type-specific gene expression analysis in high-resolution mode using the CIBERSOTx web tool, as well as for identifying differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between oxaliplatin responders and non-responders. Group-mode cell type deconvolution was also performed using the csSAM R package [23].

We also analyzed the single cell RNA-Seq data using the web toolbox of the Human Protein Atlas Ontology (HPA, https://www.proteinatlas.org/) with the UBE2H gene as the search keyword. Single-cell clusters with significantly higher UBE2H expression were recorded. The co-expression of other genes within the identified cell clusters was visually and quantitatively examined. We also investigated the cellular location of UBE2H.

Functional enrichment and immune infiltration analyses

We used g:Profiler (https://biit.cs.ut.ee/gprofiler/gost) to determine the enriched terms of Gene Ontology (GO), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways, Reactome, WikiPathways (WP), TRANSFAC (TF), and Human Protein Atlas (HPA) terms of the above differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and cell-type-associated differentially expressed genes (csDEGs). ClusterProfiler [24, 25] was also used to visualize the enriched Gene Ontology (GO) terms and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) [26, 27] was conducted to explore the underlying pathway variations between the two groups.

Immune infiltration was estimated in each sample using the CIBERSORT [28], ESTIMATE [29], and single-sample GSEA (ssGSEA) with the GSVA package [30].

Drug sensitivity

Drug sensitivity was predicted using the oncoPredict package (version 3.1) [31] on GSE72970 and GSE87211 for each sample. The samples were then classified into UBE2H-high and UBE2H-low groups, respectively. Finally the wilcox.test function in R was used to test for statistical significance. An adjusted P value of < 0.05 using Benjamini–Hochberg method was considered statistically significant for GSE87211, while an original P value of < 0.05 was considered significant for GSE72970 given that no statistically significant chemicals remained after the p value adjustment.

Statistical analysis

Sensitive and resistant groups were defined for each cell line using an empirical IC50 threshold of 10 μM [31], i.e., a cell line was defined as sensitive if its IC50 is smaller than 10 μM and vice versa. This was visually determined by examining the bimodal normal distribution (Figure S1A-C), resulting in a total of 10 sensitive and 31 resistant cell lines, This considers both the acknowledged definition threshold for sensitive cell lines on the nanomolar scale, and the ratio of sensitive to resistant cell lines with both transcriptomics and DNA methylomes.

Differentially expressed genes were analyzed using the R limma package [32]. Differentially methylated CpGs were analyzed using the ChAMP (Illumina HumanMethylation450) package in the Chip Analysis Methylation Pipeline [33]. Differences between groups were analyzed using the limma package, with a |log2FC|> 0.5 threshold for transcriptomics, and a |log2FC|> 0.1 threshold for DNA methylation. Original p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for both.

A univariate Cox proportional hazards model [34] was conducted to identify genes significantly correlated with the overall survival. A gene with an original p < 0.05 was considered to be significantly prognostic. LASSO gene selection [35] was conducted to reduce the number of genes that were independently prognostic in the overall survival. Tenfold cross-validation was performed to obtain the optimal lambda value. A multivariate Cox proportional hazards model analysis [34] was conducted to identify genes that were independent prognostic factors. Kaplan–Meier analysis [36] was performed using the “survimer” R package to evaluate and visualize the survival rate for each group [37], and patients were assigned to two groups according to the optimal cut-off value.

Copy number variation and independent DNA methylation data of UBE2H from cBioPortal were analyzed using the MEXPRESS database [38].

Quantification of DNA methylation of UBE2H and IFI30 based on the bisulfite conversion method

FFPE samples from seven patients were collected from the Pathology Department at Hefei Cancer Hospital, Chinese Academy of Sciences (institutional review board approval number: SL-KY2022033, 2022). The clinicopathological data for these seven patients are provided in the Supplementary Data File 2. Total DNA was extracted using a Qiagen GeneRead DNA FFPE kit. One section up to 10 μm thick was cut from the FFPE sample. Total DNA was extracted from the FFPE sample. The DNA methylation level of the UBE2H gene 5’ promoter region was measured using he commonly employed bisulfite reduction method [39]. Specifically, the bisulfite-converted DNA was amplified using nested PCR (2xTaq PCR Master Mix II Reagent) to improve the detection specificity and sensitivity [40]. The target sequence for UBE2H is UBE2H chr7: 129,590,559–129590608 CGGGTTTTATTTTTGTTTAGAAACTAGGAAATGACATAGTGGCAGCATCT. The four UEB2H primers (two inner and two outer) are: Outer forward primer: GAATAAAAGGTATTGTATGGAAAAGG, Outer reverse primer: CAAAAATAAAATATTTTTCTCCTTACTC, Inner forward primer: GGTAGAAATTTGTATTAGGTGTGATAG, Inner reverse primer: CCAATAATATTAACTTTTCATAAAACTAC. The IFI30 primers are: Outer forward primer: GATTGGAGGGAGGAGATTTGG, Outer reverse primer: GAGAGTGGGTTTTGGGTTTTAAG, Inner reverse primer: CCCTCCTCCACTCCTTCC. The nested PCR product was separated using 2% agarose gel electrophoresis, and the UBE2H band was recovered from the gel using a Universal DNA Purification Kit (Tiangen Biotech Co., LTD., Beijing China, Cat. # DP214-02). The purified UBE2H gene promoter amplification products were then conjugated and cloned with a T vector before being sequenced.

Results

Identification of DEGs and DMGs in COAD

To screen for genes and methylation sites associated with oxaliplatin resistance, the IC50 values of 40 colorectal cancer samples were extracted from the GDSC database [13]. The sensitivity of cancer cell lines to drugs is defined by the IC50 value, which is the concentration of oxaliplatin required to kill half of the tumor cells in vitro. Based on a threshold of 10 μM IC50, as stated in the Method section, these cell lines were divided into sensitive and resistant groups. This resulted in nine sensitive cell lines and 31 resistant cell lines as stated in the Method section. We searched for differentially expressed genes and differential methylation sites using the limma [32] and ChAMP packages [41], respectively, in sensitive and resistant cell lines. Gene expression microarray analysis of sensitive cell lines compared to resistant cell lines identified 494 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) with original p < 0.05 and |log2FC|> 0.5, including 258 upregulated genes and 236 low-expression genes in the GDSC dataset (Fig. 1A). The gene methylation microarray analysis identified 2033 unique hypermethylated genes (3301 CpG probes) and 924 hypomethylated genes (1479 CpG probes) in sensitive cell lines compared to resistant cell lines (Fig. 1B). Further analysis of the overlap between hypomethylated/upregulated genes and hypermethylated/downregulated genes revealed 71 Hypo_Up and 21 Hyper_Down genes, respectively, in sensitive samples compared with resistant samples (Data File 1: sheets 4–5, and Fig. 1C, D). High expression of UBE2H was associated with a poor prognosis for CRC patients and was found to interact with the four CRC CMS subtypes

We sought to identify the gene markers associated with colorectal cancer prognosis among these drug-resistant genes. Using a univariate Cox proportional hazards model and Lasso analysis (see Fig. 3A and B) on the 71 hypermethylated/downregulated and 21 hypomethylated/upregulated genes in the TCGA colorectal cancer cohort, four genes were obtained. These four genes are MPP7, IFI30, C4orf19 and UBE2H. The 95% confidence intervals and the hazard ratios can be found in Fig. 3C. For UBE2H, the 95% confidence interval is [1.07, 2.7] and the hazard ratio is 1.70 in the univariate Cox’s proportional hazards model analysis. We explored the prognostic role of IFI30 and UBE2H in CRC overall survival on three independent GEO cohorts profiled on a microarray platform, GSE17536, GSE17537 and GSE38832. The results show that higher UBE2H expression predicts poorer overall survival (OS) and relapse-free survival in TCGA and GEO cohorts (GSE17536, GSE17537 and GSE38832) cohorts (Fig. 3F-H). Univariate Cox proportional hazards model analysis of two further datasets (GSE72970 and GSE39582) also shows the predictive role of the UBE2H (Figure S3). The results suggest an unfavorable role for UBE2H in colorectal cancer prognosis, while IFI30 was not detected on the microarray platform.

However, multivariate Cox proportional hazards analysis combining stage, age, sex, and CMS subtypes for on a TCGA dataset revealed that UBE2H has a hazard ratio of 1.69, with a 95% confidence interval of [0.76,3.75], and a p-value of 0.195 (see Data file 1, Sheet 8). When CMS subtypes and UBE2H are combined, the hazard ratio of UBE2H is 1.91, with a 95% confidence interval of [0.87, 4.19], and a p-value of 0.11 based on 45, 43, 6, and 9 samples of MSS & UBE2H-H, MSS & UBE2H-L, MSI & UBE2H-H and MSI &UBE2H-L (data file 1, Sheet 8). The statistical insignificance of the multivariate Cox proportional hazards model analysis may be due to the small sample size.

Analysis of the copy number variation and independent DNA methylation data of UBE2H from the cBioPortal database and the MEXPRESS database revealed that copy number variation among different patients is also correlated with UBE2H expression (r = 0.334, p < 0.001), as well as with the DNA methylation level of three CpG sites on the promoter (p = 0.017) (see Figure S4).

The interactive effect of UBE2H and CMS subtypes was examined by comparing the overall survival of UBE2H-high versus UBE2H-low in CMS1–4 subtypes, CMS1–4 subtypes in the UBE2H-high group, CMS1–4 subtypes in the UBE2H-low group and CMS1–4 subtypes irrespective of UBE2H expression level. The results showed that the CMS1 and CMS4 subtypes had significantly higher overall survival rates than the CMS2 and CMS3 subtypes in the UBE2H-high group (Figure S5A), whereas there was no statistically significant difference in survival in the UBE2H-low group (Figure S5B). However, there was a significant survival difference between the CMS2 & CMS3 subtypes versus CMS1 and CMS4, irrespective of UBE2H-H or UBE2H-L (Figure S5C). These results suggest that there is an interactive effect between the transcriptional level of UBE2H and CMS subtypes on the survival rate. However, when we used the respective median level as the threshold for the UBE2H-high and –low groups in each CMS subtype, we observed an opposite prognosis, albeit marginal, prognosis (P = 0.055; Figure S5D). In fact, there is a statistically significant higher transcriptional level of UBE2H in CMS4 than in CMS1–3 (Figure S5E). Normalizing the raw counts data for each gene expression in each cell to transcripts per million (TPM) and analyzing colorectal cancer scRNA-seq data (GSE132465) revealed that UBE2H expression is lower in the MMRd epithelial subtype than in the MMRp epithelial subtype (Figure S5F). This is consistent with the results obtained from bulk transcriptomics analysis (Fig. 3E).

When we compared the expression levels of the immune checkpoint genes, PD-1 (CD274), PD-L1 (PDCD1), CTLA-4, LAG3, TIGIT, and HAVCR2 in the UBE2H-high and –low groups of TCGA, we found statistically significant differences for CTLA4, PDCD1, and TIGIT (Figure S6A). UEB2H expression and subcellular location in colorectal cancer cells

Searching the HPA database revealed that UBE2H is a mitochondrial protein (Figure S7A) that is differentially expressed across tissues (Figure S7B) and cell lines (Figure S7C). We then investigated which cancer cell states UEB2H is most highly expressed in by analyzing scRNA-Seq data through the HPA database. We observed that UBE2H is most highly expressed in cluster 8 of undifferentiated cells (Figure S7D), which are located at the base of crypt of the colonic crypt and also express the CR2(CD21), SLC2A1 (GLUT1) and MKI67 genes (Figures S7E and S7F). This suggests that these cells are counterparts of very malignant cell clusters in corresponding cancer tissues. As undifferentiated cells in normal colon tissue are mainly located at the base of the crypt and consist of intestinal stem cells, transit-amplifying cells and progenitor/precursor cells, the resistant colorectal cancer cells (UBE2H-high epithelial cells) may originate from these types of stem and proliferative cells as in cancer initiation and progression by acquiring new mutations in genes such as APC, SMAD4, TP53 and KRAS. A previous study demonstrated that UBE2H determines the neurogenic potential of hESCs for UBE2K [47], suggesting its involvement in development. The highly expressed CR2 (CD21) gene, also known as complement receptor 2, is predominantly expressed on mature B cells and follicular dendritic cells to prevent B cell proliferation. Its expression is lower in colon cancer tissue, and the presence of glucose addiction (high GLUT1 gene expression) suggests that UBE2H, a proliferative and stem-associated mitochondrial marker of colorectal cancer cells, hijacks the B cell proliferation mechanism system through the complement system-mediated antigen presentation and B cell proliferation pathways in the cancer cells, to promote the resistance in these clusters of colorectal cancer cells. In fact, our spatial transcriptomics analysis revealed an enriched complement signalling pathway network between cancer cells and intermediate, myofibroblast and stromal2 cells in (Figure S2). This result merits experimental validation.

Cell-type-specific functions associated with oxaliplatin as revealed by cell-type-specific pseudobulk scRNA-seq data analysis at cell-type level

We generated the cell-type-specific pseudobulk transcriptome data from scRNA-Seq data and performed the DEGseq2 analysis on this data for each patient, after grouping the scRNA-seq data into UBE2H-high and UBE2H-low groups (see Data File 5). In the UBE2H-high group of epithelial cells, the up-regulated genes were enriched for immunity and inflammation, while the down-regulated genes were enriched for artery development. This suggests that immune therapy could complement oxaliplatin therapy, and that anti-angiogenic could enhance its efficacy. In the UBE2H-high group of CD8+ T cells, the down-regulated genes were enriched for oxidative phosphorylation, electron transfer activity, down-regulation of TGF-beta signalling pathway, and RNA and DNA synthesis. The up-regulated genes were enriched for antigen binding, immune system processes, extracellular exosome and FCGR3A-mediated IL-10 synthesis. In the UBE2H-high group of fibroblast cells, down-regulated genes were enriched for transcription regulator activity, chemoattractant activity, vascular endothelial growth factor receptor 1 binding, positive regulation of transcription by RNA polymerase II, reactive oxygen species, and oxidative phosphorylation, respiratory chain complex (gamma subunit) mitochondrial, and RNA and DNA synthesis. The up-regulated genes were only enriched for fatty acid binding (S100A8 and NME2), monocarboxylic acid binding (S1008 and NME2), and protein histidine kinase activity (NME2). These results suggest that UBE2H may downregulate the cancer stromal fibroblast activity in the tumor microenvironment to promote the cancer spread. In macrophages, the upregulated genes in the UBE2H-high group are enriched for antigen binding and immune processes involving immune checkpoint molecules and tumor-supporting factors such as LILRA1 [48], MIF [49], CRIP1 [50], PSMB10, NME2 [51], CAMP, S100A8 [52] and S100A1 [52]. In contrast, the genes that are downregulated, including MT-ND3, CEACAM5, PLS1, KLF5, VIL1, EP300, TNFSF9, ZNF787 and AGAP1, are enriched for regulation of microvillum organization and AP-2 transcription factor binding motifs. The involvement of these macrophage markers in respective pro- [53] or anti-cancer processes suggests that UBE2H may affect the function of cancer-associated macrophages in regulating the response to and prognosis of oxaliplatin treatment for colorectal cancer. In endothelial cells, the UBE2H-high group suppresses genes enriched for the cell cycle process, the DNA damage response, the regulation of primary metabolic processes, circadian rhythms, striated muscle cell differentiation, replication fork processing, and cell population proliferation. However, it activates genes enriched for chemokine receptor binding, Toll-like receptor 4 binding, fatty acid binding, immune system processes, the sequestration of zinc ions, neutrophil aggregation, autocrine signaling and extracellular space. This consistently suggests that an exhausted inflammatory microenvironment is unfavorable for the colorectal cancer response to oxaliplatin treatment (see Supplementary Data Files 3, 5–6 and Table 4 ).

We further integrated the three oxaliplatin-treated bulk transcriptome datasets using comBat [20] and then deconvoluted the cell-type-specific markers associated with the response to oxaliplatin using the top 500 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in bulk. We observed there are significant lists of cell-type-specific differentially expressed genes for the nine cell types, particularly for the epithelial cells (see Data File 6). The up-regulated genes associated with epithelial cells in the sensitive group were enriched for DNA-binding transcription activity, RNA polymerase II-specific activity, mononuclear cell differentiation, the response to oxygen-containing compounds, CD4-positive, alpha–beta T cell differentiation, the apoptotic process and chromatin and tissue development. Down-regulated epithelial-associated genes in the sensitive group were enriched for serotonergic neuron axon guidance, involving CELSR3 and FZD3, as well as abnormal male internal genitalia morphology, involving HGD, SERPINA1, UNG and BRNP1 (see Data File 6). These results are consistent with those of the scRNA-seq analysis. In the bulk analysis, cell death is related to oxaliplatin resistance, whereas in the deconvoluted epithelial cell-associated DEG results, epithelial cell death is related to oxaliplatin sensitivity. This suggests the prognostic roles of the differentially enriched pathways obtained from bulk analysis are specific to cell subtypes. See Fig. 6 for the computationally generated hypothesis illustration.

Discussion

In this study, we proposed a bioinformatics pipeline for identifying prognosis biomarkers. By integrating the transcriptomes and DNA methylation of multiple GDSC cell lines with different IC50 values for oxaliplatin treatment, we identified differentially expressed genes that were potentially regulated by DNA methylation in oxaliplatin resistance. Upregulated genes in oxaliplatin-sensitive cell lines showed enriched Gene Ontology terms relating to gene expression and amino acid metabolism, as well as a variety of enriched transcription factor motifs. These included motifs for c-Myc:Max, Sp1, ZF5, Pax-6, E2F-2, Egr-1, Ap-2, E2F-3, Tcf-1, E2F-1, Churchill, Tfap2a, Sp2, Elk1, Sp4, Ap-2 alpha, Rox, Irx2a, WT1, Tfap2a, Mycn, Zxdl, E2f-4, Klf3, Tfap2c, N-Myc, Hif-1 alpha, Kaiso, Sp3 and Bteb4. and Ap-2 gamma. MYC:MAX interaction has been reported to be an important drug target for MYC-dependent tumor cell proliferation [54]. LSD1/LINC01124/SP1/p62 has been reported to play a critical role in oxaliplatin resistance in HCC [55]. The inconsistency of the reported resistance roles of these transcription factors in resistance and our results of a favorable role are likely due tothat the fact that these transcriptions are modified by different modulators. These transcription modifiers may exert a similar effect in pro-oxaliplatin or anti-oxaliplatin treatment by significantly sensitizing or anti-sensitizing the effect of oxaliplatin, as with the anti-oncogenic IRF1 modification by the oncogenic IRF2. We hypothesize that if these transcription factors are lost, oxaliplatin will lose its targets, and thus lead to a higher IC50. However, the true mechanism merits further study.

Enriched terms for up-expressed genes in resistant cell lines, included immunity processes, cell death, system development, interspecies interactions, and neurogenesis regulation. Binding motifs for the transcription factors IRF-1, IRF-2, IRF-7, IRF-8, IRF-5, IRF-3, IRF-9, STAT2 and Helios were also enriched. There are nine IRF family members, and these nine IRF family members have varied functions [56]. Oxaliplatin binds to DNA, forming crosslinks that inhibit DNA replication and transcription. This leads to DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs), apoptosis, and tumor cell death [57]. IRFs respond to the DNA breaks and can promote colorectal cancer during oxaliplatin treatment, probably through their context-dependent pro-oncogenic functions. In this context, IRF2 is oncogenic and represses IRF1 activity and that of other IRFs by binding to them [58]. Tt is also generally anti-inflammatory, whereas IRF1 is anti-oncogenic [59]. It has also been reported that IRF2 is post-translationally regulated in a cell growth-dependent manner, whereby acetylated IRF2 binds preferentially to the H4 promoter in proliferating cells only [60], where UBE2H is also highly expressed as revealed in this study. Another study has reported that IRF2 restricts anti-cancer immunity by driving interferon-mediated CD8 + T exhaustion [61]. Tumor-associated macrophages expressing IRF8 promote T cell exhaustion in cancer [62]. Furthermore, IRF3, IRF7 and IRF9 are upregulated in tumor tissues, and patients in the low-IRF score group have a better prognosis. IRF3 and IRF7 expression is related to worse prognosis in patients with CRC [63] and a IRF3 is a master regulator of an aberrant and malignancy-specific gene expression program in multiple myeloma [64]. IRF7 was identified as an unfavorable prognostic factor in a pan-cancer analysis [63], while another study found that introducing IRF7 into macrophage had an anti-tumor effect [65]. The contradictory roles of IRF7 in different studies may be associated with its cell-type-specific functions. The rich profiles of enriched binding motifs for a variety of transcription factors suggest that the identified genes are likely to be regulated by DNA methylation [66]. The transcriptional reprogramming of the colorectal cancer cells to a different response when exposed to oxaliplatin.

When we examined the enriched TF binding motifs for UBE2H, we did not observe any motifs, suggesting that UBE2H was possibly down-regulated by the chromatin compartment in the sensitive group. Interestingly, we observed a greater number of the hypermethylated resistant genes (71) than hypomethylated sensitive genes (21), the mechanism behind this is worthy of further study.

Enrichment of an amino acid metabolism and growth transcription factors in the oxaliplatin sensitivity genes suggested an association with the DNA methylation regulatory mechanism in oxaliplatin sensitivity and resistance. The products of the amino acid metabolism may regulate the cell proliferation and dedifferentiation via related transcription factor or modifiers via through post-transcriptional modification [67], which further dysregulates the expression of genes involved in development, cell death and immunity, thereby conferring sensitivity to oxaliplatin treatment. Examining the roles of these oxaliplatin-resistance-associated genes in survival prognosis and their independent association with overall survival in colorectal cancer revealed that UBE2H is a prognostic factor in independent datasets. Colorectal patients with higher UBE2H expression is a shorter survival time. UBE2H is an ubiquitin conjugating enzyme E2 that was first identified and named as Ubc8 in yeast [68]. It belongs to a conserved family of E2s. In humans, UBE2H expression is found in the placenta, as well as in skeletal and cardiac muscles. It is controlled by tumor necrosis factor-alpha/nuclear factor kappa B (TNF-α/NF-κB) [69], which is involved in immune suppression. UBE2H has also been associated with neurodegenerative diseases [70]. Other investigators have observed higher expression of UBE2H in lung adenocarcinoma, which is linked to poor outcomes and metastasis [71]. Further investigation into the UBE2H clinical significance by pan-cancer overall survival analysis revealed that UBE2H is also an unfavorable prognosis marker for pancreatic cancer. Consistently this study also revealed UBE2H as a resistance marker of the colorectal cancer to oxaliplatin.

Another study [72] showed that colorectal cancer can be classified to four molecular subtypes: CMS1 (microsatellite instability immune, 14%), CMS2 (canonical, 37%), CMS3 (metabolic, 13%) and CMS4 (mesenchymal, 23%). CMS1 is a hypermutated, microsatellite unstable subtype with strong immune activation. CMS2 is characterized by WNT and MYC signaling activation in epithelial cells. CMS3 has evident metabolic dysregulation and a relatively higher mutation burden than CMS2 and CMS4. CMS4 has prominent transforming growth factor–β activation, stromal invasion and angiogenesis. There are also samples with mixed features (13%). The results of our immune infiltration analysis agree with the consensus molecular subtypes. We propose that CMS1 (an immune hot colorectal subtype), which are enriched with dMMRand CMS4 (mesenchymal) may not benefit from oxaliplatin treatment, while CMS2 and CMS3 possibly will. Interestingly, our survival analysis results for the UBE2H-high and –low groups are consistent with the FOxTROT cohort study reported at 2020 ASCO Annual Meeting IA [73], which indicated that pMMR, an immune-cold environment, is more favorable for oxaliplatin treatment than dMMR. Co-expression of immune regulatory genes and checkpoint inhibitors suggests that the exhausted microenvironment in the UBE2H-high group, which has a higher number of checkpoint molecules may benefit from the immune treatment.

A previous study showed that colorectal cancer cells could originate from both stem and non-stem cells [74] after acquiring an APC mutation, as well as additional mutation or activation of the NF-kappa B pathway (inflammation) events. Another report [75] proposed that cancer cells usually maintain the phenotype of the original cell type and that the different origins may confer sensitivity or resistance to different treatments. Our scRNA-Seq data analysis shows that UBE2H is highly expressed in cluster 8 of dedifferentiated colon tissue cells, suggesting that oxaliplatin-resistant cancers may be associated with cancer cells that are counterparts to this cluster of cells. These cells also highly express CR2, SLC2A1 (which encodings the GLUT1 protein), and MKI67. These cancer cells, which highly express CR2, SLC2A1 and MK167, could also proliferate and dedifferentiate [76], hijacking the B cell complement system [77] by highly expressing the CR2 gene, which is likely a mechanism for the high proliferation of cancer cells [78]. We also observed that these cluster of cells was characterized by glucose addiction as indicated by high levels of GLUT1 (SLC2A1) expression.

Additional analysis of its tissue, subcellular and cellular location through the HPA databaserevealed that UBE2H is primarily located in the mitochondria of A-431 cells (a human skin cancer cell line), U-251MG cells (a glioma cell line derived from a male patient), and U2OS cells (a human osteosarcoma cell line). These results suggest that UBE2H may be involved in mediating oxaliplatin resistance in different cell types including both epithelial and non-epithelial cells. Therole of UBE2H mediating the oxaliplatin function in mitochondria warrants further study [79]. Our GSEA analysis suggests suppressed mitochondrial function in the UBE2H-high colorectal cancers.

Our immune infiltration analysis indicates that UBE2H is positively associated with several cell types except type 17 helper cells in the colorectal cancer tissues. These results suggest that UBE2H is associated with metabolic and immune exhaustion pathways. In related work, we also observed an association between UBE2B and neoadjuvant radiotherapy resistance in rectal cancer (GSE3493). Other ubiquitin-conjugating enzymes (E2s), including UBE2I in GSE45404 and UBE2Q2 in GSE35452 were also unfavorable for neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (data provided elsewhere). Our results suggest that UBE2H is involved in pathways that respond to oxaliplatin and chemoradiotherapy, including cell stress response and immunity. Using bisulfite DNA sequencing to measure the DNA methylation level of the UBE2H promoter region in patient-derived samples, we found no methylation in anyof the seven colorectal cancer samples collected.

Our work has limitations. The UBE2H-risk model is built only from the computational analysis of publicly available patients’ data of transcriptome and DNA methylome. Experimental characterization of the methylation of UBE2H CpG sites was performed on a limited number of colorectal cancer samples. We did not validate the causal effect of the UBE2H on the oxaliplatin resistance. However, our results provide a testable hypothesis that suggests cellular heterogeneous UBE2H is a marker of cancer resistance. Our proposed computational pipeline can also be used to identify prognostic markers for treatment resistance in other diseases. Our future work will include an in vitro immune organoid cohort and in vivo UBE2H functional validation.
