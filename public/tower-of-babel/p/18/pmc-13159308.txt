Efficacy and safety of GLP-1 receptor agonists for adolescents and children with obesity: a meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
Authors: Qirong Chen, Haixia Liu, Jie Xu, Yu Zhou, Junying Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the efficacy and safety of glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists in the treatment of obesity among children and adolescents.MethodsWe conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) that compared GLP-1 receptor agonists with placebo in children and adolescents with obesity. Literature retrieval, study selection, and data extraction were performed according to the PRISMA guidelines. Pooled mean differences (MDs) and risk ratios (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using RevMan 5.3 software.ResultsFourteen RCTs comprising 1349 participants were included, with 810 patients assigned to GLP-1 receptor agonist treatment (liraglutide [n = 317], exenatide [n = 125], semaglutide [n = 265], dulaglutide [n = 103]) and 539 to placebo. Compared with placebo, GLP-1 receptor agonists significantly reduced body weight (MD = -4.50; 95% CI: -6.40 to -2.60; P < 0.0001), body mass index (BMI) (MD = -1.65; 95% CI: -2.05 to -1.26; P < 0.00001), hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) (MD = -0.34%; 95% CI: -0.63 to -0.05; P = 0.02), and fasting blood glucose (MD = -0.21; 95% CI: -0.41 to -0.01; P = 0.04). The overall incidence of adverse events was similar between groups, with no significant difference in serious adverse events. However, gastrointestinal adverse events were more frequent in the GLP-1 receptor agonist group.ConclusionsGLP-1 receptor agonists are effective in reducing weight and improving glycemic parameters in children and adolescents with obesity, with an acceptable safety profile. Variations in efficacy may exist among different agents within the drug class.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12902-026-02248-4.Keywords: GLP-1 receptor agonists, Obesity, Children, Adolescents, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Over the past five decades, the global incidence of childhood obesity has risen markedly. By 2016, the prevalence of obesity among children and adolescents reached 5.6% in girls and 7.8% in boys. It is approximately 206 million individuals aged 5–19 years living with obesity by 2025, and this number is expected to rise to 254 million by 2030 [1–3]. China is predicted to become the country with the highest number of children and adolescents with obesity by 2030 [4]. Obesity during childhood and adolescence frequently persists into adulthood [5], underscoring the importance of early intervention.

Childhood and adolescent obesity represents a major public health challenge. It is not only a nutritional disorder that impairs growth and development but also a significant risk factor for chronic conditions such as diabetes, hyperlipidemia, asthma, and essential hypertension [6–9]. Additionally, obesity adversely affects mental health in this population [10–11]. Identifying safe and effective interventions is therefore crucial for managing childhood obesity.

Current treatment strategies primarily focus on lifestyle modifications, including dietary changes and increased physical activity [12–14]. However, adherence to a balanced diet and regular exercise is often slow and challenging for adolescents with obesity, necessitating adjunct pharmacological therapy. The glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists liraglutide and semaglutide have been approved by both the U.S. Food and Drug Administration and the European Medicines Agency for long-term weight management in adolescents aged 12 years and older [15–18]. GLP-1 receptor agonists lower blood glucose levels by suppressing glucagon secretion from pancreatic alpha cells and stimulating insulin release from beta cells. Additionally, they promote satiety, reduce appetite and energy intake, and delay gastric emptying [19–23]. However, the efficacy and safety of these agents in children under 12 years of age remain controversial.

Although several studies have investigated the use of GLP-1 receptor agonists for treating obesity in children and adolescents, inconsistencies in study design and sample size have led to divergent conclusions. This meta-analysis aims to systematically evaluate the existing evidence to clarify the efficacy and safety of GLP-1 receptor agonists in the management of childhood obesity, thereby supporting evidence-based health management strategies for affected children.

Methods

This meta-analysis was arranged in accordance with the PRISMA guidelines [24].

Literature search

A systematic search was performed in the PubMed, EMBASE, and Cochrane Library databases for literature published between January 2005 and January 2025, focusing on obesity in children. The search strategy incorporated the following MeSH terms and keywords in various combinations: “obesity” OR “obese” OR “overweight” AND “children” OR “child” OR “paediatric” OR “boy” OR “girl” OR “teen” AND “glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonist” OR “GLP-1” OR “exenatide” OR “liraglutide” OR “dulaglutide” OR “semaglutide”.

Study selection and inclusion criteria

Two investigators independently screened the retrieved records to identify studies meeting the inclusion criteria. This meta-analysis focused on trials evaluating the efficacy and safety of GLP-1 receptor agonists for weight management in children and adolescents. The screening process involved an initial exclusion of irrelevant studies based on title and abstract, followed by a full-text review of potentially eligible articles to exclude those not conforming to the criteria.

The inclusion criteria were as follows:

(1) Population: children and adolescents ≤ 18 years of age who met the diagnostic criteria for obesity; (2) Study type: randomized controlled trials (RCTs); (3) Intervention: use of a GLP-1 receptor agonist in the experimental group, with a placebo as the control; (4) Exclusion criteria: studies where the intervention group received additional pharmacotherapy besides GLP-1 receptor agonists; retrospective observational designs; conference abstracts, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, narrative reviews, editorials, reports, and case series.

Any discrepancies during the screening process were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached among all authors.

Data extraction and outcome measures

Data were extracted independently by two authors using a standardized form. The following information was collected from each included study: first author, year of publication, patient age and gender, drug type and dosage, BMI, body weight, HbA1c, fasting blood glucose, adverse events, study design, and follow-up duration. Discrepancies in data extraction were resolved by re-examining the original articles.

Subgroup analyses were planned based on the type of GLP-1 receptor agonist and patient age.

Quality assessment

The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool. The evaluation covered the following domains: random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of participants and personnel, blinding of outcome assessment, completeness of outcome data, selective reporting, and other potential sources of bias. An overall judgment of ‘low risk’ was given if the study was at low risk of bias in all domains ‘Some concerns’ was given if the study was rated as having some concerns in at least one domain. A ‘high risk’ judgment was given if the study was at high risk of bias in at least one domain, or had some concerns for multiple domains.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted using RevMan software version 5.4. Treatment effects were expressed as relative risk (RR) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for dichotomous outcomes. Heterogeneity among studies was quantified using the I² statistic; an I² value greater than 50% indicated substantial heterogeneity, warranting the use of a random-effects model; otherwise, a fixed-effects model was applied. Potential publication bias was assessed through visual inspection of funnel plot asymmetry supplemented by regression analysis if applicable. Sensitivity and subgroup analyses were conducted to explore potential sources of heterogeneity. For studies that reported outcomes using medians and ranges (or interquartile ranges) instead of means and standard deviations, we estimated the latter using the validated method described by Hozo et al. [25]. This method provides formulas for converting the median, minimum, and maximum values to an estimated mean, and the range to an estimated standard deviation, based on the sample size.

Results

The screening process is outlined in detail in Fig. 1. After a full-text review of 33 articles, 14 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) were ultimately included in the analysis [26–39]. These studies encompassed a range of focuses: some assessed weight loss efficacy and glycemic control, others examined the relationship between dosage, pharmacokinetics, and therapeutic outcomes, and several emphasized adverse events. The meta-analysis incorporated a total of 1349 children and adolescents, with 810 assigned to the intervention group (comprising 317 receiving liraglutide, 125 exenatide, 265 semaglutide, and 103 dulaglutide) and 539 to the control group.

Flow diagram of studies included in the meta-analysis

Among the included trials, one study compared dulaglutide to placebo, seven compared liraglutide to placebo, four evaluated exenatide against placebo, and two assessed semaglutide versus placebo. The risk of bias was categorized as low, medium, or high. All included studies were RCTs, among which six trials (42.8%) exhibited issues with missing data. Subgroup analyses were conducted based on the type of medication and age. Participants older than 12 years were categorized as the adolescent group, while those younger than 12 years were categorized as the pediatric group. Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots, Egger’s test, and Begg’s test. No significant bias was detected for BMI (Begg’s test, p = 0.710).

Weight

Nine of the 14 studies reported changes in body weight. Compared with placebo, children in the intervention group showed a significant reduction in weight (MD = − 4.5, 95% CI: − 6.4 to − 2.6, P < 0.0001; Fig. 2). Semaglutide was associated with the most substantial weight loss, showing a reduction of 14.7%. Subgroup analysis indicated that GLP-1 receptor agonists produced significant weight loss effects both in children and adolescents. Although most agents demonstrated efficacy, one study noted weight gain among adolescents even after receiving adequate dosing.

BMI

A total of 1267 participants from 13 studies reported changes in BMI. Treatment with GLP-1 receptor agonists resulted in a significantly greater reduction in BMI compared to placebo (MD = − 1.65, 95% CI: − 2.05 to − 1.26, P < 0.0001; Fig. 3). Subgroup analysis by drug type revealed that all active-treatment groups achieved significantly greater BMI reduction relative to controls (Fig. 4), with the most pronounced decrease observed in the semaglutide subgroup. Reductions were significant across both children and adolescents when stratified by age (Fig. 5).

Glycosylated hemoglobin and fasting blood glucose

GLP-1 receptor agonists were more effective than placebo in reducing HbA1c levels (MD = − 0.34, 95% CI: − 0.63 to − 0.05, P < 0.0001). In subgroup analyses, both semaglutide and liraglutide significantly lowered HbA1c. Seven studies reported changes in fasting blood glucose, indicating that GLP-1 receptor agonists conferred a greater reduction compared to placebo (MD = − 0.21, 95% CI: − 0.41 to − 0.01, P = 0.04). Liraglutide and exenatide each showed significant effects in lowering fasting glucose in subgroup analyses.

Complications

No significant difference was observed in overall adverse events between the GLP-1 receptor agonist group and the placebo group (RR = 1.02, 95% CI: 0.96–1.09, P = 0.52; Fig. 6). However, gastrointestinal reactions—such as nausea and vomiting—were more frequent in the intervention group (RR = 3.17, 95% CI: 2.31–4.35, P < 0.0001). There were no statistically significant differences between groups in the incidence of serious adverse events. In addition, no differences in overall adverse events were detected between age subgroups (children vs. adolescents) or among different types of GLP-1 receptor agonists.

Discussion

The rising prevalence of childhood and adolescent obesity represents a critical global health challenge. Given the multifactorial etiology of obesity, its management remains particularly difficult. Current therapeutic strategies include lifestyle modification, pharmacotherapy, and bariatric surgery. Although lifestyle interventions constitute the first-line approach, long-term adherence to dietary and physical activity recommendations is often low among children and adolescents. Consequently, adjuvant treatments such as anti-obesity medications and metabolic surgery are commonly required.

A variety of GLP-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs)—including short-acting liraglutide, long-acting exenatide, and semaglutide—are available for clinical use. These agents facilitate weight loss and improve glycemic control by enhancing insulin secretion, suppressing glucagon release, delaying gastric emptying, reducing appetite, and promoting satiety through activation of GLP-1 receptors. This meta-analysis comprehensively evaluates the efficacy and safety of GLP-1RAs in the management of pediatric obesity. The results demonstrate that, compared with placebo, GLP-1RAs significantly reduce body mass index (BMI), improve glycemic parameters, and are generally well tolerated.

Notably, one included study reported weight gain in adolescents despite adequate dosing, although the weight increase was significantly greater in the control group than in the GLP-1RA group [33]. This still supports the overall efficacy of GLP-1RAs in weight control, and the observed weight gain in both groups may be attributed to normal adolescent growth. Several studies highlighted a correlation between drug dosage and therapeutic efficacy [40], although no significant association was found between treatment duration or dosage and the incidence of complications [28]. Beyond weight and glucose benefits, some evidence suggests that GLP-1RAs may also improve lipid profiles and blood pressure [39], though their long-term cardiometabolic benefits warrant further investigation.

Once-weekly semaglutide appears to offer superior efficacy in reducing body weight and BMI compared to other GLP-1RAs. Since no significant differences were observed among agents regarding safety profiles, semaglutide may represent a preferable option within the drug class.

This meta-analysis confirms that GLP-1 receptor agonists significantly reduce body weight, BMI, fasting blood glucose, and glycated hemoglobin levels in obese children and adolescents compared to placebo. Subgroup analyses indicated consistent treatment effects across different age groups, with no age-related differences in the incidence of adverse events. Limitations

This study has several limitations. The number of available randomized controlled trials is still limited, and sample sizes are generally small, which may affect the robustness and generalizability of the findings. Most trials had relatively short follow-up periods, making it difficult to evaluate long-term efficacy and safety. There is also a lack of head-to-head comparisons between different GLP-1RAs or with other anti-obesity agents. Fourth, our meta-analysis included populations with heterogeneous underlying causes of obesity. For instance, one included trial enrolled patients with Prader-Willi syndrome, a genetic disorder characterized by hyperphagia and distinct pathophysiology. It is plausible that the efficacy of GLP-1 receptor agonists on weight loss may vary across these different etiologies. This clinical heterogeneity is a potential limitation that may have influenced our pooled effect estimates and contributed to the statistical heterogeneity observed in some of our analyses. Future research should focus on large-scale, long-term randomized trials to further assess the durability and safety of GLP-1RA therapy in pediatric populations. And future studies targeting specific etiological subgroups are warranted 0Investigations into combination therapies involving GLP-1RAs and other treatment modalities are also needed to enhance therapeutic outcomes. Furthermore, studies evaluating the impact of long-term GLP-1RA use on growth and development in children and adolescents are essential to ensure its safety.

Conclusion

This meta-analysis demonstrates that GLP-1 receptor agonists are effective and well-tolerated for weight management in children and adolescents with obesity. Clinical application should involve comprehensive patient evaluation, individualized treatment planning, and ongoing monitoring.
