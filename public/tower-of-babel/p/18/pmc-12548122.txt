A systematic review of the mechanisms of cardiovascular disease reduction by dietary flavonoids: the impact of anthocyanins on flow-mediated dilation and blood rheology
Authors: Shiva Khani, Jeremy Spencer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEpidemiological studies have suggested that anthocyanin, a dietary flavonoid found in a variety of foods, may protect against cardiovascular disease (CVD). The purpose of this review is to assess the effects of anthocyanin and anthocyanin-rich extracts on flow-mediated dilation (FMD), a vascular function marker, in healthy people and investigate their potential significance.MethodA thorough search of databases, including PubMed and Web of Science, was done to locate randomised controlled trials (RCTs) published before July 1st, 2024. The evaluation concentrated on English-language RCTs involving persons aged 20 to 65 years. Studies that did not examine anthocyanin or had insignificant results were eliminated. Data extraction comprised study characteristics, participant demographics, interventions, and results. The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias in Rob 2 tool.Results15 studies out of 381 were found to meet the inclusion criteria. Anthocyanin-rich meals or extracts significantly improved flow-mediated dilation (FMD) (P < 0.05), with most studies reporting significant benefits within two hours of ingestion. However, some research found no significant differences, underscoring the diversity of the results.ConclusionThis comprehensive systematic review supports the idea that anthocyanin-rich foods or extracts can improve vascular function in healthy people, as evidenced by FMD. However, small sample sizes, varying anthocyanin doses, short intervention periods, and diversity in study methods limit the present body of evidence. Future studies should concentrate on well-powered, long-term randomised controlled trials using standardised anthocyanin formulations and dose regimens together with studies across varied demographics to improve generalisability in order to create unambiguous clinical guidelines.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40795-025-01086-2.

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) continues to be one of the leading causes of death worldwide, significantly straining populations and healthcare systems [1, 2]. CVD is any condition characterised by heart and/or blood artery anomalies, such as atherosclerosis and coronary artery disease [3]. Concurrently, as the global population ages, so do concerns about emergence of neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer's [4]. However, epidemiological studies show that interventions and preventive measures in diets high in fruits and vegetables reduce the risk of such disorders and help to improve or maintain vascular function as we age [5, 6]. One area that has received much interest is the effect of dietary components, particularly flavonoids, on cardiovascular health [7]. Flavonoids are a type of poly phenolic chemical found throughout the plant kingdom [8]. These secondary metabolites give vibrant colours to many fruits and vegetables and have been linked to health benefits [8, 9]. Anthocyanin, which are mostly present in berries [10] and other vividly coloured fruits, have risen to prominence due to their possible health advantages [11]. Several studies have connected their consumption to a lower risk of chronic diseases such as cardiovascular disease [12]. FMD is one of the most common approaches for measuring vascular reactivity [13]. FMD evaluates arterial health by assessing its response to increased blood flow; impaired FMD predicts future CVD risk [14]. As a result, enhanced FMD is commonly seen as a sign of improved endothelial function and cardiovascular health [15]. It is known that polyphenols, anthocyanin included, stimulate vasodilatory pathways like nitric oxide synthase (NOS) and cyclic guanosine monophosphate (cGMP) signaling, leading to relaxation of vascular smooth muscle cells [16, 17]. Additionally, they boost the production of endothelial nitric oxide (NO) and enhance insulin sensitivity, aiding in endothelium-dependent relaxation [18]. Further mechanisms could include the adjustment of redox signaling, which encompasses the activation of the Nrf2 antioxidant pathway and the reduction of NF-κB-mediated inflammation [19]. The exact molecular pathways are still not fully understood, and due to inconsistencies in bioavailability, study design, and population characteristics, it has been difficult to reach definitive conclusions. Emerging research suggests that the health of our circulatory system, particularly the rheological qualities of blood, may be inextricably linked to cardio metabolic stroke [20]. Hemorheology, or the study of blood flow qualities, focuses on parameters such as blood viscosity, plasma viscosity, and red blood cell deformability and aggregation [21]. Changes in these factors might disrupt the transport of oxygen and nutrients to important organs, including the brain [22]. A diet high in flavonoids, particularly anthocyanin, may offer a CVD decline prevention strategy [23]. While the health advantages of flavonoids, particularly anthocyanin, have been extensively researched, the precise processes by which these chemicals exert their beneficial effects on cardiovascular health are unknown. There is a specific gap in understanding how anthocyanin regulate vascular function, specifically through blood rheology modification and flow-mediate dilution. This systematic review intends to fill a knowledge vacuum by synthesising evidence from randomised controlled trials (RCTs) to determine the effect of anthocyanin consumption on vascular function markers such as FMD. Furthermore, this study investigates the possible benefits of anthocyanin on cardiovascular health, specifically whether the favourable effects on blood flow might be used as a preventive strategy against CVD decline. This review aims to uncover the processes underlying the protective effects of flavonoids by investigating the function of blood viscosity and its interaction with cardiovascular risk factors, informing future preventative and treatment methods in cardiology.

Method

The PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) statement [24] was used to conduct a systematic review. It contained all relevant PRISMA checklist items (see appendix in the Supplementary Information).

Eligibility of criteria

Studies that examined the impact of anthocyanin consumption on flow mediated dilation (FMD) in humans were included in the current review. Studies have to be RCTs of acute or chronic anthocyanin ingestion and characterise the anthocyanin flavonoid content in order to be considered. Studies were disregarded if they were published in a language other than English without a readily available translation. Only persons between the ages of 20 and 65 who were in generally good health at baseline were eligible to participate. People with long-term illnesses including diabetes, inflammatory diseases, or cardiovascular disease were excluded. Search strategy

A systematic literature search was conducted using the PubMed and Web of Science databases in January 2024 (updated search conducted in July 2024). The following search strategy was used to find terms related to anthocyanins, berries, flavonoids, and flow-mediated dilation: ("anthocyanin"[All Fields] OR"anthocyanins"[All Fields] OR"anthocyanins"[MeSH Terms] OR"anthocyanins"[All Fields] OR"anthocyanin"[All Fields] OR ("berries"[All Fields] OR"berry"[All Fields]) OR ("flavonoid"[All Fields] OR"flavonoid"[All Fields] OR"flavonoids"[All Fields] OR"flavonoid"[All Fields] OR"flavonoids"[MeSH Terms] OR"flavonoids"[All Fields] OR"flavonoid"[All Fields])) AND ("FMD"[All Fields] OR"flow mediated dilation"[All Fields]) in pubmed, and the other search strategy which was used in Web of science was (anthocyanin* OR berr* OR flavonoid*) AND (FMD OR “flow mediated dilation” OR"Blood vessel dilation").

There was no time limit included in our search strategy. Unpublished studies will not be sought. The search strategy included only the key terms appropriate for the topic of interest and the filters specific to each database. The search strategy was narrowed to RCT studies.

Study selection

The study selection process is shown in Fig. 1. Studies were eligible for inclusion if the following applied: randomized controlled trial (RCT) design, comparison of anthocyanin or anthocyanin-rich extracts with a placebo or control, adult participants aged 20–65 years, FMD evaluation, clear anthocyanin dose reporting, published in English. The age range was chosen to allow for a more accurate evaluation of early vascular responses to anthocyanin intake by concentrating on typically healthy adult populations prior to significant age-related vascular degeneration. Only research that explicitly stated the dosage of anthocyanin was included. We didn't use estimates from outside sources like Phenol Explorer. Both authors (Sh.Kh. and J.S.) separately screened the titles and abstracts of every publication that was found. After that, full-text publications of possibly pertinent research were evaluated in light of the inclusion criteria. Any disagreements were settled by consensus-building and dialogue.Fig. 1Flow diagram of the literature search process based on the PRISMA statement

381 records in all, including 185 from PubMed and 196 from Web of Science, were found using database searches. 211 articles underwent screening after duplicates (n = 95) were eliminated and 75 records were eliminated based on title and abstract screening. The exclusion of 57 papers was based on their focus on irrelevant populations (such as improper age ranges) or their use in animal research. After that, 154 full-text articles were looked for and obtained. Nevertheless, 45 articles were either not retrievable or did not evaluate a recognized cardiovascular function measure, like FMD. The eligibility of 109 items in total was evaluated. 94 of these were eliminated, for a variety of reasons, including the lack of a control or placebo group, the anthocyanin dose not being reported, the absence of baseline data, or the ambiguity of the results reported, 94 of these were eliminated. In the end, 15 articles were included in the qualitative analysis after fulfilling all inclusion requirements.

Procedure for study selection

Data were extracted from each from studies eligible for inclusion. The first author's name, registered trial number, sample size at the stages of recruitment and enrolment, inclusion and exclusion criteria, study design, dietary products utilized during the interventions, as well as the Δ change in FMD as outcome, was extracted from each study.

Risk of bias or quality assessment

The risk of bias in the eligible trials was assessed by following the Cochrane risk of bias 2 (RoB2) tool for Systematic Reviews of Interventions criteria [26]. To generate the resulting scores, the following elements of the risk of bias were graded using the parameters (shown in brackets): (1) selection bias (sequence generation and allocation concealment); (2) performance bias (blinding of participants and staff); (3) detection bias (blinding of outcome assessment); (4) attrition bias (incomplete outcome data); (5) reporting bias (selective reporting); and (6) potential other sources bias. Both a low and a high risk of bias were determined by the evaluation of each score. Additionally, many research used diverse types of control foods or drinks, which were not standardized across trials. Because variations in flavour, appearance, or nutritional content may impact participant blinding and physiological responses unrelated to anthocyanin ingestion, this variation may induce performance or detection bias. Limitation

One main limitation of this review is the inconsistent use of anthocyanin treatments in different research. The delivery of anthocyanins, whether in the form of whole foods, juices, or refined extracts, varies widely, which may have an impact on how the body absorbs and processes them. This could be one of the reasons why different experiments had different results. Individual differences in gut flora, genetics, age, sex, nutrition, and health state can also affect how anthocyanin intake reacts in different people. The body's ability to metabolise anthocyanins can be impacted by these factors, which can also obscure significant effects on vascular outcomes like FMD. Finally, it was not possible to conduct a meta-analysis because of significant variations in study design, intervention type, and outcome reporting. Rather, to best represent the current data on anthocyanin intake and vascular health, a qualitative synthesis was carried out [27].

Of the studies, 8 investigated the effects of acute interventions [28–35], 4 were chronic interventions [36–39], and 3 of the studies [38, 40, 41] specifically examined the effects of acute and chronic consumption of anthocyanin. The primary conclusions of the current review include a summary of the research on the impact of various anthocyanin sources (mostly blueberries, cranberries, strawberries, strawberries, blackcurrants, raspberries, and oranges) on vascular function. Raw fruits, beverages/smoothies, or extracts in capsule form were offered as the anthocyanin sources. Both the type of anthocyanin-rich food and the dosage used had an impact on the flavonoid anthocyanin content. Acute studies of FMD

Table 2 reports the main results obtained in 11 short-term studies of anthocyanin interventions that used flow-mediated dilation to assess vascular function.

A considerable percentage (about 80%) of the analysed trials showed that anthocyanin-rich therapies significantly improved flow-mediated dilatation (FMD). Interestingly, standardised polyphenol-rich juices or extracts, especially cranberry juice [28], blueberry beverages [32], açaí smoothies [29], and pure anthocyanin capsules [33], were consistently associated with acute effects 1–2 h after ingestion. Moderate to high anthocyanin dosages (> 160 mg) were generally administered by these interventions, demonstrating the significance of both dose and bioavailability in obtaining vascular effects. For instance, cranberry juice with 1238 mg of total polyphenols (including approximately 30 mg of anthocyanin) increased FMD by + 2.6% at 4 h after intake [28], whereas blueberry drinks (containing 196–339 mg of ACNs) and açaí (493 mg of ACNs) consistently improved FMD by 1%–2% [32]. Endothelial function has been demonstrated to be temporarily improved by acute consumption of fruits or extracts high in anthocyanin. Remarkably, within 1–2 h after intake, single-dose treatments with aronia extract, strawberry drinks, and high-flavonoid apples improved FMD by + 0.8% to + 1.5% [34] These benefits were stronger in overweight or healthy men, which may indicate population-specific responsiveness [30, 35].

Standardised anthocyanin dosing may be essential to attaining quantifiable vascular benefits, as evidenced by studies that used whole fruit formats with low anthocyanin content or mixed-polyphenol profiles (e.g., aronia whole fruit, low-flavonoid apple) and which tended to produce smaller or non-significant effects [42]. It's interesting to note that some data suggests a dose–response relationship, with half-maximal benefits in FMD occurring at anthocyanin dosages of 131–150 mg [35]. However, effects were not significantly stronger at excessively high doses. Lastly, certain studies with smokers or mixed-gender groups had more inconsistent outcomes, indicating that anthocyanin efficacy may also be influenced by sex, baseline vascular function, and lifestyle (smoking) [31, 41].

Chronic studies of FMD

Table 3 displays the key findings from 7 long-term trials of anthocyanin treatments that evaluated vascular function using flow-mediated dilation.

Most of the anthocyanin therapies, whether from juices, powders, or capsules, improved flow-mediated dilatation (FMD) in long-term trials that lasted 2–12 weeks, especially when the daily consumption was ≥ 50 mg of anthocyanin [37, 41, 42]. Statistically significant increases in FMD relative to baseline or control were found in about 80–90% of studies [36, 37, 40–42]. It is noteworthy that several forms, including anthocyanin capsules [41], blood orange juice [37], aronia extract [42], whole fruit powder [42], and blackcurrant juice [36], demonstrated similar vascular effects, indicating no consistent superiority of one form over another. For instance, over a 12-week period, FMD was enhanced by + 1.2% and + 0.9%, respectively, by aronia extract and whole fruit [42]. Overall, the evidence supports that chronic anthocyanin intake, when sufficiently dosed and sustained, improves endothelial function, with the greatest impact seen in those at elevated cardiovascular risk. Studies using lower doses or conducted in healthy participants with normal baseline endothelial function often showed minimal or no effect [38, 39]. Higher doses and longer durations were generally more effective, especially in populations with impaired vascular function such as overweight or hyper-cholesterolemic individuals [40–42].

Comparing the bioavailability of fresh fruits and juices with extracts and capsules

The form of the intervention can have a substantial impact on the bioavailability of anthocyanin and related (poly)phenols [43]. Although extracts and capsules frequently provide more consistent and concentrated dosages of particular bioactive, such anthocyanin, they might not contain the complex food matrix that whole fruits or juices have, which might affect absorption and digestion [44]. According to certain research in this review, both formats help FMD, however the time and strength of vascular responses can differ [28]. According to Rodriguez-Mateos et al. (2013) [28], for example, cranberry and blueberry juices demonstrated dose-dependent acute improvements in FMD, while purified extracts such as aronia extract [42] and anthocyanin capsules [41] also had acute and/or long-term effects. Whole fruit therapies, such as high-flavonoid apples or aronia whole fruit, however, demonstrated less pronounced or more gradual effects [40, 42]. These results might be due to variations in the gut microbiota's metabolism, absorption kinetics, and the presence of co-nutrients such fibres, which might change or slow the bioavailability of anthocyanin [43].

Potential risks of bias

The participants'and the outcome assessment's blinding during RCTs were the factors linked to the largest risks of bias. In the latter case, a few researches acknowledged the incomplete outcome data and allocation concealment. The many research covered in this evaluation regularly discussed and examined selective reporting. When it was impossible to make the control product and the fruit product indistinguishable from one another, bias concerns were considerable. Despite the fact that all of the studies in this analysis were RCTs, the majority of them only included a mediocre explanation of the randomization procedures employed, if any. The possibility of an uneven allocation, which can have an adverse impact on the dependability of the data, should be taken into consideration while attempting to blind the randomization process in nutritional interventions.

Discussion

This review analysed 15 RCTs exploring the cardiovascular effects of flavonoids, and anthocyanin in fruits. The studies primarily utilized flow-mediated dilation (FMD) as an indicator of vascular endothelial function, reliable measures associated with cardiovascular health. RCT are pivotal for determining causal relationships between interventions and effects [13, 45]. The consistent improvements in FMD shown across investigations give strong support for the cardiovascular advantages of fruit-derived (poly)phenols [46]. The reviewed studies, despite varied methodologies, converge on anthocyanin's positive impact on vascular function, evidenced by improved FMD. While numerous intervention studies have assessed flavonoid-rich foods'effect, few systematic reviews have summarized the often inconsistent results [47, 48]. This review's findings show that acute therapies with anthocyanin improved FMD (marker of vascular reactivity) at 2 h. Also, studies collectively indicated that a broad range of anthocyanin-rich fruits consistently enhanced FMD, indicating improved endothelial function. This suggests that the protective effects are not confined to a specific fruit but are more likely associated with the broader class of flavonoid present across these fruits [28]. The majority of RCTs reviewed showed significant improvements in vascular reactivity after consuming anthocyanin-rich foods or extracts, despite varying effect sizes. Studies involving cranberries [28], açaí [29], red raspberries [30], aronia berries [42], blackcurrants [31], apples [40], blueberries [35], and strawberries [34], consistently support this finding. Extracts tended to produce slightly larger and more consistent improvements, especially at lower doses and over shorter durations, even though both whole fruits and extracts showed positive effects on flow-mediated dilatation (FMD). This is probably because extracts have a higher anthocyanin concentration and are more bioavailable [41, 42]. Whole fruits, however, frequently offered metabolic advantages that went beyond FMD and might work better in groups with pre-existing vascular impairment [34, 40].

The reviewed studies were highly heterogeneous in terms of regimens, dosages, duration, settings, and populations, complicating direct comparisons and potentially limiting the generalisability of the findings. This heterogeneity can be explained by changes in the types of anthocyanin-rich meals consumed, variations in bioavailability, and the research populations'various characteristics.

Furthermore, many of the studies had potential biases, such as insufficient blinding and small sample numbers, which could jeopardise the accuracy of their findings.

Dosage and Timing Dependence

Time and dose dependencies were also prevalent themes in the reviewed studies. Several studies observed a dose-dependent relationship between anthocyanin intake and improvements in FMD at varying times, with many reporting multiple time points at two-hour intervals, which is inconsistent across all studies. This inconsistency makes it challenging to compare them directly. Some studies have specifically reported changes in FMD at the two-hour mark, and examining these similarities is insightful. Some of these studies such as [28, 30, 33, 35, 42], and [49] with blueberry, cranberries and raspberries, demonstrate dose-dependent increases in FMD, suggesting that higher concentrations of anthocyanin do not consistently produce greater benefits. This plateau effect may be due to saturation of anthocyanin absorption pathways and limited bioavailability at higher doses, where excess compounds are likely excreted or metabolized without contributing further to vascular effects [27]. The peak effects observed at different time intervals post-consumption (ranging from 1 to 8 h) across the studies further indicate that the timing of the assessment plays a critical role in capturing the maximal benefits of polyphenol consumption on vascular function. Rodriguez-Mateos et al. found a dose-dependent effect of blueberry flavonoid intake on FMD, implying an ideal (poly) phenol dosage for maximum vascular effects. Several investigations found that peak FMD benefits occurred at varying time intervals after consumption (1 to 4 h), implying that (poly) phenol bioavailability and metabolism have a role in timing vascular effects. In a trial of ten healthy males, cranberry juice consumption resulted in dose-dependent FMD augmentation, with peak effects at 4 h and best results from juice containing 1238 mg of total cranberry (poly) phenols. Plasma metabolites associated with better FMD were found [28]. Also, Rodriguez-Mateos et al. found similar peaks in postprandial FMD in healthy men who consumed blueberries and a second peak at 6 h, and this correlated with peaks in plasma concentrations of phenolic acid metabolites and a decrease in neutrophil NAD(P)H oxidase activity in that study. In another study, we observed peaks in FMD at 1–2 h post consumption of the AS [35]. This time course might represent the appearance of phenolic metabolites in plasma following modest intestinal absorption and, at 6 h, the release of phenolic compounds during colonic fermentation [50]. It's interesting to note that studies using high dosages of anthocyanin did not detect significantly more of an effect than those using anthocyanin-rich extracts with more physiologically feasible ingestion [28, 30, 33, 35, 42]. The additional vascular effect of anthocyanin may be limited at higher doses due to poor bioavailability or intestine transporter saturation, when excess anthocyanin are quickly metabolised or eliminated. Furthermore, moderate dosages might be more in line with the body's ability to efficiently absorb and use anthocyanin [51]. Also, in all of these studies, the peak improvement and the most increased level of FMD was after 2 h, and The RCTs that were a part of this review did not provide any evidence of dose–response correlations. The presence of additional dietary bioactive substances may have synergistic effects or may contribute to a threshold effect in the anthocyanin-rich extracts [52]. Collectively, these results support the use of anthocyanin-rich foods and supplements in cardiovascular disease prevention efforts, while more research is needed to completely comprehend the processes and full range of effects. Accordingly, research shows that anthocyanin-rich therapies have a favourable impact on cardiovascular health by raising flow-mediated dilation (FMD). These repeatable findings point to the potential of anthocyanin to reduce cardiovascular risk via a variety of mechanisms. HDL cholesterol levels and blood pressure have improved, but the full impacts and mechanisms, particularly those on blood viscosity, are still poorly understood, underscoring the need for more research in this exciting field. Epidemiological studies have also shown an inverse relationship between anthocyanin consumption and cardiovascular disease outcomes. For example, McCullough et al. found a significant dose–response relationship, showing that higher anthocyanin intake (3.8–22.2 mg/day) was associated with lower age-adjusted CVD mortality among 38,180 men and 60,289 women [53]. This current review suggests that future research should focus on the long-term effects of supplementation in order to find more significant improvements in vascular function. In acute vs chronic investigations, anthocyanin ingestion is also more effective at boosting FMD responses [54]. Although acute studies frequently showed bigger impact sizes than chronic studies, the review's limited coverage of acute trials makes it difficult to draw conclusive conclusions. Overall, the evidence supports both acute and sustained improvements in FMD following anthocyanin consumption. The interpretation of continuous study changes may indicate improvements in vascular tissue health or function. Some chronic studies, however, did not show improvements due to high FMD data heterogeneity. For example, Dohadwala et al. found no change in vascular reactivity in CHD patients following four weeks of cranberry juice treatment (94 mg/day anthocyanin) [55]. Given that established CVD has been associated with a substantially defective or aberrant endothelial response to vasodilatory stimuli, it is probable that anthocyanin's efficacy in this population is limited [56, 57]. Additionally, compared to other chronic studies demonstrating a significant improvement in FMD (12 to 320 mg/day), Vaisman and Niv given a significantly lower amount of anthocyanin (6.7 mg/day for 12 weeks) [58]. An earlier systematic evaluation discovered a non-linear dose-dependent connection between FMD responses and consumption of particular flavonoids classes [54]. This suggests that, to detect a significant change in vascular function, a minimum dose must likely be consumed. Both acute and chronic dietary interventions with other plant polyphenol–rich foods, such as pomegranate juice [59], dark chocolate [60], and cocoa [61–64], induce similar changes in vascular function. Similar to the results of the articles included in this review,, Flow-Mediated Dilation (FMD) increased after the ingestion of cocoa, specifically at the 1-h and 2-h post-ingestion marks on each study day [65].

Methodological Variations Strengths and limitations

Although the results were generally consistent, methodological differences between the research should be considered. These include variations in sample size, participant traits, doses, and other outcome measures besides FMD. Additionally, some studies included flaws including incomplete blinding or small sample numbers that could have affected the reliability of the findings. When assessing the results as a whole, it's crucial to take into account these variances and limitations. Due to the significant study variability regarding berry type, dose, delivery technique, and polyphenol bioavailability, it is important to use caution when interpreting the effects of berries on vascular function. Although combining different berries adds value, the variations in their phenolic components can have a big impact on results. Berry fruits are known to have different anthocyanin profiles, which may be a key element influencing there in vivo impact on vascular function [66, 67] which makes it difficult to compare study findings because anthocyanin consumption varies based on the berries and fruits eaten. The absence of reliable data regarding the bioavailability of anthocyanin and associated metabolic products is another drawback of this review. The pH and bacteria of the stomach have a significant impact on the metabolic destiny of anthocyanin after consumption [68]. Anthocyanin, like other phenolic chemicals, are known to have a low bioavailability of less than 15% [69, 70]. Furthermore, because various other phenolic compounds contained in berries are similarly metabolised in the gut, it might be challenging to link the biological effects of berry consumption to microbial derivatives of anthocyanin in the gut [71]. The majority of the imitative investigations were done on healthy persons and varied in design, duration, participant characteristics, and sample size, which contributed to the variability and equivocal conclusions of the original studies. Some well-conducted research had tiny participant groups, making the findings tentative. Limitations include a lack of uniform protocols and different approaches to evaluating vascular function results. The manipulation of nitric oxide-dependent and -independent vasodilation processes could have affected the results under various experimental settings. To overcome the discrepancies and limitations found in the examined studies and to validate the preliminary findings, more research is necessary [72].

The review focuses on the constant vascular benefits of foods high in flavonoids, especially anthocyanin, which have an impact on FMD both acutely and over time. However, the methodology of the study and dietary factors affect the results. Results are influenced by variables such as anthocyanin content, metabolite types, and individual metabolism; however, only certain markers were examined, leaving out variables like blood pressure and cytokine levels. The mechanisms involve interactions with gut flora, antioxidant activity, and direct vascular effects. To determine the best flavonoid doses, frequency, and synergistic effects with other dietary components for vascular health, more research is necessary. Investigating the structure–activity correlations of berry polyphenols could reveal underlying mechanisms. Studies typically point to advantages at dosages higher than 200 g/d, which directs more research. Research on the relationship between dose and time can help produce vasoactive products that can prevent cardiovascular disease in particular populations. More research is needed to validate these findings and discover the best settings for consuming anthocyanin to maximise cardiovascular benefits.

Conclusion

This review shows that, despite some limitations and confounding variables in the studies examined, there are signs of potentially beneficial effects of berries on vascular function, affecting flow-mediated dilation in both short- and long-term studies. The results indicate that the biomarker alterations may vary based on the study techniques and duration of the food intervention. To determine the true impact of supplementing, it is stressed that more thorough, extended trials across a range of groups are required.
