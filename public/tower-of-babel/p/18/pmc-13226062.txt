Building a gene editing lexicon: a model for rare and inherited disorders
Authors: Leonard A. Valentino, Cedric Hermans, Donna Coffin, Wolfgang Miesbach, Maria Elisa Mancuso, Carmen Unzu, Micheala Jones, David E. Gutstein, William McKeown, Craig M. Kessler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
As more advanced cell and gene therapies, including gene editing technologies, progress through drug development, there is increased emphasis on the importance of stakeholders, including people living with disease, caregivers, and healthcare professionals, to communicate using clear, accurate, and consistent language. Lexicons explaining advanced gene therapies will support patients’ and clinicians’ understanding, enabling shared decision-making and informed consent for clinical trial participation and, in the future, healthcare choice. Early lexicon development is crucial for standardizing communication across clinical sites, geographies, clinicians, and patients. A lexicon for clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats-associated protein 9 (CRISPR-Cas9) gene editing for hemophilia was developed using comprehensive methodologies, gathering insight through qualitative research and in-depth interviews, language audits, and workshops, with input from lived experience experts, leading clinicians in hemophilia, gene therapy experts, and scientific and patient organizations. This lexicon serves as a gold standard template for future comprehensive patient lexicon development strategy and could be applied to other therapeutic areas where treatments are being developed and standardized, or where accessible vocabulary for patients, healthcare professionals, and the affected community is lacking. This communication highlights the need for lexicon development for advanced gene editing treatments across therapeutic areas to support standardized understanding and enhance communication.

Introduction

Although the past 30 years have seen rapid progression in the development of treatments for hemophilia, breakthrough bleeding, progressive joint disease, and the continued need for regular, long-term repeated intravenous infusions or subcutaneous injections remain a substantial burden for many people with hemophilia (PWH) [1–4]. Advanced therapy medicinal products (ATMPs), such as the clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR)-associated protein 9 (Cas9) system, may offer the potential to address the unmet needs of the hemophilia community [5–7]. In this era of precision medicine, gene editing could transform outcomes for PWH by addressing the fundamental and monogenic causes, thereby eliminating the need for continuous treatment [7–9]. To ensure successful adoption of gene-based therapies in the future [10], it is critical to establish a vocabulary that is understood and shared across multiple stakeholders. Lexicons will support understanding by patients and clinicians, thereby enabling shared decision-making and informed consent for clinical trial participation [11] and, in the future, healthcare choice.

What is a lexicon?

For the purposes of this review, we defined a lexicon as an entire vocabulary – including words, their meanings, and usage – to explain terms in a specialist field at a foundational level, using clear and consistent language that can be understood globally by different stakeholder audiences. Although the terms “lexicon” and “glossary” can be used interchangeably, a lexicon comprises an extensive database, whereas a glossary may be more focused and limited in scope, encompassing a list of commonly used terms in a particular specialist field.

In recent years, hundreds of lexicons and glossaries have been published in peer-reviewed journals on medical, phonological, and linguistic topics, with increasing numbers in the past five years (a general PubMed search for “lexicon” or “glossary” in the title/abstract returned >2100 citations published from 2020 to 2025 compared with <1300 citations from 2015 to 2020). However, most lexicons do not take a robust approach to their development methodology, including rationale, testing, and application, which is necessary for adoption by disease communities. For example, the National Institute of Standards and Technology developed a lexicon for gene editing aimed at the biotechnology community that supports accurate communication of concepts, data, and results [12]; however, the content is technical and requires a relatively high level of understanding of scientific concepts that would likely reach beyond the scope of some of the hemophilia community. For CRISPR-Cas9, a gene editing lexicon must explain key concepts of the technology and be understood by the broad hemophilia community. Healthcare professionals (HCPs) and patient advocacy groups should be key contributors to the development of a lexicon in this setting.

Lexicons support communication between all stakeholders

A strong rationale for developing a lexicon is the unification of care and communication across the entire disease community: patients and caregivers (termed lived experience experts, LEEs) [13], physicians, patient advocates, nurses, reimbursement agencies, drug developers, and regulators. Quality communication exchange is a key variable linking health literacy and improved health outcomes [14, 15]. It must be tailored to the understanding of each individual and allow them to access relevant, targeted, and trustworthy information from their national, regional, or global hemophilia or healthcare patient organization [4]. An example of refining verbiage for improved understanding and perception of disease among HCPs and patients includes consensus approaches taken to redefine nonalcoholic fatty liver disease to metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease [16], and subsequently to metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease [17]. These updates extend beyond a semantic revision and remove stigma for people living with steatotic liver disease, as previous links to alcohol were associated with decreased healthcare seeking (e.g., among children and religious groups in which alcohol intake is prohibited) [18]. Further, the change in nomenclature has facilitated clearer diagnostic criteria, increased public awareness, and improved research funding [18, 19].

Aligned communication is also important for promoting shared decision-making, which empowers patients to make informed treatment decisions, fostering transparency and adherence to treatment plans, and contributing to improved health outcomes and overall wellbeing [20]. A united approach to communication supports multidisciplinary teams, facilitates patient-centric drug development through patient advocacy group involvement, and enables discussions between regulatory authorities and payors. Such information would encompass the risks and benefits of treatment, the complexities of treatments and dosing, the eligibility criteria for treatment and clinical trial participation, and what to expect from treatment.

With hemophilia therapies evolving from clotting factor replacement to advanced treatment, so too must the education on the disease and its remedies. Although identification of the specific genetic mutation was important for family screening purposes when clotting factor concentrate replacement therapy was the mainstay of treatment, it held no direct therapeutic implications. Fundamental shifts in the treatment landscape mean that diseases can be treated at the genetic level – either by introducing a functional copy of the defective genetic material into the body or by correcting the original genetic error through gene editing. This profound transition demands a solid understanding of basic genetic concepts and gene therapy mechanisms, articulated through clear and appropriate language.

In this review, we highlight the need to develop a lexicon for advanced gene editing treatments such as CRISPR-Cas9, discuss current approaches in hemophilia and in other therapy areas, and demonstrate how lexicons can support standardized understanding and enhanced comprehension in the medical community.

Recent approach to developing a gene editing lexicon in hemophilia

Rationale for developing a gene editing lexicon in hemophilia

The robust development of a gene editing lexicon for advanced treatments for hemophilia can serve as a template for other therapy areas in the gene therapy space, in which opportunities and barriers for patients are similar. Partnering with patient advocacy groups to support the adoption and dissemination of the lexicon ensures that patients remain the priority. Patient organizations could, in turn, influence how language is used in broader discussions of medical and scientific innovations in the media and encourage mainstream journalism to align with robust lexicons. Studies show that educational materials for patients can also deliver considerable cost savings for healthcare systems [21]. A freely available gene editing lexicon made accessible to the hemophilia community has the potential to save HCPs and patient organizations considerable expense.

Below, we describe an approach to a challenging but critical task: developing a gene editing lexicon that meets the needs of the hemophilia community to ensure an improved understanding of gene editing platforms and their potential utility. One of the biggest challenges associated with implementing new and advanced treatment options like gene editing is the involvement of complex concepts and technologies that could be unfamiliar to some members of the hemophilia community. The main goal of this lexicon was to develop a globally aligned language – one that can establish a communications framework that facilitates shared decision-making and informed consent regarding gene editing and treatment considerations in hemophilia [10].

Approach to the design and creation of a lexicon for the hemophilia community

An additional layer of complexity is introduced due to existing heterogeneity in the language used to discuss technical vocabulary among key audiences, i.e., LEEs, HCPs, patient advocacy groups, regulatory agencies, and payors. Owing to these challenges, a gene editing lexicon was developed through an innovative partnership among hemophilia, gene and cell therapy, and biotechnology organizations (Fig. 1) [10, 22, 23]. This collaboration facilitated insight gathering through qualitative research and in-depth interviews, language audits, and workshops with LEEs and hematologists across the USA, the UK, and Germany (Fig. 1, Stage 1–5) [22]. The lexicon was finalized following input from a lexicon steering committee composed of global experts from leading scientific and patient organizations in hemophilia and gene editing fields (Fig. 1, Stage 6) [22]. These efforts led to the development of optimized language recommendations for a clear, consistent gene editing lexicon (Fig. 1, Stage 7) [22]. The major learnings can be categorized into three key themes: insights into audience mindsets, guiding language principles, and optimized terminology for key topics like gene editing concepts and post-treatment considerations [22]. Audience mindsets revealed cautious optimism about gene therapy, with more skepticism toward gene editing [22]. Guiding language principles indicated a preference for plainspoken over technical language, definitions that link to patient benefits, and explanations that highlight the precise nature of gene editing [22]. This collaborative approach ensures broad adoption of the lexicon in the hemophilia community and readiness for real-world environments [23]. Testing and disseminating a lexicon for the hemophilia community

The lexicon has been beta-tested with two large hemophilia patient advocacy groups, the National Bleeding Disorders Foundation in the USA and the European Haemophilia Consortium. The collaborative, community-based approach used in the development of the hemophilia lexicon makes it particularly well-suited for further testing, as the initial participants represent a cross-section of key stakeholders involved in hemophilia gene editing therapy.

Subsequently, the lexicon can be disseminated in different media and used in various channels, depending on the target audience and its utilization. Partnership with leading organizations and patient advocacy groups is key, enabling open discussions with stakeholders, access to patient groups for beta testing, and alignment of lexicons across multiple platforms. Furthermore, the publication and citation of robust lexicons in peer-reviewed journals adds credibility, supporting the implementation of a standardized language. Key partners in lexicon dissemination and implementation include organizations integrating scientific expertise with patient engagement, such as the World Federation of Hemophilia, and scientific societies like the International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis. These organizations can leverage engaging resources, such as gene editing infographics, plain language summaries, videos, and social media, similar to such materials covering gene therapy hosted on the World Federation of Hemophilia website [24]. Scientific organizations can communicate the lexicon to HCPs and LEEs via training and educational materials, manuscripts and presentations outlining scientific data and concepts, and engaging publication extenders, such as podcasts and videos. Infographics are particularly useful for operationalizing the lexicon and for reaching and empowering LEEs. The manuscript on the practical application of the hemophilia gene editing lexicon exemplifies this approach with a four-page infographic visualizing lexicon language, which is available upon request from the authors [23].

Importance of a shared lexicon in the hemophilia community

Shared decision-making

Complex scientific concepts must be translated such that people living with disease understand the information required for shared decision-making and informed consent, enhancing control over their medical care. Implementing a robust approach to developing a gene editing lexicon will strongly support shared decision-making by enabling clear and consistent communication on key aspects of treatment, such as eligibility, treatment effectiveness, side effects, and duration of effect. Shared decision-making, closely related to patient empowerment, patient-centered care, and evidence-based patient choice, should take a collaborative approach whereby patients and physicians share information and the responsibilities for decision-making [25]. Shared decision-making is very important for ATMPs such as gene therapies and gene editing, as treatment is intended to have lifelong effects. Therefore, patients should understand the characteristics of these therapies, such as efficacy, durability, ability to re-dose, and side effects, to inform evidence-based treatment decisions. For example, patients receiving adeno-associated virus-based gene therapy will develop neutralizing antibodies that currently preclude repeat therapy [26], and the durability of response to such treatment is yet unknown; therefore, patients need to know what to expect from their treatment and what remains uncertain [27].

Informed consent

Related to shared decision-making is informed consent, i.e., the autonomous authorization of an individual to accept a medical intervention; it comprises an important and mandatory, formal, legal, and ethical step before allowing treatment. Informed consent should occur during physician and patient discussions regarding a proposed treatment, including alternative approaches, and the risk–benefit profile of these options [4, 28]. Lexicons should be created prior to initiating clinical trials or receiving marketing authorization, providing an early opportunity to optimize communication to potential participants, caregivers, and clinicians. However, many lexicons or glossaries are developed after clinical trials have started. Thus, informed consent requires a full understanding of the possibilities and outcomes of receiving a particular treatment.

Impact of a lexicon for ATMPs

The principles of precision medicine can be met by considering a broad audience for a lexicon. For ATMPs, educated patients can be empowered to advocate for their care, i.e., identify that a particular treatment is right for them or that they are the right patient for that treatment. Improving health literacy is a global goal recognized by the World Health Organization [29], and the burden of health literacy regarding a condition or treatment should not fall solely on the patient; instead, providers and other stakeholders should be both respectful and culturally competent in considering the patient’s level of understanding while fostering trust in this relationship [30, 31]. Given the novelty of ATMPs such as CRISPR-Cas9, and the associated lack of long-term data, several uncertainties exist (e.g., off-target effects [32] or immunogenicity [33]). These should also be acknowledged in discussions with patients.

In contrast, developing ATMPs without considering a common language to communicate such complex concepts and the potential impacts on care and patient outcomes, risks clinical trials failing to sufficiently recruit or achieve informed consent, high drop-out rates, and bias [11, 28, 34, 35]. Therefore, consistent terminology should be established for any fundamental concepts during the patient’s treatment journey, particularly in a rapidly evolving modality such as gene editing. A rigorous approach to the development of a lexicon will further aid discussions with patients and have a positive impact for ATMPs.

Lexicon adoption beyond hemophilia

In recent years, efforts have been made to standardize, simplify, and employ medical terminology for conditions and their advanced treatments in several therapeutic areas beyond hemophilia. However, the huge growth in ATMPs, reflected in >2000 gene therapies in development globally [36], demonstrates the clear and urgent need for tailored educational content to help stakeholders understand how these treatments work. The resources on ATMPs described herein mainly comprise website-based, patient-focused educational materials and glossaries. However, for these internally developed lexicons, the methodology used in their development and for determining preferred vocabulary is not commonly reported, and it is unclear whether these documents align across platforms and organizations. A robust approach to lexicon development is critical, as the entire hemophilia community demands participation and an active role in their development.

The biotechnology company bluebird bio developed a suite of educational resources for patients on gene therapy aimed at an audience of “beginners to advanced learners”, including concepts and terms used to describe lentiviral gene therapy for beta-thalassemia and sickle cell disease [37]. Topics include descriptions and examples of genetic diseases, gene therapy approaches and clinical trials, a comprehensive glossary defining terms used in their resources, and step-by-step explanations of the gene therapy process [37]. The Thalassaemia International Federation has also produced a comprehensive patient glossary with scientific terms, biomarkers, symptoms, and treatments for beta-thalassemia and sickle cell disease [38], with gene therapy definitions linking to further educational resources [39]. Together, these resources aim to improve patients’ and other stakeholders’ understanding of advanced gene therapy. Similarly, gene editing terms are described in the GenePossibilities educational materials developed by Vertex Pharmaceuticals for patients and HCPs, hosted on separate dedicated sites [40, 41]; notably, the patient glossary includes phonetic pronunciation. It is interesting that, despite the availability of lexicons for diseases such as hemophilia, beta-thalassemia, and sickle cell disease, patient interest and uptake of gene therapies have been slow [42].

Intellia Therapeutics has developed a glossary defining CRISPR terms for the rare, progressive disease [43], transthyretin amyloidosis. A full-spectrum approach also covers terms used to describe their CRISPR treatments for other genetic diseases under investigation, such as hereditary angioedema [43]. Généthon, Ultragenyx, and Verve Therapeutics are actively developing gene therapies for other rare metabolic liver diseases, including Crigler–Najjar syndrome, ornithine transcarbamylase deficiency, and hypercholesterolemia, and have also developed patient educational materials [44–46]. Pharmaceutical companies with advanced gene therapies approved or under investigation for rare genetic neurologic conditions also have patient educational materials available. For example, uniQure hosts a patient website that provides information on their range of neurologic diseases under investigation (e.g., Huntington’s disease, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, and mesial temporal lobe epilepsy), including patient stories and patient advocacy resources [47]. Other resources of note include the Cystic Fibrosis Foundation website, which hosts educational materials, including types of questions for patients to ask before enrolling in clinical trials and a glossary specific to cystic fibrosis gene therapy [48]. More generally, the American Society of Gene & Cell Therapy provides educational materials for many indications with a genetic origin, as well as information on gene therapy, gene editing, and clinical trials [49].

In a PubMed literature search of peer-reviewed journal articles with “lexicon” appearing in the title, Hermans et al. [10] appear to be unique in outlining a robust methodology (importantly incorporating input from LEEs) for a published lexicon encompassing an ATMP, with the goal of educating various stakeholders in the community, including people living with disease (eTable 1). This further emphasizes the need for robust methodology in the development of lexicons across therapeutic areas and positions the gene editing lexicon as the potential blueprint for the construction of these resources.

Dissemination of the lexicon

With the development of ATMPs, more than one company may be responsible for the journey to approval of a specific treatment. The development of independent, siloed resources or lexicons risks including inconsistent terminology and language describing the same treatment. Therefore, information and language describing concepts should be aligned between resources, with efforts taken to maintain up-to-date information [23]. A combined approach taken by patient advocacy groups, professional organizations, and pharmaceutical companies would demonstrate a recognition of the importance of patients self-advocating for potentially life-changing treatment decisions. However, the methods for determining preferred language, key topics, and level of understanding are usually not documented, making it unclear whether language aligns across platforms. Thus, pharmaceutical companies should prioritize aligning resources and terminology when partnering with other companies or patient advocacy groups. Further, adopting the proposed model of lexicon development that is importantly vetted by all stakeholders should help to ensure that pharmaceutical companies avoid commercial bias when presenting their products.

Conclusions

The advent of gene editing in hemophilia treatment may offer the opportunity to transform the lives of PWH, but may add to the complexity of treatment decisions. HCPs and patient advocacy groups will play a crucial role in the development of lexicons; identifying patient information needs, assessing how best to communicate information, and providing appropriate personalized guidance to ensure that PWH fully understand the available options. Although many published lexicons and educational resources exist for patients and HCPs, most do not document a robust methodology. The involvement of a wide group of stakeholders, including a steering committee, expert HCPs, and groups representing patient perspectives, and global research in developing a lexicon can improve understanding of gene editing and other advanced therapies for the entire hemophilia community; such approaches can be adopted into other disease communities. Access to such tailored educational resources could ultimately support shared decision-making and informed consent to participate in potentially transformative clinical trials.
