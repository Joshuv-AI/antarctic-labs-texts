Advancing ultrafast (S)TEM with the combination of an RF cavity and an electrostatic beam blanker
Authors: Gabriele Bongiovanni, Mark M. J. van Rijt, Ondřej Sháněl, Erik R. Kieft
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) has significantly advanced fields such as materials science, nanotechnology, and structural biology by providing detailed structural and analytical information at picometer resolutions. To further enhance TEM's capabilities, time-resolved electron microscopy introduces the temporal domain, using ultrafast electron pulses to capture dynamic processes. Traditional methods generate these pulses via photocathode illumination by femtosecond lasers and face challenges like complex alignment and limited repetition rates. An alternative approach employing electronic devices as beam choppers, specifically resonant RF cavities in combination with electrostatic beam blankers, simplifies alignment and increases repetition rates, achieving picosecond and sub-picosecond pulses. Additionally, these devices do not compromise the performance of the microscope in any other imaging mode. The microscope can be rapidly toggled between continuous and pulsed imaging, providing flexibility of operation in modern research labs. This study integrates these beam choppers into high-end TEMs and demonstrates their effectiveness in achieving high temporal resolution for pump–probe experiments. Results show that these methods maintain high spatial resolution and coherence, making them a promising solution for ultrafast electron microscopy.

INTRODUCTION

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) has revolutionized numerous fields, including materials science, nanotechnology, and structural biology.1–3 By providing structural and analytical information with spatial resolutions reaching a few tens of picometers4,5 TEM reveals crucial details about the structure, bonding, morphology, and composition of a wide array of samples.

Moving beyond static images, time-resolved electron microscopy introduces the temporal domain to structural and analytical studies. In this technique, samples are transiently excited inside the electron microscope, and their responses are recorded in a movie-like fashion. To surpass the temporal resolution offered by electron detectors, the electron beam is modulated into short pulses, striking the sample at precise delays after the stimulus. The creation of electron pulses of sub-picosecond duration, pioneered by Ahmed Zewail,6,7 opened the field of ultrafast electron microscopy (UEM). Since its inception, UEM has been applied to uncover a plethora of fascinating nanoscale phenomena, ranging from ultrafast phase transitions8,9 to the quantum aspects of light–matter interactions,10–12 and recently even sub-light cycle attosecond dynamics.13,14

In most UEM studies, ultrashort electron pulses are generated by illuminating a photocathode with a femtosecond laser pulse. After adjusting the parameters of the electron emitter to suppress continuous emission, photons with appropriate energy extract photoelectron packets. These packets then strike the sample while the dynamics, typically initiated by a pulse derived from the same laser, are unfolding.

In recent years, beam chopping has been proposed as an alternative to photoemission.16–19 In a typical implementation (see, e.g., Figs. 1 and 2), the beam chopper periodically deflects the electron beam away from the optical axis, where it is blocked by an aperture. The undeflected portion of the beam forms electron pulses that ideally retain the same properties as the continuous beam during normal operations. Interest in beam choppers is due to several of their properties. First, this approach does not require aligning a laser to the gun filament, which can be challenging and time consuming. Beam choppers do not directly interact with the gun, which is kept under normal operation, minimizing the probability of filament burnout or instability.15 Finally, RF modules can deliver pulses at GHz repetition rates, which represents an increase in current by 2–3 orders of magnitude compared to photoelectron pulses with a similar brightness.21,22

Pulses of a few picoseconds,19 or even sub-picosecond duration20 have been reported in electron microscopes utilizing RF beam choppers. Synchronization between RF cavity modules and pulsed laser systems is generally known from particle accelerators. However, contrary to conventional photoemission based UTEM, the pump and the probe pulses are not inherently synchronized, as the two originate from independent sources. One such implementation for RF cavity to laser synchronization in a UTEM has been demonstrated by Borrelli et al.23

In this contribution, we describe the integration of two different beam choppers in two high-end transmission electron microscopes (Fig. 1): a prototype R&D microscope and a Spectra Ultra (Thermo Fisher Scientific).24 A resonant RF cavity (DrX Works), based on the work of Jom Luiten and colleagues,16–18,25 is used to chop the continuous electron beam into pulses of picosecond and sub-picosecond duration. The resonant nature of the cavity is crucial for achieving such short pulses. In addition, the cavity design allows mitigating any emittance increase introduced during pulsing if used in conjugate blanking mode.17

The second beam chopper is an electrostatic beam blanker (ESBB), which can be used in combination with the RF cavity as a pulse picker (when a lower pulse frequency is needed) or as a standalone blanker capable of generating pulses as short as 10 ns. Together, these two modules present an easy-to-use solution to add temporal resolution to a Spectra-based microscope.

In the following, we show the alignment steps that are needed to operate our beam choppers. We characterize the resolution that can be achieved using ultrashort RF-based pulses and demonstrate pump–probe experiments with sub-picosecond temporal resolution.

CHOPPING TECHNOLOGY

Figures 2(a)–2(d) illustrate the basic operating principle of the resonant RF cavity. The module, specifically designed for microscopes based on the Titan column (e.g., Illiad, Spectra, Krios, and Metrios), is situated between the first condenser (C1) aperture and the lens itself. Its slightly oval shape is designed to support two orthogonal TM110 modes at different frequencies. The superposition of these modes deflects the electron beam, creating a Lissajous pattern with the electron beam at the second condenser (C2) aperture plane, repeated at a frequency equal to the difference between the two modes [Figs. 2(b) and 2(c)]. This difference is designed to be 75 MHz, matching a typical frequency of commercial laser oscillators. Inserting a beam limiting aperture between successive line crossings [Figs. 2(c) and 2(d)] selects only a small fraction of the entire pattern, generating electron pulses at a frequency of 75 MHz. Any other electron outside these time periods is blocked and does not travel further down the column. With this procedure, electrons can only pass through the aperture for one specific combination of cavity mode phases, ensuring that every pulse has the same tilt without the need for any additional demodulation component. Similarly, this design eliminates the need for added lenses or deflectors. The tilt introduced by the RF cavity is irrelevant for most applications but can be corrected by pre-tilting the incoming beam using the gun deflectors.

Pulsed operation requires a few additional alignment steps (Method A), which allow us to increase the collection efficiency from the gun and maximize the coherence and/or the intensity of the electron pulses. Crucially, these additional alignment steps can be performed using a continuous electron beam, unlike those required for conventional photoemission. The intense signal greatly facilitates the process, which is especially important for tuning a probe corrector or other sensitive components.

The electron pulse duration (and, proportionally, the beam current) depends on the fraction of the Lissajous pattern that is cut by the C2 aperture. This fraction is influenced by several parameters, including the intensity of the EM field inside the cavity, the acceleration voltage, the specific settings of the first and second condenser lenses, and, of course, the size of the C2 aperture itself. To maximize the current per pulse (and consequently the pulse duration) for a given illuminated area, we typically increase the distance between successive crossings of the Lissajous pattern by tuning the phase difference of the two modes [Figs. 2(b) and 2(c)]. We then match this distance to the size of a C2 aperture by adjusting the C1 lens excitation (through the “spot size” parameter).

Figures 2(e)–2(h) show the basic operation scheme of the ESBB used in our experiments. When activated, it deflects the beam away from the optical axis [Figs. 2(f) and 2(g)], allowing it to be blocked by inserting a suitably sized C2 aperture [Fig. 2(h)]. The deflection angle can be tuned by changing the deflection voltage. Rapid unblanking of the beam can generate electron pulses as short as 10 ns, which is shorter than the interval between two RF pulses (i.e., one Lissajous cycle of roughly 13 ns). This allows the beam blanker to act as a pulse picker for the ultrafast pulses generated by the RF cavity, enabling the generation of ultrafast electron pulses at arbitrary repetition rates up to 1 MHz [Fig. 2(i) and supplementary material Fig. 1]. In addition, the beam blanker can function as a standalone beam chopper for applications ranging from micro- and nanosecond time-resolved electron microscopy to dose modulation.

Ultrashort electron pulses selected by the beam blanker can be synchronized with the arrival of amplified laser pulses at the sample simply by using the activation of the laser's Pockels cell pulse picker as a trigger for the unblanking of the electron beam (see Appendix S1B). This method ensures that our electron pulses are synchronized with laser pulses emitted directly from the laser oscillator as well as those amplified by a regenerative amplifier.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

We installed the ESBB and two different RF cavity prototypes on two Thermo Fisher microscopes. A prototype R&D microscope was fitted with an earlier (A) version, while a double-corrected Spectra Ultra received a more refined (B) version (see Appendix S1A for further details). Cavity A was used in early development, while cavity B allowed performance verification on the latest generation of tools.

Following the introduction of these modules, we verified that the instrument's performance remained within specifications. To this end, we performed high-resolution STEM imaging of a crystalline gallium nitride sample (supplementary material Fig. 2) with the Spectra Ultra microscope. The instrument's specification is a resolution of 50 pm, and our micrograph reveals well-resolved atomic columns with an achieved resolution of 46 pm, as determined by a Fourier transform of the image. This test is one of the most stringent for the microscope, demonstrating that its performance remains undiminished.

Next, we turn our attention to the operation of the cavity itself. The generated Lissajous pattern remains highly stable for several consecutive days, as demonstrated in supplementary material Fig. 3. For typical microscope settings (see Method A), RF pulsing results in a measured decrease in probe current to 0.008% of the original value. Combined with a pulsing period of 13.3 ns, this allows us to derive an effective pulse length of 1 ps. Similarly, based on measured counts, we estimate a current of roughly 0.5 electrons per pulse for an initial current of 75 nA. The relative probe current is consistent with simple geometric considerations, i.e., which part of the full Lissajous pattern is being transmitted by the chopping aperture.

Cavity-generated ultrashort electron pulses are well-suited for recording diffraction patterns. As shown in supplementary material Fig. 4, pulsed electron diffraction results in sharp patterns, and confirms that all electrons reach the sample with the same tilt without needing additional demodulation. Similar conclusions can be reached by observing diffraction patterns recorded using the ESBB as a beam chopper.

Next, we focus our efforts on imaging. Despite being a pervasive technique in the materials science community,26 applications of high-resolution STEM in an ultrafast setting have remained elusive. While the combined value of high-resolution structural and analytical information of ultrashort processes is undeniable, ultrafast high-resolution STEM is hindered by several challenges, including severe lack of signal and stage drift. The weak electron current, in particular, has several repercussions. To achieve high-resolution STEM imaging, the electron source needs to be highly coherent. For integration times to then remain reasonable, virtually every electron emitted past the gun aperture needs to be collected, which conflicts with the need to form a small probe at the sample.27 In addition, fitting aberration coefficients on images acquired under low-signal conditions is extremely challenging, making the tuning of a probe corrector arduous. Finally, long acquisition times lead to increased sample drift, an issue exacerbated by the laser illumination.

Pulsing with the RF cavity offers a solution to mitigate these issues. Pulses chopped from a X-(C)FEG are inherently bright,16 and pulsing at 75 MHz (or even 3 GHz in single-mode) boosts the signal by nearly two (or more than three) orders of magnitude compared to conventional photoemission.21,22 This is demonstrated in Fig. 3, which was acquired after activating the RF cavity with a tuned probe corrector of our Spectra Ultra. The corrector was tuned for RF-pulsing mode by operating the microscope with the optimal alignments for the RF cavity without operating the cavity. The ESBB could be used during the corrector tuning to modulate the current on the detector. The STEM micrograph of an individual gold nanoparticles on a gold cross grating [Fig. 3(a)] demonstrates a resolution of 235 pm, as evidenced by the Fourier transform analysis of the micrograph [Fig. 3(b) and its inset]. This result aligns with theoretical expectations, as STEM resolution degrades rapidly with large probe current values. With a 1.2 nA beam current before the RF cavity, 20 mrad convergence angle and an average X-CFEG brightness value, we estimate that the resolution achieved is near the theoretical limit (200 pm probe size), even without a beam chopper.27

Finally, we demonstrate that we can synchronize the frequencies of the RF driver and a laser oscillator to reach sub-picosecond FWHM overall instrument response. To reach this goal, the synchronization jitter needs to be controlled down to r.m.s. values of a few hundred fs or less. Precise temporal overlap of laser and electron pulses is not trivial in our implementation, since they originate from two distinct sources.

Details of our synchronization scheme can be found in Appendix S1C. In short, we use the beating produced by electronically mixing signals derived from the laser oscillator and the RF driver as feedback to dynamically adjust the phase of the laser oscillator, and keep it tightly locked to that of the RF cavity.

To demonstrate the temporal resolution capabilities of this setup in a pump–probe scheme, we performed photon-induced near-field electron microscopy (PINEM).10–12,28 In these experiments, free electrons traverse a localized electromagnetic field, typically an evanescent field generated by illuminating a nanostructure with intense femtosecond laser pulses. This confinement is crucial to bridge the energy-momentum mismatch between free electrons and photons. As a result of the interaction, the electrons emerge in a superposition of states, spaced equally by the photon energy ℏω (where ℏ is the reduced Planck constant and ω is the photon frequency). Moreover, if the electric field is uniform during the passage of the electron pulses, the coherent nature of the process is revealed, manifesting as interference between the states that comprise the superposition.11

Analyzing the energy spectra of electron pulses arriving at different delays with respect to the laser pulses thus provides valuable information about the system's temporal resolution.

Figure 4 shows the results of this experiment, performed on the prototype R&D microscope. As illustrated in Fig. 4(a), we irradiate a thin film of evaporated aluminum with laser pulses of 210 fs duration (central wavelength of 1030 nm; see Appendix S1B for additional details), creating an evanescent field through which electrons and photons interact. Thanks to the tight focus (Appendix S1B), we achieve high laser intensity on the sample without inducing damage. As a result, energy spectra acquired at the optimal spatiotemporal overlap of laser and electron pulses show signal up to around 84 eV, corresponding to the absorption or emission of up to 70 photons, as shown in Fig. 4(b) and supplementary material Fig. 5.

By observing the behavior of the energy spectra as we vary the delay between the laser and electron pulses, we can measure the so-called “instrument response.” The interval of delays at which photon sidebands are visible results from a convolution of the electron and laser pulse durations and the synchronization jitter. The contribution of the laser pulse can be partially eliminated by integrating only over the higher-order sidebands, which appear exclusively at the highest intensities of the evanescent field. Figure 4(c) shows an example of a delay scan, along with the energy-gain region we integrated to obtain the instrument response. The result is a full width at half maximum (FWHM) of 0.7 ps, as shown in the inset of Fig. 4(c).

Small further improvements to the temporal resolution can be achieved by lowering the acceleration voltage and thereby generating shorter electron pulses. At 80 kV we obtain an instrument response with an FWHM of 0.65 ps [Fig. 4(d)], which demonstrates that the resonant RF cavity-based approach is capable of sub-ps temporal resolution. These results were achieved with the earlier (A) version of the RF cavity. With an improved (B) version, we expect that sub-500 fs time resolution is in reach through improvements in the synchronization; see Appendix S1C for details.

These experiments can be carried out at repetition rates of up to at least 1 MHz, confirming that the ESBB, the RF cavity, and the laser are all synchronized. As an example, we repeated the experiments in Figs. 4(b) and 4(c) using temporally stretched laser pulses, which are only available from the amplified output of our laser system. As shown in Figs. 4(e) and 4(f), several PINEM orders are nearly fully depleted due to multipath interference experienced by the electrons after interacting with the evanescent field generated by laser pulses of 2 ps. This result clearly demonstrates that every electron pulse is synchronized with a laser pulse even at lower repetition rates, and that at their best overlap, a 2 ps laser pulse already provides nearly uniform interaction strength for the duration of the electron pulse [see the fitting with a Bessel function in Fig. 4(g) and Appendix S1D].11

CONCLUSIONS

In conclusion, we have introduced two beam choppers on a prototype R&D microscope and a Spectra microscope, enabling modulation of a continuous electron beam into pulses covering a wide temporal range down to less than 1 ps. We have characterized the spatial resolution achievable with RF-based ultrashort electron pulses and demonstrated synchronization with laser pulses at the sample with sub-ps precision at flexible repetition rates up to 1 MHz or at a fixed frequency of 75 MHz. We note that a similar result in terms of time resolution was recently achieved by Borrelli et al., on a Tecnai RF-UTEM23 using a somewhat different approach for the synchronization, further illustrating the general feasibility of using resonant beam choppers in UTEM pump–probe experiments. By reducing the jitter between the RF cavity, pulse picker and the laser in combination with improving the time resolution of the RF cavity we believe a higher time resolution should be achievable in the future.

We summarize the advantages of our beam pulsing method in the following points: •Ease of use. This instrument configuration allows for quick and easy switching between pulsed and continuous modes, requiring minimal alignment steps and presenting significantly fewer challenges than preparing an electron microscope for photoemission. This gives users the flexibility necessary to benefit from the full functionality of a modern electron microscope. For example, our technology allows users to align the microscope and fully characterize the sample before rapidly switching to pulsing mode, saving time and enabling imaging modes that would otherwise be hard to access, such as probe-corrected ultrafast STEM.•High repetition rates. The RF cavity-based technology provides extremely high repetition rates, even reaching into the GHz regime. Pulses delivered at such high frequency can be useful to image dynamics with short relaxation times (e.g., collective electronic excitations29,30). High repetition rates reduce integration times, which helps alleviate issues with drift and detector noise.•Potential for high-resolution imaging. Combined with the high brightness of our electron pulses and the tight laser focus, we believe it should be possible to obtain atomic-resolution micrographs of ultrafast dynamics, an ambitious goal that has so far remained elusive.7,31,32 Moreover, the probe frequency can be a large multiple of the pump frequency. Using a fast detector capable of distinguishing successive electron pulses, structural transitions with longer relaxation times can be triggered at low frequencies while being probed at much higher rates. This enables sample tracking and digital drift correction in post-processing. With recent detectors capable of distinguishing electron events with few-ns resolution, the 75 MHz pulsing rate of the cavity can be used to image the sample between laser shots with high signal, irrespective of the actual timescale of the experiment. This capability addresses a significant challenge in ultrafast electron microscopy, where high resolution has been difficult to achieve due to sample drift, exacerbated by the low repetition rates required for stroboscopic experiments to allow samples to return to their initial conditions.32•Electronic synchronization and time delay scanning. Another unique feature of our approach is that the synchronization between the electron and the laser pulses is fully electronic. This enables scanning the time delay over the full period of the experiment substantially without any moving parts and allows probing complex dynamics that span a large range of timescales.

We also note two minor limitations: •Low current per pulse. One of the main limitations is the lower electron current that can be delivered by an X-FEG gun compared to the intense pulses that can be generated with photoemission.33,34 When intensity is more important than electron beam coherence or repetition rate, laser-based approaches are, at present, a better solution. Photoemission allows for the extraction of extremely intense electron pulses.35 However, while the highest brightness values reported for photoemission are similar to those of commercial X-CFEG,21,22 we note that the quality of the beam and the temporal resolution rapidly deteriorate due to Coulombic repulsion (typically in combination with reduced extraction fields) as the probe current is increased.36,37•Gun lens limitations. The gun lens excitation is restricted to a very narrow range of values to ensure that the beam is focused in the center of the cavity, and the condenser lens system is furthermore somewhat constrained to reach sufficient magnification of the Lissajous pattern for chopping at the C2 aperture plane. Nevertheless, the condenser system retains enough degrees of freedom to freely set the field of view or convergence angle and even reduce the beam current if necessary.

Future work will explore high-resolution ultrafast electron microscopy in more detail. As an intriguing example, it remains to be determined what the ultimate resolution limit of ultrafast STEM is. The design allows minimizing the spot size by lowering the current, at the expense of the signal in the image. We expect in the future to find an optimum where the resolution is minimized as a trade-off between probe size and overall stability.
