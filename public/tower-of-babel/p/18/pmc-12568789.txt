Genomic insights into Shigella species isolated from small ruminants and manure in the North West Province, South Africa
Authors: Tshepang Motlhaping, Tsepo Ramatla, Oriel Thekisoe, Ntelekwane George Khasapane, Kgaugelo E Lekota
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAbstractThis study investigated Shigella species’ antibiotic resistance patterns and genomic characteristics from small ruminants and manure collected in Potchefstroom, North West, South Africa. Whole genome sequencing was used to determine resistome profiles of Shigella flexneri isolates from small ruminants’ manure and Shigella boydii from sheep faeces. Comparative genomics was employed on the South African 261 S. flexneri strains available from GenBank, including the sequenced strains in this study, by investigating the serovars, antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs), and plasmid replicon types. The S. flexneri strains could not be assigned to known sequence types, suggesting novel or uncharacterized lineages. S. boydii R7-1A was assigned to sequence type 202 (ST202). Serovar 2A was the most common among South African S. flexneri strains, found in 96% of the 250 compared human-derived isolates. The shared mdf(A) was the most prevalent gene, identified in 99% of 261 S. flexneri genomes, including plasmid replicon types ColRNAI_1 (99%) and IncFII_1 (98%). Both species share a core set of resistance determinants mainly involving β-lactams (ampC1, ampC, ampH), macrolides (mphB), polymyxins (eptA, pmrF), multidrug efflux pumps (AcrAB-TolC, Mdt, Emr, Kpn families), and regulatory systems (marA, hns, crp, baeRS, evgAS, cpxA, gadX). However, S. boydii possesses additional resistance genes conferring resistance to tetracyclines (tet(A)), phenicols (floR), sulphonamides (sul2), and aminoglycosides (APH(3’’)-Ib, APH(6)-Id), along with the acrEF efflux pump components (acrE, acrF). In contrast, S. flexneri harboured unique genes linked to polymyxin resistance (ugd) and regulatory functions (sdiA, gadW) that were absent in S. boydii. These findings highlight Shigella strains’ genomic diversity and antimicrobial resistance potential in livestock-associated environments. Moreover, S. boydii highlights the potential risk of multidrug-resistant bacteria in farming and environmental routes.Key points• First whole genome study of Shigella from manure and small ruminants in South Africa.• Shigella boydii strain carried multiple resistance genes to β-lactams and tetracycline.• Multidrug efflux pump gene mdf(A) was detected in 99% of South African Shigella flexneri strains.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00253-025-13632-w.Keywords: Shigella flexneri, Shigella boydii, Antibiotic resistance gene, Virulence genes, Comparative genomic analysis

Introduction

Shigella species are a significant cause of gastrointestinal infections worldwide, particularly in developing countries, where they contribute significantly to childhood morbidity and mortality (Taneja et al. 2012; Shen et al. 2017). The genus Shigella comprises four species: Shigella flexneri, Shigella dysenteriae, Shigella boydii, and Shigella sonnei (Faruque et al. 2002; Rahman et al. 2011). Among these, S. flexneri is the most prevalent and clinically significant, responsible for the majority of shigellosis cases in low- and middle-income regions, where it causes severe diarrheal disease and accounts for more deaths than any other Shigella serotype (Nyarkoh et al. 2024).

S. flexneri and S. boydii are closely related members of the Shigella genus and share substantial genetic similarities, reflecting a common evolutionary origin (Nochi et al. 2009). However, their geographic distribution, prevalence, and clinical significance differ. S. flexneri is the most prevalent species in developing countries and is a significant cause of shigellosis, especially in regions with poor sanitation (Muzembo et al. 2023). It is highly pathogenic due to its ability to invade intestinal epithelial cells using key virulence factors such as biofilm formation, the type III secretion system (T3SS), and actin polymerization, which contribute to host cell invasion, immune evasion, and severe intestinal inflammation (Agaisse 2016; Chiang et al. 2021).

In contrast, S. boydii is less frequently isolated and is mainly restricted to certain parts of South Asia (Von Seidlein et al. 2006; Kahsay and Muthupandian 2016). Although genetically similar to S. flexneri, S. boydii is less associated with severe disease outbreaks and displays lower global clinical importance (Mattock and Blocker 2017; Hmar et al. 2024). The reduced prevalence and limited geographic distribution suggest that S. boydii may lack or underutilize some virulence mechanisms that make S. flexneri a dominant and highly pathogenic species (Gabor et al. 2025) .

The pathogenicity of Shigella species arises from its ability to invade and replicate within human intestinal epithelial cells, including inflammation and tissue damage (Schnupf and Sansonetti 2020). The bacterium’s virulence is enhanced by its capacity to trigger apoptosis, undermining the host’s immune response (Ashida et al. 2015). Critical virulence determinants, such as biofilm formation, host cell invasion, and acid resistance, are regulated by cyclic di-GMP signalling pathways (Zhou et al. 2024). The type III secretion system (T3SS) plays a central role in pathogenesis, injecting bacterial effector proteins into host cells to facilitate epithelial cell invasion, cytoskeleton manipulation, and immune evasion (Slater et al. 2018). Once inside the colonic epithelium, the bacterium induces actin assembly through the autotransporter IcsA, promoting bacterial motility and cell-to-cell spread (Goldberg and Theriot 1995). Recent genomic studies have revealed additional mechanisms by which Shigella species subvert host immune responses, including the secretion of E3 ligase inhibitors that disrupt key signalling pathways (Zhou et al. 2025). Alarmingly, these virulence strategies, combined with rising antibiotic resistance, pose a serious public health threat, particularly resistance to first-line antibiotics such as ampicillin and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole and tetracycline, through the acquisition of resistance genes, often carried on plasmids or integrative genetic elements (Wang et al. 2019a, b).

The growing antibiotic resistance of Shigella species is a global concern, especially in regions where shigellosis is endemic (Nyarkoh et al. 2024). This resistance is primarily driven by horizontal gene transfer, allowing Shigella to acquire and disseminate resistance genes across strains and populations rapidly (Baker et al. 2018). Environmental reservoirs such as agricultural settings where antibiotics are regularly used can facilitate the emergence and spread of resistant Shigella strains, posing a risk to human health through contaminated food, water, or direct contact (Ifedinezi et al. 2024). Resistance to fluoroquinolones, once considered effective second-line treatments, has been increasingly reported among Shigella isolates (Al-Khafaji et al. 2024). Additionally, the rise of resistance to third-generation cephalosporins, such as ceftriaxone, is attributed to the production of extended-spectrum β-lactamases (ESBLs), which compromise the efficacy of these critical antibiotics (Husna et al. 2023). Understanding Shigella species’ genetic diversity, serotype distribution, and antibiotic resistance mechanisms is essential for guiding treatment strategies and mitigating the global burden of shigellosis.

Studies have identified multidrug-resistant S. flexneri isolates in non-human primates carrying plasmid-encoded quinolone resistance genes like quinolone resistance gene qnrS (Strahilevitz et al. 2009; Domman et al. 2024). Additionally, a chromosomally encoded quinolone efflux pump A (qepA) variant, reducing ciprofloxacin susceptibility, has been detected in travellers returning from regions like India and East Asia (Yamane et al. 2007). A recent study showed that the number of antibiotic genes (ARGs) recognized by S. flexneri has increased over time and that mobile genetic elements (MGEs) play an essential role in disease transmission and antibiotic resistance (Fang et al. 2023). Furthermore, strains isolated from S. flexneri, particularly serotypes 1 and 2, are associated with antibiotic resistance, such as ampicillin and chloramphenicol (Nisa et al. 2023). Efflux transporters such as the Yersinia virulence factor A are also crucial for the immune response and complicate the treatment strategies for different types of antibiotics (Sen and Verma 2022). Advances in genomic and molecular research have improved the understanding of S. flexneri pathogenesis and drug resistance, facilitating the development of new approaches for treatment and prevention (Mukhopadhyay et al. 2024).

S. boydii may be the least commonly detected species within the Shigella genus. However, it still holds clinical importance in certain parts of the world, especially in South and Southeast Asia (Nyarkoh et al. 2024). Although it shares several disease-causing features, such as virulence genes, with other Shigella species, S. boydii is rarely linked to major outbreaks and generally shows lower levels of antibiotic resistance compared to S. flexneri (Nüesch-Inderbinen et al. 2016). However, the emergence of multidrug-resistant strains, including those resistant to certain antibiotics like ciprofloxacin and third-generation cephalosporins, has raised alarms about their potential role in the growing global problem of antibiotic resistance (Afrad et al. 2024). Although shigellosis is a significant public health threat, particularly in rural areas with poor sanitation and poor healthcare, it remains understudied in African countries (Nyarkoh et al. 2024). The use of whole genome sequencing for this pathogen is underreported in South Africa (Stenhouse et al. 2023). This lack of scientific investigation is concerning, given the rise in antibiotic resistance, which limits treatment options and increases the risk of large-scale outbreaks. Few studies have been conducted on Shigella species from humans in South Africa (Keddy et al. 2012; Stenhouse et al. 2023). To our knowledge, no report exists regarding the antibiotic resistance patterns of Shigella species from the environment, such as manure and small ruminants, in South Africa. Therefore, this study was carried out to investigate the genomic characteristics of Shigella species and their antibiotic resistance and virulence gene profiles. Additionally, the investigation included genomic diversity, antibiotic resistance genes, and plasmid replicon types across the available South African genomes of S. flexneri.

Materials and methods

Bacterial isolates

The three Shigella isolates used in this study were randomly selected from a curated culture collection available in the Microbiology Biobank at North-West University (Potchefstroom Campus, South Africa). These isolates form part of a broader surveillance initiative focused on enteric pathogens from small livestock in communal and emerging farming systems. The isolates originated from samples collected during routine field surveillance in the Matlwang and Ventersdorp communities within the Dr. Kenneth Kaunda District of North West Province, South Africa. The original specimens consisted of fresh faecal material from sheep and goats and animal manure samples. Pure Shigella isolate colonies, previously stored in nutrient broth with 15% sterile glycerol at −80 °C, were subcultured on xylose lysine deoxycholate (XLD) agar (Merck, Wadeville, South Africa) and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h.

Identification of the isolates using the SensiTitre system

The SensiTitre system (Thermo Scientific™ Inc., Waltham, MA, USA) was used in this study to identify Gram-negative bacterial isolates. Bacterial colonies were first cultured on nutrient agar and incubated overnight at 37 °C. Isolated colonies were then emulsified in sterile saline (0.85% NaCl) to achieve a turbidity equivalent to a 0.5 McFarland standard, which was confirmed using a densitometer. The prepared bacterial suspension was dispensed into the wells of a Gram-negative SensiTitre identification panel, containing specific biochemical substrates, following the manufacturer’s instructions (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA). The plates were sealed and incubated at 37 °C for 18–24 h under aerobic conditions. After incubation, the results were either visually read or interpreted using an automated reader. Biochemical reactions, such as colour changes and gas production, were recorded and compared against the SensiTitre database using the system’s software or manual reference tables for species identification.

Genomic DNA extraction of the isolates

The genomic DNA was extracted from overnight culture broths using a ZymoBIOMICS DNA extraction kit following the manufacturer’s instructions (Zymo Research Corp., Irvine, CA, USA). A NanoDrop Lite 1000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific™, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA) was used to determine the concentration and purity of the DNA. For whole genome sequencing (WGS), DNA quantification was performed by a fluorometric assay using the Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Finally, the DNA was stored at −80 °C for further analysis.

Molecular characterization of Shigella isolates

Primer sets specific to Shigella rfpB-F-TCTCAATAATAGGGAACACAGC and rfpB-R-CATAAATCACCAGCAAGGTT targeting the rfpB gene of a 537-bp long fragment were used to amplify the gene using PCR, as described previously by Sabour et al. (2022). The PCR reactions constituted 12.5 µL of 2X DreamTaq Green Master Mix (New England Biolabs Inc., Ipswich, MA, USA), 8.5 µL of RNase-nuclease free PCR water, 1 µL of each primer (10 µM each), and 2 µL of template DNA. The amplification conditions were as follows: 94 °C for 4 min, 32 cycles at 94 °C for 45 s, 56 °C for 1 min, followed by a 40-s extension at 72 °C, and the final extension cycle at 72 °C for 10 min. This study used standard reference strains as a positive control (S. flexneri ATCC® 12022), while a DNA-free template (nuclease-free water) was used as a negative control.

Antibiotic resistance patterns of the isolates

Antibiotic testing was performed using the Kirby-Bauer disc diffusion method on the Mueller–Hinton Agar (MHA) plate (Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany) (Magiorakos et al. 2012). Pure cultures of the Shigella species isolated from manure and sheep faeces were used to test for antibiotic resistance patterns. Nine different antibiotics from six different classes included ciprofloxacin (CIP, 5 µg), ampicillin (AMP, 10 µg), kanamycin (K, 30 µg), nalidixic acid (NA, 30 µg), cephazolin (KZ, 30 µg), ertapenem (ETP, 10 µg), meropenem (MEM, 10 µg), doripenem (DOR, 10 µg), and imipenem (IPM, 10 µg) obtained from Thermo Fischer (Remel™, Lenexa, KS, USA; Oxoid™, Basingstoke, Hampshire, UK) were tested for antibiotic resistance following Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute antimicrobial susceptibility testing standards (CLSI 2021) guidelines. The MHA plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. After incubation, isolates were observed as resistant (R), intermediate (I), or susceptible (S) based on the size of the zone of bacterial growth inhibition as stipulated by CLSI guidelines (Ramatla et al. 2024). The isolates were considered multidrug-resistant (MDR) if they exhibited acquired non-susceptibility to at least one agent in three or more antimicrobial classes (Magiorakos et al. 2012).

Whole genome sequencing of Shigella species

Two S. flexneri isolates from animal manure and one S. boydii isolate from sheep faeces were randomly selected for whole genome sequencing to serve as representative strains for further genomic analysis. Library preparation of these strains was constructed using the MGIEasy FS DNA Prep Kit (BGI Genomics, Shenzhen, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The prepared libraries were sequenced using the BGI MGISEQ-2000 platform (BGI Genomics, Shenzhen, China) with paired-end 150 bp.

Bioinformatics analysis

The quality of the sequence reads was assessed using the FastQC software v.0.12.1 (Andrews et al. 2010). Readings were processed with Trimmomatic v0.39 (Bolger et al. 2014) to remove adaptive sequences and filter out low-quality bases, using a reduced window approach to ensure an average quality score (Bolger et al. 2014). Paired-end reads were assembled using the MEGAHIT v1.2.9 (Li et al. 2015), while CheckM v1.2.0 (Parks et al. 2015) was used to detect the contaminating elements. Annotation of the assembled contigs was performed using Prokka v1.14.6 (Seemann 2014). Genome assembly patterns were assessed using QUAST v.2.3 (Gurevich et al. 2013). Moreover, MLST profiles (https://pubmlst.org/) [accessed on 03–10–2024] were used to identify the sequenced genomes (Jolley et al. 2017).

The ABRicate pipeline (Feldgarden et al. 2019) detected antibiotic resistance and virulence genes in the Shigella genomes. Antibiotic resistance gene determinants were identified using the ResFinder database (Feldgarden et al. 2019) and the Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database (CARD) (Alcock et al. 2023). Virulence genes were identified on ABRicate using the Virulence Factor Database (VFDB) (Chen et al. 2016). Plasmid replicons were determined using the PlasmidFinder database (Carattoli et al. 2014), while plasmid features were determined using MOB-suite (Robertson and Nash 2018) and visualized using Proksee (Grant et al. 2023). The mobile genetic elements were identified using mobileOG-db (Brown et al. 2022). For all the above-mentioned databases, minimum identity and coverage requirements of 70% (–minid 70) and 70% (–mincov 70) were applied, respectively. For comparative genomics analysis of the antibiotic resistance genes among 261 S. flexneri strains, the ResFinder database (Feldgarden et al. 2019) was employed. Serotyping was performed using ShigEiFinder v 1.3.0 (Luo et al. 2022). Chiplot was used to visualize the presence and absence of antibiotic resistance genes and virulence genes (Ramírez et al. 2014).

Pangenomics and whole genome single-nucleotide polymorphism analysis

A total of 261 genomes were analyzed for pangenomic profiling, comprising publicly available genomes retrieved from GenBank (Supplementary Table S1) and two newly sequenced genomes generated in this study. In this study, only assembled South African genomes were included for comparative genomics, totalling 261 genomes analyzed, including the sequenced strains. Among these, two genomes were sourced from animal manure, 257 from human stool, one from a human rectal swab, and one from a human blood culture.

All genomes, including the two sequenced genomes, were annotated using Prokka v1.14 (Seemann 2014). Similarity searches among coding DNA sequences (CDSs) were performed using pairwise BLASTp (Christensen and Olsen 2018), and orthologous gene clusters were identified using the Markov cluster algorithm (MCL). Pangenome clusters were classified as core genes (present in 100% of genomes), soft core genes (present in ≥ 95% of genomes), shell genes (present in 15–95% of genomes), and cloud genes (present in < 15% of genomes). Core genome alignments were used to extract single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) with SNP-sites v2.5.1 (https://github.com/sanger-pathogens/snp-sites), and recombination events were detected and removed using Gubbins v2.4.1 (Croucher et al. 2015). A maximum likelihood phylogenetic tree was constructed from the filtered core SNPs using IQ-TREE v1.6.10 (Nguyen et al. 2015). Pan-genome presence/absence matrices were visualized in RStudio using the heatmap package to generate heatmaps of gene distribution across isolates.

Results

Identification and antibiotic resistance profiles of Shigella species

The use of the SensiTitre system and PCR amplification of the rfpB gene identified these three isolates as Shigella species. Two isolates from small ruminant manure were confirmed as S. flexneri, while one isolate from sheep faeces was identified as S. boydii. Shigella isolates exhibiting resistance to three or more antibiotic classes were classified as multidrug-resistant (MDR) (Table 1). Of the three examined Shigella spp., only S. boydii strain R7-1A was classified as MDR. The S. flexneri strains SH 253 and SH 254 showed resistance to ampicillin. Analysis using the CARD database revealed the presence of β-lactam resistance genes (ampC1, ampC, and ampH), the macrolide resistance determinant (mphB), polymyxin resistance genes (eptA, pmrF, and ugd), and the bacitracin resistance gene (bacA). Genes for multiple efflux systems were identified, including genes of the arcA, arcB, tolC, mdt, emr, and kpn families, along with genes for associated (msbA, yojI, mdfA) and regulatory genes (marA, sdiA, evgA, evgS, cpxA, gadX, gadW, crp, baeR, baeS, kdpE, hns). Conversely, S. boydii strain R7-1A also demonstrated resistance to ampicillin and ten other antibiotics via the ResFinder database (Supplementary Fig. S1), thus classified as MDR. However, the CARD database (Table 1) showed that this strain harboured a diverse resistome, including the tetracycline resistance gene tet(A), phenicol resistance gene floR, sulphonamide resistance determinant sul2, macrolide resistance gene mphB, aminoglycoside resistance genes APH(3’’)-Ib and APH(6)-Id, and genes for multiple β-lactamases (identified as blaTEM-150, ampC1, ampC, and ampH). Numerous genes for multidrug eptA and pmrF for polymyxins were also present. Numerous multidrug efflux systems and related regulators, such as arcA, arcB-tolC, arcE, arcF, mdtA, mdtB, mdtC, mdtJ, emrA, emrB, mdfA, kpnE, and kpnF, and associated global regulators (marA, hns, crp, baeRS, evgA, evgS, cpxA, and gadX), were detected. Strains of S. flexneri uniquely carried ugd (linked to polymyxin resistance), sdiA (involved in quorum sensing and regulation), and gadW (related to acid resistance regulation), which were absent in S. boydii. Conversely, S. boydii strain R7-1A possessed exclusive genes, including tet(A) (tetracycline resistance), floR (phenicol resistance), sul2 (sulphonamide resistance), APH(3'')-Ib and APH(6)-Id (aminoglycoside resistance), blaTEM-150 (β-lactamase), and acrE/acrF (components of the acrEF multidrug efflux pump). Both species shared a core set of resistance determinants mainly involving β-lactams, macrolides, polymyxins, multidrug efflux pumps, and regulation systems. However, S. boydii has additional resistance genes conferring resistance to tetracyclines, phenicols, sulphonamides, and aminoglycosides. In contrast, S. flexneri has unique genes linked to polymyxin resistance and regulatory functions not present in S. boydii.

Genomic features and assembly metrics of Shigella species

The genomic features of S. flexneri strains SH253 and SH254 (manure-derived) and S. boydii strain R7-1A (sheep faeces-derived) revealed notable variation in genome composition and assembly quality (Table 2). The genome sizes of SH253 and SH254 were 4.83 Mb and 5.1 Mb, respectively, with GC contents of 50.5% and 51%. In comparison, S. boydii R7-1A exhibited a slightly smaller genome (4.63 Mb) but a marginally higher GC content (50.89%).

Assembly statistics indicated superior contiguity in SH253, which had the fewest contigs (n = 88) and the longest single contig (702,832 bp), reflecting a higher-quality assembly. In contrast, SH254 and R7-1A displayed more fragmented assemblies with 675 and 798 contigs, respectively. The longest contigs in these two genomes were 542,888 bp (SH254) and 206,269 bp (R7-1A). The number of annotated tRNAs ranged between 81 and 92 across all isolates, further underscoring genomic variability within these Shigella strains. The number of coding sequences ranges between 4.3 and 5.3, with SH254 having the highest coding sequences (CDS) (5,311), followed by R7-1A (4,796). Compared to strain SH253, the coding sequences are slightly lower than those of the two strains at 4652. Notably, both genomes possess many mobile genetic elements (MGEs), which are genetic components capable of horizontal gene transfer and genomic rearrangement. These include insertion sequences, transposons, integrons, and plasmid-associated genes. Multilocus sequence typing (MLST) further identified these isolates, assigning S. flexneri strains with unknown sequence types, while S. boydii R7-1A was assigned to ST202.

Virulence determinants on the sequenced Shigella isolates

The analysis of virulence genes showed that both S. flexneri strains had a total of 50 genes including those responsible for fimbrial assembly (fimA, fimB, fimC, fimD, fimE, fimF, fimG, fimH, fimI, csgB, csgD, csgF, csgG, ykgK/ecpR, yagZ/ecpA, yagY/ecpB, yagX/ecpC, yagW/ecpD, and yagV/ecpE), outer membrane integrity (ompA), type II secretion system components (gspC, gspD, gspE, gspF, gspG, gspH, gspI, gspJ, gspK, gspL, and gspM), iron acquisition mechanisms (entA, entB, entC, entD, entE, entF, entS, fepA, fepB, fepC, fepD, fepG, and fes), and effector protein regulation/secretion (espX1, espX4, espX5, espR1,and espL1) (Supplementary Fig. S2). In contrast, S. boydii strain R7-1A had only 25 genes, including iron acquisition and siderophore transport (entA, entB, entC, entD, entE, entF, entS, fepA, fepB, fepC, fepD, fepG, and fes), fimbrial assembly (csgB, csgD, csgF, and csgG), outer membrane and structural integrity (ompA and fdeC), and effector proteins and host interaction (espX1, espX4, espX5, espR1, espR4, and espL1.

Plasmid genetic features of S. flexneri and S. boydii strains

Three plasmids (AA169, AA304, and AD448) were found in both S. flexneri strain SH253 and SH254 (Table 3). The 35 kbp plasmid AA169 found in both strains has a GC content of 42.26% with no plasmid replicons detected. The plasmid consists of 53 CDS with mobile genetic elements (MGEs), composed of 17 genes that include (n = 10) integration/excision, (n = 2) replicon/recombination/repair, (n = 1) phages, (n = 3) stability/transfer/defence, and (n = 1) transfer in both strains (Supplementary Fig. S3). Antibiotic resistance genes detected across South African S. flexneri strains

The analysis of antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) in the two sequenced S. flexneri genomes (Fig. 2) reveals that S. flexneri strains SH253 and SH254 both consist of only one ARG, which encodes a multidrug efflux pump mdf(A). The core gene identified in both sequenced strains is the most prevalent, also identified in 99% (n = 260) of the compared genomes. A total of 26 antibiotic resistance genes were identified across the 261 S. flexneri genomes analyzed (Fig. 2). These ARGs encoded various resistance mechanisms including β-lactamase genes (blaCMY-4, blaCTX-M-3, blaOXA-1, blaTEM-141, blaTEM-1A, and blaTEM-1B), aminoglycoside-modifying enzymes (aac(6’)-Ib-cr, aadA1, ant(3’’)-Ia, aph(3’’)-Ib, and aph(6)-Id), macrolide which modifies and inactivates macrolides (mph(A)), rifampin resistance gene which inactivates rifampin (ARR-3), chloramphenicol acetyltransferase gene (catA1), trimethoprim resistance genes which encode altered dihydrofolate reductase enzymes (dfrA1, dfrA14, dfrA15, dfrA5, dfrA7, and dfrA8), quinolone resistance gene which protects DNA gyrase and topoisomerase IV from quinolones (qnrS1), efflux pumps which confer resistance to multiple antibiotics by pumping them out [mdf(A)], sulphonamide resistance genes which encode altered dihydropteroate synthase (sul1 and sul2), and tetracycline resistance genes which encode efflux pumps that expel tetracycline (tet(A) and tet(B)). Of the 26 ARGs identified, the most prevalent genes in both sequenced and compared genomes included mdf(A) 99% (n = 260), conferring resistance to multiple antibiotics. Discussion

This study provides the first comprehensive genomic surveillance of Shigella species isolated from small stock environments in South Africa, offering novel insights into their resistome, virulome, and population structure. Shigella poses a significant public health burden as a leading cause of childhood diarrheal disease in low-resource settings (Sadredinamin et al. 2022). This is the first comparative genomics of Shigella species, suggesting the presence of this zoonotic bacterium in small ruminant environments within farming systems in South Africa. Identifying S. flexneri and S. boydii in faecal and manure samples from small ruminants within both farming settings highlights the potential role of livestock environments as reservoirs. The use of whole genome sequencing adequately placed the population structure of the environmental S. flexneri among the available South African 259 genomes. These findings showed the unknown serotypes of the ecological niche of S. flexneri, showing its persistence in animal-associated and environmental settings.

Although only three Shigella isolates were sequenced in this study, their selection was strategically informed by the scientific relevance of the only available strains in the culture collection. These isolates were randomly selected from a curated culture collection housed in the Microbiology Biobank at North-West University (Potchefstroom Campus, South Africa), which forms part of a broader “One Health” surveillance initiative targeting enteric pathogens in small livestock from communal and emerging farming systems. Among the archived isolates, S. boydii was the only species exhibiting multidrug resistance, making it a priority for further genomic characterization. Despite these limitations, these isolates provide critical insight into the resistome structure and mobile genetic elements associated with Shigella species in underrepresented agricultural contexts. Their inclusion is a foundational step toward expanding genomic surveillance in rural livestock systems, where data gaps persist and resource limitations necessitate targeted sequencing approaches (de Wet et al. 2025).

Multilocus sequence typing (MLST) could not assign the S. flexneri strains to any known sequence types, indicating the presence of novel or uncharacterized lineages. In contrast, the S. boydii strain R7-1A was successfully assigned to ST202. Comparative genomic analyses of 261 South African S. flexneri genomes revealed that serovar 2 A was the most prevalent, accounting for 96%. However, both sequenced isolates in this study exhibited untypeable serovars, raising the possibility of non-emerging variants in environmental reservoirs. While these findings do not confirm the existence of new lineages, they are consistent with the hypothesis that ecological niches may harbour genetically distinct Shigella populations, potentially with unique antimicrobial resistance profiles (Chattaway et al. 2017; Shokoohizadeh et al. 2017). This highlights the value of integrating genomic surveillance into environmental monitoring frameworks to improve early detection of atypical strains and explore Shigella’s evolutionary dynamics beyond clinical settings.

Virulence determinants showed a key difference between S. flexneri and S. boydii strains, offering insights into their potential pathogenicity and adaptation strategies (Zhu et al. 2021). Both S. flexneri strains demonstrated a diverse virulence gene profile, with 50 identified genes. These included a complete set of genes responsible for fimbrial assembly, critical for adhesion to host cells and biofilm formation (Bliven and Lampel 2017; Sadredinamin et al. 2022). These genes suggest that S. flexneri strains possess a strong capacity for surface attachment and colonization, which is essential for initiating infection (Dorman and Dorman 2018). Additionally, S. flexneri carried genes encoding outer membrane proteins important for maintaining structural integrity and interaction with the host environment (Ambrosi et al. 2012; Wang et al. 2016a, b). The detection of a complete Type II Secretion System (T2SS) gene set indicates the ability of these strains to secrete enzymes and toxins that facilitate invasion and survival within the host (Dorman and Dorman 2018). Furthermore, the presence of multiple iron acquisition genes in this species highlights an enhanced capability for iron uptake, which is crucial for bacterial growth and persistence in iron-limited host environments (Zhu et al. 2021). The presence of effector protein genes suggests a well-developed set of molecular tools that enable the management of host cellular functions during infection (Bliven and Lampel 2017).

In contrast, S. boydii strain R7-1A exhibited a noticeably virulent gene profile, with 25 detected genes. While this strain shared core iron acquisition and siderophore transport genes similar to S. flexneri, the genes responsible for fimbrial assembly were limited to the csg operon genes, lacking the additional fim operon genes identified in S. flexneri (Mattock and Blocker 2017). The differences in virulence gene content between S. flexneri and S. boydii reflect how each species may vary in its ability to cause disease and adapt to different environments. Interestingly, while both species possessed the essential outer membrane protein gene ompA, only S. boydii carried the additional fdeC gene, which may contribute to membrane stability or host interaction (Mattock and Blocker 2017; KEGG 2024). S. boydii harboured fewer effector protein genes, with notable overlap with those in S. flexneri, suggesting shared mechanisms of host interaction. However, the functional implications of this reduced repertoire remain to be fully elucidated (Bliven and Lampel 2017). The larger number of virulence genes found in S. flexneri is consistent with its well-known association with serious human infections and outbreaks (Dorman and Dorman 2018). On the other hand, the smaller set of virulence genes in S. boydii may contribute to its lower clinical impact and less frequent association with disease (Sikorski et al. 2020). Nevertheless, it retains the capacity to acquire resistance to multiple antibiotics, underscoring its potential public health relevance. These observations emphasize the need for continued genomic monitoring to understand better how variations in virulence traits influence the survival, transmission, and health risks of different Shigella species, especially those found in animal-related environments (Zhu et al. 2021).

In this study, S. flexneri harboured a core set of β-lactam resistance genes (ampC1, ampC, and ampH), and the macrolide resistance determinant mphB. Additionally, the genome encoded multiple multidrug efflux systems, including AcrAB-TolC, Mdt, Emr, and Kpn families, supported by accessory transporters (msbA, yojI, mdfA) and global regulatory elements (marA, sdiA, evgA/S, cpxA, gadX/W, crp, baeR/S, kdpE, hns). The ampC-type β-lactamases have been reported in African S. flexneri, providing significant resistance to cephalosporins (Nyarkoh et al. 2024). The mphA is commonly reported as the key macrolide resistance determinant in Shigella sp., including in African and global isolates (Sethuvel et al. 2019). Although β-lactam resistance mediated by ampC-type enzymes has been documented in African S. flexneri, there is currently no direct evidence for the occurrence of mphB, eptA, pmrF, ugd, or bacA in South African strains. Existing studies have predominantly focused on other resistance determinants, such as blaTEM, mphA, and mcr-1. Targeted genomic surveillance is therefore warranted to assess these specific genes’ distribution and epidemiological significance in local S. flexneri populations.

Among the sequenced Shigella isolates, S. boydii R7-1A carried mdf(A), blaTEM-1A, aph(3’’)-Ib, aph(6)-Id, sul2, tet(A), and floR, conferring resistance to fluoroquinolones, β-lactams, aminoglycosides, sulphonamides, tetracyclines, and phenicols, respectively. This broad resistome qualifies strain R7-1A as MDR, consistent with definitions requiring resistance to ≥ 3 antibiotic classes (Public Health Ontario 2023). These findings echo global reports of emerging MDR S. boydii (Wang et al. 2019a, b), despite it being less frequently studied than S. flexneri. Emerging reports have highlighted the increasing importance of S. boydii as a potential public health concern, particularly due to its resistance to fluoroquinolones and third-generation cephalosporins (Wang et al. 2016a, b). A meta-analysis conducted in Africa, including South Africa, reported that about 10% of Shigella isolates were resistant to ciprofloxacin and 8.5% to ceftriaxone, with S. boydii responsible for approximately 7.7% of these cases (Nyarkoh et al. 2024). Moreover, plasmid-mediated resistance genes such as blaCTX-M-14 and various qnr gene variants encoding for quinolone resistance have been detected in S. boydii strains isolated from human infections, indicating their capacity to acquire and spread antimicrobial resistance (Wang et al. 2016a, b). While our findings do not confirm clinical relevance, they support the hypothesis that S. boydii may be an underrecognized contributor to environmental and zoonotic antimicrobial resistance. The use of whole genome sequencing in this study enabled detailed resistome characterization. It provided a foundation for future surveillance efforts to understand the role of S. boydii in resistance transmission beyond clinical settings.

The phenotypic resistance profile of S. boydii strain R7-1A, which exhibited resistance to 11 antibiotics, is mainly attributable to the identified ARGs with broad-spectrum activity. The β-lactamase gene blaTEM-1A accounted for resistance to multiple β-lactams, including ampicillin, amoxicillin, piperacillin, and ticarcillin, with partial activity against cephalothin. Resistance to sulfamethoxazole was mediated by sul2, while aph(3’’)-Ib and aph(6)-Id conferred resistance to streptomycin. Tetracycline-class resistance was explained by tet(A), and floR was associated with reduced susceptibility to chloramphenicol and florfenicol. Notably, the multidrug efflux pump gene mdf(A), detected in all Shigella strains examined, may contribute to low-level resistance across multiple antibiotic classes. However, it does not appear to be the primary determinant for any specific agent tested.

Comparative genomics has shown that S. flexneri strains in South Africa show significant diversity in antimicrobial resistance genes, including extended-spectrum β-lactamases, with local persistence (Kahsay and Muthupandian 2016). The most prevalent antimicrobial resistance gene across the examined genomes is mdfA, present in 260 S. flexneri genomes (99%). The South African S. flexneri strains show significant diversity in antimicrobial resistance genes, including extended-spectrum β-lactamases. Multidrug resistance in this bacterium is widespread in Africa, including South Africa, and is resistant to multiple antibiotics, as also observed in this study among the compared genomes (Kahsay and Muthupandian. 2016). Across the compared S. flexneri genomes from South Africa, other ARGs included aminoglycosides, macrolides, rifampin, chloramphenicol, trimethoprim, quinolones, sulphonamides, tetracyclines, and antibiotics affected by multidrug efflux mechanisms. Globally, the average number of antimicrobial resistance genes in S. flexneri isolates has increased significantly from 1970 to 2010, with the human development index driving antibiotic consumption and indirectly promoting antibiotic resistance (Fang et al. 2023).

Plasmid analysis further differentiated the two Shigella species. The S. flexneri strains carried three shared plasmids (AA169, AA304, and AD448), none harbouring antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs). However, based on our annotation, plasmid AA304 exhibited a high density of mobile genetic elements (MGEs), including integration, recombination, and stability/defence genes, indicating its capacity to acquire and disseminate resistance elements under selective pressure. This is consistent with global findings that MGEs such as insertion sequences and integrons play a central role in the horizontal transfer of extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL) genes and plasmid-mediated quinolone resistance in Shigella species (Mannion et al. 2018; Al-Khafaji et al. 2024). These S. flexneri strains contained IncFIA_1 and IncFIB(AP001918)_1 replicons, typical of conjugative plasmids in Enterobacteriaceae (Rozwandowicz et al. 2018). In South Africa, IncF replicon types have been identified in multidrug-resistant Enterobacteriaceae from clinical and environmental sources, including wastewater and livestock, underscoring the importance of monitoring these replicons in Shigella as well (Bisi-Johnson et al. 2011). Given the increasing detection of ESBL-producing Shigella strains globally, the presence of MGEs and conjugative plasmid replicons in S. flexneri isolates from this study highlights the need for continued genomic surveillance (Somda et al. 2025). These genomic elements act as silent reservoirs that may rapidly enable resistance dissemination when environmental or antimicrobial pressures emerge.

Conversely, S. boydii R7-1A hosted three plasmids, including AB180 and AD312, which encoded ARGs (tet(A) and floR), conferring resistance to tetracycline and chloramphenicol, respectively. These plasmids harboured a diverse array of MGEs and replicon types, including IncFIA(HI1), IncR, and ColRNAI, possibly indicating their potential for horizontal gene transfer and stable maintenance within bacterial populations (Rozwandowicz et al. 2018). However, further functional validation would be required to confirm the mobility and persistence of these plasmids under environmental or clinical conditions. This observation aligns with broader global trends, where S. boydii, though historically considered less virulent, has increasingly been recognized as a reservoir for multidrug resistance (Asad et al. 2025). Plasmids carrying tet(A) and floR have been reported in Shigella and other Enterobacteriaceae worldwide, often associated with IncF and IncR replicons, which are well-known for their conjugative capabilities and broad host range (Rozwandowicz et al. 2018). These plasmid types have been implicated in spreading resistance across clinical, agricultural, and environmental settings (Ranjbar and Farahani 2019). In South Africa, studies have identified IncF-type plasmids in multidrug-resistant Enterobacteriaceae from livestock and wastewater sources, underscoring the ecological persistence and mobility of such replicons (Osei Sekyere and Reta 2020). Although S. boydii is less frequently isolated in South Africa than S. flexneri and other species, its documented presence and capacity to harbour transferable resistance genes raise concerns about its role in the regional antimicrobial resistance landscape (Nyarkoh et al. 2024). These findings highlight the importance of monitoring clinically dominant Shigella species and less prevalent ones like S. boydii, which may act as silent reservoirs of resistance. Detecting diverse MGEs and replicon types in S. boydii plasmids emphasizes the need for integrated genomic surveillance across human, animal, and environmental interfaces.

Interestingly, plasmids such as AA169 and AD448, despite lacking identifiable replicons and ARGs, exhibited a high density of MGEs, including insertion sequences and transposases. While these plasmids do not currently harbour resistance determinants, their structural composition suggests a latent capacity to facilitate horizontal gene transfer. MGE-rich plasmids may act as genomic scaffolds, primed for the future acquisition and mobilization of ARGs, particularly under selective pressure or in microbial communities with high plasmid exchange rates (Tokuda and Shintani 2024). This highlights the need to interpret plasmid relevance not solely based on ARG content, but also on structural features that may contribute to the long-term dynamics of resistance dissemination.

Whole-genome single-nucleotide polymorphism analysis placed SH253 and SH254 in a closely related clade, genetically distinct from other South African S. flexneri strains, indicating potential regional adaptation or a novel lineage. Both SH253 and SH254 presented unknown serovars, which were rare (< 2%) among 261 analyzed S. flexneri genomes. Moreover, whole-genome SNP phylogeny provides high-resolution discrimination between closely related strains, making it a powerful tool for tracking Shigella’s transmission dynamics and evolutionary trajectories in both clinical and environmental settings (Stenhouse et al. 2023). The distinct placement of strains SH253 and SH254 suggests that while they are part of the broader S. flexneri clade, they may represent a niche-adapted or environmentally persistent lineage, particularly given their isolation from manure (Mukhopadhyay et al. 2024). Among the 261 S. flexneri genomes analyzed, serotype 2 A was dominant (96%), consistent with global epidemiological patterns (Jennison and Verma 2004). However, strains SH253 and SH254 could not be assigned to any known serovar, reinforcing the uniqueness of these environmental strains. Their clustering reflects close evolutionary relatedness and may indicate localized adaptation to environmental reservoirs or hosts, suggesting they likely originated from a common ancestor or represent a localized transmission event. This finding is consistent with previous studies showing that S. flexneri strains often form geographically or epidemiologically distinct clades, reflecting regional adaptation or clonal expansion (Stenhouse et al. 2023). The formation of a particular clade by strains SH253 and SH254 within the broader S. flexneri lineage supports the idea that these strains are part of a unique sub-lineage circulating in the South African environment. Similar patterns have been observed in other genomic studies, where S. flexneri isolates from specific regions form well-supported phylogenetic clusters, often associated with shared antimicrobial resistance profiles or virulence traits (Mukhopadhyay et al. 2024; Gabor et al. 2025). The phylogenetic distinctiveness of S. flexneri strains SH253 and SH254 from other publicly available South African strains further underscores the genomic diversity within S. flexneri populations and highlights the importance of local surveillance to monitor emerging or underrepresented lineages. These findings emphasize the value of integrating genomic epidemiology into public health surveillance, especially in regions like South Africa, where Shigella remains a significant cause of diarrheal disease. Continued genomic monitoring will be essential to detect emerging variants, understand their evolutionary origins, and inform targeted interventions.

The limitation of this study is the small number of sequenced genomes (n = 3), which hinders the generalizability of the findings and the ability to capture the broader genomic diversity of Shigella populations from animal-associated environments. Furthermore, the exclusive use of short-read sequencing restricts the resolution of complete plasmid structures and mobile genetic elements. This limitation is significant for understanding horizontal gene transfer and the spread of antimicrobial resistance. Future research should leverage long-read sequencing technologies, such as Oxford Nanopore Technology and PacBio (Lang et al. 2020; Wick et al. 2017), to facilitate the comprehensive reconstruction of plasmids and the detailed profiling of mobile genetic elements. Furthermore, it is crucial to conduct functional characterization of hypothetical proteins, particularly those associated with virulence and resistance, to gain insight into their biological functions. Expanding the sampling scope to encompass diverse geographic regions, host species, and farming systems will also enhance epidemiological surveillance and contribute to developing targeted intervention strategies within the “One Health” framework.

Conclusion

This study provides the first genomic characterization of S. flexneri and S. boydii isolated from small ruminant manure and faeces in South Africa, revealing distinct antimicrobial resistance, virulence, and plasmid profiles. While S. flexneri strains carried β-lactam, macrolide, polymyxin, and bacitracin resistance determinants, S. boydii harboured a broader resistome, including tetracycline, phenicol, sulphonamide, aminoglycoside, and β-lactamase genes, and was classified as multidrug-resistant. Both species possessed diverse multidrug efflux systems, but their plasmid replicon types were species-specific. Virulence gene content was markedly higher in S. flexneri, particularly for fimbrial assembly and type II secretion systems. Comparative genomic analysis of South African S. flexneri strains revealed their placement within a distinct phylogenetic clade with unique serovar profiles and limited ARG and plasmid content overlap with other local strains. These findings highlight livestock-associated Shigella as a potential reservoir of clinically relevant resistance and virulence determinants, underscoring the need for integrated One Health surveillance.
