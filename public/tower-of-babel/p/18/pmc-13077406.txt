Prophylaxis with diltiazem decreases arrhythmia rate when compared to beta-blocker following lobectomy
Authors: Adam R Dyas, Christina M Stuart, Kyle E Bata, Michael R Bronsert, William G Henderson, Alyson D Kelleher, Alison R Barker, Simran K Randhawa, Elizabeth A David, John D Mitchell, Robert A Meguid
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThere is mixed evidence supporting arrhythmia prophylaxis after lobectomy in patients not taking preoperative beta-blockers (BBs). We sought to determine the safety and efficacy of postoperative-BB versus diltiazem in BB-naïve patients in preventing post-lobectomy arrhythmia.MethodsThis was a cohort study at one academic medical center (01/2019–03/2023). All patients undergoing elective lobectomy were included. Before May 2021, all patients undergoing lobectomy received low dose oral metoprolol postoperatively for arrhythmia prophylaxis. After May 2021, patients were started on oral diltiazem postoperatively unless they were receiving BB preoperatively, in which case their BB was continued. Patients were divided into pre-implementation versus post-implementation groups. The primary outcome was the rate of postoperative arrhythmia; secondary outcomes were the rates of postoperative heart failure, nonhome discharge, stroke and hypotension. Bivariable and multivariable logistic regression comparisons were performed.ResultsIn total, 606 patients were included; 318 patients (52.5%) were in the pre-implementation cohort. There were minimal differences in perioperative characteristics between groups. Patients in the post-implementation cohort had significantly lower rates of postoperative cardiac arrhythmias (5.2% vs. 9.7%, P=0.04). There were no differences in rates of postoperative cardiac failure/arrest, nonhome discharge, or stroke. After controlling for confounders, patients in the post-implementation cohort had lower risk-adjusted odds of postoperative arrhythmias (odds ratio 0.40, 95% confidence interval: 0.20–0.76) and hypotension (odds ratio 0.68, 95% confidence interval: 0.48–0.97).ConclusionsPatients had lower risk-adjusted rates of post-lobectomy arrhythmia and hypotension when using diltiazem for arrhythmia prevention compared to metoprolol. Our findings support the use of postoperative diltiazem preferentially in BB-naïve patients.Keywords: Beta-blocker (BB), calcium channel blocker, diltiazem, metoprolol, lobectomy

Highlight box

Key findings

• Patients undergoing thoracic lobectomy may benefit from atrial fibrillation prophylaxis using diltiazem rather than other agents.

What is known and what is new?

• Patients undergoing thoracic lobectomy are at increased risk for perioperative atrial fibrillation.

• Our study demonstrates improved risk-adjusted rates of postoperative atrial fibrillation with diltiazem compared to beta-blockers.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Diltiazem should be considered a preferential agent for postoperative atrial fibrillation prophylaxis after thoracic lobectomy.

Introduction

Despite advances in surgical technique including the use of minimally invasive surgery, postoperative arrhythmias remain a common complication after lung resection, occurring in 10–20% of cases (1,2). Preventing arrhythmias remains of paramount importance because they have been associated with increased intensive care use, healthcare cost, longer length of stay, more frequent readmissions, and complications like stroke and in-hospital death (3-9). The etiology of post-operative arrhythmia is multifactorial, with patient, surgical, and postoperative management factors all contributing to occurrence. Some risk factors include pre-existing arrhythmias, extensive mediastinal nodal dissection, volume overload, and electrolyte imbalances. Since this challenging problem has potentially severe consequences, there have been numerous studies comparing the effectiveness of pharmaceutical prophylactic agents with different mechanisms of action (10-14). The results of these studies to date have been mixed (10,15).

At the University of Colorado Hospital, the practice of the general thoracic surgery group historically was to administer prophylactic beta-blockers (BBs) postoperatively for all patients undergoing lobectomy, bilobectomy, or pneumonectomy. This practice was based on several studies demonstrating that BBs are effective at reducing postoperative arrhythmias after lung resection (12,16,17). However, the DECREASE trials, which showed benefit with perioperative initiation of beta blockade in noncardiac surgery and were the basis of societal recommendations regarding perioperative initiation of beta blockade, were discredited due to academic misconduct (18). The Society of Thoracic Surgeons (STS) guidelines (19), which were published prior to the reports of misconduct in the DECREASE trials, are the only major cardiothoracic societal guidelines that mention initiation of perioperative beta blockade in BB naïve patients undergoing lung resection. In fact, a more recent clinical trial and meta-analysis both suggested that starting a BB in the perioperative period for noncardiac surgery actually increases mortality by at least 25%, as well as increasing rates of stroke and clinically relevant hypotension (20,21). For these reasons, the thoracic surgery group changed our arrhythmia prophylaxis protocol to more effectively reflect current evidence and societal recommendations.

While the European Association for Cardio-Thoracic Surgery (EACTS) guidelines state that there is currently inadequate evidence to recommend routine prophylaxis for all patients undergoing lung surgery (22), the American Association for Thoracic Surgery (AATS) categorizes lobectomies as high-risk procedures for the development of postoperative arrhythmias (23). Although the AATS (23), STS (19), EACTS (22), and thoracic enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) (24) guidelines all provide strong recommendations to continue BBs in the perioperative period in patients taking BBs preoperatively, there are only moderate and class II recommendations to initiate specific agents postoperatively in BB naïve patients. Based on the summary recommendations by these groups, our group chose to use the calcium channel blocker diltiazem for arrhythmia prophylaxis in BB naïve patients. For quality assurance, tracking of postoperative outcomes related to this change was pursued. The purpose of this study was to compare the effectiveness of a post-lobectomy arrhythmia prophylaxis protocol using diltiazem versus metoprolol in BB naïve patients by analyzing differences in postoperative arrhythmia rates. We hypothesized that rates of postoperative arrhythmias would be similar, but that rates of hypotension would be lower in patients who received postoperative diltiazem. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2025-1-2488/rc).

Methods

Ethical oversight

This research study was reviewed by the Colorado Multi-Institutional Review Board and approved on January 4, 2021 (COMIRB #20-3051). This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. Informed consent was not required as it was a secondary use quality improvement study.

Patient population and setting

This was a cohort study comparing a prospective cohort against a retrospectively obtained comparison group at one quaternary academic referral medical center associated with the University of Colorado School of Medicine from January 2019 to March 2023. The general thoracic surgery group accrues patients locally, regionally through extensive referrals, and nationally including through a collaboration with the National Jewish Health outpatient clinics. About 85–90% of the anatomic lung resections performed are lobectomies. Many patients from National Jewish Health are referred for the surgical treatment of nontuberculous mycobacterial disease with pulmonary manifestations. All patients undergoing elective lobectomy were included. Patients were excluded if they underwent pneumonectomy, segmentectomy, wedge resection, or emergent surgery; had preexisting arrhythmias; or were <18 years old.

Data collection

Patients were identified using automated data extraction overseen by a trained analyst from the electronic health record (EHR) based on the presence of “lobectomy” in the “log of procedures descriptive list” column for operations performed by thoracic surgeons during the study period. These patients were cross-referenced with billing data to ensure data accuracy. Other data included in this EHR data extraction were unique patient identifiers, operative surgeon, patient demographics and medical comorbidities, medications administered, and 30-day postoperative outcomes. These data are entered directly by clinicians into the EHR under unique patient identifiers during the individual encounters for each operation. Patient outcomes in the intervention group were extracted monthly. All patients are monitored on telemetry during the inpatient hospitalization, and abnormalities to patient rhythms were verified with electrocardiogram. Changes to rhythm on electrocardiogram are then manually entered into a discrete field in the EHR, which can then be automatically extracted. Complications were tracked using Vizient groupings for International Classification of Diseases-10 (ICD-10) diagnosis codes.

Study design and outcomes

Prior to May of 2021, all patients who underwent a lobectomy received 12.5 milligrams (mg) of oral metoprolol twice daily in the postoperative period for arrhythmia prophylaxis with administrative hold parameters for heart rate under 60 beats per minute or persistent systolic blood pressure of under 100. Metoprolol was initiated on postoperative day zero and continued through discharge. In May 2021, a new arrhythmia prophylaxis protocol was implemented based on societal guidelines. Patients were started on 30 mg of oral diltiazem every 6 hours in the postoperative period with the same hold parameters unless they were receiving a BB preoperatively, in which case their outpatient BB was continued. Diltiazem was also administered on postoperative day zero and continued through discharge. Of note, epidural catheters infusing hydromorphone and bupivacaine are routinely placed for patients undergoing open operations at our institution; patients undergoing minimally invasive lobectomy are treated using intercostal nerve blocks. Patients were divided into a pre-implementation cohort, defined by the dates January 1, 2019, to April 30, 2021, and a post-implementation cohort, defined by the dates May 1, 2021, to March 31, 2023. We also performed a subgroup analysis comparing outcomes of only BB naïve patients in the pre-implementation cohort who received metoprolol versus BB naïve patients in the post-implementation cohort who received diltiazem.

Patient demographics, medical comorbidities, operative data, admission data, Social Vulnerability Index (SVI), and 30-day postoperative outcomes were tracked. The SVI is a validated comprehensive and quantitative measure of social determinants of health that has been shown to be associated with postoperative outcomes after lung resection (25). The primary outcome of interest was the incidence of a postoperative arrhythmias requiring treatment. Arrhythmias were diagnosed using electrocardiograms, and treatment was defined as the addition of medications or procedures for the purposes of rate or rhythm control in patients with a confirmed arrhythmia. For example, patients who develop atrial fibrillation at our institution are typically first treated with up to three intravenous doses of metoprolol, followed by bolus and infusion of intravenous amiodarone if patients do not convert to sinus rhythm after administration of metoprolol. Electrical cardioversion is generally reserved for patients with arrhythmia causing persistent or severe hemodynamic consequences. Changes to treatment were identified in the EHR through discrete medication fields. Secondary outcomes included rates of new postoperative heart failure including cardiac arrest, nonhome discharge, stroke, and hypotension (both including and excluding occurrences on the day of surgery). Nonhome discharge was defined as a discharge location other than prior living situation, and hypotension was defined as the occurrence of any systolic blood pressure less than 90 mmHg. Blood pressures are routinely obtained every four hours using an automated measuring system while the patient is admitted.

Statistical analysis

Bivariable comparisons for the two groups were performed using unpaired t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests for continuous variables, and chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. A multivariable logistic regression analysis controlling for patient age, sex, race/ethnicity, operative duration, operative approach, operative indication, and American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status classification (ASA class) was performed to generate risk-adjusted odds of arrhythmia and postoperative hypotension. The risk-adjusted outcomes are reported as odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Two-sided P values ≤0.05 were considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Inc, Cary, NC, USA).

Results

A total of 606 patients underwent elective lobectomy during the study period. Of these, 318 patients (52.5%) were in the pre-implementation cohort and 288 patients (47.5%) were in the post-implementation cohort. There were 46 patients (7.6%) who had a postoperative arrhythmia in the study group. The ICD-10 diagnosis codes and frequency for postoperative tachyarrhythmia, heart failure, and stroke that occurred in our patient cohort are included in Table 1. Table 2 presents the demographic characteristics and medical comorbidities of the patient cohorts. There was a higher prevalence of diabetes in the post-implementation cohort compared to the pre-implementation cohort (11.1% vs. 5.7%, P=0.02). Patients in the post-implementation cohort also had higher ASA class than those in the pre-implementation cohort (P=0.01). There was no difference in the protocol adherence between the two cohorts (P=0.51). There was also a trend towards increased median age in the post-implementation cohort that did not reach statistical significance (P=0.08). Of note, there were no differences in the number of patients who were on a stable BB regimen prior to surgery in the two cohorts (P=0.19) or in rates of medication being held rather than administered (P=0.84). There were no differences in sex, race/ethnicity, body mass index, smoking status, other medical comorbidities, or SVI between the two cohorts (all P>0.05).

Table 4 presents the unadjusted rates and risk-adjusted odds of 30-day complications for the patient cohorts, and Table 5 presents the predictors for arrhythmia in the multivariable regression analysis. Patients in the post-implementation cohort had significantly lower rates of postoperative cardiac arrhythmias (5.2% vs. 9.7%, P=0.04) compared to pre-implementation. Discussion

In this prospective cohort study of over 600 patients over a more than four-year study period, BB naïve patients who were started on diltiazem prophylaxis had lower rates of post-lobectomy arrhythmia when compared to those on metoprolol prophylaxis, even after adjusting for patient and operative factors. We also observed a lower risk-adjusted rate of postoperative hypotension, both including and excluding the day of surgery, but this was not significant in subgroup analysis. There were no differences in rates of postoperative cardiac failure, nonhome discharge, or stroke, each of which occurred either rarely or infrequently throughout the entire study. The difference in arrhythmia rate persisted on subgroup analysis when directly comparing patients who received metoprolol and diltiazem. We did not include analysis for length of stay or intensive care use because the confounding factors that impact these outcomes would make it challenging to attribute the effect of any differences to changes in postoperative arrhythmia management. Based on these results, diltiazem should be considered a preferred agent for arrhythmia prophylaxis after lobectomy in BB naïve patients.

This study provides an update on the literature for arrhythmia prophylaxis following lobectomy in a contemporary patient population reflective of the use of minimally invasive surgery, including the robotic platform. While at least one existing study suggests there may be no difference in arrhythmia rates between open and VATS operations (26), larger national studies suggest that thoracoscopy is associated with a significantly lower rate of postoperative arrhythmia than thoracotomy (27,28). In our cohort, 88.4% of lobectomies were performed using a minimally-invasive approach, providing a more current reflection and analysis of thoracic surgery practice. Interestingly, there were more open surgeries and a slightly longer median operative time in the post-implementation cohort. This could be due to operating on sicker patients by ASA class, increased use of robotic surgery, performing more reoperations, or requiring longer and more challenging dissections in this cohort. Many of these factors have been previously attributed to increased rates of postoperative arrhythmia in prior studies (2,29-31). The fact that a decrease in arrhythmia was still observed post-implementation is particularly notable.

While societal guidelines provide several pharmaceutical options for prophylaxis, we chose to include diltiazem as our prophylactic agent over verapamil and amiodarone for several reasons. First, diltiazem has been shown to have a relatively low number to treat for arrhythmia prophylaxis while also having low risk of bradycardia and hypotension (10,11). Verapamil has been shown to have higher rates of bradycardia and hypotension (13), and its infusion generally requires admission to intensive care for hemodynamic monitoring. Amiodarone has been shown to be a relatively effective prophylactic agent of choice (14,32), but its administration does come with some drawbacks. Most prophylactic regimens of amiodarone have at least some duration of intravenous amiodarone administration, which would require higher level of inpatient care at our institution, while diltiazem can be administered orally on the thoracic surgery recovery unit. While many studies have not been able to replicate this finding, a clinical trial evaluating arrhythmia prophylaxis with amiodarone after pneumonectomy was stopped due to concern for increased rates of acute respiratory distress syndrome (33). To date, the EACTS and STS still recommend avoiding amiodarone in patients undergoing pneumonectomy (19,22). While we did not evaluate operations other than lobectomies for the purposes of this study, we preferred to choose a medication that could serve as the pharmaceutical prophylactic agent of choice for the majority of patients requiring arrhythmia prophylaxis.

In the future, advancements in risk prediction may be poised to strategically delineate which patients are more or less likely to experience postoperative atrial arrhythmias. For example, a study of almost one thousand patients suggested that the CHADS2 score may be an effective tool for prediction of arrhythmia after lobectomy, but this methodology lacks external validation (34). Other validated preoperative risk prediction models (35-38) could also be used to stratify postoperative cardiac risks after thoracic surgery and develop prophylaxis protocols. Novel artificial intelligence techniques are also being developed for preoperative prediction of complications like postoperative infection (39), and similar methodology could be used to develop risk prediction models for cardiac complications and arrhythmias. The development of more sophisticated arrhythmia prophylaxis protocols could provide a more individualized strategy towards patient care. Until these are developed, routine prophylaxis on all lung resection patients at high risk for postoperative arrhythmias is a reasonable prevention strategy and is supported by societal guidelines.

The results of this study should be interpreted with the consideration of some limitations. The study used a single-center design where a significant number of patients who underwent operations for benign disease were compared to national norms, so generalizability to the national population is unclear. However, operative indication was not a significant contributor to outcomes in the multivariable logistic regression analysis. There may be some secondary outcomes, like rates of medication held and hypotension, that have multifactorial etiology rather than a direct relationship to the medication effects. The study methods included a pre- and post-implementation design, which introduces more opportunity for confounding based on time-related confounding and bias than a double blind, randomized controlled trial (40). To our knowledge, Vizient outcomes are only collected on patients up to hospital discharge. Thus, the incidence rates of postoperative complications might be underestimated. Data acquisition using ICD coding relies on accurate input of variables by clinicians and interpretation by coders, which is a potential source of error. There may be some confounding patient, demographic, or comorbid factors that may be contributing to differences in outcomes. Finally, the subgroup analysis directly comparing metoprolol versus diltiazem may have been underpowered to detect risk-adjusted differences in rates of hypotension.

Conclusions

An arrhythmia prophylaxis protocol using the calcium channel blocker diltiazem in BB naïve patients led to lower risk-adjusted rates of arrhythmia and hypotension after lobectomy compared to the BB metoprolol. There was no difference in rate of cardiac failure, nonhome discharge, or stroke. Societal recommendations should be followed and patients on preoperative BB should have their BB continued after lung resection. However, our study suggests that BB naïve patients may benefit from receiving diltiazem rather than metoprolol for improved arrhythmia prevention and lower rates of hypotension.
