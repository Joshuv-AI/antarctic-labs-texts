Epidemiology and Treatment of Patients with Essential Tremor: A Retrospective Cohort Analysis in the United Kingdom
Authors: Tabish A. Saifee, Keltie O’Neill, Evi Zhuleku, Marco Ghiani, Natasha Dzimitrowicz, Javier Sabater, Jos Becktepe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionEssential tremor (ET) carries a substantial burden and unmet need. Limited real-world evidence exists for the epidemiology, clinical characteristics, and treatment patterns of ET in the UK. We conducted a retrospective cohort study using a large electronic health records database from the UK.MethodsData were obtained from Clinical Practice Research Datalink linked with Hospital Episode Statistics inpatient and outpatient databases from 1 January 2010 to 31 December 2022. Point prevalence and cumulative incidence were assessed, including age- and sex-standardized estimates to the Office for National Statistics mid-year population estimates. In a cohort of newly diagnosed ET patients (1 January 2011–31 March 2020), we evaluated baseline characteristics, clinical features, and treatment patterns. We used descriptive statistics as well as time-to-event analyses (Kaplan-Meier methodology).ResultsAge-sex standardized prevalence ranged from 20.62 to 55.08 per 100,000 persons (2010–2021), peaking at 55.08 in 2019. Age-sex standardized cumulative incidence ranged from 3.85 to 6.81 per 100,000 persons (2011–2021), peaking at 6.81 in 2017. Common comorbidities included hypertension (23.43%), pain disorders (17.12%), and diabetes (15.64%). Most patients (86.07%) received at least one pharmacological therapy: propranolol (68.34%), primidone (27.68%), and gabapentin (22.14%) were most frequently used. Median time from diagnosis to treatment initiation was 0.43 months (95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.36–0.46); median time to discontinuation from start of first therapy was 4.63 months (95% CI: 4.27–5.19); median time to switch was 21.22 months (95% CI: 20.14–22.60).ConclusionThis study provides UK-based evidence on ET epidemiology, clinical features, and treatments. Despite lower epidemiological estimates attributable to the requirement for two confirmed diagnoses – a methodological choice prioritizing diagnostic specificity in a condition that is frequently overdiagnosed – ET burden and unmet needs remain substantial.

Introduction

Essential tremor (ET) is among the most common movement disorders, affecting an estimated 1.3% of the global population [1]. However, prevalence estimates in the literature vary significantly [1, 2]. Despite advances in understanding of ET in recent decades, its diagnostic criteria remain contentious [3, 4]. These inconsistencies in definition likely contribute to the variability in epidemiologic estimates. Additionally, the actual prevalence of ET may be higher than reported, as a significant number of individuals remain undiagnosed and/or untreated [5]. It is also widely recognized that ET is frequently overdiagnosed in routine clinical practice, with a substantial proportion of patients carrying a primary-care diagnosis of ET having a different underlying condition on specialist review [6, 7].

ET is commonly characterized by chronic action tremors in the upper and lower limbs [8] and is associated with physical and mild cognitive impairment in adults [9, 10]. It affects men and women at similar rates [1]. The age of onset of ET appears to follow two distinct patterns: early-onset (≤24 years) and late-onset (≥46 years), which may represent clinically distinct subgroups differing in progression, heritability, and alcohol sensitivity [11]. Other factors, such as depression, anxiety, and age, are also linked to increased functional disability in ET, independent of tremor severity [12].

Treatment for ET is primarily symptomatic, with pharmacotherapy as first-line. A review by the International Parkinson and Movement Disorder Society concluded that propranolol, primidone, and topiramate (at doses of >200 mg/day) were clinically useful. Alprazolam was also considered likely efficacious, though its use may be limited due to the risk of dependence. Notably, sufficient evidence was only found for the treatment of upper-limb tremor [13]. While pharmacotherapy can reduce tremors by up to 80%, complete tremor control is rare, and residual tremors can still be functionally disabling [14]. Managing ET is often complicated by delayed diagnoses, comorbidities, and the lack of effective and tolerable medications [15]. Non-pharmacologic treatments, though rarely used, include lifestyle adaptations (such as weighted utensils and, in cases of voice tremor, amplification devices) and invasive neurosurgical procedures like deep brain stimulation, radiofrequency thalamotomy, and magnetic-resonance-imaging-guided focused ultrasound thalamotomy for patients unresponsive to pharmacotherapy [15, 16].

Further research is needed to understand the epidemiological profile and characteristics of ET patients, as well as the associated standard of care. This study utilized electronic health records (EHRs) to explore the epidemiology, patient characteristics, and treatment patterns of ET in the UK.

Methods

Data Source

This retrospective cohort study was conducted using data from Clinical Practice Research Datalink (CPRD) Aurum linked with Hospital Episode Statistics (HES) admitted patient care (APC) and outpatient (OP) databases. CPRD Aurum collects patient-level EHR from consenting general practitioner (GP) practices using the EMIS Web patient management software in England (and some practices in Northern Ireland since 2019) [17]. CPRD Aurum provides detailed data on patient demographics, clinical events, diagnoses, referrals, prescriptions, immunizations, and lifestyle information.

For GP practices in England, data are linked to HES APC and HES OP, which contain data for all inpatient and OP admissions, respectively, occurring within NHS hospitals in England [18]. Approximately 50% of practices are eligible for linkage covered by CPRD. HES APC records detailed information on hospital admissions, including diagnoses and procedures, while HES OP captures data on OP appointments, including consultation details and treatment information.

We used the December 2023 build of CPRD Aurum, which contained data on approximately 46 million research-acceptable patients from 1,589 GP practices, covering 23.89% of the UK population. Approximately 75% of patients were eligible for linkage [19].

Study Population

We identified a base cohort, used to evaluate the point prevalence and cumulative incidence of ET, and a newly diagnosed cohort, used to evaluate baseline characteristics and treatment patterns. The base cohort included acceptable research patients with positive follow-up time and at least one primary care record of ET between 1 January 2010 and 31 December 2021 (ICD-10: G25.0; medcode: 297050013). Eligible patients also had at least two records of ET in primary or secondary care within a 12-month period; the first of the two diagnoses had to occur during 2010–2021, while the second (confirmatory) diagnosis could occur up to 31 December 2022. Patients were excluded if they had a diagnosis of other tremor-related neurologic disorders or diseases with common symptomatic tremor after the first observed ET diagnosis or a prescription of a tremor-inducing agent in the 12 months before diagnosis to reduce risk of misidentification (see online Supplemental File 1; for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000553098) for list of codes; numbers of patients excluded by each diagnosis and product are shown in online supp. Table 1). The index date was the date of the first recorded ET diagnosis in either the primary or secondary care databases. HES-linkage eligibility was not applied as an inclusion criterion for the base cohort; patients without HES linkage could be included provided both qualifying diagnoses were recorded in primary care.

The newly diagnosed cohort was further restricted to patients first diagnosed with ET between 1 January 2011 and 31 March 2020. Patients were continuously registered with their GP in the 12 months before the index date and the 12 months after the index date and were eligible for linkage with HES.

Cumulative incidence and point prevalence were evaluated for each calendar year from 2010 to 2021. The newly diagnosed cohort was followed from index date to the earliest of patient transfer-out date, GP practice last collection date, CPRD-derived death date, or the end of the study period (31 March 2021).

Measures

Baseline Characteristics

Baseline characteristics included sex, age, ethnicity, region of registered GP practice, year of initial ET diagnosis (index year), Charlson comorbidity index score, Elixhauser comorbidity index score, CHA2DS2-VASc score, the presence of prespecified comorbidities, alcohol use, and smoking status. Ethnicity was primarily derived from HES data and supplemented with information from CPRD if missing. Comorbidities, including alcohol use, were identified based on diagnoses recorded in CPRD or HES during the 12 months before the index date. Charlson comorbidity index scores and Elixhauser comorbidity index scores were derived in accordance with Metcalfe et al. (2019) [20]. CHA2DS2-VASc was identified based on Hindricks et al. (2021) and Lip et al. (2010) [21, 22]. Smoking status was identified from CPRD Aurum records. See online Supplemental File 1 for the list of codes.

Treatment Patterns

The use of pharmacological and non-pharmacological treatments in the newly diagnosed cohort was assessed based on the presence of at least one record of a prescription or intervention during follow-up (online Supplemental File 1). Prespecified pharmacological agents were identified using medcodes, while interventions were identified using medcodes (CPRD) or ICD-10/OPCS codes (HES). Occupational therapy and physiotherapy were recorded based on visits to specialist services (online Supplemental File 1).

Pharmacological treatment lines were defined using a rule-based algorithm. The first prescription after the index date signified the start of the first line of therapy. Any agent prescribed within 30 days of the initial agent was considered part of a combination therapy. A new prescription or a combination therapy marked the beginning of the next line of therapy. A line of therapy ended when all agents in that line were discontinued. Discontinuation was defined as a 90-day gap following the assumed end of drug supply, or a switch to a new agent or combination. The presumed days’ supply was based on dosage and quantity, using the World Health Organization’s defined daily dose. We accounted for stockpiling and assumed continuous treatment coverage during hospitalizations. The discontinuation date was the earlier of either the start of the 90-day gap or the first prescription date of the new agent or combination. The switch date was marked by the first prescription of the next line of therapy. The 30-day combination window and 90-day discontinuation gap are widely applied conventions in pharmacoepidemiological research using primary-care and administrative data. Both thresholds carry predictable directional biases that are considered explicitly in the Discussion: the 30-day window will tend to underestimate true combination therapy where add-on agents are introduced after several months, while the 90-day gap will tend to overestimate discontinuation of agents used on an as-needed basis (such as propranolol).

Statistical Analysis

Point prevalence of ET was estimated annually from 2010 to 2021. Point prevalence was calculated as the ratio of the number of prevalent patients with ET to the total number of research-acceptable patients in CPRD Aurum alive and registered with a GP on 1 January of the respective calendar year. Patients with ET had an index date before 31 December that year.

Cumulative incidence of ET was estimated annually from 2011 to 2021. Cumulative incidence was calculated as the ratio of the number of incident patients with ET to the total number of research-acceptable patients in CPRD Aurum, with no history of ET, alive and registered with a GP on 1 January of the respective year.

We calculated crude and age-sex-standardized estimates per 100,000 persons. Standardized estimates were calculated using the age and sex distributions of the Office for National Statistics mid-year population estimates for the UK each year.

Baseline characteristics and overall treatment use were summarized using descriptive statistics, including frequencies and percentages (categorical variables), and mean scores, standard deviations, and medians (continuous variables). Time-to-event outcomes were analyzed using Kaplan-Meier methodology and quantified using median time-to-event with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Analyses were carried out in R version 4.2.1 [23].

Results

Study Populations

The final base population, used to evaluate point prevalence and cumulative incidence, consisted of 16,774 patients. We further identified 6,624 patients who met the inclusion and exclusion criteria for the newly diagnosed cohort (Fig. 1). Of the 7,870 patients meeting all preceding criteria, 1,246 (15.83%) were excluded due to HES-linkage ineligibility, yielding the final newly diagnosed cohort.

Attrition chart of study population.

Epidemiology

Age-sex-standardized point prevalence and cumulative incidence increased over the study period (Table 1). Age-sex-standardized point prevalence per 100,000 persons increased from 20.62 in 2010 to a peak of 55.08 in 2019, followed by a slight decline to 54.23 in 2020 and 51.87 in 2021. Age-sex-standardized cumulative incidence per 100,000 persons increased from 5.12 in 2011 to 6.81 in 2017, followed by a decline to 3.85 per 100,000 in 2021. Crude estimates for prevalence and cumulative incidence showed similar trends (online suppl. Table 2).

Treatment Use during Follow-Up

Among the newly diagnosed cohort, 86.07% (n = 5,701) of patients received at least one ET-related pharmacological therapy during the follow-up period (Table 3). The most frequently prescribed medications included propranolol (68.34%), followed by primidone (27.68%), gabapentin (22.14%), and bisoprolol (20.56%). Topiramate (11.45%) and pregabalin (9.77%) were also among the most frequently prescribed therapies. Non-pharmacological therapies were infrequent.

First-Line Treatment

Among treated patients in the newly diagnosed cohort (n = 5,701), the first treatment received after the index date was primarily monotherapy (86.76%), while 13.24% were treated with combination therapy. Propranolol (52.94%) was the most common initial therapy (monotherapy or combination therapy), followed by primidone (9.38%), bisoprolol (9.26%), and gabapentin (5.33%). Among those who received combination therapy as the first treatment, the most frequent regimens were propranolol with primidone (1.63%) and propranolol with gabapentin (1.49%).

Time-to-Treatment Initiation, Discontinuation, and Switch

Among the newly diagnosed cohort (n = 6,624), 77.58% (n = 5,139) initiated first treatment within 12 months of their index date, while 84.15% (n = 5,574) started within 24 months, and 7.76% (n = 514) started treatment more than 48 months after the index date. The median time from index date to treatment initiation was 0.43 months (95% CI: 0.36-0.46) (Fig. 2a).

a–c Time-to-treatment initiation, discontinuation, and switch (Kaplan-Meier estimates).

Among treated patients (n = 5,701), 65.39% (n = 3,728) of patients discontinued their first treatment within 12 months, and 79.14% (n = 4,512) discontinued within 24 months. The median time to discontinuation of first treatment was 4.63 months (95% CI: 4.27-5.19) (Fig. 2b). Furthermore, 35.92% (n = 2,048) of patients switched to a subsequent treatment within the first 12 months, and 49.90% (n = 2,845) switched within 24 months. The median time from initiation of first treatment to start of subsequent treatment was 21.22 months (95% CI: 20.14-22.60) (Fig. 2c).

Discussion

This large retrospective cohort study extends evidence for the epidemiology, characteristics, and treatment patterns of ET in the UK. To our knowledge, only one other observational study of the epidemiology of ET has been conducted in the UK, which used data from UK THIN; however, it covered a smaller population of 4,970 incident patients [24].

The epidemiological estimates derived from this study were considerably lower than those reported in the literature. Existing evidence for the prevalence of ET has been heterogeneous. A meta-analysis of 42 population-based studies reported a pooled prevalence of 1.33% [1], while a recent systematic review of 29 studies estimated an overall prevalence of approximately 320 per 100,000 in 2020 [2]. The lower prevalence and cumulative incidence observed in our study reflect the stringent definition that we employed. Our cumulative incidence estimates were also considerably lower than those reported in the UK-based study by Antonazzo et al. (2022) [24]; they observed a standardized cumulative incidence of 19.51 per 100,000 persons in 2019, while our corresponding estimate was 5.81 per 100,000 persons – an approximately 3.4-fold difference. They used a more lenient definition of at least one record of ET, essential and other specified forms of tremor, benign ET, or essential and other specified forms of tremor NOS, and they applied no exclusionary diagnoses. ET is widely recognized as a frequently overdiagnosed condition, with approximately 1 in 3 patients referred to specialist movement disorder centres with a diagnosis of ET in fact having a different disorder [6], and the term having been described as having acquired the character of a “waste-basket” diagnosis in primary care practice [7]. A less restrictive case definition would therefore inflate apparent prevalence and incidence largely by incorporating misclassified cases, with consequent contamination of any downstream analyses of comorbidity and treatment. We excluded patients with several other differential diagnoses, making our definition more robust to potential misdiagnosis of ET and resulting in a more homogeneous group of patients and lower epidemiological estimates. Table 4 provides a structured comparison of the principal epidemiological studies of ET against the present analysis, highlighting the principal methodological sources of heterogeneity (case definition stringency, data source, in-person examination vs. EHR ascertainment, and age structure of the studied population). Notably, the lower prevalence may also reflect a limitation of the data, namely infrequent recording of diagnosis information in HES OP, thereby leading to decreased identification of patients with ET from OP services. Our estimates should therefore be interpreted as reflecting diagnosed and coded ET managed within English primary and secondary care, representing a conservative lower bound of true ET burden in the UK.

International comparisons further contextualize our estimates. Door-to-door population surveys in the USA, including the Washington Heights-Inwood Columbia Aging Project [25] and the Copiah County Study [26], have reported markedly higher age-adjusted ET prevalence than EHR-based studies (approximately 4% in adults ≥65 years and approximately 415 per 100,000, respectively), reflecting the capacity of in-person neurological examination to detect mild and undiagnosed tremor not represented in routinely collected healthcare data. Incidence data from the Rochester Epidemiology Project [27] reported a rate of 23.7 per 100,000 person-years, also substantially exceeding EHR-based estimates. Across the international evidence base, EHR-based estimates such as ours and those of Antonazzo et al. [24] consistently represent a conservative lower bound, capturing only those cases that have been diagnosed, coded and managed within the formal healthcare system. The treatment and comorbidity findings reported here are broadly consistent with US real-world claims-based evidence [15, 28], supporting the international relevance of the patterns observed in the UK.

While we observed a decline in cumulative incidence after 2017, this is best understood as having two phases. The modest fall from 2017 to 2019 most likely reflects an end-of-window artefact of our two-diagnosis case definition: as the observation period closes, patients diagnosed late have less opportunity to accrue a confirmatory record within the dataset, suppressing apparent incidence independently of any change in true disease occurrence. The steeper fall observed in 2020 and 2021 is unlikely to be explained by this methodological artefact alone. The COVID-19 pandemic produced substantial reductions in primary care consultations and elective OP activity in England from March 2020 onwards, very likely delaying ET presentation, reducing formal coding (particularly for a condition that benefits from in-person examination) and depressing specialist confirmatory diagnoses. The concurrent absence of HES OP data from October 2020 further compresses apparent incidence in the closing years of the study by limiting access to secondary-care confirmatory records. The 2020–2021 decline therefore reflects a combination of pandemic-related changes in healthcare-seeking and diagnostic recording, end-of-window methodological effects, and data availability constraints, rather than a true reduction in ET incidence in the UK population.

We also observed increasing prevalence and cumulative incidence of ET with age, likely due to the low mortality rates associated with this disease. While it is well documented that prevalence tends to increase with age [1, 2], some literature suggests a bimodal distribution of incidence by age [29, 30]. The distribution of cumulative incidence in our study did not appear to follow a bimodal trend. This likely reflects the fact that the cohort skews older, with a mean age of 64.17 years and a median age of 69 years. The literature also widely reports a higher prevalence of ET in males than in females. While the prevalence estimates from our study were consistent with this finding, cumulative incidence among females was similar but slightly higher than that of males before age 60. After age 60, cumulative incidence in males was higher, and the increase was substantially steeper compared with females.

There is a high comorbidity burden among patients with ET. The most frequently co-occurring conditions observed in our sample – hypertension, pain disorders, and diabetes mellitus – were similar to those observed in the wider literature [28]. However, the proportions of patients with comorbidities at baseline were somewhat lower than expected. This is likely a reflection of the limitations of the dataset due to the low recording of diagnoses in HES OP, thereby missing some patients who may have had these conditions.

While emerging evidence suggests that a large proportion of patients with ET are never treated, we observed that over 85% of patients received at least one ET-related pharmacological therapy during follow-up. The most frequently used agents (as monotherapy or as part of combination therapy) at any time during follow-up included propranolol and primidone, alongside other anticonvulsants and β-blockers including gabapentin, bisoprolol, and topiramate. The frequent use of propranolol and primidone aligns with treatment guidelines by the International Movement Disorders Society [13] and information published by NICE [31]. Topiramate was used less frequently than expected given that it is the only other medication (along with propranolol and primidone) with robust evidence of efficacy [13].

Aside from propranolol and primidone, a very wide variety of other medications were also used. This may reflect a lack of effective treatment options and/or frequent side effects for the available therapies, leading physicians to prescribe a wider range of therapy options.

The overall pharmacotherapy rate of 86.07% should be interpreted as an upper bound of ET-directed treatment rather than as a precise estimate of ET-specific prescribing. Prescriptions in CPRD Aurum cannot be individually linked to the diagnosis for which they were issued, and several of the most frequently observed agents in our cohort are widely prescribed for comorbid indications: gabapentin (22.14%) and pregabalin (9.77%) are first-line agents for neuropathic pain [32], which was recorded in 17.12% of patients in the 12 months before index and 42.16% of patients in the first year after index; bisoprolol (20.56%) is widely prescribed for hypertension, the most prevalent comorbidity in our cohort (23.43%), and has weaker evidence for ET than propranolol. By contrast, propranolol (68.34%), primidone (27.68%), and topiramate (11.45%) carry guideline-endorsed evidence for ET [13] and are far less susceptible to indication confounding; these proportions provide a more conservative lower bound for ET-specific pharmacotherapy. The 86.07% figure should therefore not be read as evidence that essentially all patients receive disease-modifying therapy for ET; rather, it reflects cumulative exposure to any of a broad list of prespecified agents, an unknown proportion of which were prescribed for comorbid indications.

The 86.07% pharmacotherapy rate observed in our cohort is also higher than estimates commonly reported in the literature and warrants careful interpretation. This figure represents cumulative exposure across a mean follow-up of over four and a half years, rather than a point-in-time measure of current treatment; over a sufficiently long observation window, cumulative exposure to any of a broad list of agents approaches a high proportion even where many patients cycle on and off therapy rapidly, as evidenced by the 65.39% who discontinued first-line therapy within 12 months. The two-diagnosis inclusion criterion further enriches our cohort for patients who sought care on multiple occasions, systematically excluding mild and undiagnosed patients who are least likely to receive treatment and who contribute substantially to lower treatment rates in population-based studies. Taken together, the high initiation rate, rapid discontinuation and frequent switching observed in our cohort are not in tension; they jointly describe frequent treatment attempts and rapid cessation, consistent with prior evidence [33] and reinforcing the persistent unmet need in ET management.

Combination therapies were observed relatively infrequently in our study. We relied on an algorithm, which defined combinations based on prescriptions of agents within 30 days of one another. This definition will tend to underestimate true combination therapy use, as in routine clinical practice, add-on agents are commonly introduced approximately 3 months after the initial agent and would therefore be classified by our algorithm as a new treatment line rather than as combination therapy.

Among patients who initiated a pharmacological intervention during follow-up, 77.58% started within 12 months post index, while relatively fewer (7.76%) started treatment more than 48 months post index. We observed a median time to treatment initiation of 0.43 months (approximately 13 days), which is considerably shorter than that observed in the study by Antonazzo et al. [24], which observed a median time to treatment initiation of 76.6 days [24].

Discontinuation of pharmacological interventions frequently occurred in our study. The median time to discontinuation of first-line therapy was 4.63 months. It is widely reported that patients with ET are undertreated or untreated due to limited treatment options, and in those treated, available medications have undesirable side-effect profiles and are associated with high discontinuation rates [15]. Interestingly, our discontinuation rates were also considerably higher at 12 and 24 months after treatment initiation compared with Antonazzo et al. [24]. However, the differences observed are likely to be influenced by differences in operationalization of treatment lines and discontinuation. While they defined treatment lines based on the presence of specific agents (e.g., propranolol, primidone, and topiramate were the first line), we used a data-driven rule-based approach. Nevertheless, like in their study, our data cannot account for the nuances of treatment use in the real world, which may have resulted in some misclassification of treatment lines, particularly discontinuation. For example, β-blockers are frequently prescribed for patients to use “as needed” and therefore, the time between prescriptions may be long, even if the patient is still using the medication. If the gap was longer than 90 days after the end of the presumed drug supply, the therapy was considered discontinued – a directional bias that will tend to overestimate apparent discontinuation in this subgroup.

There remains a significant unmet need in the treatment of ET. A large number of patients with ET are dissatisfied with their care [34]. A study by Louis et al. (2010) found that nearly one-third of patients, including those with severe tremors, discontinue their medications [33]. Beyond propranolol, primidone, and topiramate, there are very few therapies with strong evidence of effectiveness [13, 35]. Moreover, long-term use of existing therapies, such as propranolol and primidone, is often limited by pharmacoresistance and associated adverse effects [36]. Despite these limitations, pharmacotherapy remains the first-line treatment. In our study, non-pharmacological treatments were rarely used. This may reflect the limitations of general-practitioner databases, which may not capture referrals to specialist centres. The wide range of pharmacological therapies used by patients in our study, along with the high rates of discontinuation and medication switching, further highlights the need for additional research to better understand and improve treatment outcomes for individuals with ET.

Strengths and Limitations

The main strengths of using CPRD/HES data include its large size, extensive coverage, and availability of long-term follow-up. CPRD Aurum data represents approximately 13% of the English population and is broadly reflective of the wider English population [17]. Furthermore, the ability to link primary care data with secondary care (HES) data allows for a comprehensive profile of ET patients. Additionally, data quality is assured at various stages of curation, including collection, transformation, and validation checks [17].

We also applied a rigorous methodological approach, using strict criteria to define our sample. This allowed us to identify a relatively homogeneous group of patients with ET and minimize misdiagnosed cases. Given the well-documented challenges in diagnosing ET and the frequent overdiagnosis of the condition in routine clinical practice, exclusion of competing tremor diagnoses was essential.

Some limitations of this study are acknowledged. First, our two-diagnosis case definition (combined with exclusion of competing tremor diagnoses and tremor-inducing medications) was a deliberate methodological choice intended to enhance diagnostic specificity. A less restrictive definition would increase sensitivity but would do so largely by incorporating misclassified cases [6, 7], with consequent contamination of the comorbidity and treatment analyses. The quantitative impact of this trade-off is illustrated by direct comparison with Antonazzo et al. [24], who applied a single-diagnosis definition without exclusionary criteria in a comparable UK primary-care database and reported a standardized cumulative incidence of 19.51 per 100,000 in 2019, approximately 3.4-fold higher than our corresponding estimate of 5.81 per 100,000. A formal sensitivity analysis using a single-diagnosis definition was not feasible within the current data access protocol, which has now closed; future studies using CPRD or comparable databases should consider this comparison directly.

CPRD Aurum primarily covers practices in England, with only a small number in Northern Ireland. Our analyses of baseline characteristics and treatment patterns were restricted to patients eligible for linkage with HES, which excluded 1,246 of 7,870 otherwise eligible patients (15.83%), yielding a final newly diagnosed cohort of 6,624. Patients ineligible for HES linkage are predominantly those registered at GP practices outside England or at English practices not participating in the linkage program; linkage eligibility is therefore determined principally by administrative and geographic factors rather than clinical ones. Published evidence has shown that the linked CPRD subset is broadly representative of the wider CPRD and English populations [17]. Importantly, the epidemiological analyses (base cohort, n = 16,774) were not subject to this restriction, as patients without HES linkage were included provided both qualifying diagnoses were recorded in primary care; the prevalence and incidence estimates are therefore drawn from a substantially broader, less linkage-restricted population than the treatment-pattern findings. Findings may not generalize fully to Wales, Scotland, or Northern Ireland, where differences in coding practice, referral pathways and prescribing may exist.

While our study was rigorous, it only included patients with routinely collected data for ET, potentially missing undiagnosed cases or those with mild tremor. Additionally, some data may be incomplete. Diagnostic information in primary care is manually entered by GPs and may be missing or incomplete. Moreover, recording diagnoses in OP records (HES OP) is not mandatory, leading to frequent gaps in diagnostic information.

HES OP data were available only until 31 October 2020, while our epidemiological analyses extended to 31 December 2021. The absence of HES OP data in 2020–2021 would be expected to produce a downward bias in apparent prevalence and cumulative incidence in those years, as patients whose confirmatory diagnosis would have been recorded in an OP setting after October 2020 would no longer meet the two-diagnosis inclusion criterion. However, diagnostic recording in HES OP is nonmandatory and was incomplete throughout the study period, so HES OP contributed a relatively small share of confirmatory diagnoses even when fully available; primary care and HES inpatient data, both of which were available throughout the study period, were the dominant sources of case ascertainment. In addition, the decline in cumulative incidence observed from 2018 onwards is to a substantial degree explained by an end-of-window methodological artefact – under any case definition requiring confirmation within a fixed observation period, patients diagnosed late in the window have less opportunity to accrue a second diagnosis – rather than a true reduction in incidence. The steeper decline in 2020-2021 is also plausibly influenced by COVID-19-related reductions in primary-care consultation, elective OP activity and diagnostic coding for nonurgent conditions during the pandemic. Taken together, these considerations suggest that the loss of HES OP data after October 2020 is one contributor among several to the observed late-period decline, but is unlikely to have materially altered the principal findings.

Further, the absence of a matched comparator group limits contextual interpretation of comorbidities and treatment patterns and should be addressed in future research. The data capture prescriptions written by physicians, but do not include information on actual medication use or patient adherence. Moreover, CPRD Aurum does not systematically link individual prescriptions to the diagnosis for which they were issued; several of the most frequently observed agents in our cohort (gabapentin, pregabalin, and bisoprolol) are widely prescribed for comorbid indications such as neuropathic pain, anxiety and hypertension, all of which were prevalent in our cohort. The overall pharmacotherapy rate of 86.07% should therefore be regarded as an upper bound of ET-directed treatment; a more conservative lower bound restricted to guideline-endorsed ET agents (propranolol, primidone, topiramate) is more comparable with prior treatment-prevalence literature. This means treatment lines are not explicitly available, so we relied on a rule-based algorithm to identify them. Our algorithm for combination therapies included agents prescribed within 30 days of each other. However, this timeframe may have been too short, potentially misclassifying some combination therapies as monotherapies. Conversely, the 90-day discontinuation gap may overestimate discontinuation for agents used on an as-needed basis (most notably propranolol). Additionally, our algorithm may not have fully captured the nuances of real-world use, especially if patients used medications on an 'as-needed' basis rather than consistently. Sensitivity analyses using alternative thresholds were not feasible within the current data access protocol; future studies should vary these parameters to assess their impact on treatment-line classification. Finally, data on medications dispensed in secondary care and over-the-counter medications were not available. Future studies incorporating patient-level chart reviews or varying time windows for treatment classification could improve accuracy.

Conclusions

This study offers real-world insights into the epidemiology, patient characteristics, and treatment patterns of ET using data from routinely collected primary and secondary care data in the UK. Evidence-based treatments, such as propranolol, primidone, and topiramate, were frequently used as first-line options in our sample. However, a wide range of other, less evidence-based treatments were also observed, and treatments were frequently discontinued. Despite improvements in the understanding of ET, this study highlights the significant unmet need that remains in ET.
