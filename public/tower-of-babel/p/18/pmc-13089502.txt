Molecular mechanisms underlying enhanced ACE-2-RBD stability in SARS-CoV-2 variants: The impact of key SARS-CoV-2 RBD mutations
Authors: Kehinde A. Idowu, Scott Widmann, Esther A. Olaleye
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Viruses undergo mutations over time, most of which have little to no impact. However, certain changes can significantly influence viral traits such as transmissibility and disease severity. Newer SARS-CoV-2 variants, including XBB.1.5, XBB.1.16, EG.5, and BA.2.86, have emerged with key mutations—D405N, G446S, N460K, and R408S—altering their biological behavior. This study explores how mutations in the receptor-binding domain (RBD) of various SARS-CoV-2 variants affect their molecular interactions, structural stability, and binding affinity with the human ACE-2 receptor (hACE-2), offering insights valuable for drug discovery. Using computational methods, binding free energy calculations revealed that variants such as BQ.1.1, CH.1.1, EG.5, EG.5.1, XBB.1, XBB.1.5, XBB.1.9.1, XBB.2.3, and BA.2.75 demonstrate stronger binding to hACE-2 than the wild-type (WT), which exhibited a binding energy of −35.24 ± 8.97 kcal/mol. This enhanced binding is linked to increased hydrogen bonds (H-bonds) and electrostatic interactions, with correlation coefficients of 0.51 and 0.28, respectively. Structural stability analyses, based on average RMSD values, showed that most mutations did not compromise the RBD-hACE-2 complex’s integrity, except in variants XBB.1.5, XBB.1.9.1, and XBB.1.9.2. These findings suggest that specific mutations enhance interactions through H-bonds, hydrophobic effects, and electrostatic forces, contributing to overall stability. In summary, this study highlights that several SARS-CoV-2 variants bind more effectively to hACE-2 than the WT and earlier variants like BA.5 and BQ.1. These stronger interactions may underlie their higher transmission rates. The molecular insights provided here improve understanding of how specific mutations influence the virus’s behavior, aiding future therapeutic development.

Introduction

As of September 14, 2025, over 778 million cases of SARS-CoV-2 infections have been recorded globally (www.kff.org/coronavirus-covid-19/issue-brief/global-covid-19-tracker/). This extensive spread has been accompanied by continuous genetic changes in the virus due to mutations, leading to the emergence of numerous variants. Research indicates that the viral RNA of SARS-CoV-2 mutates rapidly within host cells, contributing to the diversity of viral variants [1,2].

The World Health Organization (WHO) classifies SARS-CoV-2 variants into several categories, with Variants of Concern (VOCs) being the most significant due to their enhanced transmissibility and virulence [3]. VOCs are defined by WHO based on several criteria such as 1) Genetic Modifications: Variants that exhibit changes in their genetic code, resulting in altered virus characteristics, 2) Increased Transmission and Severe Epidemiology: Variants that lead to higher rates of viral transmission and more severe forms of COVID-19, and 3) Reduced Efficacy of Therapeutics and Vaccines: Variants that show a reduced response to existing treatments and vaccines. These criteria help in identifying and monitoring variants that pose a greater threat to global public health, ensuring that appropriate measures can be taken to mitigate their impact.

Since its emergence in late 2019, SARS-CoV-2 has undergone numerous mutations, affecting the virus’s transmissibility, immune escape potential, and the severity of the disease it causes [5]. Variants of concern (VOCs) and variants of interest (VOIs) have been identified based on their impact on public health.

The S373P mutation introduces proline, which induces local structural rigidity and optimizes the RBD conformation for ACE-2 binding. The N501Y and Y505H mutations are bulky and might enhance pi-stacking and other stabilizing interactions, contributing to a more favorable binding conformation [6]. While XBB.1.16 includes mutations like G446S, R408S, F486S, N460K, K478R, S371F, and L368I. Variants EG.5 and EG.5.1 share mutations on the RBD, including G339H, G446S, S477N, Q498R, N501Y, and Y505H, which might enhance hydrogen bond formation potential, leading to more stable interactions with ACE-2. Variant BA.2.86 has mutations G339H, G446S, S477N, Q498R, N501Y, Y505H, S371F, S375F, V445H, L452W, S373P, and F486P. While BA.5 comprises mutations L452R, R493Q, and F486V. The L452R mutation was reported to introduce a positive charge, creating new electrostatic interactions with negatively charged residues on ACE-2 [7]. In variant CH.1.1, Li et. al. reported the introduction of positively charged (L452R, N460K, Q493R) and polar (S371F, D405N, R408S, G446S, F486S) residues enhances electrostatic interactions and hydrogen bonding with ACE-2 [8].

The mutations in SARS-CoV-2 variants have profound implications for the virus’s behavior and its interaction with the human host. By altering hydrogen bonding, electrostatic interactions, and hydrophobic contacts, these mutations can enhance the virus’s binding affinity to ACE-2, stability, and overall infectivity. Understanding these mutations and their structural impacts is essential for developing effective therapeutic and preventive strategies against COVID-19, as they can significantly influence the virus’s behavior and response to treatments [9,10].

Methods

Proteins acquisition, preparation and molecular docking

The X-ray crystal structures of the SARS-CoV-2 spike glycoprotein complexed with hACE-2 (PDB ID: 7WSE) were retrieved from the RSCB Protein Data Bank [8]. The structures of the SARS-CoV-2 spike glycoprotein and hACE-2 were separated, and mutations for each variant were introduced into the spike protein using the rotamer library plugin [11] in the UCSF Chimera software package [12]. The protein structures were prepared by removing water molecules, correcting nonstandard naming, and ensuring proper protein residue connectivity. Molecular docking of the Spike RBD structures for each variant with hACE-2 was performed using the Haddock molecular docking server, version 2.4 [13] with default parameters. The docked complexes were then subjected to molecular dynamics simulations.

Molecular Dynamic (MD) simulations

The MD simulation was done on the Biomolecular Research and Advanced Computing Center (BioRACC)’s cluster of Texas Southern University (TSU). The MD simulation followed the protocol described by Idowu et al. with minor modifications [14], using the GPU version of AMBER 18 [15] and the FF18SB force field [16]. The Leap module of AMBER 18 was used to add hydrogen atoms and counter ions to neutralize the systems, which were then placed in an orthorhombic box of TIP3P water molecules. Initial minimization involved 2000 steps with a restraint potential of 500 kcal/mol, using 1000 steps of steepest descent followed by 1000 steps of conjugate gradients, and a further 1000 steps without restraints. The systems were gradually heated from 0 K to 300 K over 50 ps, followed by a 500 ps equilibration at 300 K, both with a harmonic restraint of 10 kcal/mol and a collision frequency of 1.0 ps.

The total simulation time was 50 ns per run, using the SHAKE algorithm to constrain hydrogen bonds, a step size of 2 fs, and an SPFP precision model. The simulations were conducted under an isobaric-isothermal ensemble (NPT) with randomized seeding, maintaining 1 bar pressure with a Berendsen barostat, a pressure-coupling constant of 2 ps, a temperature of 300 K, and a Langevin thermostat with a collision frequency of 1.0 ps.

Post-dynamic analysis

Analysis of Root Mean Square Deviation (RMSD), Radius of Gyration (RoG), and Solvent Accessible Surface Area (SASA) was conducted using the CPPTRAJ module in the AMBER 18 suite. All raw data plots were generated with Python 3.9 using the Anaconda3 and Orange3 3.36.2 software.

Binding free energy calculations

To estimate and compare the systems’ binding affinity, the free binding energy was calculated using the Molecular Mechanics/GP Surface Area (MM/PBSA) method as described by Kehinde et al. [17] and [18]. The total binding free energy (ΔG_bind) is decomposed into van der Waals, electrostatic, polar solvation (Poisson–Boltzmann), and nonpolar solvation terms, averaged across representative MD snapshots. MM/PBSA is widely applied because it provides a balance between accuracy and computational efficiency, making it suitable for comparing relative binding affinities of protein–ligand and protein–protein complexes [19,20]. The binding free energy was averaged over 50,000 snapshots extracted from the 50 ns trajectory. The free binding energy (ΔG) computed for each molecular species (complex, ligand, and receptor) is represented as: (1) ΔGbind=Gcomplex-Greceptor-Gligand (2) ΔGbind=Egas+Gsol–TS (3) Egas=Eint+Evdw+Eele (4) Gsol=GGB+GSA (5) GSA=γSASA

Egas denotes the gas-phase energy, which consists of the internal energy Eint, Coulomb energy Eele and the van der Waals energies Evdw. The Egas was directly estimated from the FF14SB force field terms. Solvation free energy, Gsol, was estimated from the energy contribution from the polar states, GGB, and non-polar states, G. The non-polar solvation energy, SA. GSA was determined from the solvent-accessible surface area (SASA), using a water probe radius of 1.4 Å. In contrast, the polar solvation, GGB, the contribution was estimated by solving the GB equation. S and T denote the total entropy of the solute and temperature, respectively.

Results and discussion

Prediction of the binding energy between variant spike RBD and hACE-2

In this study, we employed the MM/PBSA method to estimate the binding free energies (ΔG_bind) between various SARS-CoV-2 spike protein variants and the wild-type (WT) spike protein with hACE-2. This method integrates molecular mechanics energies with solvation effects to provide a comprehensive picture of binding interactions. The MD simulations of the receptor-binding domain (RBD) of the variants’ spike proteins in complex with hACE-2 were conducted to predict binding affinities. Specifically, we analyzed the MD trajectories to calculate ΔG_bind using 50,000 snapshots from the 50 ns simulation trajectories, ensuring robust statistical sampling. Importantly, while it is recognized that entropy contributes to the absolute values of binding free energy, multiple studies have demonstrated that comparative binding trends between closely related variants are predominantly governed by enthalpic differences [18,19,21]. Therefore, our enthalpy-based MM/PBSA results are sufficient to reliably capture the relative differences in binding affinities among the variants examined.

Fig. 2., presents the thermodynamic binding energy profiles for the variants in complex with hACE-2. The estimated ΔG_bind values indicate that the variants BQ.1.1 (−48.45 ± 8.46 kcal/mol), CH.1.1 (−39.54 ± 6.78 kcal/mol), EG.5 (−42.90 ± 7.78 kcal/mol), EG.5.1 (−43.75 ± 6.97 kcal/mol), XBB.1 (−44.41 ± 8.73 kcal/mol), XBB.1.5 (−41.01 ± 7.32 kcal/mol), XBB.1.9.1 (−42.48 ± 8.38 kcal/mol), XBB.2.3 (−46.92 ± 7.64 kcal/mol), and BA.2.75 (−48.37 ± 6.94 kcal/mol) exhibit significantly higher binding affinities for hACE-2 compared to the WT, which has a binding energy of −35.24 ± 8.87 kcal/mol. Given that most of these variants are subtypes of the Omicron variant, our findings are consistent with previous studies [22,23]. For XBB.1.5, positively charged residues introduced by mutations like K417N, N440K, Q493R, and Q498R could have strengthened its electrostatic interactions with ACE-2’s negatively charged residues, boosting its binding affinity. For EG.5 and EG.5.1, the presence of positive charges mutations, such as N440K, N460K, T478K, and Q498R, might enhance electrostatic interactions with ACE-2, thereby increasing binding affinity. CH.1.1 variants exhibit mutations introducing positively charged and polar residues, enhancing electrostatic interactions and hydrogen bonding with ACE-2, significantly increasing binding affinity. Moreover, mutations T376A and F486S might reduce steric hindrance and increase flexibility, thereby optimizing the spike protein’s conformation for better fitting into the ACE-2 binding pocket. Finally, S371F and F486S might alter hydrophobic interactions within the binding interface as well, further stabilizing the spike-ACE-2 complex and increasing binding affinity as seen in this study.

Studies have reported that the Omicron variant exhibits enhanced binding affinity to hACE-2, attributed to multiple mutations in the spike protein that increase electrostatic and van der Waals interactions with the receptor [14,22,23]. This substantial increase in binding strength underscores the impact of these mutations on the virus’s interaction with the human receptor.

These results suggest that nine of the examined variants possess a higher binding affinity for hACE-2 compared to the WT. This increased binding affinity likely contributes to the higher transmissibility and rapid spread of these variants, particularly the Omicron subtypes, compared to earlier variants like the Delta variant and the original WT strain. Comprehending these molecular interactions is essential for elucidating the mechanisms of viral infectivity and can aid in the development of therapeutic interventions.

Protein (Spike protein) -protein (ACE-2) molecular interactions

This study investigated the protein-protein molecular interactions between the amino acid residues of the SARS-CoV-2 spike RBD and human ACE-2. Table 1., provides a summary of the number and types of interactions within each complex. Structural analyses have shown that specific mutations in the RBD enhance its interaction network with hACE-2, including the formation of new hydrogen bonds (H-bonds) and salt bridges/electrostatic interactions [24]. These changes contribute to a higher binding affinity, which can potentially lead to increased viral load and higher transmission rates, as observed in epidemiological studies of the Omicron variant [25].

As indicated in Table 1, all variants except XBB.2.3 and BA.2.86 showed an increase in the total number of H-bonds formed. The binding energy results indicate that all variants with higher binding energy than WT formed new H-bonds. Specifically, the BQ.1.1 variant exhibited the highest number of H-bonds (24), followed by EG.5, EG.5.1, and XBB.1, each with 20H-bonds. Variants XBB.1.5, XBB.1.16, XBB.1.9.1, and XBB.1.9.2 each formed 18H-bonds. Additionally, mutations in the RBD of variants BA.5, CH.1.1, EG.5, EG.5.1, XBB.1, XBB.1.5, XBB.1.16, XBB.1.9.1, XBB.1.9.2, XBB.2.3, BA.2.75, and BA.2.86 resulted in the formation of additional salt bridges or electrostatic interactions, ranging from 4 to 5, compared to the WT, which has 3 such interactions (Table 1). This study suggests that mutations observed in XBB.1.5, such as G446S, S477N, G496S, and Q498R, might enhance hydrogen bonding potential within the spike RBD, potentially strengthening its interaction with ACE-2. Additionally, substitutions like S371L, S375F, and N501Y in XBB.1.5 variants could replace polar residues with hydrophobic or aromatic ones, thereby enhancing hydrophobic interactions at the spike-ACE-2 interface. In XBB.1.16 variants, mutations like G446S, R408S, and F486S might introduce or reinforce hydrogen bonding potential, contributing to the stabilization of the RBD-ACE-2 complex. Moreover, mutations such as N460K and K478R in XBB.1.16 could increase the positive charge density of the spike protein, potentially strengthening its electrostatic interactions with negatively charged residues on ACE-2. Furthermore, substitutions like S371F and L368I in XBB.1.16 might replace hydrophobic residues with bulkier aromatic ones, thereby enhancing hydrophobic interactions and stabilizing the interface.

For EG.5 and EG.5.1 variants, mutations such as G339H, G446S, S477N, Q498R, N501Y, and Y505H could enhance the potential for hydrogen bond formation, further stabilizing the spike-ACE-2 interaction [26]. Similarly, in BA.2.86 variants, mutations such as G339H, G446S, S477N, Q498R, N501Y, and Y505H might have contributed to increased hydrogen bond formation, as evidenced by the higher number of hydrogen bonds (20) compared to the wild type (13). BA.5 variant mutations (L452R and R493Q) might introduce positive and polar residues, respectively, potentially enhancing electrostatic interactions and hydrogen bonding within the spike-ACE-2 complex. For XBB.1.5 variant, mutations such as N501Y and Y505H, known as bulky mutations, may enhance pi-stacking interactions and other stabilizing forces within the RBD-ACE-2 interface, thereby favoring a more stable binding conformation.

These findings suggest that the enhanced interaction networks, particularly the increased number of H-bonds and salt bridges/electrostatic, contributed to the higher binding affinities observed for these variants. This, in turn, might explain the increased transmissibility and rapid spread of the Omicron subvariants compared to the WT and earlier variants. Understanding these molecular interactions provides crucial insights into the mechanisms of viral infectivity and can inform the development of therapeutic interventions and vaccines.

An examination of the correlation matrix of bond types against the square of binding energy (Square_Gbind) revealed that H-bonds and salt bridges/electrostatic interactions exhibit significant positive correlations with binding energy, with correlation coefficients of 0.51 and 0.28, respectively (Fig. 3). This suggests that an increase in the number of H-bonds and salt bridges/electrostatic interactions between the spike RBD and ACE-2 enhances binding affinity. Moreover, a strong positive correlation was observed between the total number of bonds formed and the binding energy, indicating that more interactions lead to stronger binding. These findings imply that targeting the formation of H-bonds and salt bridges/electrostatic interactions with specific drugs or small molecules could disrupt the molecular interactions between the spike RBD and ACE-2. By inhibiting these interactions, it may be possible to reduce the binding affinity and consequently lower viral transmissibility. This approach is supported by studies demonstrating that certain antiviral compounds effectively hinder critical protein-protein interactions [27,28], thereby impeding viral entry and replication. For example, small molecule inhibitors and monoclonal antibodies have been designed to block key binding sites on the spike protein, preventing its attachment to ACE-2 and reducing infection rates [28,29]. Thus, identifying and developing compounds that can interfere with these bond formations could be a promising strategy in antiviral therapy and vaccine design.

Structural stability and flexibility evaluation of variants’ systems

Binding of a molecule (either drugs or protein) to a specific biological target is usually associated with structural and conformational changes, which in most cases influence the biological activity of that target [30]. Protein structural stability and flexibility can be evaluated using several computational metrics, such as Root Mean Square Deviation (RMSD), Radius of Gyration (RoG), and Root Mean Square Fluctuation (RMSF). In this study, the various variants’ RBD-hACE-2 complexes’ structural stability were measured following experimental simulation.

RMSD is a measure of the average deviation between the positions of atoms in a protein structure over time during a MD simulation [31–33]. It provides insights into the stability of the protein structure by comparing the conformation at each time step to a reference conformation, usually the initial structure. A lower RMSD value indicates that the protein structure is stable and does not deviate significantly from the reference structure [34,35]. Conversely, higher RMSD values suggest greater structural changes and potential instability. Figs. 4A and 4B shows the structural stability and flexibility plots for the variants. While Fig. 4., provides average values of the structural stability and flexibility each complex. For the RMSD plot, after maintaining convergence at approximately 5 ns, all the complexes exhibited favorable stability throughout the MD simulations (Figs. 4A and 4B). Their average RMSD values fall under the acceptable RMSD values (≤ 3 Å) for protein-protein simulation (14).

To understand the compactness of the alpha carbon backbones of the protein complexes, the RoG values for each complex were examined. RoG measures the compactness of a protein structure by calculating the root mean square distance of the atoms from the center of mass of the molecule [36,37]. It reflects the overall shape and folding state of the protein. A stable protein typically has a relatively constant RoG value, indicating that the protein maintains its compact structure while, fluctuations in RoG can signal unfolding or structural changes. As shown in Figs. 4C and 4D, fluctuation in RoG plots was observed for the variants and WT complexes suggesting possible structural changes. However, Fig. 5. showed no significant difference in the average RoG values was observed between the variants and WT. These results (RMSD and RoG) suggest the complexes exhibited stable structural compactness.

Figs. 6A and 6B showed the RMSF plots for all the RBD-ACE-2 complexes and Fig. 5., showed the average RMSF values for all the systems. RMSF quantifies the flexibility of specific residues or regions within the protein over the course of a simulation. It is calculated as the average deviation of each atom or residue from its average position. High RMSF values indicate regions of the protein that are more flexible or dynamic, while low RMSF values denote more rigid, stable regions [31]. This metric helps identify flexible loops, binding sites, or regions prone to conformational changes.

BQ.1 showed highest flexible movements as evidenced by higher overall average values of 2.14 Å compared to other variants and WT, with average RMSF value less than 1.78 Å (Fig. 6A and Fig. 5). Nevertheless, at amino acid residues 110 and 600, all the systems showed a noticeable increase in fluctuation, with the BQ.1 exhibiting more flexibility than others. This observation in BQ.1 variant might be attributed to the presence of K444T and S371F on its Spike RBD. A change from lysine (K, positively charged) to threonine (T, Neutral, polar) at position 444 of the spike protein might reduce the electrostatic attraction between the spike protein and negatively charged residues on ACE-2. Also, Threonine might still form hydrogen bonds, potentially maintaining some degree of interaction with ACE-2, but the nature and strength of these bonds will differ from those involving lysine. Structurally, this mutation might cause local structural changes in the spike protein’s RBD, affecting how it aligns with ACE-2. For the S371F, the bulkier phenylalanine (F) residue might cause steric hindrance or induce conformational changes in the spike protein’s RBD, potentially altering its binding affinity (this might explain the low binding affinity value of −30.11 ± 5.82 Kcal/mol) and specificity for ACE-2.

Solvent Accessible Surface Area (SASA) measures the surface area of a protein that is accessible to a solvent, providing insights into the protein’s exposure to the solvent and potential interactions with other molecules [31,38]. Changes in SASA can indicate conformational changes or the stability of the protein. A high SASA value suggests increased exposure of buried hydrophobic residues, which can disrupt the hydrophobic core essential for protein structural integrity, leading to decreased stability. In other words, increased value of protein SASA value during simulation indicates structural relaxation and hence reduced protein stability. This destabilization may cause partial unfolding or increased conformational flexibility, compromising the protein’s functional stability. Fig. 6C. and 6D., along with Fig. 5., show no significant differences in the plots and average SASA values for the variant complexes compared to the WT. This result supports the findings from RMSD, RoG, and RMSF analyses, indicating that the complexes maintain stable structural compactness despite the mutations.

This stability might be attributed to presence of certain mutations in the RBD of the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein that enhance the formation of hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic, and electrostatic interactions with the ACE-2 receptor. These interactions are crucial for the structural stability of the RBD-ACE-2 complex. Studies have demonstrated that these enhanced interactions contribute to the overall stability and binding affinity of a proteins [39–43]. For instance, mutations such as G446S, S477N, G496S, and Q498R in XBB.1.5, G446S, R408S, and F486S in XBB.1.16, L452R and R493Q in BA.5 increase hydrogen bonding potential, stabilizing the RBD-ACE-2 complex. Increase in H-bond was observed in all the variants complexes (Table 1). While mutations such as S371L, S375F, and N501Y in XBB.1.5, S371F and L368I in XBB.1.16, S371F, S375F, F456L, and N501Y in EG.5 and EG.5.1, S371F, S375F, and V445H, L452W, and N501Y in BA.2.86 increase hydrophobic interactions. N460K and K478R in XBB.1.16, L452R and R493Q in BA.5 enhance stability of the RBD-ACE-2 complex through electrostatic interactions.

An analysis of the correlation matrix between structural stability parameters (RMSF, RoG, RMSD, and SASA) and the square of binding energy (Square_bind) revealed a significant negative correlation between RMSF and binding energy, with a correlation coefficient of −0.50 (Fig. 7.). This indicates that an increase in RMSF values, which reflects increased flexibility and decreased stability of the RBD-ACE-2 complexes, is associated with a decrease in binding energy. This negative correlation suggests that greater structural flexibility of the protein complex leads to weaker binding interactions with ACE-2, thereby reducing the overall binding affinity.

Conclusion

This study has demonstrated that mutations in SARS-CoV-2 variants significantly impact the virus’ molecular interaction with the human host. Specifically, these mutations alter hydrogen bonding, electrostatic interactions, and hydrophobic contacts, thereby enhancing the virus’s binding affinity to host ACE-2. This is evidenced by increased binding energy and maintained structural stability and flexibility of the RBD-ACE-2 complex. These findings underscore the importance of understanding and targeting molecular interactions such as H-bonds and electrostatic interactions. Inhibiting these interactions through small molecules or vaccines could be crucial for developing effective therapeutic and preventive strategies against COVID-19 and spread of the virus.
