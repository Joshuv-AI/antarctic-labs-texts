Maternal and child mental health and incident migraine in adolescence: An “All Our Families” prospective cohort study
Authors: Kirsten Sjonnesen, Melanie Noel, Tamara Pringsheim, Scott B. Patten, Sheri Madigan, Prachi Khanna, Sheila McDonald, Paul E. Ronksley, Suzanne Tough, Serena L. Orr
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo examine the association between mental health symptoms in youth and their mothers, and youth incident migraine, using prospectively collected, longitudinal data from the “All Our Families” (AOF) cohort study.BackgroundInternalizing mental health symptoms, such as anxiety and depression, whether experienced by a child or his/her parent(s), have been linked to migraine and chronic pain conditions, but causal inferences have been limited by gaps in the current literature. We hypothesized a priori that elevated youth and/or maternal internalizing symptoms across childhood would be associated with elevated odds of incident migraine in early adolescence, providing insights into the modifiable mental health contributors to incident migraine.MethodsA community‐recruited sample of urban youth (N = 1062, 48% female) living in Alberta, Canada were followed through the AOF prospective cohort study from 2008 to 2023. The study outcome, youth migraine, was ascertained through a validated migraine diagnostic questionnaire, administered to 12‐year‐old youth. The exposures of interest, anxiety and depressive symptoms in youth and mothers, were derived from multiple timepoints when these symptoms were measured, across youth ages 4–12 years. The analysis compared odds of incident migraine in youth with a history of elevated internalizing symptoms, and/or exposure to elevated maternal symptoms, versus unexposed youth. Covariates accounted for included age, sex, gender and racial identity, household income, and parental migraine status.ResultsThe odds of incident migraine, diagnosed at a mean age of 12.9 years (standard deviation [SD] 0.8 years) were increased by ~29% with each additional timepoint when maternal anxiety symptoms were elevated across childhood (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] 1.29 per exposure, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.02–1.64, p = 0.036). Similarly, the odds of incident migraine increased by ~29% with each additional exposure to significant youth depressive symptoms across childhood (aOR 1.29 per exposure, 95% CI 1.03–1.61, p = 0.026). Sex did not modify this association.ConclusionFindings suggest that maternal anxiety and youth depressive symptoms have an antecedent role in youth migraine risk. Although certain migraine risk factors remain non‐modifiable (e.g., genetic inheritance), child and parent mental health symptoms may be alleviated through evidence‐based mental health treatments. Future clinical research should explore whether reducing exposure to child and parent mental health symptoms could prevent or delay the development of new migraine cases (“incident migraine”).

BACKGROUND

Migraine is a highly prevalent primary headache disorder, affecting 8–10% of children and adolescents, 1 and has been identified as the most disabling neurological condition among youth (persons <18 years old). 2 Early adolescence represents the initiation of a developmental period with elevated risk for newly developing migraine, as migraine incidence rates increase sharply. 3 , 4 The development of migraine, occurring in someone not previously affected by migraine, is termed “incident migraine.” Incident migraine results from complex interactions between genetic, psychological, and environmental factors. 3 , 4 , 5 The heritability of migraine is approximately 50%, and with rare exceptions, the >120 migraine susceptibility genes identified to date each contribute to a marginally increased risk of disease expression. 6 , 7 Being the offspring of a parent with migraine increases the odds of developing migraine approximately two to three‐fold. 8 , 9 Individual psychological tendencies, such as attentional and negativity biases, avoidance, and behavioral inactivation, contribute to the development of pediatric chronic pain disorders, and these associations may extend to pediatric migraine. 10 , 11 Among psychological disorders, anxiety and depression (“internalizing disorders”) have a well‐recognized association with migraine in children and adolescents, and the nature of this comorbidity continues to garner interest. 5 , 10

Internalizing symptoms may reflect a maladaptive, internal response to stress. 10 The two major internalizing symptom domains are anxiety and depressive symptoms, and clinically elevated symptoms (i.e., moderate to severe) are associated with the development of anxiety and depressive disorders, respectively. Importantly, functional impairment, and the possibility of benefiting from treatment, is observed not only in clinical depression or anxiety disorders, but also in those experiencing subthreshold internalizing symptoms. 12

Relative to the population at large, adults with migraine experience an estimated two‐ to five‐fold burden of anxiety disorders and two‐ to 2.5‐fold greater burden of depression. 13 Anxiety and depressive symptoms in both children and their mothers have been associated with migraine in youth. 5 , 10 A significant genetic correlation exists between migraine and depression, and co‐inheritance of these disorders is supported. 7 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 In adults with migraine, it is recognized that associated internalizing symptoms may have onset antecedent to, or subsequent to, the onset of migraine. 3 , 5 , 18 , 19 , 20

However, during earlier developmental periods, the degree to which the strong comorbidity between migraine and internalizing disorders can be explained by unidirectional causation, bidirectional causation, or a common, shared upstream etiology merits further elucidation. 10 , 21 , 22 , 23 Existing literature examining youth and/or maternal internalizing symptoms, and incident migraine, is limited. 5 , 10 , 24 , 25 To our knowledge, no existing investigations have reported on youth internalizing symptoms exposure: (a) below age 8 years; (b) at multiple timepoints; and (c) simultaneously with parent internalizing symptom exposures. An improved characterization of the association between youth and maternal internalizing symptoms, and incident migraine, may contribute new insights into youth migraine risk reduction.

Data from the All Our Families (AOF) longitudinal health study were leveraged to address this gap. The AOF is an ongoing multipurpose prospective birth cohort study of health and wellbeing determinants in children and mothers, conducted since 2008 in Calgary, Alberta, Canada. 26 , 27 , 28 This study represents the first analysis of migraine outcome data, collected in 2023, and aligning with early adolescence, when a steep increase in incident migraine occurs. A priori, it was hypothesized that exposure to elevated youth and/or maternal depression and anxiety symptoms across childhood (4–12 years of age) would have a statistically significant positive association with incident migraine at age 12 years after controlling for key covariates (age, sex, gender and racial identity, household income, and parental migraine status). Sex was predicted to modify the association between youth internalizing symptoms and incident migraine, with females predicted to have a stronger association than males.

METHODS

Data source

Study data collection methodology has been fulsomely characterized by study authors elsewhere. 26 , 27 , 28 Study exposure data were reported by mothers and youth (once ≥9 years old) at multiple timepoints between 2014 and 2021, 27 , 29 and outcome data were reported at a single timepoint by youth in 2023 when youth were expected to be ~12 years old. Among outcome survey respondents (n = 1317), study exclusion criteria were: invalid youth outcome survey responses, missing data considered ineligible for imputation (self‐reported gender identity and parental migraine status), indeterminate headache status, and prevalent headache attacks affecting youth “often” or “almost always,” as per the maternally reported exposure surveys.

The AOF longitudinal cohort study and this study (REB21‐1766) each received local research ethics board approval. All maternal participants provided informed consent, and youth participants provided assent, for research participation, and all questionnaires included mental health resources for self‐referral, as needed. 30 The study was completed in collaboration with author P.K., a youth living with migraine.

Measures

Youth incident migraine was ascertained through a seven‐item scale based on the validated pediatric migraine diagnostic criteria contained in the International Classification of Headache Disorders 3rd edition (ICHD‐3) (Figure S1). 31 In youth with migraine, its sensitivity is at least 84.4% when compared to the gold‐standard of clinical diagnosis. 32 Participants were presented with the migraine questions if they endorsed having headache attacks at least once per month. A definite migraine diagnosis was made if three of three study criteria were met, and a diagnosis of probable migraine was made if two of three study criteria were met (Table S1). 31 , 32 Though only participants endorsing headache attacks at least once per month were included, there was technically no participant‐reported data available on the specific number of migraine attacks experienced. Thus, the ICHD‐3 criterion of greater than or equal to five lifetime migraine attacks was not required to meet the study outcome, and distinction between participants with episodic and chronic migraine could not be made.

Exposure to youth internalizing symptoms was assessed using the Behavior Assessment System for Children, 2nd (BASC‐2) and 3rd (BASC‐3) editions. 33 , 34 , 35 , 36 The BASC‐2 Parent Rating Scale (PRS) was administered to mothers on earlier surveys, while the BASC‐3 was administered to youth on later surveys. Significantly elevated depression or anxiety symptoms were defined as a sex‐specific T‐score ≥60 (≥1 standard deviation [SD] above the normative mean) on a given depression or anxiety sub‐scale, respectively. 34 , 37

Exposure to maternal anxiety symptoms was assessed with the Spielberger State Anxiety Inventory 38 short‐form, 6‐item instrument (SSAI‐SF‐6), 39 validated for the AOF cohort by Bayrampour et al. 40 SSAI‐SF‐6 scores ≥1 SD above the sample mean (≥84th percentile) correspond to clinically significant anxiety. 26 Exposure to maternal depressive symptoms was assessed with the Center for Epidemiologic Studies ‐ Depression scale (CES‐D), via the 20‐item scale on earlier surveys and 10‐item on later surveys, and applying established clinical cutoffs for significantly elevated scores. 26 , 30 , 41 , 42

Exposure variables ranged in value from 0 to 4 and were derived by counting surveys when scale scores exceeded cut‐points for significantly elevated symptoms, a quantification method applied to reflect the cumulative burden of elevated symptoms. 5 , 30 , 43 Youth internalizing symptom exposure variables were termed “Youth Anxiety Exposure” (YAE) and “Youth Depression Exposure” (YDE), and maternal internalizing symptom exposure variables were termed “Maternal Anxiety Exposure” (MAE) and “Maternal Depression Exposure” (MDE).

Covariates in this study were age, sex, gender, racial identity, household income, and parental migraine status. To maintain analytical power and avoid potential collinearity, maternal education was not included due to its positive association with household income in this cohort. Sex assigned at birth was recorded by AOF investigators. On the outcome survey, youth provided self‐reported gender identity and racial identity. Mothers reported on the presence of health‐professional–diagnosed migraine or suspected migraine for each biological parent, and annual pre‐taxation household income.

Statistical analyses

Analyses were completed using R statistical software, version 4.4.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A two‐sided alpha of 0.05 was selected a priori as the threshold for statistical significance. The study sample size was determined by the number of participants available from the ongoing AOF cohort. As such, an a priori power calculation was not conducted for the primary analysis. Attrition bias was mitigated by applying inverse probability weights developed by AOF investigators using the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) method. 29

Initial descriptive analyses included measures of central tendency and with and without sample weights. Participant ages at outcome were visualized via histogram. Normally distributed continuous data were described using means with SD, and categorical/count data using percentages with respective 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). Exposure variables and covariates with missing data underwent multiple imputation using chained equations (MICE) using the R package “mice” (version 3.17.0). 44 Predictive mean matching imputed values for the exposures and income, whereas passive imputation produced the interaction terms. All terms included in the outcome model were used as imputation predictors in addition to additional predictors (Table S2). 45 , 46 , 47 The number of predictors per equation was limited to 25 variables by convention, 45 and the MICE procedure completed 20 iterations and 20 imputations, which were examined post hoc through R packages “ggmice” (version 0.1.0), and “howManyImputations” (version 0.2.5). 48 , 49

Univariable logistic regression analyses assessed for associations between incident migraine and MAE, MDE, YAE, and YDE as individual predictors. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was subsequently completed on each of the MICE datasets, combining the four exposure variables with all covariates: age, sex, gender and racial identity, household income, and parental migraine status. Two interaction terms, sex*YAE and sex*YDE, were included to examine for effect modification. The imputed models using MICE were pooled together per Rubin's rules 47 and attrition weights were applied, 29 producing the primary model estimates. The significance of interaction terms was explored using the Wald test p‐value and the likelihood ratio test of nested models. To characterize the impact of attrition bias, and the impact of data missingness, the primary model estimates were compared to those without attrition weights and complete‐case analysis models.

Logistic regression model diagnostics and MICE procedure diagnostics were each examined. The mechanism of missingness in the AOF was evaluated post hoc. A description of the sensitivity analyses can be found in the Supplement.

RESULTS

Sample characterization and descriptive analysis

Of the 3387 mother–youth participant dyads initially enrolled in AOF, 1317 youth completed the outcome survey, administered between January 16 to July 7, 2023. After applying exclusion criteria, and removing 33 and 43 participants from the analysis due to missing gender identity and parental migraine data, respectively, 1062 youth participants were included in primary analyses.

Study migraine diagnostic criteria were met in 206/1062 (19.4%) participants, as 83/1062 met definite migraine criteria (7.8%), and 123/1062 met probable migraine criteria (11.6%) (Figure 1). Headache attacks that did not meet migraine outcome criteria were present in 122/1062 (11.5%) youth participants.

Using attrition weights, there were notable trends in the characteristics of youth who developed incident migraine compared to those who did not. The youth with migraine were 64.3% female and 35.7% male, while youth without migraine were 42.8% female and 57.2% male. Gender‐diverse identity was reported in 7.6% of youth with migraine, and in 3.4% of those without migraine. Parental migraine diagnosis was found in 54.4% of youth with migraine, compared to 44.0% of youth without migraine. Within the 6 months prior to outcome ascertainment, 36.1% of youth with migraine had sought any mental health support, compared to 19.1% of youth without migraine, and 30.7% of youth with migraine had received professional support, compared to 11.8% of youth without migraine.

With attrition weighting, at least one YAE or YDE occurred in 35.1% (95% CI 32.0–38.2%) and 30.6% (95% CI 27.7–33.6%) of youth, and exposure to at least one MAE or MDE affected 39.1% (95% CI 36.0–42.4%) and 43.6% (95% CI 40.4–46.8%) of youth, respectively. Youth with migraine experienced significantly greater youth anxiety and depression exposure as well as maternal anxiety exposure compared to youth without migraine (Wilcoxon rank‐sum p < 0.001 for each) (Figure 3). At a single timepoint, between ages of 9 and 12 years, maternal‐ and youth‐reported symptoms were collected simultaneously. At this time point, maternal proxy reports for youth‐rated significant anxiety and depression symptoms were found to be 41% and 39% sensitive, and 93% and 87% specific.

MICE

Missingness affected 625/1062 participants, who were missing values for one or more of five analytical variables. Internalizing symptom data from four of four exposure surveys were required to derive each exposure count variable, and data missingness affected 35.5% (n = 378/1062) of MAE values, 36.3% (n = 386/1062) of MDE values, 53.5% (n = 568/1062) of YAE values, and 53.0% (n = 563/1062) of YDE values. Household income values were missing in 11.2% (119/1062). The R “mice” package created 20 imputed datasets, and post hoc, “howManyImputations,” verified this was appropriate. 48 Data visualization confirmed that imputed data values were plausible when compared to the observed data (Figures S2 and S3).

Primary analysis

In the primary analysis that applied MICE and attrition weights, MAE and YDE were found to significantly increase the adjusted odds of youth migraine diagnosis, by 1.29‐fold (95% CI 1.02–1.64, p = 0.036) and 1.29‐fold (95% CI 1.03–1.61, p = 0.026), respectively, for each exposure to significantly elevated symptoms from ages 4 to 12 years (Table 3). Covariates found to increase the adjusted odds of youth migraine diagnosis were age in years (adjusted odds‐ratio (aOR) 1.27 95% CI 1.01–1.59, p = 0.043), female sex (aOR 2.41, 95% CI 1.71–3.41, p < 0.001), and having at least one parent with diagnosed or suspected migraine (aOR 1.43, 95% CI 1.02–2.02, p = 0.040). Effect modification of youth internalizing symptoms by sex was not supported and interaction terms were subsequently removed, as the sex‐youth anxiety and sex‐youth depression interaction terms were non‐significant (p‐values of 0.565 and 0.523), and the likelihood ratio test of nested models was non‐significant (p = 0.756).

When results of imputed models were pooled without applying attrition weights (n = 1062), estimates for MAE (aOR 1.30, 95% 1.04–1.62, p = 0.021) and YDE (aOR 1.30, 95% CI 1.05–1.62, p = 0.017) were again found to significantly increase the adjusted odds of youth incident migraine, in addition to female sex (aOR 2.41, 95% CI 1.73–3.34. p < 0.001; Table S3). Regression modeling of complete cases (n = 437), with and without attrition weights, similarly estimated that MAE and YDE, as well as female sex, significantly increased the adjusted odds of youth incident migraine (Tables S5 and S6). When analyzed using R package “psfmi,” (version 1.4.0) models were not affected by multicollinearity or overdispersion. 50 The pooled area under the curve (AUC) of the MICE models without attrition weights was 0.68 (95% CI 0.63–0.72), the calibration plot was acceptable, and the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness‐of‐fit test did not suggest poor model fit (p = 0.941) (Figure S4). 50

Sensitivity analyses

Multiple sensitivity analyses were performed to compare obtained exposure coefficient estimates under specific conditions. When coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID‐19) pandemic exposure data (2020–2021) were removed (n = 1062), MAE (aOR 1.60, 95% 1.16–2.21, p = 0.004) and YDE (aOR 1.47, 95% CI 1.08–2.00, p = 0.014) remained significantly associated with increased adjusted odds of youth incident migraine. In analyses wherein: (1) the outcome was defined as definite migraine and youth with probable migraine were removed (n = 941), (2) youth participants with any reported headache attacks during earlier surveys were excluded (n = 568), and (3) COVID‐19 pandemic exposure data were removed simultaneously with exclusion of youth participants with any reported headache attacks during earlier surveys (n = 568), coefficient estimates were similar in direction and magnitude, though reduced in precision (Tables S6‐10). A final analysis, restricting youth exposure data to youth‐reported symptoms measured in 2020–2021, at ages 9–12 years, demonstrated a significant association between YAE (aOR 1.56 ‐fold, 95% CI 1.07–2.25, p = 0.020), MAE (aOR 1.29, 95% CI 1.02–1.65, p = 0.037), and increased adjusted odds of youth incident migraine.

DISCUSSION

Findings of the current study revealed that exposure to significant maternal anxiety and youth depressive symptoms across childhood (ages 4–12) were consistently associated with elevated adjusted odds of incident migraine at age 12 years, in a dose‐dependent manner. Whereas a single exposure to elevated maternal anxiety symptoms, adjusted for covariates, conferred 1.29‐fold greater odds of youth migraine, exposure to elevated symptoms at four timepoints increased odds by 2.77‐fold. A similar effect size was found for exposure to elevated youth depression symptoms. No effect modification by sex of the odds ratios for youth internalizing symptom exposure was found. The exposure–outcome associations were robust when analyzed non‐parametrically, in univariate regression, and with adjustment for covariates in the primary inferential analyses and further supported in most sensitivity analyses. Interestingly, maternal depressive symptoms did not significantly impact the odds of youth incident migraine, at any stage of analysis. A significant association between YAE and youth incident migraine was found uniquely when examining youth‐reported anxiety symptoms between ages 9 and 12 years (2020–2021 surveys) and excluding parent‐reported youth internalizing symptoms. Discrepant effects of parent‐reported versus youth‐reported internalizing symptoms may be related to the method of measurement (inadequate inter‐rater reliability) or it is possible that internalizing symptom sub‐type interacts with age‐specific developmental vulnerabilities.

In existing literature, there is a paucity of recent investigations specific to maternal internalizing symptoms and headache disorders in offspring 22 and limited prospective investigations into the association between pediatric internalizing symptoms and incident migraine or headache disorders. Elevated maternal anxiety symptom exposures have been prospectively associated with childhood somatic symptoms (including headache, nausea, and constipation) as early as 18 months of age. 51 A meta‐analysis of pediatric chronic pain disorders, including primary headache disorders, found parent anxiety symptoms were significantly associated with pediatric chronic pain. 52 Elevated parent anxiety symptoms significantly correlated with child depressive symptoms, and there is prospective evidence for the association between parental internalizing symptoms and incident pediatric chronic pain. 52 Contrary to our hypotheses, our study did not observe an association between maternal depression and youth incident migraine. This non‐intuitive finding aligns with findings from a recent meta‐analysis examining parental depression and youth chronic pain, which found a significant association between parental depression and combined causes of youth chronic pain, but not headache disorders. 53 To account for these findings, despite the biological similarities between anxiety and depression, a differential impact of parenting behaviors on pain in offspring may be considered. It is also possible that the choice of screening instrument may influence findings, as instruments may target underlying symptom clusters which correspond variably to expressed parenting behaviors.

To our knowledge, Hammond et al. are the only investigators to previously study early childhood internalizing symptom exposures (e.g., <8 years of age) paired with incident migraine in early adolescence. 5 , 25 Their findings, using data from the Canadian National Longitudinal Survey of Children and Youth (NLSCY), found no prospective association between parent‐reported youth anxiety and depressive symptoms, when reported by parents at a single timepoint, and health professional diagnosed adolescent migraine (affecting 3.4%) or non‐migraine headache attacks (affecting 11.8%). A Taiwanese cohort study of 13‐year‐old youth found that elevated depressive symptoms increased the adjusted hazard of incident chronic migraine 1–2 years later; however, baseline anxiety symptoms and parental symptoms were not examined. 54 Among older samples, prospective studies have found positive associations between adolescent depressive and anxiety symptoms, and incident migraine and recurrent impairing headaches attacks, when assessed in late adolescence and young adulthood. 55 , 56 , 57

The current study uniquely contributes to the pediatric migraine literature in several respects. High‐quality incident migraine data in youth is not widely available, and we highlight the study's use of a youth‐reported, validated migraine diagnosis scale for outcome ascertainment. Prevalent cases of headache attacks, potentially representing migraine, were sensitively identified and excluded at the onset of the exposure measurement period. Internalizing symptom exposures were collected from both youth and mothers, were measured serially across early school years until peri‐pubertal years in youth, and after ~age 9 years, youth provided self‐report of their internalizing symptoms. This study greatly benefits from being situated within the larger AOF cohort study, which serves as a methodologically rigorous, multi‐method, and longitudinal examination of pediatric health determinants and outcomes. 27 , 28

Migraine, anxiety, and depression are all considered brain‐based disorders with established heritability, whose phenotypic expression results from interacting biopsychosocial factors. Given this, the association between incident migraine and preceding exposure to youth and parent internalizing symptoms may plausibly occur through multiple mechanisms. A youth may experience early mental health symptoms and later migraine due to the developmentally influenced, dynamic expression of genes acting on common pathways, such as those regulating neurotransmitters, ion‐channel function, and synapse function. 58 A parent may experience significant mental health symptoms, and a child later develop migraine due to familial genes with pleiotropic effects, manifesting with variable phenotypes across generations. The environmental changes resulting from significant mental health symptoms, whether experienced directly by a youth or indirectly through adverse parenting behaviors, may lead to epigenetic modifications which increase the penetrance of migraine. 59 “Inclusive inheritance” may be an important framework for these considerations, as it recognizes transgenerational impacts on phenotype occur through germline epigenetic modifications, through autosomal, sex‐linked, and mitochondrial genes, as well as through experience‐dependent epigenetic modifications, parental effects, and social learning. 60 Additionally, the “stress‐generation model” recognizes that complex feedback loops occur between individuals and their environment (and experienced stressors), with implications for migraine and mental health conditions. 11 The influence of interacting social identities and structural factors (e.g., racism) on the trajectory of pain disorders is also increasingly recognized, although impacts specific to incident migraine remain under‐explored. 61 , 62

Strengths

The study benefitted methodologically from limited a priori hypotheses, prospective data collection, and the use of well‐validated instruments from multiple informants for data collection. The consistency of this study's exposure‐outcome association, with and without attrition weights, and in sensitivity analyses (though under‐powered), supports the face validity of the analyses. The analyses were well controlled for potentially confounding factors, and associations between analytical covariates and migraine were generally concordant with previously characterized exposure–migraine relationships.

A temporal association between migraine and exposure to maternal anxiety and youth internalizing symptoms may be cautiously considered. Consistently, associations were found between maternal anxiety and youth incident migraine in our adjusted analyses. Efforts were made to ensure measured exposures preceded the outcome. Exposure data in this study were collected prospectively, with at least 6 months in between each measurement of youth internalizing symptoms across childhood (ages 4–12 years). The clear temporal relationship between internalizing symptoms and migraine, as well as the dose–response relationship, lends credence to a potential causal role of exposure to maternal and youth internalizing symptoms in the etiopathogenesis of migraine.

Limitations

The onset of internalizing symptom measurement in our study predated the developmental period where youth self‐report is typically considered feasible (~age 9 years). Unfortunately, to our knowledge no literature exists to directly compare the performance of parent‐reported BASC internalizing symptom scales compared to a gold‐standard, clinical interviews. We strongly feel that using the available proxy‐reported data remains valuable, despite the possibility that mothers may have under‐reported significant internalizing symptoms in their youth. Additionally, rates of participant attrition may have been influenced by elevated mental health symptoms in mothers or youth, as well as by development of incident migraine. Finally, as we did not ascertain the exact number of prior migraine attacks (though participants were required to report at least one attack per month), participants with tension‐type headache or less than five migraine attacks may have been included, reducing diagnostic specificity. These potential biases, under‐reporting of exposures, differential loss to follow‐up, and potential outcome misclassification, may each plausibly lead to an under‐estimation of the true association between internalizing symptom exposure and incident migraine in youth.

The role of adverse childhood experiences (ACEs), including exposure to abuse, neglect, and parental incarceration, death, or divorce, is an active area of research in migraine causation. 22 , 25 , 63 Alterations in stress responses, including autonomic nervous system impacts, central pain sensitization, and aberrant signaling in pain‐related structures may link ACEs, internalizing symptoms, and migraine. 22 , 63 Psychologically, early life stressors and exposure to ACEs may contribute to development of early maladaptive cognitive schemas and subsequent risk of elevated internalizing symptoms. 63 , 64 Our investigation was unable to incorporate examination of childhood ACEs among the youth due to limitations in data availability. 25 Whereas intersectional methodology should ideally guide scientific inquiry into pain‐related conditions, not all potentially relevant sociocultural and structural factors could be feasibly examined in this study. 61 Additionally, there were limited data regarding active mental health treatment of youth and mothers available at each survey timepoint, and no data on paternal mental health symptoms. As such, residual confounding should be a consideration in the interpretation of this study's findings.

Previous examination of the AOF cohort pregnancy‐collected data (n = 3387) has supported it being representative of the Canadian urban population, with 40% of the sample living below the median family income, although some participants have a degree of relative socioeconomic advantage (more educated, wealthier). 28 , 65 Therefore, there are limitations to the external validity of our data, outside of urban Canadian populations. There was attrition in the cohort, which can introduce the risk of attrition bias, though we did find strong correspondence between complete case analyses and analyses using study attrition weights to restore the original AOF cohort demographic. 29

Conclusions

As results originate from a community‐based cohort of urban‐dwelling Canadian children, predominantly identifying as White and cis‐gender, careful generalization of findings to similar contexts may be considered. Whereas there have been substantial recent advances in the secondary prevention of migraine (e.g., targeted therapies to prevent attacks in those living with migraine), it is highly desirable to prevent the development of migraine in vulnerable populations altogether (i.e., primary prevention). Internalizing symptoms experienced by youths and their mothers may represent a formal target for migraine primary prevention efforts. Regardless of one's genetic risk profile, a reduction in exposure to internalizing symptoms may be achievable through evidence‐based mental health treatment. 66 , 67 Addressing pediatric mental health through family‐based interventions presents an opportunity to leverage the family system to better support mental health in both youth and parents and potentially to reduce the risk of migraine in youth. A randomized prevention trial, offering monitoring and treatment of subthreshold anxiety and/or depressive symptoms to children at elevated risk of migraine (i.e., those with affected parents), and/or to parents with anxiety, may be a next step toward preventive care in migraine. Future work should aim to determine at what level the association between internalizing symptoms and migraine risk occurs, whether genetic, physiological, psychological, or environmental.
