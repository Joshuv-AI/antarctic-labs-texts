Behind closed doors: unlocking hospital variations in the use of seclusion and mechanical restraint – a nationwide multilevel analysis in adult mental healthcare in France
Authors: E Touitou-Burckard, M Coldefy, C Bourin, A Ellini, S Saetta, C Gandré
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMonitoring and reducing restrictive practices in mental healthcare, including seclusion and restraint, is a global policy priority due to ethical concerns. However, research on these practices remains limited. This study aims to estimate the use of seclusion and mechanical restraint (MR) in mental healthcare at the national level in France, the variation in the use of these practices across hospitals, and associated factors considering a wide range of variables.MethodsWe used the French nationwide system for the routine collection of psychiatric hospital claims data, which includes a specific register on seclusion and MR, covering all hospitals providing involuntary psychiatric care for adults in France. Data from 204 hospitals were analyzed for the year 2022. Additional information on patient, hospital, and contextual characteristics was obtained by linking data from other national sources, including somatic hospital and community care claims, annual hospital surveys and census data. Using these combined datasets, we conducted multilevel logistic regressions to identify factors significantly associated with variations in the use of restrictive practices.ResultsIn 2022, among 98,271 involuntary psychiatric inpatient admissions across 204 hospitals, seclusion was used in 31,679 admissions (32%) and MR in 8,551 admissions (9%). Large variations in the use of restrictive practices were observed across hospitals, with some reporting no use of MR. Multilevel models showed that patient characteristics, including primary diagnosis and prisoner status, were among the strongest predictors of seclusion and MR use. Admissions to public hospitals with teaching activities or multidisciplinary services were associated with lower odds of seclusion, while a higher nurse-to-patient ratio and proportion of involuntary admissions were associated with lower odds of MR use. No contextual factors related to hospitals’ catchment areas were significantly associated with the use of restrictive practices.ConclusionsSignificant variations were found in the use of seclusion and MR across hospitals in France, which cannot be explained solely by patient needs. While patient characteristics were strong predictors, several hospital-level factors were also associated with these practices. These findings highlight the need for closer monitoring and targeted interventions to reduce unwarranted variations and raise concerns about equity and ethics in mental healthcare.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-025-07404-z.Keywords: Mental Health Services, Inpatients, Adult, Logistic models, France, Seclusion, Restraint

Background

Restrictive measures are used in mental healthcare systems worldwide on the premise that they can help prevent harmful situations when patients pose a threat to themselves or others. These measures include involuntary care and the use of seclusion and restraint. Seclusion involves isolating a patient in a locked room, while restraint aims to immobilize the patient and may be mechanical (e.g., bed straps), physical (e.g., manual holds) or pharmacological (i.e., forced medication). In most countries, these practices are legally regulated and permitted only as last-resort interventions after de-escalation strategies have failed. Many international bodies have called for an end to these practices, and monitoring and reducing their use is a global policy priority [1–3]. This priority reflects the lack of evidence of their therapeutic benefits and well-documented adverse effects, including physical and psychological sequelae and strain on care relationships [4–6].

The use of these restrictive practices varies widely across countries, with some banning them on ethical grounds [7, 8]. Even where they are legally permitted, reported rates remain highly variable [9], and significant variation is also observed within countries [10–12]. Research has shown that these variations are partly associated with patient characteristics, including clinical, behavioral, demographic and socioeconomic factors [13–16]. However, the extent of the observed variations suggests that some may be unwarranted, not resulting only from differences in individuals’ health needs. Preliminary evidence also points to the influence of factors beyond patient characteristics [10, 11, 17]. The high level of uncertainty regarding the appropriate use of restrictive practices in mental healthcare, combined with ongoing debates about balancing individual rights and public safety, further increases the likelihood of unwarranted variations [18, 19].

Current evidence on the use of restrictive practices in mental healthcare highlights several areas requiring further investigation. First, in many national contexts – including France – little data are available on the extent of seclusion and restraint use. Second, data on variations in these practices across care providers remain limited, and are often based on small, nonrandom samples (e.g., single hospitals or regions) [10, 11, 16], which reduces generalizability. Third, despite repeated calls in previous publications [20, 21], few large-scale studies have examined the association between the use of seclusion or restraint and patient, provider, and contextual factors combined. Such analyses are essential for designing effective strategies to reduce the use of restrictive practices in complex mental healthcare systems and for informing health policies that can better support clinical teams in achieving this goal.

In this context, our objectives were to: (1) estimate the use of seclusion and mechanical restraint (MR) in French mental healthcare, (2) assess variation across healthcare providers nationwide, and (3) identify associated factors considering a broad range of variables.

Methods

Setting

France has a universal healthcare system that ensures equal access to mental healthcare [22]. Hospital teams provide both outpatient and inpatient mental health services to the population within administratively defined catchment areas known as “psychiatric sectors”. In this frame, involuntary psychiatric care is a public service delivered in designated hospitals according to the patient’s place of residence. In 2016, legislation was introduced to regulate and limit the use of seclusion and MR (Loi n° 2016-41 du 26 janvier 2016 de modernisation de notre système de santé) [23]. These measures are permitted only on the basis of a medical decision, during involuntary admissions, and for specific durations, in accordance with national clinical guidelines. Their extension is subject to the control of a liberty and custody judge. The use of MR is further restricted to patients who are secluded [24, 25]. Since 2018, all hospitals providing involuntary psychiatric care have been required to record their use of seclusion and MR in a specific register. In our research, the term ‘restrictive practices’ refers exclusively to these last two practices, when used in accordance with French law. Involuntary care is excluded from this definition, as it lies beyond the focus of this study.

Conceptual framework

Our research focused on unwarranted variations in the use of seclusion and MR across healthcare providers. Based on the literature, these variations were defined as those not related to clinical appropriateness and patient needs and that threaten the quality, equity and efficiency of care [18, 26]. Our theoretical framework further adapted Wennberg’s classification of medical practice variations to the specificities of restrictive practices in mental healthcare. These practices cannot be considered effective care due to the uncertainty regarding their appropriate level of use, if any. Similarly, they cannot be classified as preference-sensitive care, as patients’ choices are generally not taken into account during involuntary care [18]. They also raise numerous ethical challenges in terms of respect for autonomy, beneficence, nonmaleficence and justice [27].

We therefore adopted a positive approach, comparing the use of seclusion and MR by healthcare provider with average national practices. This was preferred to a normative approach, which would have compared practices with a predefined standard of good practice [28, 29]. Second, to identify variations that may be unwarranted, we identified the factors associated with them, which makes it easier to determine whether they should legitimately influence the use of seclusion and MR [26]. Because medical practice is a complex decision-making process driven by a combination of factors, we used a typology of variables that may be associated with practice variations based on three categories. They included patient characteristics (the demand side), healthcare provider characteristics (the supply side) and characteristics of their environment (the practice context) [30–32].

Data sources

Our main data source was the French nationwide system for the routine collection of psychiatric claims data in hospital settings (RIM-P, Recueil d’informations médicalisé pour la psychiatrie), which includes the specific register of seclusion and MR use. We used data for 2022, as this year provides the most complete information since the creation of the register in 2018 and was the most recent full year available at the start of the research. To identify factors potentially associated with seclusion and MR use, we also extracted information from somatic hospital care claims data (PMSI-MCO, Programme de médicalisation des systèmes d’information en médecine, chirurgie et obstétrique) and from the French national health data system (SNDS, Système national des données de santé), which additionally includes data on community-based care [33]. Permanent access to these databases was granted to the lead institution involved in the research [34, 35]. No specific ethical approval or informed consent was therefore required to access these data, which are fully pseudonymized. Complementary databases, publicly available online, were also used. They included an annual survey of all French hospitals (SAE, Statistique annuelle des établissements de santé) [36] and census data from the French national institute for statistical and economic studies (INSEE, Institut national de la statistique et des études économiques) [37].

Study population

We included claims data from mainland France related to involuntary psychiatric inpatient admissions for patients aged 18 years or older. Child and adolescent psychiatric services are not legally allowed to use seclusion and MR aside from exceptional circumstances. We considered claims from general psychiatry only (i.e., excluding forensic units due to their organizational specificities and limited number in France) in hospitals designated to provide involuntary care for a predefined catchment area, which constituted our healthcare providers of interest. Admissions for prisoners treated in general psychiatry wards were not excluded.

To avoid bias in our analysis, we excluded admissions from hospitals that did not report data in the register of seclusion and MR when their use of such practices was documented elsewhere (i.e., in another RIM-P dataset or in inspection reports by the French national authority for health or the general controller of places of deprivation of liberty). We also excluded admissions from hospitals with fewer than 30 involuntary inpatient admissions in 2022.

Variables of interest

Our variables of interest were whether at least one measure of seclusion occurred for each included involuntary psychiatric admission and whether at least one measure of MR occurred for each of these admissions involving seclusion. We also calculated the rates of use of seclusion and MR in each hospital in 2022. The total rates of patients who experienced seclusion and MR per 100,000 adults were calculated for international comparison.

Factors potentially associated with the use of seclusion and MR

In line with our conceptual framework, we considered several factors potentially associated with the use of seclusion and MR. These variables were selected based on the international literature [10–16] and discussions with French experts from the Plaid-Care Group, which has conducted qualitative research in four hospitals with low levels of restrictive practices [38]. They were then adapted to the data available at the national scale. The variables included patients’ sociodemographic and clinical characteristics as well as characteristics of their involuntary admissions (demand-side factors) (see Table 1 for the full list). They also included the institutional, organizational and case-mix characteristics of the admission hospital (supply-side factors). Finally, contextual characteristics of the hospital’s catchment area (practice context factors) were included, such as the treated prevalence of mental disorders, socioeconomic characteristics (composite measures of social fragmentation and deprivation [39, 40]) and rurality (see Additional file 1 for the full list).

Analysis

Because several hospitals were excluded due to poor data quality or limited provision of involuntary care in 2022, the included and excluded hospitals were compared using Student’s t or chi2 tests. In the included hospitals, the characteristics of the study population were also compared according to whether seclusion or MR was experienced. Variations in the use of restrictive practices were described by examining hospital-level rates and comparing them with national averages.

To identify factors associated with variations in the use of seclusion or MR, all other things being equal, logistic regressions were conducted at the involuntary inpatient admission level. For the model on the use of seclusion, involuntary admissions without seclusion were used as the reference group. For the model on the use of MR, involuntary admissions with seclusion but without MR were the reference group, in accordance with French legislation. To account for the nested structure of the data, we used multilevel models considering the admission level (level 1) and the hospital level (level 2). Invariable patient characteristics were attributed to each of their admissions. We first fitted empty models, which included a random intercept at the hospital level but no explanatory variables. This initial step aimed to verify the presence of a center effect, i.e. systematic differences in the use of restrictive practices across hospitals independently of individual patient characteristics. Second, patient, hospital and contextual characteristics that were significant in multilevel bivariate analyses were introduced as explanatory variables after controlling for collinearity. Continuous variables were scaled using grand-mean centering to facilitate model convergence and interpretation [41]. The final models retained variables that remained significant using a manual step-down procedure. To assess the variance explained by the models, we used metrics specifically tailored to multilevel logistic regression. We calculated the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), which quantifies the proportion of total variance attributable to differences between hospitals, and the coefficients of determination R². These included the marginal R², representing the proportion of variance explained by fixed effects alone (i.e., explanatory variables at the patient and hospital levels). These also included the conditional R², reflecting the proportion of total variance explained by both fixed and random effects – capturing unobserved variability across hospitals not accounted for by the fixed effects [42, 43].

Results

Study population

In 2022, a total of 103,405 involuntary inpatient admissions for adult patients took place in general psychiatry in 231 hospitals designated to provide involuntary care for a predefined catchment area in mainland France. For our analysis, we considered 98,271 involuntary admissions from 204 hospitals (Fig. 1), representing 27% of all inpatient psychiatric admissions in these hospitals. Only a limited number of statistically significant differences were observed between the included and excluded hospitals (Additional file 1).

Study flowchart

Among the included hospitals, 31,679 admissions had at least one measure of seclusion, representing 32% of involuntary inpatient admissions and 8% of all psychiatric inpatient admissions. A total of 8,551 admissions had at least one measure of MR, representing 27% of admissions involving seclusion, 9% of involuntary inpatient admissions and 2% of all psychiatric inpatient admissions. Overall, 25,957 unique patients were subjected to seclusion in 2022, representing a rate of 55 secluded persons per 100,000 adults served by these hospitals in one year. 7,535 unique patients further experienced MR during seclusion, representing 16 restrained persons per 100,000 adults.

In the descriptive analysis, patients subjected to restrictive practices were younger, more often male and, for seclusion, more frequently deprived than other patients. On average, they spent a greater number of days in psychiatric inpatient care annually. Seclusion was more common among admissions from emergency services and for severe mental illnesses, including psychotic, bipolar, and personality disorders. Admissions associated with seclusion and MR were also more likely for patients with disorders for which psychiatric inpatient care is not the recommended treatment (e.g., mental retardation, disorders of psychological development, behavioral and emotional disorders). Additionally, there was a disproportionate representation of prisoners or individuals declared not criminally responsible in admissions with seclusion, whereas the opposite was observed for MR. Finally, admissions involving seclusion were less frequently preceded by a history of involuntary care in the same hospital and suicide attempts (Table 1).

Variations in the use of seclusion and MR across hospitals

There were wide differences across hospitals in the use of seclusion and MR, as demonstrated by all dispersion measures (Table 2). The observed variations could not be attributed solely to a few hospitals with extreme values (Fig. 2). 0.5% (n = 1) and 9% (n = 19) of hospitals reported no use of seclusion and MR, respectively.

The final models showed significant associations between patient characteristics (the demand side) and the use of restrictive practices, all other things being equal (Table 3). The primary diagnosis group that led to admission was among the strongest predictors for both seclusion and MR use. Admissions for prisoners and persons declared not criminally responsible also had one of the strongest associations with an increased likelihood of seclusion use and a decreased likelihood of MR use (Table 3).

Regarding the characteristics of care providers (the supply side), admission to a public hospital with teaching activities or to a multidisciplinary public hospital, compared with admission to a public psychiatric hospital, was significantly associated with a lower likelihood of seclusion (Table 3). A higher average ratio of nursing staff per patient and a greater proportion of involuntary admissions among inpatient admissions were both significantly associated with a lower likelihood of MR during seclusion (Table 3).

Regarding the characteristics of the environment (the practice context), none of the characteristics of hospitals’ catchment area were significantly associated with the likelihood of using restrictive practices.

Overall, despite the substantial number of potential factors associated with the use of seclusion and MR included in our analysis, the coefficients of determination (R2) of the multivariable models remained small (Table 3).

Discussion

Our research estimated the use of restrictive practices in mental healthcare at the national level in France, its variation across hospitals, and associated factors. In 2022, the use of seclusion and MR accounted for 32% and 9% of involuntary inpatient admissions, respectively. Striking variations were observed between hospitals for both practices. A substantial portion could be attributed to the hospital level but was only partly explained by the large number of factors considered in our analysis.

One encouraging finding of our research is that several hospitals did not use MR at all, which suggests that eliminating restraint in French mental healthcare could be an achievable goal in the future [44]. Importantly, this need not compromise the safety of patients or professionals, nor lead to a compensatory increase in other forms of restraint. This is supported by a pilot study conducted in a few French hospitals, which found that the complete absence of MR in one facility was not associated with greater use of high-dose medication or seclusion [45]. These findings are further corroborated by international research [46, 47]. Overall, the extent of the use of seclusion and MR in France, despite legislation introduced to reduce these practices, raises important questions. This is particularly notable given that the use of seclusion per 100,000 people appears to be greater than in most of the few Western countries for which data were available [9]. While international comparisons are complicated by differences in measurement methodologies, data collection periods and practice definitions, this still raises concerns suggesting scope for improvement in mental healthcare in France.

Another important concern is the considerable variation in the use of restrictive practices observed between French healthcare providers although all hospitals are subjected to the same legal framework. Such differences remain unstudied in most countries but have also been reported in Germany and the Netherlands [48]. Additionally, the extent of the variability observed in France was not fully accounted for by patient characteristics (demand-side factors). Nevertheless, several clinical variables were significantly associated with the likelihood of seclusion and MR and, notably, the diagnosis leading to involuntary inpatient admission. Restrictive practices were more likely to be used for severe mental disorders, in which auto- or hetero-aggressivity and psychomotor agitation may be more prevalent [49]. While this may indicate some consistency with national clinical guidelines [24, 25], an increased likelihood of use was also found for disorders for which psychiatric inpatient care is not the recommended treatment (e.g., mental retardation or autism spectrum disorders). This suggests that restrictive practices may sometimes be used to address challenges in providing care for vulnerable populations requiring tailored support. Another issue of ethical concern is the disproportionate use of seclusion among prisoners, all other things being equal. This points to a quasi-systematic implementation that is not justified for purely clinical reasons. In contrast, prisoners had a lower likelihood of being subjected to MR. This suggests that they are not secluded because they have more violent episodes than other patients. These findings are not in accordance with national guidelines that specify the need for clinical reasons to justify the use of restrictive practices, including for prisoners [24, 25, 50]. One possible explanation is that some prisoners may have required a higher degree of therapeutic security than what general psychiatric wards can provide, potentially leading to excessive use of seclusion, for instance due to perceived risk to others on the ward, stigma, or to prevent absconding. There is evidence that placing such patients in settings with insufficient therapeutic security may result in treatment failure [51–53]. Previous international comparisons of secure psychiatric bed availability have not been able to include French data [54, 55]. However, the number of such beds per capita is estimated to be lower than in most other European countries [56], complicating the provision of adequate mental healthcare for prisoners. Regarding the characteristics of healthcare providers (supply-side factors), only the type of hospital was significantly associated with the likelihood of resorting to seclusion. This likelihood was lower in hospitals with teaching activities or those that were multidisciplinary compared with psychiatric hospitals. Strong university and research links have been associated with better care provision in other settings [57], or for other mental healthcare practices in France [58]. Regarding MR, a higher ratio of nurses per patient, which can be interpreted as an indicator of relational therapeutic security for patients and staff [59, 60], was associated with a lower likelihood of resorting to this practice. Mixed findings had been reported previously on this topic in the international literature [11, 17]. Qualitative research conducted by the Plaid-Care Group in four French hospitals with low rates of restrictive practices provided additional evidence of factors linked to professionals that may prevent the use of MR and seclusion. These factors included the stability and cohesion of hospital teams and the involvement of administrative staff in patient care (e.g., social workers, secretaries, and cleaners). The attention given by management teams to the satisfaction and well-being of professionals also played an important role [61].

In contrast with a prevailing misconception [10], in our research, the severity of the case mix in inpatient care was not found to be associated with a greater likelihood of resorting to restrictive practices. On the contrary, a greater proportion of involuntary admissions among inpatient stays in the hospital was significantly associated with a lower likelihood of MR use. This may be linked to hospitals which have strongly limited inpatient care to only the most severe situations. This finding also suggests that hospitals that deal more frequently with patients in involuntary care may develop alternative coping strategies and demonstrate reduced fear and stigma toward this population. The potential role of prior exposure to challenging patients is also supported by the lower likelihood of seclusion during admissions of patients with a history of previous hospitalization in the same facility as a proxy for the fact that these individuals were known by the healthcare team.

Regarding the characteristics of hospitals’ catchment areas (practice context factors), which reflect the greater complexity of the population served, no significant association was found with the likelihood of resorting to restrictive practices. In addition, overall, the significant associations identified in our analysis accounted for a limited proportion of the observed variations in the use of seclusion and MR. This is consistent with the scarce research conducted in other countries that did not focus exclusively on patient characteristics [10, 11, 17] and suggests that there remains unidentified factors.

These findings should be interpreted with regard to the main limitations of the research. First, they are linked to data available to account for differences in patients’ needs. For example, we lacked information on the severity of mental disorders measured using standardized scales, the presence of agitation, aggression or other forms of violence or self-harm, the duration of untreated mental illness, and patients’ country of origin. Similarly, we lacked robust data on comorbidities of the main psychiatric disorder – including substance misuse. Second, conducting our research on the national scale restricted the availability of supply-side factors. For instance, we lacked information on service delivery models and clinical practice approaches, specific staff training, de-escalation alternatives, or internal policies not documented in administrative data. Regional or cultural differences within France, such as local practices or institutional and staffing culture, may also partly explain the observed variation in the use of restrictive practices across hospitals. However, they could not be fully accounted for in our analysis. Qualitative research by the Plaid-Care Group in four French hospitals with low levels of restrictive practices highlighted several factors related to professional practice and service organization that are difficult to study at a national level using quantitative methods. These include the availability of professionals for patients, a nursing office that remains open to all, the presence and diversity of activities, and the existence of flexible rules [61]. Such care cultures can also lead to significant differences in the use of restrictive practices within the hospital (e.g., wards that do not practice MR and others that do), which, to date, cannot be studied at the national level. As all these factors are not documented for each hospital in large-scale datasets, this likely accounts for a considerable proportion of the unexplained variation in the use of restrictive practices across hospitals in France. Despite these limitations, no previous research has addressed practice variations in the use of seclusion and MR in mental healthcare by conducting a nationwide quantitative analysis with considerable statistical power and considering the combined characteristics of patients, healthcare providers and the environment.

Our findings should draw the attention of clinicians and policy makers as variations in the use of these practices can undermine the quality, equity and efficiency of care while raising important ethical and legal concerns. The monitoring, reporting and analysis of medical practice variations has been identified as a fundamental step toward their diminution [62, 63]. In addition, the use of data to inform practice is a key component of the international program “Six Core Strategies for Reducing Seclusion and Restraint” [64]. Our research also suggests specific elements that can help in the design of effective interventions aimed at helping healthcare teams to reduce the use of restrictive practices in mental healthcare. They include focused and individualized care for involuntarily admitted patients whose profile exposes them to a greater likelihood of being subjected to restrictive practices, including those admitted through emergency services, those unknown to the staff, those with specific diagnoses and prisoners. Individualized care plans should be implemented by clinicians, considering such risk factors for patients. In addition, whenever possible, prisoners should be hospitalized in dedicated units to address safety concerns without compromising their right to equal care in accordance with recent guidelines [50]. Expanding the availability of forensic beds and specialized wards to better meet their needs requires targeted policy support in the French context. Sufficient staffing levels should also be ensured, with our findings suggesting that this is essential for nurses who play a central role in the prevention, de-escalation, and management of agitation in patients hospitalized in mental health settings. However, this may prove challenging in a national context where numerous positions within psychiatric wards remain vacant [65]. Improving working conditions may therefore be necessary, alongside the development of alternative strategies targeting nurses, such as specific training in de-escalation techniques, the development of communication skills, and the promotion of leadership roles in efforts to reduce restrictive practices [66]. The implementation of routine monitoring systems for the use of seclusion and MR at the hospital level, for example using validated scales to audit their proportionality and necessity [67], should also be encouraged. Finally, establishing a knowledge-sharing framework between hospitals with low and high use of restrictive practices could promote mutual learning and the dissemination of best practices.

Our findings also suggest avenues for further research. It would notably be highly relevant to develop mixed-methods studies, combining both quantitative and qualitative approaches, focused on the variations in the use of restrictive practices in mental healthcare across hospitals in France. This would allow for a deeper exploration of cultural factors, staff perceptions, and patients’ lived experiences. Overall, this would offer a more comprehensive understanding of the phenomenon under study and help to address some of the limitations of our current research. In-depth analyses of hospitals reporting no use of MR in particular could yield valuable insights into local, context-specific factors that could not be captured at the national level. This would additionally help determine whether the absence of MR is associated with a broader reduction in the use of all forms of restrictive interventions. With regard to quantitative methods specifically, future studies should capitalize on the expected improvement in the quality of data reporting in the French register of seclusion and MR over time. This includes more reliable information on the number and duration of events per admission, as well as on when they occur most during an hospitalization. Similarly, longitudinal analyses should soon become sufficiently robust to monitor annual trends and allow the impact of restrictive practices on future healthcare pathways to be addressed, including in terms of repeated use. During our data management process, we identified instances of seclusion and MR conducted outside the legal framework of involuntary care, which warrant further investigation. Finally, our findings may help advocate for the development of comprehensive data collection systems on the use of other debated measures in mental healthcare, such as forced medication. These systems should also cover the use of restrictive practices in settings without current mandatory reporting requirements (e.g., emergency departments, the health and social care sector, nursing homes, pediatric care) and in countries that lack access to population-level registers.

Conclusions

Significant variations were found in the use of seclusion and MR across hospitals in France, which cannot be explained solely by patient needs. While patient characteristics were strong predictors, several hospital-level factors were also associated with these practices. This requires the attention of researchers, clinicians and policy makers to design appropriate interventions to reduce unwarranted variations and improve the protection of patients’ rights. The policy implications of our research include the implementation of individualized care plans that take into account patient-level risk factors; the expansion of forensic beds and specialized wards for prisoners; the development of interventions to support nurse availability and ensure adequate staffing; the routine monitoring of restrictive practices in mental healthcare at the hospital level; and the promotion of mutual learning and the dissemination of best practices between hospitals with low and high use of such practices.
