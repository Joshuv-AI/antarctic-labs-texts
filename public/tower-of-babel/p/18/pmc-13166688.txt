Introduction

Septic nonunion is a formidable complication of open fractures, characterized by persistent infection, failure of bone healing, and significant bone loss, posing substantial challenges in orthopedic management. This condition arises when an infection, typically bacterial, impedes the bone’s ability to unite, leading to chronic pain, functional impairment, and recurrent discharging sinuses[1]. In patients with sickle cell disease (SCD), a hereditary hemoglobinopathy prevalent in sub-Saharan Africa, the management of septic nonunion is particularly complex due to the interplay of chronic anemia, vaso-occlusive crises, and an increased predisposition to infections stemming from functional asplenia and impaired immune responses[2]. These factors exacerbate the risk of osteomyelitis and complicate wound healing, necessitating tailored therapeutic strategies.

The Ilizarov technique, which employs distraction osteogenesis through an external ring fixator, has revolutionized the treatment of septic nonunion by addressing infection, bone defects, and deformities simultaneously[3]. The prevalence of septic nonunion in open tibial fractures is reported to be as high as 10–20%, with higher rates in patients with comorbidities such as SCD, underscoring the need for advanced reconstructive techniques[1]. This case report presents the successful management of septic nonunion in a patient with SCD, utilizing debridement, bone transport, and Ilizarov ring fixation. This case highlights the complexities of managing septic nonunion in the context of SCD, emphasizing the efficacy of the Ilizarov technique and the importance of addressing the unique pathophysiological challenges posed by this systemic condition. This case report has been reported in line with the SCARE checklist[4].

Presentation of case

A 27-year-old female with a past medical history of SCD presented to the outpatient department with complaints of right leg pain for 3 months and purulent discharge for 1 month. The patient had no other significant past medical history or relevant family history. Three years prior, the patient suffered an open fracture of the right distal tibia after a road traffic accident. The injury was managed with open reduction and external fixation at an outside hospital with minimal improvement. Over the past 3 months, the pain worsened, becoming persistent, aggravated by movement, and only partially relieved by over-the-counter analgesics (i.e., ibuprofen, paracetamol). For the past 1 month, there was purulent discharge from two sinuses on the anterior right leg and an inability to ambulate independently. No associated fevers were reported.

On physical examination, she was afebrile with mild jaundice and no pallor. The right foot was in the equinus position. There were two discharging sinuses on the anterior leg, mild tenderness to palpation, with a normal distal neurovascular exam (Fig. 1A). Laboratory investigations demonstrated an elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) of 32 mm/hour (normal: 0–20 mm/hour), leukocytosis of 18 × 103 cells/µL (normal: 4–11 × 103 cells/µL), neutrophilia of 12.31 × 103 cells/µL (normal: 2–7 × 103 cells/uL) and lymphocytosis of 4.08 × 103 cell/µL (normal: 1–4 × 103 cells/µL). Hemoglobin was 9.4 g/dL (normal: 12–16 g/dL). An X-ray of the right tibia revealed atrophic nonunion of the distal tibia with sclerosis and sequestrum (Fig. 1B). Advanced imaging such as MRI or bone scintigraphy were discussed but could not be obtained due to resource limitations and financial constraints in our setting. Figure 1.Right lower extremity. The diagnosis of septic nonunion of the right distal tibia was made based on clinical findings including purulent draining sinuses, laboratory evidence of infection with elevated ESR and leukocytosis with neutrophilia, and radiographic findings of atrophic nonunion with sclerosis and sequestrum. Culture and sensitivity testing were requested but could not be performed due to lack of laboratory reagents and financial constraints.

The patient was taken to the operating room for surgical debridement of the affected area under general anesthesia by an orthopedic consultant assisted by residents and medical officers of various levels. Prophylactic intravenous ceftriaxone 2 g was administered within 30 minutes prior to incision. Due to the patient’s SCD and chronic anemia, intraoperative transfusion of 2 units of packed red blood cells was performed, and perioperative hydration with intravenous normal saline was maintained to prevent sickle cell crisis. Thorough surgical debridement of the infected bone and soft tissue was performed, with removal of sequestrum and necrotic material, creating a 7 cm segmental defect. Fibula corticotomy was then performed to prevent fibular overgrowth during transport. Proximal tibia osteotomy was done to create the transport segment. An Ilizarov ring fixator for bone transport was then applied. The system consisted of 3 rings (modified from standard 4-ring construct due to material constraints) with multiple wires and half-pins for rigid fixation (Fig. 2). The construct was designed to allow gradual bone transport to fill the segmental defect. No intraoperative complications were observed. Figure 2.Illizarov ring fixator. Postoperatively, the patient was maintained with touch toe nonweight-bearing status, with regular wound care, analgesics, and intravenous ceftriaxone 2 g daily for 7 days, followed by oral ciprofloxacin 500 mg twice daily for 2 weeks. The patient developed a minor pin-site infection on postoperative day 4, which was successfully managed with local pin-site care, resolving uneventfully. Bone transport was initiated on postoperative day 7 at a rate of 1 mm per day, and postoperative X-ray confirmed alignment and fixation (Fig. 3A). Figure 3.Postoperative imaging. The patient was discharged on postoperative day 8. The patient reported discomfort with the bulky frame on several occasions during follow-up visits, necessitating repeated counseling and reassurance to maintain compliance. At 5 weeks, bone transport was ongoing with stable fixation (Fig. 3B). At 5 months, successful docking was achieved, indicating progression toward union and the patient was ambulating more than 10 m with the assistance of axillary crutches (Fig. 3C). The patient continued ambulating with assistance at 12 months, and frame removal was planned at 13 months postoperatively pending radiological confirmation of complete union.

Discussion

Septic nonunion is a complex orthopedic condition that combines the challenges of chronic infection and impaired bone healing, often resulting from open fractures with significant soft tissue injury or inadequate initial management[1]. In this case, the patient’s septic nonunion of the right distal tibia was a sequela of an open fracture sustained in a road traffic accident 3 years prior, initially treated with external fixation. The persistence of infection along with radiographic findings of atrophic nonunion, sclerosis, and sequestrum, confirmed the diagnosis of chronic osteomyelitis complicating nonunion. Although microbiological culture would have provided definitive pathogen identification and antibiotic sensitivity, it was unavailable in our low-resource setting, a limitation that necessitated empirical antibiotic therapy based on common pathogens associated with chronic osteomyelitis[5].

The patient’s underlying SCD significantly complicated the clinical scenario, as SCD predisposes individuals to infections due to functional asplenia, impaired opsonization, and recurrent vaso-occlusive crises that compromise tissue perfusion and healing[2]. The hemoglobin level and mild jaundice observed in this patient were consistent with chronic hemolytic anemia characteristic of SCD, which necessitates careful perioperative management to prevent sickle cell crises triggered by hypoxia, dehydration, or infection[2]. We specifically addressed the risks associated with SCD with perioperative hydration with intravenous fluids to prevent vaso-occlusive crisis and blood transfusion to optimize oxygen-carrying capacity and reduce the risk of perioperative complications[6–8].

Osteoarticular infections are common in SCD patients and must be distinguished from vaso-occlusive crises[9], though differences can be challenging, particularly in low-resource settings lacking advanced diagnostic capabilities. The patient’s localized infection with purulent drainage was more consistent with an infective process. The absence of systemic symptoms likely contributed to the favorable surgical outcome. Broad-spectrum antibiotics were administered postoperatively to control residual infection, a critical step supported by guidelines for managing osteomyelitis in nonunion cases[1].

The management of septic nonunion requires a comprehensive strategy that addresses infection eradication, bone stabilization, and restoration of limb function. Traditional approaches, such as internal fixation or bone grafting, are often inadequate in the presence of active infection or significant bone loss, making the Ilizarov technique an ideal choice[3].

The Ilizarov method leverages distraction osteogenesis to promote bone regeneration while allowing for simultaneous deformity correction and infection control through meticulous debridement and stable external fixation[10]. In this case, surgical intervention involved debridement to remove infected tissue and sequestrum, followed by fibula corticotomy and proximal tibia osteotomy to create a transport segment and for correction of the equinus deformity. The Ilizarov ring fixator provided stabilization, enabling controlled bone transport starting on postoperative day 7. The successful docking at 5 months aligns with reported timelines for bone regeneration in complex tibial nonunions, which typically range from 4 to 6 months depending on the defect size and patient factors[3].

The Ilizarov technique’s ability to achieve high union rates (up to 90%) in septic nonunion, even in complex cases, is well-documented, with studies highlighting its versatility in addressing bone defects, infection, and deformities simultaneously[11,12]. However, the technique is not without challenges, including prolonged treatment duration, the need for patient compliance with frame adjustments, and the risk of pin-site infections, which require meticulous wound care and regular follow-up[3]. To mitigate these challenges, we implemented weekly follow-up visits for wound care and compliance monitoring, with patient education on pin-site care, frame maintenance, and the importance of completing the treatment course.

The equinus deformity observed in this patient, resulting from prolonged immobilization and soft tissue contracture, underscores the importance of addressing secondary complications in septic nonunion cases. Postunion rehabilitation, including physiotherapy and potential corrective procedures, are essential to restore full function[3,13]. The intact distal neurovascular status was a positive prognostic factor, indicating preserved limb viability despite chronic infection and nonunion. This patient underwent regular physiotherapy sessions throughout the treatment course, focusing on maintaining range of motion and gradually improving weight-bearing capacity.

The Ilizarov method’s success in this case highlights its utility in managing septic nonunion in patients with comorbidities like SCD, but it also emphasizes the need for a multidisciplinary approach involving orthopedic surgeons, hematologists, and infectious disease specialists to optimize outcomes. Future research should explore strategies to enhance perioperative care in SCD patients, such as preoperative optimization of hemoglobin levels and aggressive infection prophylaxis, to further improve the success rates of complex reconstructive procedures using the Ilizarov technique.

Several limitations of both the technique and its application in this case warrant consideration. The Ilizarov method demands substantial patient compliance over a prolonged treatment period, typically spanning many months to years[3], which can be particularly challenging in low-resource settings where access to regular follow-up, specialized nursing care, and rehabilitation services may be restricted. The risk of pin-site infection, encountered in this patient on postoperative day 4, is a well-recognized complication requiring vigilant wound care[3,5,11], and in immunocompromised individuals such as those with SCD, this risk may be amplified. Furthermore, the technique demands considerable surgical expertise in frame construction, adjustment, and troubleshooting[12], limiting its widespread applicability where specialized orthopedic training is unavailable. With respect to case-specific limitations, the absence of microbiological culture and antibiotic sensitivity data necessitated empirical antibiotic therapy, potentially suboptimal compared to pathogen-directed treatment. Advanced imaging modalities, including MRI and bone scintigraphy, which would have permitted more precise characterization of the extent of osteomyelitis and soft tissue involvement, could not be obtained. Additionally, the use of a modified 3-ring construct rather than the standard 4-ring configuration may have affected construct rigidity. Although no adverse biomechanical consequences were observed clinically, this deviation from standard practice should be acknowledged. Critically, as a single case report, the findings presented herein cannot be generalized, and definitive conclusions regarding the efficacy of this approach in SCD patients with septic nonunion await larger prospective studies. Finally, radiographic confirmation of complete union has not yet been achieved at the time of this report, and the final outcome of union at 13 months is pending frame removal.

Conclusion

This case illustrates the application of the Ilizarov technique in managing septic nonunion of the distal tibia in a patient with SCD, achieving docking at 5 months with ongoing treatment at 12 months follow-up. The approach effectively addressed infection and the bone defect, despite the challenges posed by SCD and resource limitations. A multidisciplinary strategy combining orthopedic expertise, hematologic support, patient education, and ongoing rehabilitation is crucial for optimizing outcomes in such complex cases. Further studies are warranted to refine management protocols for septic nonunion in patients with hemoglobinopathies to enhance recovery and minimize complications.
