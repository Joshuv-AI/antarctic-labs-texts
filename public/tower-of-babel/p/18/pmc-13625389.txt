Electronic health records: managerial insights from an umbrella review
Authors: Nicola Belle, Alessandro Gazzetti, Paola Cantarelli, Veronica Cruciani, Stefano Gandolfi, Andrea Piazzoli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHealthcare systems are increasingly implementing tools that aid the decision-making process of professionals. There is a clear need to develop evidence-based managerial guidelines for implementing electronic health records. This umbrella review aims to identify a parsimonious yet comprehensive set of factors that enhance the efficiency and effectiveness of electronic health records in supporting professionals’ decision-making.MethodsWe analysed 199 literature reviews and meta-analyses to classify key factors influencing electronic health records’ performance into technological, human, and institutional domains. For each domain, we systematized implications and recommendations relevant to healthcare management.ResultsTechnological factors account for the largest share of existing research, with data management, artificial intelligence, and usability emerging as the most frequently studied areas, while barriers related to ICT infrastructure and software receive comparatively less attention. Human factors are also widely examined, particularly user familiarity, nudges, and system use, whereas collaboration, ethics, and enforced actions are less frequently addressed. Institutional factors primarily concern adoption processes and acceptance, followed by access and workflow optimization; privacy and guidelines are discussed to a lesser extent, while customization and evaluation remain relatively underexplored.ConclusionsHealthcare managers should prioritize cognitive usability, workflow integration, user familiarity, and alignment with organizational strategy when designing and implementing electronic health records. Their development should be guided by robust evidence and continuous, iterative refinement. Background

Electronic Health Records (EHRs) are playing an ever-growing role in the digitalization of the healthcare sector [1]. How EHRs are developed, designed and deployed is becoming increasingly relevant for steering this process and for the introduction of new technologies within healthcare settings [2, 3]. The primary role of EHRs, shared with their paper-based ancestors, is to assist healthcare professionals in collecting, storing and sharing information about patients and their clinical trajectories. However, the enhanced functionalities enabled by its electronic nature [4], combined with the constant presence throughout the care provision process, have progressively transformed EHRs into strategic components of healthcare professionals’ decision-making process. Seen in this light, the way in which EHRs are structured acquires a completely new relevance. Ceasing to be confined within the technological or informational realms, the EHR design and functionalities receive renewed attention from a behavioural point of view. Clinical workflows [5], decision-making processes [6] and, more generally, the quality of care itself [7, 8] can all be seen as directly influenced by the way in which EHRs are designed and structured. Emphasis has been further put on the impact that EHR design and use seem to exert on healthcare professionals’ well-being [9], work outside work [10], error rates [11], and interprofessional collaboration [12]. As instruments that mediate and impact on the way in which information is collected, accessed and presented, the EHR can influence key aspects that range from the timing of information availability to its visual representation.

Years of research in behavioural sciences have proven that the way in which information is presented is not neutral with respect to the judgements that derive from it [13, 14]. Contrary to what might be assumed, even high-level experts such as physicians make no exception and are equally prone to cognitive influences [14]. Furthermore, by mediating the amount of information reported and structuring its relative salience, EHRs influence which information professionals attend to. Indeed, EHRs can exert a substantial impact on clinical reasoning and decision-making processes by stirring the attention of healthcare professionals on some pieces of information instead of others and by affecting healthcare professionals’ information load – or overload - [15]. In the end, this can result in significant impacts on the professionals’ choices and behaviours [16].

Systematising the current evidence on how EHRs shape the decision-making processes of healthcare professionals thus becomes strategic to enable behaviourally informed designs and structuring. The present study aims to identify the EHR-related factors that influence healthcare professionals’ decision-making. The goal is to provide a comprehensive and parsimonious list of factors, along with their impacts, which need attention and action by healthcare managers, policymakers and EHR developers. To achieve this, an umbrella review has been conducted. Drawing on the factors identified through the umbrella review, a set of insights for the development of behaviourally informed EHR systems is gathered to further enhance the actionability of the findings. These insights are intended to actively support the design of digital tools that align with how healthcare professionals actually think, decide, and act - reducing cognitive burden, minimizing bias, and fostering safer, more effective care delivery. This is fully consistent with the vast literature calling for user-centered EHR design - taking in full account the needs of the users and working to streamline their experience [17]. Among the tools introduced by the digital transformation of the healthcare sector [18], EHRs stand out as one of the most pervasive and influential tools, reshaping how healthcare professionals work, communicate, and decide. Understanding the factors that shape decision-making in this evolving environment is essential to effectively harness the potential of digital tools and ensure enhancements, rather than constraints, of professional judgements. Against this backdrop, the central question guiding this study is: which EHR-related factors have been found to influence the decision-making processes of healthcare professionals, and in what ways? Answering this question carries both theoretical and practical value. On the one hand, it advances scientific understanding of the factors that make electronic health records efficient and effective in aiding professionals’ decision-making. On the other hand, this study aims to 1) inform the design and implementation of EHR systems that actively support healthcare professionals in their daily practice, and 2) provide an actionable evidence base for healthcare managers, policymakers, and developers.

Methods

Research question and design

We conducted an umbrella review of relevant reviews. The protocol has been pre-registered on PROSPERO (CRD420251000239). We included literature reviews and meta-analyses published between January 1, 2020 and March 7, 2025 that investigate factors that make EHRs efficient and effective in aiding professionals’ decision-making. This timeframe was selected to capture the most recent evidence and generate actionable insights grounded in current research, while reducing the risk of including findings that may have become obsolete given the rapid evolution of digital health technologies. Only published articles were considered. Searches were limited to studies published in English. This restriction was applied for reasons of access, feasibility, and the language competencies of the review team, and is consistent with common practice in systematic literature reviews. This choice is further supported by the fact that reviews and meta-analyses are commonly published in English. The search was conducted on March 7, 2025.

Search strategy

In accordance with the pre-registered protocol, the literature search was conducted across the Scopus, PubMed, and Web of Science databases. The search yielded a total of 1,173 records (search strings are provided in Appendix A). After removing duplicates, 568 unique records were left, and 18 studies were further excluded because their journals were not indexed in Scopus or they were not classified as articles in Scopus. To be indexed in Scopus, journals are first evaluated against technical criteria and subsequently reviewed based on editorial policy, content quality, scholarly standing, publishing regularity, and online availability, and are subject to ongoing re-evaluation. Given these standards, excluding records from non-indexed journals or non-article publications was considered a reasonable measure to maintain the quality of the selected studies. For the sake of full transparency and replicability, however, we acknowledge that this exclusion criterion was not specified in the pre-registration protocol. This decision was made during the screening stage of the review process.

Study selection and data extraction

550 records were thus left to be assessed for content eligibility. Two groups of investigators independently screened titles and abstracts to identify studies for full-text analysis. Studies were selected if they examined EHR-related factors with potential relevance to the decision-making processes of healthcare professionals. Articles flagged for inclusion by both groups were selected for full-text review. Articles flagged for inclusion by only one of the two groups were discussed with a third investigator and final decisions were made upon consensus. The final sample includes 199 studies (Fig. 1). The in-depth analysis of the 199 studies was conducted independently by the two groups, with discrepancies resolved through consensus and discussion with the third investigator. Appendix B contains the full list of the studies featured in the review, together with their IDs.

PRISMA flow chart

Information was extracted through a template specifically developed for this umbrella review. Each review was represented as a single row within a tabular dataset, managed manually using Microsoft Excel. The extraction template included general descriptive fields (e.g., review type, number of primary studies, registration status, adherence to reporting guidelines, and years covered) as well as a dynamic set of columns representing specific EHR related factors to be filled in. Factor identification followed an iterative, inductive process. The first review served to define an initial set of relevant EHR-related factors, each assigned a dedicated column. A factor was defined as any EHR-related characteristic, feature, or condition identified in the reviewed literature as having a discernible influence on decision-making processes. As subsequent reviews were analysed, any new factor not already captured was added as a new column, allowing the framework to emerge from the data rather than being imposed a priori. For each review, the direction of impact associated with each identified factor was recorded - classified as positive, negative, or null/mixed/inconclusive - based on the conclusions reported in the primary review or meta-analysis. Recommendations were similarly extracted directly from the included studies. Where a review formulated practice-oriented suggestions, these were recorded in the cell of the pertaining factor. All recommendations reported in the result section reflect those identified in the reviewed studies.

Data analysis and synthesis

Once data extraction was complete, factors were examined holistically to hierarchically organise them. This classification process was conducted through iterative discussion among the research team, with disagreements resolved by consensus. Where multiple reviews referred to the same underlying construct using different terminology, labels were harmonised through team discussion until consensus. Each factor was uniquely assigned to one of three overarching domains - technological, human, or institutional. These three overarching domains constitute Level 1 of the framework. The assignment of factors to domains was based on the primary locus of the construct: whether it pertained primarily to system features and infrastructure, to individual and interpersonal behaviour, or to organisational and governance conditions.

Within each domain, factors showing strong thematic similarities or logical connections were grouped into aggregated Level 2 categories. The others remained as standalone Level 2 factors. Level 3 factors represent the subcomponents of aggregated Level 2 categories. The resulting framework consists of three overarching domains (Level 1), a set of Level 2 factors - comprising both aggregated categories and standalone factors - and Level 3 factors representing the subcomponents of the aggregated Level 2 categories. The framework reflects a bottom-up classification grounded in the extracted evidence, refined through structured team deliberation.

Drawing on the evidence systematically collected and organised through this process, the authors developed a set of six actionable insights presented in the Discussion section. Unlike the factors and recommendations - which stem directly from the reviewed studies - these insights constitute a generative synthesis. They were formulated by the authors to translate the accumulated evidence into practically oriented guidance for the design and implementation of behaviourally informed EHR systems. Their development was guided by the patterns and priorities emerging from the data, with the goal of bridging the gap between research findings and managerial practice.

Results

Focusing on review types, the included studies present a diverse range of formats but show a highly concentrated distribution among two types that together account for almost 90% of the final sample. The most common type is the systematic review (111 studies; 55.8% - of these, three are also meta-analysis). Scoping reviews are also prominent (64 studies; 32.2%). Other review types among the included studies are integrative reviews (7), and narrative reviews (5). Additional types include meta-analyses (5), rapid (2), umbrella review (2), metanarrative (1), methodological (1), qualitative (1), non-systematic (1) and targeted review (1).

Concerning pre-registration, most reviews (177 out of the 199) show no evidence of having been pre-registered. For 22 reviews, instead, the authors claim to have pre-registered the study. Of these 22, 20 were registered in PROSPERO and the remaining 2 in Open Science Framework.

Focusing on the distribution of included studies over time, the number of studies included increases steadily from 26 in 2020 to 56 in 2024. As to the years 2021, 2022, and 2023, there has been a consistent growth, with included papers that, respectively, amount to 33, 36 and 37. The lower number of studies for 2025 - 11 - is fully explained by the search having been conducted in early March 2025. Overall, a growing scholarly interest and output in the field over recent years can be observed.

The number of included studies within each primary document analysed ranges from a minimum of 3 to a maximum of 401, with an average of about 42 studies per review. The modal value is 17 studies, which appears in 11 reviews. The median equals 25.

Full detail of impacts and recommendations for each factor is reported in Appendix C.

Technological factors are the most frequently addressed, being featured in 108 of the 199 studies (54.3%). Data emerges as the most studied area (61 studies; 56.5% of the domain). Interoperability and standardization - the most examined subfactor (36 studies) - are most consistently associated with positive effects on collaboration and integration among professionals, safety, and disease surveillance. However, the evidence also points to mixed effects on blockchain implementation, usability, workload, and workflow, as well as a potential negative effect on the ability to record patient-specific nuances when standardization becomes overly rigid. Data Type (13 studies) shows positive effects mainly on care quality, clinical decision support, and disease identification. Data Quality (12 studies) positively impacts, mainly, care quality, disease surveillance, and safety. Blockchain (9 studies) is primarily associated with positive effects on interoperability, data integrity, and traceability, although with mixed effects on privacy and security. Data Amount and Data Management receive more limited attention. For the former, evidence focuses mainly on AI performance and wellbeing, for the latter, on confidentiality and collaboration.

Artificial Intelligence is the second most present factor, discussed in 35 studies (32.4%). Machine Learning dominates (27 studies), with the strongest evidence on risk prediction, adverse event prediction, and screening and early detection, alongside concerns around practical clinical implementation and interpretability. Natural Language Processing (10 studies) shows positive effects mainly on data structuring and standardization, clinical utility of data, and clinical decision support. Automation remains marginal (2 papers).

Usability is addressed in 31 studies (28.7%). Information Entry (15 studies) generates a nuanced picture: structured entry improves mainly efficiency, data quality, and screenings, but introduces trade-offs with flexibility and usability. Generic usability features (13 studies) show positive effects mainly on well-being, safety, and satisfaction. Searchability (8 studies) is associated with positive impacts on usability, safety, and navigability, with mixed effects on cognitive load. Speed (4 studies) and Consolidated View (4 studies) show positive effects, including work performance and documentation, and on usability and efficiency respectively.

ICT Barriers (9 studies) and Software (5 studies) receive comparatively limited attention. ICT Barriers consistently show negative effects on system introduction and implementation, use, and usability; Software is associated with positive effects on care quality, safety, integration and time efficiency.

Human factors emerge as the second most prominent domain, appearing in 85 studies (42.7%).

Familiarity is the most studied factor in this domain (35 studies; 41.2%), encompassing training, repeated use, and long-term exposure. Its effects are near-uniformly positive, involving user experience and system usage, attitude towards EHRs, and patient safety.

Nudges are discussed in 34 studies (40.0%). Educative nudges (30 studies) – such as alerts, clinical decision support, predictive analytics - show broad positive impacts, mainly on care quality, vaccination and screenings, and timely interventions. Mixed evidence is found for patient safety, clinician burden and clinical effectiveness, and negative impacts are recorded on workflow integration. Architectural nudges - defaults, order sets, adaptive templates - show positive effects primarily on medication management, laboratory testing optimization, and documentation, with mixed effects on health outcomes and safety, though the evidence base (8 studies) remains smaller compared to educative nudges.

Use is addressed in 32 studies (37.6%) and generates the widest spread of mixed evidence within the identified factors. Positive effects are most consistently documented on accuracy, cost reduction, and safety, while mixed effects are most prominent around efficiency, well-being, and communication. Negative effects are most recurrently identified on face-to-face interaction, autonomy and human interpretation.

Collaboration (8 studies) covers two distinct areas: teamwork support design (7 studies), showing positive effects primarily on interprofessional collaboration, care quality, and safety, alongside mixed effects on communication and negative effects on both responsibility clarity and workaround reduction; and shared decision-making tools (1 study), with positive effects on clinical workflow efficiency. Ethics (2 studies) and Enforced Actions (1 study) receive limited attention, focusing respectively on confidentiality and consent, and on negative impacts on workaround reduction.

Institutional factors appear in 79 studies (39.7%).

Adoption Process and Acceptance is the factor addressed the most in this domain (31 studies; 39.2%). The evidence converges on the need to address technological, human, and organisational challenges simultaneously, with sustained investment in training, change management, and user engagement identified as critical enablers. Within this factor, four distinct areas are covered: Generic barriers and enablers to the adoption process (25 studies), which account for the bulk of the evidence; User engagement (8 studies), showing positive effects primarily on adoption and use and user support and system improvements, with involvement of users in design and implementation emerging as the key recommendation; Endorsement (4 studies) showing positive effects on implementation, standardization, well-being and use; and Trust (1 study), positively affecting system use.

Access is examined in 20 studies (25.3%). Patient accessibility (14 studies) shows a mixed profile: positive effects are most consistently documented on patient engagement and control, communication and interaction, and safety, while negative effects are most recurrently identified on burden, misinterpretation of data, privacy and confidentiality. Access procedures (8 studies) present another tension, with improvements in security and privacy and traceability offset by negative effects on usability and flexibility.

Optimized Workflow (15 studies) is most consistently associated with positive effects on efficiency, safety, and care quality. Privacy (15 studies) shows positive effects primarily on care quality, safety, and healthcare integration, with mixed effects on blockchain and implementation. Guidelines are discussed in 8 papers. Use guidelines (5 studies) show positive effects primarily on usability, care quality, and safety. Developer guidelines (4 studies) is found to positively affect mainly decision support, disease management and standardization.

Customization (6 studies) impacts positively primarily safety, usability, and care quality. For Evaluation (6 studies), a positive impact on appropriate treatments is found.

Discussion

Our umbrella review shows that EHRs have technological, human, and institutional dimensions that shape how decisions are made, how work is coordinated, how care is delivered, and the degree to which economic resources are used effectively. Following Mergel et al.’s [19] considerations on digitalization, EHRs should not be viewed as mere automation tools, but rather as evolving processes involving deeper transformations of clinical and organizational processes. Viewed through a behavioural science lens, EHRs appear to shape the clinical choice architecture, influencing how information is presented, prioritised, and acted upon in everyday decision-making processes. Understanding how these systems influence clinical and organizational decision-making is essential to foster better choices and improve outcomes in practice.

Given their potential to act as key enablers for the integration of Artificial Intelligence in healthcare delivery processes [4], gaining insight into how these systems shape human behaviour and organisational processes is ever more crucial to enable responsible and sustainable approaches [20]. Our umbrella review shows that Technological Factors account for the largest share of extant research, with Data, Artificial Intelligence, and Usability emerging as the most studied areas, while barriers related to ICT and Software result less frequently addressed. Human Factors also receive significant attention, with emphasis on Familiarity, Nudges, and Use, though Collaboration, Ethics, and Enforced Actions are discussed less often. Institutional Factors form another important category, focusing mainly on Adoption Process and Acceptance and Access, with Optimized Workflow, Privacy and Guidelines also noted, while Customization and Evaluation are comparatively less explored.

The present study provides a structured account of the EHR-related factors that shape the decision-making processes of healthcare professionals, systematically analysing the current evidence and developing a clear, structured list of factors. For each factor, the study presents a clear indication of what is the identified impact and its direction. Stemming from the results of the review, a set of six directly actionable insights has been conceived to support behaviourally informed EHRs. The overarching perspective that emerges is that designing behaviourally informed EHRs requires more than usability tweaks; it demands a deep integration of cognitive science, user-centred design, and strategic systems thinking. To make these implications practically useful, they are presented in a sequence that mirrors the EHR lifecycle: from aligning with strategic priorities and long-term governance to foundational design principles, to embedding in workflows, to building user capacity, and finally to optimizing decision-making.

Strategically align design with institutional priorities

EHRs are not merely technical systems - they are strategic assets. Their design should reflect institutional goals, values, and operational constraints, while being flexible enough to evolve with changing needs (cf. Table 4). Overall, the evidence positions interoperability as a central short-term priority, acting as a cross-cutting enabler that underpins multiple downstream benefits - most notably improvements in communication, care quality and the viability of data-intensive digital health technologies (cf. Table 2). EHRs, and their data, should be able to communicate across hospitals, ambulatory care, home care, and public health systems. Vuokko et al. [21] show that SNOMED CT can be integrated into EHR systems as a standardized and scientifically validated terminology for representing clinical information, with reported benefits for the consistent indexing, storing, retrieving, and aggregation of clinical data. Healthcare managers can translate this evidence into practice by requiring SNOMED CT encoding, or equivalent controlled clinical terminologies. This would help ensure that clinical data generated in one care setting remain interpretable and reusable in other settings and for other purposes, including care coordination, quality monitoring, disease surveillance, and research. Integration may be also achieved through flexible technologies such as Application Programming Interfaces, which enable different systems to exchange data efficiently [22], and Blockchain, which supports secure, decentralized data management [23]. Organizations should also reject the myth of interface neutrality. Every architectural decision - what data is shown, what defaults are set, what order options appear - affects behaviour and aligns (or misaligns) with institutional priorities (cf. Table 3). EHRs should be then recognized as active instruments of governance, not passive repositories. Technology defines what is possible, human factors determine what is usable and acceptable, and institutional structures shape what is permissible and sustainable. Only by attending to all three dimensions can EHR systems achieve long-term effectiveness [24]. To adapt over time, systems should be built with Kaizen principles - continuous, incremental improvement - by embedding user feedback loops [25] and iteratively refining workflows [26]. In a fast-changing technological environment, adaptability is key. Some long-standing dilemmas, such as standardization versus customization, may be overcome through smart system architecture and advanced technologies [27, 28]. Finally, Artificial Intelligence is the elephant in the room - already a challenge and opportunity for EHR evolution – as the wealth of studies focusing on it emphasizes (cf. Table 2). Its integration raises concerns about reliability, transparency, and ethical use, highlighting the need for careful governance and oversight [29, 30]. At the same time, following Topol [3], it offers unprecedented opportunities to advance EHRs by improving efficiency, enhancing accuracy, supporting clinical decision-making, and enabling more personalized care [31, 32].

Design for cognitive usability: reduce noise, bias, friction, and mental burden

Critically, there is no such thing as a neutral EHR design - every interface shapes choices and workflows, implicitly privileging certain decisions over others. Designing for cognitive usability is not just about comfort; it is a precondition for behavioural effectiveness, safety, and efficiency (cf. Table 3). This insight directly addresses the high volume of research found concerning Usability and its relevant impact on Safety [33] and Healthcare Professionals Well-being [34]. At the foundation of any effective EHR is a design that respects human cognition. Healthcare professionals work under time pressure and cognitive load, often juggling multiple patients and decisions. As suggested by Dixit et al. [35], a poorly designed interface can introduce noise, delay decisions, or even lead to harmful errors. EHRs should therefore be constructed to minimize friction, reduce decision noise (i.e., unwanted variability [14]), and mitigate bias (i.e., systematic errors [36]), while enhancing attentional focus. This begins with interface clarity - information should be presented hierarchically, using layouts that prioritize clinical relevance, suppress clutter, and minimize friction [35, 37]. Information Entry should be streamlined through structured inputs, automation, and minimal redundancy [38, 39]. Hill et al. [40] review EHR search functions and note that effective clinical use of EHR information is challenged by inadequate and inefficient access to patient information at the point of care. Accordingly, organizations can improve cognitive usability by redesigning Searchability functions within the patient record, reducing unnecessary navigation, and making clinically relevant information easier to retrieve during decision-making. Following Lee et al. [41] and Raban et al. [42], Alert systems should be calibrated to avoid fatigue and preserve salience. Raban et al. [42] demonstrate that non-interruptive interventions - such as making information more salient within existing screens - improved care delivery in 79% of cases. Managers might thus instruct clinical informatics teams to review alert volumes periodically, suppressing low-yield interruptive alerts and replacing them with passive visual cues where appropriate. As Nguyen et al. [43] point out, the reduction of Data Amount burden on Healthcare Professionals is also critical. Every screen, click, and form should serve a clinical or operational purpose, not just a technical one.

Embed EHRs into clinical workflows through co-design with different job roles

Once usability is established, EHRs should be embedded seamlessly into the real-world practices of healthcare delivery. As emerged from the studies falling under Optimized Workflow, systems should map directly onto care processes [44] and the distinct responsibilities of job families [45] - physicians, nurses, nursing assistants, allied healthcare professionals, administrators, and support staff - across settings such as hospitals, primary care, home care, and community health. Concretely, Blijleven et al. [44] present the revised Sociotechnical EHR Workaround Analysis (SEWA) framework as a tool to identify, analyse, and resolve EHR workarounds. Accordingly, organizations can make improvement more concrete by systematically collecting recurrent workarounds, classifying why they occur, and optimizing workflows to prevent unsafe or inefficient deviations from intended use. Embedding requires co-design. Developers should engage clinicians and operational staff in iterative design through interviews, usability tests, and experimentation before scaling up [25]. Busse et al. [46] identify think-aloud sessions, interviews, and prototypes as frequently used methods to involve healthcare professionals in EHR development. Accordingly, organizations can embed co-design directly into the development process by interviewing users about workflow needs, asking them to complete representative EHR tasks while thinking aloud, and testing prototypes before full-scale deployment. Simultaneously, partnership among users and developers should be fostered [47]. Following Benoit et al. [39] and Kariotis et al. [48], navigation should minimize screen switching, support multitasking, and remain consistent across modules. Aligning with Ghosh et al. [49], integrated tools - such as messaging, collaborative care plans, task lists, and sign-out aids - should be built into workflows to support team-based care and coordination [50]. Summing up, embedding EHRs in practice is not only a matter of convenience; it is essential to ensure that digital systems support rather than obstruct the delivery of high-quality, coordinated care.

Build familiarity through training and contextual fit

Even the best-designed systems might fail if users are not confident in using them [51]. Building comfort and competence takes sustained investment in training and contextual adaptation (ibid [52]). For example, following Nguyen et al. [52], who claim that EHR training programs should be role-specific, organizations should avoid generic one-size-fits-all EHR training and instead develop role-specific training pathways. Echoing Marsilio et al. [53], EHR education should begin early - embedded in the curricula of medical, nursing, and allied health training - to cultivate awareness of both technical functions and behavioural consequences [54]. In the workplace, training should be ongoing and collaborative [55], using simulations [56], peer-coaching [55], and scenario-based exercises [57]. EHRs should also allow for contextual tailoring: different specialties and care settings require distinct data views, terminologies, and workflows [58, 59]. Systems should offer enough flexibility for local adaptation without undermining broader standards. Striking a balance between Customization and consistency is crucial. As highlighted by Blijleven et al. [44], systems that fail to account for local realities are likely to encounter resistance or workarounds that compromise data quality and safety.

Apply behavioural science to inform the architecture underpinning decisions

With a usable and embedded system supported by trained users, EHRs can be optimized to guide decision-making through behavioural insights. Behavioural science does not replace clinical judgment; it supports it by structuring choices in ways that reduce errors, limit overload, and promote evidence-based actions. This insight stems from the body of research related to the human factor Nudges (cf. Table 3 [13]). Educative nudges - such as personalized prompts [60], predictive analytics [41], or targeted reminders [61] - can help healthcare experts make informed decisions without being intrusive [62]. Architectural nudges - smart defaults [42], adaptive templates [63], and pre-populated fields [38] - can streamline decision pathways, standardize care, and reduce unwarranted variation [64]. As a concrete example, Raban et al. [42] report EHR nudge interventions such as changing test quick-list options, removing low-value options from frequently ordered tests, displaying laboratory costs at ordering, and changing default selections. Accordingly, order menus and default settings might be audited to identify where the system currently makes low-value care easier than evidence-based care, then redesign the menu structure or defaults accordingly. Yet these tools seem to be only effective if backed by robust evidence [37, 65]. Nudges should be carefully timed, context-sensitive, and continuously evaluated [42, 66]. Poorly integrated interventions risk being ignored, misused, or resented.

Build on what works: use robust scientific evidence, don’t duplicate prior innovation efforts

A behaviourally informed EHR system should be built on a foundation of scientific rigor. This means embedding evaluation into every stage of the design and deployment process - not just testing features ad hoc, but conducting A/B tests, randomized controlled trials, and other robust methods to assess behavioural, clinical, and economic outcomes [26, 67, 68]. To manage this complexity, healthcare organizations and systems should create internal nudge units or evidence antennae - interdisciplinary teams responsible for reviewing new proposals, monitoring impacts, and ensuring alignment with best practices [2]. As Dellavigna and Linos [69] show, even well-tested nudges can fail if they do not match local workflows, institutional values, or staff capacity. Scaling requires not just replication, but contextual adaptation and internal ownership (cf. Table 4). Hence, not duplicating prior innovation efforts should be highly valued. For example, Miake-Lye et al. [70] suggest that evidence from previous EHR-to-EHR transitions can help healthcare managers and frontline clinicians anticipate transition-related challenges and avoid common pitfalls. Accordingly, EHR innovation and modernization should not be approached as isolated one-off projects, but should draw systematically on accumulated transition evidence to anticipate workflow disruption, implementation burden, user support needs, and post-go-live risks before scaling new systems or modules. In digital health, the strategic advantage seems to lie often in being adaptively late, not aggressively early. Innovation should focus on integration and user alignment - not novelty for its own sake [2].

Limitations

Our research synthesis is subject to limitations similar to those affecting studies of the same nature; therefore, the findings and the guidelines we propose should be interpreted in light of these constraints. In particular, several judgment calls were required when defining the search algorithm, identifying the key variables to be coded, and constructing the coding taxonomies. These choices were made to achieve a reasonable balance between relevance and feasibility. Similarly, judgment calls were made during the inclusion/exclusion process. As noted in the Methods section, 18 articles were excluded because the journals were not indexed in Scopus or because they were not classified as articles in the Scopus database. To address this potential concern, we also examined these 18 articles. Of these, 16 would have been excluded anyway (15 were out of scope and 1 was not a review paper). The remaining two articles could arguably have been included: one falling under the Technological-Data-Blockchain category and the other under the Human-Nudges-Architectural Nudges category. Including these two articles would have slightly increased the total number of reviewed studies, but would not have significantly altered the findings of this review. Another limitation shared with comparable reviews is the inability to report an exact number of disagreements among authors during the coding process. Because the review was conducted in multiple stages, disagreements were not systematically tracked as the process progressed from one stage to another. This issue may be particularly relevant for the classification of articles according to second- and third-level factors, where the breadth and complexity of the coding taxonomies make consistent categorization more challenging. Future research is needed to further examine and validate the components of our framework, which classifies key factors influencing electronic health record performance into technological, human, and institutional domains. Although we attempted to mitigate subjectivity by transparently disclosing our methodological choices and requiring full agreement among coders before advancing in the review process, these measures only partially address the inherent judgment involved in such decisions.

Conclusion

This work contributes to the field of research exploring how digital transformation is reshaping the healthcare sector [18], providing insights into a key infrastructure of the current and future of the healthcare sector and of the healthcare delivery process. The study offers a comprehensive synthesis of the EHR-related factors that shape the decision-making processes of healthcare professionals. Through an umbrella review, current scientific evidence on this topic has been systematically analysed, producing a clear and structured list of factors that influence healthcare professionals’ decision-making. For each factor, a clear indication of what was the identified impact and its direction was presented. A prevalence of technological factors over human and then institutional ones has emerged from the classification of factors into these three domains. This likely reflects the fact that, within the timeframe covered by this review, EHR research has focused more on system functionalities, data, usability, interoperability, and AI-related applications than on the organizational and behavioural conditions shaping their use. This imbalance could inspire future research that more deeply examines the human and institutional dimensions through which EHRs are adopted, embedded, and sustained in practice.

Stemming from the results of the umbrella review, a set of six directly actionable insights has been conceived to support behaviourally informed design of EHRs. As EHRs are used by professionals, within a system made of people and for people - the healthcare system - their design cannot overlook cognitive and behavioural evidence. By placing behavioural considerations at the centre of EHR design, we hope to contribute to a new generation of systems that not only meet technical standards but also actively support clinical reasoning and patient well-being. The insights retrace the entire EHR lifecycle. Accordingly, behavioural science should systematically guide choices, aligning them with institutional priorities. Developing behaviourally informed EHR systems requires designing for cognitive usability - minimizing noise, bias, and cognitive load - while ensuring seamless embedding into clinical workflows, also through participatory co-design. Sustained training and contextual adaptation are essential to build familiarity and trust. Finally, progress should rest on robust empirical evidence and iterative refinement, ensuring that innovation builds upon proven principles rather than duplicating prior efforts.
