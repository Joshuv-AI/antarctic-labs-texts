Successful Balloon Valvuloplasty for Paravalvular Leak 4 Months After TAVR in a Bicuspid Aortic Valve
Authors: Mohammed Mosaad, Saleh Algazlan, Khalid Alshamardl, Abdelmohsen Alsalman, Hafiz Omer, Awadelkarim Elneama, Mohammed Alghamedi, Abdulaziz Algathami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLate detection of paravalvular leak (PVL) after transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) presents unique treatment challenges, especially in bicuspid valves, likely because of valve geometry and the mechanism of percutaneous anchoring.Case SummaryA 70-year-old man underwent TAVR for bicuspid aortic stenosis using a balloon-expandable valve. Moderate to severe PVL was detected 4 months later. A multidisciplinary team evaluated the PVL etiology, patient anatomy, and available treatment options including valve-in-valve and plug closure. A partially fused commissural raphe causing focal underexpansion was identified as the origin of PVL, and late balloon valvuloplasty was performed successfully.DiscussionUnlike surgical-valve PVL, where percutaneous plug closure is the main treatment option, post-TAVR PVL offers additional approaches. Multimodality imaging helps define mechanism, feasibility, and risk.Take-Home MessagesLate balloon valvuloplasty is a safe option for underexpanded TAVR in bicuspid valves. Assessment of calcified raphe anatomy and the underexpansion site guides procedural risk and informs treatment selection.Key words: balloon valvuloplasty, bicuspid aortic valve, PVL, TAVR

History of Presentation

A 70-year-old man presented to the cardiology clinic with exertional symptoms consistent with NYHA functional class III despite adequate hemodialysis. He had been on hemodialysis for 10 years without prior history of such symptoms. On examination, vital signs were blood pressure 148/98 mm Hg, heart rate 82 beats/min, and oxygen saturation 98% on room air. Cardiovascular examination revealed a slow-rising pulse, muffled heart sounds, and a crescendo-decrescendo systolic ejection murmur best heard over the aortic area.

Past Medical History

The patient was known to have hypertension, dyslipidemia, end-stage renal disease on hemodialysis, ischemic heart disease, preserved left ventricular ejection fraction of 55%, and severe degenerative bicuspid aortic stenosis with a partially fused commissure between the left and noncoronary cusps (type I), together with mild to moderate aortic regurgitation.

Investigations

Coronary angiogram on August 29, 2024, showed mild mid–left anterior descending artery disease of 40%, with significant proximal left circumflex stenosis of 90%. Aortic cardiac computed tomography (CT) on October 9, 2024 showed favorable right femoral access. During structural heart clinic follow-up, repeat echocardiography showed significant aortic regurgitation despite the patient remaining asymptomatic. Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) on January 28, 2025, showed a well-seated valve with significant posterior paravalvular leak (PVL).

Management

The patient initially underwent percutaneous coronary intervention to the mid left circumflex on October 9, 2024, along with TAVR using a 26-mm balloon-expandable valve, and he was discharged on November 10, 2024. After repeat echocardiography showed significant aortic regurgitation, he was reviewed in the structural heart clinic. TEE on January 28, 2025, confirmed significant posterior PVL with a well-seated valve. Findings from transthoracic echocardiography (TTE), TEE, and cardiac CT were integrated into a multidisciplinary team discussion, which highlighted focal valve underexpansion secondary to a severely calcified raphe and the absence of aortic root calcification. Based on this multimodality imaging assessment, the team favored TEE-guided balloon valvuloplasty as the preferred strategy. The balloon valvuloplasty was performed on February 17, 2025, resulting in a very good procedural outcome and reduction of PVL to mild. The patient was discharged in stable condition on February 19, 2025.

Outcome and Follow-Up

Balloon valvuloplasty achieved marked improvement, reducing PVL to mild. The patient remained clinically stable and asymptomatic, with no complications during 6 months of clinic follow-up. Follow-up TTE showed stable mild PVL, with aortic valve peak/mean gradients of 29/15 mm Hg.

Discussion

What are the challenges of PVL detection after TAVR?

Assessment and quantification of PVL after TAVR remain a challenge.1 Aortic angiography performed immediately after valve deployment provides unidimensional images affected by operator and interobserver variability and cannot differentiate between paravalvular and transvalvular regurgitation.2,3 In contrast, TTE and TEE, including 3-dimensional echocardiography, allow quantitative assessment of aortic regurgitation and are better in follow-up assessment. The routine use of echocardiography post-TAVR might catch PVL earlier. Two-dimensional TTE has been shown to underestimate aortic regurgitation by at least 1 grade when compared with magnetic resonance imaging in patients who undergo TAVR.4 Usually, the shape of PVL is crescentic and is commonly affected by acoustic shadowing from the prosthesis and surrounding calcification.

What are the reasons for post-TAVR PVL? What is the incidence of post-TAVR PVL in bicuspid aortic valve?

There are 3 main mechanisms for post-TAVR PVL: 1) calcification of the annulus or native valve leaflets leading to incomplete apposition of the valve;5, 6, 7 2) undersizing of the prosthesis; and 3) suboptimal placement of the prosthesis and incomplete sealing of the annulus by the valve.7,8 The incidence of post-TAVR PVL is lower with balloon-expandable valves than with self-expanding valves. However, balloon-expandable valves have a higher risk of annulus rupture. The incidence of PVL in type 1 bicuspid valve reaches up to 4%.9

What is the role of post-TAVR cardiac CT?

Cardiac CT is used to detect underexpansion or deformation of the implanted valve. Excessive calcification of the raphe in type 1 bicuspid aortic valve has been associated with all-cause mortality.8,9 Cardiac CT has shown comparable diagnostic accuracy to TEE for the detection, localization, and assessment of severity of prosthetic PVL. Moreover, CT has been shown to be useful in detecting other findings such as leaflet thickening and thrombosis.10 Cardiac CT can help in procedure planning regarding device selection and fluoroscopic projection during treatment.

Subsequently, our patient underwent TEE, which showed a wide posterior paravalvular gap of 0.7 to 1.0 cm at the site of the native aortic valve partially fused commissure (Figure 1, Videos 1 and 2). The cardiac CT scan showed severe calcification of the ascending aorta; the implanted aortic valve (TAVR) was oval with nonuniform expansion toward the left coronary sinus of Valsalva given a severely calcified raphe between the left and noncoronary cusps. The dimensions of the aortic valve after implantation were 18.6 × 23.9 mm. These findings were discussed in a multidisciplinary meeting regarding treatment options.

What are the treatment options for post-TAVR PVL?

Using late balloon dilation for underexpanded valves has always been a controversial subject given the potential risks of injuries to the leaflet or root and the potential for promoting transvalvular regurgitation.11,12 Other options such as using a plug might be considered after exclusion of underexpansion and malposition, based on different experiences, because device-plug interaction with the valve frame can affect deliverability. On the other hand, malposition may require repeat transcatheter or surgical valve replacement.11,12

The multidisciplinary meeting consensus was for TEE-guided balloon valvuloplasty. Given the cardiac CT images, the underexpansion of the valve was caused by the calcified fused raphe between the left and noncoronary cusps. It prevented the full expansion of the valve, but the calcification did not reach the aortic root, nor was there aortic root calcification (Figure 2). These findings suggest that the potential risk for aortic rupture is low. The plan was to use a gradual upsizing of balloon dilatation to help prevent oversizing or injury to the root or leaflet. Krishnamoorthy et al13 reported that the double-inflation (“double-tap”) technique in the aortic valve is safe and improves valve expansion of balloon-expandable valves.

Subsequently, the patient was taken to the catheterization laboratory, where the aortic valve was dilated sequentially using a 22 × 40 mm noncompliant balloon and then a 23 × 40 mm balloon. TEE showed improved aortic valve gradients of 27/14 mm Hg, with PVL improvement from moderate to mild (Figure 3). Aortic CT showed a TAVR inflow inner diameter of 19.4 × 24.5 mm (compared with 18.6 × 23.9 mm before ballooning) and a waist inner diameter of 18 × 24.1 mm (compared with 17.5 × 24.1 mm before ballooning). Roughly, there was a 2% to 4% increase in dimensions, more pronounced at the inflow than at the waist. The eccentricity index was 0.23; effective orifice area was 1.45 cm2 before balloon valvuloplasty and 1.75 cm2 after (Figures 4A to 4D, Video 3). The patient remained clinically stable and asymptomatic, with no complications during 6 months of clinic follow-up; follow-up TTE showed stable mild paravalvular leak with aortic valve peak/mean gradients of 29/15 mm Hg.

What is the target point for successful PVL treatment?

Across datasets, the most consistent determinant of benefit is residual PVL severity: Procedural efficacy is mostly defined as achieving ≤mild residual regurgitation. The different tolerance to residual PVL depends on the clinical presentation of heart failure versus hemolysis. The guidance of surgical or transcatheter closure is dependent on patient long-term outcomes and phenotype of clinical presentation and anatomy-specific threshold.

Conclusions

Late balloon valvuloplasty can be first choice of the treatment of PVL with an underexpandable bioprosthetic aortic valve with a partially fused raphe in native valve. Minor changes of the inflow sizing have a significant impact on the severity of PVL.

Take-Home Message

•Balloon valvuloplasty is a safe option for underexpanded TAVR in bicuspid valve, even if performed late. Studying the calcified raphe anatomy and point of underexpansion on multimodality imaging can help assess the risk of the procedure.
