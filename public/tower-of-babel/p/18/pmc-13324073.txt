Toward development of soil-derived Bacillus isolates as lung cancer cytotoxic agents
Authors: Dhruba Deb, Filippo Liguori, Bentley M Shuster, Ruoqi Huang, Stefani Shoreibah, Steven Wang, Nathan Emmanuel Rojas Ocampo, Kyle P Murray, Tal Danino
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe wide-ranging impact of the human microbiome on health and disease has sparked growing interest in employing bacteria as live therapeutics. Natural properties of bacteria have been enhanced using synthetic biology to treat diverse diseases, from infections to inflammation and cancer. However, a major obstacle in this area is identifying specific bacterial hosts and molecular payloads that are both safe and effective for specific diseases or cancers. In this study, we explored environmental microbial diversity as a promising source of new therapeutic agents that could be engineered for bacterial drug delivery systems. We collected and characterized soil bacteria from 25 urban public parks, then evaluated their secreted metabolites for anti-cancer activity using both monolayer and three-dimensional spheroid models of lung cancer. Metagenomic analysis, toxicity profiling, and co-culture assays revealed that several Bacillus species isolated from Manhattan park soils produced compounds with strong, dose-dependent cytotoxic effects on lung cancer cells. Furthermore, we demonstrated that Bacillus subtilis—a well-characterized, gram-positive model organism—was capable of colonizing lung tumor spheroids, suggesting its potential as a safe and effective chassis for bacterial cancer therapy. Complementing these experiments, we developed a mechanistic ordinary differential equation (ODE) model of the bacteria–spheroid co-culture that is consistent with our bacterial and spheroid growth data. Overall, our findings highlight a discovery platform for the screening of environmental microbes as chassis or payload sources for microbial cancer therapies.Keywords: Bacterial synthetic biology, Environmental microbial diversity, Cancer therapy

Introduction

Microbes have been demonstrated to occupy diverse niches in the body, and widely impact human health, presenting a promising opportunity to supplant these niches with engineered microbes [1] to manipulate host physiology and disease [2,3]. One particular area of interest in this field is the use of live bacteria to treat lung diseases. This approach is motivated by the presence of the natural microbiome in lung tissue, the advantageous delivery route through inhalation, and the potential to engineer microbes using synthetic biology. Several recent efforts have demonstrated these properties of live bacterial therapeutics. In one example [4], intranasal delivery of a probiotic Lactobacillus johnsonii strain significantly mitigated lung injury in neonatal mice exposed to high oxygen, supporting the potential of systemically-delivered bacteria to treat diseases of the respiratory system. In another notable example, an attenuated strain of Mycoplasma pneumoniae was engineered genetically to produce three enzymes that dissolve biofilms formed upon infection in patients with ventilator-associated pneumonia correlated with Pseudomonas aeruginosa [5]. In one last example, S. typhimurium was genetically-engineered to express 10 different bacterial toxins heterotopically and inducibly, showing strong cytotoxic activity against six patient-derived non-small cell lung cancer cell lines, in spheroid in vitro models and in vivo [6].

While the development of bacterial therapies is advancing, a major discovery challenge is to determine specific host strains and therapeutic compounds effective for a particular disease [7,8]. For example, innate properties such as adhesion to specific niches in the host, immunogenicity, and tissue tropism differ across bacterial species and represent an enormous search space for identifying promising therapeutic candidates [9]. Over a century ago, William Coley noted one such success, in the use of injections of gram-positive Streptococcus pyogenes, along with gram-negative Serratia marcescens species, to cause tumor regressions in sarcomas, but did not demonstrate efficacy in carcinomas [9]. More recently, with the advent of genetic engineering and synthetic biology, most of the work in this field has utilized gram negative bacteria strains that are easy to modify, such as E. coli and S. typhimurium, to design therapeutic candidates with synthetically engineered properties [10,11]. However, also gram positive species such as Bacillus spp. present several advantages as therapetutic candidates: many species are generally recognized as safe (GRAS) [12], they have the ability to natively produce high levels of proteins and secrete them, and can stably integrate DNA into the genome [[13], [14], [15]]. Several recent studies have developed synthetic biology toolkits for gram positive model systems such as B. subtilis [9,10]. A main challenge related to the use of Bacillus spp. as engineered therapeutics is that they have been historically regarded as obligate aerobes, and therefore it was thought that they would not thrive in the hypoxic core of solid tumors. Additionally, their potential toxins have not been explored in cancer applications.

Soil is a rich source of gram positive microbes with dominant genera including Bacillus, Streptomyces, and Clostridium, that have developed a diverse arsenal of bioactive molecules (e.g., antibiotics such as streptomycin, vancomycin, and bacitracin; toxins such as botulinum toxin and cereulide; and enzymes such as proteases, cellulases, and chitinases) over long evolutionary timescales [16]. In this study, we explored bacterial isolates from 25 parks in New York City to assess the therapeutic potential of the secreted compounds from the identified bacteria.

We identified two toxic strains (Bacillus wiedmannii Bag5X-2 and Bacillus amyloliquefaciens plantarum) whose lung cancer cytotoxicity is mediated by high-molecular weight compounds (>100 kDa), and two additional toxic strains (Bacillus thuringiensis YBT-1518 and Bacillus cereus Hub2-9/toyonensis) whose cytotoxicity is predominantly mediated by compounds with low molecular weight (<3 kDa). We also developed a protocol and demonstrated that the model gram positive organism B. subtilis is able to grow in a 3D culture model that mimics tumor microenvironments. These studies set the stage for advancing research efforts in identifying and engineering novel gram positive organisms to express therapeutic compounds that can be used as live bacterial therapies.

Methods

Collecting natural isolates

Soil was collected from 25 public parks across the island of Manhattan, New York City. As the landscape of the parks is heterogeneous, ranging from large lawns to patches of grass near large trees, playgrounds and other management regimes, in each park, soil was collected from four locations, 6 cm deep from under the ground, and at least 10 m away from large trees. Those four collections were bulked to equal one sample per park. The samples were maintained in a −20 °C freezer for long term storage.

To isolate dominant forms of bacteria from soil samples, they were serially diluted in sterile water and plated over LB agar petri dishes. After adequate incubation at 37 °C, plates were imaged. Colonies that grew visually separated from other colonies on the plate were picked and streaked on fresh LB agar petri dishes. Finally, single colonies were grown in LB media at 37 °C overnight and were suspended in 80% glycerol in 1:3 ratio to be stored in a −20 °C freezer.

Sequencing natural isolates

For 16s rRNA sequencing, bacteria colonies grown on LB agar plates were shipped to GeneWiz (Agenta Life Sciences, USA). The sequence files received from GeneWiz were used as input in nucleotide BLAST (NCBI NLM) and based on the maximum sequence similarity and highest confidence interval, the samples were identified.

For Next gen Sequencing, paired-end reads were processed using Unicycler with default parameters to generate an assembly of long contigs. The top ten similar contigs were used to determine the species. The sequences of contigs were then blasted using the Whole Genome Shotgun Submission and High-throughput Genomic Sequences NCBI databases. Genome annotation was performed using Prokka.

Collecting supernatant

We streaked natural isolates of Bacillus for single colonies onto LB Lennox agar plates and grew overnight at 37 °C. The next day, we inoculated an isolated colony into 4 mL of LB media and incubated it in a rotator drum overnight. Overnight cultures are centrifuged at 3000 RPM for 10 min at room temperature and the supernatant is filtered with a 0.22 μM filter attachment in a 10 mL syringe. We use fresh Bacteria-free supernatants for viability assays or flash freeze the supernatants in −80 °C for future use. For heat-inactivation experiments, the supernatant is heated at 56 °C for 1 h, before being exposed to cancer cell monolayers, or stored for future use. 15 μg/mL of gentamicin may also be supplemented depending on the downstream application of supernatant to ensure no viable bacteria remain in the sample.

MTT – In vitro toxicology assay

We cultured cell lines in RPMI with 10% FBS (R10) and split into treated flat bottom 96-well plates at a density of 2000 cells per well. Each 96 well plate is incubated at 37 °C for 3 days. After incubation, the cells are supplemented with fresh R10 and treated with either 50 μL of natural isolate secretion, 50 μL of LB media as a negative control, or 5 μL of diluted theta toxin as the positive control. Treated monolayers are returned to 37 °C for 24 h. We use the TX-kit according to the manufacturer's protocol and measure viability by plate reader. All readings are normalized to background signal and each sample is collected in triplicate to calculate the standard error between samples.

3D cell-titer Glo

We cultured cell lines in R10 and split into treated round bottom plates at a density of 5000 cells per well. The plates are centrifuged for 10 min at 500 G to aid in creating in vitro tumor spheroids. Each 96 well plate is incubated at 37° for 3 days, after which 50 μL of media is removed, fresh R10 is added to feed the cell, and the plates are returned to the incubator for another 3 days. After incubation, we feed the cells fresh R10 with 15 μg/mL of gentamicin and either 50 μL of natural isolate secretion, 50 μL of LB media as a negative control, or 5 μL of diluted theta toxin as a positive control. We incubate the cells for another 4 days and proceed with the Promega Cell-titer Glo protocol for spheroid viability, measuring the luminescence by plate-reader. All readings are normalized to background signal and each sample is collected in triplicate to calculate standard error between samples.

Results

Bacillus spp. Isolates from Manhattan parks produce bioactive molecules with dose-dependent toxicity against lung cancer models

Previous studies reported that the soil from the public parks of Manhattan, New York, is highly enriched with a variety of bacterial species [17]. To further profile the abundance of different bacterial lineages, we isolated 17 colonies from 30 soil samples, enriching for fast-growing bacteria that could form colonies overnight at human body temperature, 37 °C. 16S rRNA sequencing identified over 80% of these isolates as members of the Bacillus genus. We named the identified strains referencing the public park and culture sample they were isolated from (e.g. P1C1: park 1 culture 1).

Next, we built a pipeline to assess each strain's ability to produce cytotoxic bioactivity against human lung cancer cells (Fig. 1A). First, we screened the secretion or the spent media of 17 bacteria cultures grown overnight, across 5 diverse lung cancer cell lines grown in monolayers. We found that all 17 bacterial supernatants killed lung cancer cells in a dose-dependent manner, with IC50 values ranging from 0.1 to 43 percent volume of bacteria secretion added to the cancer cell culture media (Fig. 1B). For 4 of the toxic candidates, we carried out whole genome sequencing to identify the strains. The MegaBlast hit results confirmed that all of the isolated strains belonged to the Bacillus genus, but the species- and subspecies-level classifications varied (Fig. 1C). To assess the robustness of toxicity from the selected strains, we tested their effect on human lymphoma and mouse colon cancer cells. We found that the spent media from our isolated Bacillus spp. were toxic to these other types of cancer cells as well (Fig. 1D). Finally, from the already mentioned lung cancer cell lines we developed spheroids, which are known as the most physiologically relevant in vitro models of cancer [13]. The secretions of the identified bacteria eliminated lung cancer spheroids in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 1E). In summary, we identified 4 strains of soil Bacillus with dose-dependent toxicity in in vitro cancer models. The growth curves of all strains are shown in Fig. S1.

Amidst strain-related variability, the toxicity of all Bacillus spp. to lung cancer is higher in the larger-sized fractions of the bacteria

In order to understand any overarching patterns of bacterial toxicity in our isolated strains, we first fractionated by molecular size the supernatants of 4 bacterial strains we identified. For both strains P1C8 (Bacillus wiedmannii) and P21 (Bacillus amyloliquefaciens plantarum), the larger fractions (>100 kDa, 50-100 kDa) were consistently more toxic than the smaller molecular weight fractions (3-10 kDa, <3 kDa), suggesting the likelihood of protein-mediated toxicity. On the contrary, in P2 (Bacillus thuringiensis) and P14 (Bacillus cereus Hub2-9/toyonensis), the smallest molecular weight fraction (<3 kDa) was more toxic than other fractions, suggesting a small molecule-based toxic mechanism (Fig. 2).

Given the known properties of these species, we can hypothesize the identity of some cytotoxic compounds. The activity of B. wiedmannii, the most potent we identified, is likely due to proteinaceous toxins such as hemolysin BL (HBL), which was shown to have cytotoxicity against HeLa cells in vitro [18] and falls within the observed molecular weight range [18]. Exposing H460 monolayers to heat-inactivated B. wiedmannii supernatant (see Methods) confirmed the thermolability of the toxic compounds (Fig. S2), consistently with reported properties of HBL [19]. The cytotoxicity from B. thuringiensis YBT-1518 is likely mediated by a low molecular weight β-exotoxin (thuringiensin), a thermostable secondary metabolite in the 700Da range, which has insecticidal activity and might exhibit also cancer cell cytotoxicity [20,21]. Heat-inactivation experiments confirmed the thermostability of the toxic compounds (Fig. S2) in the supernatant of this strain.

While we have not identified the toxic compounds responsible for the loss of viability in spheroid models, we demonstrated that diverse species in the Bacillus genus exhibit potent lung cancer killing mediated by different classes of compounds. Given the aforementioned advantages of this genus for therapeutic purposes, our results suggest that further efforts in the characterization and engineering of Bacillus strains can lead to the development of more effective microbial therapeutics for lung tumors.

Bacillus subtilis can localize at the central cores of the lung cancer spheroids

Previous studies established the benefits of employing bacteria as a delivery system for toxins or drugs in the human body [22]. Specifically, when localized inside tumors in vivo or spheroids in vitro, certain types of bacteria can deliver payloads and eliminate interior tumor cells that may be impenetrable by traditional systemic therapies [[23], [24], [25]]. As our natural isolates were toxic to lung cancer cells, they did not allow formation of mature spheroids with hypoxic cores colonized by bacteria. Thus, we optimized a protocol to allow GFP-expressing Bacillus subtilis, a non-toxic strain, to colonize spheroids developed from a lung cancer cell line, H460 (Fig. 3A). As we previously demonstrated with coculture of spheroids and Salmonella, we observed that using a poorly diffusing antibiotic like gentamicin in the co-culture media promotes Bacilli to move inside the spheroids (Fig. 3B). We noted a decrease in the GFP signal each time we replaced the culture media, as expected, followed by replenishment of the GFP signal after 2-3 days, indicating that bacteria are growing and expressing their genes within the spheroids. During the days of co-culture, the spheroid sizes increased consistently (Fig. 3C). After 4 days co-culture with spheroids, we detected ∼400 colony-forming units of Bacillus subtilis in each spheroid of ∼500 μM size (Fig. 3D). In summary, we demonstrated that the nontoxic species Bacillus subtilis can home in the central core of 3D lung cancer spheroid models, and employed a mathematical model to recapitulate its growth dynamics inside the spheroid. Our results suggest the opportunity of using Bacillus subtilis as a host species to heterotopically express the potent cancer cell cytotoxic compounds produced by the new isolates we identified in this paper.

The model suggests that further numerical characterization of the potency of novel Bacillus-derived toxic compounds can foster the development of rationally-designed Bacillus strains with predictable time dynamics in the reduction of lung cancer spheroid growth and therefore therapeutic efficacy.

Discussion

Lung cancer causes the highest number of cancer-related deaths worldwide [26]. There are approximately 700 therapies available in various stages of clinical trials to treat lung cancer [27]. However, the biology of lung cancer and the intratumor heterogeneity leave several advanced-stage patients with ineffective treatment options [28,29]. Thus, new therapeutic modalities are always in demand. Here, we propose natural compounds from soil bacteria in the Bacillus genus as a novel treatment option for lung cancer.

New York City presents a highly diverse microbiome. Previous studies highlighted this diversity in soil microbes of Central Park [16,30] and New York soil, fungal signature in damaged buildings [17], virus and bacteria carried by urban rats [31,32], viruses in the waste water [33], and microbes in the air of public transit systems [31]. In our study, we found that the dominant form of soil bacteria are Bacilli. In fact, sequencing results for natural isolates from 25 parks in diverse neighborhoods of Manhattan identified Bacillus, a Gram-positive bacteria, as the most common genus. Gram-positive bacteria are defined by the construction of their cell wall, composed of a plasma membrane, the periplasmic space, and a thick peptidoglycan layer. Secretion efficiency is much higher in this system due to the lack of a secondary outer membrane where translated proteins are subject to accumulation in the cytoplasm, targeting for degradation, or encountering disruptive endogenous proteins [34].

Here, we found that the secretion from these Bacilli showed dose-dependent cytotoxicity to a diverse array of lung cancer models. Our findings reveal two distinct classes of toxic strains based on the size of their putative active components: Bacillus wiedmannii Bag5X-2 and Bacillus amyloliquefaciens plantarum exhibit cytotoxicity mediated by high-molecular weight compounds (>100 kDa), whereas Bacillus thuringiensis YBT-1518 and Bacillus cereus Hub2-9/toyonensis predominantly employ low-molecular weight molecules (<3 kDa).

We observed that these four strains did not colonize the spheroids in our in vitro model, given their high toxicity that prevented co-culture with the mammalian cells. Our findings suggest that the key translational barriers posed by the safety concerns associated with pathogenic Bacillus strains may be addressed with a synthetic biology approach. A wide range of attenuation strategies have been reported in the literature to increase biosafety of gram-positive bacteria like Clostridium [35] or Listeria [36]: the most common approaches include deletions of enzymes critical for the production of primary toxic metabolites, and induction of auxotrophy, generally restricting the bacteria's nutrient requirements to increase tumor selectivity. For the case of gram-negative bacteria, a notable example is Salmonella typhimurium, a pathogenic bacteria rendered safe through strategies that include alteration of lipopolysaccharide (msbB gene) and purine auxotrophy (purI gene) that reached several clinical trials [37]. Another common synthetic biology approach to address biosafety is employing probiotic bacteria as chassis for the heterologous expression of toxic compounds: one notable example of gram-negative chassis is the prescription probiotic E. coli Nissle 1917 [38], which demonstrated selective colonization of tumor masses with size as little as 1 mm3 and decoupling of cancer cell cytotoxicity from systemic toxicity in a therapeutic context. Decoupling can be obtained by engineering expression of potent toxins under native promoters that are selectively activated in tumors [39], hypoxia or lactate-sensing promoters [11,40]. The ability of microbes to colonize tumor niches in vivo while being cleared in the healthy tissue upon local or systemic delivery was also exploited to build genetic circuits like the quorum-sensing based Synchronized Lysis Circuit (SLC) [41], which was employed to decouple tumor cytotoxicity from systemic toxicity in toxin-expressing Escherichia and Salmonella [42]. In analogy to the successful case of EcN, the strategy we propose to address safety concerns related to the administration of pathogenic Bacillus strains is heterologous expression of Bacillus-derived toxic compounds into probiotic gram-positive chassis. Many gram-positive species are certified as GRAS by the Food and Drug Administration and feature in the European Food Safety Authority Qualified Presumption of Safety (QPS) [43]. Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium strains have a significant precedent as bacterial therapeutics for cancer that have progressed to Phase I and II clinical trials [44]. B. subtilis var. Natto has been used for commercial food production in East Asia for more than a century [45], and probiotic genetically engineered B. subtilis strains for the delivery of enzymes levansucrase [46] or acetaldehyde dehydrogenase [47] are safe and commercially available.

We demonstrated that Bacillus subtilis is able to colonize the hypoxic core of tumor spheroids, which provide a fast and effective platform for in vivo tumors and they retain biological properties such as hypoxia and low pH [48]. Previous studies have shown the utility of spheroids in high-throughput screening of microbial therapeutics for cancer [42,49,50]: such systems enable stable co-culture of cancer and bacteria populations, and recapitulation of native tumor environments in the geometry and tissue stoichiometry of cancer cells, creating a physiological distribution of nutrients, metabolites, pH and oxygen. A protocol including repeated wash steps and addition of the poorly diffusible antibiotic gentamicin to the spheroids before inoculation of bacteria addresses the limitation caused by bacterial overgrowth in the surrounding media, increasing the physiological relevance of the in vitro model and facilitating downstream assays. While spheroid colonization alone does not establish tumor specificity or in vivo relevance, e.g. due to the lack of healthy tissue control and cell type heterogeneity found in the solid tumor microenvironment, it provides a promising platform to assess behavior and responses of both bacteria and cancer cells in conditions similar to the in vivo context. Our findings provide an approach for future in vivo experiments to assess spatial colonization and safety profiles of B. subtilis in mouse tumor models. Demonstrating colonization of lung cancer spheroids for B. subtilis is a proof-of-concept demonstration for spatial localization and supports an approach of employing it as a probiotic chassis for the heterologous expression of Bacillus-derived toxic compounds in tumors. Indeed, while B. subtilis was historically regarded as an obligate aerobe, research since the late 1990s has confirmed it can survive and grow in environments with little to no oxygen [51], aligned with our findings of growth in spheroids.

Given the inability of the toxic strains we identified to colonize tumor spheroids, we developed an exploratory mathematical model to recapitulate the experimentally-observed dynamics of probiotic B. subtilis growth inside lung cancer spheroids, and evaluate the potential for cancer therapy of using it as a chassis for the heterologous expression of toxic Bacillus compounds. The parameters of the model were fitted to the experimental data of bacterial and lung-cancer spheroid growth upon B. subtilis colonization. The heterologous expression of toxic compounds is assumed to be spatially homogeneous in the spheroid core. Our findings suggest a future potential for engineering the cytotoxic compounds of soil Bacilli into a model B. subtilis organism, alleviating the toxic burden and controlling the compound production though synthetic biology and genetic circuits. The model predictions will require building and characterization of the B. subtilis engineered strains to be validated. Nevertheless, this approach provides a conceptual framework for advancing bacteria-based cancer treatments by leveraging novel Bacillus-derived cytotoxic compounds identified via microbial bioprospecting.
