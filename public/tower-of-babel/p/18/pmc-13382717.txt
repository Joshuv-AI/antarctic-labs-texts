Protocol for establishing rectal tumor organoid-derived monolayers in a microphysiological system to model radiotherapy
Authors: Eloïse Bouges, Charlotte Segers, Marco Lezzerini, Natalie Leys, Sarah Lebeer, Jianbo Zhang, Felice Mastroleo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryPatient-derived organoids offer greater human translational relevance than mouse models or immortalized cell lines, owing to their multicellular composition and patient-specific responses. Here, we present a protocol for establishing a human in vitro rectal tumor model for radiotherapy studies using patient-derived tumor organoids (PDTOs) and monolayer derivatives integrated into a microphysiological system (MPS). We describe steps for cultivating PDTOs, their transition into monolayers, and transfer into an MPS. This protocol enables radiotherapy modelling, toxicity testing, and adjuvant therapies screening.

Before you begin

As the third most prevalent cancer worldwide, colorectal cancer is commonly treated with radiotherapy as part of standard clinical protocols. However, conventional preclinical models of the gastrointestinal tract, such as mice and 2D immortalized cell lines, do not adequately represent human-specific epithelial damage and radiotoxicity responses. This protocol describes the establishment of a human in vitro rectal tumor model for radiotherapy studies using patient-derived tumor organoids (PDTOs) and monolayer derivatives integrated into a microphysiological system (MPS).

Before starting, the culture media and aliquots should be prepared as described below. The sterile work should be carried out in a Class II Biosafety Cabinet (BSC2). The MPS consists of PhysioMimix® Barrier plates (CN Bio) and a PhysioMimix® Core Microphysiological System (CN Bio).An Xstrahl 320 kV tube was used for the irradiation of the samples.

General handling guidelines

To maintain the integrity and density of the PDTO culture, the following handling practices are recommended:1.Gentle pipetting: Always pipette along the wall of the tube to minimize disturbance of the PDTO pellet when removing supernatant.2.Optimized centrifugation: Always use a swing-bucket rotor during centrifugation steps to ensure that PDTOs are collected efficiently at the bottom of the tube, rather than being distributed along the tube walls where recovery is more difficult.3.Minimize sample loss due to adhesion: Use Protein LoBind tubes and 0.1% OncoPro BSA solution (mentioned in the preparation) pre-coated pipette tips to prevent PDTO from adhering to plastic surfaces, which can result in significant sample loss and reduced recovery. A coated pipette tip refers to a pipette tip coated with 0.1% OncoPro BSA unless otherwise stated.4.Preparation of pipette tips for Matrigel® handling: Prior to pipetting PDTO in Matrigel solution, coat pipette tips with Matrigel® by aspirating and dispensing the solution back into the aliquot. This reduces PDTO adhesion to the tip. Additionally, set the micropipette to a lower volume when resuspending pellets in Matrigel® to avoid creating bubbles in the suspension while dissolving the pellet.5.Temperature control for Matrigel® handling: Use a pre-warmed culture plate to facilitate proper drop formation of Matrigel®, ensuring consistent embedding conditions.

Innovation

This protocol enhances current preclinical radiobiology models by incorporating fractionated irradiations of rectal PDTOs in a MPS. Existing in vitro models typically rely on immortalized cell lines or static organoid cultures exposed to a single dose of radiation, which fail to represent human rectal tumor physiology and do not reflect clinically relevant fractionated radiotherapy treatment conditions. PDTOs preserve the intrinsic cellular heterogeneity which represents rectal physiology and allows disease modeling, as previous work showed that rectal cancer organoids can represent patient specific response to chemoradiation.1 Deriving PDTOs into monolayers on Transwell® inserts provides experimental access to both the apical and basolateral sides. This configuration enables the implementation of the model into a MPS with continuously perfused wells, thereby modelling the flow passing the cells at the interface of the rectal epithelial tissue and the lumen with basolateral monolayers, or at the lamina propria with apical monolayers.

Expected outcomes

This protocol was developed to establish a monolayer model of PDTOs. Once the culture reaches sufficiently high cell density, monolayers can be seeded. After seeding, brightfield microscopy images show that confluence is typically reached within approximately 10 days. This can vary by a few days, with the objective of having fully confluent monolayers before the start of the irradiation (day 14). TEER measurements further assess the confluence status, as the values progressively increase until reaching a plateau indicative of full confluence. These values are PDTO-specific, may vary between samples and also depend on the initial seeding density, indicating that cell density influences barrier properties (Figure 13A). A decrease in TEER after the plateau occurs due to monolayer differentiation and tight junction maturation,3 reaching TEER values ranging from 1500 to 2500 Ω. cm2. When working with 1.12 cm2 Transwell® inserts, each insert could be used as one biological replicate for proteomics, while pooling three 0.33 cm2 Transwell® inserts was necessary to reach sufficient starting material for proteomic analysis (Figure 13B). Fractionated irradiation at 4 and 6 Gy resulted in significant proteomic changes, with 1,726 proteins identified and clustered into pathways related to programmed cell death, cellular stress response, and mitotic cell cycle regulation (Figure 13C). Upregulated proteins were primarily associated with stress adaptation and apoptosis, while downregulated proteins were linked to cell cycle progression. In this work the objective was to assess the dose-response of a PDTO line to increasing fractionated irradiation, related to rectal cancer radiotherapy procedure. While the selected doses and administration regimen were chosen accordingly, these parameters can be tailored to address other research questions. Besides, twelve monolayers that had not reached full confluence during our experiment were pelleted 18 days after seeding, and total RNA was subsequently extracted using the RNeasy Mini kit (Qiagen). Pooling three 0.33 cm2 Transwell® inserts delivered a total of 8.4 ± 2.4 μg (n = 4) of high quality RNA (RIN values above 9). Therefore, even one 0.33 cm2 Transwell® insert would be sufficient for e.g., RNA-seq transcriptomics analysis.

Working with 0.4 μm porous membrane Transwells® inserts, histological analysis showed that the flow applied to the apical side of the monolayers for five days induced a trend of increased thickness compared to the static model monolayers (Figure 13E). Whereas flow applied to the basolateral side of the monolayers had minimal effect compared to the static monolayers (Figure 13H). Stratification was more pronounced in both apical and basolateral MPS cultures (Figures 13F and 13I), confirming that dynamic conditions could promote tissue-like architecture.8,9,10

Limitations

Due to the rapid renewal of the human rectal epithelium, working with rectal PDTOs samples does not allow the maintenance of a fully confluent monolayer for more than 5 to 7 days without impacting human relevance. This limits the ability to study long-term responses to treatment. Although similar workflows were applied for both apical and basolateral monolayer seeding to enable direct comparison between the two configurations, the basolateral seeding approach introduces inherent limitations. In particular, the inversion of the Transwell® inserts exposes the seeded fragments to gravitational forces. This can result in partial loss of material and reduce the initial seeding density. Consequently, the establishment of a confluent monolayer is delayed, as reflected by a lag of several days in reaching TEER values plateau. This delay indicates a slower formation of a fully mature and tight epithelial barrier compared to apical seeding conditions. Moreover, the current model lacks key features of the gut microenvironment, including the characteristic oxygen gradient, and immune system components. The integration of potential radioprotective biotic agents and immune cells remains to be implemented.

This protocol describes methods for the establishment of efficient subculture of colorectal PDTO lines and monolayer seeding. The handling guidelines and troubleshooting steps provide tools to facilitate reproducibility. However, inter batch and patient to patient variability can occur, particularly at the level of TEER values and the time needed to reach confluence.

Potential solution

To support the growth of viable PDTOs, prioritize high cell density in the Matrigel® -PDTO drops. If you notice low cell density when seeding the drops after splitting/thawing, use a smaller volume of Matrigel® (use 40 μL instead of 120 μL and seed one test drop of 10 μL and one drop of 30 μL) at the next splitting after using the TrypLE (step 40) instead of increasing the volume. Refer to Figures 14A and 14C for a visual cue of lower density.

Inaccurate cell counting and low cell density when seeding the monolayers leading to monolayers not reaching confluence (Figure 15) (related to step 81).Figure 15Rectal PDTO monolayer formation(A) When the seeding density is too low, the PDTOs do not reach confluence to form a mature monolayer. Potential solution

To seed more precisely and reach the right cell concentration, use a small volume of medium (1 mL) to resuspend the pellet after the TrypLE fragmentation (step 81) to make the cell suspension before counting. Pipette well before taking the Trypan blue/cell suspension mix. This can help diminish pipetting error. Moreover, make sure that the counted cell concentration is within the range of the cell counter (104–107 cell counted per mL).

If the counting is out of range, add medium to the cell suspension or centrifuge at 300 × g for 5 min the tube containing the cell suspension, remove the supernatant and resuspend the pellet in a smaller volume of medium to prepare another Trypan blue/cell suspension mix.

Potential solution

Monolayers with a homogeneous distribution of fragments at the time of seeding tend to reach confluence more reliably than those in which fragments are concentrated at the center of the Transwell® inserts. To prevent uneven distribution, minimize movement of the Transwell® inserts right after the seeding and allow the fragments to adhere to the membrane undisturbed for 20 min (steps 86 and 98).

Problem 4

Basolateral seeded monolayers that take longer to reach confluence despite proper seeding and homogenous fragment distribution (related to step 87).

Potential solution

When basolateral monolayers are seeded they are exposed to gravity once the Transwell® inserts are inverted back into the wells after the 3 h of attachment. This can lead to loss of fragments, that detach from the insert’s membrane and fall into the wells. To mitigate the impact of this detachment, the cell suspension used to seed can be further concentrated. The PDTO fragments that detach and accumulate at the bottom of the wells can be counted (as described in the step 81) to estimate how much additional fragments should be included in the cell seeding suspension to compensate for that loss. In our case, 4 × 105 cells per mL were used instead of 3 × 105 cells per mL.

Problem 5

Unstable TEER measurement values (related to step 93).

Potential solution

To minimize the occurrence of unstable TEER measurement values, electrodes should not stay in ethanol for more than 5 min at a time. The electrode head or any cable or connector portion must not be in contact with liquid, only the electrode blades can be immersed (step 93).

Do not sand or abrade the electrode surfaces and replace any blades showing deep scratches (especially the tips). Under correct operating conditions, the lifetime of electrode blades is about 1 year. If the values are not stabilizing after a minute of motionless measurement, change the electrode blades.
