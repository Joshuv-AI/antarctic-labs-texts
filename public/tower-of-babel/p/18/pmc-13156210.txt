Inhaled Corticosteroid/Long-Acting β2-Agonist Selection for Patients with Moderate-to-Severe Asthma: Considerations for Real-World Practice, A Narrative Review
Authors: John D Blakey, Lorenzo Cecchi, Christian Domingo, Tony D D’Urzo, Dave Singh, Manish Verma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe current standard of maintenance care for patients with moderate-to-severe asthma is the use of inhaled corticosteroid/long-acting β2-agonist (ICS/LABA) medications; some patients may also require additional therapies including long-acting muscarinic antagonists or biologics to establish disease control. Presently, there is a striking discrepancy between the positive outcomes reported in randomised clinical trials (RCTs) of these therapies and real-world outcomes that may be independent of treatment adherence. Patients with asthma included in RCTs are selected using stringent eligibility criteria, for example they have never been heavy smokers. Because current recommendations rely on results from such exclusive RCTs, this calls into question the extent to which these recommendations are applicable in daily practice. Therefore, generalising information from RCTs can be a difficult task for a number of reasons, including differences between ICS/LABAs, varied responses to medications among patients and the limited time busy general practitioners have to bridge the care gaps that exist. Factors in choosing a desirable ICS/LABA may include (a) considerations in clinical decision-making; (b) differences in pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic properties of ICS/LABA molecules, therapeutic index; (c) individual patient factors which may influence or facilitate successful adherence to treatment; (d) ease of inhaler use; (e) underlying inflammation; and (f) balancing efficacy and long-term safety, including adverse events and long-term exacerbation risk, based on data from both RCTs and real-world evidence. This review article discusses factors that healthcare professionals may utilise when selecting an ICS/LABA treatment for their patients, by considering data from RCTs and real-world evidence in addition to geographical/environmental, personal, and disease factors, which may also influence the decision process, such as availability and affordability.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12325-026-03512-8.Keywords: Asthma, Inhaled corticosteroid, Long-acting β2-agonist, Shared decision-making, Effectiveness, Real world evidence

Key Summary Points

Inhaled corticosteroids in combination with long-acting β2-agonists are the current standard of care for patients with moderate-to-severe asthma to manage their symptoms.Despite positive outcomes noted for these medications in randomised clinical trials, there are discrepancies in published real-world data, which may be independent of treatment adherence; generalising clinical trial data can be difficult.There are several factors to consider when choosing the optimal inhaled corticosteroid/long-acting β2-agonist combination: differences in pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic properties of the molecules; balancing efficacy and long-term safety and individual patient factors, which may influence long-term treatment adherence.This review highlights the importance of these and other factors when choosing an inhaled corticosteroid/long-acting β2-agonist medication for personalised asthma management and provides an overview of practical, clinical practice-relevant guidance, listing the benefits and evidence for various treatment choices.

Introduction

The cornerstone treatment for asthma management is inhaled corticosteroid (ICS)-containing medications [1]. These medications have demonstrated long-term efficacy and safety [1–4]. Randomised clinical trials (RCTs) and comparative studies have shown benefits in adherence and effectiveness when ICS is used in combination with a long-acting β2-agonist (LABA), with patients with moderate-to-severe disease experiencing fewer exacerbations and improved lung function compared with ICS alone [5, 6]. However, there are differences when comparing efficacy between data from RCTs and real-world evidence (RWE) studies [7–9]. Patients with asthma frequently do not use ICS/LABAs as prescribed, leading to suboptimal symptom control, overuse of short-acting β2-agonists (SABAs), and increased risk of exacerbations due to uncontrolled inflammation [10].

In patients with milder disease severity, as-needed ICS/LABA is suggested by some guidelines as a preferred treatment option, particularly for those with poor adherence [1, 11]. However, the use of ICS/LABA rescue therapy is associated with a similar number of exacerbations and reduced day-to-day asthma control and lung function compared to daily maintenance ICS [12]. ICS/formoterol (FOR) as maintenance and reliever therapy (MART) has been suggested to improve adherence for patients with more severe asthma since it requires only a single inhaler and was found to be beneficial in RCTs. However, extrapolation of trial findings to patients in the real world should be done with caution, in part because many patients struggle with adherence [13].

Challenges may exist for healthcare professionals (HCPs) classifying asthma severity in primary care. Further compounding this issue, there are also clear discrepancies between actual asthma control and how it is perceived—most patients and HCPs perceive asthma to be well controlled according to international guidelines, while in reality validated control measures such as Asthma Control Test scores suggest otherwise in most patients [14, 15], or the Asthma Control Questionnaire, which is not as commonly used in everyday clinical practice [16].

Recommendations for a consensus, evidence-informed approach to asthma management have been developed by the Global Initiative for Asthma (GINA), which outline the primary goal of asthma management for patients to achieve their best possible long-term outcomes [1]. Long-term asthma risk minimisation centres on individual patients experiencing no exacerbations, having optimised lung function, having no requirement for maintenance oral corticosteroids, and/or experiencing no side effects of medication [1]. The GINA recommendations describe two different ‘Tracks’ to guide asthma treatment using a stepwise approach according to asthma severity and control [1]. For moderate disease, Track 1 recommends that low-to-medium dose ICS/FOR is prescribed as MART, while Track 2 recommends low-to-medium dose maintenance ICS/LABA alongside low-dose ICS/SABA or SABA. Low-dose MART is the recommended treatment option for patients with uncontrolled disease or patients whose asthma is uncontrolled on an as-needed only regimen according to GINA and national guidelines from countries such as Australia, New Zealand, and the United Kingdom (UK) [1, 11, 17–19]. However, national guidelines for treatment of adult and paediatric patients with asthma from countries including Italy, Japan, and Spain do not give preference to MART [20–22] and MART is not currently approved by the US Food and Drug Administration [23].

Notably, current recommendations largely rely on results from exclusive RCTs, which are typically conducted to evaluate the efficacy and safety of interventions to gain drug approval. Most of these RCTs have compared ICS/LABAs with ICS rather than other ICS/LABAs or other regimens [7–9]. The patient populations included in RCTs are selected using stringent eligibility criteria, for example by focussing on patients with moderate-to-severe asthma, with acute reversibility to bronchodilators, and excluding patients that smoke or have ever frequently smoked, as well as those with certain comorbidities [7–9]. As such, RCT populations typically are not representative of the broad range of patients who will take the medication in the real world [7–9]. Furthermore, RCTs have a very constrained treatment ecosystem where adherence to medication is high, as treatments are provided for free and patients also have other advice and monitoring, such as with inhaler technique.

Despite a plethora of available asthma treatments, including antileukotriene therapy and immunotherapy, barriers to achieving optimal asthma management still exist, not least in the broad variation in recommendations between different asthma guidelines and the extent to which these recommendations are applicable in daily practice. Disease burden remains high, and a large proportion of patients exhibit suboptimal control [1, 12]. The aim of this review article is to analyse factors that HCPs may utilise when selecting an ICS/LABA treatment for their patients, utilising RCT and RWE data. This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors. The following ICS/LABA treatments are the focus of this review: beclomethasone dipropionate (BDP)/FOR; budesonide (BUD)/FOR; fluticasone furoate (FF)/vilanterol (VI); and fluticasone propionate (FP)/salmeterol (SAL).

Factors for HCP consideration can also be determined by scale, from larger geographical factors (such as whether the treatment is approved and affordable in the country, availability and whether the treatment is suitable for the climate) to personal and specific disease factors. These include patient biomarkers, preferences for device type and dosing, potential side effects and likelihood of adherence, as well as the type of active asthma process, lung distribution and therapeutic index of treatment, as highlighted in Fig. 2. The differences in approval and affordability of medicines between countries is an important topic but is beyond the scope of this review.

Within the range of approved and available ICS/LABA treatment options in a given country, in selecting an ICS/LABA for their patients HCPs may find it helpful to consider an expansion of the Assess, Choose, Train [24] algorithm: Confirm, Assess, Choose, Train, Understand, Support (CACTUS). In this context, the steps refer to confirming lung function and patient symptoms; assessing breathing rate for inhaler device suitability; choosing an ICS/LABA with a favourable therapeutic index and that the patient will use as prescribed; training in the correct use of prescribed devices; ensuring that patients understand what their treatment is for and when it should be taken; and support in the form of an asthma action plan. Assessing peak inspiratory flow rate is particularly important for considerations of device suitability, given that approximately a third of patients with asthma exceed the recommended inspiratory flow rate for correct use of pressurised metered-dose inhalers (MDIs), while more than 90% achieve rates compatible with dry-powder inhaler (DPI) use [25]. The following sections consider these and other aspects in more detail.

Pharmacokinetics (PK)/Pharmacodynamics (PD) and Therapeutic Index

When choosing an ICS/LABA treatment for a patient, it is important to consider the PK and PD properties of the individual drug components. Therapeutic index is widely used as a parameter to assess the dose of drug that exerts a potent therapeutic airway effect relative to the risk of toxicity and adverse events (AEs) [26]. The wider the therapeutic index, the more tolerable (in terms of toxicity and AEs) the drug is whilst retaining efficacy. As a result of high adherence in RCTs, all ICS treatments may appear similar in clinical trial settings. However, this assumption may not be correct based on the differing PK and PD characteristics between the molecules, especially when used in a real-world clinical setting. In addition, as these treatment decisions require selection of a drug combination and the accompanying device, both components influence the final delivery.

Few RCTs comparing the therapeutic index of LABAs are available. In an escalating-dose, placebo-controlled, cross-over, randomised study, FF demonstrated wider therapeutic index than BUD and FP, providing more protection against airway hyperresponsiveness and less systemic activity [27]. Individual Patient Characteristics

Previous studies have identified factors including body mass index (BMI), smoking status, sex, Asthma Control Questionnaire scores, past history of exacerbations, and percent predicted forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) that can influence the risk of future asthma exacerbations [16, 28, 29]. The Modelling ASthma TrEatment Responses (MASTER) study highlighted the importance of considering not only the molecular properties of different ICS but also individual patient characteristics (baseline BMI, smoking status, and sex) in addition to seasonal variation that can impact therapeutic effect and act as determinants of future risk [30]. Additional modelling studies confirmed the importance of these factors, irrespective of whether patients received ICS monotherapy or ICS/LABA combination therapy [30, 31]. Furthermore, exacerbation risk was found to be individualised to each patient, despite different patients achieving a comparable level of symptom control [30]. Relative to ICS monotherapy, ICS/LABA therapy demonstrated a better response and significantly reduced the risk of exacerbations [30]. It is currently not clear whether different ICS/LABA combinations have differential effectiveness based on individual factors such as obesity or smoking.

Ease of Use in Clinical Practice and Patient Perspectives

Asthma care centres focus on choosing the right inhaled therapy for each patient to optimally meet their needs as part of a personalised treatment approach [32]. Patient preference should therefore be a key influence in treatment decisions [33, 34].

The complexity of the regimen (frequency of administration, number of inhalations per day) is an important consideration. Some patients may prefer less frequent dosing as this can be less intrusive on daily activities, whilst other patients may benefit from more frequent dosing and the greater reassurance that their symptoms are controlled, especially in the context of nighttime symptoms [14, 35, 36]. Use of a single-inhaler ICS/LABA combination has been associated with improved adherence, compared to the components being delivered separately or to ICS alone [37]. In other areas of medicine, such as hypertension, once-daily treatment is also associated with improved adherence versus twice daily or multiple daily dosing [38]. Ultimately, the optimal asthma regimen is one that a patient will adhere to, since poor adherence has been linked to increased exacerbation rates as well as poor asthma control [39–42].

The long duration of action for once-daily ICS/LABAs may also benefit patients with poor adherence [26]; for example, some residual clinically meaningful effect of the ICS component of FF/VI can persist for 3 days [43].

Ease of use is also a key attribute for a device and increases the likelihood of correct inhaler technique, optimal medication delivery, and outcomes [44]. In a cross-sectional study of different inhaler devices, patients generally preferred multi-dose DPIs over single-dose DPIs and MDIs [44].

Patients are increasingly aware that current MDIs contain propellant gases that contribute to global warming. Amongst the broad range of mitigation strategies, it is appropriate to consider using DPI ICS/LABA in preference to pressurised MDI ICS/LABA if they are felt to be in equipoise in terms of likely clinical benefit [45].

Focus on Inflammation Control Rather Than Just Providing Symptomatic Control to Achieve Long-Term Outcomes

Given that symptoms and inflammation are not well correlated, using biomarkers to measure the level of inflammation can facilitate a treat-to-target approach in asthma, as opposed to the symptom-driven dosing that underpins the ICS/FOR-based treatment approach. Key biomarkers of airway inflammation include eosinophil count and fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO) levels, with high levels of both parameters being indicative of higher exacerbation rates and an excess decline in FEV1 [46, 47]. An elevated blood eosinophil count of mainly above 400 cells/µL is associated with increased risk of exacerbations, acute respiratory events, and poor asthma control [48–51].

In clinical practice, FeNO levels are used to predict patient responsiveness to ICS [46, 52], assess treatment adherence (in patients with mild-to-moderate asthma), and facilitate stepdown in ICS dose in patients with well-controlled asthma. Despite the advantages of being easy to use, non-invasive, and cost-effective [53], lack of knowledge, time constraints, and access delays also contribute to the limited use of FeNO testing, particularly in primary care [54]. Whilst 53% of primary care networks in the UK have access to FeNO testing [55], wider use within clinical practice and improved understanding of its significance for identifying the underlying inflammatory endotype, predicting likely ICS responsiveness and the risk of exacerbations, could ultimately help in the selection of ICS/LABA treatment for patients. The results of the Development and Evaluation of an online FeNO-guided asthma management INtervEntion in primary care (DEFINE) trial are expected to add clarity to the effectiveness of FeNO testing in primary care settings [56], which may assist in providing a more personalised treatment approach for patients with asthma [57].

These tools should be used when available, especially to facilitate treatment in patients with other risk factors for exacerbation. If these tools are unavailable, then patients with uncontrolled asthma should be referred to centres where FeNO levels can be measured; lack of FeNO testing may lead to asymptomatic inflammation being missed in some individuals with asthma and regular therapy with an efficacious longer-acting steroid should be considered.

Overview of RWE Studies with ICS/LABAs

RCTs have proven ICS/LABA treatments, such as FF/VI [58], BUD/FOR [59], FP/SAL [60], and BDP/FOR [61], are effective at reducing exacerbations and improving asthma control, and are well tolerated [62–64], as summarised by Roche et al. [65]. Patients often demonstrate worse symptom control outside the stringent confines and limited duration of an RCT [66]. Simulation of patient data in a real-life setting has shown that differences between ICS/LABA molecules may result in different long-term effects (e.g., exacerbation risk) despite comparable effects in the short term, such as on symptom improvement [31]. The findings of RWE studies are therefore as important, if not more so, as those from RCTs when considering prescribing ICS/LABA for the treatment of asthma. Overall, individual RWE studies show similar effectiveness and safety for ICS/LABAs but head-to-head comparisons have shown differences, seemingly due to varying levels of adherence [62, 67]. The Salford Lung Study, which compared FF/VI to usual care, including ICS/LABAs in a real-world population, determined there was an improvement in asthma control and health-related quality of life in patients treated with FF/VI when compared with usual maintenance care [68]. Furthermore, better patient adherence and lower exacerbation rates have been observed with once-daily FF/VI compared with other twice-daily ICS/LABAs in RWE studies [62, 68–72].

Adverse Effects

Overall, ICS/LABA treatments are well tolerated, with few adverse effects reported in RWE studies and RCTs [67–69, 72–79]. It is important to note that, given clinical safety reporting requirements, the most frequently reported AEs may not reflect the pharmacological effect of ICS/LABAs. AEs considered to be potentially related to the mode of action of ICS/LABA molecules include suppression of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, osteoporosis, thinning of the dermis and purpura, cataracts, reduction in linear growth, diabetes, and an increased risk of infections including pneumonia [80]. Oropharyngeal AEs are also common if a spacer in not used or post-dose mouth rinsing is not performed [81]. Potential effects on the HPA axis are a primary concern associated with long-term ICS/LABA treatment as suppression of endogenous glucocorticoids may result in adrenal insufficiency (a life-threatening disorder characterised by weakness, fatigue, anorexia, abdominal pain, weight loss, and salt craving) [82]. High ICS doses are independent risk factors for adrenal insufficiency [83], hence treatment with high-dose ICS is recommended to be limited to durations of 3–6 months where possible [1]. As inhibition of cortisol production is a marker of HPA axis hyporesponsiveness [80], the therapeutic index for an ICS considers cortisol as a marker for systemic effects [27]. The wider therapeutic index of FF, defined as the daily dose that produces 20% cortisol suppression, compared with other ICS molecules, such as FP or BUD as shown in Fig. 1, translates to significant differences in modelled treatment performance [27, 31].

Conclusion

This article provides HCPs with an overview of practical, clinical practice-relevant guidance in the selection of appropriate ICS/LABA treatments for asthma management. The listed benefits and evidence for various ICS/LABA choices enable therapy selection based on patient characteristics as part of a personalised treatment approach. The limited number of head-to-head clinical trials comparing different ICS/LABAs available in clinical practice has contributed to a general misconception that all ICS/LABA treatments are essentially similar despite their differing binding affinities and therapeutic indices. On the basis of the differing PK/PD properties between available ICS/LABAs, and their different dosing regimens, this is not true. Compared with ICS/FOR regular dosing, the benefits of MART with ICS/FOR as recommended by GINA include exacerbation reduction and asthma control improvements. Other ICS/LABA combinations, such as FF/VI, have additional benefits in terms of therapeutic index, once-daily treatment, better adherence and exacerbation reduction as demonstrated in many recently published large real-world database studies [62, 68, 69, 71, 85–88]. Selecting an ICS/LABA should go beyond symptom control and aim to control the underlying inflammation, whilst taking patient preferences into account. To facilitate prescriber decision-making, our article includes an overview of factors to consider within an individualised patient-context (Fig. 2), the proposed CACTUS algorithm of practical steps, and a summary of ICS/LABA combination features (Table 3).
