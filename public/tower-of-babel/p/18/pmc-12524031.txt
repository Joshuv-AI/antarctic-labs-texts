Exploration of a DDX3X Gene Supplementation Therapy Including Expanded Characterization and Novel Findings of Sleep Disturbances in Ddx3x Haploinsufficient Mice
Authors: Andrea Boitnott, Anjala Jiji, Erik J. Plautz, Yuhui Hu, Xin Chen, Steven J. Gray
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDDX3X syndrome is a neurodevelopmental disorder caused by mutations in the DDX3X gene. It is one of many rare diseases for which there is no adequate treatment, but it has characteristics that make it potentially amenable to gene supplementation therapy.MethodsAn AAV9 (adeno-associated virus serotype 9)-mediated gene supplementation therapy was designed and tested for efficacy and safety in a mouse model of DDX3X syndrome. A total of 182 female Ddx3x+/− mutant and Ddx3xflox/+ control littermates were dosed at postnatal day 1 with AAV9/DDX3X or vehicle and assessed throughout development for phenotypic benefit. To expand the readouts, sleep—a behavior disrupted in patients—was assessed by wireless telemetry.ResultsThere was no effect of treatment on early postnatal developmental milestones. At 3 months, a dose-dependent amelioration in anxiety-like behavior was observed in treated Ddx3x+/− mice, and a dose-dependent worsening in anxiety-like behavior was observed in treated Ddx3xflox/+ control mice. These treatment effects were not sustained at 12 months and were absent in mice that had not been subjected to prior developmental milestone testing. Significant sleep disturbances were observed in the Ddx3x+/− mice at 4 months, but treatment had no effect on sleep.ConclusionsWhile the gene therapy design used in this study was not effective, the data suggest that behavior can be modified by altering Ddx3x expression, warranting further exploration of gene therapy as a potential treatment approach. Additionally, the sleep disturbances present in Ddx3x+/− mutant mice phenocopy the reduced sleep quality seen in patients, further validating this model for research.

DDX3X syndrome was identified as a rare monogenic neurodevelopmental disorder in 2015 and is now considered a leading cause of intellectual disability in females, with about 1100 known cases worldwide (1,2). Multiple groups have published clinical phenotyping studies outlining common features seen in patients (2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8). While intellectual disability and developmental delay are hallmark features seen in almost all patients, the entire clinical profile can be variable. Some patients have behavioral abnormalities including anxiety, autism spectrum disorder, and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder. Movement disorders, including gait disturbances and poor coordination, and hypotonia are observed in most patients. Other features can include speech delay, structural brain changes, sensory symptoms, seizures, gastrointestinal abnormalities, vision abnormalities, scoliosis, and congenital heart abnormalities. More recently, sleep disturbances have been reported as part of the clinical presentation, with the most recent study estimating that most patients experience sleep disturbances (5).

DDX3X syndrome is caused by mutations in the ubiquitously expressed DEAD-box helicase X-linked DDX3X gene (2). DDX3X escapes X chromosome inactivation, and thus typically developing females express 2 DDX3X alleles, while males express 1 allele (9,10). Most identified cases of DDX3X syndrome are females with de novo heterozygous mutations that cluster in 2 critical functional domains of the gene; however, some males have been identified (2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8,11,12). In females, about one half of the known mutations are missense mutations and in-frame deletions, altering normal protein function, while the other half are frameshift and nonsense mutations, acting as loss-of-function mutations, and thereby result in haploinsufficiency (4,5). A Ddx3x haploinsufficient mouse model has previously been developed and characterized (13). Ddx3x null male mice are embryonic lethal, consistent with the human syndrome. The female Ddx3x+/− mutant mice are delayed in reaching physical, sensory, and motor milestones during early postnatal development and show altered behavior as adults, including increased anxiety-like behavior and motor impairments (13), reminiscent of the human condition.

Despite the clinical burden of DDX3X syndrome and the known genetic cause, no published study to date has explored whether gene-based therapies would be an effective treatment. For DDX3X haploinsufficiency, a gene supplementation approach, in which a functional copy of DDX3X is introduced into cells to make up for the nonfunctional copy, is potentially an effective approach. Gene supplementation, also known as gene replacement or gene augmentation, is a one-time gene delivery treatment that has been used successfully for central nervous system (CNS) diseases (14, 15, 16, 17, 18). However, developing gene therapies for neurodevelopmental disorders can create unique challenges (19,20), and it is unknown in the case of DDX3X whether abnormal embryonic development results in changes that are reversible postnatally. DDX3X is highly expressed both before and after birth and has been implicated in multiple processes across brain development (4,13,21, 22, 23, 24, 25). We hypothesize that DDX3X gene supplementation during early postnatal development may restore proper protein function and thereby ameliorate disease symptoms preclinically. Based on previous studies that showed negative consequences of DDX3X overexpression (26, 27, 28), we anticipated that treatment effects would be sensitive to DDX3X gene dosage.

With a focus on translational gene therapy research, we took the standard tools and technology currently available for CNS disease gene supplementation and applied them to DDX3X syndrome. We designed an AAV9 (adeno-associated virus serotype 9)-mediated gene supplementation therapy (AAV9/DDX3X) and administered it to the previously characterized Ddx3x haploinsufficient mouse model via an intracerebroventricular (ICV) injection. For CNS gene delivery, recombinant AAV9 is favored due to its ability to cross the blood-brain barrier and diffuse widely across cells and tissues (29, 30, 31, 32, 33). Administering AAV9 into cerebrospinal fluid, like with an ICV route of administration, can improve gene delivery to regions of interest in the CNS (14,30,31). Biodistribution of a transgene packaged in AAV9 after ICV administration in mice is widespread, including to peripheral organs, with the highest expression concentrated in the CNS (30,34,35). The safety and efficacy of AAV9/DDX3X was evaluated by assessing phenotypes in mice across early postnatal development and in adulthood. In addition to the previously reported phenotypes, sleep was evaluated and incorporated into the behavioral assessment to expand the readouts with relevance to the human condition.

Methods and Materials

Gene Therapy Plasmid Design and Virus Production

A self-complementary (sc) vector expressing a codon-optimized human DDX3X transgene (hDDX3Xopt), driven by the weak ubiquitous JeT promoter, and stopped by the synthetic polyadenylation element SpA (JeT-hDDX3Xopt-SpA), was designed. The plasmid was cloned and sequence verified using standard techniques. The plasmid was packaged into AAV9 to generate the AAV9/DDX3X vector at the Translational Gene Therapy Core, UT Southwestern Medical Center. The same production lot was used for all experiments (NC-19-21-004), which had a titer of 1.3 × 1014 vg/mL and endotoxin level <1 EU/mL (the Certificate of Analysis is included in the Supplement). The AAV9/DDX3X vector was formulated in phosphate-buffered saline containing 5% D-sorbitol and 0.001% Poloxamer 188, and this formulation buffer was used as the vehicle and dilutant in all experiments. The AAV9/DDX3X vector was stored at −80 °C until use, and thawed aliquots were stored at 4 °C. Vector genome integrity and in vitro expression were assessed before injections into mice (Figure S1).

Mice

All procedures were approved by UT Southwestern Medical Center’s Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. Female Ddx3xflox/+ and male Ddx3xflox/y mice were provided by Dr. Silvia De Rubeis’ laboratory at the Seaver Autism Center for Research and Treatment at the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai. The mice were bred to obtain female Ddx3xflox/flox mice, which were crossed with male Sox2-cre/+ mice (#008454; The Jackson Laboratory) to produce female Ddx3xflox/+ control and Ddx3x+/− mutant littermates for all experiments. The Ddx3xflox/+ control mice present like wild-type mice, with the only difference being the presence of 2 loxP sites flanking exon 2. Further information about genotyping and breeding this line has been presented elsewhere (13). The colony was maintained on a C57BL/6J background with 16% protein irradiated chow and water provided ad libitum and housed in a controlled facility on a 12-hour light/dark cycle. All experimental mice were weaned on postnatal day (P) 21.

ICV Injection

A bilateral ICV injection of vehicle, AAV9/DDX3X at a low dose of 1.3 × 1011 vg, or AAV9/DDX3X at a high dose of 2.6 × 1011 vg, was performed in 182 female Ddx3xflox/+ control and Ddx3x+/− mutant littermates at P1 (Table S1). All injections were performed blinded to genotype. Pups were cryoanesthetized for 10 minutes and moved to a precooled clay mold to stabilize the body for a freehand ICV injection. A stereotaxic injection syringe (65460-06; Hamilton) with a 33-gauge needle (65461-02; Hamilton) set to a stop depth of 2 mm was used, and needle placement was determined based on lambda, the midline, and the eye, such that if a line were drawn from lambda to the eye, the placement would be 1 mm from lambda toward the eye and about 0.5 mm from midline. A light was used to help visualize the skull suture lines under the skin. Then, 1 μL was injected over 1 minute per hemisphere, with a subsequent 1-minute hold before withdrawing the needle. Pups were paw tattooed for identification and placed on a heating pad until fully conscious before returning to their home cage. Correct injection placement and vector distribution in the brain were assessed in a separate pilot cohort of mice 3 weeks postinjection by RNAscope with custom probes from Advanced Cell Diagnostics (the RNAscope protocol is included in the Supplement). All injections showed correct vector distribution, suggesting high targeting accuracy.

Developmental Milestones and Adult Behavior Testing

Physical, sensory, and motor developmental milestones were assessed in pups every day from P2 to P21 (13). Litters were homogenized to 6 pups per dam to control for potential litter size effects, and sires were removed from breeding cages on P1. Baseline anxiety-like behavior and activity were assessed by a 10-minute open field test at 3 and 12 months. Motor coordination was assessed on the accelerating rotarod and vertical pole at 12 months. All behavior was conducted blinded to genotype and treatment group during the light phase (the open field, rotarod, and vertical pole protocols are included in the Supplement).

Wireless Telemetry

Wireless telemetry devices (HD-X02; Data Sciences International) were surgically implanted in anesthetized mice at 3.5 months of age for electroencephalography (EEG) and electromyography (EMG) recordings using an aseptic technique by the Neuro-Models Facility, UT Southwestern Medical Center. The sensor body was implanted subcutaneously along the flank between the forelimb and hindlimb. One pair of leads extended to the skull and were placed in 2 burr holes on the dura mater—one at anteroposterior (AP) +1.0 mm, mediolateral (ML) −1.5 mm and the other at AP −2.0 mm, ML +2.0 mm—for surface cortical EEG recording. The other pair of leads were secured to the dorsal muscles of the neck for EMG recording. All components of the device were fully enclosed under the skin or dental acrylic, allowing the mouse to freely move in its home cage postsurgery. Telemetry signals were recorded 1.5 weeks postsurgery in the mouse’s home cage via an integrated communication system (PhysioTel Hardware; Data Sciences International) continuously from Friday afternoon to Monday morning. Cleaning and sterilization of telemetry devices between uses was conducted per Data Sciences International protocol.

NeuroScore EEG/EMG Data Processing

NeuroScore software (version 3.4; Data Sciences International) was used to score continuous 10-second epochs of wake, slow wave sleep (SWS), and paradoxical sleep (PS) from the wireless telemetry recordings. A customized view with the EEG periodogram; EEG delta power ratio; EEG theta power ratio; EMG root mean square value; and raw EEG, EMG, and activity signals were created for each recording (Figure S2). All periods were restricted to 64.5 hours—Friday afternoon to Monday morning—with a darkness interval from 6 pm to 6 am. Each mouse was analyzed separately, with investigators blinded to genotype and treatment group. All recordings were analyzed with the Rodent Sleep Scoring protocol with the following settings: delta band defined as 0.5 to 4 Hz, theta band defined as 6 to 9 Hz, activity threshold set to 0.1, active wake scored as wake, and EEG and EMG artifacts detected at 1 mV. The EEG delta ratio threshold, EEG theta ratio threshold, and EMG threshold were set individually for each mouse, as the signals were not always the same quality due to variability in surgeries from the contact of the wire with the dura (Table S2). Correct scoring was confirmed with the synced video player. One Ddx3xflox/+ vehicle-injected mouse had to be removed from downstream analyses due to a poor EEG/EMG signal during recording. The 3 Ddx3xflox/+ mice injected with a high dose of AAV9/DDX3X that were implanted with wireless telemetry devices were removed from all sleep analyses due to low numbers.

Statistical Analyses

Data were analyzed blinded to genotype and treatment group. Statistics and plots were generated using custom R scripts. Normality was assessed by the Shapiro-Wilk test, and homogeneity of variance was assessed by the Levene test. For comparisons between 2 groups, Student’s t tests were used for normally distributed data, and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were used for non-normally distributed data. For comparisons between more than 2 groups, 2-way analysis of variance was used for normally distributed data, and the Kruskal-Wallis test was used for non-normally distributed data. Repeated-measures analysis of variance was used for repeated observations.

Results

AAV9/DDX3X Vector Expression In Vivo and Treatment Design

An AAV9/DDX3X gene supplementation therapy (scAAV9_JeT-hDDX3Xopt-SpA) was designed to express a codon-optimized human DDX3X transgene at low levels across all tissue and cell types to mimic endogenous DDX3X expression (Figure 1A). The promoter, transgene, and polyadenylation signal just fit within the 2.2-kb packaging constraints of an sc vector to ensure efficient cellular transduction. Two experimental treatment arms were designed to determine the safety and efficacy of AAV9/DDX3X in neonatal mice treated at P1 (Figure 1B, C and Table S1). In one arm, 127 female mice across 6 genotype/treatment groups (Ddx3xflox/+ vehicle, Ddx3xflox/+ low dose, Ddx3xflox/+ high dose, Ddx3x+/− vehicle, Ddx3x+/− low dose, and Ddx3x+/− high dose) were assessed phenotypically throughout early postnatal development and as adults (Figure 1B and Table S1). In the second arm, 55 female mice across 4 genotype/treatment groups (Ddx3xflox/+ vehicle, Ddx3xflox/+ high dose, Ddx3x+/− vehicle, and Ddx3x+/− high dose) were assessed only as adults and were also enrolled in a sleep study using a wireless telemetry system (Figure 1C and Table S1). At 1-year postinjection, a significant increase in DDX3X expression in the brain of Ddx3x+/− mice injected with a high dose of AAV9/DDX3X was apparent compared with a vehicle injection; however, no increase in DDX3X expression was observed in Ddx3xflox/+ control mice injected with a high dose of AAV9/DDX3X compared with a vehicle injection, potentially suggestive of an endogenous regulation mechanism to dampen DDX3X overexpression (Figure 1D). The vector distribution of hDDX3Xopt RNA was widespread in both CNS and peripheral tissues, with the highest expression in the cortex, hippocampus, and olfactory bulbs (Figure 1E). AAV9/DDX3X Treatment Does Not Affect Developmental Milestones in Female Ddx3xflox/+ Control or Ddx3x+/− Mice

Consistent with previously published data (13), Ddx3x+/− mice took longer to reach physical, sensory, and motor developmental milestones than Ddx3xflox/+ control littermates (Ddx3xflox/+ vehicle vs. Ddx3x+/− vehicle comparisons) (Table 1; Table S3 and Figure S4). In the physical domain, Ddx3x+/− mice had reduced weight and were delayed at opening their eyes. In the motor domain, Ddx3x+/− mice took longer to master the negative geotaxis and grip strength tests; however, no significant delay in surface righting time was observed. In the sensory domain, Ddx3x+/− mice were delayed in developing the ear twitch, auditory startle response, and placing response. Across all developmental milestone tests, AAV9/DDX3X treatment at a low or high dose had no effect in Ddx3xflox/+ control mice compared with a vehicle injection (Ddx3xflox/+ vehicle vs. low-dose and vehicle vs. high-dose comparisons) (Table 1; Table S3 and Figure S4). The only exception to this was eye opening for the Ddx3xflox/+ control mice injected with a low dose of AAV9/DDX3X; however, because this change was not dose dependent, the effect was not clearly gene therapy related. Overall, the data suggest that AAV9/DDX3X is safe during early postnatal development, where overexpression in Ddx3xflox/+ control mice did not overtly change the trajectory of reaching developmental milestones. AAV9/DDX3X Ameliorates Anxiety-Like Behavior in Female Ddx3x+/− Mice but Causes Negative Effects in Ddx3xflox/+ Control Littermates

Ddx3x+/− mice have increased anxiety-like behavior, as measured by cumulative duration and frequency to enter in the center of an open field, compared with Ddx3xflox/+ control littermates at both 3 months (Figure 2, top row) and 12 months (Figure 2, bottom row), a phenotype consistent with previously published data (13). The anxiety-like behavior was not confounded by any changes in activity as measured by distance moved (Figure 2A, D). The anxiety-like behavior was significantly ameliorated with a high dose of AAV9/DDX3X in Ddx3x+/− mice compared with a vehicle injection at 3 months but not 12 months as measured by the cumulative duration in the center (Figure 2B, E). Conversely, anxiety-like behavior was significantly worse with a high dose of AAV9/DDX3X in Ddx3xflox/+ control mice compared with a vehicle injection at 3 months as measured by the cumulative duration in the center; however, again there was no statistically significant association at 12 months (Figure 2B, E). Anxiety-like behavior, as measured by frequency to enter the center, was not affected by AAV9/DDX3X (Figure 2C, F). Motor coordination was also assessed at 12 months with the accelerating rotarod and vertical pole tests, but no significant differences between Ddx3x+/− and Ddx3xflox/+ control mice were observed at baseline (Figure S5; rotarod statistics included in Table S3), negating the possibility of efficacy assessment in treated Ddx3x+/− mice. Female Ddx3x+/− Mice Have Sleep Disturbances, but AAV9/DDX3X Treatment Has No Effect on This Abnormal Behavior

In the second arm of the study, where the mice had wireless telemetry devices implanted for EEG/EMG recordings, the goal was multifaceted: 1) to assess the effect of AAV9/DDX3X treatment and 2) to expand the model characterization to include additional relevant phenotypes to the human condition. EEG/EMG recordings allow for both seizure and sleep analysis. No differences in seizure activity between Ddx3x+/− and Ddx3xflox/+ control mice were observed (data not shown). However, many aspects of sleep were disrupted in Ddx3x+/− mice compared with Ddx3xflox/+ control littermates (Figure 3). In this study arm, the first 2 cohorts of mice went untreated and were used for an EEG/EMG pilot study (Table S1). Statistical tests were conducted for the mice that were not treated and the mice that were treated with vehicle for each genotype (Table S4). Ddx3x+/− mice spent less time in wake and more time in SWS compared with Ddx3xflox/+ control littermates, with no overall change in time spent in PS (Figure 3A). These significant differences were driven by changes in the dark phase, which is a mouse’s active phase (Figure S6). Ddx3x+/− mice had more bouts of SWS and PS compared with Ddx3xflox/+ control littermates, with no difference in the number of wake bouts (Figure 3C). These significant differences were driven by changes in the light phase, which is a mouse’s inactive phase (Figure S7). The average time spent in each bout of PS was significantly decreased in Ddx3x+/− mice compared with Ddx3xflox/+ control littermates, with no change in average wake or SWS bout time (Figure 3D). This significant difference was driven by changes in both the light and dark phases (Figure S8). Additionally, Ddx3x+/− mice transitioned more from PS to SWS and from SWS to PS, with no significant differences in transition number to or from wake (Figure 3E). Across the total recording time, the single longest bout of wake was shorter in Ddx3x+/− mice compared with Ddx3xflox/+ control littermates, with no significant difference in longest recorded bout of SWS or PS (Figure 3B). Additionally, the delta and beta bands during PS were increased in Ddx3x+/− mice compared with Ddx3xflox/+ control littermates (Figure 3F). Discussion

Reproducing data across institutions, equipment, and experimenters is a critical component of scientific research, ensuring reliability and validity of findings. In this study, we reproduced developmental milestone delays and altered adult behavior reported in a previously characterized mouse model of DDX3X syndrome (13), which further validates this model for use in research. Not observing a motor phenotype at 12 months (Figure S5) was expected in this study, as it had previously been shown that prior handling and behavioral interventions could attenuate motor phenotypes in aging Ddx3x+/− mice, and the mice used in this study had undergone developmental milestone testing from P2 to P20 (13). In addition, the phenotype of these mice was extended to include sleep disturbances.

Sleep disturbances have been mentioned in case reports and clinical observational studies of patients with DDX3X syndrome (3,5,6,36). Recently, numerous parental anecdotes of sleep disturbances have increased attention toward this feature; however, a formal sleep study in patients has not been published. Specific aspects of sleep disturbances that have been discussed in the literature include difficulty falling asleep, middle of the night wakings, early waking, hypersomnia, and hyposomnia (5,6). Our data show that Ddx3x+/− mice spent more time in SWS than in wake, which is consistent with the hypersomnia phenotype observed in patients. Beyond that, our findings may offer insights into the sleep disturbances that patients may experience, especially regarding the 2 phases of sleep, SWS and PS. We showed that compared with control littermates, Ddx3x+/− mice transitioned to and from SWS and PS at an increased frequency, had more bouts of SWS and PS, and spent less time in each bout of PS. This data suggest reduced quality of sleep, particularly disruptive maintenance of PS. PS has been studied at length, with effects on memory consolidation, learning, and emotional regulation (37, 38, 39). Because sleep has such a central role on cognitive functioning and behavior, it is possible that being able to regulate and modify these sleep disturbances could help ameliorate other symptoms; however, we were unable to modify sleep behavior with AAV9/DDX3X.

The most interesting results from the gene therapy treatment experiments come from the open field data. While not a full rescue, we showed a dose-dependent amelioration in anxiety-like behavior in the Ddx3x+/− mice with treatment, as measured by cumulative duration spent in the center of an open field. Conversely, in control mice, DDX3X overexpression or unregulated expression induced by AAV9/DDX3X treatment caused a dose-dependent increase in anxiety-like behavior. This data suggest that Ddx3x is a dose-sensitive gene, and attention to expression and dose will need to be considered when designing subsequent vector designs for more effective treatment of DDX3X syndrome. At the 12-month time point, the amelioration in anxiety-like behavior in the Ddx3x+/− mice was no longer apparent. The AAV9-mediated gene supplementation approach should result in permanent transgene expression in postmitotic neurons, so the reason for the loss of this rescue is not apparent. Anxiety-like behavior was also assessed in the cohort of mice that were used in the second arm EEG/EMG study (Figure S9). Interestingly, these mice, which notably did not go through developmental milestone testing, did not show any amelioration or worsening of anxiety-like behavior in an open field at 3 months. While the number of mice was not as robust as in the first study arm, which might have masked subtle behavior changes, another possible explanation for this is that the developmental milestone battery of tests and handling during early postnatal development enhanced the therapeutic efficacy of AAV9/DDX3X treatment. Phenotypic benefit has been enhanced in multiple instances when a therapeutic has been paired with behavioral intervention (40, 41, 42, 43, 44).

Regarding the physical, sensory, and motor milestone tests, AAV9/DDX3X had no effect on either Ddx3x+/− or Ddx3xflox/+ control mice. Transgene expression after scAAV-mediated gene therapy administration typically peaks a few weeks postinjection (45, 46, 47), so it is possible that testing for developmental milestones from P2 to P20 is too early to see any phenotypic change in these mice. However, it is important to note that studies have shown clear phenotypic benefit within 1 week of AAV-mediated gene therapy administration in other disease models (48, 49, 50).

Conclusions

Together, 2 main conclusions come out of this research. First, the lack of efficacy in disease-relevant phenotypes with AAV9/DDX3X warrants additional studies on the biological mechanisms underlying DDX3X syndrome and the effect of DDX3X mutations during development. It is possible that the harm caused by DDX3X haploinsufficiency during prenatal development is not reversible postnatally. Likewise, there may be a certain time window during which therapeutic intervention is most beneficial, and some phenotypes could be reversible to different extents, such as what is seen with Angelman syndrome (51). Time windows for therapeutic intervention have been shown in many other mouse models of neurodevelopmental disorders (51, 52, 53, 54). While treatment timing is a possible explanation for the lack of efficacy, another possibility is that the current gene therapy tools and technology are not sufficient at targeting enough cells, the correct cell types, and/or the correct amount of Ddx3x expression necessary for phenotypic rescue. The increase in DDX3X expression in the brain of Ddx3x+/− mice after the high-dose injection of AAV9/DDX3X was significant but not back to normal Ddx3xflox/+ levels. It is possible that greater restoration of protein levels is required for complete phenotypic benefit and/or that critical regions of the brain were not adequately transduced by this neonatal ICV approach with AAV9. A previous report showed that after an ICV injection at P1 in mice, about 60%, 30%, and 1% transduction occurred in cortical, hippocampal, and cerebellar cells, respectively, with a very similar vector (55). Additional research on the role of Ddx3x in different cell types will help inform innovations in the vector design that may have better efficacy, notwithstanding improved AAV capsid technology that may permit more efficient and more homogeneous transgene distribution across the entire CNS. Second, significant findings related to sleep disturbances in the Ddx3x+/− mice warrant additional clinical studies on the sleep disturbances that patients have reported. Knowing more about the specific aspects of sleep that are altered in patients can help physicians devise individual treatment plans and could potentially reveal a biomarker that would be useful for translational research.
