Radiotherapy-induced alterations in trabecular microarchitecture and bone mass of the mandibular condylar process: A retrospective analysis using fractal dimension and pixel intensity on digital panoramic radiographs
Authors: Luiz Felipe Palma, Rafael Verardi Serrano, Luana Campos, Marcelo Marcucci, Luís Otávio Carvalho de Moraes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo evaluate the impact of conventional 3-dimensional (3D) conformal radiotherapy on the mandibular condylar process in patients with head and neck cancer using pixel intensity and fractal dimension analyses of digital panoramic radiographs.Materials and MethodsPre- and post-radiotherapy radiographs from 32 patients were digitally analyzed by a single experienced researcher. Regions of interest were selected in the right mandibular condylar process, specifically at the most constricted area corresponding to the condylar neck. Pixel intensity and fractal dimension analyses were subsequently performed.ResultsComparison between the evaluation periods demonstrated a statistically significant decrease in both mean pixel intensity and fractal dimension after radiotherapy. The mean pixel intensity was 115 ± 35.4 before radiotherapy and 104 ± 34.0 after radiotherapy. The mean fractal dimension values before and after radiotherapy were 1.30 ± 0.110 and 1.23 ± 0.129, respectively.ConclusionConventional 3D conformal radiotherapy appears to lead to deleterious changes in the microarchitecture and bone mass of the mandibular condylar process in patients with head and neck cancer. Further studies are warranted to determine whether patients with radiation-induced low bone quality are more susceptible to complications such as mandibular fractures and temporomandibular joint dysfunction.

Introduction

Conventional treatment modalities for head and neck cancer—radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and surgery—may be used individually or in combination. Although radiotherapy is often the standard treatment for early-stage disease, advanced cases typically require more intensive regimens that include chemotherapy and/or surgery.12 Whilethe deleterious effects of radiation on bone tissue have been extensively investigated to clarify the pathogenesis of conditions such as osteoradionecrosis,345 the mechanisms remain incompletely understood.

Digital panoramic radiography is a rapid, comfortable, and low-cost imaging modality that involves relatively low radiation exposure. Digital panoramic radiographs have been shown to be useful for evaluating systemic bone quality by assessing mandibular bone mass andmorphology;67 however, several alternative methods are also available for this purpose. A digital image consists of a matrix of small units known as pixels, each assigned a specific numeric value.6 In an 8-bit digital panoramic radiograph, pixel intensity values correspond to a grayscale range from 0 (black) to 255 (white),8 which is considered an indirect measure of local bone mass.6

Fractal analysis is a mathematical method used to describe and quantify complex geometric patterns, allowing this structural complexity to be expressed numerically as a fractal dimension.6 Among the various techniques developed for fractal analysis,9 the box-counting method has been shown to be the most appropriate for binary images10 such as radiographs. Consequently, fractal dimension analysis can characterize the intricate architecture of trabecular bone and may serve as an indicator of bone quality.11

Given the limited data available on the deleterious effects of ionizing radiation on bone tissue, the present study aimed to evaluate the impact of conventional 3-dimensional (3D) conformal radiotherapy on the mandibular condylar process in patients with head and neck cancer using pixel intensity and fractal dimension analyses of digital panoramic radiographs.

Materials and Methods

This study was approved by the Research Ethics Committees of the Federal University of São Paulo (CAEE 91440318.3.3001.5449) and Heliópolis Hospital (CAE 59618822.4.3001.5449) and adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki. Participants’ privacy and the confidentiality of their information were maintained throughout all phases of the study.

Digital panoramic radiographs obtained between March 2015 and July 2025 were retrieved from the electronic archive of the Stomatology and Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery Center at Heliópolis Hospital. Eligible patients were required to be at least 18 years of age, to have undergone 3D conformal radiotherapy for head and neck cancer (cervicofacial and supraclavicular fossa fields), and to have at least 2 panoramic radiographs—1 acquired before and 1 after radiotherapy.

Exclusion criteria included systemic diseases affecting bone metabolism, use of medications known to influence bone metabolism, poor patient positioning or low-quality radiographs, and mandibular pathologies involving the regions of interest.

Digital panoramic radiographs were originally acquired using a PaX-400C device (Vatech, Hwaseong, Korea) with a 14-second exposure time and 8 mA current. The kilovoltage peak (kVp) was set according to the manufacturer’s protocol: 68 kVp for male patients and 67 kVp for female patients. Images were stored in the digital archive as 8-bit grayscale bitmap files (.bmp) with pixel dimensions of 2900 × 1532.

Pixel intensity and fractal dimension analyses

The methods for pixel intensity and fractal dimension analyses were adapted from White and Rudolph12 and Tosoni al.6 This approach followed the same evaluation pattern established in a previous study by the authors’ research group.7 All image-analysis steps were performed using ImageJ 1.54p (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD; https://imagej.net/) and available plugins.

The software selection tool was used to define the region of interest on each radiograph. First, a rectangle of arbitrary size was created by placing the vertices of 1 diagonal at the most constricted points of the right mandibular condylar process, corresponding to the condylar neck (Fig. 1). The rectangle was then standardized to a fixed size of 50 × 50 pixels, and the resulting square was automatically centered at the midpoint of the condylar neck. Mean pixel intensity for each region of interest was obtained directly from the software histogram, and fractal dimension analysis was then performed using a standardized workflow.

For fractal dimension analysis, the region-of-interest image was duplicated. The duplicate image was blurred using a Gaussian filter with a 35-pixel radius to preserve only large density variations and was then subtracted from the original image, generating a third image. A constant value of 128 (corresponding to half the gray levels in the 8-bit scale) was added to each pixel in this third image to ensure that brightness variations corresponded to specific features (trabeculae and medullary spaces). The image was then converted to a binary format using a threshold value of 128, thereby segmenting components visually resembling trabeculae and medullary spaces. To reduce noise, erosion and dilation were applied, followed by image inversion to emphasize the trabeculae. The image was then skeletonized (i.e., eroded until only the central pixel lines remained) and superimposed on the original image to confirm correspondence with the trabeculae (Fig. 2). Finally, the box-counting method was applied to determine the fractal dimension.

Sample size calculation, data presentation, and statistical analysis

Sample size was calculated based on a previous study by the authors’ group,7 with α = 0.05 and 80% power. The minimum required number of participants was 27 for pixel intensity analysis and 11 for fractal dimension analysis.

All analyses were performed by a trained observer (a dentist specialized in oral radiology). A second expert independently analyzed 20% of the sample, and inter-examiner reproducibility was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) for pixel intensity.

Participant characteristics and oncological treatment data were tabulated and summarized descriptively. Distributions of pixel intensity and fractal dimension values were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. When the normality assumption was violated, a logarithmic transformation was applied to achieve an approximately normal distribution before conducting the paired-samples t-test. All statistical analyses were performed using Jamovi (ver. 2.6.44),13 with the significance level set at 5% for inferential tests.

Results

Thirty-two individuals were included, yielding a total of 64 digital panoramic radiographs. Most participants were male (78.1%), had the oral cavity as the primary tumor site (53.1%), and had squamous cell carcinoma as the histological type (81.3%). Most received a total radiation dose of 70 Gy (68.75%) and underwent concomitant chemotherapy (71.9%). The mean interval between the end of radiotherapy and acquisition of the post-treatment radiograph was 12.8 ± 8.34 months (range, 4–35 months). Detailed sample information is summarized in Tables 1 and 2.

Normality was rejected for the pixel intensity data (W = 0.917, P < 0.05). Inter-examiner reproducibility was confirmed to be good14 (F = 14.2, ICC = 0.868, P < 0.05). Mean pixel intensity was 115 ± 34.2 before radiotherapy and 104.0 ± 34.0 after radiotherapy, representing a statistically significant difference (t(31) = 4.63, P < 0.05). Mean fractal dimension values before and after radiotherapy were 1.30 ± 0.110 and 1.23 ± 0.129, respectively, and also differed significantly (t(31) = 3.57, P < 0.05). Discussion

This study evaluated radiation-induced alterations in the mandibular condylar process using digital panoramic radiography, a commonly used imaging modality in dental practice. Although this imaging method has inherent limitations related to tissue properties and technical factors (e.g., anatomic overlap, software and receptor configuration, and exposure parameters), evidence supports its use for bone tissue assessment.715 In contrast todual-energy X-ray absorptiometry—the costly gold standard for osteoporosis diagnosis—panoramic radiography offers a simple, low-cost alternative for identifying reduced mandibular bone density while also capturing a range of clinically relevant orofacial features. This may facilitate early identification of reduced systemic bone quality and prompt referral to a specialist.16

A pixel is the smallest controllable unit in a digital image and represents a single point with variable intensity. In grayscale radiographs, each pixel value reflects image intensity. For pixel intensity analysis, an indirect method of assessing local bone mass, established approaches can normalize variation in noise and image acquisition, enabling reliable comparisons even among radiographs obtained using different devices and parameters. These approaches include the use ofdensity markers,6 binarization, skeletonization.17 In the present study, consistent with the authors’ groups previous work,7 the software histogram provided a practical, rapid, and low-cost method for pixel intensity analysis.

Another useful approach for indirect assessment of bone tissue is fractal dimension analysis, a low-cost, noninvasive, and accurate method for quantifying the complex interface between trabecular bone and bone marrow; higher values indicate a more intricate structure.11 This methodidentifies scale-invariant patterns that are relatively robust to variation in radiographic acquisition parameters.910 dental research, many image-processing workflows for fractal dimension analysis are derived from the original study by White and Rudolph,12 which served as the primary reference for the methods used herein.7

Bone quality is determined by tissue microarchitecture and degree of mineralization.18 Repair of fatigue damage—a process involving viable osteocytes, bone turnover, and potentially bone marrow cellularity as a source of stem cells and growth factors—is also a key determinant of bone quality.19 In , a skeletal disorder characterized by compromised bone quality,20 several oral implications are well established, including accelerated progression and increased severity of periodontal disease and lower long-term survival rates of dental implants.21 Although patients with osteoporosis may have a higher risk of fractures, including low-trauma fractures,20 no studies have investigated these events in craniofacial bones.

The findings of the present study indicate that 3D conformal radiotherapy induced significant reductions in trabecular microarchitectural complexity and bone mass at the condylar neck, potentially because ionizing radiation affects non-neoplastic cells within or adjacent to the treatment fields.2223 A widely acceptedpathophysiological hypothesis, based primarily on animal models, proposes that radiotherapy reduces intraosseous vascularization, leading to changes in the bone cell pool, progressive fibrosis, and local necrosis.3 Radiographically, thesealterations may manifest as reduced bone mineral density, trabecular fracture, and loss or destruction of cortical structures.18 However, substantialheterogeneity in methodology and samples across available studies limits the ability to establish a definitive link between radiation-induced damage in human bone and the indirect radiographic measures used in this study.

Although this study is the second to evaluate the impact of radiotherapy on mandibular bone in patients with head and neck cancer using pixel intensity and fractal dimension analyses of panoramic radiographs, it is the first to focus on the mandibular condylar process, specifically the condylar neck. Among mandibular fracture patterns, fractures involving the condylar process are the most prevalent, with reported incidence rates ranging from 17.5% to 50%.24 Thecondylar neck was selected as the region of interest because it is relatively fragile compared with the mandibular head, which is protected by its position within the mandibular fossa25 Mandibular trauma may cause a fracture directly at the impact site or indirectly at a distant weak point due to energy transmission. For example, a lateral force applied to the mandibular body or parasymphysis often results in an indirect fracture of the condylar neck, which is the most vulnerable structure of the condylar process.26

Despite its originality and relevance, this study has limitations that suggest directions for future research. Because the authors did not have access to radiation dose–volume histograms for each patient’s facial region, eligibility was limited to radiotherapy fields in which the condylar region was highly likely to be included. In addition, statistical inference using the paired-samples t-test primarily evaluated within-individual differences between time points. More precise information on the radiation dose delivered to each region of interest would be required to accurately correlate dose with the extent of bone change and to extrapolate these findings to the general population.

Similarly, the interval between completion of radiotherapy and follow-up radiography should ideally be standardized to improve robustness, as it ranged from 4 to 35 months. However, the minimum observed interval (4 months) was sufficient to avoid the clinical effects of acute radiotherapy toxicity.

Another consideration is that reproducing the same region of interest across different time points can be challenging. To address this issue, automated software tools were used to maximize measurement consistency. In addition, inter-examiner reproducibility was good when a second researcher performed the analyses, including region-of-interest selection.

Finally, the longitudinal study design, with radiographs acquired months apart, introduces potential bias because changes in patient habits and body composition could affect pixel intensity values. However, this concern is less applicable to fractal dimension analysis, given its technical properties and lower susceptibility to such variation.

In summary, these results suggest that conventional 3D conformal radiotherapy is associated with deleterious changes in the microarchitecture and bone mass of the mandibular condylar process in patients with head and neck cancer. Further studies should investigate whether patients with radiation-induced low bone quality are more susceptible to complications such as mandibular fractures or temporomandibular joint dysfunction.
