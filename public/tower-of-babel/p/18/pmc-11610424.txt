Co‐prevalence of chronic rhinosinusitis and inflammatory bowel disease: A large aggregated electronic health record study

Published: 2024

INTRODUCTION

The prevalence of chronic rhinosinusitis (CRS) in the United States is approximately 10% of the population. 1 One of the mechanisms believed to underlie the pathogenesis of this inflammatory disease is epithelial barrier disruption accompanied by inflammation. 1 Similarly, inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), which includes subtypes of ulcerative colitis (UC) and Crohn's disease (CD), affects approximately 1.4 million Americans and is also thought to result from disruption of the epithelial barrier. 2

Due to the inflammatory nature of both diseases secondary to epithelial barrier disruption, our study investigates the co‐prevalence of CRS and IBD (including its subtypes) within the US population. Additionally, it seeks to elucidate the temporal association between CRS and IBD development.

METHODS

Study design

This observational study utilizes the US Collaborative Network from the TriNetX Analytics platform, a federated health research network that aggregates and de‐identifies electronic health record (EHR) data from more than 105 million patients, across 64 US healthcare organizations.

Data extraction and analysis

A bidirectional approach was utilized, first by examining IBD (and its subtypes) and non‐IBD patient groups for ≥2 CRS encounter diagnoses, then by examining CRS and non‐CRS patient groups for ≥2 IBD (and its subtypes) encounter diagnoses. Prevalence values, relative risks, and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated. To investigate the temporal relationship between the two conditions, we explored the risk of patients within the IBD and CRS cohorts developing a new diagnosis of either CRS (in the IBD group) or IBD (in the CRS group) at any time after the initial encounter diagnosis.

RESULTS

The study demonstrates a 4.04‐fold elevated likelihood of CRS in individuals with existing IBD compared to the non‐IBD group (Table 1). UC patients exhibited a 4.26‐fold risk of having CRS, and CD patients had a 3.45‐fold increased risk of having CRS. This study similarly shows a 4.23‐fold elevated likelihood of IBD in individuals with existing CRS compared to the non‐CRS group, with a higher relative risk of UC (RR: 4.48) than CD (RR: 3.56) (Table 2). This suggests a stronger association between UC and CRS than CD and CRS.

DISCUSSION

Our results align with existing literature exploring the association between IBD and CRS. In 2022, Dhillon et al. reported an elevated prevalence of IBD among 92 individuals with CRS compared to the general Canadian population. 3 A single institution investigation in 2003 by Book et al. studying the prevalence of CRS in 241 patients with IBD found that 48% of patients with IBD reported chronic sinonasal disease symptoms. 4 Further, a nationwide study conducted in 2021 in Taiwan by Lin et al. encompassing 8313 patients with IBD revealed an increased likelihood of the subsequent development of CRS in comparison to a control group (RR: 1.26, 95% CI: 1.17–1.35). Also consistent with our findings, the relative risk for UC was higher than CD at 1.73 (95% CI: 1.48–2.05) and 1.20 (95% CI: 1.11–1.29), respectively. 5

Our results revealed a five times higher risk of having one or more CRS encounter diagnoses after having ≥2 IBD encounter diagnoses than having one or more encounter diagnoses of IBD after having ≥2 CRS encounter diagnoses. This is consistent with a study conducted in 2021 that stratified 214 patients with coexisting IBD and rhinosinusitis, revealing that 176 patients had IBD preceding CRS, while 38 patients had the reverse sequence. 6

The pathophysiology of CRS is increasingly attributed to epithelial dysfunction, which involves architectural changes and disruption of the epithelial barrier. The disruption allows pathogens to penetrate and triggers an inappropriate immune response leading to chronic inflammation. 1 Likewise, a defective mechanical barrier, facilitating the entry of luminal pathogens into the intestinal walls, plays a key role in IBD development. 2

Other shared mechanisms in the pathophysiology of CRS and IBD include the dysregulation of the adaptive immune system, 7 an imbalance in the microbial composition and diversity, 8 , 9 and the gut–lung axis, which allows migration of bacteria or their products from the gut to the sinuses and vice versa. 10 More research is needed to elucidate factors that may create a potential overlapping etiological relationship between these conditions, including the role of medication.

Limitations

This study has limitations inherent to the use of aggregated EHR data, including inaccurate and inconsistent documentation in the EHR. Additionally, it is challenging to control for confounding variables. For example, providers may be more likely to identify CRS in patients with IBD due to more frequent medical visits.
