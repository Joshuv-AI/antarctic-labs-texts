Enhanced glutathione production by a non-GMO Saccharomyces cerevisiae mutant isolated via acrolein resistance-mediated screening
Authors: Soo Bin Nho, Sang-Hun Do, Seongjin Oh, Yong-Cheol Park, Sun-Ki Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to develop a non-genetically modified organism (non-GMO) Saccharomyces cerevisiae strain with enhanced glutathione production using UV-induced random mutagenesis. Among 30 wild-type strains, S. cerevisiae KACC 48331 was selected for its high glutathione production. A #14 mutant with significantly higher glutathione content was isolated via acrolein resistance screening. Optimal batch fermentation was established at 20 °C and pH 4.5 using a cost-effective medium of molasses and corn steep liquor (CSL). In fed-batch fermentation, the #14 mutant achieved 1.98 g/L of glutathione using molasses and CSL as carbon and nitrogen sources, comparable to yields from expensive complex media containing peptone and yeast extract. Notably, over 99% of the glutathione produced was in the reduced, biologically active form. These results demonstrate the feasibility of using non-GMO yeast and industrial by-products for sustainable and economical glutathione production.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10068-025-01995-9.

Introduction

Glutathione is a biologically active tripeptide composed of glutamic acid, cysteine, and glycine (Kobayashi et al., 2022). It plays a central role in maintaining cellular redox balance, functioning in antioxidant defense, detoxification, and redox homeostasis (Lu, 2013; Vašková et al., 2023). Glutathione exists in reduced (GSH) and oxidized (GSSG) forms, with GSH serving as a potent antioxidant that protects cells from oxidative stress. Owing to its diverse physiological functions, glutathione has gained interest in the food, cosmetic, and pharmaceutical industries (Al-Temimi et al., 2023). In particular, its immune-modulating and skin-brightening effects have increased its popularity in health and wellness markets (Lu et al., 2021).

Saccharomyces cerevisiae has been widely used for glutathione production owing to its Generally Recognized As Safe (GRAS) status, well-characterized genetics, and its extensive use in industrial biotechnology (Engel et al., 2013; Lemos Júnior et al., 2022; Ostergaard et al., 2000). Its natural safety and regulatory acceptance make S. cerevisiae ideal for non-genetically modified organism (non-GMO) approaches, increasingly important in the food and cosmetic industries owing to strict GMO use limitations.

Various strain engineering strategies have been used to further enhance glutathione production in S. cerevisiae. Among these, random mutagenesis techniques such as ultraviolet (UV) irradiation effectively generate phenotypic diversity without using recombinant DNA technology (Jeyachandran et al., 2024). These methods enable selection of mutant strains with enhanced metabolic capabilities, including elevated intracellular glutathione levels, while maintaining non-GMO status. This non-GMO approach is particularly advantageous in the food and cosmetic industry applications, where consumer preference and regulatory frameworks often favor non-GMOs (Adalja et al., 2023; Siddiqui et al., 2022).

Therefore, this study aimed to develop a non-GMO S. cerevisiae strain with enhanced glutathione production through UV-induced random mutagenesis. Efficient screening methods are essential to isolate and identify high-glutathione-producing mutants from many variants. Therefore, resistance to acrolein—a toxic α,β-unsaturated aldehyde detoxified by glutathione conjugation—was used as a functional selection marker (Moghe et al., 2015; Tomitori et al., 2012). This approach effectively identified mutant strains with elevated intracellular glutathione levels, facilitating the development of promising candidates for industrial applications in food and cosmetics (Fig. 1). Finally, to evaluate industrial applicability of the selected high-glutathione-producing strain, fermentation was conducted using cost-effective agro-industrial byproducts—molasses and corn steep liquor (CSL)—as carbon and nitrogen sources (Hajar-Azhari et al., 2018; Singh et al., 2017).Fig. 1Overview of the strain development and process optimization strategy for enhanced glutathione production. Materials and methods

Saccharomyces cerevisiae strains

A laboratory S. cerevisiae strain D452-2 (Hosaka et al., 1992) and 30 wild-type S. cerevisiae strains obtained from the Korean Agricultural Culture Collection (KACC) and Korean Culture Center of Microorganisms (KCCM) were used in this study. Detailed strain information is provided in Table S1.

Media and culture conditions

Wild-type yeast strains were pre-cultured in 5 mL of YP20D medium containing 10 g/L yeast extract, 20 g/L Bacto Peptone, and 20 g/L glucose at 30 °C and 250 rpm for 48 h. For batch fermentation, pre-cultured cells were harvested and inoculated into main cultures to an initial optical density at 600 nm (OD600) of 1.0. The main fermentation was conducted in 100 mL of YCK50D medium (Cha et al., 2004) containing 30 g/L yeast extract, 5 mM cysteine, 0.6 g/L KH2PO4, and 50 g/L glucose. Cultures were incubated at 25, 30, or 35 °C and 250 rpm for 48 h.

For medium composition optimization, molasses (Konem Bio Co., Republic of Korea) and CSL (Shanghai Aladdin Bio-Chem Technology Co., China) were used as carbon and nitrogen sources. In this experiment, molasses concentration was fixed at 10% (v/v), whereas CSL concentrations varied at 0%, 5%, 10%, 15%, 20%, and 25% (v/v) to evaluate its effect on glutathione production. All other fermentation conditions were identical to those described above.

For pH optimization, fermentations were conducted in a 2.5 L bioreactor containing 1 L of YCK50D medium under controlled pH conditions. The pH was constantly maintained throughout the fermentation process at 3.5, 4.5, 5.5, or 6.5. Temperature, agitation speed, and aeration were set at 20 °C, 700 rpm, and 2 vvm, respectively.

Fed-batch fermentation was conducted in a 2.5 L bioreactor (Kobiotech Co., Republic of Korea) containing 1 L of either YP20D medium or a molasses/CSL-based medium. The molasses/CSL medium consisted of 4% (v/v) molasses and 5% (v/v) CSL. After glucose depletion in YP20D medium, a feeding solution containing 200 g/L yeast extract, 100 g/L peptone, and 600 g/L glucose was continuously supplied. Similarly, after sugar depletion in molasses/CSL-based medium, a feeding solution consisting of 50% (v/v) molasses and 25% (v/v) CSL was continuously added. The medium temperature, pH, aeration, and agitation speed were maintained at 30 °C, 4.5, 2 vvm, and 1,000 rpm, respectively.

Screening of mutants with improved glutathione content and concentration

A mutant library was constructed using UV exposure, following the method described by (Lee et al., 2023). Briefly, S. cerevisiae KACC 48331 cells cultured at 30 °C and 250 rpm for 48 h in 5 mL of YP20D medium were exposed to UV radiation to obtain ~2% survival rates. The population was plated on solid YP20D medium containing 15 g/L agar and incubated at 30 °C for 48 h. Thereafter, 376 colonies were randomly selected from solid YP20D plates. The colonies were inoculated into 200 μL YP20D medium containing 14 mM of acrolein, and cultivated at 30 °C and 900 rpm for 48 h. The variants that grew in these conditions were selected and cultured at the flask level, and their glutathione content and concentrations were measured.

Analytical methods

Cell growth was monitored by measuring OD600 using a spectrophotometer (OPTIZEN POP, Mecasys, Republic of Korea). Dry cell weight (DCW) was estimated by multiplying the OD600 value by a conversion factor of 0.6 g/L/OD600. Ethanol concentration was determined using a high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) system (Ultimate 3000, Thermo Fisher Scientific) equipped with a Rezex ROA-Organic Acid H+ column (Phenomenex, USA). Elution was performed with 5 mM H2SO4 at 60 °C and a flow rate of 0.6 mL/min, using a refractive index detector for detection.

Glutathione extraction from S. cerevisiae cells for concentration determination was performed according to a previous study (Hara et al., 2012). The concentrations of reduced (GSH) and oxidized (GSSG) forms of glutathione were measured using an HPLC system (Thermo Fisher Ultimate 3000) equipped with a YMC-Pack ODS-A column (5 µm, 120 Å, 4.6 × 150 mm; YMC, Kyoto, Japan). Separation was conducted at a constant temperature of 30 °C using a mobile phase of 5% (v/v) acetonitrile, 0.05% (v/v) trifluoroacetic acid, and 0.1 M sodium perchlorate at a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min. Detection was performed using a UV detector at 220 nm. Glutathione content (%) was expressed as the glutathione concentration per dry cell weight.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS Statistics software (v.28.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation. A one-way analysis of variance was performed, and statistical significance was assessed using Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) test at a significance level of p < 0.05.

Results and discussion

Selection of a wild-type S. cerevisiae strain for glutathione overproduction

To identify a GRAS yeast strain capable of high glutathione production, 30 wild-type yeast strains were batch-cultured in 100 mL flasks. The top five glutathione-producing strains (KACC 48331, KACC 48332, KACC 48333, KACC 48334, and KCCM 12485) produced 2.6–2.8 times more glutathione than the laboratory strain S. cerevisiae D452-2 (Fig. 2). Among the five candidate strains, S. cerevisiae KACC 48331, originally isolated from Makgeolli brewed in Yeongam, Korea, was selected as the glutathione-producing strain for its superior glutathione yield and development potential. Identification of glutathione-overproducing mutants via acrolein resistance

A genetically diverse S. cerevisiae mutant library was constructed using UV-induced random mutagenesis to isolate strains with enhanced glutathione production. UV irradiation induces DNA damage that can lead to nucleotide alterations and beneficial mutations (Lawrence et al., 1982). A survival rate of 2–3% is generally considered optimal for generating a diverse pool of stable mutants (Shibai et al., 2017; Taloria et al., 2012). Based on this criterion, a mutant library of S. cerevisiae KACC 48331 was constructed using UV irradiation, resulting in a survival rate of approximately 2%. To identify glutathione-overproducing mutants, acrolein was used as a selective agent, given glutathione’s key role in protecting cells from acrolein-induced toxicity (Mano et al., 2009; Patzschke et al., 2015; Vollenweider et al., 2010). When cultured in YP20D medium supplemented with 14 mM acrolein, the parental KACC 48331 strain failed to grow, whereas 21 out of 376 UV-induced mutants exhibited detectable growth. These selected mutants were further analyzed for intracellular glutathione content and concentration. Notably, three mutants showed more than a 50% increase in both glutathione parameters compared to the parental strain. Mutant #14 exhibited the highest improvement, with a 2.3-fold increase in intracellular glutathione content and a 3.2-fold increase in concentration relative to the wild-type strain (Fig. 3). These results suggest that acrolein resistance may be associated with higher glutathione production, though this relationship does not hold under all conditions. Some acrolein-resistant mutants showed reduced glutathione content, suggesting that additional mechanisms—independent of glutathione accumulation—may also confer acrolein resistance. Patzschke et al. (2015) used acrolein as a selective agent to isolate glutathione-overproducing strains through adaptive laboratory evolution (ALE) under gradually increasing concentrations. However, since factors other than glutathione contribute to acrolein resistance, the ALE strategy often yielded strains with reduced glutathione content (Nho et al., 2025). Optimization of culture conditions and medium composition for enhanced glutathione production

To further enhance glutathione production, the effects of culture temperature, pH, and medium composition were evaluated using the #14 mutant with improved glutathione content. Glutathione levels were first assessed at 20 °C, 30 °C, and 35 °C. Although growth was slower at 20 °C than at 30 °C—the optimal temperature for S. cerevisiae growth—the highest intracellular glutathione content and concentration were achieved at 20 °C (Fig. S1). At this temperature, glutathione content and concentration increased by 31% and 24%, respectively, compared to 30 °C. Comparable fermentation profiles were observed at 25 °C and 30 °C, indicating minimal differences in metabolic activity under these conditions (data not shown). In contrast, a significant reduction in glutathione production was observed at 35 °C, potentially due to impaired cell growth under thermal stress (Fig. S1). Therefore, 20 °C was selected as the optimal temperature for glutathione production in the #14 strain.

The effect of pH on glutathione production was further examined under controlled conditions. Fermentations were conducted in a 2.5 L bioreactor containing 1 L of YCK50D medium and a fixed temperature of 20 °C. The #14 mutant was cultured at pH values of 3.5, 4.5, 5.5, and 6.5. Lower pH conditions increased the maximum DCW; however, the highest glutathione content and concentration were observed at pH 4.5 (Fig. S2). Therefore, pH 4.5 was identified as optimal for glutathione production in this strain.

Owing to the high cost of components such as cysteine and yeast extract, the original YCK50D medium formulation was considered economically unsuitable for large-scale industrial fermentation. As a cost-effective alternative, molasses and CSL, by-products of the sugar and corn wet-milling industries, were investigated as nutrient-rich media components (Tan et al., 2016). CSL is a low-cost nitrogen source (Vu and Kim, 2009) and molasses contains approximately 50% fermentable sugars (fructose, glucose, and sucrose), 25% polysaccharides, and vitamins and nitrogenous compounds (Najafpour and Shan, 2003). Glutathione production by the #14 mutant was tested in YCK50D and media composed of 10% (v/v) molasses and varying CSL concentrations (0%, 5%, 10%, 15%, 20%, and 25% v/v). The molasses concentration was fixed at 10% (v/v) to match the sugar content of YCK50D, which contains 50 g/L glucose. Increasing CSL concentration to 5% and 10% (v/v) significantly increased glutathione production concentration, resulting in 2.1- and 2.4-fold improvements, respectively, compared to the YCK50D medium (Fig. 4B). Meanwhile, the highest glutathione content and productivity were observed at a CSL concentration of 5%, showing increases of 30% and 180%, respectively, compared to YCK50D (Fig. 4). Enhanced glutathione production via fed-batch fermentation using the #14 mutant strain

To assess the feasibility of industrial-scale glutathione production, fed-batch fermentation was performed in a 1 L bioreactor using the #14 mutant strain. Process parameters were adopted from preceding optimization experiments, with the medium pH set to 4.5 and the temperature maintained at 20 °C. After sugar depletion in the initial medium containing 4% (v/v) molasses and 5% (v/v) CSL, a feeding solution of 50% (v/v) molasses and 25% (v/v) CSL was continuously added to the bioreactor. Under these conditions, strain growth was limited, achieving a maximum DCW of only 27 g/L after 68 h of cultivation (Fig. S3). Consequently, glutathione concentration reached only 367 mg/L, notably lower than the 791 mg/L achieved under batch fermentation conditions. These findings suggested that optimal fermentation conditions for glutathione production differ between batch and fed-batch systems.

Assuming that the lower temperature (20 °C) enhanced glutathione biosynthesis in batch fermentation and hindered cell growth in the fed-batch system, the culture temperature was increased to 30 °C—the optimal growth temperature for S. cerevisiae—in the subsequent fed-batch fermentation. In addition, glutathione production was compared between the YP-based medium—composed of yeast extract and peptone, commonly used for laboratory-scale yeast cultivation yet less cost-competitive—and the molasses/CSL-based medium. In Crabtree-positive S. cerevisiae, excess glucose promotes ethanol production, reducing energy efficiency and biomass yield (Zhang et al., 2022). To mitigate ethanol accumulation, feeding solutions were added at rates matching the glucose consumption rate, effectively maintaining ethanol levels below 15 g/L while supporting both cell growth and glutathione production. After 20 g/L glucose was depleted in YP20D medium, a feeding solution containing 200 g/L yeast extract, 100 g/L peptone, and 600 g/L glucose was supplied at 2.4−15.8 mL/h. Similarly, after approximately 20 g/L of soluble sugars was consumed in the medium containing 4% (v/v) molasses and 5% (v/v) CSL, the feeding solution composed of 50% (v/v) molasses and 25% (v/v) CSL was introduced at 3.5−13.6 mL/h. As anticipated, elevating the fermentation temperature to 30 °C led to a substantial increase in the maximum DCW, reaching 146 g/L in the YP-based medium and 80 g/L in the molasses/CSL-based medium, compared to the values obtained at 20 °C (Fig. 5). Although the maximum DCW obtained in the molasses/CSL-based medium was 45% lower than that in the YP-based medium, glutathione production levels were comparable, reaching 1.98 g/L in the molasses/CSL-based medium and 2.03 g/L in the YP-based medium. This was attributed to the higher intracellular glutathione content in the #14 mutant when cultured in the molasses/CSL-based medium (2.6%), 87% higher than in the YP-based medium. This result is likely due to the high levels of glutamic acid and glycine, precursors of glutathione, present in CSL (Lusby et al., 1982). After attaining the peak level, glutathione concentrations declined progressively over time (Fig. 5). This decrease is probably caused by glutathione-degrading enzymes in S. cerevisiae, such as γ-glutamyl transpeptidase and Cys-Gly dipeptidase (Baudouin-Cornu et al., 2012). Notably, the #14 mutant produced 1.98 g/L of glutathione with a volumetric productivity of 26.36 mg/L/h in the molasses/CSL-based medium, showing efficiency comparable to genetically engineered strains. For instance, the SKSC222 strain, engineered with a synthetic isozyme system, achieved 2.0 g/L of glutathione with a volumetric productivity of 29.85 mg/L/h under fed-batch fermentation conditions (Jeon et al., 2023).

These findings demonstrate that industrial by-products such as molasses and CSL are cost-effective substrates for glutathione production using non-genetically modified S. cerevisiae strains. This approach also supports the potential use of non-GMO yeast as a sustainable, food-safe platform for industrial glutathione biosynthesis. Further optimization of fermentation strategies may enhance production efficiency and expand the industrial applicability of this system.
