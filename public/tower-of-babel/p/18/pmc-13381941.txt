AgroQuali-FSL: a few-shot deep learning framework with QualiProtoNet for automated quality grading of fruits and vegetables
Authors: Rashmi Mothkur, Bondu Venkateswarlu, S. Gokulakrishnan, U. Pavan Kumar, G. Santhosh Kumar, Santosh Kumar Jankatti, Danthuluri Sudha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
As the supply chain moves towards uniformity, objectivity, and scalability, it is imperative that fruit and vegetable quality grading be conducted using automated methods. Traditional computer vision-based systems are effective only in controlled environments, but are commodity-specific and data-hungry; they require large annotated datasets to train for new defect types or emerging quality categories, making them less flexible. Most models based on CNNs and transformers are highly representational but do not generalise well in scenarios with few labelled samples, making them infeasible for real-life agricultural conditions where quality patterns change rapidly. These constraints further emphasise the urgent need for a flexible architecture to perform resilient quality grading under low-data conditions. In response, this work introduces AgroQuali-FSL, a hybrid deep learning and few-shot learning framework that incorporates an AgroVision-Backbone (a CNN-Transformer feature encoder) and a QualiProtoNet (a prototype-based classification module trained via episodic N-way K-shot learning). We propose a method that integrates a decision fusion scheme to merge the confidence scores from the backbones and the distance to the prototypes, thereby improving prediction stability, and also summarises the model’s reasoning in an interpretable manner via Grad-CAM visualisations. The experiments on the Fruits Fresh and Rotten dataset and multiple defect datasets validate the effectiveness of our model, as it outperforms baseline CNN, ViT and ResNet models with substantial gains on both supervised and few-shot settings. AgroQuali-FSL, in particular, gains ∼3–7% accuracy in 1- and 5-shot cases and ∼2–4% gains via decision fusion-based refinement. Our results further demonstrate that AgroQuali-FSL is a resilient, scalable, and explainable system that can be tested in real-world agricultural grading systems and requires only a small amount of labelled data for fast adaptation to new types of commodities and defects.

Introduction

Intensive research has been directed towards computer vision–based systems for fruit and vegetable quality grading due to increasing pressure for agricultural supply chains to become industrialised. Although manual examination persists in many areas, it is inherently subjective, labour-intensive, and inconsistent across inspectors and locations. Automated deep learning–based solutions, however, have been shown to provide a viable alternative given the increasing demand for standardised and scalable grading procedures from international markets. Initial investigations mainly focused on ripeness detection, size classification, and surface defect identification of different commodities, including mango, tomato, and apple, using conventional convolutional neural networks (CNNs)1. Recent approaches have included transformer-based architectures and hybrid deep learning pipelines to better model global context and achieve illumination-invariant robustness2. Even with such advances, most of the systems mentioned still rely on fully supervised learning paradigms that require an enormous number of annotated datasets, ultimately hindering their real-world applicability in the field, where new defect types and quality categories often emerge with few labelled samples.

An in-depth review of the literature identifies several ongoing problems. First, (supervised) quality-grading models are static and thus cannot generalise to unseen quality classes, requiring expensive retraining3. Second, while hybrid CNN–Transformer and CNN–LSTM models offer greater representational power at the cost of increased computational cost, they remain data-hungry and do not fully address the label-scarce problem. Third, existing solutions are commodity-specific and thus limited in their ability to scale across a wide variety of fruits and vegetables, restricting their applicability across heterogeneous agricultural ecosystems. All these limitations together highlight the need for a flexible framework that can learn to generate strong visual embeddings while generalising reliably to unseen quality classes with very sparse annotation.

Motivated by the need to build solutions to tackle these issues, this work presents AgroQuali-FSL. This few-shot learning infrastructure efficiently combines hybrid feature extraction and prototype-based few-shot learning to enable reliable fruit and vegetable quality grading. Our framework integrates a CNN–Transformer feature encoder that captures both local defect patterns and global appearance patterns, along with a few-shot classification module designed for low-data regimes. Moreover, we adopt a decision-fusion mechanism to stabilise predictions across visually ambiguous grading for commodity types and defect classes.

This research contributes to three aspects. To begin with, the proposed AgroQuali-FSL framework presents a task-oriented hybridisation of a few-shot learning prototype, using a prototype-based approach with a hybrid CNN/Transformer backbone to facilitate the effective modelling of fine-grained defect patterns by jointly capturing local texture and global contextual information. This coordinated design enables strong adaptation to the new quality grades using a small number of labelled samples. Second, they use an episodic N-way K-shot training paradigm to learn stable, discriminative class prototypes that enable generalisation across a wide range of fruit, vegetable, and defect categories without retraining. Third, an explainability grad-cam-based decision model is added to provide interpretable visual explanations of model predictions, increasing transparency and usability in agricultural inspection processes. The comprehensive experimental assessment of the proposed framework using various publicly available datasets demonstrates that it consistently outperforms CNN, Vision Transformer, and transfer-learning baselines in both supervised and few-shot tasks.

Few-shot, meta-learning, and prototype-based approaches in agriculture and beyond

The biggest bottleneck in building a high-quality grading model is data scarcity, especially for rare grades, new cultivars, or region-specific standards. To tackle this issue, few-shot learning (FSL) and meta-learning are increasingly being employed in NLP and related domains. Yang et al.41 provided a survey of few-shot learning in smart agriculture, focusing on disease classification, pest identification, and phenotypic trait characterisation with scarce annotated data. Few-shot learning for classifying biotic stresses in coffee leaves has been demonstrated by Tassis and Krohling42, who achieved generalisation to new classes or stress types with just a few samples using a meta-learned model. Porto et al.43 presented a more exhaustive literature review of few-shot and meta-learning in agriculture, describing challenges such as domain shift, task heterogeneity, and imbalance, which remain open43.

Xue et al.44 at the application level, integrated an improved YOLOv7 with few-shot learning to detect small impurities in tobacco stems, tackling the challenge of sparse anomaly data in industrial processing. Lately, Singh and Sanodiya45 proposed a zero-shot transfer learning framework for plant leaf disease classification using semantic information to recognise classes that were never seen before. Continual learning for not only wheat disease classification in Alharbi et al.46, where the models can be incrementally updated based on new disease instances, but also for data-limited disease classification is possible. Peng et al.47 proposed a convolutional Transformer–based few-shot method for cross-domain hyperspectral image classification, demonstrating that hybrid CNN-Transformer architectures can effectively address spectral and spatial variations with minimal supervision. Dong et al.48 introduced a support-sample synthesis model for few- or zero-shot surface defect recognition, leveraging generative and contrastive learning to synthesise informative prototypes.

Few-shot and metric learning approaches from outside agriculture are also broadly applicable. Rezaei et al.49 few-shot learning (FSL) has been applied to plant disease detection in low-data scenarios and is thus likely applicable to agricultural imaging. Siraj et al.50 proposed a few-shot lightweight SqueezeNet architecture for induction motor fault diagnosis using limited, if not scarce, thermal images, demonstrating better deployability and efficacy. Shi et al.51, Proto-Meta: Few-Shot Meta-Learning to Inferring Power Systems Waveform Patterns From Scarce Labels, Zhang et al.52 sample poverty few-shot classification feature space representations metric-learning few-shot classification solution. Ijaz et al.53 used Gramian Angular Field transformations to operate on small samples of faults and combined transfer learning with prototypical networks for fault classification in wireless sensor networks. Yang et al.54 however, the scope of these methods can be expanded to be more comprehensive, and recent advances in the field aim to fill some of these gaps55,56. For example, we showed how causal interventions can improve few-shot named entity recognition, and demonstrated that current debiasing strategies stabilise prototypes when the training dataset is too small. Siamese GC Capsule Networks based on capsule structures and adjusted cosine similarity were designed by Zhang et al.55 for small-sample cow face recognition and yielded stable identifications, but only with limited sample sizes.

Latest developments in single-stage detection have also enhanced the efficiency of defect localisation and readiness for deployment. Wang et al.57 created YOLOv9, which uses programmable gradient information to learn features and detect accurately. Wang et al.58 proposed YOLOv10, an NMS-free end-to-end architecture that minimises post-processing overheads while maintaining real-time functionality. The resulting developments justify the use of modern YOLO-based architectures for detecting and localising agricultural defects. Together, this work shows that prototype-based and metric-learning frameworks can perform well in low-data regimes but remain vulnerable to domain shift, background noise, and class imbalance, the three primary modalities of the AgroQuali-FSL problem setting.

Preliminaries

This section presents the theoretical background to support the AgroQuali-FSL framework. It begins by introducing deep learning concepts relevant to fruit and vegetable quality evaluation and challenges, including texture heterogeneity and sparse annotations. It then presents the concepts of few-shot learning and prototypical networks and motivates the use of meta-learning to quickly adapt to grading scenarios with poor-quality data.

Deep learning for food quality evaluation

Deep learning has quickly become the state-of-the-art approach for visual inspection and quality assessment of agricultural produce, especially fruits and vegetables. A CNN can automatically learn hierarchical features that map colour, texture, and shape differences associated with ripeness, freshness, and surface defects such as bruises, cuts, or fungal infections. CNN representations adapt to complex illumination changes, non-uniform backgrounds, and intra-class variability across cultivars. They are thus more suitable than hand-crafted features for deployment in real-world environments, e.g., grading and sorting. In agriculture, high-quality labelled datasets are costly to curate, since expert graders need to assess produce across multiple maturity stages, storage conditions, and defect severities. In addition, commercial grading lines often include new varieties, packaging conditions, or defect categories not present in the original training set. This introduces a train-deploy drift, as the supervised models require expensive retraining because they cannot handle the gap between the training and deployment environments.

The second challenge in quality grading based on deep learning is that adjacent quality grades differ very little visually. The salient features of coarse-grained classification, such as distinguishing between Grade A and Grade B fruit, are based on subtle variations in glossiness, localised blemish density, or non-uniformity of colour that may cover only a small fraction of the image. To capture such details, deeper CNN architectures and attention or Transformer-based modules are often combined to integrate local texture cues with global contextual information. In this context, the feature extractor must not only be discriminative but also invariant to noise, viewpoint changes, and heterogeneous imaging setups (mobile-phone cameras, conveyor-belt cameras, and controlled laboratory rigs).

In addition, food quality assessment datasets are inherently imbalanced, as samples with rare defects or borderline quality grades are typically underrepresented. As a result, standard training strategies skew predictions towards dominant classes, leading to performance degradation on minority but practically relevant courses (e.g., early spoilage or minor but perceptible storage harm). This imbalance is compounded when scaling to multiple fruit and vegetable types, for which each commodity has at most a few hundred labelled examples across multiple quality states. This has led to a rising demand for models with a shared structure across commodities and quality levels, as well as the ability to specialise quickly to new classes (with few data points).

Such limitations encourage using representation learning combined with data-efficient paradigms. Fundamentals of few-shot learning and prototypical networks

Few-shot learning is one of the main limitations of deep models, namely their inability to generalise reliably without massive annotated datasets. When it comes to quality grading of fruits and vegetables, new defect types, maturity stages, or commodity varieties encountered are typically labelled with only a few examples. Open Set Recognition Method via Multi-Label Generative Classifier. Few-shot learning attempts to alleviate this limitation by training models to quickly adapt to new, unseen classes with only K labelled samples/class. Rather than learning decision boundaries specific to each class, few-shot models learn transferable inductive biases that define visual structures across different tasks and allow generalisation from limited supervision. Few-shot classification often adopts the N-way K-shot setting, where N classes are sampled and, for each class, K labelled support images are provided to the model, which then classifies query images based on the learned features from the same class.

The idea behind meta-learning is partially operationalised through episodic training, in which the model is exposed to a sequence of small classification tasks that mimic the few-shot setting at deployment time. At each episode, we build a support set and a query set, and the task is to learn an adaptation within an episode that generalises across subsequent episodes. This step motivates the feature extractor to yield discriminative embeddings, using less tuning. Prototypical Networks are a simple yet efficient approach for few-shot classification8 where we represent each class with a prototype defined as the mean embedding of its support examples. Let the support set S comprises K labeled samples for class c, its prototype pc is calculated as the mean of their embeddings. Given a query sample q, it is classified by comparing its distance to each class prototype in the embedding space and assigning it to the class with the minimum distance. with d(·) being the appropriate distance function like Euclidean or cosine distance. Prototypes represent the centre of mass of each class in the embedding space, which removes variability even with very few support samples, making this formulation especially well-suited to agricultural settings where defect categories or new quality stages may have only a few samples available for training.

What is delightful with Prototypical Networks is that you do not need to retrain every time you want to add a few classes, but only compute the new prototypes from θ which has generalization learnt from all the classes. This characteristic is essential for applications in quality grading of fruits and vegetables, where new anomalies or intermediate or ratit stages are often present. Training the embedding function via episodic meta-learning makes the model sensitive to slight variations while remaining robust to task shifts. Thus, few-shot learning provides a suitable solution for quick adaptability, enabling the agricultural quality grading system to scale seamlessly across goods, seasons, and changing quality parameters without a significant annotation overhead. Materials and methods

This section details the key public datasets, preprocessing methods, and the feature-extraction plan using the AgroVision-Backbone. This section describes the QualiProtoNet few-shot classifier, the episodic meta-learning procedure, the decision mechanisms used for quality grading, and the evaluation metrics and baseline configurations used for empirical validation.

Summary of AgroQuali-FSL framework

A unified deep learning framework, AgroQuali-FSL, shown in Fig. 1, is proposed for automatic fruit and vegetable quality grading in few-label data settings. The architecture sequentially integrates representation learning, meta-learning, and prototype-based classification, allowing efficient generalisation across a wide range of produce types and grading standards. We present a novel framework that builds on an adapted collection of key datasets, including large-scale, class-diverse datasets, a binary \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{D}_{FR}$$\end{document} dataset, a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{D}_{FS}$$\end{document} dataset, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{D}_{DEF}$$\end{document} images. These datasets, as a whole, provide the visual variation needed for learning generic representations and support realistic few-shot settings where only a few instances are available at certain quality levels.

System-level architecture of the AgroQuali-FSL framework for few-shot fruit and vegetable quality grading.

A uniform preprocessing step for every input image, including resizing, background removal, and colour normalisation, is shown to ensure consistent feature extraction across various imaging conditions. Next, the preprocessed images pass through the AgroVision-Backbone. This hybrid feature encoder fuses convolutional layers for localised texture and defect sentiment analysis and Transformer layers that learn global colour consistency and ripeness patterns. Based on few-shot classification, this encoder is designed to yield discriminative embeddings. Taking these embeddings as a starting point, QualiProtoNet employs a prototypical learning strategy: support examples combine to form category prototypes, and query samples are mapped to these prototypes in the embedding space based on their distance to them.

The whole system is episodically meta-learned to allow the model to generalise rapidly to new fruit types or even new defect categories, whilst the simulated tasks involve only few-shot learning. At inference time, it integrates new classes by simply computing their prototypes without retraining the feature extractor. The output shows how quality is predicted, and to help interpret the prediction, it is accompanied by Grad-CAM to highlight regions for each quality grade. This end-to-end design is both data-efficient and scalable across the whole grading lines and sorting systems, and is well-suited for practical deployment in Intelligent Agricultural Inspection platforms (IIAPs).

Datasets and data preparation

The AgroQuali-FSL framework, which we propose in this study (using four publicly available datasets), provides an effective solution to the challenges posed by diversity in quality conditions, limited available labelled samples, and differences across fruit and vegetable categories. Fruits 36063 – it is an extensive dataset of high-resolution images of more than ninety classes - fruits and vegetables - collected using controlled conditions. The dataset is used to pretrain the AgroVision-Backbone, enabling the model to learn generic colour, texture, and shape features that transfer well to the downstream quality-grading task. The second dataset is known as Fruits Fresh and Rotten64, which contains binary labels for fresh vs. rotten samples of common fruits and is used as a core benchmark for freshness condition detection tasks. The dataset has a clear label structure and is well-balanced, making it useful for analysing model performance on basic quality differentiations.

As agricultural environments often involve many low-data situations, two additional datasets are introduced to better mimic realistic conditions. We use small fresh–stale image collections65 containing images of varying freshness, captured under unconstrained backgrounds, to facilitate few-shot evaluation. These data sets model real-life conditions (only a few annotated samples exist for particular freshness levels or specific produce types). Additionally, crop-specific defect datasets, such as mango or apple defect collections66, include images with fine-grained defect classes, including anthracnose, bruising, spots, and early spoilage features. With relatively small size yet fine-grained differences in quality, these datasets pose more challenges. They are suitable for evaluating QualiProtoNet’s adaptability in the N-way K-shot setting.

All images from these datasets pass through a uniform preprocessing pipeline designed to mitigate visual variability and make feature extraction robust to perturbations. As above, images are resized to 224 × 224 pixels for use within the AgroVision-Backbone architecture. HSV thresholding followed by morphological operations to reduce colour and illumination variations due to capture under different conditions, to perform background normalisation. Images are next transformed into the RGB and HSV colour spaces to retain important information related to fruit ripeness. Normalises pixel intensities to the [0,1] range to reduce sensitivity to lighting changes. During training, a collection of augmentations random rotation, flip (horizontal and vertical), brightness jitter, and Gaussian noise is applied to enhance generalisation.

Once the combined dataset has been preprocessed, we can split the data for hierarchical learning. Only samples from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{D}_{F360}$$\end{document} are used for feature encoder pretraining, and we use \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{D}_{FR}$$\end{document} dataset images for baseline supervised fine-tuning and evaluation. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{D}_{FS}$$\end{document} and the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{D}_{DEF}$$\end{document} datasets are exclusively set aside for few-shot episodic training and testing. The skilful arrangement of datasets ensures that the AgroQuali-FSL system is trained on a wide range of visual diversity. In contrast, stringent evaluation under limited-sample conditions corresponding to real-life agricultural quality inspection situations relies.

To ensure a rigorous evaluation of few-shot generalization, strict class-wise separation is maintained across all stages of training and testing. Specifically, the classes used for backbone pretraining (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{D}_{F360}$$\end{document}), supervised evaluation (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{D}_{FR}$$\end{document}), and few-shot meta-learning tasks (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{D}_{FS}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{D}_{DEF}$$\end{document}) are mutually disjoint. Furthermore, within the few-shot setting, classes sampled during meta-test episodes are entirely unseen during meta-training, ensuring that the model is evaluated on novel categories without prior exposure.

AgroVision-backbone: CNN–transformer feature encoder

As shown in Fig. 2, this is the Feature Extraction Module of the AgroQuali-FSL framework, which captures orthogonal features by leveraging the complementary capabilities of convolutional neural networks and Transformer-based attention mechanisms. This hybrid architecture will be able to extract both localised features related to surface defects and more general features related to ripeness, colour uniformity, and overall fruit/vegetable shape. Each preprocessed input image is fed into the backbone, a series of convolutional layers that learns local texture descriptors, such as blemishes, wrinkles, and fungal spots, as well as finer degradations. The convolutional responses reveal discriminative visual cues through an abundance of spatial proximity and hierarchical feature learning. Although convolutional layers are good at capturing local texture, global patterns, such as average colour, uniformity, and shape consistency, are also relevant for assessing fruit and vegetable quality. The multi-head self-attention implemented in the Transformer module is used to model long-range dependencies, so that the network can link together distant parts of the image and learn how local patterns generalize to ripeness (or decay) over the whole fruit. Episodic training strategy and optimisation

The episodic training strategy used in AgroQuali-FSL closely resembles the real-world few-shot scenario that fruit and vegetable quality grading needs to operate under. Rather than training on class-balanced, large batches, this system is trained with a series of N-way K-shot episodes, where each episode is a small classification problem involving a few samples. Such an episodic formulation allows our model to learn inductive biases that enable it to quickly adapt to new quality classes, defect types, and commodity categories. A pool of classes is created from the available training pool, and N classes are sampled from it. K labelled support images and query images are then used to create an episode for evaluating performance. These samples are fed into AgroVision-Backbone to produce embeddings, which serve as the base representations for prototype computation and query classification.

In each episode, class prototypes are calculated from the support embeddings (using (5)), and the query image embeddings are compared with these prototypes to obtain predicted labels. The episodic loss is calculated as a cross-entropy function over distances between query embeddings and class prototypes, promoting class clustering in embedding space whilst also separating samples from different classes. Since class boundaries can be memorised, this loss is consistent with the few-shot learning objective—encouraging structures in the feature space to generalise. The model parameters are optimised with the Adam optimiser, and a learning rate is chosen to balance stability and convergence speed. Training is further stabilised by sampling meta-validation episodes from held-out classes after every few episodes to test generalisation performance, ensuring the model is not overfitting to a specific commodity or defect pattern.

This flexibility is one of the main advantages of this episodic strategy, as it allows the embedding function to learn from episodes with different configurations (e.g., number of fruits, variations in quality categories, and so on). Since episodes consist of different class compositions and support–query set distributions, the model can learn features that are discriminative under varied sampling conditions. This guarantees that the embedding function \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{f}_{\theta\:}\:$$\end{document}is generalized enough to form separated prototypes even when classes are not seen. Furthermore, during inference, parameters do not need to be updated, as we compute new prototypes from available support examples, making our system deployable across environments that require rapid adaptation. The AgroQuali-FSL framework is trained with episodes of diverse tasks and continuous meta-updates, which help it reach a good compromise between stability and adaptability, leading to a good balance between computational efficiency as a model and robustness in a low-data regime. It has been learned that this is the case for agricultural quality grading.

Quality grading and decision fusion

In the last step of quality grading in the AgroQuali-FSL framework, prototype-based classification is integrated with a decision-fusion mechanism to provide generalizable, interpretable quality estimates at the fruit and vegetable levels. We then attach the AgroVision-Backbone to an inductive classifier based on the scoring function, which retrieves class prototypes from the support set and classifies each query sample by comparing its embedding with the class prototypes. From Eq. (2), the predicted class is the one with the minimum distance to the prototype. Although, as a prototype-based decision, this provides a perfect baseline accuracy, it lacks the careful, standard, and detailed interpretation of quality in the agricultural context due to the need to reflect both global appearance and localised defect features.

To improve decision-making robustness, we design a fusion scheme that combines complementary cues from the embedding space, colour distributions, and defect severity scores obtained during preprocessing and via attention responses. Where classes share similar traits — for example, slight bruising vs. early-stage decay — these additional cues bolster decision-making. During this fusion process, the decision based on prototype-based prediction serves as the main driver, while secondary evidence in the form of auxiliary indicators modifies or confirms the assigned quality grade. Assuming the prototype distance alone favours a high-grade label. Still, if the defect severity exceeds a preset threshold, the decision fusion module may re-adjust the quality classification to indicate visual deviations.

Specifically, the fusion mechanism, via a weighted combination, integrates prototype similarity and auxiliary feature responses to yield a refined confidence score for each quality class. With these confidence scores, the system can determine subtle differences in how fresh or damaged an item is, and also support multiple grades (e.g., grade A, grade B, or grade C) in addition to binary fresh–spoiled outcomes (Schuetz et al. 2018). In inference, fusing maintains the final quality label, ensuring that predictions remain stable across natural variations in illumination, colour, and small defects/structures. Grad-CAM visualisations help identify the areas that needed the most attention, enabling graders to understand what the model leveraged to execute its classification. AgroQuali-FSL thus achieves a more robust, context-dependent quality grading result for real-life agricultural inspection through practical prototype-based inference and evidence-aware fusion.

Explainability using Grad-CAM

In agricultural inspection workflows, accurate but interpretable classification decisions are vital to facilitate trust, accountability, and adoption of automated quality grading systems. Thus, visual explanations are crucial as they provide an explicit visual signal showing which regions of an image influence model predictions, and are not assumed to be directly in-line with human salience. This interpretability assists inspectors and stakeholders in making informed decisions and enables the responsible adoption of automation grading systems in practical agricultural environments.

Grad-CAM is identified in the AgroQuali-FSL framework to visualise the spatial regions responsible for the predicted quality grade. When a query image is processed through AgroVision-Backbone and classified via prototype-based comparison, we apply Grad-CAM based on gradients flowing into the last convolutional layer to generate a localised importance map. The gradients tell us how each feature map channel affects the class decision, and together they form a class-discriminative heatmap that is then projected onto the original image.

We generate this heatmap by taking the global average pooling of the gradients to obtain the importance weight of each convolutional channel k for class c. This process combines a linear combination of these weights with the corresponding individual activation maps from the final convolutional layer to form the Grad-CAM localisation map. Then it maintains only the positive contributions from the individual activation maps through a ReLU. The class-discriminative map can be represented as6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{L}_{\mathrm{Grad-CAM}}^{c}=\mathrm{ReLU}\left(\sum\:_{k}{\alpha\:}_{k}^{c}{A}^{k}\right),$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{A}^{k}\:$$\end{document}is the activation map of channel k, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\alpha\:}_{k}^{c}\:$$\end{document}is the importance weight for class c derived from gradient. This provides us with a spatial representation that shows which regions contributed most to whether the model classified the image as defective, including areas of bruising, discolouration, fungal spots, and other defect patterns. The heatmap shows, when overlaid on the input image, whether the model’s prototype-based decision correlates with meaningful quality indicators or whether irrelevant artefacts e.g., lighting shifts, background textures, etc. may have driven the prediction.

Since the decision pipeline of AgroQuali-FSL follows the same series as Grad-CAM, using this method allows for a more transparent model that can be more easily implemented in practical agricultural quality control settings. The predictions of higher grades typically align with heatmaps that focus on areas where the surface is intact and defect-free, whereas the predictions of lower grades point to localised areas of impurity. This mechanism of interpretability empowers both inspectors and practitioners to review automated decisions, trace back failures, and confirm consistent grading across changes in image domains. By integrating few-shot classifiers (to limit the cost of an expensive annotated dataset) with gradient-based semantic visual explanations, the AgroQuali-FSL framework provides an out-of-the-box solution for robust, accountable automatic quality grading of fruits and vegetables for on-site inspection and sorting (with robustness to diverse real-world operating conditions).

The uniqueness of the new framework introduced is that the components are designed in harmony to be used in unison and not in isolation. Although the CNNs, Transformers and prototype learning are investigated separately, their integration to address the problem of quality grading in agriculture along with a fusion-based stabilization mechanism allow them to achieve better generalization in case of limited data.

Evaluation metrics and baseline models

Standard classification metrics used to measure predictive reliability follow the AgroQuali-FSL framework20 and are used to evaluate its performance across different fruit & vegetable quality categories. Since agricultural quality grading is generally an imbalanced dataset, where some defect types or intermediate freshness levels occur in fewer numbers, multiple metrics are utilised to achieve a balanced view of the model behaviour. Overall accuracy is the primary evaluation metric, which calculates the ratio of correctly predicted samples in the total number of query samples. Although accuracy is a global estimate of predictive performance, it can be deceptive in cases where the class distributions are skew. We thus employ precision and recall to be as precise as possible in capturing classifier reliability for defect-prone or minority quality categories. Precision tells us how many of the predictions described as positives are actually positives, and recall is the ratio between actual positives and ones that are accurately identified. These are expressed as8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{Precision}=\frac{TP}{TP+FP},$$\end{document}9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{Recall}=\frac{TP}{TP+FN},$$\end{document}

Using the F1-score, we can particularly balance precision and recall, which is desirable when misclassifying between successive quality grades impacts grading outcomes. The F1-score is the harmonic mean of precision and recall and is defined as10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{F1-score}=2\times\:\frac{\mathrm{Precision}\times\:\mathrm{Recall}}{\mathrm{Precision}+\mathrm{Recall}},$$\end{document}

Such an evaluation metric is beneficial for few-shot performance evaluation related to specific quality grades or defect states, given the limited available samples.

Along with these metrics, confusion matrices are produced to categorise misclassifications by quality, helping identify systematic errors such as confusion between early decay and mild bruising. To assess the effectiveness of the QualiProtoNet-based few-shot learning mechanism, several baseline models are implemented under the same training and evaluation settings. We consider the following baselines: a CNN-only model, a pure ViT encoder, and the traditional transfer-learning backbones ResNet50 and MobileNet. All baselines are trained via standard supervised learning on the core quality-grading dataset, providing a reference point for evaluating the benefits of prototype-based inference and episodic meta-learning. By combining quantitative metrics with baseline comparisons, the performance of AgroQuali-FSL is rigorously assessed, providing an in-depth evaluation of the proposed method in low-data regimes for agricultural quality assessment.

Ablation studies

We performed several ablation experiments to verify the contribution of different components in the AgroQuali-FSL framework, as well as other factors affecting few-shot performance. The first ablation aims to evaluate the impact of the hybrid CNN-Transformer architecture by removing one component. The transformer block was shown to improve accuracy. When it was removed, the model appeared to rely solely on convolutional features, leading to a steady decline in accuracy across all shot settings, especially on defect datasets where global colour and shape cues are significant factors. On the other hand, without the CNN block, the network was unable to capture more subtle texture details, such as bruising or signs of early decay, and thus continued to mistake similar-looking quality classes for one another. This work argues that embeddings should include both local and global representations to create an adequate embedding space for prototype-based classification.

Regarding data augmentation, the pretraining procedure was repeated without random rotations, flips, or colour jitter. The omission of augmentation resulted in significant performance drops on few-shot tasks, with the most pronounced reductions observed on 1-shot tasks. Without augmented variability, the embedding space became more sensitive to orientation and lighting conditions, as well as minor surface deviations, and prototype formations became less stable. The overarching theme of this experiment is the need to expose the model to diverse transformations during the pretraining phase to generalise well across heterogeneous agricultural images.

Ablation results are summarised in Table 5, with backbone components, augmentation, distance metrics, and the prototype strategy each contributing to few-shot performance. An additional analysis was conducted to evaluate the effect of distance metrics used when comparing prototypes. Although the default metric is Euclidean distance, we also tried cosine similarity, but it generally led to lower performance and smaller, less compact clusters in the embedding space. This issue was exacerbated in datasets where assured features, such as subtle textural cues, were needed, indicating that a magnitude-sensitive metric, such as the Euclidean distance, better matches learned feature distributions. This strengthens the rationale for choosing the Euclidean distance to achieve consistent performance in few-shot classification.

Lastly, the prototype computation strategy was examined by comparing simple mean-based (i.e., unweighted) prototypes with weighted prototypes computed from embedding norms. At the same time, weighted prototypes provided some benefit at high-shot levels; however, they caused instability at 1-shot and 5-shot levels due to their sensitivity to outlier embeddings. It confirmed the suitability of mean-based prototypes, as they showed more stable performance across all datasets. In summary, the ablation studies confirm the design choices made in the AgroQuali-FSL framework and demonstrate QualiProtoNet’s superior performance compared to other approaches under varying and few-shot conditions.

The organisation may explain the Euclidean distance’s better performance in the learned embedding space. The AgroVision-Backbone is trained to produce feature representations of samples from the same class as compact clusters, with significant spatial dissimilarity across classes. Here, the Euclidean distance is an effective measure of magnitude and location differences between embeddings, consistent with the prototype-based clustering objective of QualiProtoNet. Conversely, cosine similarity is based solely on angular orientation and does not account for magnitude; hence, it is not very sensitive to changes in inter-class distances. Consequently, Euclidean distance offers a more discriminative measure of the separation in closely similar quality categories in few-shot scenarios.

Decision fusion evaluation

The Decision Fusion module was specifically tested to demonstrate improvements in the robustness of final quality-grading outputs when multiple predictive cues are combined. First, we evaluated the individual classifiers AgroVision-Backbone logits, prototype distance-based predictions, and class-wise confidence scores for their reliability. Prototype distance classification achieved state-of-the-art results in low-shot cases, but it suffered from ambiguity for visually borderline samples, where the distances to different prototypes were similar. On the other hand, although the backbone classifier was very sensitive to defect classes, it was less specific due to noise, illumination changes, and slight differences in texture standards.

To evaluate the advantages of fusion, we conducted a set of experiments using the weighted confidence aggregation rule presented in Sect. 4.6. By combining the complementary strengths of both components, the fusion mechanism continuously improved prediction stability. More specifically, the backbone classifier was responsible for discriminative feature directionality, while prototype distances provided geometric consistency in the embedding space. The joint decision produced greater margin separation between the confidence scores of both the top and second-ranked classes, reducing label ambiguity for complex examples such as early decay or mid-grade freshness categories.

In few-shot agricultural grading, we achieve consistent accuracy gains, as shown in Table 7, by fusing decisions across datasets and shot settings. The results showed that decision fusion increased overall accuracy by 2–4% across most datasets and shot settings, peaking at 1-shot, where prototype estimates are very noisy. Fusion provided further evidence of the potential for a benign combination of estimates via backbone logits, effectively avoiding underestimation in defect-class results due to drift in prototype centroids during training. In addition, class-wise F1-score increases confirmed that fusion improved both precision and recall, especially for visually subtle categories. These results demonstrate that decision fusion is a central stabiliser of AgroQuali-FSL, supporting robustness across crop-commodity types and different imaging conditions.

Explainability analysis

While CNN–Transformer hybrids have seen broad use in generic vision tasks, we include them as they cater to agricultural grading requirements, where decision-making is based on signal cues from local defects and long-range context. For practical grading, quality depends not just on minor blemishes or bruises, but also on the overall uniformity of the surface (e.g., dispersed discolouration and other cues related to ripening or shape). As such, the CNN branch captures coarse-grained textures, while the Transformer branch composes long-context cues across large areas of the fruit surface. Qualitative Grad-CAM visualisations in this section provide evidence for this design choice, as attention spreads over the entire fruit for the class of “healthy/uniform” and then shifts to spatially separated regions of defect for ambiguous grades.

In such a way that only visual cues are seen by observers (in agricultural decision-making), an essential prerequisite for validating and interpreting AI-based quality-grading systems is explainability. For the five critical categories in the MangoFruitDDS dataset (Alternaria, Anthracnose, Black Mould Rot, Healthy, and Stemend Rot), Grad-CAM visualisations were generated to maintain the model’s transparency and reliability. These visualisations emphasis the spatial areas that contribute most strongly to the model predictions, allowing us to qualitatively evaluate whether the AgroQuali-FSL framework is paying attention to defect areas that are medically or agriculturally relevant.

In the case of Alternaria, Grad-CAM heatmaps consistently highlight the dark necrotic lesion at the fruit surface, as those signs of a characteristic concentric fungal spot relate well to human graders. This means that the model correctly focuses on the area in most cases where infection is localised, and not the area that is not of concern, background noise. For Anthracnose, the visualisation highlights the dotted, blotchy black spots on the mango peel, enabling the model to learn the scattered pattern of fungal growth seen in these diseases. In case of Black Mould Rot, Grad-CAM focuses on the greyish, mould-infected area near the stem end, indicating a gradient of discolouration and the presence of greyish fuzz in the tissues (for advanced decay).

For healthy mango samples, the attention is distributed almost uniformly across the entire fruit surface, indicating that the model does not incorrectly attend to noise or texture variations. Such behaviour highlights the generalisation without overfitting the defect pattern at the essence of the system. Lastly, for Stem and Rot, the heatmaps emphasise the unique brown-to-black soft-rot zone at the basal portion of the stem, which aligns with earlier tissue degradation, a phenomenon regularly characterised in post-harvest decay.

To summarise, the Grad-CAM analysis qualitatively explains which image regions affect the AgroQuali-FSL framework’s prediction. The visualisations suggest that the model attends to surface patterns and defect-prone areas relevant to fruit and vegetable quality assessment, irrespective of the disease/degradation type. These results provide quantitative evidence that the AgroVision-Backbone learns salient multi-scale local and global visual cues that inform classification; meanwhile, the QualiProtoNet decision mechanism is structured as a similarity in embedding space. While Grad-CAM is a post-hoc interpretability method and does not provide causal inference, we interpret the attention patterns as evidence supporting the interpretability and potential practical applicability of the proposed framework in agricultural grading workflows.

Summary of findings

Overall, the experimental results validate that AgroQuali-FSL consistently improves performance on both supervised and few-shot quality-grading tasks. Compared to baselines of CNN and transformer-only, without recognising the hybrid embedding of the proposed AgroVision-Backbone in the supervised settings, AgroVision-Backbone can indeed converge and yield well-separated embeddings and high accuracy, achieving state-of-the-art performance on the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{D}_{FR}$$\end{document} dataset. QualiProtoNet shows competitive performance on 1-shot, 5-shot, and 10-shot tasks, even in the few-shot setting, with accuracy gradually increasing with the number of support samples (the survey demonstrates the advantages of prototype-based meta-learning in low-data settings).

This framework also demonstrates clear adaptability to new forms of corruption and to unseen categories of material. We show, through experiments on crop-deimo-specific defect datasets and mixed fruit-vegetable collections, that learned embeddings transfer seamlessly across disparate visual domains and that prototypes can be reliably formed for classes never seen during pretraining. AgroQuali-FSL consistently outperforms non-meta-learning baselines and standard transfer learning models (ResNet50 and MobileNet) by achieving higher accuracy, better F1 Scores, and greater robustness, thereby proving it to be a competitive and scalable solution for practical automated quality grading.

Comparison with existing methods

Recent research has tackled automated grading of fruits and vegetables using deep learning, but has mainly focused on a single crop or quality attribute in a fully supervised manner. Sikder et al. show that features extracted using CNNs achieve 96.28% accuracy in predicting the mango ripening stage and compare them with traditional machine learning approaches and CNN- and VGG16-based transfer learning techniques for mango ripeness grading. Its pipeline, on the other hand, continues to rely on large labelled datasets and does not achieve fast adaptability across different cultivars and/or defect categories.

Fischer-Brandies et al. introduce a ResNet50-based framework coupled with Gaussian filtering, achieving balanced accuracies of over 99% in identifying fresh–rotten across eight fruit types. While robust preprocessing and high-accuracy binary decisions are the focus, the model is too rigid for few-shot extension to new defect types or nuanced multi-grade quality labels. Likewise, ViT-SENet-Tom presents a hybrid vision transformer integrated with squeeze–excitation blocks for classifying three tomato classes (ripe, unripe, reject), achieving 99.90% accuracy under fivefold cross-validation. While remaining fully supervised with a small, augmented dataset and no direct rapid adaptation under sparse labels, this architecture remains robust.

Yuan et al. fusion of deep features and temporal dependencies to learn spatial–contextual correlations, implementing a CNN integrated with a BiLSTM for the classification of freshness of fruit and vegetables, attaining 97.76% accuracy. Although this combination achieves greater robustness, it relies on a large number of labelled samples per class. It does not use episodic training nor prototype-based inference for low-data settings. Knott et al. provide a more readily applicable Vision Transformer-based method for fruit quality assessment that requires less development effort and is suited to small datasets without sacrificing performance, achieving close to 1% accuracy, matching the highest-performing CNN while using one-third as many training samples. However, their approach is “few-data friendly”; it avoids a meta-learned few-shot system built with an N-way K-shot episodic design to optimise a standard classifier.

On the other hand, the AgroQuali-FSL framework presented here is explicitly designed for data-scarce scenarios. It incorporates a hybrid CNN-Transformer backbone (AgroVision-Backbone), a few-shot prototype-based grader (QualiProtoNet), episodic N-way K-shot training, decision fusion, and Grad-CAM explainability capabilities. In contrast to supervised models that aim to improve performance for specific defects in commodity-specific datasets, AgroQuali-FSL can quickly adapt to novel defect types and new commodities with just a few labelled examples, while maintaining competitive performance with fully supervised benchmarks. As a qualitative comparison (Table 8), the proposed system takes a step forward by overcoming the limitations of current methods: adaptability for grading across domains, generalisation in low-label regimes, and interpretability in real-life grading environments.

Discussion

Automated assessment of qualitative traits of fruits and vegetables has been well received over the past few years, mainly due to the popularisation of large-scale, objective, on-the-spot evaluation devices in the agricultural supply chain. Conventional computer vision models and early deep learning models have shown strong performance on commodity-specific grading problems, especially when large numbers of annotated datasets are available. Nevertheless, there is still a significant gap in their generalisation across diverse categories of produce, as well as in their adaptability to novel defect types and to the scarcity of annotated data, which often occurs in real agricultural settings. Current CNN-based systems are generally not expressive enough to capture the fine features of minor defects, and transformer-based methods, although very effective, are data-hungry and slow to adapt. In addition, the majority of recent models are fully supervised and therefore lack robustness in environments where new quality classes emerge, but there are not enough labelled instances.

Consequently, the AgroQuali-FSL framework presented here directly tackles these challenges through hybrid deep learning and meta-learning components suitable for low-data agricultural applications. The AgroVision-Backbone is thus characterised by the fusion of CNN layers, conducive to the extraction of fine-grained yet non-spatially mediated features, with long-range contextual dependencies captured by transformer blocks—an architectural synergy that we find better reproduces the representational richness needed to produce imagery that is complex and varied. A discriminator would not converge to a working model for an unseen defect category while only using a few labelled examples; however, QualiProtoNet enables this in part by episodic training, a few-shot learning mechanism that allows our model to not only classify the input data but also prototype inference, which addresses the issue of adaptation to unseen defect categories. The decision fusion strategy, which combines backbone logits and prototype distances, further stabilises predictions by alleviating uncertainty in borderline cases. Together, these novelties form a methodical improvement on fully supervised baselines.

The superiority of the proposed approach is evident from the experimental results. In supervised pretraining, the AgroVision-Backbone outperforms previous work, whether a conventional CNN or a transformer-only model, demonstrating its high-quality embeddings. QualiProtoNet demonstrates considerable improvements at both 1, 5, and 10 shots, showcasing its ability to retain high accuracy in corner cases such as 1-shot. We demonstrate that every step is required: removing the transformer block, the CNN encoder, data augmentations, or the Euclidean distance metric drastically reduces accuracy (ablation studies in 3.4). Combining and fusing the decisions of these methods yields an additional 2–4% improvement in performance across experiments, showcasing the complementary nature of geometric & confidence-based evidence. AgroQuali-FSL is the only solution that maintains high precision while being flexible across various commodities, as evidenced by comparisons with existing methods such as a VGG-based graph for ripeness grading, ResNet50-based rot detection, CNN-BiLSTM for freshness prediction, and ViT-based quality assessment. In summary, this work offers a novel, scalable framework that extends the state of the art in automated quality assessment of plant produce by combining rapid yet explainable inference with efficient data modelling. Providing generalisation over categories and a low label requirement makes AgroQuali-FSL a relevant option for real-world implementation.

Limitations and failure modes

As effective as AgroQuali-FSL is, it has a number of limitations. First, performance is also responsive to the quality and representativeness of the selected support samples in few-shot (or in other words) settings whereby low quality or noisy examples can result in poor estimation of the class prototypes. As a measure of this effect we examine the variability of performance, on a collection of random support sets, of the same N-way K-shot configuration. The findings show that AgroQuali-FSL has a fairly consistent performance overall, though fluctuations in support samples can cause significant changes in the accuracy especially in low-shot (1-shot) cases. This phenomenon is indicative of a sensitivity of prototype representations to the quality and the variety of support cases. Moreover, qualitative observations indicate that prototype centroids can change with respect to the samples of support chosen, and thus affect the boundaries of classes in the embedding space.

Second, the changes in the domain of operation that occur in real-world conditions (e.g. the changes in illumination, the background scene or imaging devices) can negatively influence the generalization performance unless explicitly defined domain adaptation mechanisms are in place. Third, as much as Grad-CAM is a helpful tool in qualitatively understanding the behavior of the model, it is a post-hoc explainability tool, and does not entail causal explanations of how the model arrives at decisions.

Moreover, there can be variability in Grad-CAM explanations between the two chosen support samples to form class prototypes in few-shot learning environments. Given that the embedding space and decision boundary are determined by sparse support examples, attention maps may change between various support set configurations on the same query image. It puts a question mark on the way that the visual explanations are consistent and points at the weakness of using post-hoc interpretability techniques within a meta-learning contexts.

The challenge is aligned with recent findings of interpretability of meta-learning models, where the stability of explanations is influenced by age-changed adaptation and small data credible. Future efforts can consider more robust and intrinsic explainability methods, e.g., prototype-level explainability or attention-consistent learning, to enhance the stability of explanations.

Besides that, the class imbalance in actual-world data can affect the activist of prototype, with underrepresented classes providing fewer examples to the prototype prediction, which may result in biased embeddings. Though, the episodic training approach partially addresses this point through sampling balanced N-way K-shot tasks, the leftover imbalance effects can still have an effect on classification boundaries. To enhance the robustness to imbalanced data conditions, future work can investigate the use of balanced prototype learning and re-weighting methods.

These constraints indicate key future directions for work. Specifically, resilience to counteract sample differences can be enhanced by augmenting the support sets through prototyping, refining prototypes, or even building prototypes with confidence weights. On the same note, domain-adaptive and test-time adaptation strategies can be used to achieve higher performance under distribution shifts, whereas combining supplementary explainability strategies can provide greater insight into how the model explains data. Nevertheless, AgroQuali-FSL offers an excellent platform for further development, as outlined in the next section (Sect. 6.2).

Limitations of the study

While the AgroQuali-FSL framework demonstrates good adaptability and performance across different fruit and vegetable quality classification tasks, several limitations remain to be addressed in future work. First, the accounting is based exclusively on publicly available datasets that, although covering a variety of produce classes, may not be sufficiently diverse to capture the actual variability observed in supply-chain environments (e.g., extreme illumination changes, occlusions, humidity effects, and rapid post-harvest quality changes). The datasets used should be real-world applicable, ideally industry-grade, large-scale, and heterogeneous, and exhibit greater environmental diversity to improve generalisation during deployment.

Second, although the few-shot learning module enables learning new quality classes with only a few samples, the prototype-based inference strategy may be limited in its generalisation when defect types exhibit high intra-class variance or inter-class boundaries are too broad. Consequently, even well-formed prototypes do not necessarily exhaust the class’s structural variation, which may affect classification stability. This limitation could be lessened with further developments in adaptive prototype refinement or generative augmentation.

Thirdly, the present work considers only visual-quality cues. To facilitate accurate grading, numerous real-world systems support multimodal signals, such as hyperspectral signatures, firmness measurements, and volatile compound analysis. However, how the framework behaves under such a multimodal condition remains unexplored to date. The coupling of complementary sensors is likely to increase detection accuracy for low-level external defects or internal quality loss, providing an opportunity for AgroQuali-FSL extension.

Conclusion and future scope

We present the data-efficient, transfer-driven AgroQuali-FSL model, focused on addressing the age-old challenges of automated quality evaluation of fruits and vegetables. The hybrid CNN-Transformer backbone and prototype-based few-shot learning module enable the proposed framework to overcome the major limitations of traditional supervised methods, as the former relies on sizeable annotated datasets and is constrained in adapting to new types of defects. Experimental analyses across various datasets show that AgroQuali-FSL delivers superior performance compared to CNN, Transformer, and hybrid baselines, even in 1-shot, 5-shot, and 10-shot learning settings. Moreover, the decision fusion process increases predictability, and explainability via Grad-CAM enables easy visual reasoning, which can underpin the feasible implementation in real-world agricultural detection systems. All these findings confirm the usefulness of the proposed framework in situations where labelled data are scarce and defect patterns are constantly changing.

In the future, further development of the AgroQuali-FSL would be synchronised with recent advances in artificial intelligence in agriculture, particularly the development of domain-specific foundation models and multimodal learning paradigms. More recent vision-language models, including AgroGPT and AgriCLIP, show that large-scale models can learn fine-grained agricultural semantics and generalise across tasks in low-data settings. Incorporating such base models into the agroqualifsl pipeline can greatly improve its flexibility and applicability across a wide variety of commodities and grading criteria. Simultaneously, multidimensional quality analysis systems are an emerging trend, in which visual information can be enriched with hyperspectral images, textual commentaries, environmental metadata, and sensor-specific measures. Such multimodal integration would enable the system to capture latent quality features, such as internal defects and biochemical differences, that are not visible in RGB images alone.

The other area of significance in the research is the use of test-time adaptation and domain generalisation techniques to address variability in agricultural environments. Shifts in the domain due to lighting conditions, seasonal changes, and differences among commodity types may degrade model performance during deployment. Dynamically updating model representations without retraining can provide a viable solution to this challenge for adaptive inference mechanisms. Moreover, self-supervised learning methods may be combined to minimise dependence on large pretraining datasets and enhance generalisation to heterogeneous conditions.

A continuous developmental process may be imagined to ease the gradual move of the research prototype to a real-world implementation. In the short term, one can consider emphasising multimodal representations and integrating vision-language models to enhance features and improve context perception. During the mid-term, one can work on creating standardised cross-domain benchmarks and including adaptive learning to support high performance in the presence of a distribution change. Over the long run, the framework can be scaled for real-time implementation on edge devices and coupled with IoT-enabled smart agriculture systems to provide automated, scalable quality grading across heterogeneous supply chain contexts. Extensive field validation research will be necessary to test the system’s reliability, scalability, and usability in real-world contexts.

In addition, further work could consider multi-instance and temporal modelling methods to handle commodities with complex surface patterns and quality that changes over time. Robustness in dynamic agricultural situations can be further enhanced by sequence-based learning and continual adaptation strategies. Although this has been performed well, issues such as the efficiency of its performance, access to multimodal databases, and its deployment limitations are essential areas of interest to be explored further. On the whole, AgroQuali-FSL offers a powerful and scalable foundation for creating next-generation agricultural quality assessment systems that are both data-efficient and scalable, readable, and adaptive to real-life circumstances. Further cross-domain benchmarking, e.g., training on one commodity and scoring on another, will be included in future work to further measure the robustness of generalisation.
