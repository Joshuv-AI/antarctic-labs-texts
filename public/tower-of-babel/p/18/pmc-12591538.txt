Measuring quality of life in clinical research - Part 2
Authors: Priya Ranganathan, Jammbe Z. Musoro, C. S. Pramesh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This article deals with the various aspects of health-related quality of life (QoL) assessments, building upon a previous discussion of the concept and its measurement types. Specifically, the article will delve into number and timing of QoL assessments, calculating scores from QoL instruments, determining clinically meaningful changes in QoL, sample size calculation when QoL is an endpoint and handling missing data in QoL assessments.

INTRODUCTION

In the previous article in this series, we examined the concept of health-related quality of life (QoL) and discussed the various types of measures available to assess it.[1] In this article, we will focus on the aspects related to the number and timing of QoL assessments, calculating scores from QoL instruments, determining clinically meaningful changes in QoL, sample size calculation using QoL as an endpoint, and handling missing data in QoL assessments.

NUMBER AND TIMING OF QUALITY OF LIFE ASSESSMENTS

The number and timing of QoL assessments will depend on the condition being studied, the expected course of the disease, the research objectives, and the profile of the interventions being studied. A baseline assessment, collected prior to treatment initiation, is helpful to establish a reference point and identify preexisting differences between groups which could impact outcomes. Sometimes, a baseline evaluation may be the part of the screening process for inclusion in a study. Subsequent assessments are carried out when the maximum impact or change is expected. For example, the short-term impact of systemic chemotherapeutic agents will need to be measured within weeks of chemotherapy, whereas cumulative toxicity may take months or even years. Frequent measurements can lead to participant fatigue, whereas assessments which are spaced too far apart could lead to recall bias, attrition, and loss of valuable information. Therefore, assessments should be scheduled at clinically meaningful timepoints (e.g., treatment cycles and disease milestones) to capture relevant changes over time, with a frequency that balances detecting meaningful effects and minimizing patient burden.

CALCULATING QUALITY OF LIFE SCORES

Standardized QoL questionnaires have an accompanying manual with instructions on scoring responses. Researchers should familiarize themselves with the manual before using the questionnaire. Broadly, numerical values are assigned to the response for each item on the questionnaire. Users need to be aware that negatively worded questions may have reverse scoring. For each domain, a summary score is generated by adding the scores of the individual items. Raw domain scores are converted into a standardized score using a formula provided in the manual. Some questionnaires report only domain scores; others may weight and combine scores across domains to generate a single value for an overall QoL score. Questionnaire scoring manuals also typically include guidance on managing missing responses within multi-item domains. As an example, in the next article in this series, we will discuss the EORTC QLQ C30 questionnaire and how to score it.[2]

CLINICALLY MEANINGFUL CHANGE IN QUALITY OF LIFE

When interpreting QoL outcomes, it is important to go beyond statistical significance and ensure that observed changes are meaningful to patients, as even small mean differences can be statistically significant without reflecting real benefit. The minimum clinically important difference (MCID) defined as “the smallest difference in score in the domain of interest which patients perceive as beneficial and which would mandate, in the absence of troublesome side effects and excessive cost, a change in the patient’s management” is one framework for interpreting QoL data as clinically meaningful.[3] Other terms, such as minimal important difference (MID) and minimally important change (MIC), are used inconsistently and have been critiqued.[4] This heterogeneous terminology complicates the selection of appropriate thresholds and may lead to misapplication in clinical research. It is also critical to distinguish between QoL score interpretation thresholds applied at the individual patient level (meaningful within-patient change) versus the group level (meaningful between-group differences), as confusing these can affect QoL interpretation and sample size calculations. The SISAQOL-IMI project (www.sisaqol-imi.org) has developed a terminology framework to clarify these contexts in clinical trials.[5]

It is difficult to establish a single value or score interpretation threshold for what would be considered a clinically meaningful change in QoL for several reasons. First, there are a variety of QoL instruments, each with different units, domains and ranges of scores. Second, the definition of “meaningfulness” may vary across patient populations, disease conditions, interventions, patient-versus group-level interpretation, improvement versus deterioration, and other characteristics. Third, different stakeholders may have varying perspectives on clinical meaningfulness. For an individual patient, this would require substantial improvement in symptoms or functioning. Clinicians may focus on changes that affect prognosis or treatment. However, for a policy maker, even small changes in QoL would be considered important if many individuals would be benefitted. Therefore, selection of QoL score interpretation thresholds (e.g., MCIDs) must be context-specific, as no single value is appropriate across all settings.

DETERMINING THE SCORE INTERPRETATION THRESHOLDS FOR A QUALITY OF LIFE MEASURE

Broadly, score interpretation thresholds such as MCID can be determined using any of the following methods:

Anchor-based approach

where changes in QoL scores are linked to a change in another parameter (known as the anchor). The anchors could be clinical variables (e.g., radiological response, weight, performance status, and biochemical markers) or patient-reported (e.g., visual analog scale for pain and global rating of change), with patient-based anchors generally preferred. An appropriate anchor should be conceptually related to the QoL domain of interest, available, interpretable, and have good correlation with the QoL measure. Whereas some authors have suggested that a correlation coefficient of 0.3 or above between the anchor and the new scale is acceptable, Guyatt recommend a higher correlation coefficient of 0.5.[6] When possible, it is recommended to apply the multiple anchors to increase confidence in the plausibility of the interpretation threshold estimate.

Different anchor-based methods are applied to estimate interpretation thresholds depending on context. Thresholds for within-group change over time are commonly derived using the mean change method, where the threshold is defined as the average QoL score change among patients classified as improved or deteriorated by the anchor; in trials, a mean change within a treatment arm that meets or exceeds this threshold is considered clinically meaningful. Thresholds for between-group differences are derived using linear regression, with QoL change scores as the outcome and the anchor as a predictor; the estimated slope defines the threshold for improvement or deterioration. A difference in mean change between trial arms that meets or exceeds this threshold is considered clinically meaningful. For within-patient change, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis has been used to derive patient-level thresholds by comparing those who achieve at least a MIC on the anchor with those who do not; this can be used to identify responders and compare responder proportions between treatment groups.[7]

Distribution-based approach

This is based on the statistical properties (distribution) of the QoL scores, such as proportions of the standard deviation (SD) (e.g., 0.2 SD, 0.3 SD, and 0.5 SD), or the standard error of measurement (SEM).[89] For example, if a QoL domain score has a baseline SD of 20 points, an effect size of 0.5 (reflecting a moderate change) would correspond to 0.5 × 20 = 10 points, which could serve as the threshold for interpreting a clinically meaningful change in QoL scores. In general, distribution-based estimates are recommended as supportive evidence for anchor-based estimates, as they do not provide information on clinical relevance from the perspective of patients or clinicians.[8] When determining interpretation thresholds for QoL scores, it is generally recommended to combine anchor-based and distribution-based approaches to enhance confidence in the selected thresholds. For more details, readers are referred to these reviews by Guyatt et al. and Mouelhi et al.[1011]

Liu et al. established the MCID for the QoL scale for chronic diseases of Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (QLICD-SLE).[12] They used one item from the short form health survey (SF-36) as the anchor (“In general, how would you say your health is?”). This was a longitudinal follow-up study and patients filled out the anchor item and the QLICD-SLE questionnaire on the day of hospitalization and just before hospital discharge. The anchor question had a 5-point response scale based on which patients were classified as “no change,” “improved” or “deteriorated” and this was the reference or gold standard. The authors focused on patients who had no change or improved as per the anchor item response and compared this with the difference between scores on the QLICD-SLE scale. As this field evolves, mixed methods combining qualitative and quantitative evidence are increasingly emphasized. Recent reviews and the FDA’s PFDD guidance highlight the value of qualitative patient interviews to complement anchor-based methods, ensuring that clinical relevance is grounded in patient perspectives and outcomes most meaningful to them.[14]

SAMPLE SIZE CALCULATION FOR QUALITY OF LIFE ENDPOINTS

Sample size calculations for QoL endpoints in clinical research depend on the study objective and the selected endpoint (e.g., magnitude of change, time to QoL deterioration, or responder improvement).[15] For example, when aiming on comparing mean change scores between two treatment groups, calculations are generally based on the anticipated difference in mean QoL scores, the variability of the measure, the desired statistical power, and the significance level. When using QoL outcomes, it is important to account for potential missing data and the score interpretation threshold (e.g., MCID) to ensure adequate power to detect clinically meaningful changes. Depending on the study design, additional adjustments may be required for, repeated QoL assessment per subject, or stratification factors.

MISSING QUALITY OF LIFE DATA

In a previous article, we have discussed various ways to deal with missing data in research.[16] With QoL outcomes, this problem is even more relevant because the reason for missing data is often related to poor QoL. Therefore, data are more likely to be missing not at random (MNAR) rather than missing completely at random or missing at random.[16] Standard techniques such as complete case analysis or simple or multiple imputation may give biased results, and advanced modeling methods which account for the MNAR pattern are required. It is recommended to document and report both the reasons and frequency of missing data, and to incorporate this information into sensitivity analyses. Not all unobserved QOL data should be considered missing; for example, QoL data are not expected after death and should be treated differently from data that are unobserved due to a missed visit for instance. This distinction is extensively discussed in the SISAQOL-IMI recommendations.[5]
