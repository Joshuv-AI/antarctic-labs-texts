Mechanism of Scutellaria baicalensis extracellular vesicles in attenuating Mycoplasma gallisepticum-induced inflammation via TRPC1 - STIM1/ORAI1 channel inhibition
Authors: Fangbing Xu, Lige Bao, Yecheng Yao, Yifan Li, Yu Liu, Mingyu Yu, Shun Wang, Weiqi Liu, Zhiyong Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstactMajor ObjectivesThe infection caused by Mycoplasma gallisepticum (MG) has had a significant impact on the poultry industry. Scutellaria baicalensis extract has shown great potential in anti-MG infection, but the role and mechanism of its extracellular vesicles (EVs) remain unexplored. The aim of this study is to investigate the potential mechanism of Scutellaria baicalensis derived extracellular vesicles (SEVs) against MG, especially by regulating TRPC1-STIM1/ORAI1 signaling pathway, which is a key component of store-operated calcium entry (SOCE).MethodsSEVs were obtained from Scutellaria baicalensis and subsequently subjected to characterization. Through in vitro and in vivo MG infection models, experimental techniques including non-targeted metabolomics, qRT-PCR, Western Blot and Fluo-4 AM calcium imaging were employed. Additionally, synergistic and antagonistic effects were demonstrated via siRNA-mediated STIM1 knockdown and plasmid overexpression experiments to validate its pivotal role.ResultsNon-targeted metabolomics analysis showed that SEVs treatment regulated metabolic pathways related to calcium signaling pathway compared with MG-infected group, which aided our study to determine that SEVs may affect inflammatory injury by affecting calcium signaling. The results of molecular experiments showed that SEVs markedly downregulated SOCE molecules (TRPC1, STIM1 and ORAI1) at mRNA and protein levels, effectively suppressing MG-induced abnormal intracellular calcium influx and reducing pro-inflammatory cytokine production.Conclusions: SEVs alleviate MG-induced inflammation through the TRPC1-STIM1/ORAI1 pathway, restoring calcium homeostasis and inhibiting NF-κB signaling and cytokine release.Practical Applications: This study reveals a novel mechanism by which plant-derived exosome-like nanoparticles (PELNs) exert anti-inflammatory effects, highlighting their potential as a natural nanotherapeutic strategy for controlling MG infection in poultry.

Introduction

MG, the prokaryotic agent of Chronic Respiratory Disease in poultry, lacks a cell wall and primarily infects via the respiratory tract (Feberwee et al., 2022; Yehia, et al., 2023). It adheres to epithelial cilia, causing persistent tracheitis and airsacculitis, which leads to significant economic losses due to reduced growth and egg production (Hu, et al., 2021; Mugunthan, et al., 2023; Wu, et al., 2019; Yadav, et al., 2022). Current reliance on antibiotics has spurred drug resistance and residue concerns, highlighting the need for novel control strategies (Gautier-Bouchardon, 2018). In recent years, the discovery of PELNs as novel of bioactive substances has provided a fresh perspective on deciphering the complex mechanisms of traditional Chinese medicine (Lian, et al., 2022). EVs are nanoscale particles actively secreted by cells that possess a phospholipid bilayer structure and play a pivotal role in intercellular communication (van Niel, et al., 2018). The primary role of EVs is to serve as precise intercellular messengers. Functioning as biological parcels shuttling through bodily fluids, they carry crucial informational molecules such as proteins and nucleic acids (Krylova and Feng, 2023). Upon uptake by recipient cells, these vesicles can reprogram cellular functions, thereby establishing an extensive intercellular communication network (Tang, et al., 2021). In a healthy organism, this communication system is fundamental for maintaining vital physiological processes: it participates in immunomodulation, capable of both activating defense mechanisms and sustaining immune tolerance (Rasihashemi, et al., 2022); it drives tissue repair and regeneration by promoting angiogenesis and cellular proliferation; furthermore, it supports normal neuronal signaling within the nervous system (Kok and Yu, 2020). While research on extracellular vesicles derived from animal cells is well-established, extracellular vesicles derived from plants represent an emerging field that is rapidly gaining prominence (Jin, et al., 2024; Mu, et al., 2023). Compared to synthetic nanoparticles, PELNs offer distinct advantages, including superior biocompatibility, low immunogenicity and the ability to traverse biological barriers (Nemati, et al., 2022). We assume that EVs might be effective candidates for combating MG infection (Rome, 2019), these EVs may function as a “natural drug complex”, delivering multiple anti-inflammatory signaling molecules synergistically to the interior of target cells (Zeng, et al., 2024).

Calcium ions occupy a central position within these processes as ubiquitous second messengers, activating their signaling pathways (Lv, et al., 2025). In non-excitable cells (such as epithelial cell and immune cell), SOCE is the main pathway that mediates the sustained influx of extracellular calcium ions (Rosenberg, et al., 2019), playing a vital role in maintaining intracellular calcium homeostasis and driving the expression of inflammatory genes (Courjaret, et al., 2024). Previous studies by our research group have shown that the antagonistic effects of baicalin on MG infection-mediated inflammatory injury include inhibition of STIM1-regulated calcium homeostasis imbalance, oxidative stress, endoplasmic reticulum stress and MG proliferation (Chen, et al., 2023). Therefore, targeted intervention in the TRPC1-STIM1/ORAI1 calcium signaling pathway is considered a potentially effective strategy for suppressing excessive inflammatory responses. Against this backdrop, we propose the scientific hypothesis that SEVs function as highly efficient natural nano modulators. They inhibit the inflammatory response and tissue damage caused by MG infection by regulating the TRPC1-STIM1/ORAI1 calcium signaling pathway in host cells. Through a series of in vivo and in vitro experiments, this study systematically reveals a mechanism of action. SEVs specifically target the TRPC1-STIM1/ORAI1 axis, which is a core component that regulates calcium influx (Silva-Rojas, et al., 2020), this effectively mitigates the inflammatory damage induced by MG infection in chicks. Our experimental evidence conclusively demonstrates that, as natural nanocarriers (Shao, et al., 2020), SEVs not only suppress the expression and function of key components within this calcium signaling axis, but also regulate cellular metabolic reprogramming and NF-κB pathway activation. This synergistically curbs excessive inflammatory responses at multiple levels (Noonin and Thongboonkerd, 2021; Sultania, et al., 2025).

The aim of this study is to systematically elucidate the molecular mechanisms by which SEVs combat MG infection at three levels: 'traditional Chinese medicine active ingredients - cellular signaling pathways - disease pathological processes'. This provides a novel 'vesicle drug' perspective that deepens the modern scientific understanding of traditional Chinese medicine's 'clearing heat and detoxifying' theory. It also establishes an experimental foundation for developing green formulations based on PELNs to control MG infection in poultry, as well as other inflammatory diseases.

Materials and methods

Extraction and characterization of SEVs

One kilogram of fresh Scutellaria baicalensis root was washed, added to twice its volume of double-distilled water, and crushed to collect the juice. The collected plant juice was subjected to differential centrifugation at 1000 × g at 4°C for 10 min, 3000×g at 4°C for 20 min, and 10,000 × g at 4°C for 30 min. The supernatant was collected. This supernatant was placed in a fixed-angle rotor and centrifuged at 50,000 × g at 4°C and 180,000 × g at 4°C for 1 h respectively. The precipitates were suspended in 1 mL of PBS, resuspended by pipetting, yielding a solution of SEVs for subsequent characterization and identification experiments. Employ the Zeta View PMX 110 instrument and corresponding Zeta View 8.04.02 software to measure the particle size and concentration of EVs via nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) (the Zeta View system was calibrated using 110 nm polystyrene particles, with temperature maintained at 23 and 37 °C).

Transmission electron microscope (TEM)

Using a pipette, place a small drop of sample onto the wax dish. Remove the copper mesh and blot away excess droplets with a filter paper strip, allowing it to air-dry briefly. Take a 3% phosphotungstic acid solution and place a small drop onto the wax dish. Position the copper grid bearing the sample onto the surface of the staining solution (ensuring contact between sample and solution), and allow to stand for 3 - 5 min. Remove the copper grid, blot away excess droplets using a filter paper strip, and air-dry under an incandescent lamp. Perform TEM imaging (scale bar: 500 nm) to observe the morphology of SEVs. (Lai, et al., 2022).

MG culture

MG-Rlow strain was procured from the China Veterinary Culture Collection Centre. MG was cultured in MG basal medium (BasalMedia, China) supplemented with 10% foetal bovine serum (Gibco HyClone, China) at 37°C conditions. MG was passaged every 2 - 4 d. When the MG reached the 3rd generation, this was the optimal growth stage for MG. 13 turbidity tubes were filled with 9 mL of culture medium. 1 mL of MG culture solution was added to the first tube, and the remaining 12 tubes were diluted 10 times. The 13th tube was used as the control tube. They were placed in a 37°C incubator for 3d. The highest dilution resulting in the most significant color change was taken as the color change unit (CCU). The concentration unit of the MG bacterial solution was CCU/mL. The concentration was adjusted to 1 × 109 CCU/mL for backup.

HD-11 cells culture

The HD-11 cells were cultured at 37°C in 5% CO₂ in 1640 basal medium supplemented with 10% foetal bovine serum (Gibco HyClone, China), 1% penicillin and streptomycin (Beyotime, China).

The cells used in the siRNA transfection experiment were divided into the following eight groups (Group A served as the blank control; Group B as the si-NC blank control; Group C as the si-STIM1 group; Group D as the SEVs blank control; Group E as the MG infection model group; Group F: SEVs treatment group; Group G: si-NC + SEVs + MG group; Group H: si-STIM1 + SEVs + MG group). Following the manufacturer's recommended protocol, siRNA was introduced into HD-11 cells in Groups C, D, G, and H using Gencefe Super Trans transfection reagent. Transfection efficiency was assessed after 12 h. Following incubation with MG (400 MOI) at 37°C for 6 h at both 24 h and 48 h post-transfection, SEVs (200 μg/mL) were administered for 18 h.

The cells that underwent the transfection of the overexpression plasmid were divided into the following eight groups: Group A served as the blank control; Group B as the blank control for the overexpression plasmid; Group C as the STIM1 overexpression group; Group D as the SEVs blank control group; Group E as the OE-NC + SEVs + MG group; Group F as the OE-STIM1 + SEVs + MG group; Group G: MG infection model group; Group F: SEVs treatment group). Plasmid was introduced into Groups B, C, E, and F cells using Lipofectamine™ LTX and PLUS™ transfection reagents. Cells were incubated in a CO2 incubator at 37°C for 12 h. Total cellular RNA and proteins were then harvested for analysis

Cell viability assessment

Test cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well in 96-well plates and infected with MG (400 MOI) for 6 h (Guo, et al., 2024). SEVs were treatmented through 0.22 µm filters and added to individual wells at concentrations of 0, 25, 50, 100, and 200 µg/mL for 18 h. Assays were performed using the Cell Counting Kit (CCK8, Biosharp, China). Add 10 μL of CCK8 reagent to each well. The entire process must be conducted in the absence of light. The 96-well plate was then incubated at 37°C under 5% CO₂ for 1 h. Finally, the optical density values of each well were read at a wavelength of 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Non-targeted metabolomics study

In collaboration with Paisen Bio (Shanghai, China; http://www.personalbio.cn/), cell samples were submitted to Metabolon for non-targeted metabolomics analysis. The detailed experimental procedures can be found in Supporting Material 1.

siRNA interference and overexpression plasmid transfection

The siRNA sequences designed to inhibit STIM1 expression comprised: siRNA-STIM1#1 (SENSE: 5′-GGAUGACGUGGAUCAUAAATT-3′), siRNA-STIM1#2 (SENSE: 5′-GGAAGACCUCUGAAGUGUATT-3′), siRNA-STIM1#3 (SENSE: 5′-GGUUUGCCUACAUCCAGAATT-3′-), and the sense sequence for the negative control siRNA (siRNA-NC) was 5′-UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGUTT-3′-. The designed siRNAs targeting the STIM1 gene (si-STIM1) were procured from Saisuo Fei Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The siRNA exhibiting the highest silencing efficiency was selected for subsequent experiments. The overexpression vector for the STIM1 gene (STIM1 chicken in pcDNA3.1 (+) -EGFP) was purchased from Jiansu Gene Technology Co., Ltd.

Cellular reactive oxygen specie

ROS levels were measured using a commercial assay kit. Briefly, cells were incubated with the fluorescent probe DCFH-DA at 37°C for at least 20 min. Cells were then washed three times with serum-free cell culture medium to completely remove extracellular DCFH-DA. ROS levels were detected under a fluorescence microscope.

Cellular calcium ion concentration assay

HD-11 cells in logarithmic growth phase were seeded into 6-well plates. Upon reaching 70–80% confluence, cells were transfected to establish STIM1 knockdown and overexpression models. Remove the medium and gently wash three times with PBS. Add 500 μL Fluo-4 AM working solution to each well and incubate at 37°C for 30 min. After incubation, remove the working solution and wash cells three times with HEPES buffer saline. Observe intracellular calcium ion staining under a fluorescence microscope.

Animal grouping and drug administration

Eighty one-day-old chicks were reared in a constant-temperature brooder until seven days of age, then randomly divided into eight groups (A–H): Group A served as the blank control; Group B as the MG infection control; Group C as the DMSO control; Group D as the Zegocractin group; Group E: Tylosin treatment group; Group F: High-dose SEVs group; Group G: Medium-dose SEVs group; Group H: Low-dose SEVs group, with 10 chicks per cage in each group. At 7 days of age, groups B, C, D, E, F, G, and H established MG infection models by direct air sac inoculation 0.2 ml MG (1 × 10⁹ CCU/mL). All groups were normally fed until 10 days of age. Group C received oral administration of 0.5 mL DMSO, Group E received oral administration of Tylosin (450 mg/kg), while Groups F, G, and H received oral administration of different concentrations of SEVs (200µg/mg, 100µg/mg, 50µg/mg) and 0.5 mL Zegocractin (6.5 mg/mL), administered for three consecutive days. Groups A and B received no treatment at this stage. On the fourteenth day of age, six chickens were randomly selected from each group for humane euthanasia, and lung samples were collected. All animal experimental protocols employed in this study complied with institutional guidelines for animal research. All experiments were approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Northeast Agricultural University (approved No.: NEAUEC20220324).

Histopathological examination

Following fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde solution for 48 h, lung tissue from each group of chicks underwent graded alcohol dehydration, clearing, paraffin embedding, sectioning, routine hematoxylin and eosin staining, and final observation of pathological changes under light microscopy. Concurrently, paraffin sections underwent fluorescent homologous double-staining. Paraffin sections underwent dewaxing, rehydration, and antigen retrieval, followed by endogenous peroxidase blocking and serum blocking. After discarding the serum, the TNF-α primary antibody was added and incubated overnight at 4°C. The fluorescent secondary antibody (Green) was added and incubated at room temperature for 50 min. IL-1β (Red) was labeled following the same procedure. Finally, nuclei were stained with DAPI, autofluorescence quenched, and slides mounted. Samples were observed using an upright microscope (NIKON ECLIPSE C1), and images were processed (coloring and merging) with Slide Viewer software.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was isolated from HD-11 cells and tissues using Trizol reagent (TaKaRa, Dalian, China) and reverse transcribed with the PrimeScript™ Fast RT Reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (TaKaRa). The resulting cDNA served as the template for qRT-PCR using specific primers. The primer sequences employed were as Supporting Materials 2. mRNA levels were analyzed using the Power SYBR Green 2 × Ready-to-Use Reagent (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer's instructions. GAPDH served as the endogenous reference, and amplification of the target gene was normalized to the amplification level of GAPDH. Gene expression levels were determined using the(2^(-ΔΔCT)) method.

Western blot

Cells and tissues were lysed using RIPA buffer (Beyotime, China). Protein concentration was determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime, China). Following heat denaturation, 20 μg of protein was separated on a 4%−20% precast gel (Beyotime, China). Proteins were then transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes (PALL, Port Washington, NY, USA). The membrane was blocked with blocking reagent (5% skimmed milk) and incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibody. The membrane was then incubated with the corresponding horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody. Protein bands were visualized using ECL Western Blot Detection Reagent (Beyotime, China). The antibodies used in this study were as follows: TRPC1 (HuaBio, China), NLRP3 (Wanleibio, China), STIM1 (Wanleibio, China), NF-κB (Abmart, China), ORAI1 (Abclonal, China), CHOP (Bioss, China), and GAPDH (Abmart, China).

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation and analyzed using GraphPad Prism 10.0. Multiple comparisons were assessed using one-way analysis of variance. P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

SEVs alleviate MG-induced inflammatory damage in HD-11 cells

Following MG infection, cells were treated with varying concentrations of SEVs. Subsequent cell collection and CCK-8 assays revealed that these vesicles promoted cell proliferation in a dose-dependent manner. Cell viability significantly increased at 200 μg/mL but showed a decreasing trend at 500 μg/mL (Fig. 1D). Consequently, SEVs at 200 μg/mL were selected for subsequent experiments.

Non-targeted metabolomic analysis

Non-targeted metabolomics analysis was conducted on HD-11 cells from the NC, MG, and EVs treatment groups to observe intracellular material changes following MG infection and the effects of extracellular vesicle treatment. Multivariate statistical analysis has proved that the model is accurate and effective. in Supporting Material 3. Differential metabolites were screened using the VIP values from OPLS-DA analysis and P-values from univariate analysis, with the screening criteria set as VIP ≥ 1 and P < 0.05. Volcano plots illustrated the expression trends and quantities of differential metabolites across groups (Fig. 2A). Comparison between the NC and MG groups revealed 50 downregulated metabolites and 60 upregulated metabolites. Analysis of the MG versus EVs group identified 46 downregulated metabolites and 189 upregulated metabolites. The clustering heatmap revealed significant intergroup expression differences in 110 differential metabolites between the NC and MG groups, and in 235 differential metabolites between the MG and EVs groups (Fig. 2B). To clarify the metabolites between these groups, a Venn diagram comparison of differential metabolites between the NC and MG groups and between the MG and EVs groups revealed 40 common differential metabolites (Fig. 2C). Using a screening criterion of VIP ≥ 1 and P < 0.05, 13 significantly common differential metabolites were ultimately identified. Among these, ten metabolites exhibited an upregulation trend, while three metabolites showed a downregulation trend (Supporting Material 3). Finally, functional enrichment analysis of the differentially expressed metabolites using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database revealed enrichment in six pathways. Overall, the enriched metabolic pathways are closely related to the calcium signaling pathway. Downregulation of STIM1 inhibits the effect of SEVs on MG-induced inflammatory damage

To explore the role of SEVs through STIM1 in endoplasmic reticulum stress and inflammatory injury induced by MG infection, this study designed one FAM-fluorescently labeled blank control siRNA and three siRNAs for downregulating STIM1. The blank control group was co-transfected with the siRNA knockdown group. Fluorescence microscopy verified successful transfection (Fig. 3A), subsequent detection of STIM1 protein and RNA expression identified siRNA-STIM1#3 as the most potent downregulatory, selected for subsequent experiments. Results demonstrated successful suppression of STIM1 expression via siRNA (Fig. 3B and 3C). Compared to the MG group, the ev-MG group exhibited significantly reduced expression of calcium channel-related proteins. Compared to the si-NC-ev-MG group, the combination of SEVs and siRNA-STIM1 significantly increased TRPC1, ORAI1, NLRP3, and NF-κB p65 expression without significant differences from the MG group, indicating that STIM1 knockdown blocked the inhibitory effect of SEVs on MG (Fig. 3D). Furthermore, compared to the si-NC-ev-MG group, STIM1 inhibition markedly exacerbated MG-induced inflammatory damage, manifested by elevated mRNA expression of proinflammatory factors IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, NF-κB, IFN-γ, and calcium channel-related markers TRPC1, STIM1, ORAI1, NLRP3, CHOP, and NF-κB (Fig. 3E). Compared to the MG group, ROS levels were significantly reduced in the ev-MG group. Relative to the si-NC-ev-MG group, the si-STIM1-ev-MG group exhibited elevated ROS levels, indicating that SEVs could not further suppress MG infection-induced oxidative stress (Fig. 3F). Fluo-4 AM-based calcium fluorescence analysis revealed markedly elevated intracellular calcium levels in the MG group compared to the NC group, which were significantly reduced in the SEVs-treated group. Calcium levels were restored following co-treatment with SEVs and si-STIM1 compared to the si-NC-ev-MG group (Fig. 3G). These findings indicate that SEVs suppress MG-induced upregulation of endogenous STIM1 expression. STIM1 overexpression promotes MG infection-induced inflammatory damage

To investigate whether MG infection induces intracellular calcium accumulation via STIM1, an STIM1 overexpression plasmid with EGFP fluorescent tagging was designed using pcDNA3.1 as the vector. This was transfected into HD-11 cells, with fluorescence expression confirming efficient transfection (Fig. 4A). The results showed that, compared with the NC group, the expression of STIM1 protein in the oe-NC group and the MG group was significantly increased; compared with the oe-NC group, the expression level of STIM1 protein in the oe-STIM1 group was significantly higher (Fig. 4B). Compared to the OE-NC-ev-MG group, the OE-STIM1-ev-MG group exhibited significantly elevated expression of calcium channel-related proteins TRPC1, STIM1, and ORAI1, which were also higher than in the MG group. Concurrently, STIM1 upregulation promoted MG-induced inflammatory responses, manifested by markedly increased protein expression levels of NLRP3 and NF-κB p65; and this led to endoplasmic reticulum stress, manifested by a significant increase in the expression level of the CHOP protein (Fig. 4C). Moreover, STIM1 overexpression markedly amplified inflammatory responses by activating the NF-κB pathway, evidenced by increased mRNA production of pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, IFN-γ, and inflammation-associated NF-κB p65, CHOP, and NLRP3. Concurrently, mRNA expression levels of calcium channel-related markers TRPC1, STIM1, and ORAI1 were elevated (Fig. 4D). Furthermore, ROS and calcium ion expression levels were assessed in HD-11 cells. Results demonstrated significantly elevated ROS expression in STIM1-upregulated and SEVs groups compared to the OE-NC-ev-MG group (Fig. 4E). Concurrently, overexpression plasmid treatment promoted intracellular calcium ion accumulation (Fig. 4F). These results indicate that the OE-STIM1 plasmid can effectively increase the expression level of STIM1 protein in cells. The exogenous overexpression of STIM1 overcomes the inhibitory effect of the SEVs on STIM1, thereby weakening the anti-inflammatory effect of the SEVs. SEVs mitigate MG-induced inflammatory damage in chickens by suppressing STIM1 expression

In vitro experiments revealed that SEVs modulate inflammatory responses by influencing STIM1. To investigate whether this effect persists in vivo (Fig. 5A), hematoxylin and eosin staining was employed to examine pathological alterations in chick lung tissue following intervention with calcium channel inhibitors and SEVs. Compared with the blank control group, the MG-infected group and the group treated with both inhibitor and extracellular vesicles exhibited extensive alveolar rupture in lung tissue. Alveolar fusion formed alveolar cysts, interalveolar septa were enlarged, alveolar walls thickened, intercellular spaces widened, and inflammatory cell infiltration caused congestion and hemorrhage. Compared with the MG group, the Tylosin and Zegocractin groups exhibited intact alveolar structures in lung tissue, with partial thickening of alveolar walls and minimal inflammatory cell infiltration (Fig. 5B). Immunohistochemical staining of lung tissue sections assessed IL-1β and TNF-α expression to evaluate MG-induced inflammatory damage. Studying these cytokine expressions provides deeper insight into inflammatory responses following SEVs and Zegocractin. As depicted (Fig. 5C), red and green fluorescence represent inflammatory cytokines. Compared to the NC group, both the MG-infected group and DMSO control group exhibited intense fluorescence, indicating elevated inflammatory cytokine levels in lung tissue. Compared to the MG-infected group, the Zegocractin group and Tylosin group showed significantly reduced immunofluorescence intensity. Compared with the Zegocractin group, the EVs group supplemented with SEVs and Zegocractin exhibited significantly elevated levels of inflammatory factors. Subsequent extraction and analysis of lung tissue RNA and proteins revealed that, compared to the MG group, the Tylosin group and Zegocractin group exhibited significantly reduced mRNA levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, IFN-γ, and inflammation-associated NF-κB p65, CHOP, and NLRP3. Calcium channel-related genes also showed a downward trend. Compared with the Zegocractin group, mRNA expression of cytokines and calcium channel-related genes was significantly upregulated in the medium-dose and low-dose SEVs groups (Fig. 6A). Finally, the calcium channel-related proteins were examined. Compared with the MG group, the Zegocractin group exhibited significantly reduced expression of TRPC1, STIM1, and ORAI1 proteins. Treatment with different concentrations of SEVs enhanced inflammatory damage and endoplasmic reticulum stress compared with the Zegocractin group, manifested as increased expression levels of NF-κB p65, CHOP, and NLRP3 proteins (Fig. 6B). Discussion

The emergence of antimicrobial resistance is a major challenge for global public health, with particular prominence in veterinary clinical practice and animal husbandry. This highlights the urgent need to develop new alternative therapeutic strategies against pathogens such as MG (The, 2024). In the quest for alternative therapies, Chinese herb has demonstrated immense potential (Wang, et al., 2023). Scutellaria baicalensis is particularly noteworthy, being renowned for its heat-clearing, dampness-drying, fire-purging and toxin-resolving properties (Zhao, et al., 2019). The dried root of this classic Chinese medicinal herb is widely used in clinical practice to treat inflammation-related conditions such as lung-heat cough, abscesses, boils, and toxic sores(Liao, et al., 2021). Modern pharmacological research has confirmed that its primary active constituents (such as baicalin and wogonoside) exhibit anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, antiviral and immunomodulatory effects (Bao, et al., 2021; Guo, Miao, Chen, Wang, Wang, Wang, Wu and Li, 2024; Ishfaq, et al., 2021). In addition, their pharmacologically active components and target mechanisms have not yet been fully elucidated and their limited bioavailability. These have to some extent constrained their modern application and the elucidation of their mechanisms of action (Wang, et al., 2018).

In recent years, an increasing number of studies have revealed that EVs play a role in regulating diseases, immune imbalances and inflammatory damage by transmitting information between cells (Li, et al., 2024; Zhang, et al., 2019), for example, EVs derived from tubular epithelial cells and carrying microRNA-21 can activate fibroblasts in the kidneys via the miRNA-21/PTEN/Akt pathway, thereby accelerating the development of renal fibrosis (Zhao, et al., 2021). At the same time, EVs derived from natural compounds also play an important role. PELNs primarily regulate the immune response to pathogens and can disrupt the dynamic balance between pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory effects to promote the maintenance of cellular immune microenvironment homeostasis (Yi, et al., 2023). Edible ginger-derived nanoparticles have been shown to alleviate acute colitis, enhance intestinal repair and prevent chronic colitis and colitis-related cancers (Zhang, et al., 2016), for instance, cabbage exosome-like nanovesicles can promote the proliferation of mammalian cells, and also inhibit the inflammatory response of immune cells as well as the apoptosis of human keratinocytes and fibroblasts (You, et al., 2021). However, there is no relevant research report on the role of PELNs in treating MG infections. This study establishes SEVs as a potent and unique agent against MG infection. Although the anti-inflammatory effects of baicalein and other flavonoids have been confirmed (Luo, et al., 2024; Wen, et al., 2023), this study reveals that it is the extracellular vesicles themselves that are the key effectors. This aligns with the perspective that plant-derived extracellular vesicles serve as natural delivery systems for cross-species communication (Barzin, et al., 2023; Skotland, et al., 2019).

This study explored the potential of SEVs to treat inflammatory damage caused by MG infection. The inflammatory response involves the coordinated action of immune cells in response to external stimuli (Zhang, et al., 2023). Calcium ions play a crucial role in this process as signaling molecules, specific calcium signaling patterns can activate transcription factors such as NF-κB, NFAT and AP-1, thereby driving the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines (Yong, et al., 2022). Concurrently, an increase in calcium ion concentration activates the CaMKKβ/AMPK pathway (Werner and Wagner, 2022). AMPK can enhance mitochondrial function and decrease the production of mitochondrial reactive oxygen species, a potent activator of the NLRP3 inflammasome (Wu, et al., 2025). The core molecular mechanism of this process lies in the following: When the calcium storage in the endoplasmic reticulum is depleted, the calcium sensor protein STIM1 undergoes oligomerization and move to the vicinity of the cell membrane to bind with the ORAI Calcium Release-Activated Calcium Modulator 1 (ORAI1) protein to form a STIM1/ORAI1 dimer, thereby activating the calcium channel and completing calcium influx (Carreras-Sureda, et al., 2023; Conte, et al., 2021). Furthermore, the transient receptor potential channel 1 (TRPC1) has been shown to interact with STIM1 and is regulated by it (Ambudkar, et al., 2017), forming a more complex TRPC1-STIM1/ORAI1 signaling module that synergistically modulates the amplitude and kinetics of SOCE (Elzamzamy, et al., 2020). This activates key downstream transcription factors, including NF-κB and NFAT (Berry, et al., 2018, 2021), which drive the explosive release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β and IL-6, ultimately causing tissue damage (Elzamzamy, Penner and Hazlehurst, 2020; Shin, et al., 2023). This discovery led us to focus on the TRPC1-STIM1/ORAI1 core axis of SOCE, and to explore its role in the anti-MG infection effect of SEVs.

The most significant breakthrough of this study is the identification of TRPC1-STIM1/ORAI1 as the primary molecular targets of SEVs. Experimental results demonstrate that treatment with SEVs significantly reduces the expression of pro-inflammatory factors (IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, NF-κB and IFN-γ) induced by MG infection, while simultaneously downregulating the expression of calcium channel-related genes (TRPC1, STIM1 and ORAI1) at both the mRNA and protein levels. Furthermore, Fluo-4 AM fluorescence probe detection clearly demonstrated that SEVs effectively suppressed MG-induced abnormal increases in intracellular calcium ion concentration. Genetic knockdown and overexpression experiments conclusively established the causal role of this pathway in MG-induced inflammation. Knocking down STIM1 in HD-11 cells using siRNA and administering the calcium channel inhibitor Zegocractin in vivo demonstrated that STIM1 downregulation suppressed the therapeutic effect of SEVs against MG-induced inflammatory damage. The SEVs failed to reduce expression further. This is consistent with previous studies (Duan, et al., 2025). Knockdown of the expression of the key channel inhibited the function of SEVs. Conversely, STIM1 overexpression exacerbated injury, confirming this pathway as a key driver of the pathological process and a reliable target for intervention capable of reversing the inhibitory effect of SEVs on STIM1.

Although this study systematically validated the anti-inflammatory effects and mechanisms of SEVs in cellular and chick models, certain limitations remain. Firstly, the generalizability of these findings to other species requires further validation. Secondly, the in vivo pharmacokinetic characteristics of SEVs remain to be elucidated. Moreover, the specific miRNAs or other functional components within SEVs that exert these effects remain unidentified, and their direct regulatory relationships with host cell target genes require further experimental confirmation. Moving forward, we shall pursue deeper exploration through the following avenues: employing dual luciferase reporter assays, miRNA sequencing, and target validation to identify key functional RNAs within SEVs and their respective targets. This will enable a comprehensive elucidation of their regulatory network at the molecular level.

Conclusion

In summary, this study sheds light on SEVs suppress inflammatory responses induced by MG infection by regulating the TRPC1-STIM1/ORAI1-mediated calcium signaling pathway. This discovery deepens our understanding of the immunomodulatory functions of PEVs and provides new theoretical foundations and potential intervention strategies for preventing and treating mycoplasma infection in poultry.
