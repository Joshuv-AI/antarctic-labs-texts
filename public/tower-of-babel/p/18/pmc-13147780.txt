A rapid and sensitive yellow fever virus detection method based on CRISPR/Cas13a and reverse transcription recombinase-aided amplification with special lateral-flow test strips
Authors: Yujie Yan, Tong Wei, Xue Dong, Mengwei Niu, Yao Han, Yansong Sun, Hao Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•An on-site assay for yellow fever virus (YFV) is established using clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR)-CRISPR-associated proteins (Cas) with reverse transcription recombinase-aided amplification (RT-RAA) and easy-readout, sensitive enhanced lateral flow strip (ERASE LFS).•This assay enables the detection of YFV ribonucleic acid (RNA) at 100 copies/µL within 35 min.•The reaction runs on a single portable heat block, with no other equipment needed.•A lyophilized kit that can detect YFV 17D virus at 102 plaque-forming units (PFU)/mL was developed.Keywords: Reverse transcription recombinase-aided amplification (RT-RAA), Clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR) / CRISPR-associated proteins (Cas) 13a (CRISPR/Cas13a), Detection, Yellow fever virus (YFV), Lateral flow strip (LFS)

Introduction

Yellow fever virus (YFV), a member of the Flavivirus genus (Flaviviridae family), is the causative agent of Yellow fever (YF). YF is an acute viral hemorrhagic fever transmitted primarily by Aedes (Ae.) mosquitoes, such as Ae. aegypti and Ae. Albopictus [1]. While endemic in tropical regions of Africa and South America, the disease poses a global health risk due to sporadic cases in unvaccinated travelers, highlighting its global relevance. Between 29 December 2024 and 26 April 2025, the Americas alone reported 212 confirmed YF cases, including 85 deaths [case fatality rate (CFR): 40 %] [2]. Since the beginning of 2023, and as of 25 February 2024, preliminary data indicate a YF fatality rate of 11 % in the African region, covering 13 African countries [3]. Most are located in underdeveloped regions without adequate medical resources. Epidemiological data in these areas are often characterized by fragmentation, limited integration with routine health information systems, non-standardized case definitions, and substantial underreporting. Collectively, these factors compromise the accuracy of epidemiological data and contribute to the high mortality observed in YF outbreaks. Therefore, strengthening surveillance and preventive measures is imperative, and timely detection of YFV is crucial for effective epidemiological control.

At present, reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) is regarded as the reference method for viral detection [4,5], but it requires costly equipment, expert personnel, and post-assay analysis, limiting its accessibility in resource-limited regions with inadequate healthcare infrastructure. By contrast, recombinase-aided amplification assays like reverse transcription loop-mediated isothermal amplification (RT-LAMP) largely overcome the limitations of specialized instruments; however, their sensitivity is not optimal and may lead to cross-reactivity with other flaviviruses [such as dengue virus (DENV), Zika virus (ZIKV)], resulting in false-positive outcomes [6,7]. Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) is another commonly used assay for detecting infectious agents, characterized by high specificity; however, its reagent development requires considerable time [8], underscoring the urgent requirement for a YFV detection method that is rapid, convenient, and sensitive.

Reverse transcription recombinase-aided amplification (RT-RAA) represents an isothermal nucleic acid amplification technique enabling rapid nucleic acid amplification at low temperatures, usually ranging from 39-42 °C. The reaction is swift. Reverse transcriptase first converts the target ribonucleic acid (RNA) into complementary deoxyribonucleic acid (cDNA), followed by the formation of recombinase-primer nucleoprotein complex that mediates specific recognition and strand invasion of the cDNA template, with single-stranded DNA binding protein (SSB) stabilizing the open single-stranded DNA conformation to prevent reannealing, and finally strand-displacing DNA polymerase initiating continuous DNA synthesis and displacement amplification to generate massive target amplicons exponentially within 10–30 min. Compared with conventional PCR, RT-RAA offers faster reaction kinetics, more straightforward operation, and minimal equipment requirements.

Additionally, as a programmable gene-editing system, the clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR) system is based on the principle that CRISPR RNA (crRNA) guides CRISPR-associated protein (Cas) nucleases to a specific RNA/DNA sequence. Through base-pairing complementarity, the crRNA-Cas complex associates with the target RNA/DNA, inducing a strand break at the predetermined site. The outstanding advantage of the CRISPR system for detection is its exceptional programmability and precision-based editing mechanism. To date, multiple CRISPR/Cas systems have been engineered for pathogen detection, with specific examples including the development of SHERLOCK and DETECTR platforms [9,10]. As a unique member of the CRISPR/Cas family, CRISPR/Cas13a selectively targets RNA (instead of DNA) and possesses a specific collateral cleavage activity. Its sensitivity and specificity, rapid detection capabilities, and lack of protospacer adjacent motif (PAM) sequence limitation make it a powerful tool for diagnostic applications [[11], [12], [13]].

The CRISPR lateral flow strip (LFS) is significant for CRISPR nucleic acid detection, enabling it to move beyond fluorescence-based quantitative instruments. Moreover, the easy-readout and sensitive enhanced (ERASE) LFS is a specialized variant developed and optimized based on CRISPR LFS [14,15]. The ERASE strip comprises three main components arranged sequentially along the flow direction: a conjugate pad containing colloidal gold-labeled rabbit anti-fluorescein amidite (FAM) antibodies, a test line (T line) coated with streptavidin, and a control line (C line) coated with goat anti-rabbit IgG. Its distinctiveness lies in the calibrated amounts of reporter RNA, streptavidin, and the secondary antibody. The reporter RNA used was modified with twenty uracil (U) bases and labeled at the 5′ and 3′ ends with a FAM group and biotin, and it forms a complex with gold-labeled anti-FAM antibodies in ERASE LFS. With the target nucleic acid, the intact complex is captured at both T line (via biotin-streptavidin interaction) and C line (via antibody-antigen interaction), generating a double-band signal. In the absence of the target nucleic acid, cleavage of the reporter RNA disrupts the biotin linkage, abrogating T line capture and yielding a single-band signal instead. This design prevents faint or dim T-band signals from being misinterpreted as false-positive results. This clearly defined binary readout significantly reduces the risk of false-positive interpretation caused by incomplete cleavage and subsequent faint T-band formation.

In this work, we combined RT-RAA, CRISPR-Cas13a, and ERASE LFS to develop a time-saving and sensitive on-site method for YFV detection. Due to geographical distribution restrictions of yellow fever, clinical positive samples were unavailable for efficacy evaluation; however, data from simulated clinical samples still demonstrate the method's merits to some extent. The RT-RAA-CRISPR-ERASE LFS (RCE) assay we developed is designed to rapidly and reliably detect YFV in resource-limited settings. It alleviates disease surveillance burdens in epidemic regions, guides YF prevention, and reduces transmission, thereby contributing to global public health initiatives.

Materials and methods

Design and preparation of target sequence and primers

The genome sequences of YFV (Ref Seq accession NC_002031.1) were downloaded from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database. The whole-genome sequence comparison analysis was performed using MAFFT v7.526 and Jalview 2.11.4.0. We selected the target fragment (224 bp) with 95 % conservation at the 5′-non-coding region (NCR), and designed 16 groups of RT-RAA primers and 4 crRNAs within the target fragment. The upstream primer sequence of RT-RAA was preceded by the T7 promoter, enabling acquisition of crRNA via T7 transcription. All sequences utilized in this work were provided in the Table S1.

We constructed a recombinant pUC57 plasmid carrying the synthesized YFV target sequence to complete primer screening and establish a preliminary detection system. Additionally, the YFV 17D vaccine strain, which shares 99.37 % nucleic acid sequence identity with the parental wild-type Asibi strain [16,17], was used for further validation. The nucleic acids of West Nile virus (WNV), Dengue virus (DENV1-4), Zika virus (ZIKV), Japanese Encephalitis Virus (JEV), and Tick-Borne Encephalitis Virus (TBEV) are from the State Key Laboratory of Pathogen and Biosecurity.

Synthesis of crRNA

We used PCR to amplify the crRNA template (crDNA). The reaction system contained 100 µL of 2 × Super Pfx Master Mix, 80 µmol/L (µM) F and R primer, 8 µL of crRNA template, and nuclease-free water to a final volume of 200 µL. Thermal cycling conditions were set as 95 °C incubation for 5 min; 35 successive cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 55 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 45 s; and a final incubation step at 72 °C for 10 min. Products were stored at −20 °C. The HiScribeT7 Quick High Yield RNA Synthesis Kit was used to set up the crRNA transcription system, with overnight incubation at 37 °C. Afterwards, RNA Clean XP was added to the post-incubation system at a 1:1.8 ratio to purify crRNA.

RT-RAA amplification

RT-RAA amplification was executed using the Nucleic Acid Amplification Kit (Hangzhou ZC, China). In accordance with the manufacturer’s recommended dosage, 5 µL of template, 400 nmol/L (nM) of primers, 25 µL of A Buffer, and 13.5 µL of Nuclease-free water were pipetted into the base reaction unit containing lyophilized powder. Subsequently, 2.5 µL B Buffer solution was added to activate the RT-RAA amplification reaction. Then incubate at 42 °C for 30 min. After the time optimization experiment, the incubation time was reduced to 10 min at 42 °C.

Fluorescence CRISPR/Cas13a detection

For each reaction of the fluorescent CRISPR/Cas13a assay system, we added 2 µL NTP Mix, 1 µL murine RNase inhibitor, 1 µL Cas13a, 0.5 µL T7 RNA polymerase, 0.25 µL MgCl2, 0.5 µL 4-hydroxyethylpiperazine ethanesulfonic acid (HEPES), 2.5 µL reporter RNA, 1.5 µL crRNA and 5 µL RT-RAA product, then made up the volume to 25 µL with nuclease-free water. The condition for fluorescence CRISPR/Cas13a detection is as follows: Reaction temperature, 37 °C; FAM channel (excitation: 490 nm, emission: 520 nm); real-time fluorescence signal measured every 2-min intervals for 30 consecutive cycles, resulting in a total detection period of 60 min. After the time optimization experiment, the reaction time was reduced to 20 min.

RT-qPCR detection of YFV

For the reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) Detection of YFV RNA, we used the Yellow Fever Virus Nucleic Acid Test Kit (Fluorescent PCR method) (BioGerm, China). Thermal cycling conditions were set as 50 °C for 10 min, 95 °C for 5 min, with subsequent 40 cycles comprising 95 °C for 10 s and 55 °C for 40 s.

ERASE LFS-based assay

The detection procedure comprised RT-RAA, CRISPR reaction, and strip visualization. The RT-RAA system components were as described above, with incubation at 42 °C for 10 min. Each reaction unit of the CRISPR/Cas13a strip detection system was composed of 2 µL Murine RNase Inhibitor, 4 µL NTP Mix, 0.5 µL MgCl2, 1 µL T7 RNA Polymerase, 2 µL Cas13a, 1 µL HEPES, 3 µL crRNA, 5 µL report RNA, 5 µL RT-RAA product, and add Nuclease-free water to 50 µL. After mixing, the mixture was centrifuged at low speed for 10 s, then incubated at 37 °C for 20 min. Add at least 40 µL of the reaction products to the ERASE LFS sample well. The result was visualized in 2–5 min. The C and T lines were identified as negative. Conversely, if there were only a C line, it should be considered a positive result. The ERASE LFS were individually packaged in sealed aluminum with a desiccant and stored at 4 °C.

Operation procedure for the lyophilized kit

YFV lyophilized kit including lysis buffer, reconstitution buffer, RT-RAA lyophilized bead, CRISPR lyophilized bead, and ERASE LFS. The primary components of the lysis buffer are hydrochloric acid and Trion X-100. Simulated clinical samples, prepared by spiking infectious YFV 17D virus into human serum (GeminiBio, US), were used to validate the lyophilized kit's performance. The operation steps are as follows: firstly, mix 100 µL of the sample with an equal volume of lysis buffer and incubate at room temperature for 5 min. Then add 30 µL of lysate to the RT-RAA reaction tube. Invert the tube 6–8 times to mix, gently tap the wall five times, and briefly centrifuge or flick the tube twice to ensure the sample settles at the bottom. Then place the tube in the heat block and incubate at 42 °C for 10 min. Reconstitute the CRISPR lyophilized bead with 45 µL of reconstitution buffer, then transfer 5 µL of the RT-RAA product to the CRISPR reaction tube. Invert gently 6–8 times to mix, centrifuge briefly, and incubate at 37 °C for 20 min. After incubation, dispense at least 40 µL of the product into the sample port of ERASE LFS, and interpret results in 2–5 min. The RCE lyophilized kit should be stored at 4 °C.

Statistical analysis

Statistical significance was analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Dunnett's test in GraphPad Prism. All experiments were performed in triplicate technical replicates (n = 3). Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Significance levels are denoted as *, P < 0.05; **, P < 0.01; ***, P < 0.001; and ****, P < 0.0001. “ns” indicates not significant.

Results

Screening and optimization of detection components (crRNAs and RT-RAA primers)

To develop a highly sensitive YFV detection system, we integrated preamplification techniques (PCR/RT-RAA) with fluorescence detection based on CRISPR/Cas13a. The principle of RT-RAA-CRISPR detection for YFV is depicted in Fig. 1A. We selected a 224 bp target sequence within a relatively conserved gene region and designed four candidate crRNAs and 16 pairs of candidate RT-RAA primers (Fig. 1B, Table S1).

Using the constructed YFV plasmid at a concentration of 103 copies/µL, we screened four candidate crRNAs to identify the one with the highest detection efficiency, employing PCR amplification coupled with CRISPR detection (PCR-CRISPR). Results showed that fluorescence signals increased over time (Fig. 1C). At 10 min of CRISPR detection, all crRNA groups except crRNA4 exhibited statistically significant differences versus the negative control (NC). Among these, crRNA2 displayed the highest fluorescence value (4,681.7 ± 285.2), followed by crRNA1 (3,983.0 ± 114.0). By 20 min, all four crRNA groups showed statistically significant differences from the NC. Notably, crRNA2 maintained the strongest fluorescence signal (5,824.3 ± 403.5), while crRNA1 remained in second place (4,315.7 ± 131.8). By the end of the assay at 60 min (Fig. 1D), crRNA2 still stood out as the optimal choice, achieving a fluorescence value of 8,902.7 ± 564.1, 1.7 times that of the second-best crRNA1 (5,173.7 ± 163.5). In summary, crRNA2 demonstrated statistically significant differences from the NC as early as 10 min and consistently exhibited the highest fluorescence value throughout the entire detection period. Therefore, crRNA2 was selected as the most sensitive crRNA in the PCR-CRISPR system.

To meet the requirements of resource-limited areas, we replaced PCR-based nucleic acid pre-amplification with RT-RAA, which is more rapid and less equipment-dependent. We obtained 16 primer pairs by combining four upstream RT-RAA primers with four downstream primers. RT-RAA-CRISPR fluorescence detection with crRNA2 was then employed to verify the efficiency of the RT-RAA primers. When detecting the YFV plasmid at 101 copies/µL (Fig. 1E, 1F), the group using primer pair F3R3 yielded the highest fluorescence signal (19,025.0 ± 507.7) at 60 min, which was 1.6-fold greater than that of the second-best F3R4 (12,182.5 ± 869.0). Accordingly, the F3R3 primer set was selected for the RT-RAA-CRISPR system.

To re-validate the testing efficiency of four crRNAs in RT-RAA-CRISPR detection, we performed RT-RAA-CRISPR assays with each crRNA, which used RT-RAA primer pair F3R3 and a plasmid at 101 copies/µL. As shown in Fig. 1G, 1H, at 60 min of the detection, only crRNA2 showed a significantly higher fluorescence value than the negative control group, with an average of 18,009.0 ± 2,286.5. The detection results are consistent with the aforementioned experimental outcomes. Thus, crRNA2 was selected as the optimal candidate. In summary, RT-RAA primer F3/R3 and crRNA2 were selected for the establishment of the RT-RAA-CRISPR detection assay.

Sensitivity and specificity of RT-RAA-CRISPR detection

For the assessment of YFV detectability via RT-RAA-CRISPR, we prepared a 10-fold serial dilution series of YFV RNA (ranging from 103 copies/μL to 10-1 copies/μL) and simultaneously compared it with the reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR). As shown in Fig. 2A, except for the 10-1 copies/µL, the fluorescence intensity of 103/102/101/100 copies/µL was markedly elevated compared with the negative control group (4,551.7 ± 39.8) at 60 min. The fluorescence signals from all groups that could be detected increased over time and then plateaued. By contrast, RT-qPCR can achieve a detection sensitivity of 100 copies/µL within 80 min (Fig. 2B). The results demonstrated that the RT-RAA-CRISPR assay exhibited the same sensitivity as RT-qPCR, with a detection sensitivity of 100 copies/µL.

We next evaluated the detection specificity of the RT-RAA-CRISPR system containing crRNA2 by targeting other flavivirus RNAs with similar clinical symptoms. These viruses include four DENV serotypes (DENV1-4), JEV, TBEV, ZIKV, and WNV. The 103 copies/µL viral RNA was employed for RT-RAA-CRISPR detection. As illustrated in Fig. 2C, the kinetic profiles of the fluorescence signal showed no statistical difference between additional pathogen species and the NC, except for the YFV group. The result demonstrated that the RT-RAA-CRISPR detection method we developed for YFV has high specificity.

Time optimization of RT-RAA-CRISPR detection system for YFV

As shown in Fig. 2A, during CRISPR detection, the fluorescence signal in the 100 copies/μL group reached a plateau before 60 min, suggesting that the RT-RAA-CRISPR assay still has room for optimization in terms of reaction timing. Therefore, we set up different combinations of RAA amplification and CRISPR detection durations to determine the shortest feasible time for YFV RT-RAA-CRISPR detection.

The results demonstrated that following 30 min of RT-RAA amplification, the CRISPR assay can achieve sensitivity comparable to RT-qPCR within 10 min. The 100 copies/μL sample group exhibited a fluorescence value of 11,408.0 ± 601.9, while the NC group had a value of 4,267.7 ± 80.3, with a statistically significant intergroup difference (Fig. 2D); After 20 min of RT-RAA amplification, a CRISPR detection time of 20 min was required to reach a sensitivity of 100 copies/μL, yielding fluorescence values of 17,465.3 ± 1,666.9 for the 100 copies/μL group and 8,817.3 ± 287.0 for the NC group (P < 0.01); With 10 min of RT-RAA amplification, the CRISPR assay also attained a detection sensitivity of 100 copies/μL at 20 min, with fluorescence intensities of 15,847 ± 701.2 for the 100 copies/μL group and 9,088.3 ± 1,233.9 for the NC group, indicating statistical significance (P < 0.001). Hence, the RT-RAA-CRISPR assay for YFV RNA was optimized effectively, achieving the same sensitivity as RT-qPCR with a total turnaround time of 30 min (10 min RT-RAA amplification followed by 20 min CRISPR reaction). Building on the 30-minute RT-RAA-CRISPR reaction, the addition of a 2–5-minute ERASE LFS detection step brings the total reaction time of the RCE assay to 35 min.

We evaluated the sensitivity of the RCE detection method by testing a 10-fold serial dilution of YFV RNA (ranging from 102 to 10-1 copies/µL). The result showed that groups of 102/101/100 copies/µL exhibited only the C line, which was determined as positive in accordance with the testing principle. By contrast, the 10-1 copies/µL group and the NC group exhibited both C and T lines, interpreted as negative (Fig. 3B). Therefore, the sensitivity of the RCE assay for YFV RNA was determined to be 100 copies/µL.

On the other hand, specificity evaluation was conducted using RNA from 5 other flaviviruses, DENV1-4, ZIKV, WNV, TBEV, and JEV (each at 103 copies/µL). In the RCE assay, only the “C” line appeared for the YFV nucleic acid sample, indicating it was positive. In contrast, the strips generated both “C” and “T” lines for the other flavivirus and the negative control group, yielding negative results (Fig. 3C). This outcome demonstrated the assay's high specificity.

Validation of the lyophilized kit

Based on prior research, we developed a lyophilized RCE assay kit for YFV, with primers and reagents encapsulated in lyophilized beads. The kit comprised ERASE LFS, RT-RAA lyophilized bead, CRISPR lyophilized bead, lysis buffer, and reconstitution buffer (Fig. 3D), covering all necessary consumables for the entire process of viral lysis, RT-RAA amplification, CRISPR cleavage, and LFS visualization reaction. We spiked infectious YFV 17D virus into human serum to prepare simulated clinical samples. These samples, containing viral loads of 105/104/103/102/101 PFU/mL, were then validated using the lyophilized kit. Detection results showed that the strips for the 101 PFU/mL group and the negative control displayed both C and T lines, indicating negative results. Conversely, samples with 102 PFU/mL of YFV 17D virus or higher produced strips with only a C line, indicating positive results (Fig. 3E). These results indicated that the RCE lyophilized kit could detect infectious YFV 17D virus in human serum at a concentration of 102 PFU/mL, thus providing experimental support for its on-site application.

Discussion

In this study, our results demonstrate that the RCE assay serves as a rapid, sensitive platform for YFV detection. It allows YFV identification under routine laboratory conditions, rendering it adapted for resource-scarce settings. This method can detect YFV RNA with a sensitivity of 100 copies/µL and infectious virus at 102 PFU/mL within 35 min.

The crRNA2 used in this study (TAGGCAATAAACACATTTGGATTAATTT) is located in the 5′ untranslated region (UTR) of the YFV genome, and has successfully detected 100 copies/μL YFV RNA. The 5′ UTR region is crucial for YFV RNA replication and contains several significant regulatory elements. Among them, the stem-loop A (SLA) structure is essential for recruiting and correctly positioning the YFV NS5 RNA polymerase, the structural protein to initiate negative-strand RNA synthesis. This region is important and highly conserved, and SLA comprises the first ∼70 nucleotides of the genome [18,19]. It should be noted that the most effective crRNA2 in this work is located at 51–78 bp, mainly within the SLA structure, which may underpin its high detection efficiency. Additonally, after basic local alignment search tool (BLAST) analysis (E-value was 0.05), crRNA2 showed 100 % homology with YFV in the NCBI database (Fig. S1), indicating that this sequence was specific and theoretically ruling out non-specific detection results.

According to the study by A. J. Basile et al. [20], which used the YFV RT-PCR kit to detect YFV strains, the detection sensitivity was approximately 1.245 RNA copies/μL. This value matches the RT-qPCR results (100 copies/μL) observed in this work. C. Escadafal et al. [21] determined a sensitivity of 44 genome equivalent copies per reaction (8.8 copies/μL) for YFV RNA using LFS RT-RPA. By comparison, the RCE detection method developed in this work exhibited sensitivity up to 100 copies/μL, comparable to RT-qPCR, demonstrating high sensitivity. Also, our testing showed no cross-reactivity with other flaviviruses tested, highlighting the RCE assay's specificity for YFV and reducing cross-reactivity in flavivirus diagnostics.

Instrument-dependent techniques, such as RT-qPCR and ELISA, require specialized laboratory settings, trained personnel, and sophisticated analysis, making them impractical for resource-limited basic medical institutions. In this study, the RCE assay uses only a simple heat block for detection, reducing the reliance on costly equipment. Besides, compared with RT-qPCR, which takes 80 min, the CRISPR/Cas13a-based assay developed in this study requires only 35 min. RT-RAA reaction was performed in a 42 °C incubator for 10 min to reverse-transcribe and amplify the targeted fragments of YFV RNA. Subsequently, the target fragment was transcribed and detected using the CRISPR/Cas13a system at 37 °C for 20 min. Results for the LFS can be observed within 2–5 min. Briefly, the RCE detection takes 35 min. In addition, we provided and validated a simple viral lysis protocol that requires only 5 min of incubation at room temperature. Adding this lysis step (5 min) and pipetting (5–15 min), the complete workflow has a total hands-on time of 45–60 min. It is less equipment-dependent and has a rapid render practical value for YFV detection in resource-constrained primary healthcare settings.

This study had a limitation. This study only employed the YFV 17D vaccine strain for simulated clinical sample detection, which cannot fully represent the information of all YFV genotypes. Nevertheless, the crRNA2 target is located within a fragment in the 5′ UTR with 95 % sequence conservation. Results obtained with the YFV 17D vaccine strain have validated the detection efficacy of this assay for YFV to a certain extent.
