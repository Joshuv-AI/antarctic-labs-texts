Intermittent androgen deprivation therapy for biochemical recurrence or prostate-specific antigen (PSA) persistence after radical prostatectomy: efficacy and prognostic factors
Authors: Jian Chen, Qiming Chen, Xuzhi Yan, Ziqian Wang, Yapeng Wang, Yao Zhang, Jun Zhang, Jing Xu, Junying Zhang, Dianzheng Zhang, Qiuli Liu, Weihua Lan, Jun Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe optimal protocol for intermittent androgen deprivation therapy (IAD) in managing biochemical recurrence (BCR) and persistent prostate-specific antigen (PSA) levels after radical prostatectomy (RP) remains undefined. This study aimed to introduce a novel IAD protocol, conduct an initial assessment of the protocol’s effectiveness in real-world settings, and develop a risk-based model to identify patients most likely to benefit from this approach.MethodsThis study included 175 post-RP patients receiving IAD, with a median follow-up of 44.77 months [interquartile range (IQR), 31.87–69.50 months] post-androgen deprivation therapy (ADT). The IAD protocol used a PSA threshold of 0.2 ng/mL to define progression during the first intermittent phase. Key clinical and pathological parameters, including PSA kinetics and testosterone levels, were analyzed. Cox regression assessed the duration of the first intermittent phase, while logistic regression analyses identified factors associated with the efficacy of ADT in the second treatment phase. Nomograms were developed for clinical applications.ResultsCox regression analysis revealed that the duration of the first intermittent phase was significantly associated with castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC)-free survival. Pathological Gleason grade group 5 (GGG 5) was a risk factor for a shorter first intermittent phase, while PSA doubling time (PSADT) ≥3.32 months and testosterone doubling time (TDT) ≥5.03 months were protective factors. Logistic regression identified combined metformin use, testosterone recovery to normal levels during the first intermittent phase, and a PSA response rate ≥90% after the first luteinizing hormone-releasing hormone (LHRH) injection in the first treatment cycle as protective factors against a PSA response rate <90% during salvage ADT in the second treatment phase [area under the curve (AUC) =0.84].ConclusionsThe duration of the first intermittent phase was a significant predictor of CRPC-free survival. PSADT, TDT, and pathological GGG 5 were independent predictors of this duration. Furthermore, combined metformin use appeared to preserve ADT sensitivity in patients with PSA progression during the first intermittent phase.

Highlight box

Key findings

• We introduced a novel intermittent androgen deprivation therapy (IAD) protocol; its first intermittent phase duration correlates with castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC)-free survival, with Pathological Grade Group 5 (GGG 5) as a risk factor and PSA doubling time (PSADT), testosterone doubling time (TDT), metformin as protective factors.

What is known and what is new?

• The optimal IAD protocol for post-radical prostatectomy (RP) biochemical recurrence (BCR) and persistent PSA and its predictive factors remain undefined.

• We introduced a novel IAD protocol, identified its predictive factors, and developed a risk model [area under the curve (AUC) =0.84], finding metformin preserves androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) sensitivity.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• The novel IAD protocol and risk model (AUC =0.84) should guide post- RP IAD management. Routine PSADT, TDT, and GGG 5 assessment, plus metformin adjuvant use, are recommended to optimize outcomes.

Introduction

Radical prostatectomy (RP) is a guideline-recommended first-line treatment for localized or oligometastatic prostate cancer (1,2). However, a significant proportion of patients experience persistent elevated prostate-specific antigen (PSA) levels or biochemical recurrence (BCR) after RP, with reported rates ranging from 5–20% and 27–53%, respectively (2,3). Salvage radiation therapy (SRT) is a common management approach; but it is associated with substantial treatment-related toxicity including urinary, bowel, and sexual dysfunction, which leads many patients to refuse this option (4). Moreover, SRT may not be suitable when the precise location of PSA recurrence (local, regional, distant, or combined) is uncertain. Androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) is an alternative recommended treatment for patients with PSA persistence or BCR following RP (5,6). However, the optimal duration and strategy for ADT in this population remain unclear. Continuous ADT (CAD) carries an increased risk of adverse effects, particularly cardiovascular events, suggesting the need for alternative ADT approaches (7,8).

Intermittent androgen deprivation therapy (IAD) involves alternating treatment periods with off-treatment intervals, allowing for hormonal recovery (9). The rationale for IAD is that androgen suppression induces a shift in tumor cells from an androgen-dependent to an androgen-independent state, and temporarily halting therapy before this transition can help reduce treatment-related toxicity while maintaining oncologic efficacy (9,10). While no well-defined clinical guidelines currently recommend IAD as a standard approach for patients with persistent PSA or BCR after RP, several studies have suggested that IAD may be a more attractive than CAD as an option (10,11). For example, a randomized controlled trial (RCT) led by Crook et al. found that IAD using a PSA threshold of 4 ng/mL was not inferior to CAD in terms of overall and cancer-specific survival, while also improving certain quality-of-life measures (11). Similarly, Salciccia et al. demonstrated that using a PSA nadir lower than 0.2 ng/mL to trigger the off-treatment phase, and resuming ADT when PSA exceeded 2.0 ng/mL, significantly reduced the risk of developing castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC) without metastases compared to CAD (12).

Despite the potential advantages of IAD, the optimal drug protocol and duration are yet to be established. Moreover, prostate cancer exhibits significant racial and geographic heterogeneity, with high-risk cases being more prevalent in certain populations, such as Chinese patients (13,14). This suggests that treatment strategies developed in other settings may not be entirely suitable for Chinese population. Therefore, the aim of this study was to introduce a novel IAD protocol and evaluate its effectiveness in a real-world setting of Chinese patients with persistent PSA or BCR after RP. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://tau.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tau-2025-1-901/rc).

Methods

Patient selection

Of the 696 patients who underwent RP at Daping Hospital between August 2009 and June 2022, 175 were ultimately enrolled. The inclusion criterion was histologically confirmed prostate adenocarcinoma. The exclusion criteria included patients with distant metastatic disease confirmed by bone scan or prostate-specific membrane antigen positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PSMA PET/CT); patients receiving post-RP adjuvant ADT not in accordance with the Intermittent Androgen Deprivation (IAD) Therapy Protocol (Figure 1A); and patients with incomplete clinical and pathological information. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments (15). This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Daping Hospital (No. 2024_311) and individual consent for this analysis was waived due to the retrospective nature.

Study variables

The clinical and pathological parameters of participants were retrospectively reviewed using the electronic medical record system. The parameters retrieved were as follows: age, body mass index (BMI), free prostate-specific antigen (fPSA), PSA at diagnosis, combined use of metformin, clinical T staging, pathological T staging, pathological Gleason grade group (GGG), perineural invasion, vascular invasion, date of RP, SRT in the first treatment cycle, and duration of each intermittent phase.

The PSA levels and examination dates throughout the entire follow-up period were collected to calculate PSA kinetics. The parameters evaluated included PSA doubling time (PSADT) in the first intermittent phase, calculated using the formula: PSADT = ln (2)/(slope of ln (PSA) during the first intermittent phase) (16). Testosterone doubling time (TDT) in the first intermittent phase was derived using a similar method, with the formula: TDT = ln (2)/(slope of ln (testosterone) during the first intermittent phase). PSA half-life (PSAT1/2) was calculated as PSAT1/2 = dt × ln (2)/(slope of ln (PSA) during the first treatment cycle), where dt represents the time interval between measurements (in months) (17). Using the ultrasensitive PSA assay, an undetectable PSA level was considered equivalent to 0.01 ng/mL. Rapid deep testosterone suppression was defined as testosterone ≤20 ng/mL following the first luteinizing hormone-releasing hormone (LHRH) injection (18). Testosterone recovery to normal levels, defined as testosterone >1,750 ng/mL, was assessed during the first Intermittent Phase (18). The PSA response rate after the first LHRH injection was evaluated as the percentage reduction in PSA levels.

IAD protocol

Patients who underwent RP were divided into two subgroups based on the timing of ADT initiation: the immediate ADT group and the BCR group. ADT was administered as monotherapy with LHRH analogs. Patients were classified into the immediate ADT group if they exhibited persistent PSA levels (PSA ≥0.1 ng/mL at 6 weeks post-RP) and commenced adjuvant ADT within 3 months following RP. For patients with PSA levels <0.1 ng/mL at 6 weeks post-RP, PSA and testosterone levels were monitored every 3 to 6 months until BCR occurred, at which point salvage ADT was initiated, classifying them into the BCR group (Figure 1A). BCR was defined as a PSA level >0.2 ng/mL on two consecutive tests taken two weeks apart, with the recurrence date assigned to the day the PSA first reached ≥0.2 ng/mL (18).

Adjuvant ADT was administered for a fixed period of 18 months, with treatment completed if the final PSA level was confirmed to be ≤0.01 ng/mL, after which patients entered the intermittent phase. During follow-up, PSA and testosterone levels were measured every 3 months during the on-treatment period, every 3 months during the first year of the intermittent phase, and every 6 months thereafter. If PSA levels exceeded the prespecified threshold of 0.2 ng/mL, a new cycle of ADT, lasting another 18 months, was initiated. Each cycle was defined as the completion of both an “on” and “off” phase, with 18 months of ADT followed by a variable off-treatment period (Figure 1B). Patients continued cycling on and off ADT until CRPC developed, defined as a PSA increase of at least 2.0 ng/mL and a 25% rise from nadir, confirmed by a second PSA test at least 4 weeks later (Figure 1A) (2).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics 26, GraphPad Prism 8, and R (version 4.2.2). Continuous variables with a normal distribution are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD), while those with a skewed distribution are presented as median [interquartile range (IQR)]. Categorical data were reported as counts and percentages. Kaplan-Meier analysis was used to assess CRPC-free survival. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression identified independent factors for CRPC and the duration of the first intermittent phase.

To explore factors influencing a first intermittent phase shorter than 12 months, baseline characteristics such as fPSA and BMI were balanced using 1:1 propensity score matching (PSM). Subsequently, univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to evaluate factors associated with a shorter first intermittent phase and the efficacy of ADT after initiating the second treatment phase. A nomogram was developed for clinical application, and its performance was assessed using ROC curves, C-index, and calibration curves. Statistical significance was set at P<0.05 (two-tailed).

Results

Patient characteristics

Between August 2009 and June 2022, a total of 175 patients were enrolled in our institution’s novel IAD protocol after RP. All 175 patients completed the first treatment phase and successfully entered the first off-treatment intermittent phase. Table 1 outlines the baseline characteristics of the study cohort. Specifically, among the 117 patients with ≥ pT3a disease, 46 cases were pT3a, 34 cases were pT3b, and 37 cases were pT4. The efficacy of our IAD protocol

The median follow-up duration after the initial ADT period was 44.77 months (IQR, 31.87–69.50 months). During this follow-up, 20 patients (11.4%) progressed to CRPC, including 8 patients (4.6%) with non-metastatic CRPC (nmCRPC) and 12 (6.8%) with metastatic CRPC (mCRPC), of whom 2 patients succumbed to mCRPC. The median time from the initiation of ADT to CRPC development was 50.28 months (IQR, 42.48–64.42 months). The 5- and 10-year CRPC-free survival rates for the entire cohort were 85.9% and 66.3%, respectively (Figure 2A).

The cohort was stratified into an immediate treatment group and a BCR group based on whether patients received ADT immediately after RP or upon BCR. The cohort included 124 patients (70.86%) in the immediate treatment group and 51 patients (29.14%) in the BCR group. The 5-year CRPC-free survival rates were 82.6% for the immediate treatment group and 93.3% for the BCR group, with 10-year rates of 69.6% and 66.8%, respectively (Figure S2A,S2B). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis showed no significant difference in CRPC-free survival between the two groups (Figure S2C,S2D).

In subgroup analyses, 27 patients received SRT during the first ADT phase, while 36 underwent neoadjuvant ADT prior to surgery (Table S1). Neither SRT [hazard ratio (HR), 95% confidence interval (CI): 2.082, 0.845–5.133, P=0.10, Figure S2E] nor preoperative neoadjuvant ADT (HR, 95% CI: 1.560, 0.523–4.658, P=0.42, Figure S2F) significantly impacted CRPC-free survival. Additionally, under our IAD protocol, as treatment cycles progressed, a descriptive analysis of the number of patients and the median duration of each intermittent phase in the total cohort and the two subgroups (immediate treatment and BCR groups) revealed a gradual decline in the median duration of each phase (Figure S3).

Prognostic factors for our IAD protocol

While the overall CRPC-free survival rates with our IAD protocol were promising, there were some patients who progressed to CRPC more quickly. To further explore factors that may influence the efficacy of our IAD protocol, we evaluated potential predictors of CRPC occurrence. Univariate Cox regression analyses identified pathological GGG 5 as a risk factor (HR, 95% CI: 4.46, 1.70–11.69, P=0.002) and the duration of the first intermittent phase as a protective factor (HR, 95% CI: 0.89, 0.84–0.94, P<0.001). Multivariate analyses confirmed that a longer first intermittent phase was significantly associated with a prolonged CRPC-free survival. Specifically, each additional month in the first intermittent phase increased the CRPC-free survival rate by approximately 1.12 times (Figure 2B). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis showed that patients with a first intermittent phase ≥16.37 months had significantly longer CRPC-free survival time compared to those with a phase <16.37 months (HR, 95% CI: 0.142, 0.054–0.374, P<0.001, Figure 2C). Overall, these results indicate a strong association between the duration of the first intermittent phase and CRPC-free survival time with our IAD protocol.

Among patients with pN1 disease, the median time to CRPC in our IAD cohort was 42.15 months (95% CI: 32.18–58.83 months). Furthermore, we conducted a comparative analysis between pN0 and pN1 patients treated with IAD. The results showed no statistically significant differences in CRPC-free survival (P=0.08) or the duration of the first off-treatment interval [pN0: 17.53 months (95% CI: 9.32–26.62 months) vs. pN1: 14.80 months (95% CI: 8.03–23.80 months), P=0.25] between these two subgroups.

Prognostic factors for the duration of the first intermittent phase

As above mentioned, the duration of the first intermittent phase was strongly associated with CRPC-free survival. To further explore the factors influencing the length of the first intermittent phase, we defined PSA progression (PSA ≥0.2 ng/mL during the intermittent phase) as the endpoint event and performed univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses. The analysis revealed that pathological GGG 5 grade was a risk factor (HR, 95% CI: 2.03, 1.23–3.34, P=0.005) for PSA progression, while a PSADT ≥3.32 months during the first intermittent phase (HR, 95% CI: 0.08, 0.05–0.15, P<0.001) and a TDT ≥5.03 months (HR, 95% CI: 0.47, 0.27–0.80, P=0.005) were protective factors (Figure 3A). To facilitate clinical application, we developed a nomogram using the multivariate Cox model to estimate the probability of maintaining the first intermittent phase for 12, 18, and 24 months (Figure 3B). The model’s concordance index (C-index) was 0.82 (95% CI: 0.77–0.86), indicating strong predictive accuracy. Both the calibration curve and clinical decision curves demonstrated good predictive performance (Figure 3C,3D).

To further investigate the characteristics of cases with a first intermittent phase shorter than 12 months, we divided patients into two groups (>12 vs. ≤12 months) and applied PSM at a 1:1 ratio to balance baseline differences in fPSA and BMI (Table S2). The baseline clinical characteristics after matching are presented in Table S3. Subsequent univariate and multivariate binary logistic regression analyses identified a PSADT ≥3.32 months during the first intermittent phase as a protective factor against a phase duration shorter than 12 months [odds ratio (OR), 95% CI: 0.01, 0.00–0.13, P<0.001]. Additionally, the use of metformin was also associated with a longer first intermittent phase (OR, 95% CI: 0.13, 0.02–0.74, P=0.02) (Table S4).

PSA response after initiating the second ADT treatment phase: analysis and contributing factors

While the majority of patients remained responsive to ADT during the second treatment phase, the proportion of those achieving a PSA response rate of ≥90% after the first phase showed a declining trend, indicating a gradual decrease in the efficacy of salvage ADT over treatment cycles. This trend was observed in both the overall cohort and the two subgroups (immediate treatment and BCR groups) (Table S5).

To further investigate the factors associated with a PSA response rate of <90% during salvage ADT in the second treatment phase, we performed univariate and multivariate binary logistic regression analyses. The analyses identified the use of metformin (OR, 95% CI: 0.24, 0.07–0.83, P=0.02), testosterone recovery to normal levels during the first intermittent phase (OR, 95% CI: 0.07, 0.01–0.37, P=0.002), and a PSA response rate of ≥90% after the first LHRH injection in the first treatment cycle (OR, 95% CI: 0.21, 0.05–0.81, P=0.02) as protective factors (Figure 4A). These factors were associated with maintaining sensitivity to salvage ADT during the second treatment phase. To facilitate clinical application, we developed a nomogram based on the multivariate logistic model to predict the probability of a PSA response rate of <90% during salvage ADT in the second treatment phase (Figure 4B). The model’s area under the curve (AUC) was 0.84 (95% CI: 0.76–0.92, Figure 4C), indicating strong predictive accuracy. The calibration and clinical decision curves demonstrated good predictive performance (Figure 4D,4E).

Discussion

IAD therapy was initially introduced as a treatment option for patients with metastatic prostate cancer (mPCa) to mitigate the significant side effects associated with long-term CAD (19). Several meta-analyses have demonstrated that IAD not only provides oncologic outcomes comparable to CAD in mPCa but also significantly reduces treatment-related adverse effects, making it a more favorable choice for improving patients’ quality of life (20-22). However, the application of IAD in patients with BCR or persistent PSA following RP has been less explored. The limited available studies in this patient population have associated IAD with improved overall survival and a higher 5-year recurrence-free rate (12), highlighting the potential clinical value of this treatment strategy. In the present study, we developed and evaluated a standardized IAD protocol in Chinese PCa patients with BCR or persistent PSA after RP. Our key findings demonstrate that this IAD approach can achieve favorable oncologic outcomes, with a median time from ADT to CRPC of 50.28 months. Moreover, we identified several factors that influence the duration of the first intermittent phase, which is a critical determinant of long-term prognosis for our IAD approach. Specifically, a PSADT ≥3.32 months during the first intermittent phase and a total duration of treatment (TDT) ≥5.03 months were associated with a prolonged first intermittent phase. Additionally, while most patients remained responsive to salvage ADT during the second treatment phase, the proportion of those achieving a high PSA response rate (≥90%) showed a declining trend over subsequent treatment cycles. Factors such as the use of metformin, testosterone recovery to normal levels, and a high PSA response rate in the first treatment cycle were found to be protective against decreased salvage ADT efficacy.

The selection of appropriate PSA thresholds for treatment initiation and discontinuation is one of the most critical aspects of IAD protocols that have shown inconsistency across studies. In previous explorations of IAD protocols, Kaneko et al. used a PSA threshold of 2 ng/mL for resuming treatment without a fixed ADT duration, indicating that IAD could prolong hormone sensitivity while mitigating side effects (23). Yu et al., in the first prospective clinical trial, implemented a PSA threshold of 1 ng/mL with a 9-month ADT duration, concluding that a longer first “off” phase improved overall survival and delayed progression to CRPC, with a median time to CRPC of 2.9 years following the first cycle (24). In another study, Sciarra et al. adopted a flexible ADT duration, pausing treatment at PSA levels below 0.2 ng/mL and resuming it when PSA exceeded 4 ng/mL, reporting a median time from RP to CRPC of 90.0 months (25). More recently, Kano et al. employed a fixed 24-month treatment cycle, and one course was completed after PSA levels were confirmed to be less than sensitive (<0.008 ng/mL). Failure of intermittent treatment was defined as a rise in PSA level above 0.2 ng/mL after ADT was discontinued (26). Although specific CRPC-free survival times were not reported, their findings similarly indicated that a longer initial “off” phase was associated with significantly improved CRPC-free survival, further supporting the role of intermittent ADT in delaying disease progression (26). Similarly, the results of our study, which demonstrated a significant correlation between a longer first “off” phase and improved CRPC-free survival, align with previous reports, further validating the efficacy of our IAD protocol. Additionally, our IAD protocol offers unique advantages by setting a PSA threshold of ≤0.01 ng/mL to pause treatment and 0.2 ng/mL to resume ADT, ensuring tumor control at a lower PSA level. This rigorous approach enhances tumor control safety while minimizing the risk of disease progression. Matsumoto et al. demonstrated that undetectable PSA levels (<0.01 ng/mL) were associated with an extremely low risk of subsequent BCR (27). Additionally, Ellis et al. highlighted the clinical utility of ultrasensitive PSA measurements in the comprehensive evaluation of prostate cancer patients (28). Thus, despite potential assay variability, repeated PSA testing may not be necessary for values ≤0.01 ng/mL. Furthermore, each cycle includes a fixed 18-month ADT treatment phase, allowing for prolonged castration, which facilitates consistent patient follow-up and more effective disease management.

Our analysis revealed that among patients with pN1 disease, the median time to CRPC in our IAD cohort was 42.15 months (95% CI: 32.18–58.83). This is comparable to the previously reported median CRPC-free survival of 36 months for pN1 patients treated with CAD, with no statistically significant difference observed (P=0.36) (12,29). Furthermore, we conducted a comparative analysis between pN0 and pN1 patients treated with IAD. The results showed no statistically significant differences in CRPC-free survival (P=0.08) or the duration of the first off-treatment interval [pN0: 17.53 months (95% CI: 9.32–26.62 months) vs. pN1: 14.80 months (95% CI: 8.03–23.80 months), P=0.25] between these two subgroups. These findings suggest that lymph node involvement (pN1 status) does not preclude the use of IAD as a treatment strategy. In summary, our data indicate that IAD achieves comparable CRPC-free survival to CAD in pN1 patients while reducing adverse events. Therefore, IAD can be considered a viable treatment option for patients with pN1 disease.

Identifying the key factors that influence the prognosis of patients undergoing our IAD protocol is critical for optimizing patient selection and treatment outcomes. As we mentioned above, the duration of the first intermittent phase is closely related to the prognosis of patients undergoing our IAD protocol. Therefore, we investigated the key determinants influencing the duration of the first intermittent phase. Our results showed that pathological GGG 5, PSADT, and TDT are strongly associated with the duration of the first intermittent phase. Among these, pathological GGG 5 is well-established as a key determinant of tumor biological behavior and an important predictor of treatment efficacy (30,31). Similarly, PSADT is recognized as an effective marker of tumor proliferation rate, with shorter PSADT closely associated with tumor recurrence (16). In addition, TDT represents a time-dependent dynamic measure that reflects the rate of testosterone recovery during the intermittent phase, capturing more detailed tumor progression information than a single testosterone value. The method for calculating TDT is derived from PSADT, and we are the first to define and apply it. Moreover, multivariate Cox analysis revealed that a longer TDT serves as a protective factor against PSA progression during the first intermittent phase of IAD. This suggests that low testosterone levels may inhibit cancer cell proliferation by suppressing androgen receptor (AR) signaling pathways. However, during the second treatment phase, patients whose testosterone levels did not recover during the intermittent phase exhibited reduced sensitivity to ADT compared to those with normal testosterone recovery. This reduced sensitivity following PSA progression could be attributed to prolonged low testosterone levels, potentially promoting the transition of prostate cancer cells to AR-independent subtypes (32). These findings highlight the importance of optimizing the intermittent phase duration and maintaining appropriate testosterone levels to ensure sustained responsiveness to salvage ADT, thereby improving the overall efficacy of the IAD approach.

Interestingly, we found that metformin may offer potential benefits in prolonging the first intermittent phase and preserving sensitivity to ADT during IAD therapy, likely due to its non-specific anti-inflammatory properties (33,34). This is supported by our previous research, which demonstrated that metformin suppresses inflammatory infiltration by downregulating COX2 and PGE2 in tumor cells, thereby inhibiting prostate cancer recurrence and metastasis (35). In addition to its anti-inflammatory effects, metformin exerts antitumor activity through multiple mechanisms, including activation of the AMPK/mTOR pathway, inhibition of epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), downregulation of IGF-1R signaling, and regulation of tumor metabolism (36-38). However, the clinical efficacy of metformin in combination therapies remains controversial. While A. Dal Pra et al. reported no significant improvement in progression-free survival when metformin was used as an adjuvant during postoperative radiotherapy for prostate cancer (39), Huynh et al. found that metformin significantly reduced the risk of BCR within five years following prostatectomy or radiotherapy (40). Our findings suggest that combining ADT with metformin may represent a more effective therapeutic strategy for prostate cancer treatment.

This study has limitations, including a single-center retrospective design and relatively small sample size due to the strict IAD protocol, requiring validation in large-scale multicenter prospective studies. Additionally, short follow-up limited in-depth analysis of the first intermittent phase, necessitating long-term studies to explore the efficacy and safety of subsequent phases. Finally, the impact of ADT on patients’ quality of life (QoL) and treatment-related adverse events was not systematically evaluated, though no severe adverse events occurred during treatment.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the duration of the first intermittent phase is a significant predictor of CRPC-free survival. Additionally, PSADT, TDT, and pathological GGG 5 are recognized as independent prognostic factors for PSA progression during this period. Furthermore, the concomitant use of metformin provides a potential new therapy for maintaining ADT sensitivity in patients experiencing PSA progression during the initial intermittent phase.
