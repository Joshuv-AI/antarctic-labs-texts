Development of the Angelman syndrome video assessment: quantifying meaningful change
Authors: Kriszha A Sheehy, Mindy G Leffler, Rebecca J Woods, Robert Komorowski, Rebecca Crean, Christina K Zigler, Jessica Duis, Olivia Boorom, Nancy Brady, Lauren DeValk, Nicole Harris, Amber Sapp, Caroline Woeber, Anjali Sadhwani, Wen-Hann Tan, ASVA Delphi Panelists
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Angelman Syndrome Video Assessment (ASVA) is a clinician-reported outcome measure that was developed to assess the functional ability of individuals with Angelman Syndrome (AS) in a familiar environment. Through standardized tasks and associated scorecards, clinicians assess four meaningful domains of functioning: communication, activities of daily living (ADLs, which include fine motor skills), gross motor, and external direction (i.e., the ability to follow directions) via scorecards with pre-established criteria. The aim of this project was to develop and refine the scorecards using a rigorous process in partnership with caregivers, clinicians, and researchers in the AS community.MethodsThe Scorecard development process included four phases: (1) video source material study, (2) identification of initial scoring criteria, (3) scorecard drafts, and (4) two (Caregiver and Clinician panel and PT panel) two-round modified Delphi processes to reach consensus. All phases were conducted remotely except for Round 2 of the Caregiver and Clinician Delphi Panel, which was conducted in person. Votes were held for each scoring criterion and consensus was defined as ≥ 70% agreement.ResultsIn the communication, ADLs, and external direction domains, scorecard criteria reached 80 to 100% agreement among caregivers (n = 8) and clinicians (n = 2), resulting in a total of 218 scoring criteria and levels across 10 tasks. In the gross motor domain, scorecard criteria reached 100% agreement among physical therapists (n = 8) with a total of 347 scoring criteria and levels across 8 tasks.ConclusionsThe ASVA was developed with insights from the AS community, including caregivers of individuals with AS, clinicians, and researchers. The ASVA is a novel, disease-specific, clinician-reported outcome measure that uses standardized video capture and scorecards that were developed through a rigorous process, resulting in well-developed criteria to quantify meaningful changes of function in individuals with AS in communication, ADLs, gross motor function, and external direction.Keywords: Angelman syndrome, Neurodevelopmental, Video assessment, Outcome measure, Patient outcome assessment, Activities of daily living, Meaningful clinical outcome, Patient-focused drug development

Background

Angelman Syndrome (AS) is a rare neurodevelopmental condition with an estimated prevalence of 1/22,000 to 1/52,000 [1]. Severe developmental delay is a prominent feature of AS and is usually noted within the first year of life. Most individuals with AS are developmentally equivalent to 14- to 27-month-olds, making slow developmental gains of around one to two months per year depending on domain [1, 2]. Consistent clinical features include limited expressive language, seizures, movement or balance disorders (e.g., ataxic gait and/or tremulous limb movements, lurching, unsteadiness, and jerky motions), and unique behavioral traits (e.g., hyperexcitability, hand-flapping, and hypermotoric behavior) [1–4]. Additional features of AS include mouthing behaviors and anxiety (generalized and/or separation) [2, 4, 5]. Despite their limited expressive language skills, individuals with AS have relative strengths in social and receptive language skills, albeit far weaker relative to their neurotypical peers [1, 2]. Individuals with AS also display maladaptive behaviors such as behavioral noncompliance and temper tantrums, often associated with anxiety or excitability, a desire for attention, or frustration over an inability to communicate effectively [1–5].

Current outcome measures

The Bayley Scales of Infant and Toddler Development (Bayley) [6] and Vineland Adaptive Behavior Scales (Vineland) [7] are commonly used to assess the developmental and adaptive skills in people with AS and have also been used in clinical trials and natural history studies [1, 8, 9]. Recently, adaptations of the clinical global impression (CGI) [10] scale focusing on AS-specific domains such as seizures, growth, motor skills, scoliosis, communication, behavior, and sleep as well as broader concepts like global neurodevelopment have been developed and deployed in clinical trials [11–14].

Despite the historical use of the Bayley and the Vineland in the AS population and the recent development of AS-specific CGIs, these measures have limitations. The Bayley was initially developed as a tool to diagnose developmental issues during early childhood [15], while the Vineland was developed to measure an individual’s adaptive level of functioning. However, their ability to detect meaningful change with successful intervention in a clinical trial in those with AS has not been established. The Bayley, standardized for use in children between one and 42 months, includes tasks that may not be developmentally appropriate for older children, which can result in lack of attention or motivation and negatively impact scores [1, 2] Furthermore, some subscales of the Bayley utilize standardized tasks that may not be relevant to families and are reliant on verbal speech and fine motor skills, which are often lacking in those with AS, thereby limiting their ability to demonstrate their skills and capabilities [1]. Although the CGI scales have been adapted for AS, they are highly subjective, and intra-rater and inter-rater reliability has not been established [16]. Additionally, clinic-based assessments (e.g., Bayley) may be negatively affected by factors such as anxiety, travel fatigue, and caregiver separation [5]. While efforts are underway to modify the administration and scoring of the Vineland for individuals with moderate or severe intellectual disability, recall bias or placebo effects may continue to impact scores on caregiver-report measures like the Vineland and affect the interpretation of the CGI scales [8, 17, 18]. Additional direct observation measures designed in collaboration with caregivers and clinicians may help to better understand the impact of potential treatments in AS [3, 19].

Angelman Syndrome Video Assessment (ASVA)

A collaborative group of researchers has begun developing the Angelman Syndrome Video Assessment (ASVA), a clinician-reported outcome measure consisting of videos of patients performing standardized tasks in their home environment at prescribed timepoints that are then scored by trained and certified central raters, allowing for blinding to both treatment and assessment timepoints. Function is assessed in four domains: communication, activities of daily living (ADLs, which includes fine motor skills), gross motor, and ability to follow external direction. The process to develop the ASVA followed the same steps to develop the Duchenne Video Assessment (DVA), a reliable, disease-specific outcome measure designed to detect clinically meaningful change in Duchenne Muscular Dystrophy [20–22]. Initial development of the ASVA included drafting, piloting, and refining the video capture procedures [23]. The current manuscript describes the development and refinement of the ASVA quantification methodology.

Methods

Scorecard development and refinement consisted of four phases (Fig. 1). Phase 1 included the initiation of a source material study to collect videos for the development and validation of scorecards. Phase 2 consisted of workshops with key opinion leaders (KOLs) in the AS clinical community and researchers to identify a list of potential scoreable criteria. Phase 3 consisted of researchers drafting an initial version of scorecards. Phase 4 consisted of two 2-round modified Delphi processes [24–28] with caregivers and clinicians of individuals with AS (Caregiver and Clinician panel) and physical therapists (PT Panel) to evaluate the clarity, comprehensiveness, and meaningfulness of the scorecards.

Phase 1: Source material study

Videos were collected during the ASVA source material study (NCT05637697), which was approved by Advarra IRB (Pro00057202). The source material study was a longitudinal observational study to capture the video data required to design and validate ASVA scorecards. Caregiver-subject dyads (AS and neurotypical) were recruited through advocacy organizations and clinicians. Neurotypical caregiver-subject dyads were included to account for functional gains associated with neurotypical development. To be eligible for the study, subjects with AS had to be one year or older and have a laboratory-confirmed diagnosis of AS. Neurotypical subjects had to be between 1 and 8 years old. All caregivers had to be comfortable reading and speaking in English. Caregivers were provided with a video capture manual and training videos. Videos were collected at three assessment timepoints (baseline, baseline retest that commenced after submission of the initial baseline videos, and 12 weeks after baseline). Additionally, Vineland and Caregiver Global Impression of Change were conducted at 12 weeks to evaluate the construct validity of the ASVA.

Phase 2: Initial scoring criteria identification (workshops)

In the second phase of scorecard development, the team that participated in the drafting, piloting, and refining of the video capture protocol (Table 1) was asked to independently watch and review videos from an earlier pilot study and the source material study to identify potential scoring criteria that differentiated the functioning of participants with AS from those who were neurotypical and to identify meaningful differences between the levels of functioning in people with AS. Reviewers were asked to continue watching the videos until saturation was reached (i.e., no new potential criteria observed in 3 consecutive videos). Once independent reviews were completed, the lists of scorable criteria were collated and online workshops were hosted to discuss the potential criteria for tasks in each domain: communication, ADLs, gross motor, and external direction. The team continued discussion until 100% agreement was reached on the list of scorable criteria for each task. See Table 2 for list of scoring criteria for each domain.

Phase 3: Initial scorecard drafts

The Casimir team then structured the criteria developed by the team and the functional levels within each criterion into scorecards. The development team reviewed the scorecards and provided feedback. Feedback was then compiled and addressed by revising the scorecards where applicable for use in the Modified Delphi Process.

Phase 4: Modified Delphi process

Design and panel selection

The team chose to determine meaningful score difference in the newly designed scorecards in alignment with the Food and Drug Administration’s (FDA) Patient-Focused Drug Development (PFDD) Guidance on assessment of what patients would regard as clinically meaningful within-patient change [29]. The Delphi panel is considered a valid methodical process for integrating viewpoints from multiple contributors to reach a consensus in the development of patient-centered outcome measures [26, 29, 30]. The purpose of the Delphi was to gain consensus from appropriate reporters on the clarity, comprehensiveness, and meaningfulness of each criterion and its functional levels. Caregivers were selected as reporters for the communication, ADL, and external direction domains due to the inability of people with AS to self-report [30]. Two clinicians joined that panel to ensure that opinions raised were not unique to individuals with AS. Because of the technical nature of the gross motor scorecard criteria, physical therapists were selected as the reporters for the Delphi on that domain.

The Delphi was held in two rounds: the first consisted of asking participants to independently evaluate the meaningfulness, clarity, and comprehensiveness of the scorecard criteria. The second round consisted of a collaborative discussion designed to reach consensus on the opinions raised on the questionnaires (Fig. 2).

Caregiver and clinician panel Round 1

Panelists were provided with a brief overview of the ASVA, example videos, and the scorecards. For each scorecard, the Caregiver and Clinician panel was asked for feedback in a questionnaire, including whether: a) scorecards were clear or easy to understand, and suggestions on how to improve clarity, if needed; b) moving from one level to another within a criterion would be a meaningful change (i.e., a change that would improve the life of the individual with AS, make caring for them easier, or improve interaction with the individual with AS); c) the symptoms or behaviors identified were representative of key aspects of functioning within individuals with AS; d) the functional levels were arranged accurately in correct order of severity; e) any level or criterion should be added or removed; and f) they had any additional comments or feedback. Responses were collated, and any question from the questionnaire with less than 100% agreement from respondents was put on the discussion list for the in-person portion of the Delphi. Edits were made to the scorecards when respondents suggested small, common-sense changes to improve clarity. For suggestions that represented more substantive changes, such as adding or deleting a functional level within a criterion, the change was made if at least 70% of respondents made the same suggestion, with the rationale that 70% was a high threshold for agreement on spontaneously elicited suggestions. Changes suggested by fewer than 70% of respondents were included as part of the in-person discussion for Round 2.

PT panel Round 1

The PT Panel followed the same procedures with a few exceptions. First, clinically meaningful change was defined as “a change that makes it easier or harder for the patient to move around, requires more or less support from caregivers, or makes the patient more or less prone to falls”. Second, the question, “Are the levels equal in meaningfulness of change they indicate?” was added to the questionnaire to improve efficiency for group discussion regarding the weightage of criteria and levels.

Caregiver and clinician panel Round 2

The goals of Round 2 were to discuss areas of disagreement from Round 1, reach consensus (≥80% agreement) [31] on all revisions to the scorecards, and establish weightage for criteria and levels. Researchers facilitated an in-person 6-hour group discussion, and panelists were encouraged to openly share their views throughout discussion. The researchers presented the panel with an overview of the ASVA development process and fully anonymized feedback that required discussions from Round 1. Round 2 discussions focused on solutions to address issues raised in Round 1, additional changes suggested in Round 2, clarity of wording, adding or removing criteria or levels to ensure accuracy and sensitivity to meaningful change, and weighting criteria and levels to better reflect the degree of meaningful change they represent. Each scorecard was discussed, and a vote was held on each recommendation. Votes were recorded in a discussion worksheet and discussions continued until consensus was reached. Scorecards were revised based on recommendations that reached consensus in Round 2. Additionally, a follow-up discussion for a subset of the Caregiver and Clinician panel (caregivers n = 6, clinicians = 2) was hosted online to evaluate new criteria language that was developed based on Delphi discussions.

PT panel Round 2

The PT Panel followed the same procedures as the Caregiver and Clinician panel with a few exceptions. The PT Panel was hosted virtually on a video-conferencing platform and over two 2-hour discussions.

Results

Videos for the ASVA source material study (Phase 1) were provided by 21 AS caregiver-subject dyads and four neurotypical caregiver-subject dyads with subjects aged 1 to 40 years old and were available for review during Phase 2 and Phase 3.

Phase 4: Modified Delphi process - Round 1

Based on questionnaire responses from the Caregiver and Clinician panel, a total of 79 changes were made to scorecards (182 total criteria and levels), with 22 changes in the communication domain, 33 in the ADLs domain, and 24 in the external direction domain. All scorecards had at least one change. Changes consisted of 51 wording changes to improve clarity, 7 removed criteria or levels, 20 added criteria or levels, and 1 hierarchical change with a new total of 195 criteria and levels. Based on questionnaire results from the PT Panel, a total of 71 changes were made to scorecards (282 total criteria and levels) in the gross motor domain and all scorecards had at least one change made. Changes consisted of 24 wording changes, 9 removed criteria or levels, 38 added criteria or levels, 0 hierarchical changes, and 0 weighted criteria or levels. During this round, changes were made to scorecards when wording changes were recommended to improve clarity or spontaneously proposed by at least 70 percent of panelists. Phase 4: Modified Delphi process - Round 2

Caregiver and clinician panel

Eight scorecards from the communication (n = 2), ADLs (n= 3), and external direction domains (n = 2) were discussed. There were several proposed revisions in the ADL domain that required writing new criteria that needed further evaluation. The Caregiver and Clinician panel reached at least 80% agreement on all changes made to the scorecards in the communication, ADLs, and external direction domains. There were 158 total changes to scorecards by the Caregiver and Clinician panel (in-person and remote follow-up included), with 52 changes in the communication domain, 82 changes in the ADLs domain, and 24 changes in the external direction domain. Changes consisted of 39 wording changes, 22 removed criteria or levels, 71 added criteria or levels, 0 hierarchical changes, 26 weighted criteria or levels, resulting in total of 218 criteria and levels for scorecards in communication, ADLs, and external direction across 10 tasks.

PT Panel

The PT Panel reached 100% agreement on all changes made to the scorecards in the gross motor domain and 83 total changes were made. Changes consisted of 26 wording changes, 11 removed criteria or levels, 46 added criteria or levels, 0 hierarchical changes, 0 weighted criteria or levels, resulting in 347 criteria and levels for scorecards in gross motor across 8 tasks.

Discussion

The current manuscript describes the development and refinement of scorecards for the ASVA, a novel, remotely collected AS-specific assessment to assess functional abilities in multiple domains via standardized video capture in the home environment using a previously described methodology [20–22]. This rigorous, multidisciplinary approach included four phases: source material study, initial scoring criteria identification, initial scorecard drafts, and two modified Delphi processes. All phases, except Round 2 of the Caregiver Delphi process, were conducted remotely, allowing for ease of collaboration and scheduling among individuals located in different geographic locations. Round 2 of the Caregiver Delphi process was conducted at the Angelman Syndrome Foundation Family Conference venue prior to the start of the conference, allowing for ease of scheduling and identification of Caregiver and Clinician panelists who were already planning to attend. The remote process allowed for the incorporation of perspectives from multiple stakeholders: clinicians, researchers, and caregivers of individuals with AS. Those involved confirmed that scorecards and criteria represented patterns seen in the AS population, were clear and comprehensive, arranged logically, weighted appropriately, and likely to be sensitive to changes considered clinically meaningful.

Development of the scorecards used in the ASVA was done with a multi-step and rigorous process, beginning with the generation of scoring criteria by a panel of experts. After these criteria were structured into scorecards, a two-step Delphi process ensured that finalized scorecards represented the perspectives of clinicians and families. Delphi panels were asked to assess the comprehensiveness, clarity, and meaningfulness of the levels within each scorecard criteria. The first round used a high threshold of agreement for changes made without discussion (70%), allowing for an efficient way to reduce discussion on items that already had agreement. This first round resulted in 79 changes made to scorecards addressed by the Caregiver and Clinician Panel and 71 changes made to scorecards addressed by the PT Panel. The second phase of the Delphi panel reached higher levels of agreement on discussion items, with the Caregiver and Clinician Panel reaching 80% agreement on changes (158 total) and the PT Panel reaching 100% agreement on changes (83 total). The high level of agreement between Delphi participants underscores the validity of the finalized scorecards.

Although the Delphi method is useful for determining what is important to patients, the modified Delphi process used in Phase 4 was not without limitations. First, all panelists resided in the U.S., and it is possible panelists from different countries may have differing views on the ASVA scorecards regarding clarity, comprehensiveness, ordering, weighting, and sensitivity to changes considered clinically meaningful due to cultural differences. Future research may be needed to evaluate the ASVA for its applicability and relevance in patients with AS in other countries.

Second, panelists were not provided with their peers’ feedback from Round 1 prior to Round 2, which is typical of traditional Delphi methods. Instead, panelists were provided with a brief overview of feedback during group discussion in Round 2. Providing panelists with anonymized peer feedback may have better prepared panelists and decreased the amount of time needed for group discussion. As such, the Caregiver and Clinician panel may not have needed an online follow-up discussion with a subset of the panelists to conclude discussion on the remainder of the topics. Furthermore, not all PT panelists from Round 1 were able to participate in Round 2. It is possible that the two PTs who were unable to participate in the group discussions may have had differing views from the group. In keeping with best practices for Delphi methods, future processes should minimize attrition by ensuring panelists can participate in all rounds of the process [28, 32]. However, the PT panel did reach 100% agreement on all items discussed. In addition, traditional Delphi methods do not typically involve interactions between panelists to minimize potential social influences (e.g., caregivers defaulting to the opinion of clinicians due to expertise, some panelists not voicing their opinions because they oppose the views of the majority) [27]. Due to the complexity and heterogeneity of AS, allowing interactions between panelists provided a diversity of perspectives and allowed us to reach consensus in a timely fashion compared to multiple rounds of questionnaires. To minimize potential social influences, researchers facilitated the group discussions and actively encouraged all panelists to openly share their views throughout discussion.

Currently, there are no standardized assessments developed specifically to assess functional domains in AS, and those that are in use are either in-clinic assessments which can be impacted by variables such as anxiety or travel fatigue, or caregiver reports which can be affected by recall bias [17, 33, 34], and they may not fully capture the wide range of abilities within AS individuals [2, 3]. The ASVA addresses some of the limitations of existing measures. Remote assessment of subjects minimizes travel and avoids the potential confounds of the in-clinic assessments that are typically used (e.g., Bayley). While recent work has been done to improve the use of the Vineland with individuals with moderate to severe disability, including validating the use of ability scores over norm-referenced scores to overcome floor effects of the VABS in AS [35] and modifying and scoring to make the measure more sensitive, the ASVA has the potential to address two VABS shortcomings: the use of trained and certified central raters to assess videos eliminates errors in recall that are common in measures relying on caregiver report such as the Vineland, and it provides the opportunity to blind raters to timepoint (i.e. baseline versus final visit) via the scoring web application when scored at the end of a study [36]. Blinding clinicians to timepoints can add rigor to early stage, single-arm clinical trials, which is particularly helpful in programs in rare disease with small populations. Clinicians may be highly invested in seeing improvement in the rare disease communities they treat. As such, they, like caregivers, may be susceptible to bias in favor of improvement. Unlike the ASVA, blinding clinicians to timepoints is impossible for measures such as the Bayley or in-clinic CGIs that rely on successive evaluation at each timepoint, often conducted by the same clinician.

Conclusions

The ASVA is a clinician-reported outcome measure developed with the assistance of caregivers of people with AS and experts (e.g., PTs, SLPs, OTs) in the AS clinical community. The development and refinement of the ASVA scorecards utilized a rigorous process, resulting in well-developed scoring criteria that can potentially capture change using video capture methodology in a standardized manner. The criteria used to quantify functional ability of those with AS on the ASVA scorecards were developed by AS experts and researchers specializing in outcome measure development and reached agreement amongst appropriate reporters on its clarity, comprehensiveness, and hierarchical order. The group also confirmed the meaningfulness of the changes represented by each functional level within the criteria. The use of the ASVA in clinical trials has the potential to address the challenges of existing outcome measures. Future research will evaluate the psychometric properties of the ASVA.

Videos were collected during the ASVA Pilot study, approved by the central Institutional Review Board IntegReview (Austin, TX) and the ASVA source material study (NCT05637697), approved by Advarra IRB (Pro00057202). All caregivers were legal guardians who provided written consent for themselves and subjects and received compensation for their participation. All participating Delphi panelists signed written agreements to provide consultant services for the purposes of Delphi panel, and they were provided with an honorarium for their time. Since panelists provided expert opinion through consultant services, this was not considered human subjects research.
