Small airways disease is common in mild COPD and symptomatic subjects at risk with normal spirometry: baseline data from CAPTO-COPD
Authors: Frederik Trinkmann, Mustafa Abdo, Felix J.F. Herth, Klaus F. Rabe, Anne-Marie Kirsten, Henrik Watz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCurrent guideline-based diagnosis of airflow limitation in COPD focuses on spirometry, which is insensitive to early small airway changes of symptomatic smokers or subjects at risk. Therefore, we sought to determine the frequency of small airways disease (SAD) in these groups compared with mild COPD, and evaluate associations with symptoms as well as health-related quality of life (HRQoL).MethodsPatients with mild COPD (post-bronchodilator forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1)/forced vital capacity (FVC) <70%, FEV1 ≥70% predicted) or (ex-)smokers at risk (post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC ≥70%, ≥10 pack-years, COPD Assessment Test score ≥10 or long-acting bronchodilator treatment) were included in the Change in Airway Peripheral Tone in COPD (CAPTO-COPD) longitudinal observational study. Comprehensive lung function testing included conventional (spirometry, body plethysmography, diffusing capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide, exhaled nitric oxide fraction) and advanced techniques (oscillometry, multiple-breath washout).ResultsWe present cross-sectional baseline data from 90 COPD patients (mean±sd age 65±9 years; mean±sd FEV1 77±12% pred), 62 subjects at risk (60±10 years, 89±13% pred) and 59 controls (45±199 years, 98±12% pred). Abnormal SAD-values were found in up to 58% of (ex-)smokers at risk and 60% of COPD patients. Meaningful HRQoL differences were most pronounced for SAD-parameters of peripheral resistance (frequency dependence of resistance p=0.03) and elastic lung properties (reactance area p=0.06, resonance frequency p=0.01).ConclusionSAD is common even in symptomatic (ex-)smokers not fulfilling spirometric criteria with a similar frequency and pattern as compared to mild COPD patients. SAD is associated with impaired HRQoL, even in patients without established COPD.

Introduction

COPD is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide. Nevertheless, COPD remains an underdiagnosed [1, 2] and undertreated condition. This can be mainly attributed to the unmet need for COPD diagnosis in symptomatic smokers in early stages of the disease [3, 4]. Current recommendations focus on the use of spirometry for the diagnosis of airflow limitation in COPD [5]. However, there is growing acceptance that spirometry is relatively insensitive for detecting early changes. This results in a desire to treat the disease before the damage is more extensive [6, 7]. Several morphological changes on computed tomography can be observed in symptomatic patients with normal spirometry [8] and early changes in lung function might be overlooked when solely relying on spirometry [9]. Clinically, this of relevance as symptomatic patients already have an impaired health-related quality of life (HRQoL) [10], but studies relating advanced lung function techniques in symptomatic (ex-)smokers at risk of COPD are scarce.

Small airways disease (SAD) has been increasingly recognised as the predominant pathophysiological feature in COPD, even in the mild stages of the disease [4, 9, 11]. More than 50 years ago, Hogg et al. [12] could already demonstrate that considerable destruction must be present in the small airways before it can be detected clinically. Further investigation demonstrated that commonly used spirometry does not reliably identify SAD [9].

Nowadays, small airway function is increasingly understood as a complex interaction of breathing including ventilation, diffusion and perfusion. Reliable identification of SAD will therefore require a multimodal diagnostic approach rather than a single technique [13]. Moreover, it should be noted that SAD is a complex term currently lacking a consistent definition in the literature. In recent years, several promising methods have been investigated. Cross-sectional studies demonstrated that oscillometry is sensitive to subtle, hence subclinical, changes in the small airways [9, 14]. Likewise, multiple-breath washout (MBW) testing allows measurement of ventilation heterogeneity [15], an indirect feature of SAD [16, 17]. In addition, early impairment of small airway function is supported by recent data from imaging studies. A reduction in total airway count in quantitative computed tomography was hypothesised to precede the appearance of emphysema [18] and predicts lung function decline [19].

Therefore, addressing SAD in clinical routine bears hope for early diagnosis, improved phenotyping, and timely treatment. However, the ideal measurement technique(s) for initial detection and monitoring of SAD warrant further investigation. We sought to 1) determine the frequency of SAD in subjects at risk and COPD patients with mild disease using a multimodal diagnostic approach and 2) describe the impact on COPD symptoms and HRQoL.

Methods

Study protocol and study subjects

Change in Airway Peripheral Tone in COPD (CAPTO-COPD) is a longitudinal observational multicentre study. Subjects were recruited at two sites in Germany (Pulmonary Research Institute at LungenClinic Grosshansdorf and Thoraxklinik at Heidelberg University Hospital). Following the baseline characterisation there is a planned follow-up of 2 years. The protocol was approved by the ethics committee of Schleswig-Holstein (092/19 I) and the medical ethics committee of Heidelberg University (S-737/2019). It is compliant with the Declaration of Helsinki and registered at clinicaltrials.gov (NCT04166812). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrolment. Key inclusion criteria include patients with mild COPD (≥10 pack-years of smoking; post-bronchodilator forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1)/forced vital capacity (FVC) <70%; and FEV1 ≥70% predicted) or (ex-)smokers at risk (≥10 pack-years of smoking; post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC ≥70%; ≥10 pack-years; and COPD Assessment Test (CAT) score ≥10 or long-acting bronchodilator treatment), aged ≥35 years. Key exclusion was other lung disease except previous asthma (supplementary material). At each visit, all subjects undergo conventional lung function testing including spirometry, body plethysmography and carbon monoxide transfer factor (TLCO). Additionally, we perform airwave oscillometry, sulfur hexafluoride (SF6)-MBW testing, and fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FENO) measurements. Laboratory testing includes a differential blood count. Validated questionnaires are used to assess symptoms and HRQoL. MBW, airwave oscillometry, body plethysmography, TLCO and FENO are conducted prior to all forced lung function manoeuvres. Cross-sectional data on the relevance of sputum eosinophils in a subset of the CAPTO-cohort patients have been reported previously [20]. Here we report the cross-sectional data collected at the baseline visit. Respiratory healthy, asymptomatic, never-smoking subjects were added from a previously published cohort (clinicaltrials.gov identifier NCT04099225) serving as controls for the machine learning-based approach aiming at identification of early disease [21].

Lung function testing

Spirometry and body plethysmography

Pre- and post-bronchodilator (400 µg of salbutamol) lung function testing including spirometry and body plethysmography was performed (MasterScreen Body; CareFusion, Höchberg, Germany). Parameters included FEV1, inspiratory vital capacity, FVC, maximum expiratory flow (MEF) at 50% and 25% of FVC, total lung capacity (TLC), residual volume (RV), functional residual capacity (FRC) and specific total airway resistance (sRtot). Reference values were derived from all-age Global Lung Function Initiative (GLI) reference equations for spirometry [22] and static lung volumes [23], respectively. European Coal and Steel Community reference values were used for MEF values [24] and Jaeger equations for Rtot and sRtot [25].

T LCO

TLCO was derived from pre-bronchodilator TLCO/ventilated alveolar volume measured using the single-breath technique (MasterScreen Body). Reference values were derived from the all-age GLI equations for TLCO [26].

F ENO

FENO was measured according to American Thoracic Society (ATS)/European Respiratory Society (ERS) recommendations [27] (NIOX VERO; Circassia, Bad Homburg, Germany).

Airwave oscillometry

A commercially available oscillometry system (Tremoflo; Thorasys Europe, Teningen, Germany) was used. We conducted three consecutive measurements during tidal breathing and derived parameters of airway resistance (resistance at 5 Hz, resistance at 19 Hz, absolute frequency dependence of resistance (FDR), relative FDR) and reactance (reactance at 5 Hz, resonance frequency (fres), reactance area (AX)) respectively. A coefficient of variation <15% was required for all directly measured parameters. Reference values were derived from the KORA (Cooperative Research in the Region of Augsburg) cohort equations [28].

Multiple-breath washout

A commercially available closed-circuit system (Innocor; COSMED Nordic, Odense, Denmark) was used for MBW measurements, as described in detail previously [29]. The device consists of a 3-L rebreathing bag filled with a mixture of room air and test gas (94% oxygen, 1% SF6 and 5% nitrous oxide; COSMED Nordic) from an on-board gas cylinder (bolus fraction 20%). Subjects were breathing tidally and the test was stopped when end tidal SF6 had fallen below 1/40 of the starting concentration for three consecutive breaths. Only patients with at least two technically acceptable MBW measurements [30] based on slightly modified ATS/ERS criteria (supplementary material) were included in final analysis. MBW parameters were derived from up to three consecutive washouts. Local ventilation heterogeneity was assessed in each of the the acinar (Sacin) and conductive (Scond) airways. Lung Clearance Index (LCI) representing global ventilation heterogeneity was calculated for the 2.5% stopping point. Proprietary software provided by the manufacturer (software version 8.0β1) was used. References values were derived as published previously by our group [21].

Questionnaires

HRQoL and symptoms were evaluated using the St George's Respiratory Questionnaire for COPD patients (SGRQ-C) and the CAT, respectively. Additionally, the modified Medical Research Council dyspnoea scale ranging from 0 to 4 was used to stratify dyspnoea.

Statistical analysis

Values are presented as mean±sd, unless stated otherwise. Differences between groups were assessed by t-test for continuous variables or Chi-squared test for categorical variables. Multiple group differences were evaluated using ANOVA. Correction for multiple testing was performed using Tukey's honestly significant difference test. An α-error of <5% in two-sided testing was considered statistically significant. Multiple imputations were used to complete missing conventional lung function data. Upper (ULN) and lower (LLN) limits of normal were defined as a z-score >1.645 and <−1.645, respectively. Detailed information on all grouping definitions is given in the supplementary material. R statistical software (v4.1.2; Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) was used for all data analysis. Sample size calculation was primarily performed for detection of longitudinal changes. 150 patients would provide 80% power to detect a meaningful change in small airway parameters with effect sizes of 0.5 and 15% missing data. This scenario is based on an estimated 1:4 ratio of patients with and without changes in small airway function, respectively.

Results

Final analysis was performed in 211 subjects including 152 patients from the CAPTO-COPD cohort. Of these, 90 (42%) patients were diagnosed with COPD (male/female 59/41%, age 65±9 years) and 62 (29%) were (ex-)smokers at risk (male/female 45/55%, age 60±10 years). Additionally, 59 (28%) controls were included (male/female 56/44%, 45±19 years). Detailed baseline characteristics are given in table 1. Neither SGRQ total score, CAT total score nor the subscores or subitems differed between COPD patients and (ex-)smokers at risk (table 1). However, it should be noted that subitem analyses are exploratory in character, not being validated for this purpose. Comorbidities and blood eosinophils with no significant differences between both groups are summarised in supplementary table S1. Lung function parameters stratified for groups are given in table 2. Peripheral obstruction was the most frequent finding in 38% of patients (abnormal FDR, AX, sRtot, Sacin, Scond and/or LCI) followed by central obstruction (FEV1/FVC 21%) and gas trapping/ hyperinflation (17%, abnormal RV/TLC, RV and/or FRC). All three components were present in 10%, with the largest overlap between peripheral and central obstruction (10%; figure 1a). Peripheral obstruction was even more frequently present in subjects at risk (58%; figure 1b). While most lung function parameters were statistically significantly different between either COPD subjects and controls or (ex-)smokers at risk and controls, statistically significant differences could also be observed between mild COPD subjects and (ex-)smokers at risk, even though some differences in parameters reflecting small airways disease where numerically rather small between COPD subjects and (ex-)smokers at risk compared to the differences between (ex-)smokers at risk and controls (e.g. AX, LCI, Sacin; table 2).

Frequency of small airways dysfunction

Abnormal values of SAD according to ULN or LLN in oscillometry and MBW were found in up to 60% of COPD patients and up to 58% of (ex-)smokers at risk (figure 2). Patterns of SAD were similar in both groups, with abnormal values being most frequent for global and acinar ventilation heterogeneity (LCI and Sacin) as well as elastic properties (AX, fres). In contrast, conventional lung function parameters were less frequently impaired in (ex-)smokers at risk except for peripheral parameters (sRtot, MEF50) and TLCO. MEF50 was abnormal in 90% of COPD patients, but only in 30% of (ex-)smokers at risk.

Symptoms and HRQoL

Patients with mild COPD and subjects at risk were dichotomised according to SGRQ-C in better (<25 points) or worse (≥25 points) HRQoL. Differences in the percentage of abnormal lung function parameters between the two groups were found for SAD parameters (AX, FDR, fres and Scond) as well as central obstruction (FEV1). This was most pronounced for peripheral resistance (FDR p=0.03) and elastic lung properties (AX p=0.06, fres p=0.01; supplementary figure S1). Symptomatic burden was assessed based on the respective single CAT items in an exploratory approach. Patients reported worsening dyspnoea more often (CAT item 4) when elastic properties were abnormal (AX p=0.02, fres p<0.01) or central obstruction (FEV1 p=0.01). Trends were found for gas trapping (RV/TLC p=0.11) and gas transfer (TLCO p=0.21; supplementary figure S2, top). Cough (CAT item 1) was primarily increasing with parameters of central obstruction (FEV1 p=0.01, MEF50 p=0.02, hyperinflation (FRC p=0.01) and a trend for gas trapping (RV/TLC p=0.08; supplementary figure S2, middle). There was only one meaningful finding for phlegm with lung function measures including hyperinflation (FRC p=0.02) as indicated by CAT item 2 (supplementary figure S2, bottom).

Discussion

We demonstrate that abnormal parameters of SAD are frequently present in patients with mild COPD and subjects at risk. Additionally, in patients at risk for COPD, SAD was associated with an impaired quality of life and respiratory symptoms, especially dyspnoea. Compared with mild COPD, oscillometry and MBW showed similar abnormal patterns in subjects at risk not covered by conventional parameters.

Our findings extend available knowledge and seamlessly add to a most recent claim for a fundamental change in thinking about COPD including definition, diagnostic and therapeutic approaches [4]. Historically, suspected early stages not covered by commonly used definitions were labelled as, e.g. GOLD 0 [31] or preserved ratio impaired spirometry [32, 33]. Recently, the term pre-COPD has been proposed [34, 35] comprising a heterogenous group of patients. Until today, no universally accepted nosology summarising the aforementioned conditions has existed, nor is there a consistent definition of SAD.

We demonstrate that abnormalities in small airway parameters are similar between subjects at risk and patients with established, albeit mild, COPD. This reasonably extends the data by Crisafulli et al. [11] demonstrating that SAD is present in half of Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) A and nearly 90% of GOLD B patients. Similarly, we found SAD more frequently with increasing typical symptoms, most notably dyspnoea, which in turn was linked to impaired quality of life, even in subjects at risk.

Compared with COPD, there is more lung functional data available phenotyping patients with asthma regarding the prevalence and clinical consequences of SAD. Previously, the ATLANTIS trial was able to demonstrate that SAD is present in >90% of asthmatic patients [36] and is associated with disease control and exacerbation [37]. Longitudinal data from ALLIANCE recently identified airway eosinophilic inflammation as the main driver of lung function impairment and poor outcome, possibly associated with dynamic SAD even in patients with stable FEV1 [38, 39]. In line with the data on the role of eosinophilic inflammation for SAD in asthma we could recently show that sputum eosinophils are closely correlated with acinar ventilation heterogeneity in our cohort of patients with mild COPD or COPD at risk [20]. Therefore, eosinophilic pathway may provide a promising target for anti-inflammatory treatments targeting eosinophils (e.g. by inhaled corticosteroids) in eosinophilic COPD with SAD. We intentionally allowed participation of patients with a history of asthma. There is increasing evidence merging functional aspects of either disease. Persistent or irreversible airflow limitation typical in COPD may also be present in asthma [40] speculating that these patients are risk for developing COPD. Conversely, reversibility can also be seen in COPD, even in early stages [41, 42].

Furthermore, we report that comprehensive lung function testing provides additional diagnostic value. Guideline-based spirometric assessment can reliably detect airflow obstruction in the large airways as FEV1/FVC ratio is a central part of current COPD definitions. We added a commonly used clinical approach based on body plethysmography and TLCO. This improved identification of early disease, and can be explained by the well-known diagnostic value of body plethysmography for the detection of hyperinflation or gas trapping (frequently already present in early or mild disease [43, 44]) and transfer factor for the assessment of parenchymal abnormalities. Although the absolute number of individuals with abnormal FRC remains small in the subjects at risk, this trend supports the notion of early impairment. Both functional phenotypes are also linked to small airway obstruction and ventilation heterogeneity. This bears hope for an additional diagnostic value of oscillometry and MBW, as they specifically target the small airways [45–47]. In contrast, MEF25 had been considered to resemble small airway function in spirometry. Available clinical data at least limit its value in clinical decision-making [48]. Additionally, we found a concordant change in central obstruction parameters such as FEV1 and MEF25, questioning an additional value in terms of early disease detection.

When interpreting our results, additional aspects and limitations should be taken into consideration. Males were more frequently present in the COPD group and similarly frequent in controls. In contrast, females were more prominent in subjects at risk. Control patients were investigated using impulse oscillometry (MasterScreen IOS), technically differing from the airwave oscillometry used in our cohort. However, we do not consider this a major problem, as larger deviation between the two techniques is primarily seen in high-resistance or high-reactance values [49, 50], whereas we investigated primarily mild disease or healthy subjects with lower ranges. Moreover, control patients differed from COPD patients and subjects at risk in age and bodyweight. Using a rather strict definition for controls made it particularly challenging to identify elderly subjects without lung function impairment. This did not allow matching between groups. However, reference equations correcting for influencing anthropometric variables were used for comparisons only. Meaningful differences in MBW outcome measures can be attributed to the test gases used [15, 51]. Two principles can be pursued. An exogenous tracer such as SF6 will require a wash-in phase, while endogenous nitrogen (N2) must be washed-out using oxygen. SF6 may not reach very poorly ventilated regions during wash-in. N2 may prolong wash-out from these regions, resulting in higher LCI values. In addition, there are differences in physical and chemical properties. N2 has a higher diffusion rate and smaller molar mass compared to SF6. This results in a more proximal diffusion–convection front [52]. SF6 may have a theoretical advantage in terms of early detection due to its more distal diffusion–convection front. However, no direct comparison is available at the moment. Despite the aforementioned potential advantages of SF6-MBW, it should also be noted that it is associated with considerable costs and is not allowed for medical use in several countries. Hence, attention must be paid to test gas choice when interpreting MBW results [53]. Finally, it should also be noted that some functional parameters, such as sRtot, are still lacking well-validated clinical thresholds. Therefore, we used the >130% predicted value as a pragmatic reference, in line with clinical convention. While sRtot is not specific for SAD, its normalisation to lung volume may enhance sensitivity to early peripheral changes.

Conclusion

SAD is frequently present even in patients with mild COPD and (ex-)smokers at risk. The latter do not yet fulfil spirometric criteria for COPD, but already show similar patterns of small airways dysfunction as compared to COPD patients. The clinical relevance of this lung functional abnormality is further underlined while being associated with an impairment in disease-specific HRQoL and typical symptoms of COPD. Using these more sensitive diagnostic tests may allow an earlier diagnosis, improve therapeutic options, and might finally open new doors for preventive medicine.
