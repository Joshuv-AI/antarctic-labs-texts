A Clinical Prediction Model for Selective NT‐proBNP Testing in Hypertension
Authors: Anping Cai, Lin Liu, Xiangbing Yu, Donglong Wu, Xiang Lin, Songshan Luo, Ke Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTN‐terminal pro‐B‐type natriuretic peptide (NT‐proBNP) testing is recommended for pre‐heart failure (pre‐HF) screening. However, universal NT‐proBNP testing is infeasible and inefficient. We analyzed data from 4642 U.S. adults with hypertension in NHANES 1999–2004. Heart stress, a biomarker‐defined pre‐HF state, was defined using age‐specific NT‐proBNP cutoffs. Multivariable logistic regression was used to identify factors associated with prevalent heart stress, and a point‐based score was constructed. Model discrimination and calibration were internally validated using 1000 bootstrapped samples. Heart stress was present in 31% of individuals with hypertension. Factors associated with heart stress included older age, current smoking, prior coronary heart disease/myocardial infarction, higher systolic blood pressure, lower diastolic blood pressure, and reduced estimated glomerular filtration rate. The derivation model showed acceptable discrimination (AUC 0.71; 95% CI 0.70–0.73) and good calibration. An optimal cutoff of 6 points was identified using the Youden index and was internally validated (AUC 0.71; 95% CI 0.69–0.74). Model performance was consistent across race/ethnicity and body mass index subgroups, with relatively lower discrimination in females than in males (AUC 0.66 vs. 0.78). When stratified by a predicted heart stress probability threshold of 50%, individuals in the higher‐probability group had higher cumulative risk of all‐cause (hazard ratio 3.90; 95% CI 3.55–4.29) and cardiovascular mortality (hazard ratio 5.18; 95% CI 4.44–6.04) than those in the lower‐probability group. These findings suggest that a simple model using readily obtainable clinical variables can help identify individuals with hypertension who are likely to have prevalent heart stress, offering a practical strategy to guide selective NT‐proBNP testing.Keywords: biomarker, screening, hypertension, discrimination, mortality

Introduction

Early identification of pre‐heart failure (pre‐HF) is crucial to implement targeted interventions that can prevent progression to symptomatic HF [1, 2], a condition that continues to impose substantial health and economic burden worldwide despite major therapeutic advances [3, 4]. The Universal Definition and Classification of HF and the HF Guidelines recommend the use of cardiac biomarkers (e.g., N‐terminal pro‐B‐type natriuretic peptide; NT‐proBNP) to screen for and diagnose pre‐HF [5, 6, 7]. Elevated NT‐proBNP indicates subclinical myocardial stress and is associated with increased risk of future HF events [5, 6, 7]. However, the uptake of NT‐proBNP for pre‐HF screening has not been widely adopted by non‐HF specialists, understood by patients, or incorporated in coding, billing, or health coverage plans [8].

A fixed NT‐proBNP cutoff of ≥ 125 pg/mL is commonly used to define a pre‐HF state in asymptomatic individuals [5, 6, 7]. However, since NT‐proBNP concentrations increase with age [9, 10], applying a fixed cutoff may reduce sensitivity in younger individuals and diminish specificity in older adults [6]. To address this limitation, the European Society of Cardiology Heart Failure Association proposed age‐specific NT‐proBNP cutoffs to define a distinct pre‐HF state namely heart stress [11]. Our recent work demonstrated that such age‐specific approach outperforms conventional fixed NT‐proBNP cutoff in screening pre‐HF and predicting 10‐year risk of incident HF hospitalization among asymptomatic individuals free of cardiovascular disease (CVD) [12]. Importantly, prior studies showed that pre‐HF either defined by age‐specific or fixed NT‐proBNP cutoffs affects around 20%–40% of community populations [9, 12, 13, 14], suggesting that universal NT‐proBNP testing would be inefficient and that more targeted testing strategies are needed. Clinical variables that are routinely collected and associated with NT‐proBNP elevation may offer a practical tool to estimate an individual's probability of heart stress and guide selective NT‐proBNP testing.

Individuals with hypertension represent a key population for targeted pre‐HF screening as it is the leading cause of HF [15, 16] and contributes the greatest population‐attributable risk of HF across all age groups [17]. In addition, most people with hypertension are asymptomatic for many years [18], providing a critical opportunity for early detection of subclinical myocardial stress before symptoms occur. Accordingly, we herein used data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) to develop a heart stress prediction model using readily obtainable clinical variables among people with hypertension, evaluate its discrimination and calibration, and examine whether higher predicted heart stress probability was associated with long‐term mortality.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

NHANES is a cross‐sectional survey of the non‐institutionalized U.S. population based on a complex, multistage probability sampling design. We used data from three consecutive cycles (1999–2000, 2001–2002, and 2003–2004), during which NT‐proBNP was measured. Although NHANES can provide nationally representative estimates when sampling weights and survey design variables are applied, these were not incorporated in the present analysis; therefore, all analyses were conducted using the unweighted analytic sample. Among 15 332 participants aged ≥ 20 years in the 1999–2004 cycles, we identified 5761 people with hypertension, defined as a mean systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥ 140 mm Hg, mean diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥ 90 mm Hg, or self‐reported antihypertensive medication use [13]. After excluding participants without NT‐proBNP measurements (n = 715), those with undetectable NT‐proBNP (n = 83), or those with a history of HF (n = 321), a total of 4642 participants with hypertension were included (Figure S1).

Briefly, history of HF was defined based on self‐reported physician diagnosis in NHANES, using the question asking whether a doctor or other health professional had ever told the participant that they had congestive HF. Participants who answered “yes” were considered to have a history of HF. Because systematic clinical adjudication, echocardiography, and detailed HF phenotype information were not available in NHANES 1999–2004, guideline‐defined HF based on symptoms/signs and objective evidence of structural or functional cardiac abnormality could not be retrospectively applied.

Data Collection and Study Variables

Information on participants’ sociodemographic information (age, sex, and race/ethnicity) and health behavior was self‐reported and collected using a computer‐assisted personal interview system. Race/ethnicity was categorized as Non‐Hispanic White, Non‐Hispanic Black, Mexican American, or Other [13]. Smoking status was classified as never, former, or current. Prior history of coronary heart disease (CHD) or myocardial infarction (MI) was based on self‐report. Diabetes mellitus was defined as treatment with antihyperglycemic medications, fasting blood glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL or glycated hemoglobin A1c ≥ 6.5%. Dyslipidemia was defined as treatment with lipid‐lowering medications or total cholesterol ≥ 240 mg/dL. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared and categorized as < 25 kg/m2 (normal weight), 25–29.9 kg/m2 (overweight) or ≥ 30 kg/m2 (obese). Three consecutive auscultatory BP measurements were obtained using a mercury sphygmomanometer with an appropriately sized cuff after participants had been seated at rest for 5 min. The mean SBP and DBP were calculated from all available readings. Estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using the Modification of Diet in Renal Disease equation. Current use of medications for hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia was obtained through self‐report. For the development of heart stress prediction model, key continuous variables were categorized as below to facilitate clinical interpretability and to minimize influence of non‐linear relationship. Age was grouped into 20–39, 40–64, 65–79, and ≥ 80 years; SBP into < 120, 120–129, 130–139, 140–149, and ≥ 150 mm Hg; DBP into < 60, 60–69, 70–79, 80–89, and ≥ 90 mm Hg; and eGFR into ≥ 90, 60–89, and < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2.

NT‐proBNP Measurements and Heart Stress Definition

Stored serum samples from the 1999–2004 cycles were analyzed between 2018 and 2020 at the University of Maryland School of Medicine using a Cobas e601 analyzer (Elecsys, Roche Diagnostics) [19]. According to the consensus statement from the European Society of Cardiology Heart Failure Association [11], heart stress was defined as NT‐proBNP ≥ 75 pg/mL for adults < 50 years, ≥ 150 pg/mL for those aged 50–74 years, and ≥ 300 pg/mL for those aged ≥ 75 years.

Development and Assessment of Heart Stress Prediction Model

To develop a heart stress prediction model, we first conducted univariable logistic regression analyses to examine the association between each baseline variable and prevalent heart stress. Candidate factors included age category, smoking status, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, history of CHD/MI, and categories of BMI, SBP, DBP, and eGFR. These predictors were selected based on their biological plausibility or established associations with NT‐proBNP concentrations, as well as consistency with prior studies [9, 12, 13, 14]. Variables with a p‐value < 0.10 in univariable analyses were considered for inclusion in the multivariable model. Those that remained statistically significant and showed positive associations with prevalent heart stress in the final multivariable model were retained as independent factors. As a sensitivity analysis, we also assessed whether these factors were independently associated with NT‐proBNP ≥ 125 pg/mL. Each factor was assigned a weighted point value proportional to its β‐coefficient, scaled and rounded to the nearest integer to generate a simplified scoring system. The total heart stress risk score for each participant was calculated by summing the points of all factors present. To support clinical interpretability, classification performance was additionally summarized using a score‐based decision rule. An “optimal” score cutoff was determined using the Youden index (maximizing sensitivity + specificity − 1) from the area under the receiver operating curve. Internal validation was performed using bootstrap resampling to account for overfitting and to quantify the stability of the selected cutoff and corresponding classification performance. A total of 1000 bootstrap samples were generated by sampling with replacement from the original dataset. Model calibration was assessed using bootstrap calibration with 1000 resamples, comparing predicted probabilities with observed outcome frequencies. A bootstrap‐corrected calibration curve was plotted against predicted probability to visually assess agreement between predicted risk and observed event rates. As a post‐hoc analysis, we additionally developed sex‐specific models to evaluate their performance in males and females, respectively.

All‐Cause and Cardiovascular (CV) Mortality Ascertainment

Mortality status and cause of death were determined using the NHANES‐linked National Death Index public access files through December 31, 2019. Cause‐specific mortality was classified based on the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD‐10) codes, according to the underlying primary cause of death. For this analysis, CV mortality was defined as deaths in which the underlying cause was either heart disease or cerebrovascular disease (I00‐I99).

Statistical Analyses

Continuous variables with normal distribution were reported as mean ± standard deviation, and those with skewed distribution as median (interquartile range; IQR). Categorical variables were expressed as n (%). Differences in continuous variables between heart stress and no‐heart stress participants were assessed using Student's t test or the Kruskal‐Wallis test as appropriate, and differences in categorical variables were assessed using the χ2 test. Performance of the heart stress prediction model was also assessed across subgroups defined by sex, race/ethnicity, and BMI status. Distribution of predicted heart stress probabilities was displayed using a histogram to examine how the model stratified participants by estimated heart stress risk. Participants were stratified into two groups based on a predicted probability threshold of 50%, chosen as a pragmatic midpoint to distinguish between lower‐ and higher‐risk individuals and to facilitate comparisons of mortality outcomes. Cox regression analysis was conducted to evaluate the risk of all‐cause and CV mortality associated with a higher probability of heart stress. All analyses were conducted using R software, version 4.5.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). A two‐tailed p‐value <0.05 was considered statistically significant

Results

Baseline Characteristics

Among the cohort, the prevalence of heart stress in people with hypertension was 31%. Those with heart stress (versus no‐heart stress) were older and more likely to be female and non‐Hispanic White, and less likely to be never smokers (Table 1). They also had a higher prevalence of diabetes mellitus and prior CHD/MI, but a lower BMI and a lower prevalence of obesity. Furthermore, they had higher mean SBP but lower mean DBP, as well as lower mean eGFR and a greater proportion of individuals with eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2. Use of lipid‐lowering, antidiabetic, and antihypertensive therapies was more common in those with heart stress.

Performance of the Heart Stress Prediction Model

Based on the Youden index, a cutoff of 6 points was identified as the optimal cutoff. At this cutoff, the area under the curve (AUC; 95% confidence interval; CI) for predicting prevalent heart stress was 0.71 (0.70–0.73; Figure 2), with a sensitivity of 0.57 and a specificity of 0.75. With internal validation using 1000 bootstrapping, the AUC (95% CI) was 0.71 (0.69–0.74), with a sensitivity of 0.58 and a specificity of 0.72. The sensitivity and specificity for alternative cutoff values are presented in Table S2. The calibration plot indicated well calibration of the heart stress prediction model. In both the derivation and internal validation analyses (Table 3), the model using a 6‐point cutoff performed well in males, and across race/ethnicity and BMI subgroups; however, the AUC was slightly lower in females.

Distribution of Predicted Heart Stress Probability and Mortality Outcomes

Distribution of predicted probabilities of prevalent heart stress showed significant variability in people with hypertension (Figure 3). Most participants had predicted probabilities below 0.25 (∼ 53%), but a proportion exhibited high probabilities extending beyond 0.50 (∼ 16%; Table S3). After a median (IQR) follow‐up of 15.9 (9.8,18.0) years, compared with individuals in the low heart stress probability group (< 50%), those with a high heart stress probability (≥ 50%) had significantly higher cumulative rates of all‐cause and CV mortality. Individuals in the higher‐probability group had higher cumulative risk of all‐cause (hazard ratio 3.90; 95% CI 3.55–4.29) and cardiovascular mortality (hazard ratio 5.18; 95% CI 4.44–6.04).

Discussion

In this nationally representative population‐based study, heart stress was present in nearly one‐third of people with hypertension; and its distribution varied moderately, supporting the premise that universal NT‐proBNP testing would be inefficient. Using readily obtainable clinical variables, we developed a simple clinical model that showed acceptable discrimination and good calibration for prediction of prevalent heart stress. Notably, individuals classified as having a high heart stress probability exhibited substantially higher long‐term cumulative rates of all‐cause and CV mortality, supporting the clinical relevance of selective NT‐proBNP testing.

Factors incorporated into the heart stress prediction model reflet well‐established pathophysiologic mechanisms linking traditional cardiovascular risk factors to subclinical myocardial stress and neurohormonal activation. Specifically, advancing age was one of the important factors associated with prevalent heart stress, which is consistent with age‐related increases in NT‐proBNP driven by vascular aging, progressive myocardial stiffening, and a growing burden of comorbidities [20]. Current smoking was also associated with prevalent heart stress, likely reflecting its effects on myocardial wall stress through direct cardiovascular injury [21]. Elevated SBP, low DBP, and reduced kidney function were also strong factors, supporting the role of increased afterload, arterial stiffness, and reduced volume clearance in promoting myocardial wall stress. The strong association with prior CHD/MI suggesting accumulated myocardial injury and reduced myocardial perfusion could increase myocardial wall stress [22].

Overweight and obesity was not included in the model due to its inverse relationship with NT‐proBNP, and its inclusion might counterintuitively attenuate the predicted probability of heart stress, thereby undermining the model's prediction utility. Several studies have demonstrated that elevated NT‐proBNP is significantly associated with increased risk of HF [23, 24, 25], and the American Diabetes Association also recommends annual NT‐proBNP testing in patients with diabetes [26]. However, in the present study, diabetes was not significantly associated with prevalent heart stress after multivariable adjustment, which may be due to overlapping pathophysiologic pathways already captured by other covariates such as age, SBP, renal function, and history of CHD/MI. It is also possible that the heterogeneity of diabetes duration, glycemic control, and presence of diabetic complications, which were not fully accounted for, had attenuated the association. Notably, the factors retained in the model are established cardiovascular risk factors and are consistent with prior evidence linking greater cardiometabolic burden to elevated NT‐proBNP. Thus, the value of this model does not lie in identifying novel determinants of heart stress, but in integrating routinely available clinical variables into a simple and interpretable framework for identifying individuals with hypertension who may be more likely to have occult heart stress.

Prior studies have relied on a single fixed cutoff (≥ 125 pg/mL) to define elevated NT‐proBNP [13, 27, 28, 29]. However, our recent work demonstrated that age‐specific cutoffs outperform a fixed cutoff for screening pre‐HF cases [12]. Another study also showed that age‐specific NT‐proBNP cutoffs, although differing from ours, effectively identify individuals at increased risk of developing HF [30]. Furthermore, our recent post‐hoc analyses of clinical trials showed that heart stress can serve as a useful indicator for identifying individuals with diabetes who are more likely to obtain cardiorenal benefits from canagliflozin therapy [31]; and among older adults free of CVD, heart stress has shown potential value in guiding personalized BP management [14]. These findings together indicate that the concept of heart stress provides a more physiologically grounded and clinically relevant framework for defining a pre‐HF state. Specifically, in individuals younger than 50 years of age, applying a lower age‐adjusted cutoff could aid in identifying more young adults at risk of HF, which could be clinically relevant given the rising incidence of HF among young adults globally [32]. Conversely, since NT‐proBNP increases with age, using higher cutoffs in individuals older than 75 years can better distinguish those who are truly at high risk and enable more targeted allocation of preventive interventions.

Routine use of NT‐proBNP testing for pre‐HF screening remains limited, likely due to concerns about health expenditure and the practicality of universal testing [8, 33]. By developing a model anchored in age‐specific diagnostic criteria and relying on easily obtainable clinical variables, our study offers a pragmatic approach to guide selective NT‐proBNP testing in large at‐risk populations. The discrimination observed in our model is comparable to other widely used clinical prediction tools in cardiovascular medicine, including scores for predicting incident HF [34] and atrial fibrillation (AF) [35], supporting its clinical utility as a first‐line triage tool. Nevertheless, we have to acknowledge that the sensitivity was modest, meaning that a substantial proportion of individuals with heart stress would not be identified if the model were used as a stand‐alone gatekeeper for NT‐proBNP testing. Therefore, this model should not be interpreted as a replacement for biomarker screening, but rather as a pragmatic triage tool to improve testing efficiency when universal NT‐proBNP screening is not feasible. In practice, health systems may choose a lower score threshold to prioritize sensitivity over specificity, depending on resource availability and the intended screening objective. An additional important consideration is the profile and prognosis of individuals missed by a model‐guided screening strategy. Characterizing these false‐negative cases would clarify whether they represent a lower‐risk subgroup or whether clinically important heart stress and adverse outcomes remain undetected. Future studies should evaluate their characteristics and outcomes to better define the trade‐off between screening efficiency and missed risk, and to inform threshold selection in different clinical settings.

Because sex, race/ethnicity, and BMI status were not incorporated into the model, we performed subgroup analyses to assess model performance across these populations. The model demonstrated acceptable discrimination in all subgroups except for females, indicating that the predictive value of the model is largely consistent and generalizable across diverse population groups. The lower discriminatory performance in females than in males suggests that the current model may capture biomarker‐defined heart stress less well in females. This may reflect sex differences in the determinants and pathophysiologic correlates of elevated NT‐proBNP [10, 36], as well as the absence of relevant female‐specific factors in the model. These findings suggest the need for further refinement and evaluation of sex‐specific screening strategies.

Notably, the factors included in the model were largely consistent with those associated with NT‐proBNP ≥ 125 pg/mL, reinforcing that the factors captured by the model represent key clinical determinants of subclinical myocardial stress irrespective of the diagnostic criteria. The clinical relevance of identifying individuals with heart stress or a pre‐HF state is further reinforced by findings from prior clinical trials, which demonstrated that renin‐angiotensin‐aldosterone system inhibitors [37, 38] and intensive BP lowering [39, 40] significantly reduce the risk of incident HF in at‐risk populations. However, biomarker‐based definition of heart stress should be viewed as a pragmatic, risk‐enriched construct rather than an imaging‐confirmed HF stage. Because systematic cardiac imaging was unavailable, it may capture a spectrum from subclinical myocardial stress and pre‐HF to some individuals with unrecognized overt HF. Accordingly, we could not reliably distinguish pre‐HF from undiagnosed symptomatic disease. Future studies incorporating cardiac imaging are needed to refine phenotypic classification and determine whether predictors differ by underlying structural or functional abnormalities.

Clinical Implications

The predictive model offers a practical strategy to improve NT‐proBNP testing efficiency by identifying individuals with hypertension who are likely to have prevalent heart stress. Integrating this model into routine clinical workflows may help reduce unnecessary biomarker testing, lower healthcare costs, and enable more precise allocation of diagnostic resources. For primary care clinicians and non‐HF specialists, the model may provide a simple decision aid to identify individuals who warrant NT‐proBNP testing or further cardiovascular evaluation. The strong association between high predicted heart stress probability and long‐term mortality further underscores the importance of early heart stress recognition. As international guidelines increasingly emphasize the importance of early detecting pre‐HF [5, 6, 7], this risk‐based approach may serve as an important bridge between guideline recommendations and real‐world implementation.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study warrant consideration. First, the cross‐sectional design limits the ability to infer causal relationships between clinical factors and heart stress. Second, although the model performed well across most subgroups, discrimination was modestly lower in females, suggesting potential sex‐related differences in biomarker physiology that merit further study. Third, the applicability of this model to populations outside of the U.S. remains uncertain and requires validation in diverse settings. Fourth, echocardiographic parameters were not available and could not be incorporated into the model. Nevertheless, the model was intended as a first‐step triage tool to identify individuals with hypertension who may warrant NT‐proBNP testing; and those with positive NT‐proBNP findings may require subsequent echocardiography. Future studies incorporating echocardiography could help refine the phenotypic classification of biomarker‐defined heart stress and to determine the incremental value of imaging after biomarker‐based screening. Fifth, although validated internally, external validation in contemporary cohorts, along with evaluation of clinical utility such as net reclassification improvement and real‐world impact on testing efficiency will be essential to establish its readiness for broader clinical implementation. Finally, AF status was not incorporated into the present analysis. As it can elevate NT‐proBNP, some participants classified as having heart stress may have had NT‐proBNP elevation partly related to AF. Future studies with systematic AF ascertainment and electrocardiographic data are needed to validate the model.

Conclusion

In individuals with hypertension, a simple model based on routinely available clinical characteristics can help identify those at higher likelihood of biomarker‐defined heart stress. This pragmatic approach may support targeted NT‐proBNP testing and risk stratification in clinical practice, particularly where universal screening is not feasible.
