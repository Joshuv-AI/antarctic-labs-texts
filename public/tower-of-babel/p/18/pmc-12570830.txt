The association of thyroid peroxidase antibodies with microvascular invasion in patients with papillary thyroid carcinoma
Authors: Jinlong Huo, Youming Guo, Yan Wu, Wei Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposePapillary Thyroid Carcinoma (PTC) is the most prevalent thyroid malignancy. Current evidence on the association between thyroid peroxidase antibody (TPOAB) levels and microvascular invasion (MVI) in PTC is limited. The aim of this study was to investigate the association between TPOAB and MVI in patients with PTC.MethodsThis retrospective study included patients with PTC who underwent surgery at the First People’s Hospital of Zunyi between May 22, 2022, and March 06, 2024. Preoperative TPOAB levels were recorded, along with several potential confounding factors, including sex, age, BMI, smoking, drinking, tumor size, multiple foci, thyroglobulin antibody (TGAB), thyroglobulin (Tg) and preoperative thyroid function. TPOAB levels were log-transformed and denoted as lnTPOAB for analysis. MVI was determined based on the postoperative pathology reports. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was conducted to investigate the association between lnTPOAB and MVI risk, adjusted for confounding factors. A restricted cubic spline model was employed to examine the nonlinear relationship between ln TPOAB and MVI. The inflection point was calculated using a two-piece linear-regression model. Subgroup analyses were performed to verify the association between TPOAB and MVI.ResultsA total of 409 participants were included in the study. In the multivariable logistic regression model, after adjusting for all potentially confounding variables, the odds ratio (95% confidence interval) of lnTPOAB associated with MVI was 1.18 (1.03, 1.34; P = 0.013). The restricted cubic spline demonstrated a non-linear association between lnTPOAB and MVI. The two-piecewise regression model indicated that the inflection point was − 1.1, corresponding to a raw estimate of TPOAB of 0.33 IU/ml. Subgroup analyses further validated the positive association between TPOAB levels and MVI in patients with PTC.ConclusionsElevated TPOAb levels were independently associated with higher odds of MVI. Therefore, TPOAb may act as a supportive preoperative risk marker, helping to guide the extent of surgery and, together with tumor size and biochemical markers, customize early postoperative follow-up strategies. However, its clinical value still requires confirmation in prospective studies.Trial registrationChiCTR2400084886, date of registration: May 27, 2024, retrospectively registered.Keywords: Thyroid peroxidase antibodies, Papillary thyroid carcinoma, Microvascular invasion, Restricted cubic spline

Introduction

Papillary Thyroid Carcinoma (PTC), the most prevalent form of thyroid malignancy, remains a central focus in epidemiology owing to its increasing incidence and inert biological behavior [1–3]. Microvascular invasion (MVI), which is the presence of tumor cells in blood vessels, is a significant factor in the progression and prognosis of cancer [4–6]. Clinically, histological MVI in PTC is a recognized marker of aggressive disease. Patients with vascular invasion have higher odds of distant metastasis at diagnosis and an elevated risk of distant recurrence on follow-up, independent of age and nodal status [7]. Multiple retrospective series and reviews have further associated vascular invasion with a greater metastatic burden and worse prognosis, underscoring its importance for risk stratification and management decisions [8]. Currently, the potential predictive factors of MVI in patients of PTC remain unclear.

Hashimoto's thyroiditis(HT), also known as Hashimoto's disease, is an autoimmune condition affecting the thyroid gland [9]. The presence of thyroid peroxidase antibodies(TPOAB) serves as a key diagnostic marker for HT because elevated levels in the bloodstream indicate ongoing autoimmune activity against the thyroid gland [10]. Despite extensive research on the role of HT in PTC progression, results remain inconsistent [9]. Some literature have suggested a potential association between HT and increased risk of thyroid cancer, establishing a new area of interest within the field [11–13]. However, an existing body of research has found that HT may be protective against PTC progression [14–18]. Logically, understanding the relationship between TPOAB levels and MVI in PTC can indirectly clarify the relationship between HT and PTC progression. However, there are few published data on the relationship between TPOAB levels and MVI in PTC.

Therefore, the objective of this study was to determine the relationship between TPOAB levels and MVI in patients with PTC. By clarifying this potential correlation, our research aimed to illuminate the prognostic implications of TPOAB in PTC and provide novel perspectives that may refine diagnostic protocols, therapeutic strategies, and prognostic evaluations of PTC.

Materials and methods

Patients

This retrospective study included patients with PTC who underwent surgery at the First People’s Hospital of Zunyi between May 22, 2022, and March 06, 2024. Informed consent was obtained from all patients, and the Ethics Committee of the First People’s Hospital of Zunyi approved this study. This study was registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2400084886) and reported according to the STROCSS criteria [19].

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) PTC confirmed by core-needle biopsy or fine-needle aspiration; and (3) no history of neck radiotherapy or family history. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) other types of thyroid cancer diagnosed by biopsy; (2) pathological examination suggesting invasive subtypes of PTC, including the high cell subtype, columnar cell subtype, and hobnail subtype; (3) tumor invasion of the esophagus, trachea, or recurrent laryngeal nerve; and (4) thyroid dysfunction treated with drugs or radioactive iodine. The electronic medical records of 542 patients with PTC who underwent thyroidectomy between May 22, 2022, and March 06, 2024, were reviewed. Patients with other types of thyroid cancer (n = 15), invasive subtypes (N = 12), tumor invasion of the esophagus, trachea, or recurrent laryngeal nerve (N = 23), thyroid dysfunction treated with drugs or radioactive iodine(n = 8), and incomplete data (N = 75) were excluded. A total of 409 patients were included in the study. Among the 409 included patients, sporadic missingness in covariates was addressed using multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE), including lnTPOAB (exposure), MVI (outcome), and all the model covariates and auxiliary predictors. Preoperative evaluation and treatment

All patients were admitted for preoperative evaluation, including complete blood count, thyroid function tests, TPOAB, thyroglobulin(Tg), thyroglobulin antibody(TGAB), coagulation tests, laryngoscopy, and imaging evaluations. Thyroid function primarily involves the measurement of free thyroid hormones (FT3 and FT4) and thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) levels in the blood. Ultrasonography was performed to assess the tumor and the cervical lymph nodes. CT scans of the neck and chest were performed to identify cervical lymph nodes and distant metastases. Electronic laryngoscopy was used to preoperatively diagnose the vocal cord paralysis.

All surgical procedures were performed by the same surgeon (Youming Guo). The primary intervention method was either endoscopic or an open surgery. The detailed surgical procedures were described in our previous study [20, 21]. In our study, surgical protocols were performed according to the Chinese guidelines for thyroid cancer, including lobectomy or total thyroidectomy, with central lymph node dissection, and therapeutic neck dissection was performed in patients with standard indications [3].

Exposure and outcomes

The primary exposure was the continuous TPOAb level. TPOAB levels were measured using a Beckman Diagnostics E-Anti-TPO kit, with a normal range of 0–9 IU/mL. The biomarker data of TPOAB values often exhibit a non-normal, right-skewed distribution, leading to skewness in the data. To normalize the data and meet the prerequisites of parametric statistical tests, log transformation is beneficial [22, 23]. Therefore, we took the natural logarithm of TPOAB(lnTPOAB) and then conducted the analysis. For robustness, we performed a sensitivity analysis by dichotomizing TPOAB at 50 IU/mL, a commonly used antibody-positivity threshold in prior clinical/epidemiological studies [24]. HT was confirmed by two independent pathologists on postoperative histopathology. HT required diffuse lymphoplasmacytic infiltration with lymphoid follicles and reactive germinal centers, follicular atrophy with Hürthle-cell change, and variable fibrosis in non-tumorous parenchyma; focal peritumoral inflammation alone was not considered HT. Disagreements were resolved by consensus agreement.

Routine histopathology was performed on all surgical samples, with a specific focus on detecting MVI. The assessment was performed by two independent pathologists. According to previous studies, MVI is defined as the presence of a tumor thrombus within a blood vessel, characterized by at least one of the following: direct attachment of the tumor to the vessel wall, coverage of the intravascular tumor by endothelial cells, or coexistence with a fibrin clot. Tumor emboli within intracapsular or extracapsular vessels, or thrombi mixed with fibrin, were also classified as MVI. In contrast, simple protrusion of tumor tissue into slit-like spaces without endothelial lining or fibrin was not regarded as MVI [25]. All assessments were performed on H&E-stained sections by two independent pathologists with consensus resolution of discrepancies, following ICCR/WHO-aligned criteria and pathology guidance referenced by ATA guidelines [2]. Briefly, identifying MVI involves the detection of cancer cells within the thyroid veins or large vessels in the capsule surrounding the thyroid tissue [6]. These vessels are lined with endothelial cells and can only be identified through microscopic examination.

Sample size considerations and Restricted cubic spline (RCS)

Prior to analysis, we targeted model parsimony to achieve acceptable events-per-variable (EPV) for logistic regression analysis. We limited the number of parameters in the primary adjusted model to maintain EPV near ≥ 10, a range associated with acceptable bias and interval coverage in simulations, with contemporary evidence indicating that the traditional 10-EPV rule can be reasonably relaxed under careful model specification and sensitivity analysis [26, 27]. We also conducted a post hoc sensitivity analysis to estimate the power for the observed lnTPOAB effect size at α = 0.05, acknowledging the limitations of post hoc power [28].

RCS terms were specified with four knots at fixed quantiles to provide flexibility while minimizing overfitting; the overall spline effect was tested using a joint score test; methodological details and parsimony rationale have been reported in previous studies [29].

Covariates

The covariates included sex, age, BMI, smoking, drinking, tumor size, multiple foci, TGAB, Tg, and preoperative thyroid function. Data on these variables were collected from the hospital’s electronic medical records. BMI was calculated based on weight and height measured during physical examinations. TGAB levels were right-skewed; therefore, we used the natural logarithm of TGAB(lnTGAB) for analysis. Complete conditional multiple interpolations were performed for missing values.

In subgroup analyses, TPOAB positivity for subgrouping was defined as ≥ 50 IU/mL, a high-range threshold commonly used across assays and close to upper percentile cut-offs associated with increased thyroid dysfunction risk reported in population studies [24]. The cut-off value of plasma Tg was set at 50 ng/mL based on previous reports [30].

Statistical analysis

The analysis included measures such as the mean, median, standard deviation, range, and quartiles for continuous variables and frequency tables for categorical variables. Data distribution was assessed using normality tests, and appropriate descriptive statistical methods were applied to both normally and non-normally distributed variables. For comparison between groups of categorical data, we used the Fisher exact test for expected frequencies < 5; otherwise, we used the chi-squared test. Logistic regression was used to calculate the odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association between lnTPOAB and MVI, adjusting for potential confounding factors. Model 1: no covariates were adjusted; Model 2: adjusted for age and sex. Model 3, added to Model 2, included BMI, smoking, drinking, tumor size, multiple foci, lnTGAB, Tg, and preoperative thyroid function as covariates.

Subsequently, the association between the natural logarithm of TPOAB and MVI was modeled using RCS with four knots at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of the log scale. A two-piece linear approximation was used to summarize the point of maximum slope change (‘inflection’) on the log scale, and this value was back-transformed to raw units for improved readability. Because modeling and inference were performed on the logarithmic scale, back-transformed values near zero can appear small in absolute units and should not be interpreted as biological thresholds.

Subgroup analysis were performed to investigate whether other potential factors modified the association in the fully adjusted model. All statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.2.2, and statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics of study population

The baseline characteristics of the study population stratified by MVI are shown in Table 1. There were 103 MVI events, yielding an EPV consistent with the accepted practice for the primary model. No statistically significant differences were observed in the distribution of sex, age, BMI, smoking, or drinking status between the groups with and without MVI. However, a significant difference was found in tumor size, with a mean of 0.66 ± 0.46 cm in the group without MVI compared to 1.31 ± 0.84 cm in the group with MVI (P < 0.001). Additionally, a significant difference was observed in the presence of multiple foci, with 77.5% of the group without MVI having no multiple foci compared to 71.8% in the group with MVI (P = 0.249). Furthermore, differences in lnTPOAB (P = 0.002) were observed, indicating a higher mean value in the MVI group. No significant differences were observed in FT3, FT4, TSH, lnTGAB, or Tg levels between the two groups.

Association between LnTPOAB and MVI

As shown in Table 2, lnTPOAB levels were significantly associated with MVI (Table 2). lnTPOAB was positively associated with the odds ratio of MVI across all three models (Model 1: OR = 1.17, 95% CI:(1.06,1.28), P = 0.001; Model 2: OR = 1.17, 95% CI:(1.07,1.29), P = 0.001; and Model 3: OR = 1.18, 95%CI: (1.03,1.34), P = 0.013). Based on this finding, we performed RSC models based on Model 3. The results suggested a non-linear association between lnTPOAB and MVI(Fig. 2). We observed an increased risk of MVI with increasing lnTPOAB level. The inflection point of the RCS curve was identified at lnTPOAB = − 1.1, for a corresponding raw estimate of TPOAB of 0.33 IU/ml, representing an inflection point in the relationship between lnTPOAB and MVI (Fig. 2). Using the inflection point, the data were stratified into two groups: lnTPOAB < − 1.1 and lnTPOAB ≥ − 1.1. Segmented regression was then performed for each group separately, and the results are presented in Table 3.

Subgroup analysis

As shown in Fig. 3, univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were used in the subgroup analysis to identify the relationship between TPOAB and MVI. In addition to TPOAB levels, tumor size and Tg levels were significantly associated with MVI. Patients with TPOAB levels ≥ 50 IU/mL had a higher risk of MVI, with an OR of 2.02 (95% CI: 1.06, 3.87; P = 0.033), suggesting that higher TPOAB levels could increase the risk of MVI, as observed above. Patients with a tumor size ≥ 2.3 cm had a significantly increased risk of MVI (OR 13.50;95% CI: 3.89, 46.85; P < 0.001). Higher Tg levels (≥ 50 ng/ml) were also correlated with a higher risk of MVI, with an OR of 2.50 (95% CI: 1.11, 5.63; P = 0.027). However, no statistically significant differences were observed in the distribution of sex, age, BMI, smoking, and drinking between the groups with and without MVI. This suggests that these factors may not significantly influence MVI occurrence in patients with PTC. It is noteworthy that subgroup findings are interpreted as exploratory due to their limited power.

Discussion

This study identified a positive correlation between lnTPOAB levels and the odds of MVI in patients diagnosed with PTC. It is plausible that a threshold level exists for lnTPOAB, beyond which its impact on MVI risk escalates. To further explore this relationship, subgroup analysis was performed, adjusting for several potential confounders. This analysis suggests that TPOAB levels may act as prognostic indicators for MVI in patients with PTC, offering significant insights for clinical decision-making and patient management.

Prior to the statistical analysis, the decision to apply natural logarithmic transformations to the TPOAB and TGAB data was advantageous for several reasons: Firstly, it facilitates the normalization of skewed data distributions, aligning more closely with the assumptions of several statistical tests [31]. Secondly, the transformation helped stabilize variance across the dataset, reducing the influence of outliers and ensuring more consistent variance, which is crucial for robust statistical inference [22]. Thirdly, it enhanced our ability to identify the underlying relationships and trends in the data, thereby improving the sensitivity and specificity of our statistical analyses [31]. Lastly, interpreting the results on a logarithmic scale provides a more nuanced understanding of the observed effects, reflecting multiplicative rather than additive relationships [32] In conclusion, the utilization of natural logarithmic transformations was instrumental in enhancing the quality, reliability, and interpretability of the statistical analyses.

Notely, the back-transformed ‘‘inflection’ around 0.33 IU/mL reflects a turning point of the fitted curve on the natural log scale rather than a biologically prescriptive threshold. At such low absolute concentrations, values lie near the assay’s lower reportable range, where small analytic fluctuations may map to large differences on the log scale. Accordingly, this statistical turning point should not be overinterpreted; the clinically relevant message is the overall, dose-dependent trend in which higher TPOAB levels are associated with higher odds of MVI.

Numerous studies have identified tumor size as a significant risk factor for MVI in PTC. For example, multivariate analysis of prognostic factors for intrathyroid papillary thyroid carcinoma found that tumor size was a significant predictor of MVI [33]. For example, Cheng et al. (2020) reported in their study of 795 PTC patients that patients with a maximum tumor diameter of 1 cm or more had a significantly higher risk of MVI compared to those with a maximum tumor diameter less than 1 cm [34]. This correlation may be due to larger tumors experiencing increased hypoxia, which in turn stimulates angiogenesis through the elevated expression of vascular endothelial growth factor [35]. In our study of 409 patients with PTC, we observed a significant positive correlation between tumor size and the presence of MVI. Patients were categorized based on a median tumor size of 2.3 cm, rather than the traditional 1 cm threshold. Specifically, patients with a tumor diameter of ≥ 2.3 cm exhibited a substantially increased risk of MVI, with an odds ratio of 13.50 (95% CI: 3.89, 46.85; P < 0.001). Our findings are consistent with those of Cheng et al. [34], further supporting the notion that tumor size is an independent risk factor for MVI in PTC.

Furthermore, it is noteworthy that the high odds ratio for tumor size ≥ 2.3 cm reflects the biological and methodological factors. Biologically, larger tumors are associated with hypoxia-driven angiogenesis and invasive growth, linking to MVI. Vascular invasion in PTC co-occurs with aggressive features and predicts a higher recurrence risk. Methodologically, dichotomizing size at the cohort median rather than guideline cut points may yield high categorical OR when event rates differ across strata and the sample size in the 'large' stratum is modest. Collinearity between size and adverse pathology can magnify the effect, even after adjustment, if residual confounding persists. As a single-center surgical cohort, the spectrum and selection effects could have contributed to the observed magnitude. These considerations support this association while suggesting cautious interpretation and external validation. Additionally, it is important to note that in subgroup analysis, the very large odds ratios, accompanied by wide 95% confidence intervals (for tumor size ≥ 2.3 cm), likely indicate a limited number of events within certain strata. This results in imprecise and potentially unstable estimates that require cautious interpretation. Such patterns are common when subgroup sizes are small or outcomes are sparse, which inflates the uncertainty around effect sizes. Future studies should prespecify subgroups, ensure adequate sample size and events-per-variable, and consider methods tailored for sparse data to improve the stability and precision of estimates.

The association between HT and thyroid cancer has been the subject of debate and remains controversial. A growing body of research indicates a potential association between HT and an elevated risk of developing thyroid cancer. In a comprehensive study of 64,628 patients, Resende De Paiva et al. (2017) reported a higher incidence of PTC in HT (OR 2.36, 95% CI 1.55–3.29) [36]. This finding were further supported by additional studies [11, 37, 38]. One potential explanation for this association is the chronic inflammation observed in HT, which may create a microenvironment conducive to tumor invasion and migration [39]. However, some studies have reported that HT may exert a protective effect against the aggressive features and progression of PTC, particularly in cases with BRAF mutations [15, 40]. In contrast,in a large retrospective series, Li et al. found that a lower preoperative TPOAB concentration was independently linked to central compartment lymph node metastasis (CLNM), along with factors such as male sex, younger age, larger tumors, multifocality, and vascular invasion [41]. These complementary findings suggest that TPOAB may reflect distinct aspects of tumor biology related to vascular invasion and nodal spread, highlighting the need for integrated models that assess both invasion and metastasis phenotypes.This protective effect may be attributed to the altered immunological environment resulting from chronic lymphocytic infiltration in HT, which could modulate the tumor microenvironment and enhance antitumor immune responses [40].

To further explore the relationship between HT and thyroid cancer, we investigated the association between TPOAB levels and MVI in patients with PTC using both lnTPOAB levels and subgroup analyses. The findings of this study indicate a positive correlation between lnTPOAB levels and the occurrence of MVI in patients with PTC. This finding suggests that elevated TPOAB levels may increase the MVI risk. In the subgroup analysis, patients with TPOAB levels ≥ 50 IU/ml (defined as HT) exhibited a higher risk of MVI, with an odds ratio of 2.02 (95% CI: 1.06, 3.87; P = 0.033). The main analyses addressed the dose–response relationship between TPOAb levels and MVI. The sensitivity analysis of TPOAb ≥ 50 IU/mL provides a complementary categorical perspective. Our study, conducted from multiple perspectives and at the microscopic level, provides compelling evidence that HT may be an independent risk factor for PTC progression. Prospective validation of these findings is warranted.

In the subgroup analysis, an unexpected correlation was observed between Tg levels and MVI. In clinical practice, Tg is frequently employed as a tumor marker in patients who have undergone thyroidectomy for differentiated thyroid cancer, with the most common types being papillary and follicular [2, 3]. Given that Tg is produced by thyroid tissue, it can be expected that the absence or removal of thyroid tissue post-surgery should result in undetectable or very low levels of Tg [42]. Consequently, the presence of measurable or increasing Tg levels postoperatively may indicate the presence of residual or recurrent disease [2, 3]. To the best of our knowledge, no studies have reported the association between Tg and MVI in PTC. This study demonstrated that elevated Tg levels are associated with an increased risk of MVI. Patients with Tg levels ≥ 50 ng/ml exhibited a higher MVI risk, with an odds ratio of 2.50 (95% CI: 1.11, 5.63; P = 0.027). This suggests that Tg levels may serve as a significant indicator of MVI in patients with PTC. Notwithstanding these findings, the observed association between preoperative Tg levels and MVI in this cohort should be considered hypothesis-generating. Prior studies have shown that Tg correlates with tumor burden and nodal disease in differentiated thyroid cancer; however, the results are inconsistent and are influenced by assay methods and preanalytical/analytical variability. Moreover, TGAB can interfere with Tg quantification, complicating interpretation across centers [30]. Given these limitations, the current results warrant external validation in larger, multicenter cohorts with standardized Tg assays and rigorous handling of TgAB-positive cases, as well as mechanistic studies to clarify whether Tg elevation is a proxy for tumor bulk, biology, or both. The exact mechanisms underlying this association are not yet fully understood and require further research.

The results of this study indicate an association between TPOAB levels and MVI in patients with PTC. This information could assist physicians in tailoring their treatment strategies and possibly opting for more aggressive approaches to counteract the risk of MVI. In terms of prognosis, patients with PTC and elevated TPOAB levels may have a more challenging disease course and require rigorous monitoring. The potential of reducing TPOAB levels to mitigate the risk of MVI presents new avenues for research, including the exploration of immunosuppressive therapy. However, this hypothesis requires further validation.

One other point to note is that the per-unit effect of ln(TPOAB) (OR 1.18) indicates a modest, dose-dependent increase in risk. In clinical practice, TPOAB should be considered within a broader preoperative risk profile, together with tumor size and Tg, to assist in tailoring surgical extent and postoperative surveillance, rather than serving as an independent determinant of management. If postoperative pathology reveals adverse features, this preoperative signal may help clarify the patient’s risk trajectory; however, in the absence of such findings, antibody levels alone should not prompt escalation of treatment.

This study had some limitations. First, this study was retrospective and conducted at a single center, which may have introduced selection bias. Although we observed a significant association between TPOAB and MVI, the lack of follow-up data restricts conclusions regarding recurrence or survival; therefore, further studies with long-term follow-up in larger populations are required to validate our findings. Second, although the primary model met the accepted EPV thresholds, some subgroups had few events, which may reduce power and increase estimator variability; these exploratory findings warrant external validation in larger cohorts. Third, the precise mechanisms underlying the observed associations remain to be elucidated, and further research is required to address this knowledge gap. Moreover, other confounding factors (e.g., BRAF-V600E) that may potentially influence the outcomes should be considered. Consequently, it is of paramount importance to conduct basic research to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying TPOAB and MVI.

In addition, missing values represent an inherent limitation of observational clinical datasets and can bias estimates when the mechanism of missingness is not completely random. In this study, 75 cases(13.8%) were excluded due to missing data, which may have introduced selection bias. To address this, we performed multiple imputation under the Missing at Random assumption using a broad set of auxiliary variables, and the stability of the results was evaluated across complete-case analyses as well as best-case and worst-case scenarios [43]. While these steps mitigate the risk of selection and information bias attributable to incomplete data, some residual bias cannot be excluded, and independent external validation remains necessary.

In conclusion, TPOAB levels may serve as a valuable predictor of MVI in patients with PTC, aiding in more informed clinical decision-making and patient management. Future research should validate these findings through prospective studies and further investigate the underlying mechanisms.
