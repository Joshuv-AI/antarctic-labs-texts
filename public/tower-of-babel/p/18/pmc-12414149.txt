Young-onset type 2 diabetes and associations with high disability rate, low educational level and immigrant background: a study from Norwegian general practice
Authors: Katrina Louise Bø Tibballs, Lars Kirkebøen, Anne Karen Jenum, Jørund Straand, Tore Julsrud Berg, Esben Selmer Buhl
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionYoung-onset type 2 diabetes (YOD), diagnosed before 40 years of age, entails a high disease burden and potential for early dependence on disability benefits. The risk of type 2 diabetes (T2D) varies with socio-economic status and ethnic background, yet the relationship between these factors and age at diagnosis is insufficiently explored. We aimed to study associations between YOD and living on disability benefits, educational level and country background.Research design and methodsCross-sectional data on 8640 individuals with T2D, linked to data on educational level and country background, were compared with population data from the same residential areas. Similar comparisons were made for data on disability benefits among 3854 individuals of working age (<65 years). Using negative binomial regression, we analysed risk of disability by age at diabetes diagnosis, and risk of YOD by educational level and country background.ResultsThe risk of being dependent on disability benefits was three times higher in YOD (adjusted incidence rate ratio, aIRR (95% CI) 3.1 (2.7 to 3.5)) and twice as high in later-onset T2D (1.9 (1.8 to 2.1)) as in the general population. People of Norwegian background with low educational levels had threefold higher YOD risk (3.3 (2.4 to 4.4)) than those with a tertiary degree, while people of non-Western backgrounds with low educational levels had a smaller increase in YOD risk (1.5 (1.1 to 2.1)). People of non-Western backgrounds had higher YOD risk than those of Norwegian background (4.2 (3.5 to 5.0)), while people of south Asian background had an even greater relative YOD risk (9.0 (7.3 to 11.0)), threefold higher than for later-onset T2D (3.2 (2.8 to 3.7)).ConclusionsLifetime risk of being dependent on disability benefits was substantially higher for individuals with YOD than in later onset T2D. Non-Western and particularly south Asian backgrounds were associated with increased YOD risk. Low education was an important YOD risk factor only for people with Norwegian background.

Introduction

Despite advances in treatment, type 2 diabetes (T2D) remains a leading cause of cardiovascular disease, blindness, chronic kidney disease and limb amputations.14 In recent decades, the incidence of T2D has increased among younger age groups with diagnosis before 40 years of age, classified as young-onset T2D (YOD). YOD is associated with higher morbidity and mortality than later-onset T2D.5 6 As more individuals develop T2D within normal working age, the risk of falling out of the labour market due to the disease and its complications has become a significant concern.7 8

The burden of T2D and YOD is not evenly distributed in the population. Non-Western immigrants and their descendants, as well as individuals with low socio-economic status (SES), are groups at high risk of acquiring T2D at a younger age.6911 In a Norwegian general practice (GP) sample of people with T2D, non-Western immigrants were found to be diagnosed 5–15 years earlier than their western counterparts.12

SES, encompassing education, occupation, income, residential area and housing conditions, impacts T2D risk.13 Educational attainment is the most commonly used measure of SES and is independently and consistently associated with T2D risk.11 14 15

To develop effective prevention strategies for T2D, and especially YOD, it is necessary to map the distribution of T2D and YOD in the population by country background and SES. In addition to assessing the health consequences of developing T2D at a younger age, it is important to examine associations between YOD and ability to participate in the workforce.16 Dependence on economic disability benefits is an important and clinically relevant indicator of disease burden, with reduced functional capacity and psychosocial well-being, as well as economic disadvantage to both the individual and society.

For individuals who develop T2D in early adulthood, the associations with work force participation, educational level and country background are insufficiently studied.7 Therefore, this study aimed to investigate possible associations between age at T2D diagnosis and risk of being dependent on disability benefits, as well as associations between country background, education level and risk of T2D at different ages of onset.

Research design and methods

Design, data collection and variables

Cross-sectional clinical data on all individuals with T2D 18 years or older were collected from the electronic medical records (EMRs) of 77 Norwegian GP clinics in 2015 (the ROSA four study).17 The GP clinics represented both rural and urban areas, serving populations with diversity in socio-economic characteristics and country background. Clinics in Eastern Oslo, where a larger share of the population has non-Western backgrounds, were intentionally oversampled to secure sufficient numbers to study this group.

Variables included patients’ sex, year of birth and year of diabetes diagnosis.

ROSA 4 data were linked to data from Statistics Norway on the area of residence, employment status, disability benefit, educational level and country background. Statistics Norway provided a separate dataset containing the same variables for the whole adult (18 years and above) population of Norway in 2014.

Patient and public involvement

The Norwegian Diabetes Association is a partner in The ROSA 4 Study, ensuring patient involvement in the planning. They have informed their members of the possibility to withdraw from the study and report to the public when results are published.

Matching of ROSA 4 to population data cells

To compute the risks of receiving disability benefit and of acquiring T2D at different ages, we first constructed a comparative dataset comprising all residents aged 18 and older in the municipalities served by the ROSA 4 GP clinics. All residents of Norway are allocated a regular GP. This allowed us to calculate how many GP clinics were represented in ROSA 4 within each municipality, a number that varied between 50% and 100%. The residential population data were weighted to reflect the proportion of residents covered by the ROSA 4 clinics, using the total number of patients in these clinics (n=3 16 000) relative to the total population in the respective municipalities (n=5 40 000). It was not possible to link the population dataset to ROSA 4, so the datasets were organised into cells with common characteristics to facilitate comparative analyses of age at diabetes diagnosis, current age, sex, residential area, disability benefit status, educational level and country background.

Educational level was categorised as (i) completed compulsory education or less, (ii) upper secondary education or (iii) higher education (university or similar). Country background was defined as the individuals’ own, their parents’ or grandparents’ country of birth, if any were born outside Norway. It was categorised as (i) Norwegian; (ii) European or north American; (iii) Middle Eastern, north African, from the Horn of Africa, central Asian (MENA+); (iv) south Asian; or (v) other. Individuals with T2D were stratified into three age-at-diagnosis groups: age <40 years (YOD), 40–49 years and ≥50 years.

Statistical analysis

Individuals in ROSA 4 were compared with the matched population from the same residential areas. Descriptive data are presented with means±SD and proportions as appropriate.

For the risk of receiving disability benefits, only individuals below 65 years of age were included as these financial benefits only apply during working age. For risks of T2D and YOD, all ages were included, and risks were calculated as proportions of ROSA 4 individuals relative to the matched population. Associations between disability benefits, educational level, country background and T2D or YOD were estimated using a population-weighted, negative binomial regression analysis, to account for the cell structure of the data. Directed acyclic graphs were drawn prior to the analyses to identify potential confounding factors, with age, sex, country background and educational level included as appropriate. Exposures and potential confounders were analysed for significant interactions. Adjusted incidence rate ratios (aIRR) with 95% CIs were calculated and presented in forest plots. Predicted prevalences of disability benefits by age were calculated from the aIRRs and plotted using the mimargins and marginsplot packages. Statistical analyses were conducted in Stata SE 16 (StataCorp).

The mean age was 45 years among those with YOD and 47 years in the matched population. Employment levels were lower in the YOD group (74 vs 80%), and the proportion receiving disability benefit was higher (10% vs 7.2%). Having only compulsory education was more common in the YOD group (37% vs 23%) and having a university degree or similar was less common (19% vs 34%). People with non-Western, and particularly South Asian backgrounds, were overrepresented in the YOD group compared with the matched population (22% vs 3.1 %) (p<0.001 for all comparisons).

Comparing people with YOD (mean age 45 years) to those with age at T2D diagnosis >50 years (mean age 71 years), those from non-Western backgrounds were also overrepresented among patients with YOD (38% vs 7%). Among individuals of working age (<65 years) in the ROSA 4 sample (n=3854), receiving disability benefit was more common among patients with late-onset T2D (10% vs 25%).

Associations between disability benefit and T2D at different ages of onset

Among individuals of working age (<65 years) and after adjusting for age and sex, the risk of receiving disability benefit was twofold higher for those with T2D compared with the matched population (aIRR, 95% CI 2.2 (2.0 to 2.3); figure 1). Among individuals with YOD, this risk was threefold higher than in the matched population (3.1 (2.7 to 3.5)), and 1.5 times higher than for the overall population with T2D. Further adjustments for country background and educational level gave a reduced risk of dependence on disability benefits both for those with T2D at all ages (1.9 (1.7 to 2.0)) and those with YOD (2.6 (2.3 to 3.0)), with a Norwegian background and low educational attainment being associated with higher risk (online supplemental figure 1a). The relative differences were similar for men and women, although rates of disability benefits were higher among women across all groups (online supplemental figures 1b, c).

Associations between educational levels and the risk of T2D and YOD

After adjusting for age and country background, individuals with only compulsory education had an almost doubled risk of T2D compared to those with a tertiary degree (1.8 (1.7 to 1.9); figure 2). Low educational attainment was associated with a greater relative increase in the risk of YOD than of T2D at ≥50 years of age (2.3 (1.9 to 2.9) vs 1.5 (1.4 to 1.7)).

For the risk of YOD, but not for the overall T2D risk, a significant interaction was found between educational level and country background. Individuals of Norwegian background with low education had a more than threefold increase in the relative risk of YOD (3.3 (2.4–4.4)) compared to those with a tertiary degree. The YOD risk for individuals with a non-Western background was less associated with educational level (1.5 (1.1 to 2.1). These differences in association between educational level and YOD risk for different country backgrounds appeared more pronounced for men than women, but this trend did not reach statistical significance (online supplemental figure 2).

Associations between country background and risk of T2D and YOD

After adjusting for age, all non-Western background groups had an elevated risk of T2D compared to those with Norwegian background (figure 3). People of non-Western backgrounds had a fourfold increased risk of YOD (4.2 (3.5 to 5.0)), and a doubled risk of being diagnosed with T2D after the age of 50 years (2.0 (1.8 to 2.2)). This excess risk was most pronounced among those with a south Asian background, who had a threefold risk increase of T2D diagnosed ≥50 years (3.2 (2.8 to 3.7)) and a ninefold increased risk of YOD (9.0 (7.3 to 11.0)).

Differences in educational level did not seem to mediate this increased T2D risk among people with non-Western backgrounds (data not shown). However, a significant interaction was found between sex and country background, as all non-Western background groups had an excess risk of T2D for women compared to men, a pattern not observed among those with a Norwegian background (online supplemental figure 3). A similar pattern was detected for the risk of YOD, although numbers were too low to establish a significant interaction.

Discussion

Summary of main findings

Our most important finding from this cross-sectional study from Norwegian general practice was that individuals diagnosed with T2D before 40 years of age (YOD) have a threefold higher risk of being dependent on disability benefits than the general population, a risk 1.5-fold higher than for those with later-onset T2D. Further, individuals with only compulsory education have a greater risk of YOD compared to those with a tertiary degree, particularly among individuals of Norwegian background who show a more than threefold increase, with smaller differences observed for those with non-Western backgrounds. Lastly, we found that people of non-Western background have a fourfold increase in the risk of YOD compared to those with Norwegian background, which is twice the relative increase seen for later-onset T2D.

Risk of economic dependence on disability benefits

The substantially higher risk of being dependent on disability benefits for those with YOD compared to later-onset T2D warrants attention. We have previously described the higher complication rates among people with YOD than later-onset T2D in Norway, which most likely contribute to their increased risk of living on disability benefits.16 Acquiring T2D in early adulthood leads to a higher likelihood of complications, affecting the ability to work. T2D is also associated with a greater load of discordant comorbidities, which can further impact general health and functional capabilities.18

We interpret the higher rates of disability benefits among those with Norwegian backgrounds compared with immigrant populations as primarily related to differences in labour market participation and employment rates. Regardless of whether the lack of labour market participation is due to reduced employment opportunities or by choice, it precludes the individual’s right to disability benefits.

Interestingly, there is a greater increase in the risk of receiving disability benefits among individuals of Norwegian background with T2D than for those of non-Western background. A possible explanation is that T2D is more strongly linked to low SES among ethnic Norwegians than among those with a non-Western background.

Risk of T2D and YOD by education level and country background

A recent review found associations between low SES and YOD in agreement with our findings.7 However, we are not aware of any previous studies focusing on the combined effects of ethnic and socioeconomic factors in relation to YOD risk.

The higher relative risk of T2D for those with only compulsory education in younger age groups than in older age groups may reflect an increase in the relative importance of education as a social determinant of health in recent decades. As greater parts of the population have tertiary degrees, having only compulsory education becomes more strongly associated with social adversity, with fewer job opportunities for these individuals than in the past. Further, age is an important risk factor for T2D, making other factors such as education relatively less important.

That people of non-Western backgrounds have an increased risk of YOD compared to those with Norwegian background is in line with previous studies.9 19 A predisposition to hyperglycaemia from a young age has particularly been studied in south Asian populations, where it is found to be present from birth through to adulthood, contributing to earlier onset of T2D compared with Europeans. This susceptibility continues throughout life, but differences in T2D risk between people of different country backgrounds diminish as the incidence increases among Europeans from around 50 years of age.

The non-significant trend suggesting a higher relative risk of T2D and YOD for non-Western men than women is in line with existing research showing that women of several non-Western backgrounds have higher average body mass index (BMI) and higher T2D risk at the same BMI level than men.52022 Conversely, men of European background seem to develop T2D at lower BMI than women.23

Previous studies have shown that immigrant background populations are more likely to also have low SES and these two factors act together in their association with increased T2D risk.24 25 In our study, YOD risk was generally lower in the majority population with a Norwegian background, while low SES was an important risk factor in this group. Among non-Western immigrants, especially those of South Asian background, other factors seem to dominate in explaining the higher YOD risk, making educational status a less important contributor. Non-Western immigrants typically had lower educational levels, and few of them had attained a tertiary degree. A UK study reported a 20% lower risk of T2D in second- vs first-generation non-Western immigrants, pointing to adiposity and continued social disadvantage as important factors to explain the persistent excess risk.26

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this study is the quality of the dataset. All clinical EMR data in the ROSA 4 sample were manually validated by a study nurse. The external validity and representativity of ROSA 4 for all patients with T2D in Norwegian general practice was sought through balanced recruitment of participating GP clinics, representing urban and rural populations and different country backgrounds.17 We further complemented the ROSA 4 dataset with the linkage to demographic data from Statistics Norway, providing individual-level information on country background and SES for the entire adult population of Norway. This allowed for comparisons between ROSA 4 and the general public as a control group based on all adults living in the same residential areas, without having to create random matched samples.

Limitations included the lack of clinical data on the general population and inability to adjust for clinical variables such as BMI. Data on the diagnoses leading to allocation of disability benefits and other relevant comorbidities were also not available. Additionally, the ROSA 4 dataset was collected in 2015 and does not include the more recent immigrant populations from Syria and Ukraine.27 The number of individuals with YOD provided insufficient power for subgroup analysis.

Implications for healthcare, society and further research

Our findings highlight the increased risk of being dependent on disability benefits for individuals with YOD and the greater risk of acquiring T2D at a younger age among those with non-Western backgrounds or low educational levels. This emphasises a need for more effective measures to prevent, identify and manage T2D in these high-risk groups. The GPs have a unique role in this, given their high accessibility, continuity of care and comprehensive understanding of patients’ health and social situations, factors associated with reduced morbidity and mortality.28 Norwegian GPs have been found to provide the same quality in processes of diabetes care to patients of all country backgrounds.12 However, individuals of non-Western backgrounds and those with low educational attainment were less likely to reach treatment targets, reflecting the challenges of providing sufficient care to vulnerable population groups and indicating a need for a more individualised approach.29 Norwegian diabetes guidelines recommend annual HbA1c measurements for persons with Asian or African background starting at the age of 25 years. However, it is not known to which extent this recommendation is followed.30 In addition, national population-based T2D prevention strategies are necessary, supplemented with age-adjusted and culturally customised policies targeting at-risk groups highlighted in this study.13 31 32

Further studies with larger samples and comprehensive information about all comorbidities may elucidate whether differences in total disease burden in different country backgrounds contribute to the higher proportion receiving disability benefits among the Norwegian majority population with T2D than among those with non-Western backgrounds. New studies focusing on non-Western immigrants and their descendants, with a higher proportion expected to have completed tertiary education, will allow quantification of T2D risk at different ages when education levels are more comparable to the majority population.

Conclusions

The risk of living on disability benefits was threefold higher among individuals with T2D diagnosed before 40 years of age than in the general population. The risk of developing T2D before the age of 40 years was four- to ninefold higher among individuals of non-Western backgrounds than in the Norwegian majority population, and over twice as high among individuals with only compulsory education compared to those with a tertiary degree. Low educational attainment is a significant risk factor for young people developing T2D in the majority of Norwegian population but less so for people of non-Western backgrounds. Further comprehensive research is needed to guide future prevention strategies and disentangle the relative importance of education and challenges faced by immigrant populations to decrease the risk of young people developing T2D.
