Adverse mental health outcomes and alcohol misuse among UK Armed Forces personnel: fourth phase of a 20-year cohort study of military personnel who served during the Iraq and Afghanistan conflicts
Authors: Marie-Louise Sharp, Margaret Jones, Sofia Franchini, Ray Leal, Lisa Hull, Niamh Molloy, Howard Burdett, Daniel Leightley, Amos Simms, Jenny Stone, Neil Greenberg, Dominic Murphy, Deirdre MacManus, Simon Wessely, Sharon A M Stevelink, Nicola T Fear
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTwenty years since the start of UK Armed Forces participation in the Iraq and Afghanistan conflicts post-2001, the extent to which these deployments continue to impact mental health outcomes and alcohol misuse in UK military personnel is unknown. This is the reporting of the fourth phase, cross-sectional study of a longitudinal cohort study that has assessed the health and well-being of UK serving and ex-serving personnel since 2004.MethodsParticipants were eligible for the most recent phase (2022–2023) if they took part previously (2014–2016) and consented to recontact. Primary outcome measures included symptoms of common mental disorders (CMD), such as depression and anxiety, probable posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD), complex PTSD (C-PTSD) and alcohol misuse.ResultsIn the overall sample (n=4104, response rate=54.6%), CMD were the most prevalent outcome (27.8%), followed by probable PTSD (9.4%) and alcohol misuse (8.4%). The majority of PTSD experienced met the criteria for C-PTSD (72.7%). Ex-serving Regulars compared with serving Regulars reported a higher prevalence of PTSD (10.5% vs 7.4%, adjusted OR (AOR)=1.68, 95% CI 1.12 to 2.51) and C-PTSD (6.5% vs 3.9%, AOR=1.80, 95% CI (1.07 to 3.05); a higher prevalence of both disorders was also reported in serving/ex-serving Regulars whose last deployment to Iraq/Afghanistan was in a combat role.ConclusionAlthough the majority of those who deployed to Iraq or Afghanistan remain well, there is an enduring impact of combat deployment on PTSD. Attention should continue to be directed towards the prevention, early detection and treatment needs of this cohort.Keywords: Epidemiology, Mental Health, Occupational Health, Psychology, Military Personnel

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

From 2004–2016 UK serving and ex-serving personnel who served during the Iraq/Afghanistan era of conflicts most commonly reported common mental disorders (CMD) (20%–22%), followed by alcohol misuse (10%–15%), and probable posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) (4%–6%). Deployment to Iraq/Afghanistan in a combat role was associated with significantly worse mental health outcomes and alcohol misuse in ex-serving Regular personnel but not in currently serving Regular personnel.

Introduction

It is over twenty years since the start of the UK Armed Forces involvement in the conflicts in Iraq and Afghanistan. The UK Armed Forces experienced over a decade of active and concurrent combat operations in Afghanistan from 2001 to 2014, and in Iraq from 2003 to 2011. Over that period, approximately 280 000 UK Armed Forces were deployed to one or both of these conflicts.1

The King’s Centre for Military Health Research has surveyed the health and well-being of serving personnel (Regulars and Reserves) and ex-serving personnel (veterans) in a longitudinal cohort study since 2004. There have been three previous cohort time points with data collected between 2004–2006 (Phase 1),2 2007–2009 (Phase 2)3 and 2014–2016 (Phase 3),4 with this study now reporting on Phase 4 cross-sectional findings (2022–2023).

Over the first three phases, there were stable rates of common mental disorders (CMD), which we define as symptoms of depression and anxiety, between 20.0% to 21.9%, decreasing rates of alcohol misuse from 15.3% to 10.0%, but increases in the rates of posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) from 3.9% to 6.2% between Phase 2 and Phase 3. Previous phases of this cohort identified an increased risk of PTSD in those who deployed in a combat role due to more frontline exposure to traumatising events.5 Phase 3 identified a higher risk of CMD, PTSD and alcohol misuse in ex-service personnel compared with service personnel due to individuals leaving service because of being unwell, and the difficulties ex-service personnel experienced in transition to civilian life.4 6 Previous longitudinal cohort studies reporting on ex-serving populations in the US and Canada have found increases in depression, anxiety and PTSD over time.7 8 While the UK cohort was last surveyed at the end of combat missions to Iraq and Afghanistan4 (Phase 3), many have since left service, and there is a need to update evidence on this cohort.

Since the last phase of this study, the UK Government established the Office for Veterans’ Affairs in 2019 to better coordinate policies towards veterans. The Armed Forces Covenant (enshrined in law in 2011, details how serving/ex-serving personnel should not be disadvantaged by their service), has been extended in 2022 by placing a new duty on public bodies to pay ‘due regard’9 to the needs of the Armed Forces community in delivering statutory services. The Ministry of Defence (MoD) and NHS have additionally focused on improving access to and provision of mental health services for serving/ex-serving personnel.10 11 Hence, the impact of these changes is also unknown for this community.

We now provide the main outcomes for Phase 4 (2022–2023). We describe the prevalence of adverse mental health outcomes and alcohol misuse for the whole cohort (serving/ex-serving, Regulars and Reserve personnel) and investigate whether these outcomes differ in Regular personnel by serving status, deployment to Iraq/Afghanistan, and role on deployment.

Methods

Study design and participants

Phase 1 of the cohort study collected data between 2004–2006 to compare a range of health outcomes from a random sample of Regular and Reserve personnel who were initially deployed to Iraq and, for comparison, a random sample of trained but non-deployed personnel. An overall response rate of 61.0% (n=10 272)2 was achieved. Phase 2 of the study (2007–2009) followed up participants from Phase 1 who consented to recontact. In addition, a random sample of personnel who had been deployed to Afghanistan was included, as well as a ‘replenishment’ sample of UK Armed Forces who had joined service since April 2003. A response rate of 56.1% was achieved (n=9990).3 For Phase 3 (2014–2016), all participants from Phase 1 and 2 who consented were followed up. Phase 3 included another replenishment sample of trained personnel who had joined the military since August 2009. The response rate was 44.3% (n=8093).4 The cohort has included male and female personnel, all three branches of the Armed Forces (Naval Services (Royal Navy and Marines), Army and the Royal Air Force (RAF)), Regulars and Reserves and current serving/ex-serving personnel. By Phase 3, 42.8% of the sample had left service. These samples have been detailed previously.2,4

Phase 4 of the cohort study (2022–2023) recontacted participants who took part at Phase 3 and consented to further contact. There was no replenishment sample in Phase 4 as the aim was to transition the cohort to be an ‘era’ cohort of those UK Armed Forces who served during the conflicts of Iraq/Afghanistan. The follow-up sample comprised 7608 participants. Please see online supplemental figure 1.

Procedures

Data were collected via a self-administered online or paper questionnaire. The questionnaire covered a range of health/well-being topics; however, for the purposes of this study, the sections used were sociodemographic, military characteristics, and the health and alcohol use sections, specifically: symptoms of CMD, such as depression and anxiety, using the 12-item General Health Questionnaire (GHQ-12);12 probable PTSD using a blended version of the 20 item PTSD Checklist for DSM-5 (PCL-5),13 and the 17-item National Centre for PTSD Checklist (PCL-C)14 to accommodate the change in definition of PTSD between DSM-IV and DSM-5; alcohol use was measured using the 10-item WHO Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test (AUDIT);15 and a measure of complex PTSD (C-PTSD) was newly included using the International Trauma Questionnaire (ITQ).16 Binary health outcomes were defined with the following cut-offs: 4 or more for the GHQ (score range 0–12); 38 or more for the PCL-5 (range 0–80); 50 or more for the PCL-C (range 17–85); 16 or more for the AUDIT (range 0–40), indicating alcohol use harmful to health, termed ‘alcohol misuse’; C-PTSD was defined according to the published diagnostic algorithm as per Cloitre16 where individuals must meet criteria for probable PTSD, as well as meet additional criteria for C-PTSD.

Specific sociodemographic and military characteristic data were taken from baseline (at sampling) or Phase 3 data as collecting this data again was not deemed appropriate (see table 1).

Data collection

Data collection was carried out between January 2022 and September 2023. Participants were invited to take part using contact details previously provided or from updated contact details provided by the MoD for participants who were in service at the time of Phase 3. Participants were contacted through email, SMS text message and post, and invited to take part in the survey online or by paper questionnaire. Up to two reminders were sent to those who had not completed the survey and had not withdrawn. Following the reminder invitations, non-responders were traced by telephone. The full study protocol is detailed by Sharp and Jones.17

Analyses

Sample weights were calculated as the inverse probability of an individual being sampled at the appropriate phase. Response weights were calculated as the inverse probability of responding once sampled, driven by factors shown empirically to predict response (ie, age, sex, rank, service, sample, serving status, enlistment status and the interaction between sample and enlistment status). Combined sample and response weights were used to account for differential response rates and sampling fractions in all analyses. Missing items on mental health screening measures (GHQ-12, PCL-C/PCL-5 and AUDIT) were handled by the lowest value being imputed to missing item scores if fewer than four items of the measure were missing. If four or more items were missing, the participant was categorised as missing for the whole measure with no score/no case status.

The prevalence of adverse mental health outcomes, alcohol misuse and comorbidity was described for the whole sample (Regulars/Reserves, serving/ex-serving personnel). Weighted percentages are presented along with unweighted cell counts. Logistic regressions were used for the main analysis which compared mental health outcomes (CMD, PTSD, C-PTSD) and alcohol misuse in Regular personnel. Reserves were dropped from logistic regression analyses to allow comparison with previous phases and enable coherent comparisons within those who have served on full-time rather than voluntary/part-time contracts. Mental health prevalence in Regulars is reported with 95% CIs and ORs with 95% CI, to describe the effect size between; current serving personnel versus ex-serving personnel; groups deployed to Iraq/Afghanistan versus not deployed to Iraq/Afghanistan and those deployed in a combat role to Iraq/Afghanistan versus those deployed in a combat support role respectively. Further analyses compared the percentage of probable PTSD reported in those deployed/not deployed to Iraq/Afghanistan. Analyses compared changes in main outcomes from Phase 3 of the cohort study to Phase 4 in those who completed both Phases using an adjusted Wald test for difference in proportion, and further analyses stratified by serving status. Regression analyses controlled for a priori confounders including sex, age (as a continuous variable), educational status, marital status, service branch and rank. All statistical analyses were performed using the statistical package Stata (V.18),18 with survey commands to account for weighting.

Results

At Phase 4, 4104 respondents participated. The adjusted response rate was 54.6% (4104/7520 - adjusted for deaths and those with no contact details in the database for initial contact). Most participants responded through the online survey (79.1%, n=3245). Responders were more likely to be female, older, have held Reserve engagement status at baseline, hold Officer rank, serve(d) in the RAF (compared with Army), and were more likely to respond if they reported symptoms of CMD at Phase 3. Participants were less likely to respond if they had been part of the Phase 3 replenishment sample (online supplemental table 1).

Characteristics of participants

Most participants were male (86.0%, n=3531) with a mean age of 50.8 years (SD 10.3 years), ranging from 27.0–80.6 years old (table 1). The majority reported education to degree level, were married, were Non-Commissioned Officers and had regular enlistment status. Most of the sample were ex-serving at Phase 4 (71.8%, n=2944) and had deployed to Iraq/Afghanistan (69.0%, n=2827). Of those who deployed, 28.6% (n=807) held a combat role on their last deployment to Iraq/Afghanistan.

Prevalence of adverse mental health outcomes, alcohol misuse and comorbidity

The prevalence of CMD was 27.8% (n=1051), 9.4% for probable PTSD (n=313) and 8.4% (n=306) for alcohol misuse (online supplemental table 2). Overall, 66.9% (n=2782) of respondents did not report any adverse mental health outcomes or alcohol misuse. In terms of comorbidity, as a percentage of the whole sample, 22.3% (n=888) were a case for one outcome only (one of either the GHQ-12, PCL-5 or AUDIT), 9.2% (n=319) were a case for two outcomes and 1.5% (n=48) were a case for all three outcomes (online supplemental table 2).

Using the ITQ measure that defines PTSD and C-PTSD as two separate diagnoses, 2.1% (n=84) met the criteria for probable PTSD only and 5.7% (n=191) met the criteria for C-PTSD (total therefore meeting criteria for probable PTSD and C-PTSD was 7.8% (n=275) using the ITQ). Therefore, 72.7% of the PTSD in the cohort was complex ((5.702/7.844) ×100).

Younger age, lower rank, single relationship status, deployment to Iraq/Afghanistan and deploying in a combat role were all positively associated with CMD, PTSD and C-PTSD. Being male, of younger age and having deployed to Iraq/Afghanistan were associated with alcohol misuse. Service in the Army compared with Naval Services or RAF, and enlistment as a Regular rather than as a Reserve was associated with PTSD and C-PTSD (online supplemental table 3).

Adverse mental health outcomes and alcohol misuse by serving status in Regulars

The sample size of serving and ex-serving Regulars personnel (excluding Reserves) was 3222 (78.5% of the total sample). In adjusted analyses, there were increased odds of reporting PTSD (1.7 times increased odds), including C-PTSD (1.8 times increased odds), in ex-serving Regular personnel compared with serving Regular personnel. In all analyses (unadjusted and adjusted), serving status was not associated with reporting CMD and alcohol misuse (table 2).

Discussion

Two thirds of participants in the cohort study did not report adverse mental health outcomes or alcohol misuse (66.9%). However, one third of the cohort did report problems, with CMD the most prevalent (27.8%), followed by probable PTSD (9.4%) and alcohol misuse (8.4%). Of the outcomes investigated, just under a tenth of the cohort reported these as comorbid conditions. The majority of the PTSD experienced in the cohort met the criteria for C-PTSD (72.7%). Ex-serving Regulars compared with serving Regulars reported higher prevalence of PTSD (10.5% vs 7.4% respectively), including C-PTSD (6.5% vs 3.9% respectively). A higher prevalence of PTSD/C-PTSD was reported in serving and ex-serving Regulars who deployed in a combat role during their last deployment to Iraq or Afghanistan. The prevalence of CMD and PTSD has increased relatively since the previous phase of the cohort study, while the prevalence of alcohol misuse remained high and relatively stable.

Enduring impact of deployment in a combat role on PTSD prevalence

Symptoms of probable PTSD were associated with deployment in a combat role to Iraq/Afghanistan in both serving and ex-serving Regulars and represented the highest prevalence of PTSD at 13.0% and 18.4% respectively. This supports findings from the US Millennium Cohort that found a three-fold increased risk of PTSD in those deploying in combat roles versus non-deployed peers.19 Research in Australian Defence Forces (ADF), however, reports on the cumulative negative effect of all sources of trauma impacting higher PTSD symptoms (no matter timing or whether antecedent, deployment, combat or non-military trauma).20 Hence, the occurrence of probable PTSD cannot be explained solely by combat exposures, as probable PTSD was also reported in the non-deployed group. This non-deployed group to Iraq/Afghanistan may have experienced further exposures during military training or other deployments. Equally in line with the ADF study, a meta-analysis identified that while combat is one factor associated with PTSD in military personnel/veterans, other factors such as experiencing adverse life events or previous trauma21 are also important in predicting PTSD.

Prevalence of probable PTSD

The relative prevalence of probable PTSD may have risen in the cohort because there is a higher prevalence of probable PTSD in ex-serving Regulars compared with serving Regulars (10.5% vs 7.4%), and the Phase 4 sample has a greater percentage of ex-serving personnel compared with Phase 3 (71.8% vs 42.8% respectively). The higher prevalence of PTSD in ex-serving Regulars may be explained partly by survivor bias in the serving personnel sample, in that those who are unwell may be more likely to leave service. Equally, transition from service may be difficult for some as the loss of support structures may impact later development or worsening of PTSD symptoms.22 Ex-service personnel may also have less direct access to military psychological support, which in turn may delay help-seeking and exacerbate PTSD symptoms.23

The larger sample of ex-serving personnel in Phase 4 only partly explains the rise in probable PTSD. It was evident that there have been proportionally steeper increases in probable PTSD in serving personnel compared with ex-serving personnel. The increased prevalence could be explained by accumulation of training/deployment exposures in serving personnel,20 and there may be reporting biases due to more in-service mental health awareness and acceptability of help-seeking.24

As this phase of the cohort study was conducted post-COVID-19, we contend that pandemic impacts were less likely to influence PTSD prevalence. A systematic review found the direct impact of COVID-19 infection on PTSD outcomes was minimal.25 Equally, a study of healthcare workers with shorter-term COVID-19 distress found longer-term mental distress returned to baseline levels post-COVID-19.26 It is, however, possible in our sample that certain groups were more at risk of pandemic impacts on PTSD, such as those with previous PTSD symptoms which might minimally contribute to the prevalence of PTSD found at this phase.27

C-PTSD prevalence

This is the first time C-PTSD has been measured in a UK cohort sample of those who served during Iraq and Afghanistan conflicts (C-PTSD reported at 5.7%, and percentage of PTSD that was complex was 72.7%). In a study of Northern Ireland veterans, C-PTSD was estimated at 23.3% which as a percentage of PTSD experienced was 80.1%.28 In a UK veteran treatment-seeking sample, 56.7% of the sample reported C-PTSD, which as a percentage of PTSD was 80.2%.29 These higher levels of C-PTSD might be expected due to differences in the samples such as a higher prevalence of PTSD in Northern Ireland veterans at 36.8%.30 Equally, there are differences in the nature of the samples which are all veteran samples (at higher risk of PTSD) and chosen on the basis of trauma exposure and treatment seeking. However, despite these differences, the percentage of PTSD that is complex is at a similarly high level to this cohort. Recent general population studies in Ireland, Israel, the US and UK report the prevalence of C-PTSD to range between 3.9% and 12.9% and find C-PTSD as a percentage of PTSD to range between 42.2% and 70.9%.31 Hence, the overall prevalence of C-PTSD in the cohort is within the range of these general population studies, but the percentage of PTSD that is complex is at the higher end. C-PTSD has implications for healthcare providers, as ex-serving personnel who report C-PTSD take longer to seek help, have a greater burden of comorbid mental health conditions and need higher intensity interventions.32

Prevalence of CMD and alcohol misuse

The prevalence of CMD has risen relatively since the last phase of the cohort. In terms of UK population trends, Zhang et al33 found a rising trend in mental health problems in the general population pre-COVID-19 between 1991 to 2019. The UK Office for National Statistics post-COVID-19 data in Autumn 2022 found higher levels of depression in UK adults of 16% compared with levels in 2019 of 10%.34 Hence, these general population trends with impacts from COVID-19 and the cost-of-living crisis are likely reflected in the cohort sample. Additionally, responders at Phase 4 compared with non-responders were more likely to have reported symptoms of CMD at Phase 3 (with a small effect size), and therefore this sample bias may have caused a small effect on the reported prevalence.

All previous cohort phases have found higher levels of alcohol misuse in the cohort compared with the UK general population,2,4 and this remains the case. Using an AUDIT cut-off of 16 or more (indicating harmful use or dependence), serving and ex-serving personnel in the cohort were still drinking at two to three times the level of the UK general population.35

Relative cross-sectional changes over the four phases of the cohort and comparisons with allied countries’ Armed Forces

Comparing relative frequencies of mental health outcomes across cohort Phases 1–4, CMD has risen in Phase 4 from a prevalence of 20.0% to 21.9%2,4 to 27.8% and probable PTSD has seen a rise over Phases 1–4 from 3.9% to 6.2%2,4 to 9.9% (using the PCL-C as a comparative measure). Other international coalition countries find increases in mental health problems over time, with a higher risk also found in ex-serving personnel. For example, the US Millennium Cohort study found higher levels of PTSD symptoms in veterans compared with serving personnel in all PTSD trajectory classes.7 Likewise, a representative cohort study of Canadian Regular serving personnel and veterans found consistent rises in mood, anxiety and PTSD disorders from 2002 to 2018.8 These studies are most similar to our cohort and provide some initial confirmatory comparisons of this trend internationally. However, specific longitudinal analyses are required to build on previous UK military mental health trajectory research.36 37

Strengths and limitations

The study strengths include a prospective longitudinal design, with an initial sample chosen randomly from all UK personnel who deployed to Iraq/Afghanistan, recruitment from a population where underlying characteristics are known, and the use of validated measures for adverse outcomes. Study limitations include cross-sectional analyses, by which we can only comment on associations and relative trends, recruitment from a specific cohort that served during the Iraq and Afghanistan conflicts; hence the study may not reflect experiences of older cohorts or more recent recruits in the UK Armed Forces. The study has limitations due to loss of follow-up such that the cohort has a high proportion of Officers and those with high levels of education, hence it may not be representative of those of lower socioeconomic status, therefore potentially underestimating adverse outcomes. However, the study has implemented response and sample weights to mitigate these biases. Lastly, the cohort experiences the healthy worker survivor bias, where those who become unwell may leave service. This may explain some differences between serving and ex-serving groups.

Implications

CMD is still the most prevalent condition with relative increases. There is evidence of the continued impact of combat in Iraq/Afghanistan on the prevalence of PTSD, of which a large percentage is C-PTSD. This new finding highlights the importance of complex and co-morbid conditions. Mental health providers should be prepared to see increasing numbers of serving and ex-serving personnel who served in Iraq/Afghanistan access their services for CMD and PTSD symptoms and should assess how well their services provide or refer for complex needs. There have been pilot trials examining the best treatment for C-PTSD in UK veterans with initial positive results.38 Previous relative declines in alcohol misuse have levelled off and are an on-going concern for alcohol prevention and treatment services.

Conclusions

The majority of those who served during the era of the post-2001 Iraq and Afghanistan conflicts do not report adverse mental health outcomes or alcohol misuse. There is, however, a substantial minority who do experience difficulties, particularly those who are ex-service personnel and those who deployed in a combat role. This study is of particular importance to public health bodies, government policy makers (both local and national), those undertaking health protection for current Armed Forces, and those providing health and support services who must fulfil the legal requirements placed on them by the Armed Forces Covenant.
