Can we use MRI for timing estimation of rotator cuff tears?
Authors: Domenico Albano, Salvatore Gitto, Carmelo Messina, Aldo Rizzo, Riccardo Giorgino, Carlo Perfetti, Giuseppe Davide Albano, Kholoud I A Abdelmaguid, Saleha Irfan Arodia, Federico Ambrogi, Angelo Vanzulli, Luca Maria Sconfienza
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo evaluate whether routinely assessed MRI features can reliably estimate the timing of rotator cuff tears (RCTs) and to assess the performance of an MRI-based logistic regression model in distinguishing recent from chronic tears.Material and methodsIn this retrospective single-center study, 255 patients with clinically and MRI-confirmed RCT following shoulder trauma underwent MRI between 2011 and 2024. Tears were classified as acute (< 6 weeks), subacute (6–12 weeks), or chronic (> 12 weeks) based on the reported date of trauma; acute and subacute tears were grouped as “recent”. Ten predefined MRI features were independently assessed. Univariable diagnostic performance metrics were calculated for each feature. A multivariable Firth-penalized logistic regression model was developed to discriminate recent from chronic tears, with internal validation performed using bootstrap resampling.ResultsAmong the 255 patients (mean age 58.3 years; 65% male), intra-/peri-muscular edema (42% in recent vs 12% in chronic tears) and frayed, hyperintense tendon fibers emerged as independent predictors of recent injury (odds ratios 4.15 and 6.78, respectively). However, traditional markers of chronicity—including fatty infiltration, muscle atrophy, tendon retraction, and superior humeral head migration—showed limited discriminative value. The multivariable model demonstrated modest performance, with an ideal area under the curve (AUC) of 0.74 and an optimism-corrected AUC of 0.68. Sensitivity and specificity at the optimal threshold were 56% and 82%, respectively.ConclusionAlthough selected MRI findings are associated with recent RCT, an MRI-based logistic regression model provides limited accuracy for timing estimation.Keywords: Rotator cuff, Tendon, Trauma, Injury, Forensic, Magnetic resonance imaging

Introduction

Distinguishing the traumatic origin of rotator cuff tears (RCTs) from degenerative causes is often challenging, as tendon injuries frequently occur on a background of pre-existing degenerative changes, with trauma often acting as a contributing or precipitating factor. Another significant challenge is the dating of RCT—an area in which radiologists may be called upon to assist, particularly in the context of medico-legal evaluations related to compensation claims following injury or accidents. Such assessments are typically based on the patient’s self-reported history regarding the mechanism of trauma, along with risk factors such as age, occupational or athletic activity, clinical findings, and imaging results. Radiologists are increasingly being engaged, particularly in their role as private consultants, to provide expert evaluations for courts or insurance companies concerning musculoskeletal injuries resulting from trauma [1]. The radiologist is tasked with determining whether a causal relationship exists between the traumatic event and the RCT. A central aim of this process is to estimate the time elapsed since the injury occurred, as this helps establish a reliable timeline and clarify the correlation between trauma and the resulting pathology, particularly in cases where discrepancies arise between medical findings and the patient’s account. Furthermore, both the timing estimation and the traumatic nature of RCT may influence the clinical decision-making by the surgeon in terms of treatment indication and surgical strategy [2]. However, this evaluation is often challenging, especially considering the high prevalence of minimally symptomatic RCT. Indeed, approximately 25% of individuals in their 60 s present with full-thickness RCT, with the prevalence rising to 50% in those over 80 years of age [3], thus making it difficult to attribute a clear link between a specific traumatic event and the radiological detection of RCT.

Ultrasound is the first-line imaging modality commonly used for the evaluation of the shoulder, offering diagnostic accuracy for RCT comparable to MRI and MR arthrography [4–6]. In addition to identifying tendon tears and assessing the degree of tendon retraction, ultrasound also enables the detection of certain secondary findings potentially related to chronicity, such as glenohumeral osteoarthritis and muscle atrophy. However, its use in medico-legal settings is limited, primarily because it does not generate a standardized, permanent record of images that can be independently reviewed by a second expert for verification or second opinion. In the context of RCT, MRI plays a pivotal role, providing objective, reproducible data that can be independently reviewed by third parties and is highly informative for evaluating injury chronicity by identifying various stages of tendon damage, degeneration, and healing. Indeed, compared with ultrasound, MRI allows a more detailed—and, importantly, simultaneous—assessment of tendon morphology, muscle quality, and secondary joint alterations, which may evolve differently over time and provide complementary temporal information.

Accordingly, we conducted a retrospective analysis of patients with clinically and MRI-confirmed RCT at varying stages of lesion evolution. The aim was to identify which MRI features most significantly correlate with lesion chronicity and to develop a logistic regression model capable of estimating the age of the RCT. The goal is to provide objective accuracy data that may assist radiologists and clinicians in distinguishing recent traumatic RCT from chronic degenerative tears.

Materials and methods

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Lombardia 1, Milan, Italy (RETRORAD protocol, CE: 61/INT/2017, approved on 12th January 2026), which granted a waiver for informed consent. Following the matching of imaging, laboratory, and surgical data, the database was fully anonymized to eliminate any links between the data and patients’ identities, in compliance with the General Data Protection Regulation for Research Hospitals.

Study protocol

This study was conducted at (IRCCS Ospedale Galeazzi-Sant’Ambrogio, Milan, Italy), in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) declaration. A retrospective review was performed of emergency department discharge records for shoulder trauma between 2011 and 2024. The study included patients who received a clinical diagnosis or had a clinical suspicion of RCT in the emergency setting and subsequently underwent shoulder MRI at our institution within days, weeks, or months following the traumatic event, with MRI confirmation of RCT. All included patients also underwent follow-up orthopedic evaluations at our Institution after MRI. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (i) absence of MRI examination; (ii) MRI negative for RCT; (iii) non-diagnostic MRI due to motion or other imaging artifacts; (iv) unknown date of trauma; and (v) history of prior rotator cuff repair surgery or glenohumeral arthroplasty.

MRI protocol

MRI examinations were retrospectively reviewed and acquired using various 1.5 T scanners (Siemens Avanto 1.5 T, Siemens Espree 1.5 T, GE Signa 1.5 T) and a 3.0-T scanner (GE Premier 3.0 T). Surface coils were used, and a standard field of view of 16 cm—adjusted according to patient body habitus—was applied. The imaging protocol consistently included coronal T1-weighted and fat-saturated proton density (FS-PD) sequences, sagittal T2-weighted and FS-PD sequences, and axial FS-PD sequences.

MRI interpretation

Few imaging studies have specifically investigated the temporal classification of RCT [7]. Consequently, a set of ten imaging features was evaluated on MRI to identify direct or indirect indicators of RCT. Similar MRI studies focused on ACL injuries have proposed varying temporal classifications, typically dividing lesions into acute, subacute, intermediate, and chronic phases using different thresholds [8, 9]. In the present study, RCT were categorized as acute (within six weeks), subacute (between six and twelve weeks), and chronic (beyond twelve weeks). For further sub-analyses, acute and subacute tears were grouped together and referred to as “recent tears”.

All MRI features were independently and randomly evaluated by a board-certified radiologist with four years of experience in musculoskeletal imaging under the supervision of a senior musculoskeletal radiologist with 13 years of experience. The first 50 cases were evaluated together by the two reviewers, while, in the remaining examinations, doubtful cases were addressed through discussion and agreement among the reviewers themselves. For each conventional MRI feature, a binary response (present/absent) was recorded, except for Patte and Goutallier parameters that were associated with a grade according to the relative classification (1–3 and 0–4, respectively). In patients with concomitant multiple rotator cuff tendon lesions, when both recent and chronic findings coexisted (e.g., a supraspinatus tendon without muscle edema and an infraspinatus tendon with muscle edema, or one tendon showing rounded, T2/FS–PD hypointense tendon fibers and another showing frayed, T2/FS–PD hyperintense tendon fibers), only the findings attributable to a recent lesion were included in the analysis.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics reports mean ± SD unless otherwise specified. Group comparisons across independent continuous samples used the Kruskal–Wallis test. Comparisons across categorical variables used the Pearson’s Chi square test or the Fisher test when appropriate. The number of subjects and the statistical test used are indicated in the figure legends or in the appropriate text. The dataset initially included multiple entries per patient corresponding to injuries obtained at various dates. To avoid duplication, we retained only one entry per patient, prioritizing acute injuries over subacute, and subacute over chronic. For all imaging findings, sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) were calculated. All variables were then included in the final logistic regression model.

Firth multivariable logistic regression model was used to handle some variables who were almost perfect separators. We estimated odds ratios based on the model’s regression coefficients. The model type and the number of subjects included in the analysis are indicated in the text. p-values of less than 0.05 were considered significant. Internal validation was performed using bootstrap resampling. Statistical analysis was performed using R.

Discussion

The ability to estimate the timing of RCT based on MRI findings represents a recognized challenge in musculoskeletal radiology, particularly in medico-legal and compensation-related settings, where radiologists are increasingly asked to provide expert opinions on the causal relationship between trauma and tendon injury. Unlike purely diagnostic tasks, temporal assessment requires imaging biomarkers that evolve in a predictable and sufficiently discriminative manner over time. The present study sought to evaluate whether routinely assessed MRI features could be combined into a logistic regression model capable of distinguishing recent from chronic RCT. Our results demonstrate that, although certain MRI findings show statistically significant associations with injury chronicity, the overall predictive performance of an MRI-based model remains limited. Indeed, in the context of rotator cuff tear pathology, the biological complexity of the disease may be predominant over the temporal resolution provided by conventional morphological imaging [13].

At the univariable level, several MRI features demonstrated partial discriminatory ability. Intra- and peri-muscular edema and frayed, hyperintense tendon fibers were more frequently observed in recent tears and emerged as the only independent predictors retained in the multivariable model. These findings are biologically plausible, as muscle edema reflects acute myofibrillar injury, while frayed, hyperintense tendon margins likely correspond to recent fiber disruption with interposed fluid and hemorrhage. Conversely, features traditionally associated with chronicity—such as fatty infiltration, muscle atrophy, tendon retraction, and superior humeral head migration—showed limited specificity for chronic tears when evaluated in isolation. This aspect supports the concept that chronic findings on magnetic resonance imaging may reflect pre-existing degenerative changes rather than necessarily corresponding to true temporal markers of the index injury.

Despite these associations, the overall performance of the multivariable model was modest. The optimism-corrected AUC of 0.68, together with a sensitivity of only 56% at the optimal threshold, indicates limited reliability for clinical or medico-legal decision-making. This contrasts with more favorable results reported for temporal estimation in other musculoskeletal contexts, such as anterior cruciate ligament injuries, where clearer injury mechanisms and more distinct temporal imaging patterns are present [8, 9]. The relatively weak performance observed in our study likely reflects fundamental differences in the pathophysiology of RCT, which often arise on a background of chronic tendon degeneration, with trauma acting as a triggering or aggravating event rather than a discrete cause.

The limited discriminative power of MRI in dating RCT highlights an important conceptual issue: Many imaging features traditionally interpreted as markers of chronicity may precede the traumatic event and therefore do not reliably reflect injury age, potentially due to previous unrecognized or asymptomatic RCT. Indeed, fatty infiltration, muscle atrophy, tendon retraction, and superior migration of humeral head may be present in asymptomatic individuals or develop gradually over years, blurring the distinction between acute and chronic lesions [14]. Similarly, inflammatory findings such as bursitis and joint effusion, although common in acute settings, were highly prevalent across all injury stages and demonstrated low specificity. These observations underscore the difficulty of deriving robust temporal information from static imaging features in a condition characterized by overlapping degenerative and traumatic processes.

From a methodological standpoint, the use of Firth-penalized regression allowed for stable coefficient estimation despite sparse data and quasi-complete separation in some predictors. Internal validation with bootstrap resampling confirmed limited generalizability, with a noticeable drop in AUC after optimism correction. Importantly, this rigorous statistical approach strengthens the credibility of the negative findings and supports the conclusion that model weakness reflects intrinsic limitations of the imaging biomarkers rather than analytical shortcomings.

Clinically and medico-legally, these results have relevant implications. While MRI remains indispensable for diagnosing RCT and characterizing tendon morphology, its role in reliably estimating injury timing appears constrained. The findings of this study caution against overreliance on MRI alone for determining the traumatic origin or temporal proximity of RCT, particularly in compensation and litigation settings. Imaging findings should instead be interpreted in conjunction with clinical history, physical examination, surgical findings, and—when available—prior imaging. The modest performance of the proposed model suggests that MRI-based temporal estimation should be framed probabilistically and communicated with appropriate uncertainty.

In the study by Loew et al. on 50 patients with RCT [7], certain criteria such as muscle edema and fluid collections demonstrated moderate discriminative ability in separating acute from chronic RCT, with inflammatory signs observed in a substantially higher proportion of acute tears compared with chronic ones (e.g., increased signal intensity patterns and peritendinous changes reported in > 70% of acute cases). In contrast, in our cohort bone marrow edema was present in only 18% of acute and 9% of chronic tears, and intra-/peri-muscular edema occurred in 42% of acute versus 12% of chronic lesions, yielding a modest odds ratio of 4.15 (95% CI 1.89–9.95, p < 0.001) in multivariable analysis. This disparity suggests that demographic differences, underlying tendon degeneration, and variability in injury mechanisms likely attenuate the specificity of such reactive signs in a broader clinical population. Similarly, Ilyas et al. [15] reported strong correlations between symptom duration and both tendon retraction and muscle atrophy grades in young patients with isolated full-thickness supraspinatus tears. In our more heterogeneous cohort, however, neither Patte retraction stage nor muscle atrophy achieved significance in the regression model, and features of chronicity such as fatty infiltration showed a limited predictive value (all p > 0.2). This divergence may stem from the more uniform injury patterns and age range in Ilyas et al., compared with the broad age distribution and mixed degenerative background in our series. Collectively, these comparisons underscore that MRI features associated with RCT are influenced by type of the lesions and chronic degenerative processes common in older adults, which diminish their utility as stand-alone temporal biomarkers and likely contributes to the modest discriminative performance of our model.

Some limitations of this study must be pointed out. Its retrospective, single-center design and the inherent heterogeneity of MRI scanners and acquisition protocols may have influenced feature assessment, although this variability mirrors real-world practice. The categorization of injury timing relied on reported trauma dates, which may be subject to recall bias. Additionally, clinical variables were intentionally excluded to isolate the contribution of imaging features, but future integrative models combining MRI, clinical examination, and functional data may achieve improved performance. Then, patients were not stratified by age, which may have limited the discriminative value of some parameters that could be more informative within specific age groups [16]. Furthermore, when multiple RCT coexisted, with both recent and chronic MRI features, only the “recent” findings were considered in our analysis. This approach was necessary because, in rotator cuff pathology, multiple tendon lesions with different temporal characteristics may be present simultaneously, also in patients without history of other traumas. Indeed, RCT may be degenerative, associated with age and working/sports activities, occurring also without and trauma [17]. Last, a nonnegligible limit is the high frequency of pre-existing, degenerative, asymptomatic RCT, which acts as a confounding variable. Indeed, chronic atraumatic tears often remain asymptomatic until imaging is performed due to trauma. Hence, there is a significant risk that chronic RCTs are misclassified as acute or subacute based patient’s clinical history. This may limit the model’s predictive accuracy, making it challenging to understand the radiological timeline of RCT and to differentiate recent traumatic lesion from previous chronic tears.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that, although certain MRI findings are associated with the recent occurrence of RCT, an MRI-based logistic regression model provides only limited accuracy for injury timing estimation. These results highlight the intrinsic complexity of RCT pathophysiology and the substantial overlap between traumatic and degenerative imaging features. MRI should therefore be regarded as a supportive rather than definitive tool for temporal assessment of RCT, and its findings should be integrated within a comprehensive clinical and medico-legal framework. Future research should focus on multimodal approaches and prospective validation to better address this unmet clinical and forensic need.
