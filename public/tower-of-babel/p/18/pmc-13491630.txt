Assessing and quantifying the saturation effect of forest aboveground biomass mapping using Landsat 8, Sentinel 1/2
Authors: Zhaohua Liu, Yiru Wang, Sijia Li, Guangyi Mu, Kaishan Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The application of remote sensing to forest aboveground biomass (AGB) mapping has received increasing attention, and substantial progress has been achieved. Nevertheless, saturation of remotely sensed signals remains a major challenge, often leading to the underestimation of AGB in high-density forests. In structurally complex forests, saturation in forest AGB mapping is influenced by multiple factors, including data sources, tree species, and modeling approaches. Although numerous methods have been proposed to mitigate saturation, a standardized metric for quantifying saturation and evaluating the effectiveness of these methods is still lacking. In this study, by separating saturation effects from mapping errors, SAT is proposed as a quantitative metric for assessing saturation in forest AGB mapping. The saturation levels of widely used and freely available Landsat 8 and Sentinel-1/2 remote sensing data were evaluated for forests dominated by ten common tree species in southern China. In addition, a quantitative analysis was conducted to examine the effects of multi-source data integration and nine different machine learning models on saturation levels. The results showed that tree species had a pronounced influence on estimation performance and saturation levels, with SAT values ranging from 48.15 Mg/ha to 147.93 Mg/ha. Integrated decision tree–based ensemble models produced more stable estimation results and higher saturation levels than single models. Moreover, texture features derived from optical images and Sentinel-1 data improved both the accuracy and saturation level of forest AGB estimation, with Sentinel-1 features showing a stronger contribution than texture features alone. Overall, the proposed SAT goes beyond conventional accuracy assessment by explicitly quantifying saturation-induced underestimation in high-biomass forests. This provides practical support for comparing sensor combinations and modeling strategies, and improves the reliability and interpretability of remote-sensing-based forest AGB products in dense forests.

Introduction

Forests, as the largest terrestrial ecosystems, play a pivotal role in mitigating climate change through carbon sequestration and in providing and sustaining essential ecosystem services [17, 33, 44, 50]. As a key component of these services, forest aboveground biomass (AGB) has been recognized as an essential climate variable (ECV) by the Global Climate Observing System (GCOS) since 2003, substantially enhancing our understanding of, and capacity to monitor, the Earth’s climate system [6, 7]. Accurate and timely mapping of forest AGB is therefore critical for quantifying carbon stock dynamics, evaluating ecosystem productivity, and supporting global initiatives related to climate change mitigation and sustainable forest management [5, 24, 33, 39, 59, 73]. Remote sensing has emerged as a transformative approach by enabling consistent measurements across broad spatial and temporal scales, offering a timely and cost-effective alternative to traditional forest inventory surveys [10, 14, 21, 38, 47].

Previous studies have demonstrated that vegetation indices derived from optical data and backscatter intensities derived from radar data are strongly correlated with forest structural parameters, making them effective for large-scale forest AGB mapping [25, 28–30, 52, 56]. Despite substantial progress in remote sensing–based forest AGB mapping, significant challenges remain, particularly the saturation of remotely sensed signals in regions with high AGB [19, 38, 48, 70]. Saturation refers to the reduced sensitivity of remotely sensed variables to increases in forest AGB under dense canopy conditions, which often results in a systematic underestimation of forest AGB [19, 38]. As forest canopies become increasingly dense, their structural complexity and thickness substantially limit the penetration of optical signals [2, 19, 38]. Although radar signals, such as backscatter intensity, are capable of penetrating vegetation, their sensitivity also declines at high biomass levels due to enhanced signal scattering and energy saturation [4, 23, 36].Consequently, saturation severely constrains the accuracy and reliability of forest AGB mapping, particularly in tropical rainforests and other densely forested regions, where biomass levels often exceed the effective detection range of conventional remote sensing signals.

Saturation in forest AGB mapping using remotely sensed data is governed by multiple interconnected factors. Among them, limitations in the spectral, spatial, and radiometric resolution of optical remote sensing data play a critical role. Spectral resolution determines the number and range of electromagnetic bands captured by a sensor [43, 68], and insufficient spectral resolution can hinder the detection of complex reflectance characteristics within forest canopies. Spatial resolution, defined by pixel size, may obscure fine-scale forest heterogeneity when it is too coarse, thereby reducing sensitivity to local variations in AGB [32, 34, 45, 58]. Radiometric resolution reflects a sensors ability to detect subtle variations in reflectance, and inadequate radiometric resolution can restrict the dynamic range of reflectance signals in high-AGB areas, exacerbating saturation effects. For radar remote sensing, signal wavelength strongly influences the interaction between radar waves and vegetation. Different wavelengths exhibit varying penetration capabilities and sensitivities to vegetation structure. In general, longer SAR wavelengths, such as the L-band (approximately 15 cm), penetrate deeper into forest canopies and provide information on sub-canopy structure, but they are also more susceptible to topographic effects [8, 61]. In contrast, shorter wavelengths, such as the C-band (approximately 5 cm, are more sensitive to canopy surface characteristics but have limited penetration depth, making them less effective in dense forest conditions [62, 65].

Tree species and forest AGB models also play critical roles in determining saturation levels. Differences in biological characteristics and spectral responses among tree species directly affect the degree of saturation in remote sensing–based AGB estimation, particularly in high-biomass regions [19, 70]. Coniferous forests typically exhibit more homogeneous canopy structures, whereas broadleaf forests often display greater structural complexity and higher vertical heterogeneity. The selection and combination of variables determine the input feature space of a model and, consequently, influence the accuracy of forest AGB estimation and overall model performance [25, 66]. Saturation is also strongly dependent on the modeling approach employed. Simple regression models often struggle to capture complex nonlinear relationships, which can exacerbate saturation effects in high-AGB conditions. In contrast, advanced machine learning and deep learning approaches are better suited to handling such complexity by learning hierarchical representations and capturing subtle variations in remotely sensed signals within dense forest canopies [28–30, 38]. Overall, the appropriate selection of tree species–specific variables and modeling approaches is essential for improving estimation accuracy and mitigating saturation effects in forest AGB mapping.

Various approaches have been explored to mitigate saturation effects in forest AGB mapping, among which the fusion of multi-source remote sensing data is one of the most widely adopted strategies. Optical remote sensing provides detailed information on vegetation spectral characteristics, while synthetic aperture radar (SAR) data can penetrate clouds and shadows, and LiDAR data offer accurate measurements of canopy structure [9, 12, 22, 40, 42, 57]. The complementary nature of these data sources helps compensate for the limitations of saturation inherent in any single dataset, thereby improving estimation accuracy, particularly in high-AGB regions [48, 49]. In high-density and structurally complex forests, high-resolution texture features can capture spatial patterns such as tree distribution, canopy roughness, and branch and leaf arrangements, thereby enhancing sensitivity to forest structural variability and improving the accuracy of forest AGB estimation [26, 32, 34, 38, 49, 72]. In addition, in regions with pronounced climatic variability, multi-temporal remote sensing data can reflect different vegetation growth stages. Analyzing such data enables the capture of seasonal and phenological effects on AGB estimation, thereby alleviating the limitations associated with single-temporal observations [35, 73].

Saturation of remotely sensed signals in dense forests represents an inherent challenge in forest AGB mapping. Assessing saturation is therefore essential for ensuring the accuracy and reliability of forest AGB estimates derived from remote sensing data. Although several studies have attempted to evaluate saturation effects, most have focused on relationships between individual variables and forest structural parameters [19, 36, 70, 71]. However, forest AGB mapping in complex ecosystems typically relies on multivariate modeling approaches. Assessing the saturation level of such models based solely on the behavior of a single variable is highly challenging and often inadequate. While the slope of the fitted relationship between predicted and measured AGB values can provide a qualitative indication of saturation, it does not allow for the explicit identification of a well-defined saturation threshold. Consequently, there is a clear need to develop a robust and quantitative metric that can explicitly characterize saturation effects in forest AGB mapping by integrating multiple relevant variables.

Accurately quantifying saturation in forest AGB mapping helps to reveal the performance of remotely sensed data across different biomass ranges and to identify their limitations in dense forest conditions, thereby providing a foundation for model optimization and data selection. In this context, the objectives of this study are to: (1) develop a quantitative indicator (SAT) that explicitly characterizes saturation-induced underestimation in forest AGB mapping; (2) compare the saturation behavior of Landsat 8, Sentinel-1, and Sentinel-2 data in forests dominated by ten common tree species in southern China; and (3) evaluate how tree species, variable combinations, and modeling approaches influence saturation levels and the reliability of AGB estimates.

Material and method

Study area

The ChangZhuTan Urban Agglomeration is a major urban region in central China, encompassing the cities of Changsha, Zhuzhou, and Xiangtan in Hunan Province. Located in northeastern Hunan Province, the region lies in the middle reaches of the Xiangjiang River and covers an area of more than 28,000 km2. The mean annual precipitation ranges from 1,300 to 1,600 mm, and the mean annual temperature ranges from 16 °C to 18 °C. With approximately 60% forest cover, the region supports diverse forest resources, with dominant tree species including camphor, cedar, ponytail pine, and oak. Ground data

The Forest Management Inventory (FMI) is China’s primary system for monitoring forest resource dynamics and is updated every five years. The FMI consists of irregularly distributed sample plots, each characterized by attributes including tree species, diameter at breast height (DBH), mean stand height, stand density, forest stock per unit area, as well as a range of geographic, environmental, and ecological variables. To assess and quantify saturation effects in forest AGB mapping using different remote sensing datasets, this study focused on tree species that are commonly distributed in southern China and well represented in the FMI database. Each FMI sample plot was treated as an independent observation, and forest AGB per unit area was calculated for each plot based on field-measured DBH, mean stand height, stand density, plot area, and species-specific allometric growth equations. The allometric equations used to estimate forest AGB were adopted from Li and Lei [27], who developed biomass models for forest vegetation across China.

Remote sensing data

S1 data and pre-processing

S1 is a C-band synthetic aperture radar (SAR) mission that acquires imagery in two polarization modes, namely VH and VV, and is widely used for monitoring surface deformation, wetland dynamics, deforestation, and other environmental processes [64]. The S1 data were processed to generate backscatter coefficients expressed in decibels through logarithmic scaling. Standard preprocessing steps, including thermal noise removal, radiometric calibration, and terrain correction, were applied. In this study, S1 data with a spatial resolution of 10 m covering the study area from June to September 2019 were used.

S2 data and pre-processing

S2 is equipped with a multispectral imager comprising 13 spectral bands and has a revisit cycle of approximately 12 days [15, 64]. The instrument provides data at three spatial resolutions: 10 m for the blue, green, red, and near-infrared bands,20 m for the three red-edge bands, the narrow near-infrared bands, and the two short-wave infrared bands [64]. In this study, all available S2 data with a spatial resolution of 20 m covering the period from June to September 2019 were acquired from the Google Earth Engine (GEE) platform. These data were atmospherically corrected and converted to surface reflectance products, making them suitable for direct extraction of spectral variables and texture features.

Landsat 8 OLI data and pre-processing

LC8 data, provided by NASA and the U.S. Geological Survey (USGS), have a spatial resolution of 30 m and include multispectral bands covering the visible, near-infrared, and short-wave infrared regions, making them suitable for analyses of vegetation, land cover, and urban expansion [16]. The LC8 surface reflectance products were generated using the Landsat-8 Surface Reflectance Code (LaSRC) and were accessed via the Google Earth Engine (GEE) platform. All available LC8 images acquired from June to September 2019 were used to generate cloud-free composite imagery covering the entire study area.

Extraction of remote sensing variables

In general, variables used for forest AGB modeling can be extracted from remotely sensed imagery. These variables typically include raw spectral reflectance bands, vegetation indices, and texture features. Vegetation indices, which are derived from combinations of spectral reflectance bands, provide dimensionless indicators of vegetation condition across the land surface. Texture features characterize the spatial distribution and variation patterns of pixel values within an image, thereby offering additional information on forest structure and canopy heterogeneity. Variable selection method

Selecting an appropriate combination of variables is critical for improving the accuracy and reliability of forest AGB mapping. Conventional approaches often rely on correlation coefficients between variables and forest AGB to evaluate variable sensitivity. However, in complex forest ecosystems, the relationships between forest AGB and explanatory variables are frequently nonlinear. To better capture such nonlinear dependencies, the distance correlation coefficient (DC) was employed. Variables were ranked according to their DC values and sequentially incorporated into the regression model. The optimal variable set was determined as the subset of variables corresponding to the minimum model error.

For clarity, different variable combinations were defined based on data sources and feature types. Specifically, LC8_A and S2_A represent variable sets consisting of spectral bands and vegetation indices derived from LC8 and S2, respectively. LC8_B and S2_B include both spectral variables and texture features extracted from the corresponding optical data. The combinations LC8_B + S1 and S2_B + S1 further integrate polarization features from S1 data.

Model development and evaluation

Nine different models were used to improve the accuracy and reliability of the predictions. These models include: multivariate linear regression (MLR), a simple statistical model based on the linear relationship between the independent and dependent variables. Decision tree regression (DTR), which models nonlinear relationships by constructing a tree structure. K-nearest neighbor regression (KNN), which predicts based on the output values of neighboring samples, and is suitable for complex data. Support vector regression (SVR), which is able to effectively deal with both high-dimensional and nonlinear problems. Random forest (RF), which improves prediction stability and accuracy by integrating multiple decision trees. Adaptive boosting regression (ADA), a method that improves model performance by combining weak classifiers. Bagging regression (BR), which reduces variance by resampling the training of multiple models and averaging the results. Extreme gradient boosting regression (ETR), which reduces variance by randomly selecting features and samples to train decision trees and reduce overfitting. Gradient boosted regression (GBR), which improves prediction accuracy by progressively optimizing the loss function. Each model performs differently with different datasets and feature combinations, providing a multifaceted prediction tool for forest AGB estimation.

The three-fold cross-validation method was employed to generate predictions for all samples and the root mean square error (RMSE) and PC between measured AGB (AGBM) and predicted AGB (AGBP) were used to assess the accuracy of models. Higher PC values and smaller RMSE indicate more accurate predictions.

Quantification of saturation level

The error in forest AGB estimation typically consists of two components: random error (REr), which arises from data uncertainty, and underestimation caused by saturation [38]. These errors can be visualized by a scatter plot of AGBP and AGBM (Fig. 3). Line b represents the AGBP = AGBM, and line e is the fitted line between AGBM and AGBP. The area bounded by lines ac, indicates that the AGBP values are influenced only by random error (REr), with the AGBP values being symmetrically distributed around line b. The green area, between lines d and f, represents the true prediction results, where the AGBP values are affected by both random error and saturation. The blue triangular area indicates that the error of AGBP can be fully explained by the REr. Discussion

The saturation process and factors influencing of forest AGB mapping

In regions with high AGB in forests, the relationship between remotely sensed signals and AGB shifts from linear to nonlinear, resulting in data saturation [19, 38, 70]. Optical remote sensing signals interact with the forest canopy mainly through reflection and absorption, and they are susceptible to signal saturation in high biomass forests due to limitations in canopy density and structure [35, 37, 48, 70]. In contrast, radar bands depend primarily on the scattering characteristics between the canopy and the ground, resulting in higher saturation levels for polarization features derived from SAR compared to those from optical features [36, 41, 54]. This study confirms these findings, showing that the SAT from S1 was higher than that from the two optical datasets by 2.4 Mg/ha to 46.65 Mg/ha (Fig. 12). The higher saturation observed for C-band SAR from S1 is primarily related to its penetration capability in dense forest canopies. Previous studies have shown that longer-wavelength SAR data, such as L- and P-band, provide stronger canopy penetration and are therefore less prone to saturation at high biomass levels. Incorporating L- or P-band SAR observations may further improve sensitivity to high AGB and reduce saturation effects in future studies [36, 41]. Furthermore, it was observed that the SAT from S2 was slightly higher than that from LC8, which can be attributed to the contribution of the red edge band in S2 [1, 11, 13, 63].

The complexity and heterogeneity of forest structure represent another critical factor contributing to the saturation of remotely sensed signals. Forest AGB encompasses the cumulative AGB of all individual trees and is influenced by both the AGB of individual trees and forest density. In structurally complex forests, the variability in the number, size, canopy architecture, and spatial distribution of trees results in significant spatial variability in the response of remotely sensed signals [32, 34, 58]. This structural diversity complicates the relationship between remotely sensed data and forest AGBs, thus exacerbating the saturation of remotely sensed signals. Additionally, variations in tree species further influence the response of remotely sensed signals. As shown in Figs. 10 and 11, the difference in SAT among forests with varying tree species can exceed 30 Mg/ha when using the same set of variables and models for AGB estimation. Similar findings have been reported in studies by Gao and Zhao et al. [19, 70].

The model selection and training process directly determines the ability to mine the relationship between remotely sensed variables and forest AGB [28–30, 36]. Linear regression models may perform well in low-AGB areas, but in high-density forests, these methods lose predictive accuracy due to saturation of remotely sensed signals. In contrast, machine learning and integrated learning algorithms are able to capture the complex relationships between remotely sensed signals and forest AGB through sophisticated nonlinear mapping and automatic learning of features [28–30]. As in Fig. 14, the SAT from integrated decision tree-based learning models is always higher than that coming with MLR, and also these models are accompanied by more stable and more accurate prediction results. In addition, we also find that the SAT from DTR is always much higher than that from the rest of the models. This is because SAT relies on the lower REr of AGBM and AGBP, and SAT from DTR is unreliable due to the localized prediction mechanism and relatively simple structure of DTR, which results in excessively high REr covering the saturation error.

Application and limitations of SAT to quantify saturation levels of forest AGB

Accurate quantification of saturation levels can increase confidence in using remote sensing to map forest AGBs. In prior research, a spherical model was utilized to establish the relationship between the red spectral band and forest AGB. The extremities of this model were employed to determine the saturation threshold [70, 71]. However, the model of forest AGB incorporates multiple variable combinations, making it challenging to accurately represent the saturation of these combined variables using the saturation of a single variable. While the slopes of the fitted linear regressions for AGBP and AGBM can indicate saturation to some degree, pinpointing the exact saturation point remains challenging, particularly in regions with high AGB, where the saturation effect exhibits greater complexity.

The saturation effect observed when mapping AGB of forests using remotely sensed data primarily manifests as an underestimation of high AGB values. This phenomenon has been consistently documented in the results of prior studies on forest AGB mapping [28–30, 35, 38, 48]. This study quantified the saturation effect by calculating it within the bounding ranges of random and saturation errors, based on the error patterns observed in forest AGB mapping, to accurately measure the degree of saturation when combining multiple variables. By explicitly identifying saturation points, SAT provides a clear correction framework for remotely sensed data that accurately reflects the degree of saturation in areas of high AGB, thus avoiding the ambiguity of relying solely on slope assessment. As shown in Fig. 9, SAT can accurately indicate that the underestimation caused by saturation exceeds the range of random error, and the effect of remote sensing data saturation on forest AGB mapping can be more clearly observed. SAT can also be used as an evaluation index to give the confidence interval of AGBP, which provides an important basis for analyzing the results of forest AGB mapping.

In the absence of independent LiDAR-derived biomass data or additional field-based structural measurements, the robustness of SAT was evaluated through internal consistency. Specifically, SAT exhibited a strong and stable positive relationship with K across different tree species, variable combinations, and modeling approaches. This consistent relationship supports the ability of SAT to capture saturation behavior in multivariate forest AGB mapping. The agreement between SAT and slope-based indicators across diverse conditions suggests that SAT provides a coherent and internally consistent measure of saturation.

More broadly, the significance of SAT lies in its ability to diagnose where remote-sensing-based AGB estimation becomes unreliable because of saturation, especially in dense forests. This is important because underestimation in high-biomass regions is not merely a modeling issue, but a source of bias in the interpretation of biomass patterns and forest carbon storage. By enabling explicit comparison of saturation levels across sensors, feature combinations, and models, SAT provides a practical framework for improving model selection and for interpreting AGB products with greater confidence.

Potential ways to alleviate saturation

Mitigating data saturation is the key to improving the accuracy of forest AGB remote sensing mapping and has been a hot topic in the field of quantitative remote sensing of vegetation. Since data saturation can be caused by different factors, such as the remote sensing data, vegetation, and topography, many potential solutions can be used to minimize data saturation problems. Compared to optical data, the C-band of SAR captures the roughness of the canopy, while the P and L bands have greater penetration, and mapping forest AGB using polarization features from SAR tends to result in higher saturation levels [3, 36, 60, 69]. Therefore, the integration of optical and SAR data may be an effective means to enhance saturation levels [18, 31, 46, 53]. Similarly, in our study, after integrating variables from optical data with polarization features from SAR, the model accuracy and saturation level were significantly improved.

Texture features, by analyzing the spatial relationships between image elements, can provide detailed information on canopy structure, tree distribution and growth patterns that traditional spectral information fails to capture. Numerous studies have demonstrated the importance of incorporating texture features from remotely sensed images into forest AGB models [12, 32, 34, 51, 55, 67]. The effectiveness of texture features frequently hinges on the spatial resolution of the remotely sensed data and the dimensions of the window utilized for texture extraction. The spatial structural relationships among pixels may vary considerably due to the distinct canopy structure information conveyed by individual pixels in images with varying ground resolutions. For low-resolution data, texture features predominantly capture heterogeneity at the community level, while high-resolution data emphasize more detailed characteristics at the individual tree level [32, 34]. Therefore, the selection of appropriate spatial resolution and texture extraction parameters, combined with the actual characteristics of the region, is crucial to give full play to the role of texture features in mitigating saturation.

Vegetation types usually have their own unique climatic characteristics, including seasonal leaf area index and canopy structure changes. Multi-temporal remote sensing data can reveal dynamic changes that cannot be captured by data at a single point in time [35, 73]. This information is particularly important for distinguishing between different vegetation types, estimating AGB, and improving signal capture in areas of high AGB [20, 52]. Moreover, the progressive transition of forest floor spectra from an unsaturated to a saturated state can be more intuitively observed through the use of multi-temporal data. During the initial phase of vegetation development, the canopy exhibits a sparse structure, leading to the spectral signal more accurately reflecting the actual conditions. As vegetation progresses and canopy density escalates, the remotely sensed signal progressively approaches saturation. It enables a more precise quantification of the saturation point’s occurrence mechanism through the observation of the gradual process.

Conclusion

In this study, we used SAT to quantify the effects of ten tree species, three remote sensing data sources, two different variable selection methods, and nine models on the accuracy of forest AGB estimates and saturation levels. The results show that differences in tree species lead to differences in estimation results and saturation levels, and the ten tree species can be categorized into two echelons, with P.E, S.S, S.T, T.T, and C.L having similar and higher saturation levels than C.O, P.M, L.F, B.P, and Q.G. The saturation levels of the forest AGB estimation were significantly affected by the models, and compared to a single model, the integrated decision tree-based learning algorithm yields the most stable estimation results along with higher saturation levels. Texture features and S1 of optical images have the potential to improve the accuracy and saturation level of forest AGB estimation, and S1 improves the saturation level more than texture features. Quantifying the saturation phenomenon of forest AGB is of great value, which not only helps researchers to clarify the performance of remotely sensed signals in high-AGB regions and the limitations of their estimation ability, but also provides a scientific basis for evaluating the performance of models and improving algorithms. The saturation quantification method proposed in this study provides a new perspective for assessing the results of forest AGB estimation and a more reliable analytical framework for forest carbon stock assessment and ecological monitoring, which can help to address climate change and formulate sustainable development policies.
