Myricetin suppresses whey protein allergenicity: In vitro and in vivo study
Authors: Wenqing Yang, Huaigu Yang, Lin He, Zhaowei Yang, Jinpeng Zhuang, Naijian Li, Shan Wang, Huai Huang, Jin Zhao, Jian Wang, Qiaozhi Zhang, Linglin Fu, Xin Li, Xueming Liu, Jing Li, Mo Xian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Reducing the allergenicity of milk-derived proteins is essential for the development of hypoallergenic dairy products. This study was designed to test the hypothesis that myricetin attenuates the allergenicity of whey protein by masking immunoreactive epitopes and modulating immune responsesIn this study, the effects of myricetin on whey protein allergenicity were evaluated using in vitro and in vivo models. Twenty-seven dietary polyphenols were screened for their ability to inhibit β-lactoglobulin antigenicity using an indirect enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay, and myricetin was identified as the most effective compound. Molecular docking analysis and free thiol group determination suggested that myricetin preferentially interacted with the free thiol group of cysteine 121, suggesting epitope masking as a potential mechanism.In a BALB/c mouse model of whey protein–induced allergy, dietary administration of whey protein combined with myricetin reduced serum mast cell protease-1, antigen-specific immunoglobulin G, and type 2 helper T-cell-associated cytokines, compared with whey protein alone. In addition, myricetin supplementation altered the composition of the intestinal microbiota.These findings support our hypothesis that myricetin can reduce the allergenicity of whey protein, likely through epitope masking and immune-modulatory effects involving the gut microbiota, and highlight its potential application as a natural strategy for producing dairy products with reduced allergenic potential.

Introduction

The overall prevalence of IgE-mediated food allergies is increasing and, although the proportion of children with cow's milk protein allergy (CMPA) remains relatively stable and most outgrow CMPA by school age (Flom & Sicherer, 2019; Zhao et al., 2025), nevertheless food allergies represent a growing global public health concern(Crespo & Cabanillas, 2023). Cow's milk protein allergy (CMPA) is primarily observed in infants, with a global prevalence estimated at 2.0%–7.5%, and an increasing trend has been reported in recent years. (Cela et al., 2023; Hao et al., 2025; Nivatya et al., 2025). These allergic reactions are primarily driven by abnormal Th2-type immune responses, characterized by the production of specific IgE antibodies and the activation of mast cells, which trigger a range of clinical manifestations from mild cutaneous symptoms to life-threatening anaphylaxis (Cela et al., 2023). The major allergenic proteins in cow's milk include typical casein and whey proteins, and include β-lactoglobulin (β-LG), α-lactalbumin, serum albumin (BSA), and immunoglobulins (Guo et al., 2025). Among these proteins, β-LG, which makes up roughly 50% of whey protein, is recognized as the primary allergen, often triggering severe reactions in sensitized individuals (Golkar et al., 2019; Guo et al., 2025). Interestingly, disruption of the gut microbiota associated with modern lifestyles, is closely linked to the onset and progression of food allergies (Lin et al., 2020; Xian et al., 2025). Current clinical interventions, including allergen avoidance, symptomatic management, desensitization, and targeted therapies, remain limited, as they do not provide preventative or radically curative solutions that address the underlying immune imbalance (Xian et al., 2025).

Polyphenols are a large group of plant-derived secondary metabolites widely distributed in fruits and vegetables (Taheri et al., 2020). These compounds can be classified into phenolic acids, hydroxycinnamic acid derivatives, flavonoids, stilbenes, and lignans (Dubeau et al., 2010; Wu et al., 2018). Among these, flavonoids are the most abundant in the human diet, and possess multiple bioactivities, including modulation of immune responses, antioxidant effects, and anti-inflammatory properties. These multi-target activities, together with their low toxicity, provide a mechanistic basis for their potential role in preventing and managing food allergies (Dębińska & Sozańska, 2023; Rahmani et al., 2023). In particular, flavonoids can bind to milk allergens and modify or shield their antigenic epitopes, thereby reducing protein allergenicity. This mechanism provides a promising strategy for the development of hypoallergenic dairy products (Lai et al., 2025; Lešnik et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2024).

On the other hand, flavonoids are believed to have potential anti-allergic effects, yet the mechanisms and their influence on gut microbiota are still unclear.(Liu et al., 2021). The gut microbiome, a key regulator of immune system development, has been shown to have a causal relationship with the onset of allergic diseases (Ionescu & Nagler, 2024; Yang et al., 2022a). Children with allergies have been reported to exhibit a reduction in gut microbiota that specially produce short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), and these microbial metabolites can directly regulate regulatory T cell function via G protein-coupled receptors (Tan et al., 2023).

Myricetin, a naturally occurring flavonoid found in foods such as berries, grapes, tea, and onions, has demonstrated anti-diabetic, anti-cancer, immunomodulatory, cardiovascular protective, analgesic, and anti-hypertensive effects, and may improve gut barrier function by modulating gut microbiota (Taheri et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2024). However, its ability to modulate the allergenicity of whey protein and its potential mechanisms remain unclear. In this study, we evaluated the anti-sensitization effects of a ‘Whey Protein + Myricetin’ complex using in vitro and in vivo models, and investigated its potential mechanisms involving epitope masking and modulation of the gut microbiota.

In this context, the present study explores the effects of myricetin on whey protein allergenicity using complementary in vitro and in vivo models, with particular attention to protein–polyphenol interactions, allergic immune responses, and gut microbiota alterations. The findings aim to provide further insight into the potential role of dietary flavonoids in reducing milk protein allergenicity and supporting the development of hypoallergenic dairy products.

Materials and methods

Polyphenols

In this study, a total of 27 polyphenols were employed, including myricetin, epigallocatechin, catechins, taxifolin, kaempferol, rutin, isoquercitrin, epicatechin gallate, cyanidin-3-O-glucoside, cyanidin-3-O-rutinoside, luteolin, pelargonidin-3-O-glucoside, epicatechin, quercetin, gallic acid, chlorogenic acid, vanillic acid, ferulic acid, cryptochlorogenic acid, syringic acid, ellagic acid, caffeic acid, p-coumaric acid, protocatechuic acid, procyanidin B2, procyanidin B1, and corilagin. All polyphenols were provided by the Sericultural & Agri-Food Research Institute, Guangdong Academy of Agricultural Sciences. These polyphenols were selected to represent major structural subclasses of dietary polyphenols (including flavonoids, phenolic acids, and tannins) and to capture structural diversity, forming an exploratory screening panel rather than a comprehensive polyphenol library.

Preparation of stock solutions and WP complexes

Whey protein (WP) powder (Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology, Shanghai, China) was dissolved in 1500 mL of ultrapure water and stirred at room temperature for 2 h. The solution was then stored at 4 °C overnight to ensure complete dissolution. All subsequent calculations were based on the molecular weight of β-lactoglobulin (18.4 kDa). Myricetin stock solutions were prepared by dissolving myricetin (Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology, Shanghai, China) in 55 mL of methanol. The polyphenol stock solutions were stored in the dark at −20 °C.

For complex preparation, the WP or β-LG solution was mixed dropwise with myricetin or other polyphenol solutions and incubated at room temperature for 2 h to achieve a 2:1 M ratio of polyphenol to protein. The molar ratios between β-LG and the polyphenols were determined according to the added mass and molecular weights of the reactants. These ratios reflect the feeding molar proportions applied during complex preparation and do not represent experimentally validated binding stoichiometry. After incubation, the mixtures were subjected to nitrogen blowing for 2 h to remove the bulk of the methanol. The samples were then frozen at −80 °C and subsequently freeze-dried to remove any residual methanol and water, resulting in a solid powder. The powder was stored in a desiccator until use in downstream experiments.

Assessment of IgE binding inhibition percentage by polyphenols

The IgE-binding capacity of polyphenol–β-lactoglobulin (β-LG) complexes was evaluated by indirect ELISA, based on previously reported methods with minor modifications (Lin et al., 2022; Tankaew et al., 2018). β-LG–polyphenol complexes were used as coating antigens (4 °C, overnight), followed by incubation with human serum samples (primary antibody, Plasma Lab International, Everett, WA, USA)) and ensuing HRP-conjugated anti-IgE antibody (secondary antibody). Color development was performed using TMB substrate, and absorbance was measured using a microplate reader (Synergy H1, BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA). The IgE-binding inhibition percentage was calculated relative to native β-LG. The absorbance of the blue reaction mixture was measured directly at 370 nm using a microplate reader without the addition of stop solution. The IgE-binding inhibition percentage was calculated as follows: The IgE-binding inhibition (%) = (1 - OD370 nm of complex / OD370 nm of pure protein) × 100%.

Molecular weight determination of complexes

SDS-PAGE (containing no reducing agent) was used to assess the molecular weight of the prepared complexes as previously described (Pi et al., 2023). The complexes were prepared by mixing β-lactoglobulin (β-LG) with polyphenol at a 1:2 protein-to-polyphenol molar ratio, as described above. Briefly, a 12% separating gel and 5% stacking gel were used for vertical electrophoresis. Samples were mixed with sample buffer at a 4:1 ratio and heated in boiling water for 5 min, with 8 μL of each sample loaded onto the gel. The voltage was set to 80 V and maintained until the samples entered the lower part of the gel, after which the voltage was increased to 110 V. Electrophoresis was stopped when the blue dye reached approximately 0.5 cm from the bottom of the gel. The gel was carefully removed, stained in G-250 for 2 h, and then destained. Gels were imaged using a gel imaging system (GelDoc Go, Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA).

Fluorescence spectroscopy

Intrinsic fluorescence spectra of protein samples (0.5 mg/mL) were recorded using a Fluorolog-3 spectrofluorometer (HORIBA Scientific, Edison, NJ, USA). Samples were excited at 290 nm, and emission spectra were collected from 300 to 400 nm. Both excitation and emission slit widths were set at 5.0 nm. Each spectrum represents the average of three scans.

Mice

BALB/c mice (3–4 weeks old, female) were purchased from Guangdong Yaokang Biological Co. (China). The mice were housed in the Laboratory Animal Center of Guangzhou Medical University (Guangzhou, China) under SPF conditions (24–26 °C, 50–60% humidity, and weak light exposure). All experiments were approved by the Guangzhou Medical University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and were conducted in accordance with institutional and national guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals.

Food allergen sensitization and challenge in mice

Based on previously published models of milk allergen sensitization in mice(Feehley et al., 2019; Schouten et al., 2009; Yang et al., 2022b; Zhang, Zhang, et al., 2022), 3–4-week-old specific pathogen-free (SPF) BALB/c mice were acclimated for one week prior to experimentation. Mice were randomly assigned to one of three experimental groups using a computer-based random number generator to ensure unbiased allocation: Control group (Control), Whey protein group (WP), and Whey protein + myricetin group (WP + M). Allocation concealment was maintained by using coded cages, ensuring researchers were blinded to group identity during the initial assignment. The initial group size was n = 6 for all experimental groups, as previously reported in murine models(Schouten et al., 2009; Vonk et al., 2017; Yang et al., 2022b). One mouse in the Control group was excluded due to an incidental death unrelated to the allergic challenge. The final number of biological replicates (n) analyzed for each outcome is specified in the respective figure legends. Variations in sample size occurred primarily due to: (i) insufficient serum volume for duplicate ELISA measurements following serial blood sampling (survival and terminal), and (ii) exclusion of intestinal RNA or fecal DNA samples that failed to meet stringent purity and integrity thresholds A260/A280 ratio required for qPCR or 16S rRNA sequencing. Cage positions were randomly assigned and periodically rotated throughout the experimental period to minimize potential environmental bias. The experimental unit was one individual mouse. No a priori power calculation was performed, as the study was designed as an exploratory analysis.

All oral gavage procedures were performed by trained personnel using appropriately sized gavage needles, and mice were habituated to handling prior to the first gavage to minimize stress and discomfort. The Control group was sensitized and challenged with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; Acmec, Shanghai, China). The WP group was sensitized with β-lactoglobulin and challenged with whey protein, whereas the WP + M group was sensitized with β-lactoglobulin and challenged with the whey protein + myricetin complex. Prior to each sensitization, mice were fasted for 4 h and then administered sodium bicarbonate (200 mM) by oral gavage (Macklin, Shanghai, China) to neutralize gastric acidity. Sensitization was initiated 30 min later by oral gavage of 0.5 mL β-lactoglobulin (20 mg/mL, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) combined with cholera toxin (20 μg per mouse) as a mucosal adjuvant (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). Sensitization was performed once weekly for five consecutive weeks. The use and dosage of cholera toxin were selected based on previously established food allergy models to induce IgE-mediated immune responses. Body weight was monitored weekly as a general indicator of health status, with measurements taken the day before each procedure. It was not considered a primary efficacy endpoint.

One week after the final sensitization, mice were orally challenged with a high dose of whey protein (100 mg) or an equivalent dose of the whey protein–myricetin complex (100 mg; approximately 2:1 M ratio). The challenge was performed twice with a 30-min interval between administrations. Blood samples were collected via the retro-orbital venous sinus under brief inhalational isoflurane anesthesia to minimize pain and distress. Each mouse underwent retro-orbital blood collection only once during the study, in accordance with institutional frequency limits. The procedure was performed by trained personnel using heparinized capillary tubes, and refined techniques were applied to minimize tissue damage and ensure rapid recovery. No additional analgesia was administered due to the brief and minimally invasive nature of the procedure under anesthesia. Blood samples were collected via the retro-orbital sinus under brief isoflurane anesthesia 1 h after the final challenge for measurement of mouse mast cell protease-1 (mMCPT-1). Isoflurane was delivered via inhalation at an anaesthetic concentration sufficient to induce brief surgical anesthesia. Mice were euthanized the following day in accordance with institutional animal care guidelines. Intestinal tissues, blood, and fecal samples were collected for subsequent analyses. Fecal samples were collected for microbiome profiling as an exploratory outcome. Serum levels of allergy-related markers, including specific immunoglobulin E (sIgE) and specific immunoglobulin G (sIgG), were determined, and the mRNA expression levels of interleukin-4 (IL-4) and interleukin-5 (IL-5) in ileal tissue were quantified. Morphological changes in the ileum were evaluated by histological examination. Blinding was not performed during the administration of treatments; however, to minimize assessment bias, all histological and molecular analyses were conducted using coded samples.

The co-primary outcomes of this exploratory study were serum mMCPT-1 (1 h post-challenge) and serum sIgE, defined a priori as indicators of mast cell activation and allergic sensitization. Secondary outcomes included serum sIgG, ileal IL-4 and IL-5 expression, histological evaluation, and exploratory microbiome profiling.

Animal welfare and study limitations

Mice were maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions, and all experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. To reduce animal use, the minimum number of mice considered sufficient for exploratory analysis was employed. Throughout the study, animal welfare was closely monitored, and all efforts were made to minimize distress during sensitization, gavage, and sample collection procedures. Following allergen challenge, animals were monitored more frequently for clinical signs of distress, including reduced activity, abnormal posture, labored breathing, or impaired grooming. Predefined humane endpoints were applied. Animals that met these criteria were immediately evaluated by trained personnel and humanely euthanized without delay. At the experimental endpoint, mice were euthanized by overdose of isoflurane anesthesia. Death was confirmed by the absence of respiration, heartbeat, and corneal reflex prior to tissue collection.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) for blood biochemical indices

Mouse serum mMCPT-1 levels were measured 1 h after the second challenge using a commercial ELISA kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Mouse serum antigen-specific IgG (sIgG) and IgE (sIgE) levels were determined by indirect ELISA using whey protein (WP) as the coating antigen. Plates were then incubated with mouse serum samples (regarded as primary antibody), followed by HRP-conjugated anti-mouse IgE or IgG antibodies (secondary antibody). Color development was performed using TMB substrate. After termination by 2 M sulphuric acid, the absorbance of each well was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Synergy H1, BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA). Detailed procedures are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Histopathologic analysis

To evaluate allergic symptoms in mice, ileal tissues were collected immediately after euthanasia. The tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 4 h. After dehydration in ethanol, the samples were embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and fixed in tissue fixative for H&E staining or periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) staining.

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR analysis

Tissue samples (5–20 mg) were placed in pre-chilled grinding tubes containing extraction buffer and grinding beads. Following homogenization, the mixture was centrifuged, and the supernatant collected. Chloroform substitute was added, and after a second centrifugation, the supernatant was collected for RNA precipitation using isopropanol. The RNA pellet was washed, dissolved, and its quality verified before dilution to 200 ng/μL. cDNA synthesis was performed using a reverse transcription kit (Servicebio, Wuhan, China), and RT-qPCR was conducted with GAPDH as an internal control to determine the relative mRNA expression of target genes. Primer sequences are listed in the Supplementary Material.

Molecular docking analysis

Protein structures were downloaded from UniProt and the Protein Data Bank (http://www.rcsb.org/pdb) (PDB ID: 3NQ3). The structures of the following polyphenols were downloaded from PubChem: myricetin (M, PubChem CID: 5281672), catechin (C, PubChem CID: 9064), epigallocatechin (EGC, PubChem CID: 72277), chlorogenic acid (CA, PubChem CID: 1794427), taxifolin (T, PubChem CID: 439533), procyanidin B2 (PB2, PubChem CID: 122738), vanillic acid (VA, PubChem CID: 8468), and gallic acid (GA, PubChem CID: 370). Using PyMOL software, excess ligands and water molecules were removed from the protein structure, and hydrogens were added. All files were saved in PDB format. Subsequently, the MMFF force field was used to minimize the energy of the small molecules. Molecular docking simulations of the interactions between β-lactoglobulin (β-LG) and the polyphenols (M, C, EGC, CA, T, PB2, VA, GA) were performed using Autodock version 1.5.6 (Scripps Research, San Diego, CA, USA).

Free Thiol Group Detection Assay:

Ellman's reagent was used to measure the free thiol group content [26]. Briefly, 1 mL of the sample solution was mixed with 4 mL of Tris-Gly buffer solution (86 mM Tris, 90 mM Glycine, and 4 mM EDTA dissolved in ultrapure water, adjusted to pH 8.0). Then, 5 mL of each sample was mixed with 40 μL of 4 mg/mL Ellman's reagent at 25 °C for 15 min to prepare the final solution. The absorbance was measured at 412 nm (A412) a UV–visible spectrophotometer. Tris-Gly buffer containing Ellman's reagent and a separate polyphenol solution was used as a blank control. The free thiol group content was calculated using the following formula:SHumol/g=73.53×A412×DC

C is the sample concentration (mg/mL), D is the dilution factor.

16S rRNA Gene Amplification and Sequencing:

Genomic DNA was extracted from mouse fecal samples, and the V3-V4 regions of the 16S rDNA were amplified using barcoded primers: 341F (CCTACGGGNGGCWGCAG) and 806R (GGACTACHVGGGTATCTAAT). Purified amplicons were ligated with sequencing adapters to construct libraries for Illumina sequencing. A 2% agarose gel was used to assess the quality of the amplicons, followed by PCR product purification using AMPure XP Beads (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA) and quantification with Qubit 3.0. Sequencing libraries were prepared using the Illumina DNA Prep Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) and quality checked on the ABI StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System (Life Technologies, Foster City, CA, USA). Qualified libraries were pooled and sequenced on the Novaseq 6000 platform using the paired-end 250 bp (PE250) mode.

After obtaining raw reads, DADA2 (version 1.14.1) was used for quality filtering, denoising, and error correction, generating non-redundant reads with corresponding abundance information. Forward and reverse reads were merged, and chimeric sequences were identified and removed using the DADA2 algorithm. The resulting amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) and their abundances were used for downstream analysis. To account for differences in sequencing depth among samples, the ASV table was rarefied to the minimum library size prior to alpha and beta diversity analyses. For taxonomic composition and differential abundance analyses, Total Sum Scaling (TSS) normalization was applied to convert counts into relative abundances.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3.3–4.5.1. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) of independent replicates. For in vitro assays, each sample was measured in at least two technical replicates, and the mean of technical replicates was used for statistical analysis. For in vivo assays, each mouse was treated as a biological replicate.

The co-primary outcomes of the study were defined a priori as serum sIgE and mMCPT-1 levels, based on their established roles as key indicators of allergic sensitization and mast cell activation in food allergy models. All other immunological and microbiota-related endpoints were considered secondary or exploratory outcomes. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and homogeneity of variances using the Brown–Forsythe test. For three-group comparisons, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post hoc test was used when assumptions were satisfied; otherwise, the Kruskal–Wallis test was applied. For immunological endpoints, statistical inference was based on the omnibus three-group test (one p value per endpoint). To control multiplicity within the immunology domain, Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction was applied to these omnibus p values. Post hoc pairwise comparisons (Tukey or Dunn) are shown to illustrate the direction of differences and are interpreted descriptively.

For RT-qPCR analysis, statistical comparisons were performed using ΔCt values to preserve normality and variance structure. For visualization, relative expression levels were calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method and are presented in the figures.

Following methods from the literature (Yang et al., 2022a), raw data obtained from sequencing were processed for assembly and filtering. Relative abundances were used to address data compositionality, with p-values adjusted by the Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) method. ASV clustering and taxonomic classification were performed using effective data (Clean19 Data), including chimeric tag detection using the UCHIME algorithm. The resulting effective tags were used to compute ASV abundance and other subsequent analyses. The ASV sequences were aligned against the SILVA database (v138.2), Greengenes2 (v2022.10), or UNITE (v10.0), and species classification was performed using the RDP annotation software (v2.2). Krona (v2.6) was used to display the abundance statistics of each taxonomic classification. A species abundance stacked plot was generated using the R package ggplot2 (v3.5.2). Venn diagrams were created using the R package VennDiagram (v1.7.3) to show the shared and unique species between all groups.

Machine learning–based approaches, including LEfSe (v1.0) and random forest analysis (randomForest v4.7–1.2), were applied solely for feature identification and hypothesis generation rather than confirmatory statistical inference. pROC (v1.19.0.1) and labdsv (v2.1–2) were used for model evaluation and visualization. Differential bacterial comparisons were conducted with an LDA score threshold of 4. The Shannon diversity index was calculated in R based on the formula provided (https://mothur.org/wiki/calculators/). Alpha diversity differences between two or multiple groups were analyzed using the Vegan package (v2.7–1). Multivariate statistical analyses, including PCoA (principal coordinate analysis) and NMDS (non-metric multidimensional scaling), based on (un)weighted UniFrac, Jaccard, and Bray–Curtis distances, were performed using Vegan (v2.7–1), and plots were generated using ggplot2 (v3.5.2).

Results

Screening polyphenols for β-LG IgE binding inhibition and conformational changes

Both linear and conformational epitopes contribute to the antigenicity of β-lactoglobulin (β-LG), and molecular structural differences often lead to variations in protein–ligand binding interactions. Therefore, we used indirect ELISA to measure the effect of 27 polyphenols on the inhibition of β-LG IgE binding (Table 1). In total, we identified 8 polyphenols with good inhibitory effects, including myricetin (M), catechin (C), epigallocatechin (EGC), chlorogenic acid (CA), taxifolin (T), procyanidin B2 (PB2), vanillic acid (VA), and gallic acid (GA). These polyphenols were further analyzed for changes in the conformation and epitope of β-LG after binding, aiming to explore the relationship between structural changes or binding sites and antigen inhibition percentage. SDS-PAGE analysis was performed to determine the molecular weight changes of β-LG after binding with these 8 polyphenols. The main band of native β-LG was observed around 18 kDa, with no significant changes in the bands of the samples treated with each of the 8 polyphenols compared to native β-LG (Fig. 1). Molecular docking was performed for all eight selected polyphenols to predict their potential binding sites on β-LG (Fig. 2). To experimentally validate the predicted interaction for the most potent inhibitor, myricetin (M), fluorescence spectroscopy was conducted, which revealed marked quenching of the intrinsic fluorescence of WP upon myricetin binding (Fig. 3), indicating conformational changes in the protein's tertiary structure. Ultimately, this data suggests that binding of the top 8 polyphenols to β-LG did not induce protein aggregation. WP+M complex mitigates allergic responses

The anti-allergic effects of myricetin were evaluated in a β-LG-sensitized mouse model (Fig. 4). One hour after the final allergen challenge, serum mMCPT-1 levels were altered across groups (FDR q = 0.025; η2 = 0.48, 95% CI [0.03, 0.75]). Specifically, mMCPT-1 levels were elevated in the WP group compared with controls (Hedges' g = 1.49, 95% CI [−0.06, 3.03]). M binding reduces free thiols at a key epitope residue of WP

To investigate whether myricetin could affect the structural features and allergenic potential of β-lactoglobulin (β-LG), the free thiol (free-SH) content of β-LG was measured before and after polyphenol binding. This approach allows the assessment of potential interactions between myricetin and cysteine residues, which may influence protein conformation and epitope integrity. As shown in Table 3, the free thiol (free-SH) content of β-LG was 54.39 μmol/g. After binding with myricetin, the free-SH content decreased. Two possible mechanisms may explain this phenomenon: 1) polyphenols promote protein cross-linking via disulfide bond formation, leading to aggregation, or 2) myricetin directly reacts with or binds to the free-SH group of Cys121. Based on the result of SDS-PAGE (Fig. 1), where polyphenols did not induce protein aggregation, the latter mechanism was more likely. Notably, Cys121 in β-lactoglobulin (β-LG) resides within the major antigenic epitope spanning residues 102–124, which is recognized by 97% of human sera(Selo et al., 1999). Binding of myricetin at this site may disrupt the conformational integrity of the epitope through steric hindrance, preventing effective recognition by the immune system. In addition, the interaction between myricetin and the thiol group at position 121 may directly disrupt the formation of the antigenic determinant cluster, thereby reducing β-LG's sensitizing potential. These results suggest that reduced β-LG allergenicity is achieved through the binding of myricetin at Cys121, which alters key epitope structures. To explore potential interactions between host immunity and the gut microbiota, we analyzed the correlation between cytokine mRNA levels in ileal tissue and the relative abundance of specific bacterial taxa. The serum sIgE level in ileal tissue tended to correlate negatively with the relative abundance of Oscillospiraceae (Spearman's r = −0.43, p = 0.218; Fig. 7D), although this was not statistically significant. The abundance of Oscillospiraceae also showed a negative correlated with sIgG, although this was not statistically significant (Spearman's r = −0.38, p = 0.313; Fig. 7E). Akkermansiaceae and Oscillospiraceae abundances showed a moderate negative correlation approaching significance (Spearman's r= −0.48, p= 0.072; Fig. 7C), suggesting potential ecological competition between these taxa. These findings indicate a potential association, rather than a causal relationship, between mucosal Th2 immune responses and specific bacterial taxa in the ileum, which may be influenced by WP+M treatment.

These findings suggest that myricetin may modulate gut microbiota composition, which coincides with changes in immunological parameters in β-LG-sensitized mice. However, the present results support an associative relationship rather than direct causality, and further mechanistic studies will be required to clarify the functional role of microbiota alterations.

Discussion

This study suggests that plant polyphenols, especially myricetin (M), may attenuate the sensitizing potential of β-LG by potentially binding to its core IgE antigenic epitopes (102–124). Molecular docking analyses suggested that the eight polyphenols exhibiting the strongest IgE-binding inhibition could spontaneously associate with these regions, with predicted binding energies lower than −6.0 kcal/mol. Among them, myricetin was predicted to form the densest hydrogen-bond network (five hydrogen bonds), while PB2 was predicted to engage the most amino acid residues (21 residues). Notably, myricetin binding was associated with a reduction in free thiol (free-SH) content near Cys121 within epitope 102–124. This observation is consistent with the hypothesis that myricetin sterically masks critical WP epitopes and hinders IgE recognition (Cunha et al., 2020; Kontopidis et al., 2004). Cys121 is the only free thiol residue in β-LG and plays a crucial role in its redox reactivity and conformational flexibility, which has been linked to allergenic epitopes (Kontopidis et al., 2004). Therefore, myricetin-induced modification or shielding of Cys121 may alter the local microenvironment and reduce the IgE-binding potential of β-LG (Cunha et al., 2020). This putative “physical epitope masking” represents one possible mechanism by which myricetin may reduce β-LG antigenicity. In line with this interpretation, myricetin showed an in vitro IgE-binding inhibition of approximately 25%, indicating a measurable inhibitory effect in the polyphenol screening assay.

Building on its in vitro IgE-binding inhibition and epitope specificity, we further evaluated myricetin's effects in a β-LG-sensitized mouse model. The results are consistent with a potential dual mechanism for its anti-allergic activity. First, through immune modulation and epitope masking, myricetin treatment was associated with attenuated mast cell activation and decreased serum levels of mMCPT-1 (Hedges' g = −1.38, 95% CI [−2.63, −0.12]) and sIgG (Hedges' g = −1.47, 95% CI [−2.87, −0.07]) relative to the WP group, accompanied by reduced Th2 cytokine expression (IL-4: Hedges' g = 3.03, 95% CI [1.21, 4.85]; IL-5: Hedges' g = 4.90, 95% CI [2.11, 7.70]) (Table S1). These pairwise effect sizes indicate a strong reduction in mast cell– and immunoglobulin-associated markers in the WP+M group compared with WP, consistent with partial suppression of pathogenic Th2 immunity. Second, via gut microbiota modulation, myricetin treatment was associated with the enrichment of SCFA-producing families such as Oscillospiraceae and a reduction in mucin-degrading Akkermansiaceae (Fusco et al., 2023; Niu et al., 2025). Overall, the data support the possibility of a dual mechanism involving both immune modulation and microbiota-associated effects.

Regarding the immunomodulatory aspect, WP+M complexes were associated with the alleviation of allergic responses during the acute phase, as evidenced by decreased serum mMCPT-1 levels. The reduction in mMCPT-1 and sIgG levels, accompanied by the downregulation of Th2-type cytokines (IL-4/IL-5), suggests a suppression of pathogenic Th2 immunity (Wang et al., 2022). Persistent ileal tissue damage observed in histology may reflect the incomplete protection or the instability of the WP+M complex in the gut environment. Thus, WP+M complexes appear to primarily attenuate acute allergic responses through the modulation of mast cell activation and Th2-associated immunity.

Regarding the microbiota-mediated mechanism, the WP+M complex was associated with shifts in gut microbiota composition. Analysis revealed a trend toward the recovery of alpha diversity (Shannon index) and the enrichment of Oscillospiraceae, which showed a negative association with sIgE and sIgG levels. While the magnitude of alpha diversity changes was modest, the directional shift toward increased diversity and SCFA-associated taxa aligns with microbiota patterns previously associated with reduced allergic sensitization. Furthermore, the WP+M group showed a reduction in Akkermansiaceae abundance relative to the WP group. Although Akkermansiaceae is generally considered beneficial, its excessive abundance under specific conditions may be linked to mucin degradation; thus, its reduction might contribute to gut barrier preservation (Cani et al., 2022). Previous studies suggest SCFAs can promote regulatory T cell differentiation and inhibit Th2 activity, supporting the potential mechanistic link between microbiota shifts and immune regulation (Furusawa et al., 2013; Jiang et al., 2024; Lucas et al., 2018; Smith et al., 2013). However, given the short intervention duration in this study, these associations are preliminary, and further mechanistic studies are required to confirm the functional role of these microbiota alterations. Although rarefaction was applied to standardize sequencing depth for diversity analyses, this approach may reduce statistical power and does not fully account for compositional characteristics of microbiome data. Therefore, the microbiome-related findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution.

Flavonoids can specifically bind allergens by altering surface charge or hydrophilicity, which can reduce IgE binding and enhancing digestibility to further mitigate allergic risk (Geng et al., 2024; Zhang, Wen, et al., 2022). Additionally, digestion of flavonoid-allergen complexes may influence immune homeostasis by modulating gut microbiota balance (Zhou et al., 2024). In this study, myricetin was associated with Oscillospiraceae enrichment, suggesting a potential microbiota-mediated mechanism in allergy intervention. Despite incomplete recovery of sIgE levels and ileal tissue, the rapid suppression of acute allergic symptoms and modulation of microbiota highlight the preventive potential of WP+M complexes. These findings support the potential for myricetin to influence both allergen structure and host–microbiota interactions.

Taken together, these findings provide preliminary support for a tentative “dual-action” hypothesis underlying the anti-allergic effects of myricetin. The observed epitope-level inhibition, suppression of acute allergic biomarkers, and microbiota shifts in a protective direction collectively suggest a potential food-relevant relevance of myricetin as a natural allergen-modulating compound. In the acute phase, partial masking of β-lactoglobulin (β-LG) epitopes may reduce IgE recognition and subsequent mast cell activation; over longer time frames, myricetin-associated modulation of gut microbiota may contribute to restoring immune homeostasis and attenuating Th2-dominant sensitization. It should be emphasized that this dual-action hypothesis is primarily supported by functional outcomes and associative microbiota changes rather than direct mechanistic evidence. Molecular docking was employed as an exploratory, hypothesis-generating approach to suggest potential M–β-LG interactions; however, as complementary spectroscopic analyses (e.g., UV–Vis and FTIR) were not performed in the present study, confirmation of the predicted involvement of Cys121 will require further validation using approaches such as molecular dynamics simulations, site-directed mutagenesis, complementary structural analyses, and spectroscopic techniques to further assess binding interactions and potential conformational changes of the WP+M complex.

Finally, we must acknowledge several limitations of this study. First and foremost, the relatively small sample size limits the statistical power, particularly for high-dimensional microbiome data and immune parameters. In addition, microbiome analysis was based on relative abundance rather than compositional methods (e.g., ANCOM-BC, ALDEx2 or CLR-based models) and lacked FDR correction for multiple comparisons. Consequently, the statistical comparisons performed herein should be interpreted as exploratory and descriptive rather than definitive. Second, molecular docking was employed as a hypothesis-generating approach; confirmation of the predicted involvement of Cys121 will require further validation using complementary structural analyses (e.g., UV–Vis, FTIR) and site-directed mutagenesis. Third, regarding experimental design, the exclusive use of female mice limits generalizability given the sexual dimorphism of immune and microbiome responses, and despite random cage rotation, group housing introduces potential cage effects that warrant future validation using both sexes and hierarchical statistical models. Alongside these design factors, procedural stressors like cholera toxin and retro-orbital bleeding—though standardized across groups—may have influenced the physiological baseline. Moreover, the stability of the whey protein–myricetin complex under gastrointestinal conditions, its effects on protein digestibility and peptide formation, the involvement of other immune regulatory pathways (including Th1- or regulatory T cell–associated responses such as IFN-γ and IL-10), its potential impact on intestinal barrier function, including gut permeability and tight junction regulation, as well as its possible effects on sensory attributes, solubility, and techno-functional properties relevant to food applications, were not directly assessed in the present study and warrant further investigation. Despite these limitations, the proposed dual mechanism offers a plausible food-relevant framework for understanding how natural flavonoids such as myricetin may be leveraged to design protein-based food ingredients with reduced allergenic potential. Overall, the present study provides early insight rather than definitive evidence, and further mechanistic validation, safety evaluation, and digestion- or human-based studies are required to establish translational relevance.

Conclusion

This study provides preliminary evidence that the whey protein + myricetin (WP+M) complex may help attenuate the sensitizing potential of β-lactoglobulin. In vitro screening and mechanistic analyses were consistent with trends suggesting that myricetin reduced β-lactoglobulin antigenicity, partly through epitope masking, as reflected by an approximate 25% reduction in IgE-binding activity. In vivo observations further suggested that myricetin could influence allergic responses in mice, potentially through modulation of Th2-associated immunity and alterations in gut microbiota composition.

This study has several limitations. First, the predicted molecular binding sites were derived from exploratory molecular docking and therefore require further experimental validation, and the stability of the whey protein + myricetin complex under gastrointestinal digestion conditions remains to be assessed. Second, although compositional changes in the gut microbiota were observed, a causal relationship with functional outputs such as short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) production has not been established. Additionally, the relatively short (24-h) intervention period limits conclusions regarding sustained microbiota remodeling and immune adaptation. Future studies incorporating longer-term interventions, direct SCFA quantification, and mechanistic approaches such as fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) will be important to clarify these aspects.

In summary, these observations provide tentative support for a possible dual mechanism by which myricetin could mitigate food allergy responses, involving partial epitope masking and microbiota-associated immune modulation. This “epitope shielding–microbiota interaction” framework offers a conceptual basis for exploring plant polyphenol–based strategies for allergy prevention and provides preliminary insight into developing natural compounds for potential nutritional applications.
