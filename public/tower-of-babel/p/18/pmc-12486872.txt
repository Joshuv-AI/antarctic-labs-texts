Maternal Euglena gracilis supplementation alters sow and piglet gut microbiota, reduces diarrhea incidence, and enhances piglet growth
Authors: Jakavat Ruampatana, Naraporn Somboonna, Pakkawan Kamolklang, Naruemon Tunsakul, Unchean Yamsrikaew, Takele Feyera, Morakot Nuntapaitoon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGut microbiota plays a crucial role in swine health and performance, with diet as a key modulator. Euglena gracilis supplementation has shown immunomodulatory effects that benefit sows and piglets. However, its prebiotic effect on gut microbiota remains unclear. This study investigated the impact of maternal E. gracilis supplementation on gut microbiota, piglet body weight, and the incidence of diarrhea.MethodsSixty-one crossbred sows (Landrace × Yorkshire) were assigned to either a standard diet (Control; n = 30) or a standard diet supplemented with 1 g/sow/day of E. gracilis (E. gracilis; n = 31) from day 85 of gestation until day 21 of lactation. On day 109 of gestation, 30 sows (15/group) were randomly selected for performance assessment and fecal sample collection. After farrowing, one corresponding piglet per selected sow (15/group) was randomly chosen for fecal sampling. In total, 180 fecal samples were collected, 90 from sows and 90 from piglets. Sow samples (15/group/timepoint) were collected on day 109 of gestation and days 3 and 21 of lactation, while piglet samples (15/group/timepoint) were collected on days 3, 10, and 21 of age. Gut microbial composition was determined by 16S ribosomal RNA gene sequencing. Piglet body weight was measured from birth until weaning, while the incidence of diarrhea was monitored from days 1 to 21 of age.ResultsOn average, the number of piglets alive at birth and weaning was 13.9 ± 2.4 and 10.9 ± 2.2 piglets/litter, respectively. Piglets nursed by E. gracilis-supplemented sows had greater body weight at weaning (+ 0.28 kg; P = 0.100) and a lower incidence of diarrhea on days 10 (P ≤ 0.05), 11 (P ≤ 0.10), and 12 (P ≤ 0.05) of age. For alpha diversity, E. gracilis-supplemented sows exhibited a lower Chao1 index on day 21 of lactation (P ≤ 0.05), while a higher Inverse Simpson index on day 3 (P ≤ 0.05) and both Inverse Simpson and Shannon indices on day 21 (P ≤ 0.05) of lactation. In piglets, the Inverse Simpson index was lower on day 10 of age in those nursed by E. gracilis-supplemented sows (P ≤ 0.10). Gut microbial composition revealed that E. gracilis-supplemented sows exhibited a higher relative abundance of Bacteroidetes (g_Prevotella and o_Bacteroidales) and Spirochaetes (g_Treponema), while lower Firmicutes (g_Clostridium and f_Peptostreptococcaceae) than control sows, during the lactation period. Similarly, piglets nursed by E. gracilis-supplemented sows had higher Bacteroidetes (g_Bacteroides) and Proteobacteria (g_Escherichia) but lower Firmicutes (g_Lactobacillus) during the suckling period than those nursed by control sows. The non-metric multidimensional scaling analysis showed a correlation between piglet gut microbiota in those nursed by E. gracilis-supplemented sows and their body weight on days 10 (P = 0.094) and 21 (P = 0.031) of age.ConclusionMaternal E. gracilis supplementation during late gestation and lactation exerts prebiotic effects, alters both sow and piglet gut microbiota, and potentially reduces piglet diarrhea incidence and tends to increase piglet body weight.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s42523-025-00467-z.Keywords: Diarrhea, Euglena gracilis, Gut microbiota, Prebiotics, Sow

Background

Gut microbiota plays a crucial role in the health and performance of sows and piglets, shaping their well-being through interaction with diet, gut physiology, and growth stage [1, 2]. In sows, physiological stress during gestation and lactation disrupts microbial stability, leading to gut dysbiosis [3, 4], which may impact piglet gut development. The piglet gut microbiota is primarily influenced by maternal-infant transmission through the birth canal, milk, and direct contact [5]. Early-life microbial colonization plays a critical role in piglet health, as a stable gut microbiota supports optimal growth and immune system development [6]. Conversely, microbial instability has been associated with increased diarrhea incidence and impaired piglet performance [7, 8]. Therefore, maintaining gut microbial stability in both sows and piglets is essential for optimizing production efficiency and overall health outcomes.

Maternal prebiotic supplementation represents a promising strategy to support gut microbial stability and health in sows and piglets. β-glucan is a well-recognized prebiotic found naturally in cereal and non-cereal sources, with its origin and chemical structure influencing its biological activity [9, 10]. In cereal sources, β-glucans are found at 4.5–5.5% in oats and < 1% in wheat [11, 12]. In contrast, Euglena gracilis, an algae-derived source, contains 70–90% paramylon, the β-(1,3)-glucan by dry weight [13, 14]. In addition to paramylon, E. gracilis is rich in amino acids (AA), fatty acids (FA), and minerals [14, 15], contributing to its additional functional benefits. Supplementation with E. gracilis during late gestation and lactation in sows has been shown to enhance colostrum immunoglobulin G (IgG), Immunoglobulin A (IgA), AA, and FA levels, thereby supporting piglet growth [16–18]. In vitro studies further suggest that paramylon and other components of E. gracilis exert prebiotic effects by promoting the growth of beneficial bacteria [14, 19]. Additionally, algae-derived β-glucan supplementation, whether maternal or direct, has been associated with improved gut health and reduced pathogen load in weaned pigs [20, 21]. Despite these benefits, the effects of maternal E. gracilis supplementation on sow and piglet gut microbiota dynamics, piglet growth, and diarrhea incidence remain unclear. This study aimed to evaluate the impacts of E. gracilis supplementation during late gestation and lactation on the dynamics of sow and piglet gut microbiota, piglet body weight, and incidence of diarrhea during the suckling period. We hypothesized that maternal E. gracilis supplementation would alleviate stress-induced dysbiosis in the sow gut microbiota, promote gut stability in piglets, reduce diarrhea incidence, and improve piglet growth.

Materials and methods

The use of animals in this experiment was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Chulalongkorn University (Approval number: 2331062).

Experimental design and animal management

A total of 61 gestating sows (Landrace × Yorkshire) at day 85 of gestation were assigned into two groups: Control (n = 30) that received a standard diet or E. gracilis (n = 31) received the control diet supplemented with 1 g/sow/day of E. gracilis as a top dressing during the morning meal (0730 h). Supplementation was provided from day 85 of gestation until day 21 of lactation. The fixed dose of 1 g/sow/day was selected based on previous findings demonstrating that this level of E. gracilis supplementation improved sows’ reproductive and lactation performance [17, 18]. On day 109 of gestation, 30 sows (15/group) were randomly selected for performance assessment and sample collection, along with one corresponding piglet per sow after farrowing.

The details of animal management, feeding protocol, and the composition of the standard diet, as well as E. gracilis, have been published previously [17, 18]. Briefly, the gestating sows were individually housed in stalls within a gestation house equipped with an evaporative cooling system after insemination until day 108 of gestation. During this period, sows were fed a standard gestation diet twice daily at 0730 h and 1330 h, receiving an average of 3.0 kg/day. The gestation diet was formulated to meet or exceed NRC [22] recommendations, and its ingredients and nutrient levels are presented in Table 1. Euglena gracilis (Aleta™), provided by Kemin Industries (Asia) Pte Ltd, SG, was reported by the supplier to contain at least 500 g/kg of β-(1,3)-glucan. The proximate composition, determined according to AOAC [23] methods, included 3,799 kcal/kg of energy, 16.1% crude protein, 5.8% crude fat, 1.7% crude fiber, 4.4% ash, 1.3% moisture, and 70.7% nitrogen-free extract (as-fed basis). The sows were moved to the farrowing unit 7 days before the expected farrowing and housed in farrowing pens equipped with an evaporative cooling system. All sows received the standard lactation diet from day 109 of gestation until weaning, and the ingredients and nutrient levels are detailed in Table 1. Feed was provided twice daily (0730 and 1330 h), with feed allowance gradually increased by 0.5 kg/day after farrowing until it reached a maximum allowance of 6.0 kg/day on day 7 of lactation, and fed this level in the remaining days until weaning. The farrowing process was monitored, and farrowing assistance was provided when the birth interval exceeded 45 min or uterine contractions stalled. All sows received an intravenous oxytocin injection (10 IU/mL, VetOne®, MWI Veterinary Supply Co. Ltd., Boise, ID, USA) after the birth of the eighth piglet to facilitate the fetal expulsion. At the end of farrowing, all sows were administered an antibiotic (150 mg/ml, amoxicillin trihydrate, Amoxoil®, AMCOVET Co. Ltd., Bangkok, Thailand) and an antipyretic drug (500 mg/ml, Dipyrone, Novalcin™, Thainaoka Pharmaceutical, Samut Sakhon, Thailand).

Sample collection and analyses

Sow performance data, including backfat thickness (BF), number of piglets alive, and litter weight, was collected from 30 selected sows (15/group). The BF was measured on days 85 and 109 of gestation and day 21 of lactation using A-mode ultrasonography (Renco Lean-Meater®, Renco Corp., Minneapolis, MN, USA) at the last rib, approximately 65 mm from the midline. Changes in BF were calculated as the difference between the respective timepoints (days 85 to 109 of gestation and day 109 of gestation to day 21 of lactation).

The number of piglets alive and the litter weight were recorded at birth, and on days 1, 3, 10, 21, and at weaning (33.0 ± 2.9 days of age). Litter weight was measured using a digital balance (B6S Weighing Indicator, ZEPPER Instrument Co., Ltd., Nonthaburi, Thailand). The body weight of piglets was calculated by dividing the litter weight by the number of piglets alive at each point of time (at birth, on days 1, 3, 10, 21 of age, and at weaning). Diarrhea incidence in piglets was monitored from days 1 to 21 of age.

Fecal samples (2 g) were collected from sows (n = 90; 15 sows/group/timepoint) on day 109 of gestation and on days 3 and 21 of lactation. Corresponding fecal samples from their piglets (n = 90; 15 piglets/group/timepoint) were collected on days 3, 10, and 21 of age. Sow samples were collected by manual stimulation of the rectum with a latex-gloved hand and directly placed into sterile sample containers. Piglet fecal samples were obtained by rectal stimulation using a cotton swab, ensuring immediate collection into sterile containers after defecation. All samples were transferred into 5 mL centrifuge tubes using aseptic techniques and temporarily stored at 4 °C between sampling until they were transported to the laboratory and stored at -80 °C for microbiota and metabolic analyses.

Incidence of piglet diarrhea

The fecal consistency score was assessed using a four-point scale, as described by Cappai et al. [24]: 0 = solid, 1 = semi-solid, 2 = semi-liquid, and 3 = liquid. Metagenomic DNA extraction and 16s RNA gene library Preparation and miseq sequencing

The metagenomic DNA of sow and piglet fecal samples (250 mg) was extracted using DNeasy PowerSoil Pro Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions [26, 27]. The quality and quantity of the extracted DNA were assessed using 0.75% agarose gel electrophoresis and NanoDrop spectrophotometry (A260 and A260/A280 ratios), and then the DNA samples were stored at − 20 °C until further analysis. The V3 – V5 hypervariable region of the 16S ribosomal RNA (rRNA) gene was amplified with the barcode fusion primers 1369 F (forward; 5′-CGGTGAATACGTTCYCGG-3′) and 1492R (reverse; 5′- GGWTACCTTGTTACGACTT − 3′) with appended barcode and adaptor sequences [26, 28–30]. The polymerase chain reaction (PCR) conditions included an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 28 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 50 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 1 min 30 s, then followed by 72 °C for 10 min. To minimize PCR stochastic bias, a minimum of two independent PCR were performed and pooled together. The resulting ~ 123 base pairs amplicons were excised, purified by PCR Clean-Up & Gel Extraction Kit (GeneDireX, Inc., Keelung, Taiwan), and quantified with a Qubit 3.0 Fluorometer and Qubit dsDNA HS Assay kit (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA). The equal DNA concentrations, a minimum of triplicate PCR amplicons, were pooled for sequencing according to Illumina MiSeq protocols [31]. The DNA library and PhiX control were denatured by 0.2 N NaOH, mixed, and diluted to 7 pM. The prepared library was then loaded into 4 chambers of reagent cartridges, along with Read1, Read2, and index primer as sequencing primers [31]. Sequencing was performed at Omics Sciences and Bioinformatics Center, Faculty of Science, Chulalongkorn University.

Statistical and bioinformatic analyses

The BF changes and piglet body weight were analyzed using SAS (version 9.4, SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Descriptive statistics for continuous variables (parity number, number of piglets alive) were analyzed using the MEANS procedure. The effects of dietary treatment on BF changes, piglet body weight, and the incidence of diarrhea were analyzed using the general linear mixed model (PROC MIXED) of SAS, with dietary treatment (Control and E. gracilis) as a fixed effect and sow identification (ID) as a random effect.

For sow and piglet gut microbiota analyses, sequencing data were processed using Mothur’s Standard Operating Procedure for MiSeq [26, 32]. Forward and reverse reads were assembled into contigs, and sequences were screened to remove reads < 100 nucleotides, ambiguous bases, and chimeric sequences. High-quality sequences were aligned against SILVA (version 1.38.1) and Greengenes (version 13.8) databases, and operational taxonomic units (OTUs) were classified at the phylum (abbreviation p_), class (abbreviation c_), order (abbreviation o_), family (abbreviation f_), genus (abbreviation g_), and species (abbreviation s_) levels [33, 34]. Mitochondria, chloroplasts, and unclassified bacterial domains were removed, and sequences were normalized to an equal depth across groups.

Comparison of bacterial alpha diversities (species richness, Chao1; species evenness, Inverse Simpson and Shannon) between and among groups was analyzed using the PROC MIXED of SAS. The statistical model included the dietary treatment, sampling timepoints (day 109 of gestation, and days 3 and 21 of lactation for sows; days 3, 10, and 21 of age for piglets), and their interactions as fixed effects, with sow ID included as a random effect. The relative abundance, beta diversity based on Bray-Curtis dissimilarity, and non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS), together with Spearman's correlation, were determined using Mothur version 1.39.1 [26, 32, 35]. For the relative abundance of bacterial phyla or genera representing < 1% of the total bacteria were grouped in the “Others”, while bacterial phyla or genera representing more than 1% of the sow and piglet feces were plotted. The NMDS, along with the piglet body weight (at birth, days 1, 3, 10, and 21 of age, and at weaning), was performed to assess the impact of gut microbiota on piglet body weight in response to dietary treatment [32, 36]. Microbial metabolic profiles were estimated using Phylogenetic Investigation of Communities by Reconstruction of Unobserved States (PICRUST), based on the reference genome annotations in Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes pathways (KEGG) [37]. To evaluate the impacts of dietary treatment-induced gut microbiota changes on piglet body weight, correlation coefficients were analyzed across all 2nd-level KEGG pathways associated with AA, energy, carbohydrate, lipid, and other AA metabolism. The statistical comparison of these metabolic pathways between groups was analyzed using the PROC MIXED of SAS. The statistical model included the dietary treatment and sampling timepoints as fixed effects, with sow ID included as a random effect. The Benjamini-Hochberg False Discovery Rate (FDR) multiple testing correction was also performed [38]. Least-square means were obtained and compared using the Tukey-Kramer test. A P-value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses, while a P-value ≤ 0.10 indicated a tendency.

Results

Sow backfat thickness changes and piglet body weight

The average sow parity number in this study was 2.9 ± 1.6, with 13.9 ± 2.4 piglets alive at birth. At weaning, the number of piglets was 10.9 ± 2.2 piglets/litter, with a piglet body weight of 7.72 ± 0.5 kg. The effects of E. gracilis supplementation on BF changes and piglet body weight are presented in Table 2. E. gracilis supplementation had no effect on piglet body weight at birth nor on days 1, 3, 10, and 21 of age. However, piglets nursed by E. gracilis-supplemented sows tended to exhibit a higher weight at weaning than those nursed by control sows (P = 0.100).

Incidence of diarrhea in piglets

The effects of E. gracilis supplementation on the incidence of piglets’ diarrhea are presented in Fig. 1(A-B). Piglets nursed by E. gracilis-supplemented sows demonstrated a lower incidence of diarrhea on days 10 and 12 of age (P ≤ 0.05; for both) and tended to be lower on day 11 of age (P ≤ 0.10) than those nursed by control sows (Fig. 1A). However, the average incidence of diarrhea in piglets from days 1 to 21 of age did not differ among groups (P = 0.153; Fig. 1B).

Alpha diversity of gut microbiota dynamics of the sows and piglets

The sample ID at different sampling timepoints for sows and piglets are presented in Table S1A and S1B, respectively. The 16S rRNA gene sequencing generated 9,250,162 raw reads from 90 sow fecal samples and 7,673,050 raw reads from 90 piglet fecal samples, with an average of 308,339 and 255,768 reads per sample, respectively. After quality filtering, 5,995,418 reads remained for sows and 4,874,494 for piglets, with an average of 199,847 and 162,483 reads per sample for sows and piglets, respectively. The average Good’s coverage and value of the alpha diversity indices at the genus level were > 98% in sows and > 99% in piglets, as shown in Table S1C and S1D, respectively. The effects of E. gracilis supplementation on the alpha diversity of gut microbiota in the fecal samples of sows and their corresponding piglets were evaluated using Chao1, Inverse Simpson, and Shannon indices (Fig. 2A-F). The average alpha diversity indices for sows and piglets are presented in Fig. S1A and S1B, respectively. In sows, alpha diversity assessed on day 109 of gestation and on days 3 and 21 of lactation revealed distinct differences between groups. The average Chao1 index was lower (P = 0.010), while Inverse Simpson (P = 0.002) and Shannon indices (P = 0.009) were higher in E. gracilis-supplemented than control sows (Fig. S1A). On day 21 of lactation, Chao1 index was lower in E. gracilis-supplemented than in control sows (P ≤ 0.01; Fig. 2A), while no differences were observed between groups on day 109 of gestation or day 3 of lactation. In contrast, E. gracilis-supplemented sows exhibited higher Inverse Simpson index on both days 3 (P ≤ 0.05) and 21 (P ≤ 0.001) of lactation than control sows (Fig. 2B). In control sows, Inverse Simpson index declined on day 3 of lactation relative to day 109 of gestation (P ≤ 0.05), whereas in E. gracilis-supplemented sows, it increased by day 21 of lactation compared to day 109 of gestation (P ≤ 0.05; Fig. 2B). Similarly, Shannon index was higher in E. gracilis-supplemented sows than in control sows on day 21 of lactation (P ≤ 0.01; Fig. 2C). While Shannon index remained stable over time in E. gracilis-supplemented sows, a decline was observed in control sows from day 109 of gestation to both days 3 (P ≤ 0.001) and 21 (P ≤ 0.01) of lactation (Fig. 2C).

For piglets, alpha diversity was assessed using samples collected on days 3, 10, and 21 of age. The average Inverse Simpson index was higher in piglets nursed by E. gracilis-supplemented sows compared to those nursed by control sows, while no group differences were observed for Chao1 or Shannon indices (Fig. S1B). Maternal E. gracilis supplementation did not affect Chao1 index at any timepoint (Fig. 2D). However, piglets nursed by control sows exhibited an increased Chao1 index from days 3 to 10 of age (P ≤ 0.05) and from days 3 to 21 of age (P ≤ 0.05 Fig. 2D). On day 10, piglets nursed by E. gracilis-supplemented sows tended to show a higher Inverse Simpson index than those nursed by control sows (P ≤ 0.10; Fig. 2E). While no group difference was observed on days 3 and 21 of age, Inverse Simpson index increased from days 3 to 21 of age in both nursed by control sows P ≤ 0.1) and E. gracilis-supplemented sows (P ≤ 0.05; Fig. 2E). For Shannon index, piglets nursed by E. gracilis-supplemented sows showed an increase from days 3 to 10 of age (P ≤ 0.05) and from days 3 to 21 of age (P ≤ 0.01; Fig. 2F). Similarly, piglets nursed by control sows also exhibited an increase over the same period (P ≤ 0.10 and P ≤ 0.05, respectively; Fig. 2F).

Relative abundance of gut microbiota of the sows and piglets

The individual sample data on the relative abundance of sow fecal microbiota at both the phylum and genus levels are presented in Fig. S2A and S2B, respectively. On average, 6 bacterial phyla and 21 genera were identified in sow fecal samples. Regardless of the dietary treatment or sampling timepoints, Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes remained the predominant phyla in sows’ fecal samples (Fig. 3A). The relative abundance of Firmicutes decreased while Bacteroidetes increased in E. gracilis-supplemented sows, whereas the opposite trend was observed in control sows. Although gut microbiota composition showed minimal alterations on day 109 of gestation and day 3 of lactation, more pronounced alterations were observed by day 21 of lactation, with the relative abundance of Firmicutes being lower but Bacteroidetes and Spirochaetes higher in E. gracilis sows compared to control sows (Fig. 3A). In control sows, both g_Clostridium and f_Peptostreptococcaceae increased during lactation. In contrast, in E. gracilis-supplemented sows, both g_Clostridium and f_Peptostreptococcaceae gradually declined. On day 21 of lactation, E. gracilis sows had lower g_Clostridium and f_Peptostreptococcaceae but higher g_Treponema, g_Prevotella, o_Bacteroidales, and p_Bacteroidetes than control sows (Fig. 3B).

Regardless of the dietary treatment or sampling timepoints, the predominant phyla in piglets’ fecal samples were Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, and Proteobacteria (Fig. 3C). Although the piglet gut microbiota demonstrated a similar pattern between groups at all sampling points, piglets nursed by E. gracilis-supplemented sows exhibited a higher relative abundance of Bacteroidetes and Proteobacteria and lower Firmicutes than those nursed by control sows (Fig. 3C). At the genus level, g_Lactobacillus, g_Clostridium, g_Bacteroides, and g_Escherichia were identified as the predominant genera in fecal samples of the piglets (Fig. 3D). Throughout the suckling period, piglets nursed by sows supplemented with E. gracilis demonstrated a higher relative abundance of g_Bacteroides and g_Escherichia but lower in g_Lactobacillus on days 10 and 21 of age than those nursed by control sows (Fig. 3D). Interestingly, g_Oscillospira was higher on day 10 of age in piglets nursed by sows supplemented with E. gracilis.

Discussion

Gut microbiota plays a crucial role in sow health and performance and is influenced by several factors, including diet, physiological status, and age [1, 2]. Gaukroger et al. [39] reported a decline in bacterial richness on day 3 postpartum compared to both prepartum and one week postpartum. During the periparturient period, physiological stress arising from health and environmental challenges often disrupts microbial balance, leading to gut dysbiosis [3, 4]. Additionally, the routine administration of antibiotics to all sows after farrowing, as a general practice in this study, may have further contributed to reduced microbial diversity [40]. Despite these factors, no change in bacterial richness was observed between day 109 of gestation and day 3 of lactation in both groups. However, a decline in bacterial evenness was observed in control sows during this transition, while E. gracilis-supplemented sows maintained higher evenness during early and late lactation. These findings suggest that E. gracilis supplementation may support microbial stabilization by preserving bacterial evenness without altering richness. This effect is driven by paramylon, a β-(1,3)-glucan, along with AA, FA, and minerals present in E. gracilis, serving as substrates for microbial proliferation [14, 19]. Algae-derived prebiotics, such as E. gracilis and laminarin, have been shown to promote the growth of certain lactic acid bacteria [19, 41], further supporting their role in enhancing microbial evenness.

Sow gut microbiota undergoes dynamic changes during the gestation and lactation period [42]. In this study, E. gracilis supplementation influenced sow microbial composition throughout the lactation period. Although Yosi et al. [43] reported a decline in bacterial evenness, with stable richness as lactation progressed, E. gracilis-supplemented sows exhibited higher evenness and lower richness by day 21 of lactation. This modulation may reflect the prebiotic activity of E. gracilis, promoting the selective proliferation of Bacteroidetes and Spirochaetes while suppressing Firmicutes, possibly through competitive exclusion of pathogens [44] and increased microbial similarity coefficients [45]. No difference was observed between groups on day 109 of gestation, potentially due to a shorter supplementation period (~ 24 days) compared to days 3 (~ 34 days) and 21 (~ 55 days) of lactation. This is consistent with previous findings by Zhang et al. [46], who reported no alterations in sow gut microbial richness and evenness after 20 days of algae-derived prebiotic supplementation. These findings indicate the competitive exclusion properties of E. gracilis supplementation and the importance of adequate supplementation duration to effectively modulate the gut microbiota.

The major bacterial phyla in sows primarily comprised Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, and Spirochaetes [39, 42], consistent with our findings. In this study, E. gracilis-supplemented sows exhibited an increasing trend in Bacteroidetes and a corresponding decrease in Firmicutes as lactation progressed. However, these findings contrast with Zhang et al. [46], who reported a decrease in Bacteroidetes and an increase in Firmicutes in sows supplemented with laminarin, the other algae-derived β-glucan. These differences in microbial shifts may be attributed to variations in the physicochemical properties of the β-glucan sources. Euglena gracilis contains paramylon, a β-(1,3)-glucan, along with other metabolites that function as bioactive compounds [14, 19], whereas laminarin, derived from brown algae, consists of a β-(1,3)-glucan backbone and β-(1,6)-linked side chains [9, 46]. These structural variations in β-glucan likely influence microbial responses, emphasizing that while both compounds exhibit prebiotic effects, their specific responses depend on source characteristics.

On day 21 of lactation, alterations in sow gut microbiota were observed at the phylum level among groups, with similar trends at the genus level. Euglena gracilis-supplemented sows demonstrated higher abundances of Bacteroidetes (g_Prevotella, o_Bacteroidales) and Spirochaetes (g_Treponema), while lower abundances of Firmicutes (g_Clostridium and f_Peptostreptococcaceae). The higher abundance of g_Prevotella indicates enhanced fiber digestion, as Wu et al. [47] associated this genus with improved digestion of plant-based or fiber-rich diets. Similarly, higher g_Treponema and o_Bacteroidales in E. gracilis-supplemented sows suggest elevated gut microbiota activity related to improved fiber digestibility [48]. Despite these shifts toward fiber-degrading taxa, E. gracilis supplementation did not alter sow BF, consistent with the previous findings [16, 18]. Together with results from de Carvalho et al. [16] and Ruampatana et al. [17, 18], this suggests that maternal E. gracilis supplementation primarily supports piglet and lactation performance rather than improving sow body condition. The lower abundances of g_Clostridium and f_Peptostreptococcaceae in E. gracilis-supplemented sow may reflect competitive exclusion, promoting the proliferation of beneficial taxa within Bacteroidetes and Spirochaete while restricting certain taxa within Firmicutes.

Early-life gut microbiota plays a critical role in long-term piglet health and growth, with its diversity and composition being key determinants of diarrhea susceptibility [6, 8]. Microbial instability during this period has been associated with an increased risk of diarrhea [7, 8], suggesting the importance of stable gut microbiota in piglets. In this study, piglets nursed by control sows exhibited a progressive increase in bacterial richness from days 3 to 10 and days 3 to 21 of age, which may reflect microbial instability. In contrast, piglets nursed by E. gracilis-supplemented sows demonstrated an increase in bacterial evenness with age, indicating enhanced gut microbial stability. In addition, these piglets demonstrated higher evenness on day 10 of age, coinciding with a lower incidence of diarrhea compared to those nursed by control sows. These findings suggest that maternal E. gracilis supplementation supports gut stability and may increase piglet resilience against diarrhea during the suckling period.

Neonatal piglets primarily comprised Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, and Proteobacteria as core gut microbiota [2, 6], consistent with our findings. In this study, piglets nursed by E. gracilis-supplemented sows exhibit a higher abundance of Bacteroidetes and Proteobacteria and a lower abundance of Firmicutes than those nursed by control sows. At the genus levels, these shifts corresponded with phylum-level alterations, characterized by a higher abundance of g_Bacteroides (Bacteroidetes) and g_Escherichia (Proteobacteria), and a lower abundance of g_Lactobacillus (Firmicutes). The higher abundance of g_Bacteroides may indicate enhanced milk utilization by piglets, as this genus metabolizes carbohydrate substrates present in sow’s milk [49], making it dominant in the piglet gut. The concurrent higher g_Escherichia may be facilitated by g_Bacteroides through cross-feeding. As g_Escherichia, a facultative anaerobe incapable of degrading complex polysaccharides, relies on metabolic by-products from polysaccharide-degrading bacteria like g_Bacteroides [50, 51]. In contrast, the lower g_Lactobacillus may be attributed to its limited amylolytic enzyme expression [52], restricting its ability to metabolize complex nutrients. These microbial shifts reflect functional adaptations to milk-derived nutrients, shaped by E. gracilis supplementation.

During the early age, piglet gut microbiota composition is shaped by maternal microbiota, which plays a crucial role in microbial colonization and long-term health [5, 8]. In this study, piglets nursed by E. gracilis-supplemented sows exhibited gut microbiota profiles similar to their dams, characterized by a higher abundance of Bacteroidetes and a lower abundance of Firmicutes. This similarity suggests potential maternal-infant microbial transmission, which may occur prepartum via the uterus [53], intrapartum through contact with the birth canal [54], or postnatally through sow feces or the gut–breast axis [5, 55]. Although these routes were not directly assessed, sow feces were analyzed as a representation of gut microbiota. Therefore, these findings suggest that maternal E. gracilis supplementation altered the sow gut microbiota and may have influenced piglet gut microbial colonization.

On day 10 of age, piglets nursed by E. gracilis-supplemented sows exhibited a lower incidence of diarrhea despite a higher abundance of g_Escherichia and a lower abundance of g_Lactobacillus. Such microbial shifts are typically associated with gut microbiota instability and a higher risk of diarrhea in piglets [8, 56–58]. However, the higher g_Escherichia abundance observed in this study may reflect a predominance of commensal, non-pathogenic strains rather than pathogenic ones. This interpretation is supported by potential cross-feeding from g_Bacteroides, a genus capable of degrading complex polysaccharides and producing metabolites usable by g_Escherichia. These interactions could promote the selective proliferation of commensal over pathogenic strains. Certain non-pathogenic E. coli strains benefit the host by synthesizing vitamins [59], and their administration into piglets has been shown to improve feed conversion efficiency [60]. Although the association between lower g_Lactobacillus and lower diarrhea incidence remains unclear, it may reflect the limited nutrient availability for its proliferation. Furthermore, a higher abundance of g_Oscillospira was observed in piglets nursed by E. gracilis-supplemented sows on day 10 of age. As one of the butyrate-producing genera [61], g_Oscillospira may support gut development and epithelial integrity through butyrate production [62, 63]. These microbial shifts may contribute to improved gut health and lower risk of diarrhea, although further studies are required to identify the specific g_Escherichia strains involved and clarify their functional roles.

Beyond its prebiotic effects on piglet gut microbiota and the associated reduction in diarrhea incidence, maternal E. gracilis supplementation may further enhance piglet growth and health through improvements in colostrum and milk composition. Piglet growth and survivability are influenced by several factors, including colostrum intake and colostrum composition [64]. Supplementation with E. gracilis has been shown to increase concentrations of colostrum IgG, IgA, protein, casein, AA, and FA, thereby supporting immune protection and growth in piglets [16–18, 65]. Elevated colostrum IgA levels have been associated with reduced diarrhea incidence [66]. These reductions may further promote piglet growth, as diarrhea impairs growth [8, 67]. In addition to these nutrient and immunological aspects, E. gracilis supplementation may influence the milk microbiome. Colostrum and milk contain diverse microbial populations, including both beneficial and pathogenic bacteria, collectively referred to as the milk microbiome [68]. Milk microbiome plays an important role in shaping the early-life gut microbiota of piglets [69]. Although this study did not directly assess the effect of E. gracilis on the milk microbiome, previous studies have shown that maternal probiotic supplementation with lactic acid bacteria modulates the milk microbiome and reduces diarrhea incidence in piglets [55, 70, 71]. These combined effects on nutritional, immunological, and potentially milk microbial benefits regarding maternal E. gracilis supplementation may underlie the improved piglet performance observed in this study.

The NMDS analysis combined with Spearman's correlation, indicated that the gut microbiota composition of piglets nursed by E. gracilis-supplemented sows was associated with body weight on days 10 and 21 of age. During the suckling period, piglet growth depends on milk composition, which is enhanced by higher protein and fat contents [72, 73] but impaired by elevated lactose levels [74]. In parallel, gut microbial composition supports the utilization of milk-derived nutrients. Piglets nursed by E. gracilis-supplemented sows exhibited a higher abundance of g_Bacteroides, a β-glucan-metabolizing genus capable of producing butyrate [75, 76]. Butyrate, a short-chain fatty acid known to promote gut development, strengthens barrier integrity [62, 63], thereby improving nutrient absorption, particularly from milk. The KEGG-based predictions further supported this mechanism by showing elevated AA and energy metabolism in these piglets, particularly on day 10 of age. On the contrary, predicted carbohydrate metabolism was reduced, coinciding with a lower abundance of g_Lactobacillus, a key carbohydrate-fermenting genus [52]. Lactobacilli preferentially utilize mono-, di-, or oligosaccharides rather than complex polysaccharides such as β-glucan, due to their limited amylolytic capacity [52]. Only certain strains, such as L. fermentum and L. plantanum, possess extracellular amylase activity [77, 78]. These findings suggest that the reduced carbohydrate metabolism may reflect strain-specific limitations in utilizing complex substrates. These functional predictions, generated using 16S rRNA data through PICRUSt, should be considered exploratory. Therefore, further investigation through metagenomic or metabolomic approaches is required to confirm these findings and the underlying mechanisms.

Conclusions

Maternal E. gracilis supplementation during late gestation and lactation enhanced gut microbial evenness in both sows and piglets, reduced piglet diarrhea incidence, and tended to increase piglet body weight at weaning. Furthermore, piglets nursed by E. gracilis-supplemented sows showed gut microbial shifts that were positively associated with body weight on days 10 and 21, suggesting potential benefits on nutrient utilization. However, further studies are required to elucidate the underlying microbial and metabolic pathways and to assess the long-term effects on piglet health and performance.
