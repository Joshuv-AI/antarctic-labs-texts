Synergistic LiPF6 and LiBF4 dual-salt electrolyte enables high performance lithium-ion batteries at low temperature
Authors: Jingwen Cui, Yidan Zhang, Jinyu Tan, Xiaotao Wang, Shixiang Sun, Yingqun Li, Yukun Zhang, Yuan Zhao, Huiwen Ji, Yujia Wang, Wei Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This paper investigates the effect of electrolyte systems containing lithium hexafluorophosphate (LiPF6) and lithium tetrafluoroborate (LiBF4) as mixed solutes on LFP//Li batteries at low temperatures. It was found that electrolytes containing LiBF4 exhibited lower polarisation and superior electrochemical performance at low temperatures, with a capacity of 84.55 mAh g−1 at −20 °C and 0.2C (capacity retention of 95.3%). Furthermore, the LFP electrode after cycling exhibited a richer inorganic structure dominated by boron and fluorine-based inorganic compounds.

Introduction

Since the commercialization of lithium-ion batteries (LIBs), their high energy density, high power density, fast charging capability, and long cycle life have facilitated their widespread adoption in portable electronics, electric vehicles, energy storage systems, and other applications. They represent one of the most promising energy technologies today.1,2 However, LIB performance is significantly impacted under low-temperature conditions, exhibiting issues such as capacity decline, poor cycle life, and excessive energy density degradation.3,4 Studies have identified the electrolyte as the primary factor affecting LIB low-temperature performance. Under cold conditions, increased electrolyte viscosity, decreased ionic conductivity, elevated charge transfer resistance, lithium dendrite growth and sluggish desolvation processes collectively contribute to the deterioration of LIB performance at low temperatures.5–7

Electrolyte engineering is one of the most effective ways to tackle the challenges of LFP batteries. The selection of a suitable electrolyte can accelerate the desolvation of ions, minimise side reactions between the electrolyte and the electrode, facilitate the formation of a stable CEI/SEI film, and effectively improve the battery's electrochemical performance.8–12 Currently, commercially available electrolytes predominantly utilize ethylene carbonate (EC) and dimethyl carbonate (DMC) as solvents due to their high dielectric constants and favorable chemical stability.

However, the high melting point of EC (36.4 °C) leads to an increase in electrolyte viscosity at low temperatures, and the strong affinity between EC and Li+ hinders the rapid desolvation of Li+ during charging, particularly under high current density and low-temperature conditions;13 furthermore, lithium hexafluorophosphate (LiPF6) is frequently selected as the solute owing to its significant advantages in conductivity, charge/discharge rates, and solubility.14 LiPF6 readily decomposes upon contact with water to form HF, which corrodes the current collector and disrupts the structural stability of the electrode. Furthermore, due to its high ionic dissociation energy, the concentration and migration rate of Li+ decrease sharply at low temperatures, resulting in a reduction in battery capacity.15–17 Therefore, replacing strong coordinating solvents such as EC with weakly solvating molecules can lower the desolvation barrier for Li+ at the electrode surface, thereby accelerating ionic kinetics, particularly under low-temperature conditions. For example, Wu et al. employed a nitrile-based electrolyte containing N,N-dimethyltrifluoroacetamide (FDMA), which regulates lithium ion solvation through hydrogen bonding with the nitrile solvent (IBN); the hydrogen bonding's coordination ability with Li+ is far weaker than that of various carboxylates and ether solvents; the hydrogen bonds of FDMA weaken the strong Li+ coordination with IBN to optimise the solvation structure, whilst IBN's low viscosity and high dielectric constant enhance conductivity.18 Furthermore, lithium salts can precipitate at low temperatures, resulting in a shorter battery life.19 Recently, Qiu et al. added 1.0% (by volume) lithium tetrafluoroborate (LiBF4) as an additive to a conventional electrolyte, thereby improving the battery's cycling performance and low-temperature discharge performance by forming stable, low-impedance CEI and SEI films on the surfaces of the battery's cathode and anode.20 Furthermore, whilst LiBF4 exhibits low ionic conductivity and poor film-forming properties when used as a lithium salt, it has low impedance at low temperatures and offers excellent overall performance.21

Consequently, to improve the low-temperature performance of LFP//Li batteries, this study developed an electrolyte based on a mixed lithium salt system of LiPF6 and LiBF4, whilst replacing EC with the weakly solvating solvent EMC. As fewer solvent molecules surround Li+, the weak solvation effect of EMC facilitates the desolvation of ions at low temperatures (Fig. S1), which promotes the migration of Li+ in the electrolyte and overcomes the poor performance of lithium-ion batteries at low temperatures; these properties have been verified through experimental observations. LFP//Li batteries utilising this electrolyte all exhibit lower polarisation and can undergo 200 stable cycles at −20 °C, with a capacity retention rate of 94.3%; batteries based on the P7B3 system also demonstrate a discharge capacity of 51.9 mAh g−1 at a 1C. SEM and XPS analyses jointly indicate that the incorporation of LiBF4 plays a significant role in the formation of an inorganic-rich CEI layer.

Results and discussion

Solvation structure and electrochemical performance of LFP cathode

To determine whether electrolytes with different formulations meet the fundamental voltage requirements for LFP//Li batteries, linear voltammetry (LSV) tests were first conducted on electrolytes with varying ratios (Fig. 1(a)). When the external voltage rises to 4.0 V, the electrolyte system reaches its limit. The results indicate that all possessing an electrochemical stability window of at least 4.0 V, which is sufficient to support the normal charging and discharging of LFP//Li batteries.

The flowability of electrolyte at low temperatures plays a crucial role in battery performance. After standing different electrolyte compositions at −30 °C for 12 hours (Fig. S2). P1B0 completely solidified, P8B1, P7B3, P1B1 and P3B7 maintained good flowability.

To investigate the differences in solvation structures among various electrolytes, Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FT-IR) was conducted. With the addition of LiBF4, a B–F vibrational peak appears near 1050 cm−1, showing an increased peak area with increasing LiBF4 content, the strength, width, and peak area of the P–F peak at 860 cm−1 and 550 cm−1 significantly increase (Fig. 1(b)), indicating BF4− and greater PF6− participation in the formation of contact ion pairs (CIP), these changes alter the coordination state of Li+.22 Changes in the solvation structure promote enhanced Li+ transport performance, enable the formation of a dense and stable cathode electrolyte interphase (CEI), and lead to improved low-temperature performance.23,24 The Raman spectrum (Fig. S3) further illustrates this trend. In addition, it is clearly observable that the coordinated DMC/EMC exhibits a slight blue shift and a gradual decrease in intensity, indicating that the interaction between DMC/EMC and Li+ is weakened under the influence of B–F, thereby strengthening the interaction between the anion and Li+.22,24,25

An excellent low-temperature electrolyte should possess high ionic conductivity to ensure Li+ migration rates at low temperatures (Fig. 1(c)), as temperature decreases, the advantages of P8B1 and P7B3 electrolytes become increasingly prominent, at −20 °C, the conductivities of the P8B1 and P7B3 systems are 2.74 mS cm−1 and 3.75 mS cm−1, respectively, whilst that of the P1B0 system is only 1.26 mS cm−1. At −40 °C, the conductivity of the P7B3 system is approximately 0.9 mS cm−1, in contrast, the conductivity of P1B0 electrolyte systems is nearly zero. Next, viscosity tests were carried out on the electrolyte, and the other chemical properties mentioned above were summarised (Table S1). Overall, adding an appropriate proportion of LiBF4 to the electrolyte can improve its performance at low temperatures.

Further studies were conducted using Galvanostatic Intermittent Titration Technique(GITT) to investigate the electrochemical kinetic characteristics of different electrolytes during the first charge/discharge cycle at 25 °C and −20 °C, and the Li+ diffusion rate within the electrode materials (Fig. 1(d), S4 and S5). Calculations reveal that the average log D values for P1B0, P8B1, P7B3, P1B1 and P3B7 at 25 °C are −11.59, −12.86, −12.88, −12.73 and −12.51 respectively; at −20 °C, they are −17.51, −15.58, −14.44,-14.79, −16.79, respectively. The higher value indicates a greater Li+ diffusion rate and better diffusivity.26 At 25 °C, the Li+ diffusion rate in P1B0 is higher; this may be because LiBF4 has a lower dissociation constant in ester solvents, leading to a reduction in free Li+ and consequently a lower Li+ diffusion rate; Conversely, at low temperatures, the diffusion rate increases, likely because the binding energy between Li+ and BF4− is lower than that between Li+ and the solvent molecules. In other words, BF4− tends to ‘displace’ the solvent molecules, forming a special structure in which the anion participates in coordination. This structure has a smaller steric hindrance than the original solvent molecules, making desolvation of Li+ easier.27–29

To investigate the impact of different electrolytes on the electrochemical performance of LFP//Li batteries at low temperatures and to validate their practicality under such conditions. During the first cycle (Fig. S6), the discharge capacities of P1B0, P8B1, P7B3, P1B1, P3B7 are 49.5 mAh g−1, 72.2 mAh g−1, 83 mAh g−1, 53.5 mAh g−1, 61.5 mAh g−1, respectively. Compared to electrolytes without LiBF4, the addition of a small amount of LiBF4 enhances low-temperature capacity. P7B3 exhibits the highest average capacity of 82.12 mAh g−1 (the capacity retention rate remained at 95.3%) at −20 °C and also demonstrating superior cycling performance (Fig. 1(e)). This demonstrates that the addition of LiBF4 enhances the low-temperature performance of LFP//Li batteries. However, when the proportion of LiBF4 is too high, a counterproductive effect occurs (P3B7 of Fig. 1(e) and S7), when 1 M LiBF4 is used as the solute (P0B1), the battery not only exhibits a low capacity (45.3 mAh g−1) but also demonstrates particularly poor. Conversely, when BF4− is present in small amounts along with PF6−, the two anions regulate the Li+ solvation shell structure through ionic dipole interactions. This reduces the constraining effect of solvation shell on Li+, lowers desolvation impedance, and facilitates rapid Li+ migration, thereby enhancing battery capacity.30,31 To verify the practicality of the different electrolyte, long-cycle testing was conducted on LFP//graphite cells (Fig. S8). The results were consistent with those of the LFP//Li batteries (Fig. 1(e)), further demonstrating the excellent performance of the P7B3 electrolyte at low temperatures.

Fig. 1(f) shows the rate performance of the different samples, indicating good stability at low current density. However, when the current density increases to 0.5C, the P1B0 and P3B7 batteries exhibit rapid capacity decay, and they are barely able to discharge any energy at 1C. Fig. 1(g) presents the cyclic voltammetry (CV) curves of the LFP electrode in each electrolyte system. At room temperature, the CV curves across different electrolyte systems exhibit well-defined symmetry between oxidation and reduction peaks, indicating excellent reversibility in the Li+ insertion and extraction processes.

To investigate the degradation mechanism of the batteries, Electrochemical Impedance Spectroscopy (EIS) analysis was performed on cells after 5 cycles and 100 cycles. After 5 cycles (Fig. S9), the impedances of the batteries showed little difference. After 100 cycles (Fig. 3(h)) (Fig. S10 is the equivalent circuit model to fit the Nyquist plots obtained from the cell), distinct differences emerged. Notably, P1B0 exhibited the highest impedance, while P7B3 and P8B1 showed the lowest. This indicates that the introduction of a small amount of LiBF4 reduced battery impedance and minimized side reactions.

To verify the polarization levels of different batteries, voltage differences were calculated for the same cycle (20th) across different batteries (Fig. 1(i)). The potential difference for P1B0 is 0.46 V, while that for P7B3 is 0.24 V. P7B3 exhibited lower polarization and superior electrochemical kinetics. Fig. S11 shows that the voltage differences for P8B1, P1B1 and P3B7 are 0.32 V, 0.32 V and 0.31 V respectively. This indicates that the addition of LiBF4 reduces battery polarization, mitigates electrode reaction polarization and enhances the Li+ diffusion coefficient.32

Characterization of the electrodes

To investigate the elemental distribution and degree of damage in the LFP electrode after the reaction, as well as the composition of the CEI film, the battery was disassembled in a glove box after 100 cycles and subjected to energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS), scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis. The EDS results clearly show that the elements are evenly distributed across the LFP electrode following cycling (Fig. 2(a)–(c) and S12).

SEM clearly reveals changes in LFP after reaction (Fig. 2(d)–(i)). Compared to the pristine LFP with a smooth surface and no obvious deposits, the LFP particles in P1B1 and P3B7 exhibit significant structural disruption. With “porous” loose layers in some areas and disordered interparticle voids. This indicates that excessive LiBF4 content intensifies interfacial side reactions and initiates particle structure degradation. In P1B0 and P8B1, severe particle agglomeration obstructs Li+ transport pathways. Conversely, P7B3 exhibits uniform surface deposits, excellent particle dispersion, and no significant agglomeration or cracks. It is the high-quality CEI that enables excellent cathode structural integrity. Fig. S13 illustrates this property more clearly.

The B1s spectrum clearly reveals the influence of LiBF4 on the CEI structure. In the P7B3 sample (Fig. 3(f)), the B–O peak intensity reaches its maximum, while the B–F peak signal simultaneously increases. This show that at this LiBF4 decomposition ratio, the formation of abundant boron oxides/fluorides on the electrode surface is effectively promoted. These inorganic species enhance the Li+ conduction efficiency of the CEI film and facilitate the formation of a dense and stable CEI. For cathode using P8B1 as the electrolyte, the B–O and B–F peak signals are relatively weak, the intensities of the B–O and B–F peaks show no significant increase in the electrolyte of P1B1 and P3B7 (Fig. S17). The incorporation of LiBF4 optimizes the low-temperature cycling performance by inducing the formation of boron-based inorganic CEI components on the electrode surface. As summarized in Fig. 3(c), the atomic percentages and organic components in the CEI film are summarised (Fig. S18, S19 and Table S2). It is clearly evident that P7B3 contains higher levels of fluorine and boron; consequently, it can be concluded that a CEI layer richer in lithium and containing boron has formed in P7B3. This feature positively influences both Li+ transport rates and interfacial stability in LFP//Li cells under low-temperature conditions.

Conclusions

To improve the low-temperature performance of LFP//Li batteries, we designed a low-temperature electrolyte containing a mixture of lithium salts and verified that the optimal ratio is LiPF6 : LiBF4 = 7 : 3, comparing it with a LiPF6 based electrolyte without LiBF4. The electrochemical performance of the LFP//Li battery was tested at low temperatures. The results showed that the addition of an appropriate amount of LiBF4 significantly improved both the low-temperature performance and cycling performance of the LFP battery; after 200 cycles at −20 °C, the capacity retention rate remained at 95.3%. Furthermore, this addition promoted the formation of boron-based inorganic compounds on the surface of the CEI film, which enhanced the stability of the CEI film and helped to reduce side reactions.

In conclusion, incorporating LiBF4 into the electrolyte can effectively enhance the low-temperature performance of LFP batteries. Future research shall focus on developing binary or multi-component lithium salt composite systems to integrate the merits of different lithium salts and achieve complementary properties. Combining functionalized lithium salts with LiPF6 to construct a synergistic system is capable of optimizing the ionic conductivity and solvation structure of the electrolyte, remedying defects at the electrode interface, and alleviating the rapid capacity degradation of batteries induced by single lithium salt electrolytes during cycling and low-temperature operation, which further improves the low-temperature performance of batteries.

In addition, novel perfluorinated and partially fluorinated solvent systems can be developed. Owing to the strong electron-withdrawing effect of fluorine atoms, the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) energy level of solvents can be reduced, so as to improve the oxidation resistance, decomposition stability and thermal stability of electrolytes. Furthermore, a modification strategy using trace functional additives can be adopted. Film-forming, flame-retardant, low-temperature, water-scavenging and acid-scavenging additives can be selectively matched according to the characteristics of battery systems.
