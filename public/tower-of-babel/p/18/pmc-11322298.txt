Rapid determination of antibiotic susceptibility of clinical isolates of Escherichia coli by SYBR green I/Propidium iodide assay
Authors: Xianglun Cui, Shuyue Liu, Yan Jin, Mingyu Li, Chunhong Shao, Hong Yu, Ying Zhang, Yun Liu, Yong Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractInfections caused by pathogenic Escherichia coli are a serious threat to human health, while conventional antibiotic susceptibility tests (AST) have a long turn-around time, and rapid antibiotic susceptibility methods are urgently needed to save lives in the clinic, reduce antibiotic misuse and prevent emergence of antibiotic-resistant bacteria. We optimized and validated the feasibility of a novel rapid AST based on SYBR Green I and Propidium Iodide (SGPI-AST) for E. coli drug susceptibility test. A total of 112 clinical isolates of E. coli were collected and four antibiotics (ceftriaxone, cefoxitin, imipenem, meropenem) were selected for testing. Bacterial survival rate of E. coli was remarkably linearly correlated with S value at different OD600 values. After optimizing the antibiotic concentrations, the sensitivity and specificity of SGPI-AST reached 100%/100%, 97.8%/100%, 100%/100% and 98.4%/99% for ceftriaxone, cefoxitin, imipenem and meropenem, respectively, and the corresponding concordances of the SGPI-AST with conventional AST were 1.000, 0.980, 1.000 and 0.979, respectively. The SGPI-AST can rapidly and accurately determine the susceptibility of E. coli clinical isolates to multiple antibiotics in 60 min, and has the potential to be applied to guide the precise selection of antibiotics for clinical management of infections caused by pathogenic E. coli.Keywords: Rapid antibiotic susceptibility testing, SYBR Green I, Propidium iodide, Escherichia coliSubject terms: Biotechnology, Microbiology

Introduction

Although Escherichia coli (E. coli), is not formally a member of “ESKAPE” pathogens (Enterococcus faecium, Staphylococcus aureus, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Acinetobacter spp., Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Enterobacter spp.), it is an important human pathogen commonly isolated from clinical specimens, especially in urine and blood, and often associated with antimicrobial resistance (AMR)1–4. Pathogenic E. coli can cause assortments of intestinal and extraintestinal infections, such as diarrhea, hemolytic uremic syndrome, colitis bacteremia, sepsis, meningitis, and urinary tract infections, posing a serious threat to human health5,6. Recently, cephalosporins and carbapenems are the important antibiotics and last barrier against E. coli infections, however, resistant rates of E. coli to cephalosporins and carbapenems are increasing year by year7,8. Rapid identification of antibiotic susceptibility and early rational use of antibiotics are of great significance to save lives, effectively reduce antibiotic resistance and improve the prognosis of patients.

Currently, the standard antibiotic susceptibility testing (AST) methods used in clinic are broth dilution susceptibility testing or agar zone inhibition (Kirby-Bauer) tests9, however, it takes 16–24 h to obtain the inhibition zone or the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) results9. Even commercial semi-automated AST such as Vitek 2 (bioMérieux) or BD Phoenix M50 still requires 4–16 h10–14. And before accurate susceptibility results are available, broad-spectrum antibiotics are regularly administered empirically, which inevitably leads to increased resistance rates. Consequently, there is an urgent need for rapid antibiotic susceptibility identification with high sensitivity and specificity. Various rapid methods for antibiotic susceptibility measurements have been developed, based on microfluidic platform13,15,16, Raman spectroscopy-based methods17, MALDI-TOF18, and nanomotion detection19. Nevertheless, high equipment costs and limited throughput restrict the application and popularization of these methods, which remain in experimental stage.

In previous studies, we showed that the SYBR Green I/Propidium Iodide (PI) assay was able to determine bacterial viability with high accuracy20. As a commonly used nucleic acid dye, SYBR Green I dye can permeate through cell membranes and stain nucleic acid with green fluorescence in live cells, whereas membrane-impermeant PI dye can only stain nucleic acid in dead cells with red fluorescence20. On the one hand, the SYBR Green I/PI double-staining has been proven to be useful for screening novel compounds with high activity against Borrelia burgdorferi and Bartonella henselae from FDA-approved drugs20–24. On the other hand, it has been demonstrated that this method can rapidly identify antibiotic susceptibility phenotype in just 60 min for representative clinically important bacterial pathogens, including Staphylococcus aureus, Escherichia coli, Acinetobacter baumannii and Mycobacterium tuberculosis25. Subsequently, the utility of the SYBR Green I/PI assay for rapid AST was validated in two separate studies using a relatively large number of K. pneumoniae clinical isolates (close to 200 strains) for different antibiotics with high sensitivity and specificity26,27. In order to further explore its application prospects in clinic, it is necessary to verify its feasibility for antibiotic susceptibility testing in different bacterial species.

In this study, we collected 112 clinical isolates of E. coli with different antibiotic susceptibility profiles, and evaluated the SYBR Green I/PI assay to discriminate their susceptibility to four antibiotics, i.e., ceftriaxone, cefoxitin, imipenem, meropenem. The results indicated that the SYBR Green I/PI method can rapidly differentiate whether an isolate is sensitive or resistant, with high concordance with the conventional AST. These findings further demonstrate that the SYBR Green I/PI assay has good potential for use in clinical antibiotic susceptibility testing, which is of great significance for guiding antibiotic use and reducing the spread of antibiotic-resistant bacteria in the clinic.

Result

Conventional AST results

Conventional AST was conducted to determine susceptibility of 112 E. coli clinical isolates to ceftriaxone, cefoxitin, imipenem and meropenem. The results showed that 52, 51, 42 and 44 isolates were resistant to ceftriaxone, cefoxitin, imipenem and meropenem, and 60, 60, 69 and 68 isolates were susceptible to them, respectively. Furthermore, one isolate was intermediate resistant to cefoxitin (the inhibition zone diameter was 16 mm), another isolate was intermediate resistant to imipenem (the MIC was 2 mg/L) (Supplementary Dataset S1).

The viability of E. coli was linearly related to S value based on SYBR green I/PI assay

To determine whether SYBR Green I/PI assay could be used to quantify the survival rate of E. coli, bacterial suspensions at different OD600 values (0.05, 0.1, 0.2 and 0.5) and mimetic viability were detected by the SYBR Green I/PI assay. The acquired green and red fluorescence were normalized as S value, which effectively mitigated the effect of bacterial density. The result of linear regression analysis indicated that the S value was linearly related to the viability of E. coli with R2 higher than 0.93 at all bacterial concentrations (Fig. 1). Accordingly, the SYBR Green I/PI assay for E. coli had high applicability with distinct survival rates and concentrations. In the following experiments with clinical isolates, we chose the regression equation: S = 0.006507*V − 0.9867 of OD600 0.2 to determine the survival rate of E. coli.

The stationary phase and dead E. coil ATCC25922 (treated with isopropyl alcohol) were mixed at different viability (0, 15%, 30%, 50%, 70%, 85%, 100%), and bacterial suspensions were prepared into OD600 0.05 (a), 0.1 (b), 0.2 (c) and 0.5 (d). The linear regression analysis of normalized fluorescence intensity (S) and live bacteria proportion was performed to generate standard curves.

Optimizing antibiotic concentrations of SGPI-AST

To obtain the culture time of the stationary phase E. coli, we monitored the OD600 values at different time points with a spectrophotometer. As the growth curve shown in Supplementary Figure S1, we chose 10 h as the culture time for isolates. Under the influence of antibiotics, the survival rate of susceptible bacteria was far removed from that of resistant bacteria, which is what SYBR Green I/PI assay detects. To easily distinguish between resistant and sensitive bacteria, we optimized the incubation concentration of antibiotics. Six sensitive and resistant clinical strains were selected at random and incubated with ceftriaxone, cefoxitin, imipenem and meropenem at different concentrations, respectively. The results showed that sensitive and resistant clinical strains had the greatest significant differences at 200 mg/L (ceftriaxone), 100 mg/L (cefoxitin), 400 mg/L (imipenem) and 25 mg/L (meropenem), respectively (Fig. 2). Hence, antibiotics in these concentration were selected for SGPI-AST in subsequent experiments.

The clinical application of SGPI-AST

To evaluate the clinical application of SGPI-AST, we detected the RS value of 100 E. coli clinical isolates upon treatment with ceftriaxone (200 mg/L), cefoxitin (100 mg/L), imipenem (400 mg/L) and meropenem (25 mg/L), respectively. The results showed that the average RS values were 100.74 and 73.74% in ceftriaxone resistant and sensitive group, 99.42 and 72.96% in resistant group, intermediate resistant and sensitive group, 101.08 and 72.56% in imipenem resistant group, intermediate resistant and sensitive group, 101.68 and 76.16% in meropenem resistant and sensitive group, respectively (Fig. 3) (Supplementary Dataset S1). Therefore, RS values of sensitive isolates were significantly lower than resistant isolates, indicating that the SYBR Green I/PI assay has the potential to be applied in differentiating resistant and susceptible strains in antibiotic susceptibility analysis.

Determination of cut-off values for optimal performance

To further explore the diagnostic value of the SYBR Green I/PI assay in antibiotic susceptibility testing, we plotted ROC curve according to RS value and antibiotic susceptibility (Fig. 4). The AUC of four antibiotics ceftriaxone, cefoxitin, imipenem and meropenem were 1.000, 0.9972, 1.000 and 0.9915, respectively. Next, we took the cut-off values as criteria for rapid antibiotic susceptibility testing, divided 100 E. coli clinical isolates into sensitive and resistant bacteria, and compared them with the results of conventional AST. The result indicated that SGPI-AST has almost-perfect sensitivity, specificity and concordance with conventional AST (Table 1). Accordingly, the SYBR Green I/PI assay has excellent diagnostic value in antibiotic susceptibility testing of E. coli.

Discussion

In this study, we found that S values obtained from SYBR Green I/PI assay correlated well with the survival rate of E. coli, and the assay was applicable to bacterial suspensions at different concentrations. After optimizing antibiotic concentrations, the SYBR Green I/PI assay could rapidly distinguish susceptibility or resistance of 100 E. coli clinical isolates to ceftriaxone, cefoxitin, imipenem and meropenem with high sensitivity and specificity.

Novel rapid ASTs such as microfluidic platform13,15,16, Raman spectroscopy-based methods17, MALDI-TOF18, and nanomotion detection19, mainly based on highly sensitive analytical equipment, are able to rapidly obtain antibiotic susceptibility results. Nevertheless, the expensive equipment and limited throughput have prevented the clinical application of these approaches. Furthermore, the nitrocefin assay is an affordable rapid screening method for ESBL producers with high relative sensitivity and specificity28. This approach is based on observing the color change of nitrocefin, a chromogenic cephalosporin substrate28. However, this principle limits its application in the screening of strains resistant to other antibiotics. In our study, based on the SYBR Green I/PI assay, antibiotic susceptibility testing results of E. coli clinical isolates could be obtained rapidly in 60 min and the results exhibited perfect concordance (κ-values were close to 1) with the current conventional AST methods. The high throughput and low cost of the SGPI-AST approach makes it easy to be promoted and applied in the clinic.

In this study, we used significantly higher concentrations of antibiotics than those used for MIC testing, as in previous studies25–27. Antibiotics at a high concentration can discriminate between resistant and sensitive isolates more rapidly, which is one of the main reasons for the shorter incubation time of the rapid SGPI-AST. However, this may misclassify low-level and intermediate resistant isolates as sensitive. In addition, the antibiotic susceptibility results of a few isolates obtained by the SYBR Green I/PI assay were not consistent with the conventional AST, which may be due to abnormal membrane structure, or MIC values close to the cut-off threshold (Table 2). Nonetheless, the rapid SGPI-AST for ceftriaxone, cefoxitin, imipenem and meropenem showed almost perfect concordance with the conventional AST.

Although the SYBR Green I/PI assay has a high diagnostic value in antibiotic susceptibility testing for E. coli, only β-lactam antibiotics were evaluated in this study. Future studies could further evaluate the SGPI-AST for fluoroquinolones, aminoglycoside antibiotics and other antibiotics that are commonly used for treating E. coli infections29,30. Furthermore, the current SGPI-AST was performed on cultured isolates, and future studies should evaluate if it can be adapted for direct antibiotic susceptibility analysis of clinical samples, such as positive blood cultures, to further reduce turn-around time for AST. In addition, even though it has been proved that SGPI-AST has excellent application prospects in clinical antibiotic susceptibility testing of E. coli as shown in this study and also in K. pneumoniae26,27, further attention should be paid to its feasibility for other Gram-negative as well as Gram-positive bacterial pathogens in future studies.

Conclusion

In summary, we applied SGPI-AST for the first time to analyze the susceptibility of E. coli clinical isolates to ceftriaxone, cefoxitin, imipenem and meropenem, and demonstrated that the ultra-rapid 60 min test has favorable sensitivity and specificity compared with conventional AST. The rapidity, excellent concordance and high throughput of SGPI-AST make it a potential useful method for rapid antibiotic susceptibility testing in the clinic, which will help to reduce bacterial resistance and improve patient outcomes and prognosis.

Materials and methods

Isolates, culture media, and antibiotics

All 112 E. coli clinical isolates were collected from Shandong Provincial Hospital affiliated to Shandong First Medical University, from 2018 to 2021 (resistant isolates) and 2020 to 2021 (sensitive isolates). The E. coli ATCC25922 was used as a control susceptible strain. Cryopreserved clinical isolates were recovered on Columbia blood agar, and incubated at 37 °C for 18–24 h. Then, the bacteria were inoculated into Luria–Bertani (LB) medium for the indicated time. Bacterial suspensions with different OD600 values were prepared by turbidimeter (BioMérieux Densichek). Four antibiotics ceftriaxone, cefoxitin, imipenem, meropenem obtained from the National Institute for Food and Drug Control (Beijing, China) were dissolved with appropriate solvents, filter-sterilized, and diluted to the indicated concentrations. All experiments were carried out in the Biosafety Level 2 laboratory of Shandong Provincial Hospital affiliated to Shandong First Medical University in accordance with laboratory safety requirements.

Conventional antibiotic susceptibility test

The broth microdilution method (Bio-Kont, China) was performed to determine the minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) of clinical isolates to ceftriaxone and imipenem, and the susceptibility to cefoxitin and meropenem were measured by the Kirby-Bauer (K-B) method based on the inhibition zone diameter. The E. coli ATCC25922 was used as a control strain. The protocol and breakpoints of four antibiotics were referred to the standards of the 2021 CLSI M100 manual.

SYBR green I/PI assay and standard curve

The staining dye was prepared according to our previous studies26,27. Briefly, SYBR Green I (10,000 × stock, Invitrogen) and PI (20 mM, Sigma) were thoroughly mixed in 1000 µl of distilled water in a 1:3 ratio. 10 µl of dye and 100 µl bacterial suspension were added to sample wells in 96-well microtiter plate and incubated for 30 min at room temperature. The green and red fluorescence intensity was detected by SpectraMax Gemini EM fluorescence microplate reader (Molecular Devices, CA, United States) at a wavelength of 485 nm for excitation, 538 nm and 612 nm for emission, respectively. The entire experiment was conducted in the dark.

In order to determine linear relationship between the percentage of surviving bacteria and the normalized fluorescence intensity (S), the standard curve was plotted. The stationary phase and dead E. coli ATCC25922 (treated with isopropyl alcohol) were mixed in different proportions (0/100%, 15%/85%, 30%/70%, 50%/50%, 70%/30%, 85%/15%, 100%/0), and bacterial suspensions were prepared into OD600 0.05 to 0.5. The mixture of cells and staining dye was examined by SpectraMax Gemini EM fluorescence microplate reader as described above. The S value based on green (G) and red (R) fluorescence was calculated by formula: S = (G − 3R)/(G + 3R). Linear regression analysis of S value and survival bacterial proportion was performed to generate a regression equation. The survival bacterial proportion of each sample was determined by the regression equation.

Rapid antibiotic susceptibility testing based on SYBR green I/PI assay

The 112 clinical isolates, which were divided into resistant group, intermediate resistant and sensitive groups according to conventional AST results, were grown overnight in LB broth to stationary phase. Then, bacterial suspensions with OD600 value of 0.2 (equivalent to 1 × 108 CFU/ml) and antibiotics in different concentrations (ceftriaxone at 50 mg/L, 100 mg/L, 200 mg/L, 300 mg/L, 400 mg/L; cefoxitin at 100 mg/L, 200 mg/L, 300 mg/L, 400 mg/L, 500 mg/L; imipenem at 50 mg/L, 100 mg/L, 200 mg/L, 300 mg/L, 400 mg/L; meropenem at 12.5 mg/L, 25 mg/L, 50 mg/L, 75 mg/L, 100 mg/L) were prepared. Subsequently, 90 µl bacterial suspension was incubated with 10 µl antibiotics or their solvent for 30 min at 37 °C. After incubation, each sample was stained with SYBR Green I/PI and the fluorescence was measured as described above. The percentage of surviving bacteria (V) was determined by the regression equation and normalized fluorescence intensity (S), and the relative survival rate (RS) was identified by the formula: RS = Vtreated/Vcontrol.

Receiver operating characteristic curve

The area under the curve (AUC) and cut-off values were determined by the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, which was constructed based on RS value and antibiotic susceptibility. The sensitivity, specificity and concordance of SGPI-AST were obtained by comparing antibiotic susceptibility result discriminated by SGPI-AST or conventional AST criteria. Since SGPI-AST cannot distinguish between sensitive and intermediate resistant strains, the intermediate resistant isolates were identified as sensitive.

Statistical analyses

The student’s t test and two-way ANOVA were used for statistical analyses. κ-values were used to evaluate concordance, and a kappa value of 0.81–1.0 was considered almost perfect concordance. At least three independent experiments were performed for each isolate. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
