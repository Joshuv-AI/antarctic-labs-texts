Predicting mortality and readmission in UGIB: a comparative study of rockall, glasgow-blatchford, and AIMS65 scores
Authors: Mehdi Nasr Isfahani, Masoud Fallah, Peyman Adibi Sedeh, Alireza Abootalebi Ghahnavieh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUpper gastrointestinal bleeding (UGIB) is a frequent emergency requiring prompt risk stratification for effective management. This study evaluates the predictive accuracy of three pre-endoscopic scoring systems—Glasgow-Blatchford Score (GBS), AIMS65, and pre-endoscopic Rockall Score (pRS)—in forecasting 30-day mortality and hospital readmission among UGIB patients in Iranian tertiary hospitals.MethodsA prospective observational study was conducted at Al-Zahra and Khorshid Hospitals (Isfahan, Iran) between April 2024 and April 2025. Adult patients presenting with UGIB symptoms were assessed using pRS, GBS, and AIMS65. Data were collected prospectively at the time of admission and during hospitalization using standardized clinical forms and patient records. Statistical analyses—including receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, area under the curve (AUC) comparisons, sensitivity, specificity, and predictive values—were performed to determine each system’s prognostic utility.ResultsAmong 290 enrolled patients, 30-day mortality occurred in 23.4%, with shock, tachycardia, syncope, altered mental status, and severe comorbidities significantly associated with adverse outcomes (p < 0.001). pRS exhibited the highest predictive accuracy for mortality (AUROC = 0.815) and readmission (AUROC = 0.605), outperforming GBS and AIMS65, though the difference between pRS and AIMS65 was not statistically significant (p = 0.924). At low-risk thresholds (pRS < 1, GBS < 2, AIMS65 < 1), all three systems demonstrated high sensitivity and negative predictive value (NPV), with pRS showing the best balance of prognostic performance.ConclusionThe pre-endoscopic Rockall Score proved most effective for identifying high-risk UGIB patients and guiding early management. Its strong sensitivity and negative predictive value support its role in safe discharge planning and risk stratification in emergency settings. Further validation in diverse clinical contexts is recommended to enhance UGIB management strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-025-04344-6.

Introduction

Upper gastrointestinal bleeding (UGIB) is one of the most common complaints encountered in emergency departments (ED), with approximately 80% of patients presenting with clinical symptoms of UGIB eventually requiring hospitalization [1]. The incidence of UGIB in the general population in the United Kingdom has been reported at 0.1%, with an average hospitalization rate of 7.63% [2]. The global mortality rate ranges from 2% to 10%, while in Iran, it varies more broadly, from 0.5% to 12.5% [3–6]. These statistics underscore the clinical importance of UGIB and the significant burden it places on healthcare resources.

UGIB can be broadly categorized into variceal (e.g., esophageal and gastric varices) and non-variceal causes (e.g., peptic ulcer disease, erosive gastro-duodenitis, reflux esophagitis, malignancies, and vascular ectasias), which may present with clinical symptoms such as hematemesis, melena, hematochezia, dyspepsia, epigastric pain, coffee-ground vomiting, fatigue, and syncope [7, 8].

In the emergency setting, rapid diagnosis and intervention significantly improve clinical outcomes, as misdiagnosis may lead to inappropriate discharge and serious complications, including severe gastrointestinal bleeding, hypotension, anemia, infection, myocardial infarction, renal failure, ischemic hepatitis, respiratory distress, shock, and death [9]. However, the limited availability of 24/7 endoscopy, especially in non-tertiary centers, often necessitates a more conservative management strategy. This contributes to high admission rates (7.63% in 2020) and increased costs to both patients and the healthcare system. Accordingly, a rapid diagnostic strategy based on clinical criteria is essential to guide patient management, predict short-term outcomes (30-day mortality or readmission), and determine the need for hospitalization or referral center care [10, 11].

To address this clinical need, several risk scoring systems have been developed for patients presenting with UGIB symptoms. These tools classify patients into high- and low-risk groups to support decisions on hospitalization, further interventions, or safe discharge. Common scoring systems include pre-endoscopic Rockall Score, Glasgow-Blatchford Score (GBS), AIMS65, PNED, Original Rockall Score, CSMCPI, Bleeding Severity Score (BBS), and Forrest Classification. These scoring systems rely on clinical history, comorbidities, laboratory findings, and in some tools, endoscopic findings, assigning numeric weights to each parameter to predict patient risk and inform clinical decisions [12].

Among these, the most notable scales are the pre-endoscopic Rockall Score, GBS, and AIMS65 that have gained favor because they are based purely on clinical data and do not require endoscopy, making them particularly useful in resource-limited EDs.

While various studies have compared these tools in different countries, their results have been inconsistent, reflecting the need for population-specific validation. Given the lack of similar studies in Iran and the unique characteristics of Iranian emergency departments—including referral patterns and resource constraints—this study aims to determine which of these scoring systems performs best in predicting short-term outcomes in UGIB patients locally.

Method

Study design

This was a prospective, observational study. The study was approved by the institutional review board, and verbal consent for phone contact was obtained from the patients.

Study setting and population

The study was conducted from April 1, 2024, to April 1, 2025, in the emergency departments of Al-Zahra and Khorshid Hospitals in Isfahan, Iran. These hospitals were selected due to their status as high-volume referral centers for gastrointestinal emergencies. Patients profile and lab data were retrieved from the hospital database.

Adult, non-trauma patients presenting to the ED with overt upper GI bleeding symptoms (hematemesis, coffee-ground vomit, melena, hematochezia) and those suspected with upper GI bleeding who presented with fatigue, dizziness, or syncope were evaluated. They were allocated to be eligible for the study at the time of upper GI bleeding diagnosis. Patients were excluded if they had received prior treatment at another facility, presented with rebleeding from a prior UGIB episode, had incomplete data, declined participation, were unavailable for follow-up, or whose source of bleeding was in the lower GI tract. All upper GI bleeding cases from either variceal or nonvariceal sources were included.

Study protocol

Eligible patients were identified by emergency physicians or emergency medicine residents who were blinded to both patient outcomes and other predictive variables, and collected patient information using a pre-designed checklist. After verbal and written explanation of the study, informed consent was obtained. Age, sex, clinical history(including cardiovascular, hepatic, renal, chronic inflammatory, hematologic disease, heart failure, diabetes mellitus, and malignant neoplasm), medications, previous GI bleeding or surgery, coffee-ground or bloody vomitus, blood in stool or melena, presence of syncope, presenting vital signs, hemodynamic status, mental status, physical examination findings, and laboratory test results (hemoglobin, blood urea nitrogen, international normalized ratio of prothrombin time, and albumin) were recorded using a structured checklist.

During hospitalization, patients received all necessary standard treatments, including medical interventions, surgical procedures, endoscopy, blood transfusion, and ICU care if required. No additional procedures, such as further diagnostic tests or alternative treatments, were performed as part of the study.

All participants were thoroughly informed about the study procedures and provided written informed consent prior to enrollment. They were also offered the opportunity to receive further clinical evaluation or consultation as needed. Patient data were collected confidentially, adhering strictly to ethical guidelines. Information regarding discharge status and in-hospital mortality was extracted from the hospital database. For discharged patients, follow-up was conducted via telephone one month post-discharge to assess re-admission or mortality outcomes.

To mitigate selection bias, all eligible patients presenting with upper gastrointestinal bleeding during the study period were enrolled consecutively on randomly selected days, irrespective of the initial clinical presentation or bleeding etiology. Data were captured using a standardized collection form to ensure consistency and reliability across cases. Risk scores were calculated independently by a trained investigator blinded to clinical outcomes and other patient-related variables, further reinforcing objectivity in assessment.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (WMA, 2013) and was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Isfahan University of Medical Sciences (Approval No: IR.MUI.MED.REC.1403.261). Informed consent was obtained for participation and follow-up contact. Confidentiality was maintained through anonymized data storage and restricted access. No interventions beyond standard care were introduced, and assessing physicians were blinded to outcomes to minimize bias. Participants retained the right to withdraw without affecting their treatment. Follow-up procedures prioritized patient welfare while ensuring ethical integrity in UGIB risk stratification research.

Scoring systems

At admission, all patients were assessed using the Glasgow-Blatchford Score (GBS), AIMS65, and the pre-endoscopic Rockall Score (pRS). GBS evaluates the need for urgent endoscopy based on clinical history, vital signs, and lab data. AIMS65 predicts in-hospital mortality using five variables: albumin, INR, mental status, systolic blood pressure, and age. The pRS estimates risk based on age, hemodynamics, and comorbidities. High-risk patients were defined as having GBS ≥ 7, AIMS65 ≥ 2, or pRS ≥ 5 (see Table 1).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY) and MedCalc version 23.2. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test, and Yates’ continuity correction was applied where appropriate. Continuous variables were analyzed using independent samples t-tests or Mann-Whitney U-test. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test, while continuous variables were analyzed using independent samples t-tests or Mann-Whitney U-test. A two-tailed p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated for clinically relevant outcomes (30-day mortality and re-admission). The prognostic ability of the scores was compared through the area under the curve (AUC) using the method described by DeLong et al. For each scoring tool, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) were calculated at relevant threshold scores.

Results

A total of 323 patients presented to the emergency department with acute upper gastrointestinal bleeding (UGIB), of whom 33 patients were excluded from the study for the following reasons: 23 patients were excluded due to lower gastrointestinal bleeding, 7 were excluded due to loss of follow-up, and 3 patients had missing data. The remaining 290 patients were included in the study (Figure. 1).

Key clinical parameters—shock, tachycardia, syncope, and altered mental status—were significantly more common in the mortality group (p < 0.001). Comorbidities affected 80.3% of patients overall, with the highest prevalence in those who died (98.5% vs. 85% in readmission; p < 0.001). Malignancy and hepatic failure were also strongly linked to mortality, present in 14.7% and 10.3% of deceased patients, respectively (p < 0.001).

Significant lab differences were noted across groups. The mortality group showed lower mean hemoglobin (9.73 ± 2.37) and albumin (2.83 ± 0.70), alongside elevated BUN (45.76 ± 26.2) and INR (1.53 ± 0.83) compared to others (all p < 0.01). ESRD, heart failure, and melena distributions did not differ significantly.

According to the AUC analysis, the predicted 30-day mortality for AIMS65, GBS, and pRS scores were found to be statistically significant for the estimation of mortality (AUROC; AIMS65 = 0.813, GBS = 0.762, pRS = 0.815, all P < 0.001)) (Table 3; Fig. 2, and Appendix 2). When comparing AUROCs between scoring systems, both the pRS and AIMS65 scores were superior to the GBS score, although the difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.157). Discussion

Acute UGIB is a critical condition to diagnose and manage in the emergency department (ED). Despite improvements in intensive care and advances in endoscopic treatment of UGIB, mortality remains significant. In our study, the 30-day mortality rate (23.44%) was markedly higher than those reported in previous researches (typically around 10% for 30-day or in-hospital mortality) [13, 14]. The mortality rate reported in our study was significantly higher than those in comparable studies.

The observed discrepancy does not stem from methodological bias but rather reflects the composition of our study population, drawn exclusively from tertiary referral hospitals. These facilities predominantly admit patients with severe and unstable presentations, while lower-risk individuals—typically managed in primary or secondary care—are underrepresented. This selection pattern inherently contributes to a higher observed mortality rate.

Further contributing factors include referral bias, such as delayed transfers, greater disease severity at admission, and preferential intake of complex, high-risk cases. These elements collectively influence outcome distributions and may impact the discriminatory performance of prognostic scoring systems in tertiary settings.

Acute UGIB is a medical emergency in which timely management can lead to significantly improved outcomes. Therefore, early risk stratification is essential to identify high-risk patients who require urgent intervention and hospitalization. The prognostic scoring system has the potential to ensure a more objective and repeatable risk assessment than individual clinical judgments; and the scores can be easily and repeatably communicated to different clinicians responsible for the management of UGIB patients. Knowing which patients are at true elevated risk of morbidity or mortality can guide limited resources (such as emergent endoscopy and ICU beds) to their most useful ends.

This study evaluated the prognostic accuracy of three commonly used UGIB risk stratification tools that do not utilize endoscopic results (GBS, AIMS65, and pRS) in predicting 30-day mortality and readmission among patients presenting with upper gastrointestinal bleeding in two Iranian tertiary referral hospitals. Appendices 2 and 3 summarize the AUROC values, which reflect the overall discriminatory performance of each scoring system by balancing sensitivity and specificity. Based on AUROC as a measure of overall accuracy, the pRS demonstrated the highest discriminatory power, followed by GBS and AIMS65. However, given that each scoring tool’s threshold affects sensitivity, specificity, and predictive values, the optimal cut-off should be selected based on clinical context and resource availability.

The pre-endoscopic Rockall Score Developed in 1996 from data on 4,185 UGIB patients in the United Kingdom [15]. this tool removes endoscopic parameters from the original Rockall Score and relies entirely on clinical criteria and co-morbidities. Our findings showed that the pRS achieved the highest performance, with an AUC of 0.815 for predicting mortality and 0.605 for 30-day re-admission within 30 days. In previous studies, it has shown variable performance in predicting death, with AUROC values ranging between 0.63 and 0.80, and a narrower range of 0.60 to 0.63 for readmission [16, 17], along with lower sensitivity and predictive value but slightly higher specificity across various cut-off points compared to our findings [18–20]. Liu et al. reported that while the pRS score outperformed other tools, its AUROC was lower than in our study for mortality (0.711) and it showed higher specificity at the same cut-off values [16]. PRS with a cut-off value ≤ 1 has been proposed for identifying low-risk patients. In our analysis, this threshold was associated with high sensitivity (0.954) and a strong NPV (0.875), although its specificity remained relatively low (0.192).

The Glasgow-Blatchford Score (GBS) Introduced in 2000, this scoring system assessed a larger population (1,748 patients) and evaluated mortality, the need for hospital admission, and therapeutic intervention [21]. In this study, GBS had a lower predictive accuracy compared to the pRS (AUROC 0.762), yet still demonstrated good performance in predicting the mortality of patients with UGI. However, it failed to show adequate performance in predicting 30-day readmission (AUROC = 0.585 and p-value = 0.092). It has shown varying performance in predicting death, with AUROCs between 0.67 and 0.80 in previous studies, which is consistent with the findings of this study. Few studies have demonstrated a clear superiority of the GBS over other scoring systems [18, 22, 23]. However, study by Stanley et al. found no significant difference in prognostic performance between the pRS and GBS in predicting patient outcomes [19]. Previous studies have identified a cut-off score of GBS ≤ 2 for early discharge in patients with UGIB. In our findings, this numerical threshold demonstrated high sensitivity (0.935) and NPV (0.867), but low specificity (0.253) which are consistent with those reported in earlier research. However, considering the emergency setting and the need for safe discharge, this score is acceptable due to its high sensitivity.

The AIMS65 Score is a relatively simple scoring tool Designed in 2011 using data from 29,222 hospitalized patients across 187 United States hospitals to predicts mortality and hospitalization risk [24]. External studies have shown that this scoring system can reliably predict mortality (AUROC 0.72–0.83) [25–28], but it has not shown acceptable performance in predicting rebleeding (AUROC 0.56 to 0.69) [16, 29, 30]. Our findings indicate that this AIMS65 score had strong performance in predicting 30-day mortality, with an AUROC closely similar to that of the pRS (0.813). However, it did not perform well in predicting 30-day readmission in patients with upper gastrointestinal bleeding (AUROC = 0.548). Kilic et al. found no significant difference between AIMS65 and pRS in predicting mortality among UGIB patients [31]. However, the study by Robertson et al. reported that AIMS65 score performed better than both GBS and pRS in predicting mortality of UGIB patients [25]. Current guidelines recommend that patients with AIMS65 < 2 are at low risk for early discharge [32]. In our study, this cut-off demonstrated low sensitivity (0.50) but acceptable specificity (0.86). While this may reduce unnecessary hospital admissions for patients presenting with UGIB symptoms, the low sensitivity limits its reliability in safely identifying candidates for early discharge in emergency settings.

Efficient management of inpatient capacity and strategic resource allocation are critical for optimizing emergency department operations and preventing unnecessary hospitalizations. Given the unpredictable nature of UGIB cases, early and precise risk assessment is essential to ensure that high-risk patients receive urgent care while low-risk individuals can be safely discharged. Our findings highlight the utility of scoring systems with high sensitivity and negative predictive value (NPV) in guiding these decisions. Specifically, applying cut-off values of pRS < 1, GBS < 2, and AIMS65 < 1 allows for the reliable identification of patients at minimal risk, facilitating timely discharge without compromising safety (Appendix 5). This approach supports more effective utilization of healthcare resources while maintaining high standards of patient care.

This study was conducted in two tertiary referral centers in Isfahan, which may limit its generalizability to primary or secondary care settings. These hospitals predominantly manage high-risk patients, potentially inflating the prognostic performance of the scoring systems compared to broader populations. Additionally, reliance on telephone interviews for 30-day follow-up could introduce recall bias or misclassification in readmission data. Due to resource constraints, newer scoring tools such as PNED or ABC scores were not evaluated, which may have provided further comparative insights.

Despite these limitations, the study’s prospective design and standardized data collection enhanced accuracy and consistency. The integration of both clinical and laboratory parameters allowed for a robust assessment of the real-world applicability of three widely used pre-endoscopic scoring systems (GBS, AIMS65, and pRS). Furthermore, this research fills a critical gap in UGIB risk stratification data from Iranian referral centers, while the evaluation of multiple outcomes—30-day mortality and readmission—strengthens its relevance for emergency department decision-making.

Conclusion

This study highlights the value of pre-endoscopic scoring systems in early risk stratification for UGIB patients. Among the tools evaluated, the pre-endoscopic Rockall Score (pRS) demonstrated the highest predictive accuracy for 30-day mortality. Its strong sensitivity and negative predictive value at lower thresholds suggest practical utility in identifying low-risk patients suitable for safe early discharge, aiding resource optimization in emergency care. While GBS and AIMS65 showed reasonable performance for mortality prediction, their utility for readmission remains limited. Tailoring cutoff values to clinical context is essential, and further validation across varied hospital settings is recommended to strengthen UGIB risk stratification strategies.
