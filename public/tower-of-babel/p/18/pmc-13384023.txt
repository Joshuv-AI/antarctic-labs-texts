Catalytic Hydrogenolysis of Polyethylene-Based Plastics into Fuels Using Ruthenium Catalysts Supported on Micro/Mesoporous Carbons
Authors: Antigoni G. Margellou, Eleftherios G. Tosounidis, Kyriazis C. Rekos, Konstantinos S. Triantafyllidis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Catalytic hydrogenolysis is considered as a promising process for the chemical upcycling of polyethylene under mild conditions providing a homogeneous mixture of n-alkanes. This work highlights the potential of hydrogenolysis to effectively convert high molecular weight polyethylene-based polymers (LDPE and HDPE) and real plastic consumer products under mild reaction conditions using highly dispersed ruthenium catalysts supported on high surface area micro/mesoporous activated carbons (AC). The effect of the reaction conditions (temperature, time, hydrogen gas pressure, catalyst-to-feed ratio) and catalyst (metal typeRu, Pt, Reand loading, carbon support) was investigated using n-octane as a solvent/substrate and a high molecular weight (M w ∼ 110000 g/mol) low-density polyethylene (LDPE). Under the optimized conditions of 250 °C, 3 h, 50 bar H2, and a catalyst-to-feed ratio (C/F) = 0.1 with 5%Ru/AC, complete conversion of pure LDPE was achieved toward 78 wt % liquid alkanes with C5–C30 carbon number distribution. More intense conditions (i.e., higher temperature or prolonged reaction time) favored further hydrogenolysis of initially formed higher molecular weight alkanes toward smaller alkanes. The hydrogenolysis of high-density polyethylene (HDPE, M w ∼ 95000 g/mol) exhibited higher liquid alkane yields compared to LDPE (up to 91 wt % at 67–99 wt % conversion), owing to its higher melt flow index that facilitated solubility and depolymerization. The composition of the liquid products in terms of carbon number and established refinery fractions (i.e., naphtha, diesel, kerosene, heavy/lubricants) from both LDPE and HDPE was determined and correlated to reaction conditions, feedstock type, and catalyst properties. When real consumer LDPE- or HDPE-based plastics were used as feedstocks, the conversion was 67–98 wt % and the liquid alkanes yielded up to 91 wt %, similar to pristine polymers. The negative effect of inorganic additives in the real plastics was also identified, with CaCO3 and TiO2 reducing the hydrogenolysis reactivity and shifting selectivity toward larger alkane fractions. Overall, the ruthenium-based catalysts supported on high surface area micro/mesoporous activated carbons exhibited high activity in the hydrogenolysis of high molecular weight polyethylene-based plastics, exhibiting also high stability and reusability for at least three successive catalytic cycles without intermediate regeneration.

Introduction

The wide utilization of plastic products in everyday life has led to a tremendous increase in plastics production over the last decades. This overuse led to approximately 6300 Mt of plastic waste by 2015, with an estimation that it will increase to 12000 Mt by 2050. According to current metrics in the plastics industry, polyolefinsnamely polyethylene (PE) and polypropylene (PP)account for ∼45% of worldwide plastics production. Their wide use in packaging, food, and construction applications has led to huge amounts of waste, accounting for more than half of the total postconsumer plastic waste. Unfortunately, less than 10% is mechanically recycled, while the rest is incinerated toward energy production or landfilled. This approach poses severe environmental problems including microplastic accumulation in aquatic environments and the greenhouse gases formation. ,

Toward a more sustainable and viable solution, chemical upcycling of plastic waste is considered as a promising approach to mitigate severe environmental issues. Traditional thermochemical processes such as pyrolysis and gasification have been employed for the upcycling of plastic waste, mainly polyolefins, toward the production of high-value monomers. − Although the pyrolysis of plastics is already applied at high TRLs, the high reaction temperature, the low selectivity to saturated alkanes, and the complex composition of products have led to the quest for alternative thermocatalytic pathways such as hydrogenolysis, oxidation, and solvolysis. ,, Photocatalysis and biological degradation have also been suggested as promising processes for the chemical upcycling of plastic waste, but their slow reaction rates are still a challenge to overcome prior to considering potential applications. Focusing on polyethylene, its thermal (noncatalytic) fast pyrolysis is performed at elevated temperatures (400–700 °C) under an oxygen-free atmosphere leading primarily to the formation of wax-like hydrocarbons (C > 20), and fewer gases and liquid products (C < 20) with a mixed composition of (di)­olefins and paraffins, due to random depolymerization. ,, On the other hand, the main advantages of catalytic hydrogenolysis are the less intense conditions, the high homogeneity of the products comprising mainly n-alkanes depending on the nature of the catalyst, and the relatively narrower carbon number distribution. ,,

In most of the studies reported in the literature, the hydrogenolysis of polyethylene has been investigated in the absence of a solvent/substrate, usually working with polymers of low molecular weight (MW) of approximately M w = 4000 g/mol. − Less frequently, polymer feedstocks with an average MW in the range of M w = 50000–90000 g/mol have been utilized, sometimes demanding prolonged reaction times for their deconstruction. − Although solvent-free systems proved to be an efficient approach for the hydrogenolysis of polyethylenes, the utilization of solvents could reduce mass and thermal transfer limitations leading to higher reactivity and better control of the composition of liquid products. Furthermore, liquid/slurry phase reactors would be more feasible for upscaling such a process, while the use of a liquid substrate like petroleum/hydrocarbon fractions would also align with the current (bio)­refinery perspective of coprocessing. To this end, among nonpolar and polar solvents, nonpolar linear alkanes are preferred, as they enhance the yield of lower molecular weight alkanes due to their higher affinity for the targeted compounds. Xu et al. mentioned that the use of hexane as a solvent can facilitate the dispersion of molten LDPE, enhance the mass transfer in the reaction system, and lead to increased liquid product yield and a narrower carbon number distribution compared to the solvent-free process. Furthermore, short-chain alkanes favored the conversion of PE by tuning the polymer–surface interactions, which in turn affect the reaction kinetics.

Certainly, catalyst reactivity plays the most important role in the efficient hydrogenolysis of polyethylene. Among all the noble and transition metals used as active sites, ruthenium exhibited excellent hydrogenolysis activity under mild reaction conditions. ,,, The superior performance of ruthenium was mainly attributed to its lower activation energy and the balanced coverage between chemisorbed hydrogen and hydrocarbon species. ,,− On the other hand, other noble and transition metals are usually activated at higher temperatures or require prolonged reaction times to achieve comparable hydrogenolysis efficiency. Beyond the active metal site, the support type can also control overall activity, reaction mechanism, and product distribution. A wide range of supports has been investigated in the hydrogenolysis of polyethylene with ruthenium as the main metal active site, such as activated carbon, , CeO2, ,− Al2O3, , SiO2, , TiO2, ,, and ZrO2, , while the selection of zeolites as supports can shift the reaction mechanism from hydrogenolysis to hydrocracking as well as to different product distributions. − Recent studies have recognized Ru nanoparticles supported on activated carbon as a highly active catalytic formulation for the hydrogenolysis of polyethylene. An effective catalytic system should combine high reactivity and liquid product yield as well as high stability, high resistance toward sintering, and minimal deactivation due to poisoning species.

Another challenge that should be addressed is the role of organic or inorganic compounds used as additives or fillers in consumer plastics and their effect on hydrogenolysis and product composition. Although the conversion of real postconsumer wastes has been investigated in comparison with pure polymers, ,,,, few studies systematically focused on their negative effect on hydrogenolysis. Kita et al. investigated the effect of metal oxides, carbonates, sulfur- and nitrogen-containing compounds on the activity of Ru/ZrO2 in LDPE hydrogenolysis. Among all the additives tested, metal oxides, carbonates, and nitrogen-containing compounds did not poison the active sites of catalysts, while sulfur-containing compounds significantly inhibited the hydrogenolysis of LDPE, due to the formation of RuS2 on the catalyst surface. Aiming to increase the antioxidant properties of polyolefins, phenolic compounds are frequently added during plastics production. Their presence can remarkably decrease the conversion of polyethylene as well as the liquid and gaseous product yield.

Considering all the above, the present study provides further insight into the current scientific advancements on the catalytic aspects of polyethylene hydrogenolysis, with emphasis on the chemical upcycling of realistic high molecular weight PE-based plastics. Furthermore, the capability of converting plastic into hydrocarbon substrates (toward potential coprocessing) was showcased, leading to exceptionally high yields of liquid alkane hydrocarbons with tunable boiling point ranges, from naphtha to kerosene and to bunker fuels/lubricants. In more detail, this work focused on the systematic investigation of the hydrogenolysis reaction conditions (temperature, time, hydrogen pressure, catalyst-to-feed ratio) for the conversion of high molecular weight LDPE and HDPE into alkanes using highly dispersed ruthenium (as optimum among other metals) catalysts supported on micro/mesoporous activated carbons. The optimum systems were then tested in the hydrogenolysis of real plastics, and the catalyst reactivity was correlated to their structural and compositional characteristics. Finally, the reusability of the catalysts was assessed, while deactivation was elucidated via the characterization of spent samples.

Materials and Methods

Synthesis of Catalysts

All catalysts were synthesized via the wet impregnation method using RuCl3·xH2O (99.9%, Strem Chemical), NH4ReO4 (99.9%, Fisher), and H2PtCl6·6H2O (99.9%, Sigma-Aldrich) as metal precursors and micro/mesoporous activated carbons with varying physicochemical characteristics (SAE Super, CPL, and SX-Plus) purchased from Norit–Cabot. A pre-estimated amount of the precursor, corresponding to 5 wt % Ru on the final catalyst, was dissolved in H2O (3.2 wt %) under stirring. Afterward, the precursor’s solution was added dropwise into the aqueous suspension of activated carbon (8.6 wt %) and the stirring continued for 24 h. After the evaporation of water, the solid was dried at 60 °C for 6 h under vacuum. Prior to the catalytic experiments, the catalysts were reduced using NaBH4 as a reducing agent. In a typical procedure, NaBH4 was dispersed in ethanol (7.5 wt %) under stirring and the as-made impregnated catalyst was added to the solution. The mixture was kept under stirring until bubble generation stopped (4 h). The reduced catalyst was separated from the solution by vacuum filtration, washed with deionized water and ethanol several times, and dried at 60 °C overnight.

Characterization of Catalysts

The catalysts were characterized by X-ray Powder Diffraction (XRD), X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS), and N2 physisorption. The XRD analysis was performed with a Rigaku Ultima+ 2-cycle X-ray Diffractometer with Cu-Kα1 X-ray radiation in the range 2θ = 5–85° using 0.02°/step and 1.2 s/step. The porous properties of the catalysts were determined via N2 adsorption–desorption at −196 °C using an Automatic Volumetric Sorption Analyzer (Autosorb-1 MP, Quantachrome). All samples were outgassed, prior to nitrogen adsorption, at 250 °C for 19 h under a 5 × 10–9 Torr vacuum. The total surface areas were determined by the multipoint BET method, and the total pore volume was determined from adsorbed nitrogen at P/Po = 0.99. Micropore surface area and volume were determined via the t-plot method, while the mesopore size distribution via the Barrett–Joyner–Halenda (BJH) analysis. X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were conducted using a Kratos Analytical AXIS UltraDLD system, featuring an aluminum monochromatic X-ray source (λKa= 1486.6 eV) under ultrahigh vacuum conditions (10–9 Torr). Survey spectra (0–1200 eV) were collected with an analyzer pass energy of 160 eV and 105 W applied to the X-ray gun. High-Resolution (HR) spectra were acquired with a pass energy of 20 eV set for the analyzer and 150 W applied to the X-ray gun, during a three-sweep or five-sweep scan. The calibration of XPS spectra was performed with reference to the C 1s peak at 284.6 ± 0.2 eV of C–C bonds to correct for any surface charging effects. Additionally, surface etching was performed with an Ar+ ion gun, applying an etch rate of approximately 100 Å/min for 72 s across a 4 mm2 area. The background of HR spectra was subtracted using Shirley, Tougaard (nonlinear), or linear baselines, and the experimental curves were fitted using a combination of Gaussian (70%) and Lorentzian (30%) distributions. The quantitative analysis was carried out using the relative sensitivity factors (RSF) of each element, obtained from the Vision 2.2.10 software database.

Polymers and Plastic Feedstocks

Two pristine polymers and four real consumer plastics were used as feedstocks. Low-density polyethylene (LDPE, 150 E, Dow) and high-density polyethylene (HDPE, EE-5001-AAH, Ethydco) were used as pure polymers. As representative LDPE-based plastics, the package films from water bottle packages and commercial garbage bags were selected. On the other hand, bottle caps from water bottles and shampoo bottles were selected as representative HDPE-based plastic products. Prior to the characterization and catalytic experiments, the pristine polymers were cut into small particles with an average size of 1–2 mm, whereas the particle size of the plastic products was higher, ca. 5–10 mm, as shown in Figure SI-1.

The characterization of feedstocks was carried out via multiple techniques to identify their structural and thermal properties as well as to determine the presence of additives. Moisture and ash content were determined gravimetrically at 105 °C for 24 h and 575 °C for 3 h, respectively. The remaining ashes corresponding to additives (inorganic compounds) were identified via SEM/EDS. The measurements were performed on a JEOL 6300 equipped with X-ray microanalysis (OXFORD ISIS 2000). The main structural characterization of feedstocks was performed via ATR spectroscopy using the IR Tracer 100 Shimadzu instrument equipped with a QATRTM 10 Single Reflection ATR accessory with diamond crystals. The samples were scanned between 450 and 4000 cm–1 at a resolution of 2 cm–1 and 32 scans. All feedstocks were characterized via XRD analysis using the Rigaku Ultima+ 2-cycle X-ray Diffractometer with CuKα X-ray radiation in the range 2θ = 5–85° using 0.02°/step and 1.2 s/step. The crystallinity index of each polymer/plastic was determined via the equation: Crystallinityindex(%)=100·[(Areaofcrystalline)/(Areaamorphous+Areaofcrystalline)]

Melt flow index (MFI) was measured on a CEAST’s Melt Flow Quick Index meter at 140 °C with a weight of 2.16 kg. The equation used to estimate the molecular weight (Mw) of the samples from the MFI values was. MFI=(6.09·1017)·Mw−3.59

The thermal behavior of the samples was determined via thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) using the STA 449 F5 Jupiter instrument (Netzsch). The measurements were carried out in the temperature range of 25–700 °C, under a nitrogen flow of 80 mL/min and a heating rate of 10 °C/min. Py/GC-MS (micropyrolysis with online GC-MS analysis) was performed on a Multi-Shot Micro-Pyrolyzer (EGA/PY-3030D, Frontier Laboratories) directly coupled to a Gas Chromatograph–Mass Spectrometer (GC-MS) system. For each run, 1.0 mg of the solid sample was weighed into a stainless-steel cup and the measurement was performed at 600 °C. The separation of the compounds was achieved using an Ultra Alloy 5, column (30 m x 0.25 mm x 0.25 μm), and the identification of the compounds was based on the NIST mass spectral library.

Hydrogenolysis of Plastics

The catalytic hydrogenolysis experiments were conducted in a 50 mL stainless steel high-temperature/high-pressure batch autoclave reactor equipped with a magnetic stirrer. In a typical experiment, 0.7 g of pure polymers or real plastics (5 wt %), 20 mL n-octane and a catalyst (0.03–0.14 g) were placed into the reactor. After flushing with hydrogen gas three times to remove air, 30–70 bar H2 was charged into the reactor. The experiments were carried out at a temperature range of 180–280 °C for 1–5 h under stirring at 600 rpm. Under the selected stirring rate, the conversion of plastic and the liquid yield were not affected by external mass transfer. Afterward, the reactor was rapidly cooled down to room temperature, and the gaseous products were collected in a vacuum bag for further analysis. The liquid product was separated from the solids containing the catalyst and the unconverted plastic via vacuum filtration. The catalyst was washed several times with ethanol and dried at 80 °C for 6 h under vacuum.

The gaseous products were analyzed via GC-TCD/FID using an HP6890 Agilent gas chromatograph with Flame Ionization and Thermal Conductivity Detectors equipped with four columns (Precolumn: OV-101; Columns: Porapak N, Molecular Sieve 5A, and Rt-Qplot (30 m × 0.53 mm i.d.)). The liquid products were analyzed via gas chromatography using the Shimadzu Nexis GC-2030 chromatograph equipped with a capillary Mega-101 column (30 m × 0.35 mm × 0.45 μm) and a flame ionization detector (FID). The quantification of the compounds was based on the analytical standards with C5–C40 n-alkanes. The compounds with molecular weights above C40 were determined via simulated distillation (SimDis) up to 750 °C by GC according to ASTM D-6352, 7169, and EN 15199. The conversion of polymer/plastic waste and the yield of each fraction were calculated via the following equations. The amount of n-octane used as a solvent/substrate was not included in the calculations and estimation of liquid yields, while only 5% of C5–C7 amount determined via GC-FID was included in liquid yields and carbon number distribution. Based on the GC-FID analysis, the identified alkanes were classified into the following fuel ranges: Naphtha C5–C10, Gasoline C5–C12, Kerosene/aviation C9–C12, Diesel C12–C20, and wax/lubricants C20-C40. Results and Discussion

Polymers and Plastic Products Characterization

All pure polymers and the consumer plastics exhibited very low moisture content (0.0–0.3 wt %). The major difference between the feedstocks was the higher inorganics content (up to 6.0 wt %) of real plastics compared to pristine polymers which were free from inorganics. The higher inorganics content of real plastics can be attributed to the additives (i.e., CaCO3, TiO2, etc.) used in their production either as low cost fillers and/or to enhance specific properties such as mechanical properties, whiteness and opacity, protection from UV photo-oxidation, and others. Moreover, the inorganics corresponded to impurities of catalysts used during the repolymerization. To confirm that assumption, the solids (ashes) recovered after the calcination of plastics were characterized via energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) and the results are shown in Figure SI-2. As can be seen, the ash of garbage bags (6 wt %) exhibited significant amount of mainly calcium (∼70%) and titanium (∼24%), while silicon, aluminum, magnesium, and chlorine were identified at lower amounts. The high calcium content can be attributed to the presence of calcium salts (mainly CaCO3), widely used as fillers in polyolefins to induce thermal stability and decrease the price of the final product. , Similarly, the ash recovered from shampoo bottles (4 wt %) revealed the presence of mainly titanium (∼95%) along with a low concentration of calcium, aluminum, silicon, and sulfur. The high concentration of titanium can be attributed to the TiO2 frequently utilized as an inorganic pigment to give the white color of the final product. ,

The main structural characterization of feedstocks was performed via ATR spectroscopy. All samples exhibited similar bands revealing the high similarity of real plastic products to the respective pristine polymers (Figure a). It can be noted that the bands were similar not only in terms of transmittance position (wavenumbers) but also in terms of their intensity. The bands at 2910 and 2845 cm–1 were assigned to the asymmetric and symmetric stretching vibrations of C–H in −CH2 groups. At lower wavenumbers, the double band centered at 1471 and 1462 cm–1 was attributed to the bending deformation of CH3 groups. Additionally, the doublet bands at 730 and 719 cm–1 could be assigned to rocking deformations. All the bands identified in the polyethylene samples of the present study are common and in accordance with other polyethylene samples referred to in the literature. − The low-intensity band at 1650–1540 cm–1, mainly identified in the spectra of the pristine LDPE and HDPE, as well as the HDPE bottle caps, can be attributed to a low amount of nitrogen-containing additives used as antioxidants and UV stabilizers. ,

(a) ATR, (b) XRD, (c) TGA, and (d) DTG analysis of pure polymers and real plastics.

Besides infrared spectroscopy, the polyethylene origin of the pristine and consumer plastics was confirmed by XRD analysis (patterns are shown in Figure b for pristine LDPE and HDPE, and in Figure SI-3 for all plastic products). The main peaks of the polyethylene structure were observed at 2θ = 21.9°, 24.0°, and 36.2° corresponding to (110), (200), and (020) reflections, while several peaks of lower intensity were observed in the range of 2θ = 40–60°. − As expected, the pristine LDPE exhibited lower crystallinity than the pristine HDPE due to its higher branching compared to the more linear chain structure of HDPE, as is well known for these polymers. This was also confirmed by the estimated crystallinity index values (shown in Table SI-1), which further revealed the same variation between the LDPE- and HDPE-based consumer plastic products, i.e., the latter exhibited higher crystallinity index values than the former. The substantially lower crystallinity of LDPE garbage bags, compared to the pristine LDPE, can be attributed to the relatively high content of additives compared to the other plastic products.

It has been previously shown that the molecular weight of plastics can be determined by their melt flow index (MFI). The melt flow index (MFI) is widely used in the plastics industry for the determination of plastic grades, but it can be also utilized for the determination of the molecular weight and other polymer’s characteristics. ,, In general, the lower the MFI, the higher the molecular weight and the broader the molecular weight distribution. The melt flow index was determined for all the pristine polymers and plastic samples in this study. As can be seen in Table , all pristine LDPE and HDPE polymers and plastic products exhibited MFI values in the range of 0.29 to 0.82, except for the HDPE bottle caps which showed a substantially higher MFI, equal to 6.87, indicating their facile flow during heating in the molten state. By applying the equation and method described in the experimental section, the molecular weight (M w) of the samples was also calculated (Table ). Apart from the HDPE bottle caps which exhibited the lowest molecular weight (52577 g/mol), the rest of the samples exhibited higher molecular weights within the range of 95047 to 126964 g/mol. As was also mentioned in the introduction, most of the previous works on LDPE or HDPE hydrogenolysis have focused on substantially lower M w pristine polymers, with even fewer examples of testing real consumer plastic products.

The thermal degradation of the samples was studied via combined TGA/DSC analysis. The thermal degradation of LDPE samples initiated at 436–455 °C and was completed for all samples, in one stage, at approximately 480–484 °C (Figure c). The maximum rate of degradation was observed at TDTG, max = 468 °C (Figure d). The thermal degradation of HDPE samples started at a higher temperature of 456–468 °C and finished at 488–492 °C, while the maximum rate of degradation was observed at TDTG, max = 477–479 °C. The results of the present work are in accordance with literature data reporting HDPE degradation temperatures being 5–20 °C higher than those of LDPE. − Within these temperatures, the total weight loss was 94–99.6%, while the residual mass was 0.0–3.5%. The highest residual mass of ∼3.5% at 550 °C was observed for the HDPE shampoo bottles and the LDPE garbage bags, being attributed to the relatively high inorganic content (as discussed above, Table ). In the case of LDPE garbage bags, at higher temperatures of ca. >650 °C, the CaCO3 filler is decomposed, thus decreasing further the residual mass. Based on the DSC curves (Figure SI-4, Supporting Information), the melting temperature was determined and found in the range of 113–119 °C for LDPE samples and slightly higher for the HDPE samples with melting temperatures in the range of 138–143 °C. The lower melting point range of the LDPE samples is in line with their lower crystallinity compared to the HDPE samples, and in accordance with the expected properties of these polymers.

Catalysts Characterization

Two sets of catalysts were synthesized in the present study. The first set was prepared using SAE Super activated carbon as support and different metals (Ru, Pt, Re, Re–Ru) as active sites, while the second set of materials comprised Ru supported on three different activated carbons (SAE Super, SX-Plus, and CPL). The XRD patterns of the different carbons and the supported catalysts are shown in Figure . The addition of the different metals, via the method described in the Section , on SAE Super did not induce the appearance of characteristic XRD diffraction peaks of the respective metals or oxides (Figure a), which is indicative of the homogeneous, high dispersion of the metals and the formation of crystallites smaller than ca. 3 nm, in accordance with the detection limit of XRD analysis. Only in the case of 5% Pt/SAE Super, a low-intensity broad reflection at about 39.4° 2θ was identified being attributed to metallic Pt (111). The high dispersion of Ru or Pt on such high surface area micro/mesoporous carbons has been also shown in previous studies. ,, Similar observations and high dispersion of Ru on SX-Plus and CPL carbons were confirmed from the respective XRD patterns (Figure b).

(a, b) XRD and (c, d) N2 physisorption (adsorption–desorption isotherms) of the metallic catalysts supported on high-surface-area micro/mesoporous activated carbons.

Regarding the porous properties of the materials, the metallic catalysts supported on SAE Super activated carbon maintained the micro/mesoporous structure of the parent carbon, as shown in the isotherms of Figure c. The isotherms were characteristic of Type I­(a) and IV, according to the IUPAC classification. Τhe surface area of the parent SAE Super as determined by the BET equation was 988 m2/g and the total pore volume was equal to 0.685 cm3/g. The micropores’ surface area and pore volume were estimated via the t-plot analysis and found to be 637 m2/g and 0.278 cm3/g, respectively (Table ). As can be observed, 64% of the total surface area is attributed to micropores, thus indicating a balanced contribution between micro- and meso/macroporosity. All the supported metallic catalysts exhibited similar surface areas (947–1029 m2/g) and total pore volumes (0.705–0.737 cm3/g) compared to those of SAE Super, in accordance with the high dispersion of the metals indicated by the XRD results. Regarding the second set of catalysts (Figure d), both the 5%Ru/SX-Plus and 5%Ru/CPL exhibited also high surface areas (1141 and 1224 m2/g, respectively) and total pore volumes (0.872 and 1.115 cm3/g, respectively), similar to those of the SAE Super-supported catalysts (Table ). However, two differences can be noted. The catalyst 5%Ru/SX-Plus exhibits a substantially higher microporous area (and volume), while the catalyst 5%Ru/CPL shows almost double the meso/macropore area compared to the rest of the catalysts (Table ). The distinct mesoporosity of the parent CPL carbon and the respective 5%Ru/CPL catalyst can be also clearly seen from the BJH pore size distribution curves shown in Figure SI-5a (Supporting Information).

In-depth structural characterization of catalysts was performed via XPS. The Ru 3p, C 1s, and O 1s spectra of two representative catalysts, namely 5%Ru/SAE Super and 5%Ru/SX-Plus, are shown in Figure . The high-resolution Ru 3p XPS spectrum of both catalysts (Figure a and d) revealed that ruthenium was stabilized in more than one oxidation state. The spectra of both samples exhibited a prominent doublet peak centered at 462.1 eV (Ru 3p3/2) and 484.3 eV (Ru 3p1/2) characteristic of the metallic Ru0 oxidation state. Apart from metallic ruthenium, two more sets of peaks were observed at 463.4 eV (Ru 3p3/2) and 485.6 eV (Ru 3p1/2), attributed to either Ru–C interactions or the Ru4+ oxidation state. Furthermore, the peaks at 465.9 eV (Ru 3p3/2) and 487.7 eV (Ru 3p1/2) were assigned to RuO x H y or RuO2·H2O species, indicative of ruthenium in an oxidation state higher than 4+. , After etching of the catalyst surface, i.e., of the carbon particles containing the dispersed Ru nanocrystallites , (Figure SI-5b, Supporting Information), by a few nanometers (∼10 nm), it can be seen that the relative abundance of the metallic Ru0 phase increased in both catalysts, indicating that the outer surface of the catalysts was quite sensitive forming a layer of ruthenium oxide, as also observed in similar ruthenium-based materials. The analysis of Ru 3p spectra was supported by the spectra of O 1s, shown in Figure b and e. The quantitative analysis of XPS led to the estimation of ruthenium oxidation states’ relative abundance which accounted for 20% Ru0, 57% Ru4+, and 23% RuO x H y /RuO2·H2O for 5% Ru/SAE Super before etching and 38.7% Ru0, 44% Ru4+, and 17% RuO x H y /RuO2·H2O after etching (Table SI-2, Supporting Information). Similarly, the 5%Ru/SX-Plus exhibited 19% Ru0, 57% Ru4+, and 24% RuO x H y /RuO2·H2O before etching and 52% Ru0, 38% Ru4+, and 10% RuO x H y /RuO2·H2O after etching. The above results indicated a higher abundance of metallic Ru0 in 5%Ru/SX-Plus compared to 5%Ru/SAE Super in which the predominant phase was Ru4+. In order to further investigate the reducibility of the Ru nanocrystals/particles, the two catalysts (5%Ru/SAE Super and 5%Ru/SX-Plus) were treated under an H2 atmosphere at 350 °C for 3 h to ensure the reduction of Ru, in comparison with the NaBH4 reduction method that was applied in this work. The respective Ru 3p spectra are shown in Figure SI-6 (Supporting Information) and the values derived from the analysis of the spectra (before and after etching) are also given in Table SI-3 (Supporting Information), in comparison with those derived from the XPS analyses of the NaBH4 treated catalysts (already presented in Table SI-2, Supporting Information). It can be clearly seen that the trend observed from the analyses of the NaBH4-reduced catalysts is also retained in the H2-reduced catalysts, i.e., the metallic Ru species are more abundant in the catalyst 5%Ru/SX-Plus compared to the catalyst 5%Ru/SAE Super, especially by analyzing the spectra after mild etching. This is an additional indication of the small, but still clear difference in the reducibility of Ru between the two catalysts. It should be noted that the ex-situ XPS analysis of the catalysts may not be indicative of the real oxidation state of ruthenium during the hydrogenolysis reaction, where further reduction of ruthenium may occur toward the metallic state, as has been shown previously by advanced in situ and operando techniques (XPS, XAS) or other experimental setups that can minimize contact with air during the treatment of the catalysts prior to or after the reaction. − Regarding the dispersion and size of the Ru nanocrystals/particles as a possible reason for the above difference in reducibility, both SAE Super and SX-Plus carbons offer higher surface area resulting in high dispersion, as shown by XRD and HR-TEM. Previous studies on Ru/AC preparation and characterization demonstrated also that the higher surface area of the support induced enhanced dispersion and smaller metal nanoparticles. , Thus, the similarly high dispersion and small size (ca. <3–5 nm) of the Ru nanocrystals/particles in this study are not expected to induce substantial differences in the reducibility of Ru.

(a) Ru 3p, (b) O 1s, and (c) C 1s XPS spectra of 5%Ru/SAE Super and (d) Ru 3p, (e) O 1s, and (f) C 1s of 5%Ru/SX-Plus.

The deconvolution of the O 1s peak revealed the existence of four oxygen bonding environments. The peak at 530 eV was attributed to oxygen bonding with ruthenium (Ru–O), the peak at 531 eV to CO, the peak at 533 eV to C–O, and the peak centered at 535–536 eV can be assigned to either C–OH bonds of carboxyls or to the adsorbed H2O. Regarding the C 1s spectra shown in Figure c and f, various peaks were identified in both catalysts, associated with the structure of the carbon support and their characteristic surface groups. More specifically, the predominant peak centered at 284.6 eV was attributed to either CC sp2 bonds or C–C aliphatic/aromatic groups of the support, while the peaks at 285.5 eV, 286.7 eV, 286.8 eV, 288.9 eV, and 290.7 eV could be correlated to the C–O–C, C–OH, CO, O–CO, and COOH bonds of hydroxyl, phenolic, ether, carboxyl, and lactonic groups of carbons, in accordance with other studies focusing on the characterization of neat activated carbons. , Additionally, the small peak at 284.2 eV was assigned to Ru 3d3/2 which overlapped with C 1s. The different abundance of oxygen-containing groups of carbon may affect the reduction of ruthenium. Based on the XPS analysis, 5%Ru/SX-Plus exhibited a higher atomic concentration of carbon (92.7%) compared to 5%Ru/SAE Super (90.3%), while the latter catalyst exhibited a higher oxygen concentration (8%) than the former with 3.8% atomic oxygen (Table SI-7, Supporting Information). Thus, the lower metallic ruthenium phase of 5%Ru/SAE Super could be attributed to the higher concentration of oxygen functional groups which can inhibit its reduction, leading to a higher relative concentration of the ruthenium oxide phase.

Catalytic Hydrogenolysis of Pristine LDPE and HDPE

Effect of Process Parameters

The effect of reaction conditions, i.e., temperature, time, and hydrogen pressure, was investigated using pristine LDPE. The reaction temperature is recognized as one of the main process parameters which can control the conversion of plastics and tune the liquid product carbon number distribution. As can be observed in Figure a, under the milder reaction temperature of 180 °C, the conversion of LDPE was very low (7.4 wt %) receiving almost all polymer remaining in the solid residue (92.6 wt %). An increase in the reaction temperature from 180 to 220 °C (under constant conditions of time, feed, catalyst, and H2 pressure) significantly increased the conversion of LDPE from 7.4 wt % to 83.1 wt % toward the formation of liquid products whose yield was estimated at 38.5 wt %. The higher reaction temperature of 250 °C led to almost complete conversion of LDPE (98.8 wt %) toward 78.3 and 12 wt % gases. Further increasing the reaction temperature to 280 °C led to complete conversion of LDPE but at the same time favored the formation of gaseous products whose yield rapidly increased to 52 wt %. Comparing the mass-based and the carbon mole-based yields, minor differences were observed as can be seen in Table SI-4 (Supporting Information). More specifically, the carbon mole-based yield is slightly lower than the mass-based yield by about 0.1–0.2% for the liquid products and 1–5% for the gaseous products, and this discrepancy is attributed to the incorporation of hydrogen into the produced smaller hydrocarbons during hydrogenolysis.

Effect of temperature on (a) products’ yields and LDPE conversion, (b) carbon number distribution of liquid products, (c) carbon number distribution of liquid products as identified via SimDis analysis, and (d) composition of liquid products expressed as different fuel/wax fractions. Reaction conditions: 5 wt % LDPE in n-octane, 5%Ru/SAE Super, C/F = 0.1, 3 h, 50 bar H2 initial pressure.

The increase in gaseous products’ yield can be attributed to enhanced hydrogenolysis activity, leading primarily to substantially higher methane formation. Indeed, as can be seen in Figure SI-7 (Supporting Information), the predominant gas is methane, followed by ethane and propane, while butane and pentane were formed at lower concentrations, confirming the hydrogenolysis at the terminal carbon. Similar trends regarding the reduction of liquid yields and enhanced gaseous products were also established by other studies on the effect of temperature in PR and PP hydrogenolysis. ,,, The analysis of liquid products via GC-FID and GC-MS revealed the formation of n-alkanes whose carbon number distribution was strongly affected by the reaction temperature. The hydrogenolysis of LDPE at 220 °C led to n-alkanes with a broad carbon number distribution in the range of C5–C40, as shown in Figure b. The total yield of C5–C40 alkanes was determined by GC-FID at 38.5 wt %, resulting in a mass balance of approximately 56 wt %. The main reason for the lower mass balance was the formation of high molecular weight alkanes with >C40 which could not be identified via GC-FID and were determined via SimDis analysis. As can be observed in Figure c, the liquid product contained alkanes with a carbon number up to C80. Based on the SimDis analysis, the yield of >C40 alkanes is 24.4 wt % on LDPE feed, for the experiment at 220 °C. Thus, the integrated GC-FID and SimDis analysis provides a 62.9 wt % liquid yield, which corresponds to a total mass balance of 80% (Table SI-5, Supporting Information). By increasing the reaction temperature to 250 °C a narrower carbon number distribution (C5–C32) was obtained, centered at smaller alkanes C15–C16, as depicted in Figure b and confirmed via SimDis analysis in Figure c. The highest reaction temperature (280 °C) enhanced the C–C bond cleavage of longer chains toward smaller alkanes with an even narrower distribution in the range of C5–C20. It should be noted that the contribution of C5–C7 alkanes is very low in the overall liquid yield and especially under the mild reaction temperatures of 180–250 °C, as can be seen in Table SI-6 (Supporting Information). Thus, the n-octane used as a solvent/substrate is relatively stable under these conditions, and its hydrogenolysis to lower molecular weight alkanes is limited. The higher reaction temperature not only enhanced hydrogenolysis but also induced partial isomerization of linear alkanes to iso-alkanes, according to the GC-MS analysis (Figure SI-8, Supporting Information). At 220 °C, the ratio of n/iso alkanes was equal to 100/0, which decreased to 96/4 at 250 °C and 90/10 at 280 °C. Expressing the composition of liquid products as fuel carbon number ranges, it can be observed in Figure d that the lowest reaction temperature (220 °C) led to the recovery of a liquid product enriched in alkanes in the wax/lubricants range (C > 20), while a gradual increase in the reaction temperature (250 °C) shifted the composition to the kerosene/aviation (C9–C16) and diesel (C12–C20) ranges. Further increase in the reaction temperature (280 °C) favored the formation of alkanes in the naphtha (C5–C10) and gasoline (C5–C12) ranges.

In addition to the liquid and gaseous products, a solid residue is also recovered, whose composition is strongly dependent on the hydrogenolysis temperature. Thermogravimetric analysis revealed that hydrogenolysis at a low temperature (180 °C) led to the recovery of almost all LDPE in the solid fraction due to the very low conversion, as can be seen in Figure SI-9 (Supporting Information) by comparing the TGA curve of the neat LDPE and that of the solid recovered from the reaction at 180 °C. The limited effect on LDPE was also verified by the similar melting temperatures between the recovered LDPE (114 °C) and pristine LDPE (112 °C). On the other hand, the TGA curve of the recovered LDPE after the reaction at 220 °C was shifted to lower temperatures, exhibiting a quite lower melting temperature of 85 °C (Figure SI-9, Supporting Information) thus indicating residual organic species of lower molecular weight compared to that of pristine LDPE. Further increase of the reaction temperature to 250 and 280 °C, which induced high LDPE conversion (>98%), resulted in a very small amount of recovered solid residue whose TGA curves are close to that of the fresh catalyst alone. Py-GC/MS experiments were performed to further characterize the recovered solids. As can be observed in Figure SI-10 (Supporting Information), pyrolysis of the solid recovered after the hydrogenolysis at 180 °C provided a pyrograph typical of pristine LDPE, confirming that the above low temperature was not effective to depolymerize and transform the pristine polymer. The gradual increase of the hydrogenolysis temperature to 220 and 250 °C resulted in residual solid whose pyrolysis yielded products of lower carbon number. It is also clear that pyrolysis of the recovered solid after the reaction at 280 °C provided traces of low molecular weight compounds.

The second process condition studied was reaction time, at the selected temperature of 250 °C, keeping also the rest of the conditions constant (Figure ). Although complete conversion was observed for all the reaction times, ca. 1, 3, and 5 h, the products’ yields and their composition were different. Under the shortest reaction time of 1 h, the conversion of LDPE led to 60 wt % liquid alkanes and 0.3 wt % gases. As already mentioned above, the 3 h reaction led to a higher liquid alkanes yield (78.3 wt %) and 12 wt % gases, while the prolonged reaction time of 5 h led to similar liquid yield (73 wt %). The main effect of reaction time was on the carbon number distribution of liquid products. The lowest reaction time (1 h) led to 60 wt % n-alkanes and a very broad carbon number distribution in the range C5–C40 as determined via GC-FID (Figure SI-11, Supporting Information). As discussed above, for the low-temperature reaction, a low mass balance was also observed after the reaction for 1 h, being attributed to the higher molecular weight alkanes which could not be quantified via the GC-FID analysis and were mainly determined via SimDis analysis. As can be seen in Figure SI-11 (Supporting Information), the carbon number distribution of the liquid product was extended up to C60, proving that the hydrogenolysis of LDPE for 1 h (at the selected temperature of 250 °C) provided alkanes mainly in the wax/lubricants range (≥70% of the liquid product). On the other hand, the progressive increase in reaction time shifted the carbon number distribution to C5–C32 at 3 h and C5–C20 at 5 h. As expected, the prolonged reaction time enhanced the conversion of larger alkanes toward lower molecular weight compounds in the range of naphtha and kerosene/aviation (Figure b). The enhanced hydrogenolysis under prolonged reaction time and the formation of lower molecular weight alkanes is also in accordance with other studies using ruthenium-based catalysts. ,, The beneficial effect of prolonged reaction times (3–5 h) was also confirmed via the thermogravimetric analysis of the recovered solids.

Effect of reaction time on (a) products’ yields, LDPE conversion, and (b) composition of liquid products, and effect of hydrogen pressure on (c) products’ yields, LDPE conversion, and (d) composition of liquid products expressed as different fuel/wax fractions. Reaction conditions: 5 wt % LDPE in n-octane, 5%Ru/SAE Super, C/F = 0.1, 250 °C.

The effect of external hydrogen gas pressure on LDPE hydrogenolysis was also studied under selected constant conditions of 250 °C for 3 h. Like the reaction time, the different initial hydrogen pressures did not have a significant influence on LDPE conversion but significantly changed the products’ yield and their composition. Under the lowest hydrogen pressure of 30 bar (initial pressure at room temperature), the conversion of the polymer resulted in 64.7 wt % liquids and 34.3 wt % gases. An increase in the initial hydrogen pressure to 50 bar limited the formation of gases (12 wt %), while increasing the liquid fraction (78.3 wt %). A further increase in hydrogen pressure to 70 bar suppressed further the formation of gases (1.6 wt %) and increased the formation of liquid alkanes (85.9 wt %), as shown in Figure c. Methane was the most abundant gas followed by ethane and propane (Figure SI-12, Supporting Information). Regarding the liquids’ composition, the lower hydrogen pressure enhanced the formation of C5–C26 alkanes and especially naphtha and gasoline-range alkanes. The higher hydrogen pressure suppressed hydrogenolysis and enhanced the formation of higher molecular weight alkanes (C5–C32) mainly within the kerosene/aviation and gasoline range (Figure d and Figure SI-12). The lower hydrogen pressure possibly facilitates the cleavage of terminal carbon–carbon bonds in the polymer chain toward methane formation, while the higher hydrogen pressures enhanced the cleavage of internal carbon–carbon bonds. , Another possible reason suggested in the literature is the enhanced competitive chemisorption of hydrogen on the catalyst surface at higher pressures over alkane intermediates. ,,

Effect of Catalyst

The main parameters evaluated (at selected constant conditions of 250 °C, 3 h, 50 bar H2) with regard to the catalyst were the effect of catalyst-to-feed ratio (C/F), the metal content and type (i.e., Ru, Re, Pt), as well as the effect of carbon support properties (i.e., different micro/mesoporous activated carbons: SAE Super, SX-Plus, CPL). In the absence of a catalyst (C/F = 0.0), the hydrogenolysis of LDPE was minimal, leading to 9.2 wt % conversion and the recovery of 91 wt % solids. The gradual increase in the C/F ratio (studied by the use of 5%Ru/SAE Super) to 0.05 and 0.1 led to complete conversion of the polymer and significantly enhanced the recovery of liquid alkanes whose yield was in the range of 78.3–79.6 wt % (Figure a). However, a further increase in the C/F ratio to 0.2 promoted the formation of gaseous alkanes whose yield was maximized (32 wt %) leading to a lower yield of liquid alkanes (61.2 wt %), due to the higher availability of metal active sites, as also confirmed by literature studies. , Regarding the composition of the liquid, the progressive increase in the C/F ratio resulted in a gradual shift of the carbon number distribution from C5–C35 at C/F = 0.05 to C5–C32 at C/F = 0.10 and C5–C18 at C/F = 0.20 (Figure SI-13a, Supporting Information). Expressing the carbon number distribution as fuel ranges, the absence of a catalyst led to the recovery of a few liquid alkanes in the wax/lubricants range, while the use of the ruthenium catalyst shifted the alkanes toward the naphtha, gasoline, kerosene, and diesel range, as shown in Figure b.

Effect of catalyst-to-feed (C/F) ratio on (a) products’ yields, LDPE conversion, and (b) composition of liquid products; effect of metal on (c) products’ yields, LDPE conversion, and (d) composition of liquid products; effect of carbon support on (e) products’ yields, LDPE conversion, and (f) composition of liquid products expressed as different fuel/wax fractions. Reaction conditions: 5 wt % LDPE in n-octane, 5%Ru/SAE Super at varying C/F, 250 °C, 3 h, 50 bar H2 initial pressure.

As the optimum catalyst amount, the value corresponding to C/F = 0.1 was selected due to the balanced liquid composition (among the various lighter fractions) and the limited gases formation. Thus, the effect of metal loading and metal type was further examined at C/F = 0.1 under the selected constant conditions of 250 °C for 3 h under 50 bar H2. Regarding the effect of metal amount, the neat carbon support (SAE Super) led to a very low conversion of LDPE (4.8 wt %). The addition of 3%Ru on the carbon remarkably improved the conversion (98.1 wt %) as well as the recovery of liquid alkanes (47.8 wt %). However, the optimum loading of ruthenium proved to be 5% due to the highest yield of liquid alkanes (78.3 wt %), as shown in Figure c. No further impact was observed on the liquid’s composition, and both 3%Ru/SAE Super and 5%Ru/SAE Super resulted in a similar range of alkanes (C5–C32) and distribution among the various fractions (Figure d).

Previous studies have shown that ruthenium exhibits superior catalytic activity in the hydrogenolysis of plastics and model hydrocarbons. ,,,, These observations were confirmed by the results of the present study. Both 5%Pt/SAE Super and 5%Re/SAE Super resulted in negligible LDPE conversion, 5.4 and 2.6 wt %, respectively, and the recovery of polymer in the solid fraction (94.6–97.4 wt %) (Figure c). The lower activity of Pt in the hydrogenolysis of PE had also been demonstrated previously in similar studies. ,,, The higher hydrogenolysis efficiency of ruthenium could be attributed to the lower energy requirements for ruthenium activation to catalyze C–C bond cleavage. , On the other hand, the bimetallic catalyst 5%Re-5%Ru/SAE Super led to 98.4 wt % conversion toward 63.6 wt % liquid alkanes and 27 wt % gases, thus favoring the enhanced hydrogenolysis of primary larger (liquid) alkanes toward gaseous alkanes compared to the neat Ru catalyst. To this end, based on the GC-FID of liquids, 5%Re-5%Ru/SAE Super showed higher formation of C5–C26 alkanes within the naphtha, gasoline, and kerosene/aviation range alkanes, compared to the 5%Ru/SAE Super catalyst (Figure d).

Apart from metal type and loading, the effect of carbon support was also examined in the hydrogenolysis of LDPE using ruthenium as the active metal supported on different micro/mesoporous activated carbons, i.e., SAE Super, SX-Plus, and CPL (Table ). All catalysts resulted in complete conversion of LDPE toward liquid and gaseous alkanes (Figure e) but the different types of carbon support had a substantial effect on products’ yield and composition. The catalyst 5%Ru/CPL exhibited similar product yields to those of 5%Ru/SAE Super, leading to 70.4 wt % liquids and 13.8 wt % gases. Additionally, the composition of liquids was quite similar between these two catalysts, being enriched in kerosene/aviation and diesel alkanes. On the other hand, the catalyst 5%Ru/SX-Plus exhibited a noticeable increase in gaseous products, reaching 22 wt %, comprising mainly methane (Figure SI-14, Supporting Information). The liquid product was enriched in C5–C16 n-alkanes (in naphtha, gasoline, and kerosene ranges), being in higher abundance compared to the product distribution with 5%Ru/SAE Super and 5%Ru/CPL (Figure f). Considering that all three ruthenium–carbon catalysts exhibited similarly high dispersion of Ru on the carbon supports (as evidenced by the absence of any Ru or RuO2 reflections in the XRD patterns, Figure ), we investigated possible effects due to texture (porosity measurements) and surface (XPS measurements) variations among the catalysts. The 5%Ru/SX-Plus exhibits substantially higher microporosity (micropore area and volume) as can be seen in Table , in contrast to 5%Ru/CPL that shows enhanced meso/macroporosity, thus indicating a preference for carbon microporosity in the hydrogenolysis of the LDPE chains to lower molecular weight alkanes. Regarding XPS results, despite the catalyst 5%Ru/SX-Plus exhibiting higher metallic ruthenium relative abundance compared to the other two catalysts, this observation cannot be utilized to justify the different catalytic performance as during the hydrogenolysis reaction, the actual oxidation state of Ru may differ (being likely highly reduced) from that observed by the ex-situ XPS measurements (with or without etching) as also discussed above.

Comparison between LDPE and HDPE

The hydrogenolysis of pristine HDPE and comparison of its performance with that of LDPE were performed under the selected/optimum conditions derived from the LDPE hydrogenolysis investigation, i.e., catalyst 5%Ru/SAE Super, C/F = 0.1, 250 °C, 3 h, 50 bar H2 initial pressure. An almost complete conversion was observed for HDPE (93.2 wt %), as was found also for LDPE (98.6 wt %) (Figure ). However, HDPE yielded more liquid alkanes (87.8 wt %) compared to LDPE (78.3 wt %) and substantially lower carbon number (C5–C22) within the range of naphtha, gasoline, and kerosene, as can be seen in Figure and Figure SI-15, Supporting Information. This performance can be correlated with the higher melt flow index (and lower molecular weight) of pristine HDPE compared to that of pristine LDPE, as shown in Table . It can be assumed that the faster and easier melting of HDPE samples facilitated their hydrogenolysis and the subsequent formation of lower molecular weight alkanes. Additionally, the higher degree of branching in LDPE may have inhibited its mobility, adsorption, and activation on the catalyst surface compared to HDPE.

Hydrogenolysis of pristine LDPE and HDPE: (a) products’ yields and conversion and (b) composition of liquid products expressed as different fuel/wax fractions. Reaction conditions: 5 wt % LDPE or HDPE in n-octane, 5%Ru/SAE Super, C/F = 0.1, 250 °C, 3 h, 50 bar H2 initial pressure.

When a physical mixture of LDPE and HDPE (50:50, wt %) was tested as feedstock, the observed performance was an average of the individual polymers, resulting in 98.1 wt % conversion along with 80.8 wt % liquid yield and 11.7 wt % gas yield (Figure ). The liquid product was enriched in C5–C28 n-alkanes which was an intermediate range between the C5–C32 and the C5–C22 carbon number distribution determined for pure LDPE and HDPE polymers (Figure SI-15, Supporting Information). These results indicate that potential actual mixtures of LDPE- and HDPE-based plastics will follow a balanced hydrogenolysis and decomposition profile, not favoring one over the other, as is also discussed below for the hydrogenolysis of the mixture of real consumer plastics.

Catalytic Hydrogenolysis of Real Plastic Products

The hydrogenolysis tests of real plastics (i.e., LDPE garbage bags, package film) were performed under the optimized reaction conditions (catalyst 5%Ru/SAE Super, C/F = 0.1, 250 °C, 3 h, 50 bar H2 initial pressure) as determined for LDPE polymer. Compared to the pristine LDPE, the relevant plastic products led to lower conversion and liquid yield. The hydrogenolysis of package film led to 95.3 wt % conversion toward 69.9 wt % liquid and 2.2 wt % gases, while the remaining solid was 4.7 wt % (Figure a). Based on the GC-FID analysis, n-alkanes with C5–C35 carbon numbers were found in the liquid product (Figure SI-16, Supporting Information), increasing partially the wax/lubricants fraction compared to pristine LDPE polymer (Figure b). On the other hand, the hydrogenolysis of garbage bags resulted in 66.7 wt % conversion toward 22.4 wt % liquid, 0.1 wt % gases, and 33.3 wt % solids. The substantially lower conversion of garbage bags compared to pristine LDPE polymer and package film can be attributed to the relatively high inorganic content of the garbage bags, mainly due to calcium-based additives, as discussed in Section above (Table ). Despite the lower crystallinity of garbage bags which would be beneficial for depolymerization, the inorganic additives most probably poisoned the active sites of the ruthenium catalyst, leading to lower conversion and hindering the hydrogenolysis toward lower molecular weight alkanes. Indeed, alkanes with higher molecular weight and carbon numbers up to C80 were identified via the SimDist analysis shown in Figure SI-17 (Supporting Information) providing an opaque liquid product that can be observed in Figure e. To further support the above discussion, in the XRD pattern of the recovered solid (Figure SI-18a, Supporting Information), the characteristic reflections of CaCO3 were identified in addition to the reflections of unconverted LDPE.

Reaction conditions: 5 wt % plastic feed in n-octane, 5%Ru/SAE Super, C/F = 0.1, 250 °C, 3 h, 50 bar H2 initial pressure.

With regard to the HDPE-based plastic products (Figures c,d), the complete conversion of bottle caps led to a slightly higher liquid yield (90.6 wt %) and a lower gaseous product yield (22.1 wt %) compared to pristine LDPE, while the almost complete conversion of shampoo bottles resulted in a lower liquid yield (83.3 wt %) that could be related to the relatively high inorganic content (owned mainly to TiO2-based additives, as discussed in Section , Table ). As can be seen in Figure SI-18b (Supporting Information), the solid recovered via the hydrogenolysis of shampoo bottles exhibited all the characteristic reflections of TiO2 in the rutile phase, while the characteristic reflections of the HDPE structure disappeared due to its complete conversion. Analysis of the liquid products revealed a similar composition of C5–C22 alkanes for the pristine HDPE polymer and the bottle cap, with higher abundance within the naphtha, gasoline, and kerosene ranges (Figure d and Figure SI-16, Supporting Information). On the other hand, when using shampoo bottles as feedstock, the carbon number distribution shifted to C5–C31 enhancing the formation of diesel and wax/lubricant alkanes. As can be seen in Figure e, the liquid product from the hydrogenolysis of bottle caps is colorless and clear which is indicative of the higher hydrogenolysis extent and alkanes of relatively low molecular weight, in contrast to the opaque solution of the liquid obtained from the inhibited hydrogenolysis of LDPE-based garbage bags.

Similar to the tests performed with the 50:50 (wt %) LDPE-HDPE mixture, a mixture comprising equal amounts of the four real plastics was also used as feedstock. The hydrogenolysis of this plastics’ physical mixture led to 94.8 wt % conversion, 71.4 wt % liquid alkanes yield, and 6.4 wt % gas yield, as can be seen in Figure SI-19 (Supporting Information). However, the liquid product was composed of C5–C37 alkanes with the majority of the compounds being in the diesel and wax/lubricants range. The shift toward higher molecular weight alkanes was attributed to the larger alkanes formed via the hydrogenolysis of garbage bags, whose conversion provided alkanes up to C80 as shown above. The high conversion of mixed plastics was also confirmed via thermogravimetric analysis. As shown in Figure SI-20, the solid recovered after the hydrogenolysis of mixed plastics exhibits a weight loss of 8% in the range of 300–450 °C, compared to the solid recovered via the hydrogenolysis of garbage bags alone which exhibited a weight loss of 63.8% in the temperature range of 400–474 °C. Consequently, the present catalytic system is resistant to relatively low amounts (or a relatively low ratio of Ca to Ru) of calcium-based additives. More specifically, calcium content with a Ca/Ru mass ratio of about 2 proved to not influence the ruthenium active sites, while a higher ratio showed high deactivation ability. In summary, as identified above for the mixture of the pristine LDPE and HDPE polymers, the performance of the real plastic mixture as feedstock is governed by the reactivity of each type of plastic product that constitutes the mixture.

Catalyst Reusability and Spent Catalysts Characterization

The reusability of 5%Ru/SAE Super was studied under the optimized conditions (250 °C, 3 h, 50 bar H2, C/F = 0.1) but using a higher concentration of LDPE in n-octane (25 wt %) in order to further investigate the potential of coprocessing plastic waste with hydrocarbons via hydrogenolysis. Compared to 5 wt % LDPE, the higher feedstock concentration did not influence the conversion and the liquid yield (Figure ). Although the yield of liquid alkanes was similar, their composition was different in terms of carbon number distribution. The liquid product obtained via hydrogenolysis of the 25 wt % LDPE feedstock was enriched in lower molecular weight alkanes in the range of naphtha and gasoline, as can be seen in Figure b and Figure SI-21a (Supporting Information), and was further confirmed via the SimDist analysis in Figure SI-21b (Supporting Information).

Effect of LDPE concentration on (a) products’ yields, conversion, and (b) composition of liquid products, and (c) products’ yields, LDPE conversion, and (d) composition of liquid products expressed as different fuel/wax fractions during the reusability tests. Reaction conditions: 5 or 25 wt % LDPE in n-octane, 5%Ru/SAE Super, C/F = 0.1, 250 °C, 3 h, 50 bar H2 initial pressure.

The results of catalyst’s (5%Ru/SAE Super) reusability tests are shown in Figures c,d. The reactivity of the catalyst remained high (∼100%) after the first two consecutive runs with fresh feed and without intermediate catalyst regeneration. The obtained liquid products comprised C5–C25 alkanes distributed mainly within naphtha, gasoline, kerosene, and diesel, based on GC-FID and SimDist analysis results (Figure d and Figure SI-22, Supporting Information). In the third consecutive run, the conversion was still high (97.8%) but a clear shift toward higher molecular weight alkanes (C5–C35) as measured by GC-FID analysis was observed, enriching the heavier fractions of diesel and wax/lubricants (Figure d and Figure SI-22, Supporting Information). This shift can be more clearly seen by the SimDist analysis via which alkanes up to C80 were identified (Figure SI-22, Supporting Information).

The characterization of spent catalysts further supported the lower hydrogenolysis activity during the third cycle. The thermogravimetric analysis of the recovered solids after the reaction (Figure a) showed distinct weight loss in the temperature range of 400–470 °C which is more pronounced for the solids after the third catalytic run compared to those of the first run, thus revealing increased coverage of the catalyst by organic species. Furthermore, it can be seen that this weight loss happens in two steps, the second (at higher temperature) being in the same temperature range as that of the TGA profile of the parent LDPE and the first at lower temperatures, being indicative of lower molecular oligomers. A similar observation was discussed above for the residues left on the used catalyst after the hydrogenolysis of LDPE at the moderate temperature of 220 °C (Section and Figure SI-9, Supporting Information). The recovered solids after the first and third runs were also characterized by XRD analysis. As can be seen in Figure b, the XRD pattern of the latter contained a clear characteristic reflection at 2θ = 21.9° attributed to pristine LDPE, thus confirming the relatively increased amount of residual polymer on the spent catalyst. It is interesting also to notice that even in the used catalyst after the third reaction cycle, no reflections due to ruthenium (metallic or oxides) can be identified, thus showing that Ru is still highly dispersed on the carbon surface. SEM and EDS characterization of this used catalyst did not reveal changes in the morphology and microscale mapping of Ru between the fresh and used (3rd cycle) catalyst (Figure SI-23, Supporting Information). XPS analysis of the used catalyst showed that the metallic Ru phase was still present along with oxidized species (+4) (Figure SI-23, Supporting Information), as was also identified on the fresh 5%Ru/SAE Super catalyst, always considering the limitations of ex-situ XPS measurements regarding the effect of atmospheric oxygen on ruthenium.

Considering the results obtained in this study under optimized conditions, the best conversion of pristine LDPE was 98.6 wt % toward 78.3 wt % n-alkanes in the range of C5–C32 and the related values for HDPE were 93.2% conversion with 87.8% liquid yield in the range of C5–C22. ConclusionsMain Outcome

This work investigated the chemical upcycling of polyethylene plastics via hydrogenolysis toward alkanes that had the potential to be utilized as fuels (gasoline, kerosene, diesel), petrochemical feedstock (naphtha), and wax/lubricants. The study included high-molecular-weight pristine LDPE and HDPE polymers, related consumer plastics (package films, garbage bags, water bottle caps, and shampoo bottles), and their mixtures, which were subjected to hydrogenolysis by using n-octane as a solvent/substrate. The effectiveness of relevant noble metals (Ru, Pt, and Re) and the effect of different micro/mesoporous carbon supports have been also investigated to identify possible structure–reactivity relationships.

The hydrogenolysis of LDPE was systematically optimized by varying the most important parameters of the process. Under the optimized conditions, 250 °C, 3 h, 50 bar H2, and a low catalyst-to-feed ratio of 0.1, complete conversion of LDPE was achieved toward 78.3 wt % liquid alkanes in the C5–C30 carbon number region, suppressing methane and total gaseous products’ yields. It was shown that LDPE conversion and product distribution were strongly influenced by reaction temperature and time, hydrogen pressure, and catalyst-to-feed ratio, along with catalyst characteristics (metal type and loading, type of carbon support). A gradual increase in reaction temperature enhanced polymer conversion and shifted selectivity from the wax/lubricant range to lighter liquids (naphtha, gasoline, kerosene, diesel) and gaseous products, favoring methane formation under more intense conditions. Prolonged reaction times promoted secondary cracking of larger alkanes toward lighter fractions, while higher hydrogen pressures favored liquid compared to gaseous products. Ruthenium, as the hydrogenation-active metal, proved to be superior to Pt and Re, confirming its known activity for C–C bond cleavage in polyethylene hydrogenolysis and in general. The properties of the carbon support influenced significantly the product selectivity, with higher support microporosity leading to enhanced selectivity toward lighter hydrocarbons in the naphtha, gasoline, and kerosene ranges. A comparison between the two pristine polymers (LDPE and HDPE) showed that HDPE exhibited slightly higher liquid alkane yields (83–91 wt %) within the naphtha, gasoline, and kerosene ranges, likely due to its lower molecular weight and linear structure. These results highlight the importance of the combined effect of process parameters and catalyst/support properties in the hydrogenolysis of polyethylene toward targeted fuel-range hydrocarbons.

The stability and reusability of the catalysts were investigated by using a higher concentration feedstock, comprising 25 wt % LDPE in n-octane, aiming to further explore the capability of coprocessing plastic waste with hydrocarbons as well as to simulate faster potential deactivation. It was shown that the 5%Ru/AC catalyst maintained nearly complete conversion during the first two consecutive cycles without regeneration, producing mainly C5–C25 alkanes. In the third cycle, the total plastic conversion remained high but reactivity was suppressed, as the product liquid alkane distribution shifted toward heavier hydrocarbons (up to C80). Thermogravimetric analysis of spent catalysts revealed increased deposition of organic species, including both oligomeric fragments and residual polymer, especially after the third run. XRD confirmed the presence of residual LDPE on the used catalyst but showed no evidence of Ru sintering or crystalline phase formation. SEM-EDS/mapping further verified that ruthenium remained highly dispersed. These findings indicate that catalyst deactivation was limited and primarily related to surface fouling rather than structural degradation of the active phase. Toward potential upscaling, the regeneration of the Ru/AC catalysts is of paramount importance and needs further dedicated investigation.

Regarding the hydrogenolysis of real consumer plastics, it was shown that the degree of conversion and product selectivity largely depended on the presence of inorganics that are usually included as additives or fillers in such commercial products. LDPE-based package film with minimal ash (inorganics) showed high conversion and liquid yield, comparable to that of pristine LDPE. In contrast, LDPE-based garbage bags exhibited substantially lower conversion and liquid yield due to the high calcium content, which likely caused partial catalyst deactivation and hindered C–C bond cleavage. The analysis of the recovered solids after the reaction, in addition to the used catalyst, showed the presence of both CaCO3 and unconverted polymer, thus supporting the above interpretation. Similarly, the HDPE-based water bottle caps exhibited complete conversion and high liquid alkane yields, in accordance with the performance of pristine HDPE. On the other hand, the shampoo bottles that contained TiO2 additives showed slightly reduced liquid yields and a shift toward heavier alkane fractions, thus indicating a deactivation effect, although to a lesser extent compared to that of CaCO3.

Overall, the present work further supported catalytic hydrogenolysis as a promising process for the chemical upcycling of polyethylene-based high molecular weight realistic plastics toward alkane mixtures, whose composition and carbon range can be tuned via the careful selection of reaction conditions and catalyst properties. The potential exploitation of this technology should further consider engineering (reactor design), catalyst long-term stability and regeneration, and most importantly the critical effect of additives and feedstock heterogeneity when processing real mixed plastic wastes of different origins.
