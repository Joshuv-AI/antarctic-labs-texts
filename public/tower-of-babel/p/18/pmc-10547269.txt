Disrupted Lives: Caregivers' Experiences of In‐Hospital Cardiac Arrest Survivors' Recovery 5 Years Later
Authors: Molly Harrod, Katrina Hauschildt, Lee A. Kamphuis, Peggy R. Korpela, Marylena Rouse, Brahmajee K. Nallamothu, Theodore J. Iwashyna
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSurvivors of in‐hospital cardiac arrest (IHCA) experience ongoing physical and cognitive impairments, often requiring support from a caregiver at home afterwards. Caregivers are important in the survivor's recovery, yet there is little research specifically focused on their experiences once the survivor is discharged home. In this study, we highlight how caregivers for veteran IHCA survivors described and experienced their caregiver role, the strategies they used to fulfill their role, and the additional needs they still have years after the IHCA event.Methods and ResultsBetween March and July 2019, semistructured telephone interviews were conducted with 12 caregivers for veteran IHCA survivors. Interviews were transcribed, and content analysis was performed. Patterns within the data were further analyzed and grouped into themes. A predominant theme of “disruption” was identified across 3 different domains including the following: (1) disruption in caregiver's life, (2) disruption in caregiver–patient relationship, and (3) disruption in caregiver's well‐being. Disruption was associated with both positive and negative caregiver experiences. Strategies caregivers used and resources they felt would have helped them adjust to their caregiver role were also identified.ConclusionsCaregivers for veteran IHCA survivors experienced a disruption in many facets of their lives. Caregivers felt the veterans' IHCA impacted various aspects of their lives, and they continued to need additional support in order to care for the IHCA survivor and themselves. Although some were able to procure coping strategies, such as counseling and engaging in stress‐relieving activities, most indicated additional help and resources were still needed.

Recovery after In‐hospital Cardiac Arrest: Late Outcomes and Utilization

Over the past decade, in‐hospital cardiac arrest (IHCA) survival rates have increased. 1 However, many survivors have ongoing health issues and are frequently readmitted to the hospital. 2 , 3 While most are discharged home, 4 , 5 they often face ongoing physical and cognitive impairments requiring support from a caregiver, 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 such as a friend or family member. 10

There is little research specifically focused on experiences of caregivers once the IHCA survivor is discharged home. A scoping review conducted by Douma et al 11 identified 41 articles published between 1987 and 2020 focused on the care and needs of family members of patients with cardiac arrest. However, only 9 focused on IHCA and described few experiences of caregivers following discharge. Studies of undifferentiated cardiac arrest or out‐of‐hospital cardiac arrest show that caregivers experience increased responsibilities in the survivor's day‐to‐day activities, performing such tasks as driving, attending and facilitating health care appointments, dispensing medications, and monitoring diet and exercise. 12 , 13 , 14 Additionally, many survivors have memory impairments and rely on their caregiver for day‐to‐day responsibilities. 14 , 15 Caregivers help survivors make sense of their cardiac arrest and their life thereafter, 16 acting as “guardians” of the survivor's well‐being and a main source of emotional support and motivation for the survivor's sense of hope and recovery. 14 , 16

Anxiety, depression, and symptoms of post‐traumatic stress disorder are prevalent among caregivers, especially in the first 12 months of recovery. 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 These mental health conditions are attributed to the added strain caregivers may experience from witnessing the event, taking on new responsibilities at home, fear of a cardiac arrest recurrence, survivors' decreased level of physical and cognitive functioning, and changes in their relationships with the survivor. 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 Caregivers also report loneliness, isolation, and putting the survivor's needs first while neglecting their own. 16 , 24 , 25

In this study, we highlight how caregivers for veteran IHCA survivors described and experienced their caregiver role, the strategies they used to fulfill their role, and the additional needs they still have. We specifically focused on IHCA because this is an under‐studied patient and caregiver population relative to out‐of‐hospital cardiac arrest, and survivors of IHCA have higher chronic disease burden in general that can put significant pressure on caregivers. Additionally, the outcomes (physical and cognitive) related to survivors of IHCA may not have been specifically attended to if the underlying disease(s) that led to their hospitalization took precedence.

Methods

Due to its sensitive nature, data will not be made available. However, study materials, including the interview guide, are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Study Design

An exploratory, qualitative study design was used to better understand how caregivers of veteran IHCA survivors experienced their role in the survivors' recovery. This study was reviewed and approved by the veterans Affairs Ann Arbor Healthcare System institutional review board and participants gave verbal informed consent.

Sampling and Recruitment

In 2014, veterans Health Administration Health Services Research and Development funded the ResCU‐1 (Recovery after In‐hospital Cardiac Arrest: Late Outcomes and Utilization) study, which provided the sampling for this subsequent study. The ResCU‐1 study used several quantitative instruments to assess health status, cognition and Activities of Daily Living at 3, 6, 9, and 12 months with 325 veterans who survived IHCA. Surveys were administered by phone and through the mail to veterans. Although not specifically asked about the role of caregivers, survey findings indicated that caregivers were involved in veterans' IHCA recovery. Based on preliminary analysis of the surveys, 10 pilot interviews with veterans were conducted at the end of ResCU‐1 and their caregivers were again included in veterans' descriptions of recovery, indicating a need to obtain more information on caregivers' perspectives.

Recruitment procedures (Figure) of veterans for ResCU‐2 have been described elsewhere but briefly, of the 325 veterans who completed surveys for ResCU‐1, 237 were still enrolled in the study at the start of ResCU‐2. 26 Of the 237, we purposefully sampled for veterans who had a range of recovery experiences. Based on the 6‐ and 12‐month assessments from ResCU‐1, we included veterans who had achieved full independence in at least 3 activities of daily living and veterans who did not or reported a decline in their activities of daily living. Using these criteria, 76 veterans were identified for possible participation. At the start of interviews for ResCU‐2 (February 2019), 44 of the 76 veterans were still alive and therefore, eligible participants.

The 44 eligible veterans were mailed a research information letter explaining the study along with an opt‐out letter to return if not interested. Those who consented and were interviewed (N=19) were asked, at the time of the interview, to identify a family member or friend who they considered to be their caregiver and might be interested in participating in a separate interview. Seventeen veterans identified a caregiver and provided their contact information. Caregivers were then mailed the research information and opt‐out letter. One week after the mailing, caregivers were contacted via phone by a study team member to determine interest; if the caregiver agreed to participate, an interview was scheduled.

Data Collection

Between March and July 2019, 12 of 17 identified caregivers completed a semistructured telephone interview (range, 24–69 minutes; mean, 43 minutes). For the 5 caregivers who did not participate, 4 were unreachable and 1 declined to be interviewed.

Open‐ended questions (Box 1: Sample of caregiver interview questions) focused on better understanding caregiver experiences after the veteran was discharged home. Questions were developed using existing literature, survey findings, pilot interviews from ResCU‐1, and study team members' experiences treating veterans who had survived IHCA and interactions with their families. After conducting 4 interviews, the interview guide was revised based on preliminary data.

Interviews were conducted by 2 to 3 study team members with 1 leading the interview and the others taking detailed notes and asking follow‐up or clarifying questions. Interviews were audio‐recorded and transcribed for analysis. Study team members reviewed the transcripts for accuracy and removal of any identifiable information. Participating caregivers received a $10 gift card for their time.

Data Analysis

Content analysis was performed using deductive and inductive codes. 27 Deductive codes were derived from a codebook created to analyze the IHCA veteran survivor interviews. Additionally, through the process of initial coding, inductive codes specifically relating to caregivers' experiences were also identified. Team members (MH, KH, LK, PK, MR) began by reading and independently coding the same transcript. We then met to review and compare, line‐by‐line, our coded transcript. New codes and definitions were added to the codebook. We continued this process, coding 2 additional transcripts as a team to determine applicability of new codes, identify additional codes, and expand or refine existing codes and definitions. The remaining 9 transcripts were divided among the team with 2 team members coding each transcript. We coded the transcript independently, met to discuss our codes, and resolved any differences. Any newly identified codes were added to the codebook and all team members were notified of the changes. Previously coded transcripts were then reviewed to apply new or refined codes. Agreed‐upon coded transcripts were then entered into QSR NVivo 12 for data management.

Code reports, where data are aggregated under the same code, were generated for review. Review of code reports allowed for comparison of coded data to ensure accuracy of code application. Further analysis of the code reports identified patterns and themes across codes. Code reports were then grouped based on patterns to create domains in which the theme occurred. Memos, with supporting quotes, were created summarizing the major themes presented below.

Results

All 12 caregivers were female family members, of whom 9 were spouses living in the same household. Others were parent (N=1), sibling (N=1), or adult child (N=1) and did not live with the veteran. The veteran IHCA survivors were 10 male and 2 female survivors with an average age of 62 years. Although most veterans had in‐home care immediately following their IHCA, at the time of the interviews, 4 to 5 years later, caregivers were continuing to provide assistance with daily tasks. These tasks included dispensing medications, providing transportation to medical appointments, grocery shopping, cooking, and offering emotional support, including acting as a main source of information for the veteran.

Regardless of the care provided, caregivers' spoke of “disruption” that occurred in their lives, across 3 different domains: (1) caregiver's life, (2) caregiver–patient relationship, and (3) caregiver's well‐being. Disruption was associated with both positive and negative caregiver experiences. For each disruption, caregivers identified the strategies they used to manage and the resources they felt would have helped them adjust to their caregiver role. Disruption in Caregiver's Day‐to‐Day Life

Ten caregivers described the transition home from the hospital as a disruption to their life. The most common change was that caregivers no longer participated in outside‐the‐home activities. Withdrawal from social activities was mainly due to caregivers' fear that a recurrence of the cardiac arrest was possible. They also felt that time was limited and wanted to spend as much of it with the veteran, and that they could not enjoy these activities because they would be worried about the veteran and if something had happened.It's gotten to the point where I would just stay home and especially if I see where he's not doing too good…because I figure, sooner or later we'd have to take him to the emergency room. (Wife, ID#112) Caregivers who did not live with the veteran spent much of their time planning and coordinating care for the veteran. Additionally, these caregivers, when compared with caregivers who lived with the veteran, were more likely to still be working and either had to take time off work or had to coordinate with other family members to arrange care.… my daughter, when I get really busy, she's a teacher, so she took a day…, and then sometimes my nephew works at a plant, so when he's not working he jumps in and he helps me with that too…it's a tag team thing. (Sibling, ID#2402)

Strategies

In addition to the withdrawal from external strategies noted above, almost all of the caregivers (N=11) re‐prioritized aspects of their lives and developed strategies that made caregiving easier. One caregiver likened this to being in “school” again and having to learn to perform the caregiver role. This was especially true for caregivers who were not the spouse of the IHCA survivor. Nine caregivers stated that they approached the disruption to their lives with a sense of obligation and a “do what you have to do” attitude.

Ongoing Needed Resources

Although 4 caregivers had additional family members helping to care for the veteran, the veteran sometimes put limits on the care they could perform. Issues of dignity and role expectation influenced what care could be administered by whom. For example, 1 caregiver told us her husband did not want their daughter to bathe him or change his diaper; this meant all the personal care tasks fell to her.

Having someone come in to perform daily household or personal care tasks or provide direct transportation for veterans to attend medical appointments were common suggested needs across caregivers. Seven caregivers stated that having outside help meant they could use that time to attend to other responsibilities or personal interests. Indeed, the caregiver in the previous paragraph felt that if a home health nurse had been available, the nurse could have helped the veteran bathe and change diapers.

Disruption to Caregiver–Patient Relationship

Three caregivers stated that their relationship with the veteran was the same or better; in contrast, 9 spoke about disruptions to their relationships that occurred after the IHCA. Post‐IHCA cognitive issues affected veterans' memories and for some, their behavior. Caregivers detailed difficulties in accepting their family member was no longer who they were before the cardiac arrest.Just accepting the fact that the partner I thought I had, I don't have…He's just not, I can't depend on him…and not that he doesn't try to, but he just can't. (Wife, ID#3008) Four caregivers spoke about how they not only had to manage the veteran's physical care but also behavior. This usually entailed trying to control the environment so as to not upset the veteran, including limiting the veterans' interaction with others or not venturing out in public. When the veteran became agitated, caregivers felt they were often the target of the veteran's anger.… his mind's still not right. Any little change just upsets him…He likes routines now. He's changed so much since all that happened…He's gotten irate so bad a couple of times it's, he's told me, “I don't even know why I married you.” So, it's been rough. (Wife, ID#476) The caregiver role was also affected by how much the caregiver thought the veteran was willing to help him/herself. If the caregiver perceived the veteran was capable of participating in their recovery but choosing not to, it often resulted in feelings of exasperation and frustration on the caregivers' part.… he has become increasingly depressed and increasingly frustrated … and I thought, ”[It's] because you won't do anything that you used to do because it's easier to say, ‘I don't, it hurts, it hurts,’” and it's really exasperating. Strategies

Formal caregivers, such as visiting nurses or aides, not only helped the veteran with their physical disabilities, but also gave the caregivers time they could use to focus on themselves. These services also provided strategies the caregiver and veteran could use to help increase independence. One caregiver, for example, noted her appreciation that palliative care offered a solution to medication management that kept the veteran involved in his own care but also addressed her concerns about safety. Three of the caregivers received counseling that was helpful in adjusting to their new role. Having someone to whom they could ask questions offered a sense of comfort and validated that the care they were providing was the “right choice.”

Ongoing Needed Resources

For caregivers who did not have ongoing formal caregiving services, they felt this service would have reduced the strain on their relationships and that the veteran might have been more willing to engage in rehabilitation activities. For example, 1 participant told us that while her husband was in the hospital, he would attend therapy sessions but once he got home, he declined to participate. But, this caregiver felt that if it was a health care provider telling the veteran he had to do home rehabilitation, he would have been more willing.

Caregivers also stated it would have been helpful if they had received therapy for themselves. For those caregivers who were dealing with the veteran's behavioral changes due to their IHCA, having someone to talk to would have helped them adjust to changes in their relationship with the patient.

Disruption to Caregiver's Well‐Being

Caregivers had both positive and negative reactions to being a caregiver. Four found strength and pride in their ability to provide care and support to the veteran.It just made me feel better that I'm able to take care of him, … so I'm proud of myself. (Wife, ID#1072) Other caregivers felt like the stress of caregiving affected their physical and mental health, especially if the veteran's personality was more volatile after their IHCA.… it's got me rundown…I'd lost like 50 pounds… It's nerve‐wracking because I don't know when one minute to the next, he's going to go off on something and I just stay on edge and my nerves… are shot. (Wife, ID#476)

Strategies

Three caregivers found it helpful to talk about their experiences with their social support system, while others stated they were not comfortable sharing their feelings, mainly because they did not want to burden their friends and family. One caregiver felt she could not share her feelings with her husband because she did not want to upset him.

Three of the caregivers sought medical help for their mental health, including being on medications or attending therapy to help them cope. Additionally, caregivers had several strategies they used to find strength to continue caregiving. One found acupuncture helpful while another did yardwork to relieve stress and still another attended a support group for caregivers. Four caregivers stated they relied on their faith to help them and sought prayer as a means of strength.

Ongoing Needed Resources

When asked what they thought would have helped or what resources were needed to adjust to caregiving, caregivers again mentioned therapy for themselves and additional support in the home to help with providing care to the veteran or performing household tasks.

Discussion

Disruption to life, where the time after the event no longer resembles the time before, has been reported among cardiac arrest (CA) survivors. 16 , 28 In our study, we found that veterans' caregivers experienced disruption in 3 main aspects of their lives: in day‐to‐day activities, in relationships with the veteran, and in well‐being. A few studies have specifically examined the disruption caregivers experience after a family member's CA. For example, Whitehead et al 28 found that out‐of‐hospital CA survivors and their partners experience a “disrupted normality” where there is a “fracturing between the past (perceived as predicable and understandable) and a future shaped by uncertainty and insecurity,” (p. 85). Although this study included the partners of survivors, the emphasis remained on how the survivor coped with disruption. A study by Bremer et al 16 also found that significant others experience a disruption in their lives and tend to focus on the recovery of the CA survivor while ignoring their own needs. Our study furthers these findings by specifically focusing on caregivers' experiences with disruption to their lives while caring for the veteran IHCA survivor, the strategies they used to cope with these disruptions, and their suggestions for ongoing support resources.

Woven into the caregivers' stories of disruption was a sense of loss some of these caregivers experienced due to the IHCA incident. Even though most of the veterans had formal caregiving services following their CA, which helped to alleviate some of the caregivers' strain, caregivers still struggled with the changes that occurred in their lives.

In their day‐to‐day activities, caregivers either limited or no longer participated in outside‐the‐home activities. They instead chose to remain close to the veteran in case something happened, despite the fact that these interviews occurred years after the CA. However, this choice limited the caregivers’ social support network and sometimes led to a sense of loneliness. Boyce et al 17 found a similar withdrawal from desired social activities, resulting in isolation, among spouses of CA survivors. Wallin et al 24 noted that caregiver's withdrawal from social activities due to concern for the CA survivor's health lasted up to 6 months after the event. Our work suggests that withdrawal and isolation may continue several years after an IHCA event. Caregivers shared that having ongoing formal in‐home caregiving services would increase their comfort level and give them time for themselves. A few caregivers spoke of taking time off work and forgoing social activities in order to perform caregiving tasks. Even among caregivers who did not live with the veteran, coordinating care took effort and time, suggesting that both financial and social burdens are possible disruptions caregivers face.

Our study found that there was variation in how caregivers described their relationships with veterans post‐IHCA. Some caregivers felt their relationship was the same or had improved while others struggled with the physical and behavioral changes that occurred because of the veteran's IHCA. Like the findings of Eriksson et al 15 , a few caregivers found strength and pride in being able to care for the veteran. However, many of the caregivers felt the veteran's IHCA and its resulting effects had affected their own mental health. Similar to Wallin et al, 24 our study found that caregivers often withdrew from their social networks and ignored their own needs. Thus, mental health conditions may be exacerbated by not only the care caregivers provide but also by the resulting social isolation.

Some of the caregivers in this study sought care or support for the stress of caregiving or its impacts on their mental health. Individual counseling and group counseling for caregivers could provide the emotional and social support caregivers need by offering the ability to share experiences with others, which may counteract social isolation. Additionally, having access to and receiving counseling may help both the caregiver and the IHCA survivor's relationship as they adjust to the changes.

Unsurprisingly, formal caregiving services figured predominantly in caregivers' suggestions of support, and the impact of such support is likely multifaceted. Some caregivers of veterans receiving formal caregiving services felt these services not only provided a reprieve from caregiving but also offered strategies the caregiver and veteran could utilize to engage the veteran in his/her health care and recovery. We found that caregivers valued veterans' efforts towards involvement in their care and independence, distinct from the veterans' actual ability to be independent.

Currently, there is a lack of interventions specifically focused on supporting the caregiver of an IHCA survivor. Caregivers are often not offered additional support and resources because it is assumed they are competent and capable of performing the role. 25 , 29 Interventions proposed focus on screening for psychosocial needs and offering personal, social, and practical support. For example, Haywood and Dainty 18 suggest educating primary care providers on screening to identify psychosocial needs and utilizing caregivers as experience‐based experts to help with designing interventions. Others propose involving caregivers in discharge planning, rehabilitation services, and follow‐up appointments. 16 , 24 However, due to the stress that surrounds discharge to home, involving community support and outpatient settings may be more helpful to caregivers in supporting their needs. 23 In our study, we found that many caregivers wanted ongoing, in‐home support in providing various types of care including, for the IHCA survivor, rehabilitation and support with activities of daily living, and, for the caregiver, a general reprieve from their caregiving duties.

In a study known as the ALASCA (Activity and Life After Survival of a Cardiac Arrest), specially trained nurses delivered an intervention to both CA survivors and their caregivers to address the physical and emotional consequences of CA. 30 This intervention also focused on promoting self‐management strategies and procuring specialized care if indicated. Although caregivers were positive about the intervention, the study found that in terms of outcome measures for quality of life, caregiver strain, and emotional state, they did not experience significant improvements. 30 This suggests the intervention did not fully address caregiver concerns and more research is necessary to identify what caregivers need to support them and the CA survivor's recovery process.

The most common suggestion among the caregivers in this study was the need for in‐home help. Given that most of our participants had some form of formal caregiving following the veterans' IHCA and yet still felt more was needed, indicates that what is being offered may be needed for a longer amount of time, possibly permanently, and may not fully address all needs, especially caregivers' personal needs. This is particularly important considering the conflict some veterans feel between their dignity and having theoretically available informal caregivers (eg, daughters) provide certain services. Many CA survivors are discharged home without home health services, 31 and caregivers who do not have formal caregiver support may fare far worse in their adaptation to this role, potentially impacting both the caregivers' and CA survivors' long‐term well‐being.

Our findings are consonant with a growing literature on recovery after critical illness that generally suggests that patients' and caregivers' experiences are shaped by and need to be understood in a social context, 32 , 33 , 34 particularly existing social networks, access to both general and specialty medical care, and the financial resources available for the continuing costs of recovery. 35 , 36 , 37 , 38 Further, as noted by our participants, the impact of caregiving produces strain across and reshapes caregivers' social lives, not just in their specific relationship with the CA survivor, thus shaping the resources and social capital available for ongoing coping. 39 , 40 Interventions that acknowledge and address the impact of CA survivorship and caregiving demands on patients' and caregivers' broader well‐being may prove particularly beneficial across multiple domains of recovery. For example, McPeake and colleagues 41 provided optional social welfare consultation as part of a broader intensive care unit rehabilitation program, demonstrating both the need and feasibility of such an intervention (>30% of patients requested consultation). Programs that allow for direct financial assistance to veterans' caregivers have also been shown to provide multiple benefits to patients and caregivers. 42 , 43 While most of the direct medical costs faced by veteran CA survivors may be addressed by the veterans Administration's integrated health system, caregivers in our study suggest that indirect costs, such as lost income or those related to health care needs of their own, may still be an undersupported burden.

This study has limitations. First, all of the caregivers were female, so the experiences of male caregivers remain understudied. Second, many of the veterans received in‐home health care after their IHCA and therefore, may not be representative of the general population who do not have this same support. However, caregivers, even with this initial support, felt that long‐term, ongoing, in‐home help would have aided them in performing the caregiver role. Third, the sample size of 12 caregivers is small and may not be generalizable. However, the aim of the study was to explore caregiver experiences in‐depth and identify strategies and continuing needs from the caregiver perspective, and our interviews produced rich data about these experiences. We also acknowledge that discussing additional themes (eg, fear) was beyond the scope (and space) of this article. Finally, the length of time since the veterans' IHCA averaged between 4 and 5 years at the time of the interview, so information about needs early in the process may be lacking in detail. On the other hand, few studies have examined caregiver needs years after patients' IHCAs, and we found that even with the passage of time, caregivers identified continuing unmet needs.

Conclusions

Caregivers for veteran IHCA survivors experienced a disruption in many facets of their lives. Caregivers felt the veteran's IHCA impacted various aspects of their own lives and they continued to need additional support caring for the IHCA survivor and themselves. Although some caregivers were able to procure coping strategies such as counseling and engaging in stress‐relieving activities, they indicated additional help and resources were still needed several years after the initial IHCA event, suggesting there is no time limit on recovery.
