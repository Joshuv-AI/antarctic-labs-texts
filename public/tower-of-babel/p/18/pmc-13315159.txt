Dual Effects of Nicotinamide on Aging‐Related Arrhythmia: Protective at Low Dose, Proarrhythmic at Higher Doses
Authors: Liangyu Hu, Xi Qi, Yun Hu, Alexia van Rinsum, Jaap Keijer, Deli Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCardiac arrhythmia and dysfunction increase with age and are closely associated with declining nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide levels. Nicotinamide, a critical nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide precursor, has shown protective effects in experimental models of aging‐related cardiac diseases. However, its dose‐dependent cardiac‐specific adverse effects and underlying mechanisms warrant investigation.MethodsThe effects of nicotinamide (10–100 mmol/L) on the contractility and calcium transient of HL‐1 cardiomyocytes, lifespan, and cardiac function of Drosophila, as well as arrhythmia susceptibility of ex vivo Langendorff mouse hearts were assessed. SERCA2a (sarcoplasmic/endoplasmic reticulum Ca2+‐ATPase 2a) acetylation was analyzed by immunoprecipitation followed by Western blotting.ResultsAcute perfusion with 10 mmol/L nicotinamide had limited influence on aging‐related atrial fibrillation susceptibility in ex vivo mouse and Drosophila hearts. Short‐term dietary nicotinamide (10 mmol/L) in late life protected against aging‐induced arrhythmia and contractile dysfunction exclusively in male Drosophila. In contrast, lifelong exposure or doses >20 mmol/L caused dose‐dependent adverse cardiac effects, including impaired contractility and calcium transient amplitude and kinetics in HL‐1 cardiomyocyte and shortened lifespan in Drosophila, with impaired excitability and increased arrhythmia in both models. In ex vivo mouse hearts, 100 mmol/L nicotinamide induced ventricular arrhythmia and cardiac arrest.ConclusionsNicotinamide exhibits dual effects on aging‐related arrhythmia and cardiac dysfunction, with its efficacy highly dependent on dose, duration, and biological context. Although a moderate dose in late life may be protective, chronic or excessive intake induces arrhythmia and impairs cardiac function across species, likely through disrupted cardiac excitability and dysregulated calcium handling. These findings underscore the importance of cautious and context‐specific application of nicotinamide in clinical settings.

Aging, a major risk factor for cardiac diseases, drives the progressive decline of cardiac function. 1 With the increase in older people in populations, the prevalence of aging‐associated cardiac diseases, such as atrial fibrillation (AF) 2 and heart failure, 3 has increased dramatically, leading to a substantial economic burden on health care systems. Aging is also associated with a reduction in nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+) levels, 4 a critical coenzyme involved in energy metabolism, redox balance, and signaling pathways essential for cardiac function. 5 , 6 Lower levels of NAD+ have been implicated in increased AF susceptibility and cardiac dysfunction. 7 , 8 NAD+‐boosting compounds thus represent attractive therapeutic candidates for aging‐related arrhythmogenesis and cardiac dysfunction.

Nicotinamide is a form of vitamin B3 and a key precursor for NAD+ biosynthesis. 9 Supplementation with nicotinamide has shown protective effects in aging‐related cardiometabolic disorders, including AF and heart failure. A previous study revealed that nicotinamide ameliorated aging‐induced diastolic dysfunction in 24‐month‐old mice by normalizing NAD+ levels. 10 Our previous data showed that 10 mmol/L nicotinamide reduces AF‐induced contractile dysfunction in tachypaced HL‐1 cardiomyocytes and Drosophila models, 11 although it remains unclear whether similar beneficial effects could be recapitulated in aging‐related AF in mammalian models. In addition, emerging evidence suggests that the timing and duration of nicotinamide intervention also influence its therapeutic outcomes. For instance, postischemic nicotinamide intervention improved ischemic stroke, whereas longer preischemic exposure worsened infarct size and induced neurological deficits. 12 Similarly, in a rat brain‐injury model, nicotinamide improved cognitive recovery only when delivered within 4 hours postinjury, with little benefit beyond that time window. 13 These findings underscore the importance of a more comprehensive understanding of optimal dosing regimens to maximize the therapeutic potential of nicotinamide.

Although low‐to‐moderate doses are often beneficial, growing concerns have been raised about the possible adverse effects of high‐dose nicotinamide and the risks of long‐term use. 14 Nicotinamide concentrations >20 mmol/L have been reported to induce cytotoxicity and cell death. 15 Dietary intervention with up to 32.77 mmol/L nicotinamide/kg caused detrimental metabolic and epigenetic changes in developing rats. 16 However, the potential adverse effects of high‐dose nicotinamide on arrhythmogenesis and cardiac function remain largely uncertain. Cardiac arrhythmias and sudden cardiac arrest are frequently driven by reduced myocardial excitability and dysregulated calcium (Ca2+) handling, 17 , 18 which together underlie triggered activity and conduction instability in the heart. Notably, nicotinamide is an inhibitor of NAD+‐dependent sirtuins (SIRT) deacetylases, 19 known to regulate Ca2+ homeostasis by regulating ion pumps such as SERCA2a (sarcoplasmic/endoplasmic reticulum Ca2+‐ATPase 2a), 20 , 21 and nicotinamide has been reported to modulate the cardiac sodium channel and late currents. 22 These observations raise the possibility that high‐dose nicotinamide may promote arrhythmia and cardiac dysfunction by impairing cardiac electrophysiological signaling and Ca2+ handling.

To address the abovementioned research gaps, we here study the effect of previously established 10 mmol/L nicotinamide on aging‐associated cardiac arrhythmia in mouse hearts as a mammalian model and the acute effect of 10 mmol/L nicotinamide in Drosophila heart preparations, and further explore the potential cardiac‐specific (adverse) effects of higher nicotinamide doses using ex vivo mouse hearts, cardiomyocytes, and adult Drosophila models. Our findings revealed that long‐term nicotinamide intervention or concentrations >20 mmol/L increase arrhythmia incidence and exert detrimental effects on cardiac function, at least partially by modulating Ca2+ handling and SERCA2a activity through acetylation. This raises concerns on the safe and effective applications of nicotinamide, particularly in aging contexts, and highlights the need for careful dose and duration considerations in both experimental research and potential clinical use.

METHODS

The data supporting the findings of this study are available within the article and its supplementary files.

Ex Vivo Mouse Heart Electrophysiological Measurement

All animal procedures were ethically approved by the Animal Welfare Committee of Wageningen University (2020.W‐0019.009). B6JRccHsd(B6J)‐Nnt +/Wuhap (Nnt wt) male and female mice, an in‐house bred mouse line, housed with wood chips and enriched with wood shavings under a 12‐hour light/dark cycle with ad libitum access to water and food, were used for ex vivo electrophysiological measurement. Mice were heparinized and then euthanized by cervical dislocation. Hearts were explanted, cannulated, and connected to the Langendorff system (130102EZ‐220, ADInstruments) perfused with modified Tyrode's solution containing 130 mmol/L NaCl, 4 mmol/L KCl, 1.8 mmol/L CaCl2, 1 mmol/L MgCl2, 5.5 mmol/L glucose, 1.2 mmol/L NaH2PO4, and 24 mmol/L NaHCO3, pH 7.4, at constant oxygenation with carbogen. A water thermoregulator was used to maintain constant temperature (37 °C ± 0.5 °C) for the complete Langendorff system. Cardiac ECGs were recorded using 3 electrodes (MLA1213 and MLA1212; ADInstruments). The positive electrode was placed beneath the apex of the left ventricle, whereas the negative and ground electrodes were positioned on the cannula (Figure 1A). Recordings were acquired with a bio‐amplifier recording system (FE231; ADInstruments). Programmed electrical stimulation was applied using a stimulus isolator (FE180; ADInstruments) via a spring‐loaded platinum electrode (140157M‐STISO; AD Instruments) placed on the surface of the right atrium (Figure 1A). All data were acquired using the 16‐channel PowerLab system (PL3516/P; ADInstruments).

Hearts from 18‐month‐old male and female mice were used to test the effects of perfusion of 10 mmol/L nicotinamide. Twelve hearts were perfused with the control modified Tyrode's solution, and 10 hearts were perfused with 10 mmol/L nicotinamide‐supplemented solution throughout the whole procedure. Each heart in the Langendorff system was stabilized for 20 minutes, followed by a 1‐hour perfusion before the electrophysiological measurement. In the electrophysiological measurement, the pacing capture threshold was determined by applying a series of 2‐millisecond current pulses at 6 Hz and 8 Hz for consistency of stimulus capture. Then the AF susceptibility was assessed using programmed electrical stimulation, because pacing is necessary to induce arrhythmia in isolated mouse hearts, where spontaneous events are rare. 23 , 24 The programmed electrical stimulation was performed using 10‐second electrical burst pacing with a cycle length of 20 milliseconds (50 Hz) at a 3‐time capture threshold current for 3 times, with a 30‐second recovery period between each assessment. 25 AF was characterized by rapid and irregular atrial electrograms >0.5 seconds. 26 An AF‐positive heart is defined when AF occurs in at least 2 out of 3 repeats, and an AF trend heart is defined when AF occurs in 1 out of 3 repeats. AF duration was quantified as the time from AF onset after pacing to spontaneous termination. AF burden was calculated as the cumulative AF duration divided by the total monitoring time.

Hearts from 6‐month‐old male and female mice were used to assess the effects of perfusion of 100 mmol/L nicotinamide (n=6), 100 mmol/L glucose (n=5), or 50 mmol/L NaCl (n=5), with the latter 2 serving as isosmotic controls. Each heart in the Langendorff system was stabilized for 20 minutes before switching to the respective perfusates until the onset of ventricular fibrillation (VF) or cardiac arrest. The diastolic interval was quantified from ECG recordings by subtracting the corrected QT intervals according to the Bazett method 27 from the RR intervals. 28 Heart tissue was then snap‐frozen and stored at −80 °C for further molecular analysis.

HL‐1 Cardiomyocyte Culture and Contractile Function

HL‐1 cardiomyocytes were cultured in cell culture flasks or 6‐well plates coated with 0.02% gelatin (G1393; Sigma) in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂ at 37 °C. The cells were maintained in complete Claycomb medium (51800C; Sigma) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (TMS‐016‐B; Sigma), 100 U/mL penicillin–streptomycin (15140122; Thermo Fisher), 2 mmol/L L‐glutamine (25030024; Thermo Fisher), and 100 μmol/L norepinephrine (A0937; Sigma).

The arrhythmia index, defined as the standard deviation of the cycle length normalized to the median cycle length, was determined by analyzing the spontaneous contractions of HL‐1 cardiomyocytes, without electrical stimulation. Contractility parameters, including cell shortening (peak amplitude change), time to peak, and peak to baseline duration of HL‐1 cardiomyocytes, were measured under electrical field stimulation (40 V, 1 Hz, 10 milliseconds pulse duration) using a C‐Pace100 culture pacer (IonOptix). In both cases, high‐speed (120 frames per second) optical recordings of beating cells were acquired and analyzed using IonWizard 7.8 software (IonOptix). Data analysis was performed using CytoSolver software (IonOptix).

Before contractility measurements, HL‐1 cardiomyocytes were treated with nicotinamide (N0636; Sigma) at concentrations of 0, 10, 20, 50, or 100 mmol/L for 4 hours. Because a 1‐hour incubation with 100 mmol/L nicotinamide abolished spontaneous contractions (Video S1), only concentrations of 0, 10, 20, or 50 mmol/L nicotinamide were included for the 4 hours for arrhythmia index assessment.

Ca2+ Transient Measurements in HL‐1 Cardiomyocytes

HL‐1 cardiomyocytes were cultured in 35‐mm glass‐bottom dishes (P35G‐1.5‐14‐C; Mattek) and incubated with nicotinamide (N0636; Sigma) at concentrations of 0, 10, 20, 50, or 100 mmol/L for 4 hours. Cells were then loaded with 1 μg/mL Ca2+‐sensitive Fluo‐4‐AM dye (F14201; Invitrogen) for 30 minutes at 37 °C in 5% CO2, followed by 3 washes with Advanced DMEM/F‐12 (12 634 028; Thermo Fisher), Thereafter, the Fluo‐4 loaded HL‐1 cardiomyocytes were incubated with Claycomb medium and stabilized in the climate chamber with 37 °C in 5% CO2, and then imaged on a confocal laser scanning microscope (FV4000; Evident) using a ×40 objective, with excitation at 488 nm and emission collected at 500 to 540 nm.

The spontaneous calcium transient (CaT) was determined under conditions without electrical stimulation to measure intrinsic spontaneous contractions and calculate arrhythmia index based on the standard deviations of the peak‐to‐peak intervals. Thereafter, the electrical field stimulation (40 V, 1 Hz, 10 milliseconds pulse duration) using a C‐Pace100 culture pacer (IonOptix) was applied to measure and compare steady‐state CaT amplitude, time to peak, and duration, quantified as full width at 80%, 50%, and 20% of peak amplitude. In both spontaneous and stimulated conditions, CaT were recorded in a line‐scan round‐trip mode with a speed of 0.488 milliseconds per line. Each line‐scan recording consisted of 20 000 lines for nonpaced conditions, and 10 000 lines for paced conditions. Image analysis was performed using the Plot Profile function in Image J (version 1.52), followed by peak analysis with the Spiky plugin. 29

Drosophila Lifespan

Wild‐type W1118 Drosophila melanogaster were incubated at 25 °C in a 12‐hour light/dark cycle. A rearing food containing (in grams per liter) 17 agar (76050048.5000; Boom), 54 glucose (G8270; Sigma), 26 yeast (903 312; MP Biomedicals), and 1.3 nipagin (H3647; Sigma) was used as control food.

Within 8 hours after eclosion, virgin female and male flies were separated and randomly distributed into a control or dietary intervention group containing nicotinamide at concentrations of 10, 20, 50, and 100 mmol/L. Each group consisted of approximately 60 to 80 male and female flies, with 20 flies per tube. All flies were transferred to fresh food every 2 days, and mortality was recorded.

Dietary and Acute Nicotinamide Interventions in Drosophila

To test the chronic effects of 10 mmol/L nicotinamide on aging‐related arrhythmogenesis and cardiac function, flies were fed either control or 10 mmol/L nicotinamide‐supplemented diets at different times and durations, either starting from day 0 for 5 weeks (lifelong) or starting at week 4 for 1 week (late life). Flies at 5 weeks old are generally considered to show physiological aging. 30 , 31 To examine the detrimental effects of high‐dose nicotinamide on cardiac function, flies were fed either control diet or a 100‐mmol/L nicotinamide‐supplemented diet from day 0 for 1 week.

To test the acute effects of 10 mmol/L nicotinamide on cardiac function in Drosophila, semi‐intact heart preparations from 1‐week‐old flies were incubated with artificial hemolymph containing 10 mmol/L nicotinamide, or control vesicle for 1 hour 20 minutes, resembling the perfusion duration used in ex vivo mouse heart perfusion experiments. Cardiac function was subsequently analyzed in these semi‐intact heart preparations as described below. For acute exposure to 100 mmol/L nicotinamide, each semi‐intact Drosophila heart preparation was recorded immediately upon compound addition until the onset of cardiac arrest.

Cardiac Function Measurement in Drosophila

The cardiac function was measured in semi‐intact Drosophila heart preparations using a previously established method. 30 Briefly, after anesthetizing the flies with FlyNap (6353‐173010; VOS Instruments) for ≈2 minutes, the heart tubes were exposed by removing the head and internal organs with microsurgery forceps. Dissections were performed in oxygenated artificial hemolymph containing 108 mmol/L NaCl, 5 mmol/L KCl, 2 mmol/L CaCl2, 8 mmol/L MgCl2, 15 mmol/L HEPES, 1 mmol/L NaH2PO4, 4 mmol/L NaHCO3, 10 mmol/L sucrose, and 5 mmol/L trehalose, pH 7.1, at room temperature (RT). Heart activity was recorded for 20 seconds at 160 frames per second using a high‐speed digital camera (DMK 33UX174; Imaging Source) mounted on a Leica DM IL LED microscope with a ×10 lens and captured by IC Capture 2.5 software (The Imaging Source).

The cardiac physiology of the flies was assessed using a semiautomated optical heartbeat analysis program that quantifies functional parameters, including heart rate, diastolic and systolic intervals, contraction and relaxation phase intervals, fractional shortening, and shortening velocity. 30 , 32 Additionally, the arrhythmia index was calculated from the standard deviation of the heart period normalized to the median heart period. Incidence and burden of fibrillation events were evaluated based on M‐mode cardiography records of heart edge movement over time, produced through semiautomated optical heartbeat analysis.

NAD Assay

Total nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NADt = NAD+ [the oxidized form of NAD] + NADH [the reduced form of NAD]) and NADH levels were measured according to the manufacturer's instructions of the assay kit (ab65348; Abcam). In short, mouse right atrial tissues were lysed in NADH/NAD Extraction Buffer (ab65348; Abcam) and centrifuged for 5 minutes at 4 °C at 21 300g. To measure NADt, after equalizing the protein concentration of the supernatant, 50 μL of each sample was mixed with 100 μL reaction mix containing 98 μL NAD cycling buffer (ab65348; Abcam) and 2 μL NAD cycling enzyme mix (ab65348; Abcam), and incubated at RT for 5 minutes to convert NAD+ to NADH. Then, 10 μL NADH developer buffer (ab65348; Abcam) was added and incubated at RT for 3 hours. To measure NADH, NAD+ in each sample was decomposed by incubation at 60 °C for 30 minutes before measurement. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a plate reader (235 197; BioSPX) to determine NADt and NADH levels.

Immunoprecipitation and Western Blotting

Left ventricular tissue (n=3) from mouse hearts perfused with control perfusate or 100 mmol/L nicotinamide was homogenized in immunoprecipitation buffer containing 50 mmol/L Tris–HCl pH 7.4, 0.5% Triton X‐100, 150 mmol/L NaCl, 1 mmol/L ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid, 1 μmol/L trichostatin A, 10 mmol/L nicotinamide, and protease (04693159001; Roche) and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (04906837001; Roche). The homogenates were centrifuged at 1000g for 10 minutes at 4 °C. Equal amounts of protein (600 μg) were incubated with antibodies against Pan Acetylation (66289‐1‐Ig; Proteintech) or mouse IgG (immunoglobulin G) (I‐2000‐1; Vector Laboratories) as negative control at 4 °C on a rotation wheel at 12 rpm overnight. The homogenates were then incubated with protein‐G/A agarose beads (88 802; Fisher Scientific) at 4 °C for 2 hours. The resulting protein‐bead complexes were washed 3 times with immunoprecipitation buffer before elution at 95 °C for 5 minutes with the elution buffer containing immunoprecipitation buffer mixed with 50 mmol/L DTT and 1x NuPAGE LDS sample buffer (NP0007).

For Western blotting analysis, proteins were separated by SDS‐PAGE (NP0323BOX; Invitrogen) at 110 V for 30 minutes and then 150 V for 60 minutes in the XCell SureLock system (EI0001; Novex) and transferred to a methanol‐activated polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane (IPFL85R; Sigma) at 300 mA on ice for 1.5 hours. The membrane was then blocked with Intercept Blocking Buffer (927–60 001; LI‐COR) for 1 hour at RT, and incubated with primary antibodies against SERCA2a (1:1000 dilution, 4388; Cell Signaling), and Pan Acetylation (1:2000 dilution, 66 289‐1‐Ig; Proteintech) overnight at 4 °C. The membrane was subsequently incubated with secondary antibodies IRDye 680RD Donkey anti‐rabbit (1:5000 dilution, 926‐68 073; LI‐COR) or IRDye 800CW Donkey anti‐mouse (1:5000 dilution, 926‐32 212; LI‐COR) for 1 hour at RT, and visualized using an Odyssey scanner (LI‐COR).

Statistical Analysis

All the comparisons and graphs were made using GraphPad Prism software (version 9.5.1). AF incidence of ex vivo mouse hearts or fibrillation event incidence of Drosophila were tested using the Fisher exact test. AF episodes count and burden of ex vivo mouse hearts or fibrillation burden of Drosophila were tested using the nonparametric Scheirer‐Ray‐Hare test followed by Dunn post hoc test. The chronic effects of 10 mmol/L or 100 mmol/L nicotinamide on cardiac function in Drosophila, acute effect of 10 mmol/L nicotinamide on cardiac function in Drosophila heart preparations, and NAD+ levels in atrial tissue from control and 10 mmol/L nicotinamide‐perfused aged mouse hearts were tested by 2‐way ANOVA followed by a post hoc Fisher least significant difference (LSD) test. Contractility and CaT parameters among different doses of nicotinamide in HL‐1 cardiomyocytes were tested by 1‐way ANOVA followed by a post hoc Fisher's LSD test. For parameters with heavy right‐skewed distributions (arrhythmia index and diastolic intervals), statistical analyses were performed on log10‐transformed data, whereas other parameters were analyzed using the original data. All data were plotted on the original scale for visualization. Differences between survival curves of Drosophila were analyzed by the log‐rank test. The effects of high‐dose nicotinamide on SERCA2a acetylation in ex vivo mouse hearts, as well as NAD+ levels, were tested by a two‐tailed unpaired Student t test. R peak amplitude and diastolic intervals of ex vivo mouse hearts before and after 100 mmol/L nicotinamide perfusion were tested by the Wilcoxon matched‐pairs signed rank test.

RESULTS

Acute Treatment of 10 mmol/L Nicotinamide Did Not Prevent Aging‐Related AF in Ex Vivo 18‐Month‐Old Mouse Hearts or in Drosophila

Our previous study showed that 10 mmol/L nicotinamide protected against tachypacing‐induced contractile dysfunction in HL‐1 cardiomyocytes and Drosophila, primarily by restoring NAD+ levels. 7 , 11 Because aging is the most common risk factor for AF and is known to reduce NAD+ levels, 33 , 34 we further studied whether 10 mmol/L nicotinamide could also protect against aging‐related AF using isolated 18‐month‐old mouse hearts on the Langendorff system with programmed electrical pacing (Figure 1A and 1B). We found that hearts from 18‐month‐old mice exhibited a decline of NAD+ and NAD+/NADH ratio (Figure S1), and displayed a considerable incidence of pacing‐induced AF, especially in females (≈33.3% AF positive and ≈33.3% AF trend, Figure 1C). However, supplementation of 10 mmol/L nicotinamide in the perfusate did not result in significant changes in AF incidence, episode count, or burden compared with controls (Figure 1B through 1E). Accordingly, acute perfusion with 10 mmol/L nicotinamide did not significantly increase myocardial NAD+ levels (Figure S2), which may explain the lack of antiarrhythmic efficacy of acute 10 mmol/L nicotinamide perfusion.

In addition, a similar acute exposure regimen with 10 mmol/L nicotinamide was examined in semi‐intact Drosophila heart preparations (Figure S3A). Compared with controls, 10 mmol/L nicotinamide incubation did not significantly influence contractile function in either male or female fly hearts, as indicated by diastolic and systolic intervals (Figure S3B and S3C), relaxation and contraction phase intervals (Figure S3D and S3E), fractional shortening (Figure S3F), and shortening velocity (Figure S3G). Furthermore, no significant differences were observed between 10 mmol/L nicotinamide‐incubated and control Drosophila hearts in heart rate (Figure S3H), arrhythmia index (Figure S3I), as well as incidence and burden of fibrillation events (Figure S3J and S3K) in both sexes. In summary, acute exposure to 10 mmol/L nicotinamide did not alleviate AF in ex vivo aged mouse hearts, nor did it affect contractions in semi‐intact Drosophila heart preparations.

Effects of Different Timing and Chronic Intervention Durations of 10 mmol/L Nicotinamide on Aging‐Related Arrhythmia and Contractile Dysfunction in Drosophila

Because acute perfusion of 10 mmol/L nicotinamide failed to attenuate pacing‐induced AF in old ex vivo mouse hearts and did not affect either contractile function or cardiac rhythm of semi‐intact Drosophila heart preparations, we next investigated whether different timing and chronic nicotinamide intervention durations influence aging‐related arrhythmia. Using Drosophila as a recognized model for aging, 31 flies were fed with a 10‐mmol/L nicotinamide‐supplemented diet either for 1 week before measurements or for 5 weeks throughout their lifespan before assessment at 5 weeks old (Figure 2A). Our results showed that 5‐week‐old fly hearts recapitulated aging‐induced arrhythmia and cardiac contractile dysfunction, characterized by prolonged diastolic and systolic intervals, relaxation and contraction phase intervals, and increased arrhythmia index, as well as reduced heart rate and shortening velocity compared with 1‐week‐old control flies (Figure 2B through 2H, Figure S4A and S4B). Interestingly, short‐term (1 week in week 5) dietary intervention of 10 mmol/L nicotinamide ameliorated aging‐induced prolongation of diastolic intervals (Figure 2C) and the arrhythmia index (Figure 2E), exclusively in male flies, without significant influences on systolic intervals (Figure 2D), heart rate (Figure 2F), or shortening velocity (Figure 2H). Surprisingly, a lifelong (5 weeks) intervention with 10 mmol/L nicotinamide did not alleviate aging‐induced arrhythmia in male flies and even increased the arrhythmia index in female flies (Figure 2E), although it prevented the heart rate reduction in 5‐week‐old female flies (Figure 2F). Additionally, the fibrillation burden following 5 weeks of 10 mmol/L nicotinamide treatment was greater than that observed with the 1‐week intervention in week 5 in female (Figure S4F) but not in male flies (Figure S4E). No significant differences in fractional shortening (Figure 2G) and fibrillation event incidence (Figure S4C and S4D) were found among all groups. Together, these findings suggest that shorter‐term in late life, but not lifelong, chronic intervention with 10 mmol/L nicotinamide alleviates aging‐induced arrhythmia and contractile dysfunction in Drosophila in a sex‐specific manner.

Dose‐Dependent Effect of Nicotinamide on Arrhythmia Incidence, Contractile Function, and Calcium Transients of HL‐1 Cardiomyocytes

Given that a long‐term intervention with 10 mmol/L nicotinamide exhibited proarrhythmic effects in female flies (Figure 2E), we hypothesized that higher doses of nicotinamide might similarly exert adverse effects on the arrhythmia incidence and cardiac contractile function. To test this, we determined the impact of a series of high nicotinamide doses on arrhythmia index and cardiac contractility in HL‐1 cardiomyocytes. A dose of 50 mmol/L nicotinamide tended to increase the arrhythmia index (P = 0.14, Figure 3A and 3B) and diastolic intervals (P = 0.10, Figure 3A and 3C) in spontaneously beating HL‐1 cardiomyocytes, whereas no statistically significant changes were observed at 20 mmol/L. Notably, 100 mmol/L nicotinamide treatment totally abolished the spontaneous contractions of HL‐1 cardiomyocytes (Video S1), indicating blocking of excitability at this dose. In addition, nicotinamide exhibited a dose‐dependent effect on cellular contractility under pacing, where 10 mmol/L nicotinamide had no significant effects compared with the control, whereas higher doses (20, 50, and 100 mmol/L) progressively reduced contractility as measured by cell shortening percentage (Figure 3D and 3E), and shortened systolic phase and diastolic phase contraction duration (Figure 3F and 3G).

To investigate whether these contractile defects were associated with changes in Ca2+ handling, we assessed intracellular CaT in HL‐1 cardiomyocytes. Increasing doses of nicotinamide resulted in a gradual decrease of CaT amplitude, reaching statistical significance at 100 mmol/L nicotinamide under steady‐state 1 Hz stimulation (Figure 4A through 4D). The time to peak CaT was not significantly altered across groups (Figure 4E), whereas high doses of nicotinamide (20 mmol/L, 50 mmol/L, and 100 mmol/L) significantly decreased the full duration width at 20%, 50%, or 80% (full width at 20%, 50%, and 80%) of the peak amplitude (Figure 4F through 4H), consistent with the reduced contraction duration to 80% baseline (Figure 3G). These findings reflect inhibited total Ca2+ amplitude upon electrical stimulation and accelerated Ca2+ clearance during the diastolic phase. Moreover, we measured spontaneous CaT without external electric stimulation to evaluate intrinsic Ca2+ dynamics. Higher doses of nicotinamide (100 mmol/L) significantly reduced the CaT frequency (Figure 4I and 4J), and 20 mmol/L nicotinamide tended to increase the CaT arrhythmia index (Figure 4I and 4K), which was consistent with the proarrhythmic effects observed in contractile activity (Figure 3B). Collectively, these results demonstrate that high‐dose nicotinamide impairs contractile function and Ca2+ handling, leading to reduced cardiac excitability that likely underlies the observed reduced contractility and increase in arrhythmia susceptibility.

Dose‐Dependent Effect of Nicotinamide on the Lifespan of Drosophila

To evaluate whether the dose‐dependent effects of nicotinamide on cardiac function observed in HL‐1 cardiomyocytes could be translated to a whole‐body context, we assessed the lifespan of Drosophila fed the same range of nicotinamide doses. In line with prior in vitro results (Figure 3), 10 mmol/L nicotinamide intervention prolonged the lifespan of female flies but not males (Figure 5, Table S1). In female flies, the hazard ratio was 0.6039 (95% CI, 0.441–0.827), which indicated a decreased mortality risk relative to the control group (Table S1). However, higher concentrations (ie, 20, 50, and 100 mmol/L), significantly reduced lifespan in both sexes (Figure 5), with the hazard ratios all significantly >1, indicating an increased mortality risk compared with the control group (Table S1). Notably, the sensitivity to higher doses of nicotinamide appeared to be sex‐specific. At 20 mmol/L, both male and female flies showed reduced life span, with males showing a more rapid mortality after ≈50 days compared with females (Figure 5). Consistently, 50 mmol/L nicotinamide caused a shortened lifespan of both sexes, with an early and rapid loss of male flies, whereas female survival was less affected initially (Figure 5). Most strikingly, exposure to 100 mmol/L nicotinamide led to the death of all flies in both sexes within approximately 1 week (Figure 5). Overall, these findings demonstrate a dose‐dependent effect of nicotinamide on Drosophila lifespan, with males more susceptible to adverse effects of doses above 10 mmol/L.

High‐Dose Nicotinamide Reduced Heart Rate and Induced Arrhythmia in Drosophila

Because higher doses of nicotinamide, particularly 100 mmol/L, led to near‐complete mortality in Drosophila within 1 week, we further checked its cardiac‐specific effects in the few surviving flies at the end of the 1‐week dietary exposure. Remarkably, 100 mmol/L nicotinamide severely impaired cardiac contractility and rhythm (Figure 6A), as indicated by increased diastolic intervals (Figure 6B) and decreased heart rate (Figure 6D), with systolic intervals unaffected (Figure 6C) in both sexes. Arrhythmia index was increased in both male and female flies (Figure 6E), whereas the incidence and burden of fibrillation events were elevated significantly in males, but not in females (Figure 6F through 6H). However, the contraction and relaxation phase intervals, fractional shortening, and shortening velocity were not significantly influenced by a 1‐week dietary intervention with 100 mmol/L nicotinamide (Figure S5A through S5D). Concomitantly, acute exposure of dissected semi‐intact Drosophila hearts to 100 mmol/L nicotinamide induced a rapid cardiac arrest within 1 minute (average = 32.9 seconds) (Figure 6I), suggesting a direct cardiac blocking effect at this dose. Taken together, these findings demonstrate that exposure to high‐dose 100 mmol/L nicotinamide, whether through dietary intervention or acute treatment of the heart tubes, exerts detrimental effects on the cardiac function of Drosophila. A dose of 100 mmol/L dietary nicotinamide reduces physiological heart rate and induces cardiac arrhythmia and fibrillation, whereas acute exposure leads to rapid cardiac arrest, which may underlie its detrimental effect on the Drosophila lifespan (Figure 5).

High‐Dose Nicotinamide Induced VF in Ex Vivo Mouse Hearts Partly Through Regulating SERCA2a Acetylation

We finally investigated the detrimental effects of high‐dose nicotinamide on arrhythmia incidence and cardiac function in a mammalian model for further insight. After 20 minutes of stabilization, 100 mmol/L nicotinamide was perfused into the ex vivo Langendorff mouse heart, and ECGs were monitored (Figure 7A). Strikingly, acute perfusion with 100 mmol/L nicotinamide rapidly triggered VF events, impaired contractile function, and eventually resulted in cardiac arrest in all 6 tested hearts within approximately 10 minutes (average = 8.6 minutes) (Figure 7B and Table S2). Before the onset of VF, the amplitude of R peaks, corresponding to ventricular depolarization, tended to decrease compared with the baseline conditions (P = 0.094, Figure 7C), which may reflect a reduction in the strength of ventricular depolarization or cardiac excitability, despite no statistical difference in diastolic intervals (Figure 7D). In intact hearts, this loss of excitability likely underlies the observed fibrillation and subsequent cardiac arrest. In contrast, perfusion with an equivalent osmotic molar concentration of glucose or NaCl did not cause any VF in any of the 5 hearts tested (Figure S6), suggesting that the VF induction was specific to nicotinamide. Because nicotinamide is a known inhibitor of SIRT deacetylases, we hypothesized that high‐dose nicotinamide might also impair the deacetylation activity of SIRT, thereby influencing its target proteins, including SERCA2a, whose dysfunction is closely associated with cardiac arrhythmia through its central role in Ca2+ handling and electrical stability. 20 , 21 Hearts perfused with 100 mmol/L nicotinamide exhibited a trend toward increased levels of acetylated SERCA2a (P = 0.0774, Figure 7D and 7E), indicating possibly reduced SERCA2a‐mediated Ca2+ activity.

DISCUSSION

In the current study, we investigated the effects of nicotinamide on aging‐related arrhythmia and cardiac dysfunction, and explored the cardiac‐specific adverse effects of high nicotinamide doses and a plausible contributing mechanism. Taking ex vivo mouse hearts and in vivo Drosophila models together, we found that 10 mmol/L nicotinamide showed at most modest cardioprotection in the context of aging. Short‐term treatment during the late‐life phase in male flies mitigated arrhythmia and contractile dysfunction, whereas long‐term treatment prevented aging‐induced reduction in heart rate but exhibited proarrhythmic effects. In contrast, higher concentrations of nicotinamide (>10 mmol/L) dose‐dependently impaired cardiac excitability and induced arrhythmia. These effects were characterized by reduced amplitude and frequency of CaT and mechanic contractility, prolonged diastolic intervals in HL‐1 cardiomyocytes and Drosophila, as well as reduced Drosophila lifespan, with males displaying greater susceptibility to these toxicity effects. Consistently, high‐dose nicotinamide perfusion (100 mmol/L) led to reduced cardiac excitability and acute VF in ex vivo mouse hearts, potentially mediated by acetylation of Ca2+ handling proteins such as SERCA2a. These findings suggest that the cardiac effects of nicotinamide are highly dependent on dose, timing, duration, and are modified by sex.

Our electrically induced AF conditions using HL‐1 cardiomyocytes recapitulate key atrial arrhythmogenic features, such as altered Ca2+ handling, 7 , 11 providing mechanistic insights relevant to trigger‐driven AF. Similarly, ex vivo mouse hearts facilitate precise evaluation of cardiac electrophysiology independent of systemic influences, allowing preinvestigation of acute nicotinamide modulation on AF inducibility under controlled conditions. In parallel, the Drosophila is an integrated physiological model enabling rapid and cost‐effective assessment of long‐term functional insights. It provides a valuable in vivo platform to study age‐related arrhythmic susceptibility without a strict distinction between atrial and ventricular origins. Although these complementary experimental models might not fully capture the complexity of human cardiac arrhythmia, the consistent effects of nicotinamide observed across these distinct models suggest a conserved biological response and support potential translational relevance. Further validation in mammalian in vivo models and human clinical trials is however warranted to confirm the clinical applicability of these findings.

Supplementation with nicotinamide has been proposed to promote healthy aging in various models by elevating NAD+ levels. For instance, 0.2 to 1 mmol/L nicotinamide extended the lifespan of Caenorhabditis elegans in a dose‐dependent manner, 35 whereas 1 to 2 mmol/L nicotinamide protected against oxidative stress and improved mitochondrial function in both cellular and Drosophila models. 36 However, in our study, acute perfusion with 10 mmol/L nicotinamide did not significantly alter AF susceptibility in aged mouse hearts. This was unexpected given our previous evidence supporting its antiarrhythmic effects in tachypaced HL‐1 cardiomyocytes and Drosophila models. 11 One possible reason is likely that a 1‐hour acute nicotinamide perfusion was insufficient to restore the declined NAD+ level in the aged mouse heart, consistent with our measurements and prior reports. 33 , 34 The slower uptake of nicotinamide and reduced enzymatic activity in aged tissue may further delay NAD+ biosynthesis. 37 Another explanation could be the complex pathophysiological basis of aging‐related AF. Tachypacing mimics AF partially by inducing oxidative DNA damage, which acutely depletes NAD+ due to poly(ADP)‐ribose polymerase 1 activation. 7 However, aging‐associated AF may involve more complex structural remodeling and chronic oxidative stress beyond NAD+ depletion, 38 where a single acute intervention of nicotinamide may be insufficient to reverse NAD+ depletion and its chronic and multifactorial pathology.

Clinically, nicotinamide at doses ranging from 1 to 5 g per day has been shown to be safe in humans. This dosage corresponds to an immediate postdose plasma concentration of roughly 1.64 to 8.2 mmol/L nicotinamide, assuming a blood volume of approximately 5 L for a 70 kg adult. However, it is well recognized that the public use of nutritional supplements often exceeds recommended daily doses. Therefore, assessing the potential cardiac side effects of high‐dose nicotinamide is clinically relevant. In this study, we tested supraphysiological nicotinamide doses and found that higher doses of nicotinamide displayed significant whole‐body and cardiac‐specific adverse effects in HL‐1 cardiomyocytes, Drosophila, and ex vivo mouse hearts. Similar adverse effects of nicotinamide have been reported previously. Excess nicotinamide was linked with neurodegeneration in Parkinson disease, 39 and chronic high‐dose nicotinamide supplementation increased the risk of adverse outcomes in individuals predisposed to cerebral ischemia. 12 All functional effects of nicotinamide on cardiac Ca2+ handling and rhythm in this study emerged only at concentrations above 20 mmol/L, indicating a high‐dose threshold behavior inconsistent with physiological NAD+ metabolism. At these concentrations, nicotinamide reduced the amplitude and frequency of CaT and mechanic contractility, prolonged diastolic intervals, and shortened CaT decay and contractile relaxation, consistent with a marked reduction in cardiac excitability in HL‐1 cardiomyocytes and Drosophila. In intact hearts, this loss of excitability likely underlies the observed VF and subsequent cardiac arrest. 40 Notably, these findings involve different types of cardiac arrhythmias, and atrial and ventricular arrhythmias may arise from distinct underlying causes. Caution is warranted on an integrated interpretation. With this in mind, the present study focuses on the shared features, such as reduced cardiac excitability, abnormal Ca2+ handling, and impaired contractile function, providing mechanistic insights into these conserved aspects of arrhythmogenesis. These high‐dose effects appear to result primarily from nonselective inhibition of depolarizing ion currents.

Sirtuins, particularly SIRT1, influence cardiac ion channel function through posttranslational regulation. SIRT1 deacetylates the cardiac sodium channel Nav1.5, enhancing its membrane trafficking and peak current, 41 whereas SIRT1 deficiency is associated with reduced L‐type Ca2+ current, impaired SERCA2a, and intracellular Ca2+ and Na+ regulation in cardiomyocytes, thereby provoking cardiac dysfunction and arrhythmogenesis. 42 Notably, nicotinamide is a noncompetitive inhibitor of SIRT1 deacetylase, which regulates broad aspects of cellular signaling and homeostasis. 43 Even a low dose (50 μM/L) of nicotinamide has been shown to sufficiently inhibit SIRT1 activity in vitro. 19 Previous studies demonstrated that downregulation of SIRT1 increased acetylation of SERCA2a, which in turn led to reduced SERCA2a activity, impaired Ca2+ handling, and cardiac defects in both HL‐1 cardiomyocytes and mice. 20 Consistent with these findings, we found increased SERCA2a acetylation in mouse hearts perfused with 100 mmol/L nicotinamide, which suggests that a high dose of nicotinamide inhibited SIRT1 activity. The suppressed SIRT1 activity would contribute to the occurrence of VF and cardiac dysfunction/arrest by its wide involvement in modulating key ion channels such as Na+/Ca2+ exchanger and sodium channel Nav1.5. 41 , 42 Regardless of the exact mechanisms, these data highlight previously unrecognized cardiac risks of chronic high‐dose nicotinamide, particularly for individuals predisposed to cardiovascular diseases such as AF and heart failure. Because most vitamin B3 supplements, nicotinamide, nicotinic acid, nicotinamide riboside, and nicotinamide mononucleotide circulate as nicotinamide, 44 this warrants caution in taking high‐dose vitamin B3 supplementation and especially in applying intravenous NAD+ therapy.

Apart from dosage, the efficacy of nicotinamide may also depend on intervention timing and duration. We observed that only short‐term intervention of 10 mmol/L nicotinamide, initiated at a late stage of life, protected against aging‐induced cardiac arrhythmia and contractile dysfunction, but lifelong intervention did not show protection in male flies and even elevated arrhythmias in females. A comparable timing‐dependent adverse effect was reported in a study using rats, where 500 mg/kg nicotinamide significantly ameliorated acetaminophen‐induced biochemical changes and pathological injuries, but induced hepatotoxicity when given to healthy animals or before intoxication. 45 These adverse effects of nicotinamide seem to occur only when nicotinamide is supplied at a young age or under healthy conditions, when NAD+ levels are normally unaffected. In such contexts, nicotinamide supplementation may be excessive and accumulate chronically, leading to side effects independent of NAD+‐mediated mechanisms. These results are in line with our previous results, where high‐dose nicotinamide riboside intervention impaired metabolic flexibility, reduced insulin sensitivity, and exacerbated inflammation in young healthy male mice fed an obesogenic diet. 46 , 47 In contrast, treatment of nicotinamide at old age, when NAD+ levels are often reduced, could yield beneficial outcomes. These contrasting effects underscore the importance of a therapeutic window for nicotinamide intervention, especially in the context of aging and cardiovascular diseases.

Sex‐specific responses were also observed in the sensitivity to nicotinamide. Exclusively in male flies, 10 mmol/L nicotinamide alleviated aging‐induced cardiac arrhythmia, whereas 20 mmol/L and 50 mmol/L led to more rapid mortality rate, and 100 mmol/L nicotinamide induced fibrillation events, suggesting that males are more sensitive to the effects of nicotinamide, either at moderate or extremely high doses. Such sexually dimorphic responses were also reported in a mouse model of Friedreich ataxia, where male mice exhibited more severe cardiac NAD+ depletion and greater improvement in cardiac phenotypes with NAD+ precursor treatment, whereas females maintained NAD+ levels and showed less pronounced benefit. 48 Similarly, a previous study found nicotinamide alleviated heart failure in aged male mice. 10 Our previous studies further showed that male mice are more susceptible than females to both vitamin B3 deficiency 49 and the adverse effects of high‐dose nicotinamide riboside supplementation. 46 In this study, the protective effects of 10 mmol/L during late‐life in male flies seem to be due to a higher arrhythmia incidence during aging compared with females. However, previous animal and clinical studies have shown inconsistent sex differences in arrhythmia incidence. Although some studies reported that men are generally more susceptible to AF than women, 50 a study conducted in an aged population suggested that women are at higher risk for AF incidence than men for a given height and weight. 51 Further investigation into sex difference in nicotinamide efficacy for aging‐related cardiac dysfunction and arrhythmia is therefore warranted.

CONCLUSIONS

Our results revealed a dose‐ and context‐dependent profile of nicotinamide in cardiac aging and arrhythmia. Although short‐term in late‐life intervention of 10 mmol/L nicotinamide ameliorated age‐associated cardiac arrhythmia, chronic or high‐dose exposure led to detrimental cardiac effects, partly via impaired cardiac excitability and Ca2+ handling. However, the causal molecular mechanisms warrant further study. This study highlights the narrow therapeutic window of nicotinamide on cardioprotection with respect to both dose and duration, especially in aged populations with disrupted NAD+ metabolism. It also raises concerns as to the safety of supraphysiological nicotinamide doses in both healthy and diseased individuals.
