Development and validation of a prognostic model for endometrial carcinoma using causal genes identified by Mendelian randomization
Authors: Dan Yang, Yazhen Li, Xiaoling Xin, Huilan Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Endometrial carcinoma (EC) remains an ambiguous pathogenesis. This study aimed to investigate the potential of causal genes in predicting EC prognosis. The prognostic biomarkers of EC were identified using univariate Cox regression analyses based on data from The Cancer Genome Atlas Uterine Corpus Endometrial Carcinoma (TCGA-UCEC). Mendelian randomization (MR) analyses were conducted to infer causal relationships, utilizing expression quantitative trait loci (eQTLs) derived from prognostic genes as exposures, and a dataset from European populations with EC as outcomes. Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) that significantly influenced gene expression (eQTLs) were selected as instrumental variables. The inverse variance weighted (IVW) method was employed as the primary analytical approach. Sensitivity analyses were performed to ensure robustness of the findings. Causal genes with potential prognostic significance were further evaluated using multivariate Cox regression analysis, Kaplan–Meier (KM) overall survival curves, and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. Additionally, results from gene ontology (GO) and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) of differentially expressed genes (DEGs), along with immune infiltration analyses in the high- and low-risk groups, are presented. 18 genes exhibiting a negative correlation with EC demonstrated a protective effect, whereas 9 genes identified as risk factors for EC exerted an adverse effect on the disease. A prognostic model was developed consisting of 8 genes selected from 27 genes. According to the KM overall survival curve data, ECs classified with high-risk ratings exhibited significantly poor prognoses (P < .0001). The ROC curve analysis indicated that the area under the curve (AUC) for this risk model in predicting the 1-, 3-, and 5-year EC survival rates were 0.704, 0.735, and 0.766, respectively. Furthermore, GO and GSEA results of DEGs in both the high- and low-risk groups revealed strong associations with pathways related to cell motility and immune response, among others. In addition, an analysis of immune cell infiltration demonstrated significant differences between the high- and low-risk groups. A prognostic model for EC using causal genes identified using MR has good sensitivity and specificity. These findings provide new insights into ECs pathogenesis and suggest promising strategies for the diagnosis and treatment of ECs.

1. Introduction

Endometrial carcinoma (EC) is a malignancy that arises from the inner epithelial lining of the uterus and ranks among the most prevalent gynecological malignancies in developed nations.[1] It is characterized by an increasing incidence and corresponding mortality rate. Most cases are observed in perimenopausal and postmenopausal women aged between 65 and 75 years.[2] However, in recent years, the proportion of younger patients has increased. The elevated EC incidence rates in individuals may be attributed to a high prevalence of lifestyle-related risk factors, particularly obesity, which is linked to approximately 50% of EC cases.[3] The 5-year survival rate of EC patients exceeds 90%. In contrast, fewer than 20% of patients diagnosed with advanced stage disease or metastasis achieve this outcome.[4] Given the unfavorable prognosis and economic burden associated with advanced-stage EC patients, it is vital to identify effective biomarkers that can aid in predicting the likelihood of recurrence and efficacy of therapeutic interventions. Transvaginal ultrasound and endometrial biopsy are widely used for the diagnosis of EC. Additionally, supplementary immunohistochemical analyses can facilitate early identification of UCEC.[5] A systematic review and meta-analysis also demonstrated that the preoperative analyses of MRI-radiomics in EC patients serve as a reliable predictor for lymph node metastasis, tumor grade, myometrial invasion, and lymphovascular space invasion.[6] Nevertheless, there is currently no biomarker that is routinely used for early diagnostic or prognostic purposes in EC.

The TCGA Project[7] has put forward a comprehensive molecular characterization of EC and identified the most frequently mutated genes in EC, including PTEN, CTNNB1, PIK3CA, and KRAS, as well as the mutation burden and somatic copy number alterations across other histological types of the disease.[8,9] Numerous studies have also identified various novel biomarkers for the diagnosis and prognosis of EC, such as glycosyltransferases, MCM10, and metabolism-related genes.[10–12] TCGA data are valuable for identifying genes linked to the prognosis of EC; however, establishing a potential causal relationship between these genes and EC has consistently posed significant challenges. Traditional observational studies are frequently hindered by confounding variables and reverse causality, which makes it difficult to provide reliable causal inferences.[13] Mendelian randomization (MR) is an innovative methodological approach that employs genetic variations as instrumental variables to elucidate the causal relationships between exposure factors and outcomes[14] and has been extensively used to investigate causal relationships between various traits and diseases.[15,16] Notable MR in EC includes associations between gut microbiota, dietary factors, insulinemia, and EC.[17–19] Furthermore, the causal relationship between gene expression and EC has also been elucidated by integrating eQTL in peripheral blood from the GTEx database, or the Consortium for the Architecture of Gene Expression (CAGE), with EC summary statistics from genome-wide association studies (GWAS) using Summary-data-based MR (SMR), and several genes that are causally associated with EC have been identified.[20–22]

In our study, we aimed to conduct a more comprehensive analysis and deepen our understanding of the causal relationships between prognosis-related genes identified from the TCGA data and EC. To achieve this, we utilized eQTLs associated with 4580 prognosis-related genes identified through TCGA data as instrumental variables. In this framework, these 4580 genes served as exposure variables, whereas EC was designated as the outcome variable. This approach allowed us to infer causal associations between alterations in gene expression and EC incidence. As a result, we developed and validated an 8-gene risk model with the potential to enhance personalized treatment strategies and improve prognostic assessments.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Data acquisition

The TCGA-UCEC dataset, which includes RNA sequencing and associated clinical data for 554 cases of UCEC samples, along with 35 cases of normal samples, were obtained from TCGA (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). The eQTLs of 4580 genes and the EC-related dataset (ebi-a-GCST006464), including 12,906 ECs and 108,979 control samples of European individuals and corresponding 9470,555 single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), were downloaded from the Integrative Epidemiology Unit (IEU) Open GWAS (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/).

2.2. Univariate Cox regression analysis

Utilizing the gene expression data from 550 UCEC samples, along with the corresponding clinical data of these patients, a univariate Cox regression analysis was conducted on the genes using the Coxph function from the “Survival” R package. A P-value of <.05 was deemed statistically significant.

2.3. MR Analysis

Before MR analysis, SNPs selected as instrumental variables followed the following criteria: at a significance level of P = 5e−08; fulfilled a distance threshold of 10,000 kb and r2 < 0.001 to eliminate the linkage disequilibrium bias to the greatest extent feasible[23]; a minor allele frequency of more than 0.01; F-statistic >10.[24] Five regression models were used in the MR analysis: inverse variance weighting (IVW), weighted median estimation, simple mode, weighted mode, and MR-Egger regression.[25] The IVW method, which employs the inverse variance of each IV as weights to calculate the summary causal effect estimate, was designated as the principal analytical approach, whereas the other methods served as complementary techniques. The results were presented as odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). To ensure the robustness of our findings, we conducted the Cochrane Q-test to evaluate the heterogeneity of the SNP associations. Furthermore, MR-Egger intercept tests were performed to assess pleiotropy. Finally, we performed a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis to detect any significant deviations from the overall findings, thereby ensuring the dependability of the MR analyses. A P-value < 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant.

2.4. Identification of overlapping genes

The intersection of prognostic protective factors identified through univariate Cox regression analysis (HR < 1) and negative causative factors in MR (OR < 1) was analyzed using Venn diagrams. Similarly, the intersection of prognostic risk factors (HR > 1) and positive causative factors (OR > 1) was conducted using Venn diagrams.

2.5. Multivariate Cox regression analysis

Based on the findings from the univariate Cox regression analysis and MR analysis, a multivariate Cox regression analysis was performed for 27 genes using the Step function from the “Survival” package in R.

2.6. Calculation of the risk score and risk grouping

The risk score was calculated using the Predict function from the “Survival” R package. The formula used was as follows: Risk score = h0(t) * (Expgene_1 * βgene_1 +... + Expgene_n * βgene_n). This formula was used to compute the risk score for each patient. Subsequently, patients were categorized into 2 groups based on the median value of the risk scores. Those with a risk score exceeding the median were classified as belonging to the high-risk group, whereas those with a risk score at or below the median were designated as part of the low-risk group.

2.7. Evaluation of risk-model

Kaplan–Meier (KM) curve by the “survival” and “survminer” R package were used to assess the prognostic disparities between high-risk and low-risk group. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve by the “survivalROC” R package, a comprehensive metric, was utilized to evaluate the sensitivity and specificity of continuous variables. The CI function was used to assess the area under the curve (AUC) and confidence interval to evaluate the ability of the risk model in predicting the prognosis of ECs.

2.8. Data processing and identification of differentially expressed genes

Genes with zero counts in more than 75% of the samples were filtered out. All transcriptome data are shown as log2(count + 1). A PCA plot was used to indicate the differences between the high- and low-risk groups. Then, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were screened out by “limma” package in R.[26] The volcano and pheatmap plots of DEGs were visualized by the “ggplot2” package in R.

2.9. Enrichment analysis

Gene ontology (GO) and gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) analyses using the “clusterProfiler” R package were adopted to reveal the potential functions of DEGs.[27] The visualization of GSEA results was constructed via the “enrichplot” R package.

2.10. Immunity infiltration analysis

IOBR[28] containing the CIBERSORT,[29] ESTIMATE,[30] MCPcounter,[31] xCell,[32] EPIC,[33] Quantiseq[34] and TIMER[35] algorithms was used to compare cellular components or cell immune responses between the high-risk and low-risk groups simultaneously. Additionally, single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA)[36] was used to quantify the tumor-infiltrating immune cell subgroups between the 2 groups and assess their immune function.

2.11. Statistical analyses

Most of the statistical analyses were conducted using the R software. The specific analytical methodologies had been elucidated accordingly.

3. Results

3.1. 4580 genes were substantially correlated with the prognosis of ECs

To investigate the potential of genetic factors in predicting EC prognosis, we utilized the gene transcriptome data and clinical information of 550 ECs from TCGA for univariate Cox regression analysis. The results showed that 4580 of them were significantly correlated with the prognosis of ECs, among which 2294 were protective factors (HR < 1) and 2286 were risk factors (HR > 1) (P < .05) (Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q608).

3.2. The causal relationship between prognosis-related genes and ECs was demonstrated in MR analysis

To evaluate the causal relationship between alterations in the expression of 4580 EC prognosis-related genes and EC, we employed eQTLs as instrumental variables. A total of 4580 genes were identified as exposure factors, and EC was designated as the outcome. Based on the IVW results, a significant causal relationship between 46 genes and EC was identified. Among these, 31 genes were classified as protective factors (HR < 1), whereas 15 genes were categorized as risk factors (HR > 1) (Fig. 1A). Theoretically, genes that exhibit a negative causal relationship with EC should function as protective factors, whereas those with a positive causal relationship should act as risk factors. Venn diagrams were used to illustrate this consistency (Fig. 1B). Consequently, 18 prognostic protective factors (HR < 1) demonstrated a negative causal relationship with EC according to MR analysis (OR < 1). Additionally, 9 prognostic risk factors (HR > 1) exhibited a positive causal relationship with EC (OR > 1) (Fig. 1C and D).

MR analysis of EC prognosis-related genes. (A) The MR results of 46 causal genes of ECs based on IVW results. (B) Venn diagrams. (C) The MR results of 27 causal genes of ECs selected. (D) The forest plot of 27 genes presenting the result of univariate Cox regression analysis. IVW = inverse variance weighted, MR = Mendelian randomization, EC = endometrial carcinoma.

3.3. Establishment and validation of prognostic model for EC

First, we conducted a multivariate Cox analysis of the 27 candidate genes and finally obtained 8 target genes for the risk score model. The survival risk score (SRS) = (0.31366*EXPDEGS1) + (−0.22367*EXPGNA15) + (−0.94930*EXPICE2) + (−0.56010*EXPMXD1) + (−0.44978*EXPPAQR7) + (0.16530*EXPSYCP2L) + (−0.29060*EXPTLR4) + (−0.24193*EXPTMEM91). Second, based on the survival risk score, 550 ECs were classified into the high-risk group (n = 275) or low-risk group (n = 275) (Fig. 2A). KM survival analysis indicated that the prognosis of the high-risk group was significantly poorer than that of the low-risk group (Fig. 2B). The AUC was calculated and demonstrated that the AUC for the model in predicting the 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival rates of ECs were 0.704, 0.735 and 0.766, respectively, suggesting that the 8-genes EC prognosis prediction model has better sensitivity and specificity in predicting the survival risk of ECs (Fig. 2C).

Development and validation of prognostic model for EC. (A) The pheatmap of 8 genes. (B) K-M survival curve between high-risk group and low-risk group were plotted. (C) Time-dependent ROC curves analysis of 1-, 3-, and 5-yr survival. (D) PCA plot illustrating the differences between high- and low-risk group. (E) Heatmap depicting 265 DEGs. (F) Volcano plot representing 265 DEGs. EC = endometrial carcinoma, KM = Kaplan-Meier, ROC = receiver operating characteristic.

3.4. Pleiotropy, heterogeneity and sensitivity analysis for 8 genes

The causal relationship between the 8 genes and ECs was illustrated using a scatter plot. The forest plot depicted the overall effect of each gene on ECs. Subsequently, the reliability of the MR analysis results was assessed using sensitivity analysis. First, Q_pval from the heterogeneity test indicated that there was no heterogeneity among the 8 genes. Next, the P-value from the horizontal pleiotropy test suggests the absence of horizontal pleiotropy. Finally, after removing each SNP in turn via a leave-one-out test, it was determined that residual SNPs had a minimal impact on the overall results, confirming that the MR findings were not influenced by any individual SNP (Fig. S1, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q607).

3.5. Enrichment analysis of DEGs between high- and low-risk group

According to the DEGs analysis, 93 DEGs were downregulated, and 172 genes were upregulated (P-value < .05 and |log2FC| > 1). The findings are displayed as a PCA, volcano, and heatmap plot (Fig. 2D–F). GO analysis demonstrated that cilium movement (biological pathway [BP]), microtubule-based movement (BP), cilium movement involved in cell motility (BP), plasma membrane-bounded cell projection cytoplasm (cellular component [CC]), cytoplasmic region (CC), and motile cilium (CC) (Fig. 3A and B). GSEA of all genes indicated that 41 pathways were enriched, and 10 of them, including the intestinal immune network for IgA production, NF-kappa B signaling pathway, T cell receptor signaling pathway, Th1 and Th2 cell differentiation, viral protein interaction with cytokine and cytokine receptors, cell cycle, GABAergic synapse, mismatch repair, morphine addiction, and nicotine addiction were visualized (Fig. 3C and D).

Enrichment analysis of DEGs. (A) The top 10 BPs and (B) CCs in the GO analysis of DEGs are shown. (C) The 5 terms of GSEA that up-regulated and (D) 5 terms of GSEA that down-regulated are presented. BP = biological pathway, CC = cellular component, DEGs = differentially expressed genes, GO = gene ontology, GSEA = gene set enrichment analysis.

3.6. Evaluation of immune infiltration between the 2 risk groups

A heatmap of immune infiltration using IOBR is shown in Figure 4A. Concurrently, boxplots indicate a significant difference in immune infiltration between the high- and low-risk group (Fig. S2, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q607). Consequently, ECs in the low-risk cluster had significantly fewer naive B cells (CIBERSORT), memory B cells (CIBERSORT), resting memory CD4 T cells (CIBERSORT), activated dendritic cells (CIBERSORT), TumorPurity (Estimate), B_lineage (MCPcounter), monocytes (Quantiseq), T cells CD4 (Quantiseq), T cells CD4 (Timer), CD8 Tcm (xCell), and pDC (xCell). Conversely, infiltration levels of plasma cells (CIBERSORT), CD8 T cells (CIBERSORT), activated memory CD4 T cells (CIBERSORT), regulatory T cells (Tregs) (CIBERSORT), resting dendritic cells (CIBERSORT), B cells (EPIC), CAFs (EPIC), CD8_ T cells (EPIC), Endothelial (EPIC), Macrophages (EPIC), CD8_T_cells (MCPcounter), cytotoxic lymphocytes (MCPcounter), myeloid dendritic cells (MCPcounter), Macrophages M1 (Quantiseq), T_cells_CD8 (Quantiseq), and Tregs (Quantiseq), as well as StromalScore, ImmuneScore, and ESTIMATE Score in Estimate, were markedly lower in patients classified as high-risk. The significant variations in immune cell infiltration between the high-risk and low-risk groups were also clearly demonstrated by ssGSEA (Fig. 4B). The results indicated that the proportions of activated B cells, activated CD8 T cells, CD56bright natural killer cells, CD56dim natural killer cells, central memory CD4 T cells, effector memory CD8 T cells, eosinophils, gamma delta T cells, immature B cells, macrophages, mast cells, MDSC, monocytes, natural killer cells, neutrophils, plasmacytoid dendritic cells, T follicular helper cells, type 1 T helper cells, and type 17 T helper cells were higher in the low-risk group than in the high-risk group (P < .05). Instead, the levels of central memory CD8 T cells, effector memory CD4 T cells, and memory B cells were markedly elevated in the high-risk group relative to the low-risk group. The IOBR and ssGSEA analyses presented above indicate a significant presence of immune infiltration within the low-risk subgroup, suggesting that these patients may exhibit higher response rates to immunotherapy than their high-risk counterparts.

4. Discussion

Historically, ECs have been classified as either Type I or Type II. Type I ECs are predominantly composed of grade I or II endometrioid adenocarcinomas that arise in the context of unopposed estrogen stimulation. These tumors consist of low-grade cells, which are more common and are generally associated with a favorable prognosis. In contrast, Type II ECs encompass grade III endometrioid adenocarcinomas, serous clear cell carcinomas, and undifferentiated carcinosarcomas. These types are characterized by their non-estrogen-driven nature and comprise high-grade cells that are less common and are typically associated with an unfavorable prognosis.[1] To elaborate in detail, the 5-year OS for early-stage ECs, specifically stages I and II, was significantly higher than that for late-stage ECs (stages III and IV). In Asian women, the 5-year OS was 89.3% for stages I/II versus 41.2% for stages III/IV; similarly, in Caucasian women, the rates were 82.3% for stages I/II compared to 34% for stages III/IV.[37] However, considering the practical limitations of traditional classification methods for immediate clinical application, new algorithms have been adopted to more effectively determine oncological outcomes in ECs. Based on data from TCGA, ECs were reclassified into 4 distinct categories: DNA polymerase epsilon (POLE), microsatellite instability high/deficient mismatch repair (MSI-H/dMMR), copy number-low/TP53-wild-type (CNL), and copy number-high/TP53-mutant (CNH/p53abn). Furthermore, the available data confirmed the applicability of the ProMisE model (Proactive Molecular Risk Classifier for EC) to both final and diagnostic specimens.[38] Thus, to comprehensively manage EC, it is imperative to make more dedicated efforts to precisely delineate vital factors for a deeper understanding of the molecular and immunological drivers of response and resistance to ECs.

In this study, the combination of TCGA data and MR was employed to establish a prognostic model for EC based on the gene expression that causes the onset of EC. As a result, an 8-gene EC risk model comprising DEGS1, GNA15, ICE2, MXD1, PAQR7, SYCP2L, TLR4, and TMEM91 had better sensitivity and specificity. Some of these genes play essential roles in the pathogenesis of ECs has been elucidated. For example, ABTL0812 enhances the levels of cellular long-chain dihydroceramides by inhibiting DEGS1 activity, leading to prolonged ER stress and activation of the unfolded protein response through the ATF4-DDIT3-TRIB3 pathway, which ultimately promotes cytotoxic autophagy in ECs and other cancer cells.[39] PAQR7 expression is downregulated in endometrial cancer.[40] TLR4, a member of the Toll-like receptors family, can change the tumor microenvironment to escape immune surveillance and serve as a potent immune adjuvant by mediating the secretion of IFN Iα in EC.[41,42] GNA15 has also been identified as a potential prognostic factor for ovarian cancer, and is characterized by its immunosuppressive role in this malignancy. This function is associated with the polarization of M2-like tumor-associated macrophages.[43] ICE2, an integral membrane protein essential for the formation of ER-LD contact sites, is a vital player in autophagy, an important process in cancer.[44] MAX dimerization protein 1 is a basic helix-loop-helix leucine-zipper protein belonging to the MYC/MXD/MAX family. It has been implicated in the overall survival, invasion, and metastasis of various cancers, including pancreatic, breast, and gastric cancer. Mechanistically, MXD1 can functionally compete with MYC for binding to MAX, thereby forming a transcriptional repressor that impairs cell transformation, differentiation, proliferation, and apoptosis.[45] Nevertheless, the functions of SYCP2L and TMEM91 in cancers have not yet been elucidated.

ECs were classified into high- and low-risk groups based on the 8-gene risk model. Notably, the DEGs between the 2 risk groups were enriched in the necessary process of ECs formation. Acceleration of cell motility can inhibit senescence and promote cytokinesis, contributing to the aggressiveness of EC.[46] Immunity-related pathways, including the intestinal immune network for IgA production, NF-kappa B signaling pathway, T cell receptor signaling pathway, Th1 and Th2 cell differentiation, and viral protein interaction with cytokines and cytokine receptors were positively enriched, indicating that these immune responses exert a stronger activation in the prognosis of high-risk ECs compared to low-risk ones. Additionally, it is worth noting that the NF-κB and IκB gene families are crucial mediators of EC by controlling apoptosis and cell proliferation.[47]

ECs exhibit immunogenic properties and are classified into distinct molecular subtypes that are associated with varying levels of immune infiltration; however, their immune landscape remains to be further explored. The proportion of immune cells has been examined as a prognostic marker for EC. Studies have demonstrated that CD8+ T cell infiltrates are generally associated with a favorable prognosis,[48] which aligns with our finding that CD8+ T cells (identified by 3 methods including CIBERSORT, MCPcounter, and Quantiseq) were markedly higher in patients classified as low-risk by the 8-gene risk model. However, in contrast to our findings that the infiltration levels of Tregs were significantly higher in low-risk patients, Tregs were characterized as a negative prognostic biomarker.[49,50] It appears that EC were not significantly infiltrated by them,[51] but we found that more naive B cells (CIBERSORT), memory B cells (CIBERSORT), and B_lineage (MCPcounter) were clustered in high-risk ECs, indicating that their role in EC progression warrants further investigation. Significantly, the analysis revealed that high-risk ECs exhibited diminished immune and stromal components, along with an elevated proportion of tumors. This was evidenced by lower stromal Scores, immune Scores, and ESTIMATE scores, coupled with heightened tumor purity.

Although our study fosters a more profound understanding of the causal genes linked to the prognosis of EC and establishes a prognostic model with commendable sensitivity and specificity, it is difficult to overlook certain inherent limitations. First, the prognostic model and casual genes were meticulously developed using a publicly available dataset; however, its predictive efficacy has yet to be rigorously validated through extensive prospective clinical trials. Second, the present study did not encompass molecular biology experiments aimed at elucidating the functions and mechanisms of the 8 genes in EC.

5. Conclusion

In total, the novel prognostic model established explored the prognostic value of causal genes identified by MR in EC. This pioneering approach offers a profound reference for future risk stratification and personalized therapeutic strategies tailored to EC.
