Effects of dietary 5-hydroxytryptophan on growth characteristic, hepatopancreas health, antioxidant defenses, immune status, and reproductive potential of Macrobrachium rosenbergii broodstock
Authors: Li Wang, Chenying Yang, Fei Huang, Cui Liu, Jia Xu, Luxi Chen, Yubo Wu, Jingan Wang, Yuanmin Liu, Zhimin Gu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In view of the paucity of studies on 5-hydroxytryptophan (5-HTP) in Macrobrachium rosenbergii, this experiment investigated the effects of dietary 5-HTP on the growth, immune function, oxidative status, hormone levels and tryptophan metabolism via the combination of metabolome, microbiota and transcriptome analysis in M. rosenbergii broodstock. A total of 200 Female M. rosenbergii (3 months old; initial body weight 17.8 ± 1.6 g) were randomly assigned to five dietary treatments: a reference diet (REF), a control diet (CON), and the CON diet supplemented with 5-HTP at 50 (HTP5), 150 (HTP15), or 250 mg/kg (HTP25). Each treatment had four replicates, with each replicate containing 10 M. rosenbergii, and the feeding trial lasted for 8 weeks. The results showed the weight gain of prawns fed HTP15 was greater than CON (P = 0.022). Histological examination revealed that reserve cells (RC) and secretory cells (BC) in prawns fed HTP15 and HTP25 exhibited predominantly normal morphology, with minimal signs of rupture or vacuolation. Compared to CON, the density of yolk granules increased in prawn fed HTP15; the 5-HTP treatments significantly decreased the malondialdehyde (MDA) content and acid phosphatase (ACP) activity in hemolymph, reactive oxygen species (ROS)-positive rate in hepatopancreas; the HTP5 and HTP15 treatments significantly increased the levels of estradiol (E2), vitellogenin (Vg), and progesterone (Prog) (P < 0.05). The cluster analysis of bacterial genera revealed that most samples in CON and HTP15 groups clustered separately. In the transcriptome analysis, principal component analysis (PCA) revealed C and HTP15 groups exhibited similar distribution patterns. Furthermore, compared to CON group, the HTP15 group significantly increased the levels of tryptamine, xanthurenic acid, tryptophol, and 3-indoleacetic acid (P < 0.05). Integrated multi-omics analyses revealed dietary HTP15 primarily influences carbon metabolism, PI3K-Akt signaling, cell-extracellular matrix (ECM)-receptor interaction, focal adhesion, and Insulin resistance pathways. Overall, 150 mg/kg of 5-HTP supplementation exerts multiple beneficial effects on M. rosenbergii, including improvements in growth performance, hepatopancreas health, antioxidant capacity, immune function, and reproductive potential.

Introduction

The giant freshwater prawn, Macrobrachium rosenbergii, represents a species of substantial economic importance in global aquaculture, particularly in China. In recent years, there has been a notable shift in farming practices in East China, with a transition from conventional shrimp culture to intensive production of M. rosenbergii. This trend is reflected in national output, which reached 196,400 tons in 2023—a 10.42% increase from the previous year (Fisheries and Fishery Administration Bureau of the Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Affairs, 2024). However, the sustainability of this industry heavily relies on the quality of broodstock. Poor reproductive performance, characterized by delayed ovarian maturation and low egg quality, remains a critical bottleneck in intensive farming systems (Ibrahim, 2023). Therefore, nutritional strategies targeting broodstock health are essential.

To reduce costs and environmental impact, replacing fishmeal (FM) with plant protein sources like soybean protein concentrate (SPC) is a common practice. However, high levels of SPC often lead to deficiencies such as amino acid imbalance, anti-nutritional factors, and reduced palatability, which can suppress growth and impair reproductive performance in crustaceans (Venero et al., 2023). Consequently, there is an urgent need for functional feed additives that can mitigate these negative effects while enhancing physiological resilience.

Among potential additives, 5-hydroxytryptophan (5-HTP), the direct precursor of serotonin (5-HT), has gained attention. As a neurotransmitter, 5-HT regulates a wide array of physiological processes in crustaceans, such as molting, growth, feeding, reproduction, immune function, and stress response (Esmaeili et al., 2025). Empirical evidence supports the role of 5-HT in enhancing reproductive outcomes in species including Litopenaeus vannamei (Parenrengi et al., 2024; Tinikul et al., 2011), M. rosenbergii (Soonthornsumrith et al., 2018), and Penaeus monodon (Wongprasert et al., 2006). Additionally, dietary supplementation of 5-HT has been shown to improve growth in Pontastacus leptodactylus (Ozkok et al., 2022) and L. vannamei (Aktas et al., 2014). The 5-HT also modulates immune and inflammatory responses via interactions with the gut microbiome (Jones et al., 2020), and it influences specific feeding behaviors, though its effects may be phase-dependent (Yang et al., 2018). Despite these multifaceted roles of 5-HT, research on its direct precursor, 5-HTP, remains limited in aquatic species. Compared to 5-HT, the 5-HTP itself may also be involved in some metabolic regulatory processes, but its direct physiological functions have been relatively less studied separately. Furthermore, dietary supplementation with its precursor, 5-HTP, presents distinct functional and mechanistic advantages. First, 5-HTP can readily cross the blood–brain barrier, whereas 5-HT cannot. This property allows orally administered 5-HTP to serve as a systemic and brain-accessible pro-drug, enabling a sustained and broad elevation of 5-HT levels in both central and peripheral tissues following its decarboxylation. In contrast, direct dietary 5-HT would be largely confined to peripheral effects (Maffei, 2020). Second, by modulating the endogenous synthesis of 5-HT, 5-HTP supplementation offers a more physiological and potentially safer approach to fine-tuning serotonergic activity, avoiding the acute and potentially disruptive receptor overstimulation that might result from direct 5-HT administration (Burke and Heisler, 2015). Therefore, elucidation of the nutritional role of 5-HTP is imperative for the advancement of health management and productivity in crustacean aquaculture.

In view of the paucity of studies on 5-HTP in M. rosenbergii, the present study aims to evaluate the comprehensive effects of dietary 5-HTP supplementation on female broodstock fed with SPC-based diets. This study employed female broodstock as the experimental model to specifically address the reproductive and metabolic effects of 5-HTP. Unlike juveniles, broodstock undergo vitellogenesis—a process highly sensitive to nutritional and neuroendocrine regulation, in which serotonin plays a known modulatory role. Therefore, the broodstock stage provides a biologically relevant context to directly evaluate 5-HTP's efficacy in enhancing reproductive performance and overall physiological resilience, with direct implications for hatchery productivity. Therefore, the investigation will focus on key parameters including, but not limited to, growth, immune function, oxidative status, hormone levels and tryptophan metabolism. Moreover, emerging evidence points to a potential ‘gut-microbiota-gonad’ axis in aquatic species, where intestinal microbes modulate reproductive performance through metabolite signaling (e.g., tryptophan derivatives) and immune regulation (Cheung et al., 2015; Bai et al., 2025). Since 5-HTP metabolism is intricately linked to gut microbiota composition, analyzing microbial changes is essential to fully understand the mechanisms underlying 5-HTP-induced reproductive improvements. In order to elucidate the underlying mechanisms, a combined transcriptomic, microbial and metabolomic approach will be employed. The identification of the optimal dietary level of 5-HTP during reproduction will also support the development of stage-specific feed formulations. Ultimately, this research seeks to contribute to the improvement of formulated diets and sustainable practices in prawn aquaculture.

Materials and methods

Animal ethics statement

All experimental procedures involving aquatic animals were evaluated and approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee at Zhejiang Academy of Agricultural Sciences (No. ZAASLA2025022714), following the guidelines of the collaborating institution.

Five iso-nitrogenous (380 g/kg crude protein) and iso-lipidic (75 g/kg crude lipid) diets were prepared for the feeding trial. The reference formulation (REF diet) contained 300 g/kg of FM. In the control formulation (CON diet), half of the FM from the R diet was replaced by SPC, yielding final inclusion levels of 150 g/kg FM and 151 g/kg SPC. Three additional diets were produced by supplementing the CON diet with graded levels of 5-HTP: 50 (HTP5), 150 (HTP15), and 250 mg/kg (HTP25). Previous studies have showed that the suitable level of 5-HT is 20.03 mg/kg for juvenile P. leptodactylus (Ozkok et al., 2022) and 20 mg/kg for post-larval L. vannamei (Aktas et al., 2014). In the present study, the crude protein level of 380 g/kg was selected based on previous studies demonstrating adequate support for growth and reproductive performance in M. rosenbergii broodstock (Benítez-Mandujano and Ponce-Palafox, 2014; Shofiquzzoha et al., 2016). Moreover, considering that 5-HTP is the direct precursor of 5-HT and may exert biological effects either directly or after conversion to 5-HT, with potentially variable conversion efficiency, the minimum dietary 5-HTP supplementation level was set at 50 mg/kg. Higher supplementation levels were subsequently established in proportion to this baseline. Following the protocol of Sun et al. (2022), all ingredients were first ground through a 60-mesh screen to produce a consistent powder. After precise weighing, the dry mixture was blended for 15 min in a 30 L kitchen mixer with added tap water to ensure homogeneity. The moist mash was then pelleted (1.5 mm diameter × 3.0 mm length) using a laboratory-scale pellet mill from Shandong Saibainuo Machinery Co., Ltd. (Dezhou, Shandong, China). The resulting pellets were air-dried at 25 °C, sealed in plastic bags, and stored at −20 °C until the commencement of the feeding trial.

Prawns, feeding, and sampling

An eight-week nutritional study was carried out at the Huzhou Academy of Agricultural Sciences (Huzhou, Zhejiang, China). Before the trial began, giant freshwater prawns (3 months old) were acclimated for 2 weeks and provided with a commercial feed (Tongwei Feed Group Co., Ltd., Foshan, Guangdong, China) twice daily to support adaptation and nutritional stability. To standardize metabolic conditions, the prawns fasted for 24 h immediately preceding the experiment's initiation. A total of 200 M. rosenbergii with an average body weight of 17.8 ± 1.6 g were divided into 20 treatment groups (tank, 97 cm × 57 cm × 61 cm), each holding 10 female prawns. Four replicate tanks were allocated to each dietary treatment to support reliable statistical evaluation.

Throughout the 56-d study, the prawns were fed twice per day at 07:00 and 17:30. The daily ration, initially set between 2% and 5% of total biomass, was adjusted periodically in response to growth and apparent feed consumption. To ensure optimal water quality, residual waste and exuviae were removed regularly by siphoning. In addition, half of the water volume in each tank was exchanged daily with pre-aerated freshwater. Key water parameters were maintained as follows: temperature 23.0 to 30.2 °C, dissolved oxygen > 5 mg/L (achieved via continuous aeration), and pH 7.6 to 8.0.

After the 56-d experiment was completed, all prawns were fasted for 24 h before being collected, counted, and bulk-weighed by tank. Three specimens per replicate were then randomly sampled for biometric assessment, recording body weight, gonad weight, and hepatopancreas weight. After euthanasia, these individuals were stored at −20 °C for subsequent body composition analysis. Another three prawns per tank were selected for fresh tissue sampling. Hemolymph was drawn using a syringe and immediately transferred to pre-chilled tubes containing sodium citrate anticoagulant. The samples were centrifuged at 2000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was aliquoted into cryovials, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and held at −80 °C. Following hemolymph collection, the prawn's external surface was wiped with 75% ethanol for sterilization. The intestinal tract was aseptically dissected and placed into a sterile 2 mL-tube. For histological evaluation, hepatopancreas and ovarian tissue segments from four individuals per group were preserved in 4% paraformaldehyde. Remaining portions of these tissues were snap-frozen and stored at −80 °C for molecular analyses.

Chemical analysis of diet and tissues

Proximate constituents of formulated feeds and prawn tissues were evaluated using the AOAC (2006) methodology. Protein concentration was derived from nitrogen analysis, applying a standard conversion coefficient of 6.25 (method 990.03). Nitrogen determined by the a Kjeltec 2300 Nitrogen Analyzer (Foss Tecator AB, Höganäs, Skåne, Sweden). Lipid analysis (method 2003.05) was determined with an SZF-06A extraction device (Shanghai Xinjia Electronic Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). Sample moisture was quantified by desiccation in a 105 °C oven (DHG-9023A, Shanghai Jinghong Laboratory Equipment Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) until mass equilibrium (method 925.10). Organic matter (OM) was calculated as the difference between 100 and the sum of moisture and ash contents. Ash was determined by combustion at 550 °C in a muffle (SXL-1008, Shanghai Jinghong Laboratory Equipment Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) furnace until constant weight, following method 930.09.

Histological observation and reactive oxygen species (ROS) detection

After fixation for more than 24 h, hepatopancreas and ovarian specimens underwent gradual dehydration through an ascending ethanol series. Tissues were then clarified in xylene and embedded in paraffin wax according to established protocols (Ren et al., 2024). Using a Leica rotary microtome (RM2235, Leica Biosystems Nussloch GmbH, Nussloch, Baden-Württemberg, Germany), paraffin blocks were sectioned at 4-μm thickness. These sections were stained with hematoxylin-eosin following conventional histotechnical procedures, air-dried after coverslipping, and examined under a light microscope (Eclipse Ni–U, Nikon Corporation, Minato, Tokyo, Japan) at 200 × magnification for histological assessment. For morphological assessment of hepatopancreas, five non-overlapping fields per section were randomly selected at 200 × magnification. Reserve cells (RC) and secretory cells (BC) were evaluated based on established criteria: 1) intact cell membrane without rupture; 2) normal nuclear-cytoplasmic ratio; 3) absence of excessive vacuolation or cytoplasmic swelling (Chen et al., 2024; Li et al., 2022). Morphological status was recorded as “normal” or “abnormal” for descriptive comparison among groups.

For ROS detection, frozen hepatopancreas sections were incubated with a fluorogenic probe that emits red fluorescence upon oxidation. Cell nuclei were labeled with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) for blue fluorescence under UV excitation. The ROS-positive signals were quantified using Image-Pro Plus 6.0 software by measuring stained area percentage relative to total tissue area, with uniform chromaticity thresholding for positive identification.

Biochemical analysis of hemolymph

A panel of key biochemical indices in the hemolymph, including superoxide dismutase (SOD; A001-3-2), glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px; A005-1-2), total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC; A015-2-1), alkaline phosphatase (AKP; A059-2-2), and acid phosphatase (ACP; A059-2-2), was measured using established protocols Wang et al. (2024). The level of malondialdehyde (MDA; A003-1-2) was also quantified. All these assays were conducted with commercial kits supplied by the Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, Jiangsu, China). Furthermore, enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits from Shanghai Enzyme Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China), validated for shrimp samples, were employed to determine hemolymph concentrations of several reproductive and neuroendocrine markers: estradiol (E2; YJ49591B), vitellogenin (Vg; YJ56451B), progesterone (Prog; YJ522430B), and ecdysone (YJ352320B). In parallel, the levels of TPH (YJ524321B) in the intestine and hepatopancreas tissues were determined using corresponding ELISA kits from the same manufacturer. The absorbance for all biochemical and ELISA assays was read using a microplate reader (Multiskan FC, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA).

Transcriptome analysis of hepatopancreas

The RNA sequencing and related bioinformatic procedures were performed by Meiji Biomedical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Total RNA extraction from hepatopancreas specimens was followed by assessment of RNA integrity and concentration, adhering to protocols outlined by Wang et al. (2025). Starting with 1 μg of total RNA, mRNA was isolated employing poly-T oligo-attached magnetic beads. Fragmentation was conducted using divalent cations under elevated temperature in Illumina proprietary buffer. First-strand complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis utilized random hexamer primers and SuperScript II Reverse Transcriptase. Second-strand cDNA was generated using DNA Polymerase I and RNase H, with subsequent end repair to generate blunt ends. After adenylation of 3′ ends, Illumina adapters were ligated to cDNA fragments. Library amplification was performed with Phusion High-Fidelity DNA Polymerase, and products were purified with the AMPure XP system (Beckman Coulter Inc., Brea, CA, USA). Library quality was verified on an Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 (Agilent Technologies Inc., Santa Clara, CA, USA). Index-coded samples were clustered on a cBot system with TruSeq PE Cluster Kit v3-cBot-HS, followed by sequencing on the NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA).

Raw sequencing data underwent stringent quality control, including adapter trimming, poly-N sequence removal, and low-quality base filtering. De novo transcriptome assembly was conducted with Trinity software. Clean reads were mapped to the reference genome (GCF_040412425.1) using HISAT2 v2.0.5. Gene expression levels were quantified with HTSeq v0.9.1. Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 in R, applying a negative binomial model. Genes meeting thresholds of false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 and absolute fold change > 2 were defined as differentially expressed genes (DEGs). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of DEGs was conducted with ClusterProfiler R package (v3.4.4), with pathways showing P < 0.05 considered significantly enriched.

To verify the expression profiles obtained from transcriptomics analysis, some of genes were randomly chosen for quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis using the same RNA samples, including atpalpha, LOC136833721, LOC136841405, LOC136841544, LOC136843805, LOC136847066, LOC136847192, LOC136855691, and Pisd (Tables S1 and S2). The qRT-PCR was performed under the following reaction conditions: 95 °C for 2 min; 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 30 s, with fluorescence acquisition at the end of each extension step; followed by a melt curve analysis (95 °C for 5 s, then 65 °C to 95 °C in 0.5 °C increments, holding 5 s per step with continuous fluorescence reading) to confirm the specificity of amplification.

Targeted metabolome analysis of hepatopancreas

Quantitative analysis of tryptophan and its metabolic derivatives was carried out employing a rigorously validated liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) system (LC-40D X3, Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan; QTRAP 6500+, AB SCIEX, Singapore). Hepatopancreas samples (approximately 50 mg) were first homogenized on ice with 500 μL of chilled methanol/water solution (8:2, v/v) containing L-tryptophan-d5 (20 ng/mL) as an internal standard, using a Precellys homogenizer (Bertin Technologies, Montigny-le-Bretonneux, Île-de-France, France). The resulting homogenate was subjected to ultrasonic treatment for 10 min in an ice bath, then centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 15 min (4 °C). The supernatant was collected, dried under a gentle nitrogen gas stream, and the residue was subsequently reconstituted in 100 μL of starting mobile phase (0.1% formic acid in water). After vortex mixing and a final centrifugation step, the clear solution was injected into the LC-MS/MS system.

Chromatographic separation was performed using a Waters BEH Amide column (2.1 mm × 100 mm, 1.7 μm; Waters Corporation, Milford, MA, USA) kept at 40 °C. A gradient elution method was applied with mobile phase A (10 mmol/L ammonium formate and 0.1% formic acid in water) and mobile phase B (0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile), flowing at 0.3 mL/min. The gradient profile proceeded as follows: initial 90% B (0–2 min), linear ramp to 40% B (2–8 min), hold (8–9 min), rapid increase to 90% B (9–9.1 min), and column re-equilibration until 13 min. Detection was achieved with an QTRAP 6500+ mass spectrometer equipped with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source operating in positive ion mode. Multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) transitions were monitored for each analyte. Optimized source conditions were: drying gas at 300 °C and 10 L/min; nebulizer pressure 40 psi; sheath gas temperature 350 °C and flow 11 L/min; capillary voltage 3.5 kV. Quantitation relied on an external calibration curve (1–1000 ng/mL) with internal standard normalization. All data processing, including peak integration and concentration determination, was conducted using AB Sciex software (3.4.0) with 1/x2 weighted linear regression.

Gut microbiota analysis

Microbial genomic DNA was isolated from intestinal samples (about 200 mg) employing the QIAamp PowerFecal Pro DNA Kit (QIAGEN GmbH, Hilden, North Rhine-Westphalia, Germany), following the supplier's protocol. The quality and quantity of the extracted DNA were verified through 1.0% agarose gel electrophoresis and a NanoDrop One spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA), respectively. Amplification of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene V3–V4 hypervariable region was carried out with universal primers 338F (5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′). For each specimen, triplicate PCR amplifications were executed under the following thermal cycling parameters: 95 °C for 3 min; 27 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 55 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 30 s; concluding with a final 10-min extension at 72 °C. Amplified products were then purified with the AxyPrep DNA Gel Extraction Kit (Axygen Biosciences, Union City, CA, USA) and their concentrations determined using a Qubit fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA).

Following purification, amplicons from all samples were pooled in equimolar ratios for Illumina library construction. The final library's quality assessment was performed on an Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 (Agilent Technologies Inc., Santa Clara, CA, USA). Sequencing was conducted on either an Illumina MiSeq or NovaSeq platform (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) using paired-end reads, adhering to the manufacturer's guidelines. Raw sequence data were processed in QIIME 2 (v2023.5), which included demultiplexing and quality control. Denoising was performed with the DADA2 plugin to generate a table of amplicon sequence variants (ASVs), effectively equivalent to optical transform units (OTUs), after filtering, dereplication, chimera removal, and read merging. Taxonomic classification of these ASVs was achieved by applying a naive Bayes classifier previously trained on the SILVA database (v138). Alpha diversity (e.g., Observed ASVs, Chao1, Shannon, and Simpson indices) and beta diversity (based on Bray-Curtis and unweighted UniFrac distances) were analyzed, with results visualized via principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) plots. Microbial community dissimilarities among groups were statistically evaluated by PERMANOVA (999 permutations). Furthermore, the linear discriminant (LDA) analysis effect size (LEfSe) algorithm identified taxa (phylum to genus) exhibiting significant differential abundance (LDA score > 3.0, P < 0.05). Finally, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis was conducted to elucidate associated biological functions.

Calculations and statistics

Growth and physiological indices, including final body weight (FBW), weight gain, specific growth rate (SGR), feed intake, survival, gonadosomatic index (GSI), and hepatosomatic index (HSI), were calculated using established formulas from Sun et al. (2022). To examine the effects of replacing fishmeal in the diets of the REF and CON, HTP5, HTP15, or HTP25 groups, a Dunnett's test was employed. This analysis compared the REF group against the CON and treatment groups for a comprehensive set of parameters: survival, FBW, weight gain, SGR, feed intake, HSI, GSI, whole-body proximate composition, activities of antioxidant enzymes (SOD, GSH-Px, and T-AOC) and metabolic enzymes (AKP and ACP), along with concentrations of MDA, TPH, E2, Vg, Prog, and ecdysone. Separately, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied to assess the influence of different 5-HTP supplementation doses across the CON, HTP5, HTP15, and HTP25 groups. The model utilized for the data was the following equation:Yij=μ+Ai+eij,where Yij denotes the dependent variable; μ denotes the population mean; Ai denotes the fixed effect of treatment; eij denotes the random error.

Where the ANOVA indicated significant differences, a Duncan's multiple range test was used for post-hoc comparisons among the group means for the aforementioned variables. Trend analysis was performed using orthogonal polynomial contrast to determine if the significant effects were linear or quadratic models. All statistical procedures, comprising one-way ANOVA, Duncan's test, Dunnett's test, and trend analysis, were executed using SPSS Statistics (version 24.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A probability value of P < 0.05 is considered statistically significant for all tests.

Based on the comprehensive evaluation of growth performance, hepatopancreas histology, antioxidant capacity, immune parameters, and reproductive hormone levels, the HTP15 group exhibited the most favorable physiological outcomes among the 5-HTP supplementation doses. Therefore, to maximize the efficiency of resource allocation and focus on elucidating the underlying mechanisms of the optimal 5-HTP dose, comparative transcriptomic, metabolomic, and microbiota analyses were conducted between the CON group and the HTP15 group.

Diversity, taxonomic composition, and predicted pathways of intestinal bacteria

According to the OTU composition of the microbial community in the intestine, no significant shifts were observed in the α-diversity indices (Ace, Chao1, Goods-coverage, Simpson, Shannon, and sobs) of gut microbiota in the HTP15 treatment, which indicated that microbial diversity was not affected by the 5-HTP intervention (Fig. 3A). There was no statistically significant effect on the clustering of these communities (P = 0.903, Jaccard-based Adonis), and 4.6% of this variance was accounted for by the difference among groups (PERMANOVA R2 = 0.046). The PCA1 explained 25.67% of the total variations, whereas PCA2 explained 12.03% (Fig. 3B). According to the genus composition of the microbial community in the intestine, the PCoA and NMDS plots analysis indicated that most samples from CON group and the HTP15 group overlap in distribution and cannot be clearly distinguished (Fig. 4A and B), suggesting that HTP15 treatment did not induce substantial alterations in microbial community structure in intestine. The Venn (VN) plot showed that about 140 genus (25.18%) were unique in the CON group, and 44 genus (7.91%) were unique in the HTP15 group, whereas 372 genus (66.91%) were shared by both groups (Fig. 4C). Furthermore, cluster analysis of these bacterial genera (top 50 by abundance) revealed that most samples from the CON group and the HTP15 group clustered separately (Fig. 4D). Discussion

Effects of dietary 5-HTP on growth performance and hepatopancreas health in M. rosenbergii

Research has demonstrated that replacing more than 40% and 80% of FM with cottonseed protein concentrate has been shown to suppress FBW, weight gain, and SGR in M. rosenbergii and L. vannamei, respectively (Ding et al., 2022; Xie et al., 2016). The present findings are consistent with the aforementioned reports, demonstrating that substituting 50% of fishmeal with SPC significantly reduced FBW and weight gain in M. rosenbergii. As demonstrated by Chettri et al. (2007), the exposure of larval M. rosenbergii to 5-HT (100 μg/mL) and dietary supplementation (0.25%–0.50%) enhanced the growth and survival. Despite the fact that the supplemental level of 5-HTP utilized in the present study (150 mg/kg) was an order of magnitude lower than the 5-HT levels previously applied, the HTP15 group still exhibited a marked increase in weight gain. Conversely, studies on other species have reported lower optimal 5-HT levels −20.03 mg/kg for juvenile P. leptodactylus (Ozkok et al., 2022) and 20 mg/kg for post-larval L. vannamei (Aktas et al., 2014). Such discrepancies may be indicative of differences in dosage forms, species-specific sensitivity, or experimental conditions affecting bioavailability and metabolic response. It is noteworthy that among the 5-HTP supplementation groups in our trial, only the HTP15 treatment dosage elicited a significant growth-promoting effect, whereas HTP5 and HTP25 treatments showed no notable improvement. This finding suggests that both insufficient and excessive 5-HTP supplementation may be ineffective in counteracting the growth inhibition induced by high plant-protein diets in M. rosenbergii.

In M. rosenbergii, the injection of 5-HT at a concentration of 2.5 × 10−7 mol/prawn resulted in a significant increase in food intake, an acceleration in gastric emptying, an elevation in the midgut index, and an enhancement in the activities of key digestive enzymes, including amylase, protease, and lipase, relative to the control animals (Deethaisong et al., 2025). Beyond influencing feeding physiology, 5-HT exerts regulatory effects on growth and metabolism-related hormones and neuropeptides, including growth hormone-like peptides and insulin-like growth factor 1, which in turn influence molting and somatic growth (Esmaeili et al., 2025). Furthermore, 5-HT contributes to intermediary metabolism via pathways such as cAMP-PKA signaling and glycolysis, thereby facilitating the provision of energy demands associated with accelerated growth (Haleem and Mahmood, 2021). Collectively, these mechanisms suggest that 5-HT may improve growth performance by stimulating feeding activity, regulating endocrine functions, and enhancing energy metabolism. However, given that the present study did not directly assess these physiological and metabolic variables, the specific pathways through which 5-HTP enhances growth in M. rosenbergii remain to be fully elucidated. Further experimental work is warranted to clarify the underlying mechanisms.

It has been demonstrated that diets with a high plant-protein content can induce structural alterations in the hepatopancreas. For instance, in L. vannamei, such diets have been observed to induce basement membrane detachment, tubular lumen enlargement, and BC cell swelling, resulting in a distortion of the architecture of hepatopancreatic tubules (Chen et al., 2024; Li et al., 2022). Similarly, a reduced inclusion of fishmeal in the diets of M. rosenbergii has been linked to elevated levels of BC vacuolation (Liu et al., 2023). Consequently, the CON group in the present study demonstrated BC swelling, which is suggestive of oxidative stress in M. rosenbergii. RC enhance the assimilation of lipids and accumulation of glycogen, with these reserves being utilized during molting and reproductive phases (Ray et al., 2020). The augmented RC abundance evident in the CON group may be indicative of a compensatory response to RC dysfunction, potentially attributable to recurrent cell rupture, aimed at maintaining metabolic homeostasis. It is noteworthy that dietary supplementation with 5-HTP led to a significant enhancement in hepatopancreatic morphology, exhibiting a dose-dependent response. Based on histological assessment, the structural integrity of the HTP15 and HTP25 groups was largely restored to levels comparable to the REF group. These results emphasise the potential of optimised 5-HTP supplementation in maintaining hepatopancreatic health under plant-protein-based feeding regimes.

Effects of dietary 5-HTP on antioxidant and immune status in M. rosenbergii

Ding et al. (2022) previously demonstrated that the replacement of 75% of FM with soybean meal did not result in a significant alteration of MDA, CAT, or SOD levels in M. rosenbergii. In partial agreement, the current study—using a 50% FM substitution with SPC—revealed a significant rise in MDA content and ROS-positive area, though CAT and SOD activities remained unaffected. A comparable increase in serum MDA was reported in L. vannamei when 60% of FM was replaced with cottonseed protein concentrate (Ray et al., 2020; Xie et al., 2016). These findings suggest that the influence of plant-based proteins on antioxidant status depends on the protein source, inclusion level, and species. The 5-HT has been identified as a highly effective antioxidant, exhibiting significant free-radical scavenging properties. It has been demonstrated that this process can upregulate SOD expression and activity, thereby mitigating nitric oxide–induced oxidative stress (Sarikaya and Gulcin, 2013). Consistent with this, 5-HT administration increased serum SOD activity in the Pacific oyster Crassostrea gigas (Dong et al., 2017), and elevated SOD expression across gut regions in female M. rosenbergii (Deethaisong et al., 2025). Despite the fact that 5-HTP supplementation in the present experiment did not result in a significant alteration in SOD or CAT activity, it effectively reduced the ROS-positive area and MDA levels. Collectively, these findings indicate an enhancement of overall antioxidant capacity in M. rosenbergii.

The present study revealed that replacing 50% of FM with SPC led to a significant reduction in ACP and AKP activities in the hemolymph of M. rosenbergii. In contrast, earlier research by Ding et al. (2022) observed no such change in AKP activity when soybean meal was used as a substitute, suggesting that the immunological response may vary depending on the type of plant protein incorporated into the diet. The immunomodulatory role of 5-HT has been extensively documented across various invertebrate groups, including insects (Qi et al., 2016), mollusks (Jia et al., 2018), and marine shrimp (Tong et al., 2020). This suggests that it may have a phylogenetically conserved function in immune regulation. In accordance with the findings of previous transcriptomic studies demonstrating that 5-HT enhances immune-related genes in M. rosenbergii (Deethaisong et al., 2025), the HTP15 group in our study demonstrated augmented immune function, as evidenced by elevated ACP activity. Mechanistically, 5-HT has been demonstrated to influence immunity through receptor-mediated signaling, cytokine modulation, and effects on immune cell activation and proliferation (Esmaeili et al., 2025). However, the precise molecular pathways through which 5-HTP exerts its immunoregulatory effects in decapod crustaceans, particularly in M. rosenbergii, remain to be fully elucidated.

Effects of dietary 5-HTP on reproductive potential in M. rosenbergii

The results obtained from this study demonstrate that the CON group significantly suppressed gonadal development in M. rosenbergii, as indicated by reductions in oocyte number, oocyte diameter, and yolk granule density in histological sections. In this study, exogenous 5-HTP supplementation, notably the HTP15 treatment, effectively counteracted this suppression and promoted ovarian development. Correspondingly, the administration of 5-HT has been demonstrated to augment oocyte diameter and ovarian maturation in M. rosenbergii (Meeratana et al., 2006; Tinikul et al., 2008). In accordance with observations in related teleost species, such as Nile tilapia (Oreochromis niloticus) (Fontaínhas-Fernandes et al., 2000) and olive flounder (Paralichthys olivaceus) (Lee et al., 2024), where the ingestion of a plant-based diet resulted in a compromise to Vg production, the present study demonstrates that a high plant-protein diet significantly lowered hemolymph Vg content in M. rosenbergii. The 5-HT has been identified as an upstream regulator of crustacean reproduction, exerting its influence primarily on the eyestalk and central nervous system. In several crustaceans, including P. monodon (Wongprasert et al., 2006), L. vannamei (Tinikul et al., 2011), and M. rosenbergii (Tinikul et al., 2008), there is an increase in ovarian 5-HTP levels during early maturation, which peak at full maturity. In the present experiment, HTP5 and HTP15 interventions significantly elevated hemolymph E2 and Prog levels, while HTP25 also raised E2 concentration. This finding is consistent with the documented evidence that 5-HT triggers E2 release via calcium-calmodulin-mediated gonadotropin-releasing hormone induction in P. monodon (Mani et al., 2019). The serotonergic system is hypothesized to collaborate with other stimulatory pathways to regulate vitellogenesis (Esmaeili et al., 2025). Specifically, 5-HT has been shown to stimulate ovarian steroid secretion in explants from M. rosenbergii and P. monodon, supporting its role in promoting vitellogenesis via sex hormone induction (Parenrengi et al., 2024; Tinikul et al., 2009; Tomy et al., 2016). Consistent with this mechanism, HTP15 and HTP25 treatments in our study significantly increased hemolymph Vg levels, corroborating earlier findings where 5-HT injection elevated Vg concentration in M. rosenbergii (Tinikul et al., 2008; Tomy et al., 2016).

Exogenous 5-HT administration enhanced ecdysteroid synthesis and upregulated the expression of the ecdysteroid receptor gene, thereby promoting molting in Scylla serrata and P. monodon (Girish et al., 2017; Parenrengi et al., 2024). Conversely, a study on the shore crab Carcinus maenas demonstrated that 5-HT injection significantly suppressed ecdysteroid levels (Robert et al., 2016). The present study revealed that dietary supplementation of 5-HTP at varying levels did not significantly alter ecdysone concentration in M. rosenbergii. The divergent outcomes observed can be attributed to differences in species sensitivity, administration routes, or experimental conditions. Further investigation is necessary to clarify the underlying mechanisms governing 5-HT or 5-HTP's role in crustacean molting physiology.

Effects of dietary 5-HTP on gut microbiome, transcriptome and metabolome in M. rosenbergii

Gut microbiota and metabolite interactions in the context of reproductive health

In mammalian systems, the influence of gut microbiota on host immunity has been demonstrated through the modulation of 5-HT levels, a phenomenon termed the gut microbiome-5-HT axis (De Vadder et al., 2018; Levin and van den Brink, 2014). Despite the well-established significance of this interaction in vertebrates, its role in crustaceans remains largely unexplored. In the present study, alpha diversity indices (including ACE, Chao1, Good's coverage, Simpson, Shannon, and sobs) and beta diversity analyses (PCoA and NMDS) were utilized to examine the effects of 5-HTP supplementation on the intestinal microbial community of M. rosenbergii. The findings indicated that 5-HTP supplementation did not result in significant structural alterations to the intestinal microbial community. Nevertheless, several microbial genera with potential functional relevance were identified. For instance, Akkermansia muciniphila, a key member of the Verrucomicrobiota, is recognised for its role in mucin degradation and has been linked to the promotion of 5-HT precursor availability, such as tryptophan, and possible 5-HT synthesis modulation in enterochromaffin cells via short-chain fatty acids (Yaghoubfar et al., 2020). In a similar manner, certain Gammaproteobacteria, incorporating genera from the Enterobacteriaceae family, have been observed to metabolise tryptophan into indole derivatives, thereby exerting an indirect effect on 5-HT signaling and immune responses (Naskali et al., 2019). As 5-HT is synthesized from tryptophan in a two-step enzymatic process (Hou et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2018), the observed changes in amino acid biosynthesis pathways align with 5-HT-related regulation. As Haleem and Mahmood (2021) have demonstrated, 5-HT in the periphery has also been implicated in the maintenance of energy homeostasis in vertebrates. As carbon metabolism pathways (e.g. glycolysis and the tricarboxylic acid cycle) represent the primary means of ATP production, the carbon metabolism pathway in 5-HTP-treated prawns undergoes significant alterations in the present study. Overall, the administration of HTP15 did not result in a significant restructuring of the gut microbiota architecture. However, notable changes were observed in specific bacterial genera and key metabolic pathways, indicating a functional—albeit not structural—response of the microbiota in M. rosenbergii.

Transcriptomic and metabolic pathways underlying 5-HTP-mediated reproductive and physiological benefits

Transcriptomic analysis revealed substantial overlap in gene composition between the CON and HTP15 groups, indicating that 5-HTP supplementation did not introduce novel genes but modulated transcriptional abundance in the hepatopancreas. The PCA further demonstrated that the administration of HTP15 resulted in an augmentation of inter-sample variability, which is indicative of a shift in gene expression patterns following exposure to 5-HTP. In contrast to the findings in vertebrate models, where 5-HT has been shown to suppress mitochondrial metabolism and fatty acid oxidation (as evidenced by the downregulation of CPT1 in brown adipocytes (Rozenblit-Susan et al., 2018), the present study observed elevated expression of LOC136833721 (cpt1) in M. rosenbergii. This suggests a species-specific metabolic response. In accordance with the findings of the physiological data, transcript levels of LOC136841544 (vg) were found to be significantly increased, thereby reinforcing the role of 5-HTP in enhancing reproductive output through vitellogenesis in M. rosenbergii. It is noteworthy that over 90% of tryptophan, the precursor of 5-HTP, is metabolized via the kynurenine pathway, generating various bioactive metabolites (Szabó et al., 2025). The upregulation of LOC136847192 (kynurenine aminotransferase), a pyridoxal-5′-phosphate-dependent enzyme involved in kynurenine metabolism, implies an indirect regulatory link between 5-HTP and tryptophan catabolism. Mechanistically, 5-HT activates multiple G protein-coupled receptors, which can stimulate the PI3K-Akt signaling cascade (Dizeyi et al., 2011). The 5-hydroxytryptamine 2A (5-HT2A) receptor has been demonstrated to modulate focal adhesion kinase and integrin activity (Mauler et al., 2022), ECM interactions and cytoskeletal dynamics. This suggests a potential explanation for the enrichment of ECM-receptor interaction and focal adhesion pathways. Furthermore, 5-HT has been demonstrated to modulate insulin signaling and glycogen metabolism, thereby contributing to alterations in the insulin resistance pathway (Haleem and Mahmood, 2021; Watanabe et al., 2017). Collectively, KEGG pathway analysis confirms that HTP15 treatment significantly affects key signaling and metabolic pathways—including PI3K-Akt signaling, ECM-receptor interaction, focal adhesion, and insulin resistance—in M. rosenbergii, highlighting its broad regulatory influence at the transcriptomic level.

In the present study, analysis of tryptophan-related metabolites revealed that 5-HTP supplementation significantly elevated levels of tryptamine, xanthurenic acid, tryptophol, and 3-indoleacetic acid in M. rosenbergii. Tryptamine is metabolized by enzymes common to the 5-HTP pathway (Ramos et al., 1999), and its increased concentration suggests an overall upregulation of 5-HTP metabolism. Furthermore, the rise in tryptophol and 3-indoleacetic acid—both recognized as indicators of microbial 5-HTP metabolism (Fernández-Cruz et al., 2020; Hornedo-Ortega et al., 2018)—supports the conclusion that HTP15 treatment enhances serotonergic activity in M. rosenbergii. In the present study, the hepatopancreas, a central metabolic organ, exhibited significantly elevated TPH protein in the HTP15 group compared to the control group. This observation signifies an augmented anabolic processing of 5-HTP. Intriguingly, despite the exogenous administration of 5-HTP, there was an observed upregulation of endogenous synthesis in this tissue, which is likely attributable to elevated physiological demand for serotonin and its derivatives. This observation may provide a biological basis for the enhanced growth performance exhibited by the HTP15 group. Conversely, intestinal TPH protein expression was suppressed following 5-HTP supplementation. The intestine is the primary site of nutrient absorption; it is directly exposed to higher concentrations of dietary 5-HTP, which may inhibit endogenous synthesis as a protective mechanism against potential tissue stress. The hepatopancreas, being less directly exposed, may maintain or enhance its synthesis capacity. These organ-specific responses suggest a compensatory regulation of 5-HTP metabolism, though further experimental validation is needed to confirm this hypothesis.

The present study utilized integrated analysis of transcriptomic and tryptophan metabolomic data to reveal a positive correlation between kynurenic acid levels and AdipoR expression. While only approximately 5% of tryptophan is converted to 5-HTP, the majority is metabolized via the kynurenine pathway, which is catalysed by tryptophan pyrrolase in hepatopancreatic tissues (Boadle-Biber, 1993). As demonstrated in the seminal study by Meunpol et al. (1998), kynurenine and its derivatives have been shown to modulate immune responses and molting processes in crustaceans such as P. monodon. Adiponectin, acting through AdipoR, has been demonstrated to exert anti-inflammatory effects by suppressing pathways such as NF-κB signaling (Jian et al., 2019). Given the overlapping immunoregulatory roles of kynurenic acid and adiponectin signaling, it is proposed that kynurenic acid may influence inflammatory status by modulating AdipoR expression, thereby partly explaining the immunomodulatory effects of 5-HTP in M. rosenbergii. Furthermore, the adiponectin system has been demonstrated to enhance insulin sensitivity and mitigate metabolic dysfunction (Ye and Scherer, 2013), which is in accordance with the observations that 5-HTP supplementation improves insulin resistance-related pathways. This provides a plausible context for the significant positive correlation observed between 5-hydroxyindole-3-acetic acid and AdipoR expression in the present study. Integration of the microbiome and metabolites further indicated a correlation between 5-hydroxyindole-3-acetic acid and the genus Enterobacter. It has been demonstrated that certain members of the Enterobacteriaceae family are capable of directly metabolising tryptophan or 5-HT (Esmaili et al., 2009). It has been established that both Aeromonas and Enterobacter are opportunistic pathogens linked to host immune regulation (Gao et al., 2020; Li et al., 2023), and their association with kynurenic acid in this study suggests a possible microbiota-dependent modulation of tryptophan metabolism and immune function. Crucially, this microbiota-dependent modulation may extend to reproductive regulation. Given that immune status is closely tied to energy allocation for vitellogenesis, and that tryptophan metabolites can act as signaling molecules influencing steroidogenesis (Khan et al., 2017; Mosa et al., 2016), the observed correlations between specific genera (e.g., Enterobacter, Aeromonas) and tryptophan metabolites suggest that gut microbiota shifts may indirectly support the enhanced E2 levels, Vg levels, and ovarian development observed in the HTP15 group.

Limitations and future directions

There are some limitations and future research directions worth mentioning here. While the present histological evaluation followed established criteria for descriptive comparison, future studies incorporating automated morphometric analysis would provide further quantitative precision on cell proportions. Additionally, while dietary 5-HTP effectively reduced oxidative damage (lowered ROS and MDA), it did not significantly alter the activities of antioxidant enzymes (CAT and SOD). This suggests that its protective effect may be mediated primarily through non-enzymatic mechanisms. Specifically, the elevated levels of tryptophan-derived metabolites (e.g., tryptamine, 3-indoleacetic acid) detected in the present metabolomic analysis could directly act as free-radical scavengers. Additionally, the activation of the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, as revealed by multi-omics analysis, may enhance cellular resistance to oxidative stress independently of classical antioxidant enzymes. Thus, 5-HTP appears to bolster antioxidant defense via a metabolite-driven and signaling-centric strategy. However, these proposed mechanisms remain speculative, and further experimental validation is required. Consequently, this will be the primary focus of future investigations. In addition, future studies employing pharmacological inhibition or gene silencing are warranted to confirm the causal role of the PI3K-Akt pathway and other identified nodes in mediating the effects of 5-HTP.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this investigation confirms that dietary supplementation with 5-HTP at suitable levels (150 mg/kg) confers comprehensive benefits to M. rosenbergii broodstock. These advantages encompass enhanced growth, improved hepatopancreas condition, strengthened antioxidant defenses, boosted immune status, and superior reproductive outcomes. Integrated multi-omics analyses indicated that the actions of 5-HTP are mediated through modulation of critical pathways, including carbon metabolism, PI3K-Akt signaling, ECM-receptor interaction, focal adhesion, and insulin resistance, collectively illustrating the complex regulatory networks involved.
