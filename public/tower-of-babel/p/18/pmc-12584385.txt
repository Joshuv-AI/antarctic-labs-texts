DAPK1-positve macrophages facilitate immunosuppressive microenvironment and determine immunotherapy efficacy in colorectal cancer
Authors: Jiang Chang, Yuxu Niu, Shizhao Zhou, Ziqi Zhang, Weiying Zhu, Junchang Zhu, Haoran Xiu, Ke Shang, Qingyang Feng, Ye Wei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundColorectal cancer (CRC) is a highly immunosuppressive malignancy characterized by limited therapeutic options and a poor prognosis. Within the CRC tumor immune microenvironment (TIME), tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) represent the predominant immune cell population. This study aimed to characterize the specific macrophage subsets contributing to CRC progression and resistance to immunotherapy.MethodsSingle-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) was used to identify the key macrophage marker genes. The clinical significance of death-associated protein kinase 1 (DAPK1), was evaluated in clinical CRC cohorts using immunohistochemistry, immunofluorescence, Kaplan-Meier plots and Cox Proportional Hazards Models. Cytometry by time-of-flight, RNA-seq, flow cytometry and an in vitro co-culture system were performed to explore the functional role of macrophage-derived DAPK1 in the TIME. Subcutaneous tumor models and splenic-liver metastasis models were constructed to assess the potential of DAPK1 inhibition to enhance immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) efficacy.ResultsscRNA-seq analysis revealed predominant DAPK1 expression in intratumoral macrophages of CRC TIME. Elevated macrophage-specific DAPK1 level was significantly associated with worse prognosis. DAPK1+TAMs exhibited immunosuppressive properties and inhibit CD8+T cells cytotoxicity. Pharmacological inhibition of DAPK1 enhanced the efficacy of anti-PD-1 therapy in murine models.ConclusionsDAPK1+TAMs infiltration was an independent risk factor for overall survival in patients with CRC. These DAPK1+TAMs exhibit an immunosuppressive phenotype and promote the establishment of the TIME with immune evasion. Targeting DAPK1 may represent a potential strategy for synergizing immunotherapy in CRC treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07268-7.Keywords: Cancer immunotherapy, Tumor-associated macrophage, Tumor immune microenvironment, Death-associated protein kinase

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is one of the most prevalent malignancies globally and is a leading cause of cancer-associated deaths [1]. Although curative-intent resection remains the cornerstone of treatment, frequent postoperative recurrence and distant metastasis render many patients incurable [2]. Current systemic therapies, including chemotherapy and targeted agents, exhibit limited efficacy [3, 4]. The advent of immunotherapy has revolutionized the treatment paradigm for CRC. Nevertheless, immunotherapy has been reported to demonstrate clinically satisfactory responses in only a small proportion of CRC patients [5]. Strategies to enhance the efficacy in the majority of patients require further investigation. In addition, traditional tumor-node-metastasis staging is becoming increasingly inadequate for precise patient stratification in the era of emerging novel therapies. Therefore, the identification of novel and valuable biomarkers for CRC requires further investigation.

The efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) critically depends on the balance between immunostimulatory and immunosuppressive networks within the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) [6]. As the predominant immune cells, tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) display functional polarization: pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages exert anti-tumor effects, while pro-tumorigenic M2 TAMs promote cancer progression [7]. Crucially, M2 TAMs drive immunosuppression, enabling tumor invasion, metastasis, and therapy resistance across malignancies [8–10]. The significant phenotypic heterogeneity and plasticity of macrophages exert profound effects on the balance between anti-tumor immunity and immune evasion in TIME [11, 12]. Consequently, a deeper understanding of the molecular landscape and key subsets of TAMs within CRC is essential to reprogram the immunosuppressive microenvironment, enhance immunotherapy efficacy, and improve the prognosis. However, well-validated TAM markers remain unexplored.

The death-associated protein kinase (DAPK) family is an important member of Ca2+/CaM-regulated serine/threonine kinases [13], and death-associated protein kinase 1 (DAPK1) is the most studied because of its multiple biological functions [14–16]. Several studies have demonstrated that DAPK1 is a tumor suppressor, that is mainly involved in cellular autophagy and apoptosis [17–19]. In many common cancers, DAPK1 is significantly methylated, thus loss of expression can often be observed [20–22]. In CRC, while DAPK1 knockdown has been reported to enhance the pro-tumorigenic phenotype in the HCT116 cell line [23], other studies paradoxically associate elevated DAPK1 expression with adverse clinical outcomes [24]. These seemingly contradictory findings suggest complex functions of DAPK1 in CRC pathogenesis, warranting further mechanistic investigations to resolve its precise oncological role.

In this study, we identified a critical macrophage subpopulation and investigated its effect on the CRC TIME and clinical outcomes. We revealed predominant DAPK1 expression in TAMs using single-cell RNA sequencing datasets. Analysis of multiple independent CRC cohorts demonstrated that patients with high co-expression of DAPK1 and CD68 had the worst prognosis. Based on Cytometry by time-of-flight (CyTOF) analysis and co-culture experiments, we found that DAPK1+TAMs impaired CD8+T cell function, and fostered the development of an immune cell-enriched microenvironment, indicating a potential satisfactory response to immunotherapy. Furthermore, DAPK1 promoted an immunosuppressive phenotype in macrophages. DAPK1 inhibition enhanced the anti-tumor efficacy of PD-1 blockade in murine models. This study provides the first comprehensive examination of DAPK1 expression patterns within the TIME, and elucidates the clinical significance of DAPK1+TAMs in CRC.

Materials and methods

Patients and clinical cohorts

This retrospective cohort study analyzed tumor and adjacent normal tissue samples from 141 patients with CRC who underwent curative-intent resection at Huadong Hospital, Fudan University (Shanghai, China) between January and December 2017. Eligible patients met the following criteria: (1) age 18–80 years, (2) histologically confirmed CRC. Exclusion criteria were: (1) concurrent or prior malignancies, (2) receipt of neoadjuvant therapy, (3) incomplete clinicopathological data or unavailable specimens, and (4) known hereditary CRC syndromes (familial adenomatous polyposis or Lynch syndrome). The detailed cohort characteristics are provided in Supplementary Table 1. OS was defined as the time from surgery to death from any cause and was censored at the last documented follow-up. All the participants provided written informed consent and underwent standard postoperative surveillance. Clinicopathological data were retrieved from the institutional electronic medical records. The study protocol was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Huadong Hospital, Fudan University (Approval No. 20240172).

Transcriptomic profiles and associated clinical metadata for TCGA cohort were retrieved from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA-COAD and TCGA-READ) through the Genomic Data Commons portal (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). Data from the GSE39582 cohort were downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo). Differential gene expression analysis of RNA-seq data was conducted using the “limma” package (version 3.58.1) in R. Subsequent functional enrichment analyses, including Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways enrichment, were performed with the “clusterProfiler” package (version 4.10.0). Immune cells infiltration was calculated with the “GSVA” package (version 1.48.3). Besides, the single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data from a public CRC cohort (GSE178341, dataset 1) [25] were analyzed to identify macrophage-associated genes and cell-cell communication, with another independent scRNA-seq dataset (PRJNA748525, dataset 2) [26] used for validation.

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) analyses

The scRNA-seq datasets were analyzed as follows. Rigorous quality control removed doublets (cells exhibiting nFeature_RNA = 500-5,000 and nCount_RNA = 400 − 25,000) and cells with > 30% mitochondrial gene content. After normalization and log-transformation, 2,000 highly variable genes were selected for principal component analysis (PCA) based on average expression and dispersion. The top 20 significant principal components (p < 0.05) were subsequently utilized for t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) to resolve the distinct cellular subclusters. “FindAllMarkers” was used to calculate the marker genes of each cellular subcluster (both min.pct and logfc.threshold were set as the default value of 0.25, p_val_adj < 0.05), and cell populations were further subclassified through comprehensive annotation using CellMarker 2.0 (Supplementary Fig. 1B, 2 C). Subsequently, we employed the “FindMarkers” function to identify the TAM-related markers (both min.pct and logfc.threshold were set at the default values of 0.25, p_val_adj < 0.05). All analyses were implemented using the “Seurat” package (version 4.3.0) in R. To infer intercellular communication networks, cell-cell interactions were reconstructed using the “CellChat” package (version 1.6.1).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) and immunofluorescence (IF) assay

Specimens from the Huadong cohort were subjected to IHC analysis, IF staining, and subsequent prognostic correlation analyses. Related protocols [27] were applied to formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue sections. For IHC, following overnight incubation with primary antibodies at 4 °C, 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB) was used for chromogenic detection. DAPK1 and CD68 protein levels were assessed using a 9-point integrated score (staining intensity: 0 = absent, 1 = faint, 2 = moderate, 3 = intense) and positive cell density (0 = ≤ 5%, 1 = 6–25%, 2 = 26–75%, 3 = ≥ 76%). For IF, fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies were applied at room temperature after primary antibody incubation. Nuclei were counterstained with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) before whole-slide scanning. Two pathologists blinded to the clinical parameters independently evaluated five random high-power fields per section, with specimens showing >10% inter-rater discordance subjected to consensus reevaluation. Patients were stratified into high and low expression groups using X-tile software (v3.6.1)-determined optimal cutoffs according to OS prediction. The primary antibodies used were: DAPK1 (1:200, 25136-1-AP, Proteintech) and CD68 (1:1500, 66231-2-Ig, Proteintech).

Cytometry by time-of-flight (CyTOF)

Single-cell suspensions were prepared from eight freshly resected CRC specimens using enzymatic dissociation (Miltenyi Tumor Dissociation Kit, 130-096-730) followed by 70-µm filtration. After erythrocyte lysis, cells were labeled with a 41-parameter metal-tagged antibody cocktail. Mass cytometry was performed using a Helios3™ system (Fluidigm, formerly PLTTech) with calibration beads. Normalization and primary data processing were performed using a cloud-based cytobank platform. Unsupervised clustering coupled with t-SNE dimensionality reduction was performed in R using the “cytofkit” package (v1.18.0), which enables phenotype identification based on marker expression signatures. Details of the antibodies are provided in Supplementary Table 3.

Multiplex immunofluorescence assay (mIF)

Serial sections from 20 CRC specimens underwent deparaffinization and endogenous peroxidase blockade, according to the manufacturer’s specifications. After 30-minute protein blocking with 3% BSA/TBST, tissue sections were incubated with the primary antibody anti-DAPK1 (1:200, 25136-1-AP, Proteintech), followed by horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody. Following iterative heat-mediated antigen retrieval, sequential multiplex immunohistochemistry was performed using the following reagents: CD68 (1:1500, 66231-2-Ig, Proteintech), CD8 (1:200, ab101500, Abcam), PD-1 (1:500, ab237728, Abcam), and panCK (1:200, ab7753, Abcam). Nuclear counterstaining utilized 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, 10 min) prior to mounting with fluorescence-preserving medium.

Cell lines

THP-1 human monocytic leukemia cell line and MC38 murine colon carcinoma cell line were procured from the National Stem Cell Resource Center (Chinese Academy of Sciences, Shanghai). Both cell lines were propagated in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (100 U/mL) under standard culture conditions (37 °C, 5% CO2, humidified atmosphere). THP-1-derived macrophages were differentiated via 48-hour stimulation with 100 ng/mL phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA, Sigma). The DAPK1 inhibitor TC-DAPK6 (HY-15513, MedChemExpress) was utilized at a concentration of 100 nM for 24 h [28, 29].

Mouse primary cells

Primary bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) were harvested from C57BL/6J mice using established protocols [30]. Briefly, bone marrow cells were aseptically flushed from dissected femurs, tibiae, and iliac bones, followed by washing phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) washing. Erythrocyte depletion was achieved using red blood cell lysis buffer. Cells were then cultured in complete DMEM containing 10% heat-inactivated FBS, 100 U/mL penicillin-streptomycin, and 20 ng/mL recombinant murine macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF, Sigma) for 7 days to induce macrophage differentiation.

Co-culture assay

CD8+T cells were isolated from C57BL/6 mouse spleens via magnetic bead separation (480035, BioLegend). Following purification, cells were enumerated and seeded into 96-well plates pre-coated with anti-CD3 and anti-CD28 antibodies (2 µg/mL, 100340 and 102116, BioLegend). Cultures were maintained in complete RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 U/mL IL-2, 2 mM L-glutamine, 50 µM β-mercaptoethanol, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, non-essential amino acids, and 10 mM HEPES. After 24 h of activation, the cells were co-cultured with macrophages at a 1:1 ratio for 48 h. The functional capacity of CD8+T cells was evaluated using flow cytometry.

Western blot (WB) assay

Following lysis in RIPA buffer containing a protease inhibitor cocktail, protein concentrations were quantified using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay kit (Thermo Scientific). Denatured samples (in 5× loading buffer) were resolved by SDS-PAGE and electroblotted onto PVDF membranes (Merck Millipore). The membranes were sequentially incubated with primary antibodies and corresponding HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Protein signals were subsequently visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence reagents and captured using an ImageQuant LAS 4000 imaging system. The primary antibodies used are listed in Supplementary Table 4.

Flow cytometry (FC)

THP-1-derived macrophages were detached using Accutase (Sigma), centrifuged, and resuspended in PBS. Murine tumor tissues were minced and enzymatically digested for 1–2 h at 37 °C with collagenase IV (Sigma) and DNase I (Sigma). The resulting digest was then filtered through a 70-µm nylon mesh to obtain single-cell suspensions. The cells were stained with a fixable viability dye (Thermo), followed by fixation and permeabilization using a commercial kit (Thermo, Fixation/Permeabilization Kit). After washing with PBS, intracellular staining was performed for 30 min at 4 °C in the dark using fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies. Samples were acquired on a BD FACSAria flow cytometer and data were analyzed using FlowJo software (BD Biosciences). The antibodies used are listed in Supplementary Table 5.

Animal experiments

All animal experiments were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Fudan University (Approval ID: 2024-HDYY-066) and performed in accordance with established protocols [30, 31]. Subcutaneous Xenograft Model: Six- to eight-week-old C57BL/6J mice were randomly assigned to treatment groups. Mice received subcutaneous injections of 200 µL cell suspensions containing 1 × 10⁶ MC38 cells into the flank region. Tumor size was monitored every three days, and volume was calculated using the formula: V = 0.5 × (width² × length). Tumors were surgically excised 18 d post-injection to determine the final volume and weight. Splenic-liver Metastasis Model: Mice anesthetized with isoflurane received intrasplenic injections of 100 µL suspensions containing 1 × 10⁵ MC38 cells. The animals were humanely euthanized 17 d post-inoculation. The metastatic burden was quantified by enumerating macroscopic liver tumor nodules and measuring the largest diameter of each lesion.

For anti-PD-1 therapy, mice were administered 200 µg of anti-mouse PD-1 antibody (clone BP0273, BioXCell) or isotype-matched control IgG via intraperitoneal injection every 3 d for a total of 4 doses, starting 7 days post-tumor implantation. For DAPK1 inhibition, mice were injected intraperitoneally with TC-DAPK6 1 mg/kg (HY-15513, MedChemExpress) or vehicle control daily for 14 days [32, 33].

Statistical and reproducibility

All experiments were independently performed a minimum of three times. Data analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism software (version 8.0) and R software (version 4.3.1). Comparisons between two groups were performed using Student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U test when appropriate. All p-values were two-sided with statistical significance as follows: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001; ns denotes non-significance. Survival curves were compared using the log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test, where p < 0.05 defined statistical significant.

Results

Single-cell transcriptome sequencing analyses identify macrophage markers associated with CRC prognosis

To characterize the transcriptomic profile of TAMs in CRC, we analyzed a public scRNA-seq dataset (GSE178341, dataset 1, including 64 CRC tissues and 36 normal tissues). Using t-SNE dimensionality reduction based on the top 20 principal components, we identified 12 distinct cell populations in all the samples (Supplementary Fig. 1A) and in the extracted tumor samples (Fig. 1A). These populations were annotated using established canonical markers (Supplementary Fig. 1B) and comprised malignant cells, endothelial cells, fibroblasts, B cells, plasma cells, CD4+T cells, CD8+T cells, macrophages, monocytes, dendritic cells (DCs), mast cells and neutrophils. Significant transcriptional heterogeneity was evident among the sub-clusters (Fig. 1B). Characteristic macrophage-related genes were subsequently isolated for further analysis. KEGG pathway analysis revealed that these genes were predominantly enriched for phagocytosis, antigen processing and presentation, and immunoregulation (Fig. 1C). GO analysis further demonstrated their involvement in immunomodulation, with molecular functions including major histocompatibility complex (MHC), immunoglobulin binding, and immune response regulation (Fig. 1D), all of which were processes intrinsic to macrophage function. To identify key regulatory genes, we intersected three gene sets: (1) TAM-related markers derived from scRNA-seq data, (2) prognosis-associated genes (univariate Cox regression p-value < 0.05) calculated from TCGA CRC cohort (TCGA-COAD + TCGA-READ, n = 592), and (3) differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between tumor and adjacent normal tissues from the TCGA CRC cohort (Supplementary Fig. 1C). The least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression with 1000-fold cross-validation refined this intersection to 8 candidate genes: GPX3, DAPK1, HSPA1A, IFI30, GLA, TKT, CXCL2 and CXCL3 (Fig. 1E-G). Furthermore, we annotated another independent scRNA-seq dataset (PRJNA748525, dataset 2, including 13 CRC tissues) (Supplementary Fig. 2A-C), and identified the key genes in TAMs in the same way (Supplementary Fig. 2D-G). Collectively, our scRNA-seq analyses identified candidate genes potentially implicated in TAM function and influencing clinical outcomes in patients with CRC.

DAPK1 is upregulated in CRC stromal tissues and mainly expressed in macrophages

Next, we assessed the expression patterns of these genes. Bubble and t-SNE plots revealed that DAPK1 was highly expressed in macrophages but was minimally detected in other cell types (Fig. 2A, B), a finding corroborated by scRNA-seq dataset 2 (Supplementary Fig. 3A, B). Consequently, we prioritized DAPK1 in subsequent investigations. scRNA-seq analysis demonstrated increased infiltration of DAPK1+TAMs in tumor tissues (Fig. 2C), especially in those with metastasis (Fig. 2D). Furthermore, DAPK1 expression was positively correlated with macrophage infiltration scores derived using the ssGSEA algorithm (TCGA: R = 0.549, GSE39582: R = 0.503) (Fig. 2E, F). Elevated DAPK1 levels in the CRC stromal compartments (Fig. 2G, I) and their association with macrophage infiltration were validated in our Huadong cohort using IHC (Fig. 2H and Supplementary Table 2). Double-labeling immunofluorescence confirmed the co-localization of DAPK1 and CD68 in CRC specimens (Fig. 2J), which was also validated in scRNA-seq data (Supplementary Fig. 3C, D). Overall, these data indicate that DAPK1 is upregulated in CRC stroma and is predominantly expressed by macrophages.

Infiltration of DAPK1+TAMs is significantly correlated with prognosis in patients with CRC

We next assessed the prognostic significance of DAPK1+TAMs in patients with CRC using Kaplan-Meier analysis. Elevated DAPK1 expression was significantly associated with worse OS in our cohort (Fig. 3A). Meanwhile, the OS did not differ significantly between the patient groups stratified by CD68 expression levels (Fig. 3B). Notably, patients exhibiting high co-expression of both DAPK1 and CD68 had the worst OS, whereas those with low expression of both markers demonstrated the most favorable outcomes (Fig. 3C). The prognostic value of combined DAPK1 and CD68 expression was further validated in the TCGA cohort (Fig. 3D-F) and GSE39582 cohort (Fig. 3G-I). Furthermore, univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analyses confirmed that high intratumoral co-expression of DAPK1 and CD68 was an independent prognostic factor of OS in our cohort (Fig. 4J). The above results indicate that high infiltration of DAPK1+TAMs adversely affects the clinical prognosis of CRC patients.

DAPK1+TAMs correlate with immune exhaustion contexture in CRC TIME

To investigate the immunomodulatory function of DAPK1+TAMs within the TIME, we performed CyTOF on fresh surgical specimens from 8 CRC patients. Unsupervised clustering delineated 24 distinct cellular subsets across these tumors (Fig. 4A), with DAPK1 expression predominantly localized to macrophages and partially localized to plasma cells (Fig. 4B). Categorization of patients by DAPK1+TAMs abundance revealed significant immune remodeling in the tumors (Fig. 4C). Notably, these cases exhibited higher infiltration of immune cells, including CD8+T cells and regulatory T cells (Tregs), along with an increased accumulation of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) (Fig. 4D). Results of ssGSEA also showed a higher immune cell-enriched TIME in patients with high co-expression of DAPK1 and CD68 (Supplementary Fig. 4A, B). Functional characterization of CD8+T cells showed that upregulation of the exhaustion marker PD-1 was positively correlated with DAPK1+TAMs levels (Fig. 4E, F), which was further validated quantitatively by mIF (Fig. 4G-I). Cell-cell communication analysis revealed that DAPK1+TAMs exerted a markedly enhanced influence on CD8+T cells compared with DAPK1−TAMs (Supplementary Fig. 5A, B), indicating that DAPK1+TAMs might participate in the exhaustion of CD8+T cells. Together, these findings suggest that TIME with a high infiltration of DAPK1+TAMs may be enriched in immune cells; however, these cells are likely to be functionally suppressed.

DAK1+TAMs promote the exhaustion phenotype of CD8+T cells in CRC

To investigate whether promotion of the exhaustion phenotype was dependent on DAPK1, we performed subsequent in vitro experiments. BMDMs were isolated from C57BL/6 mice and differentiated into M0 macrophages by stimulation with M-CSF for 5 days in vitro. Tumor conditioned medium (TCM), prepared from the culture supernatant of the murine colon carcinoma cell line MC38 by centrifugation at 14,000 rpm and filtration through a 0.22 μm filter, was then added to the M0 macrophages. After 24 h of incubation with TCM, M0 macrophages were successfully induced to become TAM-like (Fig. 5A). Notably, TAMs exhibited significantly upregulated protein expression of DAPK1 compared to M0 macrophages (Fig. 5B), suggesting a crucial role for DAPK1 in TAMs. Subsequently, the induced TAMs were co-cultured with murine-derived CD8+T cells (Fig. 5C), and the expression of functional markers on CD8+T cells was assessed by FC. The DAPK1 inhibitor, TC-DAPK6, significantly restored CD8+T cell cytotoxicity, as evidenced by the increased production of TNF-α (Fig. 5D), Granzyme B (GZMB) (Fig. 5E) and IFN-γ (Fig. 5F). Concurrently, TC-DAPK6 downregulated the expression of exhaustion-associated molecules in CD8+T cells, including TIGIT (Fig. 5G), PD-1 (Fig. 5H) and CTLA-4 (Fig. 5I). Taken together, these results demonstrated that the contribution of DAPK1+TAMs to the formation of exhausted CD8+T cells is dependent on DAPK1. Antagonizing DAPK1 restores the immunostimulatory capacity of macrophages, thereby activating CD8+T-cell cytotoxicity.

Inhibition of DAPK1 by TC-DAPK6 reverses the immunosuppressive phenotype of macrophages

Transcriptomic profiling of THP-1-derived macrophages treated with TC-DAPK6 or vehicle revealed the mechanisms underlying the DAPK1-driven immunosuppressive phenotype. RNA sequencing identified 3566 upregulated and 1653 downregulated DEGs (Fig. 6A). GO enrichment analysis highlighted a significant involvement in cell migration, chemotaxis, signaling receptor activator activity, and immune-related processes (Fig. 6B). KEGG pathway analysis further revealed TC-DAPK6-mediated activation of key inflammatory pathways, including antigen processing and presentation, phagosomes, NF-κB signaling, TNF signaling, Toll-like receptor signaling, and IL-17 signaling pathways (Fig. 6C). Conversely, TC-DAPK6 suppressed the MAPK, PI3K-Akt, and Ras signaling pathways (Fig. 6C). Analysis of specific gene sets demonstrated TC-DAPK6-mediated downregulation of transcripts associated with M2 polarization, Treg recruitment, and T cell exhaustion (e.g., ARG1, CLEC10A, SPARC, SPP1, and TGFB3) (Fig. 6D). Simultaneously, TC-DAPK6 enhanced the expression of genes related to M1 polarization, tumoricidal activity, and T/NK cell chemotaxis (e.g., CCL3, CCL4, CCL5, CXCL10, and TNF) (Fig. 6D). Western blot analysis confirmed TC-DAPK6-induced NF-κB pathway activation and MAPK pathway inhibition in macrophages (Fig. 6E). Flow cytometry indicated that TC-DAPK6 treatment modulated macrophage surface markers, reduced CD206 expression (Fig. 6G), and elevated MHC-II levels (Fig. 6H), without altering CD80 expression (Fig. 6F). Analysis of scRNA-seq datasets also revealed that DAPK1+TAMs expressed higher levels of M2 markers than M1 markers (Supplementary Fig. 6A, B). Furthermore, apoptosis assays confirmed that TC-DAPK6 did not induce macrophage apoptosis (Supplementary Fig. 6C), excluding programmed cell death as the reported mechanism. These findings indicate that DAPK1 inhibition attenuates the immunosuppressive properties of DAPK1+TAMs.

Inhibition of DAPK1 enhances the efficacy of anti-PD-1 antibody in CRC in vivo

Based on these results, we concluded that DAPK1 promoted the immunosuppression of macrophages and induced the exhaustion of CD8+T cells. Therefore, we investigated whether the inhibition of DAPK1 synergizes with PD-1 blockade therapy. Subcutaneous tumor and splenic-liver metastasis models were established in C57BL/6 mice using MC38 cells. Mice were treated according to predefined schedules and grouped accordingly (Fig. 7A). In subcutaneous tumor models, both monotherapy with TC-DAPK6 and anti-PD-1 treatment significantly reduced tumor volume and weight. Notably, the combination therapy group exhibited the highest therapeutic efficacy (Fig. 7B-D). Similarly, in liver metastasis models, treatment with either TC-DAPK6 or anti-PD-1 monotherapy significantly decreased the number and size of the metastases. The combined inhibition of DAPK1 and PD-1 maximally reduced the metastatic tumor burden (Fig. 7E-H). To elucidate the mechanism of the enhanced anti-tumor efficacy of the combination therapy, we performed comprehensive immune profiling of MC38 subcutaneous tumors. We observed a significantly elevated frequency of MHC-II+ macrophages and CD8+T cells in the combination therapy group compared to that in the single-agent treatment and control groups (Fig. 7I, J). Collectively, these data demonstrate that pharmacological DAPK1 inhibition with TC-DAPK6 synergizes with PD-1 blockade to augment anti-tumor immunity in CRC models.

Discussion

Despite modest efficacy in solid tumors, PD-1 blockade highlights the immunosuppressive TIME as a major constraint on immunotherapy. As the predominant TIME immune cells, macrophages are considered promising combination targets for ICIs. Current TAM targeting strategies include depleting TAMs, blocking the recruitment of monocytes and the infiltration of TAMs, and reprogramming TAMs towards M1-like, anti-tumor states [34–37]. However, non-selective TAM depletion risks impair essential phagocytic and antigen-presenting functions, undermining anti-tumor immunity [10]. Depletion can also trigger compensatory tumor-associated neutrophil influx, contributing to drug resistance, or rebound monocyte mobilization, and promoting metastasis [38, 39]. Crucially, TAMs possess inherent plasticity; while typically pro-tumorigenic, they can be re-educated into tumoricidal M1 effectors that stimulate T cell responses and suppress tumors [31, 40]. This plasticity makes reprogramming the most viable strategy, and the discovery of novel TAM-specific targets is critical for advancing immunotherapy.

DAPK1, traditionally regarded as a tumor suppressor gene, induces cellular autophagy and apoptosis [41–44]. Hypermethylation of its promoter has been reported in many common cancers [21, 44–47] and thus the loss of DAPK1 expression is associated with poor patient prognosis [48, 49]. Studies have demonstrated that knockout of DAPK1 leads to a more pro-tumorigenic phenotype in the HCT116 cell line [23]. Paradoxically, however, elevated DAPK1 expression has also been linked to worse prognosis in patients with CRC [24], a finding consistent with the observation in our current study. This apparent contradiction can be explained by our results. scRNA-seq data from CRC samples revealed that DAPK1 expression was predominantly localized to macrophages, with negligible expression in tumor cells. This indicates that DAPK1 may mainly exert a pro-tumorigenic role in TAMs, rather than a tumor-suppressive role in tumor cells. Our results suggest that elevated DAPK1 expression impairs macrophage phagocytosis, suppresses immune effector pathways, activates immunosuppressive signaling, promotes M2 polarization and contributes to an immune-evasive microenvironment that supports tumor progression, which may explain why high DAPK1 expression predicts poor patient prognosis. Indeed, the reverse roles of the same gene in different cell types are not uncommon in cancer. For example, CCR7 is reported to promote epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition in cancer cells but enhance immune infiltration and the anti-PD-1 response in stromal cells [50]. Similarly, OX40 supports angiogenesis in endothelial cells yet activates anti-tumor immunity in T cells [51]. Therefore, the function of DAPK1 must be interpreted within its specific cellular context.

Another interesting finding was that CRC with high DAPK1+macrophage infiltration exhibited enriched tumor-infiltrating exhausted CD8+T cells. Tumor immune infiltration signatures critically influence the immunotherapy response [52]. Current TIME classifications include immune-excluded, immune-inflamed, and tertiary lymphoid structures [6]. We propose that DAPK1+TAMs-rich CRC resembles immune-inflamed TIME, characterized by abundant exhausted cytotoxic lymphocytes and immunosuppressive leukocytes, which are often associated with high immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) responsiveness. Critically, DAPK1 inhibition reversed TAM-induced CD8+T cell exhaustion in vitro and synergistically enhanced anti-PD1 efficacy in vivo, confirming our hypothesis. Thus, DAPK1 critically shapes an immunosuppressive TIME by mediating macrophage interactions and represents a promising predictive biomarker for CRC immunotherapy responses.

However, it should be noted that although our RNA sequencing data revealed that DAPK1 antagonism led to the activation of the NF-κB pathway and the suppression of the MAPK pathway in macrophages, the current study cannot confirm whether DAPK1 directly regulates these pathways. Future mechanistic studies are required. Furthermore, hypermethylation silences DAPK1 expression in multiple cancers including CRC, thus inhibiting DAPK1 combined with PD1 blockade can generate synergistic immunotherapeutic effects. However, it remains unknown whether DAPK1 inhibitors might paradoxically promote tumorigenesis in cancers with high DAPK1 expression, given their reported tumor-suppressive role, which requires further investigation in different tumor types. Finally, the clinical therapeutic efficacy of DAPK1 inhibitors in CRC treatment remains to be validated in larger clinical cohorts.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we report for the first time the role of DAPK1+TAMs in CRC. Through large-scale clinical cohorts, we demonstrated the infiltration of DAPK1+TAMs as an independent risk factor for OS in patients with CRC. These DAPK1+TAMs exhibit an immunosuppressive phenotype and promote the establishment of the TIME with immune evasion. Targeting DAPK1 may represent a potential strategy for synergizing immunotherapy in CRC treatment.
