Interactions between nutrition and the epigenome: how can it be harnessed for public health?
Authors: Maria Anastasopoulou, Irene Dereki, Argyro Sgourou, George Lagoumintzis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionA substantial body of evidence shows that dietary habits influence gene expression and epigenetic processes, holding significant implications for public health policies. Epigenetic modifications are increasingly associated with metabolic state, disease risk, and biological aging. Translating mechanistic results into scalable, efficient nutritional epigenetics treatments is difficult.Areas coveredThis perspective article explores the influence of nutrition on epigenetic processes, focusing particularly on DNA methylation, histone modifications, and the significance of noncoding RNAs. It further clarifies the relationship between diet-induced epigenetic changes and increased risk for obesity, cardiovascular diseases, cancer, and neurocognitive impairments. Establishing causation, integrating multi-omics data, and creating ethical, equitable dietary advice are discussed when incorporating nutriepigenetic insights into public health. Recent studies demonstrate that dietary intake can influence epigenetics during pregnancy, adolescence, and adulthood, with long-term effects. However, individual variability, epigenome-wide association study confounders, and tissue specificity remain methodological challenges.Expert Opinion/CommentaryTo advance the field, causal research, methodological rigor, and ethical governance must be prioritized. Using integrated microbiome, epigenomic, and metabolomic data can improve dietary risk assessment and intervention while assuring equal access and appropriate use of sensitive biological data

Introduction

Research on gene-environment interactions during most of the twentieth century rested on the idea that the DNA sequence primarily determined biological outcomes, a perspective that is no longer rational. It is becoming more and more clear that regulating gene activity is governed by multiple layers that react to environmental signals without altering the integrity of the underlying genetic code. The field currently referred to as epigenetics comprises a collection of chemically stable yet reversible modifications that play a crucial role in determining the accessibility and utilization of genetic information within cellular contexts. Among the environmental factors influencing this layer of regulation, nutrition is important because dietary intake supplies the nutrients that can facilitate the establishment and preservation of epigenetic modifications, including DNA methylation, histone modifications, and the regulation of non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs). Moderate effects are observed at individual genomic loci, but when added together, their effect becomes significant over time.

An important turning point in this conceptual framework was the successful completion of the Human Genome Project [1]. The dynamic regulation of gene expression within living systems has taken priority over the static cataloging of DNA variants. Covalent epigenetic modifications constituting the epigenome link the inherited genetic templates and the environmental experiences. This way, cells are allowed to combine metabolic, nutritional, and external inputs to keep their cellular identities, generating specialized tissues [2–4].

Folate, selenium, retinoids, polyphenols, fatty acids, isothiocyanates, and allyl sulfides are associated with alterations in DNA methylation and chromatin state via various molecular pathways [5]. Exploring the biological encoding of nutritional information from these and other compounds in the epigenome establishes a foundation for linking dietary behaviors to strategies aimed at preventing health issues at the population level.

Mechanistic basis: nutrition-epigenome interactions

Nutritional effects on epigenetic programming are mostly explained by changes in DNA methylation, histone modifications, and non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs). Gene accessibility to transcriptional machinery is modulated by these changes. Following this, we explore particular food categories that may behave as enzyme inhibitors, cofactors, or methyl donors for epigenetic enzymes.

One-carbon metabolism: methyl donors and cofactors

One-carbon metabolism (OCM) provides the methyl groups needed for macromolecule methylation in the cell, linking micronutrient status to epigenetic stability. Folate, some B complex vitamins, choline, betaine, and methionine contribute to this circle. Serine hydroxymethyltransferase (SHMT), a pyridoxal-5′-phosphate-dependent enzyme, transfers a carbon unit from serine to tetrahydrofolate (THF), producing glycine and 5,10-methylenetetrahydrofolate (5,10-methylene-THF). This intermediate is then irreversibly reduced by methylenetetrahydrofolate reductase (MTHFR), a FAD-dependent enzyme that requires riboflavin (vitamin B2), forming 5-methyltetrahydrofolate (5-MTHF). 5-MTHF then donates its methyl group to homocysteine via methionine synthase, a vitamin B12-dependent enzyme that catalyzes the reaction through a methylcobalamin intermediate, thereby regenerating methionine (Figure 1). Methionine is then converted into S-adenosylmethionine (SAM), the universal methyl donor involved in DNA, RNA, histone, and lipid methylation reactions. As methyl groups are transferred, S-adenosylhomocysteine (SAH) is generated. SAH then inhibits methyltransferases (DNMTs), establishing a feedback loop that connects intracellular methylation capacity to nutrient-driven carbon flux [6]. When the availability of these vitamins and cofactors is disrupted, the effects are seen in both global and gene-specific DNA methylation patterns [7].

This diagram illustrates how dietary micronutrients (folate, vitamin B6, riboflavin (B2), vitamin B12, and choline) fuel the folate and methionine cycles to generate S-adenosylmethionine (SAM). Dietary folate is reduced to dihydrofolate (DHF) and tetrahydrofolate (THF) by DHFR. THF is converted to 5,10-methylenetetrahydrofolate (5,10-MTHF) with vitamin B6 as a cofactor and then reduced to 5-MTHF by MTHFR with vitamin B2. 5-MTHF then acts as a methyl donor to convert homocysteine into methionine, catalyzed by methionine synthase and requiring vitamin B12. The choline pathway provides an independent methyl source to recycle homocysteine. Methionine is converted to SAM, the universal methyl donor. Methyltransferases transfer the methyl group from SAM to various substrates (DNA, RNA, histones, and lipids) to generate methylated products and the byproduct S-adenosyl-homocysteine (SAH). Consequently, dietary deficiency or supplementation (indicated by plus-minus symbol) of micronutrients acting as substrates for enzymes that take part in OCM determines the availability of methyl groups required for the methylation of DNA, RNA, histones, and lipids, possibly leading to alterations in the epigenome.

The epigenetic significance of OCM during development and cell fate decisions is supported by experimental data. Impaired folate transport can reduce DNA and histone methylation, alter gene regulation, and cause neural crest-related defects [8], while vitamin B12 deficiency compromises cellular reprogramming and reduces regenerative plasticity [9].

Additionally, riboflavin, through its FAD cofactor, supports the activity of α-ketoglutarate-dependent dioxygenases, which are vital for DNA and histone demethylation. Disorders linked to FAD deficiency or impaired riboflavin metabolism display abnormal chromatin structures and disrupted DNA methylation patterns [10]. Collectively, these observations indicate the importance of adequate micronutrient availability at both individual and population levels.

Polyunsaturated fatty acids and epigenetic flux

Fatty acids also serve as epigenetic regulators. Fatty fish and fish oil are rich in n-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids (n-3 PUFAs), including eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), and demonstrate cardioprotective, anti-inflammatory, and triglyceride-lowering effects. Epigenetic alterations are included among the mechanisms that have been proposed for the link between n-3 PUFAs consumption and enhanced metabolic profile [11].

DNA methylation of CpG sites within gene promoters leads to gene silencing, while hypomethylation of promoters not associated with CpG islands disrupts gene regulation [12,13]. n-3 DHA may influence DNA methylation by modulating methyl group availability within the OCM [8]. Experimental evidence supports the epigenetic effects of n-3 PUFAs. Kulkarni and his team [14] demonstrated that DHA affects placental DNA methylation in rats on a vitamin B12-deficient diet. In vitro and animal studies show that DHA can modify histone acetylation and DNA methylation patterns, including changes observed in immune-related cancer cells as well as in the placenta and fetal liver following maternal n-3 PUFA-rich diets [15,16]. It can therefore be suggested that fatty acid status influences developmental trajectories, with potential implications at the population level.

Microbiome-derived SCFAs and histone acetylation

Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) are associated with gut microbiota, nutrition, and chromatin regulation [17]. The gut microbiome ferments dietary fibers and produces acetate, propionate, and butyrate that act as signaling metabolites [10]. A particular SCFA, butyrate, has been shown to act as a strong inhibitor of histone deacetylases (HDACs), increasing histone acetylation and promoting the transcription of genes involved in metabolic and inflammatory pathways [12,18–20]. SCFAs may also enhance chromatin accessibility by increasing intracellular acetyl-CoA availability, thereby supporting histone acetyltransferase activity [21–22]. Considering everything mentioned above, the microbiome converts dietary components into molecules that influence the host’s epigenome. The important unresolved question is how different forms of dietary fiber affect SCFA production and how these changes relate to the host’s long-term disease risk.

Polyphenols and phytochemicals: DNMT/HDAC/miRNA axis

Curcumin

Curcumin, also known as diferuloylmethane, is a natural compound found in the plant Curcuma longa. Currently, various curcumin products have been developed, including capsules, pills, and even energy drinks [23]. Recent studies have shown that curcumin is frequently used as an anti-cancer treatment because it influences non-coding RNAs through epigenetic mechanisms [24,25]. MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are short non-coding RNAs that regulate gene expression post-transcriptionally by reducing mRNA stability or inhibiting translation [26]. Curcumin has been shown to alter the expression of several miRNAs in cancer and osteoarthritic models, affecting downstream targets involved in cell proliferation and inflammation [26,27]. Furthermore, curcumin possibly modifies the methylation status of miRNA promoters, suggesting bidirectional interactions between dietary components and epigenetic regulation.

Resveratrol

Resveratrol (RVT, 3,5,4′-trihydroxystilbene) is a naturally occurring polyphenol first isolated from the plant Veratrum grandiflorum. It is nowadays produced by several plant species, especially grapevines, in response to UV radiation and fungal stress, where it acts as a natural defense compound [28]. DNA methylation in mammalian cells is controlled by DNA DNMTs. Experimental studies have demonstrated that resveratrol can affect gene expression in breast cancer cells, partly by modulating their activity [29,30]. The BRCA1 gene encodes a tumor suppressor protein involved in DNA repair, and its reduced expression is linked to sporadic breast cancer. Papoutsis and colleagues [31] observed that resveratrol reduces DNMT1 activity at the BRCA1 promoter in breast cancer cells, potentially restoring gene expression [31].

In general, these data suggest that edible phytochemicals can change how genes are turned on and off, such as by changing the activity of DNMT and HDAC and affecting the expression of miRNA. This gives us an idea of how plant-based compounds can help prevent chronic diseases.

Additional nutrient classes and chrono-nutritional context

In addition to the classes of molecules already described, chromatin-modifying enzymes and transcriptional complexes are affected by additional substances such as crucifer isothiocyanates, allyl sulfides, selenium compounds, retinoids, and vitamin D metabolites. Through this link, the epigenetic effects of a cell’s energy level can be traced. In addition, chrono-nutrition (i.e., the timing of food intake) could be significant, since circadian cycles in histone acetylation and methylation, in addition to DNA methylation, affect metabolic characteristics and govern nutrient digestion [4].

Non-communicable diseases and the epigenome: implications and public health applications

Dietary patterns, environmental exposures, and stresses connected to lifestyle all have an impact on epigenetic states, which positions the epigenome as a possible target for disease prevention. This versatility places epigenetic regulation in a position to serve as a framework for public health measures that aim to minimize the burden of non-communicable diseases (NCDs). There are a great number of dynamic epigenetic markers that have been linked to metabolic disorders, cardiovascular diseases (CVDs), neurodegeneration, and cancer. It has been found that diets that are abundant in methyl donors and bioactive compounds derived from plants are linked to epigenetic profiles that are healthier and to a slower biological aging process. Dietary habits that are typical of the Western world, on the other hand, have been associated with accelerated epigenetic aging. When these links are recognized, it will be easier to develop nutrition-based strategies that have the potential to alter the development of disease at both the individual and population levels.

Early-life programming and the DOHaD paradigm

According to the Developmental Origins of Health and Disease (DOHaD) hypothesis, epigenetic modifications during intrauterine life are a result of environmental variables [32]. Dietary habits of the mother throughout pregnancy and the early postnatal period have the potential to affect the epigenetic programming of the child [33,34]. Consequently, deficiencies in maternal nutrition have the potential to induce epigenetic alterations that have an impact on the development of the embryo [34]. Building on the mechanisms described above, maternal nutrition can affect the child’s DNA methylation through the one-carbon metabolic pathway [35–37], influencing neurodevelopment and risk of congenital defects. This is because the diet of the mother plays a significant role in the epigenetic modification and DNA repair of genes that are necessary for neurodevelopment in the children [38]. Moreover, it has an effect on the likelihood of congenital abnormalities, including neural tube defects (NTDs), congenital heart disease, and mouth clefts.

Clinical trials have confirmed that folic acid supplementation effectively reduces the risk of NTDs [39]. Additional experimental data indicate that a maternal high-fat diet can interfere with one-carbon metabolites and enzymes, resulting in genome-wide DNA hypermethylation in progeny mice [35]. Also, gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) in humans has been associated with the altered DNA methylation of key metabolic genes, such as LEP, in newborns [36]. Therefore, adequate intake of one-carbon nutrients may be vital in counteracting adverse epigenetic programming that predisposes offspring to cardiometabolic disorders later in life.

Both maternal overnutrition and undernutrition during pregnancy may predispose the fetus to metabolic imbalances and obesity later in life [38,40,41]. A maternal high-fat diet has been linked to disruptions in fetal hypothalamic regulation of appetite and energy balance, as well as to altered postnatal eating behaviors, through epigenetic regulation of genes encoding the leptin receptor, POMC, and neuropeptide Y [42,43]. Conversely, adherence to the Mediterranean diet during pregnancy has been associated with a reduced risk of overweight and obesity in children at four years of age [44]. Additionally, certain nutrients—such as DHA—administered during late pregnancy to obese women or those with excessive gestational weight gain have been positively correlated with lower infant adiposity during exclusive breastfeeding [45] (Figure 2).

Impact of maternal nutrition on fetal epigenome and metabolism. Maternal dietary patterns during pregnancy influence the offspring’s epigenetic profile in accordance with the Developmental Origins of Health and Disease (DOHaD) hypothesis. A maternal high-fat diet is linked to disrupted hypothalamic appetite regulation, possibly by altered DNA and histone marks and, consequently, the aberrant expression of appetite-regulating genes such as Leptin Receptor (LEPR), Pro-opiomelanocortin (POMC), and Neuropeptide Y (NPY), an epigenetic misprogramming that may predispose the offspring to metabolic dysfunction later in life. In contrast, adherence to a Mediterranean diet or late-pregnancy docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) supplementation is associated with a reduced risk of childhood overweight, obesity, and lower infant adiposity.

The famine of 1944–1945 in the Netherlands, caused by a wartime food embargo, offered a unique opportunity to examine how various stages of prenatal malnutrition influence health in adulthood through the Dutch Hunger Winter Families Study [46]. In a recent investigation, Tobi et al. [41] identified individuals born between 1945 and 1946 whose mothers were exposed to the famine, comparing them with matched-sex, non-exposed siblings. DNA methylation analysis of whole blood from 422 exposed individuals and 463 sibling controls revealed that methylation at CpG cg09349128 in the PIM3 gene linked to energy metabolism mediated 13.4% of the association between famine exposure and adult body mass index (BMI). Similarly, methylation changes at CpGs in TXNIP (involved in β-cell function) and ABCG1 (related to lipid metabolism) together accounted for 80% of the link between prenatal famine exposure and adult triglyceride levels. These findings strongly suggest that DNA methylation mediates the association between in utero famine exposure and increased adult BMI and triglycerides, underscoring the importance of maternal nutritional adequacy as a public health priority.

Metabolic health and obesity

Over the past 50 years, obesity has progressively grown to pandemic levels, with unhealthy dietary habits, physical inactivity, and an obesogenic environment playing the most important roles in its development [47]. During the last few decades, research has shown that genetics also contributes to this condition, as more than 50 genes have been identified as potential contributors to obesity [48]. It is well known that epigenetics is one of the mechanisms connecting environmental factors to changes in gene activity [49].

Numerous studies have investigated genome-wide DNA methylation and its link to obesity and related traits in human tissues [50]. Xu et al. [51] identified several CpG sites associated with obesity in the peripheral blood leukocytes of a young population and found that DNA methylation variability was higher in obese individuals than in lean controls. Later, an epigenome-wide association study (EWAS) identified 29 additional CpG sites associated with obesity and confirmed these findings in neutrophils, demonstrating that differences in cell-type composition did not influence the associations observed in peripheral blood leukocytes. The results also indicated a relationship between DNA methylation, gene expression, and obesity for a few genes, such as SOCS3, where obesity was linked to decreased methylation and increased gene expression [52].

In addition to these findings, Xu et al. [53] found that individuals who experienced five or more major stressful life events and had lower methylation at a CpG site within the SOCS3 gene had a 1.38-fold higher risk of obesity, additionally pinpointing the role of stress in obesity. SOCS3 expression has also been shown to induce leptin resistance, with implications for energy homeostasis [54]. Leptin is a hormone primarily produced by adipocytes and is essential for energy homeostasis, governing food consumption and use of energy. In diet-induced obesity, there is an increase in leptin gene expression and circulating leptin levels, although this elevation does not regulate body weight, indicating leptin resistance [55,56]. Zwamborn et al. [57] indicated that obesogenic high-fat diets in experimental mice correlated with heightened DNA methylation at the promoters of the leptin and PPARG2 genes.

Furthermore, a clinical trial has shown that a high-fat diet, regardless of the type of fat (saturated or polyunsaturated), increases DNA methylation in fat cells. This is especially true in promoter regions that affect adipocyte differentiation, lipid metabolism, and pathways associated with the expandability of fat cells [58]. These observations strengthen the hypothesis that epigenetic alterations may contribute to obesity pathogenesis, highlighting the potential of nutrition-based strategies to modulate metabolic risk at the population level.

Cardiovascular and inflammatory regulation

Cardiovascular diseases are a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide [59]. The progressive loss of vascular flexibility that occurs with aging contributes to the aging process [60]. Epigenetic mechanisms are increasingly recognized in this process, and nutrients can influence these epigenetic marks [61,62].

Hypomethylation of genomic DNA has been observed in several tissues, including vascular smooth muscle cells, atherosclerotic lesions, and peripheral blood leukocytes, in people with atherosclerosis [63]. Specifically, an EWAS found 10 hypermethylated and 16 hypomethylated CpG sites in genomic DNA from the aortic intima of atheromatous plaque lesions compared with control plaque-free intima [64]. A hypermethylated CpG site was identified within the long noncoding RNA HOTAIR gene, which may interact with inflammatory signaling pathways, including those involving thymic stromal lymphopoietin [65]. Additionally, some hypomethylated loci were found within genes encoding proteins involved in the Wnt signaling pathway, which is known for its role in atherosclerosis [66].

Building on this evidence, nutrient-induced epigenetic regulation can modulate genes involved in lipid metabolism and energy balance. Postprandial triglyceride responses to a high-fat dietary challenge reduced methylation of CPT1A, which promotes hepatic fatty acid β-oxidation, while increasing methylation at sites on genes such as APOA5, SREBF1, and ABCG1, which control lipogenesis and lipoprotein metabolism [67]. The findings were supported by results from the KORA and InCHIANTI cohorts, which identified new loci linked to methylation of four CpG sites in the CPT1A, APOA5, SREBF1, and ABCG1 genes across two European populations. These methylation changes are suggested to play a role in regulating abnormal blood lipid levels, thereby contributing to the development of complex lipid-related disorders [68–70]. Therefore, lipid metabolism is regulated by nutritionally responsive DNA methylation changes, supporting the role of epigenetics in cardiometabolic disease susceptibility.

Cancer prevention and adjunct therapy

In cancer, DNA, RNA, and histone modifications are often disrupted. These issues may come from mutations, changes in the expression of the enzymes responsible for these modifications, or misdirected recruitment of these enzymes to specific genomic regions [69]. As a result, cancer cells show significant changes in chromatin structure and accessibility. Currently, research is exploring small synthetic molecules targeting multiple epigenetic factors as cancer treatments [71,72]. This indicates that some of the protective effects of diet on cancer development and progression may occur through the regulation of epigenetic mechanisms by dietary compounds [73,74].

DNA methyltransferase inhibitors (DNMTi), azacytidine (AZA), and decitabine (DAC) are among the most widely used epigenetic drugs in cancer treatment, as DNA methylation is often deregulated in cancer [72]. Their exact mechanisms in cancer cells are not fully understood; however, studies indicate that DNMTi can reactivate tumor suppressor genes, such as cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor 2 A (CDKN2A), which are frequently silenced through DNA methylation in malignant cells [75]. Certain dietary polyphenols with catechol structures—such as catechin, epicatechin, and epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG)—have demonstrated DNMT1 inhibitory activity, leading to the reactivation of tumor suppressor genes in vitro [76,77]. Lastly, as mentioned previously, other polyphenols, such as resveratrol [30,31] and stilbenes [29], have been identified as regulators of cellular epigenetic events, including DNMT activity [29,31] (Figure 3).

Modulation of DNA Methyltransferase (DNMT) activity by dietary compounds and therapeutic agents in cancer. This figure compares the mechanisms of dietary polyphenols and pharmaceutical agents in epigenetic enzyme targeting. The catechol-containing dietary polyphenols, such as catechin, epicatechin, and EGCG, have been shown to function as DNMT inhibitors similarly to the way nucleoside analogs, azacytidine (AZA) and decitabine (DAC), inhibit DNMT activity by adding methyl groups to CpG islands in DNA. While synthetic DNMT is typically incorporated into DNA to form covalent complexes with the enzyme, dietary polyphenols are thought to modulate activity through direct interaction with the enzyme’s catalytic site or by depleting the methyl donor pool. Both approaches possibly reverse DNA hypermethylation, leading to the reactivation of silenced tumor suppressor genes. This loss of repressive methyl marks allows for the transcriptional reactivation of silenced genes critical for cell cycle control and DNA repair, such as CDKN2A and MGMT.

Besides providing bioactive compounds that link nutrients and epigenetic regulation, diet also supplies many substrates necessary for central metabolic processes. Several of these metabolites and cofactors, such as NAD and acetyl-CoA, are essential for regulating the function of various epigenetic enzymes [75]. In cancer, the one-carbon metabolic pathway is often upregulated to support rapid cell division. Enzymes like phosphoglycerate dehydrogenase (PHGDH) boost serine biosynthesis, which increases SAM levels, leading potentially to hypermethylation [78]. Conversely, reducing dietary methionine intake lowers SAM levels, thereby decreasing EZH2 activity and slowing tumor growth [79]. EZH2 is the catalytic subunit of the polycomb repressive complex 2 (PRC2), which methylates histone 3 at lysine 27 (H3K27), a modification involved in gene repression that is commonly upregulated in cancer [80,81].

HDAC inhibitors have also been developed with proven anticancer effects, as histone acetylation is regulated by the activity of histone acetyltransferases [69]. A high-fat diet, which has been linked to a higher risk of cancer and faster progression, lowers the acetyl-CoA/CoA ratio. This change is linked to less histone acetylation in the white adipose tissue of mice [82]. Furthermore, dietary interventions that modulate acetyl-CoA availability and are anticipated to impact histone acetylation are being increasingly investigated as potential cancer therapies [83]. Overall, metabolic modulation of epigenetic enzymes represents a potential avenue for nutrition-informed cancer prevention and therapy.

Neurocognitive function and aging

The mechanisms of epigenetics also have an effect on the development and aging of the brain [38]. Choline is essential for maintaining DNA methylation patterns in the developing brain, while n-3 PUFAs influence the epigenetic regulation of genes associated with neuroplasticity and neuronal communication [34]. Furthermore, polyphenols work to regulate genes that are involved in oxidative stress, inflammation, and neuronal survival through an epigenetic mechanism [25–28]. As an example, EGCG can influence the activity of DNMT and HDAC, which in turn has an effect on the expression of genes in pathways that are associated with neuroprotection [77]. Diets rich in whole foods, antioxidants, and n-3 PUFAs are associated with slowed epigenetic aging. A diet that is high in processed foods and saturated fats, on the other hand, has been related to a more rapid loss in cognitive function [40,44].

From bench to population: public health applications

It is quite hard to turn molecular epigenetic insights into nutrition policy for the entire population. Nutritional epigenetics provides a framework that integrates early-life programming, biomarker development, precision nutrition, and structural food policy. At the molecular level, dietary exposures interact with pathways that control metabolic balance, inflammation, brain development, and susceptibility to chronic diseases.

The intersection between long-term biological memory and nutritional exposure is where epigenetic regulation functions. B vitamins, methyl donors, choline, n-3 PUFAs, SCFAs, polyphenols, and other nutrients and bioactive dietary substances can modulate epigenomic processes [5–31]. Gene networks that regulate hunger, metabolism, the immune system, cardiovascular health, and neurocognitive aging are affected by epigenomic changes. Some epigenetic markers that are susceptible to nutritional factors are dynamic and can endure through cellular divisions or even across generations under some circumstances. Moving from identifying individual markers toward system-level understanding will be crucial for translating molecular insights into broader population frameworks. The study of the nutritional epigenome may contribute to important stages of development and ultimately improve dietary recommendations that consider biological variances and strengthen evidence-based nutritional standards. Besides, social and environmental factors affect dietary practices and biological stress responses; therefore, resource availability must be assessed.

Epigenetic biomarkers of exposure and risk

The understanding of dietary consumption has been transformed by high-throughput molecular techniques that have allowed for the examination of epigenetic patterns as markers of nutritional status. DNA methylation at CpG sites, with the most interesting case in loci linked to lipid metabolism and glucose regulation, suggests the existence of dietary effects often overlooked by traditional indicators [5]. Hypomethylation of repetitive sequences, including LINE-1, is correlated with diminished fruit intake and insufficient folate exposure, highlighting their role as molecular indicators of nutritional exposure, though their applicability in population-level surveillance remains under investigation [5]. Additionally, in weight-loss contexts, initial methylation of the TXNIP gene has been linked to changes in glucose metabolism and insulin sensitivity after calorie restriction, offering a potent index for measuring responsiveness [84–87]. Furthermore, large population studies have identified CpG methylation patterns associated with BMI and the risk of type 2 diabetes, providing insights into nutritional effects on metabolism, but their use for early stratification is still largely exploratory [88].

Biological age and how dietary habits can impact it can be assessed through a new dimension, through epigenetic aging measurements, known as “epigenetic clocks”. Weight loss, regardless of the nutritional approach, has been linked to a slowdown in epigenetic aging, indicating a molecular mechanism by which nutrition influences long-term health span [85]. Nevertheless, their implementation for dietary evaluation in humans is not yet established.

Personalized nutrition and precision public health

Genetic background, epigenetic state, metabolomic profiles, and microbiome composition are factors that contribute to variation in biological response to food among different people, and that creates the concept of personalized nutrition [86,89]. These insights may allow predictions of dietary responsiveness, though practical application remains largely experimental. For example, individuals with lipid-related methylation profiles might benefit more from omega-3 supplements; meanwhile, those with miRNA markers associated with inflammation could respond better to diets high in polyphenols.

While personalized nutrition focuses on individual responses, precision public health aims to translate molecular and environmental data into interventions that are scalable and equitable at the population level.

Precision public health frameworks evaluate how epigenetic and environmental data can identify vulnerabilities and opportunities within communities. By combining epigenomic, clinical, and environmental information, public health bodies could more accurately target nutrient subsidy programs, maternal nutrition initiatives, and early-life prevention strategies [34–36,38–46]. Combined with mapping the food environment and social determinants, these strategies can help ensure equitable delivery of interventions. As these strategies are not value neutral, without strong governance, precision approaches risk increasing inequality; therefore, molecular risk stratification must be part of broader policies that expand access to healthy foods, reduce economic barriers, and offer culturally competent nutrition education. The goal is precision without excluding anyone.

Food systems, equity, and the social epigenome

The factors that shape dietary behavior include financial status, level of education, access to food, psychological stressors, environmental influences, and systemic inequalities. Social factors create stress that leads to biological markers. Epigenetic mechanisms connect these factors to differences in overall health between diverse groups of people [75,76]. Chronic stress, discrimination, and food insecurity have been linked to pro-inflammatory methylation profiles and accelerated epigenetic aging.

Public health responses should therefore focus prior these factors. Dietary policy and system-level approaches

Research in epigenetics is beginning to impact the development of nutritional policies aimed at improving population health and reducing long-term disease rates [77]. The fortification of food with folic acid serves as a prominent illustration of a nutrient-focused policy capable of enhancing health outcomes broadly [21–23]. Ethical considerations and responsible communication

Dietary epigenetic data may encompass stress, trauma, food insecurity, and other human experiences; thus, ethical implementation is crucial. It is important to clarify that epigenetic markers are not permanent but alterable marks, and without adequate safety measures, information could be exploited. Public health messages should emphasize the potential effects of structural alteration on epigenetic changes. For health equity to progress, it is imperative to ensure that all individuals have equitable access to nutrigenomic programs, food assistance, and clinical treatment [79]. Community involvement should be a feature of biobank governance, coupled with safety measures to stop commercial misuse and strategies to share gains fairly. Overall, using this information in public health practice leads to earlier intervention, nutrition based on biomarkers, and fairer dietary policies. The question is whether it will be done carefully and on a wide scale.

Challenges and limitations

Technical advances provide new challenges and constraints in many scientific domains, including nutritional epigenetics. Establishing causal linkages between nutrition and epigenetic alterations is a major difficulty. Most current research uses observational or cross-sectional methodologies, making it difficult to determine if diet causes epigenetic alterations or if metabolic or epigenetic factors affect nutrition. In obesity, which can impact DNA methylation, this reversal of causation is problematic. This problematic condition requires long-term designs and randomized nutrition interventions. Implementing these is difficult and expensive.

Another topic is how nutrition affects epigenetic systems. Dietary treatments and restrictions can affect epigenetic markers, gene expression, obesity, and other NCDs [84,85,88,89,91–97]. Noninvasive and cost-effective, these measures are widely used and positively received. Due to adherence concerns, restricted diets are hard to maintain [97]. As cohort-based research presents practical and ethical questions about personal data management, participants may feel confused about the confidentiality and security of their sensitive information, compounding the constraint.

Polygenic genomic architecture of nutrition-related disorders, including obesity and CVDs, is another nutriepigenetics’ disadvantage. Disease risk is shown by the combined impact of multiple genetic loci, each usually having a small effect. Polygenic risk scores that include a wide range of genetic variation and many epigenetic layers may improve prediction and evaluate focused nutritional solutions in specific groups [33,98].

Nutriepigenetics biggest methodological challenge is food consumption evaluation. Recall bias, inaccuracy, and systematic errors can impair self-reported dietary data in research. These flaws can reduce correlations or create false diet-epigenetic marker connections. These restrictions reduce statistical power and interpretation, especially for modest epigenetic effects. Many diet-related epigenetic signals in the literature have tiny effect sizes and inadequate reproducibility across independent cohorts. In conclusion, study design, demographics, food evaluation methodologies, and analytical methods can greatly affect nutriepigenetic results. To improve nutriepigenetic studies’ robustness and generalizability, consistent methodology, bigger sample numbers, and coordinated replication are needed. Reproducibility issues undercut correlations.

Confounders include smoking, BMI, physical activity, socioeconomic factors, and DNA-related technical difficulties that are inconsistently handled in nutritional EWASs. Inconsistency causes interpretive difficulty because these factors can connect with dietary exposures and DNA methylation [33,37]. If BMI is not appropriately assessed, differential methylation at loci involved with lipid metabolism and obesity, like ABCG1 and SREBF1, may signal adiposity processes. This emphasizes the need for systematic covariate correction to extract diet-specific methylation signature relationships.

Conclusions

Over the last 10–20 years, nutritional epigenetics has seen major progress, focusing particularly on the role of dietary components in altering the epigenome and metabolic health. To this end, there have been clear demonstrations of the role of polyphenols (such as resveratrol), B vitamins, folate, curcumin, n-3 PUFAs, and SCFAs in modulating the levels of ncRNAs, as well as of the processes of DNA methylation and histone modifications [5–35]. Additionally, it has become clearer that nutrition during pregnancy and after birth, particularly what mothers eat and how they breastfeed, can impact molecular pathways that play a role in energy balance, fat regulation, and brain development, and all these interconnections may be related to NCDs susceptibility over time [75–82,84,87–89,91]. Moreover, epigenetic markers, such as CpG methylation at the genes TXNIP, LEP, ABCG1, and SREBF1, are increasingly utilized as biomarkers to forecast individuals’ responses to diet [93–96]. However, there are still challenges in the field to address, including a lack of interventional studies, insufficient long-term data, and inconsistent adjustments for confounding factors [33,37,86,97–99]. On the other hand, personalized nutrition, multi-omics data, and causal inference can, each one alone or synergistically, improve the creation of tailored dietary treatments. Finally, it is important to ensure that nutritional epigenetic advancements are applied with equity, fairness, and inclusivity so that the benefits are not limited to a narrow or privileged group but rather are spread across all populations.

Future perspective

Nutritional epigenetics is projected to move beyond descriptive research in the coming decade and shape clinical and public health preventative measures and risk assessments. Recent research shows that food, metabolomics, and epigenomics are linked. However, study design, data integration, and practical implementation remain, hindering field advancement [99].

Study design and timing will change. Epigenetic markers and metabolite profiles change with age, environment, and health. Although short-term studies offer advantages, they struggle to document reliable biological effects. Recent research suggests that DNA methylation profiles linked to birth weight decline by early childhood and do not predict future obesity. The transience of many early-life indicators emphasizes the need for lifelong assessments [97].

Metabolomics and epigenetics are anticipated to merge. Some circulating metabolites accurately reflect nutrition, while others are affected by gut microbiota, host genetics, and lifestyle [98,99]. Comprehensive dietary assessments, metabolite profiling, and DNA methylation data will enable mediation analysis to distinguish between direct food impacts and metabolite-driven epigenetic changes in future studies.

The extent to which these epigenetic biomarkers are responsive to dietary changes and whether they are useful for risk assessment or nutritional guidance remains a major question. Current research seeks CpG sites connected to food or metabolism. The number of robust causal inference studies or randomized controlled trials with epigenetic goals has decreased [92,95]. Intervention studies, dose-response analyses, and tissue sampling are needed to improve causal models.

Scalability presents technological and social obstacles. In resource-limited regions, multi-omics technologies’ high costs and limited availability prevent wider adoption. Regulatory and governance systems must adapt to efficiently address clinical validity, data protection, and sensitive molecular information processing and enable equal access to new instruments.

Deeper molecular layer integration is expected. Epigenomic, transcriptomic, metabolomic, and microbiome databases should reveal the causal linkages between diet and inflammatory, metabolic, and functional effects. Machine-learning technologies will become more important for spotting and predicting response patterns and enabling tailored nutritional plans.

Interventions targeting microbiome and epigenome interactions are likely to increase. Dietary fiber, polyphenols, and fermented foods may interact with microbes that produce SCFAs, which regulate histone acetylation and anti-inflammatory genes. Stool microbiome, fecal metabolite, and host chromatin analyses will likely be included in future intervention trials to improve mechanistic knowledge and diet composition. Prevention research may develop epigenetic clocks into first-stage decision-support technologies. Nutrition-related aspects of biological aging may be evaluated as indicators of intervention efficacy, but ethical oversight is needed to avoid misinterpretation.

Collectively, these developments suggest that nutritional epigenetics may play a more practical role in personalized prevention and public health over the next decade. Advancement will rely on enhancements in research methodologies, more robust evidence of causality, and the establishment of ethical and regulatory frameworks.
