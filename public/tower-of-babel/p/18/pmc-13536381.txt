The utility of 3D nanostructured polyglycolic acid fibers for novel bone regenerative medicine
Authors: Itsuki Hayashi, Sayaka Nakamura-Yamada, Kojiro Abe, Eiji Ito, Wataru Katagiri, Tatsuhiko Miyazaki, Yoichi Yamada
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground/purposeAutologous and artificial bone grafts are widely applied for alveolar bone reconstruction. However, as concerns regarding invasiveness, safety, and efficacy persist, the improved novel regenerative materials are expected. This study focuses on the bone regenerative potential of three-dimensional (3D) nanofiber-based polyglycolic acid (nPGA), whose capacity for bone regeneration remains unclear.Materials and methodsFour types of 3D PGA fibrous matrices with different fiber diameters and surface densities were used. The cell affinity, proliferative capacity, and osteogenic differentiation potential of the nPGA matrices were evaluated using human dental pulp stem cells (DPSCs). The effects of nPGA on bone regeneration were also investigated using an in vivo rat calvarial bone defect model.ResultsThe cellular responses to the PGA matrix clearly differed depending on the structural properties of the PGA fibers. Cell engraftment was significantly enhanced in the nPGA group compared to the conventional PGA group. DPSCs exhibited a time-dependent proliferation on the nPGA matrices. Scanning electron microscopy revealed cellular extensions aligned along the 3D microfiber architecture. Moreover, the osteoinductive assays demonstrated that DPSCs possessed osteogenic differentiation potential on nPGA scaffolds. Histological evaluation revealed new bone formation in the nPGA-transplanted group. Immunohistochemical staining of CD31 demonstrated that neovascularization also occurred adjacent to the newly formed bone in this group.ConclusionThe 3D nPGA matrix characterized by finer fibers and low surface density exhibited a remarkable cell affinity and an effective scaffold function in osteogenesis, suggesting that it could be useful as a novel medical material in bone regenerative medicine.Keywords: Bone regeneration, Bone substitutes, Nanostructures, Polyglycolic acid

Introduction

Bone injuries and defects frequently result from trauma, tumor resection, congenital malformations, fractures, or infections, and they continue to represent a major clinical challenge.1,2 Although autogenous bone grafting is widely recognized as the standard treatment for severe alveolar bone resorption and atrophy, it has several drawbacks and limitations. It requires a highly invasive surgery that can cause donor site morbidity, such as infection and pain, and the amount of bone that can be harvested is limited.1–6 Various materials have also been used as bone substitutes, including ceramics, metals, and natural and synthetic polymers, among others.1,6,7 However, no heterologous or synthetic bone substitutes have biological or mechanical properties that are equivalent or superior to those of a natural bone.2,6 Thus, alternative materials that are optimal for bone regeneration should be developed.

Synthetic polymers are an attractive option for bone regeneration including polylactic acid (PLA), polyca-prolactone (PCL), and their co-polymers, such as poly (lacticco-glycolic) acid (PLGA).5,7,8 Polyglycolic acid (PGA) is one of the synthetic polymer materials with biocompatibility and biodegradability and widely used in the medical field as an absorbable suture material and a tissue reinforcement material.9–12 Since PGA is hydrolyzed into glycolic acid and ultimately metabolized in the body, it is highly biologically safe material. Due to their sufficient mechanical strength, PGA is considered suitable for tissue repair.6,13

Dental pulp stem cells (DPSCs) are mesenchymal stem cells that possess remarkable bone-regenerative capabilities accompanied by a high proliferation rate.14–17 Since DPSCs can be obtained from the pulp tissue of unnecessary wisdom teeth and deciduous teeth without invasive procedures, it is attracting attention as a promising stem cell source. Various scaffolds have traditionally been examined in combination with DPSCs in bone regeneration therapy.18–21 However, the clinical application of scaffolds used in cell-based therapies remains challenging, primarily due to issues related to scaffold resorption dynamics and the complexity of manufacturing processes required for clinical-grade products.

Fibers and fiber structures are increasingly being used in various medical fields, including orthopedics, neurosurgery, general surgery, and dentistry.8 In bone tissue engineering, fibers are considered useable as temporary scaffolds mimicking the bone extracellular matrix (ECM) due to their highly porous microenvironment.8 In this study, we focused on a 3D fibrous PGA matrix as bone regeneration substitute. In general, as fiber diameters decrease, the surface area of the nanofibers increases, thereby enhancing cell adhesiveness and retention. However, if the spaces between fibers are too narrow, cells cannot adequately penetrate into the nonwoven fabric. Thus, for successful cell engraftment, the spacing of fibers needs to be optimal. Therefore, we employed four types of PGA matrices to determine usefulness for bone regeneration. We evaluated the cell affinity, proliferation, and differentiation ability on PGA, which has an extracellular matrix-like 3D structure. Furthermore, the bone augmentation potential of the nanofiber-based PGA was evaluated using an in vivo animal model to investigate its efficacy as a bone substitute material.

Materials and methods

Subjects and cell cultures

A schematic overview of the experimental protocol is shown in Fig. 1. This study was conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Gifu University Ethics Committee (No. 2021-B182). All patients provided written informed consent. Human dental pulp tissues were obtained from clinical healthy permanent teeth extracted from individuals. DPSCs were isolated and cultured as previously described.14,15 Briefly, dental pulp tissues were digested in a solution of 3 mg/mL collagenase type I (Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemicals, Osaka, Japan) and 4 mg/mL dispase (Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemicals) at 37 °C. The cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 20 % fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Global Life Sciences Technologies Japan K.K., Tokyo, Japan) and antibiotics (100 units/mL penicillin G sodium, 100 μg/mL streptomycin sulfate, and 0.25 μg/mL amphotericin B; Gibco) at 37 °C under 5 % CO2. The DPSCs from passages 5–7 was used for the subsequent experiments.

Schematic overview of the experimental protocol.

Matrix preparation

Four types of 3D PGA fibrous matrices (Gunze Co., Ltd., Ayabe, Japan): two types of nanofiber-based PGA (nPGA) and two types of conventional PGA (con-PGA) that differed in fiber diameters and surface densities were used in this study. The nPGA and the con-PGA were prepared as previously described, with fiber diameters of 2.0 and 14.2 μm, respectively.22 The surface densities of the nPGA type A (nPGA-A), nPGA type B (nPGA-B), con-PGA type A (con-PGA-A), and con-PGA type B (con-PGA-B) were 10.2, 15.6, 114.6, and 242.5 g/m2, respectively. The matrix was cut to 18 × 18 mm, rinsed with 99.5 % ethanol, and dried. The dried matrix was immersed in DMEM medium. After the culture medium was allowed to soak into the matrix surface, the cells were seeded onto the matrix and used.

Viability and proliferation analyses

DPSCs were seeded on a PGA matrix in DMEM containing 10 % FBS at various density gradients of 1.3 × 104 (×1), 2.6 × 104 (×2), 6.5 × 104 (×5), and 1.3 × 105 cells/cm2 (×10). After 1, 3, 5, and 7 days of cultivation, the cell viability and proliferation rates on each PGA matrix were assessed by Cell Count Reagent SF (NACALAI TESQUE, Inc., Kyoto, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The solution absorbance in each well was measured at 450 nm by a microplate reader (EnVision XCite, PerkinElmer, Shelton, CT, USA).

Scanning electron microscopy

For the scanning electron microscopy observation, the membranes cultured with cells were fixed with 2.5 % glutaraldehyde for 1 h and washed three times with PBS. The samples were dehydrated in graded ethanol (50, 60, 70, 80, 90, 95, and 99.5 % EtOH/ddH2O and 100 % EtOH) for 15 min each and subsequently immersed in t-butanol twice, frozen, and freeze-dried overnight. Each sample was mounted and osmium-coated with OsO4 using Neoc-Pro/P (Meiwafosis Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). The images were captured using a scanning electron microscope (S-4800, Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan).

In vitro osteogenic differentiation assay

The cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10 % FBS, dexamethasone, sodium β-glycerophosphate, and l-ascorbic acid 2-phosphate for osteogenic induction. After 3, 5, and 7 days, the cells were fixed with 4 % para-formaldehyde and stained with Alizarin red S solution (Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemicals). The cells and calcium nodule formation were imaged under a microscope. For alkaline phosphatase (ALP) staining, an ALP staining kit (Muto Pure Chemicals Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) was used following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, the cells were fixed with a fixed solution and washed with H2O. The cells were incubated with the ALP substrate at room temperature. Subsequently, ALP staining was performed at 5, 7, and 10 days of induction periods.

Rat calvarial critical bone defect model

All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Gifu University (No. 2022-108). Ten-week-old Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats (males, 180–220 g) were used in this study (CLEA Japan, Inc., Tokyo, Japan). In preparation for the surgery, the rats were anesthetized by inhalation using 2 % isoflurane and local injection of lidocaine. Rat calvarial defects were created according to the previously described methods.23 Accordingly, a T-shaped incision was made in the cranial skin to expand the skull surface. Two critical-size bone defects with 5 mm diameter were made at the calvarial bone on each side using a trephine bur. The nPGA was implanted into the defect. The flaps were sutured with a nylon thread. The rats were euthanized at 2- and 4-weeks post-operation and analyzed for histological evaluation.

Histological evaluation

The calvarial bone sample was harvested and fixed in 10 % neutral buffered formalin. Following fixation, decalcification was performed using 20 % ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid solution (pH 7.4). The specimens were then embedded in paraffin, and 5 μm-thick serial sections were prepared. These sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Masson–Goldner staining to assess the extent of the new bone formation, residual fibrous tissue, neovascularization, and infiltration of inflammatory cells under a light microscope.

Immunohistochemistry

To evaluate neovascularization at the tissue regeneration site, immunohistochemical staining for CD31 was executed as follows: Formalin fixed paraffin embedded sections were deparaffinized and hydrophilized followed by incubation with 3 % H2O2 to quench the internal peroxidase, 1 % BSA/tris buffer saline to quench the non-specific protein binding, then the primary antibody against CD31 (clone 182981, Abcam, Cambridge, United Kingdom) which were raised in rabbit were incubated at 4 °C for one overnight. After washing by Tris buffer saline/0.05 % Tween 20 (TBS/Tw), for 5 min twice, sections were incubated with HRP conjugated secondary antibody polymer with rat affinity absorption (Nichirei Simple Stain Rat, anti-rabbit #414181, Nichirei Biosciences Inc., Tokyo, Japan) at room temperature for 30 min. After washing with TBS/Tw, the signals were visualized by DAB (#k3468, DAKO, Glostrup, Denmark).

Statistical analysis

All data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation. The statistical analyses were performed using the Sigma-Plot software 15 (Grafiti LLC, Palo Alto, CA, USA). The differences among the experimental groups were examined by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) tests using Tukey’s honest significant difference test. A P value less than 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant. P-values are denoted by asterisks in all figures.

Results

Assessment of the cell behavior on the 3D PGA matrix

To determine whether cells adhered to each PGA matrix, DPSCs were seeded onto the PGA matrices on a non-coating plate, and the cell viability assay was performed following a 3-day cultivation period. The nPGA matrices (nPGA-A and nPGA-B) exhibited a higher number of surviving cells compared to the con-PGA matrices (con-PGA-A and con-PGA-B) and the control group (Fig. 2A). There were statistically significant differences between the nPGA and con-PGA groups on cell survival, regardless of the cell seeding density. As for the nPGA group, more cells survived on nPGA-A compared to nPGA-B. No statistically significant difference was found among the con-PGA group. The cell attachment ability on the nPGA matrix was higher than that of the con-PGA; hence, we focused on nPGA and performed further analyses. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was performed to assess the microstructures of the nPGA (nPGA-A and nPGA-B) and DPSCs on nPGA matrices. The SEM analyses indicated that the PGA microfibers showed non-woven mat structures, while the nPGA with DPSCs depicted cell adherence and extension along the 3D structure microfibers (Fig. 2B). The cell proliferation of DPSCs grown on each PGA matrix was examined at 1, 3, 5, and 7 days. The cells cultured on the conventional 2D culture plate were defined as the positive control (PC), while those cultured on a non-coating plate were defined as negative control (NC). At cell densities of × 2 and × 1, the number of surviving cells on both the nPGA matrices (nPGA-A and nPGA-B) showed a significant time-dependent increase during the experimental period, indicating that DPSCs can proliferate on nPGA matrices (Fig. 3). On day 3, no significant differences were found among × 10, × 5, and × 2 groups. On day 5, the × 1 density cells also proliferated comparative number to the × 10, × 5, and × 2 groups. The number of the survival cells of the × 10, × 5, and × 2 groups showed significantly higher than that of PC on days 5 and 7. In contrast, NC could not proliferate during the experimental period. No significant differences were observed in the cell proliferation trend between nPGA-A and nPGA-B.

Osteogenic differentiation ability of DPSCs on nPGA matrix

To confirm whether DPSCs possess an osteogenic differentiation ability on the nPGA matrix, DPSCs were seeded onto the nPGA matrix and cultured with an osteogenic induction medium. Alizarin red staining was performed to verify the mineral deposition. Positive staining was observed following day 5 of osteogenic induction and increased on day 7 in both the nPGA-A and the nPGA-B (Fig. 4). As for the cell seeding density, the cells of the × 2 density showed more mineral deposits than those of the × 1 density. ALP staining, a marker of osteoblast differentiation, showed positive signals on days 5, 7, and 10 in both nPGA-A and nPGA-B groups (Fig. 5). Based on the Alizarin red and ALP staining, DPSCs cultured on nPGA-A promoted an osteogenic induction more than those on nPGA-B.

Histological evaluation of bone regeneration in vivo

Rat calvarial bone critical bone defect model was used to evaluate the capacity of the nPGA for bone regeneration. In the group transplanted with the nPGA-A matrix, a newly formed bone with active osteocytes was observed at 2 weeks, and matured bone formation with a good lamellar bone was seen at 4 weeks (Fig. 6). The H&E staining results revealed the bone tissue formation with a well-differentiated marrow cavity and an abundant vascularization. The osteoblastic cells were observed lining along the newly formed bone formation area. A reduction in the fibrous connective tissue within the defect area was also observed. The Masson–Goldner staining results showed a uniform distribution of the collagen fibers stained blue within the newly formed bone, suggesting a maturation of the bone matrix. In contrast, for the defects without the nPGA transplantation (control site), few bone formations were observed, and the defects were filled by the fibrous tissue, even at 2 and 4 weeks. Immunohistochemical staining of CD31 was performed to evaluate the expression of molecular markers related to neovascularization. CD31 positive signals were observed in the endothelial cells, suggesting neovascularization at the reconstruction site (Fig. 7A–D). In the nPGA transplantation group, CD31-positive cells were more frequently detected within the regenerative area compared to the control site. Neovascularization occurred adjacent to pre-existing vasculature, localized near the area of newly formed bone (Fig. 7D).

Discussion

The key factors needed for bone regeneration are osteoconduction, osteoinduction, and osteogenesis. Osteoconduction is defined as a 3D process in which the blood vessels and the osteoprogenitor cells sprouting from the host tissue invade the porous scaffold structure.24 Synthetic scaffolds are mainly used as bone substitutes that promote bone regeneration by utilizing their osteoconductive properties. Accordingly, the fabrication of scaffolds that can mimic the properties of the ECM that controls cell migration, proliferation, and differentiation in regenerative medicine is becoming increasingly important.25

This study investigated the potential of the 3D Nanostructured PGA fibrous matrix on bone regeneration. PGA has a United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval for clinical use in humans in various medical applications. Cell behavior can alter depending on the physical, chemical, and temporal characteristics of scaffolds such as fiber diameter, porosity, surface area, and surface morphology;7,22 hence, four types of fibrous PGA matrices with different fiber diameters and surface densities were examined in this study.

The verification of the cell attachment on the PGA matrices revealed that the adhesion rate of DPSCs was higher on the nPGA matrices compared to that on the con-PGA matrices, indicating that the nPGA had been determined suitable as a scaffold. The fiber diameter and the surface density of the nPGA matrix were smaller than those of the con-PGA. The nPGA used in this study exclusively comprises PGA and features ultra-fine fibers that are randomly distributed throughout the fabric and lead to a large surface area.22 This architecture closely resembles the ECM structure. The obtained findings are consistent with those of previous reports demonstrating that nanoscale structures mimic the native ECM, thereby promoting integrin-mediated adhesion and intracellular signaling.26 In other words, the ultra-fine structure of the nPGA contributes to enhanced cell affinity. The results of the SEM analyses also revealed that cells were aligned and extended along the fibers, suggesting that the 3D structure formed by the PGA fibers provides topographical guidance for the cell migration. In addition, the DPSCs exhibited a favorable proliferation on the nPGA scaffolds. Given its structural and dimensional resemblance to the native ECM, the nPGA is considered a highly suitable scaffold for promoting cell proliferation. The 3D dimensional architecture formed by the nanofiber-based PGA is suggested to provide a microenvironment conducive to the cell proliferation.27–29

To enhance the osteogenic differentiation of DPSCs, the type of scaffold appropriate for induction must also be considered.21 Our in vitro results of the Alizarin red and ALP staining indicated that DPSCs seeded onto the nPGA matrix successfully undergo in vitro osteogenic differentiation. It is important that scaffolds used for bone regeneration mimic the natural environment promoting the differentiation of the progenitor cells into the osteogenic lineage. Based on our findings, the nPGA-A, which has a lower surface density, more effectively promotes osteogenic differentiation when compared to the nPGA-B, which possesses a higher surface density. As a result, the nPGA-A has been identified as a promising scaffold for bone tissue regeneration and selected for subsequent in vivo studies.

The effective mobilization and recruitment of endogenous stem cells to the transplantation site are critical factors for achieving successful bone regeneration.30,31 Histological observations suggest that a newly formed bone is regenerated within the defect area through osteoblast induction along the scaffold surface. According to recent studies, the nanoscale structure of scaffolds accurately mimics the natural nanoscale structure of bone ECM.7 A 3D scaffold structure supporting bone formation is essential for new bone regeneration.19 Other studies also reported that the hierarchical structure mimicking the ECM provides a favorable bone regeneration environment.25 The porous nature of the 3D architecture is believed to support cellular migration and cytokine transport, thereby contributing to a favorable microenvironment for tissue regeneration.26 It is well understood that sufficient neovessel formation is required for bone regeneration. During bone repair, various cell types including stem cells as well as osteogenic and vasculogenic precursor cells migrate to the injury site and contribute to bone regeneration.31 Adequate vascularization is indispensable for supplying oxygen and nutrients to these cells.32 Our immunohistochemical analysis revealed enhanced vasculature following nPGA transplantation. The marked bone regeneration observed in the nPGA transplantation group may be attributable to the successful vascularization. The ECM-mimicking microenvironment of the nPGA fibrous matrix might be essential for cellular ingrowth, nutrient transportation, and vascularization, which are highly desired features in bone tissue engineering.

An ideal scaffold for tissue engineering must exhibit excellent biocompatibility and possess a biodegradation rate closely matching the rate of new tissue formation.21 The degradation rate of the PGA is relatively fast, that is, taking 4–6 weeks, and known to degrade more quickly compared to the PLGA and other synthetic materials such as calcium phosphate material.11 The persistence of the bone graft material remaining in the defect site may interfere with bone regeneration; thus, the high solubility of bone substitute materials is considered a critical factor in facilitating the induction of new bone tissue.

Considering the variability in the bone defect geometry, it is essential that bone graft materials exhibit sufficient flexibility to allow precise shaping and adaptation to the defect contours. The nPGA matrix possesses flexibility and sufficient mechanical strength; therefore, it can be an appropriate bone substitute material.

Limitation of this study is that, although in vitro experiments demonstrated the cell affinity of the nPGA matrix and its suitability for supporting cell proliferation and osteogenic differentiation, and in vivo rat experiments suggested a tendency for enhanced bone regeneration compared with no transplantation, the evaluation was restricted to the use of the nPGA matrix. Therefore, future studies should investigate the combined transplantation of nPGA matrices with DPSCs in vivo, and evaluate bone regeneration histologically and quantitatively using histomorphometric analysis, which may lead to the development of more optimal therapies for bone regeneration.

The main bone substitute requirements are biocompatibility, appropriate new bone replacement through bone remodeling, little evidence of inflammation, and high manageability.2,20 This study investigated the usefulness of 3D PGA fibrous matrices for bone regeneration. The results obtained in this study demonstrate that the nanofiber-based 3D PGA scaffold supports migration, proliferation, and osteogenic differentiation of DPSCs. The nPGA matrix transplantation also demonstrated the ability to promote bone regeneration, thereby suggesting its potential efficacy as an osteoconductive scaffold. In summary, the 3D Nanostructured nPGA possesses the main bone substitute requirements and might be promising materials for bone tissue engineering.
