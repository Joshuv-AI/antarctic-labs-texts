Correlates of Anti-Toxoplasma IgG Antibodies in Females with Borderline Personality Disorder: A Pilot Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Marie Ociskova, Jan Prasko, Kamila Belohradova, Jozef Visnovsky, Jakub Vanek, Samuel Genzor, Pavel Sauer, Klara Latalova
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLatent Toxoplasma gondii infection has been associated with various mental health conditions, but its potential role in the pathophysiology of borderline personality disorder (BPD) remains unclear. This pilot study investigated associations of anti-Toxoplasma IgG seropositivity with the severity of BPD symptoms among adult females with BPD.MethodsThe sample consisted of 77 female patients diagnosed with BPD who were admitted for psychotherapeutic inpatient care for the personality disorder. T. gondii antibodies (IgG, IgA, and IgM) were measured using ELISA. Psychiatric and psychological assessments included Clinical Global Impression for BPD, Borderline Evaluation of Severity over Time, Buss-Perry Aggression Questionnaire, Suicide Behaviors Questionnaire-Revised, Beck Anxiety Inventory, and Beck Depression Inventory-II. Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, Fisher’s exact test, χ2 test, t-tests, and Mann–Whitney U-tests.ResultsOnly nine patients (11.7%) were seropositive for T. gondii IgG antibodies. Although none of the hypothesized differences reached statistical significance, several medium-sized effects indicated potentially clinically relevant differences between the seropositive and seronegative groups. Specifically, the seropositive group reported more suicidal ideations during the past year (d = 0.67) and exhibited less severe symptoms of abandonment (d = 0.67) and depression (d = 0.57). The remaining symptoms showed small (suicidality, aggression, anger, and anxiety) or marginal (overall BPD severity, impulsivity) differences between the two groups.ConclusionThis exploratory pilot study observed lower anti-Toxoplasma IgG seropositivity among patients with BPD than previously expected. Although the findings were not statistically significant, several trends indicated that seropositive patients might differ from the seronegative group. These preliminary results should be viewed as hypothesis-generating and validated in larger samples.

Background

Toxoplasmosis is a common parasitic infection caused by the coccidian protozoan Toxoplasma gondii.1 With a worldwide human prevalence of approximately 25% and general Czech seropositivity reaching 20%, this infection represents a significant public health problem both on the national and global levels.2–4 Domestic cats are the primary source of environmental contamination by shedding oocysts in their feces. Individuals get infected mainly by consuming contaminated food and drinks, rarely via congenital or donor transmission.5

Acute T. gondii infection is often asymptomatic and progresses to a latent chronic state controlled by the immune system.6,7 The acute form of the parasitosis tends to run without noticeable clinical symptoms or manifests in influenza-like symptoms. A severe course has been described in congenital toxoplasmosis and immunocompromised patients.6,8 In most cases, the disease takes a latent form during which human hosts usually do not present any symptoms of infection.5

Although latent toxoplasmosis typically lacks apparent clinical symptoms, it might exert significant biological and psychological effects on its host.9 Latent T. gondii infection has been linked to numerous somatic and mental health conditions. Beyond its established role in certain cancers and cardiovascular diseases, the parasite is increasingly associated with neurobehavioral changes, neurological disorders, and various psychiatric conditions.10,11 It is presumed that Toxoplasma affects the mental state of its host through several mechanisms, including specific interactions with genetic predispositions, glucocorticoids, neurotransmitters (such as dopamine and tryptophan), and the immune system.10,12,13

Research has revealed notable differences in behavior and personality traits between infected and non-infected individuals. For example, seropositive men tend to exhibit lower self-discipline, greater suspicion, impulsivity, and increased jealousy, whereas seropositive women display higher levels of interpersonal warmth and persistence, although some studies have also reported elevated aggression.14–16

Furthermore, T. gondii IgG seropositivity has been linked with feelings of hopelessness and dysphoria, as well as with an increased risk of suicide attempts.17 A dose-dependent relationship has been found regarding symptoms of depression and anxiety.18

The potential influence of T. gondii on mental health is profound, particularly concerning schizophrenia and bipolar disorder, where latent toxoplasmosis is more prevalent.10,19 For example, infected individuals with schizophrenia are more likely to experience treatment resistance and a chronic course of illness compared to seronegative individuals,20,21 though one study also highlighted that seropositive patients might also experience less severe symptoms of schizophrenia.22

Interestingly, Toxoplasma-seropositive patients with bipolar disorder who receive antipsychotic medication with anti-toxoplasmic activity (such as haloperidol) exhibit fewer depressive episodes than those treated with other medications.23 Other psychotropic medications with established anti-Toxoplasma effects include first-generation antipsychotics (cyamemazine, fluphenazine, levomepromazine, loxapine, thioridazine, zuclopenthixol) and second-generation antipsychotics (paliperidone, risperidone).24

While much of the research on T. gondii in mental health has focused on psychotic disorders, there is little data on the relationship to nonpsychotic disorders, including borderline personality disorder (BPD). BPD is a debilitating mental disorder characterized by feelings of abandonment, dysfunctional interpersonal relationships, impulsivity, intense anger, emotional dysregulation, unstable self-image, self-destructive behavior, and suicidal thoughts or attempts.25 Despite affecting 0.7 to 2.7% of the general population, it belongs among the most common mental health issues in outpatient and inpatient psychiatric care.26 The disorder significantly impairs functioning across multiple life domains, affecting relationships and work.27,28

The treatment typically requires specialized psychotherapeutic approaches such as dialectical behavioral therapy, transference-focused therapy, mentalization therapy, or schema therapy.29,30 These approaches have been developed and optimized for the therapeutic needs of patients with BPD.29 Still, despite recent therapeutic advances, nearly half of patients with BPD show minimal improvement with current treatments, leading to repeated inpatient admissions and suboptimal outcomes.26,31

Recently, growing interest has focused on possible psychoneuroimmunological mechanisms in BPD. Several studies have found indications of increased inflammatory activity in these patients, such as elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines and other markers of immune activation.32–34 Together, these findings raise the possibility that chronic low-grade inflammation and immune dysregulation may contribute to the emotional instability, impulsivity, and heightened stress reactivity typical of BPD.

Another line of research has focused on increased stress reactivity and dysregulation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis in patients with BPD. Such alterations have been described repeatedly and are often interpreted in the context of early adversity and trauma exposure.35,36 Disturbances in these stress-regulation systems may in turn affect brain circuits involved in emotion regulation, threat processing, and impulsive behavior. Because latent Toxoplasma gondii infection has also been linked to immune and neuroinflammatory changes, it may represent one of several biological factors potentially interacting with these vulnerability pathways in BPD.

Similarities between reported behavioral and emotional manifestations of latent toxoplasmosis and core symptoms of BPD raise essential questions. Preliminary research37 indicated higher T. gondii seropositivity among individuals with a comorbid personality disorder in a sample of individuals with schizophrenia, schizoaffective disorder, major depression, or bipolar disorder. Flegr and Horacek found that patients with BPD are seropositive equally often as the general population.38 However, information about their Toxoplasma status and mental health disorders was self-reported. The relationship between latent toxoplasmosis and BPD remains underexplored.

Study Objectives

This study investigated the potential role of latent toxoplasmosis as a biological factor influencing the severity of BPD. The specific aim was to explore the clinical correlates of Toxoplasma IgG seropositivity, with a particular focus on BPD symptomatology and related psychopathological features. We focused solely on female patients in this study to decrease heterogeneity caused by the described sex differences in neurobehavioral effects caused by latent toxoplasmosis.14–16

Method

Study Design and Sample

This pilot cross-sectional study was conducted between 2020 and 2022 in a psychotherapeutic ward of the Department of Psychiatry at the University Hospital Olomouc, the Czech Republic. The psychotherapeutic ward offers a six-week hospitalization with pharmacological care and intensive group psychotherapy.

Upon admittance, all patients were evaluated by an attending psychiatrist who confirmed the primary diagnosis and comorbidities according to ICD-10.39 A senior psychiatrist and head of the department then independently reviewed and confirmed the diagnoses and reviewed the inclusion and exclusion criteria.

The exclusion criteria were implemented to minimize potential confounding factors. Severe psychiatric or somatic illnesses could significantly alter the mental state of participants, while patients at high risk of suicide would require intensive care in a closed psychiatric ward. Positive specific IgM and IgA antibodies suggest (sub)acute rather than latent infection.42,43

Individuals who met all inclusion criteria and did not meet any of the first five exclusion criteria were invited to participate in the study. Those who accepted, signed an informed consent form, provided a blood sample for analysis of T. gondii antibodies, were assessed using a rating scale, and completed several self-evaluation scales. Basic demographic and clinical information were also collected.

The study was double-masked. None of the researchers and personnel in direct contact with the patients, nor the patients themselves, knew who was seropositive and who was not. Data were edited solely by a researcher who was not in direct contact with the participants. The researcher disclosed the information to the participants and attending psychiatrists after the data editing was complete, or immediately in cases where a patient was excluded due to suspected acute toxoplasmosis. The excluded patients with positive specific IgM or IgA antibodies underwent another sampling two weeks later to confirm a recent infection.42

Interpretation of Immunoglobulin Values

Clinical Global Impression for Borderline Personality Disorder (CGI-BPD)

CGI-BPD is a rating scale adapted from the general Clinical Global Impression, which has been routinely used in psychiatry over the last decades.45 Compared to the traditional CGI, the adaptation consists of nine items evaluating the most significant domains of borderline symptoms, based on DSM-IV-TR. A tenth item then assesses the overall severity of BPD during a specific period – in this case, one week. For example, some of the items are titled Abandonment, Impulsivity, Suicide, or Anger.46 Each item is analyzed separately; its scores are not summed up.

The scale has two versions – one for severity and another for improvement. The rating is conducted on a seven-point scale describing severity (0 – Normal, not at all ill; 7 – Among the most extremely ill subjects) or improvement (0 – Very much improved; 7 – Very much worse) of each item.46 We used the severity version in this study, rated by an experienced psychiatrist. The scale shows good reliability and validity.46 The Cronbach’s alpha of the scale was.79 in this study, and the correlation between the overall severity and the individual domains ranged from r = 0.65, p <0.001 (the Abandonment item) to r =0.47, p <0.001 (the Affect Instability item).

Borderline Evaluation of Severity Over Time (BEST)

This self-rating scale evaluates symptoms of borderline personality disorder according to DSM-IV and related behavior patterns. It consists of fifteen items organized into three subscales: Thoughts and feelings, Negative behaviors, and Positive behaviors. For example, the first item relates to “Worrying that someone important in your life is tired of you or is planning to leave you”.47

Participants respond to the items on a 5-point scale according to how much the symptoms caused them distress, relationship or functioning issues, and how often they engaged in more adaptive behaviors during the last week. The total score ranges between 12 and 72, with higher scores indicating more severe symptomatology; the subscale score might also be calculated. Norms have not been established yet. However, patients with BPD have reached scores between 37.7 and 45.6 points in several studies.47–50 The scale has good internal consistency and convergent and divergent validity.47 The Cronbach’s alpha was .83 in this study.

Buss-Perry Aggression Questionnaire (BPAQ)

The questionnaire measures several dimensions of aggression and tendency towards hostile behavior: physical and verbal aggression, anger, and hostility. These dimensions present four subscales that include a total of 29 items. To provide specific examples, one of the items related to anger states, “Some of my friends think I am a hot-head”, or one hostility-related item mentions, “I wonder why sometimes I feel so bitter about things”.51

The participants chose from a five-point scale according to how much each statement was characteristic of them. The total score varies from 29 to 145; higher scores indicate more pronounced aggressive tendencies.51 Novopsych calculated combined international percentile norms.52 Kobzova then created Czech percentile norms.53 The scale has adequate internal consistency and structure.51,54,55 The Cronbach’s alpha was 0.89 in our dataset.

Suicide Behaviors Questionnaire-Revised (SBQ-R)

This self-report questionnaire consists of four items that measure several facets of suicidality: suicidal ideations and/or attempts, the frequency of suicidal ideations during the last year, suicidal threats, and self-reported likelihood of committing suicide in the future.56 For example, the second item asks: “How often have you thought about killing yourself in the past year?” The potential answers also vary since each question explores a different suicidality dimension. In the mentioned example, participants choose from a five-point scale where one means “Never” and five means “Very often (5 or more times)”.56

The total score ranges from three to eighteen points, and two cut-off points distinguish an increased risk of suicide: the first item’s score ≥2 and the total score of ≥8 points for the adult psychiatric population.56 The questionnaire has proven good psychometric characteristics.56–58 The Cronbach’s alpha was .62 in this study, with the second item decreasing the overall internal consistency from 0.70.

Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI)

BAI is a self-report inventory created by Beck et al59 that measures somatic, autonomic, and subjective/mental symptoms of anxiety.60 The scale includes 21 items, each reflecting a specific symptom of anxiety, such as numbness or tingling, nervousness, or being unable to relax. Participants evaluate how much they have been bothered by each symptom during the last week and select an appropriate number ranging from 0 (Not at all) to 3 (Severely – It bothered me a lot).59

The total score ranges from 0 to 63 points, with higher scores presenting more severe anxiety.59 It may be divided into four categories: minimal (0–7 points), mild (8–15 points), moderate (16–25), and severe (26–63).61 The scale has good psychometric properties.59,62 The Cronbach’s alpha was .92 in this dataset.

Beck Depression Inventory-II (BDI-II)

BDI-II measures somatic and cognitive/emotional symptoms of depression.60,63,64 The inventory includes 21 typical and atypical symptoms of depression, for example, pessimism, irritability, anhedonia, or concentration difficulties. Participants answered according to how they had perceived each symptom during the last two weeks. Each answer might have a value from 0 to 3. Two somatic items (changes in sleep and appetite) offer more options, reflecting typical and atypical presentations of these symptoms.63 The maximum score of these items is still three points.

The total score thus lies between 0 and 63 points, and higher scores suggest more severe symptomatology. Beck et al63 established four severity categories: minimal (0–13 points), mild (14–19), moderate (20–28), and severe (29–63). Like the Beck Anxiety Inventory, BDI-II has good psychometric properties.63,64 The Cronbach’s alpha of the inventory was 0.91 in this study.

Demographic Questionnaire and Clinical Documentation

The demographic questionnaire included age, sex, education, employment, and relationship status. Several clinical characteristics were obtained from the clinical documentation: an onset and duration of BPD, a familial psychiatric history, a number of psychiatric hospitalizations, a currently used medication, and information on comorbidities.

Statistical Analysis

First, we estimated the sample size needed for the planned statistics. Based on a recommendation for pilot studies formulated by Whitehead et al65 participants’ likelihood of being seropositive,37,66 and expected small to medium effect sizes,15–18,38 the target number was eighty participants.

The statistical analyses were performed with SPSS 26. We applied descriptive statistics to summarize demographic and clinical characteristics of the sample. Since there were several missing values, we analyzed their pattern using Little’s MCAR test. The negative test suggested that the data were missing at random (χ2 = 93.1, df = 104, sig.: 0.77). T. gondii antibodies, BDI-II, BAI, and SBQ-R had no missing values. Most of the other variables had less than five percent. Given the extent and pattern of the missing data, we handled them by omitting them in the statistical analyses.67

Subsequently, we assessed normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Several parameters had a normal distribution, namely the specific IgM antibodies (W = 0.965, p = 0.07), the BPAQ total score (W = 0.973, p = 0.19) and the Verbal Aggression (W = 0.965, p = 0.07), Hostility (W = 0.980, p = 0.43), and Anger subscales (W = 0.973, p = 0.19), the BEST total score (W = 0.985, p = 0.64) and its Thoughts and Feelings subscale (W = 0.964, p = 0.06), and both BAI (W = 0.985, p = 0.61) and BDI-II (W = 0.993, p = 0.98). The remaining variables did not follow a normal distribution (W = 0.472–953; p ≤ 0.05).

The Mann–Whitney U-test, χ2, or Fisher’s exact test evaluated demographic and clinical characteristics differences. t-tests or the Mann–Whitney U-test were used to analyze hypotheses. An initial significance level of p < 0.05 was set for all analyses. The effect sizes were calculated with G*Power 3.168 and interpreted according to Cohen’s guidelines, where d 0.2 presents a threshold for a small, 0.5 for a medium, and 0.8 for a large effect.69

Ethics

The study was conducted according to the principles of the Helsinki Declaration and approved by the Ethical Committee of the Faculty of Medicine, Palacky University in Olomouc (No. 67/18). A researcher explained the study, including its aims, methodology, and all aspects of the potential participation, and provided written information and an informed consent form.70 All agreeing patients signed the informed consent form.

Registration and a Previous Non-Related Study

The study was initially planned to cover correlates and therapeutic consequences of suspected latent toxoplasmosis in females with BPD. We registered the protocol in ClinicalTrials.gov under the number NCT03932656. However, we did not receive funding due to the lack of a pilot study. Thus, we reformulated the design to perform the missing pilot study even with limited funds. Compared to the original design, we did not analyze the potential differences between seropositive and seronegative groups regarding the therapeutic change measured during the six-week hospitalization. We also modified the evaluations of the measures to cover the last week or two (in BDI-II) instead of the last year to avoid retrospective bias caused by such a long span. We then decided not to include several questionnaires that measured factors not sufficiently backed by studies focusing on latent toxoplasmosis. Furthermore, we updated the cut-off scores for the specific antibodies used by our hospital laboratory.

It should also be noted that the patients who attended this study also participated in another research that focused on childhood trauma and self-stigma, where they presented approximately a quarter of the total sample.71

Discussion

This study aimed to explore the potential role of latent toxoplasmosis as a biological factor influencing the severity of BPD symptoms. To date, the topic has been only marginally studied. Hinze-Selch et al66 and Flegr and Horacek38 were the only studies focusing on a similar population. We therefore conducted a pilot cross-sectional study to provide initial findings that could be followed up by further research.

The demographic characteristics of the group did not differ significantly from BPD patients who have been seeking psychotherapy in the long term.72–74 The average severity of BPD symptoms was comparable to other studies that used the standard Clinical Global Impression,73,71 or the disorder-specific CGI-BPD (see meta-analysis Mansour et al50). The mean score of the BEST scale also fell within the range reported by other research.47–50

Most participants were using psychiatric medication, mainly antidepressants (77.9%) and, less frequently, atypical antipsychotics (39.0%), benzodiazepines (33%), and antiepileptics with a thymostabilizing effect (27.3%). A total of 41.6% of the group were prescribed at least three medications. Polypharmacy is one of the significant problems of contemporary psychopharmacology in BPD75 and was also present in this group, though to a lesser extent than in other studies.76,77 Since this was a naturalistic study, the medication was heterogeneous and was not changed for research purposes. This is an important notion, as some psychiatric drugs have anti-Toxoplasma effects, and their presence could have influenced the results.23,24 Eight participants took one of these medications and were comparably divided between the groups. However, most medications have not been studied for their potential anti-Toxoplasma influences. It represents a limitation of the presented research and a challenge to conduct further research in pharmaconaïve populations or with samples using homogeneous medication.

There were only nine seropositive patients in the group (11.7%). Even if we consider the three patients who were seropositive for IgA or IgM antibodies, who were not included in the study, this is significantly less than the 25% reported global prevalence and the 20% found in the Czech population.2–4 Flegr and Horacek published that people who self-reported BPD as their diagnosis had an equal risk of being seropositive as the general population.38 Even earlier, Hinze-Selch et al66 reported that patients with psychotic or mood disorders and a comorbid personality disorder had a higher seroprevalence than non-comorbid patients.

There are several explanations. Apart from the potentially random finding, it might be a sign of a general decline in seroprevalence that has been reported by studies from various European regions.78–81 Possible causes of this decline include increased socioeconomic status, changes in eating habits, and increased hygiene measures to reduce the risk of infection, eg, in pregnant women.78,79 Furthermore, the specific IgG antibodies do not decrease at the same rate, but there is significant variability among individuals. While the antibodies can be present throughout life as residual traces, they often fall below detectable levels over time.41,82 It is therefore important to note that the nine patients with positive specific IgG antibodies in our study were the only ones who were positive at the time of measurement. These may not have been all the participants who had ever come into contact with Toxoplasma. This finding highlights the need for up-to-date monitoring of the seroprevalence in this population, which may be lower than previously thought.

In the first hypothesis, we assumed that seropositive patients have a higher overall severity of BPD than seronegative patients. We did not confirm the hypothesis. Both groups achieved the same average score in the report inventory CGI-BPD; the self-report scale BEST showed only a negligibly higher average score of the seropositive participants. Given the small size of the seropositive group, these might have been random findings. However, they may also be a consequence of the limited scope of the severity of the whole sample, as those with mild or extreme symptoms did not seek hospitalization. We cannot rule out that including broadening the scope of severity could have yielded different results.

For example, Bay-Richter et al18 found in a different patient population that individuals with moderate-to-severe symptoms were more likely to have specific anti-Toxoplasma IgG antibodies than controls, while this association was not found in participants with mild depressive symptoms. The results of their study suggest that seropositivity may be more common in individuals with more severe symptoms. On the other side, Pagheh et al,19 who examined patients with bipolar disorder, did not find a similar association. However, it is important to note that the authors assessed severity by the number of hospitalizations, which offers a different perspective than dimensional rating scales. Last but not least, Andreou et al22 have recently stated that seropositive individuals with schizophrenia have less severe psychotic symptoms than seronegative patients. It seems that the relationship between the severity of symptoms and mental disorders cannot be defined universally and that it might result from a complex interplay between the predisposing factors of the individual and the psychoneuroimmunological system that prevents simple conclusions.10,12,13

Subsequently, we hypothesized that patients with positive specific IgG antibodies are more impulsive than seronegative participants with BPD. We based our hypothesis on the study by Cook et al15 who reported greater impulsivity among infected individuals from a general population. Later, Peng et al83 found the same in young men with a high Phenylalanine-Tyrosine ratio. Coccaro et al16 then examined the relationship between seropositivity and impulsivity in healthy controls, psychiatric controls, and patients with intermittent explosive disorder. Some other linked also T. gondii infection to increased impulsivity, aggression, and emotional dysregulation.84,85 The initial analysis found a positive relationship between seropositivity and impulsivity, which, however, became insignificant once the influence of aggressive symptoms was controlled for. We did not confirm the hypothesis either. The difference in the severity of impulsivity between the two groups was negligible and insignificant. This may be a consequence of enlisting solely female participants. Two of the three studies found increased impulsivity only in seropositive men. It is therefore possible that gender-related factors, such as testosterone, could also influence how toxoplasmosis manifests itself in the mental and behavioral manifestations of infected individuals.41,86 As such, it may not always be observable in both sexes.

Regarding aggression, we expected that seropositive patients would show higher severity than seronegative patients. The differences between groups were not statistically significant, likely due to the number of seropositive participants. The effect size analysis revealed small differences in verbal and physical aggression and overall aggressive tendencies, which were more pronounced in the infected group. Cook et al15 also connected T. gondii IgG seropositivity with higher reactive aggression in a sample of general population females.15 A similar result was shown in an analysis including a mixed sample of people with intermittent explosive disorder, psychiatric controls, and a non-clinical population.16 Interestingly, individuals with intermittent explosive disorder scored significantly lower on the BPAQ questionnaire than patients in our study (74.8 ± 19.7 vs 85.2 ± 20.0 points). Even seronegative participants experienced pronounced aggressive tendencies, which could have influenced the results. If we had included individuals with less pronounced symptoms, we might have increased the sample’s representativeness and enabled a more valid and realistic differentiation between the groups. After all, marked symptomatology in both groups may obscure potential effects, leading to negative results unrelated to the variable’s absence.87

Coccaro et al16 found that seropositive individuals experience more severe trait and state anger than seronegative individuals. Similar results were published by Duffy et al88 who showed that female veterans with positive specific IgG antibodies reported more severe anger, and the difference between the groups was at the medium effect level. We found slightly higher, but statistically insignificant, readiness to experience anger in the self-assessment questionnaire BPAQ, and, conversely, mildly lower values in the Anger subscale of CGI-BPD. Given that these are small differences and the sample was underpowered, this may be a random result.89,90 However, social desirability might have affected the results as well. Individuals might hesitate to admit experiences or behaviors they perceive as undesirable when interviewing a researcher.91,92 The self-report questionnaires were completed by patients themselves; the presence of a health professional was only on request. This may have reduced the internal pressure to provide socially desirable answers in self-assessment instruments, thus potentially reflecting the experiences with anger in a more refined manner.91,93

Another hypothesis focused on the assumption that seropositive patients with BPD experience more severe suicidality than seronegative participants. As with the other hypotheses, the related analyses were statistically insignificant, likely due to the low number of infected patients. However, we found small differences between the groups in the CGI-BPD Suicidality subscale and the overall score of the self-report SBQ-R. Similarly, Kamal et al94 found more significant signs of suicidality, specifically a higher number of suicide attempts, among seropositive patients with depressive disorder. These findings are in line with previous evidence suggesting that T. gondii infection may influence host behavior through neuroinflammatory and dopaminergic mechanisms.14,95

Chronic low-grade inflammation and altered kynurenine pathway metabolism have been proposed as mediators of mood and behavioral disturbances in infected individuals.96 In the context of BPD, where emotional instability and impulsive self-destructive behavior are core features, such biological modulation could amplify existing vulnerabilities. A recent meta-analysis by Soleymani et al97 reported that the risk of suicidal behavior is 43% higher in seropositive individuals compared to healthy controls. The difference in suicide attempts was negligible in our sample. Studies included in the meta-analysis by Soleymani et al97 were partially prospective, while the SBQ-R assesses the lifetime incidence of suicide attempts, including those that may have occurred before getting infected with toxoplasmosis.

However, we found a medium-sized non-significant difference in the frequency of suicidal ideation during the last year, with seropositive patients thinking about suicide more often than seronegative participants. This is in line with the findings of Yalın Sapmaz et al98 who examined the correlates of seropositivity in children and adolescents with depressive disorder, and the results of the research of Bahceci et al,99 who focused on adults with depressive disorder. Interestingly, seropositive participants in our study held a slightly stronger belief that they would attempt suicide in the future. This may be a consequence of more frequent suicidal ideation during the last year because chronic suicidal ideation is a significant predictor of suicide attempt.100

Contrary to expectations, patients with positive IgG antibodies had less severe symptoms of depression and anxiety, though the differences were not statistically significant. Seropositive patients also used a significantly lower dose of antidepressants on average; the difference between the groups was statistically significant in this aspect. This could not be explained by the BPD severity or the frequency of comorbidities, as these were comparable between both groups. This is intriguing because seropositivity of the specific IgG antibodies has been consistently associated with more severe symptoms of depression11,38,88,94,99 and anxiety11,18,38,101 in various clinical and non-clinical populations.

Several mechanisms could account for this discrepancy. First, the low seroprevalence may indicate selection bias or a cohort effect, reflecting changes in T. gondii exposure over time or differences in regional hygiene practices.102 Second, immune responses to latent infection vary; individuals with higher resilience or different cytokine profiles might experience fewer affective symptoms despite infection.37 Third, given the modest sample size, this can lead to false-positive and false-negative results.89 These findings might represent statistical fluctuation rather than true inverse associations. A larger population could offer more valid data.

Strengths and Limitations

The low number of patients with positive IgG antibodies resulted in underpowered statistical analyses and statistically insignificant results. This is a major and unexpected limitation of the study. Carnahan and Brown recently described characteristics of potentially useful underpowered research, a category our study seems to fall into.90 The characteristics include hypotheses focused on a subset of a particular issue, data rich enough to outline interesting characteristics of the population being studied, the rarity of the phenomenon itself limiting statistical power, and preregistration of the study. For such studies, they recommended publishing sufficient information about the sample, not confusing statistical insignificance with the automatic absence of an effect, appropriately highlighting significant effects, and considering the most suitable statistical analyses.90 We followed their guidelines with the hope of inspiring other interested researchers.

From a methodological perspective, the study’s limited number of seropositive patients represents the primary constraint. Although several effect sizes approached medium magnitude, the small positive group prevented significance testing from reaching conventional thresholds and from employing more complex statistical analyses. Furthermore, the sample consisted solely of inpatients with moderate-to-severe BPD symptoms. Their characteristics were not representative of the whole patient group. There were no patients with subclinical, mild, or extreme BPD symptoms, and we focused only on females. Moreover, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference; the temporal relationship between infection and symptom manifestation remains speculative. The reliance on self-report instruments, while standard in clinical research, might also be influenced by current affective state or hospitalization. The relationship between seropositivity of specific IgG antibodies and the severity of BPD symptoms in men, adolescents, or elderly patients remains unexplored.

As this was a naturalistic study, patients also took medications, and some medications exhibit anti-Toxoplasma properties.23,24 While the seropositive and seronegative groups did not differ significantly in the frequency of use of medications with anti-Toxoplasma effects, the impact of psychotropic drugs on antibody levels against toxoplasmosis has been investigated for only a limited number of medications. It remains unclear whether other drugs have similar effects.

Future Research and Clinical Practice

Epidemiological studies are particularly needed to determine the current seroprevalence in this patient group. A decline in seroprevalence among the general population has been observed in several countries over the past decades,78,79 and only current measurements can provide realistic estimates of sample sizes for studies investigating various aspects of toxoplasmosis. Research into latent toxoplasmosis in patients with BPD is still in its infancy. Sufficiently powered studies may provide valuable insights into this population, which struggles with functioning, has limited therapeutic options, and shows increased suicidality.26,28 Studies investigating seroprevalence in this population and research on its clinical correlates and prognosis may be beneficial.

Despite being preliminary, the current results provide findings that may be of interest to clinical practitioners. The efforts to identify toxoplasmosis have been reserved mainly for pregnant or immunocompromised individuals. However, its neuropsychiatric effects may warrant attention in the psychiatric practice.103 The prevalence of toxoplasmosis in Europe seems to decline,78–81 and as a consequence, the issue of neurobehavioral effects of the latent toxoplasmosis becomes less common over time. Still, a subset of patients experience exacerbations of their primary symptomatology due to Toxoplasma activity. This has been described mostly among patients with schizophrenia.104 The current research shows that seropositive patients with BPD might struggle with pronounced difficulties in regulating auto- and hetero-aggressive experiences, potentially increasing distress and functional disability related to them. Checking for symptoms of toxoplasmosis (whether acute or latent) may thus be useful in specific cases, especially those who struggle with these symptoms and might have been exposed to the parasite.

Conclusion

This pilot study aimed to explore possible links between BPD severity and seropositivity of anti-Toxoplasma IgG antibodies. Unexpectedly, few patients exhibited significant antibody levels. Possible reasons for the low seropositivity include a general decrease in seroprevalence reported in Europe and notable variability in the decline of IgG antibodies among individuals. Future research may identify specific causes. The seropositive and seronegative groups showed several small- and medium-sized differences, but none were statistically significant, likely due to the small sample size of the seropositive group. Future studies should include larger samples to ensure sufficient numbers of seropositive patients for definitive answers.
