Bilateral scalp necrosis in giant cell arteritis
Authors: Ngoc Nguyen, Colby L Presley
Published: 2026

Introduction

Giant cell arteritis (GCA) is a systemic granulomatous vasculitis most commonly affecting adults older than 50. Although often referred to as temporal arteritis due to frequent involvement of the superficial temporal arteries, other external carotid artery branches may also be affected. Scalp necrosis is a rare and late manifestation of GCA, with fewer than 150 cases reported in the literature.1 It is associated with increased mortality and a higher incidence of irreversible vision loss than GCA without scalp necrosis.2 Its presence typically reflects extensive arterial involvement and a more aggressive disease course with a higher likelihood of irreversible ischemic complications. Therefore, early recognition is crucial to improving the clinical course. This case highlights the importance of recognizing this rare cutaneous finding in order to facilitate prompt diagnosis and prevent devastating patient outcomes.

Case report

An 81-year-old male presented with a 2-week history of darkening, crusting lesions on the left temporal-parietal scalp that started after a minor head trauma and initially appeared as contusions. The patient denied significant pain at the necrotic site but reported tenderness anteriorly. He also noted similar bruising on the right side of the scalp without any known trauma. These lesions were tender to palpation but otherwise asymptomatic, with no reported itching, bleeding, or pain (Figs 1 and 2).

During these 2 weeks, the patient developed progressive peripheral vision loss in the left eye. The patient saw an ophthalmologist who raised concerns for disseminated herpes zoster with possible ocular involvement. On examination, there was no evidence of vesicles or blisters, but severe allodynia on the posterior temporal and superior occipital scalp. The patient denied systemic symptoms, including fever, night sweats, chills, epistaxis, or hemoptysis. However, he did report headaches and jaw claudication. Notably, he experienced headaches and tongue and throat swelling in the months prior, which was misdiagnosed as thrush. Prior evaluations also considered concussion, pharyngitis, and tension headache as possible diagnoses.

On this presentation, GCA was suspected because of the combination of vision changes, elevated inflammatory markers, and continued worsening bilateral scalp necrosis. A temporal artery duplex was suggestive of inflammatory disease, and the patient was started on methylprednisolone while awaiting a confirmatory temporal artery biopsy (TAB). TAB revealed findings consistent with bilateral GCA. The patient was started on pulse dose corticosteroids for 3 days and discharged on 60 mg prednisone daily. At 1-month follow-up, the patient’s scalp lesions improved with corticosteroid therapy; however, the peripheral vision loss persisted.

Discussion

Scalp necrosis in GCA may precede or accompany irreversible vision loss and requires urgent recognition and systemic treatment. It is associated with markedly worse outcomes than in GCA without necrosis.1,2 When scalp necrosis is present, irreversible vision loss is reported in over 60% of cases compared to only 15% to 20% in those without, and mortality rates are more than doubled.1,2 Early lesions may be mistaken for traumatic injury, herpes zoster, cellulitis, or pressure necrosis. Such misdiagnoses can delay the initiation of treatment and increase the risk of ischemic complications and devastating outcomes.

The pathophysiology of GCA is thought to involve an immune-mediated response to endothelial injury. T-cell and macrophage activation releases cytokines, resulting in granulomatous inflammation of the blood vessel walls. This process ultimately leads to vessel wall destruction, luminal narrowing, and eventual ischemia.3 Scalp necrosis seen in GCA results from ischemia and occlusion of the temporal, frontal, retro-auricular, and occipital arteries that supply the frontal scalp. Necrotic lesions are found in the area of distribution of these arteries.

Classically, the scalp and temples are tender, and hyperesthesia may be present in the parietal and temporal regions of the scalp.1,3 The lesions present as crusted superficial ulcers or deep craters with full-thickness skin loss covered by adherent necrotic tissue. Associated signs include edema, urticaria, erythema, vesicles, bullae, and alopecia.3

Skin biopsies of the necrotic lesions are often unrevealing, and TAB is the gold standard for diagnosis.4 Characteristically, TAB will show disruption of the internal elastic lamina, granulomatous inflammation, intimal hyperplasia, and multinucleated giant cells.1,2 While TAB remains the diagnostic gold standard, imaging modalities such as temporal artery ultrasound, high-resolution magnetic resonance imaging, or positron emission tomography-computed tomography can support rapid diagnosis when biopsy is delayed or negative but clinical suspicion remains high.5

The mainstay of therapy is high-dose corticosteroids. Adjunctive therapies such as methotrexate can be used as a steroid-sparing agent, and tocilizumab, an IL-6 inhibitor, has demonstrated efficacy in severe and refractory cases.3,6 Scalp lesions typically resolve with adequate corticosteroid treatment, but more extensive necrosis may require surgical debridement or skin grafting.3

Conclusion

Scalp necrosis is a rare but severe cutaneous manifestation of giant cell arteritis. It should prompt urgent evaluation for underlying vasculitis, especially in older patients with concurrent vision changes, jaw claudication, and/or elevated inflammatory markers. Scalp necrosis is an indicator of extensive disease, and vision loss is often irreversible. The development of necrosis and other ischemic complications may be attributed to delayed diagnosis and initiation of effective treatment, leading to extensive or multivessel occlusion of arteries. Timely recognition of this uncommon dermatologic finding can expedite diagnosis and treatment and reduce morbidity, mortality, and the risk of irreversible complications.
