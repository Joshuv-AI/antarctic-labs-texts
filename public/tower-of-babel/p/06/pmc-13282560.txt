SARS‐CoV‐2 Infection, Vaccination Status, and Dementia Risk: A Nested Case–Control Study
Authors: Nika Zur, Nili Stein, Walid Saliba, Galit Weinstein
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesSevere acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus‐2 (SARS‐CoV‐2) may affect cognition, but its association with incident dementia remains inconsistent. We explored the association of SARS‐CoV‐2 infection and its vaccination with dementia risk in a nationwide sample of middle‐aged and older adults.MethodsThis nested case–control study used electronic healthcare data from Israel's largest health provider. Participants were dementia‐free individuals aged ≥ 50 years at baseline (March 2020), followed up until May 2022. Incident dementia cases were matched to dementia‐free controls using density sampling by age, sex, and date of entry to the study, with a ratio of 1:10. SARS‐CoV‐2 was defined by positive polymerase chain reaction (PCR) or institutional antigen tests. Multivariable conditional logistic regression models evaluated the association of SARS‐CoV‐2, its severity and vaccination, and pneumonia as a comparator, with dementia risk.ResultsAmong the 1,145,322 eligible participants, 27,280 dementia cases were matched to 272,800 controls. SARS‐CoV‐2 infection was associated with increased dementia risk (OR = 1.18; 95% CI 1.12–1.24; p < 0.001). This association was confined to hospitalized individuals with mild (OR = 2.39; 95% CI 2.07–2.76) and moderate‐to‐severe disease (OR = 1.93; 95% CI 1.70–2.20), was comparable to pneumonia (OR = 1.89; 95% CI 1.80–1.99), and was no longer evident after 6 months (OR = 1.04; 95% CI 0.96–1.12). COVID‐19 vaccination was associated with 7%, 15%, and 31% lower dementia risk after two, three, and four doses, respectively. Unvaccinated individuals with prior COVID‐19 had the highest dementia risk.ConclusionsDementia diagnoses are increased after COVID‐19, especially in hospitalized patients. Risk is comparable to other respiratory infections.

Introduction

Viruses have long been implicated in the development of neurodegenerative diseases, with research linking infections such as herpes simplex virus and influenza to increased dementia risk [1, 2]. These viruses can trigger systemic inflammation, cross the blood–brain barrier, and initiate processes, such as amyloid‐beta accumulation and neuroinflammation that are central to dementia pathogenesis [3] Additionally, common bacterial infections such as pneumonia have been documented as possible risk factors for dementia [4, 5]. The COVID‐19 pandemic has brought renewed attention to the potential impact of viral infections on brain health. The COVID‐19 pandemic, caused by Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus 2 (SARS‐CoV‐2), was declared in December 2019, causing more than 7 million deaths and infecting more than 777 million individuals worldwide as of February 2025 [6]. These figures likely underestimate the true global burden. Studies suggest that most of the world's population has been infected at least once and that the actual morbidity and mortality burden is considerably higher [7]. Current estimates also indicate that the incidence of COVID‐19 remains high [8].

The long‐term neurological sequelae of SARS‐CoV‐2 infection have been evident since the early stages of the pandemic [9, 10]. Emerging evidence suggests an elevated risk of dementia following COVID‐19, even compared to other respiratory infections [11, 12, 13]. However, existing studies show considerable heterogeneity, with follow‐up periods ranging from 3 to 24 months. Many also lack comprehensive information on vaccination status and prior COVID‐19 infections, both factors that may influence disease severity and, consequently, dementia risk. Additionally, few studies have accounted for COVID‐19 severity or hospitalization, further limiting comparability across findings [13].

Hence, we examined the association between SARS‐CoV‐2 infection and the incidence of new‐onset dementia in a large, national retrospective cohort over a 27‐month follow‐up period. We assessed whether first‐time SARS‐CoV‐2 infection, disease severity, and vaccination status were associated with dementia risk. To explore potential detection bias, such as elevated diagnoses shortly after infection that may reflect delirium or increased clinical scrutiny rather than true incident dementia, we stratified the follow‐up period into shorter intervals. While the main analyses compared individuals with COVID‐19 to those without a COVID‐19 history, a complementary analysis also compared COVID‐19 with pneumonia, including an assessment of pneumonia severity.

Materials and Methods

Database

This retrospective cohort study was based on electronic health records from the Clalit Health Services (CHS). CHS is the largest Health Maintenance Organization (HMO) in Israel, providing comprehensive healthcare for more than ~4.9 million individuals, more than 50% of the country's population. Notably, the National Health Insurance Law in Israel mandates that all residents in the country join one of the four official HMOs and prohibits discrimination based on any background or circumstances in the provision of healthcare services. This structure ensures equitable access to care and minimizes selection bias in research utilizing CHS data. Moreover, loss to follow‐up due to transfer to another HMO is negligible. The CHS maintains a database that receives information from multiple sources, including records of primary care physicians, community specialty clinics, hospitalizations, laboratories, and pharmacies.

Study Population

A flowchart illustrating the selection procedure is shown in Figure 1. A cohort of 1,232,709 adults aged ≥ 50 years was identified from the CHS database, as of March 1, 2020 (cohort entry date). We excluded individuals with prevalent dementia at cohort entry (N = 85,612), 23 individuals with data errors, and four with missing age information. The remaining 1,147,070 dementia‐free individuals constituted the underlying cohort for this nested case–control study and were followed up until a diagnosis of dementia, death, or the end of follow‐up on May 31, 2022, whichever came first. All identified patients with new onset dementia during the study period were included as cases in our study, and the date of diagnosis was defined as the index date. We used density‐based risk set sampling to select controls from individuals who did not develop dementia and were alive on each index date. For each case of dementia, we randomly selected 10 controls matched by age (± 1 year), sex, and index date. To maintain proper risk set representation, controls were allowed to be matched to more than one case.

Flowchart illustrating the selection of the study population.

Outcome Ascertainment

All‐cause dementia was diagnosed if at least one of the following criteria was met: 1. An active diagnosis recorded using International Classification of Diseases, Ninth or Tenth Revision (ICD‐9/10) codes; 2. The purchase of at least one anti‐dementia medication (ATC code N06DX). This algorithm was selected to enhance the validity of dementia identification beyond ICD codes alone and is used in the internal CHS dementia registry [14].

Exposure Ascertainment

Diagnosis of SARS‐CoV‐2 was confirmed using either a positive polymerase chain reaction (PCR) test (the gold standard) or a positive institutional antigen test (IAg), both of which are reliable and valid diagnostic tools [15]. During May 2022, rapid antigen tests for home testing became available as complementary diagnostic tools to PCR testing [15]. However, this shift led to underdiagnosis and incomplete documentation of COVID‐19 cases. Hence, we restricted the study period to March 1, 2020 through May 31, 2022 (27 months).

We obtained data on the severity of SARS‐CoV‐2 infection from the hospital discharge letters. Participants were grouped into four categories: (1) no history of COVID‐19, (2) mild disease without hospitalization, (3) mild disease with hospitalization, and (4) moderate‐to‐severe disease requiring hospitalization. A sensitivity analysis was conducted in which we considered several follow‐up periods after SARS‐CoV‐2 infection, namely, 0–3 months, 3–6 months, and > 6 months. As a complementary analysis, we examined the association between pneumonia and dementia risk to assess whether the association is specific to COVID‐19 or comparable to other respiratory infections. The sample was grouped as follows: (1) no history of pneumonia, (2) pneumonia without hospitalization, and (3) pneumonia with hospitalization.

Information on COVID‐19 vaccination status and number of doses was obtained during the follow‐up period. Vaccinations mainly included the Pfizer‐BioNTech mRNA vaccine (BNT162b2). From July 2021, the Moderna mRNA vaccine (mRNA‐1273) was also administered, along with a small number of Novavax doses. Vaccination status was defined as 1 if a person had received at least one dose and 0 if no vaccines had been administered prior to dementia diagnosis. Vaccination status for controls was determined at the case's index date. Vaccination could occur either before or after COVID‐19 infection; overall, the maximum number of vaccinations was four. To further examine the relationship between vaccination and dementia, we also captured the exact number of doses received and the time since the most recent dose, which were later used in dose–response and timing analyses.

Study Covariates

All covariates were classified as present if they were documented at the beginning of the follow‐up. Information on age, sex, and ethnicity (Jews and Arabs) was extracted from the health records. Neighborhood socioeconomic status (SES) was established by the Central Bureau of Statistics and categorized as low, medium, and high. We also extracted information on current smoking status, obesity, and the prevalence of diabetes, hypertension, stroke, chronic lung diseases (i.e., chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, asthma, and chronic bronchitis), and heart disease (i.e., chronic heart failure, cardiomyopathy, electrolytic disorders, and ischemic heart disease). In addition, the annual number of visits to the primary physician between March 1, 2019 (entry date) and March 1, 2020 was recorded. All diagnoses were obtained according to the International Classification of Diseases, 9th revision (ICD‐9) and are listed in Table S1.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were summarized with means ± standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges. Categorical variables were presented as numbers and proportions. All model assumptions were assessed visually and through appropriate tests. We used conditional logistic regression to estimate the odds ratio (OR) for the association between history and severity of SARS‐CoV‐2 infection and incident dementia. In a sensitivity analysis, we ran similar models restricting the follow‐up periods to 0–3, 3–6, and > 6 months. As a complementary analysis, we examined the association of pneumonia and its severity with dementia risk using identical models. Conditional logistic regression models were also used to assess the relationship between COVID‐19 vaccination status and dementia risk. To address potential temporal effects of vaccination, we conducted two additional analyses: First, we evaluated a dose–response relationship by modeling vaccination as a time‐varying exposure and estimating associations separately for individuals who had received 1, 2, 3, or 4 doses; second, we examined whether the time since the most recent vaccination modified the association by categorizing recency of vaccination into 0–3 months, 3–6 months, and > 6 months, modeled as time‐updated covariates.

Additionally, we tested whether an interaction existed between SARS‐CoV‐2 history and vaccination status by including an interaction term in the conditional logistic model. In case a significant interaction was found, a subgroup analysis was done using the following exposure categories: (1) COVID‐19 history, vaccinated; (2) COVID‐19 history, unvaccinated; (3) no COVID‐19 history, vaccinated; and (4) no COVID‐10 history, unvaccinated. All models were adjusted for SES, ethnicity, history of stroke, diabetes, hypertension, chronic lung disease, smoking status, cardiovascular disease, obesity, healthcare utilization, and COVID‐19 vaccination status (except when entered as exposure).

As a sensitivity analysis, we also performed Cox proportional hazards models to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) for incident dementia following SARS‐CoV‐2 infection, adjusted for age, to confirm that our findings were consistent when applying a full cohort time‐to‐event framework.

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 28, IBM Corp.) and SAS (version 9.4) software. For all analyses, a two‐sided type I error (p < 0.05) was considered significant.

Generative AI tools were used to assist with language refinement.

This study adheres to the STROBE reporting guidelines, and the completed STROBE checklist is provided in Table S2.

Results

Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of individuals with and without incident dementia. The mean age of the persons with dementia and their matched controls was 78.2 ± 9.0 and 77.5 ± 9.0 years, respectively. Among both cases and controls, 57.9% were women. Of those with incident dementia and controls, 2072 (7.6%) and 17,169 (6.4%), respectively, had a history of COVID‐19. Hospitalization due to COVID‐19 was documented in 532 (2.0%) cases and 2297 (0.8%) controls. Notably, 57.0% of dementia cases (n = 15,561) had been vaccinated against COVID‐19, compared with 57.8% (n = 157,732) of controls. Pneumonia was diagnosed in 3396 (12.4%) cases and 21,064 (7.7%) controls. Among those with pneumonia, 2227 (66%) cases and 11,206 (53%) controls required hospitalization. Additionally, 2159 of the 27,280 dementia cases (7.9%) had previously been selected as controls. Overall, SARS‐CoV‐2 infection was associated with an 18% increased risk of dementia after adjusting for potential confounders (OR = 1.18; 95% CI 1.12–1.24; p < 0.001) (Table 2). However, the association was significant only among COVID‐19 cases who required hospitalization (OR = 2.39; 95% CI 2.07–2.76; p < 0.001; OR = 1.93; 95% CI 1.70–2.20; p < 0.001 for mild and moderate‐to‐severe disease, respectively). No increased dementia risk was observed among individuals with mild, nonhospitalized COVID‐19 compared with those without a history of SARS‐CoV‐2 infection (OR = 1.02; 95% CI 0.97–1.08; p = 0.436) (Table 2).

In the Cox proportional hazards model, adjusted for age, individuals with a history of SARS‐CoV‐2 infection demonstrated an increased risk of developing dementia compared with uninfected controls (HR = 1.21; 95% CI 1.15–1.26; p < 0.001). A sensitivity analysis showed a 32% and 15% increased risk of new‐onset dementia during the first 3 months of follow‐up and between 3 and 6 months following a COVID‐19 diagnosis (OR = 1.32; 95% CI 1.23–1.42; p < 0.001; OR = 1.15; 95% CI 1.04–1.28; p = 0.009, respectively). However, no significant association was observed for dementia cases occurring more than 6 months after SARS‐CoV‐2 infection (OR = 1.05; 95% CI 0.97–1.14; p = 0.193) (Figure 2).

Dementia incidence stratified by observation period according to COVID‐19 History.

In our complementary analysis, a pneumonia diagnosis was associated with more than a 50% increased risk for new‐onset dementia (OR = 1.54; 95% CI 1.48–1.61; p < 0.001). Compared with individuals with no history of pneumonia, a history of pneumonia without hospitalization and with hospitalization was associated with a 16% and 89% increased risk of dementia, respectively (OR = 1.16; 95% CI 1.09–1.23; p < 0.001; OR = 1.89; 95% CI 1.80–1.99; p < 0.001) (Table 2).

Overall, an 11% lower risk of new‐onset dementia was observed among individuals who were vaccinated against COVID‐19 compared with those who were not (OR = 0.89; 95% CI 0.85–0.94; p < 0.001). While one vaccine dose was not significantly associated with dementia risk (OR = 0.96; 95% CI 0.89–1.04; p = 0.334), two, three, and four doses were associated with a 7%, 15%, and 31% lower risk, respectively (OR = 0.93; 95% CI 0.88–0.99; p = 0.015; OR = 0.85; 95% CI 0.80–0.90; p < 0.001; OR = 0.69; 95% CI 0.64–0.75; p < 0.001) (Table 2).

When assessing risk according to time since the last vaccination, a 14% and 11% reduced risk of new‐onset dementia was observed during the first 3 months and between 3 and 6 months after the most recent dose, respectively (OR = 0.86; 95% CI 0.81–0.90; p = < 0.001; OR = 0.89; 95% CI 0.84–0.95; p = < 0.001). No significant association was observed for dementia cases occurring more than 6 months after the last vaccination (OR = 1.03, 95% CI 0.96–1.10; p = 0.398).

Finally, a significant interaction between COVID‐19 diagnosis and vaccination status was observed (p < 0.001). Compared with individuals who were neither vaccinated nor previously infected, those who had COVID‐19 and were unvaccinated exhibited the highest risk of new‐onset dementia (OR = 1.42; 95% CI 1.31–1.58; p < 0.001). In contrast, vaccinated individuals without a history of COVID‐19 had a modestly lower dementia risk (OR = 0.93; 95% CI 0.89–0.98; p = 0.011). Among participants with prior COVID‐19 infection who were vaccinated, dementia risk did not differ significantly (OR = 1.02; 95% CI 0.95–1.10; p = 0.567) (Figure 3).

Conclusion

Findings from this nationwide nested case–control study suggest that COVID‐19 may be associated with an increased risk of dementia among older adults. However, this association was time‐limited, restricted to hospitalized individuals, and comparable in magnitude to that observed following pneumonia, suggesting that the elevated dementia risk may reflect consequences of acute illness and hospitalization rather than a long‐term, SARS‐CoV‐2–specific neurodegenerative process. In addition, COVID‐19 vaccination was associated with a reduced risk of new‐onset dementia, demonstrating a clear dose–response and time‐limited protective effect, indicating that vaccination may mitigate short‐term cognitive vulnerability following acute infection.

Our estimated 18% increase in dementia risk was lower than pooled effect sizes reported in previous meta‐analyses (49%–66%) [11, 12, 13], likely reflecting differences in follow‐up duration, case definitions, and circulating SARS‐CoV‐2 variants across studies [11]. Importantly, results from the sensitivity analyses using a cohort study design were highly consistent with findings from the primary analyses, reinforcing the robustness of the association between prior SARS‐CoV‐2 infection and dementia and reducing the likelihood that the findings are driven by model‐specific assumptions.

The short‐term elevation in dementia diagnoses, maximal within the first 3 months and no longer evident beyond 6 months, may reflect increased diagnostic activity following recent infection, particularly among individuals with preexisting cognitive vulnerability. Infection‐related healthcare encounters, hospitalization, or episodes of delirium may accelerate recognition of cognitive impairment, leading to a dementia diagnosis shortly after COVID‐19. Reverse causality is also plausible, whereby early cognitive decline increases susceptibility to infection or severe illness, resulting in a diagnosis in close temporal proximity to COVID‐19 [11, 13]. The attenuation of the association at longer intervals suggests that the observed pattern is driven primarily by recent exposure around the time of diagnosis, rather than indicating a sustained increase in dementia occurrence over time. This interpretation is consistent with findings from Taquet et al. [16], whose variant‐specific analyses showed reduced risk beyond 6 months for the Alpha and Beta variants, despite overall longer term associations. Israel's high vaccination coverage, approximately 60% of participants, may have further influenced disease severity and diagnosis patterns, potentially contributing to fewer new dementia diagnoses [11, 17]. Supporting this interpretation, another cohort study similarly found no association between COVID‐19 and incident dementia after 1 year [18].

The association between COVID‐19 and dementia was confined to individuals who were hospitalized, including those classified as having mild disease. Notably, the highest estimates were observed among patients with mild illness, whereas those with severe disease showed somewhat lower estimates. This nonlinear pattern may reflect a higher competing risk of mortality among individuals with severe COVID‐19, which can reduce the likelihood of receiving a subsequent dementia diagnosis. Previous studies have reported heterogeneous findings, with some observing increased dementia risk primarily among hospitalized individuals [11], while others reported elevated risk even in nonhospitalized cases [12, 19]. The pattern observed here likely reflects hospitalization‐related detection mechanism, as hospital stays increase clinical surveillance, facilitate cognitive assessment, and may lead to misclassification of acute conditions such as delirium as dementia. Additionally, hospitalization practices early in the pandemic did not consistently reflect disease severity, as many individuals with relatively mild illness were admitted for isolation or monitoring [20]. In Israel, discharge policies initially required two consecutive negative PCR tests, prolonging hospital stays even among clinically stable patients [21]. These factors likely increased opportunities for cognitive evaluation and diagnosis around the time of infection. Because hospitalization itself is a well‐established risk factor for subsequent cognitive decline [22], particularly in the context of acute infection [23], the elevated dementia diagnoses observed among hospitalized COVID‐19 cases likely reflect a combination of increased clinical attention and underlying vulnerability, rather than a direct effect of disease severity alone.

Similar to COVID‐19, pneumonia was also associated with an increased likelihood of dementia, consistent with prior evidence linking bacterial pneumonia to new‐onset cognitive impairment [5]. While previous studies comparing COVID‐19 with other respiratory infections have often reported a higher dementia risk following COVID‐19 over extended follow‐up periods [13, 16], our analyses did not include a direct head‐to‐head comparison. Instead, stratification by disease severity provides additional context. We observed comparable or even higher likelihood of dementia following COVID‐19 but only among individuals who were hospitalized. In contrast, mild COVID‐19 cases not requiring hospitalization were not associated with increased dementia diagnoses, whereas mild pneumonia cases were. Taken together, these findings suggest that the observed increase in dementia diagnoses is not unique to SARS‐CoV‐2 but may also occur following other infectious illnesses, albeit to varying degrees [2, 24].

COVID‐19 vaccination during the follow‐up demonstrated a clear dose–response association with dementia risk, with two, three, and four doses associated with progressively lower odds of dementia diagnosis. This pattern is consistent with prior evidence suggesting that routine vaccinations [25], including those for herpes zoster [26, 27], pneumonia [28], and influenza [29, 30], may be associated with reduced dementia incidence. At the same time, some preliminary reports have raised concerns regarding a possible increase in Alzheimer's disease diagnoses following COVID‐19 vaccination, although these findings remain inconclusive [31, 32]. Several mechanisms may contribute to the observed association. Vaccination reduces the likelihood and severity of SARS‐CoV‐2 infection, which may in turn limit downstream inflammatory and vascular effects implicated in cognitive decline [33]. In addition, vaccinated individuals, particularly those receiving multiple doses, may differ in health behaviors, healthcare engagement, and underlying health status, factors that could also influence dementia diagnosis patterns. The stronger association observed within the first 3–6 months after vaccination, with no apparent effect beyond 6 months, suggests that the relationship is time‐dependent and may reflect short‐term reductions in vulnerability around the time of infection or diagnosis. This temporal pattern is broadly consistent with waning vaccine effectiveness over time [34]. The interaction between infection and vaccination further supports this interpretation. Individuals with prior COVID‐19 who were unvaccinated had the highest odds of dementia, whereas vaccinated individuals without prior infection had modestly lower odds. Together, these findings suggest that vaccination status and recent infection jointly shape the likelihood of dementia diagnosis, particularly in the period surrounding acute illness.

If the observed associations between COVID‐19 and dementia reflect causal processes rather than factors related to hospitalization or diagnosis, several biological mechanisms may be involved. These include potential viral effects on the central nervous system (CNS), endothelial injury, exacerbation of neuroinflammation, and processes that promote the aggregation and toxicity of amyloid proteins in the brain [10, 31, 32, 35, 36, 37], However, similar mechanisms have been described for other infectious agents. Emerging evidence suggests that pathogens such as Chlamydia pneumoniae [38] may contribute to the development of dementia through CNS invasion, disruption of the neural microenvironment, and activation of inflammatory cascades. In addition, respiratory infections more broadly can impair lung function and lead to hypoxia, which may further promote neurodegenerative processes [39, 40]. In this context, our findings indicate that elevated dementia diagnoses are not unique to SARS‐CoV‐2 infection. Rather, they may reflect broader biological and clinical responses to acute infection. Further investigation is needed to determine whether the underlying mechanisms are specific to SARS‐CoV‐2 or represent pathways shared across a range of infectious diseases.

The strengths of this study include a large sample size that is broadly representative of the Israeli population and captures individuals with a wide range of clinical presentations. The use of electronic medical records reduces the risk of recall and selection bias. In addition, the availability of detailed information on disease severity and vaccination status enabled more nuanced analyses. Several limitations should be considered. First, COVID‐19 cases, particularly asymptomatic infections, may have been underdiagnosed. This misclassification is likely to be nondifferential and would therefore tend to attenuate observed associations. To mitigate this concern, we restricted the study period to times when testing was conducted primarily through institutional settings rather than home‐based testing. Second, we lacked data on SARS‐CoV‐2 variants and dementia subtypes. Third, the findings are limited to the variants circulating during the early phases of the pandemic. Fourth, although the follow‐up period extended up to 27 months, longer observation is needed to assess potential long‐term associations, given the prolonged prodromal phase of dementia. Fifth, due to the nested case–control design, death could not be explicitly accounted for as a competing risk. Mortality may preclude the clinical diagnosis of dementia, and some degree of competing risk bias cannot be ruled out. Relatedly, analyses stratified by follow‐up intervals should be interpreted as associations within defined observation windows rather than as estimates of risk over time since infection. Finally, despite adjustment for multiple confounders, residual confounding by unmeasured or incompletely captured factors remains possible.

In conclusion, we found that SARS‐CoV‐2 infection is significantly associated with an increased likelihood of subsequent dementia diagnosis in a representative sample of middle‐aged and older adults. However, the temporal pattern, limited to the months immediately following infection and concentrated among individuals who were hospitalized, suggests that this association is more consistent with the effects of acute illness, healthcare utilization, and diagnostic processes rather than with a SARS‐CoV‐2–specific neurodegenerative mechanism. Importantly, COVID‐19 vaccination was associated with a dose‐dependent and time‐limited reduction in dementia diagnoses, supporting a potential role in mitigating short‐term cognitive vulnerability following acute infection. As COVID‐19 remains an active disease and the global burden of dementia continues to rise, future research with extended follow‐up, detailed characterization of infection severity and viral variants, and careful assessment of dementia subtypes and cognitive trajectories will be essential to better understand the longer term relationship between these conditions.
