Performance of recombinase polymerase amplification assay for cutaneous leishmaniasis detection: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Behailu Taye, Myrthe Pareyn, Eyob Getu, Nicole Berens-Riha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSensitive and robust point-of-care (PoC) assays are urgently needed for the diagnosis of cutaneous leishmaniasis (CL). Recombinase polymerase amplification (RPA), an isothermal molecular assay, has shown promise for CL diagnosis using either a lateral-flow assay (LFA) or a mobile-suitcase-laboratory (MSL) platform. However, its diagnostic performance has been inconsistent across studies. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to determine the pooled sensitivity and specificity of RPA for CL diagnosis.MethodsA comprehensive search was conducted in Google Scholar, EMBASE, Web of Science, PubMed, and Scopus to identify studies that evaluated the diagnostic accuracy of RPA in suspected CL cases. The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed using the Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies-2 (QUADAS-2) tool. Using the GRADE approach, certainty of evidence was evaluated. A bivariate random-effects model was applied to perform the meta-analysis using R and Stata 14.2.ResultsNine articles comprising 14 datasets were included, with sample sizes ranging from 18 to 226 participants. Sensitivity in individual studies ranged from 55% to 100%, while specificity varied from 36% to 100%. The pooled sensitivity and specificity were 86% (95% CI: 72–93%, p < 0.001) and 93% (95% CI: 65–99%, p = 0.01), respectively, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.88 against microscopy and PCR. Subgroup analysis revealed that RPA-LFA had higher pooled sensitivity (93%, 95% CI: 78–98%) compared to MSL (69%, 95% CI: 51–83%). Blinding was unreported in 44% of the included studies, resulting in an unclear risk of bias that may skew the diagnostic accuracy results. The certainty of evidence for RPA sensitivity was rated as moderate.ConclusionsRPA demonstrated high specificity and good sensitivity for CL diagnosis but fell short of the 95% sensitivity threshold outlined in the WHO target product profile. In contrast to MSL, LFA appears to be a promising PoC method for diagnosing CL. Data on RPA-MSL remains limited, and its applicability to other Leishmania species causing CL is unclear. Background

Cutaneous leishmaniasis (CL), manifesting in disfiguring skin lesions, is an infection caused by Leishmania parasites through the bite of female sandflies [1]. More than 20 different Leishmania species can cause human CL; the major species are Leishmania (L.) tropica, L. major, L. aethiopica, and L. infantum in the Old World (Asia, South Europe, the Middle East, and Africa), while L. Viannia braziliensis, L. (V.) panamensis, L. (V.) guyanensis, L. mexicana, and L. amazonensis are prevalent in the New World (South and Central America) [2]. In Sri Lanka, CL is solely caused by a certain L. donovani strain, whereas other L. donovani strains cause mainly visceral leishmaniasis (VL) in the rest of the Old World [3]. Every year, an estimated 0.7 to 1.2 million CL cases occur globally with around 350 million people living at risk of the disease [4].

Clinically, CL has a spectrum of manifestations such as localized CL (LCL), including self-healing nodular or ulcerative lesions at location of the bite; mucosal CL (MCL), with destructive nasal, mouth, and throat mucosa; anergic diffuse CL (ADCL), presenting as multiple non-ulcerative nodules; and disseminated leishmaniasis (DL), manifesting in multiple papules in two or more non-contiguous areas [5]. The severity is determined by the parasite species and immunity of the host [6]. Although not fatal, CL can cause severe skin disfigurement and permanent scars, potentially resulting in poor quality of life, societal stigma, and psychiatric problems [7].

Diagnosis of CL in disease endemic countries is performed by microscopic examination of Giemsa-stained skin-slit, needle aspirate, or scraping samples. However, these diagnostic techniques have poor and variable sensitivity [8, 9] that lies between 15% and 83%, depending on different parameters: the reference test used [10–12], lesion duration [12], parasite load [13], examiner’s expertise [14], and the prior use of traditional treatments [15]. Besides, invasive sampling causes pain, bleeding, and scars [16].

Immunological tests targeting antibodies are infrequently employed to diagnose CL due to the incapability to differentiate active from prior infection on the one hand and the low sensitivity due to inadequate humoral immunity induced by CL patients on the other hand [10]. Similarly, the Leishmanin skin tests (LST) or Montenegro tests (MST): which are based on the anti-Leishmania delayed-type hypersensitivity reaction, cannot distinguish between present and past infections, although they are highly sensitive [10, 17]. Conversely, antigens are better markers of active infection and several antigenic targets have been evaluated for CL immuno-diagnosis [18]. However, the CL Detect Rapid Test (InBios) that targets the Leishmania peroxidoxin antigen and is in the pipeline as a CL point-of-care (PoC) test [19] showed insufficient sensitivity compared to polymerase chain reaction (PCR), especially in chronic lesions older than four months [20].

In contrast to both microscopy and immunoassays, molecular tests tend to have superior diagnostic accuracy and applicability on minimally invasive samples [17, 21, 22] as well as better results for chronic lesions [23]. Conventional or real-time quantitative (q) PCR are the most widely used methods. Furthermore, Leishmania-specific nested PCR (Ln PCR) with elevated sensitivity and multiplex PCR with various species detection are also utilized [24]. However, PCR methods are not available for routine diagnosis of CL in resource-constrained endemic settings where most patients live. The primary reason for this is the high cost of reagents and the necessity for cold chain storage. Furthermore, the handling of these materials requires the use of sophisticated laboratory facilities and expertise [25].

Instead, loop-mediated isothermal amplification (LAMP) is simple, rapid, and amplifies target genes at a constant temperature using a single enzyme. While several in-house LAMP methods have been developed [26], the Loopamp™ Leishmania detection kit (Eiken Chemicals) is commercially available and does not require cold-chain storage [27]. LAMP demonstrated high sensitivity and specificity [28], yet only partially meets requirements for a PoC test for CL [29].

Another isothermal platform and a promising candidate for a molecular (near-) PoC diagnostic method for CL is recombinase polymerase amplification (RPA). It amplifies target sequences at a constant temperature (37 to 42 °C) using three main enzymes: recombinase, single-stranded binding (SSB) protein, and strand displacement DNA polymerase. Recombinase binds to primers via the loading factor assistant to form a nucleoprotein filament. This complex locates and invades the homologous sequence in the double-stranded DNA, creating a D-loop structure. Inside the D-loop, the primer hybridizes to its complementary template strand, while the non-template strand is stabilized by single-stranded binding proteins to prevent re-annealing. Incorporation of both forward and reverse primers allows DNA polymerase to synthesize a strand in both directions simultaneously [30].

The RPA for Leishmania detection can be based on a lateral-flow assay (LFA) [31] and a mobile suitcase laboratory (MSL) platform [32]. The LFA amplifies kinetoplast DNA (kDNA) of CL-causing L. Viannia species using a simple heating device, and the result is read visually on an immunochromatographic strip [31]. Consequently, LFA could allow the diagnosis and care of CL patients to be decentralized. The MSL is primarily designed to amplify L. donovani kDNA, but it can also detect L. aethiopica, L. infantum, and L. major. It has distinct workspaces for the extraction of the SpeedXtract kit and the detection of nucleic acids using a real-time fluorescence reader (tube scanner). A solar battery powers MSL, and all reagents are independent of the cold chain, which is easy to implement at primary healthcare facilities and field settings [32].

Globally, several CL diagnostic accuracy studies have evaluated the RPA assay using an MSL or LFA platform. However, the diagnostic performance was controversial. Moreover, there has never been a systematic review or meta-analysis of the diagnostic accuracy of the RPA assay conducted [17]. The purpose of this systematic review and meta-analysis was to evaluate the pooled sensitivity and specificity of the RPA assay for CL diagnosis. The RPA test has the potential to improve CL diagnosis if it performs well.

Methods

Literature review protocol preparation

The diagnostic performance of Recombinase Polymerase Amplification assay for CL was registered on the international prospective register of systematic reviews (PROSPERO) (CRD42023451076, up dated on June 2026). In addition, this study was carried out following the guidelines of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) [33].

Information sources and search strategy

Data were gathered from PubMed, Scopus, EMBASE, Web of Science, and Google Scholar by searches using the key terms ((“Recombinase polymerase amplification assay” OR “isothermal recombinase polymerase amplification test” OR “recombinant polymerase amplification test-lateral flow” OR “rapid isothermal amplification assay” OR “RPA assay” OR “mobile suitcase laboratory” OR “RPA-LF” OR “isothermal recombinase DNA amplification” OR “recombinase polymerase amplification” OR “RPA” OR “recombinase aided amplification” OR “RAA”) AND (“Cutaneous leishmaniasis” OR “cutaneous leishmaniasis” OR “Leishmaniasis, American” OR “Leishmaniasis, New World” OR “Leishmaniasis, Old World” OR “Oriental Sore” OR “American tegumentary leishmaniasis” OR “diffuse cutaneous leishmaniasis” OR “mucosal leishmaniasis” OR “skin leishmaniasis” OR “Leishmaniasis, Cutaneous” OR “dermal leishmaniasis”)). Additionally, reference lists of eligible articles and relevant reviews were screened to identify any further studies. No publication date restrictions were applied. The last search was executed on 18 May 2026. A full description of the search strategy is presented in Table S1.

Eligibility Criteria

We included diagnostic accuracy studies that evaluated the RPA test for human CL diagnosis, directly, compared its performance with a reference standard, and reported sufficient data to construct a 2 × 2 contingency table. We excluded duplicate publications, case reports, review articles, and animal studies. See Table S2 for full inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Study selection

The retrieved articles were imported to EndNote X8 and duplicate articles were removed. Then articles were screened by their titles, abstracts, and full text according to the eligibility criteria by two reviewers (BT and EG) independently. Since there were no disagreements, no conflicts needed to be resolved by a third reviewer or by consensus.

Data extraction

Data extraction was performed by reviewers (BT and NBR) independently. Using a standard table in Excel, variables extracted were the first author’s name, year of publication, country, geographic region, study design, index test sampling method and molecular target, study population, sample size, reference test, TP, FP, FN, and TN of RPA assay. In addition, for the same study, more than one set of data were extracted depending on the reference tests used, the evaluation site where the RPA was analyzed, or the molecular target tested with the RPA.

Quality assessment

Two reviewers (BT and EG) independently assessed the methodological quality of the included studies using the Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies 2 (QUADAS-2) tool. This tool was designed to assess the quality of diagnostic accuracy studies across four domains: patient selection, index test, reference standard, and flow and timing. Every domain was evaluated for bias risk and, for the first three domains, concerns regarding applicability [34]. Evaluation results were displayed in graphs using Review Manager 5.4 software. Using the GRADE approach, certainty of evidence for each outcome was evaluated across five domains: risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias by two reviewers (BT and EG) [35]. Any discrepancies in the certainty ratings were resolved through discussion and consensus.

Statistical analysis

Data were extracted in Excel, then exported to Stata version 14.2 for analysis. A bivariate random-effects model was employed for meta-analysis using the Metadta package in Stata version 14.2 and the Mada package in R software. Results were presented in tabular format, forest plots, and/or summary receiver operating characteristic (SROC) plots. The degree of heterogeneity was quantified using I-Square (I2) statistics by Zhou & Dendukuri [36] in Stata and R software. I2 values above 25%, 50%, and 75% were assumed to be low, medium, and high heterogeneity, respectively. Spearman correlation coefficient was tested to assess whether the threshold effect was a cause of heterogeneity. A sub-group analysis by platform was performed. Deeks’ funnel plot and Egger’s statistics were done to detect publication bias. A p-value of ≤ 0.05 in Egger’s test was considered evidence of statistically significant publication bias [37].

Results

Literature search

A total of 114 studies were retrieved from the databases. After removing duplicates, 69 articles were screened by title and abstract, and 16 by full-text reading. 53 articles were removed at the title/abstract screening stage because the diagnostic tests deviated from RPA and they did not fit into the field of interest (CL). During the full-text screening phase, seven papers were excluded as tests were performed on non-CL leishmaniasis [38–41], animal studies [42], did not include participant results [43], or showed inadequate data for 2 × 2 Table [44]. Lastly, nine articles were included for the meta-analysis (Fig. 1).

Characteristics of included studies

Of the nine studies that were included, two utilized two distinct reference tests, two were conducted across two evaluation sites, and one employed two molecular targets for RPA. This resulted in a total of fourteen observations (datasets) (Table 1). Three studies with 4 datasets used MSL [45–47], while six studies with 10 datasets assessed the LFA platform [31, 48–52]. The sample sizes of the included studies varied from 18 to 226 participants. This review contains 1328 test results from 839 individuals in total. Of the 1328 test results, 1021 and 307 were evaluated using the MSL and LFA platforms, respectively. The kDNA was the target in eight studies, with the exception of one that employed the extra 18 S ribosomal RNA (18 S rRNA) gene for RPA. While PCR was used as a reference test in all studies, additional microscopy was employed in two studies to assess RPA performance. Five of the studies tested RPA accuracy in patients from the Old World (Ghana, Sri-Lanka, Tunisia, Lebanon, and Morocco), whereas the remaining four studies examined its efficacy in patients from the New World (Peru and Colombia). Of note, two studies compared the LFA accuracy in reference centers (reference laboratory scenario) to LFA accuracy in health facilities in rural endemic areas (field scenario). Six studies were classified as cross-sectional, whereas three studies employed a control group.

Methodological qualities of included studies using QUADAS-2

Regarding the applicability of patient selection, index test, and reference standard, the methodological quality of all included studies were judged as low (Fig. 2). Most of the included studies showed low risk of bias for patient selection, and flow and timing. However, for less than half the included studies the risk of bias for the index test was unclear as they lacked information on blinding during read out of the index and gold standard tests. A gold standard to accurately diagnose CL does not exist [53], hence all investigations were considered uncertain for the reference standard domain. Using the GRADE approach, the certainty of evidence was moderate for the LFA-based RPA platform (downgraded for heterogeneity) and very low for the MSL-based platform (downgraded for methodological limitations, heterogeneity, and imprecision). Pooled sensitivity and specificity of RPA for CL diagnosis

The sensitivities of RPA in the individual studies ranged from 55% to 100%, and the specificities ranged from 36% to 100% (Fig. 3). The pooled sensitivity and specificity of RPA for CL diagnosis was 86% (95% CI: 72–93%, p < 0.001) and 93% (95% CI: 65–99%, p = 0.01) respectively. There was more heterogeneity in sensitivity (I2 = 68%) than in specificity (I2 = 19%).

Diagnostic accuracy of RPA for CL diagnosis using SROC curve

The summary diagnostic accuracy of the RPA for CL diagnosis was presented by the SROC plot (Fig. 4). The data of the observed studies (arrows) were scattered around the summary point estimate (circle). The AUC value displayed 0.88, implying that the overall diagnostic accuracy was good.

Subgroup analysis

First, the Spearman correlation coefficient was tested to assess whether the threshold effect was a cause of medium heterogeneity in pooled sensitivity. A correlation coefficient of -0.232 was obtained, indicating heterogeneity was not due to the threshold effect.

Considering the two types of RPA platforms employed, a subgroup analysis was performed (Table 2). The LFA-based platform demonstrated higher pooled sensitivity (93%, 95% CI: 78–98%) compared to the MSL-based platform (69%, 95% CI: 51–83%). The value of I2 was 55% for LFA and 57% for the MSL subgroup.

Publication bias

The included studies were assessed for potential publication bias visually by Deeks’ funnel plot and Egger’s statistics. In this systematic review and meta-analysis, the Deeks’ funnel plots of the included studies were almost symmetric and the Egger weighted regression statistics had a nonsignificant value (p-value = 0.48), indicating that there was no potential publication bias (Fig. 5).

Discussion

For quick and decentralized CL diagnosis and control, especially in places with limited resources, there is a pressing need for sensitive and user-friendly PoC assays [29]. Molecular PoC diagnostics based on isothermal chemistry, such as RPA and LAMP, have been developed to diagnose CL in order to meet these requirements.

The RPA test for CL diagnosis demonstrated good pooled sensitivity (86%) in this meta-analysis. However, this falls short of the minimum sensitivity requirement of 95% outlined in the target product profiles (TPPs) for point-of-care (PoC) testing for CL defined by WHO [29]. In contrast, the RPA test achieved a robust pooled specificity of 93%, meeting the TPP criterion of at least 90% specificity needed to reliably rule out other skin conditions [29]. While the diagnostic method exhibited a strong ability to distinguish CL cases from non-cases (AUC = 0.88), further optimization is necessary to attain exceptional diagnostic accuracy (AUC ≥ 0.97) or a diagnostic algorithm [54, 55]. Overall, these diagnostic performance metrics align with the pooled estimates reported for LAMP in CL diagnosis, although the sensitivity of RPA is slightly lower than that of LAMP [28].

The subgroup analysis revealed a higher pooled sensitivity (93%) for LFA-based RPA, representing a 24% difference compared to the MSL platform. Differences in sample transportation, preservation, and extraction methods likely contributed to the variation in sensitivity [3]. However, the most significant factor appears to be variations in primer and probe design between the LFA and MSL assays. The MSL assay, also known as the LD RPA assay, targets the kDNA sequence of L. donovani, a parasite primarily associated with visceral leishmaniasis. In Sri Lanka, where a variant strain of L. donovani causes CL, reduced sensitivity is likely due to mismatches between the assay’s primers and this strain’s kDNA [46, 47, 56]. Notably, kDNA is a highly variable target. Previous studies have shown that certain primer pairs fail to match with L. aethiopica [57] and L. major species [58]. Additionally, mismatches exist between the MSL assay’s primers and probes and the kDNA targets of L. major, L. aethiopica, and L. infantum—species responsible for most CL cases in the Old World [32]. The lack of studies assessing RPA accuracy for these species limited their inclusion in this review, highlighting a critical gap in the current research.

In contrast, the LFA employs specially designed primers and probes to amplify all species within the L. Viannia subgenus across various countries [31] as well as species within the Leishmania subgenus [51]. Using the GRADE approach, the certainty of evidence for RPA sensitivity was judged as moderate, indicating moderate confidence that the true effect is likely close to the estimated effect, although it may differ substantially. RPA-LFA can be recommended for diagnosing CL caused by L. Viannia species in the New World, although its sensitivity remains below the 95% threshold required by TPP for PoC tests for CL, indicating the need for further optimization [29]. Despite this, RPA-LFA meets many essential criteria outlined in the recently released TPP for PoC diagnostic tests for CL [29, 48]. It is compatible with non-invasive samples, can be used in simple laboratory settings [49], and costs less than $5 USD per test, with a turnaround time of approximately 40 min. Additionally, its lateral flow mechanism enables results to be read visually, and the DNA extraction process involves fewer than ten steps [48]. Consequently, RPA-LFA has the potential to become a valuable tool for decentralized diagnosis of New World CL in primary healthcare settings, significantly improving patient diagnosis rates.

The study in the Volta Region of Ghana that used RPA-LFA with the same primers detected strains of the L. (Mundinia) enrietti complex. However, the results could not be confirmed by PCR due to insufficient DNA quantity and quality, rendering repetition of the test unfeasible. However, previous studies also identified members of the L. enrietti complex in patients from Ghana (49). While L. enrietti is considered nonpathogenic to humans, further research is needed to explore the diversity of circulating strains in West African CL patients [59]. In such settings, RPA could potentially serve as a valuable diagnostic tool. Even more concerning is the lack of studies assessing RPA diagnostic accuracy for the most prevalent Old World CL species. Future research is urgently needed to optimize and evaluate RPA for Old World CL, particularly for commonly encountered species and strains.

Using various search strategies, applying the PRISMA 2020 guideline, and critically evaluating the methodological quality of the included studies with the QUADAS-2 tool are the strengths of this systematic review and meta-analysis. This review, however, has a number of drawbacks. First, it should be noted that the pooled sensitivity results contain almost medium heterogeneity, which requires careful interpretation. It might be the result of a number of factors, including the demographics of the patients, the prevalence of parasite species and the differences in primers and probes [60, 61]. As mentioned earlier, nearly all studies evaluating the performance of LFA were conducted in South America, focusing on New World CL species, which could limit its applicability to Old World CL species. In contrast, all MSL assays were carried out in Sri Lanka using L. donovani CL samples. Additionally, the QUADAS-2 evaluation result indicates that less than half of the studies are unclear for the index test bias, as they lacked information on blinding during the index and gold standard test readout. This might lead to the over- or under-estimation of diagnostic accuracy. Most studies also have an unclear risk of bias for the reference standard because there is no gold standard test for CL. Finally, the limited quantity of studies that are currently accessible restricts the ability to perform meta-regression and to further analyze the differences by evaluating variables like site and reference tests.

Conclusions

RPA demonstrated high specificity and good sensitivity for CL diagnosis but fell short of the 95% sensitivity threshold outlined in the WHO target product profile. In contrast to MSL, LFA appears to be a promising PoC method for diagnosing CL. Data on RPA-MSL remains limited, and its applicability to other Leishmania species causing CL is unclear. Future research should focus on the evaluation of RPA for Old World CL species and strains.
