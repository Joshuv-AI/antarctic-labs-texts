Does ChatGPT know basic human anatomy?
Authors: Mike Yoshio Hamasaki, Alessandra Oliveira Maia, Adroaldo Lunardelli
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

The growing interest in the use of ChatGPT (OpenAI) in the education of healthcare professionals underscores the need for a critical analysis of its potential and limitations, particularly in the teaching of human anatomy. While interaction with large language models (LLMs) through textual interfaces has been partially explored and validated in the scientific literature, their applicability in anatomical sciences remains an emerging field.

Several studies indicate that ChatGPT may support students in understanding the clinical relevance and morphological details of anatomical structures. For educators, the tool has proven useful for generating quizzes, organizing lessons, planning didactic strategies, and even correcting essay responses with appropriate justification [1]. Additionally, prior evidence shows that artificial intelligence (AI)-powered chatbots can foster student engagement by creating a safer space for trial and error. Li et al. [2] reported that medical students felt more comfortable making mistakes with an AI system than with human instructors, which encouraged active participation and increased confidence in anatomy learning.

However, scientific literature also recommends caution. Ilgaz and Çelik [3] demonstrated that LLMs, still under development, can produce inaccurate or misleading answers. Thus, the educational use of these tools requires careful scrutiny of the generated content.

Until recently, interaction with ChatGPT was limited to text input. In 2025, however, the Live Camera feature was launched, available on mobile apps for paying users of the Plus, Pro, Team, and Enterprise plans. This feature enables the model to visually recognize, in real time, objects captured by the phone’s camera, functioning as a kind of video call with AI. As such, ChatGPT evolves from being merely a text generator to acting as a potential visual tutor, capable of assisting students directly in the anatomy lab by responding to questions based on the images shown to it.

This innovation, however, raises a fundamental question: is ChatGPT truly reliable in identifying human anatomical structures on models?

This study aimed to evaluate ChatGPT’s (GPT-4o) ability to recognize human anatomical structures using 59 synthetic models (Body Scientific) employed in undergraduate health education. Synthetic models are physical three-dimensional anatomical pieces made of durable resin designed to withstand frequent handling, ideal for educational settings. Due to their standardized coloring, proportional enlargement of structures, and well-defined visual markings, these models are theoretically more conducive to automated recognition by AI. Additionally, as the models were recently acquired, their preserved condition ensured that the results were not influenced by wear or damage to the pieces.

The models, representing various body systems, were classified by anatomical region, with a predominance of head and neck (n=17), followed by abdomen (n=9), whole-body/systemic (n=9), thorax (n=8), pelvis (n=6), upper limbs (n=6), and lower limbs (n=4). To explore the tool’s performance in its most natural form, a zero-shot prompting strategy was adopted, meaning no prior instructions or examples were provided. In practice, this meant that for each anatomical model, a new chat was initiated using the Live Camera function, allowing the model to process the image in real time and respond to the questions without any prior training or guidance.

For this task, a POCO X3 Pro smartphone (Xiaomi) was used, equipped with the Android 11 MIUI 12 operating system and a rear camera with 48-megapixel sensors. The camera’s maximum resolution is 8,000×6,000 pixels, with a 1/2.0 sensor, digital stabilization, touch focus, autofocus, and variable aperture (f/1.79 to f/2.4). The device also includes a wide-angle lens with a maximum field of view of 119°, which was used for all image captures during the evaluation, which favors capturing large and detailed anatomical models. These specifications were considered adequate to ensure the visual quality of the images analyzed by the tool.

The evaluation was based on four standardized questions applied to each of the 59 anatomical models: identification of the organ or structure presented; recognition of the anatomical view (anterior, posterior, lateral, etc.); identification of the anatomical section or plane (sagittal, transverse, coronal); and naming of four anatomical structures specifically pointed out on each model. The responses provided by ChatGPT were compared with an answer key previously validated by two anatomy experts, and accuracy rates were recorded for each question category, based on the correct or incorrect answer rates.

The results showed that ChatGPT’s performance with Live Camera varied depending on the cognitive demand of each task. Overall identification of the models yielded an accuracy of 88.14%, suggesting strong global categorization ability. This supports previous findings on the educational potential of the tool in gross anatomy, particularly in interactive and integrative strategies that connect anatomy to clinical, physiological, and imaging contexts [4].

Conversely, accuracy decreased for recognition of anatomical views and sections, both scoring 69.49%. This indicates limitations in three-dimensional analysis and spatial inference, likely due to the absence of depth perception and reliance on two-dimensional visual patterns.

The lowest accuracy was observed in the naming of 236 individually indicated structures, with ChatGPT correctly identifying 129 of them (54.66%). This highlights a significant weakness in fine-grained morphological recognition, underscoring the need for caution when considering ChatGPT as an autonomous virtual tutor. Such tasks demand precise correspondence between visual input and anatomical terminology, a skill that is essential for both clinical accuracy and effective anatomical education yet still limited in the model’s current form.

It is also important to note that the evaluation was performed using synthetic models, which, due to their standardized colors and markings, are easier to recognize than cadaveric specimens. Therefore, its performance in more realistic settings, such as human anatomy labs with biological material, is expected to be lower.

A particularly concerning observation was the model’s tendency to respond confidently even when incorrect. In several cases, ChatGPT presented inaccurate information with a high degree of certainty and elaboration, which could mislead users into trusting the response. This, combined with the model’s often verbose style, poses an educational risk, especially for students without sufficient prior knowledge to critically assess the information.

Moreover, the challenge of evaluating generative AI models is amplified by their constant evolution. As Wong [5] emphasizes, each new version of LLMs significantly alters performance levels, causing prior assessments to lose relevance over time. In fact, while the present study was conducted with GPT-4o, during the peer-review process a new version (GPT-5) was already released, raising the inevitable question of whether results would differ. This continuous cycle highlights a structural limitation of research in this field.

Thus, while ChatGPT with Live Camera represents a promising advancement in AI-based anatomical education, its unsupervised use by students lacking foundational knowledge is not recommended. The outcomes observed with zero-shot prompting, i.e., without any prior examples, reveal key limitations, particularly in detailed structural identification. Performance would likely improve with few-shot prompting, an approach in which the model is exposed to contextualized examples to guide its responses. In anatomical contexts, this could include presenting similar models with labeled structures, technical glossaries, or reference images.

Future investigations should also expand into comparative analyses across platforms, as suggested by a recent work evaluating ChatGPT, Gemini (Google), and Claude (Anthropic) in tasks such as answering USMLE Step 1-style anatomy questions and identifying anatomical structures in radiographs [6]. That study reported that ChatGPT achieved the highest accuracy, Claude delivered more structured and pedagogically useful explanations, and Gemini showed lower accuracy and less scientific rigor. In any case, even though unsupervised use may be associated with greater student engagement and comfort, the educational use of such tools should be approached with caution, and they are best positioned as complementary resources under supervision, with critical interpretation of their outputs.
