Device-level comparisons of sensing and stimulation in implantable closed-loop neurostimulation for epilepsy
Authors: Yanming Zhu, Ope Alabi, Nathanial D Sisterson, Matteo Vissani, Alan Bush, Pranav Nanda, R Mark Richardson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDrug-resistant epilepsy affects roughly one-third of people with epilepsy, and options remain limited for patients whose seizures arise from eloquent cortex, involve multiple foci, or persist after prior surgery. For these patients, closed-loop neurostimulation offers a nondestructive and adjustable alternative that detects pathological activity and delivers stimulation only when and where it is needed. The NeuroPace responsive neurostimulation (RNS) System, approved in 2013, remains the most established clinical example: it senses at the therapeutic target, drives stimulation from biomarkers recorded there, and stores raw neural signals for iterative programming. These same principles increasingly define a newer generation of adaptive deep brain stimulation (DBS) platforms developed largely for movement disorders, motivating a direct, device-level comparison of contemporary implantable closed-loop systems for epilepsy. We compare four clinically approved systems in depth (the NeuroPace RNS, Medtronic Percept PC, Newronika AlphaDBS, and PINS G106RS) together with three investigational platforms (the Picostim-DyNeuMo, CorTec Brain Interchange, and Cadence Neuroscience system), examining sensing architecture, artifact management, bandwidth, onboard data handling, stimulation-source design, and degree of autonomous control, and summarizing their relative capabilities across a common set of scored dimensions. Across platforms, the most consequential differences arise less from stimulation parameter ranges than from sensing design: which contacts can record, whether sensing can continue during stimulation, what frequency range is accessible, how stimulation artifact is suppressed, and whether raw waveforms or only derived features are retained. Because much of the sensing and adaptive-control evidence for the DBS platforms derives from movement-disorder rather than epilepsy applications, we interpret their epilepsy relevance conservatively and highlight where epilepsy-specific validation is still needed.Keywords: Responsive neurostimulation, Deep brain stimulation, Neuromodulation, Epilepsy, Brain recording

Introduction

The underutilization of epilepsy surgery now represents a public health crisis, even as neuromodulation and other minimally destructive approaches broaden surgical candidacy and begin to reshape the field’s therapeutic paradigms [1,2]. Epilepsy affects approximately 50 million people worldwide, with a disproportionate burden in low- and middle-income countries where access to specialist care is limited [3]. Despite the availability of more than 30 antiseizure medications, approximately one-third of patients do not achieve adequate seizure control with pharmacotherapy alone [4]. For many of these patients, surgical resection or thermal ablation offers the best chance of seizure freedom. Yet a substantial subset are not candidates for these approaches because the seizure onset zone lies within or near eloquent cortex, is multifocal or poorly localized, or has already been treated unsuccessfully [4,5].

For this population, chronic neuromodulation offers a nondestructive, reversible, and adjustable alternative. Three modalities are currently in clinical use for epilepsy: vagus nerve stimulation (VNS), which delivers intermittent open-loop cervical stimulation; anterior nucleus of the thalamus DBS (ANT-DBS), which delivers scheduled bilateral thalamic stimulation; and responsive neurostimulation (RNS), which delivers stimulation contingent on detected electrocorticographic (ECoG) activity at or near the epileptogenic zone [[5], [6], [7], [8], [9], [10]]. Across separate long-term studies, the reported median seizure reduction at three years has been approximately 44% for VNS [11], approximately 40% for ANT-DBS in the European MORE registry [12], and approximately 82% for the RNS System in its postapproval study [13]. Of these, the RNS occupies a unique position: it is the only FDA-approved device that records from and stimulates at the seizure focus itself, makes autonomous real-time stimulation decisions based on those recordings, and stores the underlying raw electrographic data for retrospective physician review [4,8,14,15]. This architecture, with sensing, processing, and stimulation co-localized at the pathological source, is what distinguishes closed-loop from open-loop neuromodulation and what makes the RNS the most instructive clinical model for understanding adaptive brain stimulation [4,8].

The lessons learned from the RNS are increasingly relevant to adaptive DBS systems developed primarily for movement disorders. In parallel, DBS hardware has advanced in areas such as current steering, directional leads, chronic local field potential recording, and embedded control algorithms [3,[16], [17], [18], [19]]. These systems were not designed specifically for epilepsy, with the earlier Activa PC + S work in epilepsy models as a notable exception, but they now raise many of the same engineering questions [20]. This convergence is especially relevant as network-level epilepsy targets such as the anterior nucleus, centromedian nucleus, and pulvinar of the thalamus gain increasing clinical interest [6,7,10,21,22]. Advances in battery technology, low-power signal processing, and miniaturized implantable electronics have made long-term ambulatory closed-loop systems practical [3,17,[23], [24], [25], [26], [27]]. Early responsive neurostimulation prototypes depended on external computers and monitored settings [28,29]. Contemporary devices integrate sensing, processing, and stimulation within a single implanted platform, with battery life measured in years [8,17,19]. As epilepsy and movement-disorder neuromodulation increasingly confront shared challenges in biomarker selection, artifact suppression, and chronic data management, direct technical comparison across platforms becomes increasingly useful [3,4,17,18].

Methods

In this review, we compare four implantable systems: the RNS System (NeuroPace, Mountain View, CA), the Percept PC (Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN), the G106RS (PINS Medical, Beijing, China), and the AlphaDBS System (Newronika, Milan, Italy). We performed a review of all available published literature describing the technical capabilities of these devices. These four systems were selected because each is an implantable closed-loop or sensing-enabled device that has received marketing approval in at least one jurisdiction for chronic use: the RNS System (FDA 2013), the Percept PC (Conformite Europeenne [CE] mark and FDA approval, 2020), the AlphaDBS System (CE 2021), and the G106RS (marketed in China). Investigational-only research platforms that also support concurrent sensing and stimulation, such as the Medtronic Summit RC + S and the Picostim-DyNeuMo, were therefore excluded from the primary device comparison and are instead considered among future directions. Specifications for these representative investigational systems are summarized in Table 2 [20,[30], [31], [32], [33]]. We focus on sensing architecture, biomarker strategy, artifact management, data handling, and degree of closed-loop autonomy. We then consider what adaptive DBS can learn from the RNS, what the RNS can learn from DBS, and which technical and biological challenges are likely to define the next generation of closed-loop neuromodulation [3,4,[17], [18], [19]].

Results

RNS system

The Responsive Neurostimulation System, approved by the FDA in 2013, was the first commercially available closed-loop intracranial neurostimulation device [8]. It is indicated for adults aged 18 years and older with medically refractory focal epilepsy arising from no more than two epileptogenic foci, including patients whose seizure onset zones cannot be safely resected because they involve eloquent cortex [5,8,21]. More recent evidence suggests a broader network-level role for the platform, including the randomized NAUTILUS trial of bilateral centromedian thalamic responsive stimulation for idiopathic generalized epilepsy and multiple thalamic case series in multifocal epilepsy [21,22,[39], [40], [41]]. The system continuously monitors electrocorticographic activity through implanted depth or subdural electrodes and delivers brief responsive stimulation when prespecified ictal patterns are detected, with the goal of interrupting seizure activity before it propagates or generalizes [4,8,14,15,42].

Electrode placement near the seizure onset zone, with recording and stimulating contacts positioned within or immediately adjacent to the seizure onset zone, enables recording with high spatial and temporal resolution; because electrocorticographic activity is a spatially local signal, effective detection generally requires contacts within the onset zone [8,[43], [44], [45]]. Depth and subdural strip leads each contain four platinum-iridium contacts, and although only two leads can be connected to the neurostimulator at a given time, additional leads may be implanted and retained for future selection if initial targets prove suboptimal [8,14,46]. Contacts can be configured for sensing, stimulation, or both, using bipolar or referential recording montages and multiple cathode-anode stimulation arrangements [4,8,46]. This design requires robust stimulation-artifact management, but it also preserves close spatial correspondence between the recorded biomarker and the delivered therapy [8,47]. Whether stimulation is most effective when delivered at the seizure focus itself, immediately adjacent to it, or within downstream propagation pathways remains an open question, and the chronic recording capability of the RNS is well suited to help address it [43,44].

Long-term clinical follow-up has shown progressive improvement in seizure outcomes. Median seizure frequency reduction was 44% at one year in the pivotal trial and 53% at two years [48]. In longer-term follow-up, reported median seizure reduction increased to 58% at three years, 66% at five years, 69% at seven years, and 75% at nine years [4,49]. This sustained improvement is a defining clinical observation associated with the platform and may reflect, at least in part, continued refinement of detection and stimulation parameters over time [4,49]. Direct head-to-head randomized comparisons of brain-responsive neurostimulation with anterior thalamic deep brain stimulation or vagus nerve stimulation are lacking, but the available long-term evidence consistently favors responsive neurostimulation across both focal and generalized epilepsies. In drug-resistant focal epilepsy, vagus nerve stimulation produces median seizure reductions of approximately 35% [50,51], and anterior thalamic deep brain stimulation improves from roughly 33% at two years to 55% at five years in long-term registry follow-up [12,52], whereas brain-responsive neurostimulation improves from approximately 62% at six months to 82% at three years and sustains median reductions of about 75% through nine years, with many patients achieving extended seizure-free intervals [13,49]. In idiopathic generalized epilepsy, pooled analyses report median reductions near 48% for vagus nerve stimulation and 65% for thalamic deep brain stimulation [53], while responsive stimulation of the centromedian nucleus produced a 76.8% median reduction at 18 months, a 62.5% responder rate, and rendered 40% of patients free of generalized tonic-clonic seizures in the first randomized controlled trial conducted in this population [41]. These cross-study comparisons cannot substitute for randomized head-to-head data and are influenced by differences in patient populations, stimulation targets, and follow-up duration; nonetheless, they position brain-responsive neurostimulation at the higher end of the efficacy range among currently available implantable neurostimulation options.

A related assumption warrants scrutiny. It is sometimes argued that certain seizure networks are so difficult to treat that they require the continuous stimulation delivered by DBS rather than detection-triggered intermittent therapy. No published data support this claim. No trial has identified a circuit phenotype or patient subgroup in which continuous stimulation outperforms responsive stimulation, and no head-to-head comparison of the two strategies within any proposed subgroup has been conducted. The premise is further undermined by the design of thalamic DBS itself: in the SANTE trial and its long-term follow-up, stimulation was delivered on an intermittent duty cycle of 1 min on and 5 min off, so even “continuous” DBS is in practice scheduled intermittent stimulation delivered without regard to brain state [6,7]. Conversely, responsive stimulation achieves its outcomes with a strikingly small stimulation dose, with typical RNS patients receiving brief detection-triggered stimulations totaling only a few minutes per day while attaining the long-term seizure reductions described above [42,49]. The available evidence therefore suggests that when and where stimulation is delivered matters more than how much, and it provides no empirical basis for the claim that some seizure circuits need continuous stimulation.

Stimulation is delivered as current-controlled, charge-balanced biphasic pulses [8,14,15,42]. The device uses a single digital-to-analog converter output pair, one anodic and one cathodic, to generate each stimulation burst. When multiple contacts share a polarity, current divides across those contacts according to impedance rather than being independently specified at each electrode [8]. Responsive therapy can be organized as sequential therapies triggered after detector events, and programming spans detector selection, pattern-specific thresholds, stimulation pathway configuration, amplitude, pulse width, frequency, burst duration, therapy order, and daily therapy limits [4,8,46]. Programmable parameters include pulse frequency from 1 to 333 Hz, pulse width from 40 to 1000 μs, amplitude from 0.5 to 12 mA, and burst duration from 10 to 5000 ms [4,8,14,15,42]. Charge density per phase must remain below 30 μC/cm2 per electrode [54,55].

The RNS implantable pulse generator is skull mounted and connects to the leads through a titanium case measuring 60 × 28 × 7.7 mm, weighing 17.2 g, and occupying 11 cm3 [8,14]. Onboard storage retains approximately 30 channel-minutes of ECoG (about 7.5 min when all four channels are recorded simultaneously) in programmable segments of 30–240 s sampled at 250 Hz, with the oldest recordings overwritten unless reserved [4,8]. Importantly, the device stores raw ECoG waveform snippets surrounding detected events rather than only derived spectral summaries [4,8,56,57]. It also records multiple event classes and summary histories that shape interpretation of device behavior, including long episodes, saturation events, magnet-triggered recordings, scheduled recordings, therapy counts, and daily Neurostimulator History metrics [4,46]. This makes the RNS distinctive among currently deployed clinical systems in providing waveform-level retrospective review of detection-linked events for programming and algorithm refinement, even though broader device behavior still must be inferred from sparse event-linked samples rather than continuous recordings [4,8,46]. Average battery life is approximately 8.4 years [14].

Detection algorithms are physician programmed and can incorporate measures such as spectral power, line length, area under the curve, and bandpass features [4]. Their optimization remains labor intensive and is often empirical. The RNS programming workflow therefore depends not only on stored ECoG review but also on interpretation of device metadata, including therapy delivery rates, detector-specific event counts, long-episode behavior, and magnet-correlated events [4,46]. Because the device stores only peri-event recordings rather than continuous data, retrospective review is limited to episodes that triggered detection or were otherwise captured by storage rules, which constrains assessment of false positives, missed events, and the broader electrophysiological context in which detections occurred [4,46]. Several limitations of the platform warrant emphasis alongside its strengths: only two leads and four sensing channels can be active at once; stimulation is delivered from a single current source without independent per-contact steering; the 250 Hz sampling rate constrains access to higher-frequency oscillations; and detection settings are deliberately biased toward high sensitivity, so the majority of detections are never independently verified against the stored electrographic record. Detection counts in some cases therefore could correlate only weakly with true seizure frequency, and detection programming remains labor intensive and empirical [4,8,46]. Nonetheless, this device recently became the first to employ an AI tool, termed “ECoG-Assistant,” that reliably detects electrographic seizure patterns in recordings stored on the Patient Data Management System (PDMS) [58].

Beyond focal epilepsy, the RNS has also been used off label in other settings, including responsive thalamic stimulation for Tourette syndrome and centromedian thalamic stimulation in idiopathic generalized epilepsy [21,59]. The Scheduled Therapy mode introduced in the RNS 320 also permits stimulation during programmed time windows or recurring intervals independent of detection, allowing the platform to approximate continuous or cyclical open-loop stimulation while preserving chronic sensing and event-triggered recording capabilities when clinically appropriate [4,8].

Percept PC

The Percept PC is a DBS platform approved by the FDA in 2020, and CE marked in Europe the same year, for the treatment of Parkinson’s disease, essential tremor, dystonia, and obsessive-compulsive disorder [17]. Its initial approval covered conventional DBS delivery together with the BrainSense sensing platform, which enables chronic recording of local field potentials during active stimulation [17,[60], [61], [62]]. The device has become one of the most important commercial platforms for adaptive DBS development because it combines widely used DBS hardware with chronic sensing capability [17,63,64].

BrainSense technology supports two distinct recording modes. BrainSense Timeline records local field potential power continuously during stimulation delivery, providing a longitudinal view of spectral activity over days to months [17]. BrainSense Survey, by contrast, records local field potential data with stimulation paused and samples across contact pairs to characterize the spectral landscape before programming [17,61]. These modes serve different purposes and should not be conflated: Timeline reflects neural activity during therapeutic stimulation, whereas Survey characterizes the unstimulated baseline [17].

Stimulation is delivered in constant-current mode through 16 independent current sources, one per available contact [17]. This multiple independent current control architecture enables current steering and directional field shaping when paired with SenSight directional leads and is also compatible with Medtronic leads 3387, 3389, and 3391 [17]. Amplitude is programmable in 0.05 mA steps up to 12.5 mA, pulse width from 20 to 450 μs, and frequency from 3 to 250 Hz [17].

Its sensing architecture imposes important constraints. During concurrent sensing and stimulation, the recording contacts must flank the stimulating cathode, and sensing and stimulation cannot occur on the same contact; when stimulation is off, by contrast, local field potentials can be surveyed across other bipolar contact pairs, so the adjacency requirement applies specifically to simultaneous sensing and stimulation rather than to sensing in general [17,60]. This limits flexibility when the best recording site is not the best stimulation site. Stimulation-artifact rejection relies on hardware blanking and is generally adequate for routine clinical use, although residual artifact can persist at higher stimulation amplitudes and frequencies [17,65,66].

Sampling is limited to 250 Hz in both BrainSense Survey and LFP streaming modes [17,60,61]. Because sensing is digitized at approximately 250 Hz, chronic BrainSense recording is also subject to sense-stimulation interactions that depend on the stimulation frequency: at lower stimulation frequencies the stimulation fundamental and its harmonics fall within the sensed band, whereas at higher frequencies the artifact is aliased to a mirror frequency. Low-frequency stimulation in particular can therefore degrade or preclude reliable chronic sensing, an interlock that constrains adaptive strategies requiring low-rate stimulation [17,60,61]. This is sufficient for beta-band biomarkers but restricts evaluation of higher-frequency signals, including stimulation-entrained gamma oscillations, which may provide useful information about therapeutic engagement in some patient groups [63]. Broader-band systems such as the AlphaDBS and G106RS address this limitation at the hardware level, although clinical validation of gamma-driven adaptive control remains early [19,63].

The Percept PC pulse generator has dimensions of 68 × 55 × 11 mm, a volume of 34.9 cm3, and a mass of 61 g [17]. The sensing noise floor is below 300 nV/√Hz, with an input range of 0.55–400 μV rms [17]. Longitudinal storage is available for up to 60 days of trend data, but those data consist of narrow-band power values rather than raw waveforms [17,[24], [25], [26], [27]]. The device is conditionally compatible with 3 T magnetic resonance imaging and uses a hybrid silver vanadium oxide primary cell [17,[24], [25], [26], [27]]. The Percept platform is also available in a rechargeable version, the Percept RC (CE marked in 2023 and FDA approved in January 2024), which shares the BrainSense sensing architecture but provides a longer rated service life at the cost of periodic recharging. The two versions share the same BrainSense sensing and BrainSense Timeline storage capabilities (the Percept PC retains up to approximately 60 days of trend data); the principal difference of the RC is its rechargeable power source and longer rated service life rather than its sensing or storage architecture [60,67]. Both the Percept PC and Percept RC are labeled MR conditional for full-body imaging at 1.5 T and 3 T under specified conditions [17].

Recent clinical studies have suggested that adaptive stimulation using the Percept platform can improve outcomes relative to conventional continuous DBS in selected Parkinson’s disease cohorts. In a blinded randomized feasibility study, chronic adaptive DBS produced greater improvement in several motor outcomes than conventional continuous stimulation [63]. In the ADAPT-PD study, long-term personalized adaptive DBS met its primary noninferiority goal relative to conventional DBS, with post hoc analyses suggesting increased on-time and reduced total electrical energy delivered in selected adaptive modes [64]. These results are encouraging, but they should be interpreted in the context of a system whose sensing flexibility and data storage design remain more constrained than those of platforms that permit sensing on any contact during stimulation, broader-band recording, and retention of raw multichannel waveforms, capabilities exemplified by the target-site waveform capture of the RNS and the artifact-tolerant any-contact sensing of the AlphaDBS [17,19,63,64].

AlphaDBS system

The AlphaDBS System is a fully implantable closed-loop DBS platform that received CE marking for conventional DBS and neural sensing in 2021 [19]. It is being evaluated in a pilot multicenter randomized crossover trial comparing adaptive DBS with conventional DBS in Parkinson’s disease [19,68]. The system was designed to address two longstanding limitations of earlier bidirectional devices: inadequate artifact rejection during simultaneous sensing and stimulation, and dependence on external processors for execution of the control algorithm [19].

The pulse generator has a volume of 20.96 cm3 and weighs 32.70 g, making it compact relative to many existing rechargeable DBS devices [19]. The system supports 16 independent current-controlled stimulation channels and accepts Medtronic lead extensions [19]. Stimulation is delivered as capacitively coupled, actively charge-balanced asymmetric biphasic pulses, with amplitude from 0 to 5 mA, pulse width from 40 to 250 μs, and frequency from 40 to 200 Hz [19]. The platform can operate in either conventional or adaptive mode [19].

In adaptive mode, stimulation amplitude is adjusted in real time using an embedded proportional feedback algorithm based on subthalamic beta-band local field potential power between 10 and 35 Hz [19]. This proportional control approach builds on earlier clinical demonstrations of beta-driven adaptive stimulation by the same group, including an 8-h adaptive DBS study in Parkinson disease [69]. The algorithm runs entirely on chip and continuously updates output within clinician-defined therapeutic limits [19]. This distinguishes the AlphaDBS from systems in which chronic sensing is available but autonomous control remains more constrained in the commercial configuration [19,68].

A central technical feature of the AlphaDBS is its stimulation-artifact-management architecture, termed FilterDBS, which derives from earlier work on analog suppression of stimulation artifact in local field potential recordings [19,70]. Stimulation-artifact suppression occurs at the analog chip level before digitization and does not rely on blanking windows, symmetric electrode constraints, or backend digital filtering [19]. As a result, sensing can be performed on any contact, including contacts actively delivering stimulation [19]. Two differential sensing channels can be multiplexed across any of the eight contacts per lead, with analog-to-digital conversion at 512 Hz [19]. In an initial three-patient pilot cohort, signal quality at the beta peak was high, wireless data loss was low, and no cardiac artifact was reported [19].

Data handling occurs in two modes. In streaming mode, raw local field potentials are transmitted on demand to the physician programmer [19]. In embedded mode, the device logs compressed spectral features to onboard memory, including beta-band power every minute, full spectra from 5 to 35 Hz every 10 min, and stimulation amplitude every 10 min [19]. These data are transferred during recharging and then relayed through a smartphone-based pipeline [19]. The rechargeable battery retains most of its capacity over repeated cycles, and firmware can be updated over the air [19].

Current limitations include a sensing bandwidth that remains optimized for beta-band control rather than for broader high-frequency biomarker exploration and a control law that remains relatively simple [19]. As noted for the Percept PC, broader-band front-end hardware of this kind is what would be required to develop gamma-driven adaptive control, although clinical validation of such strategies in either movement disorders or epilepsy remains early [19,63]. The main significance of the platform is therefore not that it resolves every adaptive DBS challenge, but that it demonstrates the feasibility of simultaneous sensing, stimulation, and embedded autonomous control within a compact commercial system [19].

G106RS

The G106RS, developed by Beijing PINS Medical Equipment Co., Ltd., is a rechargeable DBS system with integrated local field potential sensing. The device is designed for bilateral stimulation of deep brain targets in movement disorders and supports both conventional stimulation and sensing-assisted operation [71]. In the English-language peer-reviewed literature the device is characterized primarily within a device-technology review rather than in dedicated primary technical or clinical studies, and published clinical evidence remains limited relative to that for the RNS and Percept PC [71].

The implantable pulse generator measures 55 × 52 × 10 mm and weighs 38 g. Stimulation is voltage controlled, with an output range of 0–10 V, pulse widths of 30–450 μs, and frequencies of 2–250 Hz. The platform has been described as supporting multichannel local field potential acquisition with broader sampling capability than the Percept PC, transmitting up to eight channels of local field potential data at a reported 1000 Hz sampling rate, which is broader than the Percept PC and potentially useful for studying gamma-band and other higher-frequency biomarkers [71]. However, the published evidence base remains nascent, and peer-reviewed technical characterization of artifact rejection, onboard storage, and embedded closed-loop algorithms remains limited. Several device parameters attributed to the manufacturer, including stimulation-off and stimulation-on sampling rates and onboard storage behavior, have not been independently validated in peer-reviewed studies and should be regarded as provisional. For that reason, the G106RS is best viewed as an emerging bidirectional platform with promising sensing capability rather than as a fully characterized clinical standard.

Discussion

Reciprocal insights between the RNS system and DBS

Across systems, the largest practical differences arise less from nominal stimulation ranges than from sensing architecture (Table 1). The RNS and AlphaDBS rank highest in contact flexibility and autonomy, but they do so through different design philosophies: the RNS emphasizes target-site waveform capture in epilepsy, whereas the AlphaDBS emphasizes artifact-resistant embedded adaptive control in DBS. The Percept PC is strongest in current-source sophistication and now supports commercially available adaptive operation, but its sensing flexibility and raw-data retention remain more limited. The G106RS appears promising as a broader-band sensing platform, yet many of its core technical characteristics remain insufficiently characterized in peer-reviewed clinical literature.

The RNS provides one of the clearest examples of target-site sensing linked directly to responsive stimulation in a commercially deployed device [4,8]. Two features are especially instructive for adaptive DBS systems: recording from the pathological tissue itself and storing raw waveform data rather than only derived features [4,8,56,57].

The first lesson is the importance of biomarker specificity. In the RNS, stimulation is driven by ictal ECoG patterns recorded from or near the seizure onset zone [4,8]. This provides a biomarker that is spatially close to the pathological source and often more directly tied to the clinical event than the broader physiological signals used in current DBS systems. That specificity defines the platform’s usefulness as both a therapeutic and observational device [4].

By contrast, most adaptive DBS systems rely on broader biomarkers, particularly beta-band power, recorded under stricter hardware constraints [17,18,[72], [73], [74]]. In the Percept PC, sensing during stimulation requires the recording contacts to flank the stimulating cathode, the sensing bandwidth is limited to 250 Hz, and stored data are derived power envelopes rather than raw local field potential traces [17]. These design choices limit retrospective review of algorithm decisions, reduce flexibility for biomarker discovery, and make it harder to evaluate false positives or missed detections without a device upgrade [17]. For epilepsy specifically, the sensing evidence for these DBS platforms is largely extrapolated from movement disorders; direct epilepsy sensing data are so far limited to thalamic local field potential recordings at network targets such as the anterior and centromedian nuclei, and it is the chronic sensing capability, rather than the stimulation hardware, that most requires epilepsy-specific validation [60].

Stimulation-entrained gamma oscillations have emerged as a promising biomarker for DBS optimization in some patient groups [63]. The 250 Hz sensing ceiling shared by the Percept PC and the RNS limits systematic development of gamma-based control strategies on either platform [17,63]. Broader-band systems such as the AlphaDBS and G106RS address that limitation in hardware, although clinical validation of gamma-driven adaptive DBS remains early [19,63].

The RNS also illustrates the value of preserving spatial correspondence between sensing and stimulation. Any of the four contacts per lead can be assigned to sensing or stimulation, and sensing and stimulation can occur on the same contacts [8]. This requires careful artifact suppression, but it also ensures that the measured biomarker and therapeutic field remain closely aligned [8,47]. DBS systems that constrain sensing contacts relative to stimulation contacts may sacrifice some of that correspondence [17].

Finally, the RNS highlights the importance of algorithm transparency. Although its storage is limited to detected-event segments rather than continuous recordings, it still preserves raw waveforms that allow clinicians to inspect what the device actually recorded at the time of stimulation [4,8,56,57]. Future closed-loop systems would benefit from extending this principle toward more continuous raw-signal storage. Without waveform-level review, retrospective validation of device behavior remains incomplete [4,17].

DBS platforms offer several engineering advances that could inform future development of the RNS. One is lead design. Standard NeuroPace depth and cortical strip leads contain four cylindrical contacts and no directional capability [8]. By contrast, DBS has progressively moved toward eight-contact leads, including segmented directional electrodes that enable current steering and more selective shaping of the electric field [[75], [76], [77], [78], [79], [80]]. Incorporating a higher number of contacts or directional designs into responsive epilepsy stimulation could improve both recording specificity and stimulation precision, to the extent that knowledge about anatomical target engagement continues to improve [[75], [76], [77], [78], [79], [80]].

A second lesson is current-source architecture. Contemporary DBS platforms such as the Percept PC and AlphaDBS provide independent current-controlled outputs across contacts [17,19]. The RNS uses a single current source, so current divides passively across parallel cathodes according to impedance [8]. Independent per-contact current control would allow more precise field shaping and could support more flexible adaptive stimulation strategies [17,19].

DBS programming has also developed more formal approaches for estimating the volume of tissue activated by combining lead geometry, anatomy, and stimulation settings [16,17,81,82]. RNS programming remains more empirical and is often guided by detected event rates and patient-reported outcomes [4]. As understanding of circuit-level targets in epilepsy improves, volume-of-tissue-activated-informed programming may help make responsive stimulation more systematic and less resource intensive [16,81,82].

On data management, DBS platforms have often offered longer trend storage than the RNS, but storage quantity is less important than storage content. The RNS stores raw ECoG snippets, which are often more informative than longer archives of narrow-band power alone [4,8,17]. An ideal future design would combine the strengths of both approaches by supporting broader longitudinal storage while preserving raw multichannel waveform data [4,17,19].

Optimization of therapeutic settings

Optimizing device settings in DBS and RNS remains one of the most resource-intensive aspects of closed-loop neuromodulation [3,16,[83], [84], [85]]. Establishing stable settings often requires repeated programming visits over several months [3,83,84]. Even for a standard four-contact lead, the number of possible contact configurations is large, and each can be combined with many amplitudes, pulse widths, and frequency settings [17,85]. The parameter space expands further with eight-contact leads, directional electrodes, independent current sources, and adaptive control features [17,[75], [76], [77], [78], [79], [80],82]. Manual exploration of this space is inherently limited [16,[83], [84], [85]].

The principal clinical risks of suboptimal programming are under-stimulation, which leaves the intended target insufficiently modulated, and over-stimulation, which spreads current into neighboring structures and produces cognitive, psychiatric, or motor side effects [83,84]. Current strategies to reduce these risks include volume-of-tissue-activated modeling, local field potential-guided contact selection, and iterative adjustment based on clinical assessment and patient-reported outcomes [16,18,81,82,85,86].

Closed-loop systems reduce part of this burden by linking stimulation to a physiological signal, but they also introduce new optimization challenges. Detection thresholds, control laws, biomarker definitions, and stimulation bounds all require initial configuration and ongoing revision [4,18,19,64]. Experience with the RNS suggests that continued refinement of detection settings can yield meaningful clinical benefit even years after implantation [4,49]. Analogous long-term refinement is likely to play a central role in adaptive DBS as chronic sensing and algorithm design continue to improve [19,63,64].

Future directions

Several converging technical and biological challenges are likely to shape the next generation of closed-loop neuromodulation.

The first is biomarker validation. Closed-loop control is only as reliable as the electrophysiological signal on which it depends, and in epilepsy the biomarkers used to drive therapy remain incompletely validated. Chronic ambulatory recordings have shown that interictal epileptiform activity, detection surrogates such as line length and bandpass spectral power, and circadian and multiday cycles of seizure risk can be tracked over years, but their specificity for the ictal state and their stability across sleep, medication changes, and behavioral state are still being defined [4,46]. Device-derived detection counts are deliberately biased toward high sensitivity and often correlate only weakly with independently verified seizures, which complicates their use as endpoints; analyses linking chronic responsive-stimulation neurophysiology to seizure control illustrate both the promise and the present limits of epilepsy biomarkers for guiding therapy [87]. Establishing markers that are measurable over time, specific to the clinical state of interest, and robust to physiological confounding therefore remains a central prerequisite for reliable closed-loop control in epilepsy [3,18].

The second is sensing bandwidth. The 250 Hz ceiling shared by the RNS and Percept PC is adequate for current dominant biomarkers but restricts systematic study of higher-frequency signals, including gamma activity and stimulation-entrained oscillations [17,63]. Platforms with broader bandwidth, including the G106RS and, to a lesser extent, the AlphaDBS, may be better suited for evaluating next-generation biomarkers [19]. Future epilepsy systems would also benefit from supporting four simultaneously active leads rather than two, which would better enable network-level circuit assessment and therapy across bilateral cortical and thalamic nodes by allowing seizure onset, propagation, and control sites to be sampled and modulated in parallel [1,35,46].

The third is onboard computation. Current implantable algorithms remain deliberately simple, typically threshold detectors or proportional feedback controllers, because of constraints in power consumption, memory, and timing [3,4,18,19]. As implantable processing improves, more complex classifiers, multifeature detection systems, and model-based control strategies may become feasible. Progress in this area will depend not only on hardware advances but also on a better mechanistic understanding of how neural signals and stimulation parameters map to clinical state [3,18].

A fourth challenge is circuit-level biology. The progressive improvement observed with long-term brain-responsive neurostimulation suggests that chronic stimulation may drive functional reorganization of epileptic networks over time [4,49]. Longitudinal analyses support this view: chronic responsive neurostimulation is associated with progressive, treatment-related changes in network excitability and interictal activity that accrue over months to years and track with clinical improvement [87,88]. The cellular and network mechanisms underlying this reorganization remain incompletely understood, and better mechanistic insight could help guide stimulation paradigms toward more durable therapeutic effects [3,4].

Emerging investigational platforms also point toward a broader future for closed-loop neuromodulation in epilepsy. In the United Kingdom, the Picostim-DyNeuMo platform is now being evaluated in the Children’s Adaptive Deep Brain Stimulation for Epilepsy Trial (CADET) Pilot for Lennox-Gastaut syndrome, highlighting interest in cranially mounted adaptive stimulation for severe pediatric generalized epilepsies [32,33]. The same platform has also been developed for other indications, including Parkinson’s disease, multiple system atrophy, and chronotherapy-oriented neuromodulation paradigms [30,31]. CorTec’s Brain Interchange similarly represents an investigational closed-loop system designed for chronic wireless sensing, multichannel stimulation, and flexible real-time algorithm development, and may be most relevant to epilepsy at present as a research platform for biomarker discovery and adaptive control rather than as a mature disease-specific therapy [34,37].The Cadence Neuroscience system, an investigational bidirectional platform whose therapy was developed on the Medtronic Summit RC + S and is now implemented in the company’s Alera implantable neurostimulator, is being studied for chronic thalamocortical sensing paired with responsive and subthreshold cortical stimulation in drug-resistant epilepsy, with early translational and first-in-human work characterizing its recording fidelity and stimulation strategies [35,36,38]. Specifications and capability profiles for these investigational platforms are summarized in Table 2 and Fig. 2B. More broadly, each condition presents distinct challenges in target selection, state measurement, and outcome definition. Progress will depend on close collaboration across systems neuroscience, clinical neurology and psychiatry, and device engineering, as well as improved access to high-quality chronic neural data [3,18,[30], [31], [32], [33], [34],37,[89], [90], [91], [92]].

Closed-loop neuromodulation has advanced from experimental concept to established clinical practice in selected disorders [4,13,19,49,63,64]. The systems reviewed here reflect different engineering priorities (Fig. 2A). The RNS emphasizes target-site sensing and waveform-level transparency in epilepsy [4,8,56,57]. The Percept PC combines chronic sensing with flexible current steering across a broad DBS indication base [17]. The G106RS offers broader-band sensing within a still-limited published literature. The AlphaDBS demonstrates that simultaneous sensing, stimulation, and embedded adaptive control can be integrated within a compact implantable platform [19,68].

Across devices, the most important differences arise less from nominal stimulation parameter ranges than from sensing architecture. Contact flexibility, artifact suppression, bandwidth, and the nature of stored data all shape what biomarkers can be used, how algorithms can be refined, and how transparently device behavior can be reviewed over time [4,8,17,19]. Devices that can record from clinically relevant contacts during stimulation, across a broad frequency range, while preserving raw waveform information are likely to provide the strongest foundation for future adaptive therapy [4,17,19].

Further progress will require better biomarkers, broader and cleaner sensing, more capable onboard computation, and a deeper mechanistic understanding of how chronic stimulation reshapes neural circuits [3,4,18,19,93]. Open-loop and network-targeted strategies will remain important, especially in generalized or multifocal disorders, but the broader direction of the field points toward systems that measure brain state continuously and adjust therapy accordingly [4,6,7,10,63,64]. As that transition continues, the boundary between epilepsy neurostimulation and adaptive DBS is likely to become increasingly porous [4,17,19].
