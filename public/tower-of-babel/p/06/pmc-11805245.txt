Quality of Abstracts Reporting in Periodontal Randomized Controlled Trials: A Descriptive Analysis
Authors: Fahad Alharbi, Khalid Gufran, Muzammil M. Ahmed, Rawda O. Alghabban, Mohammad Meish Almohammadi, Abdullah S. Almutairi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe quality of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) abstracts greatly impacts the way readers perceive interventional outcomes, influencing their scientific applicability. Recent data about the quality of periodontal RCT abstracts that have been published is lacking. Thus, the aim of this study is to assess the reporting quality of abstracts in RCTs published in periodontal journals in recent years. The study is descriptive and cross-sectional, surveying the abstracts of RCTs published in the Journal of Periodontology (JOP), Journal of Clinical Periodontology (JOCP), and Journal of Periodontal Research (JOPR) between 2018 and 2022. The assessment of reporting quality was performed employing the CONSORT criteria for abstracts of RCTs. The quantitative values for each item on the CONSORT checklist for all published RCT abstracts were derived by descriptive statistical techniques, including univariate analysis. Comparisons were drawn between the journals and the different variables included in the study. The investigation identified 176 RCTs (11.7%) out of a total of 1506 articles published in these three journals over a period of four years. The primary observation of this research is that the mean quality scores were a mere 52.36 ± 9.9 for all the published periodontal RCTs abstracts. JOCP obtained the highest quality scores (54.7 ± 10.5), followed by JOPR (52.4 ± 9.9) and JOP (49.1 ± 7.9). RCT abstracts from Europe had higher reporting quality, whereas those from Africa were the lowest. The trials carried out in mixed settings showed better quality in the reporting of abstracts compared to those conducted in university, private, or governmental settings. RCTs with more than six authors and statisticians involved showed higher quality scores. These results are not indicative of a substantial improvement when compared to previous years (2015–2018). Abstracts of RCTs reported are below par in the preeminent periodontology journals. There has been no discernible progress over the years, notably in terms of blinding, the randomization description, and the disclosure of potential harms.

INTRODUCTION

Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) are a form of experimental study design wherein subjects are selected and allocated to study groups in an arbitrary manner with the purpose of investigating the prospective outcome of an intervention. The well-developed randomized controlled trials confirm the absence of bias and showcase minimal systematic errors. The Canadian Task Force on Periodic Health Examinations first described the hierarchy of evidence in 1979, which was later further expanded by D L Sackett in 1989, and both systems have assigned RCTs at the highest level of evidence in terms of potential bias.[12] RCTs are widely regarded as the gold standard for assessing the effectiveness of interventions.[3] Evidence-based practice, of which the findings of RCTs are a core component, must serve as the foundation for the provision of healthcare to patients.[4] RCTs are an indispensable part of periodontal research to validate the causal and outcome linkages. RCTs are valuable in periodontics for several purposes. They can be used to compare different interventions, appraise the effects of new therapies, establish preventative approaches, and recognize possible risk variables.[5] The discipline of periodontics is experiencing a steady rise in the number of published RCTs. The abstracts of these RCTs enable efficient screening of these studies, providing a broad comprehension of their content.

An abstract is a brief exposition of the purpose and outcomes of a research paper, providing key points and details from sections of the paper. Some of the most intense papers that participate in the greatest transfer of information are abstracts.[6] The glance-through of RCT abstracts allows readers to discern the research of their interest and its application in the area of clinical practice. Efficient evidence-based clinical practice demands that clinicians assimilate comprehensive information about RCTs and their implications in their clinical practice. In fact, this task is not a simple one; for instance, due to the dearth of time, the lack of access to full-text articles, and a need for expertise in critical appraisal skills, such circumstances do compel certain clinicians to rely solely on abstracts for their clinical decisions.[7] The abstracts of RCTs are employed for the purpose of screening and discerning the papers in the preliminary phases of the systematic review process. Thorough and precise reporting of RCT abstracts is of utmost importance, enabling the readers, especially the clinicians, to evaluate the methodologies, outcomes, and conclusions of the RCTs. Lamentable reporting in RCT abstracts diminishes quality and misinforms readers, while spin, a partial exposition of findings, may oftentimes mislead clinicians into embracing a clinical intervention despite its non-significant primary outcome.[8] There is a growing interest in the level of quality in reporting abstracts of RCTs in the field of periodontics. Undoubtedly, the reporting of abstracts of RCTs in periodontic journals suffers from suboptimal quality.[9] Certainly, a multitude of studies have indicated the dire need for enhancements in the quality of reporting of periodontal RCT abstracts.[1011]

In order to augment the quality of RCT abstract reporting, authors ought to abide by the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) for Abstracts guidelines, and journal editors are encouraged to establish unequivocal regulations to ameliorate authors’ adherence to these guidelines.[12] Given the significance of this matter and the lack of studies evaluating the quality of abstracts of RCTs in recent years, this study was undertaken. Thus, the aim of this study is to evaluate the standard of reporting of abstracts in randomized clinical trials that have recently been published in periodontal journals.

METHODS

The study is descriptive and cross-sectional in compliance with ethical norms. The stages listed in Alharbi et al.’s paper[9] served as the basis for the entire study methodology. The online archives of three highly regarded journals were selected, namely (1) the Journal of Periodontology (JOP), (2) the Journal of Periodontal Research (JOPR), and (3) the Journal of Clinical Periodontology (JOCP), in order to ascertain reports of randomized clinical trials published within the timeframe of January 2018 to June 2022. The title and abstract of papers were duly scrutinized for the presence of keywords such as “abstracts,” “randomized controlled trails,” “reporting quality,” or “periodontics” to acquire the complete text of articles. Only RCTs in periodontics were included in the study, while other types of research were excluded. The RCTs were thoroughly surveyed by two independent authors, and the outcomes were then collectively determined. The prospective RCTs underwent validation using a pre-tested extraction sheet. The CONSORT checklist and its corresponding explanations for reporting abstracts were utilized to assess all of the RCTs included in the analysis.[1314] An item received a score of “yes” if it was reported and a score of “no” otherwise. In certain instances, wherein the clinician’s masking was not possible, the item was awarded a score of ‘NA’ denoting its inapplicability.[9] Then, for each abstract, a total score was computed and transformed into a percentage employing the ensuing equation: total score= (total number of ’Yes’ / [19-total number of ’NA’ items]) / 100.[9]

Supplementary data on the abstracts was collected, encompassing details such as the number of authors involved, the geographical location of the first author, and the setting in which the trial was conducted.[9] The authors conducted a calibration process by evaluating a randomly selected 10% sample of the reports included in the study, with explicit reference to the CONSORT checklist, to ascertain intra-examiner reliability.[9]

Statistical analysis

The quantitative values for each item on the CONSORT checklist for all published RCTs abstracts were derived by descriptive statistical techniques. In order to ascertain the variables that are linked to the mean CONSORT score, a linear regression analysis employing a univariate approach was conducted utilizing SPSS version 28.00. Comparisons were drawn between the journals and the different variables included in the study.

DISCUSSION

RCTs hold a prominent level among the highest echelons in the hierarchy of scientific evidence, and their abstracts reflect their comprehensive scientific value to the acclaimed scientific community. In the context of academia, whether one is a seeker of knowledge, a scholar in research, or a connoisseur of literature, the initial act of looking through research papers commences with one’s eyes on the abstract. Likewise, when hundreds of scientific papers are sent to various prestigious journals, the editors encounter challenges in sifting through all of these papers, so they also prefer to have a thorough read of the abstract. If the abstract lacks finesse, failing to encapsulate the essential information in a methodical manner, it is likely that such research papers will be met with disinterest by the intended audience. In recent years, there has been a noticeable absence of studies that examine the extent to which abstracts of RCTs comply with the established reporting guidelines, which presents a research gap in the existing scientific literature. Thus, the present research is intended to verify the standard for reporting abstracts of RCTs published between January 2018 and June 2022 in high-impact periodontal journals.

The current study employs the CONSORT checklist to ascertain whether the abstracts of periodontal RCTs are compliant with its elements. Sarah Cuschieri contends in her research paper that CONSORT items serve as a fundamental framework for planning, analyzing, and interpreting RCTs and recommends that authors adhere to these items while preparing and publishing their manuscripts.[15] Three recognized periodontal journals were taken into consideration: the Journal of Periodontology (JOP), the Journal of Periodontal Research (JOPR), and the Journal of Clinical Periodontology (JOCP). The rationale for choosing these three journals lies in their indexing, remarkable impact factor, and high ranking in the journal citation report (JCR). In their analysis, Suiter et al.[16] consider these elements to be the most significant indicators that define the quality of a journal.

Our investigation identified 176 RCTs (11.7%) out of a total of 1506 articles published in these three journals over a period of four years, from January 2018 to June 2022. The current rate of RCTs being published is marginally higher compared to previous years. Al Mutairi et al.[4] reported that 9.4% (177 out of 1875) of the publications published in these journals between 2015 and 2018 were RCTs. The greatest number of RCTs are published in JOCP, followed by JOP and JOPR. It is worth noting that there is remarkable consistency between the findings of previous years and the present years in this regard.[4] When the pattern of publishing RCTs over the years is observed, there is a barely discernible disparity in the number of RCTs published, even in the years impacted by the COVID-19 pandemic. This contradicts the conclusive assertion made in the paper by Audisio et al.,[17] which stated that the pandemic had a considerable rate of cessation on non-COVID RCTs and a reduced number of newly started trials.

The primary observation of this research is that the quality scores, as evaluated by the CONSORT checklist, indicated a mere 52.49% for all the abstracts of RCTs published in the three periodontal journals from 2018 to 2022. These results are not indicative of a substantial improvement when compared to previous years (2015-2018), as evidenced in the previous study published by Alharbi et al.[9] The research of Xie et al.,[10] found that the abstracts of RCTs published in periodontal journals over a period of 10 years were of inferior quality, leading to a similar result. The systematic review performed by Montenegro et al.[18] found that the published periodontal RCTs, including their abstracts, do not fulfil the appropriate standards of quality.

When the publishing of RCTs across the continents is considered, it becomes apparent that Europe has consistently produced a substantial number of RCTs over the years, whereas the African continent has lagged in this regard. The finding is more comparable to the observation presented by Gluud et al.[19] that the European nations have exhibited an accelerating rise in the publication of randomized controlled trials over the past 60 years. Upon comparing the three periodontal journals, the JOCP, which is an official journal of the European Federation of Periodontology, demonstrated superior reporting of abstracts for RCTs. This observation aligns with the superior-quality reporting of RCT abstracts from Europe. Consistent with the findings of the present comparison period (2018-2022), the abstracts of RCTs published in JOCP in the preceding years (2015-2018) likewise demonstrated a consistent reporting quality, but unlike the recent years, previous years’ published Africa-based RCTs abstracts had the highest quality of reporting.[9] RCTs with more than six authors had better reporting quality in their abstracts. Scientific collaboration featuring a greater number of authors from diverse backgrounds has been found to enhance scientific productivity, including impact, novelty, and citation rates.[2021]

A notable finding from this research is that most of the RCTs are authored by academics at universities. Nowadays, there is immense pressure on university faculty members to publish research papers. This is because research publications have a crucial impact on faculty promotions, institutional and program accreditation, and university rankings. It remains uncertain whether the publication of RCTs in academic settings by academics has any correlation with their quality. This is because none of the previously published studies have evaluated the influence of this metric on the quality of RCTs and their abstracts, as well as their reporting. The present study considered this parameter as a characteristic of RCTs and figured out whether it affects the quality of RCTs and abstracts. RCTs carried out in mixed settings had better quality in the reporting of their abstracts, as seen by higher mean scores compared to those conducted solely in private, university, or governmental settings. The research conducted in academic settings yielded the lowest mean CONSORT scores. This is in line with the statement of Rawat et al.,[22] in their editorial, that the pressure exerted by administrators and universities on faculty members to publish research papers will result in a decline in the quality of publications and the adoption of dubious approaches. Statistical and methodological factors are crucial for assessing the reliability and validity of RCTs and minimizing possible biases during their implementation and reporting.[23] The collaborative operations between the clinical trial team and statistician throughout the research process led to a highly successful and efficacious intervention, accompanied by the dissemination of reliable information.[24] These notions are supported by the noteworthy finding of present investigation that the inclusion of a statistician resulted in higher quality scores for the RCT abstracts when compared to those without their involvement.

In the current study, the adherence of RCTs abstracts to the items outlined in the COSORT checklist for abstracts was evaluated, as detailed in the paper by Hopewell et al.[14] Only 9 items demonstrated satisfactory compliance, whereas many items had a very low compliance ratio for the RCTs abstracts. Blinding was reported in not more than 2.3% of RCT abstracts with patient blinding being the least. The abstracts of RCTs published between 2015 and 2018, as assessed by Alharbi et al.[9] demonstrated better reporting of blinding compared to those analyzed in the present study, which focused on the years 2018 to 2022. The absence of blinding could pose a significant risk of bias, particularly performance bias, and can also result in overestimated treatment effects.[2526] Inadequate patient blinding may impact the varying attrition rate, patients’ reporting of interventional symptoms, placebo effects, and so forth, as noted by Hrobjartsson et al.[27]

The negative consequences (harms) and significant adverse effects are another component that has been poorly documented in the abstracts of the evaluated RCTs in the present study. This finding is consistent with the data from prior years spanning from 2015 to 2018.[9] Similar results were observed in the research of Shaqman et al.,[8] who evaluated the reporting quality of abstracts of RCTs focused on periodontal therapy and cardiovascular disease outcomes. Ioannidis et al.[28] emphasise the need for reporting harms in order to accurately interpret and optimally utilise the RCT outcomes. Despite being identified as randomized, the abstracts of the RCTs assessed in the current study failed to provide ample details on the randomization process. Regretfully, Faggion Jr et al.[12] reported a similar finding in their evaluated RCT abstracts. In his review paper, Brijesh Nair asserts that the purpose of the clinical trial design is to optimise and streamline the conduct of the clinical trial.[29] It is startling to discover that the trial design was reported by a low proportion (27.8%) of the RCTs abstracts in the present research, which is validated by Kumar et al.’s[30] results in their assessment of 198 periodontal RCTs abstracts.

The merits of this research lie in the incorporation of a substantial quantity of RCTs abstracts and a meticulous comparison of study results in relation to prior years. Yet, the findings of this research may not be broadly relevant to all abstracts of periodontology RCTs since it only included RCT abstracts from three specific publications. However, this minor limitation is inconsequential and does not overshadow the fact that the three journals considered in this research majorly impact the scientific productivity and application in the field of periodontology.

CONCLUSION

Abstracts of randomized controlled trials reported are below par in the preeminent periodontology journals. There has been no discernible progress over the years, notably in terms of blinding, the randomization description, and the disclosure of potential harms. It appears that there has been little effort from the authors, reviewers, and editors to enhance the quality of reporting the RCTs abstracts by strictly following the CONSORT guidelines. The study findings underscore the necessity of measures to address this issue so that the scientific community can make the most of RCTs abstracts and interventional outcomes.
