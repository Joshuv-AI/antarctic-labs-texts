The Complexity and Challenges of Developing Effective Epilepsy Therapies: Lessons to be Learned from Comparing Cenobamate, Carisbamate, and Padsevonil
Authors: Wolfgang Löscher, H Steve White, Pavel Klein
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMost of the about 30 clinically approved antiseizure medications (ASMs) have been discovered using animal seizure models. Thus, such models are commonly considered highly predictive of clinical antiseizure efficacy. However, this has recently been questioned by the apparent dichotomy in clinical antiseizure efficacy between the novel ASMs padsevonil and the alkyl-carbamate cenobamate, both of which were highly potent in a battery of acute and chronic rodent models. In this review, we analyze and compare the preclinical and clinical data for cenobamate and padsevonil, along with their proposed mechanisms of action, to identify differences between the two drugs that might have predicted the contrasting clinical outcome, as well as another alkyl-carbamate, carisbamate, which was highly effective in preclinical models but failed in clinical trials. While padsevonil was more potent than cenobamate and carisbamate in suppressing seizures in most acute and chronic animal models, an important difference was observed in the amygdala kindling model of focal epilepsy. Cenobamate suppressed both focal and secondary generalized convulsive seizures, whereas padsevonil was less effective against focal versus generalized seizures, and carisbamate was only partially effective in amygdala kindled rats. Padsevonil was developed in a target-based, rational drug design program that combined high affinity for synaptic vesicle glycoprotein 2 (SV2) with moderate affinity at the benzodiazepine binding site of the GABAA receptor, presuming that effects at these two targets would lead to a synergistic increase in antiseizure activity. However, it failed in human trials. In contrast, cenobamate, which was discovered by a chemocentric approach and phenotypic screening, exerted impressive clinical efficacy with a high rate of seizure freedom. The amygdala kindled rat was the only preclinical model that correctly predicted the successful and unsuccessful outcomes of clinical development of cenobamate, carisbamate, and padsevonil. Hopefully, the present analysis of three ASM discovery programs with largely diverging clinical outcomes will help to better understand the complexity and challenges of ASM development.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40263-026-01284-x.

Key Points

There is a need to develop more effective antiseizure medications (ASMs) for drug-resistant focal epilepsy (DRE), a severe condition that affects roughly 30% of people with epilepsy.Here, we compare the development, preclinical, and clinical evaluation of three ASMs: one developed by a rational, target-based strategy and two (cenobamate, carisbamate) by a combination of chemocentric and phenotypic screening approaches.In clinical trials in patients with DRE, cenobamate exhibited impressive clinical efficacy with a high rate of seizure freedom, whereas carisbamate and padsevonil failed. This prompted us to analyze and compare the preclinical and clinical data for these drugs to identify differences that might have predicted their contrasting clinical outcomes.

Introduction

As in other areas of drug discovery [1, 2], novel antiseizure medications (ASMs) are discovered by different strategies, including phenotypic screening (in a target-agnostic assay that monitors phenotypic changes), chemocentric approaches (using compounds with known pharmacology as the starting point), and hypothesis-driven (target-based) drug-discovery programs initiated to develop a molecule with a novel mode of action [3–5]. Such strategies have led to the discovery of ~20 new ASMs between 1990 and 2018; however, the control of drug-resistant epilepsy (DRE) has not been improved significantly by any of these drugs [6, 7]. For this absence of progress in controlling DRE, many have blamed the animal models of seizures that are used in the early preclinical discovery and differentiation of novel ASMs [3, 6, 8, 9]. However, in 2019, the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved the novel ASM, cenobamate, for focal seizures in adults, a drug that was identified by phenotypic screening in animal seizure models and achieves 90–100% seizure reduction in 25–33% of patients with focal DRE, a response not observed previously with any other ASM [10]. The mechanisms of action (MOAs) of cenobamate were not known at the time of its preclinical development [11].

At about the same period over which cenobamate was discovered and developed by the South Korean company SK Biopharmaceuticals and its US subsidiary, SK Life Science, the Belgian company UCB Pharma started a rational medicinal chemistry design program to develop a molecule that interacts with two proven therapeutic targets: synaptic vesicle glycoprotein 2 (SV2) and GABAA receptors [12]. This resulted in the development of padsevonil, a novel ASM that displays robust efficacy across validated seizure and epilepsy models, including those considered to represent DRE [13]. However, the pivotal clinical studies in patients with DRE did not provide data demonstrating robust efficacy of padsevonil required for market authorization, so UCB decided not to further pursue the development of padsevonil [14].

The apparent dichotomy in clinical efficacy between padsevonil and cenobamate in patients with DRE questions the predictability of seizure models used in the preclinical characterization of these drugs. This is an important issue because animal models of seizures and epilepsy are generally believed to possess a high predictive value, demonstrated by the numerous ASMs that were discovered by these models [4, 5, 15, 16]. Indeed, it has been argued that animal models with a similarly high predictive value do not exist for other CNS disorders [3, 4, 17]. In a recent commentary, J.A. French argued that cenobamate is an example that simple traditional seizure models, such as the maximal electroshock seizure (MES) test, which have been used for decades in ASM screening, may help us find a better drug, whereas new animal models of drug-resistant seizures as used in the development of padsevonil have not yet led to a breakthrough therapy [6]. This commentary prompted us to compare the preclinical data on cenobamate and padsevonil, showing that more recent animal models of drug-resistant seizures were also used in the preclinical evaluation of cenobamate and that the MES test was not critical in its development.

In this review, we analyze and compare the preclinical and clinical data for cenobamate and padsevonil, some of which have become available only recently. Furthermore, carisbamate, an alkyl-carbamate that structurally resembles cenobamate (Fig. 1) and was highly effective in preclinical models but failed in clinical trials, was added to the analysis. We aimed to identify preclinical differences between the three drugs that would have predicted their contrasting clinical outcomes. We hypothesized that—with a carefully chosen preclinical screening battery—successful and unsuccessful outcomes of clinical development can be predicted and achieved. Furthermore, we have compared the clinical trials performed with cenobamate, carisbamate, and padsevonil and their outcome measures. We hope that the lessons learned from the development of these three drugs will help to identify and develop novel, more efficacious ASMs for the treatment of DRE.

Carisbamate and Cenobamate: Alkyl-Carbamates Identified by Phenotypic Screening

As shown in Fig. 1, cenobamate is an alkyl-carbamate that structurally resembles previously developed ASMs such as the monocarbamates carisbamate and the dicarbamate felbamate [11]. Indeed, the South Korean company SK Biopharmaceuticals and its US subsidiary, SK Life Science, which discovered and developed the monocarbamate cenobamate (see next section), previously discovered the structurally similar monocarbamate carisbamate, which was in-licensed by Johnson & Johnson in the USA in 1998 and evaluated as a novel ASM.

As shown in Tables 1 and 2, carisbamate demonstrated potent and broad spectrum antiseizure activity in a variety of acute and chronic in vivo seizure models, including models of difficult-to-treat focal seizures such as the 6-Hz mouse model and hippocampal kindling [11]. However, carisbamate failed to demonstrate consistent efficacy across regulatory trials (see Sect. 2.4) and, therefore, the clinical program in DRE was discontinued by Johnson & Johnson.

The Discovery of Carisbamate and Cenobamate

Starting in 2001, cenobamate (YKP3089) was designed by Dr. Yong Moon Choi (SK Biopharmaceuticals), who previously conceived of and synthesized the alkyl-carbamates solriamfetol (YKP10A) and carisbamate (YKP509). Carisbamate ((2S)-2-chlorophenyl-2-hydroxyethyl carbamate; Fig. 1) was identified by phenotypic screening in 1994 by modifying the structure of the major felbamate metabolite 2-phenyl-2-hydroxy-1,3-propanediol dicarbamate [18], by including a chiral center and R, S, and R&S configurations.

The aim of the program that led to the discovery of cenobamate was to develop an alkyl-carbamate that retains the broad spectrum of antiseizure activities but is more effective than the previously developed alkyl-carbamates, such as carisbamate. Importantly, cenobamate differs from other anticonvulsant alkyl-carbamates by its tetrazole substituent (Fig. 1). As described by Dr. Choi (personal communication), the main frame of the cenobamate structure was based on a compound called YKP10A (solriamfetol; (2R)-2-amino-3-phenylpropyl carbamate), a wake-promoting agent that showed anticonvulsant activities in screening models such as the MES and the pentylenetetrazole (PTZ) seizure tests in mice and rats.

Cenobamate was also designed considering structural components of two felbamate analogs, which possessed anticonvulsant activity, i.e., YKP511 ((2R)-2-chlorophenyl-1,2-ethanediol dicarbamate) and carisbamate (YKP509). Next, a variety of amino functional groups were applied as a pharmacophore, such as acyclic and cyclic amine radicals. After analog synthesis and screening in the MES and PTZ tests, the tetrazole moiety proved to be the most effective group. Next, chiral chemistry (R, S, and R&S) was applied for about 50 tetrazole/triazole carbamate analogs or derivatives to select the optimum candidates on the basis of the efficacy in screening models (MES and PTZ) and safety in rodents.

The decision process to finally select cenobamate ((1R)-1-(2-chlorophenyl)-2-(tetrazol-2-yl) ethyl) carbamate; Fig. 1) included the tetrazole moiety as a pharmacophore and the results of testing in models of refractory seizures, including the lithium-pilocarpine model (see next section).

Preclinical Characterization of Cenobamate and Carisbamate

Following in-house testing of cenobamate in seizure models, the antiseizure properties of cenobamate were also characterized in detail by the US National Institute of Neurological Disorders and Stroke (NINDS) Epilepsy Therapy Screening Program (ETSP) in a wide array of acute and chronic seizure models [19–22].

As shown in Tables 1 and 2, cenobamate is effective in the various acute and chronic mouse and rat models used in the identification and differentiation phases of the ETSP at the time when this drug was evaluated in the program. In the MES, but not the PTZ tests, cenobamate was particularly potent when compared with already approved ASMs.

In addition to such standard models of generalized convulsive seizures, models that represent difficult-to-treat types of focal seizures, such as the 6-Hz mouse model of focal seizures and the rat hippocampal and amygdala kindling models of temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE), were used (Tables 1 and 2). Cenobamate and carisbamate were both effective in the 6-Hz model. In the hippocampal kindled rat model of focal epilepsy, cenobamate displayed time- and dose-dependent reduction in the Racine behavioral seizure score from stage 5 to stage 1 [20]. This effect is consistent with an effect on both focal seizures and secondarily generalized seizures. At the highest dose tested (i.e., 40 mg/kg, intraperitoneally [i.p.]), cenobamate was observed to decrease the seizure score from 5 to 0 and increase the afterdischarge threshold by >100%, as well as decrease the afterdischarge duration from ~85 s to <30 s within 15–30 min after systemic administration. As mentioned above, these effects are consistent with its ability to reduce both focal and generalized seizures in patients with epilepsy and were observed at doses that did not impair motor function (see Sect. 2.3). As shown in Table 2, carisbamate was also effective in hippocampal kindled rats with a median effective anticonvulsant dose (ED50) (22.5 mg/kg) close to the ED50 of carisbamate (16.4 mg/kg).

More recently, Melnick et al. [21] reported the effect of cenobamate in the amygdala kindling model (Table 2). From the dose-effect data shown, an ED50 of ~4.8 mg/kg i.p. can be calculated. At the highest dose (10 mg/kg) tested, cenobamate suppressed both focal and secondary generalized convulsive seizures. Cenobamate was markedly more potent in this model than carisbamate, which failed to achieve a 50% reduction of seizure severity at 40 mg/kg, i.e., a dose close to its median neurotoxic dose (TD50) (Table 2). In this regard, the amygdala kindling model clearly differentiated cenobamate from carisbamate and most of the other ASMs presented in Table 2 (see below).

Using the rotarod test to determine “minimal neurotoxicity” [23], the “protective index”, i.e., the ratio between the median neurotoxic dose (TD50) in the rotarod test and the median effective anticonvulsant dose (ED50) in either the 6-Hz mouse model (Table 1) or the kindled rat model (Table 2), was determined. The protective (or therapeutic) index is widely used in anticonvulsant drug screening as a simple measure to differentiate between antiseizure doses and doses inducing “minimal neurotoxicity” (e.g., muscle relaxation or motor impairment). Protective indices of cenobamate were sufficiently high to separate anticonvulsant from neurotoxic adverse effects and were clearly higher than respective protective indices of carisbamate (and the alkyl-carbamate felbamate).

In contrast to cenobamate (a R-enantiomer; Fig. 1), the S-enantiomer (YKP3090) and the racemate (YKP1983) were active only in models of generalized seizures in mice and rats, but not in the rat hippocampal kindling model [20].

Further differences were also observed between cenobamate and carisbamate. While cenobamate was effective in the rat PTZ model, carisbamate was not (Table 2). In the in-house experiments that were performed at SK, cenobamate was also effective in the lithium-pilocarpine model of status epilepticus (SE) and blocked the SE with an ED50 of 7 mg/kg i.p. [22]. Interestingly, a single injection of cenobamate prevented neurodegeneration in the hippocampus, while carisbamate was ineffective. However, when high doses of carisbamate (90 and 120 mg/kg) were injected at 1 and 9 h after SE onset and continued twice daily for 6 additional days, carisbamate treatment reduced SE severity; produced strong neuroprotection in the hippocampus, ventral cortices, thalamus, and amygdala; prevented mossy fiber sprouting in the dentate gyrus of the hippocampus; and delayed or suppressed the occurrence of spontaneous motor seizures [24].

These data indicate that both cenobamate and carisbamate possess antiseizure and neuroprotective activity in the lithium-pilocarpine rat model, but that cenobamate is more potent and effective. As described in Sect. 2.1, the high potency in the lithium-pilocarpine model was crucial for the decision process to finally select cenobamate for further development. However, the pilocarpine or lithium-pilocarpine models, albeit widely used as models of pharmacoresistant SE [25], are not routinely used (or validated) as models of DRE [26].

Clinical Evaluation of Cenobamate in Patients with Drug-Resistant Focal Epilepsy

On the basis of the promising profile of cenobamate in the ETSP, the drug underwent several clinical trials in participants with drug-resistant focal epilepsy [27–30]. In the smaller and shorter of the two pivotal phase 2 double-blind, randomized controlled trials (RCTs), 30/106 (28.3%) of cenobamate (200 mg/day)-treated and 9/102 (8.8%) of placebo-treated participants were seizure-free during the 6-week maintenance phase [27]. In the larger of the two pivotal RCTs with the standard 12-week maintenance phase, seizure freedom during the 12 weeks of maintenance treatment occurred in 20/95 (21%), 11/98 (11%), and 4/102 (4%) participants with 400, 200, and 100 mg/day cenobamate, respectively, compared with 1/102 (1%) with placebo [28].

In an open-label extension of this study, treatment continued for a median of 4.5 years [31]. Overall, 10% of participants (36/354) were seizure-free during the last 12 months of the study, and 24.3% (86/354) had >90% seizure frequency reduction.

Post hoc analysis of a subgroup of 240 patients monitored for efficacy in a large phase 3 safety study in 1340 patients with DRE [29] showed that 28/214 (13%) of treated patients achieved seizure freedom and 86/214 (40%) had ≥90% seizure frequency reduction during the maintenance treatment duration, which was a median 1.5 years [30].

For comparison, in pivotal RCT studies of other ASMs approved since 1990, seizure freedom rates were <3% for most ASMs, rarely 5%, and never >7% [10]. The pronounced difference in antiseizure efficacy of cenobamate versus previously approved ASMs is also illustrated by the 75% responder rates shown in Fig. 2. Thus, cenobamate is considered a breakthrough for patients with DRE.

As discussed in Sect. 4, the high potency and efficacy of cenobamate against both focal and generalized seizures in the amygdala kindling model (Table 2) seem to predict its high efficacy in patients with focal DRE. It should be noted that at the time of the ETSP testing of cenobamate, the ETSP did not yet contain models of spontaneous recurrent focal seizures, such as the intrahippocampal and intra-amygdala kainate mouse models or the kainate rat model [23]. Thus, it remains to be determined whether these models would also have predicted the unique clinical efficacy of cenobamate.

Clinical Evaluation of Carisbamate in Patients with Drug-Resistant Focal Epilepsy

On the basis of its potent and broad antiseizure effects in acute and chronic animal models of seizures and epilepsy and potential disease-modifying activity, carisbamate was considered one of the most promising novel ASMs in the early 2000s [19, 32–34].

Following several controlled clinical trials with carisbamate in patients with refractory focal epilepsy, in 2008, Johnson & Johnson received provisional approval from the US FDA to market carisbamate [11]. However, carisbamate failed to demonstrate consistent efficacy across regulatory add-on trials in patients with focal epilepsy [35–37].

In a phase 2 study in 537 patients with focal DRE [35], the percent reduction in focal seizure rate for dosages of 300, 800, and 1600 mg/day, but not 100 mg/day, differed significantly from placebo. However, only a few patients (zero to two in any group) were seizure-free (seizure rate of zero) during the last 8 weeks of double-blind treatment. In two identical phase 3 studies in 565 and 562 participants with focal DRE, which assessed dosages of 400 mg/day and 200 mg/day, the percentage reductions in seizure rate as well as the responder rates differed significantly from placebo only for 400 mg, and only in one of the two studies [36]. Seizure freedom rates (1–3%) were not different from placebo.

In another large phase 3 RCT with 547 participants, adjunctive carisbamate therapy did not differentiate in efficacy from placebo across the dose range (800 or 1200 mg/day) [37]. Seizure freedom occurred in 2% of patients on placebo (n = 4), 6% on carisbamate 800 mg/day (n = 10), and 5% on carisbamate 1200 mg/day (n = 9), which is well below the seizure freedom rates obtained with cenobamate.

Because of the inconsistent clinical effects, the New Drug Application and medical authorization application submissions of carisbamate were withdrawn in January 2010, and the clinical program in epilepsy was discontinued by Johnson & Johnson.

In 2012, SK-Biopharmaceuticals, the company that initially developed the drug, received an Orphan Drug designation from the US FDA for carisbmate in the management of infantile spasms (West syndrome). SK Life Science is currently performing clinical trials with carisbamate in Lennox–Gastaut syndrome [38–40].

Potential Mechanisms of Action of Cenobamate and Carisbamate

The potential MOAs of cenobamate were only discovered after its preclinical and clinical development [11]. Two MOAs emerge from the few published studies on cenobamate: (1) phenobarbital (PB)-like potentiation of synaptic and extrasynaptic GABAergic inhibition by a non-benzodiazepine (non-BDZ) mode of action, and (2) an inhibitory effect on the persistent sodium current (INaP) [11].

GABA is the most prevalent inhibitory neurotransmitter in the mammalian brain and plays an important role in the pathogenesis of epilepsy and the antiseizure effect of many ASMs [41]. Cenobamate acts as a positive allosteric modulator (PAM) of GABAA receptors in hippocampal neurons, with effects on both phasic and tonic inhibitory currents and on recombinant synaptic and extrasynaptic GABAA receptor isoforms, effects that were not antagonized by the BDZ antagonist flumazenil [42].

In a barbiturate-like effect, cenobamate has been reported to displace the binding of t-butylbicyclophosphorothionate (TBPS) to the picrotoxin site of the GABA-gated Cl− channel, whereas the binding of GABA, muscimol, flunitrazepam, and Ro-15-1788 (flumazenil) to GABAA receptors was not inhibited.

Thus, the effect of cenobamate on GABAA receptors resembles that of other alkyl-carbamates and PB [11]. GABAergic drugs are particularly effective in almost all animal models of drug-resistant focal seizures [26]; thus, this MOA certainly contributes to the favorable preclinical and clinical efficacy of cenobamate.

Concerning voltage-gated Na+ channels, cenobamate inhibits the noninactivating INaP more potently than the transient Na+ current (INaT) [43], which likely contributes to the ability of this drug to suppress sustained depolarizations while sparing single action potentials and low-frequency firing. In addition, cenobamate was shown to enhance the inactivated state of voltage-gated Na+ channels [43].

Under normal physiological conditions, depolarization of the neuronal cell membrane leads to a transient inward Na+ current (INaT) that rapidly inactivates. However, a small proportion of Na+ channels appear to undergo rare, late openings in response to depolarization and give rise to a Na+ current (INaP) that fails to inactivate and is therefore termed “persistent” [44]. Persistent Na+ currents, despite their small amplitude in comparison with transient currents, play a disproportionately large role in the regulation of neuronal excitability and repetitive firing capabilities [45]. Abundant evidence supports a contributing role for abnormally large INaP with the neuronal hyperexcitability associated with epilepsy [45, 46], and blockade of INaP is relevant to the pharmacology of several ASMs [44, 47]. Thus, cenobamate is not the only ASM that inhibits INaP (see below), but, to our knowledge, it is the only alkyl-carbamate for which an effect on INaP has been reported to date. Its ability to produce a substantial block of INaP, together with the clear separation in concentrations at which it blocks persistent (half maximal inhibitory concentration [IC50] = 53.1 μM) versus transient (IC50 > 500 μM) Na+ currents [43] may set it apart from most other ASMs that share this mechanism [44].

Cenobamate’s selectivity for the INaP over the INaT likely represents action on the Nav1.6 channel (the channel most traditionally associated with INaP) over the Nav1.1 channel and is a feature not shared by traditional ASMs such as phenytoin (PHT), carbamazepine (CBZ), or valproate [44]. The effect of cenobamate’s selective INaP block on the resting membrane potential is augmented by its positive allosteric modulation of GABAA receptor-mediated tonic currents to suppress the epileptiform activity of principal neurons [48].

Thus, it is likely that the combined (and perhaps synergistic) effects of cenobamate on GABA-mediated inhibition and on INaP, which occur at similar, clinically relevant concentrations, contribute to its broad antiseizure profile and impressive clinical efficacy [48, 49], but this mechanistic profile is not unique. It is also shared, at least in part, by TPM, which both blocks INaP and enhances GABA-mediated inhibition [44, 47, 50]. However, TPM has a different efficacy profile in preclinical models than cenobamate (Tables 1, 2). These differences may be explained by the additional cellular effects of TPM (i.e., on Ca2+ channels and ionotropic glutamate receptors) that are not, to our knowledge, observed with cenobamate [49], but perhaps also by differences in the degree of blocking INaP. Thus, cenobamate has been found to block both the noninactivating and the slowly inactivating current component of the INaP with an IC50 of 50–70 μM, while TPM is a mild blocker at clinically relevant concentrations in both step and ramp protocols, with an effect size that is limited to a maximum of −30%; thus, TPM only exerts a partial block of INaP [44]. At high concentrations, cenobamate also inhibits L-type calcium currents with a half maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of 350 μM but has little effect on potassium KV7.1 (IC50 1.3 mM) and KV11.1 (IC50 1.8 mM) channels [44]. Given the current dearth of mechanistic information, we cannot rule out the possibility of other, as yet unknown mechanisms that may critically contribute to the unique efficacy of cenobamate in patients with DRE.

Several potential MOAs have been proposed for carisbamate, including use-dependent block of Na+ channels, activation of presynaptic Cl− conduction leading to depression of excitatory neurotransmission, and inhibition of voltage-gated Ca2+ channels, but apparently, there is no effect on GABAergic transmission [11]. To our knowledge, detailed studies of carisbamate on synaptic versus extrasynaptic GABA responses are missing, as are studies on potential interaction with TBPS binding to the GABAA receptor and experiments with recombinant GABAA receptors. It is currently therefore not possible to conclude whether carisbamate has a similar or a distinct mechanistic profile from other alkyl-carbamates such as cenobamate. However, the apparent lack of a GABAergic effect of carisbamate is a potential explanation for the preclinical and clinical differences between the two alkyl-carbamates.

Padsevonil: The First Rationally Developed Antiseizure Medication with Two Mechanisms of Action

After developing the ASMs levetiracetam (LEV) and brivaracetam (BRV), which both act mainly by modulation of SV2A, UCB Pharma thought about the feasibility of designing a molecule with both presynaptic and postsynaptic activity via interaction with SV2 proteins and the GABAA receptor, respectively [12]. The rationale for targeting these proteins was based on observations in the early 2000s that LEV markedly potentiated the activity of ASMs acting via GABAergic transmission, notably BDZs, in several animal models, resulting in an improved efficacy/safety ratio [51]. Figure 3 illustrates the potentiation of several BDZs by a low subeffective dose (5.5 mg/kg) of LEV in the DBA/2 mouse audiogenic seizure model (for comparison, the ED50 of LEV in this model is 30 mg/kg; Table 1). BDZs act as PAMs of GABAA receptors by binding to the BDZ binding site of the GABAA receptor chloride ionophore complex [52]. As shown in Fig. 3, the full agonists at this site (clonazepam, diazepam, and chlordiazepoxide) and the partial agonist bretazenil were markedly potentiated by pretreatment with LEV, resulting in 11–23-fold increases in anticonvulsant potencies. A similar synergistic interaction between LEV and BDZs was reported for several other seizure and SE models [51]. Importantly, these combinations were not associated with exacerbation of side effects or pharmacokinetic interactions. The anticonvulsant potency of several drugs inhibiting sodium channels (i.e., CBZ or PHT) or calcium channels (flunarizine) was enhanced by LEV to a much lesser degree [51].

Preclinical Development of Padsevonil

The focus of the subsequent rational medicinal chemistry design program performed by UCB Pharma was to develop a single molecule that could target SV2 proteins with high affinity and the BDZ binding site of postsynaptic GABAA receptors with lower affinity [53]. Low-to-moderate affinity for this site, coupled with a partial agonist profile, could minimize the potential for sedation and respiratory adverse effects and the development of tolerance and dependence, phenomena known to occur with most BDZs [54, 55]. Lead optimization efforts led to the discovery of padsevonil, an imidazothiadiazole heterocycle coupled to a pyrrolidone moiety (Fig. 1).

Padsevonil’s affinity for recombinant human SV2A (pKi 8.5) is 2000-fold greater than that of LEV (pKi 5.2) and 80-fold greater than that of BRV (pKi 6.6), respectively [53]. Padsevonil’s interaction with SV2A differed from that of LEV and BRV, i.e., it exhibited slower binding kinetics with a dissociation half-life of 30 min compared with <0.5 min for LEV and BRV, translating into longer target occupancy. Unlike LEV and BRV, padsevonil also displayed high affinity for the SV2B and SV2C isoforms of SV2 (pKi 7.9 and 8.5, respectively). Like SV2A, the other two SV2 isoforms—SV2B and SV2C—are integral membrane glycoproteins that are present in secretory vesicles of neurons and endocrine cells [56]. The precise function of the SV2 proteins remains elusive; however, current evidence suggests that they play a role in vesicle exocytosis and, thus, the release of neurotransmitters [57, 58].

Recently, the cryo-electron microscopy structures of SV2A in complexes with drugs, including LEV, BRV, and padsevonil, have been reported, indicating a central drug-binding cavity that faces the vesicle interior [59]. Padsevonil differs from racetams such as LEV and BRV in that it occupies both the central cavity (the orthosteric site) and a secondary distinct ligand-binding site (the allosteric site), functionally precluding modulation of SV2A by allosteric modulators such as UCB1244283 [60].

At recombinant GABAA receptors, padsevonil displayed low-to-moderate affinity (pIC50 ≤ 6.1) for the BDZ site. In electrophysiological studies, its relative efficacy compared with the full-agonist reference drug zolpidem was 40%, indicating partial agonist properties [53, 61]. In in vivo receptor occupancy studies in mice, padsevonil exhibited SV2A occupancy at low ED50 (0.2 mg/kg) and BDZ site occupancy at higher doses (ED50 36 mg/kg), supporting in vitro results [53]. Profiling studies confirmed high selectivity of padsevonil for its targets, excluding off-target effects at a wide variety of ion channels, receptors, transporters, and enzymes. Overall, these observations confirm the unique target profile of padsevonil that should translate into combined presynaptic SV2 modulation of neurotransmitter release and postsynaptic potentiation of GABAergic inhibitory transmission.

In proof-of-concept (POC) experiments in the 6-Hz (44 mA) mouse model of difficult-to-treat focal seizures [13], the antiseizure effect of padsevonil was compared with that of LEV and diazepam alone, and with the combinations of LEV with diazepam at doses expected to provide similar occupancy at SV2A (35%) or the BDZ site (2%) (Fig. 4A). Concerning the occupancy data illustrated in Fig. 4A, it is important to note that BDZs such as diazepam exert their anticonvulsants effects at low (~5–15%) GABAA receptor occupancy [62–65]. However, in the case of partial agonists such as bretazenil, higher receptor occupancy (>60%) is needed for anticonvulsant effects [66]. In comparison, LEV and BRV require 80% SV2A occupancy for antiseizure activity in animal models [67]. Low-level occupancy was selected for the experiments illustrated in Fig. 4 to differentiate the activity of padsevonil [13]. Protection from 6-Hz seizures offered by padsevonil, even at only 35% SV2A occupancy, was almost 70% and significantly greater than that provided by LEV in combination with diazepam (Fig. 4B). These observations suggest that padsevonil’s antiseizure properties are due to a differentiated mode of action that provides greater protection than co-administration of an SV2A ligand and a BDZ. The interaction of padsevonil with SV2B and SV2C may contribute to the enhanced antiseizure effects. Unfortunately, it was not assessed whether a BDZ antagonist such as flumazenil would decrease the anticonvulsant efficacy of padsevonil. Without such an experiment, the POC data shown in Fig. 4 do not prove that 2% GABAA receptor occupancy with only partial agonism contributed to the antiseizure effect of padsevonil.

In contrast to cenobamate, padsevonil was not tested in the NINDS ETSP program; instead, all preclinical evaluation in seizure models was performed in-house by UCB Pharma. The only exception was the intrahippocampal kainate (IHK) mouse model, which was performed by SynapCell (Grenoble, France), which also performs drug testing in this model for the ETSP [23]. As shown in Table 1, in acute mouse models, padsevonil exerted potent, dose-dependent protection against seizures induced by PTZ, 6-Hz (44 mA), and audiogenic stimulation with ED50s ranging from 0.16–4.8 mg/kg i.p. In this respect, padsevonil was markedly more potent than clinically established ASMs and cenobamate. The only exception was the MES model, in which padsevonil was less potent (ED50 92.8 mg/kg) than cenobamate (9.8 mg/kg). However, in the early 1990s, the MES model failed to detect the anticonvulsant activity of LEV, whereas this drug was highly potent and effective in the rat amygdala kindling model of TLE [68], which correctly predicted the clinical efficacy of this benchmark ASM. Thus, low or absent antiseizure activity in the MES test does not necessarily predict low or absent clinical efficacy, which is also shown by the lack of efficacy of other clinically effective ASMs (e.g., vigabatrin, tiagabine) in the MES test [7].

While cenobamate was also tested in the lithium-pilocarpine SE model in rats (Sect. 2.2), padsevonil’s antiseizure effect was evaluated in the mouse pilocarpine model [13]. Padsevonil’s ED50 in this model was 0.19 mg/kg, while the ED50 of LEV was 7.1 mg/kg, and BRV was ineffective, again demonstrating the high potency of padsevonil.

In the amygdala kindling model of TLE in mice, padsevonil suppressed secondarily generalized convulsive seizures (Racine stage 3–5) with an ED50 of 1.2 mg/kg (Table 1). Unfortunately, the ability of padsevonil to suppress focal (stage 1–2) seizures in kindled animals was not evaluated (or reported). When the afterdischarge duration (ADD) in the EEG recorded from the amygdala was used as an electrographic measure of both focal and secondary generalized seizures, padsevonil significantly reduced the ADD by ~60% only at the highest dose (13.9 mg/kg) tested, indicating a lack of efficacy against focal seizures in this model (see Sect. 4). These data would indicate that, at least in this model, padsevonil prevents seizure spread or generalization but does not block focal firing.

In mice with spontaneous recurrent focal seizures induced by intrahippocampal kainate, a widely used mouse model of mesial TLE, padsevonil suppressed focal hippocampal paroxysmal discharges (HPDs) with an ED50 of about 0.4 mg/kg. At the highest dose tested (4.33 mg/kg), HPDs were completely suppressed [13]. The TD50 of padsevonil in the rotarod test was 11.8 mg/kg, resulting in the highest protective index of all ASMs by far, including cenobamate, shown in Table 1.

As shown in Table 2, padsevonil was also highly effective in the rat amygdala kindling model (ED50 2.4 mg/kg) and in the Genetic Absence Epilepsy Rats from Strasbourg (GAERS) model of spontaneous spike-wave discharges (ED50 0.07 mg/kg). However, as in the amygdala kindling model in mice, only the ED50 for generalized convulsive and not focal seizures was determined in the rat version of the kindling model. Furthermore, no dose-dependent effect on ADD was observed, but ADD was reduced by only about 50% at 2.4, 4.3, and 13.9 mg/kg. As in mice, a high protective index (10.2) was seen in rats. Altogether, these results would suggest efficacy against generalized tonic–clonic but not focal seizures.

In additional experiments using the PTZ seizure threshold test, it was tested whether prolonged treatment with padsevonil leads to loss of anticonvulsant efficacy (i.e., tolerance) [13]. In contrast to diazepam, which is associated with a significant loss of efficacy, no such phenomenon was observed with padsevonil.

More recently, Quinlan et al. [69] reported that padsevonil suppresses PTZ-induced seizures in neonatal rats. While many ASMs, including PB and BDZs, induce neuronal cell death in neonatal rats [70], padsevonil did not. This suggests that padsevonil may offer therapeutic benefit and a favorable safety profile for the treatment of neonatal seizures [69].

Translation of SV2A and GABAA Receptor Target Engagement by Padsevonil from Mice to Humans

The advantage of the SV2A and GABAA receptor targets of padsevonil is that in vivo receptor occupancy studies can be performed in animals and humans, using highly selective 11C-labeled ligands and positron emission tomography (PET). The nonclinical studies described above showed that padsevonil is highly active in models of drug-resistant epilepsy at doses that lead to >95% SV2A occupancy and <20% occupancy on GABAA receptors. For translating SV2A and GABAA receptor target engagement from rodents to humans, UCB Pharma chose the ED50 of padsevonil (1.2 mg/kg) in the mouse amygdala kindling model, because, as described above, this model (in rats) had correctly predicted the clinical efficacy of LEV. As shown in Fig. 5, in vivo occupancy studies in mice showed that at this dose, padsevonil exhibited >90% SV2A occupancy and ~10% occupancy at GABAA receptors [71]. The differential proportionality of target engagement was retained in human volunteers as shown in PET studies following oral administration of 400 mg padsevonil (Fig. 5). Dose response experiments demonstrated that quasi full saturation at SV2A was already obtained at 100 mg of padsevonil, but simulations projected that SV2A occupancy would be >90% over the entire dosing interval for only 75% of a population receiving padsevonil 100 mg b.i.d. but for 90% of a population receiving padsevonil 300 or 400 mg b.i.d. [71]. On the basis of these data, a dose of 400 mg b.i.d. was chosen for the clinical phase 2a POC trial (see Sect. 3.3). PET studies with repeated administration of 400 mg in healthy volunteers showed that with this dosing schedule high (>90%) SV2A occupancy was sustained whereas the low (~13%) GABAA receptor occupancy was transient, in that at 2 h and 6 h post-dose, occupancy for the average of all regions of interest was ≤5% for most volunteers [71].

Clinical Evaluation of Padsevonil in Patients with Drug-Resistant Focal Epilepsy

UCB Pharma’s padsevonil drug development program goal was to make a dent in the treatment of DRE, and padsevonil was engineered specifically for that purpose [6]. For this reason, the phase 2a POC trial targeted patients with severe focal DRE: patients experienced a median of 10 seizures/week, and 75% patients had failed ≥8 ASMs [71]. The innovative POC trial design with maximal signal detection in a small patient population (n = 55) with a short treatment duration is illustrated in Fig. 6. Patients were randomized to receive placebo or padsevonil as an add-on to their stable ASM regimen. After a 3-week inpatient double-blind period, all patients received padsevonil during an 8-week outpatient open-label period. The novel high bar primary endpoint was ≥75% reduction in seizure frequency. As shown in Table 3 and Fig. 7, this primary outcome did not reach statistical significance compared with placebo. Three patients (11.1%) in the placebo group and eight (30.8%) in the padsevonil group experienced ≥75% reduction from baseline in seizure frequency during the inpatient period [odds ratio 4.14 (95% CI 0.90–19.6), P = 0.068]. The secondary outcome, median reduction in weekly focal seizure frequency from baseline during the 2-week inpatient period, was 53.7% in the padsevonil and 12.5% in the placebo groups, a difference of 34.0% (95% CI 3.0–67.5; unadjusted P = 0.026) (Table 3).

In a post hoc analysis, the incidence of all seizures (focal, generalized, and unclassified) at baseline and during treatment was assessed [72]. Almost half of the patients of the POC trial were concomitantly treated with LEV (n = 20) or BRV (n = 4), which may have affected the efficacy of padsevonil. Therefore, the “SV2A” and “non-SV2A” subgroups were separately analyzed (Table 3). Post hoc analysis of these subgroups indicated significant differences from placebo in the non-SV2A but not the SV2A subgroups. The ≥75% responder rate was 53.3% in the non-SV2A subgroup during the 2-week (only) double blind inpatient phase of the study and 0 in the SV2A subgroup (P = 0.003). Surprisingly, this outcome had not been anticipated, even though prior phase 2–3 RCTs of BRV had shown lesser, statistically nonsignificant efficacy of BRV in patients with LEV as a concomitant medication (see Sect. 3.4.2).

Following the POC trial, UCB started in parallel two large (phase 2b and phase 3) trials [14]. In the dose-finding phase 2b trial (EP0091; NCT03373383; ARISE) of 410 patients, the primary objectives were to characterize the dose–response relationship of padsevonil administered concomitantly with up to three ASMs for seizure reduction in patients with DRE and to evaluate the efficacy of padsevonil (50, 100, 200, 400 mg twice daily) compared with placebo. In the phase 3 efficacy trial (EP0092; NCT03739840; DUET), the primary objective was to evaluate the efficacy of padsevonil (100, 200, 400 mg b.i.d.) administered concomitantly with up to three ASMs compared with placebo for observable focal seizures in patients with DRE. The trial design and patient eligibility were the same for both trials, except for the target doses of padsevonil. Primary efficacy outcomes were the change in log-transformed observable focal seizure frequency from baseline and a 75% responder rate (≥75% reduction in observable focal seizure frequency from baseline) over the 12-week maintenance treatment period.

Both trials failed to reach statistical significance for any padsevonil dose group compared with placebo [14]. In the dose-finding phase 2b trial, numerical improvements were observed for the padsevonil groups compared with placebo (Table 4); however, these did not show a clear dose-dependent response. Seizure freedom during the maintenance treatment phase was achieved in 8.2% at 200 mg and 6.9% at 400 mg of padsevonil versus 0% treated with PBO, respectively, in the dose finding trial, comparable with other ASMs including BRV, lacosamide, and perampanel [10]. In the phase 3 trial, 2.3, 6.8, and 8.3% of patients on padsevonil 100 (n = 44), 200 (n = 44), and 400 mg b.i.d. (n = 36), respectively, versus 4.3% on placebo (n = 46) were seizure free [14].

Thus, the expectation that the rationally developed padsevonil with two synergistic MOAs and high preclinical potency would improve efficacy compared with available ASMs did not come true. Even worse, in patients on chronic treatment with LEV or BRV, padsevonil’s efficacy was clearly below the efficacy of add-on treatment with clinically established ASMs.

Potential Reasons for the Failure of Padsevonil

The unexpected failure of padsevonil to better the efficacy of other novel ASMs may have several reasons.

Most Animal Models of Seizures or Epilepsy Allow the Determination of Antiseizure Potency but not the Efficacy of Novel Compounds

As described in Sect. 3.1, padsevonil performed very well in animal models expected to differentiate a drug that would be effective in focal DRE, including intrahippocampal kainate, the amygdala kindled mouse, and the 6-Hz model, in some cases outperforming all existing ASMs. However, the typical approach of ASM testing in animal models primarily focuses on drug potency and not efficacy [73]. Thus, different investigational drugs are compared in terms of their antiseizure ED50s, i.e., the dose suppressing seizures in 50% of the animals, which is calculated from dose–response curves, testing one group of animals per dose. The lower the ED50, the more potent the drug, and high potency is often an important argument for selecting drugs for further development. However, it is the antiseizure efficacy that ultimately determines the clinical usefulness of a new ASM and should be considered during preclinical drug testing. The 6-Hz mouse model of focal seizures is one example of a model that allows determining both the potency and efficacy of drugs. At a high stimulation current (i.e., 44 mA), this test displays a degree of pharmacoresistance to the current standards of care and allows an investigator an opportunity to differentiate their investigational drug on the basis of potency and efficacy by varying the stimulation strength [74]. As shown in Table 1 (see more examples in the review by Löscher and White [26]), increasing the stimulation intensity of the test from 22 to 44 mA reduced the ED50 of most ASMs markedly, whereas the ED50 values of cenobamate and carisbamate were only modestly reduced. Padsevonil was only tested in the 44 mA version of the test, where it indicated high potency of the drug. However, depending on a drug’s MOA, the 6-Hz model may not reliably predict clinical efficacy against focal DRE [26]. Thus, additional models are needed to predict potential clinical efficacy.

The amygdala kindling model of TLE in rats was shown to possess a high predictive value of drug efficacy in patients with focal seizures and, in contrast to other models, correctly predicted the efficacy of LEV [26]. The sensitivity of focal seizures to drugs in this chronic model can be determined by evaluating (1) whether focal (Racine stage 1 and 2) seizures are suppressed by treatment, (2) whether the electroencephalogram (EEG) afterdischarges (recorded from the stimulated basolateral amygdala) that parallel the focal seizures are suppressed by treatment, or (3) whether the focal afterdischarge threshold (ADT) is increased by treatment. The ADT also allows determining the efficacy of the treatment (the higher the ADT increase the higher the efficacy). Unfortunately, UCB Pharma only determined padsevonil’s ED50 for suppression of secondarily generalized but not focal seizures in this model (Table 2). As shown in Table 2 and first described by Löscher et al. [75], focal seizures in amygdala kindled rats are more resistant to most ASMs than secondarily generalized convulsive seizures, which is consistent with clinical experience. Leclercq et al. [13] reported that the ADD, a measure of the duration of focal and secondarily generalized seizures in amygdala kindled rats, was not significantly reduced at 0.14 and 1.4 mg/kg of padsevonil and was only partially reduced by the same extent at 2.4, 4.3, and 13.9 mg/kg, possibly indicating that padsevonil did not suppress the focal seizures in this model. In amygdala kindled mice, ADD was partially reduced only at the highest dose (13.9 mg/kg) tested in this model [13]. In contrast, LEV and BRV block both focal and secondarily generalized seizures in amygdala-kindled rats with similar potency (Table 2) and increase ADT [76]. Thus, the lack of ability to suppress focal seizures in the amygdala kindling model may have predicted the low efficacy of padsevonil to suppress focal seizures in the clinical trials (see Sect. 4). The efficacy of cenobamate in the rat amygdala kindling model differs from that of padsevonil in that cenobamate was effective against both focal and generalized seizures [21].

Concomitant Treatment with Other SV2A Ligands Reduces the Antiseizure Efficacy of Padsevonil

As discussed above, the finding that padsevonil’s antiseizure efficacy was higher in patients who were not concomitantly treated with LEV or BRV came as a surprise to UCB. Owing to padesvonil’s high affinity for SV2A, and the high doses used in the clinical trials (beyond those needed to fully saturate SV2A binding), it was assumed that the compound would displace both LEV and BRV, but this was not proven directly (at least there are no data in the public domain). Similar to padsevonil, in the previous phase 2/3 add-on trials with BRV, which has 15- to 30-fold higher affinity for SV2A than LEV, concomitant treatment with LEV markedly reduced the efficacy of BRV [77]. Thus, UCB Pharma conducted an additional phase 3 BRV trial in which patients exposed to LEV within 90 days prior to enrollment were excluded from the trial. In this trial, the primary efficacy endpoints were met by BRV [77, 78].

The low or absent efficacy of BRV and padsevonil in patients on chronic treatment with other SV2A modulators can either be a result of competition at the SV2A protein or a general resistance to SV2A modulators, explaining that patients who do not respond to one SV2A modulator will also be resistant to other SV2A modulators. However, at least for BRV, the second scenario is not likely because patients with epilepsy who are resistant to LEV may benefit from switching to BRV [77–80]. Indeed, LEV and BRV bind to the SV2A protein at closely related sites but interact with these sites differently and recognize, induce, or stabilize different conformations of the SV2A protein, which may provide the molecular rationale for their distinct pharmacodynamic properties [76, 81–83]. Similarly, padsevonil’s interaction with SV2A differed from that of LEV and BRV both in its binding kinetics and its interaction with the allosteric modulator UCB1244283 [53].

It is possible that the in vitro and in vivo interactions between padsevonil, LEV, and BRV may have been evaluated preclinically, but such data are not in the public domain. Similarly, these interactions could have been determined by PET in humans before starting clinical trials.

Padsevonil’s Effect on GABAA Receptors Does not Contribute Significantly to its Efficacy

In the absence of evidence that a BDZ antagonist such as flumazenil reduces part of padsevonil’s anticonvulsant activity in seizure models, the data presented in Fig. 4 do not prove that the low-affinity partial agonistic effect of padsevonil at GABAA receptors critically contributes to its in vivo effects. As described in Sect. 3.1, while drugs that act as full agonists at the BDZ site of the GABAA receptor need only low receptor occupancy, this is different with partial agonists [65, 84]. For instance, the partial BDZ site agonist bretazenil, which was shown to be potentiated by LEV in the 6-Hz model (Fig. 3), needs >60% receptor occupancy for its anticonvulsant activity [66]. UCB Pharma designed padsevonil to exhibit only low BDZ site receptor occupancy at anticonvulsant doses to minimize the risk of adverse effects and the development of tolerance and dependence associated with BDZs [53]. However, the contribution of ~10% receptor occupancy at GABAA receptors to the overall antiseizure effect of padsevonil is questionable. Furthermore, as described in Sect. 3.2, the low GABAA receptor occupancy further decreased during treatment with padsevonil, so occupancy was ≤5% for most volunteers [71]. This result begs the question of whether there is some minimum level of occupancy at the GABAA receptor that is required for the marked efficacy seen in the animal models.

It is interesting to note that seletracetam, another potent SV2A modulator, exhibits similarly high anticonvulsant potency as padsevonil in several animal models, including the DBA/2 mouse audiogenic seizure model (ED50 of seletracetam 0.17 mg/kg versus 0.17 mg/kg for padsevonil) and the GAERS rat (0.15 versus 0.87 mg/kg), but is more potent than padsevonil in amygdala kindled rats, with efficacy against both focal and secondarily generalized seizures [76]. In contrast to padsevonil, seletracetam does not act on GABAA receptors but, similar to padsevonil, differs from LEV and BRV in its interaction with SV2A, including recognition pattern and binding kinetics [81, 85]. Furthermore, seletracetam is a potent inhibitor of high-voltage-activated Ca2+ currents [86], which likely contributes to its in vivo effects in seizure models and in patients with epilepsy [87].

Concerning the dual MOA of padsevonil, it is also important to note that BDZs are generally more effective against generalized than focal seizures [7]. For instance, in the amygdala kindling model of TLE, BDZs only suppress the secondarily generalized motor seizures but not the focal seizures [75]. Thus, even when the GABA component would have contributed to padsevonil’s antiseizure activities, it is unlikely to potentiate the effect of SV2A modulation on focal seizures.

The Severity of Focal Epilepsy Negatively Affected the Outcome of the Clinical Trials with Padsevonil

Patients enrolled in the phase 2b dose finding and the phase 3 efficacy trials had a high seizure frequency (median of 11.0 observable focal seizures per 28 days) at baseline (albeit much lower than the median 40 seizures/28 days in the phase 2a POC trial [71]), and many (52.1% in phase 2b and 38.5% in phase 3) had ≥8 lifetime ASMs, indicating that these were a highly drug-resistant patient population [14]. Rademacher et al. [14] suggested that it may be difficult to achieve a 75% responder rate in such a difficult-to-treat population. Indeed, in a post hoc analysis of a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled phase 3 trial evaluating BRV in 764 patients with focal seizures, Klein et al. [88] reported that in patients on BRV, 75% responder rates were 36.0, 21.2, 22.2, and 12.3% in patients with 1–2 (n = 75), 3–4 (n = 99), 5–6 (n =108), and ≥7 (n = 219) lifetime ASMs, respectively. Thus, the probability of patients achieving a sustained 75% response was generally higher in patients with a lower number of lifetime ASMs. Similar findings have been reported for other ASMs [89]. However, in the padsevonil trials, the difficult-to-treat epilepsy patients were intentionally enrolled because an improvement of >75% seemed feasible given the premise of the high efficacy of padsevonil based on preclinical animal data and human POC trial results [14].

In the pivotal phase 2 trials with cenobamate, the primary outcome measures were standard FDA and EMA requirements, namely median percentage reduction in frequency of all seizures per 28 days [27] or median percentage reduction in frequency of focal seizures per 28 days and ≥50% responder rates [28]. In the smaller and shorter of the two studies, the baseline median seizure frequency (5.5–7.5/28 days) was lower than in the padsevonil trials [27], but in the key pivotal study which was larger and longer, baseline seizure frequency ranged from 8.4–11/28 days in the cenobamate trial reported by Krauss et al. [28] similar to the padsevonil phase 2b and 3 RCTs. The number of lifetime ASMs was not reported in detail, but patients failed a median of three prior ASMs, with 30–40% of patients having failed >3 ASMs and ~21–33% of patients treated with three or more concomitant ASMs (SKL, data on file).

A subanalysis of the post hoc efficacy evaluation of the cenobamate pivotal phase 3 open-label study, which focused on patients who had previously failed resective, ablative, or disconnect surgery, showed that 35.2% of them achieved seizure freedom for >12 months, with a mean cenobamate dose of 329 mg/day [90]. This suggests that cenobamate’s efficacy applies to this most severe group of patients with focal DRE. In a long-term study of 49 participants enrolled in the three cenobamate pivotal studies at the Johns Hopkins Hospital site, with a median baseline seizure frequency of 15 seizures/28 days, 37/49 patients continued with treatment for ≥2 years, with a median exposure of 5.6 years. During their most recent 3-month treatment period, 45% of the original 49 participants achieved ≥75% seizure frequency reduction, 29% ≥90% seizure frequency reduction, and 16% were seizure free [91].

In more recent real-world studies on cenobamate, the drug was shown to be effective in patients with highly refractory and ultra-refractory focal epilepsy. The highly refractory patients were similar to the patient population included in the padsevonil trials (i.e., ≥8 lifetime ASMs), whereas ultra-refractory was defined as focal epilepsies in which ≥ 6 treatments, including ASM trials, epilepsy surgeries, and vagus nerve stimulators, failed to achieve seizure control. In 51 patients with a median of 10 (7–12) previously tried ASMs, a high (and significant) reduction in both seizure frequency and intensity was reported during add-on treatment with cenobamate; seizure freedom rates after 3 months were 8.7% (focal) and 36.4% (focal to bilateral tonic–clonic) [92]. In a study with 170 patients with a median number of 12 prior ASMs, cenobamate showed a high response regardless of prior and concomitant ASMs, with 13.3% seizure freedom at the time of last visit [93]. In 75 patients with ultra-refractory focal epilepsy, 47.4% of the cohort had a seizure reduction of at least >75% during cenobamate treatment [94]. In 24 patients with a median of 12 previously tried ASMs, 33.3% were seizure-free at the last visit, and the number of co-ASMs could be reduced by add-on cenobamate [95]. In a recent study with 32 patients with ultra-refractory focal epilepsy, 15.6% of the patients became seizure-free by add-on treatment with cenobamate [96]. Some clinical studies compared the efficacy of cenobamate in patients with ≤10 and >10 lifetime ASMs. Two studies did not find significant differences at 12 month follow-up [97, 98], while one study reported that seizure freedom was more frequently achieved in patients with <6 compared with those with >10 lifetime ASMs [99]. However, in the latter study, even in patients with highly refractory epilepsy (characterized by >10 prior ASMs, higher baseline seizure frequency, longer disease duration, and a greater likelihood of having undergone vagus nerve stimulation, deep brain stimulation, or previous epilepsy surgeries), there was 14 % seizure freedom with the addition of cenobamate. Overall, these data suggest that the efficacy of cenobamate versus the lack of efficacy of padsevonil in the RCTs was not due to a difference in the severity of focal DRE. Cenobamate is effective in patients with highly refractory and ultra-refractory focal epilepsy in which padsevonil failed.

The Targets of Padsevonil were Altered in Patients with Drug-Resistant Epilepsy

One prominent hypothesis of drug resistance is the target hypothesis that postulates that acquired (epilepsy-induced) alterations to the structure and/or functionality of brain targets of ASMs lead to a reduction in their sensitivity to treatment [100, 101]. Patients with drug-resistant focal epilepsy have been reported to have reduced SV2A gene and protein expression in affected brain regions, and this effect is recapitulated in animal models of TLE, including amygdala kindling [57, 58, 76]. Using PET with an SV2A tracer ([18F]UCB-H) in the rat kainate model of TLE, Serrano et al. [102] found a progressive decrease in SV2A expression during the development of epilepsy compared with age-matched controls in all examined brain regions, including the hippocampus. Similarly, using [11C]UCB-J PET in patients with TLE, Finnema et al. [103] found a reduced SV2A expression, which, however, was restricted to the seizure onset zone in the hippocampus. To our knowledge, it is not known whether epilepsy also affects the functionality of SV2A.

It would be highly interesting to evaluate how the decreased SV2A density in the rat kainate model affects the anticonvulsant effect of padsevonil. Chronic oral dosing with LEV reduced the frequency of spontaneous recurrent seizures (SRS) in this model by more than 50% in over half of the animals at clinically relevant plasma levels [104]. About 25% of the epileptic rats became seizure-free during chronic treatment with LEV (Fig. 8). Padsevonil is currently being evaluated in this model (see Sect. 5), and it will be important to compare its efficacy with that of LEV and cenobamate.

The expression of GABAA receptor subunits is substantially altered both in chronic animal models of TLE and patients with DRE, and this is associated with ASM resistance [101]. Thus, both alterations in SV2A and GABAA receptors could be involved in the low efficacy of padsevonil in patients with DRE.

The Rat Amygdala Kindling Model Predicted the Dichotomy in Clinical Efficacy Between Padsevonil, Carisbamate, and Cenobamate in Patients with Epilepsy

As described in Sects. 3.1 and 3.4.1, padsevonil did not suppress focal seizures in the amygdala kindling rat and mouse models of TLE, but only the secondarily generalized convulsive seizures. In contrast, cenobamate suppressed both focal and secondarily generalized seizures in the amygdala kindling model in rats [21]. Furthermore, cenobamate suppressed both focal and secondarily generalized seizures in hippocampal kindled rats [20]. Similar to our previous studies with LEV in the amygdala kindling model [68], cenobamate was evaluated in the hippocampal kindling model with two different protocols: at a suprathreshold current and at the individual ADT. At suprathreshold stimulation, cenobamate suppressed both focal and secondarily generalized seizures with an ED50 of 16.4 mg/kg (Table 2). In the ADT experiment, cenobamate (40 mg/kg) significantly increased the ADT and completely suppressed all focal and secondarily generalized seizures recorded at ADT [20]. The model characteristics and pharmacology of the amygdala and hippocampal rat kindling models are comparable [26, 49, 105]. Thus, on the basis of the correct prediction of clinical efficacy of LEV and many other ASMs characterized in kindling models [26, 49], these data would seem to predict that cenobamate is more effective than padsevonil in suppressing focal seizures. Furthermore, when compared with other ASMs (including carisbamate) shown in Table 2, cenobamate was the most potent (and effective) ASM in both the hippocampal and amygdala kindling models, which seems to predict the superior efficacy of this drug in patients with drug-resistant focal seizures.

Rodent Models with Spontaneous Recurrent Focal Seizures may Allow Differentiation in the Efficacy of Padsevonil, Carisbamate, and Cenobamate

As shown in Tables 1 and 2, the IHK mouse model of mesial TLE was the only model with chronic focal seizures for which data were available at the time of the present review. Padsevonil inhibited the electrographic HPDs in this model with an ED50 of about 0.4 mg/kg. At the highest dose tested (4.33 mg/kg), HPDs were completely suppressed [13]. Among various ASMs tested in this model, padsevonil was by far the most potent drug (Table 1), which, however, was a false-positive result when compared with the low clinical efficacy of this drug in patients with focal DRE (Table 5). In the IHK model, three types of seizures can be differentiated: (1) Electrographic HPDs, which only occur in the ipsilateral hippocampus adjacent to the kainate injection site; (2) electrographic high-voltage spike-waves (HVSWs), which occur bilaterally in the hippocampus and the cortex; and (3) secondarily generalized convulsive seizures [106, 107]. While HPDs and HVSWs occur frequently (up to ~60/h) and are mostly not associated with any observable clinical alterations, convulsive seizures occur infrequently and are typically not used as readouts in pharmacological experiments. It is a matter of debate whether the extremely frequent electrographic EEG alterations (i.e., the HPDs and HVSWs) are focal seizures that can be used as a model to differentiate novel ASMs or rather represent rhythmic EEG alterations that do not fulfill the characteristics of seizures [26, 107–109]. The data with padsevonil may indicate that the IHK model is not highly predictive for ASM efficacy in focal DRE, but a more complete analysis of all ASMs (including cenobamate) on the market and their effects in the IHK model is needed for a definite conclusion.

The high frequency of HPDs allows the use of the IHK model for single-dose drug testing, which explains its popularity. In contrast, in other models, in which spontaneous recurrent focal and secondarily generalized convulsive seizures develop after a chemically or electrically induced SE, the frequency of these seizures is much lower, thus necessitating prolonged daily drug administration and laborious 24/7 video-EEG monitoring [26]. Two of these models are currently used by the ETSP: the intraamygdala kainate (IAK) mouse model of TLE and a rat model in which SRS develop after systemic administration of kainate in rats [26]. In the IAK model, SRS are resistant to sodium channel modulators such as CBZ and PHT, whereas valproate, diazepam, and PB significantly attenuated SRS frequency relative to vehicle controls at doses devoid of observable adverse behavioral effects [110]. Only diazepam significantly increased seizure freedom [110], which is another example that rodent models of drug-resistant seizures are particularly sensitive to GABAergic drugs [26]. More recently, Mamad et al. [111] reported that chronic administration of LEV via the drinking water did not significantly decrease SRS frequency in the IAK model. However, most likely as a result of circadian variation in rodent drinking behavior and the short half-life (1.5 h) of LEV in mice, plasma levels of LEV were markedly below those that are determined during chronic drug administration in patients [112].

In the kainate rat model of TLE (see also Sect. 3.4.5), ASMs were delivered in rodent chow via an automated feeder system that ensured continuous drug delivery over 2-week periods [104]. Using a crossover design with consecutive ASM trials, chronic oral dosing with CBZ and LEV reduced seizure frequency by more than 50% in over half the animals at clinically relevant plasma levels, while lamotrigine was ineffective (Fig. 8). Seizure freedom rates were 29% with CBZ and 24–32% with LEV. The equivalent efficacy of CBZ and LEV in this model corresponds to the findings of a randomized monotherapy trial with head-to-head comparison of these drugs in patients with newly diagnosed epilepsy [113]. Thus, it will be highly relevant to compare the efficacy, including seizure freedom rate, with padsevonil versus cenobamate in the rat kainate and mouse IAK models of TLE.

Phenotypic Versus Target-Based Discovery of Novel ASMs

The discovery and development of innovative new medicines is extremely difficult, costly, and inefficient, and ASMs are no exception [2]. The story of cenobamate is an example of the usefulness of phenotypic screening with disease-relevant assays or models as a successful strategy to discover small-molecule, first-in-class medicines with high efficacy [2, 114–116]. Phenotypic drug discovery uses empirical, target-agnostic lead generation to identify pharmacologically active novel therapeutics that work through unprecedented drug mechanisms [2]. In contrast, target-based (hypothesis-driven) drug discovery is focused on a known target (or combination of drug targets) to identify compounds that modulate its expression, function, or activity. Targets are typically based on processes that are critically involved in the disease and are not affected by existing drugs. In this sense, padsevonil is an exception because its main targets, SV2A and the BDZ site of the GABAA receptor, are targets of clinically established ASMs, but the rational drug design to combine these targets in one drug is novel [117]. ASMs that were previously developed by target-based strategies include vigabatrin, tiagabine, perampanel, and ganaxolone, i.e., only a small fraction of all clinically available ASMs, most of which were developed by phenotypic screening approaches or structural variation of existing ASMs [3, 5]. Apart from any strategy, serendipity is one of the many factors that may critically contribute to drug discovery, with prominent examples such as valproate [118–121]. Indeed, serendipity (and luck) have played a pivotal role in the discovery of many drugs used today, and cenobamate is no exception because the chance to discover such an effective drug by a relatively simple phenotypic screening approach is very low.

May an Adequately Chosen Preclinical Screening Battery Predict Successful and Unsuccessful Outcomes of Clinical Development of Novel ASMs?

The present analysis of cenobamate, carisbamate, and padsevonil suggests that a battery of standardized acute and chronic animal models of both focal and generalized seizures, including the amygdala kindled rat model of focal DRE, should be used to reduce the risk of false-positive data when testing novel compounds for antiseizure effects. This view is substantiated by various previous examples of investigational drugs that failed in clinical epilepsy trials (see Online Resource 1). Importantly, amygdala kindled rats correctly predicted not only antiseizure efficacy (or lack thereof) of most investigational drugs but also severe CNS adverse effects that were not observed in nonkindled rats but led to unsuccessful outcomes in clinical trials (Online Resource 1).

When using the amygdala kindling model for testing antiseizure effects of investigational compounds, it is important to determine not only drug effects on secondarily generalized convulsive (stage 3–5) seizures (as often done) but also on the more refractory focal (stage 1–2) seizures [26]. Only then are kindled rats used as a model of focal DRE. As shown in Table 2 and first described by Löscher et al. [75], most ASMs are less effective in suppressing the focal than the generalized convulsive seizures in amygdala kindled rats, and some ASMs or investigational drugs (e.g., BDZs, lamotrigine, primidone, N-methyl-d-aspartate receptor antagonists) fail to suppress the focal seizures at all [26]. As discussed above, the lack of testing of padsevonil against focal kindled seizures may have been involved in the false positive outcome of the preclinical testing of this rationally designed drug.

A critical question is how many preclinical models are needed for the discovery and differentiation of novel ASMs for focal DRE. As outlined by Simonato et al. [122], in vivo testing of novel drugs should be performed in models mimicking relevant aspects of drug-refractory epilepsy. It has been questioned whether the acute, induced seizure models employed previously (i.e., MES and PTZ) will lead to identification of drugs more effective than those currently in use [8, 9, 122]. Furthermore, current benchmark ASMs such as LEV would have been missed by the MES and PTZ tests [76]. Thus, for initial screening, the MES test has been largely replaced by the 6-Hz mouse model of focal seizures, and the PTZ test has been abandoned in most discovery programs.

The 6-Hz model, which can be performed both in mice and rats [74, 123], should be performed at different current intensities (22, 32, 44 mA) to allow determining both antiseizure potency and efficacy of a drug as discussed in Sect. 3.4.1. Figure 9 illustrates this concept, demonstrating that cenobamate loses less antiseizure potency than other ASMs when increasing current intensity, which argues for high efficacy. However, carisbamate behaved similarly to cenobamate in this model, indicating that drug testing in the 6-Hz model alone does not reliably predict clinical efficacy.

As a second model, a kindling model, preferably amygdala kindled rats, should be used in the preclinical evaluation of novel ASMs. There are versions of this model with resistance to lamotrigine or PHT produced by pretreating the rats before testing novel ASMs, but even without any previous treatment, amygdala kindled rats are a predictive model of focal DRE [26].

As discussed in Sect. 5, it remains to be shown whether models of focal DRE with SRS, such as the mouse IHK and IAK models and rat kainate model, provide additional information in the preclinical evaluation of investigational ASMs that is critical for correctly predicting successful and unsuccessful outcomes of clinical development. As shown by a recent comparative analysis of animal models of drug-resistant focal DRE, the pharmacology of most SRS models is remarkably similar to the pharmacology of models with induced seizures, such as the 6-Hz mouse or amygdala kindled rats [26]. That said, it is not clear whether differences in seizure freedom in epilepsy models that display SRS exist between the three drugs discussed herein. This may be an important analysis given that seizure freedom is the ultimate outcome measure that matters most to the patient with epilepsy. However, continued pursuit of new screening tools beyond conventional “phenotypic” tools may not practically add much predictive value and are certainly resource consuming. A critical evaluation of the various models currently used by the ETSP may help to decide which models and outcome measures are essential.

Depending on the goals of a drug discovery program, animal models of generalized convulsive seizures (e.g., audiogenic seizure susceptible DBA/mice), absence seizures (e.g., GAERS), or genetic childhood epilepsies (e.g., Dravet mouse models) may be added to a preclinical screening battery. This would allow testing the spectrum of antiseizure effects of a novel ASM, but may or may not be necessary when the goal is to identify a novel treatment for focal and focal-to-bilateral generalized seizures.

Equally important as the choice of models are the preclinical parameters used to reach valid conclusions: the primary end point should be the therapeutic gain (i.e., efficacy, not potency). The therapeutic window should also be determined, as should the therapeutic (or protective) index, based on tolerability and safety measures. This will enable one to determine if a drug candidate may hold a potential therapeutic benefit [122]. Furthermore, appreciation of age and gender can be very important in projecting results in translational terms [124]. As shown by the present analysis of cenobamate, carisbamate, and padsevonil, an adequately chosen and conducted preclinical screening battery is likely to predict successful and unsuccessful outcomes of clinical development of novel ASMs. Furthermore, adding a comparator drug such as cenobamate to the evaluation protocol can also serve as an important benchmark for drug evaluation.

Conclusions

In this analysis of preclinical and clinical data on cenobamate, carisbamate, and padsevonil, we found differences in the preclinical profiling of the three drugs that may explain the obvious dichotomy in their clinical antiseizure efficacy. Importantly, UCB Pharma did not use the kindling models in mice and rats to assess padsevonil’s effect on focal seizures, whereas this was done for cenobamate and carisbamate during evaluation by the ETSP. Indeed, among the various models used, the amygdala kindling model was the only model that correctly predicted the marked difference in clinical efficacy of cenobamate, carisbamate, and padsevonil, provided that the focal and not the generalized convulsive seizures in this model were used as an endpoint. A limitation of this analysis is that the experiments were performed at different sites (ETSP versus UCB) with different protocols. The NINDS ETSP is currently performing a blinded face-to-face comparison of padsevonil and cenobamate in all acute and chronic animal models of the identification and differentiation phases of the program, including kindling models and models with spontaneous recurrent focal seizures, such as the IHK and IAK mouse models and the kainate rat model (Brian Klein, personal communication). We hope that the analysis of these data will provide further differences between padsevonil and cenobamate that would have predicted the contrasting clinical outcome. This comparison will also be important to validate the various models of the ETSP.

Secondly, drug–drug interactions may positively or negatively impact a new ASM’s efficacy. Cenobamate does not appear to be negatively impacted by concomitant ASMs [125, 126]. In contrast, LEV and BRV obviously reduced the antiseizure efficacy of padsevonil in clinical studies, and this had not been evaluated in animal studies prior to clinical development to better define the clinical trial, which represents a missed opportunity. The same does not apply to most other novel ASMs, including cenobamate, which do not interfere with the target engagement of currently used ASMs.

Third, the standard animal model outcome, the ED50, reflects a potential ASM’s potency, not efficacy, and the two should not be equated. Padsevonil’s potency in animal models outperformed most established ASMs, but the drug failed in clinical efficacy studies. It is a drug’s antiseizure efficacy that ultimately matters, and that needs to be studied.

While most recent ASM discovery programs have aimed at rationally designed drugs such as padsevonil, cenobamate is an example of the continued usefulness of phenotypic screening with disease-relevant assays or models, as a successful strategy to discover small-molecule, first-in-class medicines with high efficacy [2, 114–116].

Hopefully, the present analysis of three ASM discovery programs with largely diverging outcomes will help to better understand the complexity and challenges of ASM development. It remains to be shown whether animal models other than amygdala kindling would have predicted the unique efficacy of cenobamate in patients with DRE. The key may be the assessment of cenobamate-induced seizure freedom rates in epileptic rodents with spontaneous recurrent focal seizures, such as the mouse IAK [110] and rat kainate models of TLE [104]. Regardless, as noted above, the current approach to drug testing using animal models, albeit not perfect, does identify clinically effective ASMs.
