Neurotransmitter Imbalance in Cluster Headache: A Systematic Review of Mechanisms and Therapeutic Targets
Authors: Lanfranco Pellesi, Anas Mohammad, Wei Wang, Paolo Martelletti
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionCluster headache (CH) causes brief but extremely severe head pain, yet we still do not fully understand the molecules that trigger these attacks. Many studies have looked at changes in neurotransmitters and neuropeptides, but their results do not always agree. This systematic review brings together all the available data on neurochemical changes in CH, with the goal of clarifying current knowledge gaps and highlighting promising targets for future research.MethodsWe searched PubMed, Embase (Ovid), and Scopus for studies reporting quantitative measurements of neurotransmitters in human biological samples from individuals with CH, as well as provocation studies in which neurotransmitters or neurotransmitter-related compounds were administered to trigger attacks. Thirty-eight eligible studies were identified. For each, we extracted information on design, participant characteristics, sampling matrix (e.g., plasma, saliva, cerebrospinal fluid, platelets), and the neuromodulators assayed, and then synthesized the findings narratively.ResultsAltered levels of histamine and sensory neuropeptides such as calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) and substance P were documented, especially during active headache attacks. Conflicting data were reported for serotonin, whereas nitric oxide (NO), pituitary adenylate cyclase-activating peptide (PACAP), and vasoactive intestinal peptide (VIP) provoked CH attacks in experimental studies. Data on orexins and their receptors were inconclusive.ConclusionsThe evidence emphasize several potential therapeutic targets, including CGRP, substance P, histamine, VIP, and PACAP. However, interpretation is hampered by heterogeneity across studies and methodological limitations, particularly the large variance and uncertainties of CGRP assays, which make cross-study comparisons difficult. Further research is needed to elucidate the exact molecular mechanisms driving CH and to identify effective therapeutic interventions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40122-025-00778-8.

Key Summary Points

Cluster headache is associated with complex alterations in neurotransmitter levels, particularly involving sensory neuropeptides (calcitonin gene-related peptide [CGRP], substance P), parasympathetic peptides, nitric oxide, histamine, and serotonin.Pituitary adenylate cyclase-activating polypeptide (PACAP) and vasoactive intestinal polypeptide (VIP) are promising targets for future treatments, although clinical trial outcomes have been inconsistent or remain unavailable.Interpretation of CGRP and other neurotransmitter findings in CH is limited by major methodological challenges, including heterogeneous study designs and significant assay uncertainties, underlining the need for validated tools and harmonized endpoints.

Introduction

Cluster headache (CH) is a comparatively rare primary headache disorder that ranks among the most excruciating and disabling pain conditions known [1–3]. Its lifetime prevalence is estimated at roughly 0.1%. Although neuroimaging studies implicate the posterior hypothalamus and trigeminovascular pathways, the cascade that originates and sustains CH attacks remains partially defined. Emerging evidence suggests that a dysregulated neurochemical environment plays a pivotal role in modulating trigeminal nociception, cranial-autonomic outflow and circadian rhythmicity [4, 5]. Altered signaling has been documented for several mediators, including calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP), pituitary adenylate cyclase-activating polypeptide (PACAP), vasoactive intestinal peptide (VIP), serotonin (5-HT), hypocretin/orexin, and nitric-oxide-related pathways [6, 7]. Nevertheless, results across studies are heterogeneous, and the temporal relationship between biochemical changes and clinical phases (ictal, inter-bout, remission) remains uncertain. Clarifying these neurotransmitter and neuropeptide alterations could foster the development of diagnostic or prognostic biomarkers and help identify mechanism-based therapeutic targets [8]. This systematic review therefore critically appraises only human studies reporting quantitative levels of neurotransmitters (including neuropeptides acting as neurotransmitters) in CH, covering all disease phases and biofluids, while other biomarker classes (e.g., endocrine or paracrine mediators such as melatonin, cortisol, or endothelin) were deliberately excluded. By organizing the evidence according to neurotransmitter systems, we aim to map the consistency of biochemical alterations and evaluate their clinical relevance. A systematic review on CH biomarkers was published recently, with a broad spectrum of molecular markers surveyed rather than a focus on neurotransmitters [9]. In this review, we incorporate an additional database and apply no publication-year restrictions to provide a more comprehensive synthesis.

Methods

On 29 May 2025, we carried out a comprehensive literature search in PubMed, Embase via Ovid, and Scopus. Our search combined terms for CH, such as “cluster headache” and “cluster headaches,” with neurotransmitter-related keywords including “neurotransmitters,” “serotonin,” “glutamate,” and “dopamine.” Full details of the search strings appear in Supplementary Table 1. Titles and abstracts were independently reviewed by at least two authors. In case of disagreement, the record was discussed collegially until consensus was reached. Records deemed clearly irrelevant were excluded at this stage. Studies were advanced to full-text review if they reported quantification of neurotransmitter levels in human biological samples, involved participants diagnosed with CH and were available in English. We excluded reviews, editorials, conference abstracts, and oral presentations, and we hand-searched the reference lists of relevant narrative and systematic reviews as well as the bibliographies of included studies to ensure comprehensive coverage. Ultimately, only those articles reporting at least one neurotransmitter measurement in human samples from individuals with CH were retained. In addition, we included human provocation studies in which neurotransmitters or neurotransmitter-related compounds were administered to trigger CH attacks. We included investigations of any age group (children, adolescents, or adults) and both episodic and chronic forms of CH, noting any distinctions between subtypes or between ictal and interictal phases. Given the diversity of study designs, sample matrices, analytical techniques, and reported outcomes, we did not perform a quantitative risk-of-bias assessment; methodological limitations are instead addressed qualitatively in the Discussion. This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

Definition of Neurotransmitter

We regarded a molecule as a neurotransmitter if it is synthesized in neurons relevant to pain or autonomic control, stored in synaptic vesicles or dense‑core granules, released in an activity‑dependent fashion, acts on identifiable pre‑ or post‑synaptic receptors, and is inactivated by a specific clearance mechanism such as re‑uptake or enzymatic degradation. Under this definition we included the classical small‑molecule transmitters (5-HT, the catecholamines, acetylcholine, glutamate, histamine), the major peptidergic co‑transmitters implicated in cranial autonomic outflow (CGRP, PACAP, VIP, substance P, neuropeptide Y), and the endogenous opioid peptides (enkephalins, β‑endorphin, dynorphins), which fulfill the same criteria as other neuropeptides. We also considered orexins (also known as hypocretins), using the term orexin consistently throughout this review for clarity. When a well‑validated first‑pass metabolite provides a direct read‑out of synaptic turnover, such as 5‑hydroxyindoleacetic acid (5‑HIAA) for 5-HT, we accepted it as an indirect marker. Molecules whose principal mode of release is endocrine or paracrine rather than vesicular neuronal, including melatonin, cortisol, or endothelin, were considered outside this definition and therefore excluded from the systematic review.

Data Extraction

Data extraction was conducted independently by two authors, each of whom reviewed the full texts to collect information on study design, participant and sample characteristics, and the specific neurotransmitters assessed and their measured levels. Any discrepancies in extracted data were resolved through collegial discussion until consensus was reached. We confirmed that there were no overlapping or duplicate cohorts among the included studies. The template used for data extraction is provided in Supplementary Table 2, and all reference management including deduplication was handled using EndNote (version 21). Throughout the process, we followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines to ensure transparency and rigor [10].

Data Reporting

Because this review did not include a meta-analysis or pooled statistical comparisons, we did not perform additional statistical analyses. Instead, we have presented the data exactly as they appeared in the primary studies, whether expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), or median with interquartile range (IQ). We also retained the original units of measurement, the timing of sample collection, such as during CH attacks versus interictal periods, and the types of biological samples as described by the authors. In light of the heterogeneity in methodologies and outcome measures, we have synthesized the findings narratively to contextualize and compare results across studies.

Results

Our search produced 864 citations. After removing 178 duplicates, 686 records entered title- and abstract-level screening. Of these, 530 were set aside because they were reviews, protocols, guidelines, book chapters, conference abstracts, letters, editorials, or case reports. A further 55 papers were excluded for lacking any focus on CH, and an additional 55 articles were discarded because they did not investigate neurotransmitters. Full-text review of the remaining manuscripts led to seven more exclusions (six unavailable and one in German). Consequently, 38 studies met all eligibility criteria and were included in the qualitative synthesis (Fig. 1). Sensory Peptides

CGRP is a potent vasodilatory neuropeptide released by trigeminal sensory fibers and widely implicated in primary headache disorders. In the early 1990s, a study investigated salivary CGRP levels in 10 episodic CH sufferers during the cluster period, 5 episodic CH sufferers out of the cluster period, and 34 healthy subjects [11]. Patients with CH during the cluster period showed nearly twice the interictal salivary CGRP levels of healthy controls (mean ± SEM: 40.1 ± 2.3 pmol/L versus 21.2 ± 3.9 pmol/L) and that concentration rose further during an attack to 53.7 ± 5.2 pmol/L. In CH sufferers out of the cluster period, the CGRP level (mean ± SEM: 33.4 ± 7.7 pmol/L) was not significantly different from saliva sampled during intervals (40.1 ± 2.3 pmol/L). A mid-1990s study investigated the release of CGRP during spontaneous CH attacks in 13 patients with episodic CH [12]. CGRP concentrations in the external jugular vein increased to approximately three times the levels observed in age- and sex-matched controls (mean ± SEM: 110 ± 7 pmol/L versus 41 ± 6 pmol/L). Values returned to baseline after abortive treatment with high-flow oxygen or subcutaneous sumatriptan, but not after opioid analgesia. Cross-sectional sampling from the external jugular vein demonstrated higher plasma CGRP during active bouts than in remission in two independent cohorts: 19.4 ± 1.5 pmol/L (n = 18) versus 14.5 ± 1.6 pmol/L (n = 12), and 18.6 ± 1.9 pmol/L (n = 11) versus 13.1 ± 1.2 pmol/L (n = 8) [13, 14]. A randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover trial investigated whether a 20-min intravenous infusion of CGRP could provoke attacks in 32 adults with CH [15]. CGRP infusion provoked typical cluster-like pain in 8 of 9 episodic patients studied during an active bout (89%) and in 7 of 14 patients with chronic CH (50%), compared with only 1 of 9 and 0 of 14, respectively, after placebo. None of the nine episodic patients examined in remission developed CH after CGRP infusion. In the same study, baseline plasma CGRP was higher in episodic patients in remission than in chronic cases (mean ± SD: 100.6 ± 36.3 pmol/L versus 65.9 ± 30.5 pmol/L; p = 0.011) [16]. There was no attack-related increase in CGRP levels during CGRP-induced CH attacks. A prospective cohort study evaluated plasma CGRP from the external jugular vein during short-term steroid prophylaxis in nine patients with episodic CH during the active phase [17]. They received 3 days of intravenous methylprednisolone 1 g/day followed by an oral prednisone taper. In parallel to the reduction of headache frequency, CGRP levels (mean ± SEM at baseline: 21.81 ± 4.61 pg/mL) fell immediately after treatment with methylprednisolone (mean ± SEM at day 4: 15.80 ± 3.08 pg/mL), after weaning off prednisone (mean ± SEM at day 14: 15.42 ± 2.86 pg/mL and 1 week later (mean ± SEM at day 21: 17.72 ± 3.81 pg/mL). A cross-sectional study measured CGRP concentration in tear fluid and plasma of 16 episodic and 11 patients with chronic CH outside acute attacks and 60 healthy controls [18]. Patients with CH without abortive medication in the last 48 h showed significantly elevated tear fluid CGRP levels (mean ± SD: 1.78 ± 1.57 ng/mL, n = 17) compared with healthy controls (mean ± SD: 0.79 ± 0.74 ng/mL, p = 0.003) and compared with patients with CH who had used attack abortive medication in the last 48 h (mean ± SD: 0.84 ± 1.40 ng/mL, n = 10, p = 0.022). No significant difference in tear fluid CGRP levels were described between episodic (mean ± SD: 1.48 ± 1.34 ng/mL) and patients with chronic CH (mean ± SD: 2.21 ± 1.88 ng/mL, p = 0.364). Regarding plasma CGRP, there were no significant group differences. A large case–control study measured plasma CGRP from the antecubital vein in 201 patients with CH (100 episodic, 101 chronic) and 100 matched controls, with episodic cases sampled both in-bout and in remission [19]. Across the whole cohort, plasma CGRP was lower in every CH state than in controls, with episodic patients showing a clear intra-individual rise during an active bout relative to remission (mean difference: 17.1 pmol/L, p < 0.0001). CGRP levels in bout were not different from chronic CH. Substance P is another sensory neuropeptide that could drive the intense pain and cranial autonomic signs that characterize CH. A first study evaluated substance-P-like immunoreactivity in 18 hospitalized patients with CH (16 episodic and 2 chronic) and 35 healthy volunteers [20]. Significantly reduced substance P levels were found in plasma of patients with CH during a cluster headache attack (mean ± SEM: 79.6 ± 6.1 pg/mL) in comparison with controls (mean ± SEM: 113.7 ± 3.5 pg/mL, p < 0.001) and with patients with CH during an attack-free period (mean ± SEM: 101.7 ± 8.3 pg/mL; p < 0.05). A second study evaluated substance-P-like immunoreactivity salivary contents from 18 controls, 10 CH sufferers during both attack and interval, and 5 CH sufferers in the remission period [11]. During CH attacks, a significant rise was observed in substance-P-like immunoreactivity (mean ± SEM: 34.1 ± 1.9 pmol/L) from the values detected during intervals (mean ± SEM: 23.1 ± 3.1 pmol/L). No significant difference was found when comparing substance-P-like immunoreactivity levels among CH attacks, patients during headache-free intervals, and patients in the remission period (mean ± SEM: 35.5 ± 8.5 pmol/L). No significant difference was found between controls (mean ± SEM: 30.1 ± 5.9 pmol/L) and saliva collected from CH sufferers out of the cluster period. In a later study, blood was withdrawn from the external jugular vein ipsilateral to the pain during a spontaneous CH attack in 13 patients with episodic CH [12]. Substance P concentrations were comparable to those of age- and sex-matched healthy controls.

Endogenous Opioids

Enkephalins and endorphins are endogenous opioid pentapeptides that dampen nociceptive signaling, while enkephalinases are extracellular membrane-bound peptidases (e.g., neutral endopeptidase) that rapidly degrade enkephalins, thereby curbing their analgesic influence. A first study evaluated the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) levels of enkephalins in 28 idiopathic headache sufferers and 5 neurological patients used as controls [21]. The five patients with CH in remission had CSF enkephalin levels lower than the assay sensitivity (0.6 pmol/mL) and therefore defined as undetectable. A second study evaluated the enkephalinase activity in both plasma and CSF between 18 hospitalized patients with CH (16 episodic and 2 chronic) and 35 healthy volunteers [20]. Plasma values were significantly higher in patients with CH during attacks (mean ± SEM: 109.3 ± 17.4 pmol/mL/min; p < 0.02) and between attacks (mean ± SEM: 129.6 ± 21.7 pmol/mL/min; p < 0.02) than controls (64.4 ± 7.8 pmol/mL/min). CSF values were not statistically different between three patients with CH during attack-free periods (mean ± SEM: 1.89 ± 0.14 pmol/mL/min) and controls (1.99 ± 0.32 pmol/mL/min). A further study measured the cerebrospinal fluid levels of met-enkephalin and β-endorphin in seven male patients with CH (three chronic and four episodic) and five healthy male volunteers [18]. CSF met-enkephalin-like levels were significantly lower in patients with CH as compared with controls, whereas β-endorphin-like activity revealed no difference between controls and headache sufferers. Another study evaluated β-endorphin levels in the peripheral blood mononuclear cells of 65 patients with episodic CH (32 in remission and 33 in cluster period) and 50 healthy controls [23]. β-endorphin levels of patients with CH in remission (mean ± SEM: 13.53 ± 2.26 pg/106 cells) and in the cluster period (mean ± SEM: 14.35 ± 1.70 pg/106 cells) were significantly lower than those in healthy controls (39.73 ± 3.27 pg/106 cells). In a small randomized study of ten male patients with episodic CH, plasma met-enkephalin levels were measured before treatment and again after 10 days of prophylaxis with either verapamil (n = 5) or a tapering course of prednisone (n = 5) [24]. Verapamil had no effect on met-enkephalin (mean ± SD: baseline, 0.23 ± 0.05 pmol/mL; day 10, 0.22 ± 0.04 pmol/mL), whereas prednisone produced a significant rise from 0.29 ± 0.08 pmol/mL to 0.49 ± 0.10 pmol/mL (p < 0.05). A study evaluated the plasma β-endorphin secretory pattern in 12 adult male patients with CH [25]. During the cluster period, plasma β-endorphin circadian rhythm was absent and acrophase was delayed in patients with CH compared with that recorded in the remission period and in normal subjects. Overall, 18 CH attacks were recorded during the study day, 13 (72%) of which were characterized by a significant increase in β-endorphin levels.

Classical Neurotransmitters

Classical neurotransmitters include the biogenic amines histamine and 5-HT (with 5-HIAA as its turnover marker) and the amino-acid transmitters glutamate, aspartate, and glycine. Altered signaling in these systems can amplify nociceptive transmission and modulate the autonomic features of CH. Horton first postulated a histaminergic mechanism of CH after subcutaneous histamine triggered pain in 35 of 72 patients with CH and desensitization with repeated injections reportedly reduced attack frequency [26]. Subsequent work showed that 24-h urinary histamine excretion rose during the headache phase in four of eight patients with episodic CH but normalized in remission [27]. In another series, whole-blood histamine sampled immediately before, during, and after 22 CH attacks increased significantly in 19 episodes, with a mean increase around 20% [28]. Of these CH attacks, 6 headaches occurred spontaneously, 5 were induced by the ingestion of alcohol, and 11 by sublingual administration of nitroglycerin (1 mg). Another study reported an increased platelet-rich plasma histamine in five patients with CH during spontaneous attacks, compared with free periods (mean 65.0 versus 99.7 ng/mL) [29]. In a pharmacological provocation study, seven hospitalized patients with CH received intramuscular histamine (1 mg/mL), which precipitated an attack in every case [20]. Plasma substance-P‑like immunoreactivity declined from pre-challenge (mean ± SEM: 126.5 ± 9.5 pg/mL) to 92.7 ± 8.8 pg/mL during the pain phase and remained lower in the subsequent pain‑free interval (105.3 ± 9.5 pg/mL; both p < 0.05 versus baseline). By contrast, plasma enkephalinase activity, somatostatin‑like immunoreactivity, and cerebrospinal fluid substance-P-like immunoreactivity showed no significant changes at any time point. In an early serial-sampling study, plasma 5-HT was quantified before, during, and after 30 CH attacks [28]. Of these attacks, 13 were spontaneous, 5 precipitated by alcohol, and 12 triggered with 1 mg sublingual nitroglycerin. Mean plasma 5-HT concentrations remained unchanged across all three phases, irrespective of the provoking stimulus. A small longitudinal study that quantified platelet-bound 5-HT found an intra-individual rise from 92.6 ng per 105 platelets in the inter-bout phase to 112.4 ng per 105 platelets during attacks in five patients with CH [29]. A larger cross-sectional analysis revealed significantly lower whole-blood 5-HT in 33 patients with CH (27 during remission and 6 during an active bout) than in 50 healthy controls; mean values did not differ between those assessed in remission and those sampled during an active bout [30]. In a case–control study involving 19 patients with CH (16 episodic, 3 chronic) and 17 age- and sex-matched healthy controls, plasma concentrations of both 5-HT and its principal metabolite, 5-HIAA, were significantly higher in the patient group (5-HT: 5.7 ± 6.1 versus 0.2 ± 0.2 ng/mL, p = 0.02; 5-HIAA: 34.7 ± 46.1 versus 0.6 ± 0.7 ng/mL, p = 0.004) [31]. Platelet 5-HIAA content was lower in patients (3.2 ± 2.6 ng/108 platelets) than in controls (6.7 ± 4.8 ng/108 platelets, p = 0.04), whereas platelet serotonin levels did not differ between the two groups. In a smaller case–control series, 14 patients with CH studied during an active bout were compared with 57 healthy controls [32]; platelet concentrations of both 5-HT and 5-HIAA were significantly elevated in the patient group. Conversely, a case–control comparison of 23 patients with chronic CH with 16 healthy subjects found no significant differences in plasma 5-HT (mean ± SD: 0.35 ± 0.38 versus 0.22 ± 0.17 ng/mL) or 5-HIAA (6.46 ± 5.61 versus 5.78 ± 2.50 ng/mL) concentrations [33]. A recent study that focused on serum kynurenines and 5-HIAA reported significantly reduced 5-HIAA levels in 21 patients with CH (both episodic and chronic) compared with 35 age-matched controls (mean ± SD: 20.5 ± 8.4 versus 33.4 ± 13.7 ng/mL) [34]. A single study evaluated platelet levels of glutamate, aspartate, and glycine in 37 patients with CH during and between attacks and 17 control subjects [35]. Platelet glycine levels were significantly lower in patients with CH than controls, while glutamate and aspartate levels did not differ between the two groups.

Gaseous Neurotransmitters

Nitroglycerin is a well-characterized nitric-oxide (NO) donor whose NO burst excites the trigeminovascular network that links nociceptive afferents to the cranial autonomic outflow. The first provocation study showed that a single (1 mg) sublingual nitroglycerin dose elicited a typical CH attack in 28 patients examined during an active bout, whereas the same individuals were unresponsive once in remission [36]. A subsequent investigation confirmed the reproducibility of nitroglycerin-induced CH attacks: in 14 episodic patients: each provocation with sublingual nitroglycerin precipitated pain with a consistent latency of 30–65 min [37]. In a controlled biochemical study of 18 patients with episodic CH examined in bout, sublingual nitroglycerin originated attacks that were paralleled by an increase in CGRP concentrations from the external jugular vein, whereas plasma substance P remained unchanged [13]. Subcutaneous sumatriptan aborted the attacks and restored CGRP levels to baseline. Building on these observations, the same group enrolled 19 patients with episodic CH and showed that nitroglycerin-induced attacks produced an increase in CGRP concentrations from the external jugular vein at the pain peak, whereas concentrations remained stable during the pre‑pain latent period and at attack onset [14]. Subcutaneous sumatriptan aborted the provoked attacks and normalized CGRP, while individuals studied in remission neither developed pain nor exhibited CGRP fluctuations. In a cross-sectional study, plasma nitrites (the stable oxidative metabolites of NO) were quantified in 69 patients with episodic CH and 112 healthy controls [38]. Median nitrite concentrations were significantly higher in the patient group (40.9 μmol/L) than in controls (32.2 μmol/L), and this difference remained significant after stratifying patients by disease phase (active bout: n = 32; remission: n = 39).

Catecholamines

Catecholamines such as dopamine, noradrenaline (norepinephrine, NE), and adrenaline (epinephrine, E) are classical monoamine neurotransmitters that regulate trigeminovascular excitability and sympathetic outflow, factors thought to underlie the neuro-autonomic disturbances of CH. In a cross-sectional study of platelet catecholamine content, patients with CH had consistently lower mean stores of NE and E than healthy controls (controls: NE 3.45 ± 0.74 ng/1010 platelets, E 0.83 ± 0.75 ng/1010 platelets; n = 15) [39]. The reduction was similar across disease phases: in remission (NE 2.17 ± 0.50 ng/1010 platelets, E 0.28 ± 0.25 ng/1010 platelets; n = 25), in bout but interictal (NE 2.26 ± 0.61 ng/1010 platelets, E 0.22 ± 0.17 ng/1010 platelets; n = 16), and during an attack (NE 2.22 ± 0.90 ng/1010 platelets, E 0.19 ± 0.11 ng/1010 platelets; n = 11). In a small randomized cohort of ten men with episodic CH, investigators measured free and conjugated NE, E, and dopamine at baseline and again after 10 days of prophylaxis with either verapamil (n = 5) or a tapering prednisone regimen (n = 5) [24]. Free and conjugated plasma E levels were similar before and during verapamil treatment (mean ± SD, free: 55 ± 16 pg/mL and 50 ± 9 pg/mL; conjugated: 73 ± 19 pg/mL and 49 ± 14 pg/mL, respectively). Plasma levels of free and conjugated NE were also unchanged during verapamil treatment (mean ± SD, free: 150 ± 39 pg/mL and 118 ± 54 pg/mL; conjugated: 176 ± 47 pg/mL and 209 ± 51 pg/mL). Free plasma DA levels were not detectable in any of the subjects studied. Conjugated plasma DA levels were not significantly different before (1197 ± 329 pg/mL) and during (1123 ± 374 pg/mL) verapamil treatment. No significant differences in plasma levels of either free or conjugated catecholamines were found after prednisone treatment in the patients studied. A later study measured plasma and CSF levels of catecholamines in 12 patients with CH between the third and fourth week of the cluster period and 15 controls [40]. Levels of plasma E did not differ between patients with CH and controls (mean ± SD: 158.3 ± 23.7 versus 140.7 ± 23.1 nmol/mL), while the daily mean value of NE was significantly decreased in patients with CH than controls (223.2 ± 58.3 versus 328.8 ± 53.0 nmol/mL). CSF levels of NE were significantly decreased compared with controls (mean ± SD: 70.9 ± 38.3 versus 116.9 ± 41.1 nmol/mL), whereas no significant differences were observed for E (330.5 ± 122.5 versus 302.2 ± 77.7 nmol/mL) and dopamine (59.9 ± 30.5 versus 36.9 ± 23.5 nmol/mL). In a cross-sectional study of peripheral catecholamine storage, plasma dopamine was undetectable in most participants, so they focused on its platelet reservoir [41]. Platelet dopamine was significantly higher in 40 patients with CH during an active bout (mean ± SD: 0.29 ± 0.20 ng/108 platelets; n = 20) and in remission (0.39 ± 0.40 ng/108 platelets; n = 20) than in 21 matched healthy controls (0.10 ± 0.10 ng/108 platelets). In an overnight neuroendocrine‐profiling study, mean nocturnal plasma NE levels were virtually identical in nine patients with CH examined during remission (mean ± SD: 1.75 ± 0.63 nM) and nine age-matched healthy controls (1.55 ± 0.79 nM) [42]. In two cross‑sectional surveys of circulating catecholamines, 23 chronic patients with CH exhibited higher plasma levels of dopamine, NE, and E than 16 healthy controls (mean ± SD: dopamine, 10.9 ± 8.2 versus 1.9 ± 1.1; NE, 214 ± 110 ng/mL versus 72 ± 34 ng/mL; E, 21.6 ± 13.5 ng/mL versus 11.7 ± 2.0 ng/mL) [33, 43].

Parasympathetic Peptides

Parasympathetic neuropeptides including PACAP, VIP, and neuropeptide Y (NPY) act as neurotransmitters in cranial autonomic pathways, where they modulate vasodilation, lacrimation, and trigeminal excitability. In the first biochemical study of these mediators, ten patients with episodic CH were sampled during bout, and salivary VIP-like immunoreactivity rose from interictal basal values (mean ± SEM: 114.6 ± 17.9 pmol/L) to 174.8 ± 19.5 pmol/L at the pain peak, and was higher than in episodic patients tested out of bout (57 ± 7.2 pmol/L; n = 5) or in healthy controls (61.7 ± 14 pmol/L; n = 34) [11]. Four years later, blood samples from the external jugular vein were collected in 13 patients with episodic CH during spontaneous attacks and compared with age- and sex-matched controls [12]. VIP was markedly elevated in patients (mean ± SEM: 20 ± 3 pmol/L) versus controls (< 7 pmol/L), whereas NPY showed no between-group difference. An exploratory study evaluated plasma PACAP-like immunoreactivity from the peripheral cubital fossa during the ictal and inter-bout periods of nine patients with episodic CH and age-matched healthy controls [44]. Significantly lower plasma PACAP levels were detected in the inter-bout period of patients with episodic CH than in healthy controls (median and IQR: 24.4 and 2.68 versus 30.5 and 8.84 fmol/mL). In addition, PACAP was significantly elevated in the plasma during CH attacks as compared with the inter-bout phase in the same subjects (n = 5). In a randomized, double-blind, crossover trial, 14 patients with episodic CH studied in-bout, 15 patients with episodic CH in remission, and 15 patients with chronic CH each received a 20-min intravenous infusion of PACAP and VIP [45]. PACAP provoked cluster-like attacks in 6 of 14 in-bout episodic cases and in 7 of 15 chronic cases, while VIP elicited attacks in 5 of 14 and 7 of 15, respectively. Neither peptide triggered pain in patients with episodic CH examined during remission. Baseline plasma concentrations of CGRP, tryptase, and histamine were comparable across groups, and none of these biomarkers changed during peptide-induced attacks relative to attack-free periods [46].

Orexins

Orexins (hypocretins A and B) are hypothalamic neuropeptides that couple arousal circuitry to autonomic and nociceptive pathways. Dysregulated orexin signaling through altered peptide levels or receptor sensitivity could explain the attack sleep-linked onset and the characteristic sympathetic–parasympathetic imbalance seen in cluster headache. CSF levels of orexin A were evaluated in ten patients with CH during bout [47]. Orexin A levels were within the normal range (mean: 457.3 ± 105.0 pg/mL, range 304–639 pg/mL), with no clear associations between orexin A levels and the clinical features of CH. A second study measured CSF levels of orexin A in 12 patients with chronic CH, in 14 patients with episodic CH during an active bout, and in 27 healthy controls [48]. There were significantly lower orexin A levels both in patients with chronic CH (mean ± SD: 388.67 ± 59.65 pg/mL) and episodic CH (375.36 ± 38.24 pg/mL) compared with controls (430.96 ± 46.91 pg/mL). No significant relationship was found with other clinical features.

Neurotrophins

Brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and nerve growth factor (NGF) are neurotrophins that regulate synaptic plasticity and sensitization within trigeminal pathways. Aberrant elevations of either molecule can amplify nociceptive transmission and neurogenic inflammation. In a case–control study involving 14 patients with CH examined during an active bout and 57 healthy volunteers [32], platelet BDNF was significantly reduced in the patient group, whereas plasma BDNF did not differ between groups. Neither platelet nor plasma NGF showed any significant difference between patients and controls.

Discussion

The exact etiology of CH remains unclear, although both central and peripheral neural structures have been implicated in its pathophysiology [49–51]. This incomplete understanding results in the lack of targeted therapies and validated biomarkers, which complicates diagnosis, often leading to significant diagnostic delays [52–54]. Such delays have major clinical consequences, as many patients are misdiagnosed with migraine and therefore receive inappropriate acute and preventive therapies for years, further increasing the already very high disease burden of CH [8]. In this systematic review, we analyzed alterations in neurotransmitter and neuropeptide levels in patients with CH, highlighting the complex activity of trigeminovascular and autonomic pathways across different phases of the disease. Sensory neuropeptides, particularly CGRP and substance P, emerged as promising therapeutic targets, although clinical study results remain inconsistent and not fully understood. CGRP is one of the leading therapeutic candidates in CH, owing to its capacity to modulate trigeminal and vascular pathways involved in attack pathogenesis [55, 56]. Two studies have demonstrated significantly elevated levels of CGRP in saliva and blood samples collected during spontaneous CH attacks [11, 12]. Furthermore, human provocation studies have shown that an infusion of this peptide induces typical attacks in patients with episodic CH in the active phase, as well as in patients with chronic CH [13]. Nevertheless, clinical trials investigating anti-CGRP monoclonal antibodies have yielded unsatisfactory results. Among these antibodies, only galcanezumab has been approved in the USA for the prevention of episodic CH, while eptinezumab and fremanezumab failed to show positive results [57, 58]. Regarding chronic CH, negative results have been documented with erenumab and fremanezumab [59]. Galcanezumab is not currently approved by the European Medicines Agency (EMA) for the treatment of CH, but it may be used off-label in refractory cases at doses of 240–360 mg per month, supported by evidence from observational studies [60]. CGRP’s role in CH might be more complex than initially hypothesized. Leading experts have suggested that these disappointing outcomes may in part reflect the use of suboptimal endpoints in highly heterogeneous patient populations, where disease burden can vary dramatically between individuals (e.g., chronic patients with one weekly attack versus episodic patients experiencing up to eight daily attacks for months). The choice of primary outcome may not fully capture such variability, especially considering that many patients report shadow attacks without full clinical expression. These methodological issues complicate the interpretation of trial data. Nevertheless, it must be acknowledged that anti-CGRP therapy is not the “wonder drug” once hoped for in CH. A large case–control study reported lower plasma CGRP levels in both episodic and patients with chronic CH compared with healthy controls [19], suggesting that CGRP levels in patients with CH does not unequivocally support the use of anti-CGRP therapies. A recent methodological study has shown that some commercial kits used to measure CGRP may not reliably detect the mature, bioactive peptide [61]. It is possible that part of the inconsistency across studies reflects technical limitations rather than true biological variability. Future work on CGRP in CH should therefore rely on validated assays, ideally with well-defined specificity, to clarify its role as a biomarker. Another interesting sensory neuropeptide is substance P, which shows significantly elevated salivary levels during CH attacks [11]. However, conflicting data arise from a plasma study, reporting lower substance P levels during attacks compared with healthy controls and patients with episodic CH in remission [20]. To date, clinical studies specifically targeting substance P in cluster headache are lacking and further research is necessary to determine whether substance P represents a viable therapeutic target for the treatment of this condition [62]. Alterations in endogenous opioids have also been documented in patients with CH. Studies reported elevated plasma levels of β-endorphin during attacks, reduced β-endorphin concentrations in peripheral blood mononuclear cells during active cluster periods, and disruption of the normal circadian rhythm of plasma β-endorphin in patients with CH during a bout [23, 25]. Despite these observed alterations, their clinical relevance as therapeutic targets remains limited, especially considering that opioids show poor efficacy in the treatment of CH [8, 63]. Among classical neurotransmitters, histamine is historically known for its ability to provoke cluster headache attacks in patients during active cluster periods, but not during remission [26, 64]. Consistently, blood histamine levels are elevated during spontaneous CH episodes [28, 29]. Clinical trials using antagonists of histamine H1 and H2 receptors yielded negative outcomes, indicating that the exact mechanism underlying histamine-induced CH attacks remains unclear [65, 66]. It is possible that other receptors, including H3 and H4 subtypes, play a more defined role in CH [67]. 5-HT involvement in CH has also been investigated, though results regarding its plasma and platelet levels have been inconsistent across studies [28–33]. The strong clinical efficacy of sumatriptan as an acute treatment through 5-HT1B and 5-HT1D receptors underscores the role that 5-HT and its receptors play in CH mechanisms [68]. Further studies are warranted to explore serotonergic pathways as potential targets for preventive therapies in CH. NO is another important neurotransmitter that has been implicated in CH, primarily through provocation studies demonstrating its ability to induce attacks [36, 37]. The action of NO in CH appears to involve CGRP release [13, 14]; however, as previously noted, targeting CGRP has yielded mixed clinical outcomes. Therefore, a more promising approach could involve direct targeting of NO or the enzymes responsible for its synthesis. Recent research has focused on PACAP and VIP as potential therapeutic targets for primary headache disorders [69, 70]. Elevated blood levels of both PACAP and VIP have been observed during CH attacks [12, 44], and administration of either peptide can provoke attacks in approximately half of episodic patients during an active bout, as well as in chronic patients [45]. Of note, these peptide-induced attacks do not appear to be mediated by CGRP, suggesting independent pathogenic pathways [46]. CH and migraine share certain pathophysiological mechanisms and the recent demonstration of efficacy for an anti-PACAP antibody in migraine prevention suggests the hypothesis that PACAP-targeted therapies could be effective in CH [71, 72]. Regarding orexins, the available data on their involvement in CH remains limited [47, 48]. A few genetic studies have explored the possible association between CH and polymorphisms in orexin-related genes. A meta-analysis including 593 patients with CH and 599 controls from three European cohorts suggested that the G1246A polymorphism (rs2653349) in the hypocretin receptor-2 (HCRTR2) gene might influence the risk of developing cluster headache [73]. However, the largest population-based study, conducted in 2015 on 575 patients with CH and 874 controls, failed to replicate this association [74]. The same authors reported a positive association in a broader meta-analysis of six previously published studies, highlighting the complexity and potential variability among populations [74]. A study conducted on Chinese patients (112 patients with CH and 192 controls) found no significant association between orexin gene polymorphisms and CH [75]. Given these conflicting results, the genetic association between orexin-related pathways and CH remains uncertain. A CSF study based on a relatively large sample size has shown lower orexin A concentrations in patients with CH compared with controls, a finding that fits with the hypothalamic theory of CH [48]. Recently, daridorexant, an antagonist of both orexin receptor type 1 and type 2, has been developed for the treatment of insomnia, but it is currently unclear whether it has any clinical relevance or therapeutic benefit for patients with CH. Future studies investigating this compound in the context of CH would be warranted.

Conclusions

This systematic review underscores the complexity and variability of neuropeptide alterations in CH. Sensory and parasympathetic peptides such as CGRP, PACAP, and VIP represent promising therapeutic targets, but the results from clinical trials are missing or remain inconclusive, reflecting an incomplete understanding of CH pathophysiology. Interpretation of available data is hampered by substantial heterogeneity across studies in terms of sampling methods. In particular, the large variance and methodological uncertainties of CGRP assays make cross-study comparisons difficult and raise questions about the reliability of some reported findings. Clinical trials with anti-CGRP monoclonal antibodies may have been affected by suboptimal endpoints that fail to capture the highly heterogeneous burden of disease, which differs dramatically across patients. Addressing these gaps will require validated and specific assays, and carefully chosen endpoints that reflect the true impact of the disorder. Clarifying neurotransmitter pathways and overcoming current methodological limitations will be crucial for developing effective, targeted treatments, ultimately improving management strategies and outcomes for patients with CH.
