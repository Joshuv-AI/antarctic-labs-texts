Recurrent Granulomatous Mastitis in the Contralateral Breast Following Radical Mastectomy for Breast Cancer: A Case Report and Literature Review
Authors: Jie Chen, Bo Zhang, Tongtong Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGranulomatous mastitis (GM) is a rare, chronic, benign inflammatory breast disease with an unknown etiology, primarily affecting young women. It is often characterized by the presence of a painful mass, skin changes, and persistent inflammation, which can clinically resemble breast cancer. Due to the absence of specific imaging signatures and a poorly understood pathogenic mechanism, diagnosis is primarily achieved through exclusion. We present the case of a 31-year-old woman who, three years after undergoing a radical mastectomy for triple-negative breast cancer in her right breast, was diagnosed with GM in her left breast. The diagnosis was based on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), conventional and contrast-enhanced ultrasound, and confirmed through histological analysis. The patient has experienced recurrent episodes of GM over the past three years. Notably, this case represents the first reported instance of granulomatous mastitis occurring in the contralateral breast following a radical mastectomy for breast cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12905-025-03971-1.Keywords: Granulomatous mastitis, Breast cancer, Triple-negative breast cancer

Introduction

Granulomatous mastitis (GM) is a rare, benign, chronic inflammatory breast condition characterized by lobulocentric granulomas, primarily affecting young women with a history of lactation [1]. The most common clinical manifestations include breast pain, masses, hyperemia, and inflammation [2]. The etiology of GM remains unclear, making both diagnosis and treatment challenging. The pathogenic mechanism is believed to involve a combination of infectious, autoimmune, hormonal, and possibly traumatic factors [2, 3]. In this report, we present a case of refractory GM in the contralateral breast of a patient following radical breast surgery for breast cancer.

Case presentation

A 28-year-old female presented with a palpable breast mass. Diagnostic imaging and biopsy confirmed a diagnosis of invasive breast cancer. The patient subsequently underwent a right mastectomy and right axillary lymph node dissection. Postoperative pathology revealed invasive breast cancer (Histological score: 3 + 3 + 3 = 9 points, Grade III) (Fig. 1). The tumor was triple-negative, lacking expression of human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER-2), progesterone receptor (PR), and estrogen receptor (ER). Histological examination of the axillary lymph nodes identified metastatic involvement in one out of twenty nodes. Following surgery, the patient underwent a systemic course of postoperative chemotherapy according to protocol, including intravenous cyclophosphamide combined with epirubicin hydrochloride and followed by anthracycline-based agents.

Three years later, a mass was detected in the patient’s left breast, accompanied by significant premenstrual distention and pain, but without local redness or swelling. Mammography indicated that the left breast exhibited heterogeneous density with uneven distribution and patchy areas of high density; no definite masses or malignant calcifications were observed (Fig. 2). Breast magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed multiple hyperintense T2 signal lesions within the left breast tissue, while right breast represented changes after right breast resection. After the injection of Gd-DTPA, multiple nodules and patchy enhancements were observed in the lower quadrant of the left breast, with a washout-type enhancement curve (Fig. 3). Routine ultrasound identified a patchy hypoechoic area beneath the left nipple, measuring approximately 8.3 × 1.0 cm, with an irregular shape, clear boundaries, partial ductal distribution, and uneven internal echoes. Color Doppler flow imaging detected abundant strip-like blood flow signals at the periphery. Contrast-enhanced ultrasound suggested rapid, high enhancement during the arterial phase, with relatively even distribution. The overall size of the nodule appeared slightly larger compared to conventional ultrasound, and synchronous decrease was observed during the venous phase, indicating persistent high enhancement (Fig. 4), with small, non-enhanced areas visible, measuring approximately 1.1 × 0.2 cm. The patient subsequently underwent resection of the left breast lesion, and pathology confirmed granulomatous mastitis with necrosis.

Six years after undergoing radical breast surgery, the patient experienced recurrent episodes of chronic inflammation in the left breast, eventually leading to another resection of the left breast mass. Postoperative pathology revealed inflammatory exudate, granulation tissue, and both acute and chronic inflammation of the breast tissue, accompanied by abscess formation and a multinucleated giant cell reaction (Fig. 5). Follow-up ultrasound indicated multiple hypoechoic areas in the left breast, with evidence of sinus tract formation (Fig. 6).

Discussion

Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), characterized by the lack of expression of ER, PR, HER2, represents the most aggressive and lethal subtype of breast cancer [4]. TNBC is known for its aggressive behavior, poor prognosis, and limited treatment options, making it a significant clinical challenge. The local recurrence rate after standard therapy in early-stage TNBC can be as high as 72% within 5 years, while in the metastatic setting, the 5-year overall survival rate is only 12%. Due to the absence of receptor expression, targeted therapies are limited, leaving chemotherapy as the primary systemic treatment option in both the neoadjuvant and metastatic settings [5]. GM is a benign inflammatory breast disease, characterized by epithelioid and multinucleated giant cell granulomas confined to the mammary lobules, often accompanied by microabscesses. Its etiology remains unclear, though various factors have been considered [6]. GM is a benign breast disease with no ideal treatment regimen so far. Various treatment methods have been explored, including surgical and non-surgical options, but no consensus has been reached on the optimal approach [7]. The differentiation between GM and breast cancer is very important, because their treatment methods are very different. Mazlan et al. [8] reported a case of chronic GM developed progressive infiltrating ductal carcinoma, indicating the possibility that chronic granulomatous mastitis could be a precursor for malignancy and the difficulty in differentiating one from the other. Although rare, there have been cases where GM and breast cancer occur concurrently, highlighting the importance of thorough diagnostic evaluation to rule out malignancy [9]. Despite the similarities in clinical symptoms between GM and breast cancer, the relationship between the two remains inconclusive.

We report a case of breast cancer treated with regular chemotherapy following radical mastectomy, in which refractory GM developed in the contralateral breast. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first documented case of GM occurring in the opposite breast after radical mastectomy. Previous literature has described cases where both diseases co-occurred in the same patient or where GM occurred at the site of the surgical scar of after breast conserving surgery. Ichinose et al. [10] reported three cases of idiopathic granulomatous mastitis (IGM) at the site of the surgical scar following breast-conserving surgery for breast cancer. Evans et al. [11] described a 39-year-old female presenting with right-sided multifocal breast cancer and left-sided GM. Yoshida et al. [12] reported a case of a 34-year-old female with concurrent GM and ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) in the same breast.

In patients presenting with breast inflammation, malignancy must be ruled out, as it may represent inflammatory breast cancer or, rarely, DCIS [13, 14]. This is especially critical in patients with a history of breast cancer with unfavorable subtypes. Our case further suggests that, in addition to the potential recurrence of breast cancer, granuloma formation should be considered when a new mass is detected in breast cancer survivors. In such instances, multiple imaging modalities and pathological biopsies are essential for an accurate diagnosis.
