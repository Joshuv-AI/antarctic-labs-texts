Expanding Cyanide‐Bridged Weakly Coordinating Anions Through the Brominated Silver Salt Ag[BCNBBr]
Authors: Amina L Moshtaha, Tim‐Niclas Streit, Joshua Parche, Robin Sievers, Moritz Malischewski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWeakly coordinating anions (WCAs) composed of a cyanide unit bridging two triarylborane Lewis acids are an attractive but largely unexplored complement to established tetraarylborates such as [B(C6F5)4]− (BArF20). Here, we report the brominated representative [μ‐(CN)(B(C6F4Br)3)2]− ([BCNBBr]−) through its readily accessible silver salt Ag[BCNBBr], prepared from AgCN and two equivalents of B(C6F4Br)3 in CH2Cl2. Ag[BCNBBr] provides convenient access to [BCNBBr]− in weakly coordinating media and enables the introduction of this WCA through oxidation or halide abstraction. Its utility is demonstrated by the synthesis of a range of [BCNBBr]− salts containing oxidizing and electrophilic cations. These results show that [BCNBBr]− is stable under strongly oxidizing conditions, including systems reaching at least +1.14 V vs. FeCp2/FeCp2 +. [BCNBBr]− represents a bromine‐substituted WCA whose heavier bromine substituents enhance X‐ray scattering and facilitate crystal structure analysis of its salts.Keywords: boron, bromine, cyanide, fluorine, Lewis Acids, WCA

Introduction

Weakly coordinating anions (WCAs) are indispensable building blocks for accessing highly reactive cationic species and enabling their characterization. Effective WCAs combine minimal coordinating ability with high stability toward oxidizing conditions and electrophiles, typically provided by large, robust anions with delocalized charge and minimal nucleophilic sites [1].

Among the most established WCAs are halogenated tetraarylborates such as [B(C6F5)4]− (BArF20) and [B(3,5‐C6H3(CF3)2)4]− (BArF24) [1]. Both are widely used as counter anions in homogeneous catalysis, as supporting electrolytes in electrochemistry, and for stabilizing reactive cationic species [2, 3].

In many applications, silver salts are particularly useful reagents, since Ag+ enables halide abstraction and can act as a one‐electron oxidant [1]. In practice, a major limitation is that many commonly used Ag[WCA] salts are difficult to synthesize or unstable in weakly coordinating solvents [1]. Solvent‐free alkali metal salts of [BArF24]− are synthetically [4] or even commercially available. In contrast, the corresponding Ag[BArF] salts are prepared by salt metathesis in donor solvents. Accordingly, Ag[BArF20] and Ag[BArF24] are typically isolated as solvates containing strongly coordinating donor solvents (e.g., Et2O) [5, 6]. Furthermore, both salts exhibit limited stability in ortho‐difluorobenzene (oDFB) and CH2Cl2, decomposing to triarylboranes BR3 and oligomeric silver organyls AgR [5, 6]. This has prompted the development of arene‐stabilized species, such as [Ag(arene)3][BArF20] and [Ag(benzene)3][BArF24], although enhanced stability in CH2Cl2 was only observed for Ag[BArF20] solvates [5, 7]. By contrast, some donor‐free Ag+ salts of WCAs are available, like Ag[Al(OC(CF3)3)4], which is synthesized by salt metathesis in SO2 [1, 8].

A less commonly used class of boron‐based WCAs features cyanide anions bridging two triarylborane units [1]. While the parent anion, [μ‐(CN)(B(C6H5)3)2]− ([BCNBH]−), was first reported by Lippard in 1981, perfluorination affords the more robust anion, [μ‐(CN)(B(C6F5)3)2]− ([BCNB]−), which is recognized as a valuable WCA due to its very low basicity and the pronounced tendency of its salts to crystallize [9, 10]. [BCNB]− was established primarily through Bochmann's report of the tritylium salt in 1999. Further, a related system of the diborane(6) dianion [B2(CN)6]2‒ framework decorated with BCF, yielding the WCA [B2{CNB(C6F5)3}6]2‒ was recently published, illustrating the broader concept of generating WCAs through coordinating boron‐based Lewis acids to cyanide or related compounds [11]. Despite the early works of Bochmann, however, [BCNB]− chemistry has remained largely unexplored since then, with no silver salt having been reported [10, 12]. We recently revisited this platform and developed a straightforward synthesis of the silver salt Ag[BCNB] from commercially available AgCN and B(C6F5)3 in weakly coordinating solvents (e.g., CH2Cl2 or oDFB), enabling direct follow‐up chemistry and providing access to “naked” Ag+ as evidenced by coordination of the extremely weak Lewis base Ni(CO)4 [13].

Despite the many advantages of highly fluorinated tetraarylborates, this class of WCAs comprises only light elements, which can provide limited x‐ray scattering and complicate scXRD characterization of salts of reactive cations. Tetraarylborates incorporating heavier halogens are rarely described and not widely used in WCA chemistry. The chlorinated borate [B(3,5‐C6H3Cl2)4]− can reduce disorder compared to CF3‐substituted arylborates. However, only alkali metal or ammonium salts, but no silver salt, were reported [14, 15]. The perhalogenated borates [B(4‐C6F4I)4]− and [B(4‐C6F4Br)4]− have so far only been isolated as lithium salts from donor solvents [16, 17].

As we had recently reported the synthesis of the soft Lewis superacid B(C6F4Br)3 [18], we wondered whether reaction with AgCN could give Ag[μ‐(CN)(B(C6F4Br)3)2] (Ag[BCNBBr]), featuring a total of six para‐bromine substituents. If successful, the resulting silver salt would provide a convenient entry to a family of oxidizing/electrophilic ionic reagents bearing the brominated WCA, enabling access to reactive cations, while the heavy Br substituents are expected to improve x‐ray scattering and thereby facilitate scXRD characterization of the resulting salts.

Results and Discussion

Syntheses

We herein report the new silver salt Ag[BCNBBr], a para‐brominated analogue of the previously described perfluorinated compound Ag[BCNB] [13]. Incorporation of bromine provides a heavy‐atom variant of this WCA, and we evaluate the silver salt as a practical reagent for subsequent introduction of [BCNBBr]−. Ag[BCNBBr] is readily prepared from AgCN and 2.08 equivalents of the Lewis acid B(C6F4Br)3 (BCFBr) in CH2Cl2 at room temperature (Scheme 1, left). Upon stirring for several hours, the initially insoluble AgCN dissolves completely, indicating full conversion. Consequently, no filtration is required during the work‐up, and removal of the solvent followed by drying affords Ag[BCNBBr] as a white solid in 85% yield. Notably, traces of CH2Cl2 remain coordinated to Ag+, even after drying, which is reflected in the solid‐state structure.

Synthesis of [Ag(CH2Cl2)][μ‐(CN)(B(C6F4Br)3)2]•CH2Cl2 from AgCN with BCFBr in CH2Cl2 (left) and solid‐state structure of the [BCNBBr]‒ anion (right). Solvent molecules and Ag+ are omitted for clarity. Ellipsoids are depicted with 50% probability level. Color code: pink – boron, grey – carbon, yellow – fluorine, red – bromine, blue – nitrogen.

Crystallization by layering a CH2Cl2 solution with n‐pentane provided single crystals suitable for scXRD (Scheme 1, right). The compound was identified as the dichloromethane solvate [Ag(CH2Cl2)][μ‐(CN)(B(C6F4Br)3)2]•CH2Cl2.

Ag[BCNBBr] was analyzed using IR and NMR spectroscopy. The IR spectrum agrees qualitatively with the calculated wavenumbers (see Supporting Information), and displays the characteristic cyanide stretching band at ṽ(CN) = 2289 cm−1, similar to Ag[BCNB] (ṽ(CN) = 2290 cm−1) [13]. The 19F NMR spectrum shows two sets of signals corresponding to the carbon‐ and nitrogen‐coordinated BCFBr units, respectively. The −131.6 and −132.8 ppm, while the meta‐fluorine atoms are high field shifted to −137.3 and −137.6 ppm. The chemical shift differences (Δδ) between the respective meta‐ and ortho‐fluorine signals of the distinct cyanide‐bound BCFBr units are 1.1 ppm (ortho‐F) and 0.3 ppm (meta‐F), similar to Ag[BCNB] [13]. In the 11B NMR spectrum, the signals of the tetracoordinated boron centers are found at −14.6 and −24.2 ppm, which is in the same range as Ag[BCNB] [13]. The broad downfield signal corresponds to the nitrogen‐bound boron. In contrast, the sharper, high field‐shifted signal corresponds to the carbon‐bound boron. In the 13C NMR spectrum, all signals corresponding to the BCFBr Lewis acid are present, whereas the cyanide carbon signal is not observed. Synthesized with the correct stoichiometry, Ag[BCNBBr] is obtained cleanly and can be used without further purification. In contrast, excess AgCN promotes formation of persistent monoadducts, such as Ag[CN(B(C6F4Br)3)] and Ag[NC(B(C6F4Br)3)], which are difficult to remove. Monitoring the reaction and supplementing Lewis acid as needed is therefore recommended. Excess Lewis acid, however, typically introduces impurities due to its pronounced moisture sensitivity. Traces of water already lead to hydrolysis of B(C6F4Br)3 to B(C6F4Br)3·H2O, which subsequently decomposes to products including BrF4C6H, which can be observed in the 19F NMR spectra. Importantly, these impurities arise primarily from the Lewis acid during the synthesis, rather than hydrolysis of the [BCNBBr] anion. If hydrolysis products are present, the product can be dried under elevated temperature, ending up with solely traces of BrF4C6H. Alternatively, purification of Ag[BCNBBr] by recrystallisation using a mixture of CH2Cl2 and either toluene or benzene is effective. Notably, the aromatic solvent remains bound to Ag+ upon isolation and drying, yielding solvates of the type [Ag(arene)][BCNBBr]. Both solvates were analyzed by NMR spectroscopy, with the 1H NMR spectrum confirming the presence of coordinating benzene or toluene. The 19F NMR spectra of both solvates show fluorine signals and shift differences (δΔ) similar to those of the [Ag(CH2Cl2)]+ solvate. The IR spectrum of the toluene solvate shows the cyanide stretching band at ṽ(CN) = 2297 cm−1, which is only slightly blue‐shifted compared to the dichloromethane solvate. Single crystals of the toluene solvate [Ag(toluene)3][BCNBBr] were obtained by cooling an Ag[BCNBBr] solution in CH2Cl2/toluene to −70°C.

Highly electrophilic Ag+ salts act as strong oxidants in weakly coordinating solvents [19]. We therefore investigated the reactivity of Ag[BCNBBr] as a powerful oxidizer and assessed the oxidative stability of the [BCNBBr]− anion by isolating the corresponding oxidized salts.

This was realized by treating CH2Cl2 solutions of Ag[BCNBBr] with equimolar amounts of different reducing agents spanning a wide potential window (Scheme 2). The products were characterized by scXRD and IR spectroscopy. In the IR spectra, all isolated salts display the bridging cyanide band around ṽ(CN) = 2300 cm−1. Mild reducing agents such as decamethylferrocene FeCp*2 (E 1/2 = −0.48 V vs. FeCp2/FeCp2 +) [20] and ferrocene FeCp2 (E 1/2 = 0 V) [20] were oxidized immediately, as indicated by formation of a dark suspension (FeCp*2 +) or a deep blue solution (FeCp2 +), accompanied by the formation of a dark precipitate of elemental silver. Single crystals suitable for scXRD were obtained by vapor diffusion of n‐pentane into a solution of [FeCp*2][BCNBBr] in oDFB, confirming the presence of the decamethylferrocenium cation. Similarly, the much weaker reducing agent N(4‐BrC6H4)3 (E 1/2 = +0.70 V) [20] was oxidized by Ag[BCNBBr], instantly forming a deep blue solution of the corresponding “magic blue” salt N(4‐BrC6H4)3[BCNBBr]. Single crystals were obtained by cooling a CH2Cl2 solution to −70°C. The obtained salt can be stored under inert conditions and used as a strong oxidizer to introduce [BCNBBr]− as a WCA via oxidation with “magic blue”. These results demonstrate the stability of the WCA under oxidative conditions up to +0.70 V. When using the slightly weaker reducing agent thianthrene (E 1/2 = +0.86 V) [20], no oxidation was observed upon addition to the Ag[BCNBBr] solution, as indicated by the absence of a colour change. This highlights the limited oxidizing strength of Ag[BCNBBr], which is typical of silver‐based oxidizers and does not reflect the oxidative stability of the [BCNBBr]− anion. Nevertheless, it highlights constraints of the Ag+ oxidizing strength under slightly coordinating conditions in polar solvents. To further investigate the stability of the anion by introduction of different oxidizing cations, elemental iodine I2 was added to the mixture of Ag[BCNBBr] and thianthrene, exploiting the increased oxidizing strength of the synergistic Ag/I2 system [21]. Upon iodine addition, the mixture turned intense violet‐pink, and a white precipitate of AgI formed. The thianthrenium salt was isolated by filtering the mixture, removing the solvent, and washing with n‐pentane to remove excess iodine. Single crystals were obtained by vapor diffusion of n‐pentane into a CH2Cl2 solution of [C6H4S2C6H4][BCNBBr].

Oxidation of reducing agents [Red.] with Ag[BCNBBr] (right) and for weaker reducing agents with Ag[BCNBBr] and I2 (left), yielding the salts [Ox.][BCNBBr]. The oxidized cations are depicted below.

In this study, the weakest reducing agent that could be oxidized by Ag[BCNBBr] in combination with I2 was N(2,4‐Br2C6H3)3 (E 1/2 = +1.14 V) [20]. As with thianthrene, oxidation (and the corresponding visible colour change to a dark green solution) occurred only upon the addition of iodine. Following an analogous work‐up, IR spectroscopy was used to analyze the “magic green” salt, and single crystals were obtained by vapor diffusion of n‐pentane into a CH2Cl2 solution. Overall, the oxidation reactions demonstrate an oxidizing strength of at least +0.70 V for Ag[BCNBBr], which can be increased to at least +1.14 V when combined with I2. The isolation of various [BCNBBr]‒ salts proves that the [BCNBBr]− anion is stable under oxidative conditions up to at least +1.14 V (Figure 1). The scope of the reactions performed highlights Ag[BCNBBr] as a useful reagent for generating reactive cations while introducing the stable WCA [BCNBBr]−. Furthermore, the work‐up is easily performed by filtration as the only byproducts are insoluble elemental silver or silver iodide.

Redox potentials of the oxidized cations, illustrating the oxidizing strength of Ag[BCNBBr] and oxidative stability of the WCA. At increased potentials above +0.86 V, oxidation is only possible with Ag[BCNBBr] in combination with I2.

Since tritylium salts offer a high solubility and are useful reagents for halide‐ or hydride‐abstractions, it was targeted to obtain the corresponding [BCNBBr]− salt, analogous to the literature known Ph3C[BCNB], published by Bochmann et al. [10]. The synthesis of Ph3C[BCNBBr] was realized in two steps: After the silver salt was generated from AgCN and B(C6F4Br)3 in CH2Cl2, trityl chloride was added to the reaction mixture, leading to the formation of an intense yellow solution and precipitation of AgCl (Scheme 3, top). Pure Ph3C[BCNBBr] was obtained in 66% yield by concentrating the filtered reaction mixture, layering it with n‐pentane, and allowing diffusion at −24°C.

Synthesis of Ph3C[BCNBBr] and NBu4[BCNBBr].

Hence, purification of the tritylium salt proved more facile than that of the silver salt from CH2Cl2. IR and NMR analyses were performed, and the anion data are consistent with those of the other synthesized salts. The bridging cyanide band at ṽ(CN) = 2303 cm‒1 is almost identical to that of the corresponding [BCNB]‒ salt (ṽ(CN) = 2305 cm−1) [10]. The presence of the tritylium cation was confirmed by 1H and 13C NMR spectroscopy, which showed all the characteristic tritylium signals [11]. Single crystals of Ph3C[BCNBBr] were obtained by layering a highly concentrated CH2Cl2 solution with n‐pentane and cooling to −24°C.

Finally, to access a redox‐inactive and light‐insensitive counter‐cation with high solubility, we synthesized the n‐tetrabutylammonium salt NBu4[BCNBBr] by stirring NBu4CN with 2.08 equivalents of B(C6F4Br)3 in CH2Cl2 for 3 h (Scheme 3, bottom). The product was obtained as a beige solid in 86% yield. Similar to the tritylium salt, the NBu4 + salt is easily purified by recrystallization from CH2Cl2 layered with n‐pentane. The product was analyzed by IR and NMR spectroscopy, and the 1H NMR spectrum confirmed the introduction of the NBu4 + cation. The [BCNBBr]− signals in the 19F and 11B NMR spectra resemble those of the other salts; however, the boron signals are slightly downfield‐shifted by 0.5 (B─N) and 0.3 (B─C) ppm compared to [Ag(CH2Cl2)][BCNBBr]. Single crystals of NBu4[BCNBBr] suitable for scXRD were obtained from a solution in oDCB by vapor diffusion with n‐pentane.

Overall, the isolated [BCNBBr]− salts (NBu4 +, ferrocenium, Ag+, tritylium, “magic blue”, thianthrenium, and “magic green”) constitute a versatile and readily accessible toolkit for introducing [BCNBBr]− via salt metathesis, oxidation, and halide/hydride abstraction.

Crystal Structures

Across all isolated salts and solvates, the [BCNBBr]− anion exhibits highly consistent bond lengths, and the bond lengths of the bridging cyanide unit are similar to those of the perfluorinated analogue [BCNB]− [13]. However, the linearity of the BCNB unit and the C─B─B─C torsion angles of the BCFBr unit differ for the crystallized salts. In [Ag(CH2Cl2)][μ‐(CN)(B(C6F4Br)3)2]•CH2Cl2, the two bridged BCFBr units adopt a staggered arrangement, analogous to the fluorinated [Ag(oDFB)3][BCNB], with torsion angles of approximately −60° in both structures [13]. The “magic blue” and “magic green” salts display intermediate torsion angles around −30°, whereas the remaining [BCNBBr]− salts are best described as eclipsed, spanning angles from −18.1° to 7.7°, with the minimum torsion angle found in the thianthrenium salt. These results indicate substantial conformational flexibility of the molecular framework, reflecting adaptability to solid‐state packing with counter cations, alongside pronounced rigidity of the C6F5 groups compared to the more readily disordered CF3 groups found, for example, in perfluoroalkoxyaluminates. The silver salt Ag[BCNBBr] was crystallized as a dichloromethane solvate [Ag(CH2Cl2)][μ‐(CN)(B(C6F4Br)3)2]•CH2Cl2, which crystallized in the space group P1, and as a solvate of stronger coordinating toluene [Ag(toluene)3][BCNBBr], which crystallized in the space group P21/n (Figures 2 and 3). In the CH2Cl2‐solvate, the silver cation is hexacoordinated by six halogen atoms, four of which represent weak anion‐cation contacts (Figure 2, right). Interactions between cation and anion are limited to the three Ag‒Br contacts (2.712(1) to 2.803(2) Å) and one Ag‒F (2.603(6) Å) interaction. One CH2Cl2 molecule coordinates via both chlorine atoms as a chelating‐ligand to the cation, forming two Ag‒Cl contacts (2.857(4) and 3.017(4) Å). The second CH2Cl2 molecule interacts with the [BCNBBr]‒ anion via Cl‒F and Cl‒Br contacts. Each [BCNBBr]− unit coordinates four distinct Ag+ cations via four of the six C6F4Br‐groups. These Ag‒anion interactions lead to a polymeric network in the solid state. Further intermolecular interactions are observed between the [BCNBBr]− units via F‒F, Br‒F, and Br‒Br contacts.

Ellipsoids are depicted with 50% probability level. In the toluene‐solvate, the silver cation is coordinated in η4‐fashion by three toluene molecules, yielding in total 12 Ag─C contacts with d(Ag‒C) smaller than the sum of the van der Waals (vdW) radii (Figure 3). The Ag─C bond lengths, as well as the Ag–plane and Ag─centroid distances, are similar on average to those in other toluene solvates of Ag[WCA] salts, such as [BArF20]− [6]. In this solvate, the Ag+ cation is solely coordinated by the toluene molecules, and interactions between the cationic [Ag(toluene)3]+ unit and the anion are limited to the secondary coordination sphere, involving C─F, H─F, C─C, and H─C contacts between toluene and [BCNBBr]−. This contrasts with the CH2Cl2 solvate, where the silver cation is not only coordinated by one solvent molecule (as a chelating ligand), but exhibits additional cation‐anion interactions (Ag‒F and Ag‒Br).

The analogous perfluorinated salt Ag[BCNB] has so far only been crystallized as a solvate of the weakly coordinating solvent oDFB, where the silver cation is coordinated by three oDFB molecules in an η4‐fashion [13]. This compound exhibits, similar to [Ag(toluene)3][BCNBBr], a total of 12 Ag─C contacts between the aromatic solvent and the cation. The corresponding bond lengths and Ag‒arene planes/centroid distances are also similar. However, in [Ag(oDFB)3][BCNB], the silver cation further interacts with the [BCNB]− anion via two additional Ag‒F contacts, which leads to a polymeric chain structure in the solid state, similar to the CH2Cl2 solvate of Ag[BCNBBr]. Comparing both polymeric structures [Ag(oDFB)3][BCNB] and [Ag(CH2Cl2)2][BCNBBr], the latter exhibits additional Ag─Br contacts, yielding not a chain structure, but a more complex polymeric network. In this context, the additional cation‐anion interactions (three Ag─Br and one Ag─F) are best explained by the preference of the soft Lewis acid Ag+ for heavier halogen atoms. Comparing the arene solvates of both silver salts, a possible explanation for the lack of direct Ag‒anion contacts in the toluene solvate is the increased donor strength of toluene compared to CH2Cl2 or oDFB. In the toluene solvate, no polymeric chain or network is formed through Ag─X (X = F, Br) contacts between cation and anion, in contrast to the other silver salts discussed above. Instead, the [BCNBBr]− anions are interacting with each other through Br‒F, F‒F, C‒F, Br‒C, and Br‒Br contacts, while the cationic [Ag(toluene)3]+ fragment predominantly resides in the voids created by this anion framework, not exhibiting any direct Ag‒anion contacts. A striking feature of [Ag(toluene)3][BCNBBr] is a directional aggregation of the para‐bromine atoms into distorted octahedral (Br)6 motifs (see SI). Within these aggregates, only two Br─Br distances (3.446 and 3.634 Å) fall below the sum of the vdW radii (ΣRvdW = 3.7 Å), while the remaining bromine separations are mostly in the range from 3.7 to 4.0 Å.

Two distinct distorted (Br)6 octahedra occur, each formed by aggregation of three bromine atoms. Both distinct octahedra are stacked alternately to form a channel composed of discrete, noncrosslinked (Br)6 units. Although the individual (Br)6 aggregates are not interconnected, this channel resembles the intermolecular Br‒Br contacts previously reported for the free Lewis acid BCFBr, that were described as a “bromine tunnel” [18].

These bromine‐aggregation motifs are a recurring feature in several of the [BCNBBr]− solid‐state structures, and similar bromine octahedral arrangements are observed in the FeCp*2 + and in the “magic green” salt. The decamethylferrocenium salt crystallized in the space group C2/c, with an Fe‒Cp* distance of d(Fe‒C5,plane) = 1.71 Å observed in the cation. In the solid state, the Cp* ligand is oriented towards the B(Ar)3‐unit, with several H‒F and C‒F contacts between the cation and anion. The cations and anions are stacked alternately, and between these alternating chains, the para‐bromine atoms point towards each other, building a distorted octahedral motif (Figure 4). Within this (Br)6 octahedron, three Br‒Br distances (3.561, 3.633, and 3.671 Å) are below the sum of the vdW radii, while the others are slightly longer (d(Br‒Br) < 4.0 Å). The described bromine octahedra are not interconnected.

For N(2,4‐Br2C6H3)3[BCNBBr], which crystallizes in the space group P 1¯, a slightly different kind of bromine aggregation was found. While interactions between the “magic green” cation and [BCNBBr]‒ via contacts below the sum of the vdW radii are limited to Br‒F, H‒F and C‒F, an aggregation of the para‐bromine atoms of the cation with those of the anion is visible. Three para‐bromine atoms from different cation molecules are arranged in one layer. Three bromine atoms from the [BCNBBr]‒ anion form the subsequent layer, which is rotated by 60°. The arrangement of the six bromine atoms resembles a distorted octahedron, and the only Br‒Br contacts below the sum of the vdW radii found in this motif are formed between the cationic para‐bromine atoms (3.683 and 3.634 Å). While classification as weak interactions is debatable for the longer distances, this bromine aggregation clearly forms a repeating motif in the packing of the BCFBr Lewis acid [18] and [BCNBBr]− salts. This suggests that para‐bromination introduces additional, potentially structure‐directing packing preferences relative to Ag[BCNB]. Such interactions may be beneficial for crystallizing reactive cations when other intermolecular forces are weak and poorly directional, which is typical of WCA salts. In the remaining structures, no pronounced bromine aggregation motifs are observed; instead, salts with large, planar organic cations adopt more conventional alternating cation–anion stacks, as found for the tritylium and thianthrenium salts.

The tritylium salt crystallizes in the space group P 1¯, similarly to the corresponding [BCNB]− salt. The geometrical parameters are similar to those reported for tritylium[BCNB] by Bochmann et al. [10]. In the solid state, alternating cation‐anion stacking is observed. These cation‐anion chains are formed through numerous contacts (C‒C, H‒F, and C‒F). Neighbouring chains are linked via halogen‒halogen contacts between the anions and further by cation‐cation interactions. The overall packing seems similar to that of Ph3C[BCNB] [10].

The thianthrenium[BCNBBr] salt crystallizes in the space group P 1¯, and the expected planarity of the thianthrenium radical cation is clearly observed [22]. Similar to the tritylium salt, an alternating cation–anion stacking arrangement is found in the crystal structure. As observed in some other thianthrenium salts, the cations in the present structure form pairs; however, rather than adopting a directly cofacial arrangement, they are slipped with respect to each other. As a result, only two short C‒C contacts (3.377 Å) are present, while no significant S‒S contacts are observed (d(S‒S) > 6.643 Å). The planar thianthrenium unit is located between two B(Ar)3 units, one approximately centered and the other slightly offset. Cation‐anion interaction involves S‒F, H‒F, H‒Br, and C‒F contacts.

Conclusion

We report a convenient synthesis for the silver salt Ag[BCNBBr], which incorporates the novel, weakly coordinating anion [BCNBBr]−. Ag[BCNBBr] is readily prepared in the weakly coordinating solvent CH2Cl2, and solvates with more strongly coordinating solvents were isolated, such as [Ag(toluene)3][BCNBBr]. Redox experiments demonstrate the effective oxidation strength of Ag[BCNBBr], and the stability of the anion under oxidative conditions up to at least +1.14 V (vs. FeCp2/FeCp2 +). A series of oxidizing salts [Ox.][BCNBBr] ([Ox.] = FeCp2 +, FeCp*2 +, Ph3C+, “magic blue”, thianthrenium, “magic green”) was isolated, covering redox potentials from −0.48 V to +1.14 V. In addition, the redox‐inactive and highly soluble salt NBu4[BCNBBr] was synthesized. The straightforward preparation and stability in weakly coordinating solvents make Ag[BCNBBr] a valuable reagent, and the isolated series of [BCNBBr]‒ salts provides a toolbox for generating reactive cations through salt metathesis, oxidation, or halide/hydride abstraction. Incorporation of heavy bromine atoms improves diffraction quality, yielding crystals that are more readily measured by scXRD than those of the perfluorinated [BCNB]‒ analogue. Moreover, the facile crystallization of [BCNBBr]− salts [23] and the frequent occurrence of bromine aggregation motifs suggest that bromination not only facilitates structural analysis but may also promote crystallization through additional, structure‐directing non‐covalent interactions.
