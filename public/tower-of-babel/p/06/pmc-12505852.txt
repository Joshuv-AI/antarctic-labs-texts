Identification of genetically-supported new drug targets for osteomyelitis based on druggable genomes
Authors: Ruotong Yao, Yangguang Lu, Di Lu, Haiyong Ren, Xiang Wang, Bingyuan Lin, Siyao Chen, Yusheng Zhu, Feng Chen, Yukai Wang, Yi Gao, Jiawen Shen, Qiaofeng Guo, Kai Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLimited drug treatment data are available for osteomyelitis (OM), an inflammatory bone condition secondary to infection. Given its genetic characteristics, it is necessary to integrate genetics into drug development for osteomyelitis. This study applied pharmacogenomics to identify new drug targets for osteomyelitis using Mendelian randomization (MR).MethodsFollowing the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology using Mendelian Randomization guidelines, expression and protein quantitative trait loci (QTL) analysis was applied to simulate drug exposure. Single nucleotide polymorphisms were selected as instrumental variables for MR analysis using blood QTL data and independent osteomyelitis genome-wide association study datasets from UK Biobank and FinnGen R10. A random-effects model meta-analysis combining the results from two datasets was performed. Bayesian co-localization analysis was conducted to validate the targets. Sensitivity analyses were performed using various MR methods, with MR-Egger regression and Cochran’s Q test being conducted to assess the horizontal pleiotropy and heterogeneity of the instrumental variables.ResultsAt α = 1 × 10−5, the meta-analysis identified 12 drug target mechanisms. Gene expression of QDPR, TGM1, NTSR1, CBR3, and NEK6 was positively correlated with osteomyelitis risk, whereas HLA-DRB1, LAMC1, LTB4R, MAPK3, FPR1, ABAT, and LTA4H were negatively correlated with this risk. Five potential drug repurposing opportunities and three drugs that may increase osteomyelitis risk were identified. Sensitivity analyses highlighted LTA4H, LAMC1, QDPR, and NEK6 as having the strongest genetic evidence based on MR-Egger regression and protein QTL tests.ConclusionsThis study identified 12 new genetically supported drug targets for osteomyelitis, thereby providing a genetic foundation for new drug development, repurposing existing drugs, and personalized treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40246-025-00826-6.

Introduction

Osteomyelitis (OM) is an inflammatory condition that is primarily caused by an infectious process that typically affects the bone marrow, adjacent bone structures, and surrounding soft tissues [1, 2]. This condition has been found to be associated with significant morbidity and mortality worldwide [3]. Approximately three-quarters of OM cases are linked to hematogenous Staphylococcus infections [4]; however, chronic non-infectious osteomyelitis (CNO), which presents with similar symptoms, can be triggered by autoinflammatory diseases and specific genetic factors [5]. Furthermore, secondary OM may develop from infection spreading from adjacent areas or orthopedic implants, as well as due to vascular insufficiency or neuropathy [6–8]. The current standard treatment for acute OM is based on the administration of intravenous antibiotics. New therapeutic agents, including daptomycin and linezolid, as well as various non-antibiotic materials such as metals, polymers, and peptides, are currently being investigated, but their efficacy remains uncertain [9, 10]. Despite available treatment guidelines, there is a lack of comprehensive data on the appropriate use of medications for OM. Moreover, the treatment of infected bones with a compromised vascular supply remains poorly understood, and this challenge is compounded by the growing issue of antibiotic resistance [10]. Thus, there is an urgent need to identify potential drug targets for OM.

Given the possible genetic underpinnings of OM, incorporating genetic insights into drug development is a promising approach, especially as genetically supported drugs are more likely to succeed in clinical trials [11–13]. Genes encoding proteins that can be targeted by drugs, small molecules, or monoclonal antibodies are considered to be "druggable" genes [14, 15]. While several genetic loci associated with OM have been identified through various studies [16, 17], the design of these case–control studies often fails to reliably pinpoint causal genes or directly inform drug development. ​In addition, methodological limitations inherent to these investigations preclude the comprehensive characterization of OM susceptibility loci. Mendelian randomization (MR), a genetic method for causal inference, can predict drug efficacy by simulating randomized controlled trials [18, 19]. Moreover, single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) related to gene expression levels, which are known as expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL), can mimic lifelong exposure to drugs targeting the encoded proteins [15, 20]. By integrating SNP-gene expression associations with SNP-disease correlations, MR can be used to infer the causal impact of exposure on outcomes [21].

Herein, we utilized blood eQTL data to predict the efficacy of over 2,000 drug-target mechanisms within two independent genome-wide association study (GWAS) cohorts for OM. We combined the results from both cohorts through a meta-analysis for a comprehensive evaluation and, where possible, validated the findings using SNPs encoding protein levels. Leveraging large-scale, publicly available genetic data, our goal was to identify a genetically supported list of drug targets for OM, thereby providing opportunities for the repurposing of existing licensed or clinical-stage drugs.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology Using Mendelian Randomization (STROBE-MR) guidelines [22]. Publicly available blood eQTL data were utilized alongside summary data from two independent OM GWAS cohorts. Suitable SNPs were selected as the instrumental variables (IVs) for MR analysis to investigate the bidirectional causal relationship between exposure and outcomes. The application of IVs in MR analysis relies on three key assumptions: (i) the selected IVs must be strongly associated with the exposure of interest, (ii) the IVs should not be confounded by other factors influencing the outcome, and (iii) the selected IVs should affect the outcome exclusively through exposure [23] (Fig. 1A). According to the principle of Mendelian randomization, individuals are "randomized" at conception based on their IVs, resulting in genetically higher or lower expression levels of druggable genes. The aggregated effects across IVs can be reliably assessed using methods such as the inverse variance weighted (IVW) approach. Bayesian colocalization (COLOC) analysis was subsequently performed to identify SNPs that simultaneously influence the expression of drug target genes and OM [24], with validation conducted using protein quantitative trait loci (pQTL) data (Fig. 1B-C). Data sources

Blood eQTL data were sourced from a 2021 study by Urmo Võsa et al. [25] encompassing cis-eQTLs for 16,987 genes derived from 31,684 blood samples taken primarily from healthy individuals of European descent. These data were obtained from the eQTLGen Consortium website (https://eqtlgen.org/, accessed on July 28, 2024). Plasma pQTL data were derived from a 2021 study by Egil Ferkingstad et al. [26], which included pQTLs for 4,907 proteins from 35,559 blood samples taken primarily from healthy individuals of Icelandic descent. All DNA positions referenced are based on the human reference genome hg19 (GRCh37); the complete data descriptions are available in the original publications.

Data on druggable genes were obtained from a 2017 study by Chris Finan et al. [14], which identified 4,479 druggable genes. These genes were categorized into three tiers based on the strength of evidence supporting their potential as drug targets. Tier 1 genes encode proteins that are direct targets of approved drugs or candidate drugs currently in clinical trials, Tier 2 genes encode proteins with structural or functional similarities to approved drug targets, and Tier 3 genes include those with more distant similarities. To enhance the clinical relevance of the study, druggable genes located on sex chromosomes, located on mitochondrial DNA, or categorized as Tier 3 were excluded, resulting in 2,030 genes being included in the final analysis. For each viable drug target, clinical stages and drug indications were retrieved from the Clinical Trials database (https://clinicaltrials.gov/) and further confirmed using the Therapeutic Target Database (https://idrblab.net/ttd/, accessed on August 6, 2024) [27].

GWAS data for OM were obtained from the UK Biobank [28], which includes 4,836 patients with OM and 486,484 healthy controls. These data were accessed via the IEU Open GWAS Project platform (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/, accessed on July 28, 2024). Additionally, GWAS data from an independent cohort of the FinnGen consortium (https://r10.finngen.fi/, accessed on July 28, 2024) consisting of 1,881 OM patients and 391,037 healthy controls were used. Participants in both studies were of European descent and had provided informed consent. Case identification was based on International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) codes. All DNA positions referenced were based on the human reference genome hg19 (GRCh37), with detailed information available in the original studies or on the FinnGen website (https://finngen.gitbook.io). Importantly, there was no overlap between the genetic data used in these consortia and the study by Urmo Võsa et al. [25], which makes it possible to conduct a two-sample MR study.

Mendelian randomization

To ensure the robustness and reliability of the MR analysis, a stringent significance threshold of 5 × 10−8 was set for selecting IVs, consistent with criteria used in previous MR analyses of drug targets. European samples from the 1000 Genomes Project were used to clump SNPs with r2 < 0.3 [29]. IV chromosomal positions were restricted to being within 100 kb upstream and downstream of the relevant gene. The Wald ratio for each IV was calculated to determine its effect on the outcome, and the results from individual IVs were then combined using the inverse variance weighted (IVW) method, MR-Egger regression, or the MR Pleiotropy Residual Sum and Outlier (MR-PRESSO) method. When all IVs meet the three key assumptions of MR, the IVW method provides the most accurate and consistent estimates [30], and it was therefore prioritized. However, the MR-Egger regression and MR-PRESSO methods were employed to adjust for potential horizontal pleiotropy, albeit at the expense of precision or by removing outlier SNPs [23, 31]. Additionally, horizontal pleiotropy was assessed using the intercept from the MR-Egger regression [23]. The results are presented as the odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Heterogeneity was evaluated using Cochran's Q test and quantified with the I2 statistic, with heterogeneity considered to be absent if the I2 value was below 50%. The MR analysis was conducted using the R package “TwoSampleMR” (version 0.6.6).

Meta-analysis

Using the MR analysis results from the UK Biobank and FinnGen R10 outcome cohorts, a meta-analysis was conducted by combining the ORs using a random-effects model with the restricted maximum likelihood method, and the combined significance of the p-values was calculated. Given that 2,030 druggable genes were analyzed, the conventional significance level of α = 0.05 could have led to false positives. Therefore, a more stringent significance threshold of α = 1 × 10−5 was applied to identify the most strongly causally related drug targets. In the primary outcome analysis, drug targets with a p-value < 1 × 10−5 were considered significant. Additionally, the false discovery rate (FDR) method was applied for multiple testing correction as a supplementary reference; druggable genes with an adjusted p-value < 0.05 were considered to have potential causal relationships with OM even if they did not meet the α = 1 × 10−5 threshold. The results were visualized using forest plots. The meta-analysis was conducted using the R package “meta” (version 7.0–0).

Bayesian co-localization analysis

For drug targets identified as significantly associated with OM in the meta-analysis, COLOC, a statistical method that uses GWAS data and univariate summary statistics to assess whether two independent genetically associated traits share a common genetic locus, was performed for validation. In this analysis, a prior probability of 1 × 10−6 was assigned to the random variables representing causal relationships between the OM GWAS data from FinnGen R10 and the eQTL data for the target genes. This prior probability reflects the posterior probability (PP4 > 0.8) that a single shared variant exists for both the gene eQTL and OM [24]. For the identified co-localized SNPs, their minor allele frequency (MAF) was identified in the European population using the gnomAD v4.0.0 database to assess the rarity of the variant. A MAF < 0.01 was defined as a rare mutation, and a MAF < 0.001 was defined as an ultra-rare mutation. The COLOC analysis was performed using the “coloc” R package (version 5.2.3).

Results

Identification of potential drug targets to prevent OM

A total of 1,375 eligible druggable genes with blood eQTL data were identified. The random-effects model results indicated that QDPR (OR = 1.052, 95% CI 1.034–1.070, p-value = 2.33 × 10−9), TGM1 (OR = 1.174, 95% CI 1.111–1.242, p-value = 5.06 × 10−9), NTSR1 (OR = 1.104, 95% CI 1.061–1.148, p-value = 1.64 × 10−8), CBR3 (OR = 1.064, 95% CI 1.031–1.085, p-value = 1.19 × 10−6), and NEK6 (OR = 1.065, 95% CI 1.036–1.095, p-value = 6.57 × 10−6) were identified as significant drug targets, as their expression was associated with an increased risk of OM. Conversely, increased expression of HLA-DRB1 (OR = 0.898, 95% CI: 0.867–0.931, p-value = 3.10 × 10−9), LAMC1 (OR = 0.943, 95% CI 0.924–0.963, p-value = 2.29 × 10−7), LTB4R (OR = 0.794, 95% CI 0.721–0.864, p-value = 2.29 × 10−7), MAPK3 (OR = 0.934, 95% CI 0.901–0.958, p-value = 9.05 × 10−7), FPR1 (OR = 0.954, 95% CI 0.931–0.971, p-value = 1.13 × 10−6), ABAT (OR = 0.924, 95% CI 0.891–0.954, p-value = 1.14 × 10−6), and LTA4H (OR = 0.955, 95% CI 0.936–0.974, p-value = 8.05 × 10−6) was identified as a protective factor against OM (Fig. 2). Among these, QDPR, TGM1, FPR1, and NEK6 were classified as Tier 2 genes, while the remaining eight genes were classified as Tier 1. All identified drug targets were validated in single cohorts, supporting the robustness of the meta-analysis results. Although the overall effects of QDPR and LTA4H observed in the meta-analysis could not be replicated in the two individual cohorts of FinnGen R10, QDPR still showed the strongest significance in the meta-analysis. Existing counterparts for potential drug targets

Among the 12 potential drug targets identified, eight druggable genes, including HLA-DRB1, already have corresponding targeted drugs (Table 1). Of these, pyruvic acid, targeting the ABAT gene, and bestatin, targeting LTA4H, have been approved, while the remaining six drugs are in clinical trials. Regarding druggability, all genes except HLA-DRB1 express proteins that can be modulated by small molecules. Additionally, HLA-DRB1, NTSR1, LTB4R, and FPR1 encode proteins that can be targeted by biologics (e.g., antibodies, cytokines, and enzymes). We found that the use of drugs such as KB105 for treating autosomal recessive ichthyosis, ulixertinib for treating melanoma and arterial stenosis, and bestatin for treating ulcerative colitis may increase the risk of OM. However, the relationship between KB105 and ulixertinib use and the onset of OM has not been reported in related clinical trials [32, 33]. Bestatin, an early inhibitor of the LTA4H gene [34], has seen increased clinical use in recent years, and its potential impact on OM risk warrants further attention. Interestingly, clinical studies on meclinertant, an NTSR1 expression inhibitor, have reported beneficial effects on diseases such as Parkinson’s disease [35] and lower esophageal sphincter relaxation [36], suggesting OM may also be a potential indication for meclinertant. Evidence from Bayesian co-localization analysis

In the 500 kb regions upstream and downstream of all identified druggable genes, at least one co-localized locus with a sufficiently high posterior probability was found with the OM GWAS data (Table 2). Although a PP4 > 0.8 was considered sufficiently high, all co-localized loci had PP4 greater than 0.95, indicating significant co-localization effects. Notably, the five drug targets identified as risk factors for OM, QDPR (rs10020773, PP4 = 1), TGM1 (rs2180196, PP4 = 1), NTSR1 (rs2427373, PP4 = 0.9993), CBR3 (rs4817775, PP4 = 1), and NEK6 (rs72759285, PP4 = 1), exhibited the highest co-localization effects, suggesting that drug development should focus on these loci. Sensitivity analysis

To validate the sensitivity of the results, MR analysis was performed using the MR-Egger regression and MR-PRESSO methods instead of the IVW method, and horizontal pleiotropy and heterogeneity were examined among the IVs (Table S2). All genes remained significant after outlier SNPs were excluded using the MR-PRESSO method, although only the LAMC1 and NEK6 genes remained significant using the MR-Egger regression model. Nevertheless, the MR-Egger regression results indicated that there was a consistent direction of the effect on OM risk for each gene, and no significant horizontal pleiotropy was observed among the IVs, confirming that the IVW method results are the most reliable. Except for the QDPR, HLA-DRB1, and NEK6 genes, no significant heterogeneity was observed among the IVs representing the mRNA expression levels of the other genes. Combining evidence from the pQTL and COLOC analyses, all identified drug targets except HLA-DRB1 were supported by at least five lines of evidence, reinforcing the robustness of our findings. Discussion

The aim of this study was to identify novel drug targets for OM. By combining MR and meta-analysis, along with sensitivity analyses including the MR-Egger intercept test, Cochran’s Q test, pQTL studies, and co-localization analysis, we addressed pleiotropy and excluded potential measurement or unmeasured confounding factors, allowing us to identify 12 potential drug targets supported by genetic evidence. Based on the comprehensive evidence, we propose four drug targets with the strongest support: LTA4H and LAMC1, classified as Tier 1 genes, and QDPR and NEK6, classified as Tier 2 genes. These findings provide a significant genetic foundation for future drug development.

LTA4H received support from all sources of evidence except for the MR-Egger test. Increased mRNA and plasma expression levels of LTA4H are associated with a lower risk of OM. The LTA4H gene encodes leukotriene A4 hydrolase, which has epoxide hydrolase activity and can convert leukotriene A4 to leukotriene B4 (LTB4), a potent pro-inflammatory mediator [37, 38]. Additionally, the aminopeptidase activity of LTA4H can degrade certain pro-inflammatory tripeptides, such as the neutrophil chemoattractant Pro-Gly-Pro (PGP), thereby reducing inflammation [39, 40]. It also participates in the biosynthesis of resolvin E1 and 18S-resolvin E1, lipid mediators with effective anti-inflammatory and pro-resolving properties [41].

The LTA4H gene is associated with various diseases, including asthma [42]. As a Tier 1 gene, the LTA4H expression inhibitor bestatin [34] is already used in the treatment of acute myeloid leukemia [43, 44] and inflammatory conditions such as periodontitis [45]. Although no current studies report the association between bestatin and OM, its inhibitory effect on LTA4H suggests that the increased risk of OM during its use warrants further consideration. Future research could explore whether LTA4H expression agonists or its downstream products serve as potential treatments for OM. In our study, the significant association between LTA4H and OM was observed only in the meta-analysis results and the UK Biobank cohort, highlighting the need for validation in additional populations.

The Tier 1 gene LAMC1 received support from all evidence sources except for the pQTL analysis results due to missing plasma pQTL data. LAMC1 encodes the γ1 chain of laminin, an important extracellular matrix component that enhances tissue structural stability [46, 47]. This enhanced stability may prevent pathogen invasion and thus reduce infection risk. Furthermore, laminin plays a crucial role in cell adhesion and migration [48]. By promoting cell adhesion and migration, LAMC1 may help accelerate tissue repair and reduce opportunities for infection. Our study is the first to highlight the potential genetic association between LAMC1 and OM. Increasing the expression of LAMC1 and its associated proteins through relevant drugs may be a viable mechanism for treating OM. Diseases associated with LAMC1 include congenital myasthenic syndrome and hereditary nephropathy, with the integrin and ERK signaling pathways being closely related [49, 50]. Additionally, high expression levels of LAMC1 are closely related to the risk of renal cancer [50]. Therefore, drug development targeting LAMC1 should consider its effects on kidney function and potential adverse outcomes, such as renal-associated tumors.

Our analysis also provided strong evidence of the Tier 2 genes QDPR and NEK6 as drug targets. Unlike the aforementioned genes, high expression levels of QDPR and NEK6 are associated with an increased risk of OM. The QDPR gene encodes an enzyme involved in the metabolism of aromatic amino acids, which can produce excessive reactive oxygen species that may trigger inflammatory responses [51], thereby increasing the risk of OM. Furthermore, high QDPR expression might disrupt the metabolism of phenylalanine and other aromatic amino acids, potentially affecting immune system function [52]. Consequently, individuals with phenylketonuria may also need to be monitored for OM. NEK6 is associated with abnormal cell cycle regulation, increased cell proliferation, and inflammation; its gene encodes a serine/threonine kinase that plays a key role in cell cycle regulation and DNA repair, and thus can potentially impact the stability and function of bone marrow cells and increase the risk of infection and inflammation [53, 54]. In clinical practice, drugs that specifically inhibit QDPR enzyme activity could be designed to reduce metabolic disorders and oxidative stress; meanwhile, specific inhibitors of NEK6 could be developed to regulate cell cycle and DNA repair processes, an thus reduce inflammatory responses associated with OM. As no related drugs are currently in clinical practice, our study provides a reference for the future development of QDPR and NEK6 as Tier 2 drug targets.

The correlation between OM and other diseases warrants further investigation, as it could inform the repurposing of existing medications to prevent and treat OM. Our study highlights potential links between OM and diseases such as multiple sclerosis (MS), arterial stenosis, and peptic ulcers. For instance, in 2024, Ruythooren et al. [55] reported a case of a patient with MS developing OM due to persistent ureaplasma ureolyticum urethritis, suggesting a relationship between MS and OM. Using the medication glatiramer acetate to treat MS may have significant implications for managing OM in patients with MS. Additionally, in 2022, Nguyen et al. [56] revealed that patients with OM secondary to diabetic foot have a higher incidence of arterial stenosis. In this context, using the targeted drug ulixertinib may exacerbate OM by inhibiting MAPK3 gene expression, highlighting the need for caution during its use.

MR studies have also uncovered the existence of a significant genetic correlation between diabetes and OM [57]. Though our study did not find drug targets related to diabetes treatment that reached the stringent significance threshold in the meta-analysis results, these targets were individually identified in the UK Biobank and FinnGen R10 cohorts. Future studies should focus on exploring the effectiveness of diabetes-related medications in treating OM. In addition, as early as 1965, cases of acute peptic ulcers with acute hematogenous OM were reported [58]. Clinical observations of the association between diseases corresponding to identified drug targets and OM corroborates the accuracy of our MR methodology in clinical guidance.

Our identification of druggable targets like MAPK3 and LTA4H underscores the critical role of intracellular signaling pathway dynamics in inflammatory bone pathology. Specifically, MAPK3 modulates osteoblast-osteoclast crosstalk via RANKL signaling, a pathway whose suppression may exacerbate bone destruction in OM, aligning with our observed protective effect. Concurrently, LTA4H exhibits context-dependent duality: while primarily catalyzing pro-inflammatory LTB4 synthesis, it also generates anti-inflammatory resolvins [59], mirroring the complexity of cAMP/PKA-MAPK pathway interactions observed in melanogenesis dysregulation. This mechanistic parallel is strikingly evidenced in vitiligo pathogenesis [60], where compensatory elevation of melanogenesis pathway activity in unaffected skin counterbalances lesional dysfunction, a phenomenon analogous to the putative compensatory immune responses we hypothesize in perilesional bone microenvironments. Such pathway-level adaptations may fundamentally constrain therapeutic efficacy if not systematically accounted for in target selection.

Although blood-derived QTL data provided the necessary statistical power for our genome-wide Mendelian randomization approach, we acknowledge that caution is warranted in interpreting this finding. On one hand, peripheral blood transcriptomes serve as robust sentinels of systemic immune activation, exemplified by studies of bacterial meningitis where genome-wide profiling revealed > 5,500 differentially expressed genes that precisely delineated pathogen-specific host responses [60]. Our identification of chemotaxis regulators aligns with such innate immunity signatures, confirming blood's utility for detecting conserved inflammatory pathways. Conversely, tissue-divergent expression may obscure bone-specific pathology. LTB4R showed no significant plasma pQTL association despite strong mRNA effects, suggesting compartmentalized regulation within the bone marrow microenvironment where neutrophil extracellular trap formation occurs. This discordance underscores that while blood transcriptomics efficiently nominates therapeutic targets operating through systemic immunity, ultimate therapeutic translatability requires validation in spatially resolved bone atlases.

The two-sample MR approach enabled us to explore the relationship between druggable gene expression and the risk of OM. In genetic research, it is necessary to validate scientific discoveries and eliminate false positives [61]. Therefore, we utilized two large and independent GWAS cohorts, applied meta-analysis to combine results, adopted stringent significance thresholds, and designed detailed sensitivity analyses. This approach represents a significant strength of our study. During sensitivity analysis, most genes did not pass the pQTL-based tests. This may have been due to our reliance on plasma pQTL expression data, which might have overlooked protein expression in other tissues. Future research should explore the expression of druggable genes across various tissues to provide additional evidence. Furthermore, MR-Egger regression results often suggest non-significant statistical findings. It is important to note that during the sensitivity analyses, we only used the more comprehensive GWAS data from FinnGen R10. As a result, the MR-Egger results may differ somewhat from the meta-analysis summary results. However, given that we observed no significant horizontal pleiotropy across all the analyses, the IVW summary results from both GWAS databases should still be given substantial consideration and reference.

While we prioritized Tier 1–2 druggable genes based on established clinical relevance, we acknowledge that key inflammatory regulators like Interleukin (IL)-19, IL-20 and IL-24, may not have reached statistical significance due to tissue-specific expression patterns or insufficient instrumental variable strength in blood eQTL data. ​Although cytokines such as IL-19 play established roles in inflammation and may influence the risk of developing inflammatory diseases like palmoplantar pustulosis [62], their association with OM risk could not be established through genetic analytical methods. Moreover, these inflammatory mediators may not represent high-priority druggable targets. Subsequent research should leverage tissue-specific QTL data, and integrating single-cell eQTL data derived directly from infected bone tissue could elucidate these mechanisms.​

Despite these strengths, our study has several limitations. First, the GWAS data that were used to define the outcomes only represent the risk of OM occurrence. As a result, the identified drug targets are more likely to reflect preventive effects than they are to represent improvements in disease progression. Though exploring preventive-related medications can still provide significant insights for the development of OM treatment drugs, the gap between genetic evidence and clinical application cannot be overlooked. MR simulation involves examining lifelong low-dose drug exposure and assumes a linear relationship between exposure and outcome, whereas clinical trials often study high-dose drugs over shorter durations [21]. Consequently, MR results may not directly correspond to effect sizes in practice and cannot perfectly predict drug effects. Second, due to data source limitations, various subtypes of OM could not be detailed, the distinctions between infectious OM and CNO could not be thoroughly analyzed, and the progression and prognosis of OM could not explored in depth. Third, the expression of druggable genes at the transcriptomic and proteomic levels was based solely on blood data, as data from other tissues, such as muscle and brain tissue, were missing, making it challenging to determine the most appropriate tissue for drug action. Notably, ​while genetic loci may critically influence age at disease onset [63], our analysis focused predominantly on identifying associations related to druggability, thereby lacking comprehensive attention to potential correlations between genetic loci and OM onset age. Finally, our study was limited to a European population, and further research is needed to determine whether our results can be generalized to other populations around the world.

Despite these limitations, our study holds significant clinical implications. By providing new potential drug targets for the treatment of OM, it may contribute to the development of more effective therapeutic strategies. Our research also identifies existing drugs that may have potential therapeutic or preventive effects against OM, thereby offering new directions for drug repurposing. Moreover, it identifies drugs, namely KB105, meclinertant, and bestatin, that are more likely to result in adverse outcomes such as OM, providing new considerations for clinical drug application.

In light of the potential genetic factors associated with OM [5], identifying genes that may increase or decrease the risk of OM can assist clinicians in providing personalized treatment and risk assessment, especially for patients with a family history of CNO. Considering the low approval rates of new drugs in clinical trials and the high costs associated with their development [64], druggable genomic resources offer a new pathway for identifying drug targets using existing genetic data [14]. Future researchers can use the drug targets identified in our study to design new drugs, further develop Tier 2 druggable genes, or explore the use of existing drugs in preventing and treating OM to address the negative impact of increasing antibiotic resistance on the management of infectious OM [10]. Finally, subsequent research should investigate the effects of relevant druggable genes on other tissues and in non-European populations considering time factors, specific OM subtypes, and outcomes to provide further global clinical evidence.

Conclusion

This study successfully identified 12 potential drug targets associated with the risk of OM by integrating MR analysis, meta-analysis, and COLOC methods. Notably, TA4H, LAMC1, QDPR, and NEK6 emerged as the strongest genetic candidates, supported by multiple lines of evidence indicating their potential as drug targets. These findings provide valuable resources for prioritizing drug development efforts, offering robust human genetic evidence for the efficacy of OM-related drugs. Not only do these results establish a new genetic foundation for drug development targeting OM, but they also present opportunities for repurposing existing drugs and personalized treatment, highlighting their significant potential applications in the clinic.
