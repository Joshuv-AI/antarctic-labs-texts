Defining Diagnostic Criteria for Neurogenic Thoracic Outlet Syndrome: An Expert Consensus
Authors: Eric R Wagner, Constance M Sullivan, Kevin R Cuneo, Krishna N Chopra, Oluwakorede Omole, Michael B Gottschalk, Robert L Bowers
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Neurogenic thoracic outlet syndrome (nTOS) can present itself in a number of ways, often resulting in diagnostic ambiguity.Purpose:To establish a consensus among upper extremity experts using the Delphi method to determine the most clinically relevant criteria in distinguishing nTOS to help create a diagnostic algorithm.Study Design:Consensus statement.Methods:A panel of 20 upper extremity experts rated the importance of 54 different clinical items in reference to distinguishing nTOS. Items were ranked on a scale from 0 (least important) to 10 (most important). The mean and standard deviation for each item’s rankings were calculated. The Cronbach alpha (α) value was used to determine the degree of concordance among the panelists’ rankings. Analogous items with similarly high rankings were combined to help develop the final criteria.Results:A Cronbach α value of .936 was calculated, indicating a high degree of consensus among the experts’ responses. Similar items with higher rankings were combined to help develop the final criteria. The 5 criteria deemed clinically important for nTOS were (1) a positive response to injections (pectoralis minor, anterior scalene, middle scalene); (2) upper or lower trunk radiating symptoms that worsen with supraclavicular provocative maneuvers (scalene pressure or positive Tinel sign over scalenes); (3) upper or lower trunk symptoms that worsen with infraclavicular provocative maneuvers (positive Tinel sign medial to coracoid or tenderness to palpation over the coracoid process); (4) reproduction of pain with 90° of abduction in external rotation or upper limb tension test, and (5) participation in work/exercise requiring repetitive arm stress (overhead athletes, throwing sports, protracted posture at work, etc).Conclusion:This study proposed 5 clinical criteria for distinguishing nTOS that were agreed upon by 20 upper extremity, nerve, and vascular surgery experts. These criteria can be used to develop a future diagnostic algorithm for nTOS, allowing for further research and more efficient nTOS treatment.Keywords: thoracic outlet syndrome, neurogenic, Delphi method, neurogenic thoracic outlet syndrome, scapular dyskinesia

Thoracic outlet syndrome (TOS) is a rare compressive neuropathy of the upper extremity, affecting about 1 to 3 individuals per 100,000 people. 35 This disorder occurs when neurovascular structures, including the brachial plexus, subclavian artery, and subclavian vein, are compressed when passing through the thoracic outlet.3,16,17,24,37,61 The thoracic outlet is composed of infraclavicular spaces, such as the retropectoralis minor space, and supraclavicular spaces, including the scalene and costoclavicular triangles.22,49 Compression of vascular structures in the supraclavicular spaces can lead to vascular TOS (vTOS), whereas compression of neurogenic structures between the scalenes or in the infraclavicular and retropectoralis minor spaces can cause neurogenic TOS (nTOS), although deviations from the aforementioned associations oftentimes occur.2,3,34,69 Most TOS cases are neurogenic in origin, accounting for roughly >90% of cases.2,3,45,68

The wide variety in causation subsequently leads to diversity in the clinical presentation of nTOS.3,25,37 Predominantly nTOS cases can present with different symptoms based on the chronicity of a patient’s disease state.3,68 Symptom severity can be categorized into stages 1 through 4, ranging from mild pain in the anterior shoulder, trapezius, or upper chest to severe and significant pain that is located diffusely throughout the shoulder.3,13,14

The broad range and inherently complicated nature of nTOS, both in cause and clinical manifestation, lend to diagnostic imprecision and ambiguity.3,34,59 A variety of techniques have been used to help diagnose this condition, including provocative maneuvers and guided injections.11,38,52,78 Physical examination findings, such as Tinel sign, tenderness over the pectoralis minor (PM) insertion, 78 positive upper limb tension test, 51 and a positive elevated arm stress test,3,5,60,67,69 are used to help identify nTOS. Other criteria, such as measurement of ipsilateral and contralateral PM length and injection of anesthetic into the PM,3,9,11,28,43,52,74,78 can be used to support an nTOS diagnosis.1,27,51,60 Furthermore, Tinel sign, the suprascapular nerve stretch test, and limited scapulothoracic motion can be used to corroborate an nTOS diagnosis.3,5,10,30,46,67 Imaging modalities, such as radiography and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and electrodiagnostic studies can also be used to help rule out other potential causes like vTOS, scapular, or nerve pathologies § ; however, there is no current reference standard for the diagnosis of nTOS. 33

Thus, the ultimate diagnosis of nTOS in patients remains unclear, 3 supporting the need for a diagnostic algorithm to streamline patient care. The Delphi method uses expert opinions to determine the importance of a particular criterion in defining an objective or condition.20,32 This method was used previously by Graham et al, 32 to create the CTS-6 diagnostic tool for carpal tunnel syndrome and by Collins et al 20 to identify the most important criteria for a cubital tunnel syndrome diagnosis. The Delphi method can serve as a valuable tool in identifying clinically relevant criteria to develop a reliable algorithm for diagnosing and treating nTOS.20,32 The purpose of the current study was to determine which criteria are the most important in diagnosing nTOS and to help establish a standardized diagnostic tool. We hypothesized that using the Delphi method among expert panelists would provide an accurate and reliable consensus regarding diagnostic criteria for nTOS.

Methods

This study was exempt from institutional review board approval. The senior authors (E.R.W. and R.L.B.) selected a panel of 20 physicians who were determined to be experts in their field. Level of expertise was based on physicians’ demonstration of knowledge at group conferences and by the number of recent publications in their fields, with each expert treating >50 patients with nTOS per year. The group included orthopaedic surgeons, plastic surgeons, fellowship-trained physical medicine and rehabilitation physicians, a radiologist/neurologist, and thoracic and vascular surgeons (Table 1).

All panelists received information regarding the study, including the purpose and background, involvement expectations, and estimated time commitment. The experts remained anonymous among themselves to avoid confounding or cross-communication. Each panelist received an online survey of 54 items relevant to the history and physical examination of a patient who may have nTOS. Similar to previous studies that have used the Delphi method,20,31,32 a scoping literature review of textbooks and journal articles was conducted to identify items related to patient history, physical examination findings, and provocative maneuvers that may be associated with nTOS. ‖ Each panelist then assigned importance to each item in reference to an nTOS diagnosis using a Likert scale from 0 to 10, with 0 representing the least important and 10 representing the most important criterion. A sample question is presented in Figure 1. Panelists were also provided with an open-ended option to propose additional criteria not included in the original item set based on their professional experience.

An example of a question from the surveys sent to the experts.

The means and standard deviations of each item’s rankings among the 20 panelists were calculated and then ranked by higher mean and lower SD. Cronbach α, which has been used in several previous studies to determine how well items relate to one another,20,32 was then used to define the concordance and consistency between the experts’ ratings. A value of ≥.9 is defined as representative of agreement among panelists and further means that a similarly matched group of expert panelists would have responses highly correlated with those of the original group.20,32 Subsequently, a corrected item-total correlation, which measures how well each item relates to the overall set, 44 was calculated for each item to determine reliability. Like Cronbach α, larger values are indicative that a particular item is a better fit to the rest of the set.

A few related items had similarly high ratings and were subsequently combined and considered as 1 item. For example, radiating symptoms with scalene pressure and scalene muscle tenderness had means of 8 and 6.8, respectively. Due to their related nature and relatively comparable ratings, these criteria were combined and considered as 1 item. Additionally, positive Tinel sign over the brachial plexus and tenderness to palpation over the coracoid process had means of 7.45 and 6.55, respectively; these items were combined due to their infraclavicular nature. Worsening symptoms with the neck and shoulder movements (7.6) and arm pain (7.3) were combined and classified as upper trunk symptoms. Furthermore, paresthesias in the fourth and fifth fingers (7.9) and weakness in the hand (6.65) were categorized as lower trunk symptoms.

Ultimately, the 5 criteria deemed most clinically relevant were determined to be (1) a positive response to local injections (pectoralis, anterior scalene, middle scalene); (2) upper or lower trunk radiating symptoms that worsen with supraclavicular provocative maneuvers (scalene pressure or positive Tinel sign over the scalenes); (3) upper or lower trunk symptoms that worsen with infraclavicular provocative maneuvers (positive Tinel sign medial to the coracoid or tenderness to palpation over the coracoid process); (4) reproduction of pain with 90° of abduction in external rotation or the upper limb tension test, and (5) participation in work or exercise requiring repetitive arm stress (overhead athletes, throwing sports, protracted posture at work, etc).

Discussion

This study established a consensus among experts as to which criteria are the most indicative of an nTOS diagnosis. Using the Delphi method, we were able pinpoint a concise list of potential clinical diagnostic criteria that encompass nTOS.19,34 Five diagnostic criteria were identified as the most representative of nTOS: (1) a positive response to injections (pectoralis, anterior scalene, middle scalene); (2) upper or lower trunk radiating symptoms that worsen with supraclavicular provocative maneuvers (scalene pressure or positive Tinel sign over scalenes); (3) upper or lower trunk symptoms that worsen with infraclavicular provocative maneuvers (positive Tinel sign medial to coracoid or tenderness to palpation over the coracoid process); (4) reproduction of pain with 90° abduction in external rotation or upper limb tension test, 51 and (5) participation in work or exercise requiring repetitive arm stress (overhead athletes, throwing sports, protracted posture at work, etc). Our study reported a high Cronbach α value of .936, indicating concordance among the expert panel.

The lack of a standardized algorithm for clinical decision-making regarding nTOS can lead to inconsistencies in diagnosis and subsequent delayed treatment among affected patients.3,6,36,53,54 The multifaceted causes, heterogeneous set of vascular or neurologic symptoms, and plethora of comorbid conditions with overlapping symptoms, such as carpal tunnel syndrome, cubital tunnel syndrome, quadrilateral space syndrome, cervical spine pathologies, scapular muscle pathologies, and PM driven scapular dyskinesia,2,39,54,76,77 further add to this ambiguity. 3 Symptom type can also affect the time to diagnosis and treatment. 73 Talutis et al 73 demonstrated that patients with vTOS are typically diagnosed earlier than nTOS patients, causing nTOS patients to present with more severe symptoms before they receive treatment. This could be attributed to the fact that vTOS symptoms have a more acute presentation and well-defined diagnostic criteria, whereas symptoms of nTOS pathology have a more insidious onset and controversial diagnostic criteria. 69 Currently, there is a paucity of studies investigating nTOS diagnostic criteria.3,50 The Society for Vascular Surgery has proposed using 3 of the 4 following criteria to establish an nTOS diagnosis: (1) signs and symptoms of thoracic outlet pathology; (2) signs and symptoms of compressive neuropathy; (3) lack of another cause; and (4) a positive response to a scalene injection.19,34 The society further expanded upon this notion, defining an expert-acclaimed set of principal symptoms, symptom characteristics, clinical history, and physical examination findings. 19 This study aimed to expand upon recommendations by the Society for Vascular Surgery19,34 and distinguish a more rigorous scientific method in the workup for nTOS.

Thus far, diagnosis of nTOS is achieved through a thorough physical examination, ultrasound-guided injections, and characteristic clinical features. Physical examination is essential in helping identify and diagnose nTOS,2,8 especially considering its prevalence among overhead athletes. ¶ The symptoms of nTOS can be divided into 4 stages of disease progression: (1) anterior shoulder pain with overhead activity exacerbated by extreme shoulder abduction and forward elevation; (2) worsened stage 1 symptoms that may radiate to the arm and shoulder with concomitant coracoid process tenderness; (3) stage 1 and 2 symptoms with defined scapular dyskinesia, neuropathy, and a positive Tinel sign; and (4) stage 1 through 3 symptoms with scapular retraction and severe scapular and shoulder pain.2,13,14 Patients may not have clear progression through these stages,2,13,14,34,65,78 however, and more severe disease states often display symptoms that overlaps with other upper extremity pathology.2,65 Furthermore, nTOS patients generally have mixed results for thoracic outlet testing, 42 such as the upper limb tension, Roos, Wright, Cyriax, and Adson tests, as these tests have high false-positive and false-negative rates.2,3,42,48,57,66,69,78 The most reliable physical examination findings are a Tinel sign at the superomedial portion of the coracoid process, a reproduction of pain and paresthesias from the elevated arm stress test, positive upper limb tension test, and decreased PM length.3,5,51,60,67,78

Imaging also plays a dynamic role nTOS diagnosis.2,3,78 Radiographs of the ipsilateral shoulder and cervical spine are commonly taken to rule out specific causes, such as cervical rib pathology, Pancoast tumors, clavicle nonunion or malunion, and vertebral transverse process enlargement.2,7,21,64 MRI can reveal inflammation of the brachial plexus and structural causes, such as nerve sheath tumors.12,79 Neuromuscular ultrasonongraphy can dynamically assess PM functionality but is highly dependent on user technique.2,3,71 These imaging techniques, however, each possess limitations in their utility for ascertaining an nTOS diagnosis.2,3,72 Currently, ultrasound-guided injections of local anesthetics are considered the gold standard for diagnosing a subtype of nTOS caused by PM pathology, known as pectoralis minor syndrome (PMS).2,3,11,29,55,70 If ≥50% of pain or paresthesia relief is achieved after injection of anesthetics into the medial pectoral nerve and beneath the PM belly, PMS is likely.2,3,29,55,68,70 Likewise, scalene blocks can be used to diagnose and relieve supraclavicular symptoms common in other versions of nTOS.2,3,11,52

It is also important to consider anatomic variants that may cause nTOS, making the diagnostic pathway more complex. Compression of the brachial plexus and subclavian vessels as they pass through the thoracic outlet, particularly in the infraclavicular and retropectoralis minor spaces or between the scalenes, can contribute to nTOS symptoms.2,3,16,17,67 Known abnormalities, such as cervical rib pathology, an enlarged transverse process, neck injury, or tumors, may cause nTOS 47 ; however, less common variants also occur. In a scoping review and meta-analysis, including cadaveric and MRI studies, Al-Redouan et al 4 sought to define the relationship between nTOS and the subclavius posticus muscle, which lies posterior to the subclavius muscle. Due to its position, the subclavius posticus muscle can increase the risk of compression of the brachial plexus and subclavian vessels at the costoclavicular space, although reportedly it is present in only 4.9% of cases. 4 These uncommon variants can contribute to the variety of ways in which patients with nTOS can present clinically, making diagnosis much more difficult. Given that anatomic differences are only present in a fraction of cases, it is difficult to capture their importance in a Delphi method study, as even experienced surgeons may have only come across them a handful of times. Instead, we highlight these variants to encourage the use of physical examination, ultrasonongraphy, and MRI3,25,37 to reveal anatomic abnormalities and provide more holistic information for diagnosis and, ultimately, surgical planning.

Furthermore, characteristics that help diagnose nTOS include its prevalence among overhead athletes. # Athletes participating in sports like baseball, softball, gymnastics, swimming, weightlifting, and tennis are at risk for developing nTOS, because they often perform movements involving repetitive PM contraction, scapular protrusion, and scapular retraction, which can inflame the brachial plexus in the retropectoralis minor space.2,3,15,23,58,62,63,75 Athletes with scapular dyskinesia are also prone to developing nTOS because their dysregulated scapular movement can similarly irritate the brachial plexus.2,3,10,26,70 Thus, a combination of these aspects of nTOS diagnosis reflected by a clinical diagnostic method would be ideal and could benefit both patients and physicians alike. Diagnostic criteria can yield more efficient and cost-effective treatments for patients and could be conducive to a more refined clinical workflow to promote better patient outcomes. Instead of pursuing many appointments with a variety of different providers for the same issue, diagnostic criteria for an nTOS diagnosis could streamline the process to not only help people receive appropriate treatment faster but also decrease the risk of further progression of disease state. Clear diagnostic methods can also help promote efficient use of clinician time and hospital resources.

Although this study contributes much to the TOS literature, limitations must be considered. Many history and physical examination findings are associated with nTOS, including but not limited to the 5 aforementioned criteria and other characteristics and symptoms, such as presence of cervical ribs, a positive Eden test, and a positive Roos test. It is possible that one of these findings may have been omitted from the original 54-item list. Furthermore, this study relies on the expertise of physicians from several different fields: orthopaedic surgery, plastic surgery, vascular surgery, thoracic surgery, and physical medicine and rehabilitation. As a result, although this study sampled the opinions of many, only a few specialists from each aforementioned field were polled, which may not be representative of each field as a whole.

Conclusion

This study proposes 5 clinical criteria for distinguishing nTOS that were agreed upon by 20 upper extremity, nerve, and vascular surgery experts using the Delphi method. These criteria not only contribute to the paucity of literature on nTOS but also serve as the foundation for determining a standardized method for diagnosing nTOS. Further research is necessary to confirm their accuracy and validity in diagnosing nTOS in the clinical setting and in guiding efficient treatment practices.
