Pipe dream: Remyelination therapy for multiple sclerosis
Authors: Benjamin Deneen
Published: 2024

In this article by Poon et al. (1) the authors identify a new therapy to treat demyelination in multiple sclerosis (MS). MS is a debilitating and unpredictable neurological disease that can have a spectrum of clinical manifestations ranging from mild motor deficits to complete paralysis, along with impaired vision, speech, and cognitive function (2). At the root of this malicious disease is loss of myelinating oligodendrocytes (OLs), which wrap neuronal axons and are essential for nerve conduction. Loss of myelinated axons culminates in disrupted neuronal function and in some cases this loss of myelin can result in neuronal death. This cascade of events is initially triggered by an autoimmune attack on the myelin sheath, the origins of which remain shrouded in mystery. To address the autoimmune component of the disease, numerous existing treatments targeting the immunological dysregulation in MS have been developed. These include over 20 FDA-approved drugs targeting the activation, infiltration, or proliferation of adaptive immune cells that appear to drive myelin injury (3). While extremely effective, these approaches do little to prevent progression of the disease (Fig. 1).

Loss of myelin on neuronal axons is a hallmark feature of MS. The PIPE-307 compound functions as an antagonist of M1R receptors, which are expressed on oligodendrocyte precursors (OLPs). This antagonism of M1R on OLPs stimulates their differentiation into myelinating OLs. While efforts to suppress the autoimmune attack on myelin are a critical part of treating MS, repairing or replacing the myelin sheath is also essential to treatment, as neuronal function and associated neurological symptoms can only be resolved when there is a restoration of the myelin sheath. Myelinating OLs are derived from OLPs, and OLPs are the among the most proliferative cells in the adult brain, endowed with a remarkable capacity to differentiate into OLs. Moreover, the cellular and molecular mechanisms driving the OLP to OL developmental transition are well defined, making these pathways key targets for the restoration of myelin (4). Despite extensive knowledge of these pathways, there remains no approved therapies for MS that specifically target the differentiation of OLPs to support the restoration of myelin.

Against this backdrop, over a decade ago, the Chan lab set out to identify new compounds that can not only trigger OLP differentiation into OLs but also support the wrapping of myelinating OLs onto axons. Using an ingenious in vitro screening system that uses micropillars to mimic an axon, they discovered that antimuscarinic compounds promote the generation of OLs that are capable of supporting the concentric wrapping of axon-like structures (5). Specifically, treatment of mice with clemastine (a first generation antihistamine with antimuscarinic properties) was able to promote the restoration of myelin and dampen neurological symptoms in mouse models of MS. Subsequent genetic studies went on to show that cell-specific deletion of the muscarinic receptor M1 in OPCs stimulates remyelination, while dampening neurological symptoms in multiple MS mouse models (6). More importantly, these studies demonstrated that remyelination could prevent neuronal loss and promote axon integrity (6). Based on these discoveries, in 2017, Dr. Ari Green published the first successful clinical trial of a remyelinating agent, finding that clemastine improved visual evoked potentials in MS patients with optic neuropathy (7).

The clemastine trial was truly a watershed moment in the design and testing of remyelination therapies in MS patients. Follow up work from the same team established that remyelination protected axons in human disease and that targeted MRI metrics have the potential to measure the remyelinating effect of clemastine (8, 9). Furthermore, genetic and chemical demyelinating models proved that the benefit on VEP was mediated via remyelination (10). However, despite genetic evidence pointing to the M1 receptor as the key target, clemastine itself does not selectively target M1. Moreover, unequivocal evidence showing M1 expression on human OPCs present in human MS is also lacking. Therefore, there is a fundamental need to further validate M1as a key target for MS, while also identifying compounds that selectively target M1 receptors as these are likely to have more potent effects on remyelination.

To answer these pressing questions, Poon et al. sought to validate the expression of M1R in OPC populations in mouse, human, and MS patients. Because the tools for detecting M1R are limited, they generated a fluorescently conjugated version of the M1R peptide antagonist and green mamba toxin, MT7, which is highly selective for M1R. Using this probe, they found that M1R protein is present on OPCs in mouse and human brain. Examination of human MS samples also revealed expression on OPCs at the border of the demyelinated lesion and normal appearing white matter regions. Critically, there are several lines of evidence suggesting that OPCs at these lesion borders are stalled and unable to differentiate into OLs (11); thus, M1R is expressed in these key target populations that may be harnessed for remyelination.

These important validation studies further support the foundational observations that inhibition or antagonism of M1R receptors on OPCs could serve as an approach to stimulate remyelination in MS. Toward this goal, the authors identified PIPE-307 as a selective M1R antagonist that is orally bioavailable and brain penetrant. Radioligand binding–based approaches demonstrated that PIPE-307 has a Ki of 4.6 nM against human M1R and has >10-fold selectivity against the M2, M3, M4, and M5 receptors. Functionally, they further showed that PIPE-307 inhibited acetylcholine-induced calcium mobilization in cell lines. Next, they measured the affinity of PIP3-307 to M1R, finding a Kd of 6.3 nM in human and 5.4 nM in mouse, while observing nominal binding in the M1R knockout. Screening a panel of 47 other GPCRs for potential off-target effects did not reveal any other substantial interactions from this panel. Collectively, these careful and rigorous studies highlight the specificity of PIPE-307 for M1R across both mouse and human.

Having confirmed that PIPE-307 selectively targets M1R, the next phase of this study focused on how PIPE-307 impacts OPC differentiation using in vitro systems. Leveraging established primary rat OPC culture and differentiation systems, the authors found that the addition of a very low concentration of PIPE-307 can promote the differentiation of OPCs to OLs. Importantly the potency of PIPE-307 was comparable to that of triiodothtronine (T3), the gold standard for stimulating OPC differentiation to OLs in vitro. An important consideration in any remyelination therapy is how much of the compound needs to be exposed to the OPCs to stimulate their differentiation. Assessing these kinetics, the authors found that an 8-h exposure of PIPE-307 stimulates OPC differentiation to an extent similar to a 72-h exposure. Finally, to determine whether the OLs generated by PIPE-307 treatment are functional, they cocultured the OPCs with axons and found that OLs form stable myelin internodes.

Moving on from basic in vitro studies, ex vivo, organotypic slice cultures were next used to evaluate the efficacy of PIPE-307 in stimulating OLP differentiation. Using a model of demyelination, the authors treated mouse brain slices with lysolecithin, an emulsifying agent that is established for inducing focal demyelination, and assessed the extent of remyelination after treatment with PIPE-307. Consistent with their in vitro studies, they found an increase in OLs. Because this system contains neurons, they next assessed myelin structure by assessing expression of markers of node of Ranvier, finding that immature nodes had formed. These data support the notion that myelin formed by OPCs responding to PIPE-307-mediated inhibition of M1R is functional. In addition, they performed studies on slices from human brains and also found an increase in OL formation. These studies in organotypic brain slices provide key evidence that PIPE-307 can stimulate OPC differentiation in the complex brain microenvironment.

There is a paucity of bona fide animal models of MS, in part because the origins of MS remain enigmatic. Nevertheless, the field uses an in vivo inflammatory demyelination model called experimental autoimmune encephalitis (EAE) as the standard for evaluating preclinical efficacy of prospective treatments for MS. Mice subjected to EAE and treated with PIPE-307 demonstrated a significant reduction in motor deficits compared to control groups. Analysis of myelin revealed enhanced myelination in mice treated with PIPE-307, as well as an increase in the number of axons that demonstrated remyelination. Finally, the authors assessed visual evoked potentials in these groups, finding that mice treated with PIPE-307 demonstrated an increase in conduction velocity, which is consistent with remyelination and parallels earlier results from preclinical work with clemastine as well as the human clinical trial. These EAE studies illustrate that PIPE-307 can functionally restore key clinical features in animals that have suffered an inflammatory, demyelinating event.

The findings in this study are the culmination of over 10 y of work by the Chan and Green labs and their colleagues. The authors are to be commended for their steadfast approach in developing these interventions and their experimental forbearance. While such drugs represent the “holy grail” of remyelination therapy, it’s important to highlight that such basic science discoveries take an incredible amount of time and effort to yield meaningful clinical results. Using a rigorous and thorough workflow that encompasses a spectrum of experimental approaches ranging from competitive binding assays to in vivo mouse models, the authors provide indisputable evidence that PIPE-307 is a selective antagonist of M1R and that this antagonism can stimulate OPC differentiation into OLs and eventually functional myelin. Given the promising results of the landmark clemastine trial, it seems as though PIPE-307-focused clinical trials in MS should be the next major step in this line of research.
