Gut microbiota immune cross-talk in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis
Authors: Megha Kaul, Debanjan Mukherjee, Howard L. Weiner, Laura M. Cox
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is a progressive neurodegenerative disease characterized by the loss of motor neurons. While there has been significant progress in defining the genetic contributions to ALS, greater than 90 ​% of cases are sporadic, which suggests an environmental component. The gut microbiota is altered in ALS and is an ecological factor that contributes to disease by modulating immunologic, metabolic, and neuronal signaling. Depleting the microbiome worsens disease in the SOD1 ALS animal model, while it ameliorates disease in the C9orf72 model of ALS, indicating critical subtype-specific interactions. Furthermore, administering beneficial microbiota or microbial metabolites can slow disease progression in animal models. This review discusses the current state of microbiome research in ALS, including interactions with different ALS subtypes, evidence in animal models and human studies, key immunologic and metabolomic mediators, and a path toward microbiome-based therapies for ALS.

Introduction

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), also known as Lou Gehrig's disease, is a progressive and often fatal neurodegenerative disorder of both upper and motor neurons. While genetic risk factors have been identified, recent studies indicate that the microbiome and immune system play an important role in disease penetrance and progression (Fig. 1). Typically, the age onset of this disease is late middle life, presenting as progressive muscle atrophy and weakness [1]. The median survival time is 2–4 years for most patients [2]. However, the survival of ALS may vary, and up to 10 ​% of ALS cases have a survival of ten years or more following initial symptom onset. ALS has an incidence rate of 2 per 100,000 patients and a prevalence of 5.4 per 100,000. It is estimated to affect some 18,000 people in the United States. Among all the cases of ALS, 90–95 ​% are sporadic, whereas 5%–10 ​% are familial [3]. Of the familial forms, the most frequent mutations are hexanucleotide expansion of chromosome 9 open reading frame 72 (C9orf72) gene (most common), superoxide dismutase (SOD1), fused in sarcoma (FUS), TAR DNA binding protein 43 (TDP43). Since the discovery of SOD1 mutations as a cause of familial ALS (fALS) in 1993, our understanding of the genetic architecture of ALS has increased substantially.

The high prevalence of sporadic ALS indicates that environmental variables mediate ALS risk [4]. Cigarette smoking, excessive physical activity, heavy metals, pesticides, electromagnetic fields, occupations, drugs, bacterial cyanotoxins, and the gut microbiome [[5], [6], [7], [8]] have been linked to the risk of developing ALS, as well as the age of onset and the severity of disease progression [4]. In addition to sporadic cases, environmental factors may also affect disease penetrance in genetically susceptible individuals [9], highlighting the importance of gene ​× ​environmental interactions [10]. These hypotheses predominantly arose from anecdotal cases, patient's medical history, risk factors identified in other diseases, spatial epidemiology studies, and biological plausibility of the molecular mechanisms involved in neurodegeneration. Many environmental factors remain to be explored, and there is an unmet need to develop methods to investigate the exposome systematically [6], including the role of the gut microbiome.

Genetic Basis and Animal Models

The loss of motor neurons in ALS results from a combination of mechanisms, including alterations in RNA metabolism, aggregation of proteins, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, cell-autonomous toxicity, and non-cell-autonomous cytotoxicity [11,12]. A pathologic hallmark of ALS is motor neuron cytoplasmic inclusions of aggregated misfolded proteins, including SOD1, TDP-43, OPTN, and UBQLN2 [[13], [14], [15], [16], [17]]. Mutations in these genes in ALS indicate a direct role in disease pathogenesis. ALS subjects with SOD1 mutations display only SOD1, not TDP-43 inclusions [16]. However, SOD1 inclusions have been detected in other genetic forms of ALS, including C9orf72 and FUS subtypes [18]. Mutations in the TDP-43 gene represent 4–5% of familial cases and 1 ​% of sporadic cases, and TDP-43 inclusions can be detected in 97 ​% of all ALS cases and are also associated with cognitive impairment, a symptom, and pathology not present in the SOD1 subtype of ALS [16]. Mutations in C9orf72 account for 40–50 ​% of familial and 7 ​% of sporadic cases and display TDP-43 and SOD1 inclusions [16,18].

The first mouse proposed for the model of ALS was the Wobbler mouse, which was discovered in 1995 by spontaneous mutation [19]. This mouse has an ALS-like phenotype, including muscle atrophy, gait abnormalities, reduced body weight, and tremor, but does not possess typical ALS pathology and carries a non-ALS linked mutation in the Vps54 gene [19]. Mouse models that overexpress SOD1, TDP-43, and FUS human variants display hallmark inclusions, motor neuron loss, gliosis, progressive weakness, and motor dysfunction and provide an opportunity to investigate complex neuropathological and genetic aspects of ALS [16,20].

SOD1

SOD1 was the first gene discovered in familial ALS (fALS) through linkage analysis in pedigrees with autosomal dominant inheritance, localizing to chromosome 21q22.1 [21]. A recent meta-analysis showed that pathogenic variants in SOD1 contribute to roughly 15–30 ​% of familial ALS cases and less than 2 ​% of sporadic cases [22]. More than 180 disease-associated variants in SOD1 have been identified [23], the majority being missense mutations, with the D90A (aspartic acid to alanine at codon 90) being the most common [24]. Therefore, depending on the variant present, phenotype, duration of the disease, and severity can differ. Individuals with mutations in SOD1 predominantly exhibit symptoms initially in the lower limbs. A minority of cases also exhibit bulbar onset symptoms [25]. Researchers found misfolded SOD1 in motor neurons in a subgroup of patients with sporadic forms of ALS without SOD1 mutations, which suggests a role for wild-type SOD1 in sALS, potentially after secondary (oxidative) changes [26].

The SOD1 protein is a homodimer copper/zinc binding enzyme, having eight antiparallel beta strands and two metal atoms that catalyze the conversion of toxic O2- anion to H2O2 and O2 [27].SOD 1 constitutes about 0.5%–0.8 ​% of the soluble protein in the brain [28]. Previously, it was believed that this mutation caused 50 ​%–80 ​% reduction in the enzyme activity [29]. Later, studies on the SOD1 knockout mouse model found that mutation-induced conformational and functional changes of SOD1 grant toxicity through interactions with proteins via several mechanisms [30]. The SOD1(G93A) mouse model has been commonly used for preclinical trials in ALS. So far, many compounds that show positive results in this model, like creatine and gabapentin [31,32],are not effective in ALS patients. Only 1–2% of ALS patients carry this mutation, and fewer carry a G93A mutation in SOD1. There is a high overexpression of SOD1, which may account for the inconsistencies between animal and human studies. One of the most common SOD1 models is the B6.Cg-Tg(SOD1∗G93A)1Gur/J mice, which carry a high transgene copy number of the G93A mutant form of human SOD1. These mice exhibit progressive motor dysfunction, paralysis, reduced survival time, and worse disease progression with antibiotic treatment [33,34].

TDP-43

Mutations in the TDP-43 gene represent 4–5% of familial cases and 1 ​% of sporadic cases [35], but TDP-43 cytoplasmic aggregates are observed in the majority of ALS cases. In addition, subjects with TDP-43 mutations had neuronal cytoplasmic ubiquitinated inclusions in the spinal cord [36]. TAR DNA-43 is a DNA/RNA binding protein that contains 414 ​amino acids and is encoded by the TARDBP gene [1]. TDP-43 regulates gene expression and involves several RNA processing pathways [[37], [38], [39]]. The mechanism of the disease is linked to the loss of normal TDP-43 function in the nucleus and a toxic gain of function. Numerous abnormalities associated with the pathological TDP-43 Include ubiquitination, proteolytic cleavage, phosphorylation, and mislocalization to the cytoplasm, leading to the formation of ubiquitinated inclusions in the hippocampus, neocortex, and spinal cord of patients and subsequent neurodegeneration. The cryptic exons in genes like STMN2, UNC13A and HGF2L are generated when TDP-43 is knocked down in motor neurons. The presence of these crypton exon-containing transcripts and their associated neoepitopes are emerging as potential biomarkers for ALS [[39], [40], [41], [42], [43], [44]].

FUS

In 2009, mutations in the gene encoding another RNA binding protein, Fused in Sarcoma (FUS), were identified in the subsets of ALS patients [39,40]. These mutations are linked with early onset and juvenile ALS, characterized by harmful aggregation of the gene occurring in patients with abnormal genetic profiles. FUS gene encodes a 526 amino acid protein, which are ubiquitously expressed throughout the body [45]. In normal conditions, it is localized in the nucleus but can cross to the cytoplasm [46]. Additionally, this gene is involved in DNA repair mechanisms and shares functional similarities with TDP-43 in several aspects of gene regulation, like transcription, RNA transport, and translation regulation [47]. Both mutated FUS and elevated levels of FUS proteins have been linked to disease pathogenesis. More than 50 autosomal dominant FUS variants have been identified in ALS patients. A homozygous knockout mouse model that did not show disease phenotype in the adult stage indicated that loss of FUS is not sufficient to cause ALS pathology [48]. Consequently, this led to the development of further models that showed gain of function mechanisms operating in FUS-ALS. The potential mechanisms causing the pathology are an increase in FUS in the cytoplasm, toxicity caused by FUS aggregates, and the spread of the disease mechanism via its prion-like protein domain. ASO therapies targeting FUS are showing some promise in clinical trials further providing clue fir a gain of function mechanism associated with these mutations [49,50].

C9orf72

The presence of hexanucleotide repeat expansion (HRE) in the first intron of the human C9orf72 gene is the most common genetic cause underlying both familial (fALS) and frontotemporal dementia (FTD) [51]. The bidirectional transcription of RNA containing G4C2 HRE foci leads to toxicity by sequestering RNA binding proteins (RBPs). Susceptibility of the sequestered RBPs to repeat association non-ATG(RAN) translation results in the production of C9RAN dipeptide repeat proteins (DPR) [52] and hindrance of coding region transcription and translation. The transcript variants of C9orf72 are reduced in mutation carriers addition to protein encoded at the locus by the C9orf72 HRE [53]. The expanded repeats cause several downstream effects on RNA processing and splicing, and form discrete nuclear structures referred to as DNA foci [54]. These, in turn, aberrantly interact with various RNA-binding proteins and impair their function [55].

While most C9orf72 mouse models fail to show motor symptoms [[56], [57], [58]], the h(G4C2)37-500 C9orf72 model was reported to have motor neuron loss, paralysis, and reduced survival [59]. However, this phenotype was not recapitulated when the mice were deposited at The Jackson Laboratory [60] even up to 15 months of age, which may be due to changes in the microbiome. The microbiome can differ between animal facilities and is a major environmental factor that affects disease penetrance [61] and should be considered an important variable in ALS research.

In addition, several genes have been found associated with the pathophysiology of ALS through recent advances in sequencing [1]. Data suggest that mutations in genes that influence RNA processing, protein homeostasis, and cytoskeletal dynamics influence ALS susceptibility, including UNC13A (Unc-13 Homolog A), intermediate trinucleotide expansions of ATXN1 and ATXN2 increase disease risk esp. in C9orf72 patients [1].

Immune System Involvement in ALS

Neuroinflammation is a hallmark of ALS in human disease and animal models and plays a role in the pathogenesis of the disease [[62], [63], [64], [65], [66], [67], [68]]. Both peripheral monocytes, which are functionally altered and invade the CNS in ALS, as well as disruptions in microglial function, contribute to the pathogenesis of ALS through non-cell autonomous toxicity [69]. Axonal degeneration is associated with gliosis within the anterior and lateral columns of the spinal cord [70,71]. A distinctive feature of ALS is the presence of eosinophilic inclusions in the motor cells (Bunina bodies), which are detected in many cases [72]. ALS was previously reported to be more prevalent in individuals with autoimmune disorders [22,73], however, recent genome-wide association studies (GWAS) found no significant connection between the two [74]. Several genes associated with ALS, such as TBK1, OPTN, and C9orf72, produce proteins involved in innate immune responses [73]. Additionally, variations in receptors for cytokines and chemokines have been shown to affect the progression of the disease [66,67]. Thus, an imbalance in immune reactions, leading to either excessive inflammation or immunodeficiency, may arise early in the progression of the disease or function as a modulator in ALS pathogenesis [74].

Microglia

In ALS, microglia show increased proliferation, activation, and production of proinflammatory cytokines and affect both disease progression or resolution [[75], [76], [77], [78], [79], [80], [81]]. Positron emission tomography (PET) imaging reveals widespread microglial activation in the brains of ALS patients and SOD1G93A mice [82,83]. Microglia arise from primitive macrophages in the mesodermal yolk sac and invade the CNS during developmental phases [84]. They are the primary immune cells of the CNS, carrying out critical homeostatic roles and undergoing context-dependent and temporally regulated changes in response to injury and neurodegenerative diseases [[83], [84], [85]]. They serve as a first line of defense within the brain and spinal cord, monitor their surroundings for signs of distress, protect neurons from infection or injury, and regulate synaptic pruning. Misfolded proteins in ALS (e.g., mSOD1) trigger microglial activation via CD14, toll-like receptors (TLRs) 2 and 4, and scavenger receptors [86,87].

Depending on the disease state, microglia can exhibit either neurotoxic or neuroprotective function. During the early progressive stage of ALS, microglia display a neuroprotective phenotype characterized by increased expression of homeostatic markers such as CD206 and TREM 2 that help with tissue repair and neuronal survival. However, as the disease advances, injured microglia transition to neurodegenerative phenotype involving upregulation of Apoe and IL-1b [[88], [89]], which is followed by an increase in proinflammatory cytokines like TNF- α, IL-1, and IL-6. On the contrary, an interesting study done by Brenner et al showed that at late disease stage of ALS, heterozygous deletion of Tbk1 alleviates microglial inflammation and slows down disease progression confirming the dynamic role of microglia in varying conditions. Furthermore, spatial transcriptomic analyses of microglia in SOD1 mice demonstrate that changes in microglial gene expression precede the onset of motor neuron pathology and neurologic symptoms [[90], [91]], suggesting that microglia contribute to non-cell autonomous neurotoxicity. Understanding this intricate interplay between microglia activation and motor neurons in ALS is crucial for the development of targeted therapeutic interventions aimed at modulating microglial function to halt or slow disease progression.

ALS-causing mutations (e.g., SOD1, C9orf72, and TDP43) occur in genes that are expressed in many cell types, including microglia. Modulating levels of mutant SOD1 exclusively in motor neurons is not sufficient to affect disease onset or progression [64,92]. However, when mutant SOD1 is selectively silenced in microglia [64] or when WT SOD1 non-neuronal cells are transferred into chimeric mutant SOD1 mice [63], motor neuron degeneration is delayed and survival significantly extended. C9orf72, which is the most common mutated gene in genetic ALS and frontotemporal dementia, is highly expressed in myeloid cells [93]. Mutations in C9orf72 result in decreased expression in patients with ALS, and its inactivation in mice results in abnormal microglia and age-related neuroinflammation [57]. Mutant TDP-43 expression in an inducible model damages the spinal cord and motor neurons, yet microglia are relatively inactive. When TDP-43 expression was turned off, microglia became reactive, cleared neuronal TDP-43, and promoted recovery from motor dysfunction. This indicates that microglia can participate in the repair process under the right conditions [94]. TDP-43 is an RNA-binding protein. Thus, in addition to neuronal damage from aggregates, it may cause disease by altering the transcriptional profile of microglia. Taken together, these studies demonstrate the importance of microglia in the disease progression and degeneration of motor neurons in ALS. Additionally some studies that performed single cell RNA sequencing of the human brain of ALS patients shows general and specific impact of glia and microglia in the disease respectively [[95], [96]].

Astrocytes

The first evidence of astrocyte changes in ALS was shown in animal models in which mutant SOD1 was selectively expressed or deleted in these cells [63]. Two separate studies showed that astrocytes in mSOD1 are toxic to motor neurons both in vitro and in vivo [97,98]. Selective silencing of mSOD1 gene in astrocytes, or transplantation of healthy astrocytes, decreased toxicity and motor neuron loss, delayed disease progression, and prolonged the lifespan of mSOD1 mice [97,98]. Conversely, transplantation of mSOD1-expressing astrocytes induced focal motor neuron degeneration and death in the spinal cord of wild-type rats [99]. In addition, astrocytes reprogrammed from ALS patient fibroblasts impaired the survival of motor neurons [100,101]. Thus, the expression of ALS-associated mutant proteins in astrocytes contributes to non-cell autonomous toxicity [100]. Both motor neurons and non-motor neurons degenerated in the spinal cord transplanted with ALS astrocytes. Importantly, non-motor neurons were lost earlier than motor neurons, suggesting that non-cell autonomous toxicity by ALS astrocytes on neural degeneration is not specific to motor neurons, and non-motor neurons might mediate the degeneration. Recently, studies have shown that astrocytes trigger motor neuron death by activation of cell death that is independent of caspase involving mechanisms called necroptosis, which involves the loss of integrity of the plasma membranes via mixed lineage kinase domain (MLKL) and receptor-interacting protein kinase 1(RIK1) [102]. Inhibition of these factors could serve as potential new therapeutic targets for halting disease progression in ALS. Of note, astrocyte function is modulated by metabolites from the gut microbiome [103]. Bacterial tryptophan derivatives and indoles are sensed by the aryl-hydrocarbon receptor and modulate CNS inflammation and type I interferon responses in astrocytes [103].

T cells

Studies done by three independent groups showed that T cells are present in the spinal cord and areas of neuronal injury, implicating a role of the adaptive immune responses in ALS patients [[104], [105], [106]]. CD4+ T cells were observed in lumbar spinal cords of SOD1G93A mice at the early stages of the disease, and both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells were present at the end stages of the disease [66,107]. Chiu and Beers reported that genetic deletion of CD4+ T cells or functional total T cells accelerated disease progression with increased expression of NOX2 and proinflammatory cytokines in mSOD1 mice. Reconstitution of T cells increased survival of mSOD1 mice in association with dampening activation of neurotoxic microglia [68,108]. This neuroprotective feature of CD4+ T cells in ALS could be attributed to their interactions with microglia and astrocytes. Beers et al. have shown that at early disease stages, endogenous regulatory T cells (Tregs) are increased in spinal cords of mSOD1 mice, followed by an increase in the levels of neuroprotective microglia phenotype and expression of IL-4 whereas during the accelerated phase of the disease, there is a decrease in the levels and expression of these cell types along with a loss of (FoxP3) expression in T regulatory cells [108]. Adoptive transfer of Tregs obtained from donor mSOD1 mice in the stable phase stabilized disease progression and increased survival time of the recipient mice [109]. Studies done in ALS patients found a decrease in Tregs both in peripheral blood and spinal cord, as well as a decrease in the levels of key regulatory molecules, including FoxP3, GATA binding protein (Gata 3), IL-4, and TGF-B. This decrease was directly linked to the rapid progression and severity of ALS. In addition, there was a strong negative association between Treg counts and disease progression rates, suggesting a possible prognostic value of these immune findings [110]. Recent studies also showed that the loss of suppressive capabilities of Tregs was not a permanent phenomenon and that these cells regained suppressive function after being removed from their environment [111]. The expression of Th17-related cytokines (IL-17 and IL-23) was elevated in the blood, CSF, and spinal cord tissues of ALS patients, suggesting Th17 ​cells might also play a role in ALS pathogenesis [112,113]. Autologous transplantation of Tregs in ALS has shown promising results in both phase 1 and open label extension trials further encouraging a larger clinical study [114,117,115].

Macrophages, monocytes, and complement system

Studies have shown that the activation of monocytes in the peripheral blood of ALS patients and the infiltration of peripheral monocytes into the spinal cord of ALS patients and animal models contribute to motor neuron loss [77]. Activated monocytes in ALS patients are functionally altered and have decreased phagocytosis [69]. Studies have shown 233 differentially expressed genes in ALS monocytes, amongst which there was a unique gene expression profile related to inflammation, including IL-1beta, IL-8, FOSB, CXCL1, and CXCL2, showing that ALS monocytes have a proinflammatory state in the peripheral circulation, which might be related to rapid disease progression [116]. In addition, an increased neutrophil/monocyte ratio was found to be associated with disease progression and the strength of these associations were also found to be age and sex specific [[117], [118]]. Elevation of complement components have been reported in the plasma, CSF, and spinal cord of patients of ALS and animal models [119]. Complement factor C1q, along with neurofilament, was found to be localized and deposited in motor end plates in intercostal muscles of ALS, suggesting an effect of complement activation on denervation in human ALS. Genetic deletion of C1q and C3 from animal ALS models did not influence the overall onset and progression, suggesting that the classical or alternative pathway do not contribute significantly to mSOD1-mediated ALS pathogenies in mice [120]. Local activation and increased expression of the C5 signaling also play a role in recruiting macrophages that might have an impact on muscle denervation and motor neuron death in SOD1 mice. The aberrant activation or regulation of the complement system might play a role in driving non-cell autonomous damage and proinflammatory immune cell recruitment, which in turn could accelerate degeneration of motor neurons [121].

Natural killer (NK) cells increase with ALS progression [122]. NK cells infiltrate the motor cortex and spinal cord in ALS patients and mouse models. NK cells are neurotoxic to motor neurons via NK2GD, and IFN-γ produced by motor neurons polarize microglia to a proinflammatory phenotype and impair Treg infiltration [123]. In addition to NK cells, dendritic cells DC are reduced and dysfunctional in ALS [124] even though DCs are increased in ALS spinal cords [125,126]. Mast cells infiltrate skeletal muscles at the neuro-muscular junction and degranulate, which helps recruit neutrophils. Neutrophils phagocytose degenerating axons and prevent reinnervation [127]. The drug masitinib targets these cells, reduces their infiltration, and slows ALS progression in patients [128].

Microbiota

The microbiota is composed of bacteria, archaea, and fungi associated with various habitats in and on the body [129]. Microbiota encodes 100 times more functional genes than the human genome, and the total number of microbial cells within the microbiota is estimated to be equal to the human body's cells. The normal gut microbiota influences the physiologic function of the host, including nutrient metabolism, xenobiotic and drug metabolism, maintenance of structural integrity of the gut mucosal barrier, immunomodulation, and protection against pathogens [130]. Multiple factors shape the formation of the gut microbiota, including the mode of delivery, diet during infancy and adulthood, the use of antibiotics, or environmental exposure to environmental compounds (e.g., pesticides and preservatives) [131]. Another vital role of our gut microbiome is pathogen displacement, in which non-pathogenic members of the microbiota outcompete virulent organisms. For example, antibiotic treatment results in an increase in pathogenic bacteria like Clostridiodes difficile. Alterations in microbiota stability may also be crucial in shielding against infections caused by potentially neurotoxic bacteria [132,133].

Microbiome gut-brain axis and homeostasis and in neurodegenerative diseases

The gut-brain axis is a two-way bidirectional signaling pathway between the GI tract and the CNS [134], which includes the CNS, microbiome, neuroimmune system, hypothalamus-pituitary axis system, enteric system, and vagus nerve. Intestinal microbiota can influence the brain via endocrine, immune, or neural signaling pathways. Enteric bacteria and their metabolites (e.g., SCFA) can directly stimulate enterochromaffin cells to produce neuropeptides (e.g., peptide YY, neuropeptide Y, cholecystokinin, glucagon-like peptide-1, GLP-2, and substance P) or neurotransmitters (e.g., serotonin, GABA), which can reach the brain and influence CNS function. In addition, the intestinal epithelium regulates the translocation of specific bacterial products such as SCFAs, vitamins, or neurotransmitters, including acetylcholine, dopamine, nor-adrenaline GABA, or serotonin into the bloodstream, which in turn, through the circulatory system can spread upwards to the CNS [135]. Bacterial-derived neurotransmitters and neuropeptides can directly activate myenteric neurons, which, through vagal nerve ascending fibers, deliver nerve inputs to the brain. In support of this view, vagotomy prevented the anxiolytic and antidepressant effects of Lactobacillus rhamnosus wild-type mice. Furthermore, intestinal bacteria and their products can modulate intestinal and systemic innate and adaptive immune cells, which then migrate to CNS and influence brain function [136].

Microbiota alterations have been reported in neurologic diseases at different ages, including autism in early childhood, multiple sclerosis and ALS in adulthood, and Alzheimer's disease and Parkinson's disease in aging [33,34]. It is unknown the degree to which age-related changes in microbiota impact the prevalence of these diseases, thus emphasizing on careful selection of methods employed for microbial sequencing, focusing on suitable anatomical compartments and time-points while simultaneously addressing diet as a covariate. These nervous system disorders may be accompanied by gastrointestinal disorders and, in some instances, are recognized as integral components of their clinical presentation [137]. Alterations in the gut microbiota, intestinal barrier, enteric-neuroimmune system, and intestinal inflammation have been reported In the initial phases of neurological disorders [[138], [139], [140]]. As the diseases progress, alterations in the gut microbiota may be amplified through both ascending and descending communications between the gut and the CNS. Thus, microbiota alterations may either contribute to disease pathogenesis or simply reflect altered GI physiology and immune status in neurologic diseases.

Microbiota Evidence in Animal Models

The microbiome has been shown to impact mouse models of multiple neurodegenerative diseases, including MS, AD, and PD, all of which are ameliorated when animals are treated with antibiotics or raised under germ-free conditions [[141], [142], [143]]. The C9orf72 ALS animal model shows improvement following microbiota depletion [144], whereas disease is worsened in the SOD G93A model after antibiotic treatment or being raised in a germ-free environment [33,145]. Transfer of microbiota from patients with autism, MS, or PD to animal models worsens disease, demonstrating that the microbiota can have a direct impact on disease processes [143,146,147]. Transfer of microbiota from ALS subjects to animal models has not yet been reported.

SOD1G93A mouse model and gut microbiota

SOD1G93A mice carry a humanized mutant SOD1 transgene with a high copy number and display progressive motor dysfunction, paralysis, and reduced survival time. The most well-recognized mechanism associated with ALS pathology is neuroinflammation, which involves both CNS resident and infiltrating peripheral immunological cells [148,149]. Recent studies have observed an alteration in the Firmicutes/Bacteroides ratio in ALS patients [150,151], and there have also been reports of gut barrier disruption and altered microbiome in ALS mice model [152]. One possibility is that a reduction in SCFAs produced by the microbiota may affect ALS patients by directly acting on CNS cells and indirectly through immune system modulation. This has been corroborated in one mouse study using the SOD1G93A model, where butyrate supplementation ameliorated disease [153]. Mechanistically, the authors showed that butyrate supplementation in SOD1G93A mice significantly decreased intestinal permeability, reduced the number of abnormal Paneth cells, and increased life span [153]. This is one of the first studies to demonstrate that gut microbiota-derived metabolites can impact ALS-like symptoms in mice.

In contrast to other models of neurologic diseases, depleting the microbiota via antibiotics worsened motor function and survival in SOD1 G93A mice in two independent studies [33,145]. Blacher et al. reported that neuroactive metabolites derived from the gut microbiota can impact ALS outcomes [33]. Administering Akkermansia muciniphila had a protective effect on disease progression and increased nicotinamide, a metabolite reported to be decreased in ALS patients [33]. Additionally, administration of nicotinamide replicated the beneficial effects of A. muciniphila, which was mechanistically linked to improvements in mitochondrial function and a reduction in oxidative stress gene expression in the spinal cord [33]. Nicotinamide is the precursor of NAD and NADP, which are coenzymes required for multiple signaling pathways [154] and are associated with neurodegeneration related to ALS [155]. Taken together, this study demonstrates that specific metabolites from the gut microbiota slow disease progression in the SOD1 model.

In addition, Xin et al. administered probiotic containing Lactobacillus acidophilus, Bifidobacterium longum, and Enterococcus faecalis (LBE) to SOD1 mice and studied its long-term impact on the disease symptoms [156]. The authors reported that administering probiotic starting at 60 days (before the onset of disease symptoms) increased survival with improved motor function in SOD1 mice compared to vehicle treatment groups in these mice. Additionally, it was observed that administering probiotics increased the levels of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) in the serum and restored the dysbiotic gut microbiota observed compared to WT mice. Recent studies have observed that SCFAs can regulate immune cells in the CNS, including microglia [156,157]. The autophagy-lysosomal pathway has been implicated in contributing to motor symptoms in ALS in both mouse models and humans due to an increased abundance of p62 in the spinal cord [158,159]. In line with this, Xin et al. observed that the protective effect of LBE administration was due to increased autophagy that promoted enhanced degradation of autophagy substrate proteins and SOD1 aggregates in the spinal cord of these mice [156].

Recent work has shed light on the different microglial states in homeostasis and disease, helping to redefine their phenotype based on their molecular signature and regulatory mechanisms. Studies related to this have identified a homeostatic state (M0) and a neurodegenerative disease (MGnD) microglia gene expression signature or disease associated microgflia (DAM) which is conserved across multiple neurodegenerative diseases, including ALS and AD [160]. A recent study by Cox et al. found that antibiotic treatment of SOD1 mice, reduced survival, increased microglial MGnD gene expression while downregulating homeostatic genes. Recent evidence demonstrates that the microbiota can regulate microglia function and is hypothesized to play a role in neurologic diseases [[145], [161]]. The extent to which the microbiome modulates microglia in ALS and the application of this novel therapeutic approach has not been explored and is an area of significant research in the future.

TDP-43 mouse model and gut microbiota dysbiosis

As reviewed above, TDP43 aggregates are detected in the majority of ALS cases, and animal models demonstrate that bacterial inflammatory cell wall products can worsen the TDP43 animal model. TDP-43 animal models expresses human mutant forms of the TDP-43 gene primarily expressed in the brain and spinal cord and develop motor dysfunction by 3 months of age and L5 motor axons loss by 10 months. Endotoxins like LPS (lipopolysaccharide) are released from the microbiota in the gut, which can act either locally or impact other distal tissues like the CNS and impact microglia activation [162]. These bacterial products can translocation through the intestinal barrier to enter the circulatory system, which is known to cause metabolic endotoxemia [163]. A study observed inflammation in the gut together with neuroinflammation in a mouse model of Gulf War disease, indicative of the fact that gut microbiota dysbiosis can cause alterations in the gut barrier permeability and lead to endotoxemia, exacerbating neuroinflammation and activation of Toll-like receptors (specifically, TLR4) in the small intestine and brain [164]. TLR4 is the receptor for recognizing LPS and activating the signaling pathway downstream, releasing proinflammatory factors and inducing inflammatory responses [165]. There has been evidence that LPS levels might be elevated in the blood of ALS patients, indicative of the fact that inflammation in the gut and microbiota changes may be attributable to the onset of ALS [166,167]. Furthermore, the addition of LPS to microglia or astrocytes in vitro leads to improper localization and aggregation of TDP-43. More importantly, it was shown in vivo in TDP-43 (A315T) mice that the addition of peripheral LPS is sufficient to cause TDP-43 aggregation [168]. All this evidence suggests that elevated endotoxin levels, together with TDP-43 aggregation, may exacerbate neurodegeneration in ALS.

A recent study investigated the potential benefit of the probiotic VSL#3 on TDP-43 mice [169]. Mice were treated with an 8-strain probiotic consisting of Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, and Streptococcus strains [170], which was previously been shown to reduce inflammation in subjects with multiple sclerosis, suggesting it may have benefit in other neurologic diseases. In TDP43 mice, treatment with VSL#3 probiotic improved motor performance as measured by rotarod performance and improved gut barrier function. Additionally, the authors reported that treatment of TDP43 mice with this probiotic significantly decreased aggregation of GFAP, IBA1, and TDP43 in the spinal cord and also reduced the levels of inflammatory cytokines in the serum, such as IL-6, IL-17, and IFN-γ [169]. In summary, these two studies demonstrate that TDP-43 pathogenesis can be accelerated by LPS-mediated inflammation or ameliorated from probiotic treatment.

C9orf72 mouse model and gut microbiota dysbiosis

C9orf72 is the most prevalent mutation found in familial ALS. It is a hexanucleotide expansion in the non -coding intron of C9 open reading frame 72. However, there are various models of C9orf72 that either have gain of function through BAC transgenesis of the human repeat expansion or viral overexpression of the mutation or loss of function of the coding gene. However, C9orf72 models shows low disease penetrance and variability between facilities which implies that the microbiota can alter each model in a specific way based on the underlying immunological and neural biology that is altered/affected. Remarkably, Burberry et al. demonstrated that the C9orf72 loss of function mice housed in Harvard University Biological Research Infrastructure in Boston, MA showed reduced survival, increased microglial activation, and increased peripheral inflammation, whereas mice housed in an animal facility at the Broad institute in Cambridge, MA did not exhibit this disease phenotype. Remarkably, transferring microbiota between C9orf72 mice induced a pro-inflammatory Cambridge housed mice and reduced inflammation in Boston housed mice [144]. In addition, antibiotics reduced markers of inflammation in Boston C9orf72−/− mice, including splenic neutrophils, microglia, and infiltrating monocytes. Microbial screening from the two facilities showed that murine norovirus, Helicobacter spp., Pasteurella pneumotropica and Trichomonas muris were significantly more common in C9orf72 animals from Harvard facility than in C9orf72 mice form Broad facility. A major question in the field is what factors determine disease penetrance in subjects with genetic risk for ALS. This study demonstrates that a pro-inflammatory microbiota is necessary for the development of motor and immunologic phenotypes in the C9orf72−/− model and highlightes a potential challenge in the field of neurodegenerative disease research using animal models in animal facilities that have differences in the gut microbial communities.

Microbiota Alterations in ALS Patients

Studies using human samples have reported microbiota alterations in ALS as summarized in Table 1 (below). Bacteroidetes (now renamed Bacteroidota) and Firmicutes represent the most prominent phyla in the gut microbiota and several studies find an altered Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio (F/B) in most cases of ALS patients. Three independent studies [150,171,172] showed a decrease in the F/B ratio in ALS patients, whereas contrasting results were found in a separate study done by Zhai et al. [173]. Recent studies have investigated which members of the microbiota are associated with clinical sub-types of ALS, emphasizing the importance of patient stratification in context to respective analysis and differences within groups as well as with each individual. Kim et al. analyzed gut and oral microbiota from patients having spinal-onset ALS and bulbar-onset ALS and found an increase in fecal F/B ratio in patients having spinal onset ALS in contrast to a decrease in F/B ratio in oral-microbiota from patients having the bulbar form of ALS suggesting that the oral- and gut-microbiome may have a differential impact on two clinically distinct subtypes of the disease [174]. Another study done by Gong et al., showed altered gut microbial communities and decreased F/B ratio in patients with cognitive impairment as compared to patients with normal cognition further indicating the need to replicate the studies using more extensive and diverse cohorts [175]. Both the Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes phyla contain potentially beneficial and detrimental species. In a recent study Nicholson et al. used metagenomics to analyze both taxonomic and functional profiles of 66 ALS patients and 61 healthy controls and found a decrease in the relative abundance of eight prominent butyrate-producing bacteria, including Eubacterium rectale and Roseburia intestinalis and increase in Prevotella copri in ALS patients [176]. A GWAS study by Zhang et al. using 2-sample Mendelian randomization shed light on a new potential bi-directional relationship between ALS and gut microbiota and found OTU10032 unclassifed Enterobacteriaceae and unclassifed Acidaminococcaceae associated with a higher risk of ALS [177]. Hertzberg et al., in a separate study compared the gut microbiota of 10 ALS patients and their healthy spouses and found more diverse gut-microbial communities with a decrease in Prevotella spp. in ALS patients indicating a possible translational value of modulating gut microbiome by depleting Prevotella spp [178]. A similar finding of increased diversity in gut microbial communities of ALS patients (non-significant) was previously demonstrated by Brenner et al. in 25 ALS patients compared to 32 healthy controls [179]. Another study using healthy family members living in the same house as household controls [180] showed changes in the composition of microbiota of ALS patients with decrease in relative abundances of Streptococcaceae and Ruminococcaceae in the patients whereas Bacteroidaceae and Succinivibrionaceae were the dominant families in healthy controls. A major challenge in finding consensus in microbiota changes in ALS are related to smaller sample sizes given that ALS has a low prevalence in the population, altered physiology and microbiota relationships between subtypes, and a rapidly progressing disease that may, in turn, affect the gut microbiota such as through gut motility or dietary changes. Studies utilizing longitudinal samples collected from asymptomatic gene carriers as well as samples from new onset ALS will be essential to determine the mechanism by which the alterations in gut microbiota effect the disease onset and progression.

Although microbiome [181,182] and plasma metabolome [10,186,183] have been separately investigated in ALS, recently a study conducted by Guo et al. 2024, identified specific microbiome-plasma metabolite correlations in ALS. This study could strengthen the robustness of potential biomarkers and offer mechanistic insight into the disease onset of ALS. The authors integrated the fecal microbiome and plasma metabolome datasets to seek microbe-plasma metabolite correlations. Network analysis of the black module, which most strongly correlated with ALS and functional status, found seven ASVs, Akkermansia muciniphila (ASV7), Lachnospiraceae (ASV148, ASV235, ASV391), Rikenellaceae (ASV51), Marinifilaceae (ASV426) and Anaerococcus (ASV1611) that correlated to 36 plasma metabolites. Most importantly the analysis revealed that 75 ​% of the metabolites that correlated with these amplicon sequence variants. ASVs were were lipids that have been linked to disease pathogenesis in ALS patients, including acylcarnitines, medium- and long-chain fatty acids of various saturation and branching, sphingomyelins and primary and secondary bile acids [184].

Study done by Davis and colleagues linked cyanobacteria (blue green algae) to ALS and showed that toxins in the algae blooms can lead to ALS [7]. As protein aggregates in motor neurons and microglial activation is known to be the central pathology involving this disease, they implied that similar pathology may be triggered by the cyanotoxin b-N-methylamino-l-alanine (BMAA). Motor neuron degeneration was seen in BMAA-dosed vervets by TDP-43+ proteinopathy in anterior horn cells, by reactive astrogliosis, by activated microglia, and by damage to myelinated axons in the lateral corticospinal tracts. Interestingly, vervets that were given BMAA ​+ ​l-serine displayed reduced neuropathological changes which in turn shows that chronic dietary exposure to BMAA causes ALS/MND-type pathological changes in the vervet and coadministration of l-serine reduces rate of progression and motor neuron damage [7]. These studies demonstrate that in addition to alterations in gut microbiota metabolites, microbial toxins may contribute to pathogenesis in ALS.

Therapeutic Approaches to Modulate the Microbiome in ALS

There are several strategies to modulate the gut microbiota. This includes antibiotics to suppress pathogenic microbes, probiotics to administer beneficial microbes, fecal microbiota transfer to recolonize a patient with the microbiota from a healthy subject, pre-biotics (fibers, etc) and nutritional interventions designed to support the growth and function of beneficial microbes, microRNAs to selectively enhance targeted microbial populations, and post-biotics, otherwise known as bacterial metabolites or other products. Given the antibiotics have been linked to an increased risk of ALS in Sweden [185], ceftriaxone has shown mixed results in phase 3 clinical trial, that the antibiotic and immunomodulatory agent minocycline led to shortened survival in a phase 3 clinical trial [186], and that antibiotics reduce survival in the SOD1G93A mouse [33,145], caution should be used when considering antibiotics for microbiome-modulation in ALS. In cases when antibiotics are needed for the treatment of infectious diseases, a probiotic following antibiotic treatment may help restore microbes that play a protective role in ALS, but this has yet to be evaluated in a clinical setting [187].

Impact of bidirectional cross-talk between gut-microbiota and nutrition in ALS

Research has highlighted the potential of the gut microbiota to modulate the quantity of nutrients extracted from food and importantly synthesize essential nutrients themselves including different vitamins [188]. FMT experiments from obese mice or humans into germ-free animals showed weight-gain phenotypes suggesting that gut microbiota can regulate metabolic phenotypes. Weight loss is associated with rapid disease progression in ALS patients [189], and studies have established that the gut microbiota can regulate weight gain [190]. Thus, targeting microbes that enhance energy extraction may help slow the progression of ALS. In animal models of epilepsy, the ketogenic diet limited seizures, which was linked to an increase in Akkermansia [191], a bacteria shown to confer benefits in the SODG93A ALS model [33]. A recent study found that administering a modified Mediterranean diet supplemented with coconut oil to induce the production of ketone bodies had a beneficial effect on increasing muscle mass and decreasing abdominal skin folds in ALS patients [192]. Whether or not dietary outcomes are modulated by the microbiota in ALS has not yet been established and is an emerging therapeutic area.

Probiotics and live bacteriotherapeutic products (LBP) in ALS

Probiotics are live microorganisms that have demonstrated beneficial functions and improve health. Live bacteriotherapeutic products (LPB) are also beneficial bacteria that have established evidence for the improvement of clinical disease or pathologic processes and have a high degree of regulation. Growing evidence indicates that administering probiotics can restore the dysbiotic microbiota and improve cognitive and emotional deficits in Alzheimer's disease [193,194], and decrease inflammatory immune responses in multiple sclerosis [170]. A recent study demonstrated that age-dependent neurodegeneration can be improved by administering probiotic Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus HA-114 in an ALS C. elegans model [195]. Additionally, both in SOD1(G93A) and TDP43(A315T) mutant mice ALS model it was reported that treating mice with probiotics improved barrier integrity and motor functions in these mice. All these reports suggest a beneficial role of probiotics in treatment of ALS symptoms and needs further investigation in humans in the future [156,169]. A clinical trial in which a proprietary probiotic was administered to five ALS subjects was completed in 2019 (NCT03324399), however, no results have been posted.

Thus far, human probiotic trials in ALS have been limited, and studies using animal models have for the most part tested conventional lactic acid producing probiotics (Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, and Streptococcus). Since a depletion of butyrate producers has been a common finding in ALS, and Akkermansia has shown beneficial effects in monocolonized mice, increasing these groups may have benefit as live-bacteriotherapeutic agents (LPBs). However, ALS patients often have changes in gut motility and increased susceptibility to infection. There have been reports of septicemia with probiotics that are generally regarded as safe (GRAS) in aged individuals or in critically ill patients. Thus, exercising caution, monitoring for signs of infection, and preparing a contingency plan for effective treatment could limit potential adverse effects of probiotics and LBPs in ALS.

Metabolite therapy

Although an area of research in its budding stage, recent studies have identified that specific metabolic therapy can alter disease progression and improve survival in ALS by targeting glycolysis and PPP in the CNS [196]. Additionally, reports suggest that metabolites from gut microbial origin (different strains) can be used to improve neuro-inflammation and also better the response to oxidative stress [197]. Bacterial metabolites, including SCFA [153] and nicotinamide [33], increase survival in the SODG93 mouse model, suggesting that these may have beneficial roles in ALS. Furthermore, the newly approved medication, AMX0035, consisting of taurursodiol (a microbially produced secondary bile acid) and sodium phenyl butyrate has shown beneficial effects in ALS [198]. These studies indicate the potential beneficial roles of microbial metabolites for the treatment of ALS. In addition, the use of metabolites has the benefit of being more amenable to therapeutic drug monitoring and precise dosing as compared to probiotic therapy.

Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) in ALS

The concept of fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) involves transferring whole microbial community from healthy donor stool into the recipient's gastrointestinal tract. The aim of this is to restore overall intestinal microbiota composition and function. The modern adaptation of this approach was developed in response to refractory Clostridiodes difficile diarrhea, which can result from a loss of colonization barrier following antibiotics. FMT provides a method to restore the disrupted gut microbial community structure in patients being treated for Clostridiodes difficile infection (CDI), with high rates of clinical cure. The restored colon microbial community could inhibit C. difficile by multiple mechanisms: competition for nutrients; direct suppression by antimicrobial peptides; bile-acid-mediated inhibition of spore germination and vegetative growth; and activation of immune-mediated colonization resistance [199]. Recently, groups have begun investigations to evaluate FMT for chronic neurologic and inflammatory diseases.

A case report from China demonstrated that a 53-year-old ALS patient who received washed fecal microbiota transplantations for her intestinal symptoms had an improvement in her ALSFRS score. She later received antibiotics due to an episode of a sudden fall at home, which then resulted in a decline in her ALSFRS score [200]. This decline recovered with consecutive FMTs, which showed that administration of high doses of antibiotics could have detrimental impact on the disease progression. A major caveat of the study was that it was a single patient case report, but nonetheless illustrates the impact of microbiota depletion and addition in the disease course of ALS.

The cross-talk between the gut microbiota-immune system and ALS has led to recent efforts to understand whether fecal microbial transplantation can impact ALS and authors are trying to see the long term impact in ALS patients at early stages of disease (NCT03766321) [201]. FETR-ALS Study Protocol is a randomized clinical trial which will consist of 28 FMT-treated patients and 14 controls with a study duration of 12 months per patient [201]. The authors propose that FMT will restore immune-suppressive Treg cell population in these volunteers and thereby reduce inflammation in the CNS [201]. Given the importance of microglia and T regulatory lymphocytes (Treg) as critical players for modifying the course of the ALS disease symptoms, and that the gut microbiota can shape immune tolerance, it is tempting to speculate that FMT might be a therapeutic intervention to restore the homeostatic gut microbiota and modulate the immune system to ameliorate disease progression of ALS. Considerations for potential infections should also be weighed, and this approach may not be suitable for patients with advanced disease.

There has been vast research focusing on the role of the immune system in the pathogenesis of ALS. In this review we elaborate the role of the microbiota-immunity axis as a critical player in the pathogenesis of ALS. ALS is a heterogenous disease with varied disease severity symptoms and evidence suggests the gut microbiota is a driver of this heterogeneity. Thus, discovering mechanisms that impact immunity by modulating gut microbiota and preventing damage to motor neurons has the potential to be a new breakthrough in treatment for ALS. In the future, identifying specific bacterial signatures and metabolites will lead to clinical trials targeting bacterio-therapies to significantly modulate the clinical outcome of ALS.
