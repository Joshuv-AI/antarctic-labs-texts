Previous oral glucose tolerance test results as predictors of gestational diabetes mellitus in subsequent pregnancies: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Li Wu, Gaojie Fan, Li Zhang, Jianing Bi, Jing Peng, Qing Liu, Qing Fang, Lulu Song, Lijuan Zheng, Guocheng Liu, Youjie Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough the oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) is the gold standard for diagnosing gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), its potential to predict GDM recurrence or occurrence in subsequent pregnancies remains insufficiently explored. This study aimed to determine whether OGTT results from a previous pregnancy could predict GDM risk in a subsequent pregnancy.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study using data from 11,397 women with two consecutive singleton pregnancies between January 2015 and July 2024, obtained from the Guangdong Women and Children Hospital. A 75-g OGTT was performed at 24–28 gestational weeks, with GDM diagnosed according to the International Association of Diabetes and Pregnancy Study Groups criteria. Logistic regression and restricted cubic spline models were used to assess associations between prior OGTT values (fasting, 1-hour, and 2-hour glucose levels) and subsequent GDM risk. Predictive performance was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic curve analysis.ResultsElevated OGTT values (fasting, 1-hour, and 2-hour glucose levels) in the previous pregnancy were significantly associated with increased GDM risk in the subsequent pregnancy. Women with prior GDM had a markedly higher GDM incidence rate (51.6%) than those without prior GDM (10.6%). Combined hyperglycemia (elevated fasting and postprandial glucose) conferred the highest risk (adjusted OR: 16.21, 95% CI: 11.17–23.93), followed by isolated postprandial hyperglycemia (adjusted OR: 7.83, 95% CI: 6.85–8.98) and isolated fasting hyperglycemia (adjusted OR = 6.36, 95% CI: 4.64–8.68). OGTT values exhibited a J-shaped relationship with GDM risk. The model incorporating all three OGTT values demonstrated improved predictive accuracy (AUC: 0.82, 95% CI: 0.81–0.83).ConclusionsOGTT results from a previous pregnancy are strong predictors of GDM risk in subsequent pregnancies. These findings support the clinical use of routine OGTT data to identify high-risk women for targeted interventions, potentially reducing GDM recurrence and improving maternal-neonatal outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12986-026-01109-0.Keywords: Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), Oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), Predictor, Recurrent gestational diabetes mellitus

Background

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is one of the most prevalent pregnancy-related complications, affecting approximately 1 in 7 live births globally. [1] The prevalence of GDM has been steadily increasing, paralleling the global increase in obesity and type 2 diabetes (T2DM). [2] In China, recent studies report GDM rates as high as 14.8% (95% CI: 12.8–16.7%) among pregnant women, highlighting it a significant public health issue. [3] GDM is strongly associated with adverse maternal and neonatal outcomes, including preeclampsia, cesarean delivery, macrosomia, neonatal hypoglycemia, and long-term metabolic risks for both mother and offspring [4–6].

Early identification and intervention are critical for mitigating these risks. The IADPSG criteria, widely adopted in China and other countries, recommend universal screening using the 75-g OGTT at 24–28 weeks of gestation. [7] While the OGTT is the gold standard for diagnosing GDM, its utility in predicting the risk of GDM in subsequent pregnancies has remained underexplored. This gap is noteworthy given that a history of GDM is one of the strongest risk factors for its recurrence in subsequent pregnancies. [8–11] Furthermore, women with a history of GDM are at a substantially increased risk of developing T2DM later in life, with recurrence rates ranging from 20% to 58%, depending on the follow-up period [12, 13].

Studies have explored various factors associated with GDM risk to predict its occurrence, including information derived from electronic health records, [14] clinical characteristics or biochemical indicators, [9, 15, 16] changes in insulin sensitivity, inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein, [17, 18] adipokines, [19] as well as maternal weight gain between pregnancies. [20] However, existing studies have predominantly focused on women with a history of GDM, while investigations in the general pregnant women exploring the association between previous OGTT values and subsequent GDM risk remain scarce. Notably, OGTT-derived glucose values from prior pregnancies may offer unique predictive insights, as they reflect a comprehensive pattern of glucose metabolism during gestation. It is plausible that glucose values obtained during a previous pregnancy could provide valuable insights into a woman’s future risk of GDM. High-risk women identified through prior OGTT values may benefit from early lifestyle modifications, [21] dietary counseling, [22] or pharmacological prophylaxis, [23] potentially reducing GDM incidence and associated complications.

To address this gap, we conducted a retrospective cohort study using data from 11,397 Chinese women with two consecutive singleton pregnancies. We aimed to evaluate the association between OGTT values from a previous pregnancy and GDM risk in the subsequent pregnancy, and to assess the predictive performance of OGTT for clinical risk stratification.

Methods

Study design and study population

This retrospective cohort study utilized data from the Guangdong Women and Children Hospital Information System, which contains comprehensive electronic medical records of 141,163 women with singleton pregnancies who delivered between January 2015 and July 2024. To create a paired cohort for analyzing recurrence, we identified all women who had at least two consecutive live singleton deliveries recorded during this period by linking records via the unique medical record number assigned to each individual by the hospital. For women with more than two deliveries, we selected their first two consecutive pregnancies within the study period for analysis. This initial linkage resulted in a cohort of 15,093 women with paired pregnancy records. We excluded 178 women diagnosed with T2DM prior to the subsequent pregnancy and 3,518 women who had no OGTT results in either the previous or the subsequent pregnancy. We did not exclude women based on other metabolic conditions (e.g., hypertension, polycystic ovary syndrome) or obstetric history (e.g., preterm birth), allowing the assessment of prior OGTT values in a broader, real-world population. Furthermore, due to data limitations, we could not systematically identify or exclude women who may have received early interventions (e.g., lifestyle modification) for GDM between pregnancies or early in the subsequent pregnancy. After exclusions, the final analytical cohort comprised 11,397 women with two complete, paired pregnancy records. A comparison of their baseline characteristics with the excluded population is provided in Table S1.

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Guangdong Women and Children Hospital (Approved Number: 202201203). Given the retrospective nature of the study and the use of de-identified data, the requirement for informed consent was waived.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the development of GDM during the subsequent pregnancy. GDM was diagnosed based on the results of the 75-g OGTT performed at 24–28 weeks of gestation, in accordance with the IADPSG criteria. Specifically, GDM was defined as meeting or exceeding one or more of the following glucose thresholds: fasting plasma glucose ≥ 5.1 mmol/L, 1-hour plasma glucose ≥ 10.0 mmol/L, or 2-hour plasma glucose ≥ 8.5 mmol/L [7].

OGTT was conducted after an overnight fast. Following a 75-g glucose load, blood samples were collected at 0, 1, and 2 h into sodium fluoride/potassium oxalate tubes. Plasma glucose levels were measured using a BECKMAN AU5800 analyzer (Beckman Coulter, USA), with intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation (CVs) of 0.64% and 0.69%, respectively.

Exposures

The primary exposures were the OGTT values (fasting, 1-hour, and 2-hour plasma glucose) from the previous pregnancy. These were analyzed both as continuous variables and as categorical variables to explore their association with the risk of GDM in the subsequent pregnancy. For the categorical analysis, based on the IADPSG diagnostic criteria and previous studies, [24, 25] OGTT results from the previous pregnancy were classified into the following five distinct patterns: (1) Normal glucose: All OGTT values (fasting, 1-hour, and 2-hour) were below the diagnostic thresholds for GDM. (2) Any hyperglycemia or prior GDM: Any single glucose value (fasting, 1-hour, or 2-hour) met or exceeded the diagnostic threshold for GDM. This category indicates that GDM was diagnosed in the previous pregnancy and serves as an aggregate of all hyperglycemic patterns (categories 3–5). (3) Isolated fasting hyperglycemia: The fasting glucose level met or exceeded the diagnostic threshold, while both the 1-hour and 2-hour postprandial levels remained below their respective thresholds. (4) Isolated postprandial hyperglycemia: The fasting glucose level was normal, but either the 1-hour or the 2-hour postprandial glucose level met or exceeded the diagnostic threshold. (5) Combined hyperglycemia: Both the fasting glucose and at least one postprandial glucose value (1-hour or 2-hour) met or exceeded their diagnostic thresholds.

Potential confounders

Potential confounders were selected based on well-established evidence from previous studies that highlighting their significant roles in influencing the risk of GDM. [26, 27] These included maternal age, pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI), parity, inter-pregnancy interval, and inter-pregnancy weight change. All information for covariates or for calculation of covariates, was obtained from the hospital information system. Pre-pregnancy weight was self-reported, while height was measured at the first prenatal visit. Pre-pregnancy BMI was calculated using the formula: weight (kg) / height (m²). The inter-pregnancy interval was defined as the time (in months) between the delivery date of the previous pregnancy and the date of last menstrual period of the subsequent pregnancy. The inter-pregnancy weight change was calculated by subtracting the pre-pregnancy weight of the previous pregnancy from that the pre-pregnancy weight of the subsequent pregnancy.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the characteristics of the study population. Continuous variables were described using means and standard deviations (SD). Categorical variables were described using frequencies and percentages. The intergroup differences for the baseline characteristics were compared using t-test or Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables and chi-square test for categorical variables.

We utilized logistic regression models to examine the association between the risk of GDM in the subsequent pregnancy and either continuous blood glucose levels at each OGTT time point or OGTT categories (any hyperglycemia or prior GDM, isolated fasting hyperglycemia, isolated postprandial hyperglycemia, and combined hyperglycemia). Models were adjusted for maternal age, pre-pregnancy BMI, inter-pregnancy interval, inter-pregnancy weight change and parity, all measured at the subsequent pregnancy. To investigate the association in a lower-risk population, we conducted a secondary analysis focusing on women without GDM in previous pregnancy.

We further performed restricted cubic splines (RCS) to examine the potential non-linear relationship between glucose levels at different time points of the OGTT in the previous pregnancy and the risk of GDM in the subsequent pregnancy. Following recommendations, [28] four knots were placed at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of the distribution of the OGTT values to capture non-linear changes at the extremes and to ensure smoother fitting in the central region. The reference points for the RCS were set according to the IADPSG criteria for GDM: 5.1 mmol/L for OGTT_0h, 10.0 mmol/L for OGTT_1h, and 8.5 mmol/L for OGTT_2h. The model was adjusted for potential confounders, including maternal age, pre-pregnancy BMI, parity, inter-pregnancy interval, and inter-pregnancy weight change. Furthermore, a two-piecewise regression model was used to explore potential threshold effects. The threshold value was determined by testing all possible OGTT values and selecting the point with the highest likelihood.

The predictive performance of the OGTT values from the previous pregnancy for the occurrence of GDM in the subsequent pregnancy was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) was calculated to assess the discriminative ability of the OGTT values. Statistical significance of the improvement in AUC after adding an explanatory factor was evaluated using DeLong’s test.

To assess the robustness of our primary findings, we performed four sensitivity analyses by restricting the analytical cohort as follows: (1) Exclusion of women with a prior diagnosis of chronic hypertension or preeclampsia/gestational hypertension. (2) Exclusion of women diagnosed with polycystic ovary syndrome to mitigate potential confounding by insulin resistance. (3) Exclusion of women with an inter-pregnancy interval exceeding 5 years, to reduce the potential dilution of metabolic continuity between pregnancies. (4) Exclusion of participants with any missing data on the covariates.

Missing data for covariates were handled using multiple imputation via the “mice” package in R. The variables with missing values were primarily inter-pregnancy weight change (6.87% missing) and pre-pregnancy BMI (4.45% missing); all other covariates had minimal missingness (< 1%). Five imputed datasets were generated, and the pooled results were used for analysis. All statistical analyses were performed using R software). A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The results of both unadjusted and adjusted models illustrate that even within the normal range, elevated OGTT values at all three time points during the previous pregnancy are associated with an increased risk of developing GDM in a subsequent pregnancy. The pattern of association between glucose values at the three OGTT time points and the risk of developing GDM remained consistent across analyses, both for the total study population and for the subset excluding individuals with GDM in the first pregnancy. These associations remained robust across all sensitivity analyses (Table S3-S6).

Discussion

This large retrospective cohort study demonstrates that OGTT results from a previous pregnancy are strongly and independently associated with the risk of developing GDM in a subsequent pregnancy. Elevated glucose levels at fasting, 1-hour, and 2-hour time points during the prior OGTT, even within the normal diagnostic range, significantly increased this risk. Notably, women with a history of GDM face a significantly higher recurrence risk. The risk hierarchy follows a pathophysiological gradient: combined hyperglycemia poses the greatest risk, followed by isolated postprandial and then isolated fasting hyperglycemia. These findings highlight the value of prior OGTT data for risk stratification in pre-conception or early pregnancy.

A history of GDM is well established as a strong predictor of recurrence, our study advances this understanding by elucidating the quantitative and pattern-specific relationships between glycemic profiles during prior pregnancy and future GDM risk, thereby shifting the focus from binary classification to a continuous, mechanistic model of metabolic vulnerability. The observed recurrence rate of 51% in our cohort is consistent with prior large-scale studies, including a meta-analysis reporting 48% recurrence [29] and a multicenter Chinese study showing 48.9%, [30] reinforcing the robustness of GDM as a recurrent metabolic disorder. However, beyond confirming this epidemiological consistency, our findings offer novel insights into how specific patterns of glucose dysregulation, particularly their combination and magnitude modulate recurrence risk along a pathophysiological gradient. The highest recurrence risk was observed in women with combined fasting and postprandial hyperglycemia, followed by isolated postprandial and then isolated fasting abnormalities. This risk hierarchy mirrors the progressive deterioration in β-cell function and insulin resistance seen in the natural history of T2DM, [31, 32] suggesting that combined hyperglycemia reflects a more advanced stage of metabolic decompensation. Pathophysiologically, fasting hyperglycemia reflects hepatic insulin resistance and impaired basal insulin secretion, whereas postprandial hyperglycemia results from peripheral insulin resistance and a deficient early-phase insulin response. The co-occurrence of both defects likely overwhelms compensatory mechanisms, increasing susceptibility to GDM in subsequent pregnancies. This mechanistic interpretation underscores the importance of early phenotyping of glucose metabolism, not just diagnosis, in risk stratification.

Importantly, our study extends beyond women with GDM by demonstrating that even within the normoglycemic range, incremental elevations in OGTT values at all three time points (fasting, 1-hour, 2-hour) are independently associated with increased future GDM risk, following a J-shaped nonlinear relationship. The two-piecewise regression further identified specific inflection points, providing reference points where risk begins to accelerate substantially within the normoglycemic range. This finding aligns with emerging evidence that subclinical metabolic disturbances, often overlooked in clinical practice may represent early markers of β-cell exhaustion or insulin resistance. A recent study by Hiersch et al. [33] similarly reported that normal-range glucose values across all OGTT time points were associated with future T2DM risk (AUC: 0.68), albeit with modest predictive accuracy. Our data extend this concept to GDM recurrence, showing that these subtle abnormalities have good discriminatory ability for predicting future GDM, particularly when evaluated in combination. This suggests a shared biological continuum of dysglycemia across different pregnancy outcomes and long-term metabolic diseases.

These insights have important clinical implications. First, current screening guidelines typically rely on categorical thresholds to diagnose GDM, potentially missing women with subthreshold but clinically meaningful glucose elevations. Our findings support a shift toward risk-adapted, continuous assessment models, where women with higher-normal glucose levels, especially those with multiple elevated time points could be prioritized for early interventions. Second, the strong predictive value of combined hyperglycemia patterns suggests that interventions targeting both fasting and postprandial glucose metabolism, such as tailored nutritional counseling, physical activity, or metformin in high-risk cases, may be more effective than one-size-fits-all approaches. Third, given that postprandial hyperglycemia emerged as a stronger predictor than fasting levels—contrary to its association with T2DM, [34] this highlights distinct pathophysiological pathways between GDM and T2DM, possibly related to pregnancy-specific insulin demands and placental hormone-induced insulin resistance. This distinction should inform the design of pregnancy-specific prevention strategies.

Compared to earlier work, such as the Italian study linking 1-hour and 2-hour glucose levels to GDM risk, [35] our analysis provides a more comprehensive picture by incorporating all three OGTT time points and defining distinct glycemic patterns. Furthermore, while Wu et al. demonstrated high predictive accuracy using early pregnancy OGTT (AUC: 0.872), [36] the clinical utility of early screening may be limited by challenges in timely intervention and uncertainty about whether hyperglycemia before 20 weeks represents true early GDM. [37, 38] In contrast, our approach leverages routinely collected data from the standard 24–28 weeks OGTT, making it more feasible for integration into existing care pathways. This approach holds particular promise given that China’s universal mid-trimester OGTT screening generates comprehensive glucose metabolism profiles for virtually every pregnant woman, and with over 50% of births now being second or higher-order children, [39] this creates a unique opportunity for secondary prevention without requiring additional testing resources. Furthermore, the integration of OGTT results with other risk factors, such as maternal age, pre-pregnancy BMI, and inter-pregnancy weight gain, could enhance the accuracy of GDM risk prediction models, enabling more personalized and effective prevention strategies.

The strengths of our study include the large sample size and the use of comprehensive medical records from a well-established hospital information system. Furthermore, all study subjects were tested using a standardized 75-g OGTT, which was administered uniformly across the same hospital, thereby minimizing the potential for misclassification bias. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design may introduce selection bias, and although the differences of some covariates between included and excluded subjects were relatively small, the non-random selection may bias results despite adjustment. Second, the prior OGTT values represent a single time point in mid-pregnancy and may not capture the full dynamic spectrum of glycemic metabolism during that gestation. Third, although we adjusted for important potential confounders such as age, pre-pregnancy BMI, unmeasured confounders such as family history of T2DM, dietary habit, and physical activity between pregnancies or in early subsequent pregnancy may influence the observed association. Fourth, as a single-center study in China conducted after the relaxation of the one-child policy, our findings require validation in multi-ethnic and multi-center cohorts from diverse settings to confirm generalizability.

Conclusions

Our study demonstrates that OGTT results from a previous pregnancy are a powerful predictor of GDM risk in subsequent pregnancies. These findings underscore the potential of routine OGTT screening data to identify high-risk women and inform targeted interventions. Future research should explore the integration of OGTT results with other biomarkers and risk factors to further refine GDM risk prediction and improve maternal and neonatal health outcomes.
