Non-contact alcohol detection in mice using an imaging photoplethysmography (IPPG) system
Authors: Jea Young Choi, Kanghae Kim, Joo Beom Eom
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe report on a method to determine whether alcohol has been consumed using camera-based, non-contact imaging photoplethysmography (IPPG) technology. IPPG signals were collected using a visible-band CMOS camera and a 520 nm light-emitting diode (LED). The light source was set to a uniform light output of 2.8 mW/cm², and images were captured at 150 frames per second. To compare signal changes before and after alcohol consumption, hairless mice were orally administered alcohol, and IPPG signals were collected hourly. Signal analysis assessed alcohol consumption over time using frequency components of respiration and heart rate, second-harmonic components, and the frequency-to-magnitude ratio. Furthermore, alcohol consumption was assessed by analyzing the magnitude ratio and scatter plot at various frequencies, and the system’s validity was confirmed through repeated experiments. These results clearly demonstrate that non-contact IPPG technology can be used as an effective and reliable tool for detecting alcohol consumption.Keywords: Imaging Photoplethysmography (IPPG), Non-contact measurement, Camera-based, Alcohol detectionSubject terms: Biological techniques, Engineering, Health care, Medical research, Optics and photonics

Introduction

In the United States, drunk driving resulted in 12,429 fatalities in 2023, with an annual average of approximately 11,000 deaths over the 10-year period from 2014 to 20231. Drunk driving increases the risk of accidents while driving by impairing cognitive function, slowing reaction times, and reducing the ability to respond to emergencies2,3. While it is fundamentally right to avoid driving after drinking, the most fundamental solution for those who have consumed alcohol is to detect driver intoxication and prevent them from attempting to drive. To this end, numerous research groups are researching the possibility of connecting breathalyzer sensors to a vehicle’s ignition system to prevent the vehicle from starting when a certain alcohol concentration is reached.

The current gold standards for measuring individual alcohol concentration are blood and breath tests. Breath tests are the most commonly used method, obtaining alveolar breath samples to estimate breath alcohol concentration (BrAC)4,5. While this method provides immediate numerical results, it is inconvenient because the user must carry the device at all times4,6. Furthermore, concerns have been raised that direct exhalation may be a vector for the transmission of respiratory infections such as COVID-197. To address this issue, non-contact systems using infrared sensors to measure diluted breath alcohol concentration have been studied8. However, these methods have limitations, such as the potential for alcohol dilution due to air pollution or indoor ventilation, making accurate measurements difficult8. Recently, breathalyzer-based ignition interlock devices have been used in vehicles to prevent drunk driving4. However, major drawbacks of this method include the difficulty in identifying the driver’s face and the potential for measurement errors due to the breathing of passengers.

Another method is blood testing. Blood testing measures blood alcohol concentration (BAC) from blood samples collected after alcohol consumption9. While this invasive method can accurately measure alcohol concentration, it requires blood sampling, which can cause pain and psychological discomfort to the subject, and is time-consuming and expensive9,10. Furthermore, this method is nearly impossible to install in vehicles, making it difficult to collect samples or install testing equipment. To address these invasive methods, a non-invasive study using near-infrared spectroscopy to measure alcohol concentration in forearm tissue has been conducted11. While this method can quantify alcohol, the use of spectroscopic equipment makes it difficult to install in vehicles12.

Recently, research has been conducted to improve upon the shortcomings of these existing methods, focusing on determining whether alcohol has been consumed based on the direct physiological changes alcohol causes in the human body, rather than directly measuring alcohol concentration. Representative methods used to determine alcohol intoxication utilize changes in physiological signal characteristics, such as electrocardiogram (ECG) and electroencephalogram (EEG)13–17. For instance, EEG signals have been utilized to screen for alcoholism with high accuracy using advanced signal processing techniques. While these contact-based approaches, including ECG analysis, offer the advantage of real-time detection, they have the disadvantage of being difficult to apply in real-world vehicle environments due to movement limitations and the need to attach electrodes to the body or scalp for data collection. Another study proposed a method to detect pupil size before and after drinking18. While this non-contact method, utilizing a camera, offers the advantage of being installed inside a vehicle, it requires the user to hold their eyes close to the camera, as it does not automatically recognize the user’s eyes18. Furthermore, pupil size can change due to light, which can lead to accuracy issues depending on the environment.

To solve these complex systems and inconvenient measurement issues, photoplethysmography (PPG) has been used for alcohol detection using biosignals19,20. This technology uses a light source and a photodetector to detect changes in blood flow within blood vessels19,20. Using this principle, changes in respiration and pulse were detected using PPG signals before and after alcohol consumption21. The results of this study demonstrate that PPG signal detection is a useful tool for distinguishing changes in alcohol concentration. However, although this method can be used as a simple measurement system using a finger or wristband, it is inconvenient for drivers because it is attached to the body, and it has the fatal drawback of making it difficult to identify the driver’s face22,23.

Therefore, unlike previous studies that used only PPG measurements to determine intoxication, this paper introduces a novel technique using camera-based imaging photoplethysmography (IPPG) to recognize drivers and measure biosignal changes associated with alcohol consumption. IPPG uses a camera and a light source to detect dynamic blood volume changes within blood vessels and extract blood flow pulse signals24,25. In this study, we observed changes in respiration and pulse rate using a 520 nm green light source and a CMOS camera. To verify the proposed system’s alcohol consumption detection performance, we constructed an alcohol-intoxicated model using hairless mice and analyzed IPPG signals. Measurements were repeated during drinking and recovery. These experimental results demonstrate the potential of the proposed technology as a noninvasive, non-contact device for assessing driver alcohol consumption by recognizing drivers’ faces.

Materials and methods

Imaging system with green light source for measuring alcohol concentration

As shown in Fig. 1a, the alcohol consumption detection system consists of a light source and a camera system. The light source used was a 520 nm LED (2835LGC-X20; Best LED Opto-electronic, Guangdong, China). Forty LEDs were arranged in a ring shape to provide uniform illumination throughout the entire chamber, and the camera was placed centrally. The experiment was conducted in a darkroom, with a uniform illumination of 2.8 mW/cm² applied to the data acquisition area within the mouse cage. The camera used was a dual-channel camera system (FS-1600D-10GE; JAI, Miyazaki, Japan) equipped with both visible and near-infrared image sensors. The camera was equipped with a lens (58001, Edmund Optics) for focusing on the sample. The distance from the sample to the lens was approximately 16 cm. Camera parameter control and data acquisition were managed using a custom LabVIEW-based program. The frame rate and exposure time were optimized for optimal signal quality, with final settings of 150 fps and 3000 µs, respectively. IPPG signal acquisition was performed after setting the ROI at the same location for all acquired images.

Schematic diagram of the non-contact IPPG measurement system for biosignal acquisition. (a) The schematic diagram of the imaging system. (b) Experimental system setup.

Designing an animal experimental model

To determine alcohol intake using the proposed system, a mouse model with physiological characteristics similar to humans was employed26,27. In this study, male hairless mice (SKH1-hr, Orientbio, Seongnam, Korea) weighing 25–35 g were used, with variables such as strain and housing environment being controlled. To ensure validity, safety, and repeatability before applying the system to humans, mice of the same species were used to minimize inter-individual variation in physiological responses. Furthermore, to demonstrate that the results were not age-dependent, two age groups of mice, 7- and 30-week-old, were set as experimental subjects. To minimize factors other than alcohol, the mice underwent a 24-hour acclimation period in their cages before the experiment and were deprived of water and food for 6 h prior to alcohol administration.

To evaluate the effects of alcohol consumption on physiological signals, mice were divided into an control group (N = 5) and a experimental group (N = 9). For both groups, data collection began during stable moments without movement, such as turning or grooming, during the measurement period. Additionally, both groups were not anesthetized, as anesthetics can alter cardiovascular, autonomic, and respiratory functions, potentially causing errors in IPPG measurements and interfering with the pure physiological effects of alcohol28,29.

First, the control group consisted of 5 mice (N = 5) that were not administered alcohol. The control group was monitored in the same environment as the experimental group to confirm that the changes in respiratory rate and heart rate of the experimental group were due solely to alcohol intake, not to the influence of the experimental environment (e.g., stress related to the imaging environment) or movement.

Subsequently, physiological signals were compared before and after alcohol consumption in 9 mice (N = 9) of the experimental group. To observe the physiological changes induced by alcohol, a dose of 3.15 g/kg body weight was administered directly using an oral gavage needle30. Data were acquired during stable, motionless moments, and were collected repeatedly at 30-minute intervals for up to 90 min, including a non-administered baseline state.

All experimental procedures were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations, including the ARRIVE guidelines and the standard operating procedures of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety, and were approved by the Dankook University Animal Care Committee (DKU-25-010).

Signal processing

Figure 2 shows the block diagram for the signal acquisition and processing pipeline of the IPPG system. First, a Region of Interest (ROI) for signal extraction was set by monitoring the real-time video stream using a LabVIEW-based program. Subsequently, video data from the set ROI was acquired for 20 s at a rate of 150 fps (frames per second) and stored on a computer via a frame grabber. The acquired video frames were first converted to grayscale, and the raw IPPG signal, which presents a sinusoidal waveform, was then generated by plotting the average grayscale intensity within the ROI for each frame over time.

Block diagram of the process for acquiring key features from the non-contact IPPG system.

Prior to frequency analysis, the DC component was removed to isolate only the AC component, which contains the subtle fluctuations caused by heartbeats and respiration. To convert the preprocessed AC signal into the frequency domain and obtain a stable power spectral density (PSD) estimate, Welch’s method was employed. Unlike applying a single Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) to the entire dataset, Welch’s method improves the signal-to-noise ratio by computing the FFT for multiple signal segments and then averaging their individual results. The process involved dividing the signal into 5-second segments with a 75% overlap. The FFT was then computed for each individual segment to generate its periodogram. These periodograms were then averaged together. This averaging technique effectively reduces random noise and produces a smoother spectrum, though at the cost of a slight reduction in frequency resolution31. Finally, to clearly isolate the heart rate signal, which is relatively small compared to the respiratory signal, a Notch filter was applied to remove the respiratory frequency and its harmonic components, thereby distinctly extracting the heart rate signal. Additionally, to exclude exercise-induced increases in heart rate and respiration rate, biosignals were measured in a confined space that minimized mouse movement during measurement.

The final stage of signal processing was to define features capable of quantifying the physiological changes induced by alcohol. From the processed frequency spectrum, the magnitudes of key physiological frequency components were extracted. Specifically, the magnitudes of the fundamental frequencies for respiration (MRR) and heart rate (MHR), as well as their second harmonics (M2nd RR and M2nd HR​), were identified. Based on these extracted values, three key feature ratios were defined to quantify the physiological changes due to alcohol intake. These were the ratio of respiration to heart rate magnitude (MRR / MHR), the ratio of the second harmonic of heart rate to its fundamental magnitude (M2nd HR / MHR), and the ratio of the second harmonic of heart rate to the second harmonic of respiration magnitude (M2nd HR / M2nd RR). The biosignal acquisition algorithm, including Welch’s method and the Notch filter, was implemented using MATLAB.

Statistical analysis

To test the statistical significance of the differences observed among the groups in this study, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed for the three key ratios (MRR / MHR), (M2nd HR / MHR), (M2nd HR / M2nd RR). When a significant difference was found in the ANOVA results (p < 0.05), a post-hoc test was conducted using Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) method to determine which specific pairs of groups differed. All statistical analyses were performed using MATLAB, and the criterion for statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Analysis and statistical significance of key magnitude ratios

It has been reported that changes in respiration and heart rate due to exercise only involve changes in frequency, not magnitude, while changes in alcohol consumption involve changes in both frequency and magnitude21. Therefore, to more sensitively distinguish alcohol consumption status from IPPG signals, we introduced and analyzed the relative magnitude ratio of at various frequencies as a new feature.

The frequency magnitude component of respiration is denoted as MRR, and the frequency magnitude component of heart rate is denoted as MHR. The second harmonics of these components are defined as M2nd RR and M2nd HR, respectively. Figure 5 shows the quantitative changes in three magnitude ratios over time after alcohol consumption. First, Fig. 5a shows the ratio of the magnitude of the RR frequency component to the magnitude of the HR (MRR / MHR) over time. (MRR / MHR) showed a decreasing trend after alcohol consumption, reaching its lowest point at 60 min, and then increasing again at 90 min. Second, Fig. 5b shows the ratio of the magnitude of the HR frequency components and the second harmonics (M2nd HR / MHR) over time. Unlike Fig. 5a, this ratio showed an increasing trend, reaching a peak at 30 min, and then decreasing at 60 and 90 min. Finally, Fig. 5c shows the ratio of the magnitude of the HR frequency components and the second harmonics of the RR frequency components (M2nd HR / M2nd RR) over time. This ratio also showed an increasing trend, reaching a peak at 60 min, and then decreasing at 90 min. Consequently, consistent and distinct dynamic patterns of change were confirmed in all three proposed magnitude ratios over time after alcohol consumption.

The analysis results showed that all three ratios showed a significant change between 30 and 60 min after alcohol consumption compared to the initial value. (MRR / MHR) decreased by up to – 33.0%, while (M2nd HR / MHR) and (M2nd HR / M2nd RR) significantly increased by + 143% and + 278%, respectively. The overall ratio changes values returned to a level close to the initial value after 90 min. This means that the blood alcohol concentration is broken down to some extent after 90 min of alcohol consumption. (MRR / MHR) recovered to – 4.3% compared to the initial value, and (M2nd HR / MHR) and (M2nd HR / M2nd RR), which had increased significantly, also decreased to + 52.4% and + 66.0%, respectively, showing a tendency to approach the initial value.

To test the statistical significance of the group differences observed in this study, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed on the three main ratios of the magnitude of respiratory and heart rate frequencies ratios (MRR / MHR), (M2nd HR / MHR), (M2nd HR / M2nd RR).

To verify the statistical significance of the three main characteristics observed in Fig. 5, a significance test was performed on three groups: ‘before alcohol consumption’, ‘after alcohol consumption’, and ‘90 minutes after alcohol consumption’, and the results are shown in Fig. 6.

Statistical significance analysis of key features among groups.

As a result of the analysis, it was found that the mean differences among the groups were statistically highly significant for all three magnitude ratios. From the ANOVA result for the (MRR / MHR), a statistically significant difference was confirmed among the groups (F(2, 24) = 8.60, p = 0.0015), and the Tukey post-hoc analysis showed that the ‘After Alcohol intake’ group was significantly different from both the ‘Before Alcohol intake’ group (p = 0.0027) and the ‘90 minutes after alcohol intake’ group (p = 0.0064), respectively. In contrast, no significant difference was found between the ‘Before Alcohol intake’ and ‘90 minutes after alcohol intake’ groups (p = 0.9000).

The (M2nd HR / MHR) and (M2nd HR / M2nd RR) showed a much higher level of statistical significance. The (M2nd HR / MHR) showed a very strong inter-group difference as a result of the ANOVA (F(2, 24) = 20.33, p < 0.0001), and the post-hoc analysis also confirmed a distinct difference between the ‘After Alcohol intake’ group and both the ‘Before Alcohol intake’ group (p < 0.0001) and the ‘90 min after alcohol intake’ group (p = 0.0021). Similarly, the ANOVA result for the (M2nd HR / M2nd RR) was also significant (F(2, 24) = 8.86, p = 0.0013), and the post-hoc analysis indicated that the ‘After Alcohol intake’ group was statistically and significantly different from both the ‘Before Alcohol intake’ group (p = 0.0002) and the ‘90 minutes after alcohol intake’ group (p = 0.0463).

The fact that the ‘After alcohol intake’ group showed a distinct difference from the other two control groups for all magnitude ratios shows that the observed change is a statistically significant response caused by the specific condition of alcohol intake.

Comparison of data distribution in feature space

Figure 7 shows a scatter plot of the changes in the data distribution of nine mice to determine how effectively the pre- and post-alcohol consumption states can be separated, based on data from three different scales of the magnitude of the frequency signals of respiration and heartbeat in the experiment. At this time, considering that there are differences in the physiological response speed and breakdown of alcohol in each mouse, the data at the time when the effect of alcohol is most prominent in the mouse among the data 30 and 60 min after alcohol consumption were selected as the state after alcohol consumption. The scatterplot of the three ratios of respiratory and heart rate frequency magnitudes, shown in Fig. 7, clearly shows a distinct and consistent pattern between pre- and post-alcohol consumption. The pre-alcohol data is indicated by a red circle, and the post-alcohol data is indicated by a black circle. Figure 7(a) shows a scatter plot of (M2nd HR / MHR) and (M2nd HR / M2nd RR). A notable feature of this graph is that the distribution of the scatter plot tends to shift upward to the right after alcohol consumption. However, Fig. 7(b) shows a scatter plot of (M2nd HR / MHR) and (MRR / MHR), and Fig. 7(c) shows a scatter plot of (M2nd HR / M2nd RR) and (MRR / MHR), and both graphs show a tendency to shift downward to the right after alcohol consumption. These results show that not only the three ratios of the magnitude of the frequency of breathing and heartbeat in Fig. 5, but also the distribution of the scatter plot between each ratio are useful data for classifying drinking, and also confirm the validity of non-contact drinking measurement using a camera.

Conclusions and discussion

We propose a non-contact, non-invasive technique for assessing drinking status by measuring IPPG signals before and after drinking, using a camera and a 520 nm wavelength green LED light source. This system captures IPPG signals at 150 frames per second using a visible light camera. To evaluate the performance of the proposed system, a drinking model was created using hairless mice. To obtain stable signals, mice are usually anesthetized, but since anesthesia changes breathing and heart rate, anesthesia was not performed. In addition, to minimize changes in breathing and heart rate due to exercise, the cage was minimized and the IPPG signals were measured before and after alcohol consumption of the mice.

Obtained IPPG signals revealed distinct changes in heart rate and respiration after alcohol consumption. Not only the frequency and magnitude of heart rate and respiration, but also the rate of change in second-harmonic magnitudes consistently changed. The rate of change in frequency and magnitude was most prominent approximately 30–60 min after alcohol consumption, and after 90 min, all changes returned to the level before alcohol consumption. The three ratios of heart rate and respiratory magnitude changes presented in this experiment clearly differed depending on whether or not alcohol was consumed, as shown in the scatter plots before and after drinking. Furthermore, statistical analysis of the frequency-magnitude change ratios also revealed significant and consistent patterns. Therefore, the validity of camera-based intoxication assessment was demonstrated.

Changes in frequency magnitude after alcohol consumption are attributed to changes in the pulse waveform, which are due to alcohol-induced vasodilation and relaxation, such as decreased peripheral vascular resistance and arterial stiffness32,33. Increased arterial stiffness has been reported to accentuate the detailed contours of the heartbeat waveform, including the second harmonic component, thereby affecting the magnitude of the second harmonic component32–36. This confirms the same result in this study, where a decrease in arterial stiffness due to alcohol consumption increased the magnitude of the harmonic component. Moreover, the results of this study were somewhat contradictory to previous studies on the frequency change ratio between respiration and heart rate21. Previous studies were contact studies on humans. It is known that mouse PPG signals are more affected by respiration than human PPG signals37,38. Therefore, this may be due to physiological differences between mice and humans, such as heart rate, respiration rate, and alcohol metabolism, which are major factors affecting PPG signals. However, the results of this study were consistent in repeated experiments on mice. Future confirmation through clinical trials on humans is necessary.
