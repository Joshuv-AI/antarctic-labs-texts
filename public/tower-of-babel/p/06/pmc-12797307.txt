Hematogenous dissemination of malignant pleural mesothelioma: An autopsy case of gallbladder metastasis causing obstructive jaundice
Authors: Shouichi Okamoto, Mariko Shinomiya
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Malignant pleural mesothelioma (MPM) is an aggressive cancer with poor prognosis. Although hematogenous metastasis is rare, we report an autopsy case in which gallbladder metastasis had caused fatal obstructive jaundice. A 72-year-old man, who had experienced long-term exposure to asbestos, was diagnosed with epithelioid MPM. Despite immuno- and chemotherapy, the disease progressed. Nineteen months later, the patient was admitted with jaundice and renal failure. Imaging showed mild gallbladder wall thickening, but no overt obstruction. The patient died 10 days after admission. An autopsy revealed widespread metastases, including full-thickness gallbladder infiltration and bile stasis in the liver. Acute tubular necrosis of the kidneys and bile protein casts in the renal tubules suggested bile cast nephropathy. The findings of this case suggest that gallbladder metastasis from MPM may cause obstructive jaundice and multi-organ failure, potentially contributing to death. Clinicians should be aware of this rare metastatic pattern when evaluating liver dysfunction in patients with advanced MPM.

Introduction

Malignant mesothelioma is an aggressive tumor arising from the pleura, peritoneum, pericardium, or tunica vaginalis. Despite recent advances in treatment, the prognosis for unresectable malignant pleural mesothelioma (MPM) remains poor, with a median overall survival of only 12–18 months [1]. MPM typically invades adjacent structures, such as the chest wall, diaphragm, and mediastinum; it also spreads to the regional lymph nodes. Hematogenous dissemination may occur as the disease progresses. However, little is known about the clinical impact of distant metastases, particularly whether organ dysfunction due to these metastases contributes directly to mortality.

Herein, we report an autopsy case of MPM with widespread hematogenous metastases, including to the gallbladder, which led to obstructive jaundice and liver dysfunction.

Case presentation

A 72-year-old man who had never smoked presented at our hospital with a 1-week history of exertional dyspnea. His medical history included atrial fibrillation and a mild cerebral infarction. The patient had a 30-year history of occupational asbestos exposure. Chest computed tomography (CT) revealed left-sided pleural effusion and bilateral pleural plaques. After thoracentesis and positron emission tomography/CT, the patient was diagnosed with epithelioid MPM, classified as cT2N0M0 stage IB, according to the 8th Edition TNM Classification of the Union for International Cancer Control [2].

After chest tube drainage and pleurodesis, the patient received three cycles of nivolumab and ipilimumab. However, the disease was assessed as progressive, and treatment was discontinued owing to the development of immune checkpoint inhibitor-associated pneumonitis, which subsequently improved with corticosteroid therapy.

Fourteen months after the diagnosis, the disease had progressed and metastasized to the intrathoracic and axillary lymph nodes. Second-line chemotherapy using carboplatin and pemetrexed was initiated, which resulted in temporary tumor reduction. However, after four cycles, CT revealed new intraperitoneal lymphadenopathy. Radiation therapy was administered to treat cancer-related pain.

Nineteen months after the diagnosis, the patient was admitted with anorexia, nausea, peripheral edema, and jaundice. Laboratory tests revealed elevated liver enzyme levels (total bilirubin (7.0 mg/dL), aspartate aminotransferase (AST, 125 IU/L), alanine aminotransferase (ALT, 132 IU/L), alkaline phosphatase (ALP, 1,116 IU/L), and gamma-glutamyl transpeptidase (GTP, 1,707 IU/L)), along with impaired renal function, (creatinine (2.7 mg/dL), blood urea nitrogen (BUN, 51.7 mg/dL), and potassium (5.2 mEq/L)). Chest and abdominal CT revealed new mediastinal lymph node metastases and an increase in the number of subcutaneous lesions (Fig. 1A). The disease was reclassified as cT4N2M1 (stage IV). The patient exhibited marked fatigue, and continuous subcutaneous infusions of morphine and haloperidol were initiated on hospital Day 3. Ursodeoxycholic acid was administered from the time of admission to address liver dysfunction. However, total bilirubin and ALP levels, both indicative of cholestatic liver injury, continued to increase, reaching 15.5 mg/dL and 1,973 IU/L, respectively, by Day 7. In contrast, AST and ALT levels increased more moderately, peaking at 239 and 171 IU/L, respectively, whereas GTP showed a slight decline to 1,574 IU/L.

Treatment with calcium polystyrene sulfonate, a cation-exchange resin, was initiated to manage hyperkalemia. Despite this intervention, the potassium levels rose to 6.8 mEq/L, creatinine increased to 8.3 mg/dL, and BUN reached 126.2 mg/dL by Day 7. In parallel, the urine output decreased progressively, leading to oliguria. The patient's clinical status continued to worsen, and he died on Day 10.

A full-body autopsy, which excluded the brain, was conducted to determine the cause of death and to assess the extent of tumor dissemination. Histopathological examination revealed widespread metastatic involvement of both sides of the thoracic cavity (Fig. 2A), the lungs, left intercostal muscles, diaphragm, peritoneum, liver, gallbladder, kidneys, spleen, pancreas, subcutaneous tissue, and lymph nodes. Immunohistochemical staining revealed that the tumor cells were positive for calretinin and D2-40, and negative for carcinoembryonic antigen (Fig. 2B–D), confirming their mesothelial origin. Discussion

MPM primarily progresses via local invasion and intrapleural dissemination. As the disease advances, it may also spread via the lymphatic or hematogenous systems, although distant metastasis is uncommon. However, in an analysis of 318 autopsy cases, 87.7 % of MPM cases demonstrated extrapleural dissemination and 55.4 % showed extrathoracic metastasis, with more than five organs involved in 7.1 % of the cases [3]. Common extrathoracic sites included the liver (31.9 %), peritoneum (24.4 %), bones (13.8 %), and abdominal lymph nodes (11.7 %). Notably, no cases of gallbladder involvement were reported in that study [3]. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report of gallbladder and peribiliary tract metastases originating from MPM. The presence of tumor cells within the blood vessels of the gallbladder strongly suggests hematogenous dissemination.

Determining the precise cause of death in patients with advanced malignancies, including MPM, is generally difficult for clinicians. In a review of MPM, death was most commonly caused by infection or respiratory failure in association with the systemic symptoms of advanced disease, whereas metastatic spread was considered a less direct contributor [4]. The primary symptoms of MPM, such as dyspnea and chest pain, tend to worsen gradually over time. As the tumor invades the chest wall and adjacent structures, symptoms may intensify and lead to functional impairments such as restricted respiratory movement, dysphagia, vocal cord paralysis, and diaphragmatic paralysis [4,5]. Considering this clinical trajectory, respiratory failure is thought to account for a large proportion of MPM-related deaths. Furthermore, arrhythmia or heart failure due to cardiac or pericardial invasion, and bowel obstruction resulting from peritoneal metastasis can be life-threatening complications [6]. However, reports definitively identifying distant metastasis as the primary cause of death in MPM are exceedingly rare. Even in autopsy-based studies, the cause of death is clearly established in only 19.8 % of cases, with pneumonia, pulmonary thrombosis, and cardiac tamponade being among the few identified causes [3].

In the present case, the extensive gallbladder metastasis resulted in severe obstructive jaundice and progressive liver dysfunction. Simultaneously, renal failure developed owing to acute tubular necrosis accompanied by worsening hyperkalemia. Necrosis of the renal tubules and bile casts in the renal tubules were implicated in kidney injury secondary to obstructive jaundice, a condition referred to as bile cast nephropathy [7,8]. Based on the clinical course and pathological findings, obstructive jaundice likely led to liver failure and subsequent renal impairment, culminating in bile cast nephropathy. Persistent metabolic acidosis and hyperkalemia associated with renal dysfunction are potential direct contributors to mortality.

In conclusion, this case illustrates that MPM can cause extensive hematogenous metastasis to the gallbladder, a phenomenon that has not previously been reported. Clinicians should be aware of the possibility of hepatobiliary metastasis when patients with advanced MPM present with signs of cholestatic liver injury.
