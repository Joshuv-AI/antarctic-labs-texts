Effect of resveratrol supplementation on metabolic risk markers and anthropometric parameters in individuals with obesity or overweight: A systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
Authors: Hafiz Shahbaz Zahoor, Adina Arshad, Muhammad Abdullah Masood, Muhammad Abdul Muqtadir Qureshi, Javed Iqbal
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundObesity, a progressively widespread disease with a multifaceted etiology, affects nearly 800 million people worldwide. Recent trials suggest that resveratrol, a polyphenolic compound, can be effective for metabolic risk markers and anthropometric parameters in Individuals with Obesity or Overweight. However, a comprehensive analysis is necessary to consolidate these results.ObjectivesTo evaluate the impact of 4-week resveratrol supplementation on metabolic risk markers and anthropometric parameters in individuals with obesity or overweight.MethodsA comprehensive search was executed across PubMed, Cochrane Library, Scopus, Google Scholar, and clinicaltrials.gov, yielding 43 studies after the omission of 20 duplicates. RevMan 5.4 software was used to statistically analyze randomized controlled studies to gauge the impact of resveratrol on the said outcomes in Individuals with Obesity or Overweight. Quality of bias and potential publication bias were measured using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool and CMA4 software, respectively. Certainty of the evidence was appraised using GRADEpro software. Data was quantified as the standardized mean difference (SMD) and 95 % confidence intervals (CI).ResultsThe meta-analysis included 5 trials with 419 participants, evaluating the effect of intervention. Resveratrol showed no significant impact on triglycerides (SMD = 0.11, 95%CI [-0.14, 0.35], P = 0.39, I2 = 0 %), total cholesterol (SMD = 0.04, 95%CI [-0.26, 0.33], P = 0.82, I2 = 0 %), or HbA1c (SMD = 0.00, 95%CI [-0.99, 1.00], P = 0.99, I2 = 80 %). Similarly, no significant effect was observed for insulin levels (SMD = −0.00, 95%CI [-0.25, 0.24], P = 0.97, I2 = 0 %), AST (SMD = 0.15, 95%CI [-0.35, 0.65], P = 0.55, I2 = 0 %), or ALT (SMD = −0.03, 95%CI [-0.46, 0.39], P = 0.98, I2 = 0 %). Additionally, resveratrol had no significant effect on BMI (MD = 0.21, 95%CI [-0.970, 1.39], P = 0.70, I2 = 0 %).ConclusionsOur results tagged with p-value>0.05 are an evidence-based indication that resveratrol supplementation does not lead to significant improvements in metabolic or anthropometric parameters. This suggests that resveratrol may not be an effective standalone intervention for managing obesity-related outcomes. Therefore, future research should be redirected to evaluating its potential effects on other health metrics to elucidate its efficacy and therapeutic potential better.

Introduction

Obesity is a significant global health concern, characterized by excessive fat accumulation that poses risks for various metabolic disorders, including diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) [1]. Resveratrol, a polyphenolic compound abundant in various plant-based foods, has attracted considerable scientific interest for its prospective health benefits, particularly in the realm of obesity and metabolic disorders [1,2]. Extensive research has illuminated the potential of resveratrol supplementation to positively influence important metabolic risk markers, such as lipid metabolism and insulin sensitivity—factors central to the management of obesity-related conditions. Resveratrol is introduced as a nutraceutical known for its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties. It is suggested that resveratrol may improve insulin resistance, which is an important factor in the management of T2DM [3].

It is noted that resveratrol has been observed to reverse factors contributing to premature death, including obesity, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia. This suggests that the compound may have a multifaceted approach to improving health outcomes in patients with metabolic conditions [4]. Specifically, resveratrol has shown the ability to inhibit adipogenesis, the process of fat cell formation, and mitigate triglyceride accumulation. These effects are largely mediated through its interaction with important regulatory proteins, including peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ) and sirtuins [1]. Additionally, resveratrol's impact extends to the gut microbiota, where it may enhance therapeutic outcomes by modulating the microbial environment and influencing metabolic pathways [5].

Nevertheless, while some studies indicate resveratrol’s anti-inflammatory properties, showing its ability to attenuate high glucose-induced inflammation in HepG2 cells, reducing the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, and enhancing glucose metabolism [6] the variability in results underscores the necessity for further research. This includes investigating the optimal and long-term effects of resveratrol supplementation. To address these gaps, this systematic review and meta-analysis seeks to integrate existing evidence on the effects of resveratrol supplementation on metabolic risk markers and anthropometric parameters in individuals with obesity or overweight, aiming to provide a comprehensive assessment of its efficacy and potential clinical applications.

In the end, this can help patients with narcolepsy by assisting clinicians in making better-informed judgements regarding modalities for treatment.

Methods

Data sources and search strategy

A systematic search of PubMed, Cochrane, Scopus, Google Scholar, and clinicaltrials.gov databases was done from inception to August 2024. We used the following medical subject headings terms (MeSH) and keywords:

(Obesity OR "Obesity"[Mesh]) AND (Resveratrol OR "Resveratrol"[Mesh]) AND (Adults OR "Adult"[Mesh]) AND (placebo OR placebos OR "Placebos"[Mesh]) AND (outcomes OR efficacy OR effectiveness OR "Treatment Outcome"[Mesh])

Both medical subject headings and free text words were used to search PubMed.

The PICO criteria were used to screen the studies for this meta-analysis, with patients (P) being individuals with obesity or overweight; intervention (I) being the use of resveratrol supplementations; control (C) being patients receiving placebo, no treatment, or usual care; and outcomes (O) delineated by our PICO criteria involve a thorough examination of how resveratrol influences a range of biochemical and anthropometric parameters, specifically assessing its effects on biochemical markers such as triglycerides, total cholesterol levels, HbA1c, insulin, as well as liver enzymes such as AST and ALT. Additionally, our evaluation extends to anthropometric measures, with a particular focus on body mass index (BMI). Only studies in English were encompassed in this analysis.

Selection criteria

The criteria for inclusion in our analysis were diligently established as follows: (i) randomized controlled trials that juxtapose the efficacy of resveratrol treatment against a placebo, (ii) studies involving participants who are classified as individuals with obesity or overweight, (iii) trials in which participants were free from significant comorbid conditions, (iv) trials where participants were not undergoing any concurrent treatments that could potentially influence glucose metabolism or homeostasis, (v) for studies showing a crossover design, only data from the initial randomization phase were used, owing to potential concerns regarding carryover effects, (vi) only those randomized controlled trials whose results directly addressed our specified efficacy outcomes were incorporated into the meta-analysis.

Exclusion criteria were as follows: (i) clinical trials that did not involve Individuals with Obesity or Overweight, had significant co-morbid conditions, or were on any other treatment therapies that could potentially alter glucose or other metabolic pathways, (ii) trials that do not include resveratrol as the sole intervention, (iii) trials that have fewer than nine patients per treatment group.

Any observational studies such as case-control, retrospective or prospective cohort, and cross-sectional studies were excluded. Peer review articles, commentaries, letters to editors, and case reports were also excluded.

Selection process

Every paper's title and abstract were scrutinized by two separate independent authors who confirmed that all potentially pertinent studies met the inclusion requirements. Potential articles were reviewed in full text by two independent authors. The salient features of the extracted data included the major characteristics of the trials, the therapeutic designs, and the outcomes.

Outcome measures

For efficacy analyses, outcomes included the changes in biochemical parameters, namely triglyceride levels, total cholesterol level, HbA1c, insulin, as well as liver enzymes such as AST and ALT, and anthropometric parameters, namely BMI. Data was extracted at 4 weeks post-intervention. For these parameters, mean values along with their corresponding standard deviations were diligently computed from the studies included in the analysis.

Data extraction

Two authors separately extracted data using standard forms for data extraction. Data on the outcomes, as stated above, was extracted from the relevant graphs and tables. Patient characteristics and demographics (including mean age, sex, etc.) from the different studies included in the analysis were noted in tabular form (Table 1).

Quality assessment

The methodological quality of trials was measured independently by two authors using Version 2 of the Cochrane risk-of-bias tool for randomized trials (RoB 2) [7]. We measured the studies to rule out any selection, attrition, allocation, or reporting bias. If there were disagreements, those were settled by mutual discussion or arbitration with a third reviewer.

Statistical analysis

The meta-analysis was carried out using the recommendations of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [8]. This meta-analysis has been reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA), the checklist of which can be found at the end of the manuscript [9]. All statistical analyses were conducted using RevMan 5.3 software (Cochrane Management System) [10]. For continuous outcomes, we computed standardized mean differences (SMD) or mean differences (MD) with 95 % confidence intervals (CI).

P < 0.05 was the threshold for statistical significance unless otherwise stated. Whenever possible, intention-to-treat populations were the subject of meta-analysis. Heterogeneity was calculated using I2 statistics [8].

Results

Literature search

Our comprehensive electronic database search, conducted from inception through August 2024, yielded a total of 63 records. Following the elimination of 20 duplicates, 43 records were retained. A subsequent review of titles and abstracts resulted in the exclusion of 37 records. The remaining 6 records were then procured for full-text evaluation. Of these, 1 study was excluded due to the co-formulation of hesperidin with resveratrol and the inability to retrieve data from the initial randomization period, thereby failing to meet our pre-established criteria.

We selected the remaining five studies with 491 participants, and all of them were RCT [[11], [12], [13], [14], [15]]. The PRISMA flowchart for the inclusion process is shown in Fig. 1.

Results of synthesis

A total of five RCT constituting 491 Individuals with Obesity or Overweight (which fulfilled all inclusion criteria) were included in the synthesis to evaluate the efficacy outcomes when patients were subjected to intervention for 4 weeks [[11], [12], [13], [14], [15]]. Data on therapy duration were obtained at designated intervals for each RCT. The doses of resveratrol in three studies were 150mg/day, However in two of our included studies Poulsen 2012 and Batista 2020 the doses were 500 mg/day and 250 mg/day respectively.

In addressing the effect of resveratrol on biochemical parameters, the analysis suggested that there is no statistically significant change in triglyceride levels (SMD = 0.11, 95%CI [−0.14, 0.35], P = 0.39, I2 = 0 %), (high certainty) or total cholesterol levels (SMD = 0.04, 95%CI [−0.26, 0.33], P = 0.82, I2 = 0 %), (high certainty). Notably, heterogeneity among the studies was zero, indicating consistency in the results.

In addressing the anthropometric parameters, a meta-analysis was done to analyze the impact of the intervention on body mass index. This examination encompassed data from two trials [11,13] focusing on BMI (involving 112 participants). The results revealed a minimal level of heterogeneity (0 %) and statistically insignificant results, thereby offering no substantial evidence of the intervention’s influence on the patient’s anthropometric parameters.

Certainty of evidence

The evaluation of evidence certainty was conducted using the GRADEpro Guideline Development Tool [software] [16]. The information collected concerning triglyceride levels, total cholesterol levels, HbA1c, insulin, and ALT was categorized as having high certainty because of its small confidence intervals, direct applicability, and precision. This ranking is based on a solid body of trustworthy evidence.

Conversely, the evidence pertaining to the AST and BMI was characterized as having moderate certainty evidence. A detailed summary of the evidence certainty for each outcome is shown in Table 2.

Discussions

This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to evaluate the effects of resveratrol supplementation on various metabolic risk markers and anthropometric parameters in Individuals with Obesity or Overweight. We included randomized controlled trials that met strict inclusion criteria to ensure the robustness of the results. Our analysis encompassed biochemical parameters such as triglyceride levels, total cholesterol levels, HbA1c levels, insulin levels, liver enzymes (ALT and AST), and anthropometric parameters including body mass index (BMI). Despite the consistent direction of effects observed across the studies, none of the outcomes showed statistically significant results. The minimally clinically important difference (MCID) was not predefined in the studies included in this meta-analysis. However, based on previous clinical trials, certain thresholds have been identified as clinically meaningful: a reduction of approximately 20–30 mg/dL in triglyceride levels, 10–15 mg/dL in total cholesterol, 0.5 % in HbA1c levels, 1–2 μU/mL in insulin levels, 10–15 IU/L in AST and ALT levels, and about 1 kg/m2 in Body Mass Index (BMI). In our analysis, the observed effect sizes for resveratrol supplementation did not meet these established thresholds for any of the parameters assessed. This suggests that the impact of resveratrol may not be clinically significant, highlighting the necessity for further investigation to better understand its potential therapeutic benefits across these critical metabolic markers.

Post hoc power analyses were conducted to assess the achieved power for the outcomes of interest. The results indicated low power levels across the various measures, with values of 14 % for triglycerides, 5 % for total cholesterol, 15 % for AST, and 14 % for anthropometric measurements. These findings suggest that the ability to detect statistically significant effects for these outcomes was limited, highlighting the need for further studies to validate the findings and enhance the robustness of the evidence.

Our results indicate that while resveratrol supplementation consistently trended towards the anticipated beneficial effects across various metabolic and anthropometric parameters, these effects did not achieve statistical significance. This lack of significant impact on triglyceride levels, total cholesterol levels, HbA1c levels, insulin levels, liver enzymes (ALT and AST), and body mass index (BMI) suggests that resveratrol may not offer substantial improvements in these areas for Individuals with Obesity or Overweight.

The consistency in direction but the absence of statistical significance highlights the necessity for cautious interpretation of individual study results. Furthermore, these results emphasize the need for future research involving larger and more rigorously designed randomized controlled trials with standardized dosages and longer follow-up periods to more definitively ascertain the efficacy of resveratrol and to elucidate its potential role in managing obesity and associated metabolic disorders.

Additionally, exploring the mechanisms through which resveratrol might influence metabolic parameters could provide insights into its efficacy and inform future clinical recommendations.

Limitations

The studies included in the meta-analysis varied in terms of dosage regimens and baseline characteristics of participants, which may have contributed to the observed inconsistencies. Additionally, the relatively small sample sizes and short duration of some studies may have limited the ability to detect significant effects.

Due to the randomization process and selection of the reported results, one study [13] showed some concerns regarding the risk of bias, which could jeopardize the validity of its conclusions and have an impact on the study's overall results.

One limitation of our meta-analysis is the incomplete reporting of demographic and baseline characteristics across the included studies. Data such as weight, waist circumference, and body composition, which are important for assessing the full impact of resveratrol on obesity and metabolic risk markers, were not consistently provided by all studies. As a result, we were unable to include these variables in our analysis.

Another important limitation of our meta-analysis is that the majority of the included studies focused on metabolically healthy individuals. These participants typically had normal baseline values for key metabolic markers such as triglycerides, cholesterol, and HbA1c. As a result, any intervention, including resveratrol supplementation, may have had limited potential to improve already normal lab values.

This restricts the generalizability of our findings to metabolically unhealthy individuals, who may exhibit greater responsiveness to interventions targeting metabolic risk markers.

Moreover, due to low statistical power of our meta-analysis, further studies are needed to enhance the robustness of our evidence.

An important limitation of our meta-analysis is that it exclusively studied RCT focused solely on resveratrol without any co-formulations or additional interventions. This narrow scope may overlook potential synergistic effects that resveratrol could show when combined with other compounds. Future research should explore resveratrol’s efficacy within multi-component formulations, as such combinations might enhance its therapeutic impact and provide more pronounced benefits than those observed with resveratrol alone.

Conclusions

Our results, marked by p-values exceeding 0.05, provide empirical evidence suggesting that resveratrol supplementation does not yield significant enhancements in metabolic or anthropometric parameters. This implies that resveratrol, as a solitary intervention, may not be effective in ameliorating outcomes associated with obesity. Consequently, future research endeavors should pivot toward investigating the impact of resveratrol on alternative health metrics. Such an approach could offer a more comprehensive understanding of its efficacy and therapeutic potential in other domains.

The limitations of the included studies, such as biases, short therapy durations, and short sample sizes paired with the intervention of resveratrol alone devoid of any co-formulation, call for attention. Future research should address these limitations by incorporating larger sample sizes, longer follow-up periods, and more rigorous drug administration methodologies to further elucidate and extend the therapeutic benefits of resveratrol.
