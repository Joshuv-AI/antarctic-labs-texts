Switchable and expandable mid-infrared broadband metasurface absorber for adaptive infrared stealth based on VO2 phase change materials
Authors: Ye Yuan, Shaopei Wang, Mengdan Qian, Weiying Hou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryDynamic thermal emission with broadband tunability has attracted increasing interest in energy and military fields. Here, a thermally switchable and expandable metasurface absorber with broadband mid-infrared emission is proposed based on a metallic resonant element and vanadium dioxide (VO2) phase change material film. Through the insulating-metal transition of VO2, the metasurface switches between “stealth” and “non-stealth” states across the mid-infrared spectrum, and even active tuning of the absorption bandwidth and resonant peak can be achieved. At room temperature, the absorber shows near-perfect broadband absorption in the long-wave infrared atmospheric window. At high temperatures, the absorption band moves toward 5–8 μm while the atmospheric window bands present low emissivity, allowing infrared camouflage and effective heat dissipation. The proposed metasurface absorber has simple structural geometry and broad, switchable spectral response in the infrared region, showing promising applications in thermal photonics, including information storage, infrared stealth, thermal imaging, and detectors.

Introduction

Electromagnetic metasurface has recently attracted great interest in the field of micro-nano optics due to their powerful manipulation of light-matter interaction at subwavelength scale. Electromagnetic control by metasurface has wide applications in various scenes, such as imaging,1,2 infrared camouflage,3,4 polarization conversion,5 radiative cooling,6,7,8 and light trapping.9,10 Rational design of geometric structures can obtain desired magnetic permeability and permittivity for spectral control, accelerating the development of thermal radiation.11,12,13 One of the promising and attractive applications for infrared radiation regulation is infrared stealth technology, which reduces the radiative signals between objects and background to avoid being detected. The working band of infrared (IR) detectors is mainly distributed within the atmospheric transition window (3–5 μm and 8–14 μm), and it requires low spectral emissivity of objects in this region to conceal into the environment. In order to improve the low-observability feature in the high temperature, effective heat dissipation is very essential to reduce the surface temperature and further radiative energy of objects. The non-transparent atmospheric region (5–8 μm) can be used as the dissipation band without the risk of being detected. Therefore, thermal radiation regulation toward selective emission in specific waveband helps improving the working efficiency of infrared stealth devices.

The radiative spectrum, mainly determined by the materials and structural geometry, is generally invariant when the parameters are given. In reality, however, the position of target may constantly change, resulting in the failure of static stealth objects in the dynamic background environment. As opposed to unmoving stealth, variable thermal stealth technology has more forceful practicality and wider military application possibility. Recently, the phase change materials has attracted tremendous interest in dynamic thermal radiation due to their tunable optical properties. Qu et al. investigated a thermal camouflage apparatus containing the phase change material Ge2Sb2Te5 (GST), which can continuously achieve near-perfect thermal camouflage at baseline temperatures between 30°C and 50°C.14 Quan et al. developed a phase change metamaterial for tunable infrared stealth and camouflage, which can toggle between infrared “stealthy” and “non-stealthy” states via the phase change mechanism of GST.15 Vanadium dioxide (VO2) is another non-volatile phase change material, and it switches between insulation and metallic state at about 68°C, accompanying with sharp changes in resistivity, dielectric constant, and light transmittance and reflectance.16 Leveraging the phase-transition characteristics of VO2, various broadband metasurface absorbers with switchable wavelength selectivity are designed. For instance, Liang et al. designed a wavelength-adjustable metasurface absorber through the incorporation of VO2 into an Au array.17 When VO2 evolves a switch from dielectric to conductive state, the absorption peaks show bule shift in wavelength. Lei et al.18 introduced a thermally controllable wide-spectrum absorber employing a patterned plasmonic superglue surface spaced from VO2. The active tunability of the absorption span and resonant peak wavelength was simultaneously achieved in 0–7 μm through the insulating-metal phase transition (IMT) of VO2. Even though dynamic control of radiative spectrum can be realized, the tunable ranges of the broadband absorption and peak resonance wavelengths are not very large enough to cover the infrared region, where many important infrared applications such as imaging and detection are focused. And there is a dearth of reports on the ultra-broadband absorbers in the long-wave infrared (LWIR) region.

In this study, we put forward a thermally switchable and expandable metasurface absorber (SEMA) that realizes stealthy and non-stealthy switching functions in the mid-infrared (MIR) region with emissivity regulatory ability in the atmospheric transparency range and active heat dissipation within the atmospheric absorption interval (5–8 μm). By virtue of thermally induced phase transformation of VO2, the SEMA presents an ultra-broadband absorption with a bandwidth of 4.1 μm as well as a perfect absorption of 0.99 at 11.6 μm during the insulated state of VO2. The configuration of the electromagnetic field reveals that the hybrid plasma resonance mode is responsible for the broadband absorption in the MIR. Under the metallic state, the absorption of SEMA dynamically transfers to the nonatmospheric window (5–8 μm), maintaining a mean emissivity that is 74%, while the emissivity in the atmospheric open window is generally below 0.3, allowing effective infrared stealth as well as heat dissipation. The selective heat dissipation of SEMA in the metallic state of VO2 is then discussed through the electromagnetic field distribution, subsequently confirmed by LC modeling. In addition, the SEMA shows good stability and robustness to the orientation of polarization and the angle of incident light. Concurrently, the low-level emissivity achieved in the mid-wave infrared (MWIR) and LWIR wavelength intervals results in a significant decrease in the radiation temperature. The dynamic thermal tuning ability and scalable spectral response of the designed SEMA make it have promising applications in adaptive infrared stealth, optical modulator, and infrared detection.

Results and discussion

Structural design concept of SEMA

Thermotropic phase change material VO2 has been applied to metal-insulation-metal (MIM) structure to design switchable, wavelength-selective, and scalable wideband metasurface absorbers. The SEMA structure proposed in this paper for dynamic infrared camouflage is exhibited in Figure 1. The structure consists of four layers including an Ag cube layer, an SU-8 dielectric layer, a VO2 phase change material layer, and an indium tin oxide (ITO) substrate from the upper limit to the lower limit. The thickness distribution across each layer is set as 80 nm (Ag), 100 nm (SU-8), 1,400 nm (VO2), and 90 nm (ITO), respectively (Figure 1B). The SU-8 layer, known for its stability, was selected as the protective layer to prevent oxidation or damage of the VO2 layer. ITO functions as the reflective layer together with the VO2 film, accelerating resonant absorption in the infrared range. Adjusting the thickness of the ITO film exerts a negligible influence on the absorption spectrum of the SEMA (Figure S1). The duration (P) and the edge length (r) of the uppermost Ag block were set as 1.6 μm and 1.2 μm, respectively. The silver square array functions as the frequency-selective surface, supporting magnetic resonance and controlling the resonance wavelength. The phase transition temperature at which VO2 undergoes a phase transition in this work was set to 68°C. The optical parameters of VO2 in its insulating and metallic states were derived from experimental data in Ref.19; please refer to Figure S2. The complex permittivity of SU-8 was determined by fitting to Cauchy’s dispersion formula, while the permittivity of Ag and ITO was obtained from optical measurements reported in Refs.20 and,21 respectively.Figure 1Structural design for SEMA(A) Schematic diagram of the proposed SEMA.(B) Sideways depiction of the single SEMA structure.

The Bruggeman effective medium theory is applied to investigate the spectral variations of SEMA during the phase transition process, which is closely related to the fraction of VO2 volume in the metallic condition. The metal-like properties and size effects of ITO film makes it exhibit extremely high reflectance in the MIR region.21 It can effectively reflect the incident light and render the structure’s transmittance (P) nearly zero when used as the substrate. In accordance with Kirchhoff’s law of thermal radiation,24 the absorptivity (A) aligns with the emissivity (E) under thermal balance. Therefore, the emissivity of the proposed SEMA can be obtained according to the equation E = A = 1−R, where P, A, and R represent transmittance, absorptivity, and reflectivity, respectively.

Absorption properties and principle analysis of SEMA for infrared stealth

The spectral emissivity of the proposed VO2-based SEMA is simulated and the result is portrayed in Figure 3A. When VO2 is in its insulating state, the absorber enables the ultra-broadband high absorption in the LWIR with the 80% absorption bandwidth (BW0.8) achieving 4.1 μm (8–12.1 μm). The broadband absorption within the 8–14 μm band has promising applications in LWIR imaging and detection.25 At the same time, the absorber reaches near-perfect absorbance at 11.6 μm (α = 0.986). The formation of wideband absorption in the LWIR spectrum can develop comprehensive, structured approaches to direct the creation of metamaterials with good wideband absorption, thereby paving the method for improving the optical performance in infrared thermal emission, imaging, energy integration, and other applications. As the temperature goes up, VO2 transforms into the metallic state, and thus the unit presents the typical metal/dielectric/metal structure, which triggers the obvious spectral blueshift of peak wavelength from 11.6 μm to 6.2 μm. The fundamental resonance mode shows a 5.4 μm shift in the wavelength. Additionally, the emissivity corresponding to BW0.8 after phase transition reaches 1.2 μm and the mean emissivity in the 5–8 μm nonatmospheric window is as high as 0.74, allowing effective active heat dissipation for radiative cooling applications.Figure 3Spectral variations and electric distributions of SEMA before and after VO2 phase transition(A) Frequency-dependent emissivity of the proposed SEMA.(B) The average emissivity of SEMA in the atmospheric windows and nonatmospheric band when VO2 is in the insulating state and the metallic state.(C) The absorption spectrum as a function of simulated temperature during the phase transition process of VO2.(D and E) The cross-sectional electric field distributions of VO2 at the primary resonant wavelengths corresponding to its insulated (D) and metallic (E) states, respectively.

With the aim of demonstrating the infrared stealth capability of the proposed SEMA, the typical emissivity in two key wavebands (3–5 and 8–14 μm) is thoroughly investigated. At a specific temperature T1, the average emissivity of the structure within a defined spectral band is formulated based on its spectral emissivity and the irradiance of a blackbody operating as below26:(Equation 3)ε¯=∫λ1λ2ελEbλ(T1)dλ∫λ1λ2Ebλ(T1)dλ(Equation 4)Ebλ=2πhc02λ5exp(hc0/kλT1−1)where Ebλ is the spectral irradiance of the blackbody; λ1 and λ2 denote the band ranges; ελ is the absorber’s emissivity; and h, c0, k, and T1 are Planck’s constant, the speed of light in a vacuum, Boltzmann’s constant, and temperature, respectively. The average emissivity in different bands is calculated according to the above equations. As shown in Figure 3B, the average emissivity in the atmospheric window changed significantly before and after the phase transition of VO2. The average emissivity in the 3–5 μm band decreased by 12% after phase transition, switching from 0.4 to 0.28. The average emissivity in the 8–14 μm band changes from 0.86 to 0.29, reducing by 57%. After the VO2 phase transition, the average emissivities in the atmospheric window are generally lower than 0.3, while the nonatmospheric window remains a high value, which effectively suppresses the heat accumulation and further the exposure possibility of absorber from IR detectors. Figure 3C gives the absorption spectrum of SEMA as a function of stimulated temperature ranging from 300 to 340 K, which provides insight into the spectral variation during the VO2 phase transition process. When VO2 is in the insulating state below 340 K, the absorber exhibits an ultra-broadband absorption across the MWIR and LWIR, with the absorption peaks primarily centered near 11.6 μm. The high average emissivity within broadband IR range endows its promising applications in radiative cooling or thermal imaging. Further rise of temperature above 340 K results in the IMT of the VO2 layer, and obvious blueshift and narrowing of absorption peak occurs along with the increase of the volume fraction of metallic state. After the phase transition is completed, the absorption mainly concentrates in the 5–8 μm band with the peak position near 6.2 μm.

To elucidate underlying reasons for the spectral variation, we explored the fundamental physical mechanisms of SEMA. Figures 3D and 3E display a comparison of the electric field distributions within the absorber at typical resonance wavelengths of 11.6 μm and 6.2 μm for the semiconductor state and metallic state, respectively. In the insulating state of VO2 insulation, the metal induces a strong electric field. Part of the incident light is reflected by the Ag layer, while the other part is reflected by the ITO substrate through the transparent insulating VO2 film, which results in high emissivity.27,28 The absorption peak at 11.6 μm is attributed to the hybrid plasmon resonance mode arising from the coupling effect between surface plasmon resonance and magnetic resonance. As for the VO2 metal state (Figure 3E), however, the electric field is mostly confined within the SU-8 layer due to the formation of an MIM structure. When the VO2 layer undergoes the phase transition from semiconductor to reflective metal layer, the cavity length decreases by almost the entire thickness of the VO2 layer, resulting in the blueshift of near 5.4 μm on the fundamental resonance.

Geometric parameter optimization of SEMA

In consideration of the significant influence of geometric parameters on the absorption performance, we optimize some key parameters to realize the ideal spectral response. Changing the thickness of the SU-8 dielectric layer enables reconfigurable adjustment of the absorption bandwidth of SEMA while maintaining a high absorption level. The absorption spectra of SEMA with varying SU-8 thicknesses are shown in Figures 4A and 4B. It is evident that the absorption bandwidth and strength of SEMA are closely related to the SU-8 thickness, which is attributed to the mixed resonance mode within the SU-8 dielectric layer. With the increase of SU-8 thickness from 80 nm to 120 nm, the bandwidth of absorber gradually broadens to the short-wave direction, with the BW0.8 value rising from 2.1 μm to 5.3 μm. Phase transition of the VO2 layer results in the blueshift of the main absorption peak toward the nonatmospheric band. An increase in the thickness of SU-8 can efficiently enhance the absorption intensity to within 5–8 μm. Specifically, the BW0.8 in 5–8 μm for 80 nm and 120 nm SU-8 structures are measured to be 0.8 μm and 1.5 μm, with the corresponding absorptivity reaching 85.3% and 97.2%, respectively. The influence of phase transition of the VO2 layer is also investigated, and the results are shown in Figures 4C and 4D. In these simulations, dimensions h1, h2, h4, L, and r were maintained at their original lengths. Figure 4C illustrates that, at low temperatures, the absorptivity intensity in the LWIR region increases conformably with the thickness of the VO2 layer, nearly achieving perfect absorption at 11.6 μm. This characteristic is advantageous for the application of infrared thermal emitters and photodetectors, enhancing their optical properties. In contrast, as depicted in Figure 4D, altering the thickness of the VO2 layer almost has little influence on the SEMA absorptivity when it is in the metallic state. The metallic state of the VO2 layer at elevated temperature forms an MIM cavity to support the generation of magnetic resonance mode. The metallic VO2 film only functions as a reflective layer and cannot change the magnetic resonance modes. Thus, variations of the bottom reflective VO2 metal layer are ineffective to change the absorption spectrum. The absorber presents high and broadband absorption in the undetected wavelength range (5–8 μm) and low emissivity within the atmospheric window, enabling effective infrared stealth as well as heat dissipation.

Resonance absorption regulation and mechanism analysis of SEMA structure

Since the thermal radiation regulation within the 5–8 μm band is crucial for the long-term use of SEMA, the effect of the metal block’s dimensions on the absorption wavelength and the heat dissipation mechanism should be further investigated. We first analyzed the generation mechanism of absorption peaks within the atmospheric absorption band, in the case of SEMA with metallic state VO2 at high temperatures. Figure 5A shows the magnetic field distribution (in Hz) across the cross-section of the SEMA structure at the resonance wavelength of 6.2 μm. As seen in the figure, the magnetic field is confined within the SU-8 layer. It produces an oscillating current and forms a clockwise closed current loop around the center of the SU-8 layer, indicating the effective excitation of magnetic polaritons. Magnetic resonance occurs when micro-structures generate time-varying induced magnetic fields under external electromagnetic wave excitation. The resonance frequency of magnetic resonance modes can be precisely controlled by altering surface structural dimensions and optimizing the dielectric’s permittivity and permeability. The top cubic Ag patterns, together with the middle electric layer of SU-8, contribute to the selective emission. Magnetic polaritons are formed through the coupling of external electromagnetic waves with the magnetic resonance within the structure. When a time-varying magnetic field is introduced into the metallic structure, it generates an oscillating current, and an induced magnetic field is also produced according to the Lenz’s law. The induced magnetic field is strongly enhanced at the magnetic resonance frequency of the structure, where the total impedance is zero.32,33,34Figure 5Theoretical analysis for magnetic resonance of SEMA(A) Magnetic field (Hz) distribution across the cross-section of the SEMA structure at the wavelength of 6.2 μm when VO2 is in the metallic state.(B) Schematic of the LC circuit model used to predict the magnetic resonance wavelength of SEMA.(C) The emissivity of Ag blocks with different period side lengths.(D) The link between the resonant wavelength and the side length of Ag block is obtained by COMSOL simulation and LC circuit model.

The equivalent LC circuit model can be employed to make a clear explanation of the excitation mechanism of magnetic polaritons. As shown in Figure 5B, the LC circuit model has been successfully applied to predict the magnetic resonance in metal-insulator-metal (MIM) structures. In this model, SU-8 is considered as a capacitor, where the charge accumulates between the top and bottom metal layers. The Ag block at the top, together with the VO2 and ITO layers in their metallic phase at the bottom, both serve as inductors. Cg represents the gap capacitance that arises from the resonance between neighboring dipoles. The above elements form the LC circuit. The inductance is composed of mutual inductance Lm and dynamic inductance Lk, which is expressed as35,36:Lm=0.5μ0wh2r(Equation 5)Lk=−wωp2ε0εAg′δAgrwhere ωp is the angular frequency, μ0 and ε0 are the magnetic permeability and the relative permittivity of the vacuum, respectively. ε′Ag is the real part of the permittivity of the bulk Ag. δAg = λ/4πk is the penetration depth of the bulk Ag, and w = r- 6δAg, where k is the extinction coefficient of Ag. The capacitance of the parallel-plate capacitor formed by the insulating layer can be calculated by33,36:(Equation 6)C=cε0εdwrh2where εd is the real part of the dielectric constant of SU-8, and the correction factor c is a coefficient that accounts for the inhomogeneous charge distribution at the metal surface, with values ranging from 0.2 to 0.4.30 More specifically, magnetic resonance occurs when the impedance of the LC circuit becomes zero, and the impedance of this circuit can be expressed as:(Equation 7)λc=2πc0(Lm+Lk)Cmwhere λc is the resonant wavelength and c0 is the speed of light in a vacuum. According to the LC circuit model, the resonant wavelength is predicted to be 6.16 μm when the periodic side length of the Ag square is 1.2 μm, with a relative error of 0.6% compared with the COMSOL simulation results.

Figure 5C illustrates the emissivity variation of the Ag block with different periodic side lengths. As the side length of the block increases from 1 μm to 1.4 μm, the peak position of the wavelength moves from 5 μm to 7.7 μm, while the emissivity intensity at resonance peak remains essentially constant. The results show that the absorption peaks of this switchable absorber can be tuned to different wavelengths, significantly broadening the potential applications of SEMA. A linear relationship between the period side length of the Ag block and the absorption peak is observed, as depicted in Figure 5D, which can be fitted by the equation of λc = 6.7×R-1.76. The correlation between the resonant wavelength and the diameter of the Ag square is also calculated using the LC model. In the mid-IR and far-IR bands, Lk is significantly smaller than Lm,37 and the equation can be further simplified as λc = 6.72×R–1.866. The COMSOL simulation results closely conform with the predictions of the LC model, as shown in Figure 5D. Based on this equation, the absorber with specific spectral demands can be rationally designed. In terms of the infrared camouflage application, the periodic side length of the top Ag block should be within the range of 1.02–1.47 μm, ensuring the resonant absorption at 5–8 μm and low emissivity in atmospheric windows. Dependency of spectral switch performance with polarization

The sensitivity of absorber to incidence/polarization angle is also an important factor to evaluate the working performance in various applications. The dependence of emissivity on the polarization for SEMA in both VO2 insulation and metallic states is simulated under TE and TM polarization, respectively. Due to the symmetrical property of top Ag blocks, the proposed SEMA generally presents good polarization insensitivity under 60° incident angle. The angular dependence of the absorber at low and high temperatures, under oblique incidence of transverse magnetic (TM) polarized waves, is depicted in Figures 6A and 6B. When the angle of incidence exceeds 30°, surface plasmon polaritons (SPPs) near 3 μm are excited in the metasurface structure, causing relative increase of emissivity at 3–5 μm under a low temperature. In summary, a SEMA consisting of the Ag-(SU-8)-VO2-ITO structure is proposed in this work, where phase transition material VO2 is introduced to dynamically regulate the infrared performance by rationally controlling the stimulated temperature. The phase transition enables an effective switch of SEMA between the infrared stealth and non-stealth states. Thermal tunability on absorption bandwidth and resonance wavelength are simultaneously achieved using the proposed SEMA. When VO2 transitions from an insulation to a metallic state, the 80% absorption bandwidth changes from 4.1 μm to 1.2 μm. Additionally, the resonance wavelength also blueshifts from 11.6 μm to 6.2 μm. The physical mechanism was explained by the electromagnetic field distribution and also analyzed using an LC circuit model. The peak absorption rate can be regulated to different wavelengths by changing the side length of the silver block. Furthermore, the absorption bandwidth and high emissivity of the structure in the MIR region can be finely tuned by adjusting the thickness of the dielectric layer, significantly enhancing the applicability and flexibility of SEMA. It is verified that the SEMA structure’s infrared camouflage performance remains stable under polarized incidence light at large angle. Meanwhile, the metasurface also exhibits good stealth effect at high temperatures. The proposed phase-change-based SEMA features a simple structure, tunable emissivity, and angle robustness. The proposed SEMA holds significant promise for adaptive infrared camouflage and radiative heat management technologies.

Limitations of the study

This work only provides theoretical simulations of VO2-based dynamic thermal emitter. The optical parameters of used materials come from the previously reported literature. In consideration of the different film deposition and fabrication methods, the real optical parameters and final spectral emissivity of SEMA may show some deviations from the theoretical results. But the designed concept and structural model of SEMA is of significance for adaptive infrared camouflage and radiative heat management technologies.

Method details

Numerical simulations of the emission spectrum and electromagnetic distribution of SEMA were conducted using the commercial software COMSOL physics with the radio frequency module (electromagnetic waves, frequency domain). The simulation domain was defined with periodic boundary conditions for the transverse boundaries (x and y axes) and a perfectly matched layer (PML) along the z-direction to eliminate boundary scattering. In each simulation unit, the emissivity of the emitter was calculated based on the reflectance parameters according to the equation ε = 1-|S11|2-|S21|2. Polarized incident light traveling along the negative z-direction irradiated the structure, with the electric field component being perpendicular or parallel to the xz plane corresponding to TE or TM polarization, respectively.
