Psychological consequences of mammography screening recommendations: a prospective cohort study in Iran
Authors: Seyede Sara Hosseini, Hamid Reza Oreizy, Mitra Savabi-Esfahani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and purposeBreast cancer is the most common malignancy among women. Despite advances in treatment, some women still present at advanced stages of the disease. Although mammography screening can improve survival through early detection, there is ongoing debate regarding its benefits and potential harms. This study aimed to investigate the psychological consequences of breast cancer screening and the factors associated with them.MethodsThis prospective cohort study was conducted in Iran and included 320 women aged 40–69 years who attended comprehensive health centers for gynecologic care and met the inclusion criteria. According to the eligibility criteria, women aged 35–69 years with a family history of breast cancer were also eligible; however, in practice, no participants younger than 40 years were enrolled. Participants were selected using convenience sampling, while health centers were chosen randomly. In total, ten centers from the northern and southern regions of the city were selected through simple random sampling by drawing lots. Participants were classified into two groups based on acceptance or refusal of mammography. Data were collected before and after the screening recommendation using a demographic and reproductive characteristics form and the Psychological Consequences Questionnaire (PCQ), which assesses emotional, social, and physical functioning. The reliability of the data collection tools was evaluated through a pilot study and calculation of Cronbach’s alpha coefficients. Data were analyzed using SPSS version 18.ResultsBefore the screening recommendation, there was no significant difference in psychological consequence scores between the two groups (p = 0.53). After the recommendation, the total psychological consequence score and the emotional and social functioning subscales were significantly higher in the acceptance group compared with the refusal group (p < 0.05). The mean difference in total psychological consequence scores between the two groups was 0.82. In the acceptance group, total psychological consequence scores increased significantly after the screening recommendation (p < 0.001). Psychological consequences were inversely associated with age and income (p < 0.05); however, no significant associations were found with education level or number of children.ConclusionThe findings indicate that recommending mammography screening is accompanied by increased psychological consequence scores, particularly in emotional and social domains. Age and income were identified as factors associated with psychological consequences. These results suggest that the screening process may elicit considerable psychological responses among women. Therefore, future interventional studies are recommended to evaluate the effects of educational and psychological support programs prior to screening on mitigating these consequences. From a public health policy perspective, addressing the psychological aspects of screening may improve women’s experiences within national screening programs.

Introduction

In recent decades, cancer has been viewed as a social, public health, and economic problem [1]. In this regard, breast cancer is the most prevalent malignancy and the leading cause of cancer-related mortality among women [2, 3]. In Iran, with an Age Standardized Rate of 30.5, it ranks as the foremost cancer among women [4], and statistics indicate its rising incidence [5, 6].

Despite the identification of some risk factors for breast cancer and advances in treatment modalities such as surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy, approximately one-third of women worldwide still present at advanced stages of the disease. However, the prognosis of breast cancer is directly related to the stage at which the disease is diagnosed [7, 8]. Findings from various studies have shown that screening and timely treatment of certain cancers, including breast cancer, play a substantial role in reducing complications and mortality [9–11]. Nevertheless, since the implementation of screening programs, debates have emerged regarding their benefits and potential harms [12]. Similar advantages and disadvantages have been discussed in relation to mammography as the primary method for breast cancer screening [13]. Commonly reported disadvantages of mammography screening include over diagnosis, false-positive results, anxiety, and radiation-related harm [14].

Moreover, studies have indicated that abnormal findings in medical examinations, such as mammography, may be accompanied by adverse psychological consequences, including emotional reactions, stigma, psychological distress, and non-adherence to assessment or treatment recommendations [15]. Earnshaw J.J. (2019) reported that screening recommendations may reduce individuals’ quality of life [16]. Steeg et al. found that women with false-positive mammography results experienced higher levels of anxiety and lower quality of life [17]. Similarly, other studies have shown that women recalled after breast screening reported elevated anxiety levels [18]. Keyzer-Dekker et al. also demonstrated that women recalled for further diagnostic evaluation following mammography experienced higher levels of depression [19]. Some studies have reported that a considerable proportion of women (20%–60%) undergoing routine mammography experienced anxiety [20]. Additionally, evidence suggests that some women are more affected by the psychological consequences of mammography than others, particularly younger women and those with lower educational levels [21, 22]. In this regard, Al-Alawi N.M. et al. (2019) reported greater social dysfunction among younger women aged 40–50 years compared with other age groups [23]. However, Jaafar Sidek M.A. et al. (2023) found no significant association between screening-related anxiety and social, demographic, or clinical factors [24]. Furthermore, Rimer B.K. et al. demonstrated that women with a family history of breast cancer were more vulnerable to the adverse psychological effects of mammography screening [20].

From a theoretical perspective, preventive health behaviors, including participation in screening programs, can be explained within the framework of the Health Belief Model (HBM). This model, first proposed by Rosenstock and colleagues in the 1950s, suggests that individuals’ perceptions of susceptibility to a disease and the severity of its consequences (collectively referred to as perceived threat) influence their decisions to engage in preventive health behaviors. Accordingly, exposure to screening recommendations may increase perceived health threats in some individuals, thereby activating various psychological responses during the decision-making process. Within this framework, screening is not only a diagnostic intervention but also a psychological experience that may be associated with different psychological consequences depending on individual and social contexts [25, 26]. Nevertheless, the HBM primarily explains health decision-making processes and is insufficient on its own for causal inference.

Since breast cancer is one of the most common cancers among women and its incidence continues to rise [27], lack of access to screening may lead to delayed diagnosis [28, 29]. Such delays can reduce the likelihood of successful treatment and may be associated with increased mortality. In addition, delayed diagnosis often results in detection at more advanced stages, necessitating more intensive and invasive treatments [30–32]. On the other hand, providing rational recommendations regarding screening methods requires evidence on both the benefits and harms of screening. Although numerous studies have investigated the psychological consequences of breast cancer screening, most have employed cross-sectional designs, and few prospective studies have compared psychological consequences between women who accept or refuse mammography screening recommendations, particularly within the sociocultural context of Iran. Therefore, the present study was conducted to prospectively compare psychological consequences between women who accepted and those who refused recommendations for mammography screening. Accordingly, the study hypothesis was based on the existence of a statistically significant difference in psychological consequences between women who accepted and those who declined mammography screening recommendations.

Methods

Study design

This study used a prospective cohort design. At baseline (before any screening recommendation), psychological consequences were assessed in all participants, and a total of 320 women were enrolled. Then, mammography screening was then recommended uniformly to all women by midwives at comprehensive health centers. In this study, “exposure” was defined as exposure to the process of receiving a recommendation for mammography screening. At least one month after the recommendation, participants were contacted and classified according to their decision into those who accepted the recommendation (underwent mammography) and those who refused the recommendation (did not undergo mammography). At baseline, 147 women were in the acceptance group, and 173 were in the refusal group. Psychological consequences were reassessed in both groups at follow-up, at least one month after the baseline recommendation. At follow-up, data were available for 289 participants, including 147 in the acceptance group and 142 in the refusal group. Psychological consequences at follow-up were compared between the two groups. Mammography results were not evaluated in this study; the focus was solely on psychological consequences related to the recommendation and decision-making about undergoing screening.

Setting and participants

The study was conducted in comprehensive health service centers. The target population included all women attending these centers for gynecologic care. Women who attended the centers and met the eligibility criteria were recruited as study participants.

Inclusion criteria

Eligibility criteria included women aged 40–69 years without a family history of breast cancer and women aged 35–69 years with a family history of breast cancer; no personal history of breast cancer; no prior abnormal results in previous breast cancer screenings; no diagnosis of anxiety or depressive disorders; no serious disabling illnesses based on medical records or self-report; and no major adverse life events (e.g., death or severe illness of first- or second-degree relatives, or family bankruptcy) within the past six months, based on participant report and written informed consent.

Exclusion criteria

Exclusion criteria included unwillingness to continue participation and occurrence of major adverse events during the study, such as death or severe illness of first- or second-degree relatives or financial bankruptcy within the household.

Sample size was estimated using a confidence level (Z₁ = 1.96), statistical power (Z₂ = 0.84), effect size (d = 0.7), and a standard deviation (σ) derived from previous studies [33], resulting in a minimum required sample size of 300 participants. Because the number of participants in the two groups could be unequal, sampling continued until the required sample size was achieved; the minimum number specified for the refusal group (≥ 20) was intended solely to ensure the feasibility of statistical comparisons.

Health centers were selected randomly using a cluster approach: the two health networks in Isfahan (Network 1: north of the city; Network 2: south of the city) were considered clusters, and five centers were selected from each network (ten centers in total) using simple random sampling by drawing lots. Participants were recruited using convenience sampling; thus, all eligible women attending the selected centers for gynecologic care were invited and enrolled after providing written informed consent.

Procedure

First, a list of comprehensive health centers covered by Networks 1 and 2 was obtained, and ten centers were selected using a random number table. To collect data, the researcher invited women attending the selected centers for gynecologic care who met the inclusion criteria. After written informed consent was obtained, participants completed the demographic form and questionnaires. Mammography screening was then recommended.

One month after the recommendation, participants were contacted by telephone and invited to return to the health centers. They were subsequently classified into the exposed group (women who underwent mammography) and the unexposed group (women who refused mammography). After returning to the centers, participants in both groups completed the follow-up questionnaires.

Sampling was conducted from early October 2024 to late March 2025.

Measurements

Demographic and reproductive characteristics form

This form included demographic variables (education, occupation, monthly income, and number of children) and reproductive/screening-related variables (family history of breast cancer, intention to undergo mammography in the future, and acceptance or refusal of the screening recommendation).

Psychological Consequences Questionnaire (PCQ)

The Psychological Consequences Questionnaire (PCQ) was used because it was specifically designed to assess psychological responses related to cancer screening processes and evaluate emotional, physical, and social dysfunction in the screening context. In contrast, instruments such as the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) are primarily used for general screening of anxiety and depression and do not specifically capture screening-context psychological consequences. Although the PCQ is a valid instrument for assessing short-term psychological consequences of screening, evidence suggests it may not fully capture long-term psychological consequences after screening, particularly in cases involving abnormal or false-positive results [34]. The 12-item PCQ (PCQ-12) uses a Likert scale from 0 (never) to 3 (most of the time) and includes three domains: physical dysfunction (4 items), emotional dysfunction (5 items), and social dysfunction (3 items), yielding a total score range of 0–36. Higher scores indicate greater dysfunction. The questionnaire was validated by Cockburn, De Luise, Hurley, and Clover in 1992; reported Cronbach’s alpha coefficients were 0.89 (emotional), 0.77 (physical), and 0.78 (social), and validity was supported through content, discriminant, concurrent, and construct validation [35]. Rijnsburger et al. also validated the questionnaire in the Netherlands, reporting Cronbach’s alpha values greater than 0.7 across scales and evidence of face, construct, criterion, and content validity in European and Australian settings [36].

For psychometric adaptation, forward translation was performed independently by two bilingual experts familiar with research methodology. The two Persian versions were then synthesized into a single version after review by the research team and translators. This version was back-translated into English by two independent bilingual translators who were different from the initial translators and were blind to the original questionnaire and the study process.

Finally, an expert committee reviewed the Persian and back-translated English versions, and minor modifications were made to improve cultural alignment with the Iranian target population.

Reliability was assessed in a pilot study with 30 participants; Cronbach’s alpha for the total scale was 0.87, with subscale alphas of 0.88 (emotional), 0.76 (physical), and 0.80 (social).

Breast cancer and screening awareness checklist

To measure the “awareness” variable, selected items from a previously validated researcher-made questionnaire developed by Savabi (2017) were used [37]. The original questionnaire included three sections (awareness, attitude, and behavioral skills related to breast cancer screening). Awareness items had response options of “true,” “false,” and “I don’t know,” with correct answers scored as 1 and incorrect/“I don’t know” scored as 0.

In the present study, awareness scores ranged from 0 to 5, with higher scores indicating greater awareness. The selected items had established validity and reliability, and minor modifications were made in the current study to align them with the study objectives.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 18 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). The significance level was set at p < 0.05. Descriptive statistics (mean, standard deviation, and range) were calculated. Due to the large sample size, parametric tests were utilized following the central limit theorem, without formal K-S testing. Independent t-tests, paired t-tests, and correlation analyses were performed as appropriate. To simultaneously assess the effects of time (pre/post), group (acceptance/refusal), and their interaction, a two-way mixed-design analysis of variance (mixed-design ANOVA) was conducted.

Discussion

The findings of the study showed that before the recommendation for screening, psychological consequence scores did not differ statistically significantly between the two groups. This indicates that baseline psychological status was similar in the acceptance group (women who subsequently underwent screening) and the refusal group (women who did not undergo screening). However, it should be noted that the PCQ is not a diagnostic instrument and does not have a defined clinical cut-off point. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as relative changes in psychological responses rather than as defined clinical disorders.

At reassessment, the mean total psychological consequence score increased significantly in the acceptance group (from 3.20 ± 0.46 to 3.91 ± 0.47), whereas no significant change was observed in the refusal group (from 2.89 ± 0.40to 2.97 ± 0.41). In addition, the time-by-group interaction was statistically significant (p = 0.02, η² = 0.017), although the absolute scores remained low in both groups. The significant time-by-group interaction indicates that the pattern of changes over time differed significantly between the two groups. ANCOVA adjusted for potential confounders showed that the effect of time on the total psychological consequence score and the emotional and physical subscales was not significant, whereas it was significant for the social functioning score (p = 0.04). These results indicate that the increase in psychological consequence scores over time in the acceptance group coincided with undergoing screening.

This finding can be partly explained within the framework of the concept of “perceived health threat” based on the HBM. According to this model, increased perceived susceptibility to disease and perceived severity of its consequences may lead to emotional reactions such as anxiety, worry, and distress [25, 26]. Women who participate in screening programs may become more confronted with the possibility of disease, which in turn may trigger psychological consequences. This finding differs from the results of the study by Brown et al. (2008). In that study, women’s anxiety levels were high before mammography and often decreased after screening; only women who experienced more challenging situations, such as repeated imaging or indeterminate results, continued to experience high anxiety [38]. In contrast, in the present study, psychological consequences increased after the recommendation for mammography screening in the acceptance group. This inconsistency is also in line with the results of previous systematic reviews, which have shown that psychological consequences of screening may be reported differently depending on study design, measurement instruments, and timing of assessment [39]. It appears that in some women, merely hearing a recommendation to undergo mammography may be perceived as a serious health threat, thereby eliciting emotional reactions. From this perspective, the findings of the present study emphasize not only the importance of assessing psychological consequences of screening but also the need for psychological preparation of women during the screening decision-making stage.

Examination of the psychological consequences subscales showed that in the acceptance group, physical functioning scores increased significantly after the recommendation to undergo screening; however, this increase did not result in a statistically significant difference in the between-group comparison. In this regard, Hafslund et al. (2012) reported that among women participating in structured screening programs, physical functioning did not change significantly and that screening, when accompanied by adequate information, is not perceived as a threat to physical health [40]. In addition, Almutairi et al. (2025) showed that the physical dimension of the PCQ received the lowest score compared with the emotional and social dimensions [41], indicating that physical reactions are milder than other psychological consequences. At the same time, some studies have reported that under specific conditions, including subgroups with higher anxiety levels or particular individual contexts, physical or psychosomatic symptoms may occur; however, these symptoms do not necessarily indicate persistent physical dysfunction and are often transient in nature [23, 42].

In the domain of emotional functioning, the acceptance group in the present study experienced a statistically significant increase (from 1.58 ± 0.22 to 2.14 ± 0.25; time-by-group interaction: p < 0.001, η² = 0.038), such that participation in the screening process was associated with higher emotional and affective reactions. This finding is consistent with the results of some studies that used the PCQ. In this regard, Almutairi et al. (2025) reported that the emotional dimension had the highest mean among the PCQ subscales, such that even among women with mild overall anxiety, emotional components such as worry, fear, and mental distress were most prevalent [41]. In addition, in the study by Al-Alawi et al. (2019), although the majority of women obtained low overall psychological consequence scores, emotional and social consequences were reported to be significantly higher in certain groups, such as employed women or those with a family history of breast cancer [23]. This finding suggests that emotional and affective reactions may depend on lived experience, occupation, and family history and may vary in intensity across different cultures. These findings differed from those of Risica et al. (2018); in that study, participation in skin screening was not associated with increased psychological consequences [43], which may be attributed to differences in the nature of screenings and the psychological burden associated with breast cancer. Furthermore, available evidence suggests that in the United States, preventive screening programs are widely established and are typically implemented as part of routine healthcare, which may increase public familiarity with screening processes and potentially reduce the intensity of psychological reactions [44, 45]. In contrast, in the population studied here, the absence of organized psychological support, inadequate pre-screening education, and negative attitudes toward cancer may contribute to intensified emotional reactions when facing screening.

Social functioning impairment was statistically significantly greater in the acceptance group than in the refusal group (time-by-group interaction: p = 0.02, η² = 0.017). In this context, self-labeling and concerns about judgment or reactions from others may play an important role in reducing social participation. Feelings of shame, anxiety about future health, and fear of being stigmatized as a potential patient may lead to temporary isolation and limitations in social relationships [35, 46, 47]. The findings of the present study are consistent with those of Khurshid et al. (2023), who reported that social and emotional components were most affected by the screening process and that women described avoidance and feelings of insecurity in family interactions [42]. In addition,

Cockburn et al. (1992) showed that social consequences among women recalled for further assessment increased in the short term but usually subsided over time [35]. This pattern suggests that social consequences may be transient and dependent on the decision-making stage and initial exposure to screening. In contrast, Hafslund et al. (2012) reported that when adequate information and prior screening experience are present, the screening experience is accompanied by reassurance and an increased sense of control over health [40]. This difference may reflect the determining role of cultural context, the level of pre-screening education, and psychological support in shaping the social consequences of screening.

The findings of the present study, in comparing the two study groups (acceptance and refusal of mammography screening), showed that the two groups did not differ significantly in some individual and reproductive characteristics; however, women’s education levels were significantly higher in the acceptance group. Women with higher education levels may have greater health literacy, a factor that has been associated in previous studies with more active and informed health-related decision-making [48, 49]. In this regard, Musa et al. (2015) reported that higher education was a key predictor of participation in breast cancer screening in low- and middle-income countries [29]. Similarly, Seyedkanani et al. (2024) reported a statistically significant association between education level and mammography uptake, indicating that educated women participated significantly more in these programs [50]. This evidence suggests that greater attention to educational programs for women with lower education levels may be considered in future studies.

The results of the present study showed that the acceptance group also had higher awareness scores than the refusal group (76.60 ± 24.59 vs. 64.74 ± 24.37; mean difference = 11.86; p < 0.001). This difference indicates the co-occurrence of higher awareness with screening acceptance in the study sample. It appears that improving awareness, particularly among women with lower education levels, may play a key role in increasing participation. This result is consistent with the findings of Abdulla et al. (2025), who found that women who participated in breast cancer awareness programs were significantly more likely to use screening services and emphasized the decisive role of education and awareness in women’s active participation in screening [51].

Another finding of this study was an inverse relationship between psychological consequence scores and age and income, such that younger women experienced greater psychological consequences. This finding is consistent with the results of Al-Alawi et al. (2009), who also reported that women at younger ages, particularly those aged 40–50 years, experienced greater psychological consequences, especially in the domain of social functioning [23]. This pattern may be attributed to less lived experience, lower coping skills when facing health-threatening situations, and greater sensitivity to potential disease consequences at younger ages. In contrast, increasing age may be associated with greater emotional adaptation and a better ability to manage health-related anxiety. Higher income is also likely to be associated with better access to supportive resources, mental health services, and reduced financial stress [52–55], which in this study was associated with lower psychological consequences. In the present study, variables such as education, occupation, and number of children showed no significant association with psychological consequences. In addition, Mokhtari et al. (2014) reported that women’s health beliefs may influence breast screening behaviors, including self-examination and mammography, although these beliefs alone do not guarantee engagement in screening behaviors [56].

In this study, no statistically significant association was observed between the total psychological consequence score and awareness of breast cancer and its screening. This finding indicates that increasing awareness alone, without targeted education and effective emotional support, does not necessarily lead to improved psychological consequences. In this regard, Xie et al. (2019) showed that providing personalized information about breast cancer risk was associated with improved accuracy of risk estimation and changes in breast cancer–specific worry among women with higher baseline anxiety [57]. Overall, the findings of this section suggest that age and socioeconomic conditions may play an important role in the intensity of screening-related psychological consequences, whereas awareness alone is not necessarily a determining factor.

Limitations

This study had several limitations. Although family history of breast cancer, level of awareness about breast cancer and its screening, as well as variables that differed significantly between the two groups (including education level and intention to undergo mammography in the future), were entered into the analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) model as confounding factors and statistically controlled, the non-random assignment of participants to groups means that the possibility of selection bias and residual confounding cannot be completely ruled out. Women who accepted screening may differ inherently from those who refused screening in terms of psychological, motivational, or attitudinal characteristics that were not fully measured or controlled.

Another limitation of the study is that women’s previous experience with mammography screening, including the number of prior screenings and the timing of the most recent screening, was not assessed. These factors may influence psychological consequences and women’s participation behavior, and the lack of data in this regard may limit the accuracy of the result interpretation.

In Iran, a qualitative study by Lamyian et al. (2007) showed that cultural beliefs, fear of cancer, and insufficient awareness about screening are important factors influencing women’s attitudes toward and experiences of breast cancer screening [58]. In addition, during the follow-up phase, some participants withdrew from the study due to reasons such as occupational and family commitments, which may have increased the risk of attrition bias. Moreover, the mammography result status at the time of follow-up was not recorded, which should be considered when interpreting psychological consequences. Evidence from longitudinal studies indicates that psychological consequences related to screening may persist for longer periods after screening and that their assessment requires long-term follow-up [47]. Brett et al. (2001) reported that some psychological consequences associated with breast screening were observable even several years after screening or recall for further assessment [59].

Although the PCQ is widely used to assess short-term psychological consequences related to screening, previous studies have shown that this instrument may be less sensitive in identifying longer-term consequences [34]. The COS-BC instrument has been introduced as a more developed tool to provide more comprehensive coverage of screening-related consequences [48]. Given that the follow-up period in the present study was approximately one month after the screening recommendation, the use of the PCQ was considered appropriate for assessing short-term changes; however, the choice of this instrument may have limited the ability to detect more persistent psychological consequences. In addition, the standard deviation used for sample size calculation was derived from a PCQ study conducted in a non-mammography context, which, due to the lack of specific data at the time of study design, may have affected the accuracy of the sample size estimation.

Another limitation of the study was that reasons for refusing mammography were not examined in the refusal group; these reasons may be associated with different psychological characteristics and attitudes toward screening. Furthermore, the strict inclusion criteria may have limited the generalizability of the findings to all women eligible for mammography screening. Another limitation was reliance on self-reported data regarding psychological consequences and major life events, which may have exposed the study to information bias. In addition, the use of convenience sampling may have increased the risk of selection bias. Although the final distribution of the sample between acceptance and refusal groups was relatively balanced (54.1% vs. 45.9%), this does not necessarily eliminate the possibility of selection bias.

Overall, these limitations should be considered when interpreting the study findings, and future studies using longitudinal designs, random sampling, and standardized psychological instruments may help to improve the validity and generalizability of the results.

Conclusion

Overall, the findings of the present study showed that the recommendation to undergo mammography screening was associated with an increase in the mean total psychological consequence score, as well as increased emotional and social functioning impairment in the acceptance group. In addition, variables such as age and income were associated with the level of psychological consequences experienced during this process. These findings suggest that exposure to a screening recommendation, within the studied context, may be accompanied by emotional and social reactions. Given the observational nature of the present study, these results reflect associations observed within this population and screening implementation context, and causal interpretation requires future interventional studies.
