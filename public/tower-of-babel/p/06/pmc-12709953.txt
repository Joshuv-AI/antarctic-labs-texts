Development of an automated phenotyping platform and identification of a novel QTL for drought tolerance in soybean
Authors: Hakyung Kwon, Suk-Ha Lee, Moon Young Kim, Jungmin Ha
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Deep understanding of slow-wilting is essential for developing drought-tolerant crops. Existing approaches to measure transpiration rates are difficult to apply to large populations due to their high cost and low throughput. To overcome these challenges, we developed a high-throughput phenotyping system that integrates a load cell sensor and an Arduino-based microcontroller device. The system tracked the transpiration rate in real time by measuring changes in the pot weight in 224 recombinant inbred lines of Taekwangkong (fast-wilting) x SS2-2 (slow-wilting) under water-restricted conditions. Among five transpiration features we determined, stress recognition time point (SRTP) and decrease in transpiration rate by stress (DTrs) are informative parameters, that are interconnected and independently affect slow-wilting as well. Quantitative trait loci (QTL) for SRTP and DTrs were identified at the same location as the major QTL for slow wilting, qSW_Gm10, identified in the previous study. Notably, we found a novel major QTL for DTrs, qDTrs_Gm04, with a LOD value of 42 and PVE of 47 ​%. As a candidate gene for qDTrs_Gm04, GmWRKY58 was selected with differential expression between the parental lines under drought conditions as well as upstream sequence variation. Our high-throughput system is of help not only to biological research but breeding programs of drought-tolerant lines.

Introduction

Slow wilting in plants is characterized by their ability to retain leaf turgor and maintain leaf rigidity longer under water-deficit or drought conditions. This trait enables plants to continue essential physiological processes and reduce potential yield loss caused by such stressors [1,2]. The complexity of the slow wilting trait arises from diverse cellular mechanisms; a predominant theory suggests that slow wilting is primarily due to reduced transpiration rates, especially when the vapor pressure deficit (VPD) exceeds 2.0 ​kPa. This is achieved through a combination of factors, including the modulation of silver-sensitive aquaporins and precise stomatal conductance regulation [[3], [4], [5]]. Additionally, the role of increased osmoregulators—such as minerals, raffinose, stachyose, and oleic acid—has been recognized in enhancing drought tolerance [1]. Morphological adaptations also contribute to delaying wilting, such as thick leaf cuticles providing resilience against water loss [6].

To effectively identify the genetic factors underlying this complex trait, it is crucial to employ phenotyping methods that can provide accurate time-series physiological data from large populations. However, due to labor, cost, and technical limitations of conventional phenotyping methods, the number of loci identified for slow wilting remains limited, often exhibiting low logarithm of odds (LOD) scores and percentage of variance explained (PVE) [[7], [8], [9], [10]]. For example, Sarkar et al. [7] identified two QTLs for transpiration, qTR_Gm10_1 and qTR_Gm12_1, with LOD scores ranging from 4.45 to 9.26, while Menke et al. reported three QTLs for the slow-wilting trait through visual inspection, with LOD values up to 3.9, collectively explaining only 35 ​% of the phenotypic variance [11].

A major challenge arises from methods that capture only resultant phenotypes, such as visual inspection of wilting status. While these approaches are often cost-effective, they merely represent the final outcome, where the effects of different underlying strategies are confounded under drought stress. Such methods not only obscure the specific contributions of individual strategies but also introduce phenotyping biases due to the subjective timing of phenotyping assessments. Since wilting status fluctuates both between and within days, depending on environmental factors such as light and VPD, the timing of observations significantly influences phenotypic expression.

Another limitation originates from their low throughput for physiological indices such as transpiration rates, which is primarily due to their high cost and technical complexity. Methods like using leaf porometers or measuring the weight loss of de-rooted plants, while providing physiological insights, are inherently expensive and labor-intensive. This restricts their application to small sample size, typically limited to assessing only 12–20 genotypes at a time. Evaluating large populations by splitting them into subpopulations for such measurements introduce significant bias and increase the risk of erroneous phenotyping.

Recent advances in sensor technology and data processing algorithms have aimed to overcome these limitations, enabling high-throughput phenotyping; however, critical gaps remain, especially concerning temporal resolution. Techniques that utilize thermometers and RGB images facilitated the derivation of drought-related indices, such as canopy temperature depression and canopy green area, offering an estimation of the water stress level of the plant [[12], [13], [14], [15]]. Advancements in sensor miniaturization and the expanded use of unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) have greatly simplified the deployment of UAV-based thermal imaging for monitoring transpiration and water stress levels [[16], [17], [18], [19]]. While these innovations enable precise observation of the response of multiple individual plants to drought under field conditions, their temporal resolution is limited to the timing of UAV flights, hampering continuous data collection. This discontinuity is a significant drawback for studying “slow” wilting, a dynamic trait that requires high temporal resolution to capture time-dependent physiological responses.

To address the critical need for high-throughput phenotyping with high temporal resolution, promising advancements have been made with weighing lysimeter systems, which can provide detailed time-series transpiration profiles across a wide range of plant species [20,21]. Yet, the weighing lysimeters often come with prohibitive costs for construction and operation, limiting their scalability for large-population studies. The advent of affordable microcontroller unit (MCU) boards, coupled with the accessibility of open-source software, presents a transformative opportunity to create customized, cost-effective solutions for lysimeter-based high-throughput phenotyping platforms, thereby enabling continuous and detailed time-series transpiration profiling at scale [22,23].

Here, we developed a novel, affordable phenotyping platform based on weighing-lysimeter for automated and continuous transpiration profiling. Through this approach, we identified and measured five transpiration features, two of which provide a significantly more accurate assessment of slow wilting compared to conventional methods. This approach allowed us to refine the slow-wilting trait into specific, measurable components, resulting in the identification of previously known QTL with higher PVE and the detection of novel QTLs that were likely overlooked in earlier studies. Through more accurate diagnosis of slow wilting, our approach will provide valuable insights into drought stress resistance research and help advance the understanding of this critical trait.

Materials and methods (or methods)

Experimental design

The experimental design and process are illustrated in Fig. 1. The phenotyping platform was assembled using an Arduino-based system, which was calibrated to ensure precise weight measurements. After verification of the calibration and the ability to accurately measure weight, a program was implemented to record weight every hour, completing the setup of the system. The drought experiment was conducted on a Recombinant Inbred Line (RIL) population. Equal amounts of soil were placed in pots, where plants were cultivated under controlled conditions. The weight of the pots was continuously monitored to track transpiration through the weight loss profile. Raw data was collected over time, and outliers were identified and removed using statistical methods. Subsequently, regression analysis was applied to extract transpiration-related phenotypes, which were then compared and analyzed. Development of a high-throughput weight lysimeter system

The phenotyping platform employed in this study is a bundle of weight lysimeters. Each weight lysimeter is composed of three primary components: an MCU board, a load cell, and a module for the load cell. For an MCU board, Wemos d1 is used due to its affordability, compatibility of Arduino platform, built-in WiFi, and the presence of electrically erasable programmable read-only memory (EEPROM) (Table S1). Given the initial pot weight of approximately 1 ​kg, which is pre-filled with 360 ​g of soil and houses two soybean plants for 17 days in a fully hydrated state, we opted for a 5 ​kg load cell. The HX711 load cell amplifier is used for the weight sensor module. The Wemos d1 MCU board, 5 ​kg load cell, and HX711 module are connected as shown in Fig. 1. The calibration factor and zero factor are determined by applying masses of 500 ​g and 1.2 ​kg. The lysimeter is programmed to measure the weight three times consecutively per hour and save the average weight in the EEPROM. An initial 15-min delay was incorporated to secure the stable setup of the entire weight lysimeter array. The device-specific codes, including the calibration factor and zero factor, were compiled and uploaded using the Arduino Integrated Development Environment (IDE) software. Power was delivered via a 12V, 2A DC power supply adapter, which was distributed among 24 lysimeters. Plant materials and drought stress treatment

The plant materials and growth conditions used in this study are consistent with those employed in our previous research on slow wilting. This research, however, extends to include independent trials conducted in 2021 and 2022, totaling seven trials. All experiments were conducted in a greenhouse facility where environmental conditions were largely influenced by ambient outdoor conditions. Side and roof vents were kept open during non-rainy periods to facilitate natural air circulation and approximate ambient temperature and humidity as much as possible. An automated system ensured that the vents closed only during rainfall. To administer the drought stress treatment, each pot was placed on a weight lysimeter unit and the distribution of both plants and the units was randomized to ensure experimental validity. Every pot was sealed to prevent evaporation from the soil surface, ensuring that continuous decreases in pot weight directly reflect transpiration rates. Preliminary trials confirmed that empty, sealed pots showed negligible weight change, indicating that observed weight loss in experimental pots was due solely to plant transpiration. Additional border plants were placed to mitigate the potential for edge effects, such as increased sunlight exposure or wind for plants situated at the periphery of the experimental setup.

Extraction of transpiration features

A breakpoint identified through piecewise regression signifies the time plants take to recognize drought stress, termed the stress recognition time point (SRTP). Transpiration rates before and after this breakpoint are determined by the negative values of β1 and β2, respectively, labeled as baseline transpiration rate (Trb) and stress-induced transpiration rate (Trs). The cumulative transpiration amount to the stress recognition time point (CTaSRTP) is calculated by multiplying the Trb by the SRTP. The decrease in transpiration rate by stress (DTrs) is quantified with the formula: (Trb – Trs)/Trb.

Given that each trial is conducted under unique environmental conditions, the duration of the experiment varies across trials, as well as Trb and SRTP. To accommodate these variations and accurately capture the transpiration features influenced by environmental factors, we apply mixed linear models and the minimum norm quadratic unbiased estimation (MINQUE) [29] technique using R software [30] (Eq. (10)).(10)y=Xg·βg+Xe·βe+ϵwhere Xg and Xe represent the design matrices for genotype and environmental effects, respectively, and βg and βe are the corresponding fixed effect coefficients.

For cases lacking a discernible breakpoint, the SRTP was estimated by removing environmental effects from the duration of the experimental period.

Divergent selection of genotypes based on wilting traits

To enable the effective comparison of transpiration features by selecting genetically divergent groups for the slow-wilting trait, a slow-wilting index was developed. This index is based on two observed slow-wilting phenotypes; wilting score (visual inspections) and leaf moisture content (physiological measurement). While these traits, as investigated in previous study [34], are commonly used to quantify slow wilting, neither serves as a singular, definitive phenotype that fully represents the slow-wilting trait. However, as these two phenoytpes exhibit a high correlation with each other, a composite index was developed to establish a more comprehensive phenotypic ranking.

For each genotype, wilting scores and leaf moisture contents were first standardized using the scale function in R [29] to ensure equal weighting. The slow-wilting index for each genotype was then calculated as the averaged standardized score of its wilting score and leaf moisture content. Based on this averaged index, a ranking system was employed to identify the extremes of wilting response. Specifically, the 30 genotypes with the highest and lowest ranking RILs were categorized as slow-wilting and fast-wilting divergent groups, respectively.

Predictive modeling of slow-wilting traits via transpiration features

Transpiration features are subjected to a random forest [31] and extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) [32] models to predict the standardized score of slow-wilting traits. Both random forest and XGBoost models are implemented using R [30], The dataset was split into training (70 ​%) and test (30 ​%) sets and models are through 1000 bootstrapping iterations. To address the intercorrelation among transpiration features and avoid bias towards any single feature, the order of features was randomized in each iteration. Performance metrics including the root mean square error (RMSE) and coefficient of determination (R2) were calculated in every iteration to assess the model's predictive accuracy. For each iteration, the randomForest [33] trained the model with 1000 trees. Feature importance was assessed through the Mean Decrease in Gini (MDG) and Mean Decrease in Accuracy (MDA), calculating the impact of each feature on model accuracy and node purity. The XGBoost model was trained with 100 boosting rounds during each iteration, applying the squared error objective function and incorporating both L1 and L2 regularization techniques to prevent overfitting. Feature importance in the XGBoost model was evaluated based on the gain values of each feature to quantify their contribution to the model.

QTL analysis and survey of putative candidate gene

The phenotypic data on slow-wilting traits for the population derived from crossing between Taekwangkong and SS2-2, collected in 2021 and 2022, and the genotypic data for the same population were sourced from our previous study [34]. QTL analysis was conducted with the inclusive composite interval mapping option with additive effects (ICIM-ADD) using IciMapping 4.1 [35]. The multi-environment trial (MET) module utilized transpiration features from the 7 independent trials. The mapping interval was established at 1 centimorgan (cM). The threshold for the LOD for each trait was determined through one thousand permutation tests at a Type I error rate of 0.05. Loci with LOD scores exceeding the predetermined threshold were identified as QTLs for each method. To identify putative candidate genes in the qDTrs_Gm04, the same strategy used in our previous studies [34] was employed. Arabidopsis orthologues of genome-wide DEGs between Taekwangkong and SS2-2 under drought conditions, with |logFC| > 3 were subjected to Eat-UpTF [36] and iRegNet [37] to find upstream transcription factors of the DEG homologs.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software [30]. Comparisons between parental lines and divergent groups were performed using a t-test. Correlation analysis was conducted using the cor function. The distributions and the results of statistical analyses were visualized using the ggplot2 [38] package in R [30]. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using the prcomp function. The PCA results were visualized using the ggbiplot package [39].

Results

Development of an automated phenotyping platform for transpiration rate analysis

We designed an automated phenotyping platform utilizing low-cost weighing devices for hourly transpiration rate measurement, which cost approximately $5 each (Table S1). To gauge the consistency and reliability of the platform, we used a series of calibration weights of 0, 200, 500, and 1000 ​g on randomly selected 60 devices and recorded their readings over 48 ​h in the greenhouse. Linear regression analysis of the average values obtained for each calibration weight against the actual weights revealed a slope of 1.00 and an R2 value of 0.997 (Fig. S1, Table S2). This high degree of correlation indicates the platform's effectiveness in accurate data capture of weight changes, which further enables simultaneous evaluation of transpiration rate in a large-scale population under drought stress.

Automated phenotyping platform for quantitative analysis of transpiration dynamics

The automated phenotyping platform measured pot weights in the RIL population of Taekwangkong x SS2-2 while they are subjected to the drought condition. We first eliminated noise in the weight log caused by mechanical, electrical, and environmental factors using the LOWESS regression method (Fig. 1) (Cleveland, 1979). The cleaned data exhibited a distinct stair-step pattern in the load cell readings of each line; the pot weights decreased significantly during daylight hours and were steady after sunset, resulting from transpiration activity (Fig. 1). The stair-step pattern persisted until the weight log reached a virtually horizontal plateau, indicating that plants perceive drought stress and then significantly reduce the transpiration.

We segmented the weight logs into two distinct phases—before and after the onset of the plateau—to extract five potentially significant transpiration features influencing slow wilting that would not have been detectable using conventional phenotyping methods. The average weight loss rate before the plateau, which reflects the transpiration rate under normal conditions and may be influenced by factors such as stomatal density, stomatal size, and overall plant size, was designated as the baseline transpiration rate (Trb, g/h). The time required to reach the onset of the plateau, which can be monitored in real-time and provides an enhanced temporal resolution for detecting slow wilting, was labeled as the stress recognition time point (SRTP, hrs). The average weight loss during the transpiration plateau was termed the stress-induced transpiration rate (Trs, g/h) and indicates the transpiration rate under drought stress conditions. The decrease in transpiration rate due to stress (DTrs) quantifies the reduction in transpiration rate under drought stress and may reflect the plant's ability to maintain optimal gas exchange in various environments. Finally, the cumulative transpiration amount up to the stress recognition time point (CTaSRTP, g) likely reflects the threshold level of soil water deficit perceived by the plant as drought stress, which triggers the regulation of the transpiration rate to the plateau.

Validation of transpiration features through divergent groups

To evaluate whether the five newly defined transpiration features accurately reflect slow wilting, we measured these features in two divergent groups with the parental lines representing slow- and fast-wilting. The difference in SRTP was particularly pronounced (p ​= ​1.33E-07) between the divergent groups, with the slow-wilting group showing an SRTP of 157.2 ​h, compared to 125.0 ​h in the fast-wilting group (Table S3). Furthermore, the slow-wilting divergent group outperformed the slow-wilting parental line SS2-2. Similarly, the slow-wilting group showed a reduction of 47.0 ​% in DTrs, even smaller than SS2-2, while DTrs of the fast-wilting group was equivalent to Taekwangkong at 72 ​% (Fig. 2G). In Trs and CTaSRTP, although significant (p ​= ​7.23E-07 for Trs and p ​= ​4.11E-04 for CTaSRTP), the differences between the divergent groups were less pronounced than those between the parental lines (Fig. 2B, F, and 2I; See also Table S3). Trb tendency in the divergent groups was opposite to that of the parental lines (Fig. 2B and E; See also Table S3). Considering consistency with the slow-wilting traits (wilting score and moisture content), SRTP and DTrs are identified as significant indicators representing slow wilting. Specifically, a higher SRTP is beneficial, while a lower DTrs is advantageous for slow wilting.

Correlation analysis between transpiration features and slow-wilting traits

We conducted correlation analysis between the transpiration features and slow-wilting index across the entire RILs of Taekwangkong x SS2-2 (Table S4). SRTP, Trs, and CTaSRTP were in positive relationships with the slow-wilting trait, while the relationships of Trb and DTrs were negative. Correlation degrees of SRTP, Trs, and DTrs with the slow-wilting traits were moderate with the absolute values of Pearson coefficient above 0.50. Pearson coefficient values of Trb and CTaSRTP for slow-wilting index were relatively low at −0.26 and 0.32, respectively. Inter-correlation analysis among the transpiration features revealed strong correlations of DTrs with SRTP at −0.85 (Table S4). Consistent with the previous analysis comparing transpiration features in parental lines and divergent groups, the correlation analysis of the entire RILs suggests that a high SRTP and low DTrs well represent the slow wilting.

Principal component analysis of transpiration features and slow-wilting index

Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed that the variation in five transpiration features and the slow-wilting index can be explained by two components, PC1 and PC2, accounting for 73.6 ​% of the total variance (Fig. 3). Specifically, the first principal component (PC1) explained 48.2 ​% of the variation and clearly differentiated the divergent groups. The slow-wilting group was associated with PC1 and PC2, positively and negatively, respectively. The slow-wilting index contributed to PC1 with a loading value of 0.45. Notably, SRTP and DTrs made significant contributions to PC1, with loading values of 0.52 and −0.53, respectively. PC2 explained 25.4 ​% of the variation, but did not distinguish the slow- and fast-wilting groups, nor was it strongly influenced by the slow-wilting index, which had a loading value of 0.15. Instead, PC2 was mainly driven by Trb and CTaSRTP, with loading values of 0.66 and 0.69, respectively. QTL analysis

We conducted QTL analysis on two important transpiration features, SRTP and DTrs, along with the two slow-wilting traits to determine if the loci identified for transpiration features overlap with those associated with the slow-wiling traits, or if the transpiration features can reveal new QTLs that were not detectable using the previous phenotyping methods (Table 1). A major QTL, qSW_Gm10, was identified for both slow-wilting traits (wilting score and leaf moisture content) with LOD scores of 17.62 and 11.72 and PVE values of 8.94 ​% and 8.03 ​%, respectively. Identification of candidate gene for qDTrs_Gm04

Given the consistent importance of DTrs demonstrated by our various analysis, and the remarkable statistical significance of the novel major QTL, qDTrs_Gm04, we proceeded to identify candidate genes within this locus. Within the qDTrs_Gm04 interval, 25 protein-coding genes (Glyma.04G221200 to Glyma.04G223600) were identified as potential candidates (Table S5). Using the resequencing data and transcriptome data from the parental lines, we first prioritized genes that were differentially expressed (DEGs) under drought stress and also possessed nucleotide variations in their putative cis-regulatory elements (defined as the 2 ​kb upstream or downstream region from the coding sequence). This led to the identification of five genes, and among these, only one gene, Glyma.04G223300, had previously reported drought-related functions in its Arabidopsis orthologs.

Under drought conditions, GmWRKY58 (Glyma.04G223300) was significantly up-regulated in SS2-2 compared to Taekwangkong, while no significant difference was observed under control conditions (Table S6). In the putative promoter region of GmWRKY58, located 2 ​kb upstream of the transcription start site, we identified 4 SNPs, 2 insertions (Fig. 7B), and a structural variation (Fig. 7C) between Taekwangkong and SS2-2. To further investigate the candidacy of GmWRKY58, we compared its expression level with its paralogs under both control and drought conditions (Fig. 7A). Due to whole genome duplication in soybean, GmWRKY58 has several paralogs, including Glyma.06G142000, Glyma.14G186100, and Glyma.17G197500, which may exhibit functional redundancy. None of the paralogs, however, were detected as QTLs, suggesting they may not play a significant causal role in the slow-wilting phenotype in this population. To verify the function of GmWRKY58 as a transcription factor in response to drought, we analyzed its association with genome-wide DEGs. Against 643 genome-wide DEGs detected between Taekwangkong and SS2-2 under drought condition, we investigated their Arabidopsis homologs to identify their upstream transcription factors. Using Eat-UpTF, 187 Arabidopsis homologs were found to be significantly enriched in association with the homolog of GmWRKY58, AtWRKY70, at a q-value of 0.013. Similarly, iRegNet result confirmed significant correlations of expressions between AtWRKY70 and the homologs of DEGs in the parental lines under the drought condition with a p-value of 3.93e-18 (Fig. 7D and Table S7). These homologs include drought-related genes such as DREB1D [40], NAC1 [41,42], HB-7 [43]. These findings suggest that the variants in the promoter region might result in up-regulation of GmWRKY58 in SS2-2 in response to drought, influencing the expression of downstream drought-related genes and eventually enhancing drought tolerance in SS2-2.

Discussion and Conclusion

Measuring the slow-wilting traits for drought tolerance is a time-consuming and costly endeavor, particularly challenging in large populations such as breeding lines (F2 progenies or RILs). In this study, we developed a highly affordable automated platform for phenotyping transpiration-related features. This platform represents a significant advancement over conventional methods, offering high throughput, enhanced efficiency, and superior accuracy at a remarkably low cost (Table S1, Table S8). Unlike labor-intensive visual assessments or destructive leaf moisture content measurements, this automated platform provides non-destructive whole-plant monitoring, drastically reducing labor time from hours to automated processes (Table S8). This translates to high throughput, enabling continuous monitoring for over 200 ​h per individual plant, with average measurements taken three times every hour. This level of continuous data acquisition is unattainable by methods reliant on discrete measurements (e.g., UAVs). In terms of cost-effectiveness, the platform can be assembled for as little as $5 per unit, with the flexibility to combine multiple units as needed (Table S1). The platform's low electrical requirements allow it to be powered by a standard commercial power supplier (9V 2A), supporting up to 24 devices. Furthermore, our platform demonstrates superior detection accuracy, evidenced by its excellent linearity (slope of 1.00 and R2 value of 0.997) against calibration weights (Fig. S1, Table S2). Above all, its ability to collect time-series data eliminates the need to consider specific observation times and overcomes temporal resolution limits of other methods, facilitating early and objective detection of physiological changes in vivo before severe wilting symptoms and preventing seed loss of advanced lines. Thus, it should provide a powerful tool for breeding programs of drought tolerance and comprehensive understanding of plant wilting dynamics.

Using the time-series weight data for the Taekwangkong x SS2-2 RIL population, our platform enabled the observation of a stepwise weight loss pattern that reflects transpiration activity in response to diurnal changes (Fig. 1). At a certain point during drought stress, this pattern was disrupted, marking what we termed SRTP. The occurrence of SRTP is not a result of permanent tissue loss but rather a strategic physiological response of the plant. While RILs were subjected to drought stress until more than half of them kept the second trifoliate healthy without permanent tissue damage, 92 ​% of the RILs exhibited SRTP by the end of the experiment. This suggests that SRTP, followed by a plateau in transpiration, likely results not from loss of transpiration ability, but from an internal regulatory mechanism in response to drought stress.

Using the automatically obtained SRTP, we successfully re-identified the previously known slow-wilting QTL qSW_Gm10 (Table 1) with even higher PVE (35.58 ​%), demonstrating that SRTP can effectively replace earlier manual phenotyping methods (8.03–8.94 ​%). While previous methods are subject to errors due to observer's bias in judging the timing of phenotypes and time differences in the phenotyping process, transpiration features including SRTP are less prone to errors as observed continuously and simultaneously across the population with higher temporal resolution. Through a more precise assessment of slow wilting, SRTP facilitates the identification of slow-wilting QTL with enhanced PVE.

The major locus, qDTrs_Gm04, was newly discovered using DTrs with a LOD score exceeding 42. This locus was associated with drought-related traits in previous GWAS studies [[44], [45], [46], [47], [48], [49]] (Fig. 5, Table 1). However, this locus had not been identified by QTL analysis using slow-wilting traits such as wilting score and relative leaf moisture content (Table 1). The lack of detection in traditional slow-wilting traits might be attributed to their reliance on single-point measurements, which capture the net effect of various complex and interacting mechanisms. This approach may obscure the individual contributions of distinct mechanisms, as the observed phenotype reflects the cumulative result of various effects. In contrast, the transpiration features such as DTrs obtained from the time-series data through our automated platform provides continuous monitoring of transpiration dynamics. This approach allows for the dissection of the slow-wilting trait into distinct transpiration features, thereby revealing new QTLs that were previously hidden by the limitations of earlier phenotyping methods.

To control transpiration rate or water loss from the plant under stress conditions, regulation of stomatal conductance is crucial. The degree of stomatal closure varies depending on plant species and genotypes even within a species. Complex mechanisms involving various hormones such as ABA, auxin and cytokinin, along with signaling molecules including ROS and flavonoids, may influence whether the stomata close completely or partially [[50], [51], [52], [53], [54]]. Initially, a moderate accumulation of ROS under drought stress acts as a signaling molecule, aiding in stomatal closure and thereby reducing transpiration. However, prolonged stress with complete stomatal closure may lead to the accumulation of excessive ROS, which turns ROS into a source of oxidative stress potentially causing severe damage including cell death [55]. All our findings, including the reduced DTrs under stress condition in the slow-wilting divergent group compared to the fast-wilting group (Fig. 2H and Table S3), the negative correlation between DTrs and slow-wilting traits (Table S4), and the shift in allelic proportion from fast-wilting to slow-wilting RILs (Fig. 6), suggest that maintaining a moderate level of transpiration, possibly by not fully closing the stomata, could be a more effective strategy to manage prolonged stress, thereby preventing permanent tissue damage or plant death.

Previous reports of the roles of GmWRKY58 and its homologs AtWRKY54 and AtWRKY70 further support the candidacy of GmWRKY58 as a candidate gene for qDTrs_Gm04. The expression of GmWRKY58 was reported to be induced by ABA, a key hormone involved in drought stress, and H2O2, which serves as a signaling molecule in the plant's drought stress response [56]. AtWRKY54 and AtWRKY70 function in various biotic and abiotic stress including necrotrophic pathogens, salt, and drought stress in Arabidopsis [57,58]. Double knockout mutants of AtWRKY54 and AtWRKY70 exhibit reduced stomata conductance and transpiration under normal conditions and show complete stomata closure under ABA treatment [58]. Meanwhile, over-expression of MfWRKY70 of Myrothamnus flabellifolia exhibits drought tolerance in Arabidopsis with reduced stomata aperture and water loss under water withholding conditions, with a decrease of the ROS level [57]. AtWRKY30, which directly interacts with AtWRKY70, is up-regulated under oxidative stress in Arabidopsis and enhances drought tolerance in wheat [[59], [60], [61]]. The function of GmWRKY58 is thus suggested to be relevant with regulation of stomata conductance and transpiration rate leading to slow wilting.

Our phenotyping platform offers notable advancements, yet some limitations need to be considered for future investigations. While our system precisely captures continuous measurements in a controlled greenhouse, validating the identified transpiration features and QTLs in varied, complex field conditions is essential. Moreover, functional validation of the candidate gene suggested for qDTrs_Gm04, GmWRKY58, through transgenic approaches (e.g., overexpression or gene editing) is crucial to confirm their causal roles in slow wilting and drought tolerance. Computational simulation techniques, like functional-structural plant models, also show considerable promise for gaining deeper mechanistic insights and expanding predictive capabilities beyond direct experimentation, thus complementing our current findings.

In conclusion, our study introduces a cost-effective automated phenotyping platform for assessing transpiration-related features in drought tolerance. By utilizing time-series data, we identified distinct transpiration patterns and extracted five transpiration features potentially related to the slow-wilting phenotype. SRTP was identified as a key indicator of slow wilting, detecting the same locus associated with slow-wilting traits as previous methods. With high temporal resolution, SRTP offers higher precision compared to traditional manual phenotyping, greatly enhancing the PVE of the identified QTL. Furthermore, by utilizing continuous time-series data to dissect the slow-wilting trait into detailed transpiration characteristics, DTrs enabled the identification of novel QTLs, qDTrs_Gm04, that had been previously undetected, demonstrating the platform's potential for advancing the understanding of drought-related mechanisms. Through comprehensive analysis of transpiration characteristics, genomic and transcriptomic data, along with a review of prior studies, we suggest GmWRKY58 as a candidate gene associated with slow wilting, regulating drought-related downstream genes, and thereby modulating stomatal conductance and transpiration rate. Our platform offers a valuable tool for breeding programs aimed at improving drought tolerance, enhances the mechanistic understanding of plant responses to drought stress, and serves as a methodological reference for future studies.
