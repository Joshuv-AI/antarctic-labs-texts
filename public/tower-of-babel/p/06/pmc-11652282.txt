Intra-articular Synovial Ganglion of the Wrist
Authors: María Teresa Ramírez, Gustavo Molina, Cinzia Di Carlo, Vicente Fernández, Sebastian Von Unger, Nicolás Thumm, Philipp Anwandter, Gonzalo Morgan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractSynovial ganglions, common soft tissue tumors of the hand and wrist, typically occur in the dorsal and volar aspects of the wrist, but intra-articular cases are rare. We present a case of a 27-year-old equestrian with persistent left wrist pain who was diagnosed with an intra-articular radiocarpal synovial ganglion after failed conservative management. Arthroscopic resection led to complete resolution of symptoms and functional recovery. This case highlights the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges posed by intra-articular synovial ganglions and underscores the efficacy of arthroscopic intervention in such cases. Although the literature on this specific presentation is scarce, our experience advocates for arthroscopic resection as an effective treatment strategy for intra-articular synovial ganglions refractory to conservative measures.Key words: Intra-articular synovial ganglion, Wrist arthroscopy, Wrist pain

Synovial ganglions are benign cysts filled with mucinous fluid attached by a stalk to a joint or tendon sheath. They represent the most common soft tissue tumor of the hand and wrist, accounting for 50%–70% of all tumors in this area.1 Although their etiology remains unknown, one theory suggests they are secondary to acute joint stress (trauma) or, more commonly, chronic stress (repetitive wrist extension), where a tear in the joint capsule may occur, releasing synovial fluid into the surrounding tissue.2 Additionally, repetitive stress on capsule structures and supporting ligaments stimulates fibroblasts that produce hyaluronic acid, which accumulates to produce mucinous gelatinous material.3

They occur at any age but are more frequent between the second and fourth decades of life, affecting 3 times more women than men.1 Usually singular, they can affect nearly any joint, but most commonly the dorsal aspect of the wrist (60%–70%), followed by the radial volar aspect (20%–30%), and, finally, the flexor tendon sheath of the fingers (10%).2

Histologically, the ganglion’s stalk has a tortuous lumen connecting the cyst to the underlying joint. The cyst wall consists of collagen sheets randomly oriented in loose layers, with fully functional fibroblasts. Analysis of ganglion fluid reveals predominantly hyaluronic acid, along with lesser amounts of glucosamine, globulins, and albumin.2

Clinically, a ganglion appears as a hard mass of varying size, well-defined, not adherent to deep planes, and typically showing positive transillumination (ie, light passes through and illuminates the ganglion entirely). It may be associated with pain and/or functional impairment, regardless of its size and location. These masses can appear suddenly or develop over months. However, spontaneous regression has been reported in 38%–58% of patients in some series.1

There are various types of synovial ganglions, including periarticular, intraosseous, periosteal, and intra-articular ones.4 The latter are very infrequent (0.9%–1.3%), typically asymptomatic, and often incidentally discovered in imaging studies. They have been scarcely described in the literature, mostly in the context of the knee and related to cruciate ligaments.4,5 Based on our search, we could not find reports of intra-articular ganglions affecting the wrist.

Case Report

A 27-year-old woman who was an equestrian enthusiast with no significant medical history consulted a health care center in 2017 because of long-standing left wrist pain without any associated trauma, significantly impacting her daily activities, especially sports-related ones. Imaging studies revealed an intra-articular synovial ganglion using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Initial management included sports rest, wrist immobilization, analgesics, and subsequent physical therapy. As symptoms persisted, an intra-articular corticosteroid injection was performed, resulting in complete symptom relief for 1 year. With symptoms recurring, intermittent physical therapy was resumed for approximately 2 years with partial relief. Because of persistent discomfort, her physical therapist referred her to our center for reevaluation.

In January 2020, she consulted with the senior author of this article. Physical examination revealed no external or neurovascular alterations but demonstrated limited wrist flexion-extension because of pain at maximal ranges. Another MRI of the left wrist (Figure 1, Figure 2) was performed as her previous study was more than 3 years old and conducted before multiple treatments, including infiltration. It confirmed the presence of an intra-articular radiocarpal synovial ganglion in a volar position between the long and short radiolunate ligaments. Additionally, anteroposterior, lateral, and dynamic wrist radiographs (Figure 3, Figure 4) were requested to assess bone anatomy and verify no intercarpal instability.

Given this scenario, management options considered maintaining an expectant approach, a new infiltration, or evaluation and arthroscopic resection. Because of the failure of previous management and the discomfort significantly affecting daily life activities, the decision was made to proceed with surgical management.6 Under axillary anesthesia and ischemia, wrist traction was applied, and radiocarpal arthroscopy was performed through 3–4 and 6R portals. Radiocarpal exploration ruled out other lesions, identifying the intra-articular volar ganglion between the radiolunate ligaments (Fig. 5). Using a 2.9 shaver, complete ganglion resection was achieved, resulting in the expulsion of mucinous fluid. Joint cleaning was completed. After releasing traction and ischemia, portal sutures were performed, followed by dressing.

Radiocarpal arthroscopy identifying the intra-articular volar ganglion between the radiolunate ligaments.

Seven days after surgery, the patient reported minimal pain and experienced hypoesthesia in the medial forearm and thumb pulp and dorsum that was attributed to the ischemic cuff or plexus anesthesia. Wounds appeared well without discharge or signs of infection and with minimal wrist edema and good finger mobility. At the 2-week follow-up, stitches were removed, and wrist movement ranges were assessed: flexion at 50°, extension at 60°, and pronosupination at 90°–90°. Hypoesthesia in the medial forearm and thumb pulp had improved, demonstrating adequate discrimination of 2 points at 6 mm. After 1 month, she remained pain free, achieved complete range of motion, and recovered sensation in the forearm and hand.

Two years after surgery, the patient remained pain free, without complications or postsurgical sequelae. Physical examination revealed preserved hand strength and normal joint ranges compared to the contralateral side. Currently, she engages in equestrian activities and gym exercises without limitations or discomfort.

Discussion

Wrist pain is a common reason for orthopedic consultation, stemming from traumatic injuries, overuse, or progressive conditions without apparent causes. A useful approach involves compartmentalizing wrist pain into dorsal, volar, radial, and/or ulnar aspects, aiding in organizing different etiologies causing wrist pain based on anatomical segments.

A frequent cause of wrist pain, particularly dorsal, is synovial ganglions. These often spontaneously resolve after varying time periods, making expectant management the initial treatment choice. Diagnosis is usually straightforward given the characteristic ganglion mass, which is well-defined, nonadherent to deep planes, usually less painful, and variable in size and shows positive transillumination. Although not always necessary, complementing the assessment with conventional and dynamic wrist radiographs can help rule out other causes of wrist pain, sequelae of traumatic injuries, Kienbock’s disease, possible carpal instabilities, and soft tissue ultrasound to adequately characterize the tumor and exclude other potential tumor-like lesions such as giant cell tumor.7 MRI can play a role in studying wrist pain and ganglions, especially in post-trauma, recurrent, occult ganglions, or when another condition causing or associated with pain is suspected in the presence of a ganglion. It is essential to note that the presence of a ganglion does not always explain the patient’s discomfort.

In this particular case, an MRI was justified as there was no external evidence of lesions, and radiographic and ultrasound studies were interpreted as normal. Although, as mentioned earlier, expectant management of synovial ganglions is appropriate in a significant number of patients, in our patient’s case, after 3 years from the initial diagnosis until the consultation in our center, there was no symptomatic improvement, except for the short period following joint infiltration. Additionally, the presence of an intra-articular ganglion in the wrist is an unprecedented finding in the literature, making it impossible to infer treatment outcomes or natural progression. Therefore, surgical resolution was deemed appropriate, and considering its intra-articular location, the option of performing drainage and arthroscopic resection provided an excellent way to assess possible associated lesions and conduct resection with minimal soft tissue damage.8

We believe that in the assessment of wrist pain, history, physical examination, and complementary tests are essential.9 MRI demonstrates its utility in detecting hidden or uncommon lesions, and the presence of intra-articular ganglions may be a cause of pain. As there are no reports of these ganglions in the wrist, it is challenging to extrapolate our results, but we believe that the presence of a ganglion in a small and narrow joint space like the radiocarpal joint could lead to discomfort and joint impingement. Considering its intra-articular location, surgical management with wrist arthroscopy is an excellent option, causing minimal damage to adjacent soft tissues and ensuring rapid postoperative recovery.10
