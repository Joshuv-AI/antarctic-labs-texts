Rare vasculitis types and obstetric and neonatal outcomes – A population‐based study
Authors: Uri Amikam, Ahmad Badeghiesh, Haitham Baghlaf, Richard Brown, Michael H. Dahan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveVasculitis is an infrequent pathology among reproductive‐aged women. While data exists regarding pregnancy outcomes in the more common vasculitis subtypes, data is limited regarding these outcomes in rare vasculitis subtypes. We aimed to compare pregnancy and perinatal outcomes between women who suffered from rare types of vasculitis and those who did not.MethodsThis was a retrospective cohort study using the Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project‐ Nationwide Inpatient Sample (HCUP‐NIS). All pregnant women who delivered or had a maternal death in the US (2004–2014) with an ICD‐9 diagnosis of any of the following rare vasculitis subtypes: Polyarteritis nodosa, acute febrile mucocutaneous lymph node syndrome, hypersensitivity angiitis, Wegner's granulomatosis, giant cell arteritis, and Takayasu arteritis were included in the study. We divided the cohort into women with rare vasculitides (study group) and women without (control group). The obstetric and perinatal outcomes of the two groups were compared using multivariate logistic regression, adjusting for potential confounders.ResultsA total of 9 096 788 women met the inclusion criteria. Among them, 335 women (3.7/100000) had a diagnosis of rare vasculitis. Women with rare vasculitis, compared to those without, were more likely to be older; Caucasian; in the highest income quartile; be insured by private insurance; and have a body mass index (BMI, calculated as weight in kilograms divided by the square of height in meters) ≥30, pregestational diabetes mellitus, thyroid disorders, and chronic hypertension (P < 0.05, all). Patients in the rare vasculitis group, compared to those without, had a higher rate of hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] 1.89, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.4–2.56, P < 0.001); preterm delivery (aOR 1.76, 95% CI: 1.28–2.42, P < 0.001); and blood products transfusion (aOR 3.68, 95% CI: 2.14–6.34, P < 0.001); with higher rates of cesarean delivery (CD) (aOR 1.31, 95% CI: 1.05–1.64, P = 0.018); and a higher rate of congenital anomalies (aOR 4.1, 95% CI: 2.03–8.31, P < 0.001). Other perinatal outcomes, including placental abruption, hysterectomy, and venous thromboembolism, were comparable between the two groups.ConclusionWomen with rare vasculitis had a higher incidence of maternal complications, including hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, cesarean delivery and preterm delivery, as well as an increased risk of congenital anomalies.

INTRODUCTION

Vasculitis encompasses myriad disorders marked by blood vessel inflammation, which may result in damage, narrowing, or even blockage of the vessel walls. 1 This inflammation can impact arteries, veins, and capillaries, leading to end‐organ ischemia. The symptoms and complications vary based on the size and location of the affected vessels and the organs involved. While some vasculitis types are restricted to specific areas, others can be systemic, impacting multiple organs at once. The underlying causes of vasculitis are intricate, involving immune‐mediated processes that may be triggered by infections, medications, or other autoimmune conditions. 2

Vasculitis is typically classified based on the size of the affected blood vessels, as large‐, medium‐, and small vessel vasculitis. 1 Its incidence varies globally and is frequently underreported due to its heterogeneous presentation and potential for misdiagnosis. 3 Vasculitis displays significant variations in prevalence, gender distribution, and age at diagnosis across its different subtypes. Generally, many forms of vasculitis tend to affect women more often than men, although this may vary based on the specific vasculitis type.

In recent years, the number of pregnancies among women with vasculitis has significantly risen, thanks to advancements in survival rates and quality of life (QoL). 4 While some vasculitis types are more studied among pregnant women, like Bechet's disease 5 and IgA vasculitis, 6 the majority of vasculitis and pregnancy outcomes are not well‐studied in the literature. Previous studies demonstrated that women with Bechet's disease and IgA vasculitis are at increased risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes, including venous thromboembolism (VTE), preterm delivery, and hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (HDP). 5 , 6 , 7 Two recent large studies were published on rare vasculitis types, though with some important limitations. One population‐based study focused mainly on HDP and preterm birth and did not evaluate other maternal and neonatal complications, 8 whereas another evaluated women using a database of employment‐sponsored health insurance claims, which limited its scope and generalizability. 9 Given the limited data on this topic, our primary objective was to investigate and compare pregnancy, delivery, and neonatal outcomes in women diagnosed with rare vasculitis types compared to those without, using a large population‐based cohort.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design

We conducted a retrospective population‐based cohort study using the Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project‐Nationwide Inpatient Sample (HCUP‐NIS) database between January 1, 2004, to December 31, 2014. This dataset is the largest inpatient database in the US, covering approximately 20% of hospital admissions. Annually, it provides detailed information on 7 000 000 inpatient stays, including patient characteristics, diagnoses, and procedures.

Collected data included demographic and obstetric factors, labor, and short‐term maternal and neonatal outcomes through discharge. Baseline clinical characteristics included obesity (body mass index [BMI, calculated as weight in kilograms divided by the square of height in meters] ≥30), smoking during pregnancy, previous cesarean delivery (CD), and pregestational diabetes mellitus (DM).

Pregnancy and delivery outcomes assessed were HDP (including pre‐eclampsia, eclampsia, gestational hypertension and superimposed pre‐eclampsia), placental abruption, preterm delivery (defined as delivery <37 gestational weeks), CD, postpartum hemorrhage, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), and VTE.

Neonatal outcomes included small‐for‐gestational‐age (SGA) infants, congenital anomalies, and intrauterine fetal death (IUFD).

Study participants

The unit of analysis was all women who delivered or experienced maternal death between January 1, 2004, and December 31, 2014, with each pregnancy counted once. Included in the cohort were only potentially viable pregnancies (≥24 weeks' gestation), and excluded were women with earlier miscarriages.

Women were categorized into study and control groups based on vasculitis diagnosis: women with vasculitis comprised the study group, and those without comprised the control group. Vasculitis was identified using the International Classification of Diseases ninth revision (ICD‐9) diagnosis codes and included polyarteritis nodosa; acute febrile mucocutaneous lymph node syndrome; hypersensitivity angiitis; Wegner's granulomatosis; giant cell arteritis, and Takayasu arteritis (Table S1). Women with Behçet's disease, IgA vasculitis, or cryoglobulinemia were excluded due to prior comprehensive analyses, 5 , 6 , 7 or associated systemic conditions. 10 Additionally, if a woman was diagnosed with both a condition included in the study and one of the excluded conditions, she was excluded from the study group.

Statistical analysis

Once this cohort was created, demographic characteristics were assessed for missing data. The missing data were noted to be randomly distributed. Therefore, Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) method was used for imputation. The prevalence of vasculitis among pregnant women was calculated, and baseline characteristics were compared between the study and control groups using chi‐square tests. Multicollinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF); variables with VIF <5 indicated no significant multicollinearity and were included in the analysis. To account for the complex sampling design of the HCUP‐NIS, discharge weights provided by the HCUP were used to calculate national prevalence estimates. Standard errors for the multivariate logistic regression models were adjusted to account for the clustering of patients within hospitals.

Unadjusted and adjusted logistic regression models assessed the association between vasculitis and maternal and neonatal outcomes, reporting odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Models were adjusted for significant confounders identified in univariate analyses, including maternal demographics and pre‐existing conditions. Analyses were performed using SPSS version 23.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, New York, USA). The present study is reported following the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) checklist.

Informed consent was waived due to the retrospective design of the study.

This study used publicly accessible, anonymized data. Therefore, institutional review board approval was not required in accordance with articles 2.2 and 2.4 of the Tri‐Council Policy Statement (2010).

RESULTS

Among the total of 9 096 788 women meeting the inclusion criteria, 335 were diagnosed with vasculitis either prior to or during pregnancy, resulting in an overall prevalence of 3.7/100000. Notably, the prevalence of vasculitis diagnoses experienced a significant increase throughout the study period (P < 0.001) (Figure 1). Women with vasculitis, as compared to those without, were characterized by increased maternal age (17.9% ≥35 years vs. 14.7%, P = 0.003); were more likely of Caucasian race (59.1% vs. 49.3%, P = 0.011); more likely to be wealthier (P < 0.001); to have private insurance (59.1% vs. 50.6%, P < 0.001); to have a BMI ≥30 (6.9% vs. 3.6%, P < 0.001); to suffer from chronic hypertension (13.1% vs. 1.8%, P < 0.001); to have pregestational DM (2.1% vs. 1%, P = 0.032); and to suffer from thyroid disorders (9% vs. 2.5%, P < 0.001). Women with a vasculitis diagnosis, compared to those without, had a higher rate of HDP (adjusted OR [aOR] 1.89, 95% CI: 1.4–2.56, P < 0.001). They also had a higher rate of preterm premature rupture of membranes (PPROM) (aOR 2.59, 95% CI: 1.38–4.86, P = 0.003); a higher rate of preterm delivery (aOR 1.76, 95% CI: 1.28–2.42, P < 0.001); a higher rate of CD (aOR 1.31, 95% CI: 1.05–1.64, P = 0.018); and a higher rate of blood product transfusion (aOR 3.68, 95% CI: 2.14–6.34, P < 0.001). Other pregnancy and delivery outcomes, such as placental abruption, chorioamnionitis, maternal death, VTE, and DIC, were comparable between groups (P > 0.05, all).

Neonatal outcomes are presented in Table 3. Women with vasculitis, compared to controls, had a higher rate of congenital anomalies (aOR 4.1, 95% CI: 2.03–8.31, P < 0.001). There was a trend toward a higher incidence of SGA neonates in the study group, not reaching statistical significance (aOR 1.61, 95% CI: 0.95–2.72, P = 0.076). Given the size of the database, this likely represents that no difference existed. There was no difference in IUFD rate between groups.

DISCUSSION

We compared pregnancy, delivery, and neonatal outcomes between mothers with vasculitis and those without. Our key findings were: (1) We found an increasing prevalence of pregnancies in women with vasculitis across the study period. (2) Pregnant women with vasculitis were characterized by increasing maternal age, and higher rates of Caucasian race, a higher income quartile, private medical insurance, higher BMI, DM, chronic hypertension, and thyroid disorders. (3) Women with vasculitis had an increased risk of preterm delivery, HDP, and CD. (4) Women with vasculitis had higher rates of fetuses with congenital anomalies compared to women without vasculitis.

During the 11‐year study period, the prevalence of vasculitis diagnosis among women admitted for delivery nearly tripled (P < 0.001). In recent decades, increased knowledge in the diagnosis and management of vasculitis has led to a reduction in mortality and morbidity among these patients; the consequent improvement in QoL could include, for young women, the possibility of successfully carrying one or more pregnancies. 4

Regarding maternal characteristics in our cohort, women with vasculitis were more likely to be older. This finding is consistent with a previous study of women with systemic vasculitis, which found a higher maternal age among women with systemic vasculitis than among controls. 9 Additionally, women with vasculitis in our cohort were more likely to be of Caucasian race, consistent with a previous study examining 60 pregnancies with primary systemic vasculitis, which found similar rates of Caucasian race. 11 Other notable differences in maternal characteristics between the groups are the higher prevalence of chronic hypertension, pregestational DM, and thyroid disorders in the vasculitis group, aligning with previous studies demonstrating that patients with autoimmune vasculitis are at increased risk for additional comorbidities such as hypertension, DM, osteoporosis, thyroid disorders, and pulmonary disorders. 12 , 13 This highlights the importance of pre‐pregnancy consultation and evaluation of women with this diagnosis. As opposed to a previous study demonstrating a lower incidence of patients with a higher BMI in the vasculitis group as compared to controls, 12 we found almost double the incidence of women with obesity class I or higher in the vasculitis group. This can be explained by the older age in the vasculitis group in our cohort, and additionally, by the higher incidence of DM, which both are linked to increased weight. 14 , 15

Women with systemic vasculitis in our study had an increased rate of HDP even after adjusting for potential confounders such as age, BMI, thyroid disorders, and pregestational DM. The HDP incidence in the study group reached nearly 16%, similar to the rate reported in a French cohort of women with a diagnosis of Takayasu arteritis (21%). 16 Pre‐eclampsia involves systemic vascular inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and immune‐mediated vascular damage, all of which share pathophysiological features with vasculitis. 17 Additionally, histopathologic findings in pre‐eclampsia include decidual vasculopathy, characterized by perivascular lymphocytic infiltration and fibrinoid necrosis, resembling features observed in certain forms of vasculitis. 18 Therefore, the common mechanism between these two entities may partially explain the higher incidence of HDP in parturients with vasculitis. This finding may warrant further investigation into potential preventive treatments, such as low‐dose aspirin, and help identify whether certain subtypes are at increased risk. Additionally, the suggested mechanism should be assessed because there is no data regarding placental pathology.

Women with vasculitis had a higher preterm delivery rate than controls. Similar results were demonstrated in previous studies, which found an increased preterm delivery rate in women with systemic vasculitis compared to controls, ranging from a 1.5–2 times higher risk. 8 , 9 There are several plausible explanations for this finding. First, it is well‐established that systemic inflammation can trigger molecular pathways (such as nuclear factor kappa‐light‐chain‐enhancer of activated B cells) that are essential for uterine contractions and cervical ripening, thereby initiating premature labor. 19 Second, corticosteroids, which are a key component of treatment for these conditions, used by a substantial number of vasculitis patients, are known to be associated with preterm birth. 20 Notably, a previous study found that corticosteroids were the most common treatment agent among pregnant women diagnosed with vasculitis. 9 Lastly, in the database, we could not differentiate between spontaneous and iatrogenic preterm deliveries. It is plausible that even after controlling for HDP, some of the preterm births were iatrogenic due to worsening of the underlying disease. Notably, a previous study found an increased preterm delivery rate in women with vasculitis, but only for iatrogenic preterm deliveries and not for spontaneous birth. 8 These explanations should be interpreted with caution and examined in future studies, as there was no data on corticosteroid treatment or disease severity among women who delivered preterm.

Regarding mode of delivery, women with systemic vasculitis had a higher incidence of CD compared to controls after adjusting for potential confounders such as age, BMI ≥30, and pregestational DM. A previous study reported a lower CD incidence of 27.6% in the vasculitis group, 8 whereas a recent study reported a similar incidence to ours of 39.3% in the vasculitis group. 9 A possible explanation for the higher CD rates could be the increased incidence of preterm labor, which is associated with higher rates of non‐vertex fetal presentation. 21 We lack information on whether the higher CD incidence is attributable to increased intrapartum CD, elective surgeries, or both. There is need for large prospective studies evaluating the indications and incidence of CD among parturients with vasculitis.

We also found an increase in blood product transfusions in the vasculitis group, although the PPH rate was similar between groups. This may be due to a potentially lower predelivery hemoglobin level in the vasculitis group. It is well‐established that systemic inflammatory conditions are associated with anemia, 22 including during pregnancy, and particularly in active disease or flare‐ups. 23 This finding underscores the importance of optimizing pre‐delivery hemoglobin levels, especially in women with vasculitis, and anticipating the need for blood product transfusions.

Regarding neonatal outcomes, we found a higher incidence of congenital anomalies in the vasculitis group. This finding should be cautiously interpreted. Given the small number of congenital anomaly cases in our cohort (<11), it is difficult to draw definitive conclusions regarding this finding. The possibility of an alpha error in this group, given the small number of subjects with congenital anomalies, cannot be ruled out. If this association does exist, a possible explanation may be the medications used for women with vasculitis. A previous study examining pregnancy outcomes of women with systemic vasculitis found that approximately 35% received immunosuppressive treatment during pregnancy. 9 The most common treatment was a glucocorticoid in approximately one in five women. Glucocorticoid use in the first trimester has been associated with an increased incidence of congenital anomalies, including cleft lip. 24 Additionally, about 7% of women in their cohort were using non‐steroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs, 9 which, when used in the first trimester, are linked to cardiac septal defects. 25 Since the two largest studies on rare vasculitis types have focused mainly on maternal adverse outcomes, 8 , 9 a large population study focusing on the neonatal outcomes of women diagnosed with vasculitis will be needed to establish the true rate of neonatal consequences. Of note, although we did not find an association between increased risk for SGA neonates and IUFD cases in the vasculitis group, this may be due to the relatively small number of women in the study group and the study's limited power rather than a true lack of association.

Our study had several strengths. First, we identified maternal adverse outcomes not previously described, including a higher risk of blood product transfusion in the vasculitis group. Second, we investigated a wide range of pregnancy and delivery complications, offering comprehensive insights that can help inform physicians in providing more accurate counseling to pregnant women diagnosed with systemic vasculitis. Additionally, our study constitutes one of the largest cohorts to address this subject. Lastly, since we relied on a population‐based cohort, our study outcomes are generalizable to the broader US population, which is ethnically diverse, and as such may also be applicable to other populations.

Our study had several limitations. First, due to the rarity of a vasculitis diagnosis, our study was underpowered to assess the impact of each vasculitis subgroup individually. Second, the dataset used lacked information regarding disease severity, flare‐ups, and medications taken during pregnancy in the study group, all of which could potentially influence perinatal complications. Additional maternal characteristics that may affect outcomes, such as thrombophilia, autoimmune disorders, and renal disease, were not documented in our cohort.

Moreover, our study may be affected by selection bias because HCUP‐NIS includes only hospital‐based deliveries and excludes outpatient outcomes and early pregnancy losses. It is plausible that women with mild conditions could deliver in birthing centers outside hospitals, leading to a selection bias in our study toward women with more severe conditions and potentially worse perinatal outcomes. Furthermore, we studied maternal complications limited to the delivery admission; any complications occurring during the postpartum period subsequent to the initial discharge were not recorded. Notably, our cohort was limited to the period prior to 2015, which is attributable to modifications in HCUP coding and impairs the analysis of later data in a comparable manner. Over the past decade, developments have occurred in the management of patients with vasculitides. Key advances in vasculitis treatment during pregnancy include the broader application of biologic therapies, especially tumor necrosis factor (TNF) inhibitors 26 ; improved guidelines regarding medication safety; and growing evidence supporting rituximab for severe cases. 27 These developments could significantly impact perinatal outcomes for women with vasculitis and might produce different results than those we observed.

In conclusion, women with systemic vasculitis exhibit a higher incidence of obstetrical complications, including preterm delivery, HDP, and CD, as well as the need for blood product transfusion, which has not been previously described. These findings emphasize the importance of comprehensive patient counseling, multidisciplinary management, and careful obstetric surveillance for individuals with systemic vasculitis throughout their pregnancy.
