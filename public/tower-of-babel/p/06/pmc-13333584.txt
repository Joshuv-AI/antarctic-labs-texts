Facile synthesis of zinc-coordinated lignin nanoparticles derived from sugarcane bagasse waste biomass for sustainable bioactive composites
Authors: Gaurav Singh, Abhishek Verma, Ankit Srivastava, Subash Chandra Gupta, Dalia Dasgupta Mandal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA sustainable method was developed to synthesize zinc-coordinated lignin nanoparticles (LNP-Zn) from lignin derived from sugarcane bagasse, without the use of stabilizers. The direct coordination of Zn2+ within the lignin matrix creates a unique hybrid nanomaterial with several functions. At neutral pH, LNP-Zn had a mean particle size of 141.14 nm and a zeta potential of -30.41 mV, showing strong colloidal stability. BET analysis revealed a mesoporous structure with 37.16 m2/g surface area and 0.2858 cm³/g pore volume. XPS confirmed Zn+ coordination through oxygenated lignin moieties, while FTIR showed novel Zn-O functional groups having high thermal stability. XRD revealed that the LNP is amorphous, with a crystallite size of 0.42 nm. LNP-Zn demonstrated stronger antioxidant activity (IC50 21.26 ± 0.67 µg/mL), UV-shielding (SPF 23.87 ± 1.21), and efficient dye degradation. LNP-Zn showed remarkable biocompatibility (91.58 ± 2.08% viability of HEK-293 cells at 1000 mg/l) and dose-dependent cytotoxicity at high concentrations, indicating limited intrinsic anticancer activity and primarily supporting a biocompatible profile. Moreover, LNP-Zn boosted plant growth with a 189.17 ± 7.97% root enhancement in Vigna radiata (L.) R. Wilczek. Despite these promising results, further studies are needed to elucidate long-term biological safety, environmental fate, and the mechanisms underlying Zn-lignin interactions.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s11671-026-04751-y.Keywords: Agro-waste valorization, Biostimulant activity, Lignin nanoparticles, Sun protection factor, Zinc integration

Introduction

Transforming agro-industrial waste into valuable products for human consumption has emerged as a significant focus, driven by innovative processes guided by sustainability principles [1, 2]. Agro-industrial operations generate approximately 2 billion tons of waste worldwide each year, posing a significant environmental challenge [1, 3]. As byproducts of the sugar and first-generation ethanol sectors, worldwide output is projected to exceed 1 billion tons annually, making sugarcane bagasse the most abundant among these residues [4]. Approximately half of the initial weight of processed sugarcane is produced as sugarcane bagasse during the milling process that extracts juice from the stalks [5]. The annual output of sugarcane bagasse is anticipated to reach 350 million tons in India alone, highlighting the nation’s significant role in global sugarcane production [6, 7]. Sugarcane bagasse is a lignocellulosic material primarily consisting of cellulose (35–45%), hemicellulose (25–35%), and lignin (11–25%) [1, 5, 8, 9]. The resistance to biochemical and biological breakdown arises from the intricate interplay between lignin and polysaccharides within the cell wall matrix. Effective pretreatment is essential to dismantling this intricate structure, especially in eliminating lignin, which acts as a physical barrier and limits enzymatic access to cellulose, thereby obstructing efficient biomass hydrolysis [10–12]. Pretreatment techniques improve cellulose crystallinity, porosity, and biomass surface area, making them effective pretreatment methods for subsequent enzyme or chemical conversions. Conventional methods for lignin extraction from sugarcane bagasse rely on organic solvents, followed by thermochemical methods, and finally by biological pretreatments using ligninolytic enzymes or microorganisms, offering more environmentally benign and sustainable alternatives [13–15].

Lignin is a crucial structural polymer of lignocellulosic biomass. It is the second-largest renewable polyphenolic biopolymer in nature, and the largest natural pool of aromatic compounds with inherent ability to absorb UV light and remarkable redox activity [16–20]. It consists of phenylpropanoid units, building blocks originating from hydroxycinnamyl alcohols, mostly coniferyl alcohol (G-unit), sinapyl alcohol (S-unit), and to a lesser extent, p-coumaryl alcohol (H-unit) [21]. Lignin makes up 15–35% of lignocellulose biomass and plays a crucial role in binding carbohydrates, providing mechanical support to the plant’s cell walls [22]. Its intricate lignin structure results from possible inter-unit linkages, ether bonds (β-O-4, α-O-4, 4-O-5), and carbon-carbon bonds (β–β, β-5, and 5–5). Of these, β-O-4 is the most prevalent in lignin from plant species [23–25]. Lignin’s chemical diversity also stems from its functional groups, which include hydroxyl, methoxy, carbonyl, and carboxylic acids, imparting significant antioxidant capabilities and facilitating efficient chelation of metal ions via oxygen-rich coordination sites. Moreover, the ratio of these groups is influenced by the species’ genetic background and the isolation method [26–27]. In the pulp and paper industries, lignin is produced as a secondary product, as well as in lignocellulosic biorefineries, such as second-generation ethanol biorefineries [28–29]. Its substantial calorific value, ranging from 22.2 to 28.5 MJ/Kg, makes lignin suitable for use as a solid biofuel for thermal energy generation [30]. In contrast to polysaccharide biopolymers, the conjugated aromatic network of lignin offers inherent high thermal stability, ultraviolet absorption, antioxidant activity, antimicrobial, and radical-scavenging properties, rendering it particularly advantageous for photoprotective and biomedical uses [1, 31–33]. Furthermore, the structural diversity and functional complexity of lignin promote its self-assembly into nanoscale architectures, allowing for the development of multifunctional hybrid materials. The properties of lignin render it a promising candidate for various advanced applications, such as composites, UV-protective coatings, and biomedical scaffolds [34] (Fig. 1).

Advancements in lignin-derived nanomaterials provide a transformative route for lignin valorization, addressing key limitations in its recycling and sustainable utilization [35]. Although developments in biorefineries have increased the accessibility and utility of lignin, other factors, such as recalcitrance, low water solubility, heterogeneity, and structural differences across lignin sources, still pose challenges to its direct utilization [1, 36–39]. The application of nanotechnology in lignin conversion enables the production of lignin nanoparticles, nanofibers, and nanocomposites, thereby enhancing lignin’s functional and structural properties and expanding its range of applications. Lignin-derived nanomaterial composites (LNPs) are one of the most promising and effective adsorbents for environmental remediation. LNPs are highly porous, enabling them to capture harmful substances efficiently and featuring tunable surface chemistry and enhanced dispersity, making them ideal for removing heavy metals, dyes, and other organic pollutants from wastewater [1, 40–43]. Research has also been conducted to utilize LNPs in agricultural industries as a carrier to deliver slow-release of fertilizers and pesticides to the soil, which could lead to higher efficiency and the minimization of their harmful effect on the environment through their biodegradability and their protection of active agents against premature release to the environment [44–45]. The lignin-based nanomaterials also have great promise in the medical sphere because they are bioactive, less toxic, and therefore biofriendly, and can exhibit antioxidant and antibacterial effects, as well as drug-delivery capabilities [46–48]. Moreover, the tuning of lignin nanoparticles, controlled by metal doping or surface modification, can be adjusted to achieve the desired interaction with environmental or biological targets, broadening their applications [49–51]. Overall, integrating nanotechnology and lignin valorization addresses the physicochemical constraints of lignin and enables sustainable solutions in agriculture, medicine, and environmental applications [49, 50, 52]. Lignin nanoparticles (LNPs) have shown the capacity to enhance seed germination by increasing water uptake through the creation of nanometric pores in the seed coat, stimulating antioxidant defense mechanisms, or promoting specific enzyme activity. Building on this, the zinc-coordinated lignin nanocomposite (LNP-Zn) serves as a synergistic bioactive platform that enables controlled, pH-dependent release of Zinc ions while activating essential enzymatic pathways for superior early seedling development.

Although valorization of lignin is of growing interest, chemical extraction of the lignin from sugarcane bagasse and subsequent conversion to zinc-doped lignin nanoparticles has not been widely studied. The challenge in green synthesis of metal-doped lignin nanoparticles is to develop scalable, eco-friendly methods to extract and functionalize lignin from agro-industrial waste such as sugarcane bagasse and to control zinc doping to improve nanoparticle stability, bioactivity, and composite performance without toxic chemical precursors [53–56]. Most previous research has focused on either inorganic zinc-based nanomaterials derived from biomass extracts, which offer limited control over interfacial chemistry and multifunctionality, or undoped lignin nanoparticles for limited single-function applications. As a result, there is still a significant knowledge gap regarding the development of scalable, environmentally friendly methods for chemically incorporating zinc into lignin macromolecules to produce structurally cohesive, multipurpose nanohybrids [57–58]. Previous investigations have primarily focused on undoped LNPs for applications such as Pickering emulsions, UV protection, and antioxidant delivery, or on the green synthesis of ZnO nanoparticles from SCB extracts with antimicrobial properties, but there has been no integration of zinc doping into lignin matrices to synergistically boost bioactivity, stability, and dispersibility in polymer composites [50, 59–62]. Conventional LNP exhibit solvent instability (dissolution or aggregation in acidic/alkaline pH and in organic solvents), poor compatibility with hydrophobic matrices, dependence on toxic organic solvents during synthesis, and limited multifunctionality for bioactive applications, which hinder their scalability and industrial value (Supplementary Table 1) [63–66].

This study reports the first stabilizer-free synthesis of zinc-coordinated lignin nanoparticles derived from sugarcane bagasse, in which zinc is chemically integrated into the lignin matrix rather than forming separate inorganic phases. A one-pot green strategy is developed to convert agro-waste lignin directly into functional nanohybrids, eliminating the need for organic solvents and multi-step processing. The study establishes a clear structure–property relationship linking Zn–O coordination, amorphous mesoporosity, and surface charge to antioxidant, UV-shielding, catalytic, and biointeractive performances. Unlike previous studies focusing on isolated functions of lignin or zinc systems, this work demonstrates multifunctionality within a single sustainable nanomaterial platform, thereby advancing high-value lignin valorization for sustainable bioactive composites and environmental agricultural applications. In-situ Zn doping yields uniform LNP-Zn with a yield of over 70%, zero hazardous waste, and ambient conditions (< 40 °C), resulting in a reduction of energy use by more than 70% and costs, while utilizing abundant bagasse lignin without purification losses [60, 63, 67].

Materials and methods

Reagents and materials

Sugarcane bagasse was collected from a local juice vendor shop in Durgapur, West Bengal, India (23.5357646° N, 87.2979164° E, or 23° 32’ 8.7526’’ N, 87° 17’ 52.4990’’ E). Vigna radiata (L.) R. Wilczek was procured from a local market in Durgapur, West Bengal, India. The Human embryonic kidney (HEK-293) and breast cancer cell lines (MDA-MB-231) were procured from the National Center for Cell Science, Pune, India. DPPH (C18H12N5O6, Country of Origin: USA, Product Code: 15374563, Lot No.: STBB0719) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. DMEM (Country of Origin- USA, Product code- 12100046, Lot No.-) was procured from Thermo Fisher Scientific. Ethylene glycol (C2H6O2, Country of Origin- India, Product Code- S825720393, Lot No.- 1.009.492.521), zinc sulphate (ZnSO4.7H2O, Country of origin- India, Product code- MF-GMS-914-05, Lot No- 61783005001730), sodium hydroxide (NaOH, Country of Origin- India, Product Code- DH8D681954, Lot No.- 1.935.020.521), ammonia solution (NH4OH, Country of Origin- India, Product Code- 1.93580.0521), methylene blue (C16H18ClN3, Country of Origin- India, Procuct Code- G1836901251730, Lot No.- AB2AF62092), nitric acid (HNO3, Country of Origin- India, Product Code- CF8C680424, Lot No.- 1.934.060.522), and Whatman filter paper - Grade 1 (Country of Origin- China, Product Code- 1001090, Lot No.- 10,011,255) were procured from Merck. HiMedia provided FBS (Country of origin: USA, Product code: RM10409-100 mL) and a dialysis bag with a molecular weight cutoff of 5000 Da (Country of Origin: India, Product Code: LA395-10MT, Lot No.: 0000670190). Milli-Q water generated using a Millipore Type 1 Ultra-Pure Water system (Country of Origin: India) was used in all experiments. The filtration process was completed using a vacuum filter setup, polycarbonate filters, and Millipore filter paper (Country of Origin: India, Product Code: GSWP02500, Lot No.: R9JA70651).

Pretreatment of SCB biomass and extraction of lignin

The SCB was extensively rinsed with distilled water to remove grit and dried at 65℃. The dried biomasses were milled with an electric mixer-grinder (Wonder Chef Victor Classic mixer-grinder, 800 W), followed by sieving to achieve a uniform size of 1 mm × 1 mm × 1 mm. The processed SCB underwent treatment with NH4OH at a 1:10 ratio for 24 h. In accordance with established extraction methods for lignin, the NH4OH-treated solid fraction of sugarcane bagasse was subjected to thorough rinsing with Milli-Q water to eliminate residual impurities before fractionation [68, 69]. Water-soluble extractives, along with solubilized hemicellulose, were removed by hot-water extraction at 75℃ for 2 h with a 1:10 mass ratio of biomass to water, and the mixture was filtered through Whatman No. 1 to obtain an insoluble cellulose-lignin structure. Alkaline delignification was used to extract lignin (high yield) with 15% NaOH, with the liquor to solid ratio being 1:10, under vigorous stirring at 250 rpm for 180 min at 98℃; the black liquor was then acidified (2.5M H2SO4) to precipitate lignin, which was then collected, washed, and dried at 60℃, and characterized through FTIR for functional group analysis, FESEM for morphological evaluation, and XRD for crystallinity assessment [70].

Preparation and characterization of zinc-coordinated lignin nanoparticles

Lignin extracted from SCB was dispersed in ethylene glycol to a final concentration of 10 µg mL−1 in a total volume of 200 mL, and the mixture was stirred at 800 rpm for 1 h at 25 °C. To it, 400 mg of a Zinc (ZnSO4 · 7H2O) metal salt was added, and the mixture was stirred at 800 rpm for 2 h. The resulting dispersion was filtered through a 0.45 μm syringe filter to remove undissolved particulate matter. Subsequently, an equal volume of 0.025 M HNO3 was added to the filtrate at a rate of 0.5 mL min−1 under continuous stirring at 800 rpm, and the mixture was reacted for 4 h. Following the reaction, the mixture was purified by dialysis (membrane pore size, 5000 Da) against deionized water for 72 h, with water changes performed three times daily to remove residual reactants and byproducts. The dialyzed product was then thoroughly rinsed with deionized water, collected, and dried in a hot oven at 50℃ until a constant weight was achieved. This final product was designated as LNP-Zn [48].

Characterization

The morphological analysis of LNP-Zn was conducted using DLS, FESEM, and HR-TEM. The physico-chemical characterization encompassed zeta potential, EDAX, FTIR, TGA, XPS, XRD, and BET surface area analysis to comprehensively evaluate the structure, composition, and functional properties of nanoparticles [48, 71, 72].

FESEM

Field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM) was used to conduct high-resolution topographic and microstructural analyses of the biopolymeric specimens. This technique facilitated the precise estimation of marginal surface features, including morphological uniformity, compactness, surface roughness, and mesoporous architecture. Surface topography and particle size distributions of the extracted lignin and synthesized LNP-Zn were analyzed using a Zeiss Sigma VP FESEM. To mitigate charging effects and enhance secondary electron emission, samples were mounted on carbon tape and sputter-coated with a 1:1 ratio of platinum and gold utilizing a Quorum Sputter Coater (SC7680). A precise coating thickness of approximately 10 nm was maintained via controlled plasma deposition to ensure high-fidelity imaging [73]. Analysis was performed at an accelerating voltage of 20 kV, providing deep electron penetration and robust signal acquisition for integrated Energy Dispersive X-ray Analysis (EDAX). High-resolution micrographs were acquired at a working distance (WD) of 5.2–8.5 mm to optimize depth of field and topographic resolution. Concurrently, elemental composition and metal-distribution patterns were quantified via EDAX to confirm the successful chemical infiltration of Zn into the biopolymeric framework.

FTIR

The interfacial chemical interactions were characterized by Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy using a Bruker Alpha II spectrometer. The equipment was set to a spectral resolution of 4 cm−1, with data collection covering the 4000–400 cm−1 wavenumber range to discern diagnostic vibrational modes and principal functional groups. Specimens were pressed into translucent KBr pellets under uniform high pressure. This stringent analytical approach guaranteed accurate data collection, facilitating a thorough assessment of the surface chemistry and molecular integration of the produced nanomaterials [11, 52].

XRD

X-ray diffraction (XRD) served as a non-destructive, high-throughput analytical method for characterizing phase transitions, structural configurations, and long-range atomic order in the biopolymeric specimens. This procedure is crucial for determining the amorphous or crystalline nature of the lignin matrix and for evaluating the crystallite dimensions and lattice strain of the resulting nanohybrids. To assess the apparent degree of crystallinity, the extracted lignin and the synthesized LNP-Zn were analyzed using a Panalytical X’Pert PRO diffractometer. Diffraction patterns were obtained within a 2θ scanning range of 15° to 60°. The apparatus was utilized at an accelerating voltage of 40 kV and a tube current of 40 mA, employing a steady scanning rate of 6 °C min−1 [73].

Dynamic light scattering and zeta potential

The hydrodynamic diameter and colloidal stability of the LNP-Zn nanohybrids were quantified via Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS) and electrophoretic light scattering using a Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS (Zen 3600). Samples were prepared at 0.1 mg/mL in Milli-Q water and ultrasonicated to ensure a homogeneous, non-agglomerated suspension. Concurrently, the zeta potential (ζ) was determined by measuring electrophoretic mobility under an applied electric field, providing a quantitative index of electrostatic repulsion and long-term colloidal integrity.

HR-TEM

High-resolution transmission electron microscopy was used to assess the subnanometer morphology of zinc-coordinated lignin nanoparticles. The analysis utilized a Talos F200X G2 (Thermo Scientific), operating at 200 kV and featuring an X-FEG source to enhance phase contrast. During specimen preparation, an aqueous dispersion at 0.1 mg/mL was drop-cast onto Formvar- and carbon-supported 300-mesh copper grids. To ensure complete removal of the aqueous phase while maintaining the structural integrity of the organic framework, the grids were dried in a hot-air oven at 50 °C until a uniform, dehydrated state was attained. The regulated thermal process was essential to inhibit nanoparticle clustering and to ensure precise visualization of the zinc-coordinated lignin matrix in a high-vacuum environment [11].

EDAX

The elemental composition of the LNP-Zn nanocomposites was characterized using Energy-Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDX) integrated into a Thermo Scientific Talos F200X G2 High-Resolution Transmission Electron Microscope (HR-TEM) operated at 200 kV. High-speed chemical mapping and point-profile analyses were conducted in Scanning Transmission Electron Microscopy (STEM) mode utilizing the high-sensitivity Super-X G2 detector system, which features four windowless silicon drift detectors (SDD) providing a total solid angle of 0.9 srad. Atomic-scale compositional accuracy and superior signal-to-noise ratios were achieved using a high-brightness field-emission gun (X-FEG), with data acquisition and quantitative processing performed via Velox software.

TGA

To evaluate the inclusive thermal stability and decomposition kinetics of the biopolymeric specimens, thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was performed. This technique facilitates the quantitative assessment of mass reduction as a function of temperature, providing critical insights into the thermal degradation profiles, chemical compositions, and fractional dosages of organic and inorganic components within the hybrid composites. The thermal behavior of the samples was examined using a Perkin-Elmer Pyris Diamond TG apparatus. Precisely 10 mg of each sample was heated under a controlled program from 30 to 1000 °C. The experiments were conducted at a constant heating rate of 20 °C/min under a high-purity nitrogen atmosphere to prevent oxidative degradation and ensure a stable inert environment during the thermal transition [9].

XPS

To elucidate the elemental composition and precise surface chemical states of the LNP-Zn nanohybrids, X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS) was performed. Analysis was conducted utilizing a PHI 5000 VersaProbe III system equipped with a monochromatic Al Kα X-ray source (1486.6 eV). The dried specimens were securely affixed to conductive carbon tape and analyzed under ultra-high vacuum conditions. High-resolution spectra were acquired for C 1s, O 1s, and Zn 2p core levels to determine the electronic environment and coordination chemistry of the zinc ions within the lignin matrix [74].

BET

Specific surface area and pore size distribution of the biopolymeric specimens were quantified via Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) and Barrett-Joyner-Halenda (BJH) analyses, respectively. These measurements provide critical insights into the overall porosity, total pore volume, and average pore diameter of the synthesized nanohybrids. Nitrogen (N2) physisorption isotherms were recorded at 77.3 K utilizing a BELSORP-maxII manometric adsorption apparatus. Before analysis, approximately 50 mg of the sample was subjected to a rigorous degassing protocol at 120 °C for 12 h under high vacuum to remove adsorbed moisture and volatile contaminants. The specific surface area was derived from the linear region of the BET plots (P/P0 ≈ 0.05–0.30), while the pore size distributions and median diameters were calculated from the adsorption-desorption branches using the BJH model [74].

Antioxidant assay

The DPPH assay was conducted on the LNP-Zn to evaluate its antioxidant properties. Briefly, 3 mL of LNP-Zn (at concentrations of 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 40, and 50 µg/mL) was added to 1 mL of 0.1 mM DPPH stock that had been kept in the dark for 30 min. The absorbance was then measured at 517 nm. The IC50 value and the percentage of radical scavenging were tested [48, 75].

Effect of zinc-coordinated lignin nanoparticles on root growth

The seeds of Vigna radiata (L.) R. Wilczek was surface sterilized using 0.1% HgCl2, followed by 70% ethanol and distilled water. Eight seeds were carefully arranged in a petri dish measuring 15 cm. The dish was lined with a Whatman No. 1 filter paper, which was then moistened with 5 mL of various concentrations of LNP-Zn solutions in double-distilled water, and incubated in a plant growth chamber at 22 °C, with alternating periods of light and darkness lasting 14 and 10 h, respectively. Double-distilled water was used as a control. The root length of V. radiata (L.) R. Wilczek was measured using a thread and a scale on the fifth day of exposure [83].

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate (n = 3), and the data represented are mean ± SD. Probability tests were conducted to determine the degree of difference between the means. The ‘p’ value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Result and discussion

Extraction of lignin and its characterization

Alkaline extraction is a well-established technique for isolating lignin and cellulose from plant-based residues [84]. The schematic illustration of the study, including preparation, characterization, and applications of zinc-coordinated lignin nanoparticles synthesized from the extracted lignin, is represented in Fig. 2. In the present investigation, SCB subjected to NH4OH pretreatment yielded lignin at a rate of 0.12 ± 0.0015 g/g of dry biomass. This yield aligns closely with earlier studies that reported lignin and cellulose recoveries from SCB of 0.15 g/g and 0.5 g/g, respectively [85, 86]. These results underscore the reliability and efficiency of the alkaline pretreatment approach for fractionating lignocellulosic components from agricultural wastes. Lignin is preferred over cellulose for nanoparticles due to its inherent hydrophobicity and amphiphilic nature, enabling better compatibility with hydrophobic drugs and polymers in delivery systems. Additionally, lignin offers superior antioxidant and UV-blocking properties, as well as greater thermal stability, thereby enhancing nanoparticle functionality for biomedical and packaging applications.

The workflow illustrates the isolation of alkaline lignin from sugarcane bagasse, followed by the synthesis of LNP-Zn via self-assembly. Subsequent purification through dialysis and multi-analytical characterization are shown alongside diverse functional applications, including UV protection, plant growth biostimulation, and photocatalytic degradation of indigo carmine

The FESEM analysis of lignin extracted from SCB revealed an amorphous morphology with irregular structural organization (Fig. 3a), consistent with the intrinsic complexity of lignin’s crosslinked aromatic polymer network. FTIR spectroscopy was utilized to analyze the functional groups of lignin (Fig. 3b). Distinct absorption bands specific to lignin were identified, including peaks at 1423.32 cm−1 (asymmetric C–O–C stretching in aryl ethers), 1167.33 cm−1 (C–H in-plane deformation of syringyl units), 1038.31 cm−1 (C–H bending in guaiacyl aromatic rings), and 894.95 cm−1 (C–H out-of-plane bending in aromatic structures). Additional peaks of O–H stretching vibration at 3411.89 cm−1, C–H stretching vibration at 2926.52 cm−1, aromatic ring (C=C) stretching at 1640.41 cm−1, and aromatic skeletal vibration at 1509.34 cm−1 were also observed. These peaks align with the previously reported FTIR of Lignin [87–89]. The observed spectral features validate the preservation of lignin’s distinctive aromatic and oxygen-containing functionalities following the extraction process. The XRD diffractogram of the isolated lignin exhibited characteristic reflections at 19.08° and 22.58°, with a (002) d-spacing of 3.94 Å and an average crystallite size of 2.38 nm. The calculated crystallinity index of 50.76% indicates a robust semi-crystalline framework, suggesting that the π-π stacking of aromatic rings is well-preserved [90].

Preparation and characterization of zinc-coordinated lignin nanoparticles

Zinc-coordinated lignin nanoparticles were synthesized via a single-step self-assembly method, yielding 61.49 ± 1.54% of the total lignin and zinc used, which is comparable to previously synthesized polymeric nanomaterials [68, 91–93]. In lignin, phenolic hydroxyl and carboxylic acid groups, likely to have formed stable chelate-type coordination complexes, can easily bond with Zn ions (Zn2+) upon mixing zinc sulfate and lignin dissolved in ethylene glycol [94]. Ethylene glycol acts as a dispersion agent, ensuring that metal-organic complexes are uniformly dispersed without premature agglomeration [95]. Lignin ionizable groups are protonated by nitric acid, leading to decreased solubility and the precipitation of lignin-zinc complexes. The acidification process enhances the material’s rigidity and encapsulates Zn2+ within the polymer matrix by facilitating crosslinking between molecules. Thus, lignin, due to its amphiphilic nature, facilitates the formation of homogeneous, spherical NPs through spontaneous self-assembly [96].

Hydrodynamic size distribution profiling of LNP-Zn via DLS

Figure 4 depicts the hydrodynamic size distributions of zinc-coordinated lignin nanoparticles (LNP-Zn) as assessed by dynamic light scattering, highlighting a notable pH dependence. At pH 4, LNP-Zn exhibited an average hydrodynamic diameter of 108.62 nm, accompanied by a polydispersity index (PDI) of 1.0. This indicates significant heterogeneity, which can be attributed to the protonation and destabilization of lignin phenolic moieties in acidic environments. The neutral pH of 7 yielded the largest average particle size of 141.14 nm (PDI 0.259), suggesting moderate aggregation and greater size variability. At pH 9, the average diameter decreased to 111.98 nm (PDI = 0.35), consistent with the deprotonation of phenolic groups (pKa ≈ 9–10), which promotes electrostatic repulsion and partial compaction despite persistent hydrophobic interactions. The results align closely with prior studies by A. Richter et al. (2016), indicating an average size of less than 250 nm, while A. Gupta et al. (2014) indicated that the size of the LNP (60%) is below 250 nm [97, 98]. Lignin nanoparticles typically measure 320–360 nm [99]. Average sizes of 150 nm and 255 nm have been documented in certain instances [68, 99].

Evaluation of colloidal stability by zeta potential assessment

At a neutral pH, LNP-Zn nanoparticles exhibited a zeta potential of -30.41 mV, indicating robust colloidal stability, as |ζ| > ±30 mV provides adequate electrostatic repulsion according to DLVO theory [100]. Comparable zeta potential values at neutral pH have been reported for stable lignin nanoparticle systems in previous studies [68, 97–99], underscoring the strong stability of the present zinc-coordinated formulation. LNP-Zn exhibited significantly pH-dependent zeta potentials: − 44.86 mV at pH 9, − 30.41 mV at pH 7, and − 4.5 mV at pH 4 (Supplementary Fig. 1). This behavior is attributed to the deprotonation of phenolic hydroxyl and carboxylate groups under alkaline conditions, which reveals anionic sites that enhance surface charge density. This aligns with the findings of A. Worku et al. [100, 101], who observed that lignin nanoparticles exhibited a zeta potential ranging from − 10 to − 18 mV at pH 2, attributed this to the predominance of protonation effects. At pH levels ranging from 7 to 9, a zeta potential greater than 30 mV indicates significant stability, with the energy barrier far exceeding kT. Conversely, at pH 4, the observed instability, characterized by a zeta potential of approximately 4.5 mV, suggests a tendency towards aggregation.

Structural morphology of LNP-Zn: FESEM and HR-TEM Study

The morphology and size distribution of the synthesized LNP-Zn nanoparticles were characterized using FESEM and HR-TEM (Fig. 5), revealing predominantly quasi-spherical particles with smooth surfaces and diameters under 150 nm. This morphology is consistent with earlier research on biopolymer-based nanoparticles [48, 96, 99, 102]. The quasi-spherical morphology is significant, as it enhances biocompatibility and facilitates biomedical applications, such as targeted drug delivery and imaging. Research indicates that nanoparticles sized between 10 and 200 nm exhibit improved biodistribution and prolonged circulation times, thereby enhancing their therapeutic efficacy and targeting capabilities [68, 105–107].

Elemental analysis: EDAX

The EDAX analysis of zinc-doped lignin nanoparticles (LNP-Zn) reveals that carbon and oxygen are at higher percentages (Supplementary Fig. 2), with 46.41 (wt%) and 55.65 (at%) of carbon, and 42.97 (wt%) and 38.68 (at%) of oxygen. The wt % (at %) of zinc is 10.61 (5.66). This element distribution pattern provides evidence for the successful infiltration of Zn into the lignin structure, which is an organic polymer with a carbon and oxygen framework, mainly from aromatic and hydroxyl/carboxyl groups. The observed zinc contents were similar to those previously reported for zinc-lignin nanocomposites and showed that, depending on the synthesis conditions and the doping amount, Zn weight percentages varied between 5 and 15% [63, 108]. The presence of a large amount of oxygen favors the occurrence of oxygen-containing functional groups (such as phenolic and carboxylic) required for the adsorption and binding with zinc ions, as in earlier works. Compared with pure lignin without zinc, the elemental content of Zn presence at high concentration, along with carbon and oxygen content, provides proof of efficient doping and complexation rather than simple surface adsorption, hence indicating synergism in the uniform distribution of the Zn in lignin nanoparticles and the tendency for enhanced and stable properties [48].

Analysis of functional groups in LNP-Zn

The FTIR spectrum of LNP-Zn (Zinc-coordinated Lignin Nanoparticles) displays significant peaks that indicate the preservation of lignin structure and the interaction with zinc (Fig. 6). The broad band at 3444.65 cm−1 corresponds to O–H stretching, suggesting the presence of phenolic and aliphatic hydroxyl groups. The peak observed at 2926.52 cm−1 indicates the presence of aliphatic C–H stretching, which is characteristic of lignin subunits. Aromatic skeletal vibrations are detected at 1603.55, 1507.29, and 1460.19 cm−1, affirming the integrity of the lignin backbone. Prominent peaks at 1267.68 cm−1 and 1124.34 cm−1 are linked to C–O stretching from guaiacyl units and aromatic C–H in-plane deformation, whereas 1035.26 cm−1 is related to C–O stretching and aromatic C–H in-plane deformation of syringyl residues. The peak observed at 839.66 cm−1 indicates the bending of aromatic C–H out of the plane [87–89, 109]. Notably, the absorptions at 587.76 cm−1 and 450.54 cm−1 indicate Zn–O stretching, confirming an interaction and complexation between zinc ions and the functional groups of lignin in the nanocomposite [110–111].

Study of crystallinity: XRD analysis

Structural characterization of LNP-Zn via XRD confirmed the predominantly disordered nature of the matrix, evidenced by a prominent amorphous halo at 2θ = 22.46°. The pronounced line broadening (FWHM = 20.30°) and a calculated interplanar d-spacing of 0.396 nm reflect a severe lack of long-range translational symmetry, typical of highly disordered carbonaceous biopolymers. The calculated crystallinity index (CrI = 0.38) further establishes this structural disorder, aligning closely with standard amorphous carbon profiles indexed under JCPDS Card No. 75-1621. Debye-Scherrer calculations yielded a crystallite size of 0.42 nm and lattice strain of 0.446, confirming predominantly amorphous nanoparticle morphology (Supplementary Fig. 3). Critical comparison with JCPDS reference standards definitively excluded crystalline zinc oxide formation: no peaks corresponding to ZnO wurtzite structure (JCPDS 36-1451; characteristic peaks at 2θ = 31.69°, 34.39°, 47.46°) were detected. Similarly, the complete absence of ZnSO4·7H2O precursor peaks (JCPDS 39–0688; major peak at 2θ = 10.19°) confirmed quantitative conversion and zinc-lignin complexation. These results, combined with FTIR-identified Zn–O coordination bands (587.76, 450.54 cm−1) and EDAX elemental analysis (10.61 ± 5.66 wt% Zn), confirm that zinc is incorporated into the lignin matrix predominantly via chelate-type coordination complexes with phenolic hydroxyl and carboxylic acid groups, rather than forming discrete crystalline zinc oxide nanoparticles. The crystallinity index indicated a low degree of order, emphasizing the predominantly amorphous characteristics of the synthesized nanoparticles, in line with previous studies on lignin and cellulose nanomaterials [48]. The reduced crystallite size and smaller lattice strain improve surface area and reactivity, factors that positively affect drug release kinetics and the stability of nanoparticles [112] (Table 1).

Thermal stability analysis of LNP-Zn

The thermogravimetric analysis of LNP-Zn shows a multistep degradation profile, as observed for lignin-based hybrids. A preliminary mass reduction of approximately 4–5% below 150 °C is attributed to the desorption of physically bound moisture [48]. The sample demonstrates a mass retention exceeding 90% up to approximately 220 °C, suggesting commendable stability within the moderate-temperature range (Fig. 7). This thermal behavior aligns with documented TGA profiles of pristine lignin, which typically undergo initial moisture loss followed by the degradation of labile ether linkages and hemicellulose fragments between 200 and 350 °C [113]. The primary decomposition takes place between approximately 220 and 600 °C, resulting from the cleavage of lignin side chains and ether linkages, followed by a gradual breakdown of the aromatic network; the T50 is around 470 °C, indicating a relatively high thermal resistance [114]. A consistent residue of approximately 5–7 wt% is observed above 700 °C, which can be attributed to the presence of inorganic Zn-containing ash, such as ZnO, that remains following the volatilization of the organic fraction [115–117]. Comparable enhancements in high-temperature residue and enhanced thermal stability have been documented for lignin/ZnO hybrid particles and lignin-coated inorganic nanocomposites [118].

XPS analysis of elemental composition and chemical states of LNP-Zn

High-resolution XPS was employed to clarify the surface chemical states of the LNP-Zn nanocomposite (Fig. 8). The C 1s spectrum displays a prominent peak at approximately 284.8 eV, which is associated with the C–C/C=C bonds of the lignin aromatic backbone. Additionally, higher binding energy components are observed at 286.2, 287.8, and 288.9 eV, corresponding to C–O, C=O, and O–C=O groups, respectively, consistent with the presence of oxygenated lignin functional groups. The O 1s spectrum reveals a significant feature around ~ 530.2 eV, attributed to Zn–O bonds, accompanied by higher-binding-energy shoulders corresponding to surface –OH and C–O species, consistent with ZnO-modified bio-derived materials. The Zn 2p spectrum reveals distinct 2p₃/2 and 2p₁/2 peaks at approximately 1021.7 eV and 1044.8 eV, respectively, exhibiting a spin–orbit splitting of around 23 eV. This observation suggests the presence of Zn2+ within a Zn–O coordination environment, similar to the chemical states identified in lignin-templated ZnO nanostructures [119–120]. The collective analysis of the C 1s, O 1s, and Zn 2p signatures substantiates that Zn is integrated into the lignin matrix primarily as Zn2+, which is bonded through oxygenated functional groups. This finding confirms the successful development of an LNP-Zn hybrid structure characterized by robust interfacial interactions, aligning with reported studies on lignin–metal oxide composites.

Surface area and porosity characteristics of LNP-Zn

Nitrogen physisorption at 77.3 K reveals that LNP-Zn displays a combined Type III isotherm characterized by an H3 hysteresis loop, suggesting a mesoporous structure primarily consisting of macropores or slit-shaped interparticle voids within non-rigid aggregates [121]. The BET analysis conducted within the linear region (P/P0 ≈ 0.05–0.30) indicates a specific surface area of 37.155 m2/g, a monolayer capacity of 5.124 cm³/g (STP), and a BET constant of 46.76 (Fig. 9). These findings suggest moderate interactions between the adsorbate and the surface, with the surface area being predominantly influenced by external and mesoporous surfaces, rather than micropores. The material demonstrates a total pore volume of 0.2858 cm³/g. Furthermore, PSD analysis by the BJH method indicates a wide pore-size distribution, with an average pore diameter of 30.77 nm and a median diameter of 70.18 nm, consistent with the pronounced adsorption increase observed at elevated relative pressures. The result observed here closely aligns with those already reported for mesoporous ZnO and lignin–Zn nanocomposites [51, 64, 122]. In these materials, the frameworks derived from lignin and the aggregation of Zn-based nanoparticles result in hierarchical meso/macroporosity, exhibiting H3-type hysteresis [123], which is advantageous for applications requiring efficient mass transport and surface accessibility.

Antioxidant efficiency of zinc-coordinated lignin nanoparticles

The IC50 value for antioxidant activity is also significantly lower for LNP-Zn than for LNP (21.26 ± 0.67 vs. 40.04 ± 0.77 µg/mL), reflecting better free radical scavenging due to zinc doping. These results are consistent with earlier research on zinc-coordinated nanoparticles [124–125]. This cooperative effect may be caused by zinc ions precipitating onto lignin phenolic and hydroxyl groups, thereby improving electron transfer and radical stabilization. Further, LNP-Zn also exhibited better antioxidant activity as compared to β-carotene (33.82 ± 2.9 µg/mL), silver nanoparticles (63.43 µg/mL), iron nanoparticles (165 µg/mL), and zinc oxide nanoparticles (28.63 µg/mL), indicating the essential role of LNP-Zn as an efficient natural antioxidant as depicted in Table 2 [76, 126–128]. Such enhanced antioxidant potential underscores the functional role of Zn addition in augmenting the lignin’s natural antioxidative potential by enhancing physical-chemical interactions and stabilization mechanisms [129].

Evaluating the erythrocyte membrane stability of zinc-coordinated lignin nanoparticles

The erythrocyte membrane stability analysis of LNP-Zn is relatively low, measured at less than 2.5% at a concentration of 500 µg/mL (Supplementary Fig. 4). This value is significantly lower than the hemocompatibility threshold typically advised for biomedical nanoformulations, which is generally recognized as non-hemolytic and safe [48, 76, 130]. The minimal hemolytic activity observed supports the hemocompatibility of LNP-Zn and suggests significant potential for diverse medical and therapeutic applications that involve direct blood contact [131–133].

In vitro toxicity studies of LNP-Zn on HEK-293 cell line

The cytocompatibility of LNP-Zn toward human embryonic kidney (HEK-293) cells was evaluated using the MTT assay after 24 h exposure to concentrations ranging from 25 to 1000 µg/mL (Supplementary Fig. 5). LNP-Zn did not induce any statistically significant cytotoxic effect across the tested concentration range, with cell viability remaining above 90% in all cases, demonstrating its excellent biocompatibility. Notably, at 100 µg/mL, LNP-Zn maintained a high cell viability of 95.86 ± 1.31%, markedly outperforming ZnO nanoparticles (63.24%) and Ag/ZnO nanocomposites (22.47%) under identical conditions. Even at lower concentrations (50 µg/mL), Ag/ZnO nanoparticles exhibited pronounced cytotoxicity, with cell viability limited to 31.89%, whereas LNP-Zn preserved near-normal cellular metabolic activity [134–135]. These results indicate that the lignin matrix effectively mitigates the intrinsic cytotoxicity of Zn-based nanomaterials, most likely by reducing direct ion release and oxidative stress, thereby conferring significantly improved cytocompatibility. Collectively, the negligible cytotoxicity observed, even at high exposure levels, highlights LNP-Zn as a promising and safe nanoplatform for prospective biomedical and bio-interfacing applications.

In vitro anticancer potential on MDA-MB-231 cell line

Zinc-coordinated lignin nanoparticles (LNP-Zn) demonstrated anticancer activity against the triple-negative breast cancer cell line (MDA-MB-231), with the plant-derived flavonoid quercetin employed as a positive control. The results indicate that the LNP-Zn concentration ranging from 25 to 1000 µg/mL resulted in a steady decline in cell viability, decreasing from about 86% to 66% compared to the untreated control (Fig. 10). Notably, significant cytotoxicity was not observed within this concentration range, with the IC50 estimated at 1629.57 ± 2.39 µg/mL, a value considerably higher than the reported doses in comparison to the quercetin, having 134.6 µg/mL [68]. The observed effect may be a result of the interaction between zinc ions, recognized for their role in oxidative stress, and lignin, which can disturb cellular homeostasis [38, 136]. The findings indicate that LNP-Zn, despite demonstrating some anticancer properties, is insufficient, particularly when used alone, and requires higher doses or combination approaches to achieve a significant therapeutic impact. Rather than functioning as a direct anticancer agent, the high IC50 values demonstrate that LNP-Zn primarily exhibits a biocompatible profile. When considered alongside the minimal toxicity observed in HEK-293 cells and red blood cells, these results support the suitability of LNP-Zn as a safe nanomaterial platform. Such characteristics are particularly advantageous for drug-delivery applications, where low inherent cytotoxicity is essential. The coordinated lignin framework, mesoporosity, and surface functionality of LNP-Zn provide a promising platform for loading and delivering anticancer therapeutics, with the potential to enhance drug stability, cellular interactions, and therapeutic efficacy. Future studies will therefore focus on drug encapsulation and combination-therapy strategies.

Determination of the catalytic activity of zinc-coordinated lignin nanoparticles

Zinc-coordinated lignin nanoparticles (LNP-Zn) exhibited markedly enhanced catalytic efficiency in the degradation of methylene blue (MB) when compared to NaBH4 alone and uncoordinated lignin nanoparticles (LNP), as demonstrated in Fig. 11. The reduction in absorbance over time is especially striking with LNP-Zn, indicating a rapid and efficient degradation process. In contrast, the system containing only NaBH4 exhibited minimal absorbance reduction, underscoring its limited catalytic efficiency in the absence of nanoparticles. The uncoordinated LNP contributed to methylene blue degradation, yet it was less effective than the zinc-coordinated variant, as evidenced by the increased residual dye absorbance observed throughout the assay period. The enhanced activity observed with LNP-Zn can be attributed to its role as a proficient electron mediator, enabling electron transfer from NaBH4 to methylene blue. The incorporation of zinc plays a crucial role by lowering the bond dissociation energy within the dye molecule, facilitating electron transfer, and improving reduction kinetics. While NaBH4 alone initiates slow degradation, both LNP-Zn and LNP significantly accelerate the process, with LNP-Zn demonstrating the most notable effect. The findings align with established frameworks that describe the properties of green-synthesized metal nanocatalysts, emphasizing the importance of metallic doping in boosting electron transfer and enhancing dye-degradation efficiency [80, 137].

Determination of sunscreen protection factor

The SPF evaluation of LNP-Zn formulations shows a notable concentration-dependent improvement in UV protection, with SPF values rising from 3.45 at 20 µg/mL to 23.87 at 200 µg/mL, significantly surpassing the uncoordinated LNP (SPF 0.58–8.15), which is illustrated in supplementary Fig. 6. This increased activity can be linked to the in-situ transformation of ZnSO4 into ZnO within the LNP matrix during the synthesis or processing stages. ZnO is recognized for its effective physical UV blocking, with robust UV absorption and scattering, which likely enhances the exceptional photoprotection offered by LNP-Zn. This conversion enables the formation of ZnO nanoparticles embedded within the lipid matrix, thereby enhancing their crystallinity and UV-shielding efficiency [138–139]. Furthermore, including ZnO improves film formation and particle distribution on the substrate, thereby optimizing UV-blocking performance [140]. Thus, the SPF increase demonstrates the combined influence of ZnO production and improved nanoparticle interactions, positioning LNP-Zn as an exceptionally effective UV-protective system.

Biostimulant germination assays

It is crucial to ensure the biocompatibility of nanoparticles for their safe use in biological systems. Due to its sensitivity and rapid germination, Vigna radiata (L.) R. Wilczek is a prominent model for evaluating various compounds’ toxicity and biostimulant potential, including nanoparticles [83, 108]. A significant, concentration-dependent increase in primary root length was observed in seeds treated with LNP-Zn compared with the untreated control during the in vitro germination test, as shown in Table 3. Zinc ions act as vital cofactors in various enzymatic and cellular processes during early plant growth, potentially influencing root elongation by promoting metabolic activities essential for cell division and expansion [141–142]. LNP-Zn may improve the uniform distribution on the seed surface, resulting in enhanced nutrient absorption and moisture retention at the seed interface (Supplementary Fig. 7). Additionally, the antioxidant activity of lignin can mitigate oxidative stress, promoting active cell division and extension [143]. This stimulatory effect corroborates earlier findings regarding the beneficial outcomes of metallic (e.g., ZnO, Fe3O4, TiO2) and polymeric nanoparticles, which enhance root development during germination by improving micronutrient availability and microenvironmental conditions [144–145]. Metal-based nanoparticles exhibit strong antibacterial activity but may exhibit cytotoxicity, stability, and environmental persistence limitations. LNP-Zn improves the durability, bioactivity, and application of composite materials by combining lignin’s biocompatibility with zinc’s antibacterial and catalytic properties [120, 144–146].

Future prospects and challenges: techno-economic analysis (TEA) and life cycle assessment (LCA) perspectives

The shift of LNP-Zn from laboratory synthesis to industrial application requires a thorough assessment using Techno-Economic Analysis (TEA) and Life Cycle Assessment (LCA). The developed one-pot green strategy significantly reduces energy consumption by over 70% and functions under ambient conditions (below 40 °C); however, a thorough techno-economic analysis is necessary to confirm the long-term economic feasibility of processing sugarcane bagasse at a commercial scale [81]. Initial data demonstrating yields exceeding 70% without purifying losses or hazardous waste disposal indicate a favorable cost-reduction model relative to traditional metallic nanostructures. Moreover, forthcoming LCAs must rigorously examine the environmental trajectory, long-term biological safety, and mechanisms of interaction between Zn and lignin [147]. Despite the design of these metal–biopolymer nanohybrids for progressive degradation into natural phenolics and trace Zn2+ ions, the risks of environmental accumulation and the intricacies of their biogeochemical cycle pose considerable challenges [138]. Confronting these issues by combining superior structural performance with rigorous environmental compatibility will be essential for the rational design of sustainable LNP-Zn platforms in the global photoprotective and agricultural sectors.

Conclusion

Through a one-pot green synthesis method, this study shows how to transform lignin produced from sugarcane bagasse into zinc-coordinated lignin nanoparticles (LNP-Zn) in a sustainable and stabilizer-free manner. Comprehensive physicochemical characterization indicated that the lignin macromolecular framework yielded amorphous, mesoporous, and colloidally stable nanoparticles through in situ coordination of Zn2+. Instead of a physical mixture or zinc oxide phase, a genuine metal–biopolymer nanohybrid was formed, as confirmed by spectroscopic and elemental tests that confirmed Zn–O coordination with phenolic and carboxylate moieties. It was this hybrid nanoarchitecture that directly controlled the multifunctional performance of LNP-Zn. Coordinated zinc centers and lignin’s aromatic and phenolic backbone enabled effective antioxidant activity, UV shielding, and catalytic dye degradation. Lignin’s biopolymeric structure and its resistance to harsh reagents also contributed to its high hemocompatibility and cytocompatibility. It is worth noting that the high IC50 values observed in cancer cell lines suggest limited anticancer activity; as a result, the biological results primarily support LNP-Zn’s biocompatibility rather than its therapeutic potential. By linking the availability of zinc micronutrients to a lignin-based carrier system, the improvement in seedling root development simultaneously demonstrates the potential of these nanoparticles as bio-derived plant growth-supporting materials. This work offers a single, sustainable nanomaterial platform that integrates structural functionality, environmental compatibility, and biointeractive behavior, in contrast to earlier findings that focused on either lignin nanoparticles or inorganic zinc nanostructures. The current work furthers high-value lignin valorization by linking Zn-lignin coordination chemistry, amorphous mesoporosity, and surface charge with multifunctional performance. It also lays the groundwork for the logical design of zinc-lignin nanohybrids for environmentally remediation systems, photoprotective formulations, sustainable composites, and agricultural inputs. Long-term stability, zinc release behavior, biodegradation, and in vivo biosafety will be the main topics of future research. Additionally, LNP-Zn will be incorporated into polymeric matrices and coatings to confirm its effectiveness in actual bioactive composite systems. This work highlights the potential of metal–biopolymer nanoparticles generated from agricultural waste as multipurpose, eco-friendly substitutes for fossil-based nanomaterials. Additionally, the zinc-coordinated lignin nanoparticles are derived from renewable lignin and an essential micronutrient (Zn), supporting a sustainable material design. After use, the composites are expected to undergo gradual environmental or biological degradation, yielding lignin-derived phenolics and trace Zn2+ that can re-enter natural biogeochemical cycles. For agro-biological applications, the spent material may potentially be reused as a soil amendment or micronutrient source, minimizing secondary waste and aligning with zero-waste and circular-economy concepts.
