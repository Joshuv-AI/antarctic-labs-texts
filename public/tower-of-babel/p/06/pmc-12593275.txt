Assessment of engineered sustainable nitrogen fertilizers on lettuce development using physical sampling and image‐based phenotyping
Authors: Julia Farias, Matthew Conley, Matthew T. Herritt, Carson Call, Mohamed Ammar, Mohamed Eisa, Mariana Govoni Brondi, Jonas Baltrusaitis, Reagan W. Hejl, Clinton F. Williams
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractEfficient and sustainable nitrogen (N) management is critical for addressing the dual challenges of maximizing productivity and mitigating environmental impacts in short‐cycle crops like lettuce (Lactuca sativa). This study evaluates the agronomic performance and nutrient dynamics of eight N fertilizer treatments, including mechanochemically synthesized urea‐based cocrystals, conventional urea, and mechanochemically synthesized carbonate salts alongside accessible digital phenotyping methods for a greenhouse‐grown romaine lettuce system. Plant growth, biomass production, chlorophyll content, and soil N residues were measured in conjunction with digital image‐derived spectral indices from traditional photography. Engineered sulfate cocrystals (zinc sulfate urea and copper sulfate urea) performed comparable to or better than urea in promoting growth and N use efficiency, while engineered carbonates displayed moderate responses, likely due to slower N release. Traditional image‐based indices such as normalized green‐red difference index, hue angle, and green color distance metric, as well as more unique visual calculations, effectively captured plant nutritional status and correlated with chlorophyll levels. Principal component and cluster analyses further distinguished treatment performance based on growth and color metrics. These findings demonstrate the potential of combining novel N delivery systems with low‐cost phenotyping technologies to transform sustainable nutrient management practices across diverse crop systems.

INTRODUCTION

Modern agricultural systems heavily rely on plant varieties bred for efficient nutrient extraction, often with minimal regard for nutrient cycling or long‐term soil health and fertility (Jones et al., 2013; Whitcomb et al., 2014). This fast‐paced approach, characterized by near‐exponential nutrient removal, contributes to the gradual depletion and imbalance of essential minerals as elements are extracted from the soil at differing rates. Consequently, there is an urgent need to adopt more sustainable agricultural practices and materials that support nutrient recycling and soil health (Khatri et al., 2024).

Nitrogen (N) fertilization plays a pivotal role in determining plant yield, quality, and nitrate accumulation, while also influencing soil chemical and biological dynamics (Giordano et al., 2021). As agricultural practices increasingly prioritize soil health alongside productivity, managing N inputs becomes a complex challenge requiring careful balance (Mamatha et al., 2024). The globally cultivated leafy vegetable lettuce (Lactuca sativa) is especially responsive to N inputs, with N availability being a key growth and yield determinant (Becker et al., 2015; Jackson et al., 1994; Shatilov et al., 2019).

Optimizing fertilization strategies to improve both crop quality and environmental sustainability has become a major focus, involving innovations in fertilizer formulations and delivery methods. While conventional inorganic fertilizers are favored for their quick release, lower cost, and ease of use, slow‐release fertilizers have gained attention for their ability to minimize nutrient losses through runoff, leaching, and volatilization. However, synchronizing the N release from slow‐release fertilizers with the shorter growth cycles of crops like lettuce remains a significant challenge (Pew et al., 1983; Tlustos et al., 2000).

Fertilizers have played a key role in boosting and maintaining soil fertility, particularly in developed regions (Burns, 1992). Yet, sustainable agriculture goes beyond productivity; it requires a calculated balance between economic efficiency, social responsibility, and environmental protection (Bindraban et al., 2015). The nutrients removed through crops and livestock must be replenished, especially to avoid compromising ecosystems through overuse in nutrient‐poor soils while also minimizing pollution (Paramesh et al., 2023; Seleiman & Hafez, 2021). To address these challenges, advanced fertilizer materials are being developed to better align nutrient release with plant demand while minimizing environmental losses.

Recent advances in fertilizer technology offer a promising solution, particularly the development of urea‐based ionic cocrystals. Ionic cocrystals are traditionally produced via precipitation from aqueous solutions; however, these materials can now be synthesized more sustainably through mechanochemistry (Brondi et al., 2023; Casali et al., 2018, 2019; Eisa et al., 2024, 2022; Mazzei et al., 2019). This approach not only enhances the stability of the resulting fertilizers but also allows for the incorporation of waste stream products, aligning with market principles of circular economics and green chemistry.

Complementary to innovations in fertilizer chemistry, advanced monitoring tools are needed to ensure efficient nutrient use. To further enhance N use efficiency, precise monitoring of crop nutrient demand is essential (Burns, 1992; Buxbaum et al., 2022; Habibi et al., 2024). The integration of digital imaging technologies in agriculture has opened new avenues for real‐time, noninvasive crop monitoring (Cavalcanti et al., 2023; Du et al., 2020). These tools enable delineation of management zones, tracking of plant growth, assessment of nutritional status, detection of pests and diseases, and determination of optimal harvest times.

Low‐cost digital imaging supports the development of practical tools for diagnosing plant stress and managing nutrient inputs with greater precision. Image analysis based on red, green, and blue (RGB) color parameters has emerged as an effective method for assessing foliar color, an important indicator of plant health and nutritional status. Traditional color imaging fits within a range of tools that are now available to quantify leaf color changes. They span simple visual scales to advanced precision instruments like chlorophyll meters and spectrophotometers; a range of tools are now available to quantify leaf color changes (Casali et al., 2019; Du et al., 2020; Eshkabilov et al., 2022; Gang et al., 2022).

In this context, our study integrates mechanochemically engineered fertilizers with low‐cost digital imaging to assess N efficiency and crop health in lettuce production. While the mechanochemical synthesis of urea‐based cocrystals has been previously demonstrated (Brondi et al., 2023; Eisa et al., 2024), and digital imaging tools for crop phenotyping are increasingly used in research settings (Du et al., 2020), our study presents a novel integration of these two approaches with a focus on practical agricultural application. Specifically, we evaluate the agronomic performance of newly synthesized N‐efficient fertilizers within a controlled, soil‐based lettuce production system. These materials were not only characterized for their chemical stability and nutrient content but also assessed for their ability to support plant growth.

In parallel, we implemented low‐cost digital imaging using a consumer‐grade RGB camera under field‐realistic lighting and background conditions. Unlike high‐throughput platforms that require specialized sensors, image preprocessing pipelines, or machine learning algorithms, our approach employs computationally simple, standardized indices to monitor nutrient status. This methodological simplification significantly enhances the accessibility and scalability of digital diagnostics for on‐farm use, particularly for small‐ and medium‐scale growers.

Crucially, our study demonstrates that RGB‐derived indices can differentiate plant responses to distinct fertilizer formulations with high sensitivity, offering a nondestructive and cost‐effective tool for real‐time stress diagnosis and nutrient management. This represents a transformative shift from reactive to proactive nutrient management, especially critical for high‐value, short‐cycle crops like lettuce. The simplicity and affordability of our tools mean that even smallholder farmers can adopt digital diagnostics to improve yield, reduce waste, and enhance environmental sustainability.

In this study, we aim to streamline the detection of nutritional deficiencies in lettuce grown under mechanochemically engineered fertilizers by examining the relationship between several digital color metrics, biomass production, and chlorophyll content. Our goal is to identify informative color indicators that evaluate plant health and contribute to the development of efficient, sustainable practices in lettuce cultivation.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Pot experiment

The studies were conducted at the U.S. Arid Land Agricultural Research Center (ALARC), USDA, Arizona. Soil from the 0‐ to 20‐cm layer of a typical Silty Clay Loam soil (Table 1) was collected at the University of Arizona Farm, The Yuma Agriculture Center, Yuma, AZ (32.712264, −114.703621). The material was transferred to a greenhouse at the ALARC, where it was placed in bins and covered with plastic for solarization. After 2.5 weeks, the bins were uncovered and the soil was air‐dried. Dried soil was ground and sieved (maximum particle size of 2 mm).

Plant materials and growth conditions

Levels of phosphorous (P) and N were adjusted based on the results of the soil analysis and following the Guidelines for Head Lettuce Production in Arizona (Kerns et al., 1999). P levels were adjusted to 224.17 kg P2O5 ha−1 and N levels were adjusted to 336.255 kg N ha−1. N sources were added in a way to reach 17% of the required N before sowing (starter), and the remaining 83% were added at the first true leaves stage.

For the assays, air‐dried soil (1 kg) was placed in pots with a total capacity of 1.3 kg. In each pot, four romaine lettuce seeds were sown. Pelleted Sparx Romaine Lettuce Seed was purchased from Johnny Seeds, product ID: 3156JP. After 11 days, plants were thinned, and only the largest plantlet was left in each pot. Throughout cultivation, the soil humidity was maintained around 90% of field capacity using daily irrigations to replace water lost by evapotranspiration (gravimetrically measured) using deionized water. The plants were grown in a greenhouse with temperature control set to 25°C. The experimental design was completely randomized with four replicates.

Growth parameters such as number of leaves per plant and diameter were measured weekly for 8 weeks. Plant shoots were harvested and weighed to determine fresh mass. At harvest, part of the soil was freeze‐dried and ground to a fine powder using a porcelain mortar and pestle. Soil samples were dried at 65°C and ground for nutrient analysis.

Eight different N sources materials were used in this study including three engineered carbonates, (i) magnesium ammonium carbonate (MgAC, [NH4]2Mg[CO3]2⋅4H2O), (ii) zinc ammonium carbonate (ZnAC, (NH3)Zn(CO3)), and (iii) copper ammonium carbonate (CuAC, [NH3]2Cu(CO3)), and two engineered sulfates, (iv) copper sulfate urea (CuSU, CuSO4·3CO(NH2)2·H2O) and (v) zinc sulfate urea (ZnSu, ZnSO4·CO(NH2)2·2H2O), besides a control treatment with (vi) no nitrogen (NN), (vii) ammonium carbonate (AC, [NH4]2CO3), and (viii) urea (CO(NH2)2.

Mechanochemical synthesis

Engineered carbonates and sulfates

Materials were synthesized as described in previous studies (Brondi et al., 2023; Eisa et al., 2024). In summary, urea (CO(NH2)2, Sigma‐Aldrich; assay 99.0%–100.5%), basic MgCO3 (ACROS Organics, extra pure), Zn5[CO3]2(OH)6 (Alfa Aesar, assay of 97%) or Cu2CO3(OH)2 (Fisher Science), AC ([NH4]2CO3, Merck), and H2SO4 (Thermo scientific, assay 95.0%–98.0%) were purchased and used as received. All samples were first synthesized using a mechanochemical procedure using a Retsch MM300 mixer mill equipped with a stainless steel 15‐mL milling jar from Form‐Tech Scientific with a milling time of 10 min at 25 Hz using two 8‐mm zirconia balls and a total mass of 200 mg. After the conversion was confirmed using powder X‐ray diffraction, the process was scaled up using a Retsch PM100 planetary mill.

To synthesize the zinc and copper sulfate urea cocrystals (ZnSO4·CO(NH2)2·2H2O (ZnSU) and CuSO4·3CO(NH2)2·H2O) (CuSU), the urea sulfate cocrystal (US, [OHC(NH2)2]2(SO4)) was synthesized first using stoichiometric ratios of urea and sulfuric acid (H2SO4). Then the reactants, the corresponding Zn− and Cu− basic carbonates and US, were subjected into the stainless‐steel milling jar of 250 mL to be operated using Retsch PM100 planetary mill at 500 rpm for 10 min with 20 stainless‐steel balls to produce 15 g for the soil experiments.

[NH4]2Mg[CO3]2 ·4H2O (MgAC), ([HN3]2Cu(CO3) (CuAC), and (NH3)Zn(CO3) (ZnAC) were synthesized using the Retsch PM100 planetary mill using the reactant basic carbonates of magnesium (Mg), copper (Cu), and zinc (Zn) with [NH4]2CO3 via the stainless steel jar of 250 mL at 500 rpm for 10 min with 20 stainless steel balls. To ensure the crystals formation, a small amount of water was added into the reactants immediately before the milling. Products summary is given in Table 2.

Chemical analysis

Chlorophyll and carotenoid content

At the mid‐growth and harvest stage, 0.6‐cm leaf disks were collected from the third expanded leaf, avoiding major veins. The samples were immediately placed on ice, then stored at −20°C for further analysis. Total chlorophyll content (chlorophyll a + chlorophyll b) in lettuce leaves was measured according to the method described by Hiscox and Israelstam (Hiscox & Israelstam, 1979) and calculated with an adjusted formula by Wellburn (1994). Briefly, pre‐weighed frozen leaf discs were incubated at 65°C in dimethyl sulfoxide until complete tissues bleaching was achieved. Absorbance of the resulting solution was measured at 480, 649, and 665 nm to quantify chlorophyll and carotenoid contents.

Soil and tissue analysis

Following plant harvest, the soil in each pot was thoroughly homogenized, and a representative sample was withdrawn, freeze‐dried, and analyzed. Inorganic N and exchangeable acidity were extracted using KCl, and N levels were quantified as nitrates. Freeze‐dried plant tissue samples were analyzed on a Leco CN‐2000 analyzer, which employs a dry combustion method combined with thermal conductivity to measure N.

Image analysis

A Nikon D7000 camera (Nikon Corporation) (SN# 3376178) with an AF‐S DX VR Zoom‐Nikkor 18–200 mm f/3.5–5.6G IF‐ED [II] lens was employed to photograph potted plants in an environment‐controlled greenhouse at the USDA ALARC. The camera was mounted on a tripod and paired with a synchronized flash illuminant. Images were recorded in normal quality JPG format (approximately 4.1 MB), with a resolution of 4928 × 3264 pixels in size (16.1 MP). To accommodate varying plant sizes, eleven focal lengths were utilized, with zoom factors ranging between 48 and 120 mm. Photographic exposure settings included a shutter speed of 1/250 s and an International Organization for Standardization (ISO) of 100. The aperture was automatically adjusted, resulting in F‐Stops of 4.2 for 33–38 mm, 4.5 for 40–44 mm, 4.8 for 48–62 mm, and 5.3 for 95–120 mm. The camera operated with automatic white balance, and automatic focus (AF‐S mode) with 39 focal points, along with no vibration reduction, or automatic distortion control. A total of 161 images were captured across five collections, representing the fertilizer‐treated plants.

Top view area

Plant and scale were identified from images using a custom macro written in ImageJ Macro Language for ImageJ v1.54 (imagej.nih.gov). For each file with .jpg extension, the macro applies thresholds to the hue, saturation, and brightness channels of each image. The first threshold identifies a 10 cm piece of tape. Then the macro uses the Analyze Particles function to identify the tape with a size threshold of 10,000–500,000 and exclude on edge options, which excludes partial objects. The data for the image scale are stored in the results windows. To identify pixels from plant tissue, a second threshold was applied to the hue, saturation, and brightness channels. With the identified pixels from the thresholding, a binary mask was created using the Convert to Mask function and dilated six times using the Dilate function to include the areas of mixed pixels at object margins. A segmentation was applied to the hue, saturation, and brightness channels for the remaining pixels to select plant tissue areas. The Analyze Particles function was then used to identify the plant and whole leaves and record parameters in the image using a size threshold of 100,000–infinity, circularity of 0.10–1.00, and include holes options. The data for the scale and plant or whole leaves were saved to a comma‐separated value file. For each image, the pixel‐to‐cm calibration used the 10‐cm scale length to convert the pixel2 to cm2.

Image‐derived color variables

Image processing was conducted based on OpenCV 4.10.0 (Bradski & Kaehler, 2000) and Scikit‐image 0.23.2 (van der Walt et al., 2014) packages implemented in Python 3.12 software (Python Software Foundation) in a customized approach (Conley et al., 2024). Images were processed on a Dell Latitude 5420 laptop with an i7‐1185G7 1.8 GHz CPU and 16 GB of RAM (Dell Technologies Inc.) running Microsoft Windows 11 Pro 22H2 64‐bit OS (Microsoft Corporation). It took approximately 1 min to process each image. MS Excel Version 2401 (Microsoft Corporation) was used to manage tables of results.

To account for the different image focal lengths used, circular pot diameters were manually measured for three images taken at each mm of lens zoom factor focal length using ImageJ 1.54c, and a regression model was derived to determine the pixel area of pot top, as Equation pixels = 77,890 × mm‐2(−6) (R 2 = 0.99). Fractional pixel area calculations in images were then adjusted based on their lens focal length to allow spatial comparability.

Pixels containing green living plant material were segmented from the images using color thresholding in the hue saturation value (HSV) color space. Pixels representing vegetation were selected if the hue angle was between 30 and 60 (on a scale of 1–180), saturation was between 90 and 255 (>35%) (on a scale of 0–255), and brightness value was between 70 and 180 (>27% and <71%) (on a scale of 0–255). These parameters were determined by iteratively evaluating the selected pixels for three sample images from each collection. This allowed identification of the single filter parameter set that best accounted for the visible living green plant material and follows the method of Karcher and Richardson (2013).

To assess color qualities, image calculations were performed only on the fraction of green living plant material segmented from sample images. Several terms were selected for reporting, where values were calculated for individual pixels and then averaged, or a standard deviation derived.

A designated segment representing the living plant canopy was used to extract specific color characteristics from digital images. Scalar values for individual color components were calculated across multiple color spaces. To ensure data accuracy, image analysis was validated by verifying that the selected pixels corresponded to plant tissue and excluded background or non‐plant elements based on color intensity thresholds.

Statistical analysis

Mixed models

To evaluate the effects of N source (N) and time (t) on multiple phenotypic traits, a randomized factorial design was implemented. The design consisted of eight N treatments (N) and seven or five time points (t) depending on the variable (Figure 1), with four replicates per treatment combination. The variables analyzed included diameter, number of leaves, height, top area, NGRDI, ARGD, hue angle, HVI, ACI, and GCDM, which were each repeatedly measured on the same experimental units over time.

Illustrative graph with experimental workflow.

All variables were analyzed using linear mixed‐effects models accounting for repeated measurements. The model fixed effects were N source (N), time (t), and their interaction (N × t). The random effect of experimental unit (i.e., individual lettuce plant) was included to account for within‐subject correlation, modeled as ∼iid N(0, σ 2_B). To address residual autocorrelation and heteroscedasticity across time points, a heterogeneous first‐order continuous autoregressive (CAR(1)) covariance structure was used for the residuals: ∼N(0, R), where R captures both unequal variances and temporal correlation.

The mixed models were fitted using the lme() function from the nlme package (Pinheiro et al., 2017) in R. Statistical significance for fixed effects was assessed using Type III analysis of variance. Where significant differences (p ≤ 0.05) were detected, treatment means were compared using Tukey's multiple comparison adjustment via the emmeans package (Lenth & Lenth, 2018). Significance groupings were extracted using the cld() function.

All data visualization was performed using the ggplot2 package (Wickham, 2016), with additional enhancements from packages such as ggpubr, cowplot, and viridis. Group means and Tukey group letters were plotted across time for visual interpretation of treatment and interaction effects.

PCA analysis

To identify the most informative responses to N treatments over time, a principal components analysis (PCA) was performed, followed by unsupervised hierarchical clustering. The PCA was based on the correlation matrix of selected variables, which were standardized prior to analysis. The correlation matrix was computed using the corr.test() function from the psych package (Revelle, 2019) in R (R Core Team, 2022).

To improve interpretability, orthogonal varimax rotation was applied to the principal components. The number of retained components was selected based on parallel analysis and biological relevance, using the fa.parallel() and principal() functions from the psych package.

Following PCA, hierarchical clustering analysis was performed using Euclidean distance as the dissimilarity metric and Ward's method as the agglomerative clustering approach. The optimal number of clusters was selected based on minimization of within‐cluster sum of squares and biological interpretability. The clustering analysis was conducted using the dist() and hclust() functions from the stats package in R.

All analyses were conducted separately for each time point (e.g., 32 and 46 days after sowing [DAS]), and visualization was performed using the ggplot2 (Wickham, 2016) and ggpubr packages.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Plant development

The visual impact of N availability on lettuce growth is illustrated in Figure 2, which compares plant development under eight N treatments. Marked morphological differences were observed as early as 18 DAS, particularly in the NN, where plants displayed stunted growth and reduced leaf expansion (Figure 2A,B). These symptoms are characteristic of N deficiency and underscore the essential role of N in early vegetative development (Habibi et al., 2024; R'him et al., 2022).

Visual progression of romaine lettuce growth over time under different nitrogen treatments. Representative top‐view images show plant development at 4, 11, 18, 25, 32, 39, and 46 days after sowing (DAS). AC, ammonium carbonate; CuAC, copper ammonium carbonate; CuSU, copper sulfate urea; NN, no nitrogen; ZnAC, zinc ammonium carbonate; ZnSU, zinc sulfate urea. Different lowercase letters indicate significant differences among different treatments at te same evaluation time (p <.05).

Lettuce is a nutritionally demanding and fast‐growing crop requiring a steady and synchronized supply of N to sustain high photosynthetic activity and maximize biomass accumulation. When N is readily available, uptake closely matches the plant's assimilation potential, supporting optimal growth. Conversely, under N‐limited conditions, growth is constrained as the plant downregulates key physiological and molecular processes (Burns, 1992; Habibi et al., 2024; Y. Zhang et al., 2025).

Nitrogen starvation has been shown to influence the expression of numerous genes involved in photosynthesis, including components of the light‐harvesting complex, the photosystem I reaction center, and the large subunit of ribulose‐1,5‐bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase—a central enzyme in the Calvin cycle (Kumar et al., 2022). These disruptions can lead to impaired chlorophyll synthesis, reduced light capture efficiency, and overall declines in photosynthetic performance, contributing to the visual and physiological symptoms observed in N‐deficient lettuce plants (Burns, 1992; Kanash et al., 2023; Xie et al., 2025).

Visual inspection throughout the growth cycle revealed clear differences in lettuce development across N treatments (Figures 2A,B and 3A–C). A clear stratification of treatments emerged over time, delineating three distinct performance groups. While early seedling stages (4–11 DAS) showed relatively uniform growth, significant divergence appeared from 18 DAS onward (Figure 3A–C). By 46 DAS, plants treated with urea and engineered sulfate cocrystals (CuSU and ZnSU) exhibited the most vigorous growth, characterized by higher area and diameter (Figures 2B and 3A), but the differences were less pronounced in number of leaves and plant height (Figure 3B,C).

(A) Diameter (cm), (B) number of leaves (plant−1), and (C) plant height (cm), measured weekly for each treatment group. Values are an average of four replicates. AC, ammonium carbonate; CuAC, copper ammonium carbonate; CuSU, copper sulfate urea; MgAC, magnesium ammonium carbonate; NN, no nitrogen; ZnAC, zinc ammonium carbonate; ZnSU, zinc sulfate urea.

These differences visually confirmed the influence of N source and formulation on plant vigor, aligning with earlier reports that underscore the importance of N timing and availability in lettuce (Becker et al., 2015; Jackson et al., 1994). The intermediate‐performing group, which included engineered carbonates (MgAC, CuAC, and ZnAC) and ammonium carbonate (AC), showed moderate canopy expansion. These treatments supplied N at a level sufficient to sustain growth, but not enough to support the rapid biomass accumulation characteristic of lettuce under optimal fertilization. The relatively lower performance of these treatments is likely linked to incomplete synchronization between N release and crop demand.

The physicochemical properties of the carbonate‐based salts may lead to delayed or prolonged N release. While potentially beneficial for longer cycle crops, delayed or prolonged release appears to misalign with the N uptake curve of short‐cycle lettuce (Brondi et al., 2023; Eisa et al., 2024, 2022; Govoni Brondi et al., 2024). Additionally, the high volatilization potential of AC may have contributed to early N losses, further limiting its effectiveness (Brondi et al., 2023; J. Zhang et al., 2024).

In contrast, the top‐performing group composed of urea and engineered sulfate cocrystals (CuSU and ZnSU) exhibited significantly greater canopy areas from 25 DAS onward (Figures 2B and 3A–C). These treatments provided an N supply that matched the plant's physiological demand, resulting in vigorous growth with sustained increase in leaf area. Notably, the engineered sulfate cocrystals performed on par with or better than conventional urea, despite being designed to reduce N losses through improved stability (Eisa et al., 2024, 2022). This suggests that the controlled‐release behavior of these materials effectively meets crop N requirements while minimizing potential environmental losses, such as leaching or volatilization. Moreover, the co‐delivery of micronutrients (Zn and Cu) in sulfate form may confer additional agronomic benefits by enhancing enzymatic and photosynthetic efficiency (Casali et al., 2018; Jiang et al., 2021; Munyaneza et al., 2025).

Growth and biomass accumulation

Observations of biometric measurements—including top area, diameter, leaf number, and plant height—that demonstrated significant treatment effects (Figures 2A,B and 3A–C) resulted in the same pattern for biomass production at 46 DAS (Figure 4A). Urea and engineered sulfate cocrystals (CuSU and ZnSU) consistently promoted greater top area expansion and shoot elongation, especially after 25 DAS. These treatments achieved the highest biomass by the end of the experiment. This supports the premise that urea‐based slow‐release formulations can match or exceed the performance of conventional urea when release is well synchronized with crop demand (Burns, 1992; Quan et al., 2011).

Among the engineered carbonates, ZnAC and CuAC moderately improved lettuce growth, whereas MgAC showed limited benefit, likely due to slower N release or reduced solubility (Govoni Brondi et al., 2024). The sulfate‐ and carbonate‐based cocrystals (ZnSU, CuSU, ZnAC, and CuAC) not only act as N sources but also supply secondary nutrients that influence plant health.

This study does not advocate for the indiscriminate use of micronutrient‐enriched fertilizers. Instead, we emphasize the importance of assessing site‐specific conditions, including the historical management of the area and inherent soil properties, before application. Zinc is an essential micronutrient required for normal plant growth and metabolic functions in other organisms, yet, like many micronutrients, excessive Zn levels can become toxic (Guarino et al., 2020).

Zinc addition can be particularly beneficial in calcareous soils, such as those used in this study, where Zn deficiency is recognized as the most prevalent micronutrient disorder (Wissuwa et al., 2006). where Zn availability is often limited due to high pH and carbonate content. In contrast, the agronomic benefit of Cu is more context‐dependent, as Cu accumulation can pose a risk in soils with a history of repeated Cu‐based amendments (e.g., fungicides) (Cesco et al., 2021). While CuSU performed comparably to urea in terms of biomass and chlorophyll content, the broader applicability of Cu‐based cocrystals must be evaluated carefully to avoid excessive Cu buildup in the long term.

Extensive research has explored the use of Zn fertilizers across various regions (Impa & Johnson‐Beebout, 2012), including Zn fortification in multiple cereals to fight malnutrition and Zn deficiency (Ali et al., 2025). For such contexts, Zn cocrystals offer an environmentally friendly alternative, simultaneously supplying N and Zn through nutrient recovery processes.

In soils, magnesium is predominantly found in non‐exchangeable forms within primary and secondary minerals, with only a small fraction (∼5%) existing in exchangeable or soluble forms that are readily available for plant uptake. This limitation is particularly pronounced in clay‐rich soils, such as the one used in our experiment, where the release of exchangeable Mg often does not meet crop demand, thereby necessitating external inputs. Magnesium is vital for plant metabolism: it forms stable complexes with nucleophilic ligands, serves as the central atom in chlorophyll, stabilizes ribosome structure, and facilitates enzyme‐substrate interactions such as those involving RuBP carboxylase. It also plays a key role in maintaining intracellular pH and ionic balance (de Sousa Ferreira et al., 2023).

Our results suggest that the magnesium supplied by MgAC may have remained largely in non‐exchangeable forms, limiting its availability and agronomic effectiveness in this soil system. The control treatment (NN) and ammonium carbonate (AC) resulted in the poorest performance, confirming that both N absence and highly volatile sources limit productivity under greenhouse conditions.

Leaching was not observed during the experiment, as soil moisture was carefully maintained at approximately 90% of field capacity through daily gravimetric irrigation, thereby avoiding saturation and drainage. Nevertheless, N losses through denitrification and volatilization remain likely, particularly given the experimental conditions where the soil surface was exposed to water saturation during each irrigation event. Govoni Brondi et al. (2024) reported higher cumulative NH3 volatilization from AC compared to ZnAC and CuAC formulations under humid surface conditions, especially within the first 48 h after application, which aligns with our observations.

The N balance (Figure 4A–D) demonstrates that although total N inputs were equivalent across treatments, the partitioning between plant uptake, residual soil N, and unaccounted N differed markedly. Notably, AC had a higher proportion of total N unaccounted for compared to ZnAC, MgAC, and CuAC, suggesting that a substantial fraction of applied N was lost to the atmosphere before plant assimilation (Figure 4D). In contrast, ZnAC, MgAC, and CuAC treatments retained higher levels of residual nitrate‐N in the soil following cultivation, indicating that the primary limitation was not N loss but rather its availability to plants (Figure 4C). Conversely, sulfate‐based cocrystals (ZnSU and CuSU) promoted significantly greater N recovery in plant biomass, demonstrating improved synchronization between N release and plant demand.

While this pattern suggests improved N availability with sulfate‐based fertilizers, we refrain from making absolute claims of enhanced nitrogen‐use efficiency (NUE) because direct measurements of N losses (e.g., volatilization or leaching) were not conducted. Future studies are needed to quantify NUE more precisely. These findings highlight the critical role of fertilizer chemistry in reducing volatilization losses and enhancing N retention under humid surface conditions, thereby improving overall NUE in cropping systems.

Engineered sulfates demonstrating comparable or even superior growth to urea alone validate the effectiveness of mechanochemically synthesized urea cocrystals in promoting plant growth. This may also suggest that controlled‐release properties and potential synergistic micronutrient effects (from Cu and Zn) could contribute to improved N use efficiency (Cui et al., 2022; Montoya et al., 2020).

While N availability had a clear impact on plant development, there was no difference in N concentration in lettuce shoots among the tested treatments (Figure 4B). The lack of significant differences among the highest treatments suggests that both conventional urea and engineered sulfate cocrystals can support similar N accumulation in lettuce tissues. Panel C (Figure 4C) depicts the N use efficiency, as it shows the remaining N in soil after 46 days.

Interestingly, ZnSU and CuSU treatments showed the lowest values, statistically similar to urea (p > 0.05), but with a tendency toward greater uptake efficiency. This suggests that engineered sulfate cocrystals not only match urea in productivity but may utilize N more efficiently, likely due to a combination of reduced losses (e.g., leaching, volatilization) and potential synergies with micronutrients such as Zn and Cu, which play critical roles in photosynthetic enzymes and protein metabolism.

Higher residual concentrations were observed in carbonate‐based treatments and AC, which may be attributed to mismatches in N release timing or loss pathways, such as ammonia volatilization in the case of AC. These findings underscore the agronomic potential of engineered sulfate cocrystals, which combine high N availability, improved synchronization with crop uptake, and enhanced use efficiency. These characteristics are essential for achieving both high productivity and environmental sustainability in intensive cropping systems.

Colorimetric image traits

Image‐based analysis identified six descriptive calculations, NGRDI, ARGD, hue angle, HVI, ACI, and GCDM, selected for their strong theoretical correlations with normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI) and chlorophyll content. These indices captured nuanced color qualities in the canopy likely associated with leaf pigments, offering nondestructive insights into nutritional status (Figure 5A–F).

In contrast to the temporal trends observed in the growth parameters (Figure 1), which show a clear and continuous increase over time, consistent with the natural progression of plant development, the color‐related indices in Figure 2 display a more complex, sigmoidal pattern. This suggests that the dynamics of color change in lettuce leaves are not strictly linear or time‐dependent but instead reflect physiological shifts and nutrient availability throughout the growth cycle.

The sigmoidal responses observed in parameters such as NGDVI, hue, and spectral similarity index may be influenced by both internal plant regulation and external management factors, particularly N availability. Lettuce, known for its relatively inefficient root system, may struggle with consistent nutrient uptake as it matures. As such, the timing and method of N application play a critical role. Split N applications, commonly practiced to reduce N loss and optimize plant uptake, likely contribute to these nonlinear color trends (Nascimento et al., 2021; R'him et al., 2022; Zandvakili et al., 2022, 2019). These physiological and management‐related factors combine to create the wave‐like shifts in color indices, contrasting with the more straightforward upward trajectory of morphological traits like biomass and leaf area.

About 20% of the N was applied pre‐planting, and the remainder was applied at 25 DAS. The plant response to N is visible as there was an increase in NGRDI proceeding fertilizations. Similarly, hue values and ARGD exhibit the same behavior (Figure 5A,B,D). NGRDI has been reported as a proxy for green biomass, water content status, and chlorophyll level in plants, where NGRDI positively correlated with manually measured indicators (Yang et al., 2020). The hue angle progression of increasing green (37 toward 45 on a 0–180 scale) aligns well with the typical pattern of healthier plants exhibiting more vibrant green tones. Likewise, the increasing difference between green and red reflectance indicated by ARGD (20 toward 40 on a 0–255 scale) suggests increased plant health.

In this study, the color calculations showcase stratification of treatments observed affecting growth parameters, suggesting increased discrimination, with urea and engineered sulfates as most influential, followed by carbonates and AC and NN consistently less (Figures 2, 3, and 5). ARGD values showed a very similar pattern as compared to the NGRDI; however, ARGD indicated a higher sensitivity with increased separation between the three formed groups (Figure 5D).

HVI can be particularly useful in highlighting variation within the canopy—an indicator of nonuniform nutrient uptake. Interestingly, standard deviation of the hue angle was higher overall in the plants with larger biomass and increased as the plants grew. Considering that N is a mobile nutrient in plant tissue, as new leaves are produced and expand, it is expected for color variation to increase, and that was also true regarding plant growth stage. Increased color variation using HVI was also related to N concentration in the leaf tissue, which tended to decrease as the lettuce plants started forming their heads.

Plants treated with ZnSU, CuSU, and urea had higher NGRDI, hue angle, and ARGD, which is consistent with healthier, greener foliage. Conversely, NN and AC treatments showed higher ACI and GCDM values, indicating a negative change in color moving away from green for ACI as driven by an increasing u* channel toward red, but also augmented by shift in green and blue as captured by the v* channel, and likewise the greater perceptual distance from the green reference for GCDM, where both suggest a level of chlorosis or nutrient deficiency. These novel metric results support earlier findings on digital color analysis as a proxy for plant health (Casali et al., 2019; Du et al., 2020).

Correlation between chlorophyll and image‐derived indices

Our study focused on testing low‐cost RGB imaging as a practical alternative to conventional sensors such as single‐photon avalanche diode (SPAD) meters and NDVI‐based platforms, which are often costly and require specialized calibration. Although we did not include direct comparisons with these standard tools, previous studies have shown that RGB‐derived indices such as hue, NGRDI, and ARGD correlate strongly with SPAD readings and NDVI values (Casali et al., 2019; Du et al., 2020). The strong correlations between RGB indices and chlorophyll content observed in our study (r = 0.64–0.72 at 46 DAS) confirm that consumer‐grade imaging can deliver comparable diagnostic insights at a fraction of the cost and complexity. This highlights the practical relevance of RGB imaging for real‐time plant health monitoring, especially for small‐ and medium‐scale growers who may lack access to high‐cost sensing technologies.

Correlation analyses at 32 and 46 DAS revealed associations between chlorophyll content and digital color indices (Figures 6 and 7A–F). Across treatments, color indices hue, hvi, NGRDI, and ARGD (Figures 6A–F and 7A,D) generally showed positive correlations with increasing N availability at both evaluation times (32 and 46 DAS), with a higher correlation at 46 DAS (r = 0.72, 0.43, 0.64, and 0.59, respectively), confirming that greener hues can characterize higher chlorophyll contents.

Higher N levels were associated with increased vegetation indices (NGRDI), more saturated green hues (HUE), and reduced color variability (HVI). These trends were particularly strong in treatments with urea or engineered sulfates (e.g., pink and red lines), which showed steeper slopes and tighter confidence intervals. These treatments likely provided a more continuous supply of available N, supporting more uniform chlorophyll development and canopy color expression.

While urea, ZnSU, and CuSU consistently promoted greater biomass and canopy expansion, their chlorophyll contents were statistically similar (p > 0.05), indicating that all three treatments supported comparable photosynthetic performance. However, ZnSU and CuSU offer distinct agronomic advantages over conventional urea due to their controlled‐release properties. Previous work by Eisa et al. (2024) demonstrated that engineered sulfate cocrystals substantially reduce N losses compared to conventional urea, with modeled volatilization losses of ∼50% for sulfate cocrystals versus ∼89% for urea under high relative humidity conditions. These results suggest that ZnSU and CuSU can provide more controlled N release and reduced environmental losses, while in our experiment, they performed comparably to urea in terms of biomass and chlorophyll content. Additionally, these products offer co‐delivery of micronutrients (Zn, Cu) that play pivotal roles in chlorophyll synthesis, photosynthetic enzyme activation, and stress tolerance (Cui et al., 2022; Montoya et al., 2020). These benefits suggest that engineered sulfate cocrystals can maintain the productivity of urea while improving NUE and contributing to soil fertility through micronutrient enrichment.

Conversely, ACI and GCDM were negatively correlated with chlorophyll content (Figure 7A–F) in both evaluation times (32 and 46 DAS), with a higher correlation at 46 DAS (r = −0.71 and −0.63, respectively). Taken together, these findings affirm that simple, low‐cost digital imaging methods, paired with robust image processing, which may include novel calculations, can serve as reliable tools for monitoring nutritional status in lettuce and are in agreement with previous studies. ACI displayed negative correlations across treatments. This implies that higher N levels may lead to more uniform or darker canopy coloration (possibly due to increased chlorophyll), reducing angular variation in color expression across the canopy.

Our correlation analysis revealed that hue angle exhibited the strongest positive correlation with chlorophyll content (r ≈0.72 at 46 DAS), confirming its effectiveness as highlighted by recent studies (Jiao et al., 2023). In contrast, ACI displayed the strongest negative correlation (r ≈−0.70), capturing complementary aspects of color dynamics related to N status. This dual relationship, positive for hue and negative for ACI, provides a multi‐dimensional perspective on plant health, enabling better discrimination of subtle nutritional differences than single‐index approaches.

The novelty of our work lies not only in applying low‐cost RGB phenotyping to field realistic greenhouse conditions but also in introducing and validating ACI as a new, reproducible index. The ACI, calculated as the sum of the u and v components from the CIELUV color space, provides a comprehensive measure of red‐green chromaticity dominance. This index offers biologically meaningful insights into plant physiological status, as changes in chromaticity are often linked to pigment composition, stress responses, and overall leaf health. Although the computational implementation in Python was more intricate than expected for a seemingly straightforward summation, the resulting metric shows strong potential. ACI could serve as a valuable tool for monitoring vegetation health, detecting spatial variability, and assessing stress‐induced changes in plant tissues.

GCDM, a measure of color or spatial variability, also showed negative correlations, especially in high‐N treatments. This suggests that increased N results in more uniform canopies, reflecting improved nutrient uptake and more synchronized development. Unlike hyperspectral or machine‐learning methods that require expensive instrumentation and complex pipelines, our approach demonstrates that affordable, consumer‐grade cameras combined with simple but powerful indices like hue and ACI can deliver high sensitivity to chlorophyll variations. This makes our method accessible for real‐time nutrient monitoring and precision fertilizer management, even for small‐scale growers.

By integrating innovative slow‐release fertilizer formulations with low‐cost digital diagnostics, our study provides a unique contribution to precision agriculture, bridging the gap between cutting‐edge fertilizer engineering and practical imaging tools.

The separation of engineered cocrystals by salt type (carbonate vs. sulfate) reflects differences in solubility, nutrient release rates, and possibly micronutrient bioavailability. Sulfate‐based cocrystals appeared to offer improved N synchronization and potential co‐nutrient effects, supporting their value in developing sustainable fertilizer technologies.

This greenhouse‐scale study serves as a proof‐of‐concept for integrating mechanochemically engineered fertilizers with low‐cost digital imaging. While our findings demonstrate potential benefits for N management and plant health monitoring, we acknowledge that the absence of economic and environmental modeling limits the broader policy and adoption implications of this work. Future research should include life‐cycle assessments, cost‐benefit analyses, and environmental impact modeling to evaluate the feasibility and scalability of these fertilizers under real‐world conditions.

The cost of fertilizer production is a critical factor for practical adoption. Although a detailed economic analysis was beyond the scope of this study, we acknowledge that the cost‐effectiveness of mechanochemical cocrystals compared to conventional urea will be essential for scaling and farmer adoption. Preliminary assessments indicate that mechanochemical synthesis can be cost‐competitive due to its low energy requirements and use of waste‐derived inputs, but comprehensive cost‐benefit analyses remain necessary and will be the focus of future research (AlShamaileh et al., 2018, 2022).

CONCLUSION

Our study demonstrates that mechanochemically synthesized sulfate cocrystals (ZnSU and CuSU) performed comparably to conventional urea in promoting lettuce biomass and chlorophyll content under greenhouse conditions. In contrast, carbonate‐based cocrystals (AC, ZnAC, MgAC, and CuAC) did not fully meet plant N requirements, resulting in reduced N uptake and growth performance. However, the carbonate cocrystals still suggest a potential advantage in reducing N losses compared to AC, indicating that their environmental benefits may outweigh their lower growth response under certain conditions.

By integrating low‐cost RGB imaging, we revealed correlations between image‐derived indices, particularly hue angle (strongest positive correlation) and the newly introduced Aggregate Chromaticity Index (ACI, strongest negative correlation), and chlorophyll content. This complementary behavior between hue and ACI provides a more sensitive and multi‐dimensional framework for assessing N status than conventional indices alone. Our work advances precision farming by coupling engineered slow‐release sulfate fertilizers with affordable, reproducible image diagnostics capable of delivering real‐time, nondestructive insights into plant nutrient dynamics. The introduction of ACI, a novel chromaticity‐based metric, adds a unique diagnostic layer, demonstrating that simple RGB tools can rival the performance of more expensive multispectral systems in certain applications.
