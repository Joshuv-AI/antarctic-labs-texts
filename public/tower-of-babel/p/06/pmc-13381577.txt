Targeting miR‐124‐3p to Induce Antidepressant‐Like Effects in Female Mice: MicroRNA Modulation of Stress Resilience
Authors: Maria A Kuznetsova, Alexandra Fraser, Ching‐Seng Ang, Nicholas A Williamson, Nafisa Kazi, Anthony J Hannan, Thibault Renoir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMicroRNAs (miRNAs) play crucial roles in regulating gene expression and have been implicated in the pathophysiology of depression. Among them, miR‐124‐3p is one of the most abundant brain‐enriched miRNAs and has emerged as a potential therapeutic target for mood disorders. This study aimed to evaluate the antidepressant‐like effects of an acute administration of miR‐124‐3p and to identify associated molecular and neuronal changes across key brain regions. Female C57BL/6 mice received intracerebroventricular injections of a miR‐124‐3p mimic or a control oligonucleotide. Affective‐like behavioural responses were assessed using the Porsolt swim test (PST) and the light–dark box test (LDB). Proteomic alterations in the hippocampus, hypothalamus and prefrontal cortex were analysed by liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS), and stress‐related neuronal activity was evaluated using c‐Fos immunofluorescence. Mice treated with the miR‐124‐3p mimic exhibited reduced immobility in the PST, indicative of an antidepressant‐like effect and enhanced active stress coping. Proteomic profiling revealed region‐specific changes, including downregulation of immune‐ and stress‐related proteins in the hypothalamus and hippocampus, and upregulation of synaptic and metabolic proteins in the prefrontal cortex. c‐Fos analyses showed decreased stress‐induced neuronal activation in the hypothalamic paraventricular and periventricular nuclei, alongside increased activity in the hippocampal dentate gyrus. Collectively, these findings suggest that miR‐124‐3p mimic treatment is associated with antidepressant‐like effects by reducing neuroimmune activation and enhancing neuronal plasticity. Our results support a model in which miR‐124‐3p promotes stress resilience through coordinated modulation of inflammatory and plasticity‐related processes across distinct brain circuits.Keywords: c‐Fos, depression, microRNA, miR‐124‐3p, miRNA mimic, proteomics

Introduction

Depressive and anxiety disorders are among the most common mental health conditions worldwide, affecting approximately 3%–7% of the population and significantly contributing to global disability and suicide risk (WHO 2017). These affective disorders are highly heterogeneous, and existing treatments fail to provide adequate relief in 30%–50% of patients (Zhdanava et al. 2021; Ansara 2020). Understanding the molecular and cellular mechanisms underlying these disorders is crucial to improving treatment outcomes.

Epigenetic mechanisms, which regulate gene expression without altering the DNA sequence, have emerged as important contributors to the pathophysiology of depression and anxiety. These include DNA methylation, histone modifications, and small noncoding RNAs such as microRNAs (miRNAs) (Penner‐Goeke and Binder 2019). MiRNAs act post‐transcriptionally to negatively regulate gene expression and can simultaneously influence multiple genes across neural networks (Olejniczak et al. 2018). Their broad regulatory roles position them as key modulators of neurobiological processes implicated in affective disorders, including neurogenesis, neuroinflammation, oxidative stress and synaptic plasticity (Dwivedi 2018; Roy et al. 2017; Issler and Chen 2015; Smalheiser et al. 2014).

Among miRNAs, miR‐124‐3p is particularly abundant and brain‐enriched, with a widespread expression pattern especially prominent in brain regions associated with stress responses (Zhang et al. 2023). Several studies investigated the role of miR‐124 in depression and anxiety through both clinical and preclinical models. However, findings regarding its functional effects remain inconclusive and sometimes contradictory.

Preclinical evidence largely supports an antidepressant‐like effect of miR‐124‐3p, as shown in behavioural studies (Higuchi et al. 2016; Lou et al. 2019). Conversely, other studies report depressant‐like effects (Gu et al. 2019; Yi et al. 2018). This bidirectional pattern is also reflected clinically: miR‐124 is upregulated in serum and peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from major depressive disorder (MDD) patients but downregulated in plasma from similar cohorts (van den Berg et al. 2020). A longitudinal preclinical study using a chronic unpredictable mild stress (CUMS) paradigm in Sprague–Dawley rats found miR‐124 increased 5–6 weeks after stress onset but decreased by Weeks 7–8 (Huang et al. 2019). Such dynamic fluctuations suggest that miR‐124 expression and function depend heavily on the timing and nature of stress exposure and treatment, as well as on experimental parameters.

In anxiety models, miR‐124 also shows mixed effects. For example, social fear conditioning in mice increased miR‐124 expression in the septum, correlating with anxiogenic behaviour (Bludau et al. 2023), whereas lentiviral delivery of miR‐124‐3p reduced anxiety‐ and depression‐like behaviours following acute prolonged stress (Chen et al. 2022). Taken together, these data indicate that miR‐124 likely undergoes significant dynamic regulation influenced by acute versus chronic stress, treatment duration and experimental paradigms.

Mechanistically, miR‐124‐3p targets multiple pathways relevant to stress‐related disorders, including immune responses, inflammation, energy metabolism and neurogenesis. Notable among these targets is AKT2, a key protein in the PI3K/AKT/GSK3 signalling pathway that modulates brain‐derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) production and is implicated in depression (Leibrock et al. 2013). AKT2 loss‐of‐function induces anxiety‐like phenotypes and impairs fear‐conditioned learning and memory discrimination (Palumbo et al. 2021). Rescue of these deficits via PI3K/AKT pathway activation highlights AKT2's role in neurodevelopment, cognition and stress resilience.

Other targets, though less extensively studied, have also been linked to anxiodepressive symptoms. Downregulation of aggrecan (ACAN) in GABAergic neurons impairs fear memory recovery and facilitates long‐term extinction training in adult mice (Lavertu‐Jolin et al. 2023). The transcriptional repressor RERE, although not yet assessed in animal models, has been associated with white matter integrity and cortical thickness differences between MDD patients and controls (Katsuki et al. 2019), suggesting potential roles in neural circuitry underlying depression.

Pharmacological modulation of miR‐124‐3p expression further supports its involvement in mood regulation. The flavonoid luteolin increases hippocampal miR‐124‐3p levels while suppressing pro‐inflammatory targets such as TNF‐α, TRAF6, NF‐κB and IκB, coinciding with antidepressant‐like behavioural effects in BALB/c female mice subjected to breast‐cancer‐related depression (Zhu et al. 2022). Similarly, environmental enrichment (EE) in Wistar rats enhances neurogenesis in the dentate gyrus (DG) with concurrent upregulation of miR‐124 and activity‐dependent markers c‐Fos and CREB, which may underlie improved neuronal survival and function (Brenes et al. 2016).

Structural analyses complement these findings: intrahippocampal injection of miR‐124 mimic prevents CUMS‐induced reductions in dendritic length and spine density in BALB/c mice, effects that are absent in non‐stressed controls (Higuchi et al. 2016). These data indicate that miR‐124's neuroprotective and plasticity‐promoting functions may be stress‐dependent.

Given the evidence of miR‐124‐3p's complex, context‐dependent regulation and function in stress and affective disorders, we aimed to investigate the effects of miR‐124‐3p mimic treatment on depressive‐like behaviours in a rodent model. We administered a single intracerebroventricular injection of the miR‐124‐3p mimic and assessed behavioural outcomes 3 days postinjection. In parallel, we employed proteomic analyses and fluorescent microscopy to elucidate molecular and cellular changes in the brain following treatment and behavioural testing. This approach allowed us to characterise not only behavioural effects but also underlying neurobiological alterations associated with miR‐124‐3p modulation.

Acute intracerebroventricular (ICV) delivery of miRNA mimics remains underexplored compared with chronic stress paradigms, despite evidence that many miRNAs exert rapid regulatory effects on neuronal activity, neuroimmune signalling and metabolism. Mapping early, region‐specific molecular and neuronal responses therefore provides mechanistic insight into the initial phases of miR‐124‐3p action. This targeted acute approach complements previous chronic stress studies and helps to clarify why miR‐124 shows bidirectional behavioural outcomes depending on timing, brain region and stress context.

Materials and Methods

Animal Husbandry

All procedures were approved by the Florey Institute of Neuroscience and Mental Health Animal Ethics Committee (AEC#21‐035) and conducted in accordance with NHMRC guidelines. Female C57BL/6 mice (7 weeks, Animal Resources Centre, Australia) were housed five per cage until surgery at 8 weeks of age. Mice were housed under standard laboratory conditions without additional environmental enrichment beyond standard bedding and nesting material. Rooms were maintained on a 12‐h light/dark cycle (lights on 07:00) with ad libitum food and water.

Experimental Design

At 8 weeks, mice were randomized to control or experimental groups. On Day 0, right lateral ventricle cannulas were implanted. After 3‐day recovery, mice received an intracerebroventricular (ICV) injection of either 1‐nmol miR‐124‐3p mimic (VC30002, Sigma‐Aldrich, USA) or 1‐nmol miRNA mimic negative control (HMC0003, Sigma‐Aldrich, USA).

Behavioural testing was conducted 72 h after ICV delivery, a time point corresponding to the documented peak uptake and functional incorporation of synthetic miRNA mimics in the mouse brain.

Cohort #1 (n = 20): light–dark box test (LDB), Porsolt swim test (PST), brain collection for proteomics. Cohort #2 (n = 40): Group 1 (n = 20) underwent PST; Group 2 (n = 20) underwent LBD test. Brains from Group 1 were fixed for c‐Fos analysis 90 min after PST. Behavioural data were combined unless stated. See Figure 1 for design.

Experimental design. C57BL/6 mice were injected with miR‐124‐3p mimic or negative control nucleotides and tested in the LDB test and/or PST. Fresh brain tissues (hypothalamus, hippocampus and prefrontal cortex) from cohort #1 (n = 20 mice) were collected for proteomics analyses. Brain tissues from cohort #2 (n = 40 mice) were fixed and used for c‐Fos study. LC–MS/MS, liquid chromatography with tandem mass spectrometry.

Stereotaxic Surgery

Mice were anaesthetized with isoflurane (1.5%–1.8% in 1 L/min air) and injected with meloxicam (3 mg/kg, i.p.). A 26‐gauge guide cannula (cut 5 mm below pedestal; BioScientific) was implanted at: posterior −1.0 mm; lateral 0.3 mm; ventral −2.5 mm (relative to bregma). Cannulas were secured with dental acrylic and a dummy cannula inserted. Mice recovered for 3 days and were weighed daily. Oligonucleotides were dissolved in saline (0.5 nmol/4 μL) and infused at 0.5 μL/min using a 33‐gauge injector. Cannulas were kept patent with dummy inserts before and after injections.

LDBT

Anxiety‐like behaviour was assessed in the LDB. Arenas (26 × 26 × 38 cm) were divided into light (750 ± 10 lux) and dark (< 5 lux) compartments (E63‐10; Coulbourn Instruments, Allentown, PA, USA). Each mouse was placed head first into the dark compartment for a 10‐min session. Behaviour was recorded with Tru Scan 2.0 software.

PST

Coping strategies under inescapable stress were assessed with the PST. Mice were placed in a 2‐L beaker filled with 1.5 L of water (25°C ± 2°C) for 6 min. Immobility time during the last 4 min of the test was scored using Depression Scan (Clever Sys Inc.), reflecting a passive stress‐coping strategy sensitive to antidepressants.

Proteomics Analysis Pipeline

Tissue Collection

After behavioural testing, cohort #1 mice were killed by cervical dislocation. The hippocampus, hypothalamus and prefrontal cortex were manually dissected on an ice‐cold surface according to anatomical landmarks, snap‐frozen on dry ice, and stored at −80°C. The approximate longitudinal spans for the harvested tissue relative to bregma were PFC (+2.8 to +1.6 mm), hippocampus (−1.2 to −3.5 mm) and hypothalamus (−0.5 to −2.5 mm). This ‘whole‐region’ approach was selected to provide a comprehensive proteomic profile of each distinct brain area.

Protein Extraction and Protease Digestion

Frozen tissue (~20 mg per sample) was homogenized in 180‐μL TEAB (0.1 M, pH 8.8; Sigma‐Aldrich) + 20 μL 10% sodium deoxycholate (Sigma‐Aldrich). Samples were sonicated (Bioruptor; 10 cycles, 30 s on/off, 4°C, Diagenode company), reduced with 20 μL 0.5‐M TCEP (65°C, 30 min), alkylated with 20 μL 100‐mM iodoacetamide (37°C, 30 min), quenched with 20‐μL dithiothreitol (37°C, 30 min) and digested overnight with trypsin (1:20 ratio). Digests were acidified with 40‐μL trifluoroacetic acid, centrifuged (13,200 ×g, 10 min), desalted using Oasis HLB μElution plate (Waters), dried and stored at −20°C.

Liquid Chromatography data‐Independent Acquisition Mass Spectrometry (LC DIA‐MS)

Peptides were analysed on an Orbitrap Eclipse mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific) coupled to an Ultimate 3000 RSLC system (Dionex) with Pepmap trap (75 μm × 2 cm) and Pepmap RSLC column (75 μm × 50 cm). Peptides were loaded (5 μL/min, 5 min, 2% acetonitrile, 0.1% formic acid) then separated with a 91‐min gradient (3%–80% solvent B: 5% DMSO in acetonitrile +0.1% formic acid). DIA settings: full MS resolution 120,000 (m/z 200), 350–1400 m/z, AGC 250%, IT 50 ms. Fragment spectra: AGC 2000%, 50 windows of 13.7 Da with 1 Da overlap, resolution 30,000, IT 55 ms, collision energy 30%. Data acquired in centroid, positive polarity.

Database Search (Spectronaut)

DIA files were analysed in Spectronaut (v16.3) against the UniProt Mus musculus database. Parameters: trypsin (≤ 2 missed cleavages), fixed carbamidomethyl (Cys), variable oxidation (Met) and N‐terminal acetylation. false discovery rate (FDR) set at 1% for protein/PSM. Quantification was at MS2 level with automatic normalization.

Data Processing and Statistical Analysis

Data were log2‐transformed in Perseus (v1.3). Missing values (approximately 10% of the total dataset) were imputed from a normal distribution and normalized by median subtraction. Differential proteins were identified using Student's t‐test with permutation‐based FDR control set at 5%, ensuring appropriate correction for multiple comparisons across the proteome. Gene ontology enrichment was performed in FunRich (v3.1.3) with significance set at p < 0.05. Bubble plots were generated in R (ggplot2).

C‐Fos Expression Analysis Pipeline

Tissue Processing

Cohort #2 brains were perfused with 1M PBS and 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA), postfixed in PFA (24 h), then cryoprotected in 30% sucrose. Brains were coronally sectioned at 30 μm from prefrontal cortex (bregma +2.58 mm) to occipital lobe (−6.24 mm).

Immunohistochemistry

Sections were treated with 10% methanol (15 min), PBS + 0.3% Triton X‐100 (30 min), blocked in 10% normal donkey serum (NDS) in PBS + 5% Triton X‐100 (1 h) and incubated with anti‐c‐Fos (Abcam ab190289; concentration 1:1000) in PBS + 2% NDS + 5% Triton X‐100 overnight (4°C). After PBS‐T washes, sections were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488 anti‐rabbit secondary antibody (1:500, Invitrogen A‐11008, 2 h, RT, dark) and counterstained with DAPI (1:2000). Sections were mounted with DAKO medium and imaged on a Leica Thunder microscope (20×/0.8).

Quantification

Using Fiji (ImageJ), hypothalamus was cropped at 20×/0.8 dry magnification, and paraventricular nucleus (PVN), periventricular nucleus (PeVN) and arcuate nuclei delineated bilaterally. After comparing c‐Fos images with DAPI stains, images were converted to 16‐bit images and Gaussian blur (0.4 μm), overlay masks and automatic thresholds were applied. Crops were performed manually using the polygon tool at estimated regions. c‐Fos positive cells were defined as ≥ 5 μm, circularity 0.55–1. Cell size and circularity were assessed between different measures to minimize counting of foreign particles and surface edges. Experimenters counting c‐Fos positive cells were blinded to group conditions during the whole experiment. As soon as brain samples were collected, an independent researcher allocated random identification (ID) numbers to each brain. ID numbers were only cross‐referenced after quantification was completed. After hippocampal analysis, a repeated test was run using a .ijm macro to count the images according to settings provided. Areas: hypothalamus 1,748,016 μm2; PVN 94,333 μm2; PeVN 129,716 μm2; Arc 87,333 μm2. For each mouse, 4–8 slices between −0.5 and −1.24 mm from bregma were analysed. See Figure S1 for pictures illustrating the counting.

Statistical Analysis

Data are shown as mean ± SEM. Counts were normalized to area and averaged. Outliers were excluded via Grubbs' test (α = 0.05). Two‐way ANOVA was used to assess effects of PST and miRNA injection, followed by multiple comparisons in GraphPad Prism. For all ANOVA results, F‐values, degrees of freedom and post hoc group comparisons were calculated and are reported in the Results section to ensure statistical transparency.

Results

Effects of miR‐124‐3p Mimic Treatment on Anxiety‐ and Depressive‐Like Behaviour After the ICV Injection With miR‐124‐3p Mimic Oligonucleotide

Results from the LDB test showed that mice treated with the miR‐124‐3p mimic spent significantly less time in the light compartment compared to controls (p < 0.05) (Figure 2A), indicating a modest anxiogenic‐like effect.

Antidepressant and anxiogenic effects of miR‐124‐3p mimic treatment. (A) Effects of miR‐124‐3p mimic treatment on anxiety‐like behaviour in the LDB test 72 h after intracerebroventricular injection of control or miR‐124‐3p mimic oligonucleotide. Mice treated with miR‐124‐3p mimic spent significantly less time in the light compartment compared with controls (*p < 0.05). (B) Effects of miR‐124‐3p mimic treatment on immobility duration in the PST. In the PST, immobility time was significantly reduced following miR‐124‐3p mimic treatment (p < 0.001) (Figure 2B), consistent with an antidepressant‐like behavioural response in the PST. Together, these findings suggest that miR‐124‐3p mimic produces robust antidepressant‐like effects while potentially transiently increasing anxiety‐like behaviour under mildly anxiogenic conditions.

Differential Protein Expression in the hippocampus, Hypothalamus and Prefrontal Cortex of Mice After miR‐124‐3p Mimic Treatment

To determine whether miR‐124‐3p mimic treatment affected protein expression in key brain regions, we conducted proteomic analysis in the hippocampus, hypothalamus and prefrontal cortex of 8‐week‐old mice. We identified 27, 32 and 30 differentially expressed (DE) proteins in the hippocampus, hypothalamus and prefrontal cortex, respectively (Figure 3A–C).

(A–C) Proteomic changes in the hippocampus, hypothalamus and prefrontal cortex following miR‐124‐3p mimic treatment. Volcano plots show statistical significance versus fold change in proteome‐wide protein levels for the hippocampus (A), hypothalamus (B) and prefrontal cortex (C). Each dot represents a protein. Proteins significantly altered (**p < 0.01) are annotated with their UniProt accession numbers. Downregulated proteins appear on the left, upregulated proteins on the right. Differentially expressed proteins are highlighted in red; n = 7–8 mice per group.

Although miRNAs primarily exert repressive effects on direct mRNA targets, increases in protein abundance can arise indirectly through network‐level feedback or compensatory signalling pathways, which is frequently observed in miRNA mimic studies. Immediate‐early genes such as c‐Fos were not detected in the DIA‐MS dataset, which is expected given their low basal abundance and rapid turnover.

In the hypothalamus, 32 proteins were differentially expressed, including 23 downregulated and nine upregulated in the miR‐124‐3p mimic group compared to control‐treated mice (Appendix A: Table S1). Several downregulated proteins were associated with inflammatory processes, such as H‐2 class I histocompatibility antigen K‐B alpha chain (H2‐K1), interferon‐induced protein with tetratricopeptide repeats 3 (IFIT3), and signal transducer and activator of transcription 1 (STAT1). Others were linked to neural connectivity and plasticity, including Rho guanine nucleotide exchange factor 6 (ARHGEF6) and H2‐K1. Additionally, we observed decreased expression of proteins implicated in depression and emotional regulation, such as arginine‐glutamic acid dipeptide repeats protein (RERE), glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) and islet cell autoantigen 1‐like protein (ICA1L), which has been associated with loneliness. Proteins involved in apoptosis and oxidative stress were also downregulated, including 3‐mercaptopyruvate sulfurtransferase (MPST), phospholipid phosphatase 1 (PLPP1) and etoposide‐induced protein 2.4 (EI24). Together, these findings suggest that miR‐124‐3p mimic treatment was associated with a reduction of stress‐ and inflammation‐related signalling in the hypothalamus, potentially contributing to improved stress resilience.

In the hippocampus, 27 proteins were differentially expressed: 19 were downregulated and eight were upregulated following miR‐124‐3p mimic treatment (Appendix A: Table S1). Similar to the hypothalamus, several downregulated proteins were involved in neuroimmune and inflammatory responses, such as antiviral receptor RIG‐I (DDX58), interferon‐induced transmembrane protein 3 (IFITM3) and ragulator complex protein LAMTOR4. Proteins relevant to synaptic plasticity, neurogenesis and neurodevelopment were also affected, including rabphilin‐3A (RPH3A), beta‐2‐microglobulin (B2M), Ras‐related protein Rab‐33A (RAB33A), aggrecan core protein (ACAN) and amphoterin‐indced protein 2 (AMIGO2). Notably, several proteins previously linked to depression and antidepressant treatment were differentially expressed, such as BH3‐interacting domain death agonist (BID), Trafficking protein particle complex 9 (TRAPPC9) and nicotinamide phosphoribosyltransferase (NAMPT). These alterations indicate that miR‐124‐3p mimic treatment was associated with reduction of neuroimmune activation and promoted plasticity‐related processes in the hippocampus, a region critical for mood regulation and memory.

In contrast to the hypothalamus and hippocampus, where most DE proteins were downregulated, all 30 DE proteins in the prefrontal cortex were upregulated (Appendix A: Table S1). These included proteins involved in cell adhesion and ion transport, such as cadherin‐4 (CDH4), phosphatidylinositol 3,4,5‐trisphosphate 5‐phosphatase 2 (INPPL1) and contactin‐associated protein‐like 5B (CNTNAP5B). Other upregulated proteins were associated with protein phosphorylation and ubiquitination, including atypical kinase COQ8B (mitochondrial), tripartite motif‐containing protein 3 (TRIM3), phospholipase A2‐activating protein (PLAA), phosphatidylinositol 4,5‐bisphosphate 5‐phosphatase A (INPP5J) and cyclin‐dependent kinase 17 (CDK17). Lastly, we observed increased expression of proteins previously implicated in depression and anxiety, such as Pumilio homologue 1 (PUM1), apurinic/apyrimidinic endonuclease (APEX1) and RAC‐beta serine/threonine‐protein kinase (AKT2). This widespread upregulation suggests that miR‐124‐3p mimic is associated with enhancement of metabolic, plasticity‐related and neuroprotective pathways in the prefrontal cortex, potentially supporting its antidepressant‐like behavioural effects.

Gene Ontology (GO) Annotation of Differentially Expressed Proteins Following miR‐124‐3p Mimic Treatment

Gene Ontology (GO) analysis revealed significantly enriched functional categories across all three brain regions examined: the hypothalamus, hippocampus and prefrontal cortex (Figure 4).

Gene Ontology annotation. Bubble plot showing enriched GO terms in the hippocampus, hypothalamus and prefrontal cortex following miR‐124‐3p mimic treatment. The x‐axis indicates the number of proteins associated with each term, whereas the y‐axis displays GO subcategories, including biological process (BP), cellular component (CC) and molecular function (MF). Circle colour represents statistical significance, with darker colours indicating smaller p‐values (from orange to black).

In the hypothalamus differentially expressed proteins were enriched in cellular components including the cytoplasm, cytosol and cytoskeleton (CC subcategory); molecular functions such as protein binding, MHC class I protein binding and identical protein binding (MF subcategory); and biological processes including cellular response to interferon‐beta and response to drug (BP subcategory).

In the hippocampus, enriched cellular components included the membrane and endoplasmic reticulum (CC); molecular functions included calcium‐dependent phospholipid binding and beta‐catenin binding (MF); and biological processes were enriched for regulation of cell adhesion and cell migration (BP).

In the prefrontal cortex, enriched cellular components included the cytoplasm, cytosol and endoplasmic reticulum (CC); molecular functions included calcium‐dependent phospholipid binding and transferase activity (MF); and biological processes were related to protein transport, ubiquitin‐dependent protein catabolic process, tumour necrosis factor‐mediated signalling and cellular response to interferon‐beta (BP).

Together, the proteomic data reveal that miR‐124‐3p mimic treatment was associated with distinct, region‐specific molecular changes in the mouse brain. Although the hypothalamus and hippocampus exhibited predominant downregulation of proteins involved in neuroinflammation, immune signalling and stress‐related pathways, the prefrontal cortex showed a coordinated upregulation of proteins related to plasticity, protein transport and cellular metabolism. These alterations were further supported by enriched GO terms reflecting reduced inflammatory signalling and enhanced neuroplastic and metabolic processes. Collectively, these findings suggest that the antidepressant‐like effects of miR‐124‐3p may be mediated by a reduction in neuroimmune activity alongside the promotion of cellular resilience and plasticity in key mood‐regulating brain circuits.

Because the number of detected differentially expressed proteins per region was modest, the Gene Ontology enrichments presented here should be interpreted as exploratory. Nonetheless, the consistency of functional themes across regions supports their biological relevance.

Dysregulation of Neuronal Activity in the Hippocampus and Hypothalamus of C57BL/6 Mice After miR‐124‐3p Mimic Treatment

We investigated whether miR‐124‐3p mimic treatment and/or exposure to the PST altered neuronal activity, measured by c‐Fos expression, in the hypothalamus (Figure 5, Figure S1) and hippocampus (Figure 6) of 8‐week‐old mice.

Quantification of c‐Fos‐positive cells (cells/mm2) in (A) total hypothalamus, (B) paraventricular nucleus (PVN), (C) periventricular nucleus (PeVN) and (D) arcuate nucleus. Open squares on white bar = no PST; filled squares on grey bar = PST. Data are mean ± SEM; per group, n = 9–10 mice. Statistical significance: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001 and ****p < 0.0001.

Effects of miR‐124‐3p mimic treatment and PST exposure on c‐Fos expression in the hippocampus. Quantification of c‐Fos‐positive cells (cells/mm2) in (A) total hippocampus, (B) dentate gyrus (DG), (C) CA1 and (D) CA3. Open squares on white bar = no PST; filled squares on grey bar = PST. Data are mean ± SEM; per group, n = 6–9 mice per group. Statistical significance: *p < 0.05.

In the hypothalamus, there was a significant interaction between miR‐124‐3p mimic treatment and PST exposure (p = 0.0026; F 1,37 = 10.52), with region‐specific effects in the PVN and PeVN (Figure 5A–C, respectively). No significant main effects or interactions were observed in the arcuate nucleus (interaction: p = 0.4626; PST: p = 0.0904; miR‐124‐3p: p = 0.7769), and post hoc tests confirmed no significant group differences, indicating that this nucleus is not affected by PST exposure or miR‐124‐3p treatment under these experimental conditions (Figure 5D).

PST exposure increased c‐Fos expression in the hypothalamic PVN (p = 0.0005; F1,37 = 14.46) and PeVN (p < 0.0001, F = 21.36), whereas miR‐124‐3p mimic treatment decreased c‐Fos expression in both regions (PVN: p = 0.0361; F 1,37 = 4.749; PeVN: p = 0.0128; F 1,37 = 6.887) (Figure 5B,C). In addition, a significant interaction effect was found in the PVN, where c‐Fos expression was reduced by miR‐124‐3p treatment in PST‐exposed mice (p = 0.0373; F 1,37 = 4.686).

Tukey's multiple comparisons revealed that PST‐exposed control mice exhibited higher c‐Fos expression in the PVN and PeVN compared to no‐PST controls (PVN: p = 0.011; PeVN: p = 0.0010), no‐PST miR‐124‐3p‐treated mice (PVN: p = 0.0007; PeVN: p < 0.0001) and PST‐exposed miR‐124‐3p‐treated mice (PVN: p = 0.0184; PeVN: p = 0.0339).

Furthermore, c‐Fos expression in PST‐exposed miR‐124‐3p‐treated mice was significantly reduced compared to PST‐exposed controls (PVN: p = 0.0184; PeVN: p = 0.0339), demonstrating that miR‐124‐3p mimic attenuates the PST‐induced increase in PVN neuronal activity (Figure 5B,C).

In the hippocampus, no significant effects of treatment or PST were observed in the total hippocampus, CA1 or CA3 regions (CA1: p = 0.1867 for PST, p = 0.9988 for miR‐124‐3p, p = 0.7590 for interaction; CA3: p = 0.3510 for PST, p = 0.1499 for miR‐124‐3p, p = 0.5971 for interaction) (Figure 6A,C,D). However, a significant interaction between treatment and PST was found in the dentate gyrus (DG) (p = 0.0248) (Figure 6B). Neither PST alone (p = 0.2182) nor miR‐124‐3p mimic treatment alone (p = 0.0656) significantly altered c‐Fos in the DG, but multiple comparisons showed increased c‐Fos expression in miR‐124‐3p‐treated mice compared to controls (p = 0.0393). No significant differences were observed in other comparisons.

These findings demonstrate that miR‐124‐3p mimic treatment modulates neuronal activity in a region‐ and context‐dependent manner. Specifically, it attenuated PST‐induced activation in stress‐responsive hypothalamic nuclei and enhanced activity in the dentate gyrus, a subregion associated with neurogenesis and stress resilience. These functional alterations complement the observed behavioural and proteomic effects, suggesting that miR‐124‐3p may promote adaptive neural responses to stress via coordinated modulation of immune, metabolic and activity‐dependent processes.

Discussion

miR‐124‐3p Mimic Treatment Promotes Antidepressant‐Like Behaviour and Modulates Anxiety Responses

Our data show that a single intracerebroventricular (ICV) injection of miR‐124‐3p mimic significantly reduces immobility in the PST, indicating a rapid antidepressant‐like effect. These results are consistent with previous studies reporting resilience‐enhancing or antidepressant effects of miR‐124‐3p mimics in chronic stress paradigms (Higuchi et al. 2016; Lou et al. 2019; Zhu et al. 2022). However, the broader literature on miR‐124 reveals mixed behavioural outcomes, including anxiogenic or pro‐depressant effects depending on timing, brain region and experimental model (Gu et al. 2019; Yi et al. 2018; Huang et al. 2019; Wang et al. 2017).

The region‐specific patterns observed here, with downregulation of inflammatory and stress‐related proteins in the hippocampus and hypothalamus but upregulation of plasticity‐related proteins in the prefrontal cortex, help contextualize previously contradictory reports of both depressant‐like and antidepressant‐like actions of miR‐124. Such divergent effects likely reflect timing, regional specificity and the underlying state of stress exposure.

Consistent with this complexity, we observed a modest but significant anxiogenic‐like response in the LDB, particularly when data from two cohorts were pooled. This aligns with reports that chronic selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor (SSRI) treatment can increase anxiety‐like behaviour in non‐stressed mice (Baek et al. 2015) and with evidence showing dissociation between anxiolytic and antidepressant effects in rodent models (Holmes et al. 2003). Such findings imply that the anxiolytic and antidepressant effects of miR‐124‐3p may be functionally distinct and occur on different timescales or pathways, warranting further investigation.

Proteomic Signatures Reveal Brain Region‐Specific Pathways Affected by miR‐124‐3p

To uncover molecular mechanisms underlying these behavioural effects, we conducted proteomic analyses in three key brain regions: hippocampus, hypothalamus and prefrontal cortex. Our results reveal region‐specific regulation of proteins involved in neuroimmune response, energy metabolism and synaptic plasticity—pathways critically implicated in stress and depression (Duman et al. 2016; Miller and Raison 2016).

In the hypothalamus, we observed downregulation of proteins linked to neuroinflammation (e.g., STAT1 and IFIT3), oxidative stress (e.g., MPST) and apoptosis (e.g., EI24), suggesting that miR‐124‐3p mimic reduces neuroimmune activation in this region. This is consistent with prior work demonstrating miR‐124's anti‐inflammatory role in microglia and astrocytes (Ponomarev et al. 2010). Similarly, the hippocampus showed downregulation of immune‐related proteins (e.g., DDX58 and IFITM3) and modulators of neurogenesis (e.g., NAMPT and ACAN), consistent with an anti‐inflammatory and plasticity‐enhancing profile (Schafer et al. 2012; Snyder et al. 2011).

Conversely, the prefrontal cortex exhibited upregulation of proteins associated with cellular resilience (e.g., HTRA2), ion transport (e.g., SLC25A40) and neuroplasticity (e.g., CDH4 and CDK17). These findings align with studies showing that enhanced cortical plasticity supports stress resilience and antidepressant responses (Liston et al. 2006; Pittenger and Duman 2008). This divergence may reflect compensatory cortical mechanisms following hypothalamic and hippocampal suppression or regional differences in miR‐124‐3p targets, as supported by evidence that miR‐124 modulates distinct gene expression patterns across brain regions, contributing to region‐specific cellular responses (Mannironi et al. 2013).

Importantly, Gene Ontology enrichment analysis confirmed that the identified proteins participate in biological processes relevant to depression pathophysiology, including interferon‐beta signalling in the hypothalamus and prefrontal cortex, neuroplastic processes in the hippocampus and protein transport and ubiquitin‐mediated turnover in the cortex. These GO annotations support a model of miR‐124‐3p as a modulator of brain‐wide cellular stress responses.

miR‐124‐3p Modulates Stress‐Responsive Neuronal Activity in the Hypothalamus and Hippocampus

To further elucidate functional neuronal consequences of miR‐124‐3p mimic treatment, we examined c‐Fos expression—an immediate early gene marker of neuronal activity—in the hypothalamus and hippocampus following acute stress in the PST. In this context, the PST was employed as an acute physiological stressor to trigger downstream cellular signalling, complementing its primary role in validating the antidepressant‐like behavioural phenotype observed in both cohorts. Stress increased c‐Fos expression in hypothalamic nuclei involved in HPA‐axis regulation, including the PVN and PeVN. Importantly, miR‐124‐3p treatment suppressed this stress‐induced activation, suggesting attenuation of hypothalamic reactivity potentially via modulation of CRF/CREB signalling pathways (Brenes et al. 2016; Aguilera 2011; Herman et al. 2003).

Notably, miR‐124‐3p‐treated mice exhibited a subtle but significant increase in c‐Fos expression in the dentate gyrus of the hippocampus, independent of stress exposure. Given the dentate gyrus's role in neurogenesis and plasticity (Anacker and Hen 2017), and prior findings that miR‐124 promotes dendritic complexity and spine density under chronic stress (Higuchi et al. 2016; Brenes et al. 2016), these results suggest enhanced basal hippocampal plasticity. However, miR‐124‐3p appeared to have a limited effect on acute stress‐induced hippocampal activation, which may reflect regional specificity or timing constraints.

It is important to note that proteomic profiles reflect baseline molecular states following treatment, whereas c‐Fos represents rapid activity‐dependent responses to acute stress exposure. Therefore, dissociations between proteomic changes in the hippocampus and c‐Fos activation patterns are expected and reflect distinct biological timescales. The increased dentate gyrus activity observed here is nonetheless consistent with plasticity‐related changes revealed in the proteomic analysis.

Toward a Model of miR‐124‐3p‐Mediated Stress Resilience

Together, these behavioural, proteomic and cellular findings indicate that miR‐124‐3p mimic treatment modulates neuroimmune and plasticity‐related processes across stress‐responsive circuits. The dampening of hypothalamic stress‐induced activity alongside enhanced dentate gyrus engagement suggests a coordinated regulation of stress‐related signalling pathways (Duman et al. 2016). Meanwhile, increased dentate gyrus activation and upregulated plasticity‐related proteins in the cortex indicate an enhanced capacity for adaptation and recovery (Pittenger and Duman 2008).

This model aligns with previous evidence showing that miR‐124 targets multiple components of stress signalling pathways (including glucocorticoid receptor (GR), CREB and STAT3 (Gu et al. 2019)) but also provides novel insights into region‐specific proteomic effects and functional consequences at the cellular level. This is the first study integrating proteomic profiling with neuronal activity mapping to elucidate the complex effects of miR‐124‐3p on stress‐response circuitry and antidepressant effects.

Conclusion

This study provides strong evidence that miR‐124‐3p mimic treatment is associated with antidepressant‐like effect in mice, likely by enhancing stress resilience through coordinated neuroimmune suppression and synaptic plasticity. Behaviourally, miR‐124‐3p mimic treatment was associated with the reduction of immobility in the PST, suggesting a shift toward active stress‐coping while producing a modest anxiogenic‐like response under mildly aversive conditions. Proteomic profiling revealed brain region‐specific molecular changes consistent with anti‐inflammatory signalling and cellular resilience. In the hypothalamus and hippocampus, miR‐124‐3p downregulated proteins related to neuroinflammation and oxidative stress, whereas in the prefrontal cortex, proteins linked to synaptic remodelling and metabolism were upregulated. Neuronal activation analysis showed reduced c‐Fos expression in stress‐responsive hypothalamic nuclei and increased activity in the dentate gyrus, pointing to region‐specific modulation of stress‐related circuits. Together, these findings suggest that miR‐124‐3p promotes stress resilience through anti‐inflammatory signalling and synaptic reprogramming across key mood‐regulating brain regions. Future studies should examine the long‐term effects of miR‐124‐3p in chronic stress models and evaluate its therapeutic potential across sexes, species and neuropsychiatric contexts.

Limitations and Future Study

This study focused on the effects of a single intracerebroventricular injection of miR‐124‐3p mimic, assessed within 1 week of treatment. Although this provides insight into its acute actions on stress‐related behaviour and neuronal activation, further work will be required to determine whether similar effects extend to chronic stress paradigms.

Experiments were conducted in female C57BL/6 mice. Although this limits direct generalization across sexes and strains, the choice is justified by the higher prevalence of depression in women, lending translational relevance to female data.

One of the limitations of this current study is the absence of direct quantification of miR‐124‐3p levels after mimic delivery, as fresh tissue was prioritized for proteomic analysis. However, previous studies using the same mimic chemistry and route of administration demonstrate robust and brain‐wide increases in miR‐124 at similar doses and time points. While we utilized a nontargeting scrambled sequence as the primary control for all miRNA administrations, we acknowledge that an additional control with a vehicle‐only (sham) control group could have been included as well. Also, future studies validating direct mRNA targets through functional assays, such as luciferase reporter assays, would provide valuable mechanistic insights into how miR‐124‐3p modulates these pathways.

The proteomic and c‐Fos datasets highlight molecular and neuronal pathways influenced by miR‐124‐3p but necessarily represent a snapshot of complex processes. Future studies using complementary approaches (e.g., cell‐type–specific analyses or multiomic integration) may further clarify underlying mechanisms.

Finally, we acknowledge that PFC c‐Fos quantification could provide additional insights, but it fell outside the defined scope of the current cellular activation study.
