Social Determinants of Cardiovascular Disease Risk Factors Among Black Ethnic Groups: Findings From the 2013 to 2018 National Health Interview Survey
Authors: Doris Osei Afriyie, Marcia Tan, Ruth‐Alma N Turkson‐Ocran, Joyline Chepkorir, Arshiya Baig, Yvonne Commodore‐Mensah, Harold Pollack
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSocial determinants of health (SDOH) affect cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk in Black adults, but there is limited knowledge on how SDOH dimensions are linked to CVD risk factors across different Black groups. The study examined differences in SDOH dimensions and CVD risk factors among Black ethnic groups, hypothesizing stronger associations for US‐born adults.MethodsUsing the National Health Interview Surveys 2013 to 2018, we categorized 24 380 non‐Hispanic Black adults by birthplace into 3 groups: US born, Caribbean born, and African born. Thirteen SDOH variables were reduced to 4 indices (standard of living, neighborhood cohesion, socioeconomic advantage, and health care access) using principal component analysis. Associations between these indices and the prevalence of 3 self‐reported CVD risk factors (hypertension, diabetes, and high cholesterol) were assessed using modified Poisson regression models.ResultsAmong US‐born Black adults, all SDOH indices were significantly associated with hypertension, diabetes, and high cholesterol (P<0.05), except for low standard of living and poor neighborhood cohesion with high cholesterol. For Caribbean‐born Black adults, a low standard of living was associated with a higher prevalence of hypertension (prevalence ratio, 1.10 [95% CI, 1.1–1.21]) and diabetes (prevalence ratio, 1.24 [95% CI, 1.11–1.38]). Among African‐born Black adults, only socioeconomic disadvantage was significantly associated with hypertension (prevalence ratio, 1.15 [95% CI, 1.05–1.26]).ConclusionsThe relationship between CVD risk factors and SDOH indices varied across different Black groups and was not consistent for all indices and risk factors. These distinct associations underscore the importance of tailored interventions to address context‐specific CVD risk factors and social determinants to protect cardiovascular health.Keywords: cardiovascular disease risk factors, diabetes, ethnicity, hypertension, immigrationSubject Categories: Epidemiology, Social Determinants of Health

Black adults in the United States are disproportionately affected by cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk factors, such as hypertension, diabetes, and hypercholesterolemia, compared with White adults. 1 These disparities have been linked to structural, institutional, and environmental factors, including the broader social and physical environment in which they live, encompassing their neighborhood conditions, built environment, economic stability, food access, and community context.

Importantly, Black individuals in the United States are not a homogeneous group. 2 The Black population is becoming increasingly diverse, driven by a rise in foreign‐born individuals. Since 1990, the percentage of foreign‐born individuals has tripled, reaching an estimated 4.6 million in 2019 with projections suggesting 16% of the Black population will be foreign born by 2060. 3 In 2019, the top 6 countries of birth for Black immigrants were Jamaica, Haiti, Nigeria, Ethiopia, the Dominican Republic, and Ghana. 4

Despite this growing heterogeneity, few studies have examined the social determinants of CVD risk factors across these diverse groups. Using pooled nationally representative data from 2003 to 2014, Brown et al. reported that nearly half (42.8%) of US‐born Black adults had hypertension compared with 27.4% of foreign‐born Black adults. 5 Even after adjusting for sociodemographic characteristics, such as education level, health insurance status, and behavioral risk factors such as smoking and obesity, foreign‐born Black adults remained less likely to have hypertension than US‐born Black adults. Similarly, Baptiste et al, using the NHIS (National Health Interview Survey) data, found the highest age‐and sex‐adjusted prevalence of hypertension (41.9%) among US‐born Black adults compared with African immigrant adults (16.5%). 6 Other NHIS studies have also found that African immigrant adults have a lower risk of hypertension than US‐born Black adults. 7

Several studies have examined the burden of diabetes across Black ethnic groups. They found that foreign‐born Black adults have a significantly lower prevalence rate of diabetes compared with US‐born Black adults. 8 , 9 , 10 However, after adjusting for factors such as body mass (BMI) and access to medical care, this foreign‐born advantage in diabetes was no longer significant. 8 , 10 Some studies have also assessed whether acculturation, measured by the number of years living in the United States, influences diabetes disparities between US‐born and African‐born Black adults. Findings indicate that although prevalence of CVD risk increases with longer US residence among African‐born Black adults, they still have a lower prevalence than US‐born Black adults. 6 , 11

Few studies have examined whether observed differences in hypertension between US‐born and foreign‐born Black adults can be attributed to structural factors. Brown et al. hypothesized that these differences may be explained by variations in lifestyles, exposure to racism, chronic stress, and mental health distress, with US‐born Black adults experiencing higher stressors compared with foreign‐born Black adults. 5 However, there remains limited research comparing structural factors and hypertension and other CVD risk factors across ethnic groups, partly due to challenges in measuring them and limited nativity data in national surveys. One study using nationally representative data did examine CVD risk factors and a few social determinants of health (SDOH) factors, including income, education, and health insurance coverage among Black subgroups. 12 The high correlation among these social variables may limit the interpretation of the findings and their relevance for informing policy decisions. 13 Additionally, including other social needs such as food security and neighborhood factors may be necessary to fully understand the complexity of SDOH on health outcomes. 13 , 14

To address these gaps, this study aimed to construct uncorrelated SDOH indices and examine their association with CVD risk factors among Black subgroup adults. The research questions were: (1) Are there differences in the SDOH indices among US‐born Black adults, Caribbean‐born Black adults, and African‐born Black adults? (2) What is the association between SDOH indices and CVD risk factors for the total sample of Black adults and within Black subgroups?

METHODS

Data Source and Study Population

We used data from the NHIS from 2013 to 2018. The NHIS uses a cross‐sectional, cluster sampling design to obtain a nationally representative sample of civilian noninstitutionalized US adults. 15 We selected these specific years as they include comprehensive questions on multiple domains of SDOH. The NHIS, administered by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS), was selected as one of the few national representative data sets that provide detailed information on nativity, including region of birth for foreign‐born participants. The NHIS data used for this study are publicly available on the NCHS website. 15 Statistical coding for the analysis will be made available by the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

The Ethics Review Board of the NCHS reviews the NHIS survey questions and interview procedures. This study was exempted from institutional review board review because it used public deidentified files released by the NCHS.

Participants

The study focused on adults aged ≥18 years who self‐identified as Non‐Hispanic Black adults. Within this category, individuals who also reported being born in the United States were considered as US‐born Black adults. Those who reported being born in Africa were considered African‐born Black adults, whereas those born in Mexico, the Caribbean islands, or South America were considered Caribbean‐born Black adults. Individuals born in Europe, elsewhere, or had missing variables for birthplace were excluded.

Social Determinants of Health

We adopted the SDOH framework from the Healthy People 2030, developed by the US Department of Health and Human Services. 16 This framework emphasizes the impact of environmental conditions on health, health outcomes, and quality of life, considering the places where they are born, live, work, and play. The framework has 5 distinct domains, including economic stability, neighborhood and built environment, education access and quality, social and community context, and health care access and quality. The framework has been applied to illustrate how these structural barriers increase the burden of social determinants of health for underserved populations, including Black and Asian American people, thereby heightening their risk of CVD. 17

Using the SDOH framework, we selected 13 questions from the NHIS to represent the key domains, including economic stability, neighborhood and community context, education, and health care access and quality (Table S1). Specifically for the domain of economic stability, we focused on measures such as income, poverty, unemployment, and food insecurity. We incorporated questions that evaluated aspects of both neighborhood and community context, including neighborhood accountability, trust, and sense of closeness. Indicators related to education access and quality comprised measures of education and English proficiency. To serve as a proxy for health care access and quality, we used health insurance status and having a usual place for care.

Cardiovascular Disease Risk Factors

The outcomes of interest are self‐reported hypertension, diabetes, and high cholesterol. Hypertension, diabetes, and hypercholesterolemia were defined as respondents' response to the binary question, “Have you ever been told by a doctor or other health professional that you had “hypertension also called high blood pressure” or “diabetes or sugar diabetes” or high cholesterol?”

Covariates

The covariates included were age in years (continuous), sex (female/male), smoking status (current/former/never), alcohol use (lifetime abstainer/former drinker/current drinker), BMI (underweight/healthy weight/overweight/obese), and sufficiency of aerobic activity, which served as a proxy for physical activity. 18 The BMI categories were constructed based on the World Health Organization's guidelines for adults. 19 Individuals with a BMI <18.5 kg/m2 were classified as underweight. Those with BMI ranging from 18.5 to 24.99 kg/m2 were considered as healthy weight. A BMI between 25 and 29.99 kg/m2 was categorized as overweight, and a BMI ≥30 kg/m2 was classified as obese. Sufficiency of aerobic activity was classified using the 2008 Physical Activity Guidelines, which uses duration and frequency of leisure‐time physical activity. 20 Among the foreign‐born population, we included length of stay in the United States (<10 years/≥10 years) as a covariate.

Statistical Analysis

Weighted descriptive statistics were calculated to describe the demographics and SDOH factors by race and ethnicity. The weights were derived from NHIS sampling weights and adjusted for the pooling of survey years 2013 to 2018, in accordance with NCHS guidance. 21 Differences in demographics and SDOH factors among the 3 Black ethnic groups were examined using 1‐way ANOVA for continuous variables and chi‐square tests for dichotomized variables. Next, to examine the association between SDOH and CVD risk factors for the 3 ethnic groups, we tested a sequence of multivariable modified Poisson regression models.

First, to reduce the chance of bias from missing data for the 13 variables, multiple imputation by chained equations with 10 imputations were used to have complete data. Data were assumed to be missing at random. Covariates included in the imputation models were sex, marital status, diabetes status, physical activity level, smoking status, hypertension status, and cholesterol status. The imputed data were used to construct SDOH indices using principal component analysis. Principal component analysis reduces multiple variables to a set of uncorrelated linear combinations of variables with the greatest variance called principal components. We then applied the Kaiser criterion to retain principal components with eigenvalues greater than 1.0. Following alignment with the literature, we selected the dominant variables within each component based on the direction of relationship and variable loadings exceeding 0.3.

Second, we used multivariable modified Poisson regression to examine the association between each individual SDOH index and CVD risk factors for Non‐Hispanic Black adults and investigated interactions between birthplace and SDOH. Additionally, we assessed these associations separately for the 3 Black subgroups. We developed 2 models for multivariable analysis. Model 1 was adjusted for sex and age. In model 2, we adjusted for sex, age, physical activity, smoking, alcohol, BMI, and comorbidities, and among foreign‐born adults, length of residence in the United States. All continuous variables were standardized to a mean of 0 and an SD of 1. The multiple imputations using chained equations and principal component analysis were performed using R studio. All other analyses were performed using STATA version 16.0.

In our sample of 24 380 Non‐Hispanic Black adults, approximately one‐tenth (10.1% unweighted and 12.5% weighted frequency) were Caribbean or African born. There were significant differences among the 3 Black subgroups. African‐born adults were the youngest, least likely to be female, and had lived fewer years in the United States (Table 1). The age‐standardized prevalence of each CVD risk factor stratified by Black subgroups is shown in Figure S1. US‐born Black adults reported the highest prevalence across all 3 CVD risk factors compared with Caribbean‐born and African‐born Black adults: hypertension (41.9% [95% CI, 41.0%–42.7%]), diabetes (13.6% [95% CI, 13.0%–14.2%]) and high cholesterol (24.9% [95% CI, 24.1%–25.7%]). African‐born Black adults had the lowest prevalence across all 3 CVD risk factors: hypertension (28.6% [95% CI, 26.1%–31.0%]), diabetes (8.9% [95% CI, 6.62%–11.2%]), and high cholesterol (19.0% [95% CI, 14.8% –23.2%]).

Principal Component Analysis

Four principal components met the Kaiser criterion for inclusion: low standard of living index, poor neighborhood cohesion index, the socioeconomic disadvantage index, and limited health access index. The 4 components accounted for 69% of the variance in the 13 variables. The first principal component, labeled as low standard of living index, accounted for 29% and included 9 variables—income group, poverty ratio, poor neighborhood social cohesion, and food security variables as shown in Tables S3 and S4. The second principal component, poor neighborhood social cohesion index, explained 18% of the variance and was dominated by the neighborhood cohesion variables. The third component, the socioeconomic advantage index, which accounted for 12% of the variance, was highly correlated with family income, family poverty, English language proficiency, and education. The final component, limited health care access, explained 8% and was associated with no health insurance coverage and having no usual place of care.

Association Between SDOH and CVD Risk Factors

Table 3 shows the results of the modified Poisson regression analysis examining the relationship between SDOH dimensions and CVD risk factors, adjusted for sex and age. In both the overall Black adult population and US‐born adults, all SDOH dimensions were significantly associated with the 3 CVD risk factors, though the directions of these relationships varied. For US‐born Black adults, a low standard of living was associated with the prevalence of hypertension (prevalence ratio, 1.10 [95% CI, 1.08–1.12]), whereas limited health care access was associated with a lower prevalence of hypertension (prevalence ratio, 0.0.89 [95% CI, 0.87–0.92]). Among Caribbean‐born adults, a low standard of living was the only dimension significantly associated with hypertension and diabetes, and limited health access was significantly associated with high cholesterol. For African‐born adults, socioeconomic disadvantage was the only dimension associated with hypertension, and a low standard of living was associated with diabetes.

Sensitivity Analysis

We constructed a composite SDOH index using the weighted cumulative principal component scores (Table S6). For the overall Black population, only hypertension was associated with the SDOH index. Among US‐born and Caribbean‐born adults, hypertension and diabetes were associated with the SDOH index. For African‐born adults, only diabetes was associated with the SDOH index.

We used complete cases for the principal component analysis, which resulted in a slight decrease in significant associations for the overall Black population and US‐born subgroup (Table S7). Among the African‐born adults, there were no changes whereas in the Caribbean‐born adults, the relationship between diabetes and SDOH indices were stronger.

DISCUSSION

This study examined the differences in SDOH and CVD risk factors among Black ethnic subgroups (US‐born, Caribbean‐born, and African‐born adults). We found significant heterogeneity in SDOH across these ethnic groups. US‐born Black adults were disproportionately affected by economic instability, as reflected by the lowest family income, poverty status, employment, and food security. In contrast, Caribbean‐born adults reported the lowest levels of neighborhood social cohesion factors and African‐born adults had the greatest barriers to health care access with the lowest rates of health insurance coverage and access to a usual place of care. Our multiregression analysis showed that the relationship between SDOH dimensions and CVD risk factors varied by ethnicity group. US‐born Black adults had the strongest associations between adverse SDOH factors and CVD risk factors even after adjusting for biological and behavioral risk factors and comorbidities.

SDOH disparities influence health outcomes through complex mechanisms. Research has shown that SDOH factors are linked with CVD risk factors, including hypertension, diabetes, and high cholesterol. 17 , 22 , 23 By adopting the SDOH framework, we were able to understand the complexities of factors, such as health care access and neighborhood and economic stability factors and their linkage to CVD risk factors simultaneously. Among the overall Black population, we found significant associations between the 4 SDOH indices and the CVD risk factors except for cholesterol and 2 SDOH indices (low standard of living index and low neighborhood cohesion). The first 3 principal components (low standard of living, poor neighborhood social cohesion, and socioeconomic disadvantage) were negatively associated with higher prevalence of CVD risk factors. In contrast, limited health care access was positively associated with CVD risk factors. Our findings support the approach to expanding SDOH beyond socioeconomic factors. 24 Excluding factors such as neighborhood social cohesion characteristics and food security may have obscured the complexity by which SDOH is linked to CVD risk factors. Furthermore, relying solely on the first principal component or the cumulative index may not have revealed the inconsistencies in the direction of these relationships.

We found differences in the associations between SDOH dimensions and CVD risk factors. This finding is consistent with studies that reported differences in the social determinants of health among Black ethnic subgroups. 12 These results support the argument that SDOH factors affect Black subgroups in distinct ways, underscoring the importance of disaggregating data. 25 , 26 Our analysis showed that US‐born Black adults had the strongest associations for SDOH and CVD risk factors compared with the Black immigrant populations. Prior studies have consistently demonstrated significant associations between socioeconomic factors and cardiovascular risk among US‐born Black adults. For instance, the Jackson Heart Study reported that adult socioeconomic position, measured by income, education, wealth, and public assistance, was consistently associated with CVD events in Black American women and in younger Black American individuals. 27 In the same cohort, investigators reported that an SD increase in neighborhood disadvantage was associated with a 25% increase in the risk of CVD. 28 These findings among US‐born Black adults are not unexpected given that they have lived in the United States for the longest and have greater exposure to structural racism and economic inequalities compared with the immigrant populations. Cross‐national studies have reported that social factors impose a greater burden on health outcomes in the United States, possibly due to lower investments in social services. 29 , 30 , 31 Further research is necessary to determine whether similar patterns are observed for other native‐born racial groups.

For Caribbean‐born Black adults, we found few associations between the SDOH indices and CVD risk factors. The low standard of living index was associated with hypertension and diabetes, whereas limited health access was associated with high cholesterol. These findings differ from previous studies that reported no significant associations for diabetes and SDOH factors, such as income and education. 26 , 32 These contrary findings may be attributable to differences in the SDOH variables that were used. These studies selected individual SDOH variables as their independent measures, whereas we combined these indicators and others to uncorrelated variables (principal components). Alternatively, the inconsistent finding might be explained by differences in the study populations' length of US residency and age group.

Among African‐born Black adults, we found less significant associations between SDOH indices and CVD risk factors. These findings are consistent with studies from the African region that have reported null associations between socioeconomic indicators and CVD risk factors. 33 This trend is part of an epidemiological transition occurring in the African region marked by an increasing burden of noncommunicable diseases, including diabetes. 34 Rapid urbanization, increasing longevity, and globalization are the key factors driving this shift. 35 Studies have indicated that within the Africa region, noncommunicable diseases are more concentrated among higher socioeconomic groups and urban populations. 36 Unlike high‐income countries, obesity and overweight are concentrated among those in affluent groups than the poor. 37 This pattern suggests the immigrant health advantage for African populations may be less pronounced, as those who migrate could be at a higher risk of developing noncommunicable diseases compared with those who remained in their home countries. A Ghanaian study confirmed this hypothesis by showing that those living in Europe were more likely to be educated but more obese and had diabetes prevalence 3 to 5 times higher compared with Ghanaians living in rural areas. 38

Limitations and Strengths

Our findings must be interpreted in light of the following study limitations. First, we used multiple imputation to address missing data before conducting the principal component analysis. As a result, some values were statistically estimated rather than directly observed, which may introduce bias or uncertainty and could affect the accuracy of the principal components generated. Second, because we used cross‐sectional data, we could not examine life SDOH characteristics and their link to the development of CVD risk factors. This is crucial as these associations might be bidirectional, as having CVD risk could also affect SDOH characteristics such as economic and neighborhood conditions. Second, we used self‐reported measures for both SDOH and CVD risk factors, which could have resulted in social desirability bias or underreporting of CVD risk factors, potentially underestimating the strength between SDOH and CVD risk factors among these groups. Third, these Black ethnic subgroups analyzed in this study are not homogenous, and the results may not be generalizable to all subgroups. For instance, within the US‐born Black population, there is a growing number of second‐ and third‐generation Black immigrants who may differ from descendants of enslaved people. 39 , 40 Among the foreign‐born population, information on country of origin was not publicly available, limiting our ability to examine differences based on this factor in our analysis. Lastly, we were unable to account for all potential confounding or moderating factors, such as region, occupation, wealth, and culture because these variables were not publicly available or not collected in the NHIS.

Despite these limitations, our study had several noteworthy strengths. First, it is the one of the largest studies to assess SDOH and CVD risk factors for Black ethnic subgroups. We measured SDOH using various dimensions while accounting for the correlations between SDOH factors using principal component analysis. In contrast, previous studies had used composite SDOH scores, which assume each variable contributes equally to the overall SDOH burden.

CONCLUSIONS

We found that US‐born, Caribbean‐born, and African‐born Black adults were affected by SDOH in distinct ways. US‐born adults had the strongest associations between SDOH indices and CVD risk factors with African‐born adults having the weakest associations. Our findings further highlight the importance of considering ethnicity and cultural factors in efforts to reduce CVD inequalities related to SDOH disparities among Black adults.
