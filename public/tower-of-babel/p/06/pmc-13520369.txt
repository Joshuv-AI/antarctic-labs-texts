Population-based characterisation of child and adolescent oral bacterial microbiomes
Authors: Robyn J. Wright, Vanessa DeClercq, Christie L. Burton, Nicole M. Roslin, Alex W. Y. Chan, Paul D. Arnold, Paul Peters, Russell Schachar, Jennifer Crosbie, Morgan G. I. Langille
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe factors influencing the oral microbiome during childhood and adolescence remain under-explored at the population level. Furthermore, details on how the oral microbiome differs with age, varies between individuals of different ethnicities, or is associated with socioeconomic factors and diet in children and adolescents are almost entirely unknown. Saliva samples and detailed demographic, health, diet and socioeconomic data were collected from children and adolescents that attended the Ontario Science Centre (Toronto, Canada) and were enrolled in the Spit for Science cohort. We characterised the bacterial microbiota of 4812 samples using 16S rRNA gene sequencing to make this the largest population cohort of the paediatric oral microbiome to date.ResultsExploration of limited participant genotyping information and more than 50 variables encompassing the demographics, health, diet, socioeconomic status and living environment of participants revealed that almost all of the investigated variables were associated with overall community structure and/or the abundance of specific bacterial genera. However, most of these associations were modest (R2 < 0.01) and the correlation between genetic relatedness and salivary bacteriome similarity was weak (R2 = -0.014), while the strongest determinants of oral bacteriome composition were shared family/household environment (R2 = 0.61 in the subset of participants from multi-child households), age (R2 = 0.014) and ethnicity (R2 = 0.01). We show that older children and adolescents have higher richness but lower evenness than younger children, suggesting that their oral bacteriome changes as they are exposed to more influences outside the home, and that the oral bacteriome is more consistent with more core taxa among children and adolescents than adults. We also find that diet variables related to the frequency of sugar consumption have the largest impact on the oral bacteriome of children and adolescents, and that microbial differences attributed to ethnicity and diet are likely intertwined.ConclusionsThis study provides an atlas of the demographic, health and lifestyle factors that are associated with the salivary bacteriome of children and adolescents. These findings highlight the complex interplay between social, environmental, and biological factors in shaping the developing oral microbiome and underscore the importance of inclusive, demographically diverse cohorts in microbiome research. This presents a reference for the variables that are important to account for in paediatric oral microbiome studies. Background

The human microbiome refers to the community of bacteria, archaea, fungi, protists and viruses that live on or inside the human body [1]. To date, most human microbiome research has focused on the gut microbiome, but the oral microbiome is one of the most diverse habitats within the human body [2]. The oral microbiota is comprised of bacteria, archaea, microeukaryotes (predominantly fungi) and viruses [3, 4]; few studies have aimed to characterise the oral microbiota outside of the bacteria, but fungi and viruses are both thought to compose less than 0.1% of the oral microbiome in terms of CFU or nucleic acids, respectively [4], while archaea have been found to account for ~ 0.2% of metatranscriptome reads [5]. Although eukaryotic pathogenic protozoa have been found to contribute up to ~ 5% metatranscriptome reads in oral diseases, they are typically much less abundant [5] and the bacteria are numerically dominant with over 800 bacterial species known to inhabit the oral cavity (eHOMD v16.02) [6].

Different locations within the oral cavity, such as the teeth (supra- and sub-gingival plaques), cheek (buccal mucosa) and tongue, harbour distinct microbiomes, with some of the variability between locations thought to be influenced by the availability of oxygen, nutrients, salivary gland secretion and substrate characteristics [3]. While most oral microbial species exhibit strong spatial organisation and are specialised for a particular niche, they are typically also detected at other sites due to sampling limitations and because microbes from all oral sites are shed into saliva. Sites within the oral cavity can be broadly grouped by compositional similarity to (i) buccal mucosa, keratinised gingiva and hard palate; (ii) saliva, tongue, tonsils and throat; and (iii) sub- and supra-gingival plaque. The largest differences in relative abundance between these groups are in Firmicutes and Actinobacteria (now Actinomycetota), with Firmicutes being most abundant in (i) and least abundant in (iii) while Actinobacteria show the opposite trend; group (ii) sites are intermediate for both [7]. When saliva is secreted into the oral cavity, it is sterile [8]; its intermediate microbial composition within the oral cavity taken together with estimations that ~ 50% of the microbes found in saliva come from the tongue dorsum and ~ 20% from dental plaque [9] and its relative ease of sampling [7, 10] mean that it is frequently used for population-based sampling [7, 11, 12].

The oral microbiome exhibits a relatively high degree of specificity and temporal stability within individuals [13] and its composition is associated with age and sex or gender as well as other factors such as geographic location, smoking status, alcohol consumption, diet and other lifestyle and behavioural patterns [8, 11]. Poor oral hygiene practices—namely a lack of frequent brushing and flossing—have a strong impact on the oral microbiome and lead to an accumulation of dental plaque [14], which in turn leads to higher microbial richness [15] and can be conducive to the development of oral diseases such as dental caries, gingivitis, periodontitis, halitosis and oral thrush [14]. Although earlier research suggested that microbial taxa such as Streptococcus mutans and Lactobacillus were primarily responsible for diseases such as dental caries, it is now generally agreed that they are caused by a disruption to the healthy microbiome (often termed “dysbiosis”) [5, 16]. The oral microbiome is also associated with other systemic diseases or disorders like inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) [17], cancer [3, 18] or autism [19]. Identifying biomarkers within the oral microbiome is dependent upon the availability of comprehensive knowledge of the factors that impact the overall composition of the oral microbiome and population-level characterisations are therefore necessary [11].

The microbiome changes throughout life, with the gut microbiome thought to resemble that of adults by approximately age 3–6 years [1]. A longitudinal study of the oral microbiome in children aged between 3 months and 7 years of age identified increases in both bacterial load and diversity with increasing age, but these changes continue past 7 years of age [20]. The emergence of permanent teeth as well as hormonal shifts during puberty are both thought to influence the oral microbiome within children and adolescents [21]. While there is an emerging literature examining the oral microbiome in children and youth (recently reviewed in AlHarbi et al. [21]), few studies include large cohorts and these focus on relatively small age ranges [22–26]; different oral microbiome niches for different age groups [27]; a specific disorder or disease [19, 23]; or antimicrobial drug use [28], limiting their applicability to the general population.

Here, rather than testing a specific hypothesis, we aimed to investigate the composition and correlates of the oral microbiome in children and adolescents and provide an atlas of demographic and lifestyle factors that influence it. To do this, we collected saliva samples from 4812 children and adolescents when they attended the Ontario Science Centre; together, these form the Spit for Science oral microbiome cohort. To our knowledge, this is the largest population study of the oral microbiome in children and adolescents to date.

Methods

Study population details and data collection

Participants were recruited at the Ontario Science Centre (Toronto, Canada) between 2019 and 2024 for the Spit for Science 2 study. Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Hospital for Sick Children research ethics board (approval number 1000062807). Participants were assigned a family ID (hereafter called “household”) based on their reporting of either (1) living together and/or (2) being biologically related at the time of recruitment. The household variable is separate from the genotyping of participants and assessments of genetic relatedness described below. Study recruitment and cohort characteristics have been previously described [29]. Briefly, the cohort included 22,043 participants aged 5–18 years that gave saliva samples and answered questionnaires giving information on demographics, behaviour, cognition, physical health and well-being. For participants younger than 12 years, a parent or legal guardian provided written informed consent and data about the participant and the child/adolescent provided assent. Participants 12 years and older had the option to consent for themselves if they were deemed to have sufficient capacity to provide informed consent and provide self-report data; otherwise, consent and data were provided from a parent or legal guardian. This study included participants who provided information on age, gender, ethnicity, diet, diagnosed health conditions and geographical location (Postal Code). All participants provided saliva samples that were collected in an OG-600 saliva collection kit (DNA Genotek, Ottawa, Ontario). As participants were recruited while attending the Ontario Science Centre, sampling time and fasting status were not standardised between participants and oral health and hygiene information was not collected. Samples from participants of reported White, East or South Asian ethnicity were prioritized for DNA extraction for human genomics work. DNA was extracted using the ChemagicSTAR automation system with the saliva protocol. The DNA was eluted in 200 ml of DNA Hydration Solution (TE) and quantified using Biotek FLx800 Multi Detection Microplate Fluorometer with Hoechst dye.

Sociodemographic characteristics

Participants/parents provided the age (in years) of the participants at time of completing the questionnaire. Participants/parents that answered “Prefer not to disclose” or “Unknown/don’t know” to a question about their gender were excluded from this study. Transgender female, non-binary and demigirl were all grouped to “trans/gender-diverse” due to the small sample sizes. Participants/parents were asked the ethnic origins of biological grandparents and participants with all four grandparents that were White, East or South Asian were included in this study. Participants’ current, school, and in-utero postal codes were used to link participants to small geographic units (Dissemination Areas) for which several indices have been calculated: (i) Material and Social Deprivation Indices (MSDI) cover ~ 98% of the Canadian population using six indicators taken from the Canadian census. Pampalon et al. [30] found that ~ 67% of the variation could be explained by two principal components, which were primarily associated with social deprivation (33%) or material deprivation (34%), respectively. The material deprivation principal component is mainly associated with the proportion of the population aged 15 + with no high school diploma (SCOLAR), the employment/population ratio of people aged 15 + (EMPLOI) and the average income of people aged 15 + (REVENU), while the social deprivation principal component is mainly associated with the proportion of individuals aged 15 + living along (SEULES), the proportion of individuals aged 15 + who are separated, divorced or widowed (S_D_V) and the proportion of single parent families (F_MONO) [30]; (ii) an Index of Rurality calculated from the Statistics Canada Index of Remoteness [31]; (iii) Greenspace as a growing season mean of means calculated from the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NVDI) at the postal code point [32–34]; (iv) Fine Particulate Matter (PM2.5 in µg m−3) as an annual average at the postal code point from the global estimates in van Donkelaar et al. [35, 36]; and (v) Ontario marginalisation (ON-Marg) indices for household & dwellings, material resources, age & labour and racialised & newcomer populations. The ON-Marg combines a range of demographic indicators including economic, ethno-racial, age-based and social marginalisation variables and uses 2021 Canadian Census data [37]. Postal codes were also used to identify participants who lived in the Greater Toronto Area and participants within the same household that did not live at the same address; if participants within a household ID had the same postal code, then we assumed that they lived together.

Diagnosed health conditions

Parent- and self-reported health conditions were grouped to whether participants indicated that they had received a diagnosis and/or treatment for a physical health condition, a psychiatric/neurodevelopmental disorder, both or neither. Participants were asked specifically whether they had a diagnosis for 12 physical health conditions (allergies, asthma, autoimmune disorders, cerebral palsy, epilepsy, genetic disorders, migraines, visual impairments [not slight vision loss requiring corrective lenses], hearing impairments, mobility impairments, head injuries and foetal alcohol spectrum disorder) and 14 psychiatric/neurodevelopmental disorders (anxiety, Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder [ADHD], autism, bipolar/manic depression, depression, intellectual disability, learning disability, Obsessive–Compulsive Disorder [OCD], Oppositional Defiant Disorder [ODD], schizophrenia, Social (Pragmatic) Communication Disorder [SPCD], Substance Use Disorder, Tics/Tourette’s and eating disorders), or to give details on any other diagnoses given. Participants who did not indicate having any diagnosed health conditions were assumed to have none.

Diet

A modified dietary screener was used to capture information on the frequency of consumption of 24 food and beverage groups over the past month (Table S1). The modified screener was based on a National Cancer Institute (NCI) dietary screener that was used with children and adults in the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) [38, 39]. Possible answers for each of the questions were never, one time in the last month, 2–3 times last month, one time per week, two times per week, 3–4 times per week, 5–6 times per week, one time per day, two or more times per day. For the current study, data was treated as ordinal data in which the categories were ordered and ranked, where 0 or “none” represents the lowest level of consumption, and 8 or “two or more times per day” represents the highest level of consumption. A frequency distribution table was created to inform how many times each response was selected and was the frequency distribution was visualized on a bar graph showing near symmetry and close to normal distribution. Of participants that were selected for microbiome profiling, 3523 had answered some part of the diet questionnaire. Complete diet data was necessary for some downstream microbial analyses thus, participants missing data for diet questions 4–23 (see Table S1) or with the same response for all questions were removed. Additionally, outliers were determined based on Z-scores (> 3 or < − 3), and those that reported the same response to all questions were also removed from diet analyses (~ 1% of all participants with diet data). This resulted in 2978 participants with complete diet data for downstream microbial analyses. A Healthy Eating Food Index and diet variety scores were then calculated on the remaining participants. Note that the participants that were removed from the diet analyses were not excluded from this study entirely. Correlations between all diet variables were assessed using Pearson’s and Spearman’s correlation and diet variable multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs), as in Wu et al. [40]. Correlations with R2 values ≥ 0.5 were between (i) questions 13 and 14 (fruit and vegetable consumption; Pearson’s R2 = 0.58, Spearman’s R2 = 0.66); (ii) questions 16 and 17 (red meat and processed meat consumption; Pearson’s R2 = 0.51); (iii) questions 16 and 19 (red meat and poultry consumption; Pearson’s R2 = 0.50); (iv) questions 22 and 23 (desserts/sweets and salty snack consumption; Pearson’s R2 = 0.53, Spearman’s R2 = 0.53). VIFs ranged between 1.10 (question 12, coffee or tea consumption) and 1.81 (question 16, red meat consumption), indicating low to moderate collinearity between the answers to the diet variable questionnaire. Correlations and VIFs are shown in Table S2A. We therefore chose to keep all diet variables in our analyses.

The Healthy Eating Food Index included questions DSQ4-8, 10–11 and 13–23, and was based on the screener scoring regime of Hutchinson et al. [41] and the original Healthy Eating Food Index-2019 scoring algorithm proposed by Brassard et al. [42]. Each question was scored ordinally, on a scale of 0 to 8, with responses to questions that capture healthy food scored positively and foods to limit reverse-scored (Table S2C). Separate questions of similar food types were summed to create food components that reflected intake of total fruits and vegetables, whole grains, total protein foods, foods and beverages high in sugars, foods high in sodium/saturated fat and the ratios of whole grains to refined grains and plant-based proteins to animal-based proteins. In total, 7 components were created to reflect Canada’s Food Guide (CFG) healthy eating recommendations [43] and when summed resulted in a maximum score of 60 points. Diet variety scores were calculated by counting the number of different food items (DSQ4-8, 10–11 and 13–23) that participants indicated that they consumed at least once per day, week, or month, for the daily, weekly or monthly diet variety scores, respectively.

Human genetic analysis

Genotyping was performed on the GlobalScreeningArray-24v3-0_A array on 6,767 Spit for Science 2 participants. All samples were genotyped at The Centre for Applied Genomics (TCAG) in Toronto, Canada. Standard genotyping quality control (QC) was performed as previously described [44]. Individuals who had both genotyping and microbiome data and were included in the current study: 4287 participants, with 931 of East Asian ancestry, 2760 of European ancestry and 596 of South Asian ancestry. Kinship coefficients were estimated between all pairs of samples who had microbiome data and passed genotype QC using KING 2.2.6 [45] (n = 4281). Coefficients were analysed as quantitative values, and classified into relationship types (monozygotic twins, full siblings, half siblings, first cousins, unrelated) using the cutoffs suggested by the software.

Imputation was performed separately in each ancestry using the TOPMed imputation server (https://imputation.biodatacatalyst.nhlbi.nih.gov/#!) and the TOPMed version R3 reference panel. Variants with imputation quality R2 < 0.8 within each ancestry were removed. We searched for the 11 candidate loci among these high-quality variants, matching on chromosome and physical position (human reference build hg38), and found four in the East Asian ancestry samples, and seven in the samples of European and South Asian ancestries. Genotypes were converted to missing values if the maximum posterior probability was < 0.9, and alternate allele dosages were extracted to be used in statistical analyses.

Microbiome amplicon sequencing

16S rRNA gene sequencing of the saliva microbiome was conducted on 5323 Spit for Science 2 participants at the Integrated Microbiome Resource at Dalhousie University. Briefly, extracted DNA underwent PCR amplification of the V4–V5 16S rRNA gene region (515FB/926R primers) and was sequenced using an Illumina MiSeq (2 × 300 bp) by the Integrated Microbiome Resource at Dalhousie University.

Microbiome bioinformatic analysis

Initial sample processing was carried out using QIIME2 (version 2024.5) [46] following the Microbiome Helper Amplicon Standard Operating Procedure v2 [47]. Primers were trimmed using Cutadapt [48] and paired-end reads were joined using VSEARCH [49]. Reads were filtered using the default parameters within the QIIME2 plugin quality-filter using the q-score method and reads were denoised to Amplicon Sequence Variants (ASVs) using Deblur [50] and were trimmed to a length of 369 bp. Resulting ASVs were classified using a naïve bayes scikit-learn classifier [51] trained on the Human Oral Microbiome Database (HOMD) v15.23 [6]. ASVs present at a total abundance of below 0.1% of the mean sample depth (total of 18 reads across all samples, based on Illumina MiSeq potential between-run bleed-through of sequences) were removed along with ASVs that were unclassified at the phylum level. A phylogenetic tree containing all remaining ASVs was generated by inserting sequences into the Greengenes 2 [52] backbone using SEPP [53]. The median classification confidence for the lowest available taxonomic classification across all remaining ASVs is 0.999 (mean 0.952); range 0.700 (the minimum suggested for use with QIIME 2’s naïve bayes feature classifier [54]) to 1.000. Of the 7705 ASVs, 220 (2.86%) do not have a genus-level classification (Table S3). We investigated the depth at which ASV richness in each sample had plateaued (reached 97% of the ASV richness when rarefied to 95% of the read depth). At a read depth of 2000, 99.8% of samples had plateaued and samples with below 2000 sequences were therefore removed from further analysis. Samples from participants without information on their age, gender, ethnicity and physical health condition or psychiatric/neurodevelopmental disorder diagnoses were also removed from further analysis. The sparsity rate (proportion of zero counts) of the ASV table was 98.3%. Alpha diversity was calculated by repeatedly rarefying (n = 100 replicates) samples to 2000 reads (as suggested by Schloss [55]) within QIIME2 and calculation of the alpha diversity metrics Chao1 richness, number of taxa, Faith’s phylogenetic diversity, Shannon diversity, Simpson’s Index of Diversity and Simpson’s evenness was carried out using the Python package Scikit-Bio [56]. We took the mean of the 100 repeats for alpha diversity metrics for each sample (Fig. S1). Beta diversity was calculated using the compositionally aware Phylogenetic-RPCA method within the QIIME2 plugin Gemelli [57]. Samples were not additionally normalised prior to Phylogenetic-RPCA calculations. Alpha and beta diversity were both calculated at the taxonomic level of ASV.

Microbiome statistical analysis

All statistical analysis was performed using custom R and Python scripts. Significant differences in alpha and beta diversity were assessed using analysis of variance (ANOVA; alpha diversity) and permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA; beta diversity) using the stats and vegan R packages, respectively. Because some of the metadata variables of interest were not available for all participants, univariate models were run for each variable as well as nine different models that included (1) age, gender, ethnicity and diagnosed health condition (n = 4812 participants); (2) age, gender, ethnicity and diagnosed health condition for one child chosen randomly from each household (n = 3817 participants); (3) age, gender, ethnicity and diagnosed health condition, excluding one-child families (for families with 2–5 children, n = 1885 or families with two children only, n = 1588, with or without the household shuffled randomly); (4) age, gender, ethnicity, diagnosed health condition, social deprivation, material deprivation, residing in the GTA, index of rurality, greenspace and PM2.5 (n = 4178 participants); (5) age, gender, ethnicity, diagnosed health condition, residing in the GTA, index of rurality, greenspace, PM2.5, ON-Marg household & dwellings, ON-Marg material resources, ON-Marg age & labour and ON-Marg racialised & newcomer populations (n = 3776); (6) age, gender, ethnicity, diagnosed health condition and current, school and in-utero information for social deprivation, material deprivation, residing in the GTA, index of rurality, greenspace and PM2.5 (n = 2571 participants); (7) age, gender, ethnicity, diagnosed health condition, Healthy Eating Food Index, diet variety and all diet questionnaire variables (n = 2978 participants); (8) all variables for the subset of participants with complete metadata (n = 2594); and (9) all variables for the subset of participants with complete metadata that had not taken antibiotics within the last month (n = 2426). Participants who had taken antibiotics were only removed from model (9). Banjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate correction was run on each group of statistical tests (results shown only in Supplementary Tables). Differential abundance tests were carried out using MaAsLin3 (maaslin3 R package version 0.99.7) [58] at the genus level and we consider a genus to be significantly differentially abundant if q value ≤ 0.1. MaAsLin3 tests all included sample read depth as covariates. Tests using model (1) from above were used for assessing differential abundance of taxa with age, gender, ethnicity and diagnosed health condition, while all other variables included the variable of interest as well as age, gender, ethnicity, diagnosed health condition and sample read depth as covariates. PERMANOVA and MaAsLin3 tests included household as strata while ANOVA tests included household as an error term. Relationships between kinship coefficients and phylogenetic-RPCA distances were calculated using Spearman’s correlation coefficients, while phylogenetic-RPCA distances between different genetic relatedness groups or within the same or different households were assessed using Kruskal–Wallis tests with post-hoc Dunn’s tests with Holm-Bonferroni FDR correction. Mediation modelling within the Python package stats models was used to investigate the proportion of the association between Phylogenetic RPCA PC1 and ethnicity that was mediated by one or more of the dietary variables.

We examined seven loci within the human genome that were recently identified as being associated with oral microbiome composition [59]: (i) SLC2A9 (rs13129697); (ii) PITX1 (rs3749751); (iii) ABO (rs2519093); (iv) PPP1R3C/HECTD2 (rs12260868); (v) TLR1 (rs5743618); (vi) POLI/RAB27B (rs17559023); and (vii) FUT2 (rs601338; Table S4). Variants at the SLC2A9, PITX1, ABO and PPP1R3C/HECTD2 loci were present in East Asian, South Asian and European ancestry participants, while TLR1, POLI/RAB27B and FUT2 were present in only South Asian and European ancestry participants. We again used PERMANOVA and ANOVA to test for associations between the alternate allele at these loci and phylogenetic-RPCA diversity or metrics of alpha diversity, respectively, and MaAsLin3 to identify taxa that may be significantly associated with these alleles. As above, we consider a genus to be significantly differentially abundant if q value ≤ 0.1. All statistical tests included the first four ancestry-specific principal components, calculated using FRAPOSA [60] with the provided 1000 Genomes samples as a reference, as well as age, gender and diagnosed health conditions as covariates and household as strata/error term, as above. MaAsLin3 additionally included read depth as a covariate.

Adult cohort

We used a dataset that we published previously for qualitative comparisons of the oral microbiome of children and adolescents with adults [11, 17, 18, 61, 62]. This included the saliva microbiome of 1,598 adults aged 35–70 that lived in Atlantic Canada and are part of the Atlantic Partnership for Tomorrow’s Health (PATH) cohort. This dataset used the same primers for amplification and samples were sequenced at the same facility, although saliva samples from the Atlantic PATH cohort were collected in sterile tubes and stored at − 80 °C until DNA extraction with the QIAamp 96 PowerFecal QIAcube HT kit. Samples were processed through QIIME2 using the same steps and parameters as described above.

Alpha diversity metrics associated with richness (Chao1 richness, observed taxa and Faith’s phylogenetic diversity) all increase significantly with age and are significantly associated with diagnosed health condition (ANOVA p ≤ 0.05), with those with no diagnosed health condition typically having higher richness than those with diagnosed conditions (Fig. 2, Figs. S2 and S5). White participants had significantly lower richness and higher evenness than East or South Asian participants (no significant differences between East Asian and South Asian participants; Tukey’s HSD; Table S5 and Fig. S3) and evenness and Simpson’s Index of diversity were significantly higher in females compared with males (Tukey’s HSD; Table S5 and Fig. S4).

The most prevalent taxa in children and adolescents are similar to those found in adults, but the salivary bacteriome of children and adolescents is more consistent than that of adults

Overall, we identified 7705 ASVs (Table S3) and 160 genera (Table S7) in participants. These were predominantly from the phyla Bacteroidetes, Firmicutes, Proteobacteria, Fusobacteria and Actinobacteria. Many genera are significantly differentially abundant with age, ethnicity and gender, but not diagnosed health conditions

Focusing on the differentially abundant (DA; MaAsLin3 q ≤ 0.1) genera that are also within the top 20 most abundant genera overall (Fig. 3 and Figs. Four genera are DA with both age and gender (Gemella, Neisseria, Prevotella and Haemophilus); Prevotella is higher in abundance in females than males while the others show the opposite. Given the weak relationship between genetic relatedness and saliva bacteriome similarity, we next looked at the effects of shared household environment on saliva bacteriome similarity. Participants within this cohort came from 3817 households/families (based on self-reporting of living together or biological relatedness); there were 2927 families with one child, 794 with two children, 88 with three children, seven with four children and one with five children (Fig. 1A). Looking only at participants in households with ≥ 2 children (n = 1885), we find that household accounts for 61% of the variation between participants (PERMANOVA R2 = 0.614, p = 0.001; Table S6). The R2 values for gender, age, ethnicity and diagnosed health condition also dropped compared with the overall model discussed above, with gender no longer having a significant impact on saliva bacteriome composition. Distances between individuals within the same household were significantly lower than between participants from different households (Kruskal–Wallis H = 157.6, p < 0.001; Fig. 4D). Most participants within the same household had first degree relationships or were twins (n = 871), with very few pairs of participants within the same household that had second/third degree relationships (n = 29) or were unrelated (n = 20). There were also only eight households/families that indicated that some of the children/adolescents did not reside at the same address the entire time. These smaller numbers precluded more detailed analyses of the associations between shared household environment and genetic relationship.

Diet variables—particularly those linked to frequency of sugar consumption—have more impact on the saliva bacteriome of children and adolescents than marginalisation and environmental factors

Different subsets of the participants had answered an additional diet questionnaire (n = 2978) or had information on neighbourhood-level marginalisation and environmental characteristics derived from their postal code (social and material deprivation, whether participants lived in the GTA, index of rurality, greenspace, and PM2.5; Table S1; n = 4178). When we look in the subset of participants who provided dietary information and also include diet variables, ethnicity is no longer significant in beta diversity tests (Table S6), but associations with alpha diversity remain similar (Table S5). All diet variables aside from dairy milk consumption were significantly associated with ethnicity, and 15, 6 and 8 of the 22 examined variables were significantly associated with age, gender and diagnosed health condition, respectively, with only fruit consumption being significantly associated with all (Table S9). We examined the frequency with which different food items are consumed within each of the different ethnicity groups, with the largest differences being in tofu/soy, red meat, fish/seafood, eggs, processed meat and beans/legumes consumption (Fig. S12). The diet questionnaire also included a question on whether participants had taken antibiotics or not; 201 participants (6.75%) indicated that they had taken antibiotics within the last month (Fig. 2B). As expected, there were significant associations between antibiotic use and beta diversity and a significant decrease in most metrics of alpha diversity in those that had taken antibiotics (Fig. 2A and Fig. S13). See Supplementary Results for a more detailed overview of diet and antibiotic use within the Spit for Science cohort. Many of the marginalisation and environmental characteristics were only associated with alpha and beta diversity in univariate models and the effect sizes were small (Supplementary Results; Figs. S14–S16 and Tables S1, S5, S6 and S8).

Discussion

We analysed a total of 4812 saliva bacteriomes of children and adolescents aged 5–18 years in the Canadian Spit for Science cohort and identified associations between the salivary bacteriome and age, gender, ethnicity, diagnosed health conditions, genetic relatedness, sociodemographic variables and diet. To our knowledge, this is the largest population cohort study of the oral microbiome within children and adolescents to date. Saliva samples were collected from participants when they were attending the Ontario Science Centre (Toronto, Canada); unstimulated saliva sampling offers a non-invasive method for both microbiome and human genetic analyses [64]. However, this opportunistic sampling means that time of day and diurnal behavioural patterns such as eating or brushing teeth are not standardised across participants. Microbial load in saliva is highest in the morning before eating or brushing teeth (and is inversely correlated with salivary flow rate, which is lowest in the morning), although the bacterial community in saliva is relatively stable across a day and with timing of food intake or teeth brushing [10]. Saliva samples were also not originally collected for the purpose of oral microbiome characterisations, and questions on oral health, dentition devices or oral hygiene were not asked, and no dental health examinations were performed. Oral health and hygiene have a large impact on the oral microbiome and the development of oral diseases [14–16], although oral microbiome in adults have been found to be impacted more strongly by oral health habits than those of children [12]. Burcham et al. found that youth (age 8–18 years) salivary bacteriomes were more strongly impacted by biological sex and weight status, while adults were impacted by frequency of flossing and tooth brushing [12]. Both the lack of sampling time standardisation and information on oral health and hygiene are major limitations of this study.

No single variable explained more than 2% of variation between samples (beta diversity) in the entire cohort, and we therefore caution any interpretations of statistical significance because their biological significance is unknown and not tested here. However, in the subset of participants who came from families with two or more children (n = 1885), 61.4% of the saliva bacteriome variation between participants could be attributed to household environment. We also found that although oral bacteriome differences decreased with increasing genetic relatedness, similar to previous studies [65], the correlation was very small (R2 = − 0.014; Fig. 4). We did not have enough participants who shared households but were unrelated genetically to investigate this aspect in more detail—and this is something that future studies should aim to address with specific recruitment of these groups—but our results agree with other studies that have found that there is no difference in oral microbiome compositional similarities between biological or adoptive mother–child pairs [66], and that shared household is the largest contributor to oral microbiome variation within extended families [67]. Furthermore, both gut and oral microbiome strain-sharing rates have been shown to be higher between co-habiting individuals than with non-cohabiting individuals, with strain-sharing rates decreasing with increasing age as well as with time spent living apart for adult twins [68]. We also found minimal associations between saliva bacteriome composition in our cohort and 7 loci in the human genome that have previously been reported to be associated with human oral microbiome composition [59], further suggesting that household environment has a larger impact on the oral microbiome than human genetics, although future studies should investigate this more extensively. Previous studies have even shown that there is in impact of shared household even when individuals no longer cohabit [67] and is enhanced by duration of cohabitation; while this may be through mechanisms such as shared diet and hygiene routines, Valles-Colomer et al. suggest that it is through oral contact and person-to-person transmission [68]. Disentangling these mechanisms was not the aim of our study and they cannot be elucidated with our experimental design, however, this is worth exploring in future research.

Within the entire cohort, both uni- and multi-variate analyses showed that age followed by ethnicity had the largest impact on saliva bacteriome composition (Figs. 2 and 3). Twelve genera were present in 99% of participants in this cohort, with three of these (Veillonella, Streptococcus and Prevotella) being present in 100% of participants and making up ~ 45% of the relative abundance (Figs. 3 and S6). All of these genera were found in high prevalences (> 75% of samples) in saliva samples from < 10 year olds in a Dutch population-based study (n = 700) [27] and 9/12 genera in 8–16 year olds (n = 179) in a study that examined buccal swabs of people visiting the Denver Museum of Nature & Science (Colorado, USA) [12]. Both Burcham et al. [12] and our previous study [11, 17, 18, 61, 62] found fewer genera that were highly prevalent in adults (20–75 years and 35–70 years, respectively) than in children, suggesting that there are fewer core taxa in adults than in children, and Odendaal et al. [27] found that age explains more oral microbiome variance in younger than older individuals. Furthermore, we find that (1) measures of richness are significantly higher while measures of evenness are significantly lower with age (Fig. 2); (2) more differentially abundant genera that were negatively associated with age than positively associated with age (Fig. 3); (3) the number of rare ASVs and the total relative abundance of these rare ASVs is higher with increasing age, and this increase continues into adulthood (Fig. S8); and (4) the saliva bacteriome of children/adolescents of the same age becomes less similar as they get older, and there are larger differences between adults of the same age than children/adolescents of the same age (Fig. S8). Taken together, we suggest that there is more consistency within the oral microbiome of children/adolescents than adults and that these changes likely occur as children and adolescents are exposed to more different external environments as they age. However, we do note that recruitment and sample collection/analysis differ between the child/adolescent and adult cohorts and the impact that this has warrants further investigation; future studies should aim to characterise the oral microbiome of participants of all ages using the same techniques.

Despite the lower impact on microbiome composition, more genera were significantly DA with ethnicity than age and 14 of the 20 most abundant genera were DA with ethnicity (Figs. 2 and 3 and S2–S3). We note that readers should exhibit caution when interpreting our ethnicity-associated oral microbiome differences as these are likely a proxy for multiple variables (e.g. diet, environment, socioeconomics and culture, as discussed further below), rather than genetic or intrinsic differences. Typically, the DA genera between East Asian, South Asian and White participants followed alpha and beta diversity patterns in that East Asian and South Asian participants were more similar to one another (for 12/14 DA genera) than to White participants (Fig. S3). Some of these differences—namely that Prevotella are more abundant in White participants and Capnocytophaga and Porphyromonas are less abundant in White participants—have been identified previously by a study on adult sub-gingival plaque microbiomes [69]; however, we note that we found very few studies that reported on oral microbiome differences with ethnicity.

Intriguingly, when diet variables were included in beta diversity analyses, ethnicity was no longer significant. Furthermore, even though almost all of the diet variables were significantly associated with ethnicity in alpha diversity analyses (Table S9) and the frequency with which they were consumed varied with ethnicity (Fig. S12), none of the diet variables were significantly associated with beta diversity either (Table S6). The association between ethnicity and the salivary bacteriome was partially mediated by diet variables, in particular salty snack consumption (Fig. S17). Although we are cautious not to over-interpret this and the proportion of the association that was mediated by the diet was small in most cases, this could suggest that either (i) other aspects of diet and eating patterns such as food preparation methods typically used by people of different ethnicities/cultures have a larger impact than the types of food consumed, or (ii) the dietary screener questionnaires that we used were too coarse to adequately identify dietary differences that contribute to saliva bacteriome composition in people of different ethnicities. It is likely that our ethnicity variable is behaving as a proxy for multiple behavioural and dietary patterns rather than as an independent biological driver. See Supplementary Discussion for further discussion of this.

Diet variables related to sugar consumption (soda/sugary drinks, desserts/sweets and fruit juices) had the strongest impact on the saliva bacteriome within our cohort (Figs. 5 and 6). We find that increasing frequency of sugary food consumption is associated with a decrease in microbial richness as well as positive associations with several abundant taxa, such as Prevotella, Streptococcus and Veillonella, while Neisseria is negatively associated. Accordingly, a systematic review by Angarita‐Díaz et al. [70] also noted a decrease in microbial diversity and increases in both Streptococcus and Veillonella with high sugar intake, although they note that further studies are needed to further elucidate these interactions. We were surprised to find that although the sugar-associated diet variables—frequently linked with diet quality—had the strongest associations with the salivary bacteriome, the Healthy Eating Food Index and diet variety both had little association (Figs. 5 and 6), but the same has recently been found by Li et al. (2026); they also found few associations with a Healthy Eating Index, but also found positive associations between high sugar consumption and Veillonella and Streptococcus (although they also found a positive association with Neisseria) [71].

Despite the documented links between socioeconomic status and oral health, and oral health and the oral microbiome [72], we found very few differences in microbiome composition due to neighbourhood socioeconomic or environmental factors (neighbourhood social and material deprivation, household income, whether participants have a second parent/caregiver, PM2.5, greenspace, index of rurality and residence in the GTA; Fig. 2, Figs. S14 and S15). This could be because there is lower representation here of individuals with lower socioeconomic status (the 2020 median economic family income was $117,000 in Toronto [73], falling within the median household income of $100–199,000 in this study) and access to health and dental care is likely to be relatively homogeneous within this cohort; the majority (63%) of participants live in or around Toronto, Canada, and ~ 90% of Canadian children and youth have recently been found to have visited a dental professional within the last 12 months [74]. The median values for environmental variables within this cohort also reflect that this is a highly urbanised cohort; for example, the median index of rurality is 0.05 on a scale of 0–1, with 1 being the most rural. The environmental measures are also highly correlated as they are based on census dissemination areas; the positive association between PM2.5 and measures of microbial richness as well as the negative association between greenspace and microbial richness may both be due to the higher richness in more urban areas (Fig. S11). Inclusion of more individuals from outside of major urban areas may reveal other associations between the oral microbiome and the environment.

While there are several limitations that we have discussed throughout this section, there are several factors that could be addressed by future studies that would strengthen them and build upon our findings: (i) although this cohort is the largest population-based oral microbiome study to-date, we still had low representation of some groups within the variables we examined and no participants of other ethnicities than East Asian, South Asian or White, meaning that we were not able to fully investigate all variables. The large population size may have helped to reach statistical significance in some instances, but future studies are necessary to confirm our findings in other populations and ones designed to fully dissect these associations and determine their biological significance; (ii) we identified several genera that were DA with age as well as gender (Fig. 3), but do not have information on whether participants have gone through puberty. Furthermore, while the number of participants in our cohort that identify as trans/gender-diverse (n = 21 [0.4%]) is similar to the proportion reported in the 2019 Canadian Health Survey [75], it is too small to draw robust conclusions. Future studies should therefore aim to collect information on puberty and to sample a larger number of trans/gender-diverse participants; (iii) the broad groupings that we use to characterise participants as having received a physical health condition or psychiatric/neurodevelopmental disorder diagnosis are likely too coarse to detect robust differences. These should be examined in more detail in future analyses and could be further strengthened by the verification of parent/self-reported data using medical health records or trait-based analyses; (iv) longitudinal follow-up with the same participants may elucidate differences that are due to age or changes in diet and living situation; (v) the use of a more thorough/precise diet tool, such as 24-h recall or a food diary; (vi) although access to dental care is likely to be high within this cohort, oral hygiene habits, dental health and time since last dentist visit all likely have a significant effect on the saliva microbiome [11]. Information on this in addition to whether participants have lost all of their baby teeth or have orthodontic appliances such as braces—which may further impact the oral microbiome with age [76]—would benefit future studies; (vii) the collection of multiple samples from the same participant from both other niches within the mouth as well as other body sites (e.g., skin or faecal samples) would facilitate our understanding of whether changes in the saliva microbiome occur concurrently with changes in other microbiomes; and (viii) future work using metagenomic/metatranscriptomic shotgun sequencing could characterise the microbial eukaryotes, viruses and archaea within the oral microbiome as well as the functional capacity or activity of the oral microbiome; while tools such as PICRUSt2 [77] exist for predicting the functional capacity of microbiomes, we believe that these should be used for hypothesis generation rather than primary analysis and therefore did not use them here.

Conclusions

Our characterisation of the saliva bacteriome in the Spit for Science population cohort is, to our knowledge, the first study that has concurrently investigated the impact of demographic, socioeconomic, dietary and genetic variables on oral microbiome composition in a population cohort of this size. We have revealed significant—albeit mainly small—associations with all of these variables. The investigated variables with the largest association with the salivary bacteriome are household environment, age, antibiotic use and frequency of sugar consumption, while socioeconomic, environmental and genetic variables have much smaller associations, if any. The salivary microbiome of children and adolescents appears to be distinct from both infant and adult microbiomes and more consistent than adult microbiomes, highlighting the need to include participants across all age ranges in future studies that investigate associations between the oral microbiome and health/disease. Furthermore, ethnicity appears to be strongly associated with the oral microbiome, although future studies should aim to disentangle its effects from other connected cultural or lifestyle factors such as diet and food preparation methods.
