‘We don’t use the word sustainability’: an interview study exploring general practitioner views on sustainability
Authors: Sigrid Moesgaard Larsen, Torsten Risør, Kristian Damgaard Lyng, Malene Plejdrup Hansen, Jens Søndergaard, Nanna Holt Jessen, Ásthildur Árnadóttir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGeneral practice plays a central role in healthcare delivery and holds significant potential to advance sustainability by reducing its environmental footprint and promoting population health. Background

The need for sustainable development in healthcare is indisputable with the future challenges of healthcare systems such as climate change, the intersecting pressures of demographic changes and the growing incidence of chronic disease [1]. In partnership with other public health institutions, general practice plays a central role in addressing the majority of emerging health challenges [2]. General practitioners (GPs) are gatekeepers and coordinators of the Danish healthcare system, managing approximately 90% of all healthcare contacts [3]. General Practice is responsible for referrals for most specialist services and hospital treatments, and for prescribing almost 90% of all reimbursable prescription medications in Denmark, reinforcing their central role in coordinating patient care [4–6]. Denmark has initiated both national and regional initiatives and strategies aimed at reducing climate impact in hospitals and the broader secondary healthcare [7]. Similar strategies aimed at primary healthcare have yet not been methodized on a political level, however, the Danish College of General Practice introduced the concept of sustainability in general practice with a policy paper in 2024 [8]. Involving GPs through bottom-up solutions is necessary to identify pathways for sustainable development in general practice [9,10].

Sustainability is a broad and evolving concept in healthcare characterized by a significant level of novelty [11]. The term ‘sustainability’ is used across different scales (e.g. organizations, technologies, governments and institutions) as well as in different contexts [12]. The definition of sustainable development from the Brundtland report ‘development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs’ is widely accepted and embraced [13]. This understanding later evolved into the three pillars (economic, societal and environmental) of sustainability [14]. WHO further isolates one pillar and defines environmental sustainability in health care as ‘being a health system that improves, maintains or restores health, while minimizing negative impacts on the environment and leveraging opportunities to restore and improve it, to the benefit of the health and well-being of current and future generations.’ [15] The social dimension has received less attention and is theoretically and analytically considered the weakest ‘pillar’ [16,17]. Social sustainability is more difficult to quantify compared to economic and environmental outcomes. However, the importance of including the social dimensions in science and healthcare is becoming increasingly recognized [18]. The term sustainability transition refers to processes of change that transform a system or entity toward a more sustainable state. Such transitions typically involve multiple interacting changes—technical, economic, and behavioural—occurring across different levels (regional, national, and global), often over extended periods of time [19]. In healthcare, the Lancet Commission on Sustainable Health Care has highlighted the need for a rapid transition towards low-polluting, sustainable, and resilient health systems. This transition requires reframing care within a paradigm of health promotion, focusing on the appropriateness of care, and developing systems that prioritise both optimal clinical outcomes and environmental performance [20].

There are multiple frameworks to choose from when assessing sustainability (e.g. corporate sustainability (CS), environmental management (EM), and corporate social responsibility (CSR)) [21]. One of those frameworks, The Environmental, Social, and Governance (ESG) principles, are commonly used for corporate governance, in organizations and companies, including large healthcare institutions such as hospitals, to assess parameters related to sustainability, encompassing environmental impact and social responsibility [22,23]. All framework models are novel to general practice settings.

Sustainability has become increasingly important in healthcare, as the health care sector contributes to negative environmental impact such as greenhouse gas emissions (GHG) and resource consumption but also holds a key responsibility in promoting global health [24]. However, there are still multiple knowledge gaps in understanding and identifying pathways towards sustainable development in primary care [25–27].

Therefore, this study aims to explore GPs’ view on sustainability in general practice.

Method

Study design

This study employed a qualitative interview design with interpretive reflexive thematic analysis (TA), conducted in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist [28]. Semi-structured individual interviews enabled participants to provide in-depth accounts of their experiences and perspectives on sustainability in general practice, ensuring that each participant’s views were expressed individually, without the influence of colleagues. The study was informed by an understanding of sustainability drawing on ESG principles, which provided a conceptual frame while allowing participants’ own interpretations to emerge [22].

Interview guide

An interview guide (see Appendix) was developed collaboratively within the author group, based on the study aim, our previous work, and existing literature [9,29]. The guide was semi-structured and designed to allow flexibility in the interviews. The GPs were briefly introduced to a broad definition of sustainability in health care, encompassing ESG. This was done to outline the frame of the questions without imposing specific answers. The interview guide comprised both open-ended and probing questions, which were divided into three groups: E (environment, resources, medicine, climate change), S (social equality in health, social responsibility) and G (leadership, structures and well-being).

To accommodate the schedules of general practitioners and enhance participation, the interviews were deliberately designed to last no more than 45 min. The interview guide was extensive but used flexibly rather than as a rigid checklist. The guide was pilot tested with two GPs, which led to minor adjustments of questions and introductory information. During data collection, the interview guide was discussed within the research team and minor adjustments were made iteratively to improve clarity and explore emerging themes in greater depth. These adjustments did not alter the overall focus or core topics of the interviews but allowed the interviewer to follow up on issues raised by participants and adapt to the flow of each interview. All interviews were conducted, recorded and transcribed by the first author.

Participants and data collection

GPs from all five Danish regions were included in the study. Participants were required to be board-certified specialised GPs and actively engaged in clinical practice for a minimum of three days per week. Recruitment was conducted through open calls within the Danish Network of Knowledge and Competencies in General Practice (VID), the collaborative platform ‘Midtkraft’ for general practice research in Denmark, and the authors’ professional network. No relationships between the interviewer and interviewees were established prior to the interviews, and the participants chose the setting. Only the interviewer and the interviewee were present during the interviews. Participants received compensation equivalent to the standard 10-minute consultation rate in Danish general practice [30]. The aim was to ensure some diversity in age, gender and geographical location among the included participants. No dropouts occurred after recruitment.

Interviews were conducted in Danish during November and December 2024. They were conducted online in MS Teams, in a physical meeting room or at the participants’ practices, according to their preference. Field notes were made after each interview. Audio-recordings were transcribed using the software ‘Good Tape’ (Denmark) and revised critically for accuracy and sense-making. Quotes were translated into English by the first author, and fictive participant names were chosen.

Data analysis

The project originates from a joint initiative involving researchers from the general practice research units in Denmark. The interviewer was a final-year, female medical student with more than three years of work experience in general practice; this background informed the interview and was reflected upon throughout the analysis. The rest of the author group comprised five medical doctors and one physiotherapist. One author was a PhD student, while the remaining authors all held PhD degrees, two of whom were professors. Some of the authors had extensive experience with conducting qualitative research.

Interview transcripts were coded and analysed using NVivo, version 15 (Lumivero, US). All interview transcripts were analysed thematically following Braun and Clarke’s six-step framework for reflexive TA [31]. The study adopted a constructivist epistemology, recognising that knowledge is co-constructed by researcher and participants. This stance informed the interview process and analytical approach. Although ESG does not constitute a formal theoretical framework, it provided an analytic lens through which the concept of sustainability in general practice were interpreted. In line with Braun and Clarke’s guidance on conceptual and design clarity, ESG was not used as a deductive coding template [32]. Rather, it functioned as a contextual framing devise that informed the researchers’ analytic attention while allowing patterns of meaning to be generated inductively though engagement with the data.

The first author read each transcript multiple times, while listening to the audio files to ensure familiarity with the data and potential patterns and meanings. Consistent with reflective TA, coding was approached as an interpretative and iterative process. Early codes were discussed within the research team, not to achieve consensus or establish reliability, but to refine interpretative insights. Trough iterative engagement with the data and collaborative analytic dialogue, potential themes were generated, reviewed, and refined in relation to the study aim.

Ethical considerations

Transcripts were anonymised and assigned unique identification codes to protect participant confidentiality. All participants received a written document outlining the purpose of the study and their rights as participants. Informed consent was obtained at the beginning of each interview. Participation was voluntary, with the option to withdraw at any time. The study was conducted in compliance with Danish data protection legislation and recorded in the register of processing activities at the Research Unit for General Practice in Aarhus. Data were securely stored in accordance with the General Data Protection Regulation. The Research Unit for General Practice Aarhus served as the data controller. Under Danish law, qualitative studies without intervention are exempt from ethical approval.

Results

Two main themes were developed: (I) Making sense of sustainability: How GPs perceive sustainability in practice and (2)Between responsibility and reluctance: the GP’s role in a sustainable future (Figure 1). While most participants recognised the environmental pillar of sustainability as an important topic, they described challenges in transforming it into something manageable and operational that could be incorporated into everyday clinical practice. The GPs expressed differing views on the role to be played by general practice in sustainability transitions in healthcare, with several noting lack of guiding information.

The GPs were requested to provide reflections on sustainability in general practice. The participants’ perceptions of sustainability in general practice comprised four subthemes: (I)Abstract threat, concrete practices, (II) Translating sustainability into clinical language, (III) Financial sustainability as a prerequisite and (IV) When social sustainability feels imposed.

Abstract threat, concrete practices

The GPs often found it challenging to describe the association between climate change and health-related outcomes. Some perceived climate change as an issue primarily affecting other regions, particularly in the Global South, rather than posing a direct concern for their own population.

‘I don’t think that we are directly affected by climate change.’ (Peter)

‘I still think it is a problem located more in the south.’ (Josh)

Despite this, all participants identified specific areas for reducing carbon emissions or resource consumption in their own practices including waste sorting, medication use, materials, medical supplies, transport, constructions, energy and food choices. Some participants had already taken steps to become more climate-friendly by switching to green energy, using reusable speculums and implementing waste sorting.

‘Well, partly, it’s about moving away from a throwaway culture. For instance, there are two types of speculums available for gynaecological examinations: the disposable plastic ones and the old ones that are autoclaved. Then there’s also the other part about what kind of medication we prescribe. Is it a spray? Is it a powder? And something that also concerns me is overdiagnosis, unnecessary tests.’ (Josh)

Translating sustainability into clinical language

The theme encompassed various topics that received considerable attention from the GPs, including diagnostics and treatment, cost effectiveness and a cross-sectoral approach to care. Responsible and rational use of medicine and diagnostics appeared to play a significant role in shaping decision-making during consultations, as highlighted by Daniel below. This could be regarded as an economically and environmentally sustainable approach to treatment, as the aim is to provide the most effective and cost-efficient care for patients while minimising medication waste. Nonetheless, this does not necessarily reflect a deliberate concern for environmental and climate impacts; rather, it is seen as a beneficial co-effect.

‘Environmentally, it plays a smaller role in day-to-day considerations. What matters more is whether something harms or benefits the patient. The economic aspect carries quite a bit of weight when it comes to choosing treatments, but I find it difficult to also wear that ‘environmental hat’ when I’m sitting there trying to assess what benefits the patient, and what the financial implications are. Because for me, the most important thing is that the medication I prescribe is relevant and used properly, so it doesn’t go to waste. I would say that this is our main responsibility. Then there should also be someone else looking at which tablets have the least environmental impact, and which medications generate the least waste.’ (Daniel)

Sustainability as a term may not be fully integrated into the clinical language which is noticeable in Peters statement. Peter related the broad concept of sustainability to avoiding overtreatment and unnecessary diagnostics, although he did not use the exact word ‘sustainable.’

‘We don’t use the word sustainability. We use terms like avoiding antibiotic resistance and avoiding overtreatment of urinary tract infections that don’t need to be treated. Many other words than ‘green transition,’ ‘climate’ and things like that.’ (Peter)

Participants expressed general concern that defensive medicine might escalate the consumption of human and material resources within the healthcare system. The interviews also revealed a sense of pressure to do more diagnostic work ups from both patients and organisations like Marie expressed.

‘We’re just doing way more tests now. Examinations are being done today that weren’t done 14 years ago. […]in my opinion, we’re treating far too many healthy people and doing far too many tests that we assume will come back normal. We’re just doing them to be on the safe side.’ (Marie)

Financial sustainability as a prerequisite

Financial sustainability was important to the GPs, as it ensures a steady income and maintains profitability for the clinic. It appeared to be a fundamental aspect when discussing sustainability and behavioural changes within the practice, although financial sustainability was not only about their own general practice. Most of the GPs expressed awareness of the gatekeeper function of general practice, as this role is intended to enhance the cost-effectiveness of the healthcare services.

‘I am aware that we run small businesses that are financially sustainable since it’s very rare for us to go bankrupt. But also, in terms of societal sustainability, we need to provide services that are as economically beneficial to society as possible.’ (Ingrid)

Several participants believed that the gatekeeper function is becoming increasingly difficult to uphold due to rising expectations from both patients and systemic structures, including the rise in private health insurance use and political targets for diagnostic timelines.

When social sustainability feels imposed

For most GPs, the social domain of sustainability appeared vague or imposed. When asked about the connection between sustainability and social inequity, nearly all participants found it challenging to respond as they perceived sustainability as solely related to climate, resources and finances.

‘Yes, I certainly notice the inequality and inequity in healthcare. But I have a hard time placing this in the context of sustainability.’(Lars)

‘I guess I’m thinking that maybe it’s because sustainability has become a buzzword, and now we’re supposed to connect it to our work with socially vulnerable groups as well?’ (Ann)

While the GPs engaged with social inequities and cared for vulnerable patients, they found the link to sustainability somewhat contrived. Nevertheless, they emphasised the importance of this work, noting that it demanded considerable time and commitment in their daily practice. In addition, when reflecting on the importance of social well-being within their own clinical settings, they highlighted that supporting social thriving in the clinic was valued.

Main theme 2: Between responsibility and reluctance: The GP’s role in a sustainable future

The GPs agreed that healthcare professionals have a responsibility in sustainability transitions in the healthcare system. Still, their perspectives on how to fulfil this responsibility, and what matters the most, differed. Their focus varied from responsibilities as practice leaders to opportunities in consultations and setting a good example for their patients. While several participants faced challenges in incorporating sustainability in their daily decisions as GPs and adopted a more passive stance, others took a more proactive approach. These perspectives are reflected in the two subthemes (I) Waiting for direction and delegation and (II) Forming sustainability as part of the professional identity.

Waiting for direction and delegation

Many participants emphasised a need for guidance on how to initiate sustainable practices and which interventions have the greatest impact. They also highlighted that structural or policy-level preventive measures were likely to be more effective than individual efforts within general practice. Some even conveyed a sense of powerlessness in influencing environmental outcomes.

‘I must admit that the way the healthcare system is right now, it’s sometimes just about crisis management. And then climate doesn’t enter very high on the agenda.’(Ingrid)

‘I definitely think there’s a need for clear goals and a discussion of where action is feasible. As independent practitioners, which we are, it’s entirely possible to do nothing at all if that’s what you choose.’(Johanne)

Challenges in making more sustainable decisions and actions in general practice included time constraints, limited knowledge, habitual practices, financial limitations, colleagues with different convictions, powerlessness and patient expectations.

‘I also think that those who know more about sustainability have a responsibility to make it easily accessible, so that at least there isn’t a barrier when it comes to gaining knowledge about which actions have the most impact.’ (Daniel)

‘It would be great if we were provided with that information, so we didn’t need to go out and look for it ourselves.’ (Ingrid)

Although GPs like Daniel and Ingrid acknowledged that healthcare professionals have a role in a sustainable development of healthcare, they took a somewhat passive approach to concrete actions.

Others, like Josh, expressed reluctance to introducing sustainability-related considerations during consultations, as they were concerned it might compromise the doctor–patient relationship.

‘I think it’s highly relevant, but I believe it should be addressed at the level of community medicine and public information. I can imagine that, for some, it might even harm the doctor–patient relationship.’ (Josh)

Forming sustainability as part of the professional identity

Some participants described a more proactive approach to engage in changes, viewing themselves as role models who could influence patients. Motivations for including sustainability in decision-making or clinical actions included: personal motivation, not to burden the secondary sector, cultural considerations, financial issues and easily accessible guidelines.

‘We’re the ones prescribing and handling a lot of this on the front line, so of course we should take action and try to have an influence.’ (Laura)

As suggested by Ina below, environmental sustainability might be integrated into physicians’ visions, and perhaps even into their professional roles, much like other health promotion efforts.

‘Actually, I think this is something we should allow ourselves to support. I think we need to be careful not to become moralistic, but the fact is, we’re not exactly afraid to ask people to stop smoking. We know it has health consequences. So, I think it’s perfectly reasonable for us to step into that debate.’ (Ina)

In the interview, she was gradually exploring and articulating her own views on sustainability as part of her role as a GP.

‘I don’t think of it as part of the GP role. In fact, when I say this, it feels like stepping a bit outside that role. But maybe that’s not necessary anymore. As a clinic, we also have certain visions, and it would make sense to include sustainability too […] I feel like it’s increasingly on my mind, but I still think there’s quite a long way to go before it leads to action.’ (Ina)

The GPs differed in the extent to which they had integrated ideas about sustainability into their practice. However, like Ina, most of them appeared to be engaged in early consideration of change. While they acknowledged the importance of the issue, they also conveyed ambivalence about making concrete changes.

Discussion

Although all participants were familiar with the concept of sustainability, most focused primarily on its economic and environmental dimensions, with less emphasis on the social pillar. Participants highlighted the need for practical solutions to support sustainability efforts in general practice.

The GPs in Denmark work as independent contractors, managing their own finances, equipment and clinic organisation [3]. Consequently, the economic aspect related to sustainability transition is very important to the GPs, as reflected in the interviews. If environmentally sustainable choices proved cost-effective, participants were more likely to see them as motivator for change. Therefore, highlighting the economic and societal co-benefits may be important when implementing sustainable initiatives. A framework like ESG for sustainability has a theoretical value, however, the framework can suffer from a limited applicability [11]. It became evident that the participants had little knowledge of ESG and struggled to recognise how their efforts to promote equality and equity in healthcare might also advance sustainability. This suggests that the ESG framework may feel abstract or disconnected from everyday patient care. This is a well-known challenge from other sectors working for sustainable development [17,23]. The ESG framework is not the only framework for improving sustainable development in healthcare. Alternative frameworks offer complementary perspectives such as Principles of sustainable clinical practice [33].

In the interviews, GPs generally acknowledged the environmental pillar of sustainability as an important issue, noting that many current practices already align with sustainable principles. Practices such as avoiding tests and procedures that do not add significantly to patient wellbeing and prognosis. These actions are typically framed in terms of clinical quality, patient safety or cost-effectiveness, rather than being viewed as part of a sustainability agenda. This perspective was also reflected in a scoping review by Walsh et al. who argue that sustainability should be embedded as a core quality principle [27]. Increased awareness of these co-benefits may help initiate a cultural shift towards integrating (environmental) sustainability into clinical decision-making. Some even argue that actions can be taken without clear definition of sustainability concept and the actions are more important than agreeing on definitions [34–36].

Medical prescribing was a topic that most GPs associated with resource consumption, pollution or carbon emissions. Yet, within the current context of ‘biomedicalization of healthcare,’ prescribing medication is not always perceived as an intervention with potential implications for the broader bio-psycho-social system [2]. Pharmaceuticals are major contributors to healthcare sector with GHG emissions, accounting for 10% to 55% of total healthcare emissions, depending on the national healthcare system and the scope of the emission assessment [37]. General practice may mitigate such environmental impacts by addressing polypharmacy and overuse of medications. Conscious prescribing, re-evaluation and deprescribing, alongside greater use of social interventions, can both reduce environmental impact and improve patient outcomes [10]. These changes must be carefully prioritised and include public health initiatives [2,27].

Who must make a change?

Interestingly, the participants sense of responsibility in a sustainability transition of health care differed. In our study, a few GPs exhibited both a willingness to contribute and an inherent sense of responsibility to facilitate sustainable initiatives in general practice. However, there appeared to be a fine line between the autonomy valued in general practice decision-making and a desire for external guidance or strategies to address the challenges related to sustainability. This tension may explain why participants frequently emphasised top-down solutions, despite our initial hypotheses of the importance of bottom-up approaches.

Viewing general practice as a collective system of individual units, organisational change theories can help guide transformation efforts. Kotter’s widely adopted and empirically grounded model for change management emphasises that a clear and shared sense of urgency is essential to initiate and sustain change [38]. Without it, complacency can slow or block progress. This sense of urgency must be shared, not just by leadership but across the entire organisation. While the GPs in this study expressed intention to make more sustainable decisions in clinical practice, with few already having taken initial steps, their overall accounts indicated that concrete visions and strategies were scarce. Furthermore, the interviews conveyed only a limited sense of urgency concerning the local health implications of climate change. Most participants emphasised the need for top-down initiatives, such as national guidelines and strategic visions from the Danish Organization of General Practitioners or the Danish Health Authority.

The results from this qualitative study on general practitioners’ perspectives agree with previous literature on sustainability highlighting a lack of coordination and recognition of the link between human actions that influence the planet negatively, as well as low awareness from clinical community [39,40]. A link between environmental degradation and poorer health outcomes is not always apparent in daily clinical practice, and addressing it does not fall within the existing core responsibilities of GPs [41]. Engaging GPs through open dialogue about current challenges, such as climate change, system demands and potential consequences, can foster ownership and motivate collective action. The GPs already have a wide range of suggestions for making general practice more sustainable, as seen in previous literature [9].

Although professional associations and bottom-up solutions play an important role in raising awareness and driving positive change, effective climate change adaptation and mitigation often require simultaneous actions from national and regional policymakers [42]. In other countries, such as England, clear organisational and national leadership, with a long-term vision for reducing environmental impact of healthcare, has already been established [43]. Without such leadership, this issue risks remaining a low priority, despite its significance for public health and the future resilience of the healthcare system.

Strengths and limitations

To our knowledge, this is the first interview study to explore perspectives of sustainability in general practice in the Nordic countries. The study provides novel insights into how general practitioners currently identify sustainability in general practice and highlights some perceived barriers and opportunities that may inform future research, interventions, and guideline development. The use of semi-structured individual interviews allowed participants to elaborate on their own understandings and experiences, supporting the relevance of the findings to real-world general practice. Trustworthiness was enhanced through reflexive dialogue within the research team, transparent documentation of analytic decisions, and iterative engagement with the dataset. The inclusion of illustrative quotations allows readers to evaluate the coherence between data extracts and the interpretative claims advanced.

However, there are some limitations. A limitation of this study is that the initial coding was conducted by the first author, who was new to reflexive TA. Although reflexive TA does not require multiple coders or inter-rater reliability, analytic depth relies on the researcher’s familiarity with the method and capacity for reflexive engagement. Limited prior experience may have influenced early decisions and theme development, this was mitigated through sustained engagement with methodological literature and iterative discussions within a multidisciplinary research group, which supported reflexive dialogue and strengthened conceptual coherence. However, the analysis reflects the situated and developing analytic practice of the first author. The duration of the interviews was relatively short, which may have limited the depth of exploration of some topics. However, the focused interview design and the consistency across interviews supported comparability and analytical coherence. Another limitation was that most participants were recruited through open calls, which may have introduced self-selection bias by attracting GPs with a particular interest in sustainability, potentially limiting the breadth of perspectives captured. Additionally, social desirability bias cannot be excluded, as like-minded attitudes may be perceived as socially or professionally desirable. While the findings are not intended to be statistically generalisable, these qualitative indicators provide valuable insight into operational perspectives and changes necessary to embed sustainable development into healthcare practices. Although these perspectives are in some extent cultural and contextual dependent, they may nonetheless be transferable to similar primary care contexts and can serve as a foundation for further empirical and intervention-based research in sustainable development in general practice.

Implications for research

Research on sustainability transitions in healthcare is expanding, yet critical gaps remain. These include limited methods for accurately estimating disease burden and health system impacts, insufficient evidence on the effectiveness of interventions to address climate-related health effects, challenges in measuring the carbon footprint of health systems, and a lack of robust evaluations of interventions to decarbonize healthcare services and their associated supply chains [26].

Our study presents GPs’ perceptions of sustainability, which primarily emphasise climate change, resource use, medical prescribing and financial considerations, with limited attention to social dimensions. The GPs seemed aware of the social dimensions of care, but these aspects were not always explicitly framed as sustainability issues. Future research could benefit from articulating this connection more explicitly. To inform the development of targeted guidance, further research is needed. It is necessary to identify which changes and interventions in general practice can most effectively reduce environmental impact while maintaining high-quality and patient safe care. One potential method could involve identifying high-impact areas where interventions would be most effective and could inform policymakers and decision support tools for general practice [44].

Implications for practice

The GPs varied in the extent to which they incorporated sustainable practices into their daily work and expressed a need for guidance to further support the process. This suggests that GPs may benefit from a shared strategy, e.g. through the Danish Organization of General Practitioners. Furthermore, pre- and postgraduate education is important to build a better understanding of the necessary steps in a sustainability transition of health care [27].

Conclusion

Sustainability in general practice demands greater attention from researchers and policymakers. The current situation shows that GPs wish to contribute to a sustainable development of general practice. However, a challenge remains in translating sustainability into clinical language. Advancing this agenda will require clear guidance, practical tools, and further research.

Introduction

There is growing attention to sustainability, both globally and in Denmark, and this has also reached the Danish healthcare system. And with good reason. The World Health Organization has identified climate change as the greatest threat to global health in the twenty first century. The relationship between climate, health, and sustainability in healthcare is complex. Sustainability is not solely about climate; it also encompasses social, economic, and structural dimensions.

As gatekeepers in the Danish healthcare system, general practitioners handle and conclude around 90% of all patient contacts. This positions general practice as a key player in the sustainable transformation of the healthcare sector. GPs are therefore likely to contribute valuable insights and ideas for more sustainable clinical practice.
