Masked pretraining of U-Net for ultrasound image segmentation
Authors: Qi He, Xianghao Cui, Qingjing Fei, Wen Xiong, Yongjie Pang, Wenying Liu, Zhi Chen, Fang Hou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractComputer-aided segmentation of ultrasound images can assist less experienced sonographers in making a diagnosis or performing early screening for diseases in vast rural areas. U-Net is the mainstream method for ultrasound image segmentation, offering advantages of efficient feature extraction and context integration. However, existing U-Net-based segmentation methods can only yield modest performance due to the low quality of ultrasound imaging and the usually small amount of available labeled data. This research proposes a self-supervised pretraining method to promote the performance of U-Net-based models for ultrasound image segmentation, when limited labeled images but numerous relevant unlabeled ones are available, which is a common situation in ultrasonography scenarios such as rare disease detection. By randomly masking out part of pixels in the input ultrasound image, the U-Net based model is pretrained to predict the unknown content, to adapt the model to the current fitting scenario before the formal training. A lightweight but effective variant of U-Net named MS-UNet is also proposed to better fit the scenario of ultrasound image segmentation. Experimental results show that the masked pretraining can boost the segmentation performance of U-Net models on small-size ultrasound image datasets, with Dice score improvements of 6-20 percentage points across four datasets under minimal labeled data conditions. Furthermore, our proposed MS-UNet achieves a relatively high segmentation accuracy while reducing computational complexity by 80.3% in FLOPs and 53.2% in parameters compared to the standard U-Net.Subject terms: Biomedical engineering, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Medical research

Introduction

Ultrasound imaging or ultrasonography is widely used in various medical examination scenarios to create real-time pictures or videos of human organs and soft tissues. Compared with other medical imaging modalities such as X-Ray, computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), ultrasonography uses the high-frequency sound waves and has the advantages of no ionizing radiation, real-time imaging, portability, and low costs. Thus, it is applicable to various healthcare scenes in vast rural areas and emergency scenarios, and is also a safe option for sensitive groups such as pregnant women1. Despite the advantages and the large-scale application of ultrasonography, certain limitations hinder its further advancement, including reduced precision as well as blurred and indistinct borders in some examinations2,3. These inherent image quality issues often result in lower diagnostic accuracy compared to other imaging modalities4–8 such as CT and MRI. In particular, acquiring appropriate ultrasound images and analyzing them correctly to narrow down the diagnosis require a well-trained sonographer. Given the widespread use of convenient and affordable ultrasonography in rural clinics and hospitals for disease screening and follow-up, proficiency in various ultrasonography techniques is essential, such as imaging of blood vessels, the female reproductive system, abdomen, and musculoskeletal system. Additionally, a thorough understanding of anatomy and physiology is typically required. The scarcity of skilled sonographers significantly hampers the efficacy of ultrasonographic applications and may lead to compromised health care.

In recent years, computer-aided medical image processing and interpretation technologies have developed tremendously such as enhancement, semantic segmentation, and classification9,10. By dividing the ultrasound image into meaningful regions automatically based on anatomical structures or objects of interest, segmentation can assist less experienced sonographers in making a diagnosis or performing early screening for diseases. As a dominant model for medical image segmentation, U-Net leverages a U-shaped convolutional neural network (CNN) and skip connections to fuse high-level and low-level features, and has only a small or moderate model size while achieving a good performance on CT and MRI images11. In contrast, ultrasound images have lower spatial resolution and reduced contrast, making it challenging to distinguish between different anatomical structures or identify boundaries accurately. Therefore, the study of ultrasound image segmentation draws continuous attention recently12–15. Above all, having sufficiently accurate and well-annotated ultrasound data is crucial for ensuring that data-driven models, such as U-Net, achieve satisfactory performance. However, collecting such data is often challenging due to privacy policies and the lack of expert annotations16. Particularly, the amount of available labeled data is extremely small for some rare diseases, such as fetal lung occupying and malformation, which limits the role of segmentation as well as computer-aided ultrasonography in rare disease screening.

To improve the performance of segmentation models with limited labeled medical images, one common practice is pretraining the model on another large out-of-domain labeled image dataset and then modifying and fine-tuning it with the in-domain dataset17. Many recent researches on ultrasound image segmentation pretrain U-Net with a large image dataset of grayscale natural salient objects to better mimic ultrasound images by adopting pretrained CNN models like VGGNet, ResNet, or AlexNet as the encoder of U-Net18,19. These pretraining approaches rely on the classification labels or the manual segmentation delineation of cross-domain datasets and can be referred to as supervised pretraining or transfer learning methods. However, considering the intrinsically different characteristics of ultrasound images, the performance of these pretraining methods is degraded due to the possibly vast domain gap. Natural images are typically high-resolution, color images with rich detail, while ultrasound images are grayscale with lower resolution and limited anatomical detail. Additionally, ultrasound images possess unique speckle patterns, anisotropic resolution, and operator-dependent variations that are not present in natural images. Furthermore, medical images follow standardized acquisition protocols, whereas natural images are captured under diverse and random conditions. At last, medical images contain clinically relevant information in their entirety, while natural images often have only partial regions of interest. These fundamental differences make transfer learning from natural to ultrasound domains less effective. This domain gap is further evidenced by our experimental results. As shown in Tables 2, 3, 4, and 5, while cross-domain transfer learning from ImageNet-pretrained ResNet50 (U-Net-RS) does improve performance compared to the randomly initialized model (U-Net-R), there remains significant untapped potential. Our proposed approach, which combines cross-domain transfer learning with masked pretraining (U-Net-RS(P)), achieves further substantial performance gains across all datasets. These results demonstrate that despite its benefits, cross-domain transfer learning alone cannot fully leverage the model’s capacity for ultrasound image segmentation due to the inherent domain differences described above.

Although the labeled ultrasound images in the target scenario or confirming a rare disease are scarce, the unlabeled images in the same or relevant examinations are usually numerous and easy to collect. For example, the case number of the fetal lung occupying such as congenital cystic adenomatoid malformation is too small to train a segmentation model well, while there exists a huge number of normal fetal lung ultrasound images obtained in the fetal checking routine. On the other hand, the recent self-supervised pretraining methods have shown great potential to learn robust general features from massive unlabeled in-domain data without the domain gap issue20,21. Based on Vision Transformer, a series of pretraining methods learn to reconstruct the input images under corruptions by formulating different denoising autoencoders to learn an effective general encoder, such as BEiT22, MAE23, and SimMIM24. Combined with different additional subsequent neural networks, the learned encoder can be fine-tunned to fit different tasks with small-amount labeled data.

This research proposes a self-supervised pretraining method for U-Net-based architectures to promote the performance of computer-aided ultrasound image segmentation, when only few-shot labeled data but numerous relevant unlabeled ones are available. Specifically, by randomly masking part of pixels in the input ultrasound image, the target U-Net model is pretrained to predict the masked content without labels, to adapt the whole model to the current statistical fitting scenario, and the learned parameter weights in both the encoder and the decoder of U-Net are used as the initial state for the subsequent segmentation task. In this way, the feature extraction ability could be largely enhanced by the large-amount unlabeled data, and the segmentation accuracy with quite limited labeled data be greatly improved. Particularly, the resulting model could be also used in turn to segment the remaining unlabeled images to assist annotators determine the exact region boundaries. Besides, we propose another variant of U-Net named multi-scale U-Net (MS-UNet) to better adapt to the scenario of ultrasound image segmentation. Ultrasound images present unique challenges characterized by two primary limitations: low precision2,3,25,26, manifested through reduced resolution, speckle noise, and indistinct tissue boundaries that complicate feature extraction; and semantic simplicity27,28, wherein anatomical structures are represented by basic echo intensity variations with limited textural complexity compared to other imaging modalities. These inherent characteristics necessitate specialized segmentation approaches that can effectively operate despite these constraints while maintaining computational efficiency in clinical implementation. The MS-UNet adopts a novel multi-scale dilated and depthwise separable convolution (MS-DDSC) blocks to capture the dependency between remote pixels, squeeze the redundant information from adjacent pixels, and reduce the model complexity by 80.3% in FLOPs and 53.2% in parameters compared to the standard U-Net. Related works

Image segmentation has always been a hot topic in the field of computer vision, whose task is to label out each pixel of an object or range of interest (ROI). As one representative state-of-the-art model on natural image datasets for semantic segmentation, the latest model Segformer29 is built on the Vision Transformer30 backbone and has surpassed a series of powerful counterparts such as PVT31, DeepLabv3+32, Swin Transformer33, etc., at the same parameter scale. For the ultrasound image segmentation, U-Net11 and its variants still remain dominant34–38 for its data-friendly design to small datasets and the particular characteristics of ultrasound images. Similar to the milestone work fully convolutional networks (FCN)39, U-Net adopts the classic auto-encoding structure but replaces the fully connected layer with a symmetrically transposed convolutional structure to well predict the class label of each pixel. Additionally, U-Net employs skip connections to integrate shallow simple features with deep abstract ones by directly connecting the outputs of each stage within the contracting path to the corresponding stages in the expansive path. This structure has become dominant in the field of medical image segmentation for its effectiveness, and its several variants have been derived to progress the state of the art.

Instead of restricting skip connections to the same level features in the encoder-decoder path of U-Net, a U-Net variant named UNet++40 redesigned skip pathways to make the feature maps of encoder undergo a dense convolutional block like a pyramid aggregating feature of different semantic scales. Another design named Sharp U-Net41 uses a delicate sharpening spatial kernel on encoder features and then performs fusion with decoder features termed “sharp block”. This connection mechanism successfully reduces the semantic gap between the features of the encoder and decoder. Besides skip connection enhancements, multi-resolution blocks are viewed as a helpful design to tackle the difficulty of analyzing ROIs at different view scales. MultiResUnet34 develops a novel inception-like block by gradually increasing 33 filters and fusion with a residual connection to realize the multi-resolution analysis with less memory demand. Despite the effectiveness of the U-Net architecture, most medical image segmentation models still struggle to resolve the problem of limited data quantity.

Recent studies have explored the use of pretraining methods to alleviate the data deficiency problem in medical image segmentation42. By training a segmentation model on a larger dataset before fine-tuning it on the target small dataset, a better initialization of model parameters is obtained. This helps the model better extract underlying features of ultrasound images thus enabling it to converge faster and achieve better accuracy. In this way, the limited in-domain data is utilized more efficiently. Generally, the existing pretraining methods can be divided into two categories: supervised and self-supervised methods. Supervised pretraining methods use labeled data from a source domain, such as natural images, to train a model for use in a different target domain, such as medical images, which have been widely used in medical image segmentation. For example, the encoder in the U-Net backbone can be replaced by a pre-trained network such as VGG1643, ResNet5044, etc whose parameters are trained on a large dataset named ImageNet to improve the feature extraction capability. Architecture like TernausNet1645 and U-Net-ResNet5046 use pre-trained networks VGG16 and ResNet50 respectively for segmentation task. However, supervised pretraining methods have limitations such as the dependency on the large-scale labeled dataset and the domain gap between the labeled dataset and the target one.

Self-supervised pretraining methods have recently emerged as a promising alternative to supervised pretraining methods, as they can learn more general and robust features from unlabeled in-domain data without the domain gap issue. Self-supervised pretraining methods have been applied to medical image segmentation with different architectures, such as ViTs or autoencoding networks20,47–49. Autoencoding refers to a classical method for learning representations, which consists of an encoder to map the input to a latent representation and a decoder to reconstruct the input. Denoising autoencoders (DAE) are autoencoders that corrupt the input and learn to reconstruct the original uncorrupted input. A series of methods can be considered as a generalized DAE under different corruptions, e.g., masking pixels, masking visual tokens, or masking image patches. BEiT22 uses the variational autoencoder to assign a token value to each patch, making the model predict discrete tokens. Unlike BEiT, MAE23 and SimMIM24 do not predict the token value of the masked patch but directly predict the masked patch, which is an end-to-end approach. While SimMIM and MAE share this conceptual similarity, they differ significantly in that simMIM uses all patches as input including masked patches (replaced with mask tokens), whereas MAE only feeds unmasked patches to the encoder and completely removes masked patches from the input. In the decoder stage, MAE then adds mask tokens and reintegrates them with original visible patches, then lets the decoder output to reconstruct the entire image.

The proposed pretraining mechanism in this research conforms to the ideas of these methods but differs from them in many ways, as shown in Table 1. Unlike the fixed patch-based masking in transformer models like BEiT, MAE, and SimMIM, our approach enhances the image segmentation capability of U-Net models by implementing flexible block-based masking directly on the original ultrasound image, allowing masks to be positioned at any arbitrary location rather than confined to predetermined patch boundaries. Similar to MAE and SimMIM but unlike BEiT, our method performs pixel-level reconstruction rather than token prediction, preserving maximum image detail fidelity. Besides, it takes the entire masked image as input, unlike MAE which completely removes the masked portions, thereby preserving spatial positional information. Particularly, different from the other self-supervised pretraining methods, we employ a unified architecture where both encoder and decoder of U-Net are preserved from pretraining to fine-tuning, enabling all pretrained parameters to be utilized for the segmentation task without requiring additional modules.

Methods

Masked pretraining approach

Our masked pretraining is a simple autoencoder approach which reconstructs the original image given the partial observation. Different from existing masked autoencoder methods, U-Net and its variants are used as the pretraining backbone to leverage the advantages of U-Net based models in the field of medical image segmentation. The whole U-Net structure is utilized for subsequent supervised segmentation training, and the parameters in both the encoder and decoder obtained by pretraining are used as the initial state. Specifically, we randomly mask part of pixels following a masking strategy, and use the U-Net based model to map the observable image content to the latent representation and then reconstruct the masked pixels. We chose pixel-level direct reconstruction over discrete token prediction (e.g., BEiT) or masked contrastive learning primarily because of the nature of ultrasound imaging. Given the relatively poor image quality of ultrasound data, abstract token prediction approaches would likely lose important fine-grained details that are critical for diagnosis. In contrast, our pixel reconstruction approach forces the model to fit more precise image structural details, preserving clinically relevant information. Furthermore, unlike token-based Transformer models, U-Net inherently operates in the pixel domain, making direct pixel-wise mask manipulation more straightforward and computationally efficient. Additionally, the reconstruction objective perfectly aligns with downstream segmentation tasks as both require pixel-level understanding of the image. This methodological consistency also enables the pretraining and segmentation tasks to share the same model structure, thereby maximizing parameter transfer efficiency and improving overall performance. Importantly, we specifically reconstruct only the masked regions rather than the entire image, following similar reasoning to MAE and SimMIM methodologies. Since non-masked regions contain already-known information, allocating computational resources to reconstruct these visible areas is redundant. By computing loss exclusively on the masked regions, we enhance the efficiency of gradient propagation during training. This design choice is empirically validated in Table 7, where models pretrained with masked-region-only reconstruction demonstrate superior downstream segmentation performance compared to those pretrained with full-image reconstruction variants. This self-supervised reconstruction training is conducted on a large amount of unlabeled ultrasound image data, as shown in Fig. 1. This pretraining approach could be well extended to other models for the semantic segmentation of medical or other types of images, and we leave it for future investigation.

The masked pretraining architecture. A small proportion of pixels in the original image are selected by random patches and masked out, and the U-Net model is applied to the remaining pixels to reconstruct the original image in pixels. The training loss is computed by comparing the real and predicted pixels in the masked area.

Existing masked autoencoder methods such as BEiT, MAE, simMIM rely on Vision Transformer backbones which use the image patch as the input, and thus their masking operation relies on uniformly aligned non-overlapping patches and each patch in the input image is either fully masked or fully visible. In contrast, the U-Net models work directly on pixels and therefore the mask patch in the proposed masking strategy does not need to be limited by the regular image partition and can be more flexible and randomly movable. As there exist high spatial redundancy in the ultrasound image and a masked patch may easily be estimated from visible neighboring patches, we explore the influence of different masking sizes and masking ratios pretraining, to create an appropriate task that requires high-level understanding of the image beyond low-level image statistics and enable the pre-trained model to have good feature extraction capabilities.

Some existing pretraining methods reuse only part of the model such as the encoder in the fine-tunning stage35,37. However, we believe that the decoder and skip connection structures in U-Net contain rich transmission of image details and restoration of high-level semantic information from low-level pixels, and are also helpful for subsequent segmentation tasks. Particularly, discarding the pretrained parameters of the decoder makes the initial state of the encoder and the decoder unbalanced and brings trouble to the merging of the decoder and the encoder features from the skip connection, and thus reduces the fitting ability of the model on the small-scale dataset. Furthermore, the pixel values in the grayscale ultrasound image can be normalized to the range [0, 1] that is consistent with the output range of U-Net with the activation function Sigmoid for the segmentation task that differentiates the prediction at each pixel between 0 and 1. Hence, we adopt the same model structure for pretraining as the following supervised task for segmentation, and use all the parameter in the encoder and decoder learned by pretraining as the initial values in the segmentation training. For comparison, we also study a variant which only reserves the encoder parameters for segmentation training, and another one that adds additional neural structures such as convolution layers behind U-Net for the segmentation task. The experimental results are shown in Table 7.

By regarding the U-Net model as an autoencoder, the proposed masked pretraining tries to reconstruct the input image at a full resolution by predicting each pixel value in a regression manner. To evaluate the reconstruction quality, by normalizing pixel values to the range [0, 1], we employ a mean squared error (MSE) or loss function that measures the pixel-wise difference between the reconstructed and original images. The computation of the loss only pertains to the masked area to improve the training efficiency, which is akin to MAE23 and BERT50 techniques. Besides, we also consider another two variants, wherein the former computes the loss on all pixels (rather than just on masked regions as in our proposed approach) and the latter uses an or a smooth- loss on the masked area, inspired by SimMIM which employs loss. Detailed experimental results of these loss function variants can be found in Table 7. In future work, we plan to explore additional loss function variants to further optimize our pretraining approach.

We propose a simple masking strategy where each pixel has the same probability of being masked. As shown in Fig. 2, for an ultrasound image with a width of w pixels and a height of h pixels, we mask the image with a square patch of side length p which allows overlapping following a uniform distribution. By extending the image up and left by p pixels, and selecting any pixel from the extended image with equal probability as the upper left corner to locate the masking patch, any pixel in the original image is covered by this patch with the same probability . The proposed masked pretraining approach, like other self-supervised methods, incurs computational overhead only during the pretraining phase, a one-time cost. Once pretrained, the model operates with the same computational complexity during inference as conventionally trained models, adding no extra burden. During deployment, the network architecture and forward pass remain unchanged, preserving inference efficiency. This is particularly beneficial in clinical settings with limited computational resources, as the enhanced feature representations and improved performance on downstream segmentation tasks, especially in low-data regimes common in medical ultrasound imaging, significantly outweigh the initial computational investment.

MS-Unet model

The U-Net model has two prominent characteristics, namely the U-shaped structure and skip connection. The multi-layer CNN in the encoder block works to distill fine-grained features from the input raw image to coarse-grained features which are fed to the bottleneck, and vice verse the CNN in the decoder block works to obtain the pixel-level segmentation prediction. The skip connections makes the high-level features map recovered in the decoder block contain low-level ones, and features with different scales can be fused to better fit the segmentation task. However, ultrasound images have several different features that limit the effect of U-Net. Firstly, the semantics in the ultrasound image are relatively simple and the histological structure from ultrasonography is rather fixed. Secondly, due to low precision and blurring borders, both high-level semantic information and low-level features are important for segmentation. Thirdly, because of the limited amount of labeled data, the model for ultrasound image segmentation should not be too large to avoid over-fitting, which, however, makes U-Net prevalent in ultrasound image segmentation.

Considering the above characteristics of ultrasound images, there still exist three limitations of U-Net hindering its further success. At first, although its CNN-based encoder tries to refine semantic information from raw features, this process is fully end-to-end and lacks intermediate supervision or guidance to exploit both important low-level and high-level information. Secondly, U-Net is built on fully CNNs whose complexity is still relatively high for deployment at edge devices such as ultrasonic instruments. At last, the limited coverage area of convolutional operations prevents U-Net from capturing the dependency between far-apart pixels, and much information redundancy in adjacent points exist because of the simple semantics in ultrasound images.

To address above limitations, this research proposes a novel U-Net based model for the segmentation of ultrasound images, referred to as multi-scale U-Net (MS-UNet). The architecture of MS-UNet is shown in Fig. 3, following the U-shaped structure of U-Net. Different from a normal U-Net, the size of the input image is reduced with the average pooling for three times, whereby the original image of pixels is successively downsampled to produce images of , , and ultimately pixels at the smallest scale, resulting in four input images at different resolutions. The obtained multi-scaled input (MSI) images are integrated into the four layers of the encoder in sequence, to provide intermediate guidance and assist the encoder distill the appropriate high-level semantics from low-level features maps. Specifically, in each layer of the encoder, the features are extracted from the scaled image by a convolution layer with a rectified linear unit (ReLU) activation, and concatenated with the output features from the preceding layer, and then fed into the proposed multi-scale dilated and depthwise separable convolution (MS-DDSC) block with three convolution layers detailed in the next subsection. The output of the MS-DDSC block is compressed by a max pooling and delivered to the next layer. In the bottleneck of MS-Unet, this feature map is fed into an MS-DDSC block and then passed to the decoder part.

The MS-UNet architecture.

In each layer of the decoder, features from the lower layer of the decoder and the output of the MS-DDSC block in the corresponding encoder layer are concatenated, known as the skip connection, and fed into a simplified variant of MS-DDSC named “MS-DDSC Block2” without the feature fusion between layers. Note that the MS-DDSC Block2 consists of two MS-DDSC layers without the feature fusion between layers. We intentionally simplify the decoder structure to reduce parameter count. The obtained feature map is passed through a transposed convolution layer and delivered to the upper layer except in the top layer where the feature map is fed to a convolution layer with the Sigmoid activation to obtain the binary class prediction for each pixel.

MS-DDSC block

This research proposes the MS-DDSC block as the basic feature extraction unit to capture the relationship between more distant pixels to improve the fitting ability of U-Net as well as reduce its complexity. The detail of the MS-DDSC block is shown in Fig. 4. Firstly, we substitute the conventional convolutions in U-Net with dilated ones to enlarge their receptive field. By inserting holes between elements, the dilated convolution can ignore neighboring redundancy to some extent and cover a much larger area than the normal one. In Fig. 4, the proposed MS-DDSC block consists of three dilated convolution layers, whose dilation rates are 1, 2, and 4 in sequence and can provide receptive fields of , , and , respectively. With this multi-layer and multi-scale convolution operation, the MS-DDSC block has a much larger receptive field within which each point is involved in the feature calculation. For comparison, the three-layer standard convolution has a receptive field. Thus, with the same parameter number, MS-DDSC reduces the reuse of adjacent pixels and enhances information distillation ability of U-Net, and thus is especially suitable for semantically simple and single-channel ultrasound images.

Experiments

Settings

In the experiments, the input grayscale ultrasound images have one single channel and are unified to . In both the pretraining and fine-tunning processes, the online data augmentation is conducted for each training batch including random cropping and scaling with a random ratio in the range [0.67, 1], and vertical and horizontal flips. Due to the slight difference in test results of different training trials, each result shown below is the average of five independent training trials with different random seeds. In pretraining, the masking ratio and masking size are set to 0.2 and for default, respectively. These parameter settings will be discussed in detail later in this paper.

We add two MS-UNet versions into comparison. The first is the default one where the filter numbers of the four convolutional layers in the encoder for feature extraction from MSI are set to be 16, 32, 64, and 128 in sequence, and the decoder has a symmetrical structure with the encoder. For an input ultrasound image of size , the resulting MS-UNet model has about 3.6 million parameters. For comparison, we also consider a larger MS-UNet with 57 million parameters and denote it as “MS-UNet-L”, where the four layers in the encoder consist of 64, 128, 256, and 512 filters, respectively.

In both the pretraining and the formal segmentation training processes, the Adamax optimizer is adopted with and and the batch size is set to 10. The epoch number in pretraining varies with different dataset sizes. In the formal training, the appropriate learning rates and warmup ratios are different over datasets, and the learning rate for a given dataset changes following the warmup strategy and the cosine function. For simplicity, a fixed learning rate is used in pretraining.

Datasets

We verify the effectiveness of the pretraining scheme and the proposed method on four ultrasound image datasets. In all the datasets, for a patient having multiple ultrasound images, images belonging to the same patient are either used for training or testing, and in other words cannot be used partially for training and partially for testing for fairness.

DDTI: An optimized version of the open access database DDTI for thyroid nodules with a total of 637 ultrasound images and their segmentation labels from 299 cases51, which is processed on the original DDTI52 by removing damaged items, cropping large irrelevant regions, and other optimizations.

TN3k: TN3k is an open access dataset consisting of a total of 3493 thyroid nodule ultrasound images with the segmentation labels collected from 2421 cases53.

FLUIS: To facilitate the intelligent analysis of fetal lung development, we constructed a dataset named fetal lung ultrasound images for segmentation (FLUIS), including 3148 images of normally developing fetal lungs obtained by the prenatal ultrasonography at the 4-chamber heart view. Due to the lack of enough expert annotations, the manual segmentation of the largest possible fetal lung area is conducted on only dozens of images.

CDHUIS: Space-occupying lesions in the thoracic cavity is one of the main reasons of fetal lung immaturity and is mainly discovered and diagnosed through prenatal ultrasonography, and the congential diaphragmatic hernia (CDH) is one major cause. Because of data scarcity, we collected a small dataset on the CDH condition, referred to as CDH ultrasound images for segmentation (CDHUIS) that contains 94 images belonging to 17 cases, which are annotated by one senior sonographer.

The DDTI and TN3k datasets are fully anonymous and publicly available. The specific data access information will be provided in the Data Availability section. The FLUIS and CDHUIS datasets were constructed in this retrospective study, and all data were fully anonymized following standard medical data protection protocols. No personally identifiable information was involved at any stage of the data collection, processing, or analysis process. This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval, including a waiver of informed consent due to the retrospective nature of the study and use of anonymized data, was granted by the Ethics Committee of Sichuan Provincial People’s Hospital, University of Electronic Science and Technology of China (Approval No. 2024-783).

Next, we show the effect of different masking ratios in pretraining on the segmentation performance by fixing the masking patch size at , and the result is shown in Fig. 8. It can be seen that a good segmentation performance can be obtained when the masking ratio varies from 0.2 to 0.6 and the performance degrades sharply with a ratio larger than 0.7. This result differs from masked pretraining methods23 and simMIM24 which demonstrate that a high masking ratio such as 0.75 leads to a better performance in follow-up supervised tasks for natural image processing. This may be partly due to the intrinsic difference between natural and ultrasound images. With massive data resources and rich color diversity information, the masked pattern in the natural image could be well predicted with only small portion of the image. However, limited by the small data size in supervised training and the one-channel and low precision characteristics of ultrasound images, it is harder to infer the masked part from the known area, and thus a relatively lower mask ratio may be suitable for the masked pretraining on ultrasound images.

The visual comparison of reconstruction by MS-UNet pretrained with different mask ratios are shown at Fig. 9 where the masking size is fixed at . It shows that MS-UNet can predict the rough texture of the masked area while still get a blurry prediction in the masked areas when the mask ratio is no more than 0.7. For this semantically plausible reconstruction effect, we conclude that this pretrained model learned a semantically high-level representation of the ultrasound images.

We also conduct experiments with different pretraining strategies to verify their effect. The mask size and mask ratio are fixed at and 0.2 (the optimal parameter combination selected from experiments in Table 6 and Fig. 8), respectively, and the pretraining epoch number is set to 100. We tried the following pretraining variants in order, and the performance is shown in Table 7 where each score is averaged over 5 trials. Firstly, we built a larger model for pertaining (Additional layers in pretraining), that is, two additional convolution layers are added behind the output layer of MS-UNet as the model for pretraining and only the MS-UNet part is used in the subsequent segmentation training. Note that the activation function Sigmoid is shifted to the new output layer in the pretraining model. From Table 7, it can be seen that its performance is slightly worse than the default one. Secondly, with the same pretraining process with the default one, we freeze the encoder in the supervised training (Frozen encoder), or use only the pretrained encoder as the initial state in the supervised training but initialize the decoder parameters randomly (Only encoder parameters). The former results in a drastic degradation in performance, and the latter brings slight negative impact. The above results show that using the same U-Net based model in pretraining and formal training and the whole obtained parameters as the initial state for supervised training can lead to the best segmentation performance. It is quite different from existing multi-layer neural network based transfer learning, where using obtained parameters in the bottom layers as the initialization may be more effective. It may be partly due to that the skip connection in U-Net and its variants blurs the boundary between upper and lower layer features and the decoder also contains low-level features. At last, we also tried different losses in pretraining, such as restoring all the pixels in the masked and unmasked areas (Restoring all pixels), or using the smooth L1 loss or regular L1 loss of pixels in the masked area. The results show that restoring all the pixels brings down the segmentation accuracy greatly, and the smooth L1 loss has a similar effect with the default L2 one.

Limitation and future work

Despite the demonstrated effectiveness of our masked pretraining approach, we acknowledge several limitations in the current work that warrant further investigation. Our results clearly demonstrate that the benefits of the proposed pretraining approach are most pronounced when labeled training data is limited. As the volume of labeled data increases, we observe a gradual decrease in the performance gains provided by our pretraining strategy. This suggests that in scenarios where abundant data is available, the marginal utility of our pretraining approach may be constrained. Our experiments reveal that the optimal masking parameters differ significantly from those reported in natural image pretraining literature. Specifically, while methods like MAE recommend high masking ratios (e.g., 0.75) on natural image dataset, our approach achieves optimal performance with substantially lower masking ratios (approximately 0.2). This discrepancy underscores the significant domain differences between natural and medical images and indicates that parameter optimization is highly data-dependent. Although we provide quantitative and qualitative results for different masking sizes and ratios, our work lacks an in-depth discussion of the parameter trade-offs for various application scenarios. While our approach shows promising results on the medical imaging tasks investigated in this study, its generalizability across a broader spectrum of medical imaging modalities and tasks remains underexplored.

Several promising directions arise from the limitations identified. First, we aim to develop advanced pretraining methodologies that sustain performance gains even with abundant data. Additionally, future research will examine the relationship between optimal pretraining settings and dataset characteristics across diverse medical imaging modalities, including CT, MRI, ultrasound, and pathology images. This exploration seeks to establish more generalizable guidelines for parameter selection based on modality-specific image properties such as contrast, resolution, and anatomical complexity. Furthermore, a comprehensive investigation into the interplay between different masking parameters (size and ratio) and their impact on various downstream tasks is necessary. The trade-offs associated with different masking parameters across diverse application contexts represent a critical research focus. Such analysis would help identify task-specific optimal configurations and provide insights into the underlying mechanisms driving these preferences. Lastly, evaluating the transferability of representations learned through our masked pretraining approach across different medical imaging domains and tasks remains a key objective. These efforts will help determine the extent to which general medical visual representations can be shared across specialties, potentially reducing the need for specialty-specific pretraining.

Conclusion

This research proposed a novel masked pretraining mechanism for automated segmentation of ultrasound images, where only limited in-domain labeled images but abundant relevant unlabeled ones are available. A new U-Net variant, referred to as MS-UNet, is also proposed for the ultrasound image segmentation. Extensive experiments show that the masked pretraining greatly enhances the performance of U-Net based models on small-volume ultrasound image datasets, and MS-UNet shows high efficacy in ultrasound image segmentation with minimal complexity. Besides, different pretraining strategies including model, loss and masking changes, as well as new components in MS-UNet are studied to evaluate their impact.
