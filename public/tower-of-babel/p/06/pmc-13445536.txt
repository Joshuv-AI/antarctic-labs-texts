QQS‐Responsive Soybean Genes Increase Protein and Reduce Starch
Authors: Ethan Brister, Sharnali Das, Guanghui Xiao, Shuangshuang Weng, Shaoqi Wang, Lei Wang, Ling Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTImproving protein accumulation without undesirable effects on plant growth remains an important goal in plant physiology and crop improvement. The orphan gene Qua‐Quine Starch (QQS) from Arabidopsis thaliana increases protein and reduces starch across several plant species, but the downstream genes associated with these effects remain poorly defined. This study aimed to identify QQS‐responsive genes in soybean ( Glycine max ) and determine whether they can reproduce the characteristic protein and starch phenotypes of QQS when expressed in A. thaliana . Previously published RNA‐sequencing datasets from QQS‐overexpressing (QQS‐OE) Arabidopsis leaves and QQS‐expressing (QQS‐E) soybean leaves were compared to identify conserved differentially expressed genes. Nine soybean genes showing consistent QQS‐responsiveness, including eight upregulated genes and one downregulated gene, were expressed individually in A. thaliana . Total protein was quantified in leaves and seeds, whereas starch was measured in leaves. Candidate promoters were analyzed for transcription factor motif enrichment, and the reporter activation capacity of DNA‐tethered QQS was examined in yeast. The eight upregulated soybean genes significantly increased total protein and reduced starch in A. thaliana , whereas the downregulated gene produced the opposite phenotype, decreasing protein and increasing starch. Plant growth and morphology were largely unaffected. Candidate promoters shared motifs associated with nitrogen metabolism, sugar signaling, and plastidial functions. DNA‐tethered QQS activated reporter expression in yeast, although this assay does not establish transcriptional activation with these soybean genes. These results identify a conserved QQS‐responsive gene module that coordinately influences protein and starch accumulation and provides candidate genes for improving crop protein content.Keywords: Arabidopsis thaliana , Glycine max , primary metabolism, protein–starch balance, Qua‐Quine Starch (QQS)

Introduction

Carbon and nitrogen allocation is a central determinant of plant metabolism, growth, and nutritional quality. The orphan gene Qua‐Quine Starch (QQS) from Arabidopsis thaliana regulates this allocation by decreasing starch while increasing soluble protein in leaves and seeds (Li et al. 2009, 2015; Li and Wurtele 2015; Qi et al. 2019). Notably, this phenotype is transferable across species, including soybean ( Glycine max ), rice, and maize, making QQS a promising target for improving crop protein content. Previous studies have shown that QQS interacts with NF‐YC transcription factor complexes, supporting a role in transcription‐associated regulation (Li et al. 2015). However, the downstream metabolic regulators that execute QQS‐driven carbon and nitrogen reallocation remain only partly understood.

Soybean ( Glycine max ) plays a central role in global agriculture because of its high seed protein content and broad use in human food and animal feed. As demand for sustainable, plant‐based protein sources continues to increase, improving soybean nutritional quality has become a major priority. However, increasing seed protein without compromising yield remains challenging because of the inherent trade‐offs between carbon storage and nitrogen assimilation.

Cross‐species transcriptomic integration provides an opportunity to identify conserved components of QQS‐mediated pathways. If soybean genes regulated by QQS can also reproduce the characteristic increase in protein and decrease in starch associated with QQS expression when expressed in A. thaliana , this would reveal a conserved metabolic module with translational value for crop improvement. To address this possibility, we compared RNA‐sequencing datasets from wild‐type (WT) and QQS‐overexpressing (QQS‐OE) Arabidopsis leaves and from WT and QQS‐expressing (QQS‐E) soybean leaves. The objectives of this study were to identify QQS‐responsive genes conserved between G. max and A. thaliana and to determine whether these soybean genes reproduce the protein‐increasing, starch‐reducing phenotype characteristic of QQS. We hypothesized that conserved, QQS‐responsive soybean orthologs form a functional metabolic module that acts downstream of the orphan gene to regulate primary metabolism.

In this study, we identified nine soybean genes that were consistently responsive to QQS across soybean and Arabidopsis transcriptomic datasets and tested their functions by heterologous expression in A. thaliana . We then evaluated their effects on total protein and starch accumulation and examined promoter features associated with their regulation. Our results define a concise QQS‐responsive gene module that recapitulates the characteristic metabolic shift toward increased protein and reduced starch, providing new insight into the downstream regulatory framework through which QQS influences primary metabolism.

Materials and Methods

Plant Materials and Growth Conditions

Arabidopsis thaliana (Col‐0) was used for transformation and as control for all experiments. Plants were grown in controlled‐environment chambers (Percival Scientific, model LT‐105) under long‐day (LD) conditions (16 h light/8 h dark) at 22°C and 60% relative humidity. Illumination was provided by full‐spectrum LED arrays (Percival Scientific) at a photosynthetic photon flux density of 150 μmol·m−2·s−1 PAR at canopy height. Plants were grown in Fafard 4P potting mix (Sun Gro Horticulture).

Identification of QQS‐Responsive Soybean Genes

Previously published RNA‐seq datasets from QQS‐overexpressing A. thaliana leaves and QQS‐expressing G. max leaves (O'Conner et al. 2018) were reanalyzed. Soybean genes were defined “QQS‐responsive” if they and their Arabidopsis orthologs were significantly regulated and showed the same direction of change in both species. Eight genes with the strongest upregulation were selected for functional analysis. In addition, Glyma08g43780 was also included because it consistently showed reduced transcript abundance in both QQS genotypes and therefore served as a contrasting control for functional evaluation. The soybean transcriptomic data were obtained from previously generated QQS‐expressing soybean plants (Li and Wurtele 2015). In the present study, the identified candidate soybean genes were functionally evaluated by heterologous expression in A. thaliana ; candidate‐gene transgenic soybean plants were not generated or analyzed.

Cloning and Arabidopsis Transformation

Full‐length soybean cDNAs were amplified and inserted into Gateway vector pB2GW7 under the control of the cauliflower mosaic virus 35S promoter (Katzen 2007). To guide tissue selection for RNA extraction from soybean cv. Williams 82, the highest expression level of each candidate gene was identified using the Soybean electronic Fluorescent Pictograph (eFP) Browser (https://bar.utoronto.ca/efpsoybean/cgi‐bin/efpWeb.cgi) (Winter et al. 2007). Constructs were introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101 and transformed into A. thaliana by the floral dip method as previously described (Li and Wurtele 2015). T1 seeds were screened on one‐half‐strength Murashige and Skoog medium to identify positive transgenic lines, which were then transplanted to soil (Li et al. 2009). T2 plants were used for subsequent analysis. Two independent lines were analyzed for each gene.

Quantitative Reverse Transcription PCR (RT‐qPCR)

Plants grown under LD conditions were harvested at the end of the light period, and two independent samples from each line were subjected to RT‐qPCR analysis. RNA was extracted from T2 Arabidopsis leaves using TRIzol reagent, followed by DNase I treatment to remove genomic DNA and subsequent purification as described previously (Li et al. 2009). cDNA was synthesized using iScript Reverse Transcription Supermix (Bio‐Rad). Quantitative PCR was performed with PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix on a CFX96 thermocycler (Bio‐Rad). ACTIN2 was used as the reference gene (Wang et al. 2019). Relative transcript abundance was calculated using the comparative Ct method (2−ΔΔCt) with Col‐0 as the control. Three biological replicates and three technical replicates were analyzed.

Protein and Starch Quantification

Total protein was quantified from rosette leaves and mature seeds of T2 Arabidopsis using an adapted Lowry protein method (Lowry et al. 1951; Wang et al. 2024). Protein concentration was determined colorimetrically with the Pierce Modified Lowry Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Starch was quantified enzymatically using the D‐Glucose Assay Kit (GOPOD Format; Megazyme International) as described previously (Li et al. 2009). Leaves were harvested at the end of the light period to capture maximal starch accumulation.

Each measurement was based on three biological replicates from three independent experiments.

Promoter Motif Analysis

Promoter sequences corresponding to the 2 kb region upstream of the translation start codon were retrieved from Soybase (https://www.soybase.org/) for soybean genes. Motif enrichment was analyzed using the transcription factor binding site prediction tool in PlantRegMap (https://plantregmap.gaolab.org/binding_site_prediction.php) with a threshold of p < 1 × 10−6. To evaluate background enrichment, 100 randomly selected soybean promoters from non‐differentially expressed genes were analyzed in parallel.

Tethered Transcriptional Activation Assay

To test whether QQS has transcriptional activation capacity when tethered to DNA, the full‐length coding sequence of QQS obtained from TAIR (https://www.arabidopsis.org/) was amplified from A. thaliana cDNA and cloned into the pGBKT7 and pGBKT7‐VP16 vectors (Takara Bio). The empty pGBKT7 vector was used as a negative control, and pGBKT7‐VP16 served as a positive control. Recombinant constructs were transformed into Saccharomyces cerevisiae strain AH109 using the lithium acetate method.

Single colonies were selected from SD/−Trp plates and inoculated into 3 mL liquid SD/−Trp medium. Cultures were incubated at 30°C with shaking at 220 rpm for 12 h. Cell densities were then adjusted to comparable optical densities using a UV spectrophotometer. Subsequently, 2 μL of each yeast suspension was spotted onto SD/−Trp, SD/−Trp/–His, SD/−Trp/–His supplemented with 2 mM 3‐amino‐1,2,4‐triazole (3‐AT; Sigma‐Aldrich), and SD/−Trp/–His supplemented with 2 mM 3‐AT and X‐α‐Gal (GoldBio). Plates were incubated at 30°C for 3 days, after which yeast growth and colony color were recorded to evaluate transcriptional activation capacity.

Yeast One‐Hybrid Assay for Promoter Interaction

To test whether QQS directly interacts with candidate promoter sequences, promoter fragments corresponding to the 2 kb region upstream of the translation start codon were retrieved from SoyBase (https://www.soybase.org/) for soybean genes and from TAIR (https://www.arabidopsis.org/) for the corresponding A. thaliana orthologs. Each promoter fragment was cloned into the pLacZi vector. The full‐length coding sequence of QQS was cloned into the pJG4‐5 vector. The bait vector (pLacZi‐promoter) and prey vector (pJG4‐5‐AtQQS) were co‐transformed into S. cerevisiae strain EGY48 using the lithium acetate method.

Positive transformants were selected on SD/−Trp/–Ura dropout medium and transferred to fresh medium of the same composition for 1 day of expansion. Colonies were then streaked onto SD/−Trp/–Ura dropout medium containing 0.02 mg mL−1 X‐gal (5‐bromo‐4‐chloro‐3‐indolyl‐β‐D‐galactopyranoside) for growth and reporter gene detection. Plates were incubated at 30°C for 3 days, after which yeast growth and colony color were recorded. The empty pJG4‐5 (JG‐45) and empty pLacZi vectors were used as controls.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using Student's t‐test in GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software). For RT‐qPCR analyses, each biological replicate consisted of five independent plants pooled together, each biological replicate was analyzed using three technical replicates, and three independent experiments were performed (n = 15 plants total). For total protein and starch measurements, each biological replicate consisted of five pooled individual plants, and the assays were conducted in three independent experiments. Data are presented as mean ± SEM (standard error of the mean). The numbers and definitions of biological and technical replicates are also provided in the corresponding figure legends.

Results and Discussion

Identification of Nine Conserved, QQS ‐Responsive Soybean Genes

Comparison of previously published RNA‐sequencing data from QQS‐OE Arabidopsis and QQS‐E soybean identified nine soybean genes with conserved QQS‐responsive expression patterns (Figure 1A; Table 1). Eight of these genes were consistently upregulated in QQS‐E soybean leaves and their orthologs were upregulated in QQS‐OE Arabidopsis seedlings, whereas one gene, Glyma08g43780, showed the opposite pattern and was therefore included as a contrasting control in subsequent functional analyses. Because these soybean genes were identified based on conserved differential expression patterns in both A. thaliana and soybean transcriptomic datasets, their corresponding A. thaliana orthologs are also responsive to QQS at the transcriptional level. These genes were prioritized based on their association with protein accumulation and their predicted metabolic functions, and were subsequently tested by heterologous expression in A. thaliana , rather than by generating candidate‐gene transgenic soybean plants.

Functional categorization of the corresponding A. thaliana orthologs indicated that these QQS‐responsive genes are associated with subcellular compartments and molecular functions relevant to primary metabolism (Table 1). Most orthologs were localized to the chloroplast or nucleus, with one showing dual localization, suggesting roles in coordinating metabolic and transcriptional processes. Gene ontology annotations indicated enrichment in protein‐binding and DNA‐binding activities, as well as transferase activity and broader biosynthetic and metabolic processes. Notably, multiple soybean genes corresponded to the same A. thaliana ortholog, AT3G47650, suggesting potential functional convergence within the QQS‐responsive network. The negatively correlated gene, Glyma08g43780 (AT5G48150), was also associated with nuclear localization and DNA‐binding activity, indicating that antagonistic regulatory components may operate within this module. Together, these features support the existence of a conserved QQS‐responsive gene module integrating transcriptional regulation with metabolic control.

These candidate gene features are consistent with prior studies of QQS itself. In A. thaliana , QQS is strongly expressed in leaves and vascular‐associated tissues, where carbon assimilation and source–sink partitioning are highly active, and its transcript abundance responds to developmental and metabolic cues (Li et al. 2009). Subcellular localization analyses previously indicated that QQS is predominantly cytosolic (Li et al. 2009), whereas subsequent work showed that QQS physically interacts with NF‐YC4 and that the QQS–NF‐YC4 complex can be detected in both the cytosol and nucleus (Li et al. 2015), providing a plausible route by which QQS influences transcriptional regulation of downstream pathways. The enrichment of chloroplast‐ and nuclear‐associated candidate genes identified here aligns with a model in which QQS may link metabolic sensing in the cytosol to transcriptional and metabolic reprogramming that promotes carbon and nitrogen reallocation.

To determine the tissues in which these genes are most highly expressed, we analyzed publicly available soybean transcriptomic datasets using the electronic Fluorescent Pictograph (eFP) Browser (Winter et al. 2007). Six of the eight positively regulated genes showed maximal expression in leaves, whereas the remaining two were most highly expressed in green pods and flowers, and the negatively regulated gene showed high expression in leaves too (Figures S1–S9; Table S1). These tissue‐specific expression profiles guided RNA sampling and cloning for subsequent analyses (Table S2).

Overexpression of Soybean Orthologs Increased Protein Accumulation in Arabidopsis thaliana

To test the functional roles of the candidate genes, we generated A. thaliana lines expressing each soybean ortholog under the control of the constitutive 35S promoter. Two independent lines per gene were selected for subsequent analysis. All plants were grown under standardized long‐day conditions to minimize environmental variation. No major differences in plant morphology, biomass, or flowering time were observed between WT and overexpression lines, except that Glyma08g43780 overexpression resulted in early flowering (Figure 1B). These observations indicate that expression of the soybean genes did not cause broad developmental disruption. Transcript abundance in leaf tissue was quantified by RT‐qPCR using ACTIN2 as the reference gene, and all lines showed significantly elevated transcript levels relative to WT (p < 0.01; Figure 1C), confirming successful transgene expression.

To determine whether overexpression of these genes altered nitrogen allocation, we quantified total protein content in both leaves and seeds using a modified Lowry assay. The eight genes upregulated in QQS‐responsive transcriptomic datasets all led to significant increases in total protein levels in transgenic A. thaliana compared with WT (p < 0.05; Figure 2). These increases were observed in both vegetative and reproductive tissues, indicating that their effects were not restricted to a single developmental context. In contrast, overexpression of Glyma08g43780, the only negatively correlated candidate gene, reduced total protein content in both leaves and seeds (p < 0.05; Figure 2), consistent with its opposite transcriptional pattern in QQS‐expressing backgrounds.

These results mirror the characteristic protein‐enhancing phenotype previously associated with QQS, demonstrating that QQS‐responsive soybean genes can independently reproduce this metabolic effect in a heterologous system. The reciprocal phenotype produced by Glyma08g43780 further suggests that the QQS pathway includes both positive and negative regulatory components. Importantly, the consistent direction of protein changes across multiple independent genes and independently transformed lines, together with the absence of major growth defects, supports the interpretation that these phenotypes reflect coordinated metabolic reprogramming rather than gene‐specific, insertional, or nonspecific effects of constitutive transgene expression.

Overexpression of QQS ‐Responsive Orthologs Reduced Starch Accumulation

To examine whether the protein increases were accompanied by corresponding changes in carbon storage, we quantified starch content in leaf tissue. Leaves were harvested at the end of the light period, when starch accumulation is typically highest, to maximize detection of treatment differences. Seven of the eight positively regulated soybean genes caused significant reductions in leaf starch content relative to WT (p < 0.01; Figure 3). Reductions of approximately 10%–25% were observed, and the direction of change paralleled the increases in total protein, consistent with coordinated changes in protein and starch accumulation.

By contrast, Glyma08g43780 increased starch accumulation (p < 0.01) while reducing protein content, reinforcing its role as a contrasting component within the QQS‐responsive pathway. This reciprocal pattern between protein and starch is consistent with previously reported QQS‐associated phenotypes in A. thaliana and crop species (Li et al. 2009, 2015; Li and Wurtele 2015). Together with the limited effects on growth and morphology, the consistent direction of protein and starch changes across multiple independent genes supports the conclusion that these phenotypes reflect coordinated metabolic reprogramming rather than nonspecific effects of constitutive transgene expression.

Promoter Motif Analyses Reveal Coordinated Transcriptional Regulation

Promoter analysis of the selected genes, based on the 2 kb region upstream of the translation start codon, revealed enrichment of cis‐regulatory motifs associated with transcription factor families involved in carbon and nitrogen signaling. Motif enrichment was evaluated relative to a background set of non‐differentially expressed soybean promoters, confirming that these cis‐elements (p < 1 × 10−6) are more enriched in QQS‐responsive genes. In particular, bZIP, Dof, bHLH, MYB, and MADS‐box motifs were recurrently detected (Figures 4A and S10; Table S3). These transcription factor families are known to regulate chloroplast function, photosynthesis, carbon partitioning, amino acid biosynthesis, and seed development (Gu et al. 1998; Dubos et al. 2010; Le Hir and Bellini 2013; Sornaraj et al. 2016; Jia et al. 2022).

The promoter prediction is performed via the website (https://plantregmap.gaolab.org/binding_site_prediction.php) (p < 1 × 10−6). (B) Yeast strains expressing BD‐AtQQS, BD‐VP16, and BD‐VP16‐AtQQS hydrolyzed X‐α‐gal and formed blue colonies, whereas the negative control (BD) remained white. These results indicate that QQS supports reporter activation in yeast when fused to a DNA‐binding domain.

The repeated occurrence of these motifs across multiple candidate genes suggests that the nine soybean genes may be co‐regulated as part of a conserved QQS‐responsive metabolic module. Because these transcription factor families are linked to nitrogen metabolism, sugar signaling, plastidial function and reproductive development, their enrichment provides a plausible regulatory framework for the coordinated protein and starch phenotypes observed here. Although motif analysis does not demonstrate direct regulation, the shared promoter architecture supports the idea that QQS‐responsive genes are embedded within a common transcriptional network that may be influenced by QQS‐associated regulatory complexes.

The functional annotations of the corresponding A. thaliana orthologs are broadly consistent with roles in primary metabolism and cellular regulation, supporting their potential involvement in protein and starch accumulation. Together, the convergence of transcriptomic responses, shared promoter features, and functional validation in transgenic A. thaliana supports a model in which QQS‐responsive genes collectively contribute to the metabolic shift toward increased protein accumulation and reduced starch content.

To assess whether QQS can support reporter activation in a heterologous system, we examined its transcription‐related activity in yeast. When fused to a DNA‐binding domain, QQS activated reporter expression (Figure 4B), indicating that it has transcriptional activation capacity under the yeast assay conditions. This result does not establish that QQS functions as a transcriptional activator in plants. Moreover, yeast one‐hybrid assays detected no direct interaction between QQS and the promoters of the candidate soybean genes or their corresponding A. thaliana orthologs (Figure S11 and Table S4). Thus, the present data do not support direct promoter binding by QQS. Together with previous evidence that QQS interacts with NF‐YC4, these findings raise the possibility that QQS may influence transcription indirectly through protein complexes; however, this hypothesis requires experimental validation in planta.

Taken together, the coordinated protein and starch phenotypes, shared promoter architecture, and absence of direct promoter interaction support the existence of a conserved QQS‐responsive gene module that integrates transcriptional regulation with metabolic control of protein and starch accumulation. From a physiological perspective, this metabolic reprogramming may contribute to adaptive resource allocation under changing environmental conditions. Under nitrogen limitation, high light, or other abiotic constraints, plants often redirect carbon flux away from starch storage and toward nitrogen‐containing metabolites such as amino acids and proteins (Fredeen et al. 1991). The preferential expression of several QQS‐responsive genes in metabolically active tissues is consistent with a role in dynamic regulation of source–sink metabolism. Although A. thaliana provides a rapid heterologous system for functional analysis, validation in soybean remains an important next step. Stable soybean transformation and future gain‐ and loss‐of‐function studies, including genome editing approaches, will help define the native roles of these genes and further optimize QQS‐regulated pathways for crop improvement.

Conclusions

We identified a conserved QQS‐responsive soybean gene module and demonstrated that its components coordinately increase total protein and reduce starch in A. thaliana . Eight soybean genes increased total protein and reduced starch, whereas one gene produced the opposite phenotype. These findings identify downstream metabolic regulators associated with QQS and provide a targeted framework for improving seed protein content through metabolic engineering. Although loss‐of‐function analyses will be important for further validating the roles of these genes, the consistent gain‐of‐function phenotypes observed here provide strong initial evidence for their involvement in QQS‐mediated metabolic regulation. More broadly, the functional conservation observed across species suggests that orphan gene‐driven regulatory networks can act through conserved metabolic components. This QQS‐responsive module therefore represents a practical and testable entry point for enhancing protein accumulation in soybean and other crops.
