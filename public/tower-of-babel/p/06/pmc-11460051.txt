Prevalence and clinical patterns of urolithiasis in sub-saharan Africa: a systematic review and meta-analysis of observational studies
Authors: Altaseb Beyene Kassaw, Mekonnen Belete, Ebrahim Msaye Assefa, Amare Abera Tareke
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background and objectiveUrolithiasis, the presence of stones in the urinary tract, has been linked to various clinical features and reported as a worldwide health concern. Its prevalence varies across different regions as well as populations. Several primary studies have been conducted in Sub-Saharan Africa on the situation. However, their reports are inconsistent. Hence, this review aimed to assess the pooled magnitude of urolithiasis and its clinical patterns among hospital-visiting patients in sub-Saharan Africa.MethodsOnline databases such as PubMed, Hinari, the African Journals online database, and Google Scholar were used to comprehensively search articles published until June 28, 2023, about the prevalence and clinical patterns of urolithiasis in Sub-Saharan Africa. All the included studies were conducted at hospital setting. The retrieved data was exported to STATA version 16 for final analysis. A random-effect meta-analysis model was computed to estimate the pooled results. The heterogeneity of the studies was assessed using I2 and Cochran’s Q. Publication bias was examined by observation using funnel plots and statistically by Egger’s tests. Subgroup analysis was performed based on the country where the studies have been conducted.ResultA total of 26 articles (11 reported both prevalence and clinical pattern, 5 reported only prevalence, and 10 reported only clinical patterns of urolithiasis) were included in the final systematic review and meta-analysis. The pooled prevalence of urolithiasis among hospital-visiting patients was 9.4% (95% CL = 4.9–14%), with significant heterogeneity. Most of the urolithiasis was located in the kidney, with an estimated pooled proportion of 4.6% (95% CI = 2.7, 6.5), followed by bladder stone-2.0% (95% CI = 0.7, 3.4), ureteral stone-1.8% (95% CI = 0.7, 2.9), and urethral stone-0.2% (95% CI = 0.00, 0.05). The subgroup analysis showed the highest prevalence of urolithiasis was found in Mauritian, 28.1% (95%CI 24.5, 31.7), followed by Ethiopia 18.1%, and the lowest was in Eritrea, 1.0%. A sensitivity analysis using the random-effects model found no influential study on the pooled prevalence of urolithiasis. Evidence of significant publication bias was observed and trim-fill analysis was conducted for adjustment. Accordingly, two missing studies were identified, and after adjustment, the combined prevalence of urolithiasis was estimated to be 10.7%. The study also found that urolithiasis prevalence per gender was 6.3% in males and 2.9% in females. The most common clinical presentation of urolithiasis was flank pain at 58.4% (95% CL=, 45.9, 70.8), followed by low back pain at 45.9% (95% CL=, 23.1, 68.8), and nausea/vomiting at 29.9% (95% CI: 1 1.1, 48.8).ConclusionThe prevalence of urolithiasis in Sub-Sahara African Countries is increasing even though a remarkable regional variation was observed, with higher predominance in males than females. Common clinical presentations of urolithiasis were flank pain, low back pain, and nausea/vomiting.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12882-024-03780-y.

Introduction

Urolithiasis is a general term used to describe the presence of stones in the urinary system [1, 2]. This includes cystolithiasis (bladder stone, bladder calculi), ureterolithiasis (ureteral stone), urethrolithiasis (urethral calculi) [1, 3] and stones of the renal pelvis as well as the calyces [4]. The stone is mostly logged in the kidney and is a common urinary tract disorder [5]. Although it is a multifactorial disorder, the main cause of urinary stones is the deposition of calcium, phosphates, and oxalates in the urinary tracts, which pose serious health risks [6].

While the pathophysiologic mechanism of stone formation is a complex biochemical process, the condition occurs when stone crystals supersaturate the urine and begin to crystallize, commonly within the renal parenchyma [7, 8]. These crystals will aggregate together and continue to enlarge with the potential to migrate into the descending urinary tract area [7]. Irrespective of the stone type, patients appear with a similar spectrum of symptoms, ranging from asymptomatic to critically ill. These symptoms can include abdominal or flank pain that comes and goes, blood in the urine (usually only visible under a microscope), nausea, vomiting, and fever [9]. If not treated early, it may result in urinary obstruction, infection, and renal failure [10, 11]. Though approximately 90% of stones are spontaneously passed out of the urinary tract, the remaining stones generally require surgical interventions like ureteroscopy percutaneous nephrolithotomy, or non-invasive shock wave lithotripsy [12].

The epidemiology of urolithiasis varied across populations and regions and ranged from 8.8% in North America between 2007 and 2010 [13], 5–10% in Europe in the decay before 2011 [14], and 1–5% in Asia [15]. Globally, the incidence is thought to be between 1% and 5%, with affluent countries having a higher prevalence (2–13%) than developing ones (0.5–1%) [15, 16]. This regional difference may be due to a variation of determinants such as climate, diet, fluid intake, genetics, gender, occupation, and age, which all can be potential factors for epidemiological variation [17]. Although it is more common in Afro-Asian stone-forming nations, it is becoming more common in tropical and sub-Saharan African countries as a result of rising global temperatures and rising socioeconomic status, which is demonstrated by a more Westernized food and way of life [15, 17, 18].

The epidemiology of urolithiasis in the majority of Sub-Saharan Africa has been poorly studied since the estimation is often based on the number of hospital admissions, which may not accurately reflect the true incidence and prevalence in the general population [16, 19]. However, recent studies have shown a rise in the prevalence and incidence of urolithiasis in the area, even though there is an inconsistent report [2, 15, 20, 21]. Moreover, and to the extent of our search, the situation in the region has not been systematically assessed yet. Therefore, this review is intended to assess the pooled prevalence of urolithiasis and its clinical pattern among hospital-visiting patients in SSA.

Significance of the study

This systematic review and meta-analysis study may fill data gaps, inform public health strategies, enhance clinical practice, and guide future research in SSA. It could help to address inconsistencies in reports across individual studies, providing a clearer understanding of the disease burden. It highlights regional variations and informs public health policies for targeted interventions. Understanding clinical patterns, such as common symptoms, and complications, can improve clinical practice and patient management, especially in regions with limited diagnostic facilities. In addition, the finding of this study supports the health research community by providing a comprehensive overview of the disease burden in a region often underrepresented in global health studies. Furthermore, the findings can identify gaps in existing literature, ensuring future research addressing issues related to urolithiasis in SSA.

Methods

Search strategy

The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) standards were followed when executing the search techniques [22] (Supplementary File 1). The protocol was registered by the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) with registration number [CRD42023440816]. Online databases such as PubMed, Hinari, African Journals online database, and Google Scholar were used to comprehensively search articles published until June 28, 2023. The databases were searched by combining different MeSH terms and keywords, using Boolean Operator (OR, AND) (Supplementary File 2). For example for PubMed, the following search strategy were entered (((((Prevalence[Title/Abstract]))) AND ((Urolithiasis[Title/Abstract] OR “Urinary Lithiasis“[Title/Abstract] OR “Lithiasis, Urinary“[Title/Abstract] OR “Urinary Calculi” OR” Urinary Tract Stone“[Title/Abstract] OR “Urinary Stones“[Title/Abstract] OR “Urinary Stone“[Title/Abstract])) AND ((“Risk Factor” [Title/Abstract] OR “associated factors“[Title/Abstract] OR “determinants“[Title/Abstract]))) AND ((“Country name “[Title/Abstract])). To make sure that all pertinent studies carried out in the field are found, we also looked over the reference lists of the included studies. In addition, a manual search and a review of grey literature were used to find unindexed research publications.

Eligibility criteria

We used the CoCoPop approach (condition, context, and population), which is used for systematic reviews of prevalence studies, to define the criteria for including and excluding studies [23]. According to this approach, the original full-text studies that looked at the clinical patterns and prevalence of urolithiasis within a particular time, target population, and geographic area were included. Following a careful review of the articles’ content, studies that satisfied the following requirements were included.

Study participants (Population): adults.

Types of studies: observational studies.

The outcome of interests (Condition): studies reported the prevalence and/or clinical characteristics of urolithiasis.

Publication condition: All published articles and gray literature, master thesis, and dissertations.

Language: English language.

Publication setting (Context): studies conducted in SSA before June 28, 2023. Hence, observational studies that reported the prevalence and/or clinical characteristics of urolithiasis among adult patients visiting hospitals in SSA were included.

Whereas, studies published in languages other than English reviews, editorial opinions, case reports, letters to the editor, hypotheses, and abstracts from conferences were excluded.

Data extraction

Two investigators (AB and MB) independently assessed the eligibility of articles and extracted data using Microsoft Excel. Using a standardized data extraction form, the following data elements were extracted from each article. Author name, Study area, Publication year, Study design, Sample size, proportion of stone, type of stone, anatomical location of stone, means of diagnosis, and proportion of each clinical pattern. Any disagreement between the reviewers was resolved by discussion.

Quality assessment

To evaluate the methodological quality of the included studies, the Joanna Briggs Institute’s critical appraisal tool (JBI) for observational studies was utilized [24]. Studies with an average quality score of ≥ 50% were considered to be of good quality and hence included for analysis (Supplementary file 3).

Data synthesis and analysis

The retrieved data was exported to STATA version 16 for final analysis. The data were presented using narrative synthesis, and tables and figures were used to display the results. The combined effect sizes of urolithiasis, accompanied by a corresponding 95% CI were calculated using the random-effect meta-analysis model. Publication bias was examined by observation using funnel plots and statistically by Egger’s tests. The trim-and-fill analysis was then done in the asymmetrical funnel plots to integrate missing studies and provide the reliability of the effect size estimate about publication bias. We also assessed heterogeneity by using Cochran’s Q test and I2 test statistics. A p-value of < 0.05 for the I2 test was used to determine the presence of heterogeneity. A pre-defined subgroup analysis was performed based on the country where the studies were conducted to identify the source of heterogeneity. Moreover, sensitivity analysis was performed to investigate the stability or robustness of the pooled estimates to outliers and the impact of individual studies using a leaf-one-out approach.

Outcome of interest

The primary outcome of this study was the pooled prevalence of urinary tract stones, which was reported in the original paper as a percentage and/or as the number of patients with urolithiasis per total number of participants. The secondary outcome was the combined clinical patterns of and anatomical location of urinary stones.

Results

Study selection

A total of 240 articles were identified from online electronic database searching, PubMed (n = 102), Google Scholar (n = 111), Hinari (n = 13), and African Journals online database (n = 20). The title, abstract, and full text of the article were evaluated. Out of them, 57 articles were excluded due to duplication, 45 articles that had unrelated titles and abstracts were excluded since they had nothing to do with the topic in question, and 112 articles were excluded for not having other required data. 4 papers were found by cross-references of the included studies. Finally, 16 eligible articles were selected for the final systematic review and meta-analysis of the prevalence of urolithiasis, and 21 articles for clinical patterns of urolithiasis (Fig. 1).

Characteristics of the included studies

A total of 26 articles (11 reported both prevalence and clinical pattern, 5 = reported only prevalence, 10 = reported only clinical patterns of urolithiasis) were included in the final systematic review and metanalysis (Tables 1 and 2). All studies followed a hospital-based cross-sectional study design. A total of 95,922 participants were represented in the prevalence of urolithiasis and clinical patterns of urolithiasis were reported for 2709 urolithiasis cases. All the studies were published from 2010 to 2023 in different Sab-Saharan African Countries. Of the studies reporting the prevalence of urolithiasis, three studies were conducted in Ethiopia [25–27], and three studies were in Congo Brazzaville [21, 28, 29]. The total number of participants per study ranged from 140 to 32,370. (Table 1).

Prevalence of Urolithiasis

A total of 3312 urolithiasis cases were identified from 95,922 hospital-visiting participants in 16 included studies. The lowest prevalence was reported from a study conducted in Eritrea (1.0%) [30] and the highest prevalence was reported in a study conducted in Ethiopia (30.0%) [48]. The meta-analysis estimated pooled prevalence of urolithiasis among hospital visiting patients in the SSA country was found to be 9.4%% (95% CL = 4.9- 14%). The test statics showed significant heterogeneity between studies ((I2 = 99.95%, p = < 0.001), which is suggestive of using the random effects model (Fig. 2).

Prevalence of urinary stone by anatomical location

Anatomical locations of stones for hospital-visiting urolithiasis cases were reported in 15 studies. Renal stone-4.6% (95% CI = 2.7, 6.5), bladder stone-2.0% (95% CI = 0.7, 3.4), ureteral stone-1.8% (95% CI = 0.7, 2.9), and urethral stone-0.2% (95% CI = 0.00, 0.05), (Supplementary file 4).

Prevalence of urinary stone by gender

We also analyzed the combined prevalence of urolithiasis per gender of the participants and was found to be 6.3% (95% CI: 3.1,9.6) in males (Fig. 3 (A)) and 2.9% (95% CI: 1.4, 4.5) in female participants (Fig. 3 (B).

Subgroup analysis of the prevalence of urolithiasis

There was a significant level of heterogeneity among the primary studies included in this systematic review and meta-analysis. Thus, a sub-group analysis was performed based on the country where the study was conducted to investigate the sources of heterogeneity.

In the subgroup analysis, the studies conducted in Congo Brazzaville (I2 = 88.99%, p = < 0.001) and Ethiopia (I2 = 99.20%, p = 0.020) showed the source of heterogeneity. The hospital pooled prevalence of urolithiasis was 6.3% (5.0, 7.0) in Congo Brazzaville, and 18.1% (1.9, 34.4) in Ethiopia (Table 3).

Discussion

Urolithiasis, or urinary tract stone, has been identified as a worldwide health concern, affecting a million of people with varying rates of occurrence in numerous geographic areas and demographic groups [15, 49]. Even though the epidemiology and clinical presentations of urinary tract stones have been thoroughly investigated in many different regions of the globe, it is largely unknown in SSA till a few years ago. However, current individual studies have shown the rise in prevalence and incidence of urolithiasis in the region, while there is an inconsistent report [2, 15, 20, 21]. Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to assess the combined prevalence and clinical patterns of urinary tract stones among hospital-visiting patients in the SSA countries.

We compressively analyzed the prevalence of urolithiasis in 16 studies involving 95,922 participants. Accordingly, the hospital pooled prevalence of urolithiasis in this study was found to be 9.4% (95% CL 4.9–14.0). The finding was in line with the previous meta-analyses that reported the overall prevalence of urolithiasis in China (8.1%) [50], in the USA ( 8.8%) [51] and Iran (6.6%) [52]. This evidence of the increasing prevalence of urolithiasis in the region may be due to changes in lifestyle and dietary patterns (a more Westernized food and way of life). However, the combined prevalence was lower than a study reported in Russia (35.4%) [53]. This may be possibly due to the difference in healthcare access, the inaccessibility of advanced modern imaging techniques like urologic CT scans [54], and the variability of lithogenic factors including environmental, genetics, and dietary habits [55, 56]. For example, in terms of healthcare infrastructure and its capacity to manage urolithiasis, most SSA regions have limited access to advanced diagnostic tools like CT scans [57, 58], which results in the underreporting of smaller or asymptomatic and radiolucent stones. Treatment options are also constrained by a shortage of specialized healthcare providers and facilities, leading to a reliance on symptomatic management [58]. Additionally, healthcare resources in SSA are often focused on infectious diseases, reducing resources for chronic non-communicable conditions like urolithiasis, and underdiagnosis is often due to limited access to facilities [59]. Hence these reasons might be responsible for the discrepancy.

We also evaluate the prevalence of urinary calculi based on the anatomical location. The finding showed a higher combined prevalence of renal stone-4.6% (95% CI = 2.7, 6.5), followed by bladder stone-2.0% (95% CI = 0.7, 3.4). Our results are similar to previous studies [50], which reports kidney is the most involved site for urolithiasis. Our findings are also comparable with the global trend of urolithiasis, which showed that most of the urinary calculi are localized on the upper tract (kidney) [49]. Research conducted in developing countries indicates that bladder stones are more common in certain areas, and their frequency is believed to be related to sociocultural factors and malnutrition [60]. The use of traditional herbal remedies which can delay proper medical care, and worsen stone conditions, is common in some regions of SSA [61–63]. Additionally, different food taboos, water scarcity, and cultural norms related to water consumption and sanitation may further contribute to the risk of bladder stones [64, 65]. Bladder localization remained favored in older age, most likely caused by obstructions behind the bladder, which are associated with poor diuresis and urinary stasis as well as high urine concentration [47]. The pooled proportion of ureteral calculi was − 1.8% (95% CI = 0.7, 2.9),).

We analyzed the combined hospital prevalence estimates of urolithiasis by gender subgroup and the result showed a high pooled prevalence in males, 6.3% (95% CI 3.1,9.6), as compared to females, 2.9% (95% CI 1.4, 4.5). The involvement of male sex hormones in the pathogenesis of urinary tract stone formation might be the possible explanation for this result [66]. This predominance can also be explained by the role of estrogen in lithogenesis, estrogen actually has a preventive effect in females by promoting the excretion of citrate in the urine, which solubilizes calcium [67]. However, the presence of comorbidities, age, and other lithogenic factors should be considered to have a conclusive report.

Subgroup analysis by country showed that the highest prevalence of urolithiasis was found in Mauritian 28.1% (95%CI 24.5, 31.7) followed by Ethiopia-18.1% (1.9, 34.4), and the lowest was in Eritrea 1.0%. One possible explanation for this finding might be the difference in study participants and means of diagnosis. For example, in Mauritania, the study participants were all patients operated in the urologic ward, which may have led to an overestimation of the actual urinary tract stone prevalence. Additionally, differences in healthcare access [68], the difference in the availability of advanced imaging technology [54], and a variation in lithogenic factors including environmental and dietary habits likely contributed to these findings. Some SSA countries, like Mauritania, are located in the Saharan desert, where high temperatures and arid conditions lead to dehydration, causing concentrated urine and increasing the likelihood of stone formation [69, 70]. Traditional diets such as leafy greens, nuts, and certain grains are common in some areas and may be high in oxalate-rich which can contribute to the formation of calcium oxalate stones [71]. Limited access to clean water in many parts of SSA leads to the consumption of untreated groundwater or hard water [72], which has been associated with an increased risk of calcium oxalate and phosphate stones [73, 74]. In addition, low consumption of fruits and vegetables, which provide hydration and potassium to prevent stone formation [75] and diets high in animal protein and salt, which are linked to increased stone formation, are common in several SSA regions [76]. These region-specific environmental and dietary factors likely play a critical role in the variation of urolithiasis prevalence across different countries in SSA.

We also assessed publication bias and a significant small study effect was observed. The trim-and-fill analysis suggested that only two studies were missing due to publication bias. After adjustment, the combined prevalence of urolithiasis SSA was slightly increased to 10.7% (95% CI: 6.3 to 15.0), suggesting the minimal impact of publication bias on the overall estimate. Although this adjustment had a minimal impact on the finding, it highlights the importance of addressing publication bias in meta-analyses.

Our systematic review and metanalysis also provide a comprehensive overview of clinical patterns of urolithiasis among hospital-visiting patients using 21 studies involving 2,709 urolithiasis cases. The main clinical presentation of urolithiasis was flank pain, 58.4% (98% CL=, 45.9, 70.8). This may be attributed to a higher proportion of upper urinary tract stones than the lower tract. Flank pain, also called renal coli, results from obstruction of the renal pelvis [77], or a sustained contraction of ureteric smooth muscle as an upper urinary tract stone passes down [78]. Low back pain was the second reported clinical presentation of urolithiasis in this review, with a pooled proportion of 45.9% (98% CL=, 23.1, 68.8). It may result from stones obstructing the renal pelvis, ureter, or any kidney drainage channels. Prolonged stone blockage of the urinary tract causes stasis and backflow of urine in the urinary tubes, which can eventually cause ureter hydronephrosis and infection with sustained damage. A substantial combined clinical presentation including recurrent UTI − 16.8% (95%CI: 2.9, 30.8) and AUR/obstructive anuria- 12.2% (98% CI: 4.9, 19.0) could be the possible consequence.

We also found that LUTS and nausea/or vomiting were the remarkable clinical presentation in the urinary calculi cases, with pooled estimates of 28.4 (95% CI: 17.4, 39.4) and 29.9% (95%CI: 11.1, 48.8) respectively. The pathogenic mechanisms underlying LUTS in urolithiasis are intricate and can include factors like nerve irritation, inflammation, and blockage secondary to the stone [79]. The presence of urinary calculi triggers inflammatory responses and stimulates the bladder’s nerve to become more active, which can lead to overactive bladder symptoms like frequency, urgency, and dysuria [79, 80]. Urinary stones also bring a rise in urinary tract pressure, which may trigger the production of hormones and neurotransmitters that have an impact on the gastrointestinal system and cause vomiting/nausea [82].

Implications for public health and clinical practice

The increasing prevalence of urolithiasis in SSA informs the need for targeted public health interventions focusing on the primary contributing factors. The finding emphasizes the need for early detection of urolithiasis and community awareness of risk factors and prevention strategies. Public health interventions should be implemented in specific populations, addressing preventable causes of urolithiasis. Furthermore, managing modifiable risk factors, and improving diagnostic techniques have to be implemented for the early detection of diseases that could substantially decrease the disease’s burden. The high rate of stone formation rates therefore highlights the need for healthcare professionals to enhance preventive strategies, improve diagnostic accuracy, and optimize patient care.

However, this study has some limitations that need to be considered. Firstly, all the studies included in the analysis were conducted in hospitals, and most were retrospective (which are prone to bias and less informative). This could lead to an overestimation of the frequency of urolithiasis, and may not accurately represent the actual epidemiology of the problem. Secondly, only 14 countries within the SSA region were represented in the studies, which suggests that additional countries may not be effectively represented. Thirdly, we observed a significant publication bias across several subgroups, as well as significant heterogeneity between studies. These issues might influence the overall estimates of urolithiasis prevalence and clinical patterns, potentially limiting the generalizability of our results. Fourth, in most of the included studies of this analysis, the diagnosis of stone relied on routine radiography and ultrasound, which may fail to detect radiolucent stones which would result in underestimating the findings. Fifth, the a lack of detailed information regarding the specific settings in which the included studies were conducted, such as whether they were based in urology departments, emergency rooms, or any else, which may potentially contribute to the observed differences in urolithiasis prevalence. Finally, due to a shortage of free access, we were unable to conduct more database searches, including those of Scopus and EMBASE. Therefore, we suggest funding to increase database searches. Despite these limitations, our study provides valuable insights into the hospital-pooled prevalence of urolithins and its clinical patterns in the region. The evidence can serve as a foundation for making judgments and implementing preventive and early treatment policies for the condition in SSA.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis provide a comprehensive prevalence and clinical presentation of urolithiasis among hospital-visiting patients in SSA. We found a pooled prevalence of 9.04%, with the kidney being the most common site of stone localization (6.2%). The clinical presentations varied across studies and countries, with flank pain, low back pain, and nausea/vomiting being the most prevalent symptoms. These findings emphasize the significance of urolithiasis as a public health issue in SSA and highlight the importance of targeted healthcare interventions and policies to address it. However, the study also identified several limitations, particularly the need for more region-specific data to better understand the actual prevalence and underlying causes of urolithiasis across SSA. Future research should focus on large-scale, community-based epidemiological studies to establish the broader scope of the problem and to assess the effectiveness of different treatment methods adapted to SSA’s unique environmental, dietary, and healthcare contexts.

Addressing urolithiasis in SSA requires a multidisciplinary approach involving collaboration between policymakers, healthcare providers, and community leaders. Integrating clinical expertise with public health policy, cultural understanding, and community engagement is essential to developing effective prevention and treatment strategies. Such a collaborative effort will be key to reducing the burden of urolithiasis and improving patient outcomes across the region.
