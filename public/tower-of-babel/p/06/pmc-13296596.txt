“It Was Like They'd Lit a Fuse”: A Mixed‐Methods Investigation Into Rage
Authors: Thomas F. Denson, Eddie Harmon‐Jones, Johannes Larsson, Ashlee Curtis, Travis Harries, Peter G. Miller
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTImpulsive acts of violence are perpetrated by a small proportion of society, which anecdotal evidence suggests often occurs in a rage state. Rage has generally been conceptualized as extreme anger, but it may be qualitatively different from extreme anger. Is rage best considered as part of the anger continuum or is it a different phenomenon altogether? The present research addressed this question in a large sample of university students (N = 545) who completed qualitative and quantitative measures of rage attacks, anger, emotional dysregulation, trait aggression and displaced aggression, angry rumination, adverse childhood events, head injuries, drug and alcohol use, and borderline personality disorder traits. Qualitative results showed that rage experiences are similar to anger experiences, but with some unique components. Path analysis found a similar pattern of correlates for rage and anger. Emotion dysregulation, trait physical aggression, trait displaced aggression, and borderline personality disorder traits predicted both rage and anger. Finally, in a latent profile analysis, we identified two subgroups of participants who are at risk for rage and anger. These findings largely support rage as a type of extreme anger with some additional features (e.g., loss of control, tunnel vision, aggression). Targeting the dysregulated aspects of people's personalities as early as possible may assist in reducing anger and rage.

Rage first became a focus of scientific study as part of the famous “sham rage” experiments with cats in the 1920s, in which decorticated cats showed strong physiological and behavioral signs of rage (Bard 1928; Cannon and Britton 1925). Since then, rage has assumed a broad position in scientific and lay understanding. There are articles and books on road rage (Pfeiffer et al. 2016), customer rage (Patterson et al. 2009), feminist rage (Kaplan et al. 2021), political rage (Dikeç 2024), rage in the involuntarily celibate (Papandreou et al. 2025), trans rage (Skelton 2026), racial/ethnic rage (Cherry 2021), video game rage (Kahila et al. 2022; Moreau et al. 2024), online rage (Graf and Yanagida 2025), rage in mothers (Billotte Verhoff et al. 2023), and rage during air travel (Rösch et al. 2024). Given this extensive scope and the possibility that the word has been overextended, can rage be distilled into a construct based on research evidence? Rage is traditionally thought of as extreme anger, but here we attempt to discern whether the phenomenology of rage differs from instances of anger. Others have argued for a distinction between anger and rage (Hall 2009; Lewis 1993); however, to our knowledge, this question has not been empirically investigated. In the present research, we sought to determine whether rage can be differentiated from anger or whether it is best considered part of the anger continuum. We did this by comparing qualitative reports, quantitative predictors, and conducting a person‐centered analysis.

I3 theory provides a basis for understanding our framework of individual differences that may lead to anger, rage, and associated aggression (Finkel and Hall 2018). The model specifies that in the presence of instigation (e.g., provocation), impelling factors enhance the likelihood of aggression (or in our case, being in an angry or rageful state), whereas disinhibiting factors contribute to poor control over emotions and behavior. We examined several traits as impellors: trait aggression, trait rumination and displaced aggression, borderline personality disorder traits, and adverse childhood experiences. The disinhibitors we examined were emotion dysregulation, alcohol and drug use, and head injuries.

Emotion dysregulation plays a key role in our framework as a disinhibiting influence that may underlie many aspects of rage. We suggest that for rage to occur, there must be not only extreme levels of anger, but also that the anger is dysregulated (i.e., cannot be regulated or is resistant to emotion regulation attempts). Specifically, true rage is characterized by uncontrollable aggression, strong physiological reactions, tunnel vision or “seeing red,” as well as memory impairment. Early work on “blind rage” and the “beserker syndrome” highlighted this amnesia for the rage period (Simón 1987). Qualitative research also supports the emotional and behavioral dysregulation aspects associated with rage (Eatough and Smith 2006). To illustrate, Dim (2021, p. 1039) describes the experience of a male victim of intimate partner violence:“Level three is what I would call rage and when she was raging, it was often a blind rage, her body would literally be shaking, her jaw clenched and her eyes were just hatred and contempt and she gets all kinds of unpredictable things, often throwing things and it started out throwing small things or light things; like she would throw a small pad of paper like a posted notepad at me, or something and then it graduated to pens and pencils and then it graduated to cups and glasses and then bowls and then frying pans and then over time it just got bigger and more dangerous things that she would throw at me, she didn't hit me too often but this was when she was in a rage and she was very unpredictable.”

This narrative highlights the physical manifestations of rage as well as the impulsive and unpredictable nature of the aggression. Thus, rage may be an extreme part of the anger continuum or may be a unique phenomenon accompanied by unique correlates (e.g., emotion dysregulation, aggression).

Our approach to rage is in accordance with research on rage in intermittent explosive disorder. For instance, one study asked participants with intermittent explosive disorder, psychiatric controls, and healthy controls about the phenomenology and consequences of their aggressive outbursts (Kulper et al. 2015). Participants with intermittent explosive disorder reported feeling angrier and more enraged than healthy controls immediately before or during their aggressive outburst. They also reported more physiological symptoms such as racing heart, hot flashes, sweating, and trembling before and during the outburst. Participants with intermittent explosive disorder also reported feeling out of control and “exploding” in anger more so than healthy controls. Interestingly, participants with intermittent explosive disorder were more likely to endorse disappointment, embarrassment, and pleasure after an aggressive outburst than healthy controls.

Similarly, research with people with borderline personality disorder highlights the dysregulated nature of their rage experiences. Borderline personality disorder is characterized by emotion dysregulation, aggression, self‐harm, and suicidality (Lieb et al. 2004). Substantial research documents dysregulated anger and aggression in people with borderline personality disorder, especially in response to rejection (Berenson et al. 2011; Jacob et al. 2008). Angry rumination likely plays a role as well; both emotion dysregulation and angry rumination predicted self‐reported aggression in a sample of people diagnosed with borderline personality disorder (Martino et al. 2015).

Despite the rage observed in intermittent explosive disorder and borderline personality disorder, the causes of rage remain unclear. Several determinants have been found in the literature. In addition to emotion dysregulation, adverse childhood experiences are also viewed as a strong contributor to rage (Gauthier‐Duchesne et al. 2024; Flemke 2009). Furthermore, many people with intermittent explosive disorder and borderline personality disorder report adverse childhood experiences (Paliakkara et al. 2025; Yuan et al. 2023). The effects of adverse childhood experiences are complex, but they may lead to shame (Gilligan 2003; Zhang et al. 2025), emotion dysregulation (Dvir et al. 2014), insecure attachment (Oshri et al. 2015), and heightened threat sensitivity (Hein and Monk 2017; Thompson et al. 2014). All of these changes associated with adverse childhood experiences are likely to increase risk of rage by leading to heightened impellance and disinhibition.

Traumatic brain injury has also been implicated in rage. Several high‐profile instances of rage have been documented in American and Australian football players, likely due to chronic traumatic encephalopathy caused by repeated head trauma (Australian Broadcasting Corporation 2025; Wikipedia 2026). Thus, head injury may lead to disinhibition, emotion dysregulation, and impulsive aggression. Research supports this hypothesis. For instance, traumatic brain injury rates are approximately 60%–65% in incarcerated young men and adults (Perkes et al. 2011; Huw Williams et al. 2010). Compared to non‐affected siblings, those with brain injuries were more than twice as likely to be convicted of a violent offense (Schofield et al. 2015). Persons with a brain injury who have engaged in offending behavior are also more violent in prison and recidivate sooner than those who have offended without brain injury (Ray and Richardson 2017; Shiroma et al. 2010).

Another disinhibiting factor that might influence rage responses is alcohol and drug use. People prone to rage show higher levels of alcohol and substance abuse than those less prone to rage (Coccaro et al. 2017; Trull et al. 2018; Wesner et al. 1991). Finally, because the aggression elicited by rage is highly impulsive and dysregulated, we examined whether people who are prone to displacing aggression on innocent others also correlates with incidences of rage. For example, in their investigation into customer rage, McColl‐Kennedy et al. (2009) identified customers who often take out their anger on innocent bystanders when in a rage state. Thus, we included displaced aggression because, when in a rage state, people may lash out at anyone nearby due to emotion dysregulation. We also included angry rumination and revenge planning as cognitive influences that might elicit a rage state. That is, the trait tendency to engage in perseverative cognition about a provocation may enhance anger and rage via increased impellance or disinhibition (Denson 2013).

The Present Research

One aim of the research was to identify qualitative differences, if any, between rage and anger. A second aim of this research was to identify predictors of rage and compare them to predictors of anger. A third aim of this research was to identify profiles of individuals who are likely to experience rage. Specifically, after identifying variables that predicted anger and rage in our sample, we conducted a latent profile analysis with these predictors as indicators. This person‐centered analysis allowed us to determine whether people who responded in a similar manner on this cluster of variables are predisposed to rage.

In the present research, we focused on a population of undergraduate students. We suggest that the rage experience is similar across disordered individuals and healthy participants, with the exception that healthy populations experience rage much less frequently than individuals with intermittent explosive disorder or borderline personality disorder. That is, although all people are capable of experiencing rage, the threshold for a rage experience differs across individuals. Indeed, whereas people with intermittent explosive disorder reported over 100 outbursts of verbal aggression per year, healthy controls reported fewer than one (Kulper et al. 2015). Thus, although infrequent, it is likely that many healthy individuals have experienced rage one or more times during their lifetime. To this end, we asked about lifetime prevalence of having ever experienced rage.

Method

Ethics Statement and Data Availability

To attract participants who had experienced intense anger and rage, the study was presented as a survey about personal experiences of anger and rage. The University of New South Wales’ Human Research Ethics Committee approved the research (#10014). All data and R code are available here: https://osf.io/pygbs/.

Participants

A total of 616 introductory psychology students completed the survey; however, for statistical reasons, we removed data from two participants who reported another gender. Eleven participants did not pass the attention check (i.e., “Write the word dog”) and 58 participants’ data were deleted for completing 53% of the survey or less. The remaining participants completed at least 96% of the survey. This left a total of 545 participants (342 women and 203 men; M age = 19.10, SD = 2.13). Participants reported being East or Southeast Asian (64%), White (17%), Indian (6%), mixed or other ethnicity (6%), Middle Eastern (4%), Black or African (2%), or Indigenous or Latinx (both < 1%). Participants rated their subjective social status as 5.58 (SD = 1.80) out of 10 (Adler et al. 2000).

Materials and Procedure

Participants provided informed consent and completed the anonymous survey online via Qualtrics from January 2025 to November 2025.

Qualitative Rage Questions

Participants were presented with a brief description of rage: “Blind rage occurs when people become so angry that they lose control of themselves. They experience a strong state of arousal. Their rage usually appears suddenly, and they may not recall why the anger occurred. Sometimes, people say they 'see red'. Afterwards, they are sometimes not able to recall exactly what occurred.” Participants then indicated whether they had ever experienced this phenomenon (yes/no) and then how many times they had experienced this type of rage (on a scale from 0 to 10 + ) (M = 1.19, SD = 2.43, range 0–10+). Participants were then asked two questions. First, for Question 1, they were asked to write about their blind rage experience (if they indicated “yes”) or their angriest experience (if they indicated “no”). Second, for Question 2, they were asked: “What occurred just before the blind rage or intense anger experience? What do you remember about the experience? Or what did someone else tell you about your experience (if blind rage)? What happened afterwards?” We refer to participants who indicated that they had experienced blind rage as the rage group and the remaining participants as the anger group.

Rage

Participants’ tendency to experience rage was quantitatively assessed with the Rage Attacks Questionnaire ‐ Revised (RAQ‐R; Müller‐Vahl et al. 2020). This 22‐item measure was validated for use on healthy participants and showed discriminant validity from impulsivity (see also Palm et al. 2021). Items include “It's not nice to witness my rage attacks” and “When I am angry, it can happen that I destroy objects” (0 = not at all/never, 1 = a little/sometimes, 2 = strong/frequent, and 3 = very strong/very common). Internal consistency was high in the current study (α = 0.94; M = 1.62, SD = 0.54). Higher mean scores indicate a greater propensity toward rage.

Emotional Dysregulation

The 36‐item Difficulties with Emotion Regulation Scale (DERS) was used to assess emotion dysregulation (Gratz and Roemer 2004). Items include “I experience my emotions as overwhelming and out of control” and “When I'm upset, I believe that I will remain that way for a long time” (1 = almost never, 5 = almost always). Internal consistency was high in the current study (α = 0.92; M = 2.62, SD = 0.64). Higher mean scores indicate greater emotion dysregulation.

Alcohol and Drug Use

The Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test (AUDIT; Saunders et al. 1993) and Drug Use Disorders Identification Test (DUDIT; Berman et al. 2002) were used to assess alcohol and drug use, respectively. The 10‐item measures assess how often and how much alcohol or drugs is used as well as problematic behavior associated with alcohol and drug use. Internal consistency was good for the AUDIT (α = 0.85; M = 4.13, SD = 4.99; range 0–37) and DUDIT (α = 0.92; M = 0.92, SD = 3.38; range 0–26). Higher mean scores indicate more alcohol and drug involvement.

Trait Aggression

We assessed individual differences in aggressiveness using the 12‐item short form of Buss and Perry's (1992) Aggression Questionnaire (Webster et al. 2014). The measure assesses four components of aggression: physical aggression (“Given enough provocation, I may hit another person;” α = 0.69; M = 2.13, SD = 0.96); verbal aggression (“When people annoy me, I may tell them what I think of them;” α = 0.66; M = 2.73, SD = 0.93); anger (“Sometimes I fly off the handle for no good reason;” α = 0.64; M = 2.28, SD = 0.83); and hostility (“I sometimes feel that people are laughing at me behind my back;” α = 0.64; M = 2.58, SD = 0.93) (1 = extremely uncharacteristic of me; 5 = extremely characteristic of me). Higher mean scores indicate greater aggression.

Trait Displaced Aggression, Angry Rumination, and Revenge Planning

We included an individual difference measure to assess the tendency to ruminate about provocations and displace aggression toward innocent others. The Displaced Aggression Questionnaire (Denson et al. 2006) has three subscales: angry rumination (“I often find myself thinking over and over about things that have made me angry;” α = 0.94; M = 3.48, SD = 1.50), revenge planning (“If somebody harms me, I am not at peace until I can retaliate;” α = 0.93; M = 2.69, SD = 1.36), and behavioral displaced aggression (“When someone or something makes me angry I am likely to take it out on another person;” α = 0.91; M = 2.53, SD = 1.17) (1 = extremely uncharacteristic of me; 7 = extremely characteristic of me). Higher mean scores indicate greater displaced aggression, angry rumination, and revenge planning.

Borderline Personality

Borderline personality traits were assessed with the five‐factor borderline inventory‐super short form (DeShong et al. 2025). This 22‐item measure assesses various borderline personality disorder traits (e.g., “My anger often feels out of control” and “I have threatened to commit suicide”). We used the total score in the present research (α = 0.94; M = 2.17, SD = 0.85) (1 = strongly disagree; 5 = strongly agree). Higher mean scores indicate greater borderline personality disorder traits.

Adverse Childhood Events

Adverse childhood experiences were assessed with 11‐item checklist based on the Adverse Childhood Experiences study (Felitti et al. 1998). Items included physical abuse, witnessing domestic violence, sexual abuse, living with an alcoholic or offender or drug dealer, and parental divorce. For example, the witnessing domestic violence item was “Parents or adults in your home slapped, hit, kicked, punched, or beat each other up more than once.” Thus, participants were not required to judge whether certain experiences were abusive or not. Participants responded on a yes/no basis and “yes” responses were summed (M = 0.89, SD = 1.24, range 0–6). Higher scores indicate more adverse childhood events.

Head Injuries

Two questions from Schofield et al. (2006) assessed history of head injury. These were “How many times in your life have you had a head injury (blow to the head) that caused you to become ‘dazed and confused without loss of consciousness’?” (M = 0.61, SD = 1.47, range 0–19) and “How many times in your life have you had a head injury (blow to the head) that caused you to become ‘unconscious/blacked out’?” (M = 0.10, SD = 0.33, range 0–2).

Analysis Plan

Conducting a manual inductive analysis of the open‐ended questions would take an exceptionally long time in our large data set. Therefore, for the qualitative data, we used Claude.ai (by Anthropic) to analyze the open‐ended questions. Specifically, we input the responses and told the AI that there were two groups without mentioning which; thus, ensuring the Claude.ai was blind to group membership. We then asked the AI to provide an inductive thematic analysis for each group. We also asked the AI to provide key differences between the groups. This type of AI usage is becoming increasingly common for analyzing qualitative data sets. Research has compared the use of large language models with human‐coded responses and found a high degree of similarity (Lockwood et al. 2026; Morgan 2023; Sakaguchi et al. 2025; Salazar et al. 2025).

For the quantitative data, we conducted variable‐centered and person‐centered analyses in R. For the variable‐centered analyses, to account for missing data, we first established that data were missing completely at random (MCAR) as indicated by a non‐significant MCAR test, χ2(596) = 646, p = 0.076. Next, we computed 100 multiply imputed data sets using predictive mean matching with the mice package in R (Buuren and Groothuis‐Oudshoorn 2011). This procedure fills in missing data by computing each participant's scores based on other data in the data set. It then runs the analysis on 100 imputed data sets and pools the results. We calculated the Pearson's correlations on the pooled imputed data. Next, we conducted a path analysis using the study variables to simultaneously predict anger and rage. We used full‐information maximum likelihood to account for missing data. Because the data violated the assumption of multivariate normality, we used robust fit indices. A power analysis with the semPower (Moshagen and Bader 2025) package in R showed that our path model with 13 degrees of freedom would require 215 participants to detect an RMSEA of 0.08.

For the person‐centered analysis, we conducted a latent profile analysis (LPA) with the tidyLPA package (Rosenberg et al. 2018) to determine the existence of profiles in the data who may be at risk for having rage attacks. Specifically, we used the Rage Attacks Questionnaire, trait anger, and the significant predictors identified in the path analysis as indicators in the LPA. The number of profiles is determined by examining fit indices, entropy, theoretical alignment, parsimony, and the smallest group size. Smaller fit indices and entropy values greater than 0.80 are preferred (Tein et al. 2013). Missing data were addressed with single imputation with the missForest package (Stekhoven and Bühlmann 2012). All variables were standardized. Monte Carlo studies have shown that a sample size of 500 provides power to detect an accurate number of latent profiles (Nylund‐Gibson and Choi 2018; Sinha et al. 2021). We determined the number of profiles by inspecting the log‐likelihood, Akaike's information criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and Sample Adjusted BIC (SABIC). Each model is compared to the previous model. Larger log‐likelihood values indicate better fit, whereas lower values on the AIC, BIC, and SABIC indicate better fit. We also included the bootstrapped likelihood ratio test (BLRT). The LPA assigns each participant to a latent profile based on similar responding patterns in the data. In addition to these data‐driven components, there is subjective element to LPA such that preference is given for a parsimonious model with good interpretability and theoretical consistency.

Results

Qualitative Analyses

Here we highlight the main findings, but the entire thematic analysis and prompts are available here: https://osf.io/pygbs/. AI identified five themes in each group when analyzing responses describing their experiences of blind rage or anger (Table 1). The rage group was characterized by loss of control and dissociation (Theme 1), and physical manifestations such as ringing in the ears and shaking (Theme 2). Participants in the rage group also reported cognitive impairment and tunnel vision (Theme 4), such that they had difficulty thinking clearly and had a strong focus on the rage trigger. Participants in the rage group also reported regret, numbness, and confusion in the aftermath of the rage incident.

Participants in the anger group reported experiencing anger but were able to control themselves (Themes 2 and 5). They also reported physical expressions of anger, focusing largely on heart rate and breathing (Theme 3). Participants in the anger group also reported that anger was frequently aroused by family and relationship conflict (Theme 5).

In terms of similarities and differences, participants in both groups reported being triggered to anger by injustice and betrayal (Theme 3 in the rage group; Theme 1 in the anger group) and both groups reported similar and physiological expressions (e.g., breathing, heart rate, shaking). However, the rage group differed from the anger group in terms of reporting cognitive impairment/tunnel vision, loss of control, and regret, numbness, and confusion in the aftermath of the event. Participants in the rage group reported more difficulty controlling and being aware of their anger and aggressive behavior than participants in the anger group.

Table 2 shows the five themes identified for each group when describing the antecedents, memories, and aftermath of the rage or intense anger event. Participants in the rage group reported that the rage incident was preceded by a build‐up of negative emotion, such that they were easily triggered when the provocation occurred (Theme 1). Participants in the rage group also reported extreme behaviors such as hitting others and screaming at them (Theme 3). Discussion

In this exploratory investigation into the phenomenology and predictors of rage, we found that rage experiences are more similar to anger experiences than they are different. We suggest that rage is a form of anger with some additional features (e.g., tunnel vision, loss of control, aggression). We also found several common predictors of the tendency to experience rage and anger. Finally, the latent profile analysis identified two subgroups of people who scored relatively high on some of the predictors and were more likely to report having rage experiences in their lives. We discuss each of these findings in turn.

Approximately 27% of participants reported having experienced a rage attack in their lifetime. The qualitative differences that emerged between participants’ self‐reported experiences of rage versus anger were minor and largely focused on lower controllability, more tunnel vision, and aggressive behavior in the rage group. Participants who reported having experienced rage also reported regret, shame, guilt, numbness, emotional collapse, and confusion in the aftermath of the event. Memory problems were also reported in the rage group, such that some participants indicated that witnesses had to tell them what happened. These results suggest that rage is a form of anger, but may be characterized by a more severe response to provocation. Indeed, both the anger and rage groups reported similar sources of provocation (i.e., injustice and betrayal) and physiological responses. Thus, the consequences of rage seem to differ in degree rather than type.

In our variable‐centered analyses, we found that the tendency to experience rage and anger was associated with several of the variables under investigation. These included emotion dysregulation, trait physical aggression, trait displaced aggression, and borderline personality disorder. Indeed, anger, rage, and behavioral dysregulation are observed in borderline personality disorder. Similarly, it is not surprising that participants who reported being high in trait aggression endorsed experiencing more frequent rage and anger attacks. Interestingly, participants who reported engaging in more displaced aggression also reported more rage attacks. This finding is consistent with a previous study on rage in customers (McColl‐Kennedy et al. 2009) and speaks to the dysregulated aggressive behavior associated with rage.

There were also some interesting findings in terms of what did not predict the tendency to experience anger and rage. For instance, alcohol use was uncorrelated with rage and anger. Similarly, head injuries did not predict the tendency to experience rage or anger. However, alcohol use was below problematic levels for the majority of our sample and the proportion of participants who reported head injuries was relatively low. Adverse childhood events did not predict rage either. However, the maximum score was 6 out of 11, suggesting restricted range. Thus, restriction of range may have precluded finding significant relationships.

In our person‐centered analysis, we found four latent profiles in the data. There was a healthy profile, two dysregulated/aggressive profiles differing in intensity, and one drug using/dysregulated profile. The two dysregulated/aggressive profiles were characterized by a general tendency to experience rage, trait aggression and anger, borderline personality disorder traits, trait displaced aggression, and emotion dysregulation. These data do not to show that rage is unique from anger, but only that there is a subgroup of people prone to extreme anger and rage, which is characterized by relatively high scores on these traits. Interestingly, drug use was at average levels in these groups.

Perhaps surprisingly, the drug using/dysregulated profile was not prone to rage. Perhaps our young sample has not had sufficient time to develop cognitive impairment associated with substance use. Alternatively, they may be self‐medicating effectively. However, one issue is that our measure of substance use did not specify which substances people were using. Opposing effects of specific drugs may have limited our ability to detect a relationship between substance use and rage. We also do not know whether participants were intoxicated at the time of the rage event. Furthermore, our sample was predominantly Asian. Culturally diverse groups in Australia are less likely to use alcohol, cannabis, or methamphetamine (Rowe et al. 2020). It was also unexpected that, in the variable‐centered regression analysis, scores on the drug use measure were negatively associated with scores on the Rage Attacks Questionnaire.

Our research is largely consistent with the limited scientific evidence on rage experiences. Our findings suggest that rage is a form of extreme anger, with additional components. Although they share similarities in terms of triggers and physical/physiological responses, rage seems to be additionally characterized by dysregulation and uncontrollability of anger and aggression. Similarly, the cognitive correlates of rage include tunnel vision toward the provocateur and memory difficulties, with some participants reporting amnesia.

The uncontrollability element deserves further investigation as this is perhaps the most harmful aspect of rage. The uncontrollability implies impaired function in the prefrontal cortex. The earliest “sham rage” research focused on showing how subcortical activity, particularly in the hypothalamus, can elicit “automatic” aggression in cats without a cortex (Bard 1928; Cannon and Britton 1925). Research has since demonstrated that stimulating the hypothalamus with electrical current or optogenetics excites extreme aggression in several species (Yamaguchi and Lin 2018). In humans, the dorsolateral, ventromedial, and ventrolateral prefrontal cortices are thought to be the seat of self‐control and emotion regulation. The rapidity and intensity with which rage is elicited suggests a putative mechanism in which subcortical activity bypasses or overwhelms typical prefrontal control mechanisms. The implication is that improving prefrontal function in people prone to rage may help them exert control in the face of uncontrollability. Conversely, impaired function (e.g., from trauma, alcohol and drug use) should increase uncontrollability. Future research may investigate pharmacological interventions such as selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, omega‐3s, or non‐invasive brain stimulation as well as psychological interventions targeting emotion regulation and mindfulness (Butler et al. 2025; Denson and Choy 2024; O'Dean et al. 2025).

There are several limitations to the present research. The first is that, for ethical reasons, we could not manipulate rage and look for systematic differences within an experimental paradigm. Thus, participants were allowed to self‐select into the rage or anger groups. Although we consider it interesting that approximately one‐quarter of participants reported having had a rage experience during their lifetime, the population was an undergraduate sample. Future research could investigate the antecedents, correlates, and consequences of rage in forensic samples. Anecdotally, much violent offending is thought to occur when in a rage state. However, other than one important study investigating the phenomenology of rage in participants with intermittent explosive disorder (Kulper et al. 2015), there has been very little quantitative or qualitative research in this area.

Our research was also limited by the sample type. Although we conducted this first investigation with a convenience sample of university students, future research could include more diverse samples. Generally speaking, college students tend to be less prone to anger and aggression than people who did not attend college (Follingstad et al. 2024). However, there are some mixed results (e.g., Archer et al. 1995), and in a study of nearly 4000 college students, Schwartz et al. (2015) reported that 21% of college students had engaged in violent behavior.

Our research was limited in that we did not include an investigation into the role of shame. There is a rich literature showing that shame is related to anger and aggression (Elison et al. 2014; Tangney et al. 1992). Indeed, participants in our sample who wrote about their rage experiences reported associated shame and guilt. Lewis (1993) mentioned that the experience of shame is what distinguishes rage from anger in that people feel ashamed of their rage reactions more than anger reactions. Similarly, shame derived from adverse childhood experiences may increase anger and aggression (Gilligan 2003). Others have suggested that shame or protecting oneself from feelings of shame is a stronger determinant of violence than rage (Fonagy 2004).

Although we examined traits associated with borderline personality disorder, additional research could examine narcissistic personality disorder. Meta‐analyses of narcissism and aggression consistently show correlations with heightened aggression (Kjærvik and Bushman 2021). It is possible that the triggers that elicit anger and rage in narcissistic individuals differ from that seen in intermittent explosive disorder or borderline personality disorder. For instance, narcissistic individuals are known to respond with aggression when they experience an ego‐threat or their self‐image is impugned (Lambe et al. 2018; Jones and Paulhus 2010). It remains to be seen whether these also trigger rage in other populations.

In sum, the present research identified several similarities between rage and anger. We also identified predictors of anger and rage and two profiles of individuals who are at risk of experiencing rage. The variable‐centered results and the profiles we identified suggest that addressing co‐occurring problems with dysregulation (e.g., emotion dysregulation, borderline personality disorder traits, and aggressiveness) may help prevent extreme anger and rage. 1
