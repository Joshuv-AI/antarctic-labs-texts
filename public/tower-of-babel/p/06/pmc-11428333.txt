Development and validation of a risk prediction model for older adults with social isolation in China
Authors: Qiugui Li, Wenjiao Cen, Tao Yang, Shengru Tao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOlder adults are vulnerable to social isolation due to declining physical and cognitive function, decreased interpersonal interactions, and reduced outdoor activities after retirement. This study aimed to develop and validate a predictive model to assess the risk of social isolation among older adults in China.MethodsUsing data from the 2011 China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). The study cohort was randomly divided into training and validation groups in a 70:30 ratio. We used least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression analysis with tenfold cross-validation to identify optimal predictive factors and examined the correlates of social isolation using logistic regression. A nomogram was constructed for the predictive model, and its accuracy was assessed using calibration curves. The predictive performance of the model was assessed using area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve and decision curve analysis (DCA).ResultsFrom the 2011 CHARLS database, 4,747 older adults were included in the final analysis, of whom 1,654 (34.8%) experienced social isolation. Multifactorial logistic regression identified educational level, marital status, gender, physical activity, physical self -maintenance ability, and number of children as predictive factors for social isolation. The predictive model achieved an AUC of 0.739 (95%CI = 0.722–0.756) in the training set and 0.708 (95%CI = 0.681–0.735) in the validation set. The Hosmer-Lemeshow test yielded P values of 0.111 and 0.324, respectively (both P > 0.05), indicating significant agreement between the nomogram and observed outcomes. The nomogram showed excellent predictive ability according to ROC and DCA.ConclusionsThe predictive model developed to assess the risk of social isolation in the Chinese older adults shows promising utility for early screening and intervention by clinical healthcare professionals.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-20142-3.Keywords: Aged, Social isolation, Predictive model, Risk factors

Introduction

China is the country with the largest number of older adults in the world, featuring a relatively large older adults population base and scale. According to the latest figures from the Ministry of Civil Affairs of the People’s Republic of China, the number of people aged 60 and above in China will reach 280 million by the end of 2022, accounting for 19.8% of the country’s total population [1]. At present, China’s aging process is accelerating, and it is expected that by 2035, the number of older adults aged 60 and above will exceed 400 million, accounting for more than 30% of the total population, entering the stage of severe aging [2]. In addition, with the improvement of living and medical standards, the average life expectancy of older adults in China and the healthy survival time after retirement continue to increase, a variety of physiological and psychological problems are emerging, seriously affecting their quality of life [3]. Social engagement is a critical measure of quality of life for older adults. Research shows that robust social participation is essential to fostering an individual’s social adjustment and improving their physiological, psychological, and social health, thereby significantly increasing their overall quality of life [4].Active engagement in society is at the heart of “successful aging” for older adults. It’s a key strategy in addressing the challenges of an aging population and serves as an important measure of seniors’ health. Positive social participation has a positive impact on older adults’ physical function, psychological state and sense of value in life, as well as helping to reduce future long-term care costs. However, due to a variety of internal and external factors, there is a contradiction between the high desire and low actual participation in social activities among older adults, leading to a pronounced problem of social isolation.

Social isolation refers to the objective lack of social contact and the contraction of social network size [5, 6]. With the onset of old age, changes in physical function, social roles, and family structure can lead to a decline in social function and reduced social participation among older adults. This decline may result in the formation of inadequate social networks, making them particularly vulnerable to social isolation [7].

In recent years, social isolation among older adults has become a pressing social and public health issue. It has been associated with several adverse health outcomes in older adults. Studies have shown that social isolation can accelerate the decline of physical and psychological functioning in older adults and significantly increase the risk of developing diseases such as dementia [8, 9], coronary heart disease [6],stroke [10]and cancer [11].It can also slow recovery from illness and increase the prevalence of comorbidities, rehospitalization rates, disability and mortality in older adults [12],becoming a major factor contributing to premature death and loneliness.

However, current international research mainly focuses on assessing the incidence and contributing factors of social isolation among older adults, revealing its detrimental effects on health and quality of life, and consequently implementing effective interventions [13].Research on social isolation among older adults in China is still in its infancy, and the current status and influencing factors have not been adequately investigated [14]. This study aims to identify factors related to social isolation and incorporate them into the construction of a predictive model of social isolation in older adults, which will provide a reference for early screening and intervention by clinical healthcare professionals.

Methods

Data source

Our study used data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), which is publicly available at http://charls.pku.edu.cn. CHARLS is an ongoing longitudinal survey designed to assess the social, economic, and health circumstances of Chinese individuals aged 45 years and older. The baseline survey began in 2011 and included 150 county-level units and 450 village-level units, with a total of 17,008 participants. Follow-ups are conducted every two years, and to date, four waves of data are available from 2011 (wave 1), 2013 (wave 2), 2015 (wave 3), and 2018 (wave 4). The project uses multistage probability proportional to size (PPS) sampling at the county/district and village/community levels. Our study adheres to the Declaration of Helsinki and informed consent was obtained from all participants. Approval was granted by the Biomedical Ethics Committee of Peking University, Beijing, China (IRB00001052-11015). For this study, we analyzed data from 2011, and after excluding participants with missing data, 4,747 participants (aged 60 years and above) were included in the analysis.

Data extraction

Assessment of social isolation

Social isolation was assessed using four dichotomous indicators: Living Arrangement (1 point for living alone), Marital Status (1 point for being unmarried, which includes separated, divorced, widowed, and never married), Contact with Children (1 point for not contacting children in person or by phone/text/mail/email), and Social Participation (1 point for not participating in social activities such as sports or social clubs, community-based organizations, interacting with friends, providing help, volunteering or charity work, using the Internet, playing mah-jong, or playing cards) [15, 16]. These binary indicators were summed to produce a total score ranging from 0 to 4, with higher scores indicating greater social isolation. Consistent with previous research, we divided participants into two categories based on their social isolation score: those experiencing social isolation (< 2 points) and those not experiencing social isolation (≥ 2 points) [17].

Assessment of depression

The Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CESD-10) was used to assess depressive symptoms. This scale consists of ten questions, each with four possible responses: “rarely or none of the time,” “some or a little of the time,” “occasionally or a moderate amount of the time,” and “most or all of the time,” scored as 0, 1, 2, and 3, respectively. Higher scores indicate more severe depressive symptoms. The total score ranges from 0 to 30, with scores ≥ 10 defining depression and scores < 10 indicating the absence of depression. This scale has demonstrated good reliability and validity in the Chinese population, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.842, effectively measuring depression levels in the middle-aged and older adults [18].

Physical self -maintenance assessment

The Physical Self-Maintenance Assessment includes six activities: dressing, bathing, eating, getting in and out of bed, using the toilet, and controlling bowel and bladder movements. Each activity has four response options: “No, I have no difficulty,” “I have difficulty but can still do it,” “Yes, I have difficulty and need help,” and “I can’t do it. Based on previous studies [19], these are scored from 1 to 4, with a total score ranging from 6 to 24. Higher scores indicate poorer physical self- maintenance ability.

Sociodemographic data

Sociodemographic data include gender, age, education level, marital status, place of residence, and number of children. Sex is categorized as male or female. Education level is categorized as less than primary school, junior/middle school, and high school or above. Marital status is defined as married (if currently married and living with a spouse) or unmarried (if currently separated, divorced, widowed, or never married). Residence is broken down into rural and urban areas. Number of children is based on responses to the CHARLS question “Have you ever given birth to any children? If so, how many are currently living who do not live with you?”

Daily living behavior data

Daily living behavior data include smoking history, drinking history, physical activity, night sleep duration, and lunch break time. Smoking and drinking history are defined as yes or no. Physical activity is determined based on responses to the question, “Now we would like to ask you about the amount of time you spend doing different types of physical activities in a typical week?” and is categorized as “Yes” or “No.” Night sleep and lunch break time are obtained from the questions “In the past month, how many hours of actual sleep did you get at night (average hours for a night)?” and “In the past month, how long did you take a nap after lunch? These are categorized as “<6 h,” “6 ~ 9 h,” and “>9 h” for night sleep, and “0min,” “≤60min,” and “>60min” for nap duration.

Health status data

Health status data include self-perceived health status, physical disability, brain damage, vision problem, hearing problem, speech impediment, hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes, cancer, chronic lung disease, liver disease, heart disease, stroke, kidney disease, emotional disease, memory-related disease, arthritis or rheumatism, asthma, traffic accident history, history of fall, hip fracture, distance vision, near vision, cataract surgery, glaucoma, hearing aid use, wear glasses, hearing, tooth loss, and pain. Distance vision, near vision, hearing, and self-perceived health are defined as good, fair, or poor. The remaining variables are categorized as “yes” or “no”. These variables can all be obtained directly from the CHARLS survey.

Statistical methods

This study used data from the 2011 CHARLS database for analysis. Continuous variables are presented using medians and interquartile ranges, and group comparisons are made using rank-sum tests. Categorical variables are presented as percentages, and comparisons between groups are made using chi-squared tests or Fisher’s exact tests. Data were randomly divided into training (n = 3322) and validation (n = 1425) sets in a 7:3 ratio [20].

Using line graphs to illustrate the risk of social isolation among older adults in China, and using Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression to develop and validate the model. First, predictive factors of social isolation among older adults in China are identified and selected by applying LASSO regression analysis to the training set data [21, 22]. The optimal tuning parameter (λ) for LASSO regression is determined by tenfold cross-validation and the LASSO algorithm is used to select the most significant features. The filtered predictive factors are then included in a multifactorial logistic regression analysis, with predictors with a p-value of less than 0.05 included in the line graph model. All extracted variables have a maximum missing data rate of 20% and multiple imputation methods are used to address the missing data [23].

The validity of the model is assessed in terms of discriminatory ability, accuracy and clinical efficacy. In this study, the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve is used to determine the discriminatory ability of the model. Calibration curves are used to determine the agreement between the risks predicted by the model and the actual occurrence of risks. Decision curve analysis (DCA) is used to assess the clinical applicability of the prediction model.

All analyses in this study are performed using R software (version 4.1.1). All tests are two-tailed and a p-value of less than 0.05 is considered statistically significant.

Incidence and correlates of social isolation

The incidence of social isolation was 34.8%. Several factors, including age, address, marital status, and education level, showed significant differences (P < 0.05) between older adults with and without social isolation. Of the older participants, 3322 (70%) and 1425 (30%) were randomly assigned to the training and validation sets, respectively. Comparisons between the training and validation sets are shown in the supplementary information, with no significant differences between the two groups (P > 0.05).

LASSO and logistic regression

In the LASSO regression, non-zero coefficients were selected as potential predictors of social isolation (Fig. 1A and B). Using the ‘rms’ package in R software, predictors of social isolation were included in a multifactorial logistic regression model. Finally, educational level (P < 0.001), marital status (P < 0.001), gender (P = 0.028), physical activity (P < 0.001), PSMS (P < 0.001) and number of children (P < 0.001) were found to be associated with the development of social isolation in older adults (Table 2).

Predictive model development

LASSO regression analysis was used to select the best predictive factors for the model based on tenfold cross-validation. A predictive model was then developed using multifactorial logistic regression. Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) tests were performed, and all variables had VIF values < 4, indicating no covariance and good model fit. The predictive model included variables with p < 0.05 in multifactorial logistic regression, including education level, marital status, gender, physical activity, PSMS, and number of children. The model is presented as a nomogram, which is useful for quantitatively predicting the risk of social isolation among older adults in China (Fig. 2).

Nomogram

Validation of the prediction models

The incidence of social isolation among older adults was examined in both the training and validation sets, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated to assess the discriminative ability of the model, as shown in Fig. 3A and B. The model had an AUC of 0.739 in the training set with a specificity of 0.507 and a sensitivity of 0.850. In the validation set, the AUC was 0.708 with a specificity of 0.493 and a sensitivity of 0.816. These results indicate that the nomogram has good discriminatory and predictive value in correctly identifying social isolation and non-social isolation.

Model calibration

The nomogram was evaluated using calibration plots and the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test (p > 0.05 indicates very good fit). The test results showed a good fit for both the training and validation sets. Calibration plots based on the multifactorial logistic regression model for the training and validation sets are shown in Fig. 4A and B. The calibration curves of the nomogram show high agreement between the predicted probabilities of social isolation and the actual probabilities in both the training (Fig. 4A) and validation (Fig. 4B) sets.

The clinical effectiveness of the model was evaluated using decision curve analysis (DCA), as shown in Fig. 5A and B. The decision curves indicated that the net benefit of the predictive model was significantly higher than that of the two extreme scenarios for the internal validation set, demonstrating the superior net benefit and predictive accuracy of the nomogram.

Discussion

In clinical medical research, a nomogram, also known as a line graph, is a critical decision support tool [24]. Constructed based on multivariable regression analysis, it allows researchers or clinicians to add up the scores for each relevant predictive factor, resulting in a cumulative score. This score is then used to predict the likelihood of a clinical outcome. For example, this study identified marital status, gender, education level, physical self -maintenance ability, number of children, and physical activity as primary factors in predicting the risk of social isolation in older adults. Using a nomogram, the scores of these individualized factors are integrated to form a comprehensive assessment of the risk of social isolation. In this study, the incidence of social isolation was 34.8%, consistent with previously reported rates of 27.54–42.4% [25, 26]. Social isolation is closely associated with the occurrence of depression, cardiovascular diseases, stroke, and other conditions [27, 28]. Therefore, early identification of individuals at high risk is crucial for the prevention of social isolation and its associated adverse consequences.

The study demonstrated that educational level served as a predictive indicator of social isolation in older adults. The results indicated that older people with lower levels of education were more prone to social isolation than those with higher levels of education, which is consistent with previous studies [29]. The level of education reflects varying degrees of social status, social capital, influence, cognition, economic level, and value orientations, all of which influence the social networks of older adults. For those with lower levels of education, their cultural engagement and social circles are somewhat limited, which inevitably leads to a contraction of their social networks and, consequently, a higher incidence of social isolation compared to those with higher levels of education.

Our study found that marital status is associated with social isolation, with results indicating that unmarried older adults (including those who are divorced, widowed, separated, or never married) are more likely to experience social isolation than their married counterparts, consistent with the findings of Perissinotto and others [30]. Widowed seniors may experience intense loneliness due to the loss of a spouse, while those who are divorced or separated may experience loneliness related to a reduction in social connections, thereby increasing their risk of social isolation [31]. Conversely, marriage can provide stable emotional and psychological support for seniors facing significant life changes, such as retirement or family conflict, as well as physical decline, thereby reducing the likelihood of social withdrawal and the risk of social isolation [32].

The study also found that men were more likely to experience social isolation than women, consistent with research by Henning-Smith and colleagues [33]. This difference is attributed to differences in social networks and levels of social connectedness between the sexes. Research suggests that women have more robust social networks and closer social ties [34], while men tend to have smaller social networks, less dependence on social structures, and greater independence [35].

In this regard, our predictive model suggests that physical activity is associated with social isolation. Physical activity has been shown to reduce anxiety, depression and stress. Neurotransmitters released during exercise, such as endorphins and dopamine, help to improve mood and reduce negative emotions, making individuals more likely to participate in social activities. Through physical activity, individuals can improve their physical health, appearance, and fitness, which can increase their self-esteem and confidence. Increased self-esteem and confidence can make people more willing to engage in social interactions, thereby reducing feelings of social isolation [36, 37]. Some physical activities are inherently communal, such as team sports and fitness classes. Participation in these activities can provide opportunities for socialization, fostering interactions with others and helping to build social networks. In general, physical activity is beneficial for cardiovascular and immune system health and reduces the risk of disease. Better physical health can enable individuals to engage in more social activities, thereby reducing social isolation due to illness. Engaging in physical activity can provide individuals with daily structure and goals, motivating purposeful participation in social functions. This can help develop positive life habits, reducing social isolation that may result from a lack of purpose.

The study also found an association between the number of children and social isolation. Families with more children provide more opportunities for interaction and social engagement among family members. Close relationships and mutual support among siblings can reduce feelings of social isolation. Larger families tend to have stronger internal social networks, including extended relatives and cousins. Such internal networks can provide individuals with additional social support and connections. In larger families, members may be more inclined to share responsibilities, fostering a spirit of cooperation and mutual assistance. This shared sense of duty can encourage more frequent interactions among family members, reducing the risk of social isolation. Having multiple children can provide parents with a greater sense of companionship, reducing feelings of loneliness and potentially preventing social isolation.

In addition, the study found that physical self -maintenance ability is closely associated with social isolation. Deteriorating physical health can lead to a decline in self- maintenance ability. People with serious illnesses, disabilities, or chronic conditions may need more help and support, making them more vulnerable to feelings of social isolation. Physical health problems may also be intertwined with mental health problems. People with chronic illnesses or disabilities may experience psychological distress, depression, or anxiety, leading to reduced social interactions and increased risk of social isolation. Poor self- maintenance skills can lead to greater dependence on others, which can foster a reluctance or fear of engaging in social activities, increasing the risk of social isolation. Social discrimination against people with physical disabilities or poor self- maintenance skills can lead to exclusion or isolation, which in turn can contribute to social isolation. Individuals with impaired self-maintenance abilities may face greater social challenges, and a lack of support from friends, family, or social organizations may make them more vulnerable to loneliness and isolation.

In this study, we successfully established and validated a nomogram model capable of predicting social isolation in older adults. Although the model demonstrated predictive ability, it has limitations. First, because the study data were obtained from a single center, the universality and extrapolation of the results may be limited, which requires future validation in a multicenter setting and among older adults from different regions. Second, given the retrospective design of this study, one critical issue is the potential for recall bias, where participants’ memories of past events or behaviors may be incomplete or distorted, affecting the accuracy of the data collected. Furthermore, issues such as data incompleteness, potential ascertainment bias, and inability to establish causal relationships may affect the accuracy and reliability of the model. Therefore, future research should include more centers with a prospective design to increase the level of evidence and reliability of the results.

Conclusion

This study successfully established and validated a nomogram model capable of predicting social isolation in older adults. Our nomogram model, which includes marital status, education level, gender, physical activity, number of children and physical self -maintenance ability, has been internally validated as a useful tool for risk assessment. The developed predictive model is valuable for screening social isolation in older adults.
