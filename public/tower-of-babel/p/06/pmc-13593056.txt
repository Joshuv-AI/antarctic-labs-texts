Perioperative dexmedetomidine reduces the risk of postoperative complications in high-risk patients undergoing non-cardiac surgery: A randomized controlled trial
Authors: Zhaoting Meng, Ting Ding, Wei Zhao, Lina Guo, Meilin Li, Zhaolong Zhao, Yang Zhang, Chunjing Li, Dongliang Mu, Dongxin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Dexmedetomidine may suppress the surgery-induced inflammatory response, which is considered the underlying mechanism of postoperative complications. This study was designed to investigate whether perioperative dexmedetomidine could decrease the risk of postoperative complications among high-risk patients.Methods:This multicenter randomized controlled trial is a superiority trial. Central randomization was used. Elderly patients (age ≥60 years) with revised cardiac risk index (RCRI) ≥3 and scheduled for major non-cardiac surgery were enrolled. Patients in dexmedetomidine group received intraoperative dexmedetomidine (a loading dose of 0.5 μg/kg followed by 0.3 μg·kg−1·h−1) as adjuvant to general anesthesia and postoperative dexmedetomidine (2 μg/h for 72 h) as supplementation to patient-controlled analgesia with sufentanil. Patients in the control group received an equivalent dose of normal saline as placebo during anesthesia and sufentanil only for postoperative analgesia. The primary outcome was the incidence of major postoperative complications including neurologic, cardiovascular, renal, pulmonary coagulation, infectious, and gastrointestinal systems within postoperative 30 days. Secondary outcomes included pain intensity, sleep quality, postoperative length of in-hospital stay, and medical expenses during hospitalization. Neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) was used to monitor inflammatory response.Results:This study included 272 patients, with similar median age (70 years vs. 69 years) and median RCRI (both 3) between dexmedetomidine and control groups. The incidence of major postoperative complications in the dexmedetomidine group was significantly lower than that in the control group (38.2% [52/136] vs. 52.9% [72/136], relative risk [RR] = 0.722, 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.554–0.942, P = 0.015). Postoperative in-hospital stay was shorter in the dexmedetomidine group than that in the control group (mean difference [MD] = –1 day, 95% CI: –2 to 0 days, P = 0.013). Other secondary outcomes were comparable between the two groups. The highest NLR within postoperative first 3 days in the dexmedetomidine group was lower than that in the control group (MD = –2.1, 95% CI: –4.1 to –0.3, P = 0.037). The proportion of all drug-related adverse events were comparable between the two groups.Conclusion:Dexmedetomidine, infused from the beginning of anesthesia to postoperative 72 h, decreased the risk of postoperative complications in high-risk elderly patients undergoing non-cardiac surgery.Trial registration:No. ChiCTR2000030566 at www.chictr.org.cn

Introduction

The overall incidence of postoperative complications varies from 20% to 40% in elderly patients undergoing major non-cardiac surgery.[1,2] These complications are considered as detrimental contributors to deaths. For example, myocardial injury after noncardiac surgery (MINS) accounts for up to one third of postoperative deaths, whereas sepsis contributes to one tenth of postoperative deaths.[3] Other complications including surgical site infection (SSI), delirium, and acute kidney injury (AKI) are associated with increased risks of postoperative 30-day mortality.[4–6]

Surgery-induced inflammation is one of the key mechanisms of perioperative organ injury.[7,8] Dexmedetomidine and epidural anesthesia have been advocated to suppress the overexpression of inflammatory mediators.[7,8] In in vitro studies, dexmedetomidine exerts protective effects in multiple organs by inhibiting inflammatory responses and modulating immune function.[9] However, related clinical studies are uncertain. Several studies have investigated the cardioprotective effect of intraoperative dexmedetomidine in non-cardiac surgery, but the evidence quality is very low because of limitations in study design and methodology.[10,11] Whether dexmedetomidine can reduce the risks of SSI and AKI in non-cardiac surgery remains unclear because the evidence is only generated from retrospective cohorts and randomized trials with small sample sizes.[12,13] Many studies have reported the prevention effect of dexmedetomidine infused during anesthesia or in postoperative period on delirium.[14]

The administration time of dexmedetomidine, such as adjuvant to general anesthesia or supplementation to postoperative analgesia, is an important factor that affects its clinical outcomes. In most studies, “perioperative dexmedetomidine” is defined as intraoperative infusion of dexmedetomidine with or without infusion to postoperative 12 h.[11,13,14] A low-dose dexmedetomidine is usually infused through patient-controlled intravenous analgesia (PCIA) for up to postoperative 72 h.[15] Compared with intraoperative infusion, postoperative dexmedetomidine is supposed to be more effective in reducing the risk of delirium, improving analgesia management, and promoting sleep quality.[15–17] However, limited data are available to determine whether the combined application of intraoperative dexmedetomidine for general anesthesia and postoperative dexmedetomidine for PCIA can improve clinical outcomes.

This study was designed to investigate whether the combined use of dexmedetomidine for general anesthesia and postoperative analgesia could decrease the risk of postoperative complications in high-risk elderly patients undergoing major non-cardiac surgery.

Methods

Ethical approval

This multicenter randomized controlled trial was approved by the Biomedical Research Ethical Committee of Peking University First Hospital (No. 2019-343). It was registered in Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2000030566, Date of registration: March 7, 2020, https://www.chictr.org.cn/showproj.html?proj=50529). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The first patient was enrolled on September 1, 2020. The study was conducted in three teaching hospitals, including Peking University First Hospital, The Fourth Hospital of Hebei Medical University, and Liaocheng People’s Hospital, and reported following the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) statement.

Participants

Elderly patients (age ≥60 years) were enrolled if they were scheduled for major non-cardiac surgery with revised cardiac risk index (RCRI) ≥3 and expected duration of surgery ≥2 h.

RCRI is widely used to predict the risk of cardiac complications in patients undergoing non-cardiac surgery. It contains six independent predictors including: (1) elevated-risk surgery (i.e., intraperitoneal, intrathoracic, and supra-inguinal vascular surgery); (2) ischemic heart disease; (3) congestive heart failure; (4) cerebrovascular disease; (5) pre-operative treatment with insulin, and (6) serum creatinine >176.8 µmol/L. Patients with three or more predictors were classified as high risk.[18]

Patients who met any of the following criteria were excluded: (1) bradycardia (heart rate ≤50 beats per min [bpm]), sick sinus syndrome, second- or third-degree atrioventricular block without pacemaker implantation; (2) persistent atrial fibrillation; (3) scheduled for neurosurgery; (4) body weight <50 kg or >90 kg; (5) Child-Pugh C; (6) chronic renal failure requiring renal replacement therapy; and (7) conditions that impeded assessment such as dementia and coma.

Randomization and blinding

Random numbers were generated in a 1:1 ratio with a block size of 4 by using SAS statistical package (version 9.2, SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). The numbers were sealed in opaque envelopes. At one hour before surgery, designated researchers in each center who were not involved in intervention administration and postoperative follow-up assigned random numbers to patients in sequence of enrollment.

Blind method was implemented for anesthesiologists taking care of intervention administration, researchers corresponding for postoperative assessment and follow-up, and patients. The blind method was kept until the end of study. Emergency unmasking could be conducted in the occurrence of serious adverse events or in necessity of treatment.

Intervention

The doses of intraoperative and postoperative dexmedetomidine were selected based on previous studies and were validated to reduce the incidence of postoperative delirium and improve sleep quality.[2,15,19]

All study drugs were colorless liquid. They were prepared in 50 mL syringes (dexmedetomidine 4.00 μg/mL vs. normal saline) for intraoperative infusion and 100 mL reservoir bags (dexmedetomidine 2.00 μg/mL plus sufentanil 1.00 μg/mL vs. sufentanil 1.25 μg/mL) for postoperative PCIA.

Patients were randomized to receive perioperative dexmedetomidine or not. The study drugs were given at the same infusion rate between the groups. A loading dose of 0.125 mL/kg of the study drug (i.e., equivalent to dexmedetomidine 0.5 μg/kg) was given for 10 min before anesthesia induction followed by 0.075 mL∙kg−1∙h−1 (i.e., equivalent to dexmedetomidine 0.3 μg∙kg−1∙h−1) until 1 h before the end of surgery. After the surgery, PCIA was used from extubation to postoperative 72 h. The background infusion rate of PCIA was programmed at 1 mL/h (i.e., equivalent to dexmedetomidine 2 μg/h) with a bolus dose of 2 mL (i.e., equivalent to dexmedetomidine 4 μg per se) and a block time of 8 min.

Anesthesia and perioperative management

Eligible patients were screened at the day before surgery. Baseline data were collected including demographic data, comorbidities, and main examination results. Cognitive function was assessed using short-version Montreal cognitive assessment (MoCA 5-min, a 15-score scale with higher score for better cognitive function).[20] Delirium was screened using 3-min diagnostic interview for confusion assessment method (3D-CAM).[21] The severity of comorbidities was assessed using Charlson comorbidity index (CCI, a 56-score scale with higher score for worse conditions).[22] Sleep quality was assessed using Pittsburgh sleep quality index (PSQI, a 27-score scale with higher score for poorer sleep quality).[23]

After arrival at the operating room, all patients received standard monitoring including electrocardiogram, non-invasive blood pressure, peripheral arterial oxygen saturation, nasopharyngeal temperature, end-tidal carbon dioxide (ETCO2), and bispectral index (BIS). Advanced hemodynamic parameters such as cardiac index and stroke volume variation could be monitored if necessary.

Anesthesia induction was conducted using propofol (2–4 mg/kg) with or without etomidate (0.3–0.5 mg/kg) and remifentanil (effect-site concentration 2–5 ng/mL). Continuous infusion of propofol (4–12 mg·kg–1·h–1) and remifentanil (effect-site concentration 2–5 ng/mL) was used for anesthesia maintenance. Sevoflurane (1–2% inspired concentration) was used for supplementation as needed. Peripheral nerve block could be used at the discretion of anesthesiologists. The aim of anesthesia was to maintain BIS between 40 and 60, systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥90 mmHg or 80% of the baseline value, and nasopharyngeal temperature between 36°C and 37°C. Postoperative analgesia aimed to keep the numeric rating score (NRS, a 11-score scale, 0 for no pain and 10 for worst pain) lower than 4. If NRS was higher than or equal to 4 after three additional boluses of PCIA, then sufentanil 5–10 μg in post-anesthesia care unit or parecoxib 40 mg/flurbiprofen 50 mg in general ward could be given as rescue analgesics.

Face-to-face interview was conducted twice daily (08:00–10:00 and 18:00–20:00) during the first 5 days after surgery or until discharge (whichever came first). Then, patients were followed up once a week during hospitalization or by telephone after discharge until postoperative 30 days.

Primary outcome

The primary outcome was the incidence of major postoperative complications that required medical treatments (i.e., Clavien–Dindo classification grade 2 or above) within 30 days after surgery.[24]

Postoperative complications were composed of major adverse events including neurologic (delirium and stroke), cardiovascular (atrial fibrillation, acute heart failure, MINS, and cardiac arrest), renal (AKI according to the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes [KDIGO] criteria), pulmonary (pneumonia and respiratory failure), coagulation (deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism), infectious (SSI and sepsis), and gastrointestinal (bleeding, obstruction, and anastomotic fistula) systems. The definitions and diagnostic criteria of individual complications were provided in Supplementary Table 1, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C468.

Delirium was assessed twice daily within postoperative 5 days using 3D-CAM for non-intubated patients or confusion assessment method for intubated patients in the intensive care unit.[21,25] Stroke was defined as a new onset of focal neurologic deficit confirmed by imaging examination.[26] New onset atrial fibrillation was confirmed by 12-lead electrocardiogram. Acute heart failure was diagnosed by clinical symptoms (i.e., dyspnea, moist rales, and cardiomegaly) with B-type natriuretic peptide ≥2000 pg/mL. Cardiac arrest refers to the sudden termination of cardiac ejection function such as ventricular fibrillation and asystole. Serum cardiac troponin I (cTnI) and creatine were monitored for 5 continuous days after surgery. Acute myocardial injury was established if cTnI was higher than the 99% upper limit of the normal range (i.e., 0.05 ng/mL).[27] AKI was diagnosed by creatine according to the KDIGO criteria.[28] Pneumonia was diagnosed if a patient met at least one item of the following criteria and treated with antibiotics: new emerging phlegm or acute changes in characteristics, new emerging or progressive infiltration on X-ray, body temperature ≥38.3°C and leukocyte count ≥12,000/mL. Respiratory failure was defined as PaO2 <60 mmHg when breathing room air or oxygenation index <300 or mechanical ventilation ≥48 h. Deep vein thrombosis was confirmed by ultrasound. Pulmonary embolism was diagnosed according to clinical symptoms with or without confirmation by imaging examinations such as computed tomography pulmonary angiography. SSI was defined by clinical symptoms of infection at deep surgical site with or without positive pathogenic tests.[29] Sepsis was defined as infection-induced organ dysfunction and Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score ≥2.[30] Other surgery-related complications included gastrointestinal bleeding (diagnosed by endoscopic examination or clinical symptoms such as hematemesis or positive occult blood test), gastrointestinal obstruction (diagnosed by clinical symptoms and abdominal X-ray), and anastomotic fistula (confirmed by imaging examination).

Secondary outcomes

Secondary outcomes included pain intensity within the postoperative first 3 days, sleep quality within postoperative first three nights, postoperative length of in-hospital stay, and medical expenses during hospitalization.

Pain intensity with movement was assessed using NRS. Sleep quality was assessed by the Chinese version of Richards–Campbell sleep questionnaire (RCSQ).[31] RCSQ evaluates five domains of sleep including latency, efficiency, depth, number of awakenings, and overall sleep quality. RCSQ ranges from 0 to 100 with higher scores for better sleep quality. Postoperative length of in-hospital stay was defined as the duration from surgery day to discharge. Medical expenses meant the overall expense of drugs and therapy during hospitalization.

Exploratory outcomes

Neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) and hypersensitive C-reaction protein (hs-CRP) were examined at baseline and during postoperative first 3 days.[32] Previous evidences showed that elevation of the two inflammation biomarkers were associated with an increased risk of postoperative complications.[33–35]

Safety outcomes

Drug-related safety outcomes were monitored from the beginning of drug infusion to 72 h after surgery. The following events were recorded: hypotension (SBP <90 mmHg or 70% baseline value), hypertension (SBP >180 mmHg or 130% baseline value), bradycardia (heart rate <45 bpm or 70% baseline value), tachycardia (heart rate >100 bpm or 130% baseline value), hypoxemia (SpO2 <90% when breathing room air), and over sedation (Richmond agitation sedation scale ≤−2 during PCIA).

Sample size

To determine the referral incidence of postoperative complications in high-risk patients, a total of 212 elder patients (age ≥60 years) with RCRI ≥3 were screened in previous study.[2] The incidence of major postoperative complications was about 57% in these patients after non-cardiac surgery. Several studies showed that either intraoperative or postoperative dexmedetomidine decreased the risk of postoperative complications by 40% to 50%.[2,36] Our study assumed that the overall incidence of postoperative complications was 50% and perioperative dexmedetomidine could decrease the risk by 40% (i.e., from 50% to 30%). With significance level and power set at 0.05 (two-sided) and 0.9, respectively, 124 patients in each arm were required to detect the difference. Considering a drop-out rate of 10%, we planned to enroll 272 patients in total (136 patients in each group).

Statistical analysis

Normality of continuous variables was tested using histogram. Variables with normal distribution were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and analyzed using independent sample t-test. Variables without normal distribution and ordinal data were presented as median (Q1, Q3) and analyzed using Wilcoxon test. Categorical data were presented as number (percentage) and analyzed using chi-squared test or Fisher exact test. Time-to-event results were presented as median (95% confidence interval, CI) and analyzed using the Kaplan–Meier survival analysis. The difference between groups was tested by log-rank test.

The incidence of postoperative complications between the two groups was compared using chi-squared test and estimated effect size was presented as relative risk (RR) and 95% CI. The cumulative risk of postoperative complications within 30 days was analyzed using Kaplan–Meier analysis and hazard ratios (HRs) were calculated using Cox regression analysis. For secondary and exploratory outcomes, the between-group differences of pain intensity, sleep quality, postoperative length of in-hospital stay, medical expenses, and postoperative NLR and hs-CRP were analyzed using Wilcoxon test and the median differences and 95% CI were calculated using the Hodges–Lehmann estimator. Multiplicity was not adjusted among secondary outcomes. The safety outcomes were presented as number (percentage) and analyzed using chi-squared test or Fisher exact test. Statistical analysis was performed with SPSS 19.0 statistical package (SPSS Inc, Chicago, IL, USA) and STATA 9.0 (Stata Corporation, College Station, TX, USA). A two-sided P value <0.05 was considered as statistical significant.

Results

Participants

From September 1, 2020 to December 4, 2022, 326 patients were screened for eligibility and 272 patients were randomized [Figure 1]. One patient in the dexmedetomidine group died of cardiac arrest on postoperative 11th day. All other patients completed postoperative 30-day follow-up.

Primary outcome

The overall incidence of major postoperative complications in the dexmedetomidine group was significantly lower than that in the control group (38.2% [52/136] vs. 52.9% [72/136], RR = 0.722, 95% CI 0.554–0.942, P = 0.015; Table 3). The occurrence of major postoperative complications was later in the dexmedetomidine group than in the control group (20.1 days [95% CI: 17.9–22.3 days] vs. 15.6 days [95% CI 13.2–17.9 days], hazard ratio [HR] = 0.625, 95% CI: 0.434–0.899, P = 0.006; Figure 2).

Secondary outcomes

No statistical difference was found in pain intensity and subjective sleep quality at each time point between the two groups (all P values >0.05; Table 3). A shorter length of postoperative in-hospital stay in the dexmedetomidine group (7 [5, 9] days vs. 8 [6, 12] days, P = 0.013) was reported. Medical expenses during hospitalization were comparable between the two groups (P = 0.125).

Exploratory analysis

The highest NLR within postoperative first 3 days was lower in the dexmedetomidine group than in the control group (9.2 [95% CI: 6.6–12.5] vs. 11.9 [95% CI: 7.0–16.8], P = 0.037), but no statistically significant difference was found in hs-CRP [Figure 3A and B].

Safety outcomes

All drug-related adverse events including hypotension, hypertension, bradycardia, tachycardia, and hypoxia were comparable between the two groups. No patient suffered from over sedation during PCIA.

Discussion

This study found that dexmedetomidine, infused from the beginning of anesthesia to postoperative 72 h, decreased the risk of major postoperative complications in high-risk elderly patients undergoing non-cardiac surgery.

In this study, the overall incidence of postoperative complications was as high as 53%, which might be attributed to the enrollment of high-risk patients. We used RCRI, a commonly used prediction model of cardiac complications, to screen high-risk patients because cardiac complications might contribute to one third of perioperative deaths.[1,18] The top three complications were infectious, respiratory, and cardiac complications. The incidence of cardiac complications was 9.2%, which falls in the predicted range of RCRI (i.e., 9–11% in patients with three or more predictors).[18]

Dexmedetomidine was infused from the beginning of anesthesia to postoperative 72 h in this study. Although many studies have reported the effect of “perioperative dexmedetomidine” on clinical outcomes, dexmedetomidine is mostly infused either during anesthesia for several hours with a larger dose (i.e., a loading dose of 0.5–1.0 µg/kg followed by 0.2–0.7 µg∙kg−1∙h−1) or in postoperative PCIA for 2 to 3 days with mini-dose (i.e., 0.02–0.10 µg∙kg−1∙h−1).[11,13,14] The administration time and dosage of dexmedetomidine may have an important influence on clinical outcomes. For example, several meta-analyses have reported that postoperative dexmedetomidine is more efficient in reducing the risk of postoperative delirium in comparison with intraoperative infusion.[16,37] Moreover, intraoperative low-dose (i.e., <0.2 µg∙kg−1∙h−1) and postoperative mini-dose dexmedetomidine, but not intraoperative high-dose, were associated with better postoperative sleep quality.[15,38,39] However, few data are available on the combined effect of intraoperative and postoperative dexmedetomidine on major postoperative complications.

The combination of intraoperative and postoperative infusion of dexmedetomidine decreased the incidence of major postoperative complications. In cardiac surgery, dexmedetomidine was associated with low postoperative 30-day mortality rates and lower morbidity including cardiac complications and delirium.[40] This meta-analysis also included eight studies about non-cardiac surgery. In seven of them, dexmedetomidine was infused during anesthesia. However, the association between dexmedetomidine and major postoperative complications remains unclear in patients undergoing non-cardiac surgery.[40] Other studies also reported that the effects of dexmedetomidine, mainly infused during anesthesia, on AKI and cardiac injury were still inconclusive.[11,13] Dexmedetomidine exerts multiple organ protective effects by inhibiting surgery-induced inflammatory responses, which may last for 2–3 days after surgery. The elimination half-life of dexmedetomidine is about 2 h.[7–9] Thus, we supposed that continuous infusion of dexmedetomidine during postoperative 2–3 days might benefit patients undergoing non-cardiac surgery. For example, dexmedetomidine as adjuvant to PCIA could significantly lower the level of serum inflammatory mediators in patients undergoing posterior lumbar interbody fusion surgery.[7–9]

We used NLR and hs-CRP to reflect fluctuation of systematic inflammatory response. Both NLR and hs-CRP have been used as biomarkers to predict the risk of postoperative complications.[41] Our study found that the highest NLR was lower in patients who received dexmedetomidine within postoperative 3 days. One randomized trial also reported that intraoperative dexmedetomidine could decrease NLR during postoperative 48 h in patients after laparoscopic pancreaticoduodenectomy.[42] A systematic review and meta-analysis has reported that dexmedetomidine could significantly inhibit the overexpression of hs-CRP.[43] This evidence provides supplemental explanation to the organ protection effect of dexmedetomidine. Several studies also reported that dexmedetomidine might promote wound healing and reduce SSI by inhibiting the pro-inflammatory cytokine production and enhancing the immune function.[7–9] Considering the limited sample size and less infectious events in the present study, the association between dexmedetomidine and infectious events should be examined by large sample size studies.

Pain intensity and sleep quality were comparable between the dexmedetomidine and control groups. Based on previous studies, dexmedetomidine as an adjuvant for postoperative analgesia could reduce opioid consumption by 20–30% after non-cardiac surgery.[15,19] For the safety of high-risk patients, we reduced the concentration of sufentanil in PCIA reservoir bag by 20% in dexmedetomidine group than in control group. This might be the reason for the similar pain intensity between the two groups as well as the lower consumption of opioids during postoperative 72 h in dexmedetomidine group. Previous studies have reported that dexmedetomidine as an adjuvant to postoperative analgesia could improve subjective sleep quality.[15,19] This was not observed in our study because baseline sleep quality and postoperative pain intensity (two major risk factors of postoperative sleep disturbance) were comparable between the two groups.[44]

Patients in the dexmedetomidine group experienced slightly shorter postoperative length of in-hospital stay and lower medical expenses. However, it had no statistical significance in comparison between the two groups.

The incidences of drug-related adverse events were comparable between the two groups. The dose regimen is thus safe for elderly patients with high risk of postoperative complications.

There were some limitations in our study. First, long-term outcomes were not followed up in our study. Several studies indicated that perioperative dexmedetomidine was associated with better long-term survival in patients after cardiac and non-cardiac surgery.[45,46] Second, our study took the overall postoperative complications as primary outcome which was commonly used in perioperative medicine.[1,18] However, the calculated sample size was underpowered to examine the organ protective effect of dexmedetomidine on individual complications. Further studies with larger sample sizes are needed to validate the results, especially the effect of dexmedetomidine on infectious complications.

In conclusion, perioperative dexmedetomidine, infused from the beginning of anesthesia induction to postoperative 72 hours, decreased the risk of postoperative complications in high-risk elder patients undergoing non-cardiac surgery.
