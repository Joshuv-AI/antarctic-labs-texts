Research interest in nursing undergraduate students: an exploration of research-linked courses
Authors: Marisol Angulo-Ramos, César Merino-Soto, Dayana Guillinta-Herrera, Giorgio Aquije-Cardenas, Guillermo M Chans
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAmong Hispanic undergraduate students, the association between research-related coursework and research interest remains largely underexplored. This exploratory study examined differences in research interest among undergraduate nursing students according to their exposure to research-related coursework, drawing on Social Cognitive Career Theory.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted with 106 nursing students from a Peruvian university. Participants completed the Interest in Research Questionnaire (IRQ), which demonstrated excellent internal consistency (α = 0.98). Research interest was compared across four academic-semester groupings reflecting proximal and distal exposure to research-related coursework using rank-based nonparametric methods.ResultsSignificant differences in research interest were observed across groups (Anderson–Darling test = 9.41, p = 0.001), indicating a curvilinear pattern. Students currently or recently exposed to research-related coursework (levels 2 and 4) reported significantly higher research interest than those with more distal exposure (level 3; p < 0.05; medium effect sizes). First-semester students showed research interest levels comparable to those of the proximally exposed groups.ConclusionResearch interest may decline in the absence of sustained engagement in research activities, underscoring the importance of early and continuous integration of research experiences throughout undergraduate nursing curricula. The findings also provide initial evidence supporting the use of the IRQ in Spanish-speaking undergraduate contexts.Keywords: Nursing students, research interest, higher education, research training, educational innovation

Introduction

Courses aimed at developing and strengthening research skills are well recognized for enhancing students’ and professionals’ capacity to engage effectively in research [1,2]. The instructional approach chosen by the instructor significantly influences the development of these skills. Among health science students, the link between research-related courses and skill acquisition is reinforced when instruction is grounded in evidence-based approaches [1] and when students actively participate and interact in class [3]. These benefits are not limited to skills alone; positive shifts in attitudes toward research, particularly interest in research, also emerge.

Research interest can be defined as a disposition or attitude toward participating in research activities [4]. According to this view, students demonstrate interest when actively engaging in—or attempting to engage in—scientific inquiry. This construct is understood within the Social Cognitive Career Theory (SCCT) framework [4,5], and it is often included in studies examining intentions to pursue graduate studies, academic productivity, or research engagement [6–8].

Courses designed to foster research-related competencies may play a pivotal role in shaping interest in research. Studies suggest that exposure to such classes may enhance scientific thinking skills and students’ intrinsic motivation to engage in research activities [2,9]. This association may be further supported by the fact that these courses contribute to students’ outcome expectations related to research and to their self-efficacy in key research competencies [6,10,11].

From a theoretical perspective, these processes can be meaningfully understood within the framework of SCCT, which explains how cognitive and motivational factors contribute to the development of academic and career-related interests. Within the SCCT, self-efficacy refers to individuals’ beliefs about their capability to successfully perform domain-specific tasks, whereas outcome expectations refer to beliefs about the anticipated consequences of engaging in those tasks [12]. Both constructs are considered central mechanisms in the development of academic and career-related interests, as they shape how individuals evaluate their abilities and the potential benefits of engaging in a given activity [4,13]. Empirical evidence consistently highlights the mediating role of self-efficacy in interest formation, while outcome expectations also contribute, albeit with less consistent support across contexts [13].

In nursing contexts, SCCT-based research has shown that outcome expectations are positively associated with career interest, supporting the relevance of these constructs for understanding professional motivation in health-related fields [14]. Exposure to research-related coursework may be interpreted as a contextual factor that shapes these cognitive mechanisms and, in turn, students’ interest in research. Although the present study does not directly measure self-efficacy or outcome expectations, these constructs provide a useful theoretical lens to interpret potential differences in research interest across levels of exposure to research-related coursework.

While the existing literature supports these findings within Anglo-Saxon populations, equivalent evidence for Spanish-speaking populations remains limited, especially among nursing students. Most Hispanic-focused research has examined general attitudes toward research [3,15,16], whereas interest in research has received comparatively little attention as a distinct, measurable, and potentially modifiable construct.

In fact, among Spanish-speaking nursing students, there appears to be a significant gap: (a) research interest is rarely discussed as a core construct in review articles, and (b) it is not typically assessed directly but inferred from broader measures of research attitudes [17]. Additionally, studies examining the relationship between exposure to research courses during undergraduate studies and potential increases in research interest are particularly scarce.

Research plays a fundamental role in strengthening health systems [11], and offering research-related courses or workshops during training supports the development of essential research skills. These skills, in turn, contribute to more positive attitudes toward research, especially among students approaching graduation [3,15,16]. However, these courses should not only train students to be consumers of research but also to become producers of it [18]. Becoming a producer of research entails active participation in scientific activities, which aligns with the definition of research interest.

If exposure to courses covering research-related topics—such as applied statistics, research methodology, instrument development, and thesis projects— is associated with higher levels of research interest, then undergraduate students currently enrolled in these courses should demonstrate higher levels of research interest. Conversely, students who have completed such courses or are not currently taking them may exhibit comparatively lower levels of interest. To better understand the relationship between research interest and exposure to research-focused coursework, this study examined differences in research interest among nursing students based on their current or prior enrollment in research-related courses.

Methodology

Study design and participants

This study employed a cross-sectional, non-experimental design. Participants were Peruvian undergraduate students enrolled in a regular nursing program at a private university, spanning from the first semester (first year) to the eighth semester (fourth year). The total population consisted of 300 students.

All students present during the administration of the IRQ were eligible to participate, thereby determining the final sample size. The sample consisted of 106 undergraduate nursing students (85 women, 80.2%; 21 men, 19.8%), with a mean age of 29.49 years (SD = 10.47). This relatively high mean age and variability reflect the presence of non-traditional students, including individuals returning to education, pursuing a second career, or combining academic studies with professional responsibilities, which is common in nursing programs. The institution was selected based on the researcher’s accessibility and the availability of classroom settings during the data collection period. Given resource constraints, participants’ availability, and the small size of the institution, a non-probabilistic sampling approach was employed, with efforts made to maximize participation across semesters. The final distribution of participants by semester was as follows: I (10, 9.4%), II (23, 21.7%), III (1, 0.9%), IV (2, 1.9%), V (14, 13.2%), VI (21, 19.8%), VII (1, 0.9%), and VIII (34, 32.1%). This uneven distribution reflects the actual enrollment patterns of students within this small institution. Students in the ninth and tenth semesters did not participate due to access limitations during the data collection phase, as these students were primarily engaged in community-based activities with limited classroom attendance. All participants were regular students; none had prior or concurrent involvement in formal research groups at the time of the study.

Institutional review board approval and consent to participate

All participants provided written informed consent under conditions of complete anonymity. They were informed that the results would be published in academic journals upon completion of the study.

The study was conducted in accordance with relevant international ethical regulations, including the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki [19] and the National Commission for the Protection of Human Subjects of Biomedical and Behavioral Research [20], as well as applicable disciplinary [21] and local guidelines [22]. Additionally, the study received approval from the Ethics Committee of the Universidad Autónoma de Ica (approval code: CO-001-01-2024/CE).

Instrument

Sociodemographic sheet. One of the authors developed a custom questionnaire to gather sociodemographic information, including biological sex, age, place of birth, and other relevant demographic variables.

Interest in Research Questionnaire (IRQ)[23]. This 16-item self-report measure assesses interest in engaging in specific research activities. Responses are rated on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from “Very Uninterested” to “Very Interested.” Total scores are obtained by summing item responses, with higher scores indicating greater interest in research. A recent study involving Hispanic undergraduate students reported adequate psychometric properties of the IRQ [24]. The Spanish version of the questionnaire used in the present study is provided in the Supplementary Material (Table S1). In the current sample, the psychometric properties of the IRQ were evaluated, yielding satisfactory validity indicators (see Procedure and Supplementary Material, Table S2).

Procedure

Data collection. Data was collected during the first academic semester at a private institution in Ica, Peru, over a two-month period (March–May 2023). Data were collected through a structured, self-administered online survey implemented using Google Forms, which included informed consent, sociodemographic information, and the main research questionnaire.

Access to the survey was provided via a link shared by the course instructor during students’ scheduled virtual class sessions. The link was distributed approximately 1 hour before the end of the session to allow sufficient time to complete. The instructor remained present during the survey administration to ensure that students had sufficient time to complete it and to encourage attentive responses, without influencing the content of participants’ answers.

Participants were informed about the anonymity of their responses, the non-sensitive nature of the questionnaire content, the voluntary nature of their participation, their right to withdraw from the study at any time, and the absence of any incentives.

Analysis. Since no missing responses were found, the first step was to conduct a psychometric analysis of the Interest in Research Questionnaire (IRQ) using a nonparametric item response theory model with kernel smoothing to estimate the latent construct [25], which does not assume a specific parametric distribution of the latent variable. Model fit was assessed based on the following criteria: a) the magnitude of the item-total polyserial correlations (r pol > 0.60), b) the relative similarity among them, and c) the reliability coefficient, with α > 0.90.

Maximum likelihood (ML) scores were computed for each participant using item parameters to improve the precision of the estimated latent trait scores [25,26]. Compared to raw summed scores, ML-based scores incorporate item-level information by weighting the scores based on the degree of validity of each item with respect to its latent construct; this provides more accurate estimates of the underlying construct. Therefore, the primary analyses were conducted using these ML scores rather than raw scores (i.e., the unweighted sum of item responses).

The second analytical approach examined group differences in exposure to research-related courses. Given the small sample sizes per semester and to reduce Type II error, semesters were regrouped into four levels (see Supplementary Material Table S3). This regrouping was designed to reflect a proximal–distal structure in relation to exposure to research-related courses while also increasing the sample size within each analytical group. Two of these levels represented students who were proximally exposed to research courses (i.e., currently enrolled or enrolled in the previous semester): level 2 (semesters 2 and 3) and level 4 (semesters 7 and 8). The remaining levels (semesters 1, 4, 5, and 6) were considered distally exposed (i.e., not currently taking research courses). Accordingly, the semester was not modeled as an ordinal predictor, as the primary focus of the analysis was on differences in exposure to research-related coursework rather than on a linear progression across semesters.

A global test for differences across groups was performed using the Anderson-Darling test [27] to evaluate the null hypothesis that the four independent groups originated from a common unspecified distribution. Subsequently, nonparametric multiple contrast tests with simultaneous confidence intervals were performed using the independent samples framework [28]. Given the study’s focus on specific target groups—those currently or recently exposed to research courses—a many-to-one contrast procedure [29] was implemented to compare the proximal exposure groups (levels 2 and 4) against the more distal groups.

ML scores were transformed into global rankings [30]. Relative effect sizes and relative contrast effects were computed, along with nonparametric simultaneous confidence intervals and p-values [28,30]. The relative effect size was interpreted as the probability that one group would achieve a higher or lower score than another. Additionally, Cliff's delta (δ) [31] was used as a supplementary effect size measure, interpreted as a standardized difference between groups. Unlike relative effect sizes, Cliff’s δ has established interpretative thresholds. Interpretation thresholds for δ were based on Romano and Kromrey [32]: negligible (|δ| < 0.147), small (0.147 ≤ |δ| < 0.33), medium (0.33 ≤ |δ| < 0.474), and large (|δ| ≥ 0.474). All analyses were conducted in R using the following packages: effsize [33], KernSmoothIRT [26], ksamples [34], nparcomp [30], and MVN [35].

Results

The results were divided into two sections: (1) evidence of validity for the IRQ scores, and (2) group differences (including both global and specific contrasts).

Validity of interest in research scores

The items showed high and consistent polyserial correlations, ranging from 0.62 to 0.95 (M = 0.84, SD = 0.075), supporting the unidimensional structure of the IRQ as initially proposed by Bishop and Bieschke [23] (see Supplementary Material, Table S2). Internal consistency was excellent, with Cronbach’s alpha = 0.98 (95% CI [0.98, 0.98]).

Group differences

Global comparison. Using the Anderson–Darling k-sample test (number of ties = 80), the null hypothesis that all samples originated from a common population was rejected, AD = 9.412 (standardized AD = 4.992), p = 0.001. A statistically significant global effect was found (Quantile test = 2.559, p = 0.011), indicating that at least one group differed in its distribution of ML scores. As shown in Table 1, the level 3 group exhibited the lowest relative effect (0.335), with a 95% CI [0.271, 0.406] that does not overlap with the CIs of the other groups. Figure 1 displays the distribution of ML scores across groups, suggesting a possible non-linear (quadratic) pattern.

Discussion

This study presents preliminary findings on the relationship between exposure to research-related courses and research interest among undergraduate nursing students. The results indicate that students who had taken research-related courses two or three semesters earlier but were no longer engaged in such courses are associated with reported lower interest in research activities. This pattern suggests that the absence of ongoing engagement in research activities may be associated with diminished research interest. At this stage, students are often more focused on clinical training and other academic demands, which may shift their priorities away from research compared with those of students currently enrolled in courses designed to develop research skills.

Given that research interest has been consistently associated with self-efficacy [6,10,11] and outcome expectations [36,37], it is plausible that higher perceived research self-efficacy developed during these courses, along with more positive expectations about engaging in research, may be related to the higher levels of interest observed in more recently exposed groups.

From an SCCT perspective, these findings may be interpreted as reflecting variations in both self-efficacy and outcome expectations across different stages of academic progression. Research-related courses may provide mastery experiences that strengthen students’ confidence in their research abilities (self-efficacy) and shape their expectations about the value and relevance of engaging in research activities (outcome expectations). In turn, these cognitive mechanisms are theorized to play a central role in the development and maintenance of interest. Accordingly, the lower levels of research interest observed among students who are no longer actively engaged in such courses may reflect a reduction in these reinforcing experiences over time. However, because neither self-efficacy nor outcome expectations were directly assessed in this study, these interpretations should be considered tentative and serve primarily as a theoretically grounded explanation for the observed patterns. Accordingly, SCCT is used in the present study as an interpretive framework to contextualize the findings rather than as a theory empirically examined through the measured variables.

An unexpected and noteworthy finding was that first-semester students—who had no prior institutional exposure to research courses—demonstrated research interest comparable to that of students currently enrolled in such classes. This finding challenges the assumption that higher interest is necessarily linked to formal exposure to research training. In this case, self-efficacy may not yet be a relevant explanatory factor; instead, the relatively high levels of interest observed among first-semester students may reflect dispositional factors, such as individual curiosity, emerging vocational tendencies, or cognitive styles [38].

General implications

These findings offer valuable insights for nursing education. The strong initial interest observed among new students could be intentionally nurtured and sustained through structured activities that promote early engagement in research. Notably, the findings suggest that research interest may not be static but may evolve across students’ academic trajectories, potentially in relation to their exposure to research-related learning experiences.

From a practical perspective, several evidence-based educational strategies may help sustain students’ research interest, self-efficacy, and research competencies throughout their academic trajectory. Early exposure to research activities, participation in undergraduate research experiences (UREs), structured mentoring initiatives, research seminars, and scaffolded inquiry opportunities have all been associated with positive research-related outcomes. In particular, UREs have consistently demonstrated benefits for students’ research self-efficacy, sense of scientific belonging, and intention to pursue future scholarly or research-oriented careers [39–41].

In nursing education, practice-oriented approaches appear especially promising. Integrating research activities into experiential or clinical learning contexts may help bridge the gap between research theory and professional practice by enabling students to apply evidence-based reasoning to authentic clinical problems [42]. Likewise, collaborative inquiry experiences embedded in coursework, such as longitudinal scoping review projects, have been associated with gains in information literacy, appreciation of research, and evidence-based practice competencies [43].

Research mentoring represents another key mechanism for sustaining research engagement. Mentors who provide socioemotional support and serve as visible role models may strengthen students’ vicarious learning experiences and research self-beliefs [44,45]. Moreover, perceived mentor effectiveness has been linked to positive academic and post-baccalaureate research outcomes through its influence on research self-efficacy [46]. Complementarily, structured frameworks for question formulation, such as PICOT, may provide additional scaffolding for developing research and evidence-based practice skills [43,47].

Finally, journal clubs and research seminars represent accessible and relatively low-cost strategies for strengthening critical appraisal skills, evidence-based reasoning, and confidence in interpreting scientific literature among nursing students [48–50]. These strategies may help sustain research interest, self-efficacy, and research competencies throughout undergraduate nursing education.

This exploratory study on IRQ in Spanish represents an initial step toward understanding this construct within Spanish-speaking contexts, particularly among undergraduate students. These findings highlight the need for further research examining how research interests develop over time and how they relate to students’ academic and professional trajectories. A deeper investigation into this topic may yield valuable strategies for fostering research engagement from the earliest stages of higher education.

Limitations

First, the generalizability of the findings is limited because the sample was drawn from a single institution with specific geographic and administrative characteristics, eligibility restrictions for participation (i.e., availability during the data collection period), and an uneven distribution of participants across semesters. However, this distribution reflects the actual enrollment patterns within this small institution and may therefore provide a realistic representation of similar institutional contexts. Nevertheless, this imbalance may have affected the stability of the estimates and the interpretability of the regrouped exposure levels.

Second, although the effect size metrics used are independent of sample size, the unequal distribution of group sizes may have increased the likelihood of Type II errors. Third, relevant individual difference variables—such as openness to experience or need for cognition—were not controlled for, despite their known influence on attitudes toward science [51]. Consequently, pre-existing differences in student interest may also explain the observed pattern. Furthermore, given the cross-sectional design, it is not possible to determine whether the observed differences reflect the effect of exposure to research-related coursework or pre-existing differences in students’ research interests. Therefore, our results should be interpreted as associations rather than causal relationships.

Finally, although the observed pattern of results suggested a possible non-linear (quadratic) trend, this was not formally tested. Given the sample size and distribution, such analyses may have been underpowered; therefore, this pattern should be considered exploratory and warrants further investigation in future studies. Additionally, no sensitivity analyses were conducted. For example, although the grouping of semesters was based on methodological and substantive considerations, the impact of maintaining the grouped structure was not evaluated. Consequently, future research should examine the robustness of the findings across alternative grouping strategies.

Conclusions

Differences in research interest are evident among nursing students across different academic semesters. The findings suggest that higher interest levels occur among new students and those currently enrolled in research-related courses. In contrast, students who were further removed from these courses showed lower levels of interest. These results highlight opportunities to strengthen nursing students’ interest in research and to develop teaching strategies that foster greater engagement in scholarly inquiry.
