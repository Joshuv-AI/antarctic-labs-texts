Antifatigue effect of okara protein hydrolysate supplementation during cycling exercise in men: a pre-post uncontrolled pilot study
Authors: Yu-Jou Chien, Jung-Piao Tsao, Chun-Tse Tsai, I-Shiung Cheng, Chin-Lin Hsu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundProlonged exercise usually leads to exercise fatigue, which has a negative short-term impact on exercise performance and metabolic rate; thus, fatigue needs to be resolved. Okara is a protein-rich residue of soy processing. Enzyme hydrolysis is known to increase the content of branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs), which have been reported to confer benefits for exercise. The purpose of this study was to investigate the antifatigue effect of okara protein hydrolysate (OPH) on cycling exercise.MethodsA total of 16 male participants who habitually exercised (2 times or more per week and without participation in athletic contests) were instructed to receive 11.74 g of OPH once a day. They then completed two intense cycling exercise challenges before and after four weeks of supplementation. Exercise time and blood markers related to fatigue and energy metabolism were measured.ResultsThe results showed that the time to exhaustion significantly increased after the treatment. The levels of lactate during exercise and at the end of exercise were significantly lower after treatment than before. Additionally, postexercise insulin sensitivity was increased after treatment.ConclusionsThis study showed that OPH supplementation can promote endurance in exercise by decreasing the accumulation of fatigue-related metabolites during exercise and can promote energy recovery by increasing insulin function. These findings suggest that OPH has an antifatigue property.KEYWORDS: Okara, enzyme hydrolysis, protein hydrolyzate, endurance exercise, antifatigue

Introduction

Regular endurance exercise has been reported to improve muscle function and promote multiple aspects of health, such as cardiorespiratory fitness, mitochondrial function, and reduced mortality [1]. Exercise is critical in maintaining physical fitness. However, excessive exercise brings fatigue that reduces activity, vitality, and the ability to work. Fatigue is a common condition in modern life and can be divided into physiological and chronic fatigue. Physiological fatigue is the reduced capacity for normal muscle movement after intense activity [2]. In fatigue caused by exercise, sensation and metabolic reactions change; for example, the extreme depletion of blood glucose and glycogen during exercise leads to an energy deficiency and influences exercise performance. At the same time, increasing energy requirements cause amino acid degradation, muscle protein damage, and the accumulation of metabolic byproducts such as blood urea nitrogen (BUN) and lactate [3]. A study with 14 men and 14 women showed that a 30-minute exercise challenge at 60% of maximum oxygen uptake (VO2 max) resulted in an increase in lactate levels from 1.46 to 4.30 mmol/L [4]. Another study demonstrated that blood glucose decreased after cycling exercise at 80% of VO2 max [5]. While numerous nutritional products are now used to relieve fatigue, including drinks with taurine content, vitamin tablets, plant extracts, and protein hydrolyzates; these products help dissipate fatigue metabolites and modulate energy utilization, promoting exercise performance [6,7].

Protein is a necessary nutrient for growth. It has a close relationship with exercise fatigue and energy metabolism. Protein and its downstream products (amino acids and active peptides) are beneficial for eliminating harmful metabolites, decreasing oxidative stress, and protecting the body from fatigue [8]. Branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs) are the most abundant of the essential amino acids (EAAs) that account for 21% of the total protein in muscle; they are critical for regular muscle synthesis. Previous studies have shown that protein hydrolysis increases the digestion rate and solubility of protein, and a larger quantity of BCAAs increases resistance to fatigue [9,10]. Rats supplemented with porcine placenta hydrolysate for 6 weeks showed a lower lactate level and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) activity in the serum and muscle after five minutes of intense exercise than those that did not receive the supplement; the accumulation of glycogen also increased in the muscle and liver, which relieves exercise fatigue [11].

Okara, also called soy waste, soy pulp, soybean curd residue, or douzha (Chinese), is a byproduct from the manufacture of soybean products such as tofu and soy milk [12]. Approximately 1.1 kg to 1.2 kg of okara (on a wet basis) is produced from every 1 kg of processed soybeans [13]. In Japan, the tofu industry produces around 800,000 tons of okara annually, 310,000 tons in Korea, and in China, production amounts to 2,800,000 tons [14]. Although okara is a residue, a large proportion of nutrients are left in it. Okara contains a significant amount of protein (26.8% to 37.5% w/w) and is rich in all nine EAAs [13]. Okara also contains dietary fibers, isoflavones, and peptides; therefore, it should have biological benefits such as antidiabetic, anti-obesity, antihyperlipidemic, and gut-modulating effects [14]. Currently, 14 million tons of okara are produced annually in the world, but much of it goes unused due to inadequate preservation methods. Most okara is burned as fuel, dumped in landfills, or thrown away as garbage, which results in an environmental burden [12]. Thus, it is necessary to increase the value of okara and solve the related environmental problems to achieve sustainable development goals.

In our previous study, we used C2C12 myotubes to select a potential okara protein hydrolysate (OPH) and treated Sprague‒Dawley rats to determine the most effective dose to resist fatigue. The results showed that OPH promoted mitochondrial function at the mRNA level and enzyme activity in vitro, and rats treated with OPH had a longer exercise time in the exhaustive swimming test and a lower ratio of lactate increase [15]. The current study aimed to explore the antifatigue effect of OPH in adult men. We administered a cycling exercise challenge and analyzed the relative fatigue- and energy-related metabolites in blood during the exercise and rest periods. Finally, our study elucidated the antifatigue property of OPH, suggesting the negative correlation between blood lactate and exercise time.

Participants

The sample size was determined using G*Power (3.1.9.4) software, assuming an effect size of 0.89, an alpha error of 0.05, and a statistical power of 90%, as previously observed in a human study on the ergogenic effects of exercise time to exhaustion [16,17]. Thus, 18 male volunteers who habitually exercised were recruited in this study. Participants were included if they met the following criteria: (1) they were men who were 20 to 40 years old; (2) they habitually exercised 2 or more times per week for longer than 30 minutes at a time and did not participate in any group exercise classes or athletic contests during the experimental period; (3) they did not smoke tobacco or consume large amounts of alcohol (over 40 grams of alcohol once or 20 grams of alcohol 3 times a week); (4) they were willing to eat a controlled diet during the study period; (5) they had a normal liver function and no food allergies; (6) they had no cardiovascular disease or other chronic diseases that were serious or required hospitalization; and (7) they could understand the study protocol as well as the potential risks and benefits (as described in the informed consent document) and could sign the consent document on their own. The exclusion criteria included the following: (1) the use of BCAA-containing nutritional supplements; (2) an inability to complete the exercise challenge; (3) a diagnosis of kidney disease, heart disease, or cancer or the active receipt of treatments during the study period; (4) the use of drugs whose pharmacological effects could affect fatigue; and (5) systemic infections that required systemic antibiotics. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Chung Shan Medical University (IRB approval no. CS2–20042) and was registered on clinicaltrials.gov as NCT05000918.

Experimental design

This study is a pilot study with a pre-post, uncontrolled design. The experimental design is shown in Figure 1. Each participant underwent a cardiopulmonary capacity test to assess their VO2 max 1 week before the intervention (Day 1) and then consumed a pack of dried OPH powder (11.74 g) per day for 4 weeks (Day 8 to Day 35). Before and after the intervention, anthropometric measurements, a dietary questionnaire, and an endurance exercise test were performed. Blood samples from each participant were collected during two exercise challenges (before exercise, 15 minutes after exercise, at the end of exercise, and after 30 and 60 minutes of rest), and the blood biochemical parameters relating to fatigue and energy were analyzed. To ensure compliance, participants were instructed to submit a photo of packing or a text message everyday as proof of compliance and to bring their packs to each visit for pack count. They also received daily text messages or phone-call reminders to promote consistent participation. The entire experimental period lasted 35 days, and all participants were asked to maintain their habitual exercise and diet (except on the one and two days before the exercise challenge when we provided the meals) throughout the experimental period.

Experimental design.

VO2 max test

The evaluation of cardiopulmonary exercise capacity was performed following the procedure of Tsao et al. [5]. All participants were instructed to cycle on a cycle ergometer (Monark 894E, Varberg, Sweden). Participants were required to wear air collection equipment and advance through a cycling exercise with progressive intensity until exhaustion (1 kg of loading for the initial 6 minutes, then an additional 0.5 kg every 2 minutes for the next 12 minutes; 1 kg was added every 2 minutes after 18 minutes of testing). The cycling speed was maintained at 60 rpm, and exhaustion was defined by reaching any two of the following three criteria: (1) the value of the rating of perceived exertion (RPE) scale was higher than 18; (2) the participant reached the maximum heart rate (MHR) calculated as 220 minus age; and (3) the respiratory exchange rate (RER) measured by the pulmonary function testing system (Vmax Encore 29c System, CareFusion, California, USA) was greater than 1.1.

Exercise challenge

Previous studies showed that the higher carbohydrates in the diet significantly increased exercise work [18]. To prevent the influence of diet on exercise performance, we fed the participants the same meals one and two days before each exercise challenge (total energy of 2071 kcal per day with 14% protein, 28% fat, and 58% carbohydrate). On the day of the exercise challenge, the participants, who had fasted for 8 hours before the challenge, were required to ingest 1 pack of OPH 30 minutes before the challenge. They exercised on an anaerobic Wingate test ergometer until exhaustion. The challenge was carried out at 70% of their own VO2 max, and the time from the beginning of the cycling test until the onset of exhaustion was recorded. The cycling speed and criteria for exhaustion were the same as those in the VO2 max test.

Anthropometric measurements

Before the two exercise challenges, the body weight, body mass index (BMI), body fat, and lean mass of the participants were measured using a bioelectrical impedance device (Inbody 3.0, Biospace, Tokyo, Japan).

Dietary questionnaire

The dietary intake conditions of the participants were recorded with a 24-hour recall conducted by a trained dietitian before and after 4 weeks of OPH intervention. The recorded results were then entered into nutrient analysis software (E-kitchen Business Corporation, Taichung, Taiwan) [19], and the quantities of total energy, protein, fat, carbohydrate, dietary fiber, BCAAs, and water intake were analyzed.

Blood analysis

Serum and plasma were obtained using plastic vacutainer tubes (BD Vacutainer, Plymouth, UK). The serum and plasma were stored at − 80 °C after centrifugation (3000 rpm for 15 mins at 4 °C). The safety-related biochemical parameters of the serum, including aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), albumin, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine, triglyceride, total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), sodium, potassium, chloride, calcium, and phosphorus, were measured using commercial assay kits (Bayer Corporation, New York, USA). The insulin concentration in the serum was measured using a commercial ELISA kit (Mercodia, Uppsala, Sweden). The concentrations of glucose and non-esterified fatty acids (NEFAs) in the serum, as well as lactate and BUN in the plasma, were measured using a commercial assay kit (Randox, San Diego, USA). The activity of LDH in the plasma was measured with a commercial assay kit (Abcam, Cambridge, UK).

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using SPSS statistical software, version 22.0 (International Business Machines Corporation, New York, USA), and are presented as the mean ± SD. Participants who reported more than 80% compliance were included in the data analysis [19]. A paired-sample t-test was used to examine the differences between pretreatment and posttreatment measurements of body composition, intake of nutrients, serum safety biochemical parameters, and exercise time to exhaustion. The effect sizes (d) were also calculated, according to Cohen’s [20] recommendations, effect sizes are categorized as follows: d values from 0.20 to 0.49 are small (negligible importance), 0.50 to 0.79 are medium (moderate importance), and 0.80 or higher are large (crucial importance). A two-way repeated-measures ANOVA was used to compare the plasma fatigue metabolites and serum energy sources. If there was a significant interaction between the treatment and the time, we performed a simple main-effect analysis and employed the Bonferroni post hoc test to identify any significant differences (p < 0.05) between pairs of conditions. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to analyze the correlation between the lactate concentration and exercise time to exhaustion. A significant difference between pretreatment and posttreatment measurements was defined by p < 0.05.

Results

Participants

A total of 18 volunteers initially participated in the investigation. Two of them were excluded: one who was injured during the experimental period and one who suffered from a keloid from an unrelated injury between the first and second exercise challenges. Therefore, sixteen participants completed the study (age: 24.24 ± 5.01 years; height: 173.59 ± 6.35 cm; body weight: 76.46 ± 10.36 kg; body mass index (BMI): 25.33 ± 2.61 kg/m2; body fat percentage: 19.38 ± 4.84%; lean mass: 58.50 ± 6.86 kg; VO2 max: 38.57 ± 6.98 mL/kg/min). Compliance according to daily intake pictures or the number of returned packs was 100%. After 4 weeks of OPH treatment, the body weight of the men was 77.28 ± 11.12 kg (p = 0.369, d = −0.034), the BMI was 25.51 ± 2.88 kg/m2 (p = 0.367, d = −0.042), the body fat percentage was 19.78 ± 4.19% (p = 0.175, d = −0.080), and the lean mass was 58.48 ± 7.35 kg (p = 0.936, d = −0.04). There was no significant difference in the body composition of the men between the pretreatment and posttreatment time points.

Effects of OPH treatment on the relative plasma levels of fatigue-related metabolites in the participants

Relative fatigue markers were measured in the plasma during the exercise and rest periods (Figure 3a–c). The level of lactate dramatically increased during exercise both before and after treatment (baseline-pre: 12.11 ± 4.15 mg/dL; E15-pre: 93.76 ± 31.27 mg/dL; baseline-post: 10.30 ± 2.68 mg/dL; E15-post: 82.02 ± 25.71 mg/dL) and rapidly decreased during the recovery period (R30-pre: 29.24 ± 14.61 mg/dL; R60-pre: 17.34 ± 5.34 mg/dL; R30-post: 23.57 ± 9.65 mg/dL; R60-post: 16.60 ± 5.23 mg/dL). Treatment with OPH significantly lowered the concentration of lactate measured after 15 minutes of exercise (pre: 93.76 ± 31.27 mg/dL; post: 82.02 ± 25.71 mg/dL; p = 0.004) and at the end of exercise (pre: 88.25 ± 32.26 mg/dL; post: 63.81 ± 24.50 mg/dL; p = 0.010). The levels of LDH and BUN also increased after exercise (LDH at baseline-pre: 160.53 ± 42.69 U/L, E15-pre: 215.56 ± 109.70 U/L, baseline-post: 156.73 ± 80.64 U/L, and E15-post: 251.21 ± 177.15 U/L; BUN at baseline-pre: 38.85 ± 10.76 mg/dL, E15-pre: 43.77 ± 8.34 mg/dL, baseline-post: 34.61 ± 8.66 mg/dL, and E15-post: 37.00 ± 11.11 mg/dL). The BUN concentration after 15 minutes of exercise was reduced following the OPH treatment (pre: 43.77 ± 8.34; post: 37.00 ± 11.11; p = 0.058); however, there was no significant difference in LDH at either time point. Lactate exhibited significant main effects of treatment [F (1, 15) = 16.068, p = 0.001] and time [F (1.691, 25.372) = 108.458, p < 0.001] as well as a significant interaction of treatment and time [F (1.512, 22.683) = 5.34, p = 0.019]. LDH revealed significant changes over time [F (2.341, 36.460) = 4.842, p = 0.010].

Effects of OPH treatment on the serum energy sources of the participants

Energy-related markers in the serum were measured during the exercise and rest periods (Figure 3d–f). The glucose and NEFA concentrations decreased during exercise (glucose at baseline-pre: 88.20 ± 19.64 mg/dL, E15-pre: 91.95 ± 18.73 mg/dL, baseline-post: 100.95 ± 24.12 mg/dL, and E15-post: 86.39 ± 15.75 mg/dL; NEFA at baseline-pre: 0.36 ± 0.22 mmol/L, E15-pre: 0.19 ± 0.10 mmol/L, baseline-post: 0.32 ± 0.16 mmol/L, and E15-post: 0.18 ± 0.10 mmol/L) and increased at the end of exercise (glucose at End-pre: 111.17 ± 27.51 mg/dL and End-post: 102.05 ± 24.70 mg/dL; NEFA at End-pre: 0.53 ± 0.42 mmol/L and End-post: 0.53 ± 0.43 mmol/L). There was no significant difference in serum glucose or NEFAs at any of the 5-time points after 4 weeks of OPH intervention. The concentration of insulin after the start of exercise was reduced following OPH supplementation (E15-pre: 1.28%, End-pre: − 2.04%, E15-post: − 34.55%, and End-post: − 49.43% compared to the baseline), and the same was true of the insulin concentration after 30 minutes of rest (R30-pre: 7.02% and R30-post: − 25.81% compared to the baseline). These differences were significant at the end of exercise (pre: 6.90 ± 5.23; post: 3.67 ± 3.51; p = 0.021) as well as after 30 minutes of rest (pre: 7.56 ± 5.28; post: 5.39 ± 5.04; p = 0.010). Furthermore, insulin exhibited significant differences between treatments [F (1, 15) = 6.078, p = 0.026], and there were significant changes in glucose [F (2.589, 38.833) = 6.981, p = 0.001] and NEFA levels [F (15.000, 2.621) = 29.410, p < 0.001] over time.

The correlation between lactate concentration and exercise time to exhaustion

We collected the measurements of lactate content and exercise time to exhaustion in the two exercise challenges and performed a correlation analysis (Figure 4). The results showed a strong negative correlation between the lactate content 15 minutes after exercise and time to exhaustion (r = − 0.673, p < 0.001), as well as a medium negative correlation between lactate content after 15 minutes of exercise and at the end of exercise (r = −0.575, p = 0.001).

Discussion

This study investigated the effects of 28 days of OPH supplementation on exercise fatigue. We hypothesized that OPH would prolong exercise time during endurance exhaustion exercise and reduce physiological indicators of fatigue. The main findings of this study demonstrated an increase in exercise time to exhaustion, accompanied by significant improvements in physiological responses, including reduced blood lactate concentration and enhanced insulin sensitivity.

Exercise performance and the related adaptive responses are affected by factors such as nutritional state, dietary protein source, digestive capacity, food intake timing, exercise protocol, muscle type, aging, sex, and hormones [21]. In this experiment, a cycling ergometer was used to evaluate the effect of OPH on exercise fatigue. To ensure that the exercise challenge went smoothly, we recruited study participants who habitually exercised, and 70% of their VO2 max was used as the intensity for the endurance exercise test. Previous studies have shown the exercise performance of women might be affected due to hormone changes during different phases of the menstrual cycle [22]. Although the evidence is currently equivocal, this study was limited to men to avoid the influence of the menstrual cycle on exercise performance and recovery. Previous research has also provided evidence supporting the impact of dietary manipulations on aerobic performance and fatigue metabolism [23,24]. The nutrient intake results in this study revealed that the nutrient intake did not significantly change after the intervention (Table 2). This suggested that the regulation of fatigue observed in our participants was not attributable to a dietary change.

Most amino acids are catabolized in the liver, whereas BCAAs are primarily catabolized in the skeletal muscles, where they regulate muscle functions and can contribute to exercise ability [25,26]. In research into acute BCAA supplementation, eighteen recreationally active men underwent two cycling tests with the same total work. For each test, subjects received BCAAs or a placebo 30 and 10 minutes before exercise (total BCAA dose: 84 mg/kg) and during exercise for 15 minutes (BCAAs at 56 mg/kg/hour). Men taking BCAAs took a reduced length of time to complete the cycling test without any changes in the neuromuscular parameters of central fatigue [26]. Another study assigned 16 male runners to take a 14-day course of either a placebo or a BCAA supplement (13000 mg) and administered an incremental treadmill exercise test until exhaustion. The results showed that the exercise time to exhaustion increased after BCAA intervention [27]. Similarly, our results showed an increased cycling time to exhaustion by 26.36% in men after 4 weeks of OPH administration (Figure 2). This indicated that BCAAs might be beneficial to exercise performance in different exercise protocols and that OPH could promote endurance exercise, which might be due to its rich BCAA content.

Excess exercise increases oxidative stress, cell structure damage, and metabolic disorders and leads to fatigue. It is known that an accumulation of metabolic byproducts causes fatigue and affects exercise performance [28]. LDH has been shown to modulate the conversion between pyruvate and lactate, and lactate accumulation increases when insufficient oxygen is provided during exercise [29]. BUN is created through protein catabolism when energy from carbohydrates is not available in time [28]. In this study, after 4 weeks of OPH intervention, the levels of lactate and BUN measured during exercise decreased (by 12.52% after 15 minutes of exercise and 27.69% at the end of exercise for lactate; and 15.46% after 15 minutes for BUN), but there was no change in LDH (Figure 3a–c). There have been relatively few clinical trials on natural protein hydrolyzates and energy metabolites during exercise. Animal studies have indicated that treatments with protein hydrolyzates from sources such as sea bass, porcine whole-blood protein, and soft-shelled turtles can reduce the levels of lactate and BUN after exercise [8,9,29]. Our previous study showed that rats treated with OPH had reduced levels of lactate, BUN, and LDH [15]. A meta-analysis that included 31 clinical trials of BCAA supplementation on fatigue showed no significant effect of BCAA supplementation in improving LDH activity [30]. As a result, it has been suggested that OPH could improve fatigue by decreasing the accumulation of metabolites, but there are likely different mechanisms in animals and humans. Further research is needed to investigate this aspect.

In addition to the accumulation of fatigue-related metabolites, insufficient energy is another cause of fatigue, shortening the duration of physical activity [30]. Carbohydrates and fat are primarily used in oxidative phosphorylation to provide energy for the contraction of skeletal muscle in aerobic activity and to prevent muscle fatigue [31]. Our results indicated that OPH supplementation did not change the use of glucose or NEFAs (Figure 3d–e). Although several studies have shown the energy regulation potential of BCAAs during exercise, it has also been reported that carbohydrate and fat levels in blood increase approximately 10–20-fold during exercise, whereas only a 2 to 3-fold elevation has been found in BCAAs [32]. This might explain the limited contribution of BCAAs to energy regulation in this study. However, BCAAs have been reported to promote tissue glucose uptake after exercise [33]. Supplementation with OPH decreased the concentration of insulin after exercise by 46.81% at the end of exercise and by 28.70% after 30 minutes of recovery (Figure 3f), which indicated that insulin sensitivity was elevated; however, the content of glucose did not significantly change after exercise. The possible reason for this might be due to the exhaustion of energy supplies after the cycling challenge; the participants were not provided with any energy supplementation as a resource at rest to promote glycogen synthesis.

Efficient mitochondria in skeletal muscle improve oxidation capacity, resulting in high lactate clearance during exercise and improving exercise performance. A total of 14 male college students in a sports club consumed a BCAA-enriched fermented rice beverage for 3 weeks and then performed a constant-load cycling exercise. A negative correlation between blood lactate and exercise time to exhaustion was found [34]. Our results were consistent with this finding (Figure 4), indicating that LA accumulation could negatively affect exercise performance.

Leucine has been reported to be an activator of the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR), promoting muscle protein regeneration [35]. In our formulation of OPH, 1 gram of OPH contained 28.26 milligrams of BCAAs (Table 1). The ratio of leucine to isoleucine to valine was 2:1:1, which was consistent with several previous investigations measuring the effect of BCAA supplements on exercise fatigue [25,27,32]. We also measured the serum parameters of liver and kidney function, blood lipids, and electrolytes to ensure the safety of OPH ingestion. Although the content of BUN was significantly increased posttreatment, the value of BUN was in the normal range for 20- to 40-year-old men (5–20 mg/dL) [36]. As a result, the difference in triglyceride levels between pretreatment and posttreatment neared significance (p = 0.054). The possible reason was that two participants exhibited hemolysis in their blood samples, leading to higher test values (188 and 177 mg/dL). Previous research has shown that increasing hemolysis positively biases triglyceride measurements [29]. After excluding the values from the two participants with hemolysis, the p-value became 0.211 (pre: 53.71 ± 11.96 mg/dL; post: 60.86 ± 16.78 mg/dL). Additionally, the triglyceride value after treatment remained within the normal range (150 mg/dL), and the effect size analysis indicated negligible importance (d = −0.255). In our previous animal study, supplementation with OPH did not result in significant morphological changes in the liver, kidneys, or muscles of rats, and no abnormalities were noted in their growth status [15]. In this human study, participants did not report any side effects during 28 days of OPH supplementation. Therefore, we inferred that OPH was not harmful to human health and that it was suitable for development as a dietary supplement.

Since daily exercise conditions and activity levels might impact the results of supplementation in this study, we investigated the exercise frequency and type for each subject before and after the 4-week supplementation through interviews. All subjects exercised 3 to 4 times a week. However, the recruitment criteria for participants did not specify the types of exercise, resulting in a wide variety of activities among the subjects, including basketball, badminton, volleyball, track and field, jogging, and weight training. This study focuses on the anti-fatigue effects of OPH among generally active individuals without targeting specific sports. The participants were required to maintain consistent exercise habits throughout the study period; therefore, we believe that the exercise habits of subjects did not influence the results of this study.

Limitation & future direction

This study demonstrated that supplementation with OPH could improve the accumulation of fatigue-related substances in the blood and enhance exercise endurance. As energy replenishment was not provided after exercise in this experiment, no changes in glucose or NEFA levels were observed when insulin activity increased. Further research is needed to determine whether supplementation with OPH in conjunction with post-exercise energy replenishment could provide an increased antifatigue effect. In addition, a more comprehensive study involving larger and more diverse participants, including both men and women engaged in similar exercise routines, is necessary to assess the benefits of OPH. A subjective self-report of side effects experienced by the subjects should also be included. Additionally, enhancing the research by including a control group receiving a placebo and adopting a randomized double-blinded design would strengthen our findings. Furthermore, investigating the molecular interactions between OPH and its target molecules through molecular docking analyses could provide valuable insights into its anti-fatigue mechanisms and suitability as a functional material.

Conclusion

In recent years, concerns regarding clean labeling, allergen avoidance, animal welfare, and sustainable development have been emphasized, which has driven the demand for new alternatives to animal-derived foods. Plant-based protein provides similar ergogenic effects to animal-based protein for improvements in muscle protein synthesis or a reduction in body fat. Soy is one of the few EAA-containing plant-based protein sources recommended by the World Health Organization [37]. In conclusion, our results indicated that 4 weeks of OPH supplementation could increase exercise endurance time, decrease the accumulation of fatigue-related substances, and improve insulin sensitivity after exercise. This study demonstrated a favorable antifatigue effect of OPH, enhancing the value of okara and providing a new option for plant-based protein supplementation.
