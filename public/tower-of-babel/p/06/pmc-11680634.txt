Deficient salience and default mode functional integration in high worry-proneness subject: a connectome-wide association study
Authors: Youxue Zhang, Mingjun Duan, Hui He
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractWorry has been conceptualized as a relatively uncontrollable chain of thought that increases the risk of mental problems, such as anxiety disorders. Here, we examined the link between individual variation in the functional connectome and worry proneness, which remains unclear. A total of 32 high worry-proneness (HWP) subjects and 25 low worry-proneness (LWP) subjects were recruited. We conducted multivariate distance-based matrix regression to identify phenotypic relationships in high-dimensional brain resting-state functional connectivity data from HWP subjects. Multiple hub regions, including key brain nodes of the salience network (SN) and default mode network (DMN), were identified in HWP subjects. Follow-up analyses revealed that a high worry-proneness score was dominated by functional connectivity between the SN and the DMN. Moreover, HWP subjects showed hypoconnectivity between the cerebellum and the SN and DMN compared with LWP subjects. This cross-sectional study could not fully measure the causal relationships between changes in functional networks and worry proneness in healthy subjects. Functional changes in the cerebellum-cortical region might affect the modulation of external stimuli processing. Together, our results provide new insight into the role of key networks, including the SN, DMN and cerebellum, in understanding the potential mechanism underlying the high worry dimension in healthy subjects.Keywords: Worry proneness, Salience network, Default model network, Functional connectivity, Brain–behaviour relationship

Introduction

Generalized anxiety disorder (GAD) is one of the most common mental disorders in the adult population(Mackenzie et al., 2011; Wittchen & Hoyer, 2001). The high prevalence, chronicity and comorbidity of generalized anxiety cause a heavy burden on family and society (Kujanpä et al., 2017), yet its pathogenesis is less well known. The main clinical characteristic of GAD is excessive and uncontrollable psychological and somatic symptoms (Stein & Sareen, 2015; Ruscio et al., 2017; DeMartini et al., 2019). Specifically, these major symptoms are strongly associated with the process of worrying, including chronic worry and excessive worry (Battle, 2013), including chronic worry and excessive worry. Worry has been described as “negatively affect-laden and relatively uncontrollable” (Borkovec et al., 1983). Excessive worry can gradually evolve into a source of emotional discomfort (Dodd et al., 2021; Quan & Zettle, 2023). Moreover, high worry proneness is likely to lead to the development of anxiety disorders (Cox et al., 2022). Therefore, high worry proneness represents a potentially useful way to better understand the aetiology of anxiety disorders. However, the biological mechanisms of the general risk caused by high worry for healthy subjects are still unclear. Confirming these mechanisms is necessary for effectively leveraging worry to obtain novel targets for clinical intervention in anxiety disorders.

Anxiety and worry are associated with overactive salience processing, which might contribute to exaggerated salience detection (Sylvester et al., 2012). In these patients, insignificant stimulation is considered a salient event. Furthermore, it is assumed that a deficient salience network (SN) is accompanied by inappropriate functional connectivity (FC) with the default mode network (DMN) and control network (CN) in excessive worry subjects (Ball et al., 2013; Blair et al., 2012; Fitzgerald et al., 2017). Specifically, the CN might insufficiently respond to salient processing, whereas the DMN may be overactive (Sonuga-Barke & Castellanos, 2007). Therefore, alterations in this triple network functional circuit may be related to the potential biological mechanisms of high worry.

Recent research has indicated that the affective network plays an important role in worry processing. The affective dysregulation of anxious subjects might be linked to intranetwork and internetwork FC involving the SN-DMN-CN triple network (Menon, 2011). Moreover, the assessed worry scores were positively related to FC between the right amygdala and the superior frontal gyrus, anterior cingulate cortex, and supramarginal gyrus (Makovac et al., 2016). Research on repetitive negative thinking has revealed that greater worry is associated with FC between the amygdala and praecuneus (Feurer et al., 2021). However, a meta-analysis of task-based functional studies indicated that worry has a similar inconsistency across studies (Weber-Goericke & Muehlhan, 2019). Taken together, across these studies, it remains unclear precisely how the DMN-SN-CN triple functional network and affective network change in subjects with excessive worry.

Resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI) has emerged as a powerful tool in clinical neuroscience (Biswal et al., 2010; He et al., 2023; Margulies et al., 2009; Zhang, et al., 2023). Clinical neuroscience has adopted transdiagnostic methodologies to explore the neurobiological abnormalities associated with mental disorders (Dong et al., 2020; He et al., 2019, 2021). A highly synchronized network in the resting state is thought to represent distinct primary sensorimotor, emotional or cognitive processes (Biswal et al., 1995; Fox & Raichle, 2007). Aberrant communication within or among these networks may contribute to core deficits in emotional or cognitive function, which are believed to underlie the clinical symptoms of GAD (Chavanne & Robinson, 2021). Importantly, one key challenge in clinical neuroscience is to assess the relationships between phenotypic clinical factors (e.g., task performance, disease state, and psychological traits) and the functional connectome across subjects (Akil et al., 2011; Biswal et al., 2010). Notably, most previous studies have used traditional hypothesis-driven analyses, such as seed based FC analysis, which limits the ability to capture whole-brain FC-symptom relationships outside of the seeds (Power et al., 2011). A connectome-wide association study (CWAS) can provide a comprehensive voxelwise survey of these relationships across the whole-brain functional connectome using multidimensional matrix regression (MDMR) (Shehzad et al., 2014). In contrast to traditional approaches, MDMR is a multivariate statistical approach used to assess brain FC-behaviour relationships without requiring a prior selection of regions or networks. Moreover, MDMR has the advantages of interpretability and visualization. However, no studies have been conducted to assess the relationship between the functional connectome and worry scores of healthy subjects using the MDMR approach.

In the current study, a CWAS was conducted to identify associations between the intrinsic functional connectome and the worry score in high worry-proneness (HWP) subjects. After locating the brain regions highly related to the worry score, we identified the specific connectivity patterns associated with high worry, and the differences in FC between HWP and low worry-proneness (LWP) subjects. To this end, the participants were subjected to rs-fMRI. We posit that the findings may provide evidence for understanding the biological mechanisms of the general risk of high worry for psychopathology.

Materials and methods

Subject selection

A total of 1224 college students were recruited and completed the Penn State Worry Questionnaire (PSWQ) (Zhong et al., 2009). We defined a score in the top one-tenth (10%) of the PSWQ distribution as the high worry-proneness score and a score in the bottom one-tenth (10%) of the PSWQ distribution as the low worry-proneness score. Subjects were classified into high worry-proneness (HWP) and low worry-proneness (LWP) groups, and brain images were collected using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Clinical Hospital of Chengdu Brain Science Institute and performed according to the Helsinki Declaration (IRB number: 2020036). Informed consent was obtained from all the subjects included in the study.

Data acquisition and preprocessing

Brain imagings were acquired using a 3 T MRI scanner (GE DISCOVERY MR750) at University of Electronic Science and Technology of China. rs-fMRI data were acquired using gradient-echo echo planar imaging sequences (TR = 2000 ms, echo time [TE] = 30 ms, FA = 90°, matrix = 64 × 64, FOV = 240 × 240 mm2, slice thickness/gap = 4 mm/0.4 mm, number of slices = 42), with an eight channel-phased array head coil. All subjects underwent a 510-s resting state scan to yield 255 volumes. No task-based investigations were performed prior to the rs-fMRI scan. Moreover, during the rs-fMRI scan, the subjects were instructed to keep their eyes closed and to move as little as possible without falling asleep.

Functional image preprocessing was performed using DPABI image processing software according to a standard pipeline as follows(Yan et al., 2016): (1) The first 10 volumes were removed. (2) The remaining 245 images were slice timing corrected and the head motion corrected (participants whose maximal head motion exceeding 3 mm or 3 degrees of rotation were discarded). Moreover, the framewise displacement (FD) score of each subject was calculated using the following formula (Power et al., 2012):

Multivariate distance-based matrix regression

MDMR is a multivariate statistical approach that can identify whole-brain functional connectivity patterns associated with worry. As previously described, this analysis occurs in three stages (Shehzad et al., 2014; Sharma et al., 2017; Brady et al., 2019). An overview of the analytic steps is shown in Fig. 1. The following three steps were performed for the HWP group. (1) Beginning with one voxel, voxel-based functional connectivity was conducted to generate a whole-brain FC map for each subject. Computations were restricted to grey matter voxels in the brain. (2) For this voxel, the similarity of FC maps between participants was calculated using Pearson’s correlation. The distance score between two participants was subsequently assessed using the distance metric 2(1-r) (r is the Pearson’s correlation score). (3) As previously described (Shehzad et al., 2014), a pseudo-F statistical analysis was used to determine how well the phenotypic variable (high worry-proneness score) explained the intersubject distances on the connectivity maps generated in step two. Covariates in the pseudo-F statistical analysis included age, sex and mean FD. (4) A nonparametric permutation test was performed with 10,000 permutations. To correct for multiple comparisons, we used Gaussian Random Field (GRF) theory, as in prior MDMR studies (Shehzad et al., 2014; Elliott et al., 2018). For this permutation-based cluster correction, a voxel level threshold of p < 0.001 was set for statistical maps, and then this p value was corrected for multiple comparisons using a cluster size of p < 0.005. (5) These processes were repeated for every individual voxel. Finally, a map of the brain voxels with a whole-brain FC pattern significantly associated with high worry-proneness scores was generated.

Follow-up connectome-wide association analysis

MDMR is generally used to identify voxels with patterns of whole-brain functional connectivity associated with worry. However, it is unclear how high worry-proneness scores are related to these functional connectivity values. Thus, we conducted a post hoc seed-based functional connectivity analysis for each cluster identified by MDMR. The detailed processes were as follows: (1) For each cluster, seed-based functional connectivity maps were created, and correlations were converted to z scores using Fisher’s r-to-z transform for each subject. (2) Whole-brain correlations between each voxel’s connectivity score and PSWQ score were calculated across HWP subjects, including age, sex and mean FD as covariates. One correlation map was subsequently obtained for the HWP group. (3) Average z scores of this correlation map were summarized for seven Yeo networks (Yeo et al., 2011) to quantify their contribution to the associations with high worry. Because the seeds were selected from MDMR findings corrected for multiple comparisons, these follow-up analysis maps were not subjected to independent hypothesis tests. Hence, these voxelwise brain maps generated in the follow-up analyses were uncorrected with P < 0.05 (Li et al., 2023).

Seed-based functional connectivity analyses between HWP and LWP subjects

Regions of interest in the HWP group were selected through MDMR. Furthermore, to investigate the potential effect of high worry on healthy subjects, additional seed-based functional connectivity analysis was performed to assess whether there was different connectivity between the HWP and LWP groups. Two-sample t tests were subsequently conducted between the two groups’ functional connectivity maps, with age, sex and mean FD as covariates. Then, statistical maps were thresholded at a voxel level of p < 0.001 and then corrected for multiple comparisons using a cluster size of p < 0.001.

Demographic data

In this study, the mean PSWQ score of all the subjects was 49. We defined a score in the top one-tenth (10%) of the PSWQ distribution (range, 55–76) as a high worry-proneness score and a score in the bottom one-tenth (10%) of the PSWQ distribution (range, 19–37) as a low worry-proneness score. Thus, 32 HWP subjects and 25 LWP subjects were selected from among college students. No subject was excluded due to excessive head motion during the MRI scan. No significant difference was observed between the two groups in terms of age, sex or FD (Table 1).

Follow-up functional analyses reveal the connectivity pattern of MDMR

The follow-up functional connectivity analyses revealed the relationships between high worry-proneness scores and seed-based functional connectivity patterns. First, in the functional connectivity map of the right angular gyrus (Fig. 2B), the high worry-proneness score was positively correlated with subregions of the basal ganglia network (BGN) (e.g., the putamen, caudate, pallidum and thalamus) and salience network (SN) (e.g., anterior insula, middle frontal gyrus, and TPJ). The connectivity of the right TPJ with the default model network regions increased with increasing worry score (Fig. 2B). Second, the contribution analysis from the averaged map revealed that the DMN and SN were the major networks that contributed to the associations with high worry in the HWP group (Fig. 2C). Finally, we investigated the relationships between PSWQ scores and angular gyrus–SN FC, as well as between PSWQ scores and TPJ–DMN FC, in the HWP group (Fig. 2D).

Effects of high worry on functional connectivity

Significant differences in functional connectivity were observed within the DMN and SN. Within the DMN, decreased functional connectivity was found between the right angular gyrus and left lobule VI, which belong to the motor cerebellar module, in HWP patients compared with LWP patients (Fig. 3, Table 3). Within the SN, decreased functional connectivity was observed between the right TPJ and the left Crus I, which belongs to the cognitive cerebellar module, as well as the right lobule VI (Fig. 3, Table 3). Moreover, these changes in connectivity were not associated with age, sex or FD score.

Discussion

In this study, the connectome-wide MDMR approach was used to assess the potential biological mechanisms of the influence of high worry on healthy subjects. The unconstrained connectome-wide MDMR analysis revealed a relationship between high worry and the intrinsic whole-brain functional connectivity pattern in the key regions of the SN and DMN. Additional investigations revealed that worry was dominated by hyperconnectivity between the DMN and SN. Furthermore, decreased FC was observed between the cerebellum and both the SN and DMN in HWP patients compared with LWP patients. Collectively, these findings provide novel evidence for the dysconnectivity of the SN and DMN as a potential mechanism underlying high worry in healthy subjects.

Multiple large-scale functional networks, such as the DMN, SN, BGN, attention network, cognitive network and affective network (Williams 2016, 2017), have been shown to be dysfunctional in individuals with GAD. Abnormal FCs within and among these circuits might underlie the clinical symptoms of GAD (Goldstein-Piekarski et al., 2022). Previous studies have revealed that FC within the SN might partly explain why external stimuli more easily evoke anxiety in GAD patients (Guyer et al., 2008; Li et al., 2016; Monk et al., 2008). Specifically, high stress could reset the balance towards the SN in healthy subjects, leading to deficient responses to external demands and thereby high worry or anxiety (Homberg et al., 2017). The DMN is implicated in self-referential activities, future planning and emotion regulation (Greicius et al., 2003; Power et al., 2011). Numerous studies have reported altered functioning of the DMN in patients with GAD. Deficient FC within the DMN of GAD patients might manifest as difficulty in adaptively regulating emotion according to current goals (Sylvester et al., 2012), which is consistent with the hypothesis that GAD patients might exhibit hypervigilance for threatening signals (Bar-Haim et al., 2007; Behar et al., 2009). These findings support the current understanding of anxiety disorders as network-based disorders. Although there is considerable evidence of functional network alterations in GAD patients, less is known about the precise quantification of psychiatric symptom-specific brain regions. In this study, we used a whole-brain framework in an MDMR-based analysis to locate regions in the connectome associated with high worry. This novel approach revealed that the whole-brain connectivity patterns of multiple regions, which belong to the DMN and SN, were associated with the PSWQ score in HWP patients. Consistent with the deficient functional models of GAD, our findings revealed that high worry might influence the patterns of connectome-wide intrinsic FC of the SN and DMN in healthy subjects.

Although anxiety disorders can be characterized by abnormal functional circuits in the DMN and SN, it is still unclear whether anxiety symptoms are associated with DMN-SN FC. In this study, follow-up analyses revealed that high worry-proneness scores in healthy subjects were correlated with increased SN-based FC with regions of the DMN. Moreover, a high worry-proneness score was associated with increased DMN-based connectivity with key regions within the SN. Higher activity was observed in the DMN as anticipatory anxiety increased in healthy subjects (Simpson et al., 2001). Previous studies also reported that the DMN might interact with other functional circuits, such as the SN and frontoparietal network, to perform emotion regulation (Delgado et al., 2008; Menon, 2011). The SN can detect salient changes both externally and interoceptively (Menon & Uddin, 2010). Individuals with social anxiety disorder have hyper-FC between the insula and anterior DMN (Peterson, et al., 2014). It is likely that anxiety might lead to excessive processing of external stimuli (Bar-Haim et al., 2007). Taken together, consistent with the deficient functional models of GAD, our findings revealed that high worry might influence and have a positive relationship with intrinsic SN-DMN FC in healthy subjects.

Furthermore, the results of this study demonstrated decreased intrinsic FC between the cerebellum and both the DMN and SN in HWP patients compared with LWP patients. The cerebellum has functional and structural connectivity with cortical networks, which act as modulators. The main function of the cerebellum is to detect pattern changes and errors in both sensorimotor networks and thought networks among multiple cortical networks and provide feedback (Chugani & Phelps, 1986). The function of the cerebellum in relation to anxiety has been described as a salience function (Moreno-Rius, 2018). Notably, the cerebellum is connected with key regions of the SN, such as the insula, anterior cingulate cortex and temporoparietal junction (Habas et al., 2009; Shinn, et al., 2015). Therefore, combined with our findings, it is likely that high levels of worry might affect the modulation of external stimuli processing through changes in the cerebellum-SN-DMN circuits in healthy subjects. Moreover, previous studies have indicated that symptoms of anxiety can be positively modulated through repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (Kan et al., 2023; Siddiqi, et al., 2020). Although our results need to be tested and validated in future studies, these findings might provide potential targets, such as the cerebellum and key regions of the SN and DMN, for precision clinical treatment of anxiety disorders.

Despite several critical observations, this study has several limitations that should be considered. First, this cross-sectional study could not fully measure the causal relationships between changes in functional networks and worry. Second, the subjects did not complete mental health assessments in this study. Participants with high PSWQ scores might meet the criteria for a diagnosis of GAD or depression disorders. The influence of mental disorders could not be quantified in this study. Future researchers should validate our findings.

Conclusion

Our current study provides initial evidence for unique connectome-wide functional signatures of high worry factors in healthy subjects. Importantly, consistent with deficient functional models of anxiety disorders, our findings revealed that high worry’s general risk is associated with greater intrinsic functional connectivity between the SN and DMN. Furthermore, we observed decreased DMN-cerebellum and SN-cerebellum FC in high worry-proneness subjects, highlighting the fact that cerebellar alterations are likely to have far-reaching effects, particularly on the biological mechanisms of high worry. These results provide novel evidence for the alteration of the SN-DMN-cerebellum as a potential mechanism underlying the high worry dimension in healthy subjects and might help in the design of new treatment strategies for anxiety disorders (Adams et al., 2022; Young et al., 2023).
