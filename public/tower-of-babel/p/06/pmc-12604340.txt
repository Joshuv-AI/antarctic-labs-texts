Association between induced organ atrophy assessed by artificial intelligence-generated automatic segmentation and efficacy of bevacizumab in combination with chemotherapy in metastatic colorectal cancer
Authors: Malik Laich, Mathias Brugel, Pierre Henri Conze, Marwan Abbas, Mohammed Ben Abdelghanie, Faiza Khemissa, Sylvie Kircher, Karine Le Malicot, Côme Lepage, Thomas Aparicio, Christine Hoeffel, Olivier Bouché, Claire Carlier
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionBevacizumab, an angiogenesis inhibitor, is commonly used alongside chemotherapy for metastatic colorectal cancer (mCCR). While inducing necrosis in tumours, bevacizumab may also lead to atrophy in tumour-free organs. Artificial intelligence (AI) models offer user-friendly methods for measuring organ volumes. This study explores the relationship between bevacizumab-induced atrophy using AI-assisted volume measurement in tumour-free organs and treatment efficacy.MethodsThis multicenter retrospective study includes patients from the PRODIGE 9 and PRODIGE 20 trials. Organ atrophy was assessed by evaluating volume changes from diagnosis to two months after treatment initiation in patients receiving bevacizumab compared to those who did not. Statistical analyses were performed using the Wilcoxon test, with correlations between volumetric changes. Overall and progression-free survival were assessed using log-rank tests and Cox regression models.ResultsAmong the 214 patients included, 192 received bevacizumab. Both liver and spleen volumes were measured using a deep learning-based AI model and manual measurements. AI-generated volume measurements showed a strong correlation with manual measurements (Pearson coefficient > 0.8). Bevacizumab-treated patients exhibited significant atrophy of non-tumoural liver volume (p = 0.0378), while no significant changes were observed in tumour or spleen volumes in either group. Survival analyses revealed that patients with a smaller decrease in non-tumoural liver volume had improved overall survival (p = 0.016), although this association became non-significant after adjusting for age, sex, and tumour volume at diagnosis (p = 0.25).ConclusionOur findings support the feasibility and reliability of AI in organ volume measurement. While bevacizumab exposure was linked to non-tumoural liver atrophy, its impact on survival remains inconclusive after adjustment. These results pave the way for further research into bevacizumab-induced organ atrophy and the potential of AI in personalizing oncology treatments.Keywords: Artificial intelligence, Automatic organ segmentation, Colorectal cancer

Introduction

In 2020, colorectal cancer was the second leading cause of cancer-related death worldwide [1]. At diagnosis, 20% to 30% of patients had metastatic colorectal cancer (mCCR) [2]. Despite advances in treatment, between 10% and 25% of patients treated develop metachronous metastases after curative surgery [3–5].

Systemic chemotherapy is currently the standard treatment for patients with unresectable metastatic or recurrent disease [2]. The introduction of doublet or triplet chemotherapy regimens, combined with targeted therapies (including antiangiogenic agents), has increased response rates to 60%-70%, improving survival in patients with mCCR [6, 7].

Bevacizumab, an anti-VEGF (Vascular Endothelial Growth Factor) antibody, plays a pivotal role in both first- and second-line treatment of mCCR [8]. By suppressing VEGF overexpression, it inhibits tumour neovascularization. However, bevacizumab has also been shown to induce atrophy in tumour-free organs. For instance, patients with neovascular age-related macular degeneration disease treated with anti-VEGF develop retinal atrophy [9, 10]. Similarly, a pilot study in patients with high-grade gliomas also found that prolonged bevacizumab administration was associated with brain atrophy [11]. In the context of mCCR, bevacizumab treatment may contribute to skeletal muscle mass loss, potentially through mechanisms involving the CDKN1A and TIMP4 genes [12]. Additionally, a previous study reported liver and pancreatic atrophy in colorectal cancer patients undergoing bevacizumab-based chemotherapy [13], this study found a significant decrease in liver and pancreatic volume during treatment, suggesting potential organ atrophy related to the anti-angiogenic effects of bevacizumab.

Numerous studies have investigated the efficacy of bevacizumab in combination with various chemotherapy protocols. However, these benefits come at the cost of increased toxicity [14, 15]. Moreover, a meta-analysis published in 2014 revealed that adding bevacizumab to first-line chemotherapy for mCCR did not provide a clinically significant benefit in overall survival [16]. This finding raised concerns about the actual value of combining chemotherapy with bevacizumab and highlighted the need for additional markers to predict treatment response.

In this context, several approaches have been explored. Initial spleen size has been proposed as a prognostic marker in mCCR [17], while radiomics-based models have been developed to predict treatment response in colorectal cancer [18]. In this context, it is particularly relevant to evaluate the liver and spleen, two organs with complementary roles: the liver, as the main metastatic site and a highly vascularized organ directly exposed to anti-VEGF effects; and the spleen, as an immuno-vascular organ connected to the liver via the portal system, whose volume has been linked to prognosis and may reflect systemic and immunological consequences of therapy [17].

Assessing non-tumoural organ atrophy could provide valuable insight into treatment efficacy and serve as a potential new biomarker. The most precise method for evaluating organ atrophy is volumetric measurement. However, manually measuring organ volumes on imaging is often complex, time-consuming, and subject to observer variability. Artificial intelligence (AI) offers a promising solution to these challenges by enabling faster and potentially equally accurate delineation of organ volumes, allowing for more efficient and standardized analysis [19].

This study aims to evaluate the association between non-tumoural liver and spleen atrophy and the efficacy of bevacizumab in patients with mCCR using volumetric measurements generated by a deep learning-based AI model.

Materials and methods

Type of study

The BEVATROPHY study is a retrospective, multicenter, ancillary cohort study based on the PRODIGE 9 and PRODIGE 20 trials. The PRODIGE 9 study aimed to evaluate the effect of bevacizumab as maintenance therapy after 12 cycles of chemotherapy with FOLFIRI plus bevacizumab [20], while PRODIGE 20 compared the efficacy of chemotherapy alone versus chemotherapy combined with bevacizumab in patients over 75 years old [21]. All inclusion criteria, patient and disease characteristics have been published previously [20, 21]. Patients without baseline and two-month assessment CT scans were excluded from the analysis. Moreover, the images obtained had to be eligible for volumetric measurements of the liver and spleen generated by the AI. Patients whose CT scans were of sub-optimal quality and not suitable for AI-based volumetric analysis, as well as those whose imaging was unavailable at diagnosis or at the two-month follow-up, were excluded from the study. The eligibility criteria of the images were at the discretion of one of the investigators (PHC).

Clinical data had already been collected from the PRODIGE 9 and PRODIGE 20 studies, including patient characteristics (sex, age at diagnosis), disease-related information (tumour location, blood CEA levels at diagnosis, type of treatment administered, surgical or non-surgical management) and follow-up data (date of disease progression, date of death).

Organ parenchyma measurement

Measurement techniques

Organ volumes were measured both manually and using deep-learning AI techniques.

Manual segmentation

Liver segmentation was performed manually on patients included in this study from the PRODIGE 9 database. Spleen volume was measured manually for the included patients of the PRODIGE 9 and PRODIGE 20 databases. Manual liver segmentation was limited to PRODIGE 9 because the procedure is lengthy and time-consuming. The sample size of this trial provided a sufficient number of measurements to validate AI results, making it unnecessary to repeat the process in PRODIGE 20. In contrast, spleen segmentation, being technically simpler and faster due to the homogeneous parenchyma and well-defined anatomical borders, could be performed in both trials. All manual segmentations were performed by a single physician trained in abdominal imaging, ensuring consistency across cases. These manual segmentations were performed at the time of diagnosis and after two months of treatment for each patient. ITKsnap (version 3.6.2-alpha, 2017) software was used to obtain accurate organ volumes expressed in mm³ [22].

AI CT scan segmentation

Artificial intelligence-assisted liver and spleen volume segmentation was performed on all of the CT scans (baseline and two-month assessment post-randomization). Both non-enhanced and contrast-enhanced CT acquisitions were included, since imaging protocols were not harmonized across the PRODIGE 9 and 20 trials. Liver baseline and two-month computed tomography of the PRODIGE 20 trial were only measured using AI-assisted techniques. The liver volumes were generated automatically by the AI produced by one of the investigators (PHC) using a deep learning model [23]. A key stage in this process is the detection and delineation of metastases from CT scans using deep learning. Liver segmentation required the development of a dedicated model, given the complexity of this organ in metastatic colorectal cancer and the need to distinguish tumoural from healthy liver parenchyma. The SwinUNETR model has been employed for automatic segmentation due to its state-of-the-art performance in segmenting organs and other anatomical structures from CT scans [24]. This tool is based on data from the Liver Tumour Segmentation Benchmark (LiTS) [25], which represents a comprehensive and publicly available collection of 131 scans capturing a broad spectrum of liver tumour types, and the MetaBrest dataset, collected from the University Hospital of Brest, France. The MetaBrest dataset includes CT scans taken by ten different machines, ensuring variability in acquisition technology and comprising a rich collection of 264 scans, with a particular focus on tumour volume. The AI model has been trained to perform a semantic segmentation task, associating a class with each voxel for a given volumetric medical image. There may be several classes within a target image; in our case, these are healthy liver tissues and tumour volume. The total liver volume is the concatenation of the two. Model performance was evaluated using the Dice similarity coefficient, achieving an average score above 0.9 in validation.

In contrast, spleen volumes were measured using the open-source TotalSegmentator model [26], as spleen anatomy is homogeneous and its segmentation is technically simpler and already well established. This model was trained on a diverse dataset comprising 1,204 scans, with segmentations of 104 anatomical structures, including the spleen.

Validation of volume measurements

We carried out an intermediary analysis to check the validity of the measurements, comparing both manual and AI-assisted measurements. A Pearson correlation coefficient over 0.8 validated the continuation of the study. Once validated, we performed volume measurements on the entire cohort, only using deep-learning techniques. Artificial-intelligence-assisted volume measurements were used for the main analysis. We took special care in visually examining the volume segmentation of the top five outliers in terms of volume difference between AI-assisted and manual (Fig. 1).

Definition of organ atrophy

Organ atrophy was assessed as a continuous variable based on the volumetric absolute difference between baseline organ volume and two-month organ volume. The difference was expressed as a percentage decrease or increase from the initial volume, as following: ()*100. To explore a possible link between survival and changes in tumour and non-tumour volume, patients were classified into three groups (tertiles) based on volume variations between diagnosis and two months after the start of treatment: ─ the first tertile (Q1) included patients with the smallest reduction or even increase in tumour or non-tumour volume; ─ the second tertile (Q2) includes patients with moderate volume change; ─ the third tertile (Q3) includes patients with the greatest reduction in tumour or non-tumour volume. We did not predefine atrophy thresholds and took organ atrophy as a continuous variable, in mm3.

Objectives and outcomes

This study aimed to validate AI-generated volumetric measurements against manual measurements for evaluating bevacizumab-induced liver and spleen atrophy. Moreover, we aimed to analyse the correlation of these measurements with clinical outcomes, specifically progression-free survival and overall survival in patients with mCRC. Finally, we aimed to identify clinical factors (age, sex, tumour volume) associated with organ atrophy and to evaluate their relationship with the efficacy of bevacizumab.

Statistical analysis

Intermediate analysis assessed the correlation between manual and AI-assisted volume measurement using the Pearson correlation coefficient, as explained above.

The descriptive analysis was based on the entire cohort characteristics description and stratified by exposure and trials, using median, interquartile range for continuous variables, and frequencies for qualitative variables. Differences in organ volume according to bevacizumab exposure were assessed using a Wilcoxon test. A classical linear model was employed to determine the factors associated with changes in volume based on exposure or non-exposure to bevacizumab. Multivariate analysis was conducted using covariates according to their clinical relevance, namely age, sex, and type of treatment received. For the model exploring non-tumoural volume, it was further adjusted for tumoural volume in the liver, as it strongly influences the functioning of the rest of the liver.

Overall survival and progression-free survival were analyzed using the log-rank test and subsequently adjusted with a Cox regression model. Covariates included in the multivariate analysis were selected for their clinical relevance. Patients were stratified into tertiles to examine the impact of different degrees of volume reduction on clinical outcomes.

The significance threshold for all analyses was set at alpha < 0.05.

Ethics approval

This study has been approved by the French Federation of Digestive Cancerology (FFCD) board for the transfer of images and the use of patient data.

Results

Among 593 patients included in the PRODIGE 9 and 20 trials, a total of 214 patients were included in our analysis (Fig. 2). Among them, 192 (89.7%) received bevacizumab while 22 (10.3%) did not. The majority of included patients were males (n = 139, 65%) with a median age of 68.6 years and mostly tumours of the colon (n = 161, 75.2%).

Comparison of patient characteristics by bevacizumab treatment after adjustment

Without adjustment, the results indicated that the reduction in non-tumour liver volume in patients receiving bevacizumab was statistically significant, with an average reduction of 143,226 mm³ (p = 0.0055). This trend remained significant after adjusting for age and sex, and after accounting for the variation in tumour volume between diagnosis and two months of treatment, resulting in a mean reduction of 112,900 mm³ (p = 0.0378). The volume of the tumour and spleen remained non-significant in the multivariate analysis (Table 2).

Results according to adjusted models

No significant association was found between non-tumoural liver volume reduction and overall survival, whether assessed as a continuous variable (HR: 1.10, p = 0.25) or by tertiles (Q3 vs. Q1: HR: 1.03, p = 0.891. An isolated significant difference was found for Q2 (HR: 1.43 [95% CI: 1.003 to 2.043], p = 0.048).

Spleen volume variation showed no significant effect on overall survival (HR: 0.94, p = 0.456), nor when analyzed by tertiles (Q2: HR: 0.85, p = 0.374; Q3: HR: 0.84, p = 0.333).

Regarding progression-free survival, no significant association was observed with non-tumoural liver volume variation, whether continuous (HR: 1.00, p = 0.977) or stratified (Q2: HR: 1.31, p = 0.145; Q3: HR: 0.98, p = 0.932). Similarly, spleen volume variation had no significant effect on progression-free survival (HR: 0.98, p = 0.776), including stratified analysis (Q2: HR: 0.91, p = 0.595; Q3: HR: 1.11, p = 0.532). Discussion

Validation of AI for hepatic volumetry in oncology

This study confirms the strong performance of a deep learning-based artificial intelligence model for automatic organ segmentation in an oncologic setting. The AI-generated liver and spleen volume measurements showed excellent concordance with manual measurements (Pearson’s coefficient >0.8), aligning with previous studies comparing AI and expert volumetry in abdominal imaging [27–30].

The AI model offers major practical advantages: faster processing times and, crucially, the ability to automatically differentiate between tumoural and non-tumoural liver tissue. This enables more nuanced assessments of treatment effects in complex clinical settings. Nevertheless, some limitations were noted, particularly in cases involving necrotic metastases or anatomically complex structures (Fig. 1). These discrepancies highlight the need for continued refinement of segmentation algorithms to ensure broader clinical applicability.

Bevacizumab and organ volume changes

This study revealed a significant reduction in non-tumoural liver volume among patients treated with bevacizumab. This observation is consistent with the known anti-angiogenic effects of VEGF inhibitors on healthy tissues and may represent a potential imaging biomarker of systemic drug activity. Similar findings have been reported in the retina, skeletal muscle, and even the pancreas and thyroid in patients exposed to anti-VEGF or tyrosine kinase inhibitors such as sorafenib [9, 10, 12, 31–34]. Whether this liver atrophy reflects toxicity or a favorable treatment response remains to be clarified.

In contrast, no significant reduction in liver tumour volume was observed (mean reduction: − 11,322 mm³; p = 0.863). This result is consistent with prior meta-analyses suggesting that bevacizumab often stabilizes disease rather than shrinking tumours [16]. In this study, the high RECIST responses are likely driven by chemotherapy rather than bevacizumab alone, especially given the variability in regimens (e.g., monotherapy in PRODIGE 20). Furthermore, the two-month timepoint may be too early to capture maximal tumour response.

For the spleen, no significant volume reduction was noted in treated patients. This may be explained by limited splenic vascular involvement in the context of tumour volume, or by a milder anti-angiogenic effect on this organ. Alternatively, the sample size may have been insufficient to detect subtle effects.

Survival and volume-based prognostic insights

Adjusted survival analyses provided important insights into the prognostic relevance of organ volume changes. A moderate reduction in non-tumoural liver volume (Q2 tertile) was associated with worse overall survival (HR: 1.43; p = 0.048), while more extensive atrophy (Q3) was not significantly associated with poorer outcomes. Given the small sample size and the risk of multiplicity, the signal based on tertiles should be interpreted with caution. The absence of a consistent dose-response pattern between tertiles does not allow us to conclude that there is a robust biological effect. The isolated association of Q2 should be considered exploratory and compatible with sampling variability (type I error), rather than a confirmatory conclusion.

For progression-free survival, no significant association was observed between non-tumoural liver atrophy and disease progression after multivariate adjustment, indicating that atrophy may not serve as an independent predictor of tumour behavior.

Although the greatest tumour volume reductions were observed in the Q3 group, these changes did not translate into improved overall survival. This highlights the limited predictive power of tumour volume alone and supports the exploration of complementary biomarkers, including molecular and immunologic indicators of response [35].

Finally, splenic volume changes had no significant prognostic impact on either overall or progression-free survival, reinforcing their limited relevance in the context of bevacizumab treatment.

Beyond our findings, recent studies have highlighted the value of AI-assisted volumetry and radiomics in predicting oncological outcomes across cancer types. In gastrointestinal and colorectal cancers, radiomics models based on CT scans or MRI have consistently predicted treatment response, progression-free survival and overall survival [19, 36–41]. In mCCR, radiomic features extracted from tumour volume have been shown to stratify prognosis and treatment response [42] while baseline-referenced delta radiomics improved prediction of chemotherapy response in tumour volume from colorectal and breast cancer [38]. PET-scans based radiomics has also demonstrated prognostic value in stage IV mCCR, with a machine-learning score integrating tumour volume, total lesion glycolysis, entropy, and texture independently predicting overall survival [43]. Our study contributes to this field by extending volumetric analysis beyond tumours to non-tumoural organ atrophy—specifically, liver parenchyma and spleen—while validating the reliability of AI-based automatic segmentation. This dual approach allows assessment of both tumour burden dynamics and systemic treatment effects on healthy tissue, providing a more comprehensive imaging biomarker framework.

Strengths and limitations of the study

One of the key strengths of this study is the use of an AI model for organ volumetric segmentation, enabling reproducible measurements with a strong correlation between manual and automated results (Pearson’s coefficient > 0.8). Another advantage is the precise volumetric analysis, which allows for automatic differentiation between tumour and non-tumour volumes. This distinction provides a comprehensive evaluation of bevacizumab’s effects on both tumours and healthy tissues, offering new perspectives for biomarker identification.

However, the study also has some limitations. the small sample size and heterogeneity among patient groups may limit the generalizability of the results. To refine these observations, validation in a larger cohort is necessary. Additionally, the variation in chemotherapy regimens administered in the PRODIGE 9 and 20 studies may introduce bias and limit the applicability of findings to other populations. In particular, PRODIGE 9 enrolled younger and fitter patients, whereas PRODIGE 20 included older and frailer patients, which may have influenced survival outcomes. Another limitation is the heterogeneity of CT acquisitions, as both non-enhanced and contrast-enhanced scans were included due to the lack of standardized imaging protocols across the PRODIGE trials. Finally, while AI demonstrated promising results, challenges remain in detecting necrotic metastases and complex anatomical structures, highlighting the need for further refinement of segmentation algorithms.

Prospects

This study opens several avenues for future research. First, these findings should be validated in a larger cohort with longer follow-up to confirm and better understand the link between organ atrophy and bevacizumab efficacy. Additionally, integrating clinical, biological, or molecular markers into analyses could provide new insights into the correlation between organ atrophy and treatment effects. Further adjustments for factors such as chemotherapy regimens and treatment duration could also help characterize the impact of bevacizumab more precisely.

Moreover, improving AI algorithms for enhanced segmentation of organs and metastatic lesions remains an essential goal to increase the accuracy of volumetric measurements.

Conclusion

This study explored the association between organ atrophy, measured by automated volumetric segmentation, and the efficacy of bevacizumab in mCRC. The results demonstrated a significant reduction in non-tumoural liver volume in patients treated with bevacizumab, suggesting an anti-angiogenic effect. However, no decrease in tumour or splenic volume was observed, and the impact on survival remains uncertain, emphasizing the need for further research.

Additionally, AI-based volumetric segmentation proved to be a reliable and reproducible method, strongly correlating with manual measurements and offering potential for rapid analyses in oncology. This study paves the way for the integration of AI into personalized treatment strategies, with the goal of improving prediction and monitoring of therapeutic responses.
