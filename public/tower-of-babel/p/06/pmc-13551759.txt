Viral load suppression and associated factors following enhanced adherence counseling among people living with HIV/AIDS
Authors: Teshome Nadew, Tadesse Mamo Dejene, Abraraw Gebre, Abinet Dagnaw, Akine Eshete
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPersistent enhanced adherence counseling (EAC) is necessary to improve adherence and achieve viral load suppression, particularly for clients with a viral load above 1000 copies/ml. The main aim of the study is to assess the magnitude of viral load suppression after EAC and associated factors among ART clients.MethodsA retrospective study was conducted using institutionally recorded data from July 2018 to June 2023. The study incorporated 409 records of people living with HIV (PLHIV) who were enrolled in EAC. They were selected using the random sampling technique. Data were collected using the Kobo Collect toolbox, and Stata 14.1 software was used for the analysis of exported data. Using binary logistic regression, the contributing components were identified. Variables with a p-value less than 0.25 during crude analysis were included in the multivariable logistic regression. Then, an odds ratio with 95% CI was utilized, and a p-value < 5% was used to identify factors.ResultViral load suppression rate after EAC were 64.3%, with a 95% CI (59.3–68.5). Experiencing poor drug adherence (AOR 0.21, 95% CI = 0.117–0.41), being a single (AOR 0.330, 95% CI = 0.18–0.63), being in rural resident (AOR 0.35, 95% CI = 0.202-0.60), having a history of alcohol consumption (AOR 0.53, 95% CI 0.286–0.98), and chewing khat (AOR 0.22, 95% CI = 0.09–0.54) were affecting the viral load suppression in this study.ConclusionThe unsuppressed viral load after EAC was associated with alcohol usage, khat chewing, marital status, poor adherence, and place of residence. The WHO target of 70% viral load suppression was not met. Thus, this study recommends that case managers, adherence counselors, and multidisciplinary teams at medical facilities prioritize clients with low adherence and address behavioral variables to optimize viral load suppression.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

Viral load suppression is a means of outcome measurement, whether the client received follow-up therapy or not. Consequently, the World Health Organization(WHO) promoted that testing for viral loads can be performed every year after six and twelve months. Based on the outcome, after at least six months on antiretroviral therapy (ART), those who failed to achieve viral load suppression or (VL) (> 1000 cells/mL of blood) are given enhanced adherence counseling (EAC) [1, 2]. Nearly 68% of the 38.4 million people living with HIV worldwide in 2021 have successfully suppressed their viral load. ART has been widely available, and this has played a role in a decrease in the death rate due to AIDS [3]. Viral load testing is promoted by the WHO as the gold standard for tracking a patient’s reaction to ART. Unsuppressed viral loads among users of antiretroviral medication are a major concern in the ART program. The United Nations on HIV/AIDS (UNAIDS) developed the three 95s (95-95-95) strategy, which states “unequivocally that 95% of all individuals on ART should have a suppressed viral load and end the HIV epidemic by 2030” [2, 4]. The global, regional, and national burden of disease, HIV/AIDS prevalence, mortality, and HIV-associated disability adjusted life years were all high. The [5]. Globally, the incidence of HIV/AIDS is estimated to be in the interval between 1.48 and 1.8 million in the year 1990–2021 [6].In addition, the disease has serious social, economic, and health impacts globally [7]. From 37.9 million HIV-positive individuals worldwide, and 53% of them have achieved viral load suppression. Nearly 7.7 million more people to reach the goal of 73% of PLHIV having a suppressed viral load [8].

Only 76% of the 20.9 million ART clients and 25.6 million HIV positive people in the African region had suppressed viral load in 2022 [9]. By 2020, the 90-90-90 end HIV/AIDS epidemic target could suppress 72% of the infectious agent in Ethiopia; nevertheless, it will be difficult to achieve the third 95’s viral load reduction objective [10–12]. In Uganda, the prevalence of HIV and its respective viral load suppression is 39.3% and 59.6%, among children’s and adult people respectively. Nigerian and Ethiopian studies reported that 77% and 73% of the Clients had viral load suppression, respectively [13–16].

UNAIDS indicated high AIDS related mortality in 2021. Although 16.6 million people received ART in the African region, viral load suppression remains low, and the region failed to achieve the (95-95-95) targets [17].

Reduced CD4 counts, disease stage, concomitant infections or OIs, and treatment adherence affect viral load suppression [18–20].

According to the national HIV treatment guideline of Ethiopia, EAC is implemented on a monthly basis, and three consecutive EAC sessions are mandatory. After three consecutive EAC sessions viral load testing was performed, and the first EAC is recommended within seven days of confirming the initial high viral load. Even though Ethiopia employed enhanced adherence counseling to suppress the viral load, evidence gap existed in terms of viral load status after EAC intervention. Viral load suppression is the anticipated result of extensive funding, resources, and EAC intervention. However, this is not known in the study area. In addition, viral load suppression differs in different settings, such as health facilities, human resources, and individual-level variations. Therefore, this study was required to fill these gaps. The country failed to achieve the 2020 target with only 82% of the client’s o ART suppressed the viral load, which is below 90%. In addition, there were 620,000 HIV/AIDS clients in 2020, resulting in 13,000 deaths from HIV, and 480,000 clients were on treatment, of whom 72.5% had achieved viral suppression [21].

Age, gender, educational level, length of time on ART, initial viral load count, poor nutritional status, sex, and place of residence were the variables that were statistically linked to viral load suppression [1, 4, 19, 22–27].The predictors of viral load suppression were clinical and related characteristics such as CD4 cell count [23, 24, 28].Baseline WHO clinical staging, baseline opportunistic infections, and baseline CD4 are the predictors of viral load suppression [23, 25, 29–31]. Treatment and Related Characteristics, like adherence to ART, are another predictor for viral load suppression [32, 33]. Participants who adhered poorly at enrollment had a minimum 50% reduced chance of obtaining VL suppression following EAC compared to those who adhered well [34–36]. Another factor that predicts viral load reduction is the kind of regimen in the treatment category [37, 38]. Those who had only attended one or two EAC sessions had about a 70% decreased probability of achieving VL suppression [1, 39, 40]. Psychosocial, Mental, and Behavioral Related Characteristics, like strong evidence linking positive mental health screening results to viral load suppression, were found in the South African study. Disruptive behavior disorder was found to be substantially correlated with an unsuppressed viral load at a threshold of more than > 1000 copies/ml in both the New York City trial and the Vietnam study. Social and psychological variables are also linked to HIV VL that is not suppressed [23, 28]. According to a US study, variations in the duration of viral suppression were not significantly correlated with depression or the use of antidepressants [41–43].Hence, this study aims to assess viral load suppression after EAC and to present data regarding the primary context-specific variables of viral load suppression among ART clients. Additionally, this study would complete the information gap and add to the body of data, especially in post-EAC situations where no studies have been conducted in the area.

Objective

General objective

The main objective of our study was to assess viral load suppression after enhanced adherence counseling and its associated factors among ART Clients at public health facilities of Debre Berhan Regio-Politian city, North Shoa, Ethiopia, 2024.

Particularly, to assess the magnitude of Clients with viral load suppression after EAC among Clients who initiated ART, and identify factors associated with viral load suppression among Clients.

Methods and settings of the study

Study design and period

An institution-based retrospective 60-month follow-up study was conducted from July 2018 to June 2023.

Study area

The study was conducted in Debre Birhan, which is 130 km from the capital city of Ethiopia. Debre Birhan Comprehensive Specialized Hospital and other public health facilities provide HIV/AIDS care. A two-tier system, first, second, and third line ART, viral load testing, professional adherence counseling, specialized diagnostic, and drug supply services are delivered by comprehensive specialized hospital. Public ART facilities offer regular follow-up care to rural and semi-urban populations. Differentiated service delivery (DSD) strategies, such as multi-month dispensing, track refills, and adherence evaluation, are used at all public ART sites. Beza Positivity Development Organization and Emanuel Development Association, Emanuel Development Association, and the centers for disease control (CDC) are important collaborators who support ART related services.

Public ART health centers offer routine follow-up programs and services to rural and semi-urban populations. Differentiated service delivery (DSD), including multi-month dispensing, track refills, and adherence evaluation, is used in all public ART facilities. Consequently, three of the city’s five ART health facility sub-cities, a total of 39 kebeles, or the smallest administrative units were represented for this study. According to the 2016 Ethiopian calendar population projection, Debre Berhan City has a total population of 209,011. There were 110,077 females and 98,934 males among them. The city has two hospitals, eight health facilities, and twelve health posts. According to the DHIS2 report, there were 3,623 recipients of care (RoC) were enrolled in six ART health facilities under the sub-city as of June 2023.

Population

All HIV-positive individuals after six months of ART and who enrolled in the EAC program in Debre Berhan regio-politant city, Ethiopia were the source population of this study. The study population consisted of HIV-positive individuals who tested positive for the virus and who had at least one repeat result following an EAC in the designated health facilities between July 2018 and June 2023. The study subjects were those HIV-positive individuals who were randomly selected and who had viral loads > 1000 copies/ml and at least one repeat result following an EAC session between July 2018 and June 2023 in the designated Health facilities.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

All ART clients whose documented viral load > 1000 copies/ml were enrolled in the study. In contrast, patients who were lost to follow-up, transferred before finishing all EAC, had incomplete records, or had no second viral load result were not included in this study.

Determination of the sample size

Sample size was determined by using the single population proportion formula, considering the following assumption. n= (Zα/2)2p (1-p).

Accordingly, the sample size was calculated as, n= (1.96)2 0.409(1-0.409)/ (0.05)2 = 371 by adding 10% for none-response, the total sample size was 409.

Sampling technique and procedure

The Debre Birhan town has five ART provider Health facilities; all of these public health facilities were enrolled in the study. There were 510 clients who completed EAC between July 2018 and June 2023. Information collected from the viral load registration books of the five public health facilities, and the necessary sample size was distributed among them in a proportional-to-size allocation (Fig. 1). Finally, we have selected a sample at random from each of the five public health facilities’ viral load registration books using the straightforward random sampling approach of the “RAND” command on the Excel function. Study variables

Dependent variables

Viral load status after EAC (suppressed/unsuppressed).

The outcome of the study was measured as viral load status using viral assays. Suppressed when ≤ 1000 copies/mL after 3–6 months of post EAC (exposure).Whereas, high viral load refers to when viral load result is > 1000 copies/ml ART clients started EAC and after follow-up Viral load was measured [44].

Independent variables

Age, sex, Educational status, Marital status, Occupation, Residence; Nutrition status; Normal, under nutrition are socio-demographic related variables. Treatment and Related characteristics include: ART adherence, Type of ART Regimen, daily drug dosage, EAC sessions, ART duration, Other medication; Clinical and Related characteristics; WHO clinical stage, CD4 count, and opportunistic infections; Psychosocial, Mental and Behavioral Related Characteristics; Mental disorder, Alcohol drinking, Khat Chewing.

Viral load suppression refers to a viral load status using viral assays which is ≤ 1000 copies/mL after 3–6 months of EAC. High viral load refers to when viral load result is > 1000 copies/mL [14]. Virological Failure is experienced when VL is above 1000 copies/ml based on two consecutive VL measurements within 3–6 months. A PLHIV who has Virological failure will receive adherence support following the first VL test [46]. Good adherence is measured as it is measured elsewhere [14], equal to or greater than 95% adherence i.e., missing only 1 out of 30 doses or missing 2 from the 60 doses implies good adherence. This study used the pill count method, assessed by health professionals using a form to assess adherence. Fair adherence is when the level of adherence is between 85 and 94%, i.e., missing 2–4 doses out of 30 doses or 4 to 9 doses from 60 doses [14]. Poor adherence is less than 85% adherence, i.e. missing > 5 doses out of 30 doses or > 10 doses from 60 doses implies poor adherence [14].

Data collection methods

The information was collected from the client’s chart, the EMR ART smart care, the viral load registration book, the EAC page, and the laboratory request using our structured pre-tested checklist. Consequently, all charts, including detailed information about ART receiving client were analyzed. The data that was most current to the start date of increased EAC sessions were deemed baseline data if clinical parameters and laboratory findings (CD4 count and WHO clinical stage) were not found at that time. Five BSc degree nurses were chosen to gather data, and two ART data managers with extensive expertise served as supervisors and also engaged in the process. Chart abstraction and sociodemographic and clinical characteristics were both included in the data collection form. The most recent and comprehensive data for incomplete charts were also obtained by using various registers, follow-up forms, and laboratory findings. Expert content reviews and internal consistency checks were used to verify the authenticity and reliability of the tests.

Data quality management, process, and analysis

Planning well-designed data collection tools ensured the reliability of the gathered data. A one-day training on the objectives of the study, extraction of the data, and recording from the client’s chart and registration books was given to supervisors and data collectors. A supervisor was responsible for making sure no data was overlooked throughout the data gathering phase. The primary investigator of the study was in charge of the overall activities. A pretest was administered to 10% of clients selected at random from Debre Sina Health Center. Following the pretest, the tool was revised. The data was collected using Kobo collect toolbox and exported to Excel and imported to STATA software for analysis. Logistic regression was applied for the primary outcome of viral suppression at 60 months’ follow-up. Initial unadjusted models were produced, and a p-value less than 0.25 led to inclusion in multivariable analysis. Variables with a standard error (SE) or variance inflation factor (VIF less than 10) were eliminated from the analysis, and those without a collinearity effect were added to a binary logistic regression model to examine their relationship with the outcome variables. The Hosmer-Lem show test (> 0.05) or goodness of fit test was used to assess the model’s fitness. The model’s value was 0.28. The percentage of viral load suppression was shown after EAC sessions. A final model determines the independent variables with their effect size and declare statistical significance using p-value less than 0.05.

Treatment and related characteristics

Of the participants, about 174 (42.5%) chose their medication on a need basis, and 85% of them waited more than a year for the viral load to be determined. Furthermore, 304 patients, or 74.3%, have been receiving ART for longer than five years. When it came to the frequency of drug use, 335 clients (81.9%) took their medication once daily, and 303 (74%) clients did not use any other medications than ART. Furthermore, 164 individuals (24.9%) had poor adherence, whereas 47 individuals (11.9%) had fair adherence. Only 97 (23.7%) clients attended the EAC session after three to six months, and 321(78.4%) clients were on first-line medication. After undergoing a nutritional screening, 225(55%) of the total exhibited normal nutritional status. Of the individuals, 306 (75%) were defined as being in WHO clinical stage one, and 71 (17%) had infections that were opportunistic. Furthermore, 157 (17.3%) of them had a CD4 count of less than 350 at baseline.

Psychosocial, mental, and behavioral related characteristics

The client’s psychosocial and behavioral related factor indicated that 220 (53.4%) and 62 (15%) of the individuals drank alcohol and used khat. However, 395 (96%) of the individuals did not receive a mental health diagnosis (Table 2).

Factors associated with viral load suppression

Sex, marital status, adherence, initial CD4 count, residence, opportunistic infection, current ART regimen, other medication, daily drug dosage intake, duration of ART, diagnosis of mental disorder, drinking alcohol, and khat chewing were variables taken to the final model from the bi-variable model. According to the multivariable analysis, those with poor treatment adherence were 79% less likely than those with good drug adherence to have suppressed viral load during the EAC session (AOR 0.211 (95% CI = 0.117–0.411)). Compared to married individuals, the likelihood of a suppressed viral load was 67% lower in singles [AOR 0.333 (95% CI = 0.179–0.631)]. Compared to those who live in urban areas, the odds of having a suppressed viral load were 67% lower in rural areas [AOR 0.351 (95% CI = 0.202–0.601)].

Alcohol drinkers had a 47% lower chance of suppressing their viral load compared to abstainers [(AOR 0.531, 95% CI= (0.28–0.98)]. Additionally, customers who used Khat Chewing were 78% less likely to have a suppressed viral load than those who did not (AOR 0.223, 95% CI = 0.09–0.54) (Table 3).

Discussion

Based on the study findings, 64.3% of ART clients who were involved in enhanced adherence counseling (EAC) had suppressed viral load. Once potential confounding variables were taken into account, there was a reduced likelihood of a meaningful correlation between suppressed viral load determinants like marital status, poor adherence, place of residence, alcohol consumption, and khat chewing. The percentage of viral load suppression (64.3%) in this investigation was similar compared to that of a study done at North Wollo Zone Public Hospital (66.4%) [4]. This might be due to the similarity in the study design that was employed and the similarity in the healthcare institutions’ levels of delivery. Our study’s results, however, were greater than those of the study carried out in the west Gojam zone (51.73%) and 31.2% in Harare, Zimbabwe [2, 39]. The observed disparity might be probably from variations in sociodemographic characteristics, the methodologies employed in the research, and the accessibility of health services for the ART client. As a result, a high viral load has been linked to immune system malfunction, a higher risk of opportunistic infection, HIV progression, and other problems. Additionally, the results of this study were considerably greater than those of investigations conducted in health facilities in the West Gojam zone. (51.73%) and public hospitals run by the Hawassa City administration (40.9%), yet a greater suppression rate showed that the research carried out in northwest Nigeria’s, Amhara Public Health Institute, and population-based survey of Nigeria (73%), (90.4%),80.88% respectively [45–47]. With the exception of North west Nigeria and Amhara public health institute research, this amount of viral load suppression is also below the 70% of the WHO’s target [26]. This discrepancy might be explained by deviations in the standard of medical care, data collection, and documentation methods. Future investigation is necessary to determine whether barriers at the facility or client level account for the overall lower incidence of viral suppression in the study area. The number of clients at the healthcare institution may make the time allotted for counseling sessions insufficient, and the experience and education of the counselor may have an impact on how well the message is received. Numerous psychosocial factors can affect adherence at the client level; if these issues are not addressed, they may contribute to the reported ineffectiveness of counseling [48–50].

The overall impression is that patients with high viral loads may not receive adequate care, which could delay the identification of therapy failure. When it came to the marital status variable, being single had a 67% lower chance of having a suppressed viral load than married ART clients. This suggests that unmarried clients may receive less spousal support than married clients, and that people with unsuppressed viral loads are more likely to spread the virus to others through unprotected sex. Furthermore, a single client may be more vulnerable to psychological, social, and economic burdens. This could have an impact on their motivation and capacity to follow a treatment plan. However, this finding is not supported by other studies [51].The research evidence from Gojam, Ethiopia and south Africa showed that sex of the client is the determining factor for the viral load suppression, not the marital status of the client [52, 53].

Individuals who adhered poorly to antiretroviral treatment were 79% less likely to have a suppressed viral load than those who adhered well. This result is consistent with research from eastern Uganda [30]. Additionally, there have been other studies that show a strong correlation between poor adherence and viral load suppression. Poor adherence are found to be associated with low viral load suppressions in the eastern Uganda study [54] This suggests that a variety of barriers to consistent medication adherence, such as fear of discrimination or disclosure, the burden of ART medication in the form of pills, mental health conditions like depression or substance abuse, a lack of social support system, or other psychosocial challenges, may contribute to poor ART adherence behaviors [55].

The other reason might be failure of a proper counseling methods in the study area, which has a direct effect for medication adherence. If patients did not understand the counseling or if the provider failed to deliver a proper counseling method, it results in poor adherence.

This study found a correlation between alcohol consumption and the suppression of viral loads. A study conducted in South Africa reported that alcohol consumption is related with low viral load suppression; another study from Uganda also showed the effect of alcohol consumption on the viral load suppression [56, 57]; however, other investigations did not find a statistically significant relationship between alcohol consumption and viral load suppression. The reasons might be that alcohol consumption may impact drug metabolism directly or decrease medication adherence, which could compromise the efficacy of ART. This may result in less effective viral suppression, raise the possibility of drug-resistant virus strains emerging, and make medication forgetfulness more likely [58].Moreover, there is a considerable correlation between suppressing viral load and khat chewing. The idea that chewing khat could lessen the effectiveness of ART is suggested by the high connection observed among khat users. There are several possible causes for this association. First, by disrupting sleep cycles and daily patterns, khat’s stimulant effect may lead to irregular medication adherence. Moreover, khat use may have an impact on hunger and food consumption, which may further impair immunity. A socioeconomic and psychological component, such as a higher stress level or the presence of other dangerous behaviors, may also play a part in substance use. The necessity of integrated drug use therapies within HIV treatment programs is underscored by these findings. In order to address the possible effects of khat use on ART adherence and effectiveness, healthcare providers should screen for khat users and give counseling and support. with order to effectively decrease the viral load, public health activities should concentrate on informing clients who are HIV positive about the hazards involved with using khat and advocating for better alternatives. Ultimately, client households in rural locations had 65% lower odds of having a reduced viral load than those in urban settings. Our research finding is consistent with a study conducted elsewhere regarding the residence of the ART client [59] Conversely, other research revealed that the decrease of viral loads was unrelated to residency status, these variations could be due to infrastructure, time variation, fewer health care facilities, more travel times to receive care, or regional variance. Because of this, stigma and prejudice, along with the greater rate of HIV transmission, have an impact on public health and reduce economic productivity due to increased morbidity and mortality. In general, people with unsuppressed viral loads have an increased chance of developing comorbidities, AIDS-defining diseases, and opportunistic infections, among other HIV-related problems. Reduced quality of life and worse overall health outcomes may result from this.

Strengths and limitation of the study

This study makes use of and attempts to evaluate additional variables that were not included previously. The retrospective approach and secondary data evaluation of this study are among its drawbacks. For the purpose of data analysis and interpretation, only the variables that are listed in the patient records may be utilized. Due to the lack of information in secondary data, several important elements, including the distance to the medical institution and other clinically relevant data, may be very important yet go unaddressed.

The other concern regarding this retrospective study is the probability of selection bias. Due to loss to follow-up, incomplete data, or lack of repeat viral load testing, selection bias may occur even though we gave great attention to those who had data completeness at the beginning of the study. We do not know the outcomes of those lost to follow-up and excluded patients. Hence, the study only attempted to maintain data quality checks, data completeness, and analysis to alleviate the problem of selection bias. However, it is not enough to eliminate all bias through those measures alone. Therefore, caution should be exercised in interpreting the result of the finding, considering these drawbacks.

The study also acknowledges that there might be residual confounding from the lack of measurements at the base line, including potential drug resistance, the quality of counseling given to patients, baseline viral load severity, and socioeconomic variations of the patients.

Conclusion and recommendation

Lower levels of viral load suppression have been associated with variables such as alcohol usage, khat chewing, marital status, poor adherence, and site of residence. The target of 70% recommended by the World Health Organization was not achieved in terms of suppressing viral loads. Thus, it is recommended that ART case managers, adherence counselors, and multidisciplinary teams at medical facilities prioritize clients with low adherence and address behavioral variables in order to optimize viral load suppression.

Operational definition

Post EAC viral load suppression categorized as (Suppressed and Unsuppressed) based on national cut points, which is viral load suppression ≤ 1000 copies/ml and unsuppressed viral load as > 1000 copies/ ml [14].

Scheduled/unscheduled

Clients come to the health facility on their appointment follow-up date, it is labeled as “scheduled (S) and when the clients do not come on their appointment follow up date it is labeled as “unscheduled” (US) [60].

Criteria for third line regimen

The main criteria for the third line regimen are first and second line treatment failure, and based on the Ministry of Health’s national official direction, health facilities were selected to deliver third line treatment for clients’ health facilities having high case load and well-equipped or specialized health facilities [60].

Alcohol consumption was considered in this study as operationally defied by no or slight alcohol drinkers if they are used less than two times per week or not used alcohol at all labeled as (No). However, if they have a history of alcohol drinking more than two times per week it is considered as having alcohol consumption or labeled as (yes) [57].

‘Khat chewing’ were assessed as yes or no response. If they had a history of using khat more than once in a week it was considered as having a history of khat chewing otherwise if they do not have any history of khat chewing ever it was considered as ‘Not khat user’ labeled as ‘No.
