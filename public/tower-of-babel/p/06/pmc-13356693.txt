Optimizing in-context exemplars for a generalist vision-language model to support region of interest selection in ovarian tumor grossing
Authors: Dong He, Longhai Jin, Xianglin Mei, Meng Jia, Zhaoyong Wang, Bo Ren, Lanqing Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryPathological gross examination guides tissue sampling in heterogeneous ovarian tumors, where mixed components can complicate selection of representative regions. We evaluated whether a generalist large vision-language model can support gross image analysis through pathology-informed in-context exemplars. Using Qwen-2.5-Vision-Language-72B, we developed single-component exemplar optimization and histology-guided exemplar optimization to promote component-aware reasoning and region-level risk flagging. In a single-institution retrospective cohort, the optimized eight-shot strategy achieved 89.8% image-level accuracy on mixed ovarian tumors and improved region-level demarcation relevant to sampling. In borderline tumors with microinvasion or microinvasive carcinoma, model-identified regions encompassed pathologist-defined sampling areas, supporting risk flagging rather than stand-alone diagnosis. In rare malignant tumors (n = 21), the same strategy achieved 90.5% image-level accuracy. These findings suggest that domain-aligned exemplar design can enhance in-context reasoning for grossing-oriented medical image analysis.

Introduction

Pathological gross examination (PGE) and tissue sampling determine which tissue is ultimately represented on histologic slides and therefore strongly influence downstream diagnosis.1,2 In ovarian tumors, gross heterogeneity is frequent, and a single specimen may contain coexisting benign, borderline, and malignant components.3 In routine practice, the selection and prioritization of sampling sites rely on visual assessment under time and workload constraints, and variability in sampling can contribute to inconsistent representation of the highest-risk component, particularly in mixed or multiloculated masses.4,5 This variability may be accentuated in intraoperative frozen-section settings, where rapid decisions are required and diagnostic discordance has been reported.6,7 Despite the central role of grossing in the diagnostic pipeline, computational decision support for gross examination has received substantially less attention than microscopy-based computational pathology.8,9,10

Recent advances in foundation models and multimodal large models have raised the possibility of leveraging generalist vision-language systems for medical image reasoning with minimal task-specific training.11,12,13 Unlike conventional computer-vision pipelines that typically require supervised retraining on curated labels, large vision-language models (LVLMs) can be adapted through prompting and in-context learning (ICL) by providing a small set of example demonstrations that shape the model’s outputs.14,15 However, naive prompting can yield unstable performance in medical settings, and the reliability and structure of outputs remain important practical concerns.13,16 In the grossing setting, the more clinically relevant question is often not whether a model can assign a single global label, but whether it can provide consistent, auditable guidance about where to sample.

A major challenge in heterogeneous ovarian specimens is that visual similarity does not reliably correspond to sampling priority. Similarity-based exemplar retrieval (e.g., k-nearest neighbors) may select visually redundant examples and fail to teach the model how to handle mixed-component specimens where subtle high-risk foci coexist with more indolent-appearing areas.14 In gross pathology, sampling decisions are typically guided by component-level reasoning and risk-aware prioritization (e.g., targeting focal solid areas, papillary excrescences, color heterogeneity, hemorrhage/necrosis, and coarse cut surfaces) to capture the highest-grade component.1,2,4 Because gross sampling is driven by prioritizing the most suspicious foci in a heterogeneous mass rather than overall visual similarity, we hypothesized that ICL exemplars explicitly organized around component-level demonstrations would yield more consistent, auditable region-level sampling guidance than generic similarity-based retrieval.

Here, we retrospectively evaluated Qwen-2.5-Vision-Language-72B for ovarian tumor gross examination images and developed two pathology-informed exemplar optimization strategies for in-context learning: single-component exemplar optimization (ScEO), designed to reduce mixed-component confusion by providing unambiguous component-level demonstrations, and histology-guided exemplar optimization (HgEO), designed to incorporate subtype-aware variability that better reflects real-world heterogeneity. We implemented a structured reporting format to standardize region demarcation and sampling-priority outputs, enabling case-level auditing of model-suggested regions of interest. We intentionally position this approach as decision support for sampling guidance and region-level risk flagging rather than as an autonomous diagnostic system; therefore, our primary evaluation focuses on sampling utility (whether suggested regions encompass pathology-defined lesion areas used for sampling), with image-level grading performance treated as a secondary metric. We further assess performance in clinically challenging settings, including rare malignant subtypes and common acquisition artifacts, in a problem space where gross-image decision support remains relatively underexplored and prior work has largely relied on task-specific models.17 In addition, we include borderline tumors with microinvasion/microinvasive carcinoma—entities for which management and outcomes have been extensively discussed, yet microinvasive foci are frequently below the resolution of gross inspection—thereby motivating sampling-utility endpoints rather than claims of gross “detection” of microinvasion.3,18,19 Finally, because imaging-based preoperative characterization of ovarian tumors remains imperfect and intraoperative diagnosis can affect surgical management, robust sampling guidance at the grossing stage may be particularly valuable.20,21,22 An overview of the study design, exemplar-optimization workflow, LVLM inference process, and structured ROI output is shown in Figures 1A–1D.Figure 1Schematic overview of the LVLM-based workflow for ovarian tumor gross image analysis and tissue-sampling guidanceThe workflow begins with construction of a structured query for ovarian tumor gross images, including task instructions, classification rules, and standardized output requirements. Results

Dataset characteristics

An overview of the evaluation datasets is provided in Figure 2. Dataset I included 738 gross images of ovarian tumors (331 benign, 136 borderline, and 270 malignant) with clearly dissected internal structures and served as the primary benchmark set for optimizing in-context exemplar strategies. Dataset II comprised 21 images of rare ovarian malignant tumors curated to probe generalization beyond common histotypes. Dataset III consisted of 87 borderline tumors with microinvasion or microinvasive carcinoma, representing diagnostically challenging scenarios in which sampling adequacy is critical despite subtle or visually occult invasive foci. Dataset IV included 177 low-quality gross images with common specimen- or camera-related artifacts (114 benign, 29 borderline, and 34 malignant), designed to interrogate performance boundaries under suboptimal acquisition conditions. We then assessed conventional few-shot ICL using randomly sampled exemplars. Classification accuracy increased with the number of exemplars, reaching 65.0% with 4-shot (95% CI: 0.583–0.716), 70.0% with 6-shot (95% CI: 0.650–0.733), and 73.3% with 8-shot ICL (95% CI: 0.716–0.783) (Figures 3A and 3B). A kNN-based exemplar selection strategy did not improve performance over random sampling, consistent with high visual similarity among gross images across classes (see supporting information methods S2).

Analysis of error patterns

To clarify where baseline strategies failed, we manually reviewed misclassified cases and their corresponding model outputs (reasoning text and overlays). Errors clustered in heterogeneous specimens containing multiple pathological components of different grades within the same gross section (e.g., benign with borderline or borderline with malignant). In such cases, the model frequently produced overlapping predicted regions for different grades, reflecting difficulty in disentangling coexisting macroscopic patterns within a single image (Figure S3; Table S5).

Notably, random few-shot prompting improved the model’s feature recognition compared with zero-shot inference (e.g., the model more often described discordant macroscopic cues within one specimen), yet regional delineation of distinct components could worsen—suggesting that exemplar supervision at the image level (where the label reflects only the highest-grade component) may inadvertently blur the association between features and region-level grade when multiple components coexist.

Performance improvement with optimized exemplar strategies

Motivated by these error patterns, we tested exemplar libraries with controlled composition. Under the ScEO strategy, exemplar images were restricted to homogeneous, single-grade components to provide unambiguous macroscopic prototypes. Building on ScEO, two progressively refined histology-guided strategies (HgEO-ScSME and HgEO-ScSMEO) incorporated histotype-informed examples to better capture subtype-specific gross features.

As shown in Figures 3B and 3C (I–IV), overall image-level accuracy increased stepwise with exemplar optimization. Compared with 8-shot random exemplars (73.3%; 95% CI: 0.716–0.783), performance improved to 80.0% (95% CI: 0.772–0.865) with 8-shot ScEO, 85.1% (95% CI: 0.786–0.898) with 8-shot HgEO-ScSME, and reached 89.8% (95% CI: 0.847–0.926) with 8-shot HgEO-ScSMEO.

Optimization of regional classification accuracy

Because the intended use case is sampling guidance in heterogeneous tumors, we next focused on region-level performance in multi-component cases. Across grade-specific regions, HgEO strategies consistently improved regional classification accuracy over random exemplars (Figure 4A). For benign regions, accuracy increased from 74.7% (95% CI: 0.719–0.812) with 8-shot random exemplars to 94.7% (95% CI: 0.896–0.975) with 8-shot HgEO-ScSMEO (Figure 4B). Malignant-region performance showed a similar trend (72.6% [95% CI: 0.698–0.791] to 88.1% [95% CI: 0.830–0.909]) (Figure 4D). To complement quantitative evaluation, five pathologists rated the clinical plausibility of regional demarcation and the relevance of the model’s feature descriptions on a 5-point Likert scale. Across raters, the mean score was 4.35 (SD = 0.869), with individual averages ranging from 4.12 to 4.55 (Figure S4), indicating generally high agreement that the highlighted regions and described macroscopic cues aligned with sampling-relevant targets.

Finally, inspection of the reasoning traces suggested that histology-guided exemplars helped the model avoid recurrent subtype-associated confusions (e.g., hemorrhage-related color heterogeneity in benign endometrioid lesions being over-interpreted as malignancy), consistent with the intended role of HgEO in teaching subtype-conditioned macroscopic patterns (Figure 3C-IV). Under the API workflow used in this study, an 8-shot analysis completed within approximately 1–2 min per image.

The model remained relatively stable in some degradation modes (e.g., overexposure and mild defocus), but failures were concentrated in scenarios that disrupt three-dimensional interpretation from a single 2D view. In fragmented benign specimens, fixation-related folds could be misread as papillary structures or solid components. In incompletely dissected or unopened specimens, the model could correctly identify the exposed internal surface yet misinterpret the smooth external wall as a benign cystic structure or assign an incorrect grade to the overall specimen. These boundary cases highlight a limitation in spatial reasoning and viewpoint dependence when gross anatomy is compromised by preparation or imaging artifacts.

Cross-model qualitative generalization of exemplar strategies

To further explore whether the proposed exemplar strategies could be applied beyond the primary Qwen-2.5-VL-72B model, we conducted a supplementary cross-model evaluation on 12 representative ovarian tumor cases. The evaluated models included qwen2.5-vl-72b-instruct, qwen3-vl-plus, mistralai_mistral-large-3-675b-instruct-2512, and moonshotai_kimi-k2.5, tested under the same 8-shot random, ScEO, and HgEO conditions (Figure S5; Table S7). Across models, the qualitative pattern was consistent: Random exemplars tended to produce more scattered ROI outputs, ScEO improved localization focus, and HgEO provided the clearest region-selection phenotype. The two Qwen-series models showed the strongest qualitative alignment in this setting, whereas mistral-large-3 and kimi-k2.5 still exhibited visible improvement with exemplar optimization (Figure S5; Table S7). These findings support the potential cross-model applicability of pathology-informed exemplar design, while remaining exploratory because the supplementary evaluation was not powered for formal quantitative comparison.

Discussion

In this single-institution retrospective study, we evaluated whether a generalist LVLM (LVLM) can be adapted to support PGE with an emphasis on tissue sampling guidance using in-context learning (ICL) and pathology-informed exemplar optimization. PGE and sampling are clinically consequential but remain susceptible to inter-observer variability, particularly in heterogeneous specimens where mixed components can obscure the most informative foci for block selection.1,2,4 Our results show that exemplar curation strategies designed to reflect pathology practice—first by presenting single-component demonstrations (ScEO) and then by enriching exemplars with histology-guided variability (HgEO)—substantially improved both image-level grading consistency and region-level demarcation in ovarian tumor gross images. These findings support the feasibility of a “foundation model + lightweight ICL optimization” approach for grossing decision support, a setting that has been comparatively underexplored relative to microscopy-based computational pathology.8,23,24

A central observation from our baseline experiments is that gross-image heterogeneity limits the effectiveness of naive exemplar selection, including similarity-driven retrieval, which may be confounded by high visual redundancy across different tumor grades. In contrast, pathologists do not sample based on global appearance similarity; rather, gross sampling is guided by component-level reasoning and prioritization of suspicious foci (e.g., focal solid areas, papillary excrescences, color heterogeneity, hemorrhage/necrosis, and coarse cut surfaces) to ensure representation of the highest-risk component within a heterogeneous mass.1,2,5 By organizing exemplars around these component-oriented demonstrations, ScEO reduced mixed-component confusion, while HgEO further improved performance by exposing the model to histology-associated macroscopic variability that is encountered in routine practice. This design choice complements prior work showing that ICL can be effective for pathology image reasoning but highlights that exemplar selection must be aligned with domain-specific decision logic rather than treated as a purely similarity-based retrieval problem.14 Based on our error-pattern analysis, ScEO was designed to reduce mixed-component confusion by using single-component exemplars as clearer grade-specific prototypes. HgEO then broadened this strategy by adding histology-guided subtype coverage and exposing the model to a wider range of clinically relevant macroscopic appearances. Because the structured output format and frozen prompt framework were held constant during these comparisons, the stepwise improvement from random few-shot ICL to ScEO and then HgEO mainly supports the contribution of exemplar design: exemplar purity clarifies grade-specific visual prototypes, whereas histology-guided subtype coverage broadens the range of clinically relevant gross patterns available to the model.

We also implemented a structured output schema to standardize region demarcation and sampling-priority reporting, enabling case-level auditing of model suggestions. Standardization and auditability are particularly relevant for gross examination because the downstream consequence is not only a label but the selection of blocks that will determine what tissue is represented on slides.1,2 In this context, hierarchical reporting (prioritizing malignant-risk features when present) should be interpreted as a conservative sampling convention rather than a guarantee of diagnostic safety, and the model’s outputs are intended to be reviewed and accepted or rejected by the responsible pathologist. More broadly, structured prompting and ICL can be viewed as practical mechanisms to reduce format drift and improve consistency of LVLM responses in medical settings, but they do not replace the need for rigorous, endpoint-driven validation.12,13

The microinvasion/microinvasive carcinoma cohort illustrates why sampling utility is the appropriate primary endpoint for certain grossing scenarios. Borderline tumors with microinvasion involve microscopic invasive foci that are often below the resolution of gross inspection, and clinical management depends on adequate sampling to ensure these foci are captured histologically.3,18,19 Conventional segmentation metrics such as IoU may underestimate clinically useful outputs in this setting because pathologists do not require pixel-perfect boundaries during grossing; rather, they require reliable identification of regions that warrant targeted sampling. Accordingly, the sampling-utility endpoint should be interpreted as region-level risk flagging aligned with gross sampling practice, not as precise segmentation or gross detection of microscopic disease. In our study, model-identified regions of interest encompassed the lesion areas used for sampling across this cohort, supporting the role of HgEO-guided LVLM output as a tool for additional scrutiny or targeted embedding rather than definitive diagnostic adjudication.

Our findings should be interpreted in the context of existing gross-image decision-support efforts. Prior work has demonstrated that task-specific deep learning models can assist with ovarian tumor gross examination and frozen-section sampling decisions under defined training and validation regimes.17 The present study differs in that it evaluates a generalist LVLM adapted through ICL exemplar optimization rather than supervised retraining, and it emphasizes region-level guidance and auditability of suggested sampling foci, while preserving human oversight.6,7 The supplementary cross-model experiment further suggests that pathology-informed exemplar design may have portability across LVLM architectures: the qualitative direction of improvement from random to ScEO and HgEO was observed not only in the Qwen-series models but also in mistral-large-3 and kimi-k2.5. At the same time, the stronger alignment of the Qwen-series models may reflect model-specific capabilities and the fact that prompt development was performed with Qwen-2.5-VL-72B. These findings support potential transferability rather than definitive model-agnostic validation. Specialist computer-vision models and generalist LVLMs are therefore complementary: specialist models may offer strong performance under stable data distributions, whereas generalist LVLMs may offer flexibility across tasks but require careful control of exemplars, prompts, and evaluation endpoints.9,12

In conclusion, pathology-informed exemplar optimization improved the feasibility and consistency of ICL-based gross image analysis by a generalist LVLM in a retrospective setting, with the most clinically defensible utility being region-level sampling guidance and risk flagging in heterogeneous ovarian tumors.

Limitations of the study

Several considerations guide interpretation and future validation. First, this was a retrospective single-center study, and external validation across institutions, grossing practices, surgical handling, fixation, and imaging protocols remains necessary. Second, K-Net segmentation was used as a fixed preprocessing step to standardize the visible specimen field and reduce non-specimen background distractions. Because the same preprocessing workflow was applied uniformly across all prompting and exemplar strategies, it should not account for the relative performance gains observed from random exemplars to ScEO and HgEO. However, segmentation quality may still influence absolute ROI localization, particularly when specimen boundaries are incomplete or when lesion-containing areas are partially excluded. Therefore, our conclusions should be interpreted as applying to a fixed specimen-segmentation preprocessing workflow, and future work should include dedicated preprocessing sensitivity analyses comparing raw images, bounding-box crops, and specimen-segmented inputs. Third, we did not evaluate prospective workflows, real-time intraoperative decision-making, missed-lesion rates, staging accuracy, patient outcomes, formal confidence calibration, repeated-inference agreement, or abstention thresholds. Finally, because performance gains can be influenced by prompt and exemplar design, strict case-level data isolation and transparent reporting of development versus test usage remain essential. These considerations define priorities for future work while preserving the central finding that pathology-informed exemplars improved region-level sampling guidance under the evaluated conditions.

Experimental model and study participant details

Human specimens and ethics

This retrospective study used de-identified ovarian tumor gross specimen images and pathology data from the Second Hospital of Jilin University. A total of 1,119 gross examination images were collected between December 2018 and December 2024; after applying the prespecified inclusion and exclusion criteria, 1,023 eligible cases/images were included. Samples were allocated to four purpose-built evaluation datasets according to clinical and image-quality characteristics: Dataset I for model benchmarking and exemplar-strategy optimization, Dataset II for rare malignant tumors, Dataset III for borderline tumors with microinvasion or microinvasive carcinoma, and Dataset IV for low-quality gross images. The model analyses were performed on gross specimen images and pathologic labels; patient-level demographic variables, including age, race, ancestry/ethnicity, sex, and gender, were not used as model inputs. The association of sex or gender with model performance was not evaluated, which limits assessment of demographic influences on generalizability. Because this study used retrospective, de-identified clinical images and pathology data and posed minimal risk to participants, the requirement for written informed consent was waived by the Ethics Commission of the Second Hospital of Jilin University (approval number: 2025-156). All research procedures conformed to institutional ethical standards and the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Datasets and ground truth establishment

A total of 1,119 ovarian tumor gross examination images were retrospectively collected from the Second Hospital of Jilin University (December 2018 to December 2024). After applying inclusion and exclusion criteria (e.g., excluding metastatic tumors and cases receiving neoadjuvant therapy), a final dataset of 1,023 eligible cases/images was established. The definitive histopathological diagnosis for each specimen served as the ground truth. All tissue sampling followed standardized pathological principles to ensure diagnostic accuracy, with detailed protocols provided in supporting information methods S1. All diagnoses were validated according to the WHO 5th edition criteria.

To comprehensively evaluate performance across clinically relevant conditions and image-quality boundaries, we constructed four specialized datasets (Figure 2): Dataset I (n = 738) with well-dissected specimens for model benchmarking and prompt/exemplar strategy optimization; Dataset II (n = 21) focusing on rare malignant entities; Dataset III (n = 87) containing borderline tumors with microinvasion or microinvasive carcinoma; and Dataset IV (n = 177) comprising low-quality images with common acquisition artifacts (e.g., incompletely dissected specimens, fragmentation, defocus, and overexposure).

Cases were allocated to purpose-built evaluation cohorts according to clinical and image-quality characteristics, as described below. All diagnoses were validated according to WHO 5th edition criteria, and the definitive histopathological diagnosis served as the reference standard.

Case-level partitioning and data isolation. To minimize the risk of information leakage—an important concern for ICL and prompt refinement—all data splits were defined at the case level, such that all images from the same case were assigned to one split only. Dataset II–IV were reserved as held-out evaluation cohorts reflecting distinct clinical “stress-test” settings (rare tumors, microinvasion-related complexity, and degraded image quality). For Dataset I, cases were used to (i) construct exemplar libraries and (ii) evaluate performance, while maintaining case-level separation between any exemplar pools, any prompt-development validation set, and the final test cases. Exemplar images used during model testing were strictly separated from the test dataset and were never reused as test samples in any evaluation. Full details of cohort construction and data isolation protocols are provided in supporting information methods S5.

For performance evaluation, datasets were assessed using a protocol of six rounds of randomized sampling, with 30 cases tested per round (Figure 2), to reduce the influence of case selection variability and to support stable estimation of performance across strategies.

Method details

Study overview

This study was conducted and reported in accordance with the STARD-AI reporting guidelines. A schematic overview of the experimental framework is presented in Figure 1. The core objective was to evaluate and optimize in-context learning (ICL) exemplar strategies for the Qwen-2.5-Vision-Language-72B model (Qwen-VL) in zero-shot and few-shot settings for ovarian tumor gross image analysis, with an emphasis on producing consistent, auditable region-level outputs to support tissue sampling guidance. Image-level grading outputs were evaluated in parallel as a secondary summary measure of model behavior under different ICL strategies.

Image preprocessing

All gross images were preprocessed before model testing to reduce background noise and standardize inputs. K-Net, trained on same-institution gross specimen images acquired using devices from different manufacturers in our previous work, was used to isolate the tumor specimen and remove non-specimen background factors such as rulers, boards, trays, and other surrounding objects. The segmented specimen images were then uniformly resized to 1792 × 1792 pixels prior to inference. K-Net was used as a fixed specimen-isolation preprocessing step and was not optimized, compared, or evaluated as an experimental variable in the present study. This preprocessing step was intended to approximate the pathologist-centered grossing view by focusing the model on the specimen rather than on non-specimen background elements.

ICL exemplar strategies

We systematically investigated several exemplar selection strategies for few-shot ICL. As baselines, we evaluated a zero-shot approach (no exemplars) and a random sampling approach in which exemplar images were randomly selected. An alternative strategy based on k-Nearest Neighbors (kNN) feature similarity was also explored but did not outperform random sampling due to high feature similarity across different tumor classes (see supporting information methods S2, Figure S1; Table S1 for details).

To better align exemplar demonstrations with component-oriented reasoning in gross examination, we developed two structured exemplar optimization strategies:

ScEO. ScEO curated an exemplar library in which each image contained exclusively homogeneous pathological components (i.e., pure benign, pure borderline, or pure malignant). This design aimed to provide clear, unambiguous demonstrations of each class and reduce the mixed-component confusion that frequently occurs in heterogeneous gross specimens.

Histology-Guided Exemplar Optimization (HgEO). Building upon ScEO, HgEO introduced histological subtype specificity into exemplar selection. The library was refined to include distinct examples for serous, mucinous, endometrioid, and other tumor subtypes, with the goal of exposing the model to clinically meaningful subtype-associated variability in gross appearance while preserving the component-level structure of demonstrations.

The detailed constitution of the ScEO and HgEO exemplar libraries is provided in supporting information methods S3 and Figure S2.

Few-shot ICL implementation and prompt engineering

We utilized the Qwen-2.5-Vision-Language-72B model accessed via the Aliyun DashScope API (supporting information methods S4). Our few-shot implementation employed a cyclic demonstration design, in which k exemplar images (k = 4–8) preceded each test image in the ICL context. For each test query, exemplars were drawn exclusively from the designated exemplar pool according to the selection strategy (random, kNN, ScEO, or HgEO), and no test case was permitted to appear in any exemplar library.

Structured prompting. A structured prompting strategy was central to standardizing the model’s task interpretation and outputs. The prompt consisted of a system prompt, which defined the analytical role, behavioral rules, and a standardized JSON output format, and a user prompt, which provided task-specific instructions. The design included a hierarchical reporting convention (malignant > borderline > benign) to prioritize the highest-risk component when multiple components were described; this convention was used to support sampling prioritization and consistent reporting rather than as a claim of diagnostic safety. Full details of the prompt engineering process, including the complete prompt texts, are available in supporting information methods S5 and Tables S2, S3, S4.

Validation-only refinement and prompt freeze. Prompt refinement was performed on a separate validation set to prevent data leakage and to improve output consistency. After refinement, the final prompt set was “frozen” prior to evaluation on held-out test cohorts; no further edits were made during testing. Data isolation was enforced at the case level throughout prompt development, exemplar selection, and evaluation (supporting information methods S5).

Inference settings and sensitivity considerations. To ensure fair comparisons between strategies, inference settings were held constant across primary experiments. Because LVLM outputs can be sensitive to decoding parameters (e.g., temperature and top-k), these parameters were selected using the validation set and then fixed for the frozen evaluation; where applicable, validation-based sensitivity analyses are summarized in the supporting information methods.

Implementation details, including code snippets and configuration files, are available in the official GitHub repository: https://github.com/ihddcctt/Gross_ICL_QwenVL.

Evaluation metrics

Model performance was evaluated with an emphasis on clinically interpretable endpoints relevant to sampling guidance, alongside secondary grading metrics.

Overall image-level classification. An image’s final classification was determined by the highest-grade component identified by the model (malignant > borderline > benign). This metric summarizes global grading behavior under each strategy.

Regional classification accuracy. Regional accuracy was calculated for predicted regions that encompassed a single, homogeneous ground-truth component, enabling assessment of component recognition under reduced within-region mixing.

Sampling utility (region-level coverage). For cohorts in which microscopic foci may not be grossly visible (e.g., borderline tumors with microinvasion/microinvasive carcinoma), model outputs were additionally evaluated by sampling utility, defined as whether the model-identified regions of interest encompassed pathologist-defined lesion areas used for sampling. This endpoint was used to quantify region-level risk-flagging aligned with sampling practice.

Qualitative evaluation by pathologists. Five pathologists used a 5-point Likert scale to score the quality of the model’s regional demarcations and the clinical relevance of its feature descriptions in the reasoning process. This qualitative assessment was designed to evaluate interpretability and usability of the model’s sampling suggestions.

Low-quality image evaluation

No abstention threshold was applied in Dataset IV. This design was intentional because Dataset IV was constructed to evaluate model behavior under low-quality acquisition conditions, including defocus, overexposure, fragmentation, and incomplete dissection. The purpose was to characterize performance boundaries and failure modes rather than to exclude difficult images from evaluation.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Analyses were performed at the case level. We report point estimates with 95% confidence intervals for primary image-level and region-level metrics where applicable. Descriptive comparisons were made across prompting and exemplar strategies under fixed inference settings. Pathologist Likert-scale ratings are summarized as mean (SD) and/or median (IQR), as appropriate.

Pathologist Likert-scale ratings are summarized as median (IQR) and compared using nonparametric paired tests (Wilcoxon signed-rank). All tests were two-sided with p < 0.05.

Unless otherwise specified, n refers to the number of cases or images evaluated. Confidence intervals and descriptive statistics are reported in the results and figure legends where applicable. No formal statistical comparison was performed for the small supplementary cross-model evaluation; these results were interpreted qualitatively.
