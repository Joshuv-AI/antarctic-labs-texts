Redefining the High-Output Stoma and Its Bearing on Clinical Practice Results of an International Consensus Panel
Authors: Michelle Carr, Freeke Caers, Dee Waugh, Paris Purnell, Mikel Gray
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
High-output stomas are a challenge for ostomy care nurses, occurring in 16–50% of small bowel ostomy and causing 20–65% of ostomy-related readmissions. Causes are varied and often multifactorial. Such complexity requires a multidisciplinary approach. Current definitions regarding the high-output ostomy focus on output volume, but this can be arbitrary and may not be reflective of the full clinical scenario. An international clinical expert forum (ICEF) developed 12 consensus statements to redefine the high-output stoma to help improve patient management, support peristomal skin health, and emphasize a more holistic approach. These statements recognize the complexity of patient management, advocating a multidisciplinary approach to management as well as more patient-centric care.

INTRODUCTION

One of the more challenging clinical scenarios a WOC nurse may encounter is that of the stoma patient experiencing high output. High-output stomas (ostomies) occurrences range from 16% to 50% of small bowel stomas1,2 and represent 20–65%3 of the total rate of ileostomy-related readmissions for dehydration and/or acute kidney injury (AKI).3,4 The etiology of high-output ostomies is diverse, ranging from infections, pharmacological agent additions or cessations, obstructive issues, inflammatory bowel disease, short bowel (short gut) syndrome, and, in some cases, no identifiable cause.2,3,5 The complexities and multifactorial nature of the high-output ostomy mean that patients face unique challenges that require a multidisciplinary and customized approach.1,6

Current definitions for high-output ostomies frequently hinge on the volume of the output within a defined period. The challenge with volume as the primary defining measurement for high output is that it is arbitrary and self-limiting. Volume is certainly a consideration when performing patient assessment but may be only one piece of a more complex scenario, and, as such, may be more representative of a series of factors contributing to a larger clinical picture.

There is a clear need for a revised definition of the high-output stoma that encapsulates the broader clinical picture, considering not just volume but also the patient’s overall health status and the interplay of various contributing factors. To address this, an international clinical expert forum (ICEF) of ostomy care nurses convened to discuss key aspects of the high-output stoma and to identify areas of agreement through consensus statement development. The panel generated 12 statements focusing on redefining the high-output stoma, its general management, and ostomy management for supporting peristomal skin health. The goal of this article is to describe a more holistic definition that has greater patient-centricity and provide guidance for care.

METHODS

Using structured processes as outlined by Murphy and colleagues,7 11 nurse panelists from 10 countries were convened to review, discuss, and vote on a group of proposed consensus statements designed to provide a basis for clinical decision making when managing the patient with a high-output ostomy. The group was led by a facilitator with expertise in group moderation for purposes of building consensus (M.G.). Panelists were selected from a broad range of practice settings (Box 1), including private practice, community, and acute care; all worked with adult patients and had familiarity with patients with high-output stomas and their management. Clinician experience ranged from 10 to 35 years, with a median of 21 years.

A consensus process was chosen for this topic based on the high variability around the definition of the high-output ostomy with most definitions referring to inconsistent measurements and timeframes that may not adequately reflect the condition or its management. The consensus process provides a formalized process for constructing statements that integrate clinical experience from a geographically and professionally diverse group of individuals with expertise in ostomy care.7 Participants were selected based on their clinical expertise, practice settings, and countries of origin. Panel members practice in the United States, Canada, France, the Netherlands, the United Kingdom, Italy, Germany, Australia, South Africa, and Belgium. The meeting was conducted in English and held in Europe (Ireland) in April 2024.

Prior to the consensus meeting, a core group of 3 ICEF members and 1 Hollister Associate (also a clinician – P.P.) met on several occasions to develop draft statements based on literature citations and personal experiences in managing the patient with a high-output stoma. Once crafted, all consensus panelists received relevant current literature regarding high-output ostomies for pre-reading in preparation for the consensus meeting. The literature summary was generated from a scoping review of articles conducted by the research and advisory firm, NERAC Inc., based in Connecticut, USA.

All articles published within a 40-year period prior to the meeting date and written in the English language were included in the initial literature search. All results were then filtered to within the last 10 years only (2013–2023) as robust evidence can be achieved by restricting to a tighter timeframe.8 While some published sources contend 3-year9 timeframes for literature reviews as appropriate, these seem more applicable to faster-paced fields, for example nanotechnology or artificial intelligence.10 A rule of thumb for arts, humanities, and literature is that of 5 years.10 Additionally, other published sources advocate for all dates regardless of time to be included regardless;9 however, the panel members concluded that the 10-year time frame would be appropriate for this topic as there would be a balance of older and newer sources to suitably reference. Search terms (Table 1) included US and UK variations in English, for example, “hyponatraemia” and “hyponatremia” as well as pluralities, for example, “ostomy” and “ostomies.” A final total of 55 search terms were used. All article types were included in the review such as articles reporting original research, review articles (systematic, scoping, and integrative), and best practice guidelines. Grey literature such as poster presentations and single case study reports were excluded. One crucial consideration that was initially proposed and created significant discussion within the entire ICEF panel was the introduction of the concept of the high-output stoma not being a diagnosis within itself, but part of a larger condition. From this ideation, the word “syndrome” evolved. A syndrome is defined as a “collection of signs, symptoms, series of events, and/or characteristics that occur together and indicate a particular medical condition, disorder, or disease.”11,12 Considering the phenomenon of a high-volume ostomy output and related clinical features together as a syndrome provides a framework by which this complex condition can be recognized and treated. It acknowledges that high-volume ostomy output is one element of a clinical condition that is often accompanied by a predictable set of signs and symptoms. Once debated within the broader group, “syndrome” gained quick adoption. It also provided a catalyst for the core group to develop draft statements, including a new overarching definition of high output.

Lastly, and possibly of greater import, the concept of this condition being a syndrome prompts a more holistic treatment approach as it considers more the person versus their ostomy output. Considering the range of clinical features contributing to the high-output stoma syndrome, rather than a volume-based definition, allows the development of protocols to standardize and guide care. This framework also acknowledges the diversity among people with ostomies, allows for a variety of causalities, while also anticipating clinical outcomes.

CONSENSUS STATEMENTS

Prior to the consensus panel meeting, the ICEF core team members considered the varying categories of high-output stomas and associated management challenges. After a lengthy discussion, it was determined that the consensus statement development would be limited to the more frequently encountered high-output stoma patients only. Inclusion of these challenges could create “scope creep,” and this focused approach would help avoid creating statements that were overly broad and not concise. Additionally, specific management protocols are in place for some of these management challenges, for example, short-gut syndrome. The entire ICEF panel discussed these concerns prior to consensus statement voting, and all concurred with this reasoning.

Based on these concerns, statement development did not include (1) fistula management, (2) urostomies, (3) length of bowel (short bowel/short gut syndrome related to intestinal length), (4) nutritional management, (5) fluid management, (6) surgical management, and (7) pharmacological management. This facilitated a targeted approach to the more frequently encountered post-operative high-output digestive (fecal) ostomy and the associated nursing management. Short bowel/ short gut syndrome was deliberately not addressed as the core panel agreed that this cohort had specific teams and management methodologies, as there was a known causation and expected sequence of events following stoma formation.

Originally, 16 draft statements were prepared by the core team for discussion. In the development of these consensus statements, panelists engaged in this structured process to reach agreement. Typically, this involves up to 3 rounds of anonymous voting for each statement, where each statement must achieve at least 80% agreement among the panelists. If a statement fails to meet this threshold after the third round, it was discarded. All statements are viewed alone and not as a collective. Once agreement is reached, statements cannot be altered irrespective of ensuing statements. This method ensures that only statements with strong support and reliability are included, promoting rigorous, incredible outcomes. The panelists reached consensus on 12 statements. (Table 2) Four either did not reach consensus during voting or were removed prior to voting based on the 12 agreed-upon statements. One additional statement was crafted during the meeting, but this was also later discarded. DISCUSSION

Definition Statements

In the literature, defining the high-output stoma consistently remains elusive. After discussion of the various definitions used in the literature, the panel decided on a focus that was more patient-centric was required to assist in better defining the condition, which also entailed the inclusion of “syndrome” to better reflect its complexity. It is crucial for clinicians, caregivers, and patients to understand the collective signs and symptoms to accurately diagnose high-output stoma syndrome. The first statement is the new overarching definition. High-output Stoma Syndrome Occurs When the Stomal Output Exceeds the Body’s Ability to Maintain Normal Homeostasis from Hydration, Metabolic, and Nutritional Standpoints.

High-output Stoma Syndrome Occurs When the Stomal Output Exceeds the Body’s Ability to Maintain Normal Homeostasis from Hydration, Metabolic, and Nutritional Standpoints.

Globally, the terms “stoma” and “ostomy” are often used interchangeably. However, some panelists preferred using “ostomy” in the statements while others preferred “stoma.” While there was some disagreement and discussion regarding the choice of term, the decision to use “stoma” was adopted. Rationale for this final decision was twofold: “stoma” is used more frequently around the world and the ensuing acronym “HOSS” (High-Output Stoma Syndrome) is easily pronounced (compared with “HOOS”) and could be readily understood worldwide once explained. This can significantly influence the impact and usage of the acronym.

The concept of “syndrome” encompasses various factors such as hydration, metabolism, and nutrition, making it a crucial part of this statement. Rather than traditionally focusing on one factor (e.g., volume), describing high-output stoma as a syndrome highlights its multifactorial nature, which can greatly affect clinical treatment and patient outcomes.

Normal homeostasis is already well defined and manageable within acceptable values and physical outcomes. The spread of values within which such fluctuations are considered insignificant is called the normal range. Any values that deviate from the normal range indicate the risks associated with HOSS. Clinically, understanding what is normal or not through such subjective measurements means that both patients and people who care for these patients can recognize when there is a potential risk of HOSS. Causes and Consequences for HOSS

High-output Stoma Syndrome Cannot Be Defined by Volume of Ostomy Output Alone.

Although there are many causes of HOSS, volume alone should not define its presence, as no single, agreed-upon volume is consistently found in the literature. Some sources cite >1000 ml per day,15 others > 1500 ml per day,4 and others >2000 ml per day.16 Additionally, some sources apply differing time frames with volume as the sole measurement3 or once metabolic challenges are identified with a defined volume within a specified timeframe.1,13 The consensus group acknowledged that volume will always be part of the discussion on HOSS. However, they emphasized that no specific number is definitive, and volume should be seen more as a symptom rather than an empirical value.

Volume is a relative term because the threshold at which a person with HOSS becomes symptomatic can vary greatly between individuals, as described previously. This does not mean that volume is unimportant; on the contrary, it is a crucial indicator of potential HOSS and a physical imbalance. When a person with HOSS shows abnormalities in homeostasis values, such as loss of appetite, weight loss, constant thirst, and sleep disturbances, measuring ostomy output volume can provide critical information and helps in identifying causes or symptoms of HOSS and in developing a patient-centric customized management plan. High-output Stoma Syndrome Can Be Defined as Acute or Chronic.

High-output Stoma Syndrome Can Be Defined as Acute or Chronic.

While the ICEF agreed on this statement no time periods were ascribed to acute or chronic scenarios. Based on literature review, panelists acknowledged that acute (also referred to as short term/early) HOSS is defined as less than three weeks in duration and chronic (late) HOSS persists for three weeks or more.2,5 High-output Stoma Syndrome Can Occur at Any Time after Ostomy Formation.

High-output Stoma Syndrome Can Occur at Any Time after Ostomy Formation.

People with an ostomy should be informed that HOSS can occur at any time post-surgery. They should monitor changes in the frequency of emptying the pouch and consistency of the stool, as well as be aware of the signs and symptoms of HOSS. General Management Statements

The panel explored general management strategies that are necessary to guide the recognition of HOSS and required interventions. Because HOSS may occur at any time and in a variety of settings, it is essential that clinicians and people with stomas and their caregivers be able to recognize and respond to the signs and symptoms of HOSS. “Caregivers” in this instance is the term used by the ICEF to include individuals providing a broad array of health-related services for an individual. Caregivers may be health professionals, family members, friends, social workers, or members of the clergy. They may give care at home, in a hospital, or in other health care settings.17 It was acknowledged that there are a multitude of readily available protocols to treat high-output ostomies, but that any protocol used should have multidisciplinary input, and must be endorsed by the local health authority. People with Stomas or Their Caregivers Should Recognize and Respond to the Signs and Symptoms of High-output Stoma Syndrome.

People with Stomas or Their Caregivers Should Recognize and Respond to the Signs and Symptoms of High-output Stoma Syndrome.

The panel agreed that providing people with ostomies and their caregivers with relevant usable information to recognize and respond to the signs and symptoms of HOSS is essential to optimize health outcomes. People with ostomies and their caregivers should be involved in discussions and decisions about the recognition, prevention, and management of HOSS. This is likely to increase the effectiveness of care plans and protocols, as people with ostomies and their caregivers can provide information about factors that are specific to that individual. It also allows people with ostomies and their caregivers to participate in implementing prevention plans, which may assist in reducing the incidence and severity of HOSS complications.

Including family and caregivers in discussions and education recognizes the value of all caregivers in contributing to the recognition and management of HOSS. When health professionals, people with ostomies, families, and caregivers work in partnership, the quality and safety of health care improve.18 Early Detection and Intervention are Necessary to Minimize the Complications of High-output Stoma Syndrome.

Early Detection and Intervention are Necessary to Minimize the Complications of High-output Stoma Syndrome.

The panel agreed that prompt detection and intervention are necessary to manage the complications of HOSS. Early recognition of signs and symptoms allows for timely, effective, and appropriate interventions, potentially reducing the extent and duration of complications. People with High-output Stoma Syndrome Must Be Managed Using a Protocol with a Multidisciplinary Approach.

People with High-output Stoma Syndrome Must Be Managed Using a Protocol with a Multidisciplinary Approach.

Panelists agreed that the use of an endorsed protocol is vital to guide the safe and effective management of people with HOSS. Multiple members of the ICEF stressed the requirement that any protocol used must be endorsed by the relevant local health service and should be developed with input from relevant disciplines to ensure a multidisciplinary approach.

Health protocols are guidelines developed to describe an agreed set of actions to manage a specific health condition.19 Their aim is to reduce unacceptable variations in care and to reduce the risk of complications by integrating the strategies and pathways agreed by the multidisciplinary team. These evidence-based protocols are developed in consultation with relevant specialists, including doctors, dietitians, pharmacists, and stomal therapy nurses, and aim to promote individualized care while standardizing and guiding management. Panel members acknowledge the potential complexity of managing HOSS, and that people with HOSS may be under the care of various clinicians who may have little experience with managing HOSS and cared for in a variety of environments, e.g., general hospital wards, community, or other care facilities.

Literature review revealed a plethora of high-output ostomy management protocols. Figure 1 is one such example. Ostomy Management and Skin Health Statements

The role of the ostomy nurse specialist nurse is multifaceted when managing a patient with HOSS. There are a significant number of articles addressing the physiological management of the patient with HOSS but very little specifically written about clinical ostomy management and skin health. The panel discussed several statements pertaining to ostomy management and skin health, and consensus was finally reached on 4 statements. Consider a Pouching System with High-output Features to Manage the Ostomy Effluent for People with High-output Stoma Syndrome.

Consider a Pouching System with High-output Features to Manage the Ostomy Effluent for People with High-output Stoma Syndrome.

A high-output pouching system should incorporate a protective skin barrier and a pouch that has greater volume capacity. The pouch that has a large bore, soft outlet provides two functions, it: (1) it allows for ease of emptying liquid/loose/thicker stool or enteric content and (2) allows for connection to a bedside drainage system such as a urinary bedside drainage bag or a high-output flow system that has a large bore diameter (Figure 2). In countries or facilities where high-output systems are not available, regular drainage pouches can be modified to create a high-output system (Figures 3 and 4). Panelists agreed that the maintenance of the peristomal skin in patients with HOSS was more challenging due to the high volumes of the output and the corrosive nature of the enteric content. The skin barrier is the adhesive component of the pouching system which adheres to the skin, and it plays an integral role in ensuring peristomal skin health. Skin barriers are sometimes referred to as the “base plate,” “flange,” or “wafers,” depending on geographic region. The skin barrier of the pouching system plays an integral role in ensuring peristomal skin health and should withstand the volume and eroding nature of the output so as not to disintegrate and place peristomal skin at risk. Convex Pouching Systems Are Recommended to Prevent Leakage and Maintain Peristomal Skin Health in People with High-output Stoma Syndrome.

Convex Pouching Systems Are Recommended to Prevent Leakage and Maintain Peristomal Skin Health in People with High-output Stoma Syndrome.

The consensus statement “Liquid output can be an indicator for convexity to prevent or manage leakage” was recommended in an article published in JWOCN in 2017.20 The panel agreed that this statement was relevant when talking about and managing patients with HOSS.

The skin barrier seal may be at greater risk as the pouch fills, resulting in undermining and leakage of effluent. The panel discussed at length the use of a stoma belt to augment the efficacy of the convex barrier and provide additional security to the pouching system. Using a belt augments the efficacy of a convex skin barrier.21 Skin Barriers Such as Rings/Seals/Pastes May Be Required to Prevent Leakage and Maintain Peristomal Skin Health for People with High-Output Stoma Syndrome.

Skin Barriers Such as Rings/Seals/Pastes May Be Required to Prevent Leakage and Maintain Peristomal Skin Health for People with High-Output Stoma Syndrome.

Panelists agreed that the combination of complex stomas and HOSS is difficult to manage. One of the objectives of ostomy nurse specialists is to ensure that patients are prescribed a pouching system that provides a secure seal, is leak- and odor-proof, and maintains peristomal skin health. The use of accessories or support products such as seals/rings, pastes, powder, protective sheets, gel thickening sachets, and belts can enhance the efficacy of the pouch skin barrier to achieve this objective.

STATEMENTS NOT ACHIEVING CONSENSUS

Four statements either did not reach consensus or were removed prior to voting based on group discussion. Most of these statements were developed to address the routine checking of skin barrier performance, peristomal skin care, and pouching modifications as required. These were considered both overly broad and general standards of care covered in most other clinical management guidelines. One additional statement concerning the use of ostomy belts to enhance convexity for a more secure skin seal was discussed and crafted by panelists during the meeting but then removed after the first round of voting. Again, as per the previous 4 statements, this was considered a general principle of ostomy care that was not unique to the management of patients with HOSS.

CONCLUSIONS

The high-output stoma is part of a complex syndrome and presents a significant challenge for WOC nurses due to the diverse etiology and substantial impact on patient health. The current volume-based definitions are often arbitrary and fail to account for the broader clinical picture, including patient-specific factors such as age, weight, and overall health status. While volume is a critical indicator and measurement for the syndrome, it is not a standalone descriptor for the high-output ostomy. The 12 consensus statements emphasize the importance of a holistic, multidisciplinary approach that goes beyond mere volume measurements to consider the full spectrum of factors affecting patients with HOSS. Creating more patient-centric guidelines will aim to improve the management of HOSS, support peristomal skin health, and enhance the overall quality of care. As the field continues to evolve, the insights and recommendations from the ICEF will be instrumental in guiding clinical practice and improving outcomes for patients suffering from HOSS.BOX 1.
