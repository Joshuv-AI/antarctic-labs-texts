Challenges and Barriers in Implementation of National Tuberculosis Elimination Program Guidelines at Nutrition Rehabilitation Centers in India: A Systematic Review
Authors: Praveen Kumar Anand, Devanshi Patel, Ghisaram Dewasi, Janesh Kumar Gautam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTuberculosis (TB) remains a significant public health challenge in India. Malnutrition and TB form a vicious cycle, where malnutrition predisposes a child to acquire TB, while TB could exacerbate undernutrition. In India, Nutritional Rehabilitation Centers (NRCs) provide therapeutic nutrition and medical care to address malnutrition in children. The National Tuberculosis Elimination Program (NTEP) was integrated into India’s NRCs in 2012 for early detection of TB among malnourished children; however, its implementation remains suboptimal. The present study aims to identify the challenges and barriers in implementing NTEP guidelines at NRCs in India through systematic review of literature.MethodsThe systematic review was conducted across six databases including PubMed, Scopus, ScienceDirect, WHO Global Index Medicus, Semantic Scholar, and Google Scholar for studies published between 2010 and 2024. The systematic review protocol has been registered in the PROSPERO web portal with Registration ID No. CRD42025644172. The quality of individual studies included in the evidence synthesis was assessed using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT) - Version 2018. A narrative synthesis approach, following the SWiM (Synthesis Without Meta analysis) guidelines, was employed for data analysis.ResultsOut of 1,374 records identified through database searches, four studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final synthesis. Key challenges and barriers reported in implementation of TB screening and diagnosis were (1) underutilization of diagnostics, (2) human resource constraints, (3) sample collection issues, (4) treatment gaps, and (5) infrastructural limitations. Addressing these barriers with context-specific innovative strategies is essential to improve TB screening and diagnosis among malnourished children admitted in NRCs.ConclusionThis systematic review highlights that while NRCs play a crucial role in managing SAM, the underutilization of diagnostic tools, limitations in human resources, difficulties in sample collection, and infrastructural and logistical barriers have resulted in the potential underdiagnosis of TB in this vulnerable population. This systematic review provides valuable insights into the issues that must be addressed to improve TB diagnosis among SAM children admitted at NRCs in India.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s44197-026-00538-7.Keywords: Tuberculosis (TB), Nutritional Rehabilitation Centers (NRCs), National Tuberculosis Elimination Program (NTEP), Malnutrition, India

Introduction

TB remains a significant public health challenge in India, accounting for approximately 26% of the global burden [1]. In 2023, there were an estimated 10.8 million new TB cases globally, with an incidence of 134 per 100,000 population. TB caused approximately 1.25 million deaths in 2023, making it the leading cause of death from a single infectious agent globally [1]. In 2023, children and adolescents under 15 years old accounted for about 12% of the total TB cases worldwide, equating to around 1.25 million affected children [2]. Moreover, approximately 200,000 children with TB lost their lives, with most being under the age of five [3]. Despite representing a smaller share of TB cases, children and young adolescents account for 16% of global TB deaths, highlighting a disproportionate impact and the need for improved prevention and treatment efforts in this vulnerable group [3].

Malnutrition is a significant global health issue, particularly affecting children under five years of age. It is associated with increased morbidity and mortality [4]. Severe Acute Malnutrition (SAM) is the most critical form of childhood malnutrition [4]. In India, 19.7% of children under five are wasted, reflecting SAM [5]. Malnutrition and TB form a vicious cycle, where malnutrition predisposes a child to acquire TB, while TB could exacerbate undernutrition [6]. In countries with a high TB burden, 2–24% of acutely malnourished children have been diagnosed with TB [6]. Children with SAM and TB are 40% more likely to die than SAM children without TB [6]. The Government of India (GoI) has set an ambitious target to eliminate TB by 2025, which is five years ahead of the global target established by the WHO [7]. This goal is part of India’s National TB Elimination Program (NTEP), which focuses on innovative strategies for the detection, treatment, and prevention of TB across all age groups, including children [7].

In India, Nutritional Rehabilitation Centers (NRCs), established under the National Health Mission (NHM), provide therapeutic nutrition and medical care to address SAM in children [8]. NRCs serve as facility-based units that admit children diagnosed with SAM, offering nutritional rehabilitation, medical treatment, and counselling for caregivers [4]. Admission to NRCs is based on criteria such as weight-for-height Z-score (WHZ) below − 3 standard deviations, presence of bilateral pitting edema, or severe visible wasting [4]. Mild and moderate malnutrition cases are typically managed at the community level through Anganwadi Centers (AWC) under the Integrated Child Development Services (ICDS) scheme [4]. However, children diagnosed with SAM require facility-based management at NRCs, where they receive therapeutic diets (such as F75 and F-100 formulas), medical treatment, and continuous monitoring until stabilization [4]. In some cases, especially where children have difficulty consuming food orally, Ryle’s tube feeding is used to provide essential nutrients, which involves inserting a nasogastric tube to deliver nutrition directly into the stomach, ensuring that the child receives necessary nutrients even if they cannot eat by mouth [9]. Post-discharge, these children are followed up at Anganwadi Centers to ensure sustained nutritional recovery and prevent relapse of SAM [4]. The referral system for SAM children plays a crucial role in linking community-based services with facility-based care. Children with SAM are generally referred to NRCs either from Anganwadi Centers or hospitals [10].

Recognizing the intricate link between SAM and TB, the GoI has emphasized the integration of NTEP guidelines for pediatric TB within NRCs [11]. As per these guidelines, all children with.

SAM admitted in NRCs are require evaluation for TB as per diagnostic algorithm [11]. The diagnostic algorithm outlines a systematic approach for identifying TB in children. A key update in the guideline is the recommendation of a frontal chest skiagram (X-ray) as the initial specific investigation [11]. Following the chest X-ray, microbiological confirmation is conducted using advanced molecular diagnostic tests like CBNAAT (Cartridge-Based Nucleic Acid Amplification Test) [6]. This integration aims to ensure early screening, diagnosis, and management of TB in SAM children [6]. The first integration of a diagnostic algorithm for TB at NRCs in India occurred in 2012 [6]. More than a decade has passed, making it essential to evaluate the implementation status of this integrated approach, the challenges faced, and the progress made in providing comprehensive care for children affected by both SAM and TB.

Although existing literature explores various aspects of TB, there is a significant gap in research focused on integrating NTEP guidelines for pediatric TB within NRCs. A previous study has reported that the implementation of NTEP guidelines at NRC is suboptimal [12]. Hence, this systematic review is both timely and necessary. By systematically reviewing the available literature, this review aims to examine the implementation-related barriers and challenges affecting the screening, diagnostic evaluation, and treatment linkage for pulmonary tuberculosis among children with SAM admitted to NRCs in India. Thereby informing policymakers and healthcare practitioners about critical areas requiring intervention. In doing so, it addresses a significant gap in the current literature and emphasizes the need for tailored, context-specific strategies to enhance TB detection and management among SAM children in high-burden settings.

Methods

Protocol and Registration

This systematic review is designed to assess the challenges and barriers in the implementation of the NTEP guidelines at NRCs across India. The study protocol has been developed as per the guidelines outlined in the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta- Analyses (PRISMA) for systematic review protocols (PRISMA-P) [13]. PRISMA-P is the extension of the PRISMA framework and is designed to improve transparency and rigor in systematic reviews [13]. It includes 17 items covering the rationale, objectives, eligibility criteria, search strategy, data extraction, and risk of bias assessment. The systematic review protocol has been registered in the PROSPERO web portal [14]. The Registration ID No. is CRD42025644172.

Information Source and Search Strategy

A systematic search was conducted on PubMed, Scopus, Science Direct, WHO Global Index Medicus, Google Scholar, and Semantic Scholar by following PRISMA-S(Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Literature Search Extension) guidelines [15].The search strategy used a combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text keywords related to tuberculosis, severe acute malnutrition, and Nutrition Rehabilitation Centers. Key search terms included combinations of “tuberculosis,” “pulmonary tuberculosis,” “severe acute malnutrition,” “SAM,” “nutrition rehabilitation center,” “NRC,” and “India” along with terms related to screening, diagnosis, and management. Boolean operators (AND/OR) were used to combine search terms appropriately across databases. The full search strategy is provided in Supplementary Table 1. This literature search focused on articles published between 2010 and 2024, as the first integration of a diagnostic algorithm for TB at NRCs in India occurred in 2012. To ensure both accuracy and reliability, the search was conducted by two independent reviewers.

Selection Criteria

The inclusion criteria were (a) Studies which reported on tuberculosis screening, diagnostic evaluation, or management among children with severe acute malnutrition admitted to Nutrition Rehabilitation Centers in India. (c) studies evaluating the implementation of NTEP guidelines, (d) studies published in English, (e) studies published between 2010 and 2024. (f) original research articles (e.g., retrospective secondary data analysis, cross-sectional studies, cohort studies, case-control studies, qualitative research, case studies, and case series analysis).

The exclusion criteria were (a) studies focusing on adults and/or children above 6 years age with TB, (b) studies that do not include NRCs in their context, (c) studies unrelated to the NTEP guidelines, (d) studies that do not address implementation challenges or barriers, (e)studies conducted in hospitals or clinics that do not include NRCs, (f) studies conducted outside India or with no specific focus on the Indian healthcare system, (g) review articles.

Study Selection

Available literature on targeted research question was searched as per the search extension of PRISMA checklist [14]. All the identified research articles from the selected databases were imported into Zotero software, and duplicates were removed [16]. Once the duplicate entries were removed, articles were uploaded into Rayyan, a tool for the title and abstract screening process [17]. Title and abstract screening were done independently by two reviewers following the inclusion and exclusion criteria of the study. This was followed by a full-text screening by two independent reviewers to ensure that the research question was being addressed and that none of the previously determined exclusion criteria were present in the article. Studies having data or findings in line with the research question of this systematic review were selected for further assessment. Any discrepancies between the two authors were resolved by mutual discussions among all the reviewers by majority. Only peer-reviewed empirical studies were included to ensure that the evidence base could be systematically appraised using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT). Grey literature such as program reports, theses, and policy documents was excluded. While this approach ensured methodological consistency among included studies, it may have limited the inclusion of certain operational insights, which is acknowledged as a limitation of this review.

Data Extraction

The data extraction table was drafted collaboratively by all the reviewers. The table was refined through discussions to enhance its clarity and consistency and ensure its alignment with the study objectives. Data extraction was then carried out independently by two reviewers from the full-text research articles selected for evidence synthesis. The extracted data included key study details such as the author’s name, study design, sample size, geographic location (state) and key findings in terms of challenges and barriers in implementing NTEP guidelines at NRCs. Any discrepancies between the two reviewers were resolved through discussions among all reviewers to ensure accuracy and consistency in data extraction and evidence synthesis.

Study Quality Assessment

The quality of individual studies included in the evidence synthesis was assessed using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT) - Version 2018 [18]. The MMAT is a standardized critical appraisal tool designed to evaluate the methodological quality and potential biases of quantitative, qualitative, and mixed-methods studies. In cases where there was ambiguity or disagreement about the quality of study, reviewers engaged in discussions to resolve discrepancies and reach a mutual consensus. This collaborative approach ensured consistency and minimized subjective bias in the appraisal process. The systematic use of the MMAT allowed us to consider any identified biases carefully during the synthesis, enabling us to draw balanced conclusions that recognize both the strengths and limitations of the available data.

Evidence Synthesis

In this review, a narrative synthesis approach, following the SWiM (Synthesis Without Meta-analysis) guidelines [19], was employed to integrate both qualitative and quantitative findings into a cohesive analysis. Initially, data from the included studies were extracted into standardized tables to ensure consistency in capturing key details. For qualitative data, thematic analysis was conducted iteratively. Themes were generated by systematically reviewing the findings, allowing for the identification of recurrent patterns and contextual insights. For quantitative data, summary statistics were extracted. This helped in identifying trends and variations across studies. To guide the analysis, the review adopts an implementation perspective to understand factors influencing the application of NTEP guidelines within NRC settings. Barriers identified in the included studies were organized using domains derived from implementation science frameworks, including structural and health system factors, workforce-related factors, logistical and operational constraints, and policy or programmatic influences. This framework allowed the findings to be mapped across key stages of the pediatric tuberculosis care pathway—screening, diagnostic evaluation, treatment initiation, and follow-up—thereby providing a structured understanding of how implementation challenges affect tuberculosis detection among children with severe acute malnutrition in NRCs. Identified barriers were coded thematically and grouped according to implementation domains (structural, workforce, logistical, and programmatic factors) to facilitate structured synthesis.

The integration of both qualitative and quantitative evidence was achieved through a narrative synthesis, wherein themes emerging from qualitative data were used to interpret and contextualize the numerical findings. This method allowed for a comprehensive interpretation of the data, facilitating the identification of overarching themes.

Results

Search, Screening, and Study Selection

A total of 1,374 studies were identified through database searches using the search string provided in Supplementary Table 1. However, none of these studies were review articles that specifically addressed the challenges and barriers in implementing NTEP guidelines within NRC. After removing 221 duplicates, 1153 articles were screened based on titles and abstracts as per eligibility criteria, leading to the exclusion of 1122 studies. Further, the full texts of the remaining 31 selected articles were assessed. It excluded 27 studies because these did not involve the challenges and barriers in the implementation of NTEP guidelines at NRCs. Thus, only 4 studies were finally considered for data extraction and evidence synthesis. The PRISMA flow diagram for the entire selection process is shown in Fig. 1.

Underutilization of Diagnostic Tests

The studies collectively highlight a significant gap in the utilization of diagnostic tests such as the CBNAAT and chest radiography for TB among SAM children admitted to NRCs, despite the recommendation of NTEP for universal screening of TB among all SAM children admitted at NRCs [12, 20–22]. Studies by M Singh et al. and Pathak et al. highlight that NRCs are not fully utilizing available molecular diagnostic tests, leading to the potential underdiagnosis of TB in these high-risk children [21, 22]. Bhat et al. identified multiple causes for underutilization of diagnostic tests, including lack of awareness among healthcare workers and difficulties in obtaining chest radiography [20]. Training deficiencies in conducting diagnosis procedures like gastric aspirate collection are also one of the reasons for underutilization of diagnostic services [22]. Unclear guidelines on steps to take when tests were unavailable further contributed to inconsistent adherence [12].

The challenges in the theme of the underutilization of diagnostic tools and tests can be categorized into three areas: (1) logistical issues, (2) workforce-related challenges, and (3) policy-related barriers. Logistical issues related challenges include problems with transporting samples for CBNAAT testing, which can delay or hinder timely diagnosis. Workforce-related challenges arise from a lack of awareness among healthcare workers about molecular diagnostic tests, as well as inadequate training in procedures such as gastric aspirate collection. In addition, policy-related barriers exacerbate these problems, with unclear guidelines on alternative steps when recommended tests are unavailable. This leads to inconsistencies in diagnosis and treatment [12, 20–22].

Human Resource Constraints and Training Gaps

Human resource constraints, including the shortage of qualified pediatricians and inadequately trained staff, are significant barriers. This is compounded by poor motivation and a lack of clarity in guidelines, leading to suboptimal diagnostic practices [12, 21].

Singh et al. reported that the guidelines for screening and diagnosis were unclear about the steps to take when specific tests or facilities were unavailable or when specimen transport was unreliable. As a result, the screening guidelines were not consistently followed across the NRCs [12]. Pathak et al. reported that only 43% of NRCs had qualified pediatricians and noted that pediatricians were not always available at the NRCs, especially in NRCs with visiting pediatricians on fixed days [21]. Singh et al. highlighted that peripheral health workers often had not received training or did not recall the training content, as reported by NRC staff in focus group discussions (FGDs) [12]. Also emphasized were poor team skills and lack of motivation among in-charge pediatricians due to poor understanding of guidelines, limited availability of diagnostic facilities, or test non-feasibility [12].

Challenges in Sample Collection and Diagnosis

Invasive diagnostic procedures are perceived as difficult by healthcare workers, leading to reluctance to perform them [20, 22]. M Singh et al. identified sputum collection as a major challenge in childhood TB diagnosis, with very few children having specimens collected [22]. Pathak et al. reported the inappropriate strength of purified protein derivative (PPD) used for TB diagnosis and non-adherence to cut-off values for induration in tuberculin skin tests (TST) [21]. Bhat et al. highlighted reluctance among NRC staff to perform invasive procedures like gastric aspirate collection, as they perceived them as difficult tasks [20].

Treatment and Follow-up Gaps

The studies highlight significant gaps in the treatment and follow-up of TB cases [12, 21]. Weak linkages between TB diagnosis and treatment services increase the risk of poor treatment adherence, leading to higher morbidity and mortality. Many children diagnosed with TB are lost to follow-up due to inefficient tracking mechanisms and limited coordination between NRCs and TB treatment programs. Additionally, insufficient post-diagnosis support, including monitoring for treatment adherence, further weakens the effectiveness of TB management [12, 21].

Infrastructure and Logistical Barriers

Infrastructure and logistical issues, such as unreliable power supply and limited availability of on-site diagnostic facilities, create significant barriers to effective TB diagnosis in NRCs [12, 20]. Bhat et al. reported difficulties in obtaining chest radiography among children due to irregular power supply, leading to many children not undergoing the procedure [20]. Singh et al. reported logistical challenges in transporting samples for CBNAAT testing [12].

Discussion

This systematic review aimed to identify the challenges and barriers in implementing the NTEP guidelines for pediatric TB diagnosis and management at NRCs in India. India was chosen as the study setting due to the high burden of TB and malnutrition [1, 5]. With the world’s highest TB burden and a significant proportion of children suffering from malnutrition, India presents a critical setting to examine the intersection of these two health challenges. Additionally, India’s NRC model offers an established framework for integrated nutritional and medical care, making it an ideal context for evaluating the implementation of TB screening and management within facility-based nutrition centers.

Facility-based nutrition centers operate in various countries to address malnutrition in children. In Bangladesh, Community-Based Management of Acute Malnutrition (CMAM) programs integrate inpatient and outpatient care for malnourished children [23]. Similarly, Ethiopia has established Therapeutic Feeding Units (TFUs) within hospitals to provide specialized care for severe malnutrition [24]. In African nations such as Nigeria, stabilization centers serve a crucial role in treating children with SAM, often in coordination with community-level interventions [25].

In India, the government has established NRCs to manage the SAM children at indoor facility [8]. These centers serve as critical facilities aimed at providing nutritional support, medical care, and rehabilitation for SAM children. The implementation of NTEP guidelines at NRCs was driven by the increased vulnerability to diseases in malnourished children [8]. However, challenges in implementing these guidelines persist. While studies have examined individual aspects of pediatric TB and malnutrition, no systematic review has specifically assessed the challenges and barriers in the implementation of NTEP guidelines at NRCs. The purpose of this review is to fill this gap by synthesizing existing evidence and identifying key obstacles.

This systematic review reveals that underutilization of diagnostic tools, human resource constraints, challenges in sample collection, treatment and follow-up gaps, and infrastructural and logistical limitations are the primary obstacles hindering effective TB screening in malnourished children at NRCs. The barriers identified in this review are consistent with those observed in other low-resource settings worldwide that address malnutrition in children, including Tanzania, the Philippines, Ghana, Bangladesh, Nigeria and Cambodia [23, 25–29].

Several issues have been documented in studies from Tanzania that align with those in India such as insufficient training among healthcare staff and reliance on conventional diagnostic methods contributing to delayed or missed diagnoses [29]. In India, a critical challenge is the limited utilization of CBNAAT and chest radiography despite NTEP’s recommendation for universal screening. Additionally, one study found that only 43% of NRCs had qualified pediatricians, a shortfall that negatively impacts timely diagnosis and treatment [21]. Similar healthcare worker shortages have been reported in Nigeria, where limited pediatric expertise and inadequate TB training led to delays in diagnosis and underdiagnosis of pediatric TB cases [25]. Another significant concern in India is the hesitation among healthcare workers to perform invasive diagnostic procedures, such as gastric aspirate collection and induced sputum collection [21, 22]. Studies in Nigeria have similarly reported reluctance due to perceived procedural difficulty, resulting in missed cases of pediatric TB [25]. Infrastructure-related and logistics challenges, such as lack of on-site radiography, unreliable power supply, and transportation difficulties for CBNAAT sample processing, further hinder effective TB diagnosis in India [20, 22]. These findings align with global studies highlighting logistical and infrastructural constraints as key barriers to implementing TB diagnostic programs in resource- limited settings [23, 25–27, 29].

Several factors may contribute to the persistence of implementation barriers identified in this review. Limited infrastructure and diagnostic resources within many Nutrition Rehabilitation Centers may restrict the routine use of recommended investigations such as chest radiography and molecular diagnostic tests. In addition, healthcare workers in NRC settings often manage multiple clinical priorities, which may limit the time and training available for specialized procedures such as pediatric tuberculosis sample collection. Addressing the identified implementation barriers may require improving the availability of diagnostic facilities such as chest radiography and molecular testing, strengthening training and capacity building among healthcare workers, and ensuring effective referral and follow-up mechanisms between NRCs and tuberculosis treatment centers. Strengthening coordination between NRC services and the National Tuberculosis Elimination Program (NTEP) could facilitate earlier detection and timely initiation of treatment among children with severe acute malnutrition.

Children with severe acute malnutrition represent a particularly vulnerable group with increased susceptibility to tuberculosis and higher risk of adverse outcomes. Implementation gaps within NRC settings may therefore disproportionately affect children from socioeconomically disadvantaged backgrounds who already face barriers to healthcare access. Strengthening tuberculosis screening and diagnostic pathways within NRCs is therefore not only a programmatic priority but also an important equity consideration within child health and tuberculosis control efforts in India.

Recommendations

Qualitative studies to generate deeper insights on challenges, barriers and facilitators in implementation of NTEP guidelines at NRCs to be undertaken in diverse geographical settings of India. Comprehensive training programs to be developed and implemented for healthcare workers, including medical officers and peripheral health staff. These programs should focus on pediatric tuberculosis screening, proper use of diagnostic tools (such as CBNAAT, chest radiography, and tuberculin skin testing), and techniques for sample collection. Invest in upgrading the infrastructure at NRCs to include on-site diagnostic tools, such as portable chest radiography units and CBNAAT/TruNat machines. Strengthen the logistics surrounding sample collection and transportation by establishing reliable systems and contingency plans (for instance, backup power supplies) to ensure that diagnostic procedures can be consistently performed, even in remote or under-resourced centers. Establish a more structured follow-up system, enhance communication between healthcare providers, and improve the integration of NRCs with tuberculosis treatment services to ensure that diagnosed children receive timely and complete treatment.

Strength and Limitations

This review follows a systematic and transparent methodology, synthesizing evidence from multiple sources to provide a comprehensive understanding of the challenges and barriers in NTEP guidelines implementation at NRCs. It identifies key gaps that policymakers and implementers must address to improve TB control in SAM children. Another strength is its focus on the intersection of TB and malnutrition, an area that remains underexplored in India despite the high burden of co-morbidities.

This systematic review acknowledges limitations that must be noticed while interpreting the findings. First, the review includes only four studies, which restricts the generalizability of the findings as they varied in design, geographic setting, and reported outcomes, which limited the possibility of quantitative synthesis. As a result, the findings should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than definitive conclusions about implementation barriers. Second, most of the studies included were conducted in northern and central India, resulting in limited representation from other regions. Variations in healthcare infrastructure, resource availability, and regional health policies might have influenced the challenges faced by NRCs in different states. Third, differences in study design and quality may have affected the findings. There was not even a single exclusive qualitative study addressing the challenges and facilitators on implementation of NTEP guidelines at NRCs to include in this systematic review. In addition, the exclusion of grey literature may have resulted in omission of some programmatic or operational reports describing implementation experiences within NRCs.

Conclusion

Evidence indicates that while NRCs play a crucial role in managing SAM, the underutilization of diagnostic tools, limitations in human resources, difficulties in sample collection, and infrastructural and logistical barriers have resulted in the potential underdiagnosis of TB in this vulnerable population. Despite limitations such as the scarcity of qualitative studies and the geographic constraints of available data, this systematic review provides valuable insights into the issues that must be addressed to improve TB diagnosis among SAM children admitted at NRCs in India. Overall, this review calls for a coordinated effort among policymakers, healthcare providers, and researchers to implement context-specific strategies. Such efforts could enhance the effectiveness of TB screening and management in NRCs, ultimately reducing the TB burden in high-risk pediatric populations.
