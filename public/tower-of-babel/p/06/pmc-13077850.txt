Epigenetic corepressor BCORL1 predominates as a driver of clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential in patients undergoing chronic hemodialysis: a multicenter cohort study
Authors: Kuan-Chieh Wang, Wei Wang, Chiung-Ying Huang, Szu-Ying Lee, Shih-I Chen, Cheng-Fong Tsai, Chien-Yuan Chen, Yi-Ting Chen, Yu-Hsiang Chou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundClonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP) is a premalignant condition characterized by somatic mutations in hematopoietic stem cells in the absence of hematologic malignancy. CHIP predominantly affects older adults and is associated with increased risks of hematologic malignancy, cardiovascular events, and all-cause mortality. However, data on the prevalence, mutational spectrum, and clinical implications of CHIP in Asian patients with end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) undergoing chronic hemodialysis remain limited. Moreover, whether specific CHIP-associated genes confer distinct clinical characteristics or prognostic implications in this population remains unclear. In this prospective study, peripheral blood samples were obtained for whole-exome sequencing to identify CHIP-associated mutations. Clonal cytopenia of undetermined significance (CCUS) was defined as the presence of CHIP in patients with persistent cytopenia without an identifiable cause. The primary outcome was all-cause mortality, while secondary outcomes included major adverse cardiovascular events and malignancy.Results61 patients with ESKD undergoing chronic hemodialysis were included and followed for a mean of 2.1 years. CHIP-associated mutations were detected in 50 patients (81.9%), who were classified as CHIP only (n = 8) or CCUS (n = 42), while 11 patients had no CHIP or CCUS. According to the Clonal Hematopoiesis Risk Score, patients with CHIP or CCUS were further stratified into low-, intermediate-, and high-risk groups. BCORL1 was the most frequently mutated gene (24.7%), followed by GNAS, BCOR, DNMT3A, ASXL1, and ATM, and remained predominant across all risk categories. BCORL1 mutations were more prevalent than DNMT3A mutations, particularly in CCUS. Unlike DNMT3A, which showed a clear age-dependent increase, BCORL1 mutations peaked between 50 and 70 years of age. At baseline, patients with BCORL1 mutations had a higher prevalence of cardiovascular disease and lower serum albumin levels. However, overall mortality and other clinical outcomes did not differ significantly during follow-up.ConclusionsCHIP in Asian patients undergoing chronic hemodialysis shows a high prevalence and a distinct mutation spectrum dominated by BCORL1. These findings suggest disease- and ancestry-specific selective pressures beyond aging and support gene-specific risk stratification and closer hematologic surveillance. Further multi-ethnic studies are needed to define the cardiovascular and hematologic implications of BCORL1-associated CHIP.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40246-026-00944-9.Keywords: BCORL1, Clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential, Clonal cytopenia of undetermined significance, Hemodialysis, End-stage kidney disease

Introduction

Clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP) has recently been recognized as a premalignant hematologic condition characterized by the expansion of blood cell clones with acquired somatic mutations in hematopoietic stem or progenitor cells, in the absence of overt hematologic malignancy [1]. CHIP is age-related and is detected in approximately 10–15% of individuals aged 65 years or older [2]. Individuals with CHIP have an increased risk of developing hematologic malignancies, with an estimated annual transformation rate of 0.5–1.5% [3], and are also at higher risk of cardiovascular events and all-cause mortality [4, 5]. Advances in next-generation sequencing technologies have enabled sensitive detection of CHIP, even at low variant allele frequencies.

Despite the growing recognition of CHIP in the general population, data on CHIP in patients with end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) undergoing chronic hemodialysis remain limited. One study reported CHIP in approximately 20–30% of patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD), with associations with lower hemoglobin levels, higher ferritin concentrations, increased mean corpuscular volume, and a higher risk of progression to ESKD [6]. Another study found that 28.9% of patients with type 2 diabetes had CHIP, with no observed association with diabetic kidney disease incidence or progression [7]. These findings underscore the higher prevalence of CHIP in specific disease populations and suggest that disease-specific factors may modify the impact of CHIP on clinical outcomes.

In contrast to CHIP, clonal cytopenia of undetermined significance (CCUS) refers to the presence of persistent, otherwise unexplained cytopenia affecting one or more blood cell lineages in individuals who carry somatic mutations associated with myeloid neoplasms but do not fulfill the diagnostic criteria for myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS) or other hematologic malignancies [8]. CCUS represents an intermediate biological state between CHIP and overt myeloid neoplasia, characterized by both clonal genetic alterations and clinically evident hematologic abnormalities. Importantly, CCUS carries an almost 10-fold higher risk of progression to MDS or acute myeloid leukemia compared with CHIP [8]. Mutations involving genes related to epigenetic regulation, RNA splicing, and transcriptional repression-such as Tet Methylcytosine Dioxygenase 2 (TET2), DNA Methyltransferase 3 Alpha (DNMT3A), Additional Sex Combs-Like 1 (ASXL1), and BCL6 Corepressor (BCOR) are frequently observed in CCUS and are associated with ineffective hematopoiesis [8, 9]. Given its adverse prognostic implications, CCUS is increasingly recognized as a clinically relevant precursor condition that warrants closer surveillance than CHIP.

Hematologic abnormalities are prevalent among patients with ESKD and correlate with adverse outcomes and reduced quality of life [10, 11]. Among hematological abnormalities, anemia is the most prevalent and clinically significant, affecting over half of patients with CKD stage 4 or higher and up to 90% of those with CKD stage 5 [10, 12]. Large registry-based studies indicate that anemia affects approximately 60% of patients with non-dialysis-dependent CKD and over 90% of those receiving maintenance dialysis [13].

In addition to anemia, other abnormalities in blood cell counts, including leukopenia (white blood cell < 4 × 109/L) and thrombocytopenia (platelet < 150 × 109/L), have also been observed in subsets of CKD and dialysis patients [14]. Alterations in white blood cell composition, such as elevated neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratios, have been proposed as surrogate markers of chronic inflammation and are associated with increased cardiovascular risk in CKD populations [15, 16]. However, the mechanisms underlying these hematological abnormalities remain incompletely understood, and their long-term clinical implications have not been systematically investigated, especially in Asian hemodialysis cohorts.

Emerging evidence suggests that abnormal hemograms in ESKD may not solely reflect inflammation or impaired erythropoiesis but could also be linked to clonal hematopoietic disorders. MDS, for example, has been reported at unexpectedly high frequencies among hemodialysis patients. In a small cohort study, half of the hemodialysis patients diagnosed with MDS developed the condition after dialysis initiation, raising concerns that the uremic milieu or dialysis-related stressors may contribute to clonal hematopoietic evolution [17]. Given that MDS carries a substantial risk of progression to acute myeloid leukemia and is associated with excess mortality [18], early detection of pre-malignant hematologic conditions in dialysis patients is of critical clinical importance.

Despite these observations, studies focusing on Asian hemodialysis populations are scarce, and the spectrum of CHIP-associated mutations, as well as their clinical implications, has not been adequately characterized. Moreover, the relationship between CHIP and long-term outcomes, including cardiovascular events, stroke, and mortality in patients undergoing chronic hemodialysis remains largely unexplored. Accordingly, we conducted a prospective cohort study to determine the prevalence of CHIP in patients receiving maintenance hemodialysis. In addition to assessing CHIP status overall, we aimed to characterize the distribution of CHIP-associated genes, to identify the most prevalent mutation, and to evaluate whether this dominant gene is associated with distinct clinical characteristics and long-term outcomes among CHIP-positive patients. These findings may provide important insights for risk stratification and longitudinal monitoring strategies in the dialysis population.

Materials and methods

Study population and data collection

This prospective study enrolled patients from National Taiwan University Hospital, a tertiary medical center, and its branch hospitals, including the Bei-Hu and Yun-Lin branches. The inclusion criteria comprised patients with ESKD who had been receiving maintenance hemodialysis and had a stable hemogram for at least 3 months. The exclusion criteria included patients younger than 18 years and those with a definite diagnosis of MDS or hematological malignancy. This study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of National Taiwan University Hospital (No. 202207155RINA), was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and all participants provided written informed consent.

Whole exome sequencing and data analysis

Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood samples using a QIAgen DNA extraction kit, and DNA concentration was measured with a Qubit 3.0 fluorometer using the dsDNA High Sensitivity Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Whole-exome sequencing libraries were constructed with the KAPA HyperCapture Reagent Kit (Roche) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocols, followed by target enrichment using KAPA HyperExome V2 probes. Post-capture libraries were evaluated for fragment size distribution using the Qsep100 system with an S3 Kilobase cassette (BiOptic) and quantified using the Qubit 3.0 fluorometer. Indexed libraries were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform to generate 150-bp paired-end reads, achieving a mean on-target coverage depth of approximately 300× per sample.

Raw sequencing reads were subjected to quality control and adaptor trimming using FastQC (v0.11.9) and cutadapt (v3.5), with reads shorter than 50 bp discarded after trimming. Somatic variants were called using MuTect2 (GATK) by comparison with matched adjacent normal tissues and subsequently annotated using ANNOVAR (release 20210819). Duplicate reads were marked, and base quality score recalibration was performed according to GATK Best Practices (v4.2.3). Cleaned reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38) using BWA-MEM (v0.7.17).

Variant annotation and filtering

Variants were annotated and filtered according to predefined criteria. Inclusion criteria required a variant site depth of > 100 reads, a variant allele frequency (VAF) > 0.02, and a population allele frequency ≤ 0.1% in the gnomAD database. Somatic variant calling was performed using Mutect2, and variant annotation and filtering were conducted using the COSMIC database. All analyzed CHIP panel genes are provided in Supplementary Tables 1, and the list of CHIP driver genes identified in the patients is provided in Supplementary Table 2.

CHIP and CCUS designations

Participants were classified as having CHIP if they had at least one CHIP driver mutation at a VAF > 0.02, inferred to mean that the allele is present in a heterozygous state in ≧ 4% of diploid cells. Those who did not meet these criteria were classified as not having CHIP.

CCUS was defined cytopenia without an apparent cause. The presence of abnormal hemogram in CCUS was defined for the patients having at least one abnormal hemogram parameter, including anemia (hemoglobin < 9.0 g/dL), leukopenia (white blood cell < 4 × 109/L), and thrombocytopenia (platelet < 150 × 109/L). The distribution of cytopenic hematologic lineages among patients with CCUS was shown in Supplementary Table 3.

CHRS is a scoring system and prognostic model calculated based on predefined clinical and genetic variables [19]. The prognostic variables included in the CHRS comprise single DNMT3A mutation, high-risk mutations (SRSF2, SF3B1, ZRSR2, JAK2, TP53, RUNX1, IDH1, IDH2, and FLT3), number of mutated genes, variant allele fraction, red cell distribution width, mean corpuscular volume, presence of cytopenia, and age. The weighted scores assigned to each prognostic variable are shown in Supplementary Table 4. CHRS values were calculated as the sum of the weighted scores for all prognostic variables. CHRS was used to stratify patients with CHIP or CCUS into low-, intermediate-, and high-risk categories. The three risk groups were defined as follows: high risk (CHRS ≥ 12.5), intermediate risk (CHRS 10–12), and low risk (CHRS ≤ 9.5).

Variables and study outcomes

Patients were followed up from the time of study enrollment until death or January 20, 2026, to assess the primary outcome of all-cause mortality and secondary outcomes, including Major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs) and malignancy. MACEs were defined as a composite of cardiovascular death, nonfatal myocardial infarction, nonfatal stroke, and hospitalization for heart failure. Malignancies were defined as newly diagnosed solid or hematologic cancers identified through pathology reports and oncologic diagnoses. All outcomes were identified during follow-up based on clinical diagnoses documented in the medical records and corresponding ICD codes.

Statistical analyses

SAS software version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA.) was used for statistical analyses. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and were compared using the Student’s t test for two-group comparisons. Categorical variables are expressed as counts and percentages and were analyzed using Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Pairwise comparisons were adjusted for multiple testing (Holm method). For comparisons involving more than two groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s post hoc test was applied for continuous variables. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

For survival analyses, Kaplan–Meier methods were used to estimate event-free survival, and differences between groups were assessed using the log-rank test. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models were subsequently constructed to identify variables independently associated with outcomes.

Results

Clinical characteristics and CHIP mutation patterns

A total of 61 patients were enrolled and underwent CHIP analysis, with a mean follow-up duration of 2.1 years. Overall, 50 patients (81.9%) carried at least one CHIP-associated mutation (Fig. 1). In total, 93 CHIP gene mutations were identified among these 50 patients. The highest number of CHIP gene mutations detected in an individual patient was two (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Flow diagram of patient enrollment

Based on CHIP analysis results, patients were classified into three groups: non-CHIP/CCUS (n = 11), CHIP only (n = 8), and CCUS (n = 42). Patients with CHIP or CCUS (n = 50) were further stratified into low (n = 7), intermediate (n = 25), and high-risk (n = 18) categories according to the Clonal Hematopoiesis Risk Score (CHRS) (Fig. 1).

Baseline clinical characteristics demonstrated that patients with CCUS were significantly older and had lower white blood cell counts, hemoglobin levels, and platelet counts compared with those in the non-CHIP/CCUS or CHIP-only groups. In addition, patients in the CHIP-only group exhibited significantly higher serum phosphate levels and calcium–phosphorus products compared with those in the non-CHIP/CCUS groups (Table 1).

Predominance of BCORL1 mutations over DNMT3A in CHIP and CCUS

BCORL1 mutations were the most frequently observed alterations in both the CHIP-only and CCUS groups (Table 1). Across all CHIP-associated mutations, BCL6 Corepressor-Like 1 (BCORL1) was the most common gene (24.7%), followed by Guanine Nucleotide-binding protein, Alpha Stimulating (GNAS) (12.9%), BCOR (7.5%), DNMT3A (6.5%), ASXL1 (5.4%), and Ataxia-Telangiectasia Mutated (ATM) (5.4%) (Fig. 2). The number of patients with BCORL1 mutations was greater than that for any other CHIP-associated gene as well (Supplementary Fig. 2). Compared with the canonical age-related CHIP gene DNMT3A, BCORL1 mutations were more prevalent in CHIP and significantly enriched in CCUS (Fig. 3).

Age-associated enrichment of DNMT3A but not BCORL1 mutations

We next examined the distribution of DNMT3A and BCORL1 mutations across different age groups. Although the overall prevalence of BCORL1 mutations exceeded that of DNMT3A, particularly among patients aged 50–70 years, only DNMT3A mutations demonstrated a clear age-dependent increase. In contrast, BCORL1 mutation frequency peaked in the 50–70-year age group and declined thereafter (Fig. 5).

Clinical characteristics and outcomes according to BCORL1 mutation status

Among patients with CHIP mutations, 23 patients (46%) carried BCORL1 mutations, while 27 patients (54%) did not. Patients with BCORL1 mutations had a significantly higher prevalence of cardiovascular disease compared with those without BCORL1 mutations (82.6% vs. 59.3%, P = 0.04). They also exhibited lower serum albumin levels, along with borderline lower rates of hypertension and lower hemoglobin levels (Table 3).

Discussion

In this study, we performed a comprehensive analysis of CHIP in a cohort of patients undergoing chronic hemodialysis, with a mean follow-up duration of approximately two years. We observed a high prevalence of CHIP (81.9%) and identified a distinct mutation spectrum dominated by BCORL1 alterations. This pattern differs substantially from that reported in community-based or age-driven CHIP cohorts and suggests that disease-specific factors play a critical role in shaping clonal hematopoiesis in patients with ESKD rather than aging alone.

CHIP has traditionally been regarded as an age-associated phenomenon. Large population-based sequencing studies have consistently shown that DNMT3A is the most frequently mutated gene in CHIP, followed by TET2 and ASXL1, with prevalence increasing markedly with advancing age [4, 5, 20]. Hypermethylation of DNMT3A, indicative of epigenetic silencing of the DNMT3A gene, has been observed in the general population with advancing age and is associated with a greater decline in estimated glomerular filtration rate [21]. Moreover, DNMT3A mutations exhibit a strong age-dependent distribution and are widely considered canonical aging-related CHIP drivers, reflecting their ability to confer a long-term competitive advantage to hematopoietic stem cells without requiring pronounced inflammatory or genotoxic stressors [4, 22]. Mechanistically, DNMT3A loss disrupts DNA methylation homeostasis and promotes gradual clonal expansion, rather than rapid leukemic transformation, consistent with its predominant role in age-related clonal hematopoiesis [4].

In contrast, BCORL1 functions as a transcriptional corepressor with a central role in epigenetic gene regulation and, as a homolog of BCOR, serves as an essential component of the noncanonical polycomb repressive complex 1.1 (PRC1.1). Through its conserved PCGF Ub-like fold discriminator domain, BCORL1 contributes to the assembly and chromatin targeting of PRC1.1, thereby mediating transcriptional repression of genes involved in development, differentiation, and hematopoietic lineage commitment [23, 24]. Disruption of BCORL1 function has been increasingly recognized in hematologic malignancies, including acute myeloid leukemia and blast-phase chronic myeloid leukemia, where loss of PRC1.1-mediated repression results in epigenetic reprogramming, aberrant gene activation, and therapy resistance [25]. These observations underscore the importance of BCORL1 as a key epigenetic regulator of hematopoietic homeostasis.

A particularly notable finding of our study is that BCORL1 mutations were present in 46% of CHIP-positive patients, whereas DNMT3A mutations accounted for only 12% of cases. Moreover, BCORL1 mutations in our cohort did not demonstrate a clear association with chronological age, in sharp contrast to DNMT3A-driven CHIP. This divergence from canonical CHIP mutation patterns likely reflects a combination of ethnic and disease-specific factors. Our cohort consisted exclusively of an Asian population undergoing chronic dialysis, whereas most large-scale CHIP studies have focused on Western populations that are predominantly elderly [4]. Importantly, the impact of ancestry on clonal hematopoiesis remains incompletely understood, as existing studies have largely been restricted to cohorts of European or European-dominant ancestry [26]. Notably, a study from the Mexico City prospective study demonstrated substantial differences in the frequency and subtypes of clonal hematopoiesis compared with the UK biobank and identified ancestry-specific genetic determinants [27]. Although a recent multi-ancestry analysis suggests that although DNMT3A, TET2, and ASXL1 remain the most common CHIP drivers across populations, the relative contribution of specific genes and hotspot variants varies by ancestry, likely reflecting germline predisposition and environmental exposures [28].

Beyond ancestry, the chronic inflammatory and oxidative environment associated with hemodialysis may further contribute to the predominance of BCORL1-mutant clones. Patients with CKD and ESKD are characterized by persistent systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, and immune dysregulation. Accumulating evidence indicates that inflammatory and metabolic stress can accelerate clonal expansion and promote the selection of clones with mutations in epigenetic regulators [5]. In this context, BCORL1-mutant hematopoietic stem cells may gain a selective advantage under sustained inflammatory pressure, supporting the concept of context-specific, inflammation-driven epigenetic selection [24]. Our study also demonstrated significantly lower serum albumin levels in patients with BCORL1 mutations. However, this association was derived from univariate analyses and may be confounded by other clinical factors. Nonetheless, it may suggest a more pronounced inflammation-related malnutrition state that could contribute to a selective environment favoring BCORL1-mutant clones [29]. Notably, this predominance of BCORL1 does not necessarily imply synergistic co-mutation with other canonical CHIP drivers such as DNMT3A, TET2, or ASXL1. Rather than cooperative interactions, the observed mutation spectrum may reflect competitive clonal selection under dialysis-associated inflammatory stress. DNMT3A-, TET2-, and ASXL1-driven clonal hematopoiesis is typically associated with age-related epigenetic drift, whereas BCORL1 has been implicated in transcriptional repression and chromatin remodeling in stress-related hematopoietic contexts. Under sustained inflammatory and metabolic stress, BCORL1-mutant clones may therefore gain a relative fitness advantage, leading to their preferential expansion and an apparent shift in the dominant CHIP driver within the hemodialysis population. Additional potential confounders, including dialysis vintage, inflammatory markers, and medication use (e.g., erythropoiesis-stimulating agents or immunomodulatory therapies), were not fully accounted for in the present analysis. Future studies with larger cohorts and more comprehensive covariate adjustment will be required to clarify the independent contributions of these factors to clonal expansion dynamics.

Uremic toxins may represent a mechanistic link between kidney failure and the expansion of BCORL1-mutant clones. Protein-bound uremic toxins, such as indoxyl sulfate and p-cresyl sulfate, have been shown to induce reactive oxygen species generation, activate DNA damage response pathways including γH2AX signaling, and upregulate pro-inflammatory transcriptional programs in multiple cell types [30–32]. These stressors may not only increase the mutational burden in hematopoietic stem cells but also create a selective environment that supports the persistence and competitive outgrowth of clones with impaired epigenetic repression [33]. Moreover, accumulating evidence indicates a bidirectional relationship between CHIP and inflammation, whereby CHIP can both arise from and further amplify inflammatory signaling, thereby establishing a self-reinforcing cycle that promotes ongoing clonal evolution [34], offering a plausible explanation for the remarkably high prevalence of CHIP observed in our dialysis cohort.

Consistent with this interpretation, we observed a significantly higher prevalence of BCORL1 mutations among patients with CCUS and among those classified as intermediate- or high-risk according to the CHRS. This finding aligns with the established role of BCORL1 in regulating hematopoietic stem cell fate and lineage commitment. Disruption of BCORL1-mediated epigenetic control may impair normal hematopoiesis, predisposing affected individuals to cytopenias and increasing the risk of hematologic malignancy [24]. These results suggest that patients harboring BCORL1 mutations, particularly those with CCUS or high CHRS, may warrant closer hematologic surveillance, including careful monitoring of blood counts and consideration of bone marrow evaluation.

Although BCORL1-mutant patients in our cohort exhibited a higher burden of underlying cardiovascular comorbidities compared with those carrying non-BCORL1 CHIP mutations, we did not observe a significant association between BCORL1 mutations and MACEs or mortality during follow-up period. This contrasts with prior studies linking DNMT3A- and TET2-driven CHIP to increased cardiovascular risk through pro-inflammatory myeloid signaling pathways [5]. The absence of a clear association between BCORL1 mutations and cardiovascular events or mortality in our study may be attributable to limited statistical power related to the modest sample size and the relatively short duration of follow-up. Given the low incidence of major adverse cardiovascular events in this cohort, the possibility of type II error cannot be excluded. In addition, BCORL1-mutant clones may exhibit biological properties distinct from canonical DNMT3A- or TET2-driven clonal hematopoiesis, potentially resulting in different cardiovascular risk profiles. Larger, multi-center studies with longer follow-up periods will be required to more robustly assess the cardiovascular and survival implications of BCORL1-associated clonal hematopoiesis in patients undergoing chronic dialysis.

Several limitations should be acknowledged in this study. First, although a stringent hemoglobin threshold (< 9 g/dL) and stable hemodialysis duration were applied, anemia in ESKD may still be influenced by non-clonal factors such as inflammation or malnutrition. Therefore, residual misclassification of CCUS-related cytopenia cannot be completely excluded. Second, although we observed an association between BCORL1 mutations and distinct clinical features, the underlying biological mechanisms remain hypothetical. Functional and epigenetic validations were not performed in this study. Therefore, future studies incorporating functional assays and epigenetic profiling will be necessary to further elucidate the biological impact of BCORL1 mutations in patients undergoing chronic hemodialysis. Third, although CHRS was applied in this study to explore risk stratification, it has not been formally validated in patients with ESKD. Given the unique clinical characteristics of the ESKD population, the applicability and prognostic performance of CHRS in dialysis patients remain uncertain. Therefore, results related to CHRS should be interpreted with caution, and further studies are warranted to validate or adapt CHRS for ESKD-specific risk stratification. Fourth, because differences in dialysis modality, metabolic and inflammatory profiles, and genetic background may influence clonal hematopoiesis patterns, the generalizability of these findings to patients undergoing peritoneal dialysis or to non-Asian populations requires further validation. Fifth, due to the lack of comprehensive bone marrow examinations, hematologic disorders may not have been accurately or timely detected.

Conclusion

CHIP in patients undergoing chronic hemodialysis is characterized by a high prevalence and a distinct epigenetic mutation spectrum dominated by BCORL1 alterations. These findings highlight the importance of disease-specific and ancestry-related selective pressures beyond age-related processes. The predominance of BCORL1 mutations, particularly among patients with CCUS or high CHRS, underscores the potential clinical value of gene-specific risk stratification and closer hematologic surveillance. Future large-scale, multi-ethnic studies with extended follow-up are warranted to clarify the long-term cardiovascular and hematologic implications of BCORL1-associated CHIP and to elucidate its mechanistic links to uremic inflammation and epigenetic dysregulation.
