Introduction

Advances in surgical and medical management have dramatically improved survival for congenital heart disease (CHD) [[1], [2], [3]], with tetralogy of Fallot (TOF). The most common cyanotic CHD – now achieving >90 % 20-year survival [[4], [5], [6]]. However, this growing adult TOF population faces emerging challenges, particularly a 35-fold increased risk of infective endocarditis (IE) compared to the general population [7]. This vulnerability stems from cumulative hemodynamic stressors: right ventricular outflow tract abnormalities, pulmonary regurgitation, and the inevitable need for repeat surgical interventions, most frequently pulmonary valve replacement (PVR) [8]. We present an exceptional paradigmatic case of recurrent Staphylococcus epidermidis prosthetic valve endocarditis with dual pathology: (1) hematogenous seeding of sequential bioprostheses, and (2) deep sternal infection serving as a microbiologic reservoir. The temporal association between sternotomy procedures and subsequent bacteremic episodes suggests a causal relationship rarely documented in TOF patients. This case highlights the intersection of two critical issues in the care of adult congenital heart disease patients that are rarely described in the literature: the underappreciated role of sternal wounds in recurrent bacteremia and the therapeutic dilemmas posed by multiply reoperated patients.

Case presentation

A 26-year-old male has no medical or family history, does not smoke, and abstains from alcohol. With a history of repaired tetralogy of Fallot (TOF) during childhood underwent bioprosthetic pulmonary valve replacement (PVR) at the age of 23 via sternotomy, addressing severe pulmonary regurgitation. Following this surgical intervention, his access to consistent medical care was impeded over an 18-month period due to geographic challenges associated with residing in a rural area. At the age of 25, he presented with symptoms of fatigue, persistent fever, and significant weight loss, leading to the diagnosis of infective endocarditis. This condition was characterized by vegetations on the pulmonary valve as well as severe valve stenosis. Despite receiving four weeks of targeted intravenous antimicrobial therapy with vancomycin and ceftriaxone, progression of his condition necessitated a repeat PVR procedure. A 25-mm LivaNova valve was implanted during the second sternotomy.

Ten months postoperatively, he developed recurrent IE with Staphylococcus epidermidis bacteremia, treated with a 4-week course of intravenous vancomycin and imipenem.

After completing antibiotic therapy but with persistent severe pulmonary valve stenosis (PS), he was transferred to Turkey through a humanitarian mission for advanced intervention. There, balloon valvuloplasty was attempted on day 3 of admission but failed, prompting transcatheter pulmonary valve replacement (TPVR) on day 10. After 45 days of medical therapy, the patient underwent pulmonary valve replacement via median sternotomy. Sternal entry revealed dense adhesions between the posterior sternal surface and cardiac structures. During adhesiolysis, a spontaneous rupture of the pulmonary artery pseudoaneurysm occurred, resulting in profuse hemorrhage that was successfully controlled. Subsequent dissection revealed: A secondary bleeding source from the right ventricular anterior wall, which required suture repair and significant hemorrhaging from a concomitant aorto-pulmonary fistula. Intraoperative inspection demonstrated complete dislodgement of the previously implanted transcatheter valve (valve-in-valve prosthesis), which was excised en bloc with the surrounding abscess material (Fig. 4). A new 25-mm bioprosthetic valve (Uniline®, Neocor®) was successfully implanted (Fig. 5). Despite achieving initial hemodynamic stability during weaning from cardiopulmonary bypass, the patient developed refractory hypotension in the postoperative period and expired four hours later. Discussion

This case represents an exceptional clinical convergence of complications in repaired TOF: recurrent S. epidermidis prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE), deep sternal wound infection, perivalvular abscess formation, prosthetic valve dislocation, and aorto-pulmonary fistula development. While each entity has been documented separately in CHD populations [[9], [10], [11], [12]], their simultaneous occurrence in a young adult underscores a critical pathophysiological triad: (1) cumulative prosthetic material burden, (2) compromised thoracic defense mechanisms from multiple sternotomies, and (3) healthcare access disparities exacerbating delayed detection. The microbiological persistence likely reflects two synergistic factors: 1. Biofilm dynamics: S. epidermidis extracellular polymeric substances confer 100–1000 times greater antibiotic resistance to planktonic counterparts [13,14], particularly on non-vascularized bioprosthetic leaflets. 2.Sternal microischemia: Median sternotomies disrupt perforating branches of the internal thoracic arteries, with cadaveric studies showing a 38 % reduction in retrosternal perfusion after the third reoperation [15]. This created an avascular niche for microbial sequestration. Therapeutic challenges were compounded by:

Conclusion

This case highlights the critical relationship between sternal integrity and prosthetic valve longevity in patients undergoing multiple sternotomies. A multidisciplinary approach, structured follow-up protocols, and early suspicion of extracardiac infection sources are essential in managing these high-risk patients. Preoperative endocarditis prevention in patients identified as high or intermediate risk, based on established guidelines, is critical to reduce the likelihood of recurrent complications associated with this condition.
