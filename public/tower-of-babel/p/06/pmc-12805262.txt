Introduction

A 72-year-old man with severe heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) because of ischemic cardiomyopathy requiring multiple prior percutaneous coronary interventions, known aneurysmal left ventricular (LV) apex, and secondary-prevention transvenous implantable cardiac defibrillator (ICD; Medtronic Evera, 2017) for ventricular tachycardia (VT) presented to the emergency department with chest pain. He was admitted and decongested for HFrEF decompensation during which he was found to be in this rhythm. What is the rhythm and the explanation for this pattern of variation in QRS morphology?

Discussion

Figure 1A demonstrates a wide-QRS ventricular rhythm with a rate of 90 beats per minute (bpm). In this substrate, the leading differential is between accelerated idioventricular rhythm (AIVR), a term coined by Marriot and Menendez in 1966, and slow VT. AIVR is typically defined as ≥3 regular monomorphic wide ventricular complexes with a ventricular rate between 50–120 bpm.1 The 2 terminologies are sometimes used interchangeably. Characteristics to differentiate AIVR from slow VT have been described.2 AIVR is generally a hemodynamically benign and transient rhythm due to enhanced automaticity and often seen following reperfusion in ischemia, post cardiac arrest, administration of some anesthetic agents, and in digoxin toxicity.Figure 1A: Twelve-lead ECG during wide complex rhythm with HR 90 bpm. Careful analysis of the electrocardiogram (ECG) reveals intermittent variation in QRS morphology, with every third QRS (Figure 1B, QRS 1) in the rhythm strip narrower in comparison to the preceding 2 complexes. This variation is due to atrioventricular dissociation and fusion between the intrinsic QRS and VT wavefronts. For comparison, Figure 1C demonstrates the patient’s atrial paced rhythm with intrinsic QRS complexes. The patient’s dual-chamber ICD is programmed to AAI at 60 bpm (thought to be less pro-arrhythmic), and the patient presented with slow VT at 90 bpm. This 2:3 relationship conveniently explains the varying pattern in QRS morphology. QRS 1 is a fusion between the atrial paced intrinsically conducted QRS and VT. QRS 2 is due to pseudo-pseudo fusion where the VT QRS is overlapped with an atrial, rather than ventricular pacing stimulus (given device is programmed in AAI mode), with the appearance of the atrial pacing spike synchronous to the onset of the QRS. This phenomenon differs from pseudo-fusion, in which the ventricular pacing output occurs too late to noticeably alter the QRS morphology. Of note, the atrial pacing stimulus results in a P wave overlapping within the QRS causing a slight difference in morphology of QRS 2 compared with QRS 3. QRS 3 is pure VT morphology, also demonstrated in Figure 1D.

The ECG of interest also demonstrates the concept of concealed conduction of a ventricular wavefront into the atrioventricular (AV) node. As annotated in Figure 1B, one will note that the PR interval from the atrial paced P wave to the fused QRS is longer than that from the atrial paced P wave to the narrow QRS in Figure 1C (280 ms and 200 ms, respectively). This is because QRS 3 retrogradely invades the AV node and slows down its conduction, reflected by PR prolongation.

Finally, one can deduce from this ECG that the pacemaker could not have been programmed to DDD mode at the time of the tracing, assuming that atrial and ventricular sensing is adequate. This is because the VA interval (sensed V to paced A) should not vary to this degree in DDD.

In this patient with severe LV dysfunction from ischemic heart disease, the apical aneurysm contributes to a substrate for scar-based VT. The left bundle branch morphology, leftward, superior axis and negative concordance in the precordial leads support an inferior-apical exit site. Upon initial presentation, the ventricular rate was approximately 125–130 bpm. Multiple antiarrhythmic agents were used during efforts to control the VT which resulted in the slower rate recorded on the ECG of interest. ECG criteria to differentiate AIVR from slow VT were proposed by Sclarovsky et al.2 AIVR tends to be more regular and usually starts with a long coupling interval to the preceding sinus rhythm/beat. Additionally, persistent AIVR originating from the right bundle branch would typically have a left bundle branch block morphology with sharp downstroke of the S wave, a short rS duration (<70 ms) in precordial leads V1 and V2 with late precordial transition at V4 or V5.3 In this case, the ECG criteria alone cannot distinguish AIVR from slow VT; however, the clinical course and known substrate of the patient led to the determination of slow VT. There were no significant findings on repeat coronary angiography. Interestingly, RV pacing from the ICD seemed to provoke these observed slow runs of VT. This hypothesis led to re-programming of the ICD to AAI with a lower rate limit of 60 bpm which was thought to be less pro-arrhythmic; however, the patient still had recurrent slow VT during which the ECG depicted in Figure 1A was captured. Ultimately, a combination of medical therapy, radiation therapy, and re-programming of the patient’s device to DDD with a lower rate limit of 90 bpm overrode the ventricular rhythm, allowing him to be discharged with no recurrence at 1 year of follow-up thus far.

Conclusion

This ECG demonstrates a slow wide complex rhythm with regular pacing artifact and variations in QRS morphology in a patient with severe ischemic cardiomyopathy and ICD. Careful interpretation of this ECG in the context of the patient’s substrate demonstrates VT with slowed rate due to effects of several antiarrhythmic medications, with evidence of intrinsic QRS fusion, pseudo-pseudo fusion, as well as concealed AV nodal conduction.
