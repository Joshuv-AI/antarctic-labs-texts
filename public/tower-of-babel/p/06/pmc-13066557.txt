Image-to-molecule benchmarking dataset with fractal pattern and hierarchical morphology recognition
Authors: Daria M. Arkhipova, Daniil A. Boiko, Aleksandr A. Oganov, Alexey S. Kashin, Evgeniy G. Gordeev, Valentine P. Ananikov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The unique phenomenon of high morphological diversity of quaternary phosphonium salts (QPSs) has been observed via electron and optical microscopy. The molecular structure of the QPSs, which differ by one methylene group, was shown to be reflected in the microstructure of the crystallized droplets. Here, we describe experimental datasets of scanning electron and optical microscopy images at different magnifications, illustrating the versatile microstructures of 19 homologous QPSs. The unique patterns that appear in the microscopy images of the QPS are related to the molecular structure. The described datasets of microscopy images are made openly available for scientific purpose and include hierarchical morphological patterns and fractal elements. Importantly, the datasets are suitable for both directions of machine learning exploration: recognizing molecular formulas from microscopy images and, conversely, predicting morphological patterns from molecular structures. This bidirectionality establishes a benchmark for bridging the molecule–morphology gap and advancing data-driven materials design.

Background & Summary

Knowledge of the morphology, microstructure and visual appearance of the compounds is important in many areas of chemistry, especially in the development and production of functional materials. The microstructures of substances and materials are closely linked with their properties1. The microstructure plays a crucial role in many engineering applications, such as the development of polymer composites with excellent mechanical properties2, the design of organic semiconductors3, thin film formation in optoelectronics and photovoltaic technology4,5, solar cells6 and LED development7, metal additive manufacturing for superior mechanical performance8, the design of heterogeneous catalysts9, and alloy engineering for corrosion resistance10. The particular interest in studying the properties of matter at a microscale fueled the development of dedicated investigation techniques, for example, scanning electron microscopy, which is widely used for visualizing surface morphology and microstructure, enabling the rapid collection of high-resolution images11.

Currently, there is very little understanding of how a molecular structure gives rise to subtle microstructures, resulting in visual appearance and material properties. Morphology tuning for a wide variety of chemical applications is important for addressing practical tasks. For example, in metal additive manufacturing, an approach for microstructure control was developed to achieve resource adaptability to provide a constant set of parameters for the resulting product12. The microstructure modification of thermoelectric power generators by indium doping and prolonged sintering time promoted the advancement of high performance via photon and electron transport optimization13. An investigation of the resistance of the solder joint microstructure to thermal fatigue was performed to develop improved materials14. An approach to fabricate nanocomposites with target collective properties was described by varying the polymer chain architecture, which influences the phase behavior of block copolymers15. The development of an eutectic mixture of ceramics with gadolinium aluminum perovskite with submicron fibrous microstructures has allowed overcoming the brittleness of ceramics16. A quantitative descriptor reflecting the nanomorphology of polyurea was proposed17. Several material properties were correlated with the nanomorphology of different types of polyurea obtained under various conditions.

Furthermore, the ability to predict the crystal morphology of drug molecules is particularly important. Polymorphic modifications of pharmaceutical solids can influence bulk powder properties and, consequently, their behavior in living organisms. To optimize the drug production process, it is essential to study the molecular interactions that affect crystal morphology. Computer modeling has shown that the crystal shape is highly dependent on the solvent. The proposed theoretical models successfully predict the crystalline morphology of carboxylic acid polymorphs18, RS-ibuprofen19 and haloperidol20. These studies highlight the importance of understanding molecular interactions for optimizing the drug manufacturing process and improving the physicochemical properties of pharmaceuticals. Hara and coauthors demonstrated the important role of molecular structural features in the formation of crystalline phases, using benzothiazole derivatives as examples21. Changes in the molecular structure affect the process of crystallization and solubility of pharmaceutically relevant substances. Moreover, a recent study revealed that the crystal structure and morphology determine the mechanochromic luminescence properties of triphenylimidazolylbenzothiadiazole derivatives22.

A data-driven machine learning (ML) approach to morphology engineering allows the analysis of trends in chemical datasets and helps to understand the relationships between microstructure parameters and material properties23–26. It can be involved in different stages of the study, from automating the experiments for accelerating the process of data collection to data processing and model training for property prediction27–29. When applied to electron microscopy, ML has been used for automated acquisition, analysis and characterization of large-scale datasets of images30–32.

The combination of electron microscopy and machine learning allows for the comprehensive analysis of microstructures and upscales material chemistry27,33. The prediction of the alloy phase morphology via additive technology with an ML model trained on an image dataset of metal samples was proposed34. Machine vision has been applied for the recognition of complex surface molecular structures35. The prediction of microstructure evolution was performed via ML methods trained on a phase-field simulation dataset36,37. Umemoto et al. presented a classification model for a polymer characterization based on chemical imaging using high-voltage scanning transmission electron microscopy38. This approach enables effective clustering of polymer species based on their low-loss spectral data. A study of biofilm composition and morphology was performed via automated SEM and deep neural network analysis39.

Training data for artificial intelligence (AI) identification of composition–structure–property relationships are currently limited40. There is a high demand for datasets of microscopy images for projects development to address molecule-material gap, i.e. how a certain molecular structure leads to appearance of a material at a microscale. Previously, we reported a deep learning model trained on an image dataset of quaternary phosphonium salts (QPSs) morphology able to distinguish chemical compounds among a homologous series on the basis of SEM (or optical) images of the microcrystal structure41. The neural network for the recognition task includes a convolutional network encoder and a classifier. This pioneering study confirmed the possibility that the visual appearance of a homologous substance is correlated with its molecular structure. In addition, the study of the morphology of the binary mixtures of QPSs revealed unique patterns that did not correspond to individual compounds.

AI analysis of microscopy images represents a highly effective technique for studying material properties. This approach has demonstrated promise across various applications in chemical analysis. For example, a dataset of SEM images capturing the ordering effects on the nanoparticle location on the catalyst surface was collected42. Metal nanoparticles tend to create the specified patterns, which correlate with the catalytic activity. Various computer vision methods for nanoparticle detection have been suggested and cross-compared43. This approach facilitates fine-tuning of the chemical and physical properties of the catalytic substrates. The database of scanning tunneling microscope images generated using density functional theory for two-dimensional materials was obtained and used to train convolution neural network classification model for Bravais lattices44. Additionally, a dataset of cryo-electron microscopy images of protein molecules was collected and manually labeled to facilitate ML model training for automated protein particle picking45. Furthermore, the relationship between bainitic steel morphology and mechanical characteristics has been explored46. The study included SEM data collection of steel microstructures, expert annotation and morphological feature extraction. The resulting dataset enables training and testing models to address segmentation tasks.

In this work, we present experimental SEM and optical microscopy image datasets containing microcrystal structure variations related to the molecular structure. A total of >3,500 SEM images and >350 optical images of the microstructures of 19 individual and mixed QPSs were collected (29 samples in total). For each SEM image, the following information is available: molecular structure of the compound, magnification, and key acquisition parameters (electron detector, accelerating voltage, deceleration voltage, working distance, and emission current). The datasets contain unique hierarchical morphological patterns of QPSs at various magnifications, reflecting diverse molecular structures. Concerning task (i), it should be emphasized that the key possibility to recognize molecular structure from material appearance has been confirmed for these compounds41. However, the corresponding data were not published and the dataset presented in this study is novel. This Data Descriptor thus provides the comprehensive publication of the complete, curated image collection, distinguishing it from the prior methodological study which utilized only a subset. For tasks (ii) and (iii), the dataset provides the necessary foundation. The established structure-property link suggests a high potential for inverse design (generating plausible morphologies from molecular information), which now can be explored using this resource. Concerning the (iv) and (v) tasks, appearance of fractal elements and hierarchical organization are visually seen on the images. Electron microscopy images are most valuable and were the focus of this dataset (3,649 images) and supplemented with some optical images for comparison (388 images).

QPSs were subjected to SEM analysis in pure form without application of specific sample preparation procedure. The bulky phosphonium cation and inorganic compact anion increase the melting point of the compounds, and all the substances are white solids (crystalline or amorphous) at room temperature and remain solid under the electron beam in the vacuum chamber of the electron microscope. Sampling

As QPSs with long alkyl substituents (n-C16H33 or more) are poorly soluble in water, an ≈1% DMF solution of each QPS was first prepared to standardize the sample preparation procedure. QPS (0.01 g) and 1 ml of DMF were placed in a test tube and mixed. The DMF solution was subsequently diluted ten times with water by adding 9 ml of distilled water and mixing the resulting solution. The solutions, except for QPS18 and QPS20, were clear and homogeneous for months. After a few hours of preparation, a white precipitate formed in solutions of QPS18 and QPS20. The last two solutions were used immediately after preparation.

In the next step, three separate droplets of the solution were placed on the matte side of aluminum foil (for SEM) and on a glass slide (for optical microscopy) and left to dry overnight (approximately 12 hours). All sample preparation procedures were carried out at room temperature and ambient pressure in air. A piece of foil with crystallized droplets was mounted on an aluminum sample stub and fixed with conductive carbon glue. No sputter coating was applied to avoid morphological distortion or the loss of subtle microstructural details. The samples on aluminum foil were then observed via SEM (Fig. 2). The solid samples on a glass slide were observed via optical microscopy. Samples for electron and optical microscopy were prepared from the same QPS solutions.Fig. 2Sample preparation procedure: QPS solution preparation, droplet application onto the substrate, air-drying of the sample, and microscopy observation with image acquisition.

Binary mixture preparation

The solution of binary mixtures of QPS was prepared by mixing 5 ml of solutions of the corresponding individual QPSs. The rest of the sample preparation procedure was equal to the sampling of individual compounds. The following 10 mixtures were prepared: QPS1-QPS7, QPS2-QPS4, QPS2-QPS17, QPS5-QPS10, QPS5-QPS15, QPS5-QPS20, QPS6-QPS17, QPS10-QPS15, QPS10-QPS20, and QPS15-QPS20.

Data collection

Equipment

Scanning electron microscopy. All SEM images were acquired via a Hitachi SU8000 field emission scanning electron microscope. The microscope can be operated with an accelerating voltage of 0.5–30 kV (0.1–2.0 kV landing voltage in deceleration mode) and ultrahigh-resolution imaging at a low accelerating voltage (secondary electron (SE) resolution of 1.3 nm at a 1.0 kV landing voltage). It is equipped with three secondary electron detectors (upper, top and lower) and a retractable photodiode backscattered electron (PDBSE) detector. The beam deceleration mode is used to reduce the electron beam energy and improve the imaging parameters of sensitive samples.

Optical microscopy. All optical images were collected using a Leica DM IL LED Fluo inverted optical microscope. The microscope for image acquisition allows the use of brightfield, phase contrast and integrated modulation contrast. The total magnification of a microscope is equal to the product of the linear magnification of the objective lens and the angular magnification of the eyepiece. The resulting magnifications range from 50 × to 400 × . The microscope is equipped with LED illumination and a 12 MP Flexacam C1 digital camera. Las X Life Science software was used for image acquisition.

SEM image dataset collection

SEM images were acquired with various instrument settings: 0.5–2.0 kV accelerating voltage and 7–9 mm working distance. The emission current varied from 5 to 13 mA during scanning. Some of the studied QPSs have lower melting points, and the surface of the sample can melt negligibly under an electron beam, smoothing the morphology. A high accelerating voltage induces sample heating and charging when electrons of the primary beam are accumulated within the sample as a localized negative charge, which can cause image artifacts. Therefore, SEM measurements were performed in low-voltage mode to avoid sample melting and charging. The manual image acquisition mode was used: the sites in the scanned sample area were chosen by the operator. Types of detectors and electrons used in the different modes of SEM.

The backscattered (BSE) and secondary electron signals from the upper (in-lens type) and lower (out-lens type) detectors are combined in low magnification mode. Moderately large structural elements and their relative positions are presented on the images. This is the highest level of hierarchical organization of the microstructure. The characteristic patterns in the SEM image are the features of each QPS.

The secondary electron signal was recorded by the upper detector in high-magnification mode, which provided high spatial resolution and optimal contrast. The selected structural units were observed, and the middle and lower levels of hierarchical organization were visualized.

The backscattered and secondary electrons were collected via the upper detector in deceleration mode. By reducing the electron energy in deceleration mode, the impact of the beam is limited. The deceleration mode allowed the acquisition of well-defined images at high magnifications. The effects of a three-dimensional image of the microstructure and topographic features of the sample are observed.

Optical microscopy image dataset collection

The optical images were acquired in brightfield mode: white light transmitted through the sample from below to above and focused on the optical lens in the objective. The contrast in the sample is caused by attenuation of the transmitted light in dense areas of the specimen. Two resulting magnifications of 50 × and 100 × (10 × for the eyepiece and 5 × and 10 × for the objective, respectively) were used. Higher magnifications caused light aberration effects and resulted in strong image artifacts.

Structure verifications

The molecular structures of the QPSs were confirmed via NMR spectroscopy and mass spectrometry methods (see our previous publication41 for the full set of spectra for all compounds; representative spectra of selected compounds are provided in Figures S1–S20 in the Supporting Information).

NMR spectroscopy

NMR measurements were performed using a Bruker Fourier 300 HD instrument at 300.13 MHz and 75.47 MHz for 1H and 13C{1H}, respectively, in CDCl3 and a Bruker AVneo 300 instrument at 121.54 MHz for 31P{1H} in CHCl3. The spectra were processed with the TopSpin 3.5 software package.

ESI mass spectrometry

ESI–MS measurements were performed on Bruker Maxis and MicroTOF II time-of-flight high-resolution mass spectrometers in positive mode in the mass range of m/z 50–3,000. The instruments were calibrated with a low-concentration tuning mixture (Agilent Technologies G2431A). Data processing was performed using Bruker DataAnalysis 4.0 SP4 software.

The 70 pixel-wide digital captions at the bottom indicate the instrument name, accelerating (landing) voltage, working distance, magnification, mode of operation, type of detector, and scale bar. “SU8000” is an instrument name, “1.0 kV” means an accelerating voltage, “0.5kV-D” means landing voltage in deceleration mode, “8.6 mm” is a working distance, “x30” is a magnification, “LM(UL)” is the type of detector, a scale bar with a numerical scale value in millimeters or micrometers. The types of detectors used are as follows: LM(UL) – upper and lower detectors of SE and BSE at low magnification; SE(U) is an upper detector of SE at high magnification; and SE + BSE(U) is an upper detector of SE and BSE in deceleration mode. These indicators, along with other acquisition parameters and sample names, are available for each image in a separate TXT file, which also incluses image name, accelerating voltage, magnification, working distance, emission current, and lens mode.

Each optical image is 1,920 × 1,080 in size and is presented in uncompressed TIFF format (.tif). Each image has a name to identify it. The name of the image file is formed as the label of the corresponding compound, image sequence number, and resulting magnification (QPS1_1_50x, QPS3_9_100x, etc.). A separate LIF file (.xlif) with metadata that include objective magnification is available for each optical image.

Microstructure diversity and hierarchical organization

Even a small change in the alkyl substituent structure has a decisive influence on the resulting morphology observed via SEM. This is especially true for QPSs with short alkyl chains (less than 7 carbon atoms) (Fig. 6a–c) and long chained QPSs (more than 15 carbon atoms) (Fig. 6d–f). For example, the nearest homologs, QPS15 and QPS16, show marked differences in micromorphology (Fig. 6d,e).Fig. 6Some representative images of microstructure variety in the SEM (I) and optical (II) image datasets of the QPS: (a) QPS1; (b) QPS3; (c) QPS6; (d) QPS15; (e) QPS16; (f) QPS18.

Some representative images of microstructure variety in the SEM (I) and optical (II) image datasets of the QPS: (a) QPS1; (b) QPS3; (c) QPS6; (d) QPS15; (e) QPS16; (f) QPS18.

The original microcrystalline compounds (QPS1-QPS7 and QPS15-QPS20) have a more clearly defined morphology of crystallized droplets, and the amorphous powders (QPS8-QPS14) have a molten-like shape and vague edges.

Unique patterns appearing on the image correspond to each QPS, and this trend can be observed at different magnifications. Herein, different types of micromorphology could be present in one QPS. Some QPSs contain multiple domains with different patterns, as shown in the previous publication41, which can be associated with the phenomenon of crystalline polymorphism.

The micromorphology of QPSs includes a wide variety of microstructural elements, such as fractals, lines, needles, grids, lamellae, and abstract patterns. The microstructural units have sizes ranging from tens of nanometers to several millimeters and can be separated or fused.

The optical images generally repeat microstructure patterns similar to those of the SEM images (Fig. 6). In this context, optical images can be used for style transfer via generative-adversarial neural networks or can be used separately.

The SEM image dataset reflects different levels of structural organization: (i) massive structures with complex morphologies at low magnification and (ii) unique structural elements at high magnification (Fig. 7). The deceleration mode of SEM provides images of the QPS samples with a 3D effect (Fig. 7d–f). Some of the images of the morphology of the QPS binary mixtures (341 micrographs) were obtained at “nonstandard” magnifications (Table S2, see the Supporting Information for details). These images can be used to test the scalability of a developed model when analyzed via ML methods. They can be used to check how well the model can predict a sample of images that are different from the training sample.

Notably, the morphology at low magnification is partly a result of the evaporation process of the solution droplet50. The foil base has its own surface microstructure and can influence the resulting QPS morphology. To allow automated image separation, we provide a set of images of bare aluminum foil.

In addition, many QPS samples after drying exhibit a “fractal” structure; that is, the crystals characterized by some degree of self-similarity are apparent at different magnifications (Fig. 7-II). Most of these structures are dendritic crystals. The formation of a “fractal” dendrite structure in the salt crystals may be due to uneven crystal growth rates in different directions. Uneven crystal growth rates, in turn, are caused by fluctuations in the salt concentration, heat transfer features and interfacial energy anisotropy under nonequilibrium conditions51,52. The formation of dendritic salt crystals during solvent evaporation can also be facilitated by impurities53.

Balanced representation

Dataset 1 (SEM images) exhibits good balance not only across a wide range of magnifications (as discussed previously) but also across other acquisition parameters, such as accelerating voltage. Figure S21 and Table S3 (see the Supporting Information for details) show the distribution of images across the 19 individual QPSs and three accelerating voltages (500 V; 1,000 V; 2,000 V). This balanced representation across diverse compounds and imaging conditions is critical for ensuring the representativeness of the training dataset. This approach helps mitigate potential biases in the ML model, enabling more robust and generalizable segmentation of microstructural elements under varying experimental settings.

The accelerating voltage of 2,000 V was used in deceleration mode (deceleration voltage: 1,500 V; landing voltage 500 V).

Technical Validation

The molecular structure and purity of the QPSs were confirmed by NMR spectroscopy and ESI mass spectrometry (see literature data41 for the full set of spectra for all compounds; representative spectra of selected compounds are provided in Figures S1–S20 in the Supporting Information).

All images in the study were collected using the one scanning electron microscope and one optical microscope, eliminating technical inconsistencies. Image acquisition was performed independently by two operators, confirming that operator variation did not affect the observed microcrystal morphology. The microscopes were professionally serviced and aligned to confirm the consistency of the imaging conditions prior to data acquisition. Sample preparation was performed accurately to ensure the quality of the raw data. No observable evidence of morphological distortions that could indicate poor data acquisition was found in the raw data.

Three droplets of each QPS were examined in a single experiment to confirm the reproducibility of the microstructure patterns and to verify the results. The solutions of QPS2, QPS10, QPS17, and QPS20 were prepared twice; the morphology and unique patterns of each sample did not visually change between parallel experiments (Fig. 8).
