Integrative sequence–structure analysis reveals hidden WD40 domains forming stable β-propeller folds with potential biological functions in plants
Authors: Hye Jeong Cho, Su-Kyoung Lee, Min-Jeong Jang, Ki-Hong Jung, Seungill Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
WD40 proteins form β-propeller structures that are essential for plant development and signaling; however, their complete domain architecture is often missed owing to high sequence divergence. Here, we reannotated 117 plant genomes and found that many WD40 proteins contain fewer than the canonical seven domains, raising questions about their functionality as complete WD40 proteins. Structure-based modeling of 17,369 WD40-only genes that lack other associated domains revealed 41,379 additional WD40 domains that were entirely missed by sequence-based annotation but are required for the formation of stable β-propeller structures. Despite being annotated as partial, two rice genes with sequence-invisible domains form complete β-propeller folds and are critical for pollen development. CRISPR–Cas9 knockouts showed that OsWD40-31 is required for pollen tube elongation and that OsWD40-169 is necessary for pollen germination. Disruption of these sequence-invisible domains reduced binding affinity to the key reproductive regulators PME1 and Lipase3, as confirmed by structural interaction modeling, yeast two-hybrid assays, and co-immunoprecipitation assays. Residue substitution revealed that the stability of the WD40 domain depends on hydrogen-bonding residues not captured by sequence conservation, explaining why many functional domains evade sequence-based detection. These findings highlight a fundamental disconnect between sequence conservation and structural integrity, establishing a structure-guided framework for uncovering hidden domain architectures in complex, repeat-rich gene families across plant genomes.

Introduction

Recent advances in deep learning–based protein structure prediction, exemplified by AlphaFold and RoseTTAFold, have revolutionized our ability to model protein tertiary structures with near-experimental accuracy (Abramson et al., 2024; Krishna et al., 2024). Although these achievements have focused primarily on interpretation at the whole-protein level, most proteins consist of multiple structural domains, and biological functions are typically mediated at the domain level. The dissection of proteins into individual domains therefore allows more precise functional interpretation. This is particularly important for domains that are repetitive, short, or highly divergent in sequence, which are often missed by conventional sequence-based annotation methods (Behrens and Spielmann, 2024; Xu et al., 2024). Recent studies have demonstrated the power of structure-based approaches in uncovering hidden structural and functional features within poorly annotated or disordered regions (Alderson et al., 2023; Malhotra et al., 2023). For example, regions predicted to be intrinsically disordered based on sequence features were found to adopt conditionally folded structures in AlphaFold2 predictions and to harbor a high density of disease-associated mutations (Alderson et al., 2023). In the autophagy pathway, previously unrecognized domains, such as a WD40 domain in AMBRA1 and a HEAT domain in ULK4, were uncovered where sequence-based methods had failed (Malhotra et al., 2023). These findings highlight the need for robust, standardized frameworks that can effectively harness structure-based predictions for accurate domain-level functional interpretation.

The WD40 gene family is one of the largest and most functionally diverse families in eukaryotes (Zou et al., 2016; Tan et al., 2021). In plants, WD40 genes are widely recognized as scaffolding components in regulatory complexes that control key developmental and physiological processes (Fan et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2022; Zhu et al., 2025). WD40 genes encode proteins that typically adopt a β-propeller fold composed of radially arranged WD40 repeat domains, providing an extensive and rigid interaction surface that enables the simultaneous or sequential recruitment of multiple proteins (Jain and Pandey, 2018; Stirnimann et al., 2010; Xu and Min, 2011). Despite this well-established structural paradigm, many functionally important plant WD40 genes are annotated with fewer than seven WD40 domains, suggesting incomplete domain annotation (Yang et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2025; Mengxuan et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025). For example, TTG1/AN11-type WD40 genes such as PaTTG1, SaTTG1, and PscAN11, which contain four WD40 domains, regulate anthocyanin accumulation, epidermal development, and floral pigmentation (Yang et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2025; Mengxuan et al., 2025), whereas CmRACK1, with only five WD40 domains, modulates photoperiod- and gibberellin-dependent flowering pathways (Zhang et al., 2025). These examples illustrate that WD40 genes with known biological roles are often labeled as “partial” based on sequence information, potentially misrepresenting their actual structural integrity and functional relevance. This highlights the need for structure-based analyses to more precisely define domain architecture beyond the limitations of sequence information alone, particularly in complex gene families.

To address this challenge, we systematically investigated plant WD40 genes using integrative sequence- and structure-based analyses. We updated existing annotations and generated a comprehensive WD40 gene set comprising 26,549 genes from 117 plant species, including 3,081 newly annotated genes. Our large-scale analysis revealed that (1) structure-based prediction of 17,369 WD40-only genes identified 100,806 domains, including 41,379 that were missed by sequence-based methods; (2) functional analyses showed that OsWD40-31 disrupts pollen tube growth and OsWD40-169 impairs pollen germination; (3) key residues in WD40 domains differed between sequence- and structure-based annotations, helping to explain structural conservation despite sequence divergence; and (4) functional validation through domain structure disruption and protein interaction assays with two rice WD40 genes confirmed the critical roles of structurally predicted domains. Together, these findings establish a practical foundation for accurate domain-level interpretation and demonstrate how structure-guided analyses can systematically enhance the detection of functional domains across plant genomes.

Results

Characteristics of domain architectures in WD40 genes across 117 plant species

We reannotated WD40 genes across 117 plant species spanning 17 taxonomic clades (de Vienne, 2016; Schoch et al., 2020), including six eudicot, eight monocot, and three other clades (Figure 1A; Supplemental Table 1). In total, we identified 26,549 WD40 genes, comprising 23,468 previously annotated genes and 3,081 newly annotated genes (Supplemental Data 1). Although WD40 genes typically encode proteins with a stable β-propeller structure composed of seven WD40 repeat domains (Xu and Min, 2011; Jain and Pandey, 2018), our sequence-based analysis revealed that the majority contain fewer than seven domains (Figure 1A and 1B). Specifically, WD40 genes were predicted to have one (11.7%), two (15.7%), three (19.5%), four (16.4%), five (17.0%), six (9.8%), seven (7.1%), or more (2.8%) WD40 domains (Figure 1B). This distribution closely resembled that of functionally characterized WD40 genes in the same species set. At the species level, the number of WD40 genes ranged from 55 (Cyanidioschyzon merolae) to 768 (Thinopyrum intermedium), and genes with fewer than seven domains were more prevalent than those with seven or more domains (Figure 1C; Supplemental Table 1). This pattern raises questions about whether these genes encode truly incomplete proteins or whether their full structures are undetectable from sequence information alone. Notably, three-dimensional (3D) structure predictions for functionally characterized WD40 genes revealed that all genes classified as partial on the basis of sequence annotation still formed stable β-propeller structures (Figure 1D; Supplemental Figure 1; Supplemental Table 2). Although most WD40 proteins adopted the canonical seven-bladed β-propeller fold, those with additional WD40 domains formed either expanded eight-bladed β-propellers (e.g., TaHOS15-A/B/D) or two distinct β-propeller units (e.g., OsEhd5 and OsLIS-L1), which are known to function as scaffolds that provide additional interaction surfaces (Liu et al., 2019a, 2019b; Zhang et al., 2023). Variation in WD40 domain architecture across phylogenetic subgroups

To explore the genomic structure of WD40 genes, we first performed sequence-based analyses, which enable initial large-scale scanning of domain arrangement and motif composition without structural information. In total, 17,369 genes (65.4%) contained only WD40 domains and were thus referred to as WD40-only genes, and 9,180 genes (34.6%) contained both WD40 domains and additional associated domains (Supplemental Figure 2). We visualized a representative gene model, highlighting the locations of associated domains and the positions of WD40 domains, together with their motif composition (Figure 2A). Overall, WD40 genes typically contain tandemly repeated WD40 domains that share similar motif repertoires, often flanked by associated domains at the upstream or downstream ends (Figure 2A; Supplemental Table 3). We next investigated how domain composition varies across evolutionary subgroups. We constructed a phylogenetic tree using all 26,549 WD40 genes and classified them into 25 subgroups based on lineage specificity and domain architecture, distinguishing genes that contained only WD40 domains from those with both WD40 and associated domains (Figure 2B). We then examined the distribution of WD40 domain copy numbers within each subgroup by categorizing genes into three groups: partial WD40 genes with 1–3 or 4–6 WD40 domains and intact WD40 genes with seven WD40 domains (Figure 2C). Partial WD40 genes predominated in most subgroups, particularly subgroups 7, 19, and 22, in which genes with only 1–3 WD40 domains accounted for over 70%. By contrast, intact WD40 genes were more frequent (>20%) in subgroups 9, 11, 12, and 14 than in other subgroups (Figure 2C). Subgroups dominated by partial WD40 genes (1–3 domains) typically showed domain localization in a few fixed positions, whereas subgroups with more intact genes showed broader and more even domain placement across the eight positions. Thus, although the tandem arrangement of WD40 domains is broadly conserved, variation in domain number and positioning across subgroups contributes to the sequence-level diversification of the WD40 gene family.

Functional specialization of partial WD40 genes in rice pollen development

To assess the functionality of WD40 genes in japonica rice, we analyzed their expression profiles across callus, shoot, leaf, root, anther, pollen, and seed tissue using comprehensive RNA-seq data. Hierarchical clustering identified 27 WD40 genes, including GORI (LOC_Os03g52870), that exhibited strong preferential expression in anthers/pollen, suggesting reproduction-specific functions (Supplemental Table 5; Supplemental Figure 3A and 3B). Two of these genes, OsWD40-31 (LOC_Os01g71780) and OsWD40-169 (LOC_Os09g12550), were annotated as partial WD40 genes but exhibited strong pollen-specific expression (Supplemental Figure 4A), prompting us to examine their functional roles in pollen development. We generated two independent homozygous gene-edited lines for each gene (Supplemental Figures 4B and 5A). To investigate the cause of reduced fertility in the oswd40-31 and oswd40-169 mutants, we analyzed in vivo pollen tube growth using aniline blue staining (Figure 3D–3G). In WT plants, pollen tubes efficiently reached the micropyle (Figure 3D and 3G). By contrast, pollen tubes of the oswd40-31 mutant showed partial arrest at the stigma or style (Figure 3E and 3G), and oswd40-169 pollen exhibited severe defects, with most pollen tubes failing to emerge (Figure 3F and 3G). Ruthenium red staining revealed that pollen tubes of the oswd40-31 mutant had abnormal pectin accumulation along the lateral wall, in contrast to the tip-focused pattern observed in the WT (Figure 3H). In the oswd40-169 mutant, most pollen failed to germinate in vitro or exhibited abnormal morphology (Figure 3I and 3J), and Nile blue A staining indicated reduced lipid accumulation (Figure 3K and 3L). These findings suggest that OsWD40-31 and OsWD40-169 are preferentially expressed in pollen and have evolved distinct functions in mature pollen development, pollen germination, and tube elongation.

Distinct molecular roles of two WD40 genes in pollen development

To explore the molecular mechanisms directly regulated by OsWD40-31 and OsWD40-169, we performed yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) screening against an anther-derived cDNA library, using each protein as bait (Supplemental Figure 6A–6D). We identified 8 interactors for OsWD40-31 and 19 for OsWD40-169, including three that were shared (ENTH and two MIZUs). Based on gene annotations, 7 interactors for OsWD40-31 and 17 for OsWD40-169 were visualized in protein–protein interaction networks and their biological roles examined by Gene Ontology (GO)-based functional categorization (Figure 4A). OsWD40-31 interacted with PME1 (LOC_Os03g19610), a key regulator involved in pectin remodeling during pollen tube growth, supporting its role in cell-wall modification during this process (Kim et al., 2020b). Additional interactors were associated with auxin signaling, protein phosphorylation, and nitrogen metabolism. By contrast, OsWD40-169 interacted with Lipase3 (LOC_Os01g46370), a class III GDSL lipase essential for anther development and pollen fertility (Zhang, 2020), and with metabolic enzymes such as ABH, Eno2, and Pho2, which are involved in lipid and carbohydrate metabolism. We next assessed the strength of protein–protein interaction pairs through structure-based modeling, focusing on binding affinity and buried surface area (Figure 4B). PME1 and MIZU (LOC_Os04g54600) showed the strongest interactions with OsWD40-31 (ΔG = −25.8 and −21.6 kcal mol−1, respectively), whereas Lipase3 and MAK6 (LOC_Os06g30130) showed the strongest interactions with OsWD40-169 (ΔG = −36.3 and −29.0 kcal mol−1, respectively). To determine the functional consequences of these interactions in planta, we performed RNA-seq analysis using mature anthers from WT and mutant plants. We identified 5,017 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the oswd40-31 mutant and 2,371 in the oswd40-169 mutant (Supplemental Tables 6 and 7). GO enrichment analysis of the downregulated genes revealed distinct molecular effects for each gene (Figure 4C). Specifically, DEGs from oswd40-31 mutants were enriched in categories such as “cell wall modification” and “regulation of protein kinase activity”. These categories align with the function of PME1 in pectin remodeling and provide support for a role of OsWD40-31 in the regulation of cell-wall dynamics and signaling. By contrast, DEGs from oswd40-169 mutants were enriched in lipid metabolism and reproductive processes, consistent with interactions between OsWD40-169 and Lipase3, as well as other enzymes essential for lipid homeostasis and male fertility. These transcriptomic changes were closely aligned with the annotated functions of their high-confidence interactors, reinforcing a model in which WD40 proteins function as scaffolds to coordinate protein complexes within specific developmental programs. Despite being annotated as partial WD40 genes with only five (OsWD40-31) and three (OsWD40-169) domains, both proteins form structurally complete seven-bladed β-propeller folds (Figure 4D). These findings demonstrate the practical utility of complementing sequence-based approaches with structural information to identify functionally essential domains in WD40 proteins.

Decoupling sequence signatures from structural integrity in WD40 domains

To assess how frequently sequence-based annotations underestimate the number of WD40 domains, we performed structure-based predictions for 17,369 genes that contained only WD40 domains. This gene set enabled precise structural analysis of WD40 domains without confounding influences from associated domains (Figure 5A). In total, 100,806 WD40 domains were identified: 59,427 domains (3.4 per gene) were detected by sequence-based annotation, and structure-based prediction detected an additional 41,379 domains (2.4 per gene) (Figure 5A). This integrative approach revealed an average of 2.4 structurally conserved WD40 domains per gene that were missed by sequence-based methods, emphasizing the value of structure-based analysis. We next classified all 100,806 WD40 domains into four categories based on sequence-based annotation confidence: type 1 (high confidence, e-value ≤ 1e−10), type 2 (moderate confidence, 1e−10 < e-value ≤ 1e−1), type 3 (low confidence, 1e−1 < e-value ≤10), and type 4 (undetected by sequence annotation) (Figure 5A). Notably, most of the newly identified domains from the structure-based annotation were assigned to type 4 (38,375), suggesting that these domains lack recognizable WD40 signature sequences. We examined the amino acid sequence conservation of WD40 domains across the four domain types and observed a progressive decline from type 1 to type 4 (Supplemental Figure 7A). Three hallmark residues—H at position 10, D at position 32, and W at position 38—were highly conserved in type 1 domains, with frequencies of 98.9%, 99.8%, and 99.9%, respectively. To determine which residues are critical for defining WD40 domains at the sequence level, we selected one type 1 domain with the lowest e-value from each of the 117 plant species and systematically substituted one to four residues among the 40 conserved positions with “X”. Across all substitution scenarios, substitution combinations that included the three hallmark residues—positions 10 (H), 32 (D), and 38 (W)—consistently produced the largest increases in mean e-value, indicating the central importance of these residues for accurate sequence-based prediction of WD40 domains (Figure 5C). We next assessed whether residues critical for sequence-based annotation are also essential for structural stability. We selected WDR2 of OsWD40-31 and WDR2 of OsWD40-169, both of which were confidently predicted as WD40 domains by both sequence- and structure-based methods (Figure 5D). Three residues (H10, D32, and W38) were replaced with proline (P), which is known to disrupt structure formation, and the 3D structures were predicted again. Despite these substitutions, all four β-strands were preserved (Figure 5D). Overall structural integrity was maintained across the domain set, with only marginal shifts in predicted confidence metrics (median ΔpLDDT = 1.40; median ΔpTM = 0.01) (Supplemental Figure 7B). These results indicate that residues important for sequence-based recognition may not be required for maintenance of structural integrity. Our findings indicate that sequence-based domain annotation relies mainly on a narrow subset of highly conserved domain sequences, such as those in type 1. Consequently, domains with divergent or non-canonical sequences may be systematically missed when relying on sequence information alone, even when their structural integrity is preserved.

Structural determinants of WD40 β-propeller stability

Because WD40 domains retain structural integrity even in the absence of clear sequence signatures, we examined functionally characterized plant genes to identify structural features that stabilize individual β-units within the β-propeller. We first defined a representative β-unit as comprising four antiparallel β-strands (a–d) stabilized by interstrand hydrogen bonds, a configuration observed in 322 of 364 WD40 domains (83.7%) (Figure 6A). To identify core residues responsible for structural integrity, we systematically substituted P at all 22 residues that form the 11 hydrogen bonds (Figure 6A). This simulation revealed that disruption of at least three residues was sufficient to induce β-unit collapse, and substitution of five residues disrupted nearly all β-units (315 of 322; 97.8%) (Figure 6B). Based on these results, we defined three residue sets (Combination1–3), each containing 3–5 residues. Specifically, Combination1 (residues 18, 28, and 37) alone caused folding defects in 73.0% of β-units; Combination2 (adding residue 38) accounted for an additional 10.8%; and Combination3 (adding residue 27) contributed 14.0% (Figure 6A and 6B). The remaining 2.2% showed no detectable folding defects, although most β-strand regions were disordered, indicating substantial disruption (Supplemental Figure 8B). All five residues were located at the most conserved hydrogen-bond positions (2, 5, 6, and 9), highlighting their central role in maintenance of β-unit integrity. Notably, with the exception of position 38 (W), none of these residues overlapped with those important in sequence-based annotations, suggesting that folding stability is governed by structurally critical but sequence-divergent features. For example, we applied the same substitution combinations to all seven β-units of two experimentally characterized genes in this study, OsWD40-169 and OsWD40-31; all β-units were predicted to lose folding stability, reinforcing the critical contribution of these residues (Figure 6C).

We next examined whether structure-predicted domains, undetectable by sequence-based methods, are functionally important. Specifically, we focused on WDR4 of OsWD40-169 and WDR1 of OsWD40-31 (Figure 6D). These domains were confidently identified through structural modeling but undetectable by sequence-based approaches. To assess whether WDR4 and WDR1 contribute to the interactions of their host genes with Lipase3 and PME1, respectively, we introduced the same disruptive substitutions and measured binding strength (Figure 6D). Structural disruption markedly reduced complex formation. In the OsWD40-169–Lipase3 complex, the buried surface area decreased by 47% (from 2,838 Å2 to 1,327 Å2), and the binding affinity dropped by more than 50% (from −36.3 to −18.1 kcal mol−1). Y2H assays confirmed this reduction in protein–protein interaction, which was independently supported by co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) assays in Nicotiana benthamiana, showing that core-residue substitutions disrupted complex formation. A comparable decrease in interaction strength was observed in the OsWD40-31–PME1 complex after WDR1 disruption. Consistent effects on interaction strength were also observed in well-characterized WD40 protein complexes from Arabidopsis thaliana, Zea mays, and Artemisia annua (Supplemental Figure 9). In each case, substitution of interstrand hydrogen-bonding residues within these sequence-invisible WD40 β-units reduced the buried surface area and binding affinity, mirroring the weakened interactions observed in rice WD40 complexes. These findings demonstrate that structure-based annotations can identify functionally essential domains missed by sequence-based approaches, highlighting the importance of integrating sequence and structural data for accurate domain prediction and functional interpretation.

Discussion

Conventional sequence-based annotations often miss repetitive or non-canonical domains, leading to frequent misinterpretation of domain architecture, despite the essential role of accurate identification in the interpretation of protein function. Using our integrative framework, we identified 41,379 “sequence-invisible” WD40 domains, resolving the discrepancy between “partial” annotations and actual structural completeness. This reveals a fundamental disconnect: whereas sequence-based detection relies on specific conserved residues (e.g., H10, D32, and W38), structural stability is governed by specific residues involved in interstrand hydrogen bonding. From an evolutionary perspective, these hidden domains act as a key mechanism for preserving the essential β-propeller fold despite extensive sequence divergence. Although the core β-propeller scaffold is highly conserved, functional diversity is facilitated by local structural changes, such as variations in surface-exposed loop regions. These variations provide the plasticity required for lineage-specific partner interactions without compromising the rigid β-strand backbone (Jain and Pandey, 2018). Our 25 subgroups exemplify this evolutionary strategy, in which unique sequence variations drive micro-topographic changes to recruit specific interactors. For instance, OsWD40-31 (subgroup 13) and OsWD40-169 (subgroup 22) share a conserved β-propeller architecture but interact with distinct partners, specializing in pollen tube elongation and pollen germination, respectively.

We next asked whether these sequence-invisible domains were also functionally indispensable, particularly in the context of pollen development. Comparative expression profiling of 200 rice WD40 genes revealed significant pollen-enriched expression across numerous candidates. Pollen development requires rapid, stage-specific transcriptomic reprogramming, whereas pollen tube elongation relies on robust vesicle trafficking to coordinate membrane and cell-wall remodeling with tip-localized signaling. Because these processes continuously demand the assembly of multi-component complexes and compartment-specific targeting, they are likely to increase the functional reliance on WD40 β-propeller scaffold proteins. In this context, pollen-enriched WD40 genes may share an overarching mechanism of complex-based, compartmentalized regulation, even if their binding partners differ. To test this possibility at the level of interaction interfaces, we introduced substitutions into two representative pollen-enriched WD40 proteins, OsWD40-31 and OsWD40-169, and observed markedly reduced binding to PME1 and Lipase3 in in silico modeling, Y2H assays, and co-IP assays. These results indicate that sequence-invisible WD40 domains can contribute to protein–protein interaction interfaces. This interpretation is further supported by the functions of PME1 and Lipase3 and their concordance with our GO analyses and mutant phenotypes.

The significance of these sequence-invisible domains is not confined to rice but represents a fundamental architectural feature of the plant WD40 gene family. Our structural repertoire of 100,806 WD40 domains across 117 plant species demonstrates a high degree of structural invariance across diverse lineages, suggesting that the core architecture is fundamentally stable across the plant kingdom. This mechanistic consistency was confirmed by an in-depth analysis of 75 functionally characterized proteins from 30 species. Regardless of their diverse functional roles or sequence-based confidence levels, 83.7% of these domains consistently exhibited invariant interstrand hydrogen-bonding patterns. Because these core residues are the primary mechanical determinants of β-propeller integrity, these results confirm that hidden WD40 domains are a conserved structural requirement rather than random evolutionary byproducts.

Ultimately, this structurally validated repertoire provides foundational data to establish a new standard for protein domain annotation. By leveraging a high-quality training set that specifically incorporates these sequence-invisible domains, researchers can refine Hidden Markov Model (HMM) profiles to capture conserved structural features beyond canonical sequence signatures. This structure-informed retraining significantly improves search sensitivity, enabling the identification of novel WD40 genes that are currently missed by conventional sequence-based approaches. Accordingly, this integrative approach is not limited to WD40 genes in plants but can be broadly applied to other gene families with complex or repetitive domains across both eukaryotic and prokaryotic genomes.

Methods

Plant genome resources and WD40 gene family annotation

To re-annotate WD40 genes with broad phylogenetic representation across land plants, we selected 117 plant species spanning 17 taxonomic clades (six eudicots, eight monocots, and three other clades) using NCBI Taxonomy and Lifemap (de Vienne, 2016; Schoch et al., 2020) (Supplemental Table 1). A species-level phylogenetic tree for the 117 plant species was constructed using phyloT v2 (https://phylot.biobyte.de) and visualized with iTOL v7 (Letunic and Bork, 2024). WD40 genes were re-annotated using TGFam-Finder v1.20 (EXTENSION_LENGTH = 800000, MAX_INTRON_LENGTH = 165000, HMM_CUTOFF = 1e–2) (Kim et al., 2020a) with publicly available genomic and RNA-seq resources (Supplemental Table 8). Functional domain annotations were obtained using InterProScan v5.22-61.0 (-f tsv -appl Pfam) (Jones et al., 2014), and the WD40 domain was targeted using the Pfam ID PF00400 with domain models from the Pfam (Finn et al., 2014) and Hidden Markov Model (Mistry et al., 2013) databases. A refined set of 26,549 WD40 genes was obtained, each containing at least one WD40 domain with an E-value ≤ 1e−4. WD40 and associated domains were filtered using E-value cutoffs of 1e−1 and 1e−4, respectively.

Motif analysis of WD40 domains

Conserved motifs were identified in the protein sequences of 26,549 WD40 genes using MEME v5.1.1 (-nmotifs 50 -minw 10 -maxw 30 -time 18000) (Bailey and Elkan, 1994) and subsequently matched to sequences using MAST v5.1.1 with default parameters (Bailey and Gribskov, 1998). Because WD40 domains are arranged as tandem repeats, motifs located outside these arrays or inconsistently positioned across gene structures were excluded. Of the 50 predicted motifs, 14 WD40-related motifs were selected for downstream analysis based on their sequence characteristics and positional conservation within WD40 domains (Supplemental Table 3).

Phylogenetic analysis and subgroup characteristics of WD40 genes

Re-annotated WD40 genes were aligned using MAFFT v7.470 with default parameters (Katoh and Standley, 2013). Ambiguous regions were removed with TrimAL v1.4 (-gt 0.2) (Capella-Gutierrez et al., 2009). A maximum likelihood phylogenetic tree was constructed using RAxML v8.2.12 (-m PROTGAMMAAUTO -p 12345) (Stamatakis, 2014) and visualized with iTOL v7 (Letunic and Bork, 2024) as described in a previous study (Jang et al., 2024). WD40 genes were classified into 25 subgroups based on lineage specificity and domain architecture, distinguishing WD40-only genes from those that contained associated domains. Subgroup characteristics were further analyzed by comparing WD40 domain numbers and positions.

Meta-expression analysis

We used a publicly available rice Affymetrix microarray dataset from anthers and pollen obtained from the NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) to identify WD40 genes preferentially expressed in late pollen. To examine expression patterns, we used the Affy R package to normalize the signal intensities and then transformed them into log2 values. A heatmap with a color gradient was generated using MeV v4.9.0 (MultiExperiment Viewer), enabling clear visualization of the tissues with high expression levels and the degree of tissue specificity.

Plant materials and growth conditions

Japonica rice (Oryza sativa ssp. japonica cv. Dongjin) was used as the wild type (WT) for the mutant study. Plants were grown in a growth chamber for 2 weeks under a 16-h/8-h light/dark cycle, 28°C/25°C day/night temperatures, and 80% relative humidity. Plants were then transferred to the LMO paddy field at Kyung Hee University in Yongin, Republic of Korea. We generated two homozygous gene-edited lines each for OsWD40-31 and OsWD40-169 (Figure 3; Supplemental Figure 5). T2-generation knockout mutants from one representative line per gene were used for further functional characterization.

Vector cloning and rice transformation

oswd40-31 and oswd40-169 knockout mutants were generated using CRISPR–Cas9 genome editing. Single-guide RNAs (gRNAs) were designed using the CRISPRdirect tool (http://crispr.dbcls.jp/), and the annealed oligomers were ligated into the BsaI-digested pRGEB32 binary vector (Addgene no. 63142). The constructed plasmids were transformed into Agrobacterium (strain LBA4404) using the freeze–thaw method. Stable transformation was achieved using the Agrobacterium-mediated co-cultivation method as described previously. Primers used for cloning are listed in Supplemental Table 9.

Isolation of RNA and reverse-transcription quantitative PCR

Samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) combined with an RNeasy Plant Mini Kit (Qiagen) and DNase treatment. First-strand cDNA was synthesized using M-MLV Reverse Transcriptase (Promega) and an oligo(dT)₁₅ primer. RT–qPCR was performed using a Qiagen Rotor-Gene Q PCR cycler. Tissue-specific expression levels were analyzed, with rice ubiquitin1 (OsUbi1, LOC_Os03g13170) as the internal control (Jain et al., 2006). Primers were designed to target specific regions unique to each gene (Supplemental Table 9). The accuracy and efficiency of each primer set were verified by PCR amplification of gDNA to optimize the PCR conditions and melting curve. Relative expression levels were calculated using the comparative ΔCT method (Pfaffl, 2001; Silver et al., 2006). Three biological replicates were analyzed, and each reaction was performed at least three times with technical replicates.

Plant phenotyping and fertility measurement

Field-grown rice plants were photographed using a COOLPIX P900S camera (Nikon). Fertility was measured by counting the seed-setting rate of five panicles from three biological replicates. Flowers and anthers were imaged using an Olympus SZX61 stereomicroscope. To assess lipid content in pollen grains, spikelets were collected prior to anthesis, and pollen grains were manually extracted using tweezers and stained with 20 μg/ml Nile blue A for visualization. For analysis of in vitro pollen germination and pollen tube morphology, fresh pollen grains were harvested immediately after anthesis and placed onto either solid or liquid pollen germination medium (PGM). The PGM was freshly prepared with 20% (w/v) sucrose, 10% polyethylene glycol 4000, 3 mM calcium nitrate, 40 mg/l boric acid, and 10 mg/l vitamin B1, with the pH adjusted to 6.8–7.0. For solid PGM, slides containing 1% agarose were prepared and covered with a coverslip before incubation in a humid chamber at 28°C in the dark for 20 min. Pollen germination rates were quantified by examining at least 150 pollen grains per replicate in three independent experiments.

Transcriptome analysis

Anthers were collected from the WT and oswd40-31 and oswd40-169 mutants and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen. Total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Plant Mini Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After the RNA passed quality control, a sequencing library was constructed using the TruSeq Stranded mRNA Library Prep Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions (TruSeq Stranded mRNA Reference Guide #1000000040498 v00). Sequencing was performed by Macrogen, Inc. In brief, raw reads were trimmed using Cutadapt (Martin, 2011) with the following parameters: -a AGATCGGAAGAGC -A AGATCGGAAGAGC -q 30 -m 20. Trimmed reads were mapped to the rice reference genome retrieved from the Rice Genome Annotation Project (http://rice.uga.edu/) using HISAT2 (Kim et al., 2019). Raw read counts were estimated using featureCounts (Liao et al., 2014), and DEGs were analyzed using the DESeq2 R package (Love et al., 2014). DEGs were identified using the following criteria: baseMean > 10, |log2FoldChange| > 1, and adjusted p value < 0.05. Functional classification of the DEGs was performed by Gene Ontology enrichment analysis using the ShinyGO tool (Ge et al., 2020) (https://bioinformatics.sdstate.edu/go/).

Yeast two-hybrid analysis

Full-length coding sequences of OsWD40-31 and OsWD40-169 were fused with the GAL4 DNA-binding domain at the EcoRI/BamHI sites of the pGBKT7 vector. To test the self-transcriptional activity of the bait constructs, each plasmid was introduced with an empty prey vector into the yeast strain AH109. Library screening was performed by Panbionet Corp. (http://www.panbionet.com/) using our rice anther cDNA library. A total of 3.8 × 107 and 2.48 × 107 colonies were screened for OsWD40-31 and OsWD40-169, respectively, and 41 and 60 colonies that grew on SD−LWHA plates and turned blue in the presence of X-α-Gal were selected for plasmid rescue and DNA sequencing. The primary interacting proteins and the bait pGBKT7-WD40 vectors were co-transformed into AH109 yeast cells to confirm their interactions. The transformants were grown on SD/–Trp/–Leu, SD/–Trp/–Leu/–His/X-α-Gal, and SD/–Trp/–Leu/–His/–Ade/X-α-Gal media at 30°C for 3–5 days. Three biological replicates were performed.

Co-immunoprecipitation assay

Agrobacterium tumefaciens strains carrying p35S:OsWD40-31-6×Myc and p35S:PME1-3×HA, or p35S:OsWD40-169-6×Myc and p35S:Lipase-3×HA, were infiltrated into N. benthamiana leaves. Leaves were harvested 72 h post infiltration, and total proteins were extracted using IP buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl [pH 8.0], 150 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, 0.5 mM DTT, 5 mM MgCl2, and 0.1% NP-40) supplemented with an EDTA-free protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). Protein extracts were incubated with anti-Myc magnetic beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) at 4°C for 2 h, and the beads were washed five times with wash buffer (IP buffer without protease inhibitors). Immunoblots were probed with anti-c-Myc-HRP (1:4,000; Abcam, Cambridge, UK) and anti-HA–HRP (1:500; Roche, Basel, Switzerland) antibodies for detection of Myc- and HA-tagged proteins, respectively.

Deep learning–based protein structure and interaction prediction

Tertiary structures were predicted for proteins encoded by 17,369 WD40-only genes using AlphaFold2 (Tunyasuvunakool et al., 2021) via the ColabFold platform with default parameters (Mirdita et al., 2022), including both single-protein and protein–protein complex models. The top-ranked structure (rank_001.pdb) based on pLDDT scores was used for downstream analysis and visualization with ChimeraX v1.9 (Meng et al., 2023). Binding affinities of predicted complexes involving two functional rice WD40 proteins and their known interactors were estimated using PRODIGY (Xue et al., 2016), and buried surface areas were calculated using ChimeraX v1.9 (Meng et al., 2023).

Sequence- and structure-based integrative annotation of WD40 domains

WD40 domains were annotated using two integrative approaches combining sequence-based prediction using HMM searches and structure-based detection using AlphaFold2-predicted protein structures. Based on E-value thresholds, sequence-based annotations were classified into four confidence levels: type 1 (E-value < 1e−10), type 2 (1e−10 < E-value ≤ 1e−1), type 3 (1e−1 < E-value ≤ 10), and type 4 (undetected by hmm search). WD40 domains assigned to types 3 and 4 were considered “sequence-invisible.” For structure-based annotation, secondary structure elements were assigned from AlphaFold2-predicted PDB files using STRIDE (Heinig and Frishman, 2004) and DSSP (Kabsch and Sander, 1983) to distinguish β-strand regions. β-strands were then grouped into β-units, defined as WD40 domains, based on shared β-strand labels and residue-level hydrogen-bonding partners.

Substitution analysis for domain signatures and structural stability

Substitution analysis was performed to evaluate residue importance at both the sequence and structural levels. WD40 domains were aligned using MAFFT v7.470 with default parameters (Katoh and Standley, 2013), and ambiguously aligned regions were removed using TrimAL v1.4 (-gt 0.2) (Capella-Gutierrez et al., 2009). Sequence logos were generated with WebLogo v3 (Crooks et al., 2004), and conservation ratios were calculated using Jalview v2.11.4.1 (Waterhouse et al., 2009). For sequence-level assessment, one type 1 WD40 domain (high confidence) was selected from each of the 117 species. From the 40 conserved positions, combinatorial substitutions were introduced by masking one to four residues with “X” to assess how progressively conserved residues affect domain detectability. All possible combinations were generated for each substitution level and subjected to hmm searches to evaluate changes in E-values. For structure-level assessment, 364 β-units were selected from 556 intact WD40 domains across 75 functionally characterized WD40 genes. This subset represents units capable of generating valid simulation models (e.g., domains 2–6) required for the structural substitution analysis. Within this dataset, 22 residues involved in 11 β-strand hydrogen bonds were individually substituted with proline, a known structure-disrupting amino acid (Joseph et al., 2013), and the structures were re-predicted using AlphaFold2. Based on the known topology of WD40 domains, in which four β-strands are structurally arranged as a–b–c–d, despite appearing in the sequence as d–a–b–c (Wang et al., 2013), we defined a structural β-unit in the order a–b–c–d. Structural disorder was evaluated within each β-unit, and domains were classified as having folding defects if all four β-strands in a unit failed to form.
