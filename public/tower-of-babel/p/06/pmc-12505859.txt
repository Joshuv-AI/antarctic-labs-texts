Moral disengagement in critical care nurses: a conventional content analysis
Authors: Ali Khanipour-Kencha, Alun C Jackson, Saba Barati-Rizi, Fatemeh Bahramnezhad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGiven the high-stakes environment of intensive care units, understanding how nurses navigate moral challenges and engage in ethical decision-making is critical for maintaining patient safety and care quality. One important process in this field is moral disengagement, which is growing in the nursing literature. However, there is a lack of foundational and qualitative studies addressing this issue. This study presents a qualitative content analysis aimed at exploring moral disengagement among nurses working in intensive care units.MethodThis qualitative study employed conventional content analysis from June to December 2024 in Tehran, Iran. Purposive sampling was used to recruit participants. Data were collected using in-depth, unstructured interviews at the beginning, followed by semi-structured interviews. Sampling continued until data saturation was reached, resulting in 30 interviews with 25 eligible intensive care unit nurses. The conventional qualitative content analysis approach developed by Graneheim and Lundman was employed.ResultsThree main categories with eight subcategories were identified from the initial 765 codes. The findings revealed three main categories: “cognitive justification of unethical behavior,” “protective strategies for personal security,” and “normalization of unconventional practices.” These categories include subcategories such as “externalization of responsibility,” “moral-cognitive justification,” “positive reframing of the event,” “preservation of job position and security,” “psychological stabilization,” “strategic violation of regulations for survival,” “reduction of emotional and psychological pressure,” and “protective framing of behavior.”ConclusionThis study reveals various aspects of moral disengagement among critical care nurses, which can pave the way for future studies to better understand the phenomenon, its related factors, and help with the development of preventive measures.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12910-025-01282-6.Keywords: Moral disengagement, Ethic, Nurses, Intensive care unit, Qualitative, Content analysis

Introduction

Intensive Care Units (ICUs), known for their demanding and emotionally charged environments, pose unique challenges for nurses, particularly in ethical decision-making [1]. Providing care to patients in critical and life-threatening conditions typically involves the use of advanced equipment and sometimes invasive interventions. This raises challenges related to obtaining informed consent, complicates the adherence to ethical principles such as confidentiality, and may jeopardize patient rights [2]. Additionally, the nature of decision-making regarding life-saving interventions and end-of-life care is further complicated by high workloads, limited time for delivering high-quality critical care, a scarcity of available resources, and complex facilities [2, 3]. Collectively, these factors place nurses in ICUs in a clinical context that is rife with potential ethical dilemmas. The complex nature of critical care nursing often places nurses in ethically challenging situations, which can lead to the phenomenon of moral disengagement as a coping mechanism [4–6].

Moral disengagement has been conceptualized as a dynamic process through which individuals gradually detach from their moral standards over time [7]. This explains why and how morally upright people can sometimes engage in immoral behavior without experiencing guilt or self-blame [8]. In other words, moral disengagement is the process that enables individuals to engage in negative behaviors, ranging from minor misdeeds to major crimes, without believing that they are causing harm or making a mistake [9]. Hence, researchers have drawn attention to moral disengagement due to the importance of this ethical concern, despite it not being a novel phenomenon. While studies have been conducted on moral disengagement in various contexts, including doping in athletes [10], criminal behavior among prisoners [11, 12], cyberbullying [13], antisocial behavior among youth [14], and academic cheating behavior in students [15], there is a limited body of research specifically addressing moral disengagement within the nursing profession. This issue is concerning, as deviations from ethical principles in healthcare directly threaten patient safety and can lead to severe consequences, including putting the patient’s life at risk [9, 16]. Nursing is inherently a collaborative profession, so downplaying unethical behavior among staff can impact an organization’s ethical climate, potentially leading to a wider prevalence of unethical practices [17].

Therefore, it seems necessary to further understand the overt and covert aspects of the moral disengagement phenomenon in nursing practice, particularly in ICU settings, which are filled with ethical challenges and require sound decision-making.

Theoretical framework

The concept of moral disengagement was initially introduced by Albert Bandura, an American-Canadian psychologist, in his book on social cognitive theory in 1986 [18]. Bandura described moral disengagement as a cognitive process in which individuals rationalize unethical actions by altering their moral perception and attitude toward those actions [19]. Bandura suggests that eight cognitive mechanisms can contribute to moral disengagement: distortion of consequences, diffusion of responsibility, advantageous comparison, displacement of responsibility, moral justification, euphemistic labeling, dehumanization, and attribution of blame. These mechanisms facilitate engagement in morally questionable actions while maintaining a self-image as a morally upright individual [20, 21]. These processes may affect healthcare professionals’ choices and behavior, particularly in any intensely stressful situations such as dilemmas that routinely exist in ICU nursing. This qualitative study aimed to explore the experiences of nurses working in the ICU regarding moral disengagement.

Methods

Study design

The present qualitative study was conducted using a conventional content analysis approach from June 2024 to December 2024. The study was reported using the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist [22].

Participants and research context

Considering the primary objective of the study, participants were selected through a purposive sampling method among nurses working in ICUs of hospitals affiliated with Tehran University of Medical Sciences (TUMS) who met the inclusion criteria, emphasizing maximum diversity. Eligible participants included nurses with at least a bachelor’s degree in nursing and a minimum of six months experience working in ICUs who expressed a willingness to participate in the study.

Participant recruitment was conducted through a purposive sampling strategy without the use of general advertisements such as posters or public announcements. The research team, already familiar with the ICU wards and nursing personnel, consulted with head nurses and ward managers to help identify nurses who met the inclusion criteria and showed willingness to share their experiences. Based on these consultations, the corresponding author approached eligible participants directly during their shifts.

Initial contact was made in person, where the researcher briefly introduced the study, explained its objectives, and invited them to participate. The primary aim—exploring ethical challenges and the phenomenon of moral disengagement in intensive care nursing—was clearly communicated during this encounter. Participants were given time to consider their participation, and follow-up conversations were arranged as needed. Only those who voluntarily agreed were included in the study, and written informed consent was obtained before any data collection took place.

In qualitative research, an appropriate sample size is achieved when data saturation occurs [23]. Therefore, sampling continued until data saturation was reached. Beginning with the 25th interview, redundant data were identified. Consequently, five additional interviews were conducted to confirm and enrich the data, resulting in a total of 30 interviews.

Data collection

In this study, data were initially collected through in-depth unstructured individual interviews, which were then followed by semi-structured interviews. All interviews were conducted by the corresponding author. The interview guide was specifically developed for the purpose of this study (Supplementary 1).

For the interviews, participants were visited in person at the hospital. After the study objectives were clearly explained, written informed consent was obtained for participation in the study and recording of the interview. Although some participants initially hesitated to share much, the interviewer reassured them that their information would remain confidential and that no names would be used. To further encourage openness, participants were shown qualitative articles and were assured that their comments would be treated anonymously, just as in those articles. None of the 25 participants declined to continue with the interview.

Before starting the interview, a basic guide was prepared. First, each participant completed a demographic questionnaire which included age, sex, marital status, education, and work experience in the ICUs. During the in-person interviews, only the interviewer and the participant were present. All interviews were conducted at the participants’ request in the hospital’s training room, after the nurses’ shift, and at the time they preferred. After establishing contact, an open-ended question was asked to warm up the conversation, such as, “Tell me about your experience of caring in the intensive care unit.” The interview then continued with additional questions based on the interview guide and the participants’ responses.

In this study, data collection began with unstructured interviews to allow participants to freely express their experiences and perspectives on ethical issues and moral disengagement without restrictions. After conducting several initial interviews, the transcripts were carefully reviewed and preliminary themes and patterns were identified. Based on this initial analysis, a semi-structured interview guide was developed to enable a deeper and more focused exploration of important and newly emerged topics. This transition from unstructured to semi-structured interviews was intentionally and data-driven to enhance the conceptual richness of the data while maintaining the flexibility inherent in qualitative methods.

Probing questions were also used as needed throughout the interview.

Interviews lasted an average of 45 to 60 min and were typically conducted in a single session for each participant. However, three participants were interviewed in multiple sessions, depending on the circumstances, to gather more detailed information and complement previous data. In this study, all interviews were audio-recorded with the informed consent of the participants to ensure accurate capture of their responses. Subsequently, each recording was fully transcribed verbatim by the research team to preserve the original content and context of the participants’ statements. This rigorous transcription process aimed to maintain the integrity and authenticity of the data, providing a solid foundation for the qualitative content analysis. The use of verbatim transcripts also allowed for precise coding and thematic extraction, ensuring that participants’ voices were faithfully represented throughout the analysis and reporting phases.

Data analysis

In this study, the Graneheim & Lundman’s (2004)conventional qualitative content analysis approach [24] was used, and data analysis was conducted by continuous comparison. This method avoids the use of predetermined categories, allowing for the extraction of categories directly from the data. The Graneheim & Lundman method consists of five steps: (1) the interviews were transcribed verbatim. (2) the text of each interview was reviewed several times to gain an initial and general understanding. (3) based on the study objectives, important sentences related to the participants’ experiences were identified, and appropriate codes were assigned to each semantic unit. In this study, two researchers (the first author and the corresponding author) were actively involved in the coding process to enhance the accuracy and credibility of the analysis. Initially, both researchers independently coded the interview transcripts to identify meaningful units and assign appropriate codes. Subsequently, multiple sessions were held to review discrepancies and reach consensus on the definitions and classifications of the codes. This iterative, consensus-based process helped ensure the consistency and reliability of the coding. Additionally, feedback sessions with other members of the research team were conducted to further evaluate and validate the codes and categories, which contributed to increasing the credibility of the findings. Although formal statistical measures such as the Kappa coefficient were not calculated (which is uncommon in qualitative research), the combination of independent coding, consensus meetings, and peer debriefing provided a robust mechanism to ensure coder agreement. (4) the codes were classified based on similarities and differences, and subcategories were extracted. (5) the subcategories were compared with each other multiple times to create categories. Once data collection continued to yield repetitions of previous data or categories, indicating that no new information was obtained (saturation occurred), sampling was stopped.

In this study, we considered actions or decisions by nurses to be ethically problematic if they contravened established ethical principles in existing literature and hospital standards, including neglecting patient needs and failing to advocate for patients’ rights. During the data analysis phase, we explicitly differentiated between actual ethical transgressions and ethically acceptable decisions made under competing moral principles. For example, a decision to prioritize one patient’s needs over another due to resource constraints may be ethically acceptable, while neglecting a patient’s basic care needs would represent an ethical failure.

Rigor of study

To ensure the trustworthiness of the data, the criteria set forth by Lincoln and Guba—including credibility, dependability, auditability, transferability, and authenticity—were adhered to [25]. Data credibility was enhanced by the diversity of participants’ characteristics, including age, gender, and work experience in ICUs, as well as the researcher’s prolonged engagement with and immersion in the data. Data were shared with research colleagues for review of codes and categories (peer debriefing). A member check method was also used to ensure the validity of the findings. In this process, the study’s findings, codes, and categories extracted from the data were shared with participants, who expressed their feedback on the consistency of the findings with their own experiences. Transferability was enhanced by providing a rich description of the data collection, analysis processes, and findings. This approach improves the applicability and generalizability of the results to other similar contexts. To ensure authenticity, efforts were made to reflect the participants’ feelings and experiences by increasing the duration of interaction, recording audio, implementing word-for-word transcription during data collection, and providing a simple, clear, and engaging narrative in the presentation of the findings.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Tehran University of Medical Sciences under the code IR.TUMS.FNM.REC.1402.123, in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The objectives of the study were clearly explained to all participants. They were assured of confidentiality, informed of their right to withdraw from the study at any time without consequence, and provided written informed consent. With participants’ permission, interviews were audio-recorded and recordings were deleted after transcription to ensure privacy. In this study, participant confidentiality was fully ensured through data coding, and no personal information revealing identities was included in the report of the results. All sensitive interview content was securely stored and access was limited to the research team members only. Additionally, to enhance the credibility of the findings and ensure the accuracy of the analysis, member checking was employed; in this process, summaries of findings and coding were shared with some participants, and their feedback was considered in revising and validating the results.

Due to the sensitive nature of the study topic, a referral protocol for psychological support was established for participants. This protocol provided referrals to the counseling center at TUMS for individuals who exhibited signs of distress during the interview or reported distress afterward.

Category 1: cognitive justification of unethical behavior

This category encompasses three subcategories: (1) externalization of responsibility, (2) moral-cognitive justification, and (3) positive reframing of the event.

Participants in this study indicated that after engaging in behavior that may be professionally or ethically questionable, they use specific cognitive processes to render that behavior legitimate, justifiable, and acceptable in their minds. These cognitive processes can be categorized into three main forms:

For instance, a nurse with sixteen years’ experience working in the neurosurgery ICU stated:

“Are we not human? This behavior is just a natural reaction.” (P 11).

Based on this, it can be seen that nurses resort to cognitive justification processes to cope with the psychological pressures that arise from behaviors contradicting professional norms. These processes include the externalization of responsibility, moral-cognitive justification, and the positive reframing of the event. These cognitive mechanisms help nurses distance themselves from feelings of guilt while preserving their professional performance, even when their actions conflict with the principles of nursing ethics.

Category 2: protective strategies for personal security

Within this category, two main subcategories were identified: (1) preservation of job position and security, and (2) psychological stabilization.

The current study revealed that nurses, when faced with challenging situations and difficult decision-making, employ strategies aimed primarily at preserving job security and fostering a sense of psychological calm in high-stress environments. Although these strategies sometimes conflict with formal regulations and organizational guidelines, participants view them as an inevitable response to the professional and human demands of the situations. These are actions individuals take to protect their physical, psychological, or professional safety. When faced with stressful or threatening situations at work, a person might use these strategies to shield themselves from potential harm. In doing so, they might temporarily disengage from ethical commitments or justify their suboptimal behavior as necessary for self-protection.

Preservation of job position and security: In this subcategory, nurses reported that they conceal some of their actions or refrain from providing clear explanations for their behaviors to avoid potential consequences such as reprimands, shift changes, or having their professional performance questioned.

For example, a nurse with 12 years’ experience in ICUs stated:

“There are some things that I am sure are in the best interest of the patient, but I keep them between myself and God so that I don’t get punished.” (P 19).

“If you openly admit to disobeying certain orders, they will quickly issue a reprimand… I prefer to keep many of the things I do to myself.” (P 22).

2)Psychological stabilization: In this approach, nurses try to ease their professional conscience and free themselves from anxiety, fear of punishment, or feelings of guilt by reinterpreting their actions and attributing them to values such as fairness, justice, or support for underprivileged patients.

For example, a nurse with a Master’s degree in critical care nursing and two years’ experience in the ICU explained:

“At first, I felt guilty for withholding a patient’s medicine, but now I believe I did the right thing… My conscience is clear because I helped someone who truly needed it.” (P 1).

“There’s no reason to shout it from the rooftops. I dedicate my time to someone who has a chance of recovery… I don’t want to be reprimanded for saying my comment.” (P 13).

Another participant with nine years’ experience in a hospital, three years and four months of which were spent in the ICU, noted that economic conditions and resource shortages have forced them to make decisions that may be considered as violations in an official context:

“If we report it, we will be charged with nursing errors. These are the realities of our work.” (P 20).

The findings indicate that nurses employ specific mental and behavioral mechanisms to survive in challenging and stressful work environments. These strategies not only help ensure job security but also provide a basis for reducing psychological tensions and feelings of internal conflict, thereby facilitating their sustained presence within the healthcare system. Conscious concealment, redefinition of professional ethics, and prioritization of human outcomes over administrative rules are among the main mechanisms in this process.

Category 3: normalization of unconventional practices

Within this category, three subcategories emerged: (1) strategic violation of regulations for survival, (2) reduction of emotional and psychological pressure, and (3) protective framing of behavior.

The present study revealed that nurses, when compelled to perform actions that are against official regulations, reinterpret these actions in their minds and label them as ethical. This reinterpretation primarily aims to prevent professional repercussions, such as reprimands, loss of benefits, or transfers to other departments. Additionally, it serves the important function of alleviating psychological tension and feelings of guilt. This refers to the process through which inappropriate or unethical behaviors gradually become accepted as normal within a community or workgroup. Behaviors that initially seem unusual or unethical slowly become standard practice and are no longer viewed as ethical violations. This process leads to moral disengagement occurring in a systematic and structural way.

Strategic violation of regulations for survival: Some nurses consciously and purposefully violate regulations that they believe are ineffective or even harmful in specific situations. These violations are done in a manner that serves to improve patient conditions while also conserving resources.

“The albumin vial is very expensive. For some patients who really don’t need it, I don’t administer it. I save it for someone who is poor and cannot afford it. At first, I felt guilty, but now I think it’s the right thing to do.” (P 1).

“Sometimes it’s necessary to overlook certain instructions. For example, if a patient requires gavage according to protocol but their condition is critical and this would only cause them distress, I decide not to proceed. In fact, I am helping them rather than breaking the regulation.” (P 24).

2)Reduction of emotional and psychological pressure: Engaging in such behaviors helps nurses free themselves from feelings of guilt, anxiety, and psychological pressure arising from the conflict between guidelines and clinical realities.

“If a patient has a Glasgow Coma Scale of 3 (GCS = 3), why should I spend my time on him? It’s better to focus on someone who has a higher chance of recovery. But if I say this, I will be reprimanded. So, I keep to myself and do the right thing quietly” (P 13).

“Sometimes, when I know a patient’s condition is extremely critical and their chances of survival are almost zero, instead of involving myself in stressful and burdensome care, I minimize their tasks. This way, I experience less stress and burnout.” (P 22).

3)Protective framing of behavior: To avoid being judged by colleagues or reprimanded by managers, nurses have learned to redefine their decisions in terms of “protective behavior,” an approach that helps them attribute responsibility for their actions to external circumstances, thereby shielding themselves from negative reactions.

According to one ICU nurse:

“In our country, ethics is like a double-edged sword. You might do something that benefits the patient, but your colleague may say it’s wrong. So I keep some actions between myself and God so that I don’t get into trouble.” (P 19).

“If you want to be transparent about everything you do, you might be judged or even reported. I’ve learned not to justify certain important decisions; I just implement them. It’s not about being secretive, but there are some things you have to keep to yourself.” (P 10).

Accordingly, in challenging clinical environments, nurses resort to redefining their unconventional practices to cope with systemic and psychological pressures. This redefinition, viewed as a kind of normalization of rule-violating behaviors, manifests as strategic approaches aimed at job preservation, emotional stress reduction, and the establishment of psychological safety.

Ultimately, these mechanisms become ingrained in the minds and behaviors of nurses, evolving into a common pattern for dealing with ambiguous and difficult situations—one that not only seeks to justify actions that prioritize the best interests of patients but also plays a crucial role in safeguarding the professional and psychological stability of healthcare staff.

Discussion

This study aimed to explore the experiences of Iranian nurses working in ICUs regarding moral disengagement. The findings revealed three main categories: “cognitive justification of unethical behavior”, “protective strategies for personal security”, and “normalization of unconventional practices”. moral disengagement and related mechanisms are very important concepts because they may undermine professional integrity, principled patient care, and patient safety. It can also be inferred from these results that moral disengagement arises from complex interactions between individual cognition, organizational culture, and systemic constraints. Therefore, these results can provide valuable insight into the impact of various psychological, environmental, and contextual factors that can ultimately lead to nurses experiencing moral disengagement.

The category “cognitive justification of unethical behavior” includes strategies that ICU nurses use in morally challenging situations to rationalize or cope with unpleasant feelings resulting from unethical decisions. Interviews with participants indicated that these nurses sometimes try to justify their actions, presenting them as the best and most humane decisions possible. They may also evade the perception of their behavior as unethical by attributing responsibility to others or by claiming that similar actions have been taken by peers in comparable cases. Based on these results, it can be stated that in the nursing profession, especially in the ICUs where ethical challenges occur frequently, moral disengagement can occur through the mechanisms of “externalization of responsibility,” “moral-cognitive justification,” and “positive reframing of the event.” These cognitive mechanisms allow individuals to engage in behaviors that deviate from their ethical commitments without feeling guilty or remorseful. This aligns with Bandura’s theory regarding moral disengagement [26, 27]. In Bandura’s framework, the mechanism of moral justification reflects this category, while more specifically, euphemistic labeling corresponds to the subcategories of “positive reframing of the events.” Furthermore, displacement and diffusion of responsibility, along with the attribution of blame, correspond to the subcategory of “externalization of responsibility” in the current study.

Regarding the subcategory of “externalization of responsibility,” it appears that nurses probably ignored their role in performing a particular behavior to escape feelings of guilt, blame, or internal organizational penalties. Instead, they tend to blame others as the primary cause of actions that fall outside ethical norms and frameworks. Supporting this perspective, studies have shown that factors such as inadequate or inappropriate organizational support, decreased job satisfaction, an imbalance between work and personal life, and a negative organizational culture can significantly influence nurses’ tendencies to evade responsibility, ultimately leading them away from their ethical commitments [28–31]. On the other hand, commitment and responsibility are essential to the nursing profession and greatly affect nurses’ performance and the reduction of missed nursing care [32, 33]. Therefore, the use of mechanisms such as “externalization of responsibility” can increase the likelihood of harm to patients.

The subcategory of “moral-cognitive justification” represents the mechanisms that nurses use to rationalize their unethical behaviors, asserting that such behaviors are acceptable and reasonable given the external circumstances. In other words, findings indicated that nurses try to present their unethical behavior in a way that appears ethical to reduce moral pressure. This finding aligns with the results of a study by Tillman et al. (2018), which suggested that individuals are more likely to engage in unethical behavior when they can justify their actions and the resulting outcomes as necessary for achieving a higher goal [34]. Some studies have also shown that nurses working in environments characterized by unethical climates or strict power hierarchies, where nurses feel overlooked in decision-making processes, attribute their unethical practices to these conditions [35, 36]. From this study, it seems that factors such as staff shortages, overcrowding of treatment areas, and a lack of resources and medications are used by nurses to justify their unethical actions. These factors can also be considered barriers to maintaining professional ethics because such limitations significantly affect the ethical standards of nursing care [37]. In fact, under conditions of personnel shortages, each nurse takes responsibility for more patients, which can adversely affect adherence to professional and ethical standards [38, 39].

Concerning the subcategory of “positive reframing of the event,” the findings of this study showed that nurses, when faced with challenging ethical dilemmas in clinical settings, try to interpret their unethical decisions or actions in a positive light to reduce the negative burden and unpleasant feelings associated with them. These results are consistent with Bandura’s theory of moral disengagement which posits that people redefine their unethical behaviors in a way that makes them acceptable and positive in their own minds, ultimately rendering them seemingly ethical. This cognitive restructuring can be accompanied by downplaying the severity of the consequences of their unethical decisions or focusing on the positive aspects of those choices [27].

Another category related to moral disengagement was “protective strategies for personal security,” which included two subcategories of “preservation of job position and security” and “psychological stabilization.” About the former, the findings revealed that job insecurity and fear of organizational repercussions, such as dismissal or reprimand, cause nurses to prioritize hospital policies over principled and independent ethical decision-making when faced with moral conflicts. In other words, nurses may distance themselves from ethical considerations to maintain their job security or psychological well-being. This emphasizes the importance of the impact of job security and psychological peace on nurses’ performance and ethical decision-making. As indicated by the study conducted by Badran et al. (2021), a lower sense of job security among nurses correlates with a heightened feeling of alienation from their profession [40], which can adversely affect their performance in the high-stress environments in which they practice [41, 42]. From these findings, it can be perceived that a lack of job security and uncertain working conditions may place nurses in a situation where they prioritize organizational demands over patient needs, even if their ethical values ​​are compromised.

Regarding the subcategory of “psychological stabilization,” the findings indicated that the interaction of secrecy, the redefinition of ethical challenges, and the prioritization of patient outcomes creates a psychological framework that enables nurses to continue their profession despite the inefficiencies of their work environment. Conversely, the lack of psychological comfort among nurses is one of the factors that can lead to moral disengagement within hospitals. It appears that an unsafe psychological work environment, characterized by decreased job satisfaction and increased burnout, facilitates unethical behaviors [43]. In alignment with these findings, a study by Hirvikallio et al. (2024) demonstrated that a sense of psychological safety in the workplace allows nurses to make decisions with greater commitment in ethically challenging situations [44]. Also, an unethical workplace climate pushes nurses toward normalizing unethical behaviors and engaging in moral neutralization. This tendency seems to manifest through justification and attributing blame to external factors, thereby reducing their feelings of guilt to create a psychological safe margin [45].

The last category identified was “normalization of unconventional practices,” which included the subcategories “strategic violation of regulations for survival,” “reduction of emotional and psychological pressure,” and “protective framing of behavior.” These findings suggest that nurses, when compelled to engage in unethical practices, redefine their actions in a way that makes them appear ethical. This is often done to preserve their position within the hospital and protect themselves from reprimands or penalties. Furthermore, this normalization contributes to a decrease in tension and psychological stress in their work environment.

Concerning the subcategory of “strategic violation of regulations for survival,” the findings indicate that nurses consciously engage in unethical practices under the pretext that complying with some rules isn’t a requirement, making their unethical action seem moral. Supporting this, results from a qualitative study by Kalisch (2006) indicated that nurses consciously refuse to change the position of bed-bound patients, justifying this behavior by complaining about heavy workloads and not having enough time [46]. The findings from another study by Black et al. (2018) also showed that nurses do not report physicians’ errors, justifying this by stating that it jeopardizes their interpersonal and professional relationships [47]. Additionally, in a work environment perceived as unethical, nurses gradually lose sensitivity to ethical principles, reaching a point where they deliberately violate regulations [45].

Regarding “reduction of emotional and psychological pressure” and “protective framing of behavior,” the findings showed that in some cases, unethical behaviors are carried out with the intention of alleviating psychological stress and protecting oneself from laws and regulations. In accordance with these findings, Peyrovi et al. (2016) found that nurses in ICUs often refrain from reporting mistakes that happen in their wards due to fear of punishment, legal repercussions, the potential for judgment, and social stigma, as well as concerns about job security [48].

The key point in reviewing the results of this study is that, in the context of Iranian ICU nurses, moral disengagement can be significantly influenced by various cultural and organizational factors. The hierarchical nature of healthcare institutions in some organizations can lead to a uncertainty regarding the scope of authority and responsibilities [49, 50], where nurses may feel compelled to conform to directives from superiors, even when these directives conflict with their ethical beliefs. This aligns with existing literature suggesting that hierarchical structures can inhibit ethical decision-making and promote moral silence and disengagement among employees [51, 52]. Furthermore, when faced with systemic challenges, such as inadequate staffing, rigid organizational policies, or lack of management support, nurses may resort to moral disengagement as a coping mechanism to reconcile their professional responsibilities with the realities of their work environment and relieve moral distress [53]. The ethical climate within healthcare organizations also plays a crucial role in shaping the moral framework of nurses [54]. In restrictive systems, where organizational culture may prioritize efficiency over ethical considerations, nurses may feel pressured to disengage from their moral responsibilities to meet institutional demands. This highlights the need for healthcare organizations to foster an ethical climate that encourages open dialogue and supports nurses in addressing moral dilemmas.

Moreover, while this study primarily focuses on ICU nurses, it is important to consider that similar cognitive mechanisms of moral disengagement may also be present among other healthcare professionals, such as ICU physicians, who face comparable challenges and distress [55, 56]. Therefore, future research could further explore this dynamic.

Strength and limitations

One of the main challenges in this study was the sensitive nature of discussing ethical mistakes and moral issues. Interviewing involved asking participants to reflect on potentially distressing experiences, which may have resulted in discomfort or reluctance to share thoughts honestly. Some nurses might have exhibited selective data bias due to fear of judgment or repercussions, potentially affecting the richness and depth of the collected data. To mitigate this problem, we employed a supportive interview process to make participants feel secure and respected, during the interviews. A skilled interviewer clearly informed participants of the purpose of the study as well as participant information confidentiality through reference to a similar qualitative research. We also established trust by providing a quiet, secure environment where only the interviewer and participant were present. Participants were encouraged to provide more detailed responses by highlighting the potential contribution of the study’s findings on nursing practice and patient safety. Furthermore, a key critique of qualitative studies is the influence of analysts’ preconceptions and judgments. In this study, we counteracted this concern through methods such as concurrent analysis by multiple researchers, peer debriefing, as well as member checking.

This research investigated moral disengagement in ICU nurses, providing evidence and insights for future studies and interventions. Given the findings reveal moral disengagement happens among ICU nurses, especially in difficult decision-making and value conflicts, it is recommended that future studies adopt mixed methodologies (combining qualitative and quantitative approaches) to create a more holistic perspective of personal, organizational, and contextual factors influencing moral disengagement.

In addition, fear of judgment, as well as a lack of psychological safety, was reported by some participants, indicating a requirement for interventions to support “psychological safety” among nursing teams. These interventions may involve group moral debriefing sessions, workshops in ethical dialogue skills, as well as ethics-centered mentorship for head nurses and supervisors. Also, based on the data indicating a conflict between professional values and systemic constraints, policymakers should review supportive structures for promoting ethical decision-making processes in nursing practices. This necessitates a reassessment of clinical guidelines, designing supportive policies for moral disclosure, and minimizing factors contributing to “ethical silence” within healthcare organizations.

Finally, it is recommended that the development of continuing education programs for nurses extend beyond theoretical training to focus on simulating real ethical situations, enhancing ethical analysis skills, and fostering interdisciplinary dialogues. These kinds of education programs can fill the gap in bridging ethical knowledge to practice, hence promoting a culture of accountability as well as ethical sensitivity in ICUs.

Conclusion

The findings of this study indicate that nurses and healthcare staff employ three main mechanisms to justify their unethical behaviors and engage in moral disengagement: “cognitive justification of unethical behavior,” “protective strategies for personal security,” and “normalization of unconventional practices.” These mechanisms assist nurses in avoiding guilt, anxiety, and remorse related to their unethical actions, allowing them to achieve a certain level of psychological peace.

In the first category, “cognitive justification of unethical behavior,” nurses try to make these behaviors appear rational and acceptable by redefining and reinterpreting their actions. For example, some nurses stated that in critical situations, they justify inappropriate ethical decisions as necessary measures to save patients’ lives. This mechanism enables them to evade the moral pressure caused by these decisions. In the second category, “protective strategies for personal security,” nurses use methods that help them maintain their job positions and psychological well-being. Many participants noted that to avoid negative consequences from their unethical behaviors, they consciously conform their behaviors to professional principles, even when such conformity has not actually taken place. In this process, nurses attempt to distance themselves from potential punishment or professional repercussions. Finally, in the third category, “normalization of unconventional practices,” nurses and healthcare staff redefine their behaviors in such a way that these actions are informally recognized as acceptable within the healthcare environment. This change in the interpretation of behaviors allows them to reduce feelings of guilt and anxiety related to their unconventional practices. For example, some nurses consider ethical rules to be ineffective or impractical, especially under psychological pressure, leading them to violate these rules in order to morally justify their decisions.

These processes not only contribute to alleviating feelings of guilt and anxiety but also serve as strategies for creating a psychological and professional buffer in healthcare settings. By employing these approaches, nurses can experience greater stability in their profession when faced with challenging situations, enabling them to more effectively mitigate occupational stress.
