Microbial fermentation of soybeans: synergistic enhancement of bioactivity and sensory properties
Authors: Ruizhen Gong, Zsolt Zalán, Jiajia Song, Huayi Suo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Microbial fermentation plays a crucial role in enhancing the functional and sensory qualities of soybean-based products. This review summarizes the biotransformation of proteins, phenolics, lipids, and other substrates during fermentation and the associated improvements in antioxidant, antihypertensive, antimicrobial, and antidiabetic activities. The potential mechanisms of action are discussed with reference to representative fermentation-derived metabolites. In addition, the effects of fermentation on flavor modulation are reviewed, including the reduction of bitterness and enhancement of umami perception, highlighting the contribution of amino acids and small peptides to overall sensory improvement. Future research should focus on elucidating the mechanistic links between fermentation-derived compounds and functional properties through strain improvement and multi-omics approaches, thereby providing a scientific foundation for the development of value-added fermented soybean products.

Introduction

Soybeans (Glycine max) are a substantial global dietary component and are rich in protein, plant-based fats, fiber, and various vitamins and minerals (Dwevedi & Kayastha, 2011). Being a key raw material in the modern food industry, soybean products such as tofu, soy milk, and dried bean curd have a vital position in Asian diets. Microbial fermentation, an ancient and effective food processing method, is extensively used worldwide for producing foods such as Chinese fermented black beans, tofu, Japanese miso, Korean soybean paste, Indonesian tempeh, and Indian kinema. In recent years, microbial fermentation has been increasingly applied to enhance the bioactivity and sensory characteristics of soybeans, thereby improving their nutritional value and consumer appeal.

During fermentation, microorganisms secrete various enzymes and modulate their metabolic pathways to initiate a series of complex biochemical reactions within the soybean matrix. These processes release or synthesize a broad spectrum of compounds with nutritional and functional properties, significantly enhancing their bioavailability and health benefits. For example, fermentation facilitates the accumulation of beneficial compounds such as amino acids, free isoflavones, minerals, and vitamins (Liu et al., 2022), while effectively reducing the levels of anti-nutritional factors like phytic acid and saponins, thus improving the overall efficiency of nutrient absorption (Samtiya et al., 2020). In addition, fermentation of soybean meal by Bacillus subtilis can degrade antigenic proteins such as trypsin inhibitors and β-conglycinin, thereby reducing the risk of allergic reactions and immune activation (Seo & Cho, 2016). Fermentation also markedly promotes the formation of various bioactive substances with physiological regulatory functions. In terms of antioxidant mechanisms, large amounts of polyphenols, flavonoids, and low-molecular-weight antioxidant peptides are generated during fermentation, significantly enhancing the capacity to scavenge ABTS radicals (Ma et al., 2023; Shin et al., 2014). In the context of glucose metabolism regulation, soybean products fermented by Aspergillus oryzae, Actinomucor elegans, and Rhizopus exhibit enhanced α-glucosidase inhibitory activity, which helps to slow down carbohydrate breakdown and glucose absorption (Chen et al., 2007). Moreover, Enterococcus faecium EBD1-fermented soybeans can produce angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitory peptides, contributing to blood pressure regulation (Daliri et al., 2021). Certain fermentative microorganisms are also capable of synthesizing metabolites with broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity, which help maintain intestinal microbiota homeostasis and reduce the risk of pathogenic invasion (Huang et al., 2023).

In addition, microbial fermentation significantly improves the sensory quality of soybean products through multi-target biotransformation. In terms of taste, fermentation enhances umami and suppresses bitterness. In soy sauce production from fermented soybeans, microbial proteases hydrolyze soybean proteins to release umami amino acids such as glutamic acid and small-molecule umami peptides, including hydrophobic peptide segments containing Glu/Asp residues (Zhu et al., 2021). On the other hand, carboxypeptidases secreted by B. subtilis during solid-state fermentation can specifically degrade hydrophobic bitter peptides (Yin et al., 2019), while organic acids and esters generated during fermentation can also mask residual bitterness (Bertelsen et al., 2018). Regarding aroma, fermentation reshapes the volatile compound profile. Certain lactic acid bacteria promote the synthesis of esters (e.g., ethyl acetate), ketones (e.g., diacetyl), and pyrazines, contributing fruity, buttery, and roasted aromas (Wang et al., 2022, Wätjen et al., 2023)). Meanwhile, off-flavor aldehydes derived from lipoxygenase activity—such as hexanal and 2-pentylfuran—are metabolized into alcohols, effectively eliminating beany odor (Keong et al., 2023). These sensory optimizations impart a flavor complexity to fermented soybean products that is distinct from traditional soy-based foods, providing a promising biotechnological approach for the flavor design of plant-based products.

This review provides a comprehensive perspective on the production and research of fermented soybean products. It systematically discusses the bioactivity and sensory properties of fermented soybeans, thereby deepening our understanding and laying the groundwork for future development in this field.

Biological activity of fermented soybeans

Antioxidant activity

Antioxidant peptides

During soybean fermentation, microbial proteases hydrolyze soybean proteins to release a variety of peptides with antioxidant activity. Owing to their strong free radical scavenging capacity and ability to protect cells from oxidative damage, these peptides have emerged as a focal point in recent research. The accumulation of antioxidant peptides can be effectively promoted by modulating fermentation parameters such as microbial strains, duration, and temperature (Tonolo et al., 2019). In vitro studies have shown that peptides derived from fermented soybeans, such as SHECN and SHCMN, exhibit concentration-dependent scavenging activity against DPPH (2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl) and ABTS+ (2,2′-azino-bis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonicacid)) radicals. These peptides also enhance the activity of antioxidant enzymes, including catalase (CAT) and superoxide dismutase (SOD), thereby facilitating the repair of oxidatively damaged cells and restoration of mitochondrial membrane potential (Dong et al., 2023). Building upon these findings, Ma et al. employed co-fermentation of soybean meal (12 % w/w, 2:1 ratio, 48 h) using Lactobacillus spp. and Aspergillus oryzae (L6) to obtain low molecular weight (<1 kDa) antioxidant peptides, which showed an 85.8 % inhibition rate against AAPH-induced hemolysis. These peptides significantly suppressed reactive oxygen species (ROS) and malondialdehyde (MDA) production, maintained glutathione (GSH) levels, and upregulated the activities of SOD, glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px), and CAT, thereby enhancing the cellular antioxidant defense system (Ma et al., 2023). At the molecular level, Tonolo et al. applied LC-MS/MS combined with molecular docking analysis to identify 11 potential bioactive peptide sequences from Soymilk co-fermented with Lactobacillus bulgaricus and Streptococcus thermophilus. Among them, the peptide S-10-S exhibited the strongest activation of the Keap1/Nrf2 signaling pathway in Caco-2 cells, effectively inducing the expression of antioxidant response element (ARE)-regulated enzymes including SOD1, thioredoxin reductase 1 (TrxR1), glutathione reductase (GR), and NAD(P)H quinone dehydrogenase 1 (NQO1) (Tonolo et al., 2020).

In summary, antioxidant peptides generated during microbial fermentation exhibit not only potent radical scavenging properties but also the ability to activate endogenous antioxidant enzyme systems, particularly via the Keap1/Nrf2 pathway. These multifaceted mechanisms contribute to the enhanced antioxidant potential of fermented soy products and provide a scientific basis for their development as functional foods.

Phenolic compounds

Polyphenolic compounds can directly scavenge free radicals, thereby effectively preventing oxidative damage to cells. Microbial fermentation, as an important food processing technique, is widely recognized for its ability to significantly enhance the phenolic content in soybeans, thus increasing their antioxidant activity. For example, Jeon et al. isolated a strain of B. subtilis P229 from the traditional Korean fermented soybean product cheonggukjang. When inoculated at 106 CFU/g and fermented at 37 °C for 48 h, the resulting product was extracted with 70 % ethanol. The total phenolic content (TPC) of the fermented product was found to be 2.3 times higher than that of unfermented soybeans, indicating that fermentation significantly promoted polyphenol accumulation (Jeon et al., 2018). Similarly, in soy yogurt co-fermented with Saccharomyces cerevisiae CNCM I-745 and Lactiplantibacillus plantarum KU985432, a notable increase in TPC was also observed (Mehaya et al., 2023). In addition to promoting overall polyphenol accumulation, enzymatic reactions during fermentation play a key role in enhancing the antioxidant potential of soybeans. Brijwani et al. demonstrated that solid-state fermentation of soybeans inoculated with 10 % (v/v) Trichoderma reesei and Aspergillus oryzae (107 spores/mL) led to a significant increase in β-glucosidase activity (Brijwani et al., 2010). This enzyme catalyzes the conversion of glycosylated polyphenols into their aglycone forms, which exhibit higher bioavailability in the human intestine and are closely associated with antioxidant activity (Khosravi & Razavi, 2021). A similar trend was observed in the production of fermented soy sauce (clear-type): the contents of total phenolics, total flavonoids, and the conversion rate of isoflavone glycosides were significantly increased, resulting in elevated levels of aglycones (Piao & Eun, 2020). Furthermore, Zhang and Yu conducted solid-state fermentation under conditions of 30 °C, 40 % moisture content, 5 % inoculation rate, 6 days of incubation, and 20-mesh particle size. Under these parameters, most glycosylated isoflavones were efficiently converted into their more bioactive aglycone forms, with a conversion efficiency reaching 85.30 %, significantly enhancing the antioxidant capacity of the fermented soybeans (Zhang & Yu, 2019). In summary, microbial fermentation enhances the antioxidant potential of soybean products through a dual mechanism: by promoting the overall accumulation of polyphenolic compounds and by increasing the activity of key enzymes responsible for converting glycosylated forms into more bioavailable aglycones.

Fermentation-derived metabolites

In addition to known bioactive components such as peptides and polyphenols, studies have shown that other metabolites generated by microorganisms during fermentation also exhibit antioxidant activity. The functionality of these compounds is influenced by factors including microbial strains, fermentation conditions, substrate type, and microbial interactions. Their mechanisms of action primarily involve free radical scavenging, activation of endogenous antioxidant enzyme systems, and inhibition of lipid peroxidation.

For instance, Lactobacillus YF-L903 was inoculated at 0.02 % (w/v) into 100 mL of soymilk and fermented at 42 °C until titratable acidity reached 60 T. The resulting fermented product significantly enhanced DPPH and ABTS radical scavenging activities and increased the activity of SOD in Caco-2 cells. In an in vivo study, oral administration of the fermented product to DSS-induced colitis model mice for 28 days led to a significant increase in total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC) and total SOD content in serum and colonic tissues, along with a decrease in MDA levels, further confirming its potential in alleviating oxidative stress in vivo (Sun et al., 2023). Similar antioxidant effects were observed in a system fermented with Lactobacillus acidophilus MF204. Soymilk fermented for 18 h was administered to rats fed a high-fat diet, resulting in significant increases in serum SOD and GSH-Px activities, as well as elevated T-AOC levels. These findings suggest that the fermented product may alleviate oxidative damage caused by lipid metabolism disorders by modulating endogenous antioxidant enzyme systems (Chen et al., 2017). This highlights the regulatory role of metabolites from lactic acid bacteria–fermented soymilk in antioxidant defense mechanisms in vivo.

Beyond lactic acid bacteria, Bacillus species have also demonstrated promising antioxidant potential during soybean fermentation. B. amyloliquefaciens SWJS22 was inoculated at 1 % (w/v) into soybean meal and subjected to shaking fermentation at 37 °C and 120 rpm for 54 h, followed by static incubation at 50 °C for 6 h. The resulting product exhibited enhanced in vitro DPPH radical scavenging and oxygen radical absorbance capacity (ORAC). In a D-galactose-induced aging mouse model, oral administration of 250 mg/kg of the fermented product for 42 days significantly increased SOD, CAT, and GSH-Px activities in serum and liver, indicating its potential in enhancing systemic antioxidant defense and mitigating oxidative stress (Yang et al., 2019). Additionally, Sanjukta et al. reported that B. subtilis MTCC5480 and MTCC1747, used in the fermentation of the soybean product Kinema, increased DPPH and superoxide anion (O₂−) scavenging activities up to 24-fold within 24 h compared to the unfermented control. The antioxidant activity of methanol extracts was superior to that of water extracts, likely due to better solubility of hydrophobic antioxidant compounds in organic solvents (Sanjukta et al., 2015). Bacillus natto also showed similar effects during black soybean fermentation. Hu et al. reported that fermentation at 37 °C and 85 % humidity with an inoculum size of 6–7 log CFU/g for 48 h led to a DPPH scavenging rate of 94 %, which was 2.5 times that of the unfermented control, underscoring the significant role of Bacillus species in enhancing the antioxidant capacity of fermented soybean products (Hu et al., 2010). At the application level, the antioxidant potential of microbial fermentation products has also been validated in animal feed. Yan et al. employed an anaerobic solid-state fermentation of soybean meal for 3 days at 30–40 °C using a mixed microbial culture composed of B. subtilis, lactic acid bacteria, and yeast. When the fermented product was included at 4 % in the diet of weaned piglets for 13 consecutive days, serum levels of T-AOC, SOD, CAT, and GSH-Px were significantly increased, while MDA levels were reduced, confirming the antioxidant potential of the fermented product in practical applications (Yan et al., 2022).

In summary, the metabolites produced by lactic acid bacteria and Bacillus species during soybean fermentation not only exhibit strong in vitro free radical scavenging activity but also activate multiple antioxidant enzymes in vivo to maintain redox homeostasis. These findings provide theoretical support for the development of soybean fermentation products with antioxidant functionality and offer practical guidance for the application of microbial fermentation metabolites in nutritional interventions. Antihypertensive activity

Antihypertensive peptides

Angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) plays a central role in blood pressure regulation by catalyzing the conversion of angiotensin I to angiotensin II. Abnormal enhancement of ACE activity is considered a key factor in the pathogenesis of hypertension (Khurana & Goswami, 2022). Therefore, the development of natural peptides with ACE inhibitory activity is regarded as an important strategy for hypertension intervention. Soybean proteins, particularly the major storage proteins 7S β-conglycinin and 11S glycinin, can release ACE inhibitory peptides under specific conditions. Compared with in vitro enzymatic hydrolysis, microbial fermentation offers distinct advantages in modulating protein degradation pathways and enhancing the release efficiency of bioactive peptides, Notably, fermentation systems dominated by bacteria tend to produce low-molecular-weight peptides (1–4.5 kDa) that generally exhibit stronger ACE inhibitory activity (Handa et al., 2020). For example, after 2 days of storage, soybeans fermented with bacteria or fungi showed a 40 % and 17 % increase in ACE inhibitory activity, respectively, compared to raw soybeans, suggesting a superior peptide-yielding capability in bacterial fermentation. This difference may be attributed to the substrate specificity of microbial proteases. Bacterial proteases preferentially hydrolyze 11S glycinin, releasing short peptides enriched in hydrophobic amino acids such as leucine and isoleucine. These residues are critical for constructing ACE inhibitory peptides, as they enhance binding affinity to the active center of ACE (Handa et al., 2020). In a study by Daliri et al., soybean fermented with Enterococcus faecium EBD1 (2 × 108 CFU/mL) at 37 °C for 48 h showed a significant increase in ACE inhibition rate from 40 % ± 2 % to 80 % ± 5 %. Several dipeptides, including IL, VF, FD, AT, LT, LP, and KV, were identified as major contributors to this enhanced activity (Daliri et al., 2021). Similar ACE inhibitory peptides have also been identified in the traditional fermented soybean product Tofuyo. Extracts of Tofuyo exhibited strong ACE inhibitory activity with an IC₅₀ of 1.77 mg/mL. It was found that peptides such as Ile-Pro-Leu (IPL) and Thr-Leu (TL) were the main functional components. Molecular docking analysis suggested that the C-terminal leucine of TL interacts with Phe512 and Tyr523 of the ACE active site via hydrophobic interactions, while the N-terminal threonine forms hydrogen bonds with His353, effectively blocking substrate binding to ACE (Kuba et al., 2003).

In summary, microbial fermentation can significantly enhance the generation of ACE inhibitory peptides from soybeans, likely due to the specific hydrolysis of storage proteins, especially the release of short peptides rich in hydrophobic amino acids that have a high affinity for ACE binding and inhibition. These findings provide theoretical support for the development of natural antihypertensive functional foods. However, most studies to date have been limited to in vitro analyses, with a lack of systematic in vivo validation. Future research should incorporate animal or clinical studies to evaluate the bioavailability and mechanism of action of these peptides in blood pressure regulation, thereby facilitating their translation from experimental findings to practical applications.

Isoflavones

Isoflavones are a class of naturally occurring polyphenolic compounds with phytoestrogenic activity that have been demonstrated to participate in blood pressure regulation. During microbial fermentation of soybeans, specific enzymatic reactions—particularly the catalytic activity of β-glucosidase—play a pivotal role in the conversion and enhancement of the bioactivity of isoflavones. Guadamuro et al. cloned five β-glucosidase-encoding genes from Leuconostoc mesenteroides IPLA 36007 into Escherichia coli and achieved high expression levels. The recombinant enzymes efficiently hydrolyzed isoflavone glycosides, releasing their corresponding aglycones, and thus offered a feasible strategy for producing fermented soybean products enriched in active aglycone forms (Guadamuro et al., 2017). Similarly, Donkor and Shah reported that fermentation of soy protein isolate with Lactobacillus acidophilus LAFTI L10, Bifidobacterium animalis subsp. lactis LAFTI B94, and Lacticaseibacillus casei LAFTI L26 at 37 °C for 48 h significantly increased β-glucosidase activity, promoting the transformation of glycosidic isoflavones into aglycones with enhanced bioactivity (Donkor & Shah, 2008). Lodha et al. inoculated Lactobacillus strains M4, KGL3A, M16, and M9 (3 % v/v) into two commercial soymilks (NRC37 and Sofit) and cultured them at 37 °C. After 12 h, β-glucosidase activity reached 35.92–60.63 U/mL. By 24 h, most isoflavone glycosides had been efficiently converted into their aglycone forms, with genistin and daidzin conversion rates reaching 92.74 % and 93.31 % in NRC37, and 95.25 % and 93.83 % in Sofit, respectively (Lodha et al., 2021).

Beyond enzymatic hydrolysis during fermentation, isoflavones can also be further metabolized by gut microbiota into S-equol—a metabolite with stronger estrogenic activity and cardioprotective effects. Raimondi et al. screened 22 strains of bifidobacteria representing eight major human-resident species and found that most exhibited cell-associated β-glucosidase activity capable of converting daidzin to daidzein, which could subsequently be metabolized into S-equol. This process enhances both the bioavailability and physiological functionality of isoflavones (Raimondi et al., 2009). Similarly, another study demonstrated that various Lactobacillus strains not only increased the solubility and release of isoflavones during fermentation but also modulated the gut microbiota to promote their bioconversion into S-equol (Rekha & Vijayalakshmi, 2010). Notably, isoflavones also modulate lipid metabolism by regulating the AKT/mTORC1 signaling pathway, which may in turn influence blood pressure levels. Huang et al. showed that daidzein and genistein suppressed AKT phosphorylation in HepG2 cells, thereby reducing the activation of mTORC1 and its downstream target S6 ribosomal protein. This resulted in decreased lipid synthesis, enhanced lipolysis, and stimulated β-oxidation of fatty acids. These effects were further confirmed in a high-fat diet-induced obese rat model, indicating that isoflavones may indirectly regulate blood pressure by improving lipid metabolism (Huang et al., 2016).

In summary, isoflavones in soybeans can be efficiently converted into bioactive aglycones during microbial fermentation through β-glucosidase activity and further metabolized into functional compounds such as S-equol by gut microbiota. Additionally, their capacity to modulate lipid metabolic pathways may contribute to blood pressure regulation, highlighting their potential in the development of functional foods targeting cardiovascular health.

Lipid metabolites

Lipids are closely associated with hypertension. High plasma levels of LDL-C and TG can deposit on arterial walls, leading to atherosclerotic plaque formation. This, in turn, causes arterial narrowing and hardening, increases peripheral vascular resistance, and elevates blood pressure.When a high-fat diet (HFD) supplemented with 2.5 % and 5 % (v/w) B. subtilis natto (Takano Foods Co., Ltd.) was fed to C57BL/6 J mice, plasma free isoflavone levels significantly increased. Additionally, the mRNA levels of lipid metabolism-related genes Fas, Me, and G6pdx (all fatty acid synthesis pathway enzymes), as well as the transcription factor Srebp-1c in the liver, decreased. Conversely, the mRNA levels of Cpt1, which is involved in fatty acid β-oxidation, increased after 4 weeks. This modulation led to the inhibition of fatty acid synthesis and the promotion of fatty acid degradation in the liver, thereby improving lipid metabolism and indirectly affecting blood pressure levels (Kushida et al., 2018). Similarly, when steamed soybeans (koji) were fermented with 2 % (v/w) B. amyloliquefaciens CJ 14–6 at 37 °C for 36 h, the fermented product was found to alleviate HFD-induced dyslipidemia in rats by reducing TG, TC, and LDL-C levels while increasing HDL-C levels. This fermentation process also reduced adipocyte hypertrophy and inhibited the mRNA expression of key adipocyte lipid synthesis-related enzymes, including Acc, Fas, and G6pdx, effectively mitigating HFD-induced obesity and hypertension (Park et al., 2021).

These discoveries allow us to better understand how microbial fermentation enhances the health benefits of foods, particularly for cardiovascular health. By modulating lipid metabolism and reducing risk factors associated with hypertension, fermentation-derived bioactive compounds offer promising prospects for the development of functional foods and nutritional supplements aimed at preventing and managing chronic diseases such as hypertension. Antimicrobial activity

During soybean fermentation, specific microorganisms can produce antimicrobial compounds, including antimicrobial peptides and bacteriocin-like substances, thereby enhancing the microbial safety and preservation potential of fermented products. For instance, Lactococcus sp. GM005, isolated from miso, was capable of efficiently producing a bacteriocin at 30 °C under constant pH 6.0, with an antimicrobial activity eightfold higher than that observed under uncontrolled pH conditions. The bacteriocin was rich in hydrophobic amino acids and lanthionine-like residues, and exhibited potent bactericidal activity against Lactobacillus sakei JCM1157T (Onda et al., 2003). In addition to lactic acid bacteria, B. subtilis species—commonly present in soybean fermentation—have demonstrated significant antimicrobial potential in multiple studies. For example, B. subtilis SCK-2, isolated from the Korean traditional fermented soybean paste Kyeopjang, produced an antimicrobial peptide, AMP IC-1, which exhibited narrow-spectrum inhibition specifically against B. cereus. Compared with BSAP-254, a previously identified peptide from traditionally fermented soybean paste, AMP IC-1 showed superior thermal stability and pH tolerance, and possessed a unique peptide profile despite structural similarity. A partial synergistic effect was observed when AMP IC-1 was used in combination with BSAP-254, and both peptides displayed no hemolytic activity. These findings suggest that AMP IC-1 could be a promising candidate for antimicrobial agents aimed at preventing B. cereus-related foodborne infections in humans (Yeo et al., 2011). Similarly, B. subtilis HJ18–4, isolated from buckwheat sokseongjang, exhibited broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity with strong inhibition against B. cereus. This strain not only suppressed bacterial growth but also significantly downregulated the expression of virulence-related genes such as groEL, nheA, nheC, and entFM. Moreover, water extracts from soybeans fermented with B. subtilis HJ18–4 demonstrated both antibacterial and anti-toxin activities, highlighting its potential as a biocontrol agent in the fermented soybean industry (Seon et al., 2014). In vivo validation further supports the functional potential of antimicrobial peptides derived from fermented soybeans. B. subtilis E20 was reported to produce three antimicrobial peptides during soybean meal fermentation, among which FSB-AMP (HTSKALLDMLKRLGK) exhibited the strongest activity against Vibrio alginolyticus and Vibrio parahaemolyticus with a minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of 72.5 μM. The peptide exerted its antimicrobial effect by disrupting bacterial cell membranes, increasing membrane permeability, and ultimately causing cell death. Dietary supplementation of FSB-AMP or E20-fermented soybean meal (FSBM) in Litopenaeus vannamei significantly enhanced resistance to V. parahaemolyticus infection, suggesting its value as an effective biofunctional additive in aquaculture (Cheng et al., 2017).

In summary, functional microbial strains involved in soybean fermentation, particularly those belonging to the genera Lactococcus and Bacillus, are capable of synthesizing structurally diverse antimicrobial peptides and bacteriocins. These compounds exhibit promising applications in food preservation, microbial safety control, and animal health. The accumulated evidence provides a theoretical and technical foundation for the functionalization and value-added utilization of fermented soybean products.

Antidiabetic activity

Bioactive peptides

Bioactive peptides generated during soybean fermentation play a key role in modulating glucose metabolism. Studies have demonstrated that these peptides not only participate in the regulation of glucose homeostasis but also exert multifaceted physiological effects in diabetes intervention, potentially through modulation of the host gut microbiota. Yu et al. isolated two functional peptides, VY and SFLLR, from fermented soybean paste, and evaluated their hypoglycemic effects in a high-fat diet-induced mouse model. Notably, oral administration of SFLLR at a dose of 50 mg/kg for 8 consecutive weeks significantly reduced blood glucose levels. The underlying mechanism is likely associated with the activation of insulin and AMPK signaling pathways and the downregulation of key gluconeogenic enzymes, namely phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase (PEPCK) and glucose-6-phosphatase (G6PC), thereby improving insulin sensitivity and suppressing hepatic glucose output. Moreover, SFLLR markedly ameliorated gut microbiota dysbiosis induced by the high-fat diet, decreasing the relative abundance of potential pathogenic genera such as Clostridium, Oscillibacter, and Deferribacter, while increasing beneficial bacteria including Alistipes, Lactobacillus, Faecalibaculum, Akkermansia, and Bifidobacterium, suggesting strong microbiota-modulating capabilities (Yu et al., 2023). This microbial shift may contribute to metabolic balance and further enhance glucose homeostasis. In another study, Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus M9 (MTCC 25516) was used to ferment soybean protein isolate (SPI) at 37 °C for 48 h, leading to the isolation and purification of a potential antidiabetic peptide, GALGVAIPGCPETFEEPQEQSNRR. Previous research has confirmed the antidiabetic activity of dipeptides such as VA, PE, IP, GA, EP, AL, ET, GV, NR, PG, PQ, QE, QS, RR, and TF. The presence of these functional dipeptide fragments within GALGVAIPGCPETFEEPQEQSNRR suggests its potential antidiabetic effects (Vadher et al., 2025). However, further studies are required to elucidate its specific mechanisms and in vivo bioactivity. Overall, current research has preliminarily revealed the antidiabetic potential of specific peptides derived from fermented soybeans. These peptides may influence glucose metabolism via direct or indirect pathways, offering a theoretical foundation for the development of natural, safe, and multifunctional food ingredients. Future studies should focus on the mechanisms of action, stability, and bioavailability of these peptides in humans to facilitate their application in diabetes prevention and management.

Fermentation-derived metabolites

In addition to bioactive peptides, fermented soybean products have demonstrated glucose-lowering potential through inhibition of α-glucosidase activity. This enzyme, which catalyzes the hydrolysis of complex carbohydrates into glucose in the small intestine, is a critical target in postprandial blood glucose control for type 2 diabetes management. Recent studies have confirmed that soybeans fermented by specific microbes exhibit significant α-glucosidase inhibitory activity, suggesting their potential to delay carbohydrate digestion and absorption. For example, Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus M9 (MTCC 25516) fermentation of SPI at 37 °C for 48 h resulted in a product exhibiting 61.94 % α-glucosidase inhibition, confirming the feasibility of lactic acid bacterial fermentation in conferring hypoglycemic properties. Fungi have also shown significant promise in enhancing the hypoglycemic potential of fermented soybeans. In one study, Aspergillus oryzae, Actinomucor elegans, and Rhizopus arrhizus were used to ferment cooked black soybeans for 60 h with an initial inoculum of 106 spores. Among the three strains, fermentation by A. oryzae produced the highest α-glucosidase inhibitory activity (Chen et al., 2007). During the subsequent maturation phase, the fermented soybeans were treated with varying salt concentrations (5.0 %, 7.5 %, 10.0 %, and 12.5 %). The strongest inhibitory activity was observed under 5.0 % and 7.5 % salinity conditions in the A. oryzae group. This enhanced activity may be attributed to the superior salt tolerance and metabolic adaptability of A. oryzae under low water activity environments, which allow continuous production of functional metabolites. Additionally, salts such as NaCl, KCl, and MgCl₂ have been reported to promote conidiophore formation in A. oryzae, improving its survival and secondary metabolism in saline conditions and enhancing the synthesis of bioactive compounds (Song et al., 2001). In contrast, the other two fungal strains exhibited limited metabolic activity and functional product generation across all salt concentrations.

Further investigations have explored the use of edible mushroom mycelia in soybean fermentation for α-glucosidase inhibition. Suruga et al. fermented soybeans with mycelia of Ganoderma lucidum, Hericium erinaceus, and Hohenbuehelia ramosum at 25 °C for approximately 4 weeks. All three fermentation products exhibited significant α-glucosidase inhibitory activity in assays using p-nitrophenyl-α-D-glucopyranoside (pNPG) as the substrate, with inhibition rates of 62.3 %, 71.3 %, and 79.5 %, respectively. The superior activity observed in H. erinaceus and H. ramosum was likely related to higher levels of daidzein in their fermentation products. Considering that yeast α-glucosidase differs from mammalian enzymes, further experiments employed rat intestinal α-glucosidase with maltose and sucrose as physiologically relevant substrates. These sugars are naturally hydrolyzed by α-glucosidase in the mammalian intestine, providing a more accurate representation of digestive processes. Results showed that G. lucidum fermentation products achieved 55.9 % inhibition using maltose as the substrate, significantly outperforming H. erinaceus (10.6 %) and H. ramosum (11.6 %). Using sucrose as the substrate, inhibition rates were 8.1 % for G. lucidum, 6.0 % for H. erinaceus, and 1.5 % for H. ramosum (Suruga et al., 2020). These differences may be attributed not only to daidzein content but also to other bioactive metabolites produced by G. lucidum, such as hydroxylated daidzein derivatives, which may exhibit stronger inhibitory activity against mammalian α-glucosidase.

In summary, microbial fermentation significantly enhances the α-glucosidase inhibitory activity of soybean products. This effect is influenced by microbial strains and fermentation parameters including temperature, time, and salinity. These findings provide theoretical support for the hypoglycemic function of fermented soy products and offer new directions for the development of natural α-glucosidase inhibitors. Sensory quality changes of fermented soybeans

Taste improvement

Umami

Umami peptides

Umami is one of the representative flavor characteristics of fermented soybean products, and umami peptides are widely recognized as important contributors to umami perception. In a study conducted by Mu et al., solid-state fermentation of soybean meal by Aspergillus oryzae was performed with the addition of soybean protein isolate hydrolysate (SPIH). This supplementation significantly enhanced the activities of neutral protease, alkaline protease, acid protease, aminopeptidase, and glutaminase secreted by A. oryzae during fermentation, thereby promoting the accumulation of free amino acids and peptide precursors associated with umami. Further analysis revealed that SPIH addition markedly increased the levels of Asp-containing peptides, Glu-containing peptides, and four umami-enhancing peptides-Met-Glu (ME), Glu-Leu (EL), Glu-Ala (EA), and Glu-Val (EV)—by 10.0 %, 6.6 %, 54.5 %, 75.2 %, 232.0 %, and 17.5 %, respectively (Mu et al., 2025). Notably, Cao et al. also isolated and identified these four umami-enhancing dipeptides (ME, EL, EA, EV) from fermented soybean meal using Aspergillus oryzae. Through sensory-guided separation techniques, the umami-enhancing effects of these peptides were systematically verified. Sensory evaluation showed that these peptides significantly enhanced umami perception in both I + G (inosinate and guanylate) and MSG (monosodium glutamate) model systems, with ME being most effective in the I + G system and EV showing the strongest effect in the MSG solution. The umami-enhancement thresholds of these peptides ranged from 15 to 55 mg/L, and their sensory performance was influenced by sequence and concentration. Molecular docking analysis further suggested that the umami perception of these peptides was primarily mediated via hydrogen bonding with hydrophilic amino acid residues of the T1R1/T1R3 umami receptor, with major binding sites including Arg277, Ser276, and Asp147 (Cao et al., 2024).

In recent years, several studies have focused on identifying and screening key umami peptides from complex fermented products. For example, Pan et al. investigated soybean paste fermented by Tetragenococcus halophilus and screened four peptides (LLYGKVVKKKT, DKKVSVGT, TRKQALLN, and QKNSHQ) with pronounced umami properties from 161 candidates using peptidomics and AI-assisted prediction. These peptides exhibited umami thresholds below 0.14 mmol/L. Molecular docking revealed that the peptides formed stable complexes with T1R1/T1R3 via hydrogen bonds and electrostatic interactions, particularly at binding sites such as ARG249 and ALA282. Binding energy analysis indicated strong binding affinities, supporting their potential as natural umami peptides (Pan et al., 2025). Additionally, Dong et al. analyzed fermented soybean curd (FSC) and identified 25 umami peptides using ultrafiltration, ethanol precipitation, desalting, HPLC-MS/MS, and molecular docking. Among them, short peptides such as ADL, DPRVI, THEA, TD, GE, and VD demonstrated significant umami and umami-enhancing effects. Especially, the tripeptide ADL reached a concentration of 0.51 mg/mL in the sample—four times its umami threshold (0.125 mg/mL). Taste reconstitution experiments confirmed that these peptides significantly enhanced umami perception in model systems, with an overall improvement of up to 24.53 %. Molecular dynamics simulation further supported that the peptides could form stable complexes with the T1R1/T1R3 receptor, involving key residues such as Glu301, Asp147, Arg277, and Ala380 (Dong et al., 2024).

In summary, the formation of umami peptides is co-regulated by the composition of fermentation substrates and microbial protease activity. Low-molecular-weight peptides rich in Asp and Glu are more likely to contribute to umami. Recently, the integration of peptidomics, virtual molecular docking, and sensory evaluation has become a major research approach in umami peptide studies, providing effective support for improving the flavor of fermented soybean products.

Umami-related amino acids

In fermented soybean products, in addition to umami peptides, amino acid-based umami compounds—especially glutamic acid (Glu) and aspartic acid (Asp)—also play crucial roles in umami perception. These amino acids can exist in free forms or be incorporated as residues in peptides contributing indirectly to umami. They are typically derived from microbial protease-mediated hydrolysis of soybean proteins. In both solid-state and submerged fermentation systems, fungal strains exhibit diverse enzymatic profiles and capacities for amino acid production, which significantly influence the content of umami amino acids in fermented soybeans. It was found that strains such as Lichtheimia, Mucor, Rhizomucor, and Rhizopus generally produced low levels of Glu and Asp. In contrast, members of Aspergillus subgenus Circumdati (e.g., A. oryzae) demonstrated high protease and glutaminase activities during solid-state fermentation and significantly accumulated umami amino acids during subsequent liquid fermentation. Among them, A. oryzae (SP22) produced the highest Glu content at 1.1 % (w/v). Additionally, P. solitum (SP10) and S. brevicaulis (SP12) also showed strong capabilities in producing umami amino acids, with Glu levels reaching 0.64 % and 0.71 % (w/v), respectively, highlighting their potential contributions to umami. In contrast, strains such as Monascus ruber and Aspergillus subgenus Aspergillus exhibited limited protease, aminopeptidase, and carboxypeptidase activity, resulting in low levels of umami amino acids. Overall, A. oryzae and related Circumdati strains show the greatest potential for producing umami amino acids and possess promising applications in fermentation (Kim et al., 2024).

Among bacteria, lactic acid bacteria and Bacillus spp. also demonstrate strong proteolytic capacities that facilitate the release of umami amino acids. Lactiplantibacillus plantarum Lp6, for instance, significantly increased the total amino acid content in fermented soybean meal from 0.32 % in the unfermented sample to 8.03 %, with Glu and Asp contributing the most (Amadou et al., 2011). Earlier studies also reported that Lactobacillus delbrueckii subsp. lactis CRL 581 and L. helveticus CRL 1062 could efficiently release Glu, Asp, Gly, Ser, and Lys during hydrolysis of soybean protein extracts (Aguirre et al., 2008). Simultaneously, Bacillus species such as B. subtilis, B. licheniformis, and B. sonorensis have been confirmed to possess strong protein-degrading and Glu-releasing capacities, making them typical Glu-producing strains in soybean fermentation systems (Chettri et al., 2016). These findings indicate that different microorganisms vary in their ability to generate umami amino acids, and significant differences may exist in product accumulation across strains. In the future, co-fermentation and process optimization could further enhance the release of amino acid-based umami compounds, providing a strong foundation for improving the flavor of fermented soybean products.

Bitterness

Bitter peptides

Bitterness is one of the most common undesirable flavors in fermented soybean products, primarily attributed to the generation of bitter peptides during the enzymatic hydrolysis of soybean proteins by microorganisms. In practical fermentation processes, degrading bitter peptides using specific microbial proteases is an effective strategy to reduce bitterness perception. For example, during solid-state fermentation of soybean meal with B. subtilis ACCC 01746, the bitterness value of the fermented product decreased from 5 to 0 after 32 h of fermentation. At 48 h, the activities of protease and carboxypeptidase reached peak values of 469 U/g and 477 U/g, respectively. Along with increased enzyme activities, the bitter peptides were hydrolyzed into free amino acids such as phenylalanine (Phe), alanine (Ala), tyrosine (Tyr), and leucine (Leu), thereby effectively reducing bitterness in the fermented product (Haicheng et al., 2019). It is worth noting that certain bitter peptides may also exhibit flavor-enhancing properties under specific conditions. For instance, Zhu et al. isolated hydrophobic peptide segments such as KP, EF, EV, DL, LW, and DP from soy sauce, which, despite their inherent bitterness, could enhance the umami intensity of 0.35 % MSG solution. Notably, peptides KP, EF, EV, DL, and DP intensified the umami perception, while KP, EV, and DL further enhanced the umami taste of soy sauce itself. Moreover, peptides containing Leu residues (e.g., LW) may induce a kokumi effect, contributing to aftertaste and flavor persistence (Zhu et al., 2020). Overall, the formation and degradation of bitter peptides constitute a dynamic balance during fermentation. On one hand, hydrolysis of proteins generates peptides that may carry bitter taste; on the other hand, continued action of proteases—especially carboxypeptidases—can further degrade these peptides into milder-tasting or tasteless amino acids. Therefore, rational selection of fermentation strains and optimization of the enzymatic hydrolysis process are of great importance for mitigating bitterness and improving the overall flavor of fermented soybean products.

Bitter-tasting amino acids

In addition to bitter peptides, certain amino acids—particularly basic and hydrophobic amino acids—also play a key role in the development of bitterness in fermented soybean products. These amino acids may exist in free form or be incorporated into peptides that indirectly influence flavor. Common contributors include basic amino acids such as histidine (His) and arginine (Arg), as well as hydrophobic amino acids such as valine (Val), leucine (Leu), isoleucine (Ile), phenylalanine (Phe), tyrosine (Tyr), tryptophan (Trp), and methionine (Met). Due to their low taste thresholds, excessive accumulation of these amino acids during fermentation can significantly enhance bitterness perception (Tseng et al., 2005). Haicheng et al. reported that inoculating B. subtilis ACCC 01746 into a soybean meal system and fermenting at 30 °C for 64 h significantly reduced bitterness. This effect was associated with the secretion of hydrolases and carboxypeptidases by ACCC 01746. The hydrolases cleaved peptide bonds adjacent to hydrophobic residues such as Phe, Ala, Tyr, and Leu at the C-terminal, while carboxypeptidases directly released these amino acids, effectively reducing bitterness from peptide sequences (Haicheng et al., 2019). In contrast, poor strain selection or improper fermentation control may lead to excessive accumulation of bitter amino acids. Dajanta et al. studied the traditional Thai fermented soybean product Thua Nao and found that inoculation with B. subtilis TN51 at 104 CFU/g followed by fermentation at 42 °C for 72 h led to an approximately fivefold increase in basic amino acids and a sevenfold increase in hydrophobic amino acids, significantly intensifying the product's bitterness (Dajanta et al., 2011).

Thus, by optimizing microbial strain selection and fermentation parameters, the type and content of free amino acids (FAAs) can be effectively modulated. This approach helps prevent the overaccumulation of bitter-tasting amino acids, thereby mitigating bitterness perception and enhancing the overall flavor quality of fermented soybean products.

Aroma improvement

Aromatic volatile compounds

Aromatic aroma is one of the key sensory attributes of fermented soybean products, directly influencing consumer acceptance. Common desirable aroma compounds include esters, alcohols, ketones, aldehydes, and sulfur compounds, which impart fruity, floral, and dairy-like notes. Co-fermentation of Lactiplantibacillus plantarum with yeasts such as Pichia kudriavzevii has been shown to synergistically promote the accumulation of aromatic compounds including 2-phenylethanol and ethyl acetate, enhancing umami perception while enriching the overall aroma complexity (Cao et al., 2025). Additionally, L. plantarum X7021, isolated from stinky tofu brine, demonstrated outstanding flavor modulation capability in soymilk fermentation. This strain not only achieved rapid growth and acidification within 8 h, but also significantly increased the levels of aroma-active compounds such as acetic acid, acetoin, and diacetyl, outperforming commercial yogurt starters in sensory evaluations. The addition of sucrose further enhanced its aromatic impact by promoting the release of volatile compounds, thereby improving overall acceptance and flavor satisfaction of fermented soymilk (Du et al., 2022). In recent years, plant-derived Leuconostoc spp. have been increasingly applied to soybean fermentation systems. Their heterofermentative metabolism enables efficient acid production and synthesis of multiple cheese-characteristic flavor compounds. Seven Leuconostoc strains were screened and fermented in a medium based on soy flour. Among them, Leuconostoc pseudomesenteroides NFICC99 efficiently utilized carbon sources such as citrate, glucose, and sucrose, rapidly acidified the system, and significantly reduced the contents of hexanal and 2-pentylfuran within 18 h. Meanwhile, it produced abundant dairy-related aromatic compounds including acetoin, diacetyl, benzaldehyde, and 3-methylbutanoic acid. The volatile profile exhibited a high degree of overlap with those of traditional cheeses like Parmesan and Cheddar, indicating a promising potential in dairy-like aroma development (Wätjen et al., 2023).

Overall, microbial fermentation exhibits significant potential in enhancing the aromatic characteristics of soybean products. However, challenges such as flavor instability still require further resolution through strain selection and process optimization.

Beany odor-related volatile compounds

Beany odor is a major barrier to the acceptance of soybean-based products and mainly originates from the oxidative degradation of polyunsaturated fatty acids, such as linoleic acid and linolenic acid, catalyzed by lipoxygenase (LOX) and/or peroxidases. This leads to the formation of various strongly odorous volatile aldehydes, alcohols, ketones, and furans (Estelle et al., 2022). Representative compounds include hexanal, pentanal, (E)-2-heptenal, (E)-2-hexenal, 1-hexanol, 1-pentanol, 1-octen-3-ol, benzaldehyde, and 2-pentylfuran (Zhengqi et al., 2023). Fermentation has been shown to effectively reduce the concentration of these compounds, thereby mitigating beany flavor and improving the sensory quality of soy yogurt.

Zhang et al. developed a Maillard brown soy yogurt (MBSY) by inoculating soymilk with a 3 % (w/v) mixture of Lactobacillus delbrueckii subsp. bulgaricus and Streptococcus thermophilus (1:4), along with 0.04 % (w/v) L. plantarum, and fermenting at 37 °C for 10 h. Compared with non-Maillard soy yogurt (SY), commercial soy yogurt (CSY), and commercial brown yogurt (CBY), the MBSY showed a significantly reduced content of beany volatiles such as hexanal, 1-hexanol, 2-pentylfuran, and 1-octen-3-one. This improvement may be attributed to thermal inactivation of lipid-oxidizing enzymes and the adsorption of flavor-active compounds by Maillard reaction products, effectively eliminating off-flavors and enhancing sensory quality (Zhang et al., 2025). In another study, conducted solid-state fermentation of okara using B. subtilis R0179 and B. coagulans 123, and observed distinct flavor transformations. Fermentation with B. subtilis R0179 significantly reduced key beany compounds such as hexanal, 2-pentylfuran, and 1-octen-3-one, while enriching typical fermentation metabolites including acetoin, aromatic ketones, pyrazines, and short-chain fatty acids, resulting in nutty, cheesy, and fermented soy aromas. In contrast, B. coagulans 123 showed moderate deodorizing capacity, with a metabolic bias toward alcohols (e.g., 2-phenylethanol, 1-hexanol) and esters (e.g., ethyl hexanoate), imparting soft fruity and floral notes, but with relatively weaker suppression of aldehydes responsible for beany odor (Keong et al., 2023).

Beyond bacteria, fungal strains also exhibit excellent flavor-modulating abilities. Sun et al. reported that fermentation of soymilk with Naematelia aurantialba NX-20 at 25 °C for 5 days significantly reduced the contents of key beany volatiles including hexanal, 2-pentylfuran, (E)-2-hexenal, pentanal, and 1-hexanol. It also promoted the accumulation of pleasant alcohols and aldehydes. Although the degradation mechanism remains unclear, it is speculated—based on analogous lactic acid bacteria studies—that this fungus may express aldehyde dehydrogenases and related enzyme systems to catalyze the conversion of aldehydes to acids (Sun et al., 2022). Building on this, Wang et al. screened four edible fungi (Tremella CGMCC 5.466, Poria cocos CGMCC 5.528, 5.55, and 5.78) for fermentation of okara and systematically analyzed the volatile profiles before and after fermentation. Beany compounds accounted for 77.32 % of the total volatiles in unfermented okara, dominated by hexanal (91.09 μg/L, OAV 20.24), cis-6-nonenal, 1-octen-3-ol, and (E,E)-2,4-nonadienal. These were mainly derived from enzyme-catalyzed and light-induced oxidation of lipids. All four fungi exhibited significant deodorizing abilities, with P. cocos CGMCC 5.55 showing a 98.62 % degradation rate for hexanal, reducing its OAV to below the sensory threshold and effectively eliminating perceptible off-flavor. Although Tremella CGMCC 5.466 also strongly degraded hexanal, it concurrently increased 1-octen-3-ol levels, resulting in a distinctive mushroom-like aroma, likely due to species-specific metabolic traits (Wang et al., 2022).

In conclusion, various microorganisms contribute to beany flavor mitigation through distinct metabolic pathways. Rational selection of fermentative strains combined with controlled processing conditions can effectively suppress off-flavors and enhance the overall flavor quality of soybean-based products. Conclusion and future perspectives

Microbial fermentation significantly enhances both the bioactivity and sensory properties of soybeans. Through the metabolic transformation of soybean proteins, polyphenols, lipids, and other substrates, different microorganisms produce a range of functional components, including bioactive peptides, phenolic derivatives, and bacteriocin-like substances. These fermentation products endow fermented soybeans with multiple health-promoting effects, such as antioxidant, antihypertensive, antimicrobial, and hypoglycemic activities. Simultaneously, the hydrolysis of proteins and conversion of flavor precursors during fermentation contribute to the overall improvement of taste. The formation of umami peptides, umami-related amino acids, and aromatic volatile compounds enhances flavor perception, while the degradation of bitter peptides and beany odor volatiles helps mitigate undesirable sensory attributes. Collectively, microbial fermentation effectively improves the functional value and sensory performance of soybean products, thereby promoting their potential application in the development of functional foods.

Although current studies have initially elucidated the relationships between fermentation products and functional properties, most of the mechanisms underlying bioactivity remain at the in vitro level, lacking systematic in vivo validation and clinical evidence. The metabolic pathways of active compounds, as well as their stability and bioavailability in the human body, require further exploration. On the sensory side, flavor improvement research has mainly focused on end-product characteristics, with limited understanding of the underlying mechanisms of flavor formation and regulation. Challenges remain in the precise control of bitterness, sustained release of aroma compounds, and complete removal of beany flavor precursors. Future research should focus on elucidating the formation mechanisms and functional pathways of key fermentation-derived compounds, and leverage strategies such as strain improvement, co-culture fermentation, and process optimization. These efforts will contribute to the coordinated enhancement of both functionality and flavor, laying a solid theoretical and technical foundation for the high-value and standardized production of fermented soybean products.
