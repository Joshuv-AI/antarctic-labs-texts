Chronic Neuroplasticity Changes Following Neurotropic Viral Infection: Mechanisms and Implications
Authors: Aria Mohabbat, Hossein Bannazadeh Baghi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The nervous system exhibits remarkable adaptability through neuroplasticity, which allows for structural and functional changes in response to intrinsic and extrinsic stimuli. This dynamic process underpins synaptic formation, elimination, learning, memory, and brain recovery after neurological insults. However, neuroplasticity can be compromised by neurotropic viral infections, which present significant challenges to the central nervous system (CNS). Viruses infiltrate the CNS through various mechanisms, including peripheral nerves, disruption of the blood–brain barrier (BBB), and evasion of the immune system, leading to acute or chronic neuronal pathologies. Moreover, these infections may trigger encephalitis, neuroinflammation, and synaptic dysfunction, thereby impairing neural circuits and compromising brain function. Persistent viral infection and chronic responses further exacerbate neuronal damage through oxidative stress, excitotoxicity, and disruption of neural progenitor cells. Collectively, these effects hinder neuroplasticity, resulting in cognitive deficits, behavioral changes, and long-lasting structural alterations. Understanding the mechanisms by which neurotropic viruses impair neuroplasticity is crucial for developing targeted therapeutic interventions. Strategies aimed at addressing viral persistence, mitigating inflammation, and promoting synaptic repair are critical to preserving brain health and functionality. This review provides a comprehensive overview of virus-induced neuronal pathologies and their effects on neuroplasticity, highlighting the importance of innovative treatments to enhance CNS resilience and recovery in affected individuals.Graphical AbstractImpact of Neurotropic Viruses on Nervous System Function: Long-term neurotropic viral infections, driven by persistent neuroinflammation and neuronal damage (exhausted microglia and astrocytes), impair cognitive function, disrupt synaptic transmission, and compromise the blood–brain barrier. Introduction

The nervous system possesses remarkable dynamic adaptability. Among the strategies it employs, neuroplasticity has gained significant attention. Neuroplasticity refers to the capacity of the nervous system to modify its activity in response to internal or external stimuli by reorganizing its structure, functions, or connections (Cramer et al. 2011; Voss et al. 2017). The very foundation of neuroplasticity lies in molecular interactions that operate across subcellular, cellular, and neural circuit levels. A fundamental micro-principle of neuroplasticity is the adaptability of synaptic connections, which are continuously being formed and eliminated (Gulyaeva 2017). Synapses undergo long-lasting structural and biochemical changes based on the neuromodulators and stimuli, leading to secure brain function, learning, and memory (Richter and Klann 2009). Neuroplasticity is vital for brain rehabilitation to regain function in the aftermath of neurological pathologies, including infection, stroke, autoimmune complications, and illnesses (Anderson 2004; Kumar et al. 2023a, b; Li et al. 2024). However, neuroplasticity itself is not fully immune to the insults and the damages; consequently, the adaptation mechanisms are damaged.

Pathogenic infections from various microorganisms cause significant neuropathological manifestations. Encephalitis, neuroinflammation, synaptic disruption, neuronal damage, release of neurotoxins, and impaired neurogenesis, all of which reflect the impact of infectious insult on the CNS (Ghoshal et al. 2007; Sheppard et al. 2019; Poyhonen et al. 2021; Le Govic et al. 2022; Segklia et al. 2023). Viruses circulating across diverse epidemiological regions are capable of causing acute or long-term neuronal pathologies in humans (Yang et al. 2023). There is growing recognition of the wide variety of viruses infecting CNS tissue. Certain viruses, known as neurotropic viruses, have a specific affinity to the nervous system. Neurotropic viruses could initiate a broad range of neurological disorders through direct infection of neurons or glial cells. At a cellular level, these viruses intrude into the CNS via various distinct strategies, leading to a variety of neurological symptoms (Vazquez and Jurado 2022; McMillan et al. 2023). At the cellular level, inflammation, oxidative stress, and excitotoxicity act as both primary and secondary injury mechanisms playing key roles in the pathophssysiology of neurological diseases; these mechanisms contribute to a range of physical, cognitive, and behavioral impairments (Dimyan and Cohen 2011, Jiang and Dingledine 2013, Su et al. 2015, Martín-Montañez et al. 2017, Jamjoom et al. 2021). A great portion of CNS infections are successfully eliminated by the immune response; however, surviving viral agents from the immune system alter infectious threats (Nath and Johnson 2022a, b). Viral infections involving the CNS could be due to a prior infection of another system, such as respiratory or gastrointestinal (Andrade et al. 2021). The primary complexity in preventing and treating neurotropic viral infections stems from the unique characteristics of the CNS. These include a vast network of closely linked yet interdependent cells, advanced structure, restricted regenerative abilities, and sophisticated immune surveillance. (Miller and Rall 2017; Soung and Klein 2018). Not all neurotropic viruses have the capability to initiate synaptic plasticity disruption or to impair cognitive functions (Atluri et al. 2015). Long-term neurotropic viral infections can significantly impact neuroplasticity through various mechanisms, including direct damage to neurons, persistent inflammation, oxidative stress, dysfunction of neural progenitor cells, and alterations in neurotransmitter systems (Daulatzai 2016; Honda 2022; Goodkin et al. 2023; Wongchitrat et al. 2024). Altogether, these factors lead to cognitive deficits, behavioral changes, and lasting structural changes in the brain. Understanding these mechanisms is critical for creating useful clinical approaches to lower the unwanted effects of viral infections on brain well-being and functionality (Vance et al. 2014; Atluri et al. 2015). This review aims to provide a broad view of neurotropic infections and focus on their specific effect on neuroplasticity while highlighting futuristic implications on prevention, diagnosis, and treatment of patients. A graphical abstract summarizing the key concepts of the article is illustrated as well.

Neurotropic Viral Infection

Neurotropic viruses are able to invade and infect the CNS, encompassing a diverse range of viral families (Koyuncu et al. 2013). These viruses gain access to the CNS either by traveling along peripheral nerves or by crossing the BBB after spreading through the bloodstream. Different viruses target a variety of cell types within the nervous system tissue, initiating a range of symptoms that can include seizures, paralysis, or even death (Al-Obaidi et al. 2018a, b, Srichawla et al. 2023, Sun et al. 2024). They exhibit distinct characteristics, such as genome variations, differing virulence levels, and varying biosafety classifications (Li et al. 2019). Neurotropic viruses are defined by their capability of causing diseases in the CNS and possess both neuroinvasive and neurovirulent characteristics. This vast category of viruses includes a variety of viruses, such as herpes simplex virus, poliovirus, enteroviruses, parechovirus, West Nile virus, Japanese encephalitis virus, as well as measles and mumps viruses. While, a portion of these viruses exhibit strong neuroinvasive behavior and neurovirulence, others demonstrate these properties to a lesser degree. For instance, herpes simplex viruses are proven to be strongly neuroinvasive but exhibit lower levels of neurovirulence in the peripheral nervous system, whereas they are highly neurovirulent yet weakly neuroinvasive in the CNS (Abdullahi et al. 2020). Many neurotropic viruses exploit the peripheral nervous system to bypass host barrier systems and gain direct access to the CNS (Ene 2018). Additional routes for neurotropic penetration include directly crossing the blood–brain barrier or using the immune system as a Trojan horse (Sorgeloos et al. 2013; Al-Obaidi et al. 2018a, b). Once inside the CNS, neurotropic viruses cause a wide range of neuronal pathologies, which are categorized as acute and chronic infections. A significant portion of acute viral infections of the CNS are presented as aseptic encephalitis or meningitis (Deigendesch and Stenzel 2018). If not resisted, these infections may lead to severe dysfunctions, including neuronal degeneration (Dave and Klein 2023). Infection with neurotropic viruses, along with subsequent reactive immune responses, can damage the structure and the function of the CNS, leading to poor prognosis (Ludlow et al. 2016). During the post-infection phase, the presence of infectious virus particles, immune cells, imbalance of matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), release of inflammatory mediators, and resultant neuronal dysfunction are the main features of neurotropic virus-related neuropathy in the parenchymal tissues of the CNS (Al-Obaidi et al. 2018a, b; Alghamdi et al. 2024). Besides, to extend survival within neurons, these viruses apply immune evasion mechanisms such as neuromuscular junctions, direct BBB penetration, hematogenous routes, and immune cell invasion (Al-Obaidi et al. 2018a, b; Yan et al. 2024). Arenavirus

Arenaviruses are naturally maintained through chronic infections in rodents, and possibly in fruit bats for the Tacaribe virus. Humans are accidental hosts; however, LCMV frequently causes aseptic meningitis in infected individuals. Other arenaviruses may induce hemorrhagic fever, often accompanied by neurological complications. (Wilson and Peters 2014).

Belonging to the mammarenaviruses, Lymphocytic choriomeningitis virus (LCMV) is a common pathogen microorganism, with mice serving as its natural host and reservoir. Humans can become infected through contact with mouse secretions (Childs et al. 1992). LCMV gains access to host cells by connecting to the α-dystroglycan cellular surface receptor. It is then internalized through an endocytic pathway that relies on cholesterol but does not involve clathrin or caveolin. Once inside the endosomes, the virus undergoes fusion triggered by the acidic environment, allowing it to release its genetic material into the cell (Rojek et al. 2008; Vilibic-Cavlek et al. 2021a, b; Bakkers et al. 2022). While most postnatal infections in humans lead to mild illness, the effects pose a serious threat if the infection occurs within the third trimester of pregnancy or labor, as the virus can cross the placenta and infect the developing feti (Bonthius 2024a, 2024b). LCMV can infect the ependymal tissue, choroid plexus, and brain meninges, leading to BBB alterations (Kang and McGavern 2008; Matullo et al. 2010). During LCMV infection of the CNS, a variety of cells, such as neuroblasts, neurons, and astrocytes, could be affected (Bonthius 2012; Puccini et al. 2014). LCMV infection, along with the post-infection inflammatory response of the human fetus, can lead to serious neuropathological consequences, including microencephaly, hydrocephalus, localized destructive lesions, and the undevelopment of the cerebellum (Vilibic-Cavlek et al. 2021a, b, Bonthius 2024a, 2024b).

Bunyaviridae

The Bunyaviridae family is among the largest and most diverse viral families. It includes viruses that have negative-sense RNA genomes. (Whitehouse et al. 2015).

La Crosse virus (LACV) is an orthobunyavirus endemic to North America, transmitted by arthropods. A zoonotic Bunyavirus is considered a major pathogen of pediatric viral encephalitis in the United States of America (Haddow et al. 2011; Mukherjee et al. 2013). The nasal turbinate is the primary peripheral site where replication occurs at its highest levels. It is believed that LACV can access the CNS through the olfactory neurons originating from the nasal olfactory epithelial cells (Bennett et al. 2008). This virus primarily invades host cells via clathrin-mediated endocytosis, which requires the GTPase dynamin. This entry mechanism has been identified in both non-neuronal cells (Bennett et al. 2008). The pathology associated with LACV encephalitis shows significant disruption of the BBB and possible encephalitis, leading to vascular damage (McJunkin et al. 1997; Basu et al. 2023). Infection with LACV in humans often is depicted with non-significant symptoms, while serious complications such as acute fatal encephalitis, epilepsy, or neurobehavioral complications may also be demonstrated in rare cases (McJunkin et al. 2001; Borucki et al. 2002; Teleron et al. 2016).

Coronaviridae

Coronaviruses (CoVs), known for their abundant number of the members, are capable of infecting many species of mammals and avians. Human coronaviruses (HCoVs) are known to infect humans and manifest clinically with respiratory, gastrointestinal (GI), and neurological symptoms (Decaro and Lorusso 2020; Paules et al. 2020; Tsatsakis et al. 2020). As of the global spread of severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), several documentations of neurological symptoms in SARS-CoV-2-infected cases have been published. Not considered a neurotropic virus, SARS-CoV-2 remains a threat to the CNS (Aschman et al. 2022). To invade, SARS-CoV-2 attaches to the angiotensin-converting enzyme-2 (ACE2), Neuropilin1 (NRP1), and other molecular structures like transmembrane protease, serine 2 (TMPRSS2) receptors of the targeted tissues (Li et al. 2020; Paniz-Mondolfi et al. 2020; Eslami et al. 2022; Haverty et al. 2024). Additional candidate entry receptors are low-density lipoprotein receptor class A domain-containing protein 3 (LDLRAD3), Transmembrane Protein 30A (TMEM30A), and C-Type Lectin Domain Family 4 Member G (CLEC4G) (Zhu et al. 2022). SARS-CoV-2, besides viremia, has diverse neuroinvasive routes; direct disruption of the BBB or indirect induction of permeability, penetration into the olfactory pathway, trigeminal route, or other neuronal pathways, and a Trojan-horse mechanism (Alquisiras-Burgos et al. 2021; Erickson et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2021a, b; Lam et al. 2022; Xu and Lazartigues 2022, Hsu et al. 2024). Mechanisms applied by SARS-CoV-2 to induce neuropathogenesis are diverse. Directly, the virus blocks prevent IFN-I (type-I interferons) induction through the major protease Mpro (also known as 3CLpro), which can cleave the host protein NEMO (NF-kappa-B essential modulator) (Wenzel et al. 2021; Mohamed et al. 2022; Natale et al. 2022). Elevated TNF and IL-1 in severe COVID-19 boost Th17 cell differentiation and vascular permeability. This triggers Th17-mediated release of IL-17, IL-21, and IL-22, amplifying tissue damage and immune dysregulation. Th17 cells critically drive neuroinflammatory and pulmonary pathology through cytokine storms and endothelial injury. Their overactivation creates a self-perpetuating cycle of inflammation and organ dysfunction in severe infections (Huang et al. 2020; Aghbash et al. 2021a, b). Furthermore, neutrophil extracellular trap (NETs) is linked to the hypercoagulation and thrombosis during the early phase of the immune reaction to SARS‐CoV‐2 infection (Chen et al. 2021). Even more, neuroinflammation remains a threat to the nervous system activation of microglia and IL-1 enhances the production of ROS, phagocytosis, and apoptosis in the CNS, leading to neuroinflammation and damage to neural tissue. Additionally, apoptosis of epithelial cells triggered by a cytokine storm results in vascular leakage, which contributes to cerebral edema. The cytokine storm also initiates necrosis all along the endothelial tissue, disrupts the BBB, and damages neural cells (Channappanavar and Perlman 2017; Bhaskar et al. 2020; Najjar et al. 2020; Almutairi et al. 2021).

Flaviviridae

Flaviviridae is a family of enveloped single-stranded positive-sense RNA viruses. The Flavivirus genus comprises mosquito-borne and tick-borne viruses, and there is limited understanding of the factors that facilitate viral entry into CNS cells. TAM receptors (Tyro3, Axl, and Mer), integrins, and Heparan Sulfate Proteoglycans (HSPGs) play a remarkable role in the attachment of the Flaviviridae to the neurons (Meertens et al. 2017; Cagno et al. 2019, Wang et al. 2020a, 2020b). Binding and attachments to various specific receptors have been reported for many viruses that infect humans, leading to a range of clinical symptoms, varying from mild fever to worsening severe hemorrhagic fever or involvement of the CNS (Bhatt et al. 2013).

Dengue is the second most common disease affecting humans, transmitted by mosquitoes. Dengue virus pathogenesis involves a complex interaction involving the virus, host genetics, and immune system responses. Antibody-dependent enhancement (ADE) and cross-reactive T cells contribute to the severity of the disease. The NS1 protein and autoantibodies play vital roles in severe outcomes. Host genetic factors and viral variations also influence disease progression. These mechanisms lead to severe manifestations like hemorrhagic fever and shock syndrome (Bhatt et al. 2021). Neurological complications can be classified into several categories: dengue encephalopathy (which may result from hepatic failure or metabolic disorders), encephalitis (caused by direct viral invasion), neuromuscular issues (such as Guillain-Barré syndrome or temporary muscle dysfunctions), and neuro-ophthalmic problems (Carod-Artal et al. 2013). Primarily, neuroinflammation is mostly identified as an offensive mechanism of the virus via non-structural 1 antigen (NS1Ag), which is a secreted glycoprotein that functions as a cofactor for viral RNA replication and cytokine release. The neuroinflammatory response is initiated through the impairment of the endothelial cell monolayer by activating Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) on macrophages and human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs). This activation triggers the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, leading to vascular dysfunction and increased permeability (Thongrungkiat et al. 2011; Puerta-Guardo et al. 2016; Pang et al. 2017; Teo et al. 2021).

Japanese encephalitis virus (JEV), which circulated among wild-wading birds in Asia, is transmitted by mosquitoes of the genus Culex (Van den Eynde et al. 2022). Besides inducing neuronal cell death, the spread of the Japanese encephalitis virus in astrocytes and microglia leads to an increase in pro-inflammatory cytokines such as Tumor Necrosis Factor-alpha (TNF-α), (interleukin) IL-6, and IL-1β. Japanese encephalitis is directly associated with inflammation and vasculitis in the brain (Patabendige et al. 2018; Banerjee and Tripathi 2019).

Powassan virus lineage II (POWV) is a rare emerging tick-borne pathogen transmitted by the Ixodes scapularis tick and is the only flavivirus transmitted by ticks in North America that is known to cause illness in humans (Fatmi et al. 2017; Koester et al. 2020). This virus in humans causes fatal encephalitis in the elderly and long-term neurologic sequelae in long-time survivors, while the pathophysiology of the virus involves immune evasion and continuous neuroinflammation (Shah et al. 2023; Mladinich et al. 2024).

West Nile virus (WNV) infection can manifest acutely, presenting a clinical range from mild flu-like symptoms to severe neuroinvasive conditions such as meningitis, encephalitis, acute flaccid paralysis, and even death. Some patients recovering from WNV may experience long-term neurological effects (Winkelmann et al. 2016; Peng and Wang 2019). To conduct a successful neuroinvasion, WNV applies multiple approaches, such as transneural or hematogenous routes (Abdullahi et al. 2020). WNVs promote apoptosis in neurons through extensive replication. However, in non-neuronal glial cells, WNV establishes a long-term persistent infection by releasing inflammatory factors and neurotoxins to induce cellular death. Additionally, impairing neurogenesis is another strategy to disrupt cognitive function in the long term (Diniz et al. 2006; van Marle et al. 2007; Garber et al. 2018).

Yellow fever virus (YFV) is a potentially lethal mosquito-borne flavivirus that is currently endemic in certain regions of equatorial Africa and South America. Most of the individuals infected with YFV do not show symptoms; however, those who develop yellow fever may experience neurological issues, such as headaches, sensitivity to light, agitation, seizures, encephalopathy, and, in rare cases, cerebral edema and coma. Notably, patients with these neurological complications often show little or no pleocytosis in their cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and minimal inflammation in brain tissue, indicating that systemic issues may be the underlying cause rather than direct viral invasion or neuroinflammation in the CNS (McGuinness et al. 2017a, b; Desantis et al. 2022). Severe YFV infection induces a systemic inflammatory response, which impairs blood–brain barrier BBB function, enabling viral particles or immune cells (lymphocytes, monocytes, and polymorphonuclear cells) to infiltrate the CNS. The YFV is able to reach the brain via hematogenous spread, and the meningeal lymphatic system of the dura mater may serve as an alternative pathway for CNS invasion during systemic inflammation (Luethy et al. 2016; Mustafa et al. 2019, de Rezende et al. 2022).

Zika virus, transmitted by mosquitoes of the Aedes species, is associated with a non-specific acute febrile illness, mainly peripheral neuropathy, microcephaly, meningoencephalitis, myelitis, and Guillain-Barré syndrome. Zika is transmissible during the perinatal period, and the virus could be detected in amniotic fluid, placenta, and fetal brain tissue. Severe cases of microcephaly have also been documented since the 2015 pandemic of the Zika virus in Brazil (Araujo et al. 2016, White et al. 2016, Cumberworth et al. 2017, Metsky et al. 2017). Once inside the brain, ZIKV infects various developing brain cells, leading to neuropathological effects such as disruptions in the cell cycle, mitochondrial dysfunction, endoplasmic reticulum stress with UTP depletion, changes in host autophagy processes, and neuronal apoptosis (Tan et al. 2022; Metzler and Tang 2024).

Herpesviruses

The large family Herpesviridae possess enveloped, spherical virions with linear, double-stranded DNA genomes (Gatherer et al. 2021).

Human cytomegalovirus (HCMV), a β-herpesvirus, is a highly infectious virus (Santos 2016; Perera et al. 2021). Primary infections or reactivations of the virus can lead to severe multi-organ disease, particularly in individuals with weakened immune systems. Certain groups, including transplant recipients, patients in intensive care, those with acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS), and fetuses or newborns, are especially vulnerable to HCMV-related diseases due to their compromised immune responses (Griffiths et al. 2015; Buxmann et al. 2017; Griffiths and Reeves 2021). HCMV infects a new host by targeting the mucosal epithelium, or, in cases of congenital infection, by infecting placental trophoblasts. Once inside, HCMV initiates a primary infection in organs like the spleen, liver, and lungs, with myeloid cells serving as its vehicles for transportation (Sinzger et al. 2008; Jackson and Sparer 2018). CMV utilizes a variety of mechanisms for pathogenesis; disruption of the developing brain’s microvasculature, damage to the blood–brain barrier, changes in synaptogenesis, loss of neural stem/progenitor cells (NSPCs), and altered cell migration result in disorganized cellular positioning (Slavuljica et al. 2015, Adle‐Biassette and Teissier 2020). In congenital infections, CMV infection of placental cells impacts their functionality, potentially leading to placental insufficiency, which can result in fetal hypoxia. This condition may contribute to the congenital abnormalities observed in affected infants (Chou et al. 2006; Ornaghi et al. 2017, Gaur et al. 2020).

EBV, classified as a gamma 1 or lymphocryptovirus, exists in two primary states: latent infection and lytic replication, each producing different viral proteins with distinct functions. The host cells susceptible to EBV include human B-lymphocytes and epithelial cells. In most immunologically active individuals, EBV establishes latent infections in B cells and nasopharyngeal epithelial cells throughout their lives. In cerebrospinal fluid, one can detect EBV-infected cells, cell-free viruses, their gene products, and abnormally elevated EBV titers. Research indicates that this virus can directly infect neurons or indirectly affect them through infected B-lymphocytes, leading to neuroinflammation and demyelination by inducing latency, cross-reacting with myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein (MOG), neuroinflammation, or direct infection (Hassani et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2021a, b). Furthermore, it can promote the proliferation, degeneration, and necrosis of glial cells, stimulate proliferative disorders in B- and T-lymphocytes, and contribute to various neurological diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, multiple sclerosis, acute cerebellar ataxia, meningitis, acute disseminated encephalomyelitis, and brain tumors (Soldan and Lieberman 2020; Zhang et al. 2021a, b).

Herpes simplex virus (HSV)-1 and HSV-2 are members of the simplexvirus genus. Humans are the natural hosts for herpes simplex viruses, leading to mostly asymptomatic mucosal infections with the potential to cause neurological issues (Kollias et al. 2015; Wertheim et al. 2021). These viruses are believed to reside in the sensory ganglia of the peripheral nervous system and utilize transsynaptic pathways to disseminate into the CNS (Menendez and Carr 2017a, b). It has been shown that infection with HSV-1 and HSV-2 results in transsynaptic transmission through PNS neurons to the CNS, leading to lethal encephalitis. However, the incidence of herpes simplex encephalitis (HSE) in humans is relatively low when compared to conditions affecting the eyes or skin (Whitley 2006; Nassi et al. 2015; Li et al. 2016; Miller et al. 2016; Jorgensen and Mogensen 2017). HSV-1 infection begins in epithelial cells at mucosal surfaces when viral glycoproteins gB and gC bind to heparan sulfate proteoglycans on the surface of host cells (Cunningham et al. 2006; Mingo et al. 2012). Neuronal infection of HSV-1 is not only harmful to mature cells but also effective on neural progenitor cells. For instance, in vivo studies confirmed that HSV-1 infection diminishes neural progenitor cells (NPCs) numbers in the subventricular zone (Chucair-Elliott et al. 2014). Moreover, this type of viral infection alters the AMPK/Sirt1 pathway in neurons, initially reducing p-AMPK and Sirt1 while increasing pro-apoptotic p53, promoting cell death (Leyton et al. 2015). Later, HSV-1 activates AMPK/Sirt1, boosting Sirt1 activity and lowering acetylated p53, which shifts cells to an anti-apoptotic state. This time-dependent modulation helps the virus replicate early on and supports neuronal survival for long-term persistence (Martin et al. 2014). HSV-1 is proven to be capable of inducing oxidative stress because of chronic inflammation in the CNS; therefore, focal lesions and atrophy in critical brain regions are anticipated (Armien et al. 2010; Menendez and Carr 2017a, b).

Varicella zoster virus (VZV) is a neurotropic herpesvirus that infects nearly all humans. The initial infection typically results in chickenpox (varicella), after which the virus becomes dormant in cranial nerve ganglia, dorsal root ganglia, and autonomic ganglia along the neuraxis. As VZV-specific host immunity declines over the decades following the primary infection, the virus can spontaneously reactivate, leading to shingles (zoster), which is characterized by pain and a rash confined to one to three dermatomes. Reactivation of VZV can also result in various other serious neurological (encephalitis, myelitis, neuralgia, cranial neuropathies, etc.) (Mueller et al. 2008, Gilden et al. 2011, Gilden et al. 2015, Herlin et al. 2021, Ci et al. 2025). During primary infection, virions likely access sensory nerve cell bodies in the ganglia through retrograde axonal transport from skin areas where the virus replicates, or via T-cell viremia, leading to the establishment of latent infection. When the virus reactivates, it travels back to the skin through anterograde axonal transport, resulting in shingles (zoster), which presents as a vesicular rash in the dermatome innervated by the affected ganglion (Zerboni et al. 2014).

Paramyxoviridae

Paramyxoviridae viruses are enveloped and, except for bornaviruses, have prominent glycoprotein spikes on their envelopes. All viruses within this family possess genomes composed of a single molecule of negative-sense, single-stranded RNA (Zerboni et al. 2014).

Nipah virus (NiV) is associated with encephalitis, systemic vasculitis, and sometimes respiratory illnesses. Natural transmission typically occurs through inhaling or ingesting contaminated materials. In past outbreaks, direct contact with secretions or excretions from infected animals and person-to-person transmission were identified as a major source of infection (Gurley et al. 2007; Devnath et al. 2022). Once inside the body, NiV travels through the pharynx and lungs, reaching the trachea to a lesser extent before spreading systemically, primarily affecting the CNS via the blood–brain barrier. The virus targets the microvascular endothelial cells within the CNS, and it was proven that NiVs are capable of infecting neurons extending through the cribriform plate into the olfactory bulb, providing a direct route entry into the CNS. Thereafter, NiV then spreads to the olfactory tubercle and ventral cortex (Weingartl et al. 2005; Munster et al. 2012; Freitag and Maisner 2015; Devnath et al. 2022; Al-Obaidi et al. 2024). Infection with this virus triggers neutrophils in the innate immune system. Neutrophils initiate a web-like immune response (NET) while inhibiting the IFN by the JAK/STAT pathway (Liew et al. 2022). In the adaptive immune system, serum from a NiV survivor revealed early NiV-specific IgG and IgM antibodies, with viral clearance linked to IgG and elevated B lymphocytes, demonstrating the adaptive immunity’s protective role (Arunkumar et al. 2019). In mice, immunization with rLa-NiVF induced strong NiV F protein-specific CD8 + T-cell responses, identifying the first CD8 + T-cell epitope (F280) (Kalodimou et al. 2019). From a cellular perspective, the Nipah virus mainly targets neurons by attaching its G glycoprotein to the Ephrin B2 and Ephrin B3 receptors, which are abundantly present on neurons and endothelial cells within the CNS (Talekar et al. 2011; Mourya et al. 2021).

Rubulavirus

As a member of the rubulavirus genus, Mumps is a well-known neurotropic pathogen worldwide, which is mostly transmitted via respiratory or oral contact (Hahné et al. 2017; Bockelman et al. 2018). Humans are the sole natural hosts of the mumps virus. The illness is marked by painful swelling of the parotid glands, but it can also affect various other tissues and organs, leading to a range of inflammatory conditions such as encephalitis, meningitis, orchitis, myocarditis, pancreatitis, and nephritis. Mumps typically resolves on its own. However, there can be long-term complications, including cerebellar ataxia, paralysis, seizures, cranial nerve dysfunctions, hydrocephalus, corneal endothelitis, and hearing loss (Mandell et al. 1979, Nussinovitch et al. 2003, Hashimoto et al. 2009, Ando et al. 2013, Rubin et al. 2015). The virus employs its hemagglutinin-neuraminidase (HN) glycoprotein to attach to sialic acid-containing receptors on the surface of host cells, enabling viral entry. Once inside, infected cells detect viral RNA through pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) like RIG-I and MDA5, which stimulate the production of type I interferons (IFN-α/β) and various pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β. In response, immune cells release cytokines, antibodies, and other inflammatory mediators. When this immune response is sustained, it can lead to tissue damage caused by ongoing inflammation (Mahon and Flynn 2003; Riggenbach et al. 2022).

Picornaviridae

The Picornaviridae family consists of small, non-enveloped viruses that have single-stranded positive-sense RNA genomes. The majority of picornaviruses are transmitted via the fecal–oral route or through respiratory pathways (Zell et al. 2017).

Type B coxsackieviruses (CV-B) often infect the CNS, leading to neurological diseases, particularly meningitis and encephalitis. These infections primarily affect newborns, children, and even fetuses in congenital infections. Additionally, some experimental studies conducted both in vitro and in vivo suggest a potential link between CV-B and idiopathic neurodegenerative diseases like amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, as well as psychiatric conditions such as schizophrenia (Wang et al. 2014; Jmii et al. 2021). Viral particles of CVB penetrate the brain parenchyma via hematogenous and neural pathways. Later on, CVB neuropathology is involved with the direct cytolytic effect of viral infection, induction of apoptotic cell death among infected neuronal stem cells, or inhibiting their transformation to astrocytes (Feuer et al. 2003; Odeberg et al. 2007; Tsueng et al. 2011; Tabor-Godwin et al. 2012; Jmii et al. 2020, 2021).

Retroviridae

Viruses belonging to the Retroviridae family are present in a diverse range of vertebrate hosts. The genome of members of the subfamily Orthoretrovirinae consists of a dimer of linear positive-sense ssRNA (Coffin et al. 2021). Individuals with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) who are undergoing combination antiretroviral therapy (cART) often exhibit symptoms of cognitive impairment and CNS pathology, collectively referred to as HIV-associated neurocognitive disorders (HAND). Although the occurrence of severe HIV-associated dementia (HAD), caused by neurological damage from actively replicating HIV, has significantly decreased due to effective cART, the prevalence of milder forms of HAND continues to be high (Heaton et al. 2010, Sacktor et al. 2016; Alvarez-Carbonell et al. 2019, Portilla et al. 2019). HIV-1 virus utilizes multiple strategies to invade the CNS. HIV-1 can directly invade endothelial cells that express the chemokine receptors necessary for its entry. Trojan-horse mechanism eases the passage from the BBB by infected immune cells, Tat and Nef disrupt the blood–brain barrier, enabling HIV entry, and HIV-infected monocytes to increase adhesion molecules and chemokine receptors for CNS transmigration (Albright et al. 2003; Spudich and Gonzalez-Scarano 2012; Williams et al. 2013; Liao et al. 2020). HIV-1 neuropathology results from the combined neurotoxic effects of viral proteins and heightened inflammatory responses from microglial cells. The HIV proteins Tat, gp120, Vpr, and Nef can directly cause damage to neurons (El-Hage et al. 2011; Masanetz and Lehmann 2011; Kogan et al. 2013; Berman et al. 2016; Fan and He 2016). Additionally, Tat triggers neuronal cell death by stimulating FOXO3, which JNK signaling transports into the nucleus. This process elevates pro-death proteins like Bim while suppressing survival factors like Bcl-2 (Dong et al. 2019). Furthermore, microglia can contribute to neurodegeneration by releasing cytokines that harm both neurons and astrocytes (Dos Reis et al. 2020). Besides, infected microglia respond strongly to pro-inflammatory signals, leading to the production of an excess of cytokines, such as TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, C–C motif chemokine ligand 2 (CCL2), and CCL5. Notably, neuronal dysfunction is not directly related to the number of HIV-infected cells or viral antigens present in the CNS; instead, it correlates with increased levels of inflammatory cytokines (Alvarez-Carbonell et al. 2017, 2019; Sreeram et al. 2022; Honeycutt et al. 2024). Microglia, the CNS’s primary immune cells, serve as a key reservoir for latent HIV due to viral DNA integration into active host genes within open chromatin regions (Rheinberger et al. 2023; Tang et al. 2023). HIV disrupts microglial-neuronal interactions and remodels the microglial genome’s 3D structure during infection (Plaza-Jennings et al. 2022). This process triggers IFN responses, shifts chromatin into an active state, and alters viral integration patterns, perpetuating neuropathogenesis (Marini et al. 2015).

Rhabdoviridae

Rhabdoviridae viruses have negative-sense RNA genomes, typically enveloped, infecting various hosts, including plants, animals, and arthropods (Walker et al. 2022).

Rabies virus (RABV), a Lyssavirus, carries the highest fatality rate among all viral encephalitis (Hemachudha et al. 2013). The most effective method of transmitting RABV is through saliva from a bite by an infected animal. Other transmission routes include inhaling aerosolized RABV, receiving tissue and organ transplants, handling or skinning infected carcasses, and contamination of open wounds (Mitrabhakdi et al. 2005). Importantly, neuroinvasion in RABV does not occur by viremia (Reaves et al. 2012a, b). Following deep bites that penetrate the muscle, the virus can infect motor endplates located in the muscle tissue. Sensory and sympathetic nerve endings in the muscle do not facilitate viral uptake. The entry of the virus through the motor pathway relies on the presence of nicotinic acetylcholine receptors at the neuromuscular junction (but not at sensory or autonomic endings). These receptors bind to RABV, which enhances uptake by motor endplates through neural cell adhesion molecules (Graf et al. 2002, Morcuende et al. 2002, Ugolini et al. 2006, Ugolini 2011, Reaves et al. 2012a, b). Infected neurons can survive because virulent RABV strains are able to inhibit apoptosis by keeping viral gene expression below critical levels and by disrupting pro-apoptotic factors such as Bax. As a result, patients with paralytic rabies experience weakness due to peripheral nerve dysfunction, axonopathy, or myelinopathy (Wacharapluesadee and Hemachudha 2002, Sheikh et al. 2005, Lafon 2011, Mader et al. 2012, Hemachudha and Hemachudha 2021).

Togaviridae

Togaviridae is a family of small, enveloped viruses with single-stranded, positive-sense RNA genomes (Chen et al. 2018). These viruses are maintained in nature through transmission cycles involving specific mosquito species and vertebrate hosts. Historically, eastern equine encephalitis virus (EEEV), Venezuelan equine encephalitis virus (VEEV), and western equine encephalitis virus (WEEV) have been responsible for sporadic epizootics in horses and epidemics in humans, resulting in significant morbidity and mortality rates. Survivors of these infections may experience debilitating and sometimes progressive neurological complications (Steele et al. 2007; Zacks and Paessler 2010; Phillips et al. 2016).

Effect of Neurotropic Infection on CNS

Astrocytes and microglia serve as the primary immune responders in the CNS, initiating a rapid, non-specific immune response characterized by the swift release of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, and nitric oxide). This reaction is triggered by the detection of ‘danger’ signals and pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) through pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), such as toll-like receptors (TLRs) (Bsibsi et al. 2002). The expression of chemokines is frequently controlled by cytokines produced by infiltrating leukocytes. And various viral infections have been associated with a swift increase in chemokine production by astrocytes and microglia (Bajetto et al. 2001; Durrant et al. 2014). The detection of viral genetic material and envelope glycoproteins by TLRs results in the secretion of type I interferon (IFN) and activates the transcription of interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs) that play a role in the immune response (Assil et al. 2019). Microglia and astrocytes are classically categorized into an oversimplified categorization of M1 and M2 for microglia and A1 and A2 for astrocytes.

M1 activation, known as classical activation, occurs in response to interferon-γ (IFN-γ) and lipopolysaccharide (LPS). M1 microglia release pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, including TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β, IL-12, and CCL2, and express molecules like nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) oxidase and inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), which contribute to neurological damage. In contrast, M2 activation, or alternative activation, is triggered by anti-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-4 and IL-13. M2 microglia produce anti-inflammatory cytokines like IL-10 and transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β), as well as neurotrophic factors like insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1) or brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) that support neuron survival and tissue repair. They also enhance the phagocytosis of cellular debris and promote extracellular matrix reconstruction. Overall, M1 microglia are associated with inflammation and neurotoxicity, while M2 microglia are linked to anti-inflammatory responses and healing processes (Parisi et al. 2016; Colonna and Butovsky 2017; Nguyen et al. 2017; Guo et al. 2022). The microglial response to viral infections in the CNS involves the release of various chemokines and cytokines, including interleukin (IL)-6, TNF-α, IL-1β, and IFN-γ (Furr and Marriott 2012). Microglia play a crucial role in regulating T-cell-mediated immune responses, particularly through their function as antigen-presenting cells. When microglia present antigens, they activate T cells that produce IFN-γ and TNF, further stimulating microglia to engulf healthy synapses in the aftermath of infection. Additionally, the functions of microglia are influenced by astrocytes and neurons, highlighting a complex interplay within the CNS (Borst et al. 2021; Sutter and Crocker 2022).

Reactive astrocytes are classified into two forms: A1 (neurotoxic) and A2 (neuroprotective). A1 astrocytes are induced by activated microglia through factors like C1q, TNF-α, and IL-1α, leading to neurotoxicity and neuronal death. They upregulate inflammatory markers and disrupt synaptogenesis. In contrast, A2 astrocytes are activated following traumatic brain injury and promote neuroprotection by enhancing synaptic repair and neuronal survival. Induction of A2 astrocytes involves factors like nuclear factor IA (NFIA), which shifts their function toward protection. Overall, while A1 astrocytes exacerbate neuroinflammation, A2 astrocytes support recovery in the CNS (Liddelow and Barres 2017; Liddelow et al. 2017; Cragnolini et al. 2020; Ding et al. 2021). Astrocytes participate in the BBB restoration and integration during virus-induced stress. On the other hand, astrocyte activation during viral infections can happen through various mechanisms, such as direct infection by the virus, microglia activation, the release of viral particles, and the death of neuronal cells. Astrocytes can also release both pro- and anti-inflammatory molecules, and studies have shown that inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and TNF-α can induce astrocytes to release chemokines like CXCL10, CCL1, and CXCL13 that influence immune responses from other immune cells involved (Daniels et al. 2017, Szpakowski et al. 2022, Conti et al. 2024).

Chronic Inflammation

Despite the acute phase of immune responses, neurotropic viruses find ways to counter these efforts; thus, these microorganisms are able to survive in the long term. To achieve prolonged presence, neurotropic viruses develop multiple mechanisms to evade the immune system (Yan et al. 2024). Modulation of immune responses, apoptosis inhibition, and constant antigenic variation are common mechanisms of viruses. Besides, some viruses are capable of entering a latent phase in which viruses reside in neurons without producing antigens. This latency might persist for a long time, with possible reactivation triggered by various stressors (Libbey and Fujinami 2014, Weidner-Glunde et al. 2020, Nath and Johnson 2022a, b, Han et al. 2023, Yan et al. 2024).

Chronic inflammation is the response of the immune system to terminate long-term neurotropic progression. Constant activation of microglia and astrocytes leads to chronic release of inflammatory substances, resulting in heightened oxidative and nitrosative stress, which further sustains the inflammatory cycle. Afterward, oxidative stress causes lipid, protein, and genetic structural alterations that result in the degeneration of nerves (Leszek et al. 2016). Chronic viral and pathogen-mediated proteins in the presence of excess reactive oxygen species induce pathologic changes in neural tissue and lead to chronic inflammation of the brain, as seen in classical neurodegenerative diseases (Rivest 2009; Zhou et al. 2013, Leszek et al. 2016).

Neuroinflammation and excitotoxic damage lead to a decrease in synaptic markers like synaptophysin and debris. Neurodegenerative and neuropsychiatric disorders are linked to elevated levels of neuroinflammatory and excitotoxic markers, as well as increased brain arachidonic acid levels and a reduction in synaptic markers (Rao et al. 2012). Furthermore, the direct insulting effect of cytokines and other inflammatory markers such as TNF-α, IFN-γ, and IL-1β on synaptic transmission (Centonze et al. 2009; Rossi et al. 2011a, b; Yang et al. 2013). Cytokine production in glial cells is heavily influenced by the calcium-dependent protein phosphatase calcineurin, which exhibits increased activity in animal models of aging and disease. Consequently, pro-inflammatory cytokines like TNF-α can enhance the expression and activity of L-type voltage-sensitive calcium channels in neurons, resulting in calcium dysregulation, heightened calcineurin activity, and ultimately lead to synaptic depression (Sama and Norris 2013) (Fig. 1). Via another mechanism, it has been suggested that exposure to soluble inflammatory mediators can disrupt gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABAergic) transmission. Prolonged exposure of cultured neurons to a mix of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IFN-γ, and TNF-α) significantly affects the GABAergic system, resembling the effects of a GABA-A receptor antagonist. IL-1β alters GABAergic synaptic activity by impacting CB1 receptors in the presynaptic compartment, affecting both acute illness and the pre-symptomatic phase of disease (Gentile et al. 2016) (Fig. 2). Brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) is part of the neurotrophin family, which includes nerve growth factor (NGF), neurotrophin-3 (NT-3), and neurotrophin-4/5 (NT-4/5). Neurotrophins display an important role in various dimensions of neuronal and oligodendroglial growth and various cellular activities, such as differentiation, proliferation, survival, life-death regulation, and remodeling synaptic plasticity. Known for its strong protective effects, BDNF supports brain homeostasis, synaptogenesis, plasticity, and cognitive activities. Its activity is evident throughout all stages of development and aging (Huang and Reichardt 2001, Teixeira et al. 2010, Park and Poo 2013, Kowiański et al. 2018, Lima Giacobbo et al. 2019, Notaras and van den Buuse 2020, Xiong et al. 2024a, b).

Besides, the disruption of neurotransmitters is also under the influence of neuroinflammation. Pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, IFN-α, and TNF-α can diminish the availability of molecules necessary for neurotransmission, like serotonin (5-HT), norepinephrine (NE), and dopamine (DA). They activate indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO), which metabolizes tryptophan into kynurenine, reducing 5-HT synthesis. Additionally, cytokine-induced nitric oxide (NO) activation decreases tetrahydrobiopterin, impairing DA and NE production. Activated microglia convert kynurenine to quinolinic acid (QUIN), a potent N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor agonist, leading to excessive glutamate release. This excess glutamate can reduce BDNF (Harry and Kraft 2008; Miller 2009; Hashimoto et al. 2013). Free radicals could trigger cytotoxicity via different routes, such as oxidative stress, which contributes to neuronal cell degeneration. Additionally, stimulated microglia synthesize and release pro-inflammatory factors to combat and eliminate viral infections (Xiong et al. 2024a, b).

Clinical Effect on Neuroplasticity Case Studies

As described by previous mechanisms, neurotropic viral infections affect CNS and display a wide range of symptoms. Pathological features of these cellular and molecular mechanisms are generally shown in cognitive decline and neurodegenerative diseases. The correlation between neurotropic virus infection and neurodegenerative diseases is complex and multifaceted. Viral infections trigger inflammatory responses, disrupt protein homeostasis, and directly affect neurons. This sophisticated complex of viral-induced pathologies leads to neurodegeneration in the long term. A variety of diseases are linked to prior neurotropic viral infection (Davé and Klein 2023; Wongchitrat et al. 2024).

Alzheimer Disease

As a progressive neurodegenerative disease, Alzheimer’s accounts for the most significant driver of dementia in older individuals (Age > 65) (Tahami Monfared et al. 2022). The pathogenesis of Alzheimer’s disease is identified by the abnormal accumulation of proteins within the cerebral tissue, notably amyloid-beta plaques and tau tangles. Amyloid plaques form outside neurons, disrupting cell communication, while tau tangles develop within neurons, ultimately leading to cell death. This process results in significant brain atrophy, particularly in regions responsible for memory and cognitive functions (Tiwari et al. 2019; Rostagno 2022). The infection hypothesis for Alzheimer’s claims to spot a correlation between infectious pathogens and the pathogenesis of AD. Herpesviridae members such as HSV-1, EBV, Kaposi sarcoma-associated herpesvirus (KSHV), and HCMV can affect key pathological features of AD through interactions with host proteins, leading to increased ROS, Aβ formation, neuronal death, and altered autophagy. The dysregulation of these processes may play a significant role in the onset or progression of AD (Onisiforou and Zanos 2023). Infection with VZV in cells that are latently infected with HSV-1 results in the reactivation of HSV-1, leading to the aggregation of Aβ and P-tau (Cairns et al. 2022). Moreover, Herpesviridae may contribute to the progression of AD by exacerbating the aggregation of amyloid-beta (Aβ) and accelerating disease progression, with studies indicating that Aβ can trigger neurofibrillary tangles. Although the evidence suggests that severe neurodegeneration likely involves a combination of plaques, tangles, and neuroinflammation, it does not conclusively establish that herpes infections directly cause β-amyloidosis in the AD brain. Instead, the results point to a potential mechanism linked to chronic viral infections that persist in the CNS (Eimer et al. 2018, Lapeyre et al. 2024, Wang et al. 2024).

Studies indicate that SARS-CoV-2 infection disrupts the balance of amyloid-beta (Aβ42) and tau proteins. In organoid models, SARS-CoV-2 has been reported to initiate hyperphosphorylation and altered distribution of tau. Additionally, Aβ42 may enhance the attachment of ACE2 to the viral spike protein, potentially facilitating viral entry and increasing inflammatory mediators like IL-6 (Ramani et al. 2020; Chen et al. 2023; Di Primio et al. 2023). The interaction between SARS-CoV-2 and ACE2 can inhibit its function, which is crucial for regulating and supports neurogenesis and cognitive processes, and a decrease in BDNF has been associated with Aβ accumulation, tau phosphorylation, and neuroinflammation, further linking COVID-19 to Alzheimer's disease pathology (Chiricosta et al. 2021, Bahmani et al. 2022, Christ et al. 2022, Gao et al. 2022, Furman et al. 2024).

Parkinson’s Disease

Parkinson’s disease (PD), a prevalent neurodegenerative disorder, is mostly common among older adults. It is clinically marked by many symptoms and signs such as resting tremors, bradykinesia, and imbalanced movements. The core disease-driving feature of PD involves the vast loss of dopamine-producing neurons in the substantia nigra (Gerfen 2000; Sveinbjornsdottir 2016; Salamon et al. 2020). Viral infections have been proven to be involved with the induction of PD in acute or chronic forms, and pathogenic viruses could increase the risk of PD, particularly well-known infectious viruses like influenza and hepatitis C (Wang et al. 2020a, b; Smeyne et al. 2021). Neuroinflammation is the most prevalent link between pathogenic virulence with PD by causing immediate or latent offense to the host tissue. Besides, some viruses are associated with extreme aggregation of alpha-synuclein, a presynaptic neuronal protein that plays a significant role in leading to the aggregation of Lewy bodies. Moreover, direct infection and impairment of neurons could also be traced back to neurotropic viral infections (Ouchi et al. 2005; Pradhan and Andreasson 2013; Limphaibool et al. 2019). In this way, in a meta-analysis held by Camacho-Soto et al. (2021) (Camacho-Soto et al. 2021), it was concluded that inverse correlation between Herpesviridae infection and PD. While some papers point out that herpes zoster increases the likelihood of developing PD and vascular dementia, possibly having a direct causal connection to dementia (Lai et al. 2017; Camacho-Soto et al. 2021). Herpes simplex virus has also been found to suggest that HSV may play a role in the development of Parkinson’s disease. However, it is also possible that the elevated levels of HSV antibodies are merely a secondary effect, not directly related to the disease's progression (Marttila and Rinne 1978).

Moreover, in the family of Flaviviridae, Japanese encephalitis virus (JEV) is a well-known contributing factor for Parkinsonism in patients who they clinically infected with JEV. Viral replication primarily happens in specific brain regions, including the thalamus, substantia nigra, basal ganglia, cerebral cortex, cerebellum, and brain stem (Srivastava et al. 2013; Tadokoro et al. 2018). JEV manipulates dopamine signaling to enhance its infection process. The virus utilizes the dopamine D2 receptor (D2R) to activate the phospholipase C (PLC) signaling pathway, facilitating its entry. Dopamine levels and D2R activity are influenced by α-syn, which is crucial for maintaining synaptic balance (Kim et al. 2006; Simanjuntak et al. 2017). Studies revealed a significant increase in the expression of endogenous α-synuclein at both the mRNA and protein levels during the late stages of JEV replication in neuronal cells. This finding aligns with previous observations of elevated α-synuclein levels in the brains of patients infected with other viruses, including WNV and HIV (Khanlou et al. 2009; Beatman et al. 2016; Gupta et al. 2024). In addition, alphaviruses such as Western Equine Encephalitis Virus (WEEV) alter the expression of genes involved with interferon signaling and unfolded protein responses. Alphaviruses also impair mitochondrial energy production, reducing ATP levels and causing oxidative stress, factors that contribute to neuronal damage in PD and other neurodegenerative conditions. In a research performed by Bantle et al. (2019) (Bantle et al. 2019) on mice, it was revealed that WEEV infection leads to the rapid and selective loss of dopamine-producing neurons, and the formation of proteinase K-resistant α-synuclein accumulates. This process specifically targets the substantia nigra pars compacta (SNpc), a region crucial for motor control.

Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS)

As a neurodegenerative disorder, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) targets both upper and lower motor neurons, which is caused by various factors related to neuronal impairment, such as protein misfolding, genetic mutations, and mitochondrial dysfunction. The disease spreads in a manner that suggests a continuous advancement within the motor neuron network, leading to gradually worsening motor weakness (Ravits and La Spada 2009; Tzeplaeff et al. 2023). Infectious agents like bacteria and fungi, especially viruses, are believed to contribute significantly to the development of the disease. They may exert their influence by interacting with key pathways involved in motor neuron degeneration, including gene processing, oxidative stress, and neuroinflammation (Castanedo-Vazquez et al. 2019).

Enteroviruses are generally suspected to contribute as a risk factor in the pathogenesis of ALS because of the capability to invade motor neurons (Xue et al. 2022). In fact, EV infection leads to many impairments within infected neurons, such as nucleocytoplasmic trafficking, causing transactive response DNA binding protein-43 (TDP-43) mislocalization and RNA-processing defects (Xue et al. 2018). They also impair the autophagic pathway, resulting in the aggregation of different types of misfolded proteins. For instance, CVB3 is well known to directly mislocalize TDP-43 proteins, thus contributing to ALS (Fung et al. 2015).

Recently, SARS-CoV-2 has also caught rising attention for its impact on ALS through stress granules (SGs). In this way, Li et al. (2022) (Li et al. 2022), demonstrated that the liquid–liquid phase separation is strongly promoted by the N protein of SARS-CoV-2, enabling it to integrate into host stress granules. This integration alters the typical behavior of SGs, hindering their disassembly once stress is alleviated and resulting in the extended retention of SG components, such as the amyloidogenic protein family of Reoviridae has also been reported to cause ALS and ALS-like syndrome in patients (Moulignier et al. 2001; Araujo 2015; Bowen et al. 2016).

HIV has been proven to cause ALS acquisition in a significantly higher proportion compared to non-infected individuals (Alfahad and Nath 2013). The main mechanism has been discussed in various research studies. So, Douville et al. (2017a, 2017b) described that a possible link between HIV and ALS could be described by deposits of nuclear TDP-43 expression in HIV patients’ cortical neurons. HIV imposes this behavior by different mechanisms, such as elevation of TDP-43, and the correlation with human endogenous retrovirus-K (HERV-K) (Douville and Nath 2017a, b).

Multiple Sclerosis (MS)

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a long-term autoimmune condition that impacts the CNS. Unlike other myelin-related disorders that are self-limiting, MS is characterized by sustained and localized chronic inflammation within the CNS. (Olsson et al. 2017). This disease is marked by episodic, localized, and progressive damage to the protective covering of nerve fibers, resulting from focal inflammatory lesions that lead to ongoing neuroinflammatory and neurodegenerative changes in both white and gray matter, which in turn cause severe impairments affecting autonomic functions, sensory and motor abilities, and cognitive processes. (Absinta et al. 2021). There is growing evidence citing the possible link for viral pathogenesis in MS, causing inflammatory responses affecting astrocytes and microglia. (Fierz 2017; Ten Bosch et al. 2021). EBV is considered a trigger for MS by various mechanisms that raise the risk of MS significantly after EBV infection (Bjornevik et al. 2023). Molecular mimicry of Epstein-Barr nuclear antigen (EBNA1) proteins has the capacity to provoke an immunological response against similar CNS proteins like myelin basic protein (MBP) or glial cell adhesion molecule (GlialCAM) (Lanz et al. 2022; Soldan and Lieberman 2023). EBV can also put a long-lasting immune effect on B cells via dysregulation caused by EBV latent membrane proteins (LMP)-1 and LMP-2. These EBV proteins transform infected B cells into B cells in favor of EBV, thus possibly leading to MS (Serafini et al. 2019). Constant neuroinflammation and epigenetic alteration are among other mechanisms (Horakova et al. 2013).

According to a meta-analysis carried out by Khalesi and colleagues (2023), it was depicted that viral infections with EBV, HHV-6, and VZV are connected with MS, and also could be considered as potential risk factors for the pathogenesis of MS (Khalesi et al. 2023). HHV-6A initiates a primary insult to the CNS, and thereafter, this process triggers inflammatory consequences, potentially driven by HMGB1, leading to increased metalloproteinase activity and an influx of peripheral leukocytes. HHV-6A infection combines with various risk factors, including the presence of HLA-DRB115:01, smoking, limited UV exposure, low vitamin D levels, and EBV infection, to enhance the risk of multiple sclerosis (Lundström and Gustafsson 2022). Conversely, it interacts with protective factors like the HLA-A02:01 haplotype and CMV infection. This complex interplay highlights the multi-cause identity of MS, indicating that the cumulative effect of these risk factors increases the likelihood of developing the disease (Fierz 2017; Keyvani et al. 2020; Lundström and Gustafsson 2022; Sedighi et al. 2023).

Therapeutic Options

Generally, CNS infections have challenges regarding the effectiveness of the management. Complex capabilities of long-term neurotropic viral infection require clinicians to consider therapeutic options to combat multiple impairments on different levels. Lowering inflammation, removing viruses from the tissue, and perhaps repairing the damaged area could be maintained via medical interventions (Nath and Tyler 2013; Autore et al. 2021).

Anti-inflammatory and Neuroprotective Drugs

Drugs with inhibitory effects on inflammatory responses hold a significant portion of medical treatments on long-term neuroinflammatory diseases. Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) are typically prescribed against inflammation. Despite the passing of BBB, NSAIDs are not as proven to be effective against diseases caused by neuroinflammation, especially neurodegenerative diseases (Kaduševičius 2021). Galantamine, an acetylcholinesterase inhibitor, is reported to lower NF-κB activity and thus, the effectiveness of pro-inflammatory cytokines in AD (Liu et al. 2018; Saker et al. 2024). In another attempt, CA140, a dopamine analog for AD, was reported to decrease neuroinflammatory response in AD mouse models as well (Chae et al. 2024). In MS patients, immunosuppressor treatments are applied to reduce neuroinflammation. For instance, Siponimod is a sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P) receptor modulator that displays significant anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective effects on neuronal cells (Behrangi et al. 2019; Gurrea-Rubio et al. 2024). Teriflunomide is also another oral drug for MS patients with an attenuating effect on TNFα-induced inflammatory responses (Kabiraj et al. 2022).

Antivirals

In terms of antiviral therapies, antiretroviral therapies (ARTs) have been known for their vast consumption. Nucleoside analog reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NRTIs), non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NNRTIs), protease inhibitors (PIs), and integrase strand transfer inhibitors (INSTIs) inhibit the function and progression of Reoviridae while having significant side effects (Gay et al. 2024). The antiherpes subclass of antivirals also holds a crucial role in the long-term treatment of neurotropic viral infections. Nucleoside analog drugs (NADs) could be counted as one of the most prescribed medicines in antiviral treatment. Drugs such as Acyclovir (ACV), Brivudin (BVDU), Ganciclovir (GCV), and Valganciclovir (VGCV) are used against a variety of viruses, particularly Herpesviridae. In a meta-analysis conducted on randomized clinical trials for nucleoside antiviral drugs (NAds), it was reported that safety-wise, those drugs were generally reported to be well functioning with minimal adverse effects (AEs) and no significant safety issues (Chen et al. 2017). Other less common classes of drugs worth mentioning are nucleotide analogs such as Cidofovir (CDV), pyrophosphate analogs like Foscarnet (FOS), and quinazoline derivatives like Letermovir (LMV) (Majewska and Mlynarczyk-Bonikowska 2022).

Limitations and Future

The intertwined and complex nature of the CNS, besides the multilayer aspects of long-term viral infections, calls for new treatments to overcome neuroplasticity impairment. These new strategies include novel drugs, neuroenhancers, immunotherapy, gene editing, etc. Novel antivirals have been promising to target new receptors and molecular mechanisms (Lopez-Labrador et al. 2015; Padmakumar et al. 2022). For example, transient receptor potential (TRP) channel blockers are able to influence host-virus interaction by their Ca2 + permeability (Kumar et al. 2023a, b). ACE2 receptor modulators have also caught the attention of SARS-CoV-2 infections targeting viral neuroinvasion (Suvarnapathaki et al. 2022). Furthermore, latency-reversing agents (LRAs) are particularly studied in the context of HIV-1, and promoting new combinative drugs (Divsalar et al. 2020). On the other hand, nanoparticle-based strategies have advanced significantly, enabling targeted drug delivery, gene editing, and vaccine enhancements. These nanoparticles could be made from various materials like lipids and polymers, providing advantages, such as passive targeting and large surface areas (Milovanovic et al. 2017). They enhance treatment efficacy while minimizing side effects through precise targeting mechanisms like the EPR effect. Overall, nanotechnology holds great promise for revolutionizing therapies against latent infections by leveraging these nanoparticle-based approaches (Chakravarty and Vora 2021; Vasukutty et al. 2024; Mukherjee et al. 2025). Likewise, oxytocin (OXT) is also another noteworthy novel option to combat neuronal offenses while maintaining neuroplasticity (Jiang et al. 2022). Oxytocin stimulates neurogenesis in the hypothalamus and hippocampus and influences neuronal plasticity by affecting the expression of neurotrophic factors (Leuner et al. 2012; Lin et al. 2017; Bakos et al. 2018; Bukatova et al. 2023). Physiologically, OXT is also capable of offsetting the inflammation (Li et al. 2017) (Inoue et al. 2019). With a potential of being therapeutic role in the treatment of severe neurodegenerative diseases such as Huntington’s disease, Alzheimer’s disease, ALS, and frontotemporal dementia, OXT is an interesting topic to evaluate (Bergh et al. 2022; Takahashi et al. 2023). Furthermore, positive impacts of OXT for neuroplasticity expand on the social life of the patients as well (Froemke and Young 2021). Considering the accessibility of the hypothalamic oxytocin neurons and the neuroprotective traits of OXT, it would be wise to investigate further therapeutic capacities (Miyata 2015; Wang et al. 2022; Talpo et al. 2023).

The CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing method has also been proven as a useful strategy to combat neuronal damage. Reversing neuronal function in neurodegenerative diseases by viral transduction, targeting pathogenic viruses during CNS infections, and selective targeting of microglial signaling are promising therapeutic options (Kuruvilla et al. 2018; Bellizzi et al. 2019; Cota-Coronado et al. 2019; Pahan 2019). Immunotherapy has a leading edge in novel biotechnological treatments. For instance, chimeric antigen receptor (CAR-T) and CAR-NK cell therapy have recently been studied for diseases other than cancer, and focus on the elimination of infected cells with viruses (Zmievskaya et al. 2021). Moreover, neurogenesis is another field deserving more attention for novel therapeutics. Stem cell therapies emerge as a new wave of sustained treatment for diseases related to neuron loss, especially neurodegenerative diseases (Marsh and Blurton-Jones 2017; Lee et al. 2025).

Another dominating field in future is the realm of data-driven technologies combined with clinical methods to uphold the treatment of patients with critical disability (Saha 2025). Neuroplasticity and digital care, along with artificial intelligence, have already proven to be effective for advanced neurorehabilitation with personalized access (Yan et al. 2025). Certain neuromodulation techniques offer neuroprotection by dampening inflammation, preventing cell death, and minimizing oxidative damage. Deep-brain stimulation (DBS) lowers the expression of related genes to inflammation and inhibits the normal functioning of microglia and astrocytes. Similarly, vagus nerve stimulation (VNS) may safeguard the brain from injury by promoting anti-inflammatory pathways and decreasing blood–brain barrier disruption. For example, closed-loop brain-computer interfaces (BCIs) and DBS leverage neural activity data to reinforce synaptic rewiring in patients with CNS diseases (Chan et al. 2018; Srihagulang et al. 2022; Liu et al. 2026). Conclusion

The study of neurotropic viral infections reveals a sophisticated interaction between viral pathogenesis and neuroplasticity, significantly impacting the CNS. These infections can lead to both acute and chronic neuroinflammation, followed by neurological consequences, and the clinical evidence indicates that viruses might disrupt synaptic plasticity and neuronal function directly or indirectly, depending on the lasting neuroinflammation. Current understanding of neurotropic viral infection also highlights other mechanisms of neuronal impairments from a long-term perspective. Hopefully, combinative novel therapeutic options are developing to face current challenges, yet there are existing limitations, such as complex viral interactions with the immune system. Considering these limitations, novel research must put emphasis on comprehending the underlying mechanisms of neuroplasticity alterations because of infections and their long-lasting adverse effect. In conclusion, addressing the impact of neurotropic viral infections on long-term neuroplasticity is crucial for advancing therapeutic strategies.
