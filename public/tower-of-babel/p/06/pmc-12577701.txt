New magnetic topological materials from high-throughput search
Authors: Iñigo Robredo, Yuanfeng Xu, Yi Jiang, Claudia Felser, B. Andrei Bernevig, Luis Elcoro, Nicolas Regnault, Maia G. Vergniory
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
We conducted a high-throughput search for topological magnetic materials across 522 new, experimentally reported commensurate magnetic structures from MAGNDATA, doubling the number of available materials on the Topological Magnetic Materials database. This brings up to date the previous studies. For each material, we performed first-principles electronic calculations and diagnosed the topology as a function of the Hubbard U parameter. Our high-throughput calculation led us to the prediction of 250 experimentally relevant topologically nontrivial materials, which represent 47.89% of the newly analyzed materials. We present five remarkable examples of these materials, each showcasing a different topological phase: Mn2AlB2 (BCSID 1.508), which exhibits a nodal line semimetal to topological insulator transition as a function of SOC; CaMnSi (BCSID 0.599), a narrow gap axion insulator; UAsS (BCSID 0.594), a 5f-orbital Weyl semimetal; CsMnF4 (BCSID 0.327), a material presenting a new type of quasi-symmetry protected closed nodal surface; and FeCr2S4 (BCSID 0.613), a symmetry-enforced semimetal with double Weyls and spin-polarized surface states.

INTRODUCTION

For decades, electronic topological systems have been a research hotspot in the field of condensed matter physics. The development of topological quantum chemistry (TQC) (1, 2) and symmetry-based indicators (3–7) has facilitated the prediction of topological quantum materials in realistic systems. In particular, it allowed for high-throughput searches of topological quantum materials resulting in extensive databases (8–11) like the Topological Quantum Chemistry website https://www.topologicalquantumchemistry.org/. Recent developments have shown that the interplay of spin and charge degrees of freedom in magnetic systems can realize topological phases such as axion insulators (AXIs) (12–16) or nonaxionic magnetic higher-order topological insulators (TIs) (2, 17–20) These phases, unique to magnetic systems, combine the robustness of topological surface states with spin ordering, promising a wide array of applications, such as in materials for quantum computation (21), spintronics (22), and catalysis (23, 24). The current focus now lies on finding materials that display the aforementioned topological properties and can be readily grown. To enable the prediction and diagnosis of magnetic topological phases of matter, the formalism of TQC was extended to magnetic TQC (MTQC) (2), resulting in the prediction of 137 new magnetic topological materials (17, 25).

In this work, we use MTQC to conduct a high-throughput analysis on the new entries in MAGNDATA http://www.cryst.ehu.es/magndata/, extending the Topological Magnetic Materials https://www.topologicalquantumchemistry.fr/magnetic (17) database from 372 to 894 entries and adding 522 new experimentally reported stoichiometric magnetic materials with commensurate magnetic order. The main result of our work is the prediction of 250 experimentally relevant topologically nontrivial materials. We define a material as topologically nontrivial if we diagnose it as topological for at least one value of the interaction strength, parameterized by the Hubbard U value (26). We found several examples of interaction-driven topology: systems in which the interaction can onset a topological phase transition.

In our search, we found 98 new TIs, which can be broken down into different topological classes. We found 84 AXIs, 3 magnetic topological crystalline insulators (MTCIs), and 34 three-dimensional quantum anomalous Hall systems, as well as 50 magnetic obstructed atomic insulators (mOAIs), a special case of TIs that can be explained in terms of localized orbitals but whose Wannier charge center is localized away from atomic positions (1, 25, 27–29). The latter is relevant since their nonmagnetic counterparts have been predicted to have good catalytic activity (24, 25); and magnetic systems are known to improve some catalytic reactions, such as oxygen evolution reaction (30–32). We therefore speculate that mOAI may be promising materials displaying both features. In this category, we find a promising candidate, CuFeO2 https://www.topologicalquantumchemistry.fr/magnetic/index.html#?BCSID=1.347 (BCSID 1.347 http://www.cryst.ehu.es/magndata/index.php?index=1.347), which holds the same topological diagnosis for all values of the Hubbard parameter U. We also predict 182 new enforced semimetals (ESs) with symmetry-enforced degeneracies at high-symmetry points, lines or planes. One of the most relevant magnetic semimetals are Weyl semimetals, due to various proposed effects and applications, such as Veselago lenses, quantized circular photogalvanic effect, or unconventional charge to spin conversion (33–36). Contrary to nonmagnetic systems where predicting Weyl crossings is difficult in terms of symmetry indicators (SIs) (37), the MTQC formalism can predict Weyl crossings in magnetic systems under certain conditions (2). On the basis of SI, MTQC can predict the difference in the Chern numbers between high-symmetry planes, which assures the existence of a quantum critical point along the momentum separating them. This degeneracy point carrying topological charge forces the system to be semimetallic, thus indicating the presence of Weyl nodes. These systems are also known as Smith index semimetals or symmetry-indicated Weyl semimetals (2, 17, 38). In our high-throughput search, we found 13 symmetry-indicated Weyl semimetals, among which we found that the ferromagnetic Weyl semimetal Co3Sn2S2 https://www.topologicalquantumchemistry.fr/magnetic/index.html#?BCSID=0.860 (BCSID 0.860 http://www.cryst.ehu.es/magndata/index.php?index=0.860), which has been well-studied previously (39–41), can be reinterpreted by a topological index η4I=3.

From our search results, we have identified five especially promising materials, two TIs and three ESs, which we selected for further analysis of their topological properties. We selected these materials so that we could have a representative selection of the different topological phases found in our search, as well as having a clean Fermi level, so that the topological properties can be easily accessible. First, we present Mn2AlB2 https://www.topologicalquantumchemistry.fr/magnetic/index.html#?BCSID=1.508 (BCSID 1.508 http://www.cryst.ehu.es/magndata/index.php?index=1.508), a layered antiferromagnetic system that displays a nodal line to TI transition when the spin-orbit coupling (SOC) is turned on. Then, we consider materials within the CeFeSi family of small gap magnetic insulators (42). We found that one material in the family, CaMnSi https://www.topologicalquantumchemistry.fr/magnetic/index.html#?BCSID=0.599 (BCSID 0.599 http://www.cryst.ehu.es/magndata/index.php?index=0.599), displays a band inversion that drives the topological transition to an AXI. In the ES class, we present three examples, namely, UAsS https://www.topologicalquantumchemistry.fr/magnetic/index.html#?BCSID=0.594 (BCSID 0.594 http://www.cryst.ehu.es/magndata/index.php?index=0.594), CsMnF4 https://www.topologicalquantumchemistry.fr/magnetic/index.html#?BCSID=0.327 (BC-SID 0.327 http://www.cryst.ehu.es/magndata/index.php?index=0.327), and FeCr2S4 https://www.topologicalquantumchemistry.fr/magnetic/index.html#?BCSID=0.613 (BCSID 0.613 http://www.cryst.ehu.es/magndata/index.php?index=0.613). The first is a ferromagnetic Weyl and nodal line semimetal with double Fermi arcs on the surface. The second is a layered square net ferromagnetic system that displays a symmetry-protected nodal line. While already reported in (43), our analysis proves that there is a hidden quasi-symmetry (44, 45) that protects an almost closed two-dimensional (2D) nodal surface, enhancing the semimetallic behavior at the Fermi level. Last, FeCr2S4 https://www.topologcalquantumchemistry.fr/magnetic/index.html#?BCSID=0.613 (BCSID 0.613 http://www.cryst.ehu.es/magndata/index.php?index=0.613) is a ferrimagnetic system with symmetry-protected nodal lines and topologically protected Weyl nodes, as well as double Weyl nodes (46).

This work is divided as follows. In the Materials and Methods, we present the workflow and methods for the high-throughput search. In the Results, we summarize the topological properties of the five selected materials. The Discussion provides the discussion of the main results of our search. In text S1, we give an overview of the formalism of MTQC and explain the origin of the SIs and real space invariants (RSIs) (25, 27) using magnetic space group (MSG) P1¯ as an example. Text S2 gives the full analysis of the topological properties of the selected example materials. Last, texts S3 to S6 contain the full tables summarizing the results of our materials search: Text S3 includes a comparison between experimental and calculated magnetic moments, and text S4 covers the topological classification and both direct and indirect gaps. Last, in text S5, we give the physical interpretation of the systems with nontrivial SIs, and text S6 contains detailed information on the crystal structure, MSG, electronic band structure, and topological classification of all materials.

RESULTS

As representatives of the different topological classes found in this work, we selected five examples, each showcasing a different topological phase: Mn2AlB2 https://www.topologicalquantumchemistry.fr/magnetic/index.html#?BCSID=1.508 (BCSID 1.508 http://www.cryst.ehu.es/magndata/index.php?index=1.508), which exhibits a nodal line semimetal to TI transition, CaMnSi https://www.topologicalquantumchemistry.fr/magnetic/index.html#?BCSID=0.599 (BCSID 0.599 http://www.cryst.ehu.es/magndata/index.php?index=0.599), a narrow gap AXI, UAsS https://www.topologicalquantumchemistry.fr/magnetic/index.html#?BCSID=0.594 (BCSID 0.594 http://www.cryst.ehu.es/magndata/index.php?index=0.594), a 5f-shell ferromagnetic Weyl and nodal line semimetal, CsMnF4 https://www.topologicalquantumchemistry.fr/magnetic/index.html#?BCSID=0.327 (BCSID 0.327 http://www.cryst.ehu.es/magndata/index.php?index=0.327), a material presenting a new type of quasi-symmetry protected closed nodal surface, and FeCr2S4 https://www.topologicalquantumchemistry.fr/magnetic/index.html#?BCSID=0.613 (BCSID 0.613 http://www.cryst.ehu.es/magndata/index.php?index=0.613), a symmetry-enforced ferrimagnetic semimetal with double Weyls and spin-polarized surface states.

As an example of interaction strength-dependent topology, we selected Mn2AlB2 https://www.topologicalquantumchemistry.fr/magnetic/index.html#?BCSID=1.508 (BCSID 1.508 http://www.cryst.ehu.es/magndata/index.php?index=1.508) in MSG 63.466 ( Ccmcm ) (see Fig. 1A). The case with U=0 most accurately reproduced the experimentally measured magnetic momenta, in agreement with previous reports (47). The magnetic moment we compute for the Mn atoms is ∼0.8μB , very close to the experimental result ( ∼0.7μB ). The expected magnetic moment from high spin Mn2+ is ∼5.9μB . This difference is consistent with partially itinerant, reduced-moment antiferromagnetism, with further studies being required to fully determine if this is the case. This is also the only U value for which the system is a TI. In particular, it is diagnosed as η4I=2,δ2m=1 . The η4I=2 SI implies that the system is in the nontrivial axionic phase, that is, the axion angle is quantized to θ=π . Therefore, if the surface is fully gapped, then the system will present half-quantized values of surface anomalous Hall conductivity (16, 48–51). The other nontrivial SI, δ2m=1 , indicates a difference in the mirror Chern number of the kz=0,π planes. This forces the mirror Chern number to be nonzero in at least one of the planes, which implies the presence of symmetry-protected Dirac nodes on the surfaces preserving the mirror symmetry. Upon neglecting the SOC, we found symmetry-protected nodal lines, with their corresponding drumhead surface states (see Fig. 2A).

CaMnSi https://www.topologicalquantumchemistry.fr/magnetic/index.html#?BCSID=0.599 (BCSID 0.599 http://www.cryst.ehu.es/magndata/index.php?index=0.599) in MSG 129.416 ( P4′/n′m′m ), is an example of AXI which is indicated by index z2=1 (see Fig. 1B). One of the major distinctions between AXI and 3D topological insulators (3DTIs) is that the topological surface Dirac cones of a 3DTI are gapped in the AXI phase because of the time reversal symmetry (TRS) breaking (Fig. 2B). As stated previously in the case of Mn2AlB2 https://www.topologicalquantumchemistry.fr/magnetic/index.html#?BCSID=1.508, if the AXI surface is fully gapped, it presents a half-quantized surface anomalous Hall conductivity (49–51). AXIs are also predicted to display large magnetoelectric response, as well as quantized Kerr rotation (52). AXI materials are also a promising platform to study Majorana edge modes (53). The computed gap of CaMnSi https://www.topologicalquantumchemistry.fr/magnetic/index.html#?BCSID=0.599 (BCSID 0.599 http://www.cryst.ehu.es/magndata/index.php?index=0.599) using Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof functional is small, and the topology is extremely sensitive to gap closings. Thus, we improved the accuracy of gap prediction by repeating the calculation with a meta–generalized gradient approximation (GGA) functional [modified Becke-Johnson functional (54)]. The smallest direct gap using this functional is 6.1 meV, and the symmetry-based topological diagnosis remains unchanged, which reassures the topological diagnosis (see text S2 for further details).

We choose UAsS https://www.topologicalquantumchemistry.fr/magnetic/index.html#?BCSID=0.594 (BCSID 0.594 http://www.cryst.ehu.es/magndata/index.php?index=0.594) in MSG 129.417 ( P4/nm′m′ ) as an example of 5f-electron heavy fermion ferromagnetic semimetal (see Fig. 1C). We selected this compound from the family due to its relative simplicity at the Fermi level. Having a clean Fermi surface allows an easier identification of topological signatures. Similar to Mn2AlB2 https://www.topologicalquantumchemistry.fr/magnetic/index.html#?BCSID=1.508, the Hubbard U value that fits best is U=0 eV. We find Weyl nodes and nodal lines, both with their characteristic surface states. In particular, although there are only single-Weyls (topological charge 1), when projecting on the (100) surface termination, equal charge pairs of Weyl nodes are projected on the same point, thus giving rise to two Fermi arcs stemming from each of their surface projections (Fig. 2C). Heavy fermion Weyl semimetal systems are of particular interest to study the interplay of electronic correlations and topological protection of Weyl nodes, such as Weyl-Kondo systems (55, 56).

We also study a 3d-electron ferromagnetic ES, CsMnF4 https://www.topologicalquantumchemistry.fr/magnetic/index.html#?BCSID=0.327 (BCSID 0.327 http://www.cryst.ehu.es/magndata/index.php?index=0.327), in MSG 59.410 ( Pmm′n′ ) (see Fig. 1D). This material is an example of a ferromagnetic half metal, where one spin channel is fully gapped and the bands close to the Fermi level are spin polarized. Note that there are two different reported structures for this material (57, 58). We choose the first structure for this work (57), which is the one available in MAGNDATA http://www.cryst.ehu.es/magndata/ and has been studied with density functional theory (DFT) before (43). We find almost no change in magnetization with respect to the Hubbard U parameter: The system remains half-metallic for all U values, but the band structure and topological properties are affected by U. Our calculations show that this system is an ES in the range U ≤ 3 eV and presents a topological transition from ES to trivial insulator at U = 4 eV (see text S6). Further analysis identifies an approximate nodal surface in this phase (Fig. 2D). The bands close to the Fermi level mainly stem from Mn atoms, which form a square lattice with a 2 × 2 supercell. As we detail in text S2, the extra translational symmetry associated with the 2 × 2 supercell is responsible for folding the bands and creating the approximate nodal surface. The extra translational symmetry prevents the gap from opening (at first order), thereby demonstrating the recently proposed quasisymmetric-protected phase (44, 45), which can enforce small, nonvanishing gaps that can be sources of large Berry curvature. Full details on the system can be found in text S2.

Last, we analyze FeCr2S4 https://www.topologicalquantumchemistry.fr/magnetic/index.html#?BCSID=0.613 (BCSID 0.613 http://www.cryst.ehu.es/magndata/index.php?index=0.613) in MSG 141.557 ( I41/am′d′ ), which is an example of ferrimagnetic system with a clean Fermi level, where the low-energy physics is governed by a few connected bands (see Fig. 1E). In particular, there are four bands close to the Fermi level, and the relevant crossings occur between the last valence and the first conduction bands. We observed multi-Weyl nodes with a topological charge of 2 along the Γ− M line and a mirror symmetry–protected nodal line at kz=0 . The charge C>1 of these Weyl nodes is enforced by the fourfold symmetry axis that leaves the Γ− M line invariant (59–61) and gives rise to 2 Fermi arcs per node. The nodal line gives rise to drumhead surface states in the (001) surface termination, whereas the other remnant gapped nodal lines’ drumhead states are also gapped. The four bands crossing the Fermi level are purely spin-polarized: The resulting surface states are also spin-polarized.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we performed complete DFT + U calculations and topological diagnosis of 522 new experimentally reported materials from the MAGNDATA http://www.cryst.ehu.es/magndata/ database. The main result is the addition of 250 new experimentally relevant topological materials to the Topological Magnetic Materials https://www.topologicalquantumchemistry.fr/magnetic database, which now counts with 894 entries, of which 43.29% are reported as topologically nontrivial. We present a summary of topologically classified materials in Table 1. Among these, we report 50 mOAIs, although most of them failed to satisfy our criteria of ideal topological material, in this case, having an indirect gap. The most promising candidate, CuFeO2 https://www.topologicalquantumchemistry.fr/magnetic/index.html#?BCSID=1.347 (BCSID 1.347 http://www.cryst.ehu.es/magndata/index.php?index=1.347) is an mOAI for all Hubbard U values. During the preparation of this manuscript, it was predicted that CrSb https://www.topologicalquantumchemistry.fr/magnetic/index.html#?BCSID=0.528 (BCSID 0.528 http://www.cryst.ehu.es/magndata/index.php?index=0.528) is an altermagnet Weyl semimetal (62–64). Since it has an even number of Weyl nodes in each half of the Brillouin Zone (BZ), its topology cannot be diagnosed using SIs. However, we found that the system is an mOAI at the Fermi level, providing new insights into the topological properties of this highly interesting material.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

In this section, we present the main symmetry-diagnosable categories of topological materials and our computational methods.

MTQC brief overview

The formalism of MTQC has been extensively described and reviewed in prior work (2, 17, 65, 66). Here, we briefly review the fundamental building blocks and the resulting topological classification. The electronic band structures of solids can be classified according to the symmetry properties of the Bloch wave functions at high-symmetry momenta. This information is encoded in a vector containing the multiplicity of the irreducible corepresentations (coreps) of the occupied set of bands called symmetry-data-vector. A symmetry-data-vector is identified as topologically trivial if it can be expressed as a sum of elementary band representations (EBRs) with positive integer coefficients. The trivial symmetry-data-vectors, also called band representations (BRs), conform the space of trivial insulators. Physically, the sum of EBRs can be understood as the addition of trivial atomic insulators. If a system can be expressed as a collection of trivial insulators, then the studied band structure can be adiabatically connected to a trivial insulator, and it is thus diagnosed as topologically trivial.

There are five possible outcomes when we express a symmetry-data-vector in terms of EBRs:

1) Linear combination of EBRs. In this case, the symmetry-data-vector can be expressed as linear combination of EBRs with positive integer coefficients. Therefore, the system is compatible with a description in terms of localized magnetic orbitals in real space. In addition to atomic insulators that are topologically trivial, a topologically distinct phase exists, i.e., the mOAI. In the latter case, the occupied electronic bands are induced from localized magnetic orbitals away from the atomic positions and there exists an obstruction to reverting them to the original atomic location. These systems exhibit special surface states pinned by crystalline symmetries that do not connect valence and conduction bands and can be diagnosed via the RSI (25, 27).

2) Linear combination of EBRs with, necessarily, at least one negative integer coefficient in the EBR decomposition (fragile). This solution is inconsistent with a trivial insulator, but the addition of extra orbitals to the system would render it topologically trivial (1, 2, 4–6).

3) TI. The solution of the decomposition into EBRs has fractional coefficients and indicates stable topology. For instance, AXI, MTCI, or (M)HOTIs (17, 49) belong to that category. They can be compactly diagnosed by SIs (see text S1 for further details) (1, 2, 4–6, 10). There are two subcategories, split EBR (SEBR), in which the solution can be expressed in terms of EBRs and parts of sEBRs, and “nonlinear combination” (NLC), in which case there is no decomposition into EBRs and parts of sEBRs.

4) ES. This case arises if the symmetry-data-vector (1, 2, 4, 6) is incompatible with an insulator. There are two types of ESs: First, if by electron counting, a corep at a high symmetry point is only partially filled, we classify the material as ES with Fermi degeneracy (ESFD). In case the Fermi level is clean from electron or hole pockets, the Fermi energy would exactly intersect the degenerate crossing enforced by symmetry. Second, even if the bands are gapped at every high-symmetry k-point, if the compatibility relations are not satisfied (2, 17), the bands must be gapless at some point along a high-symmetry line or plane in the BZ. The formalism does not predict the exact point in which the crossing must occur, but it can predict certain high symmetry lines or planes in which the crossing happens.

5) Smith-index SM (SISM). Unlike ESs in which the crossing is protected as well as predicted by symmetry, SISMs satisfy the compatibility relations (1, 2), i.e., the symmetry-data-vector is compatible with an insulator. However, a difference in the Chern number between high-symmetry planes assures the existence of a quantum critical point along the path (58). This degeneracy point, carrying topological charge, forces the system to be semimetallic (e.g., Weyl semimetals).

The symmetry-based diagnosis poses certain limitations. In general, symmetry-based topological diagnosis is a sufficient condition to classify a compound as topologically nontrivial, but it is not a necessary condition. Therefore, in cases with scarce symmetry, MTQC can diagnose nontrivial topology as trivial. This formalism, however, serves as a computationally inexpensive diagnosing method (as opposed to, e.g., Wilson loops) for high-throughput prediction of topological materials (8–10, 17).

Methods

We start by selecting 522 new material entries from the BCS magnetic material database MAGNDATA http://www.cryst.ehu.es/magndata/ (67, 68). In this database, both the crystalline and magnetic structures have been derived from experiments. As the determination of the magnetic ground state is a computationally challenging task, we initialize our calculations with the experimental magnetization and ensure their self-consistent convergence. This is expected to yield much more accurate results than determining the magnetic ordering from first principles. The process will generally converge to distinct magnetic structures as a function of the Hubbard U parameter, which accounts for the localization properties of magnetic elements’ electrons in d- and f-shells. We summarize the resulting magnetization as a function of the Hubbard U parameter in text S3. On the basis of these results, we can estimate the most probable topological classification for a given material, by selecting the one that deviates the least from the experimentally reported magnetic structure.

We performed DFT calculations as implemented in the Vienna Ab initio simulation package (69–72). The interaction between the ion cores and valence electrons was treated by the projector augmented-wave method (73), the GGA was used for the exchange-correlation potential with the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof functional for solid parameterization (74), and the SOC was considered on the basis of the second variation method (75). A Γ-centered Monkhorst-Pack k-point grid of ( 9by9by9 ) was used for reciprocal space integration and 500 eV energy cutoff of the plane-wave expansion. The grid density of the largest materials was reduced up to ( 5by5by5 ) k-points. The determination of the best Hubbard U value depends strongly on the chemical environment of the magnetic atoms (see discussion in the Materials Project website) (76). We thus run our calculations for different Hubbard U values. For materials with d-electron elements, we performed DFT + U calculations with Hubbard U parameter values of U = 0, 1, 2, 3, and 4 eV. For f-electron elements, which tend to be more localized, we used larger Hubbard U values, U = 0, 2, 4, and 6 eV. In the presence of both d- and f-electron elements, we set the Hubbard U value of the d-electron element to U = 2 eV and vary the f-electron element U parameter, following the criteria of (17). All materials presented herein were converged self-consistently to a magnetic ground state with an accuracy of 10−5 eV per unit cell. We then run two nonself-consistent band calculations; one along high symmetry lines to get the electronic band dispersion and another one at the high-symmetry points to obtain the wave functions. We use Magvasp2trace (2, 17) on the latter to obtain the magnetic coreps and BR of the occupied set of bands, which we then upload to the BCS server (77–79) for symmetry-based topological prediction. High throughput flowchart.

We categorize the materials in the MAGNDATA database into two groups: one containing d electrons and no f electrons and another one containing f electrons. We then conduct ab initio calculations using Vienna Ab initio simulation package (VASP) (69–72) with multiple Hubbard U parameters to obtain the band structure as well as the magnetic coreps (2, 80). Subsequently, the corresponding symmetry-data-vector can be uploaded to the BCS to obtain the topological classification.
