In vitro fertilization-frozen embryo transfer in a patient with gonadotroph adenoma: Exacerbation of ovarian hyperstimulation by triptorelin: a case report
Authors: Feifei Lu, Manlin Xu, Xiaoyue Shen, Na Kong, Jingyu Liu, Yue Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFunctional gonadotropic adenoma is a rare pituitary adenoma. Only a few cases have been reported.Case presentationHerein, we describe the case of a 30-year-old Chinese woman with functional gonadotroph adenomas (FGAs) with relative hyperprolactinemia and no obvious neurologic signs. Ovarian hyperstimulation due to a follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH)-secreting adenoma was observed after treatment with a gonadotropin-releasing hormone analogue (GnRH-a). The patient was successfully treated through pituitary adenomectomy and experienced a successful pregnancy after in vitro fertilization (IVF).ConclusionOur report suggests that a patient showing ovarian hyperstimulation related to an FSH-secreting adenoma may present with high PRL and estradiol levels and indicates that transsphenoidal surgery should be the first choice for treatment.

Introduction

Functional gonadotroph adenomas (FGAs) are infrequent pituitary adenomas [1]. Given the current diagnostic and pathologic challenges, only a small number of cases of patients with clinically and pathologically confirmed FGA have been reported. Follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) hypersecretion caused by gonadotroph adenomas typically might not exhibit any specific signs or symptoms. Under the influence of excessive gonadotropin stimulation, ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome due to FGA is rarely reported. Here, we present another case of a live birth in a childbearing-age woman with hyperprolactinemia and primary infertility associated with FGA.

Case report presentation

The patient, a 30-year-old Chinese woman, experienced menarche at 15 years of age and sought medical advice for primary infertility and hyperprolactinemia. She did not present any neurological or visual complaints. Laboratory tests conducted in August 2018 indicated hyperprolactinemia, with a serum prolactin (PRL) level of 53.4 ng/mL(< 20 ng/mL, normal reference range), and the patient appeared to have been successfully treated with bromocriptine. In May 2019, initial laboratory tests disclosed the following results: FSH 8.58 mIU/mL, LH 0.42 mIU/mL, anti-Mullerian hormone (AMH) 3.33 ng/mL, estradiol 1468 pg/mL, and PRL 10.9 ng/mL (Table 1). Upon pelvic transvaginal ultrasonography, the ovaries were observed to be bilaterally enlarged, with a endometrial stripe of 3.5 mm (Fig. 1A). For the assisted reproduction procedure, we employed a long-acting gonadotropin-releasing hormone agonist (GnRH-a) protocol to attain pituitary downregulation. In July 2019, a single dose of 3.75 mg of long-acting triptorelin (Germany, Ferring GmbH) was administered. After 1 month of triptorelin therapy, the patient exhibited distinct clinical and radiological symptoms of ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome (OHSS). The relevant laboratory data were as follows: serum estradiol 6031 pg/mL, progesterone 13.5 ng/mL, FSH 45.5 mIU/mL, and LH 0.52 mIU/mL (Table 1). After 2 months of triptorelin therapy, the patient's serum estradiol level was significantly above normal, at 3000 pg/mL, while her other hormone levels were as follows: progesterone 9.75 ng/mL, FSH 71.5 mIU/mL, and LH 0.3 mIU/mL (Table 1). Follow-up pelvic ultrasonography indicated the recurrence of enlarged ovaries. Subsequently, oral contraceptive therapy was administered for 1 month.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was conducted because of the paradoxical hormone release observed under the influence of GnRH-a treatment. which revealed a sellar lesion measuring 2.5 cm × 1.4 cm (Fig. 3). Thus, we suspected the possibility of FGA. In November 2019, an uncomplicated transsphenoidal resection of the pituitary adenoma was performed. Histopathological examination showed a 1.5 cm × 1 cm × 0.7 cm, gray-red solid mass. Immunocytochemical examination of the excised tissue indicated weak positivity for FSH and p53, whereas no positivity was detected for GH, LH, PRL and ACTH. The Ki-67 labeling index was 3%, and the synaptophysin (Syn) and CD56 expression showed strong reactivity (Fig. 4). Laboratory tests after the operation showed an estradiol level of 80.3 pg/mL, an FSH level of 4.32 mIU/mL, and an LH level of 1.86 mIU/mL (Table 1). Postoperative transvaginal ultrasonography revealed normal ovaries, with six antral follicles in the left ovary (23 × 26 × 17 mm3) and nine antral follicles in the right ovary (25 × 23 × 20 mm3) (Fig. 5). After 5 months, an in vitro fertilization procedure was carried out. Recombinant follicle-stimulating hormone (rFSH; Gonal-f®, Merck Serono) was utilized for controlled ovarian hyperstimulation (COH), starting with an rFSH dose of 150 IU on days 2–3 of the menstrual cycle, followed by a daily rFSH dose determined based on the patient's response. A GnRH antagonist (Cetrotide®, Merck Serono) was added to the fixed protocol daily when the largest follicle measured more than 12 mm to prevent premature ovulation before the human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) trigger. Recombinant hCG (Ovidrel; Merck Serono, Geneva, Switzerland) was administered once there were a minimum of three follicles measuring at least 17 mm as per an ultrasound scan. Oocyte retrieval was performed 36 h after the hCG injection, and a total 15 oocytes were retrieved. The oocytes were then fertilized with a freshly processed semen sample. Two cleavage-stage embryos and five blastocysts were cryopreserved through vitrification.

Two months after oocyte retrieval, the patient received oral estradiol valerate (Aburaihan Co., Tehran, Iran) at a dosage of 6 mg/d, starting on the second day of the menstrual cycle. Endometrial thickness was measured using vaginal ultrasonography on the 14th day of the menstrual cycle. When it reached ≥ 8 mm, progesterone in oil (60 mg/d) was administered through intramuscular injection. The frozen embryo transfer (FET) of a single blastocyst was performed 6 days after progesterone administration. Testing for β-hCG was positive 14 days later, and a singleton pregnancy was confirmed by transvaginal ultrasound conducted in the sixth week of gestation. She delivered a healthy boy transvaginally at 40 weeks of gestation (3200 g, Apgar 9/10).

Discussion

Gonadotroph adenomas compromise 15% to 40% of pituitary adenomas. However, functioning gonadotroph adenomas, which secrete FSH and LH, are rare. FGAs are benign tumors of the pituitary gland that secrete biologically active gonadotropins (FSH and/or LH) [2]. The most common clinical manifestations of FGA in premenopausal women include menstrual irregularity, infertility, galactorrhea, and other neurological symptoms (mainly headaches and visual deterioration) [3]. Ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome is usually an excessive ovarian response to ovulation induction therapies and has serious life-threatening complications [4].

Our patient visited an outpatient clinic for evaluation due to abnormal uterine bleeding and infertility. Transvaginal ultrasound examination revealed enlarged ovaries with multiple cysts, which were attributed to inappropriately elevated FSH levels resulting from a pituitary adenoma. As most gonadotroph adenomas are non-functional, the pathogenesis of FGAs remains unclear, and research in this area is hampered by the rarity of this condition. In patients with gonadotroph adenomas, the amount of LH is lower than that of FSH. The decrease in LH production may be attributed to the compression of the normal pituitary tissue by the gonadotroph adenoma or the suppression of high estradiol levels [5]. The long-term spotting (low volume and flow) observed in such cases is probably due to the relatively inadequate production of progesterone in the presence of large amounts of estradiol. This leads to incomplete destruction of the endometrium and long-term dysfunctional uterine bleeding. Persistent exposure to FSH promotes a continuous ovulatory state, which can lead to recurrent breakthrough bleeding due to hyperstimulation of the endometrium.

Pituitary adenomas are mostly associated with tumor mass effects such as visual disturbance or other neurological symptoms. However, our patient did not experience any of these clinical symptoms [6]. A retrospective review of her case report revealed that the first clinical sign of a pituitary adenoma was hyperprolactinemia, which was detected 2 years before the final diagnosis. The moderate increase in her prolactin level was probably due to the stimulation of thecal cells by LH or pituitary stalk compression [7]. Moreover, FSH immunostaining of the resected tumor confirmed the diagnosis of an FSH-secreting pituitary adenoma. In addition, the preoperative multicystic ovarian enlargement, together with complete postoperative normalization of ovarian morphology and reproductive hormone profiles, strongly indicates that the adenoma secreted biologically active FSH capable of direct ovarian follicular stimulation, further supporting the diagnosis.

The mechanism and pathophysiology of FGA are rather complex (Fig. 6). Spontaneous ovarian hyperstimulation attributable to gonadotroph adenoma is rare. OHSS was revealed by the exacerbation of ovarian hyperstimulation after GnRH injection [8]. The GnRH agonist treatment administered to our patient caused a significant increase in the plasma estradiol concentration as well as in the size and number of follicular cysts. Patients with gonadotroph adenoma exhibit multiple bilateral enlarged ovarian cysts, similar to those with the common iatrogenic OHSS. However, there are significant clinical differences between the two conditions. The common iatrogenic OHSS is usually the most common medicinal complication of the administration of a GnRH agonist and ovulation induction treatment, whereas gonadotroph adenoma is a condition resulting in endogenous hyperstimulation of the ovaries. Moreover, patients with the common iatrogenic OHSS show alterations in vascular permeability and fluid dynamics, while patients with gonadotroph adenomas do not [9]. In our patient, the administration of GnRH-a to reduce estradiol and FSH levels was unsuccessful. In fact, this treatment has even been reported to further stimulate gonadotropin secretion and exacerbate OHSS in women [10]. It needs to be emphasized that OHSS caused by FGA must be differentiated from more common iatrogenic OHSS (hCG triggered after ovulation induction /IVF), pregnancy-related delayed-type OHSS (high β-hCG trophoblastic disease or multiple pregnancies), familial/spontaneous OHSS (FSH-receptor activation mutations), severe hypothyroidism and PCOS differentiation such as estrogen breakthrough. In the absence of exogenous gonadotropins or hCG, if large polycystic ovary syndrome and effusion in the third space occur, and serum FSH is not inhibited by high estrogen (or even elevated), FGA should be highly suspected, and pituitary MRI should be performed immediately.

For FGA, surgical resection of the adenoma remains the optimal therapy [11]. Our patient underwent only transsphenoidal surgery, which led to the normalization of gonadotropin secretion and estradiol levels, resolution of OHSS, reduction of ovarian size with cyst remission, and recovery of normal menstrual cycles.

Conclusion

This case report demonstrates that it is essential to consider gonadotroph adenoma in women with abnormal uterine bleeding and multicystic enlarged ovaries based on elevated estradiol levels and suppressed LH levels. The exacerbation of ovarian hyperstimulation was observed in response to treatment with a GnRH agonist and should be considered in the differential diagnosis of spontaneous OHSS. Furthermore, transsphenoidal resection should be considered if MRI reveals a pituitary adenoma.
