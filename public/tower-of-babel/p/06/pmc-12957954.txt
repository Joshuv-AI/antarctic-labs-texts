Anxiofit‐1 and reduction of subthreshold and mild anxiety: Evaluation of a health claim pursuant to article 14 of regulation (EC) No 1924/2006
Authors: EFSA Panel on Nutrition, Novel Foods and Food Allergens (NDA), Dominique Turck, Torsten Bohn, Montaña Cámara, Jacqueline Castenmiller, Stefaan de Henauw, Karen‐Ildico Hirsch‐Ernst, Angeles Jos, Alexandre Maciuk, Inge Mangelsdorf, Breige McNulty, Androniki Naska, Kristina Pentieva, Frank Thies, Ines Drenjančević, Ionut Craciun, Alfonso Siani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFollowing an application from Anxiofit Ltd. pursuant to Article 14 of Regulation (EC) No 1924/2006 via the Competent Authority of Hungary, the EFSA Panel on Nutrition, Novel Foods and Food Allergens (NDA) was asked for an opinion on the scientific substantiation of a health claim related to Anxiofit‐1 and reduction of subthreshold and mild anxiety. The food/constituent, Anxiofit‐1, an Echinacea angustifolia root extract, standardised for the content of echinacoside (at least 3%) and the profile of alkamides, is sufficiently characterised. The Panel considers that reduction of subthreshold and mild anxiety, risk factors for anxiety and depressive disorders, is a beneficial physiological effect. Two short‐term human intervention studies showed a selective effect of Anxiofit‐1 (80 mg/day for 7 days) on state anxiety and no effect on trait anxiety in subjects with subthreshold and mild anxiety. A randomised controlled trial (RCT) lasting 6 weeks showed no effect of Anxiofit‐1 at 40 or 80 mg/day on anxiety symptoms using a different psychometric tool. No evidence has been provided to establish that the short‐term, selective effect of Anxiofit‐1 on state anxiety observed in two RCTs can be sustained with continuous consumption of the food/constituent, or that a short‐term reduction of subthreshold anxiety (or state anxiety alone) reduces the risk of anxiety and depressive disorders in individuals with subthreshold anxiety. No human intervention studies investigating the effect of Anxiofit‐1 on the risk of anxiety and/or depressive disorders have been provided. The information submitted by the applicant does not provide evidence for a plausible mechanism by which Anxiofit‐1 could exert the claimed effect in vivo in humans. The Panel concludes that the scientific evidence is insufficient to establish a cause‐and‐effect relationship between the consumption of Anxiofit‐1 and the reduction of subthreshold and mild anxiety as risk factors for anxiety and depressive disorders.Keywords: anxiety, Anxiofit‐1, depression, Echinacea angustifolia, health claim

INTRODUCTION

Background and Terms of Reference as provided by the requestor

Regulation (EC) No 1924/2006 harmonises the provisions that relate to nutrition and health claims and establishes rules governing the Community authorisation of health claims made on foods. As a rule, health claims are prohibited unless they comply with the general and specific requirements of this Regulation, are authorised in accordance with this Regulation, and are included in the lists of authorised claims provided for in Articles 13 and 14 thereof. In particular, Articles 14–17 of this Regulation lay down provisions for the authorisation and subsequent inclusion of reduction of disease risk claims and claims referring to children's development and health in a Community list of permitted claims. According to this Regulation, an application shall be submitted by the applicant to the national competent authority of a Member State, which will make the application and any supplementary information supplied by the applicant available to the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA).

Interpretation of the Terms of Reference

EFSA is requested to evaluate the scientific data submitted by the applicant in accordance with Article 16(3) of Regulation (EC) No 1924/2006. On the basis of that evaluation, EFSA will issue an opinion on the scientific substantiation of a health claim related to: Anxiofit‐1, an Echinacea angustifolia root extract, and reduction of subthreshold and mild anxiety as risk factors for anxiety and depressive disorders.

The present opinion does not constitute, and cannot be construed as, an authorisation for the marketing of Anxiofit‐1, a positive assessment of its safety, or a decision on whether Anxiofit‐1 is, or is not, classified as a foodstuff. It should be noted that such an assessment is not foreseen in the framework of Regulation (EC) No 1924/2006.

It should also be highlighted that the scope, the proposed wording of the claim, and the conditions of use as proposed by the applicant may be subject to changes, pending the outcome of the authorisation procedure foreseen in Article 18(4) of Regulation (EC) No 1924/2006.

The general approach of the NDA Panel for the evaluation of health claim applications is outlined in the EFSA General guidance for stakeholders on health claim applications (EFSA NDA Panel, 2021a).

In assessing each specific food/health relationship, which forms the basis of a health claim, the NDA Panel considers the following key criteria:

ASSESSMENT

The NDA Panel has previously evaluated two applications on Anxiofit‐1 in relation to the reduction of subthreshold and mild anxiety, both of which resulted in a negative outcome (EFSA NDA Panel, 2016, 2020). In these evaluations, the Panel concluded that the scientific evidence was insufficient to establish a cause‐and‐effect relationship between the intake of Anxiofit‐1 and reduction of subthreshold and mild anxiety.

According to the applicant, the characterisation of the food/constituent in the current application remains unchanged compared to previous submissions and is consistent with the chemical properties, manufacturing process, analytical methods and stability information assessed in the previous EFSA opinion (EFSA NDA Panel, 2020), where the Panel concluded that the food/constituent was sufficiently characterised. The claimed effect, reduction of subthreshold and mild anxiety as a risk factor for the development of anxiety and depression disorders, also remains the same as in earlier applications.

The present assessment will therefore focus on whether the newly submitted data provide additional evidence for the scientific substantiation of the claimed effect.

Characterisation of the food/constituent

The food/constituent proposed by the applicant as the subject of the health claim is Anxiofit‐1, an Echinacea angustifolia hydroalcoholic root dry extract.

Subspecies of E. angustifolia used in the product are E. angustifolia DC var. angustifolia and E. angustifolia DC var. strigose. Anxiofit‐1 is a hydroethanol extract standardised for the content of echinacoside (at least 3%) and the profile of alkamides. The concentration of alkamides and of echinacoside in the final product is measured by high‐performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). The alkamide profile of the product was presented in graphic form, together with data on the total content of alkamides and the amounts of individual alkamide compounds in several batches. Additional data on alkamide profiles from new production batches since 2019 were also provided (claimed as confidential information).

The applicant provided evidence on the bioavailability of alkamides present in Echinacea (Guiotto et al., 2008; Matthias et al., 2005; Woelkart et al., 2005; Woelkart et al., 2008). Detailed specifications of the manufacturing process, stability information and batch‐to‐batch variability were provided by the applicant and claimed as confidential information.

The Panel considers that the food/constituent Anxiofit‐1, an Echinacea angustifolia root extract standardised for the content of echinacoside (at least 3%) and the profile of alkamides, which is the subject of the health claim, is sufficiently characterised.

Relevance of the claimed effect to human health

The claimed effect proposed by the applicant is the reduction of subthreshold and mild anxiety symptoms. The proposed target population is ‘those having subthreshold and mild anxiety symptoms but who are otherwise healthy’ and are aged 7 years and older.

As explained in the two previous EFSA opinions on the same food/constituent and health effect (EFSA NDA Panel, 2016, 2020), subjects with subthreshold anxiety can be defined as subjects having symptoms of anxiety but not meeting the full diagnostic criteria for anxiety disorders in relation to the number of symptoms and/or their duration. ‘Mild anxiety’ refers to symptom severity/intensity within the mild range as assessed by validated scales.

The applicant specified the criteria used for the definition of mild anxiety: a score of 21–30 in the clinically useful anxiety outcome scale (CUXOS), a score of 8–10 in the hospital anxiety and depression scale – anxiety subscale (HADS‐A), or a score of 45–57 points for subscales and 90–114 points for the total score in the state–trait anxiety inventory (STAI). The CUXOS is a 20‐item self‐report questionnaire that assesses the severity of anxiety symptoms over the past week, covering both psychic anxiety (6 items on mood and feelings) and somatic anxiety (14 items on bodily sensations), with greater emphasis on somatic symptoms. The HADS‐A primarily measures psychic anxiety, focusing on cognitive and emotional symptoms over the past week. The STAI is a 40‐item questionnaire that focuses mostly on psychic symptoms and distinguishes between state anxiety and trait anxiety. State anxiety reflects transient psychological reactions directly related to adverse situations at a specific moment, whereas trait anxiety refers to a stable personality tendency to experience anxiety across many situations.

The applicant proposes that the presence of subthreshold and mild anxiety is a risk factor for the development of anxiety disorders (particularly agoraphobia, generalised anxiety disorder, obsessive compulsive disorder, panic disorder, simple phobia and social phobia) and depressive disorders (particularly major depressive disorder, dysthymia, manic episodes and suicidality as symptoms of bipolar disorders).

As described in the two previous EFSA opinions (EFSA NDA Panel, 2016, 2020), the applicant submitted two types of evidence to substantiate the relationship between the proposed risk factor and the aforementioned diseases: observational studies showing an association between the proposed risk factor and an increased risk of anxiety and depressive psychiatric disorders, and two intervention studies documenting that a reduction in the risk factor decreases the risk of these psychiatric disorders (Aune & Stiles, 2009; van't Veer‐Tazelaar et al., 2009).

The study by Aune and Stiles (2009) is a randomised clinical trial (RCT) involving 11‐ to 15‐year‐olds with subthreshold social anxiety. Over a one‐year period, the Norwegian Universal Preventive Program for Social Anxiety significantly reduced both the severity of social anxiety symptoms and the incidence of syndromal social anxiety compared to a no‐treatment control group. In the study by van't Veer‐Tazelaar et al. (2009), a group of elderly, aged 75 years and older, with subthreshold depression or anxiety as assessed by the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES‐D) scores (> 16) were randomised to a preventive stepped‐care programme involving several forms of cognitive behaviour therapy administered in steps or usual care for 1 year. CES‐D scores were also measured at 3, 6, 9 and 12 months. The intervention significantly reduced CES‐D scores at all time points during the study as compared to usual care and the risk of developing a MINI/DSM‐IV depressive or anxiety disorder by 50%. The Panel notes that, in these studies, a sustained reduction of subthreshold anxiety also reduced the risk of developing anxiety and depression disorders.

The applicant now submitted two additional observational studies (An et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2025) and a systematic review of observational studies (Volz et al., 2022). The Panel notes that only human intervention studies can provide evidence for subthreshold anxiety being an independent risk factor for anxiety and depression disorders (EFSA NDA Panel, 2021a).

The Panel considers that reduction of subthreshold and mild anxiety is a beneficial physiological effect. Subthreshold and mild anxiety are risk factors for anxiety and depressive disorders.

Scientific substantiation of the claimed effect

The applicant performed a literature search in MEDLINE (Medical Literature Analysis and Retrieval System Online), accessed via PubMed. The full search strategy with keywords was provided. Eligibility criteria included RCTs conducted in humans receiving any Echinacea preparation, with at least one psychometric measure reflecting anxiety states. No time limits were applied. Only research articles and reviews published in English were considered.

Separate literature searches were also conducted to identify relevant mechanistic and bioavailability studies, as well as evidence on the relationship between subthreshold and mild anxiety and the development of anxiety and depressive disorders. The applicant also provided the specific search strategies and eligibility criteria applied for each of these searches.

The applicant identified five human intervention studies as being pertinent to the health claim (Lopresti, unpublished; Haller et al., 2013; Haller et al., 2020; Haller et al., 2025; Lopresti & Smith, 2021). Three of these studies (Haller et al., 2013; Haller et al., 2020; Haller et al., 2025), had already been evaluated by the Panel in previous assessments (EFSA NDA Panel, 2016, 2020). However, the publication by Haller et al. (2025) includes statistical analyses on the study data that had not been provided in the unpublished study report submitted in previous applications (previously submitted as Haller et al., 2025). No human intervention studies investigating the effect of Anxiofit‐1 on the risk of anxiety and/or depressive disorders have been provided.

Four of the five human intervention studies provided were conducted in adults with subthreshold anxiety (mild or high) at Anxiofit‐1 doses ranging from 20 to 80 mg/day. Three of these studies had intervention periods of 7 days, while one lasted 6 weeks. The remaining study was conducted in adult patients with generalised anxiety disorder (GAD) and also lasted 6 weeks. Anxiety was assessed using different instruments: three studies employed the STAI (state and trait subscales), one used CUXOS and one used HADS‐A. These three instruments differ in their focus on psychic versus somatic symptoms.

The Panel notes that none of the human intervention studies provided for the scientific substantiation of the claim have been conducted in children or adolescents.

Human intervention studies in individuals with subthreshold anxiety

Three studies conducted in individuals with subthreshold anxiety lasted 7 days, two of which were already assessed by the Panel in a previous application (EFSA NDA Panel, 2020) and are only briefly described below (Haller et al., 2013; Haller et al., 2020). These three short‐term studies used the STAI to assess anxiety.

A randomised, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled, parallel, two‐arm study (Haller et al., 2020) was conducted in subjects with subthreshold anxiety, defined as STAI scores more than one standard deviation above the general population mean on either the state or the trait anxiety subscale (≥ 46 points). Sixty‐four participants (34 women, 30 men; mean age 37 years) were randomised to receive Anxiofit‐1 (80 mg/day) or placebo (n = 32 per group) for 7 days. STAI scores were assessed repeatedly during the intervention and for 3 weeks thereafter. The primary outcome of the study was the STAI scores for the trait and state anxiety subscales. A significant treatment‐by‐time interaction was observed for state anxiety (p < 0.0001), with lower scores in the Anxiofit‐1 group (−11.7 ± 1.7) compared to placebo (−3.3 ± 0.9) at the end of the intervention and throughout follow‐up. No significant differences were found for the trait anxiety subscale. The Panel considers that this study shows an effect of Anxiofit‐1 given at 80 mg/day for 7 days on STAI state anxiety subscale in subjects with subthreshold anxiety, but not on the STAI trait anxiety subscale.

Another randomised double‐blind, parallel, two‐arm, placebo‐controlled trial study lasting 7 days has been provided for the first time in this application (Lopresti, unpublished). A total of 82 adults (57 women, 25 men; mean age ≈48 years) with subthreshold anxiety and self‐reporting high anxiety were randomised to receive Anxiofit‐1 at 80 mg/day or placebo (n = 41 per group) for 7 days. Study products were identical in appearance, and randomisation was performed using 10 randomly permuted blocks via a coded system. Compliance was assessed by tablet count on day 8.

Inclusion criteria comprised healthy adults, self‐reporting experiencing, high stress and/or anxiety, scores ≥ 45 on both the STAI State and Trait subscales at screening and baseline, non‐smoking status, BMI 18–30 kg/m2 and no planned treatment changes during the study. Exclusion criteria included receiving currently or in the previous 3 months psychological intervention for anxiety or depression, diagnosis of psychiatric or neurological disorders, recently diagnosed or unmanaged chronic diseases, regular medication use (including psychotropics), substance abuse, high alcohol intake, recent surgery, shift work, pregnancy or breastfeeding, major lifestyle changes and allergy to Echinacea, chamomile or sunflower oil.

The primary endpoint was the STAI State score. STAI‐State was administered at online screening (days −7 to −3), at baseline (day 0), and on days 4 (i.e. after 3 days of treatment), 8 (i.e. after 7 days of treatment) and 21 (i.e. 2 weeks after stopping the intervention). Secondary endpoints included STAI‐Trait and PROMIS sleep disturbance and impairment scores. The Panel considers that only STAI‐State and ‐Trait scores are appropriate for assessing anxiety symptoms in this study and relevant to the claimed effect.

Sample size calculation assumed an effect size of 0.6 for state anxiety, requiring 72 participants for 80% power at a two‐sided 5% significance level, and 80 participants were targeted to account for dropouts. Changes from baseline to day 7 were analysed using generalised linear mixed models (GLMMs) with a group × time interaction term. The full analysis set (FAS) included all randomised participants who consumed at least one dose of the intervention product (n = 82). The per‐protocol set (PPS) included randomised participants who consumed at least one dose of the intervention product and had no major protocol deviations. Three participants withdrew, two consumed < 80% of tablets, and one completed a questionnaire outside the time window, all reasons considered as major protocol deviations by the study authors. The PPS included 76 participants (Anxiofit‐1 = 37, placebo = 39). No baseline differences were observed between groups. Participants showed high anxiety levels on both STAI subscales, with mean state and trait scores of 57.98 (SD = 7.70) and 58.80 (SD = 6.91) in the Anxiofit‐1 group, and 59.29 (SD = 7.80) and 59.78 (SD = 6.84) in the placebo group.

A significant group × time interaction was found in the change in STAI State scores from baseline to day 7 (p = 0.047, FAS), with statistically significant reductions of 15.71% in the Anxiofit‐1 group and 9.01% in placebo. Between‐group differences in STAI State change from day 0 to day 7 were statistically significant (p = 0.032). Analyses based on the PPS yielded similar findings. No significant group × time interaction was observed for STAI Trait (p = 0.424, FAS).

The Panel considers that this study shows an effect of Anxiofit‐1 at 80 mg/day given for 7 days on STAI‐State anxiety scores in participants with subthreshold and high baseline anxiety, whereas no effect was observed on STAI‐Trait anxiety scores.

The Panel notes that two short‐term (7‐day) RCTs show a selective effect of Anxiofit‐1 at 80 mg/day on state anxiety as measured by the STAI and no effect on trait anxiety (Lopresti, unpublished; Haller et al., 2020).

In an open‐label, randomised, parallel, dose‐finding study (Haller et al., 2013), volunteers (22 women, 11 men; mean age 40.6 years) with subthreshold anxiety defined as STAI scores more than one standard deviation above the general population mean in each of the subscales (i.e. state anxiety and trait anxiety ≥ 45 points) were randomised to Anxiofit‐1 at doses of 20 mg/day (n = 15) or 40 mg/day (n = 17) for 7 days. A significant treatment‐by‐time interaction was reported for total STAI scores, with significant reductions observed only in the 40 mg/day group. Pairwise comparisons between the groups indicated lower STAI state and trait anxiety scores in this group at the end of the intervention. In the context of a previous application (EFSA NDA Panel, 2020), the Panel considered that this study provided some evidence that Anxiofit‐1 (40 mg/day) may decrease anxiety measured by STAI (state anxiety, trait anxiety and total anxiety) in subjects with subthreshold and mild anxiety. However, the Panel noted that the lack of blinding and the absence of a placebo group were important methodological limitations for self‐reported outcomes and considered that the results of this study could not be used on their own for the scientific substantiation of the claim. In the context of the current application, the Panel notes that this study at high risk of bias does not support the selective effect of Anxiofit‐1 on state anxiety that has been shown in two RCTs (Lopresti, unpublished; Haller et al., 2020), and considers that no conclusions can be drawn from the study for the scientific substantiation of the claim.

The fourth study in individuals with subthreshold anxiety (Lopresti & Smith, 2021) was a double‐blind, parallel, three‐arm, placebo‐controlled trial in which 108 adults (mean age ≈41 years) with mild‐to‐moderate anxiety were randomised to receive Anxiofit‐1 at 40 mg/day (n = 36), 80 mg/day (n = 37) or placebo (n = 35) for 6 weeks. Inclusion criteria included a CUXOS score of 11–40, symptoms persisting for more than 1 month, medication‐free status for at least 4 weeks, non‐smoking and BMI between 18 and 35 kg/m2. Exclusion criteria covered severe anxiety, psychiatric or neurological disorders, chronic disease, high alcohol consumption and pregnancy. Randomisation was performed using seven randomly permuted blocks of 15 participants each. Compliance was monitored through tablet return and count, with a mean adherence of 93% among participants who completed the study.

The primary endpoint was the CUXOS total score. Secondary outcomes included the Positive and Negative Affect Schedule (PANAS), the Bergen Insomnia Scale (BIS) and the Short Form‐36 Health Survey (SF‐36). The Panel considers that only the CUXOS is appropriate for assessing symptoms of subthreshold anxiety and therefore relevant to the claimed effect. The Panel notes that the CUXOS was administered with reference to the previous 2 weeks, whereas it is typically used to assess symptoms over the past week, including the day of assessment. CUXOS assessments were performed at baseline and at weeks 1, 2, 4 and 6.

An a priori power analysis was conducted based on an effect size of 0.7 observed in a previous randomised controlled study (Haller et al., 2020). Assuming 80% power and a two‐sided 5% significance level, 26 participants per group were estimated to be required to detect an effect on the primary outcome (CUXOS total score). To account for potential dropouts, the planned sample size was 35 participants per group (105 in total). The statistical analysis included repeated‐measures ANOVA to assess group (high dose, low dose, placebo) × time interaction effects, with planned contrasts comparing placebo with each active group (40 mg and 80 mg). No correction for multiple testing was applied.

There were no significant baseline differences between groups except for a lower PANAS positive score in the 40 mg/day group (p = 0.012). Fifteen participants withdrew (7 in placebo, 4 in 40 mg/day and 4 in 80 mg/day groups), mostly with no identified reason; two withdrawals were due to digestive complaints in the treatment groups.

Significant reductions in CUXOS total scores occurred in all groups, but between‐group differences were not statistically significant (placebo vs. 40 mg/day: p = 0.896; placebo vs. 80 mg/day: p = 0.751; placebo vs. active: p = 0.788). A similar pattern was observed for the CUXOS psychological and somatic subscale scores, with no statistically significant differences between groups. The Panel notes that state anxiety was not assessed in this study.

The Panel considers that this study does not show an effect of Anxiofit‐1 at doses of 40 mg or 80 mg/day for 6 weeks on anxiety symptoms as measured by CUXOS scores in adult participants with mild‐to‐moderate anxiety.

Human intervention study in patients with generalised anxiety disorder

The study is a randomised, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled, five‐centre trial in patients with GAD diagnosed according to DSM‐IV criteria (Haller et al., 2025) and was submitted in a previous application (EFSA NDA Panel, 2020) as an unpublished manuscript (Haller et al., 2025). The evaluation by the Panel at the time is briefly described below.

A total of 26 adult patients with GAD (19 women, 7 men; mean age 43 years) were randomised to receive Anxiofit‐1 at 40 mg/day or placebo (n = 13 each) for 6 weeks, of which 12 patients per group completed the study and were considered for data analysis. The primary outcome was HAM‐A scores, with HADS‐A assessed as a secondary outcome on eight occasions (screening, randomisation and days 2, 7, 14, 16, 28 and 42). The original analysis used two‐factor ANOVA with factors for time and treatment and Newman–Keuls post hoc comparisons. In its previous assessment, the Panel noted that the HAM‐A scale was considered an inadequate outcome measure because it poorly discriminated between GAD and depression, and therefore these results were not further discussed. For HADS‐A, statistically significant differences between groups of completers were observed at days 16 and 28 of the study. No treatment‐by‐time interaction was found, and no between‐group differences were observed at the end of the study (day 42). The Panel concluded that this study provided only limited evidence for a transient effect of Anxiofit‐1 at 40 mg/day on anxiety in patients with GAD, and that the results could not be extrapolated to the target population for the claim.

The published paper of this study (Haller et al., 2025) includes statistical analyses data not provided in the previously submitted study report. Because HADS‐A scores changed significantly between screening and randomisation, with higher baseline anxiety in the Anxiofit‐1 group, the authors reanalysed the data using repeated measures ANCOVA to adjust for this pre‐treatment difference. The analysis was performed on the per‐protocol sample, with treatment and time as categorical factors and the randomisation day as a continuous predictor. Post‐hoc comparisons were conducted using the Duncan test, and effect sizes were calculated using Hedges' g. HADS‐A scores showed statistically significant differences between groups at day 16 and day 28 (Hedges' g = 0.899 and 1.034, respectively; p < 0.05 for both), but no difference was observed at the end of the intervention (day 42; p > 0.05). A significant treatment‐by‐time interaction was observed (p < 0.05). The Panel notes that the use of repeated measures ANCOVA cannot account for the inadequate randomisation (i.e. the lack of comparability of the intervention and control groups at baseline regarding the variable of interest, HADS‐A scores) and the potential bias that this may introduce in the results.

In addition, an analysis using HADS‐A score cut‐offs for normal, moderate and severe anxiety states (0–7, 8–10 and 11–21, respectively) is presented, which confirms the lack of comparability of the Anxiofit‐1 and placebo groups regarding HADS‐A measures of anxiety: about 90% of patients in the Anxiofit‐1 were classified as having severe anxiety vs. only 25% in the placebo group at baseline. The Panel notes that this categorical analysis was not pre‐planned. The Panel also notes that the inadequate randomisation limits the conclusions that can be drawn about the independent effect of Anxiofit‐1 on anxiety in patients with GAD.

Taking into account the new data and data analyses provided in the publication of this study (Haller et al., 2025), the Panel considers that no conclusions can be drawn regarding an independent effect of Anxiofit‐1 on anxiety in patients with GAD.

Conclusions on human intervention studies

Out of the three RCTs, all conducted in adults with subthreshold anxiety and from which conclusions could be drawn for the scientific substantiation of the claim, two short‐term (7‐day) RCTs (Haller et al., 2020; Lopresti, unpublished) showed a selective effect of Anxiofit‐1 at 80 mg/day on state anxiety as measured by the STAI, and no effect on trait anxiety. The third RCT, lasting 6 weeks, showed no effect of Anxiofit‐1 at 40 or 80 mg/day on anxiety symptoms measured by CUXOS (Lopresti & Smith, 2021).

The Panel notes that no evidence has been provided to establish that the short‐term, selective effect of Anxiofit‐1 on state anxiety observed in two RCTs can be sustained with continuous consumption of Anxiofit‐1, or that a short‐term reduction of subthreshold anxiety (or state anxiety alone) reduces the risk of anxiety and depressive disorders in individuals with subthreshold anxiety (Section 3.2). The Panel further notes that no human intervention studies evaluating the effect of Anxiofit‐1 on the risk of anxiety and/or depressive disorders have been provided.

Mechanism of action proposed

The applicant proposed that the anxiolytic effects of Anxiofit‐1 are mediated by alkamides present in Echinacea angustifolia root extracts. According to the applicant, these alkamides may interact with the endocannabinoid system by binding to cannabinoid CB1 and CB2 receptors and by inhibiting fatty acid amide hydrolase (FAAH), the enzyme responsible for degradation of the endocannabinoid anandamide, thereby potentially increasing endocannabinoid signalling. The applicant also suggested that other, as yet unidentified, constituents of Echinacea extracts may activate transient receptor potential vanilloid 1 (TRPV1) receptors, which are involved in peripheral nociception and have been implicated in the regulation of affective behaviours. In addition, the applicant cited a study indicating that Echinacea angustifolia root extracts modulate excitatory, but not inhibitory, synaptic transmission in rat hippocampal neurons, a brain region involved in the regulation of anxiety‐related behaviours.

The Panel notes that the proposed mechanisms of action and supporting references provided by the applicant are the same as in previous applications (EFSA NDA Panel, 2016, 2020). The Panel also notes that, although the applicant has discussed limitations previously identified by the Panel regarding the proposed mechanisms of action, no new mechanistic data or additional evidence have been provided in the present application.

The Panel considers that no evidence has been provided by the applicant for a plausible mechanism by which Anxiofit‐1 could exert the claimed effect in vivo in humans.

Weight of evidence

In weighing the evidence, the Panel took into account that two human intervention studies lasting 7 days showed a selective effect of Anxiofit‐1 at 80 mg/day on state anxiety and no effect on trait anxiety in adults with subthreshold and mild anxiety. A RCT lasting 6 weeks showed no effect of Anxiofit‐1 at 40 or 80 mg/day on anxiety symptoms using a different psychometric tool. The Panel also took into account that no evidence was provided to establish that the short‐term, selective effect of Anxiofit‐1 on state anxiety observed in two RCTs can be sustained with continuous consumption of the food. Furthermore, no evidence was provided that a short‐term reduction in subthreshold anxiety (or state anxiety alone) reduces the risk of anxiety and depressive disorders. There were no human intervention studies investigating the effect of Anxiofit‐1 on the risk of anxiety and/or depressive disorders. Finally, the Panel considered that no evidence was provided for a mechanism by which Anxiofit‐1 could exert the claimed effect in vivo in humans.

The Panel concludes that the scientific evidence provided is insufficient to establish a cause‐ and‐effect relationship between the consumption of Anxiofit‐1 and the reduction of subthreshold and mild anxiety as risk factors for anxiety and depressive disorders.
