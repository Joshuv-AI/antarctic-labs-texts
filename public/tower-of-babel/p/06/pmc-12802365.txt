Introduction

Chikungunya fever (CHIKF) is a mosquito-borne infectious disease caused by the chikungunya virus (CHIKV). The incubation period typically ranges from 3 to 7 days, with acute-phase symptoms including fever, rash, and severe arthralgia [[1], [2], [3]]. Children may develop neurological complications such as seizures and impaired consciousness. While most patients recover, approximately 30 %–40 % progress to chronic arthritis with pain persisting for months or even years. In endemic areas, the incidence rate can exceed 35 %, with a case fatality rate of approximately 0.1 % [4,5]. Elevated mortality risk primarily affects the elderly, neonates, and individuals with chronic comorbidities [6]. Beyond individual morbidity, the chronic manifestations of CHIKV leads to impaired daily functioning and reduced work productivity, imposing a substantial societal and economic burden [7]. For instance, in Colombia, the estimated cost attributable to CHIKV during the 2014 epidemic exceeded 70 million dollar, reflecting long-term healthcare utilization and productivity losses [8]. Current treatment remains primarily supportive and symptomatic [9].

The first documented CHIKF outbreak occurred in Tanzania in 1952 [10,11]. The term “Chikungunya” originates from the Makonde language, meaning “to become contorted,” vividly describing the stooped posture of patients afflicted by debilitating joint pain. Humans and non-human primates serve as natural reservoirs of CHIKV, with Aedes aegypti (Ae. aegypti) and Aedes albopictus (Ae. albopictus) being the primary vectors. Driven by expanding vector distribution and viral adaptive evolution (e.g., the E1-A226V mutation enhancing transmission efficiency in Ae. albopictu), epidemics have intensified globally [12]. CHIKV has now spread to 119 countries and territories worldwide, posing a significant public health threat [13].

In China, locally acquired outbreaks triggered by imported cases were reported in Dongguan, Guangdong (2010) [14,15] and Ruili, Yunnan (2019) [16,17]. In July 2025, Guangdong Province experienced a larger-scale outbreak originating from imported cases, with subsequent interprovincial spread [18,19]. Although CHIKV has not yet achieved widespread endemicity in China, frequent international travel and climate change continue to amplify the risks of imported cases and local transmission.

This review systematically synthesizes four key aspects of CHIKF: virological characteristics, epidemiological evolution, vaccine development, and vector control strategies.

Chikungunya virus

Etiological classification

CHIKV belongs to the arthritogenic subgroup of the genus Alphavirus within the family Togaviridae. The virion is spherical with a diameter of approximately 60–70 nm, featuring an icosahedrally symmetric nucleocapsid structure enclosed by a host-derived lipid envelope embedded with viral spike proteins. The viral core contains a single-stranded positive-sense ribonucleic acid (RNA) genome of −11.8 kilobase (kb), characterized by a 5′-terminal cap structure and a 3′-terminal poly(A) tail. The genome comprises two open reading frames (ORFs): the 5′-proximal ORF1 encodes nonstructural proteins nsP1–nsP4, which collectively constitute the viral replicase complex responsible for viral RNA replication and transcription; the 3′-proximal ORF2 encodes a structural polyprotein precursor (C-E3-E2-6 K/TF-E1) that is proteolytically cleaved by host proteases to ultimately form the capsid protein (C) and envelope glycoproteins (E3, E2, 6 K/TF, E1) [20]. Among these, the envelope glycoproteins E2 and E1 represent the most critical protective antigens. The E2-E1 heterodimers assemble into trimeric spikes on the viral surface, mediating viral attachment to host cells and membrane fusion, while also inducing neutralizing antibodies that block infection [21].

Genotyping

Although the virus was first isolated during the 1952 Tanzanian outbreak, phylogenetic studies indicate its earlier existence (500 years ago) in the sylvatic cycle of sub-Saharan Africa, with non-human primates as natural hosts, primarily transmitted among animals via Ae. aegypti. During its epidemic evolution, CHIKV diverged into three major genotypes (based on E1 glycoprotein gene sequences): West African (WA), East/Central/South African (ECSA), and Asian. Among these, the ECSA genotype represents one of the oldest lineages of CHIKV—the first strain isolated in 1952 belonged to this genotype—though its divergence predates 1952. It subsequently evolved into key sublineages: the Indian Ocean Lineage (IOL) (carrying the E1-A226V mutation that enhances transmission by Ae. albopictu) [22] and the ECSA-2 sublineage. The WA genotype exhibits the highest genomic divergence, having diverged from ECSA approximately 350 years ago. It remains confined to WA regions such as Senegal and Nigeria, with lower transmission efficiency [23,24]. The Asian genotype emerged as an independent clade following the expansion of ECSA into Asia, with the earliest record being the 1958 Thailand strain. Due to the cross-immunity protection effect between different genotypes, the CHIKV currently has only one serotype [25,26].

Infection characteristics

CHIKV is primarily transmitted through the bites of Ae. aegypti and Ae. albopictus mosquitoes. After replicating in the mosquito midgut, the virus invades the salivary glands and is transmitted to new hosts through subsequent bites. Humans serve as the primary amplification host for CHIKV; following infection, viremia develops, making them effective infection sources for mosquitoes. Non-human primates act as natural reservoir hosts in the sylvatic cycle.

Upon entering the human body via mosquito bite, CHIKV initially replicates in keratinocytes, fibroblasts, and Langerhans cells in the skin, then spreads to local lymph nodes, causing viremia.. The virus disseminates via the bloodstream to target tissues (e.g., joints, muscles), specifically infecting synovial macrophages, fibroblasts, and chondrocytes. This recruits immune cells (including macrophages, natural killer [NK] cells, CD4+ T cells) into the synovium, releasing pro-inflammatory mediators (e.g., interleukin [IL]-6, IL-17, granulocyte–macrophage colony stimulating factor [GM-CSF], tumor necrosis factor [TNF]-α) that cause synovitis [27].

The principal functional receptor for CHIKV is matrix-remodelling associated 8 (MXRA8) [28]. The envelope glycoproteins E2 and E1 bind directly to the D2 and D1 domains of MXRA8, respectively, forming a unique 3:3 binding pattern (i.e., trimeric E2-E1 spikes binding three MXRA8 molecules). The stalk region of MXRA8 is critical for high-efficiency infection [29,30]. High MXRA8 expression in joint tissues—particularly synovial fibroblasts and chondrocytes—is central to CHIKV-induced arthritis, enabling precise joint targeting that drives acute inflammation and chronic pathology.

Other potential receptors include CD147 (basigin) [31] and prohibitin-1 (PHB1) [32], though their physiological significance requires further validation. Co-attachment factors such as heparan sulfate (HS), phosphatidylserine receptors (TIM-1/TIM-4), and AXL receptor tyrosine kinase (RTKs) enhance infection [33,34]: HS strengthens initial viral attachment via electrostatic interactions, while phosphatidylserine receptors mediate hydrophobic binding to promote internalization. However, these factors cannot independently mediate infection.

Epidemiology and evolution

Global surveillance and genomic evolutionary analyses reveal that CHIKF has caused recurrent pandemics since its 1952 emergence, each accompanied by key adaptive evolutionary events in the virus. Its evolutionary trajectory is characterized by major genotype succession and critical site mutations that drive vector switching or enhanced adaptability, ultimately leading to continuous geographical expansion.

The 1952 Tanzanian outbreak marked the beginning of CHIKV's global epidemic history, with the isolated pathogen belonging to the ECSA genotype. The virus subsequently spread to Southeast Asia: CHIKV was first isolated in Thailand in 1958, representing the earliest Asian isolation. In 1962, it co-circulated with dengue virus in Bangkok, causing a large-scale outbreak with 44,000–70,000 physician-diagnosed pediatric cases [35,36]. By 1963, the epidemic reached India, rapidly sweeping major cities and infecting 300,000 people by 1965 [37]. Strains from Thailand and India during this period differed genetically from the Tanzanian strain, forming the Asian genotype. Phylogenetic studies confirm this genotype as an independently evolved branch derived from ancestral ECSA strains introduced into Asia. Before 2005, both ECSA and Asian genotypes relied primarily on Ae. aegypti as the vector. Due to the tropical confinement of this mosquito’s ecological distribution, outbreaks were strictly limited to tropical zones. No significant adaptive mutations occurred during this phase, resulting in low infection efficiency in Ae. albopictu and failure to breach tropical ecological barriers.

In 2004, a CHIKF outbreak emerged in coastal Kenya, spreading to Indian Ocean islands (e.g., the French overseas department of Réunion Island: 266,000 cases, 35 % population incidence) and India (1.3 million cases) by the following year. From 2006 onward, it expanded across Southeast Asia, resulting in over 6 million global cases with an average case fatality rate of approximately 0.1 %. This epidemic marked the largest-scale outbreak since the virus's discovery in 1952 [38]. The causative strain belonged to ECSA but acquired a critical mutation: E1-A226V (alanine-to-valine substitution at position 226). This mutation significantly enhanced viral replication in the midgut and salivary transmission of Ae. albopictu by reducing cholesterol dependence on host cell membranes [39], defining the Indian Ocean Lineage (ECSA-IOL). Through this single-site mutation enabling vector switching, ECSA-IOL spread via widely distributed Ae. albopictu (temperate to tropical) to India, Indonesia, Malaysia, Sri Lanka, and Singapore (2006–2008), and caused Europe's first autochthonous outbreak in Italy (2007) [[40], [41], [42]]. As ECSA-IOL persisted, additional Ae. albopictu-adaptive mutations (e.g., E2-L210Q) emerged, potentially synergizing with E1-A226V to increase transmission efficiency in endemic areas [43]. In southern India (primarily Ae. aegypti-driven), some IOL strains lacking A226V evolved alternative adaptations (e.g., E1-K211E/E2-V264A), enhancing infectivity in Ae. aegypti [44].

In 2013, the Asian genotype CHIKV entered the Caribbean via the Philippines, spreading to 41 countries/territories in the Americas with over 3 million reported cases [45]. The Dominican Republic reported 400,000 cases, while Brazil and Colombia experienced persistent outbreaks. The Asian genotype displaced ECSA-IOL as the dominant strain in the Americas [45]. Transmission remained dependent on Ae. aegypti (widely distributed in the Americas) without cross-vector capacity like E1-A226V. However, a E2-V368A/6K-L20M double mutation (divergent from IOL's evolutionary path) emerged, potentially enhancing vector/host adaptation by promoting virion assembly and release. Strains carrying this mutation defined the Caribbean/Americas epidemic clade (CO-clade). Co-circulation of Asian and ECSA genotypes was observed in Brazil [46], with ECSA strains harbouring E1-V156A/K211T at the E1-E1 interface. This double mutation altered glycoprotein conformation, conferring dual traits: high vector transmission efficiency and enhanced pathogenicity in animal models, posing a risk of spread to North America.

In March 2025, Réunion Island experienced a renewed outbreak of CHIKF after an 18-year hiatus [47]. The epidemic began in August 2024, initially with low-level transmission, but entered an explosive phase after January 2024. By the end of March, it had spread across the entire island, peaking at 8,000 new cases per week. As of July 2025, the World Health Organization (WHO) reported over 47,500 confirmed cases and approximately 170,000 suspected cases (accounting for 35 % of the island’s population) —a recurrence of the 2005–2006 pandemic. The virus has diffused globally via international travelers to 119 countries and territories, including Guangdong Province and Macao Special Administrative Region of China, with ongoing transmission [13]. The circulating strain is genetically homologous to the 2005 Réunion outbreak, belonging to the ECSA genotype adapted to Ae. albopictus mosquitoes. Beyond the key cross-vector mutation E1-A226V, synergistic mutations E2-I211T and E2-L210Q were identified, indicating enhanced vector adaptability. This strain is now defined as the ECSA-2 lineage [48]. In early July 2025, amid a global resurgence of CHIKF, China reported local clusters of CHIKF cases in Foshan City, Guangdong Province, triggered by imported cases, for the first time in six years [18,19].

Characteristics of mosquito vectors

Primary and potential vectors

The primary and potential transmission vectors of CHIKV are the Ae. aegypti and the Ae. albopictus mosquito. Ae. aegypti is the primary vector of CHIKV in urban areas worldwide and is widely distributed in tropical and subtropical regions. In China, it is found in areas south of the 22nd parallel. It prefers to feed on humans and breeds in small containers of standing water in and around buildings, with its activity peak occurring during the day [49].

However, in China, the Ae. albopictus plays a more critical role in transmission. It has a wider distribution than the Ae. aegypti, ranging from the tropical island of Hainan to the temperate region of southern Liaoning, and is expanding northwards and westwards due to global climate change and urbanisation. Its eggs are highly resistant and can survive in dry conditions. It feeds on humans and livestock, and is active in both the wild and human settlements. It can utilise both artificial containers and natural habitats such as tree holes and bamboo tubes, enabling it to adapt to urban, suburban and rural environments simultaneously [50]. For example, the African mosquito (Ae. africanus) and other wild mosquito species in African jungle-type reservoirs maintain the CHIKV natural cycle by biting non-human primates. It should be noted that, although the A226V mutation significantly enhances the virus's replication and transmission in Ae. albopictus, Ae. albopictus has become as important as Ae. aegypti in many regions, if even more so [51].

In addition to these two main vectors, some mosquito species in specific geographical regions or ecological conditions may also participate in the viral cycle. For example, in African forest-type reservoirs, wild mosquito species such as Ae. africanus maintain CHIKV natural circulation by biting non-human primates. It should be noted that, although CHIKV has been detected in Culex tritaeniorhynchus, Culex quinquefasciatus, Mansonia africana and Aedes furcifer have been found to carry CHIKV [52,53], this does not necessarily mean that they are capable of efficiently transmitting the virus to humans, and further research is needed.

Infection and transmission ability for vectors

CHIKV replicates and spreads within mosquitoes along a specific pathway. After mosquitoes feed on the blood of patients with viremia, the virus first invades and replicates extensively in the midgut epithelial cells of the mosquitoes. Subsequently, the virus breaches the midgut barrier, enters the mosquito's open circulatory system hemolymph, and spreads throughout the body, ultimately infecting the salivary glands. After replicating again in the salivary glands, the virus mixes with saliva. During the mosquito's next blood meal, the virus can be transmitted to a new host. The time from when the mosquito ingests infected blood until its saliva becomes infectious is referred to as the extrinsic incubation period (EIP). The EIP is a key parameter determining the speed and scale of an outbreak. CHIKV has a relatively short EIP, typically ranging from 2 to 7 days, meaning infected mosquitoes can rapidly acquire the ability to transmit the virus. This is also one of the key factors contributing to the local outbreak of CHIKV in Foshan City, Guangdong Province, China, in 2025.

After Ae. aegypti and Ae. albopictus mosquitoes are infected with CHIKV, the virus replicates and spreads rapidly within their bodies, with a short extrinsic incubation period and high transmission efficiency. Ae. aegypti typically begins transmitting the virus 3 to 7 days after infection, with transmission rates peaking between days 7 and 14. The Ae. albopictus mosquito’s transmission onset time is comparable to that of the Ae. aegypti mosquito, and in some cases even faster. For example, viruses carrying the E1-A226V mutation can be detected in the Ae. albopictus mosquito as early as the second day post-infection. In both mosquito species, the salivary glands accumulate large amounts of virus approximately 7 days post-infection, and even maintain high levels up to 21 days post-infection [[54], [55], [56]].

Vector competence refers to the ability of a pathogen to infect, replicate, and transmit within a mosquito, and is profoundly influenced by the interaction between mosquitoes, viruses, and the environment. Vectorial capacity, on the other hand, considers additional ecological factors and describes the overall ability of a mosquito population in a given region to transmit pathogens over a specific period [57]. In addition to vector-related determinants, host factors also play an important role in dynamics of CHIKV transmission. Host immune status can significantly influence both susceptibility to infection and the duration of viremia, thereby affecting on transmission to mosquitoes. For instance, early neutralizing IgG responses targeting epitopes on the E2 glycoprotein have been shown to limit viral dissemination in patients and short the potential infectious window for vectors [21]. Host genetic background lead to the inflammatory response, that coincides with raised levels of immune mediators and infiltration of immune cells with differences in immune response pathways, contributing to variability in disease severity and transmission potential [6]. These host-related factors highlight the complex interplay between vector and host in determining CHIKV epidemiology.

Laboratory studies have confirmed that both Ae. aegypti and Ae. albopictus can transmit CHIKV to their offspring via eggs, i.e., vertical transmission. However, the efficiency is generally low (approximately 0.1 % to 5.0 %), far below that of dengue or yellow fever viruses [[67], [68], [69]], and cannot yet be considered the primary mode of transmission. Nevertheless, vertical transmission is significant during epidemic intermissions, as the virus can persist in the ecosystem through the offspring of a few infected mosquitoes after human cases have disappeared, potentially serving as a latent source for the resurgence of the epidemic the following year.

Behavioral characteristics of vector mosquitoes and their public health implications

The behavioral patterns of vector mosquitoes directly influence their frequency of contact with humans and the efficiency of viral transmission, making them critical for the development of control strategies. Fig. 1 represents two critical sections that influence CHIKV transmission in the Aedes mosquito reproductive cycle. Ae. albopictus and Ae. aegypti are typical diurnal mosquitoes, with peak activity occurring during the day, typically concentrated in early morning and late afternoon [70], rendering traditional nighttime mosquito net protection less effective. Additionally, they exhibit a jumping blood-feeding behavior. During a single feeding session, they often switch between multiple hosts and fly away at the slightest disturbance (Fig. 1), typically requiring 4–5 bites to become fully satiated [71,72]. This behavior significantly increases the transmission potential of individual virus-carrying mosquitoes and markedly enhances the efficiency of viral spread within populations.

Female Aedes mosquitoes tend to lay their eggs in multiple small water-holding containers during a single reproductive cycle, a behavior known as “jumping oviposition” in Fig. 1. Studies indicate that when faced with low-quality breeding sites (such as crowded environments), female mosquitoes are more likely to disperse their eggs to hold the mosquito population [72]. Although this behavior may be influenced by multiple factors in real-world environments [73], it undeniably poses a significant challenge for control efforts: even if most obvious standing water is removed, residual, hidden micro-water points (such as flower pot trays or discarded bottles and cans) can still sustain mosquito populations, reducing the effectiveness of “source reduction” strategies. Autodissemination traps (ADTs) can be used to lure female mosquitoes into oviposition traps coated with trace amounts of larvicides. When these female mosquitoes lay eggs, they transfer the IGR to multiple other inconspicuous water containers, achieving “indirect larval control”.

Vaccine development

Chikungunya vaccines

Currently, based on studies demonstrating a significant correlation between neutralizing antibody titers (with thresholds of PRNT80 ≥ 100 or PRNT50 ≥ 150) and viral clearance/disease protection [74,75], the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and European Medicines Agency (EMA) accept neutralizing antibody levels as biomarkers of protective immunity and approved two vaccines for marketing authorization: a live-attenuated vaccine and a recombinant virus-like particle (VLP) vaccine.

Live-attenuated vaccine IXCHIQ®

Developed under the code VLA 1553 [76], this vaccine is a genetically attenuated live vaccine by French company Valneva. It is based on the epidemic ECSA-IOL genotype strain CHIKV LR2006-OPY1, with an attenuation strategy involving deletion of 62 amino acids in the hypervariable region of nsP3 protein and replacement with a short linker peptide (Fig. 2). Clinical trial results showed that 98.9 % of subjects achieved protective neutralizing antibody levels (PRNT50 ≥ 150) 28 days post single-dose vaccination, with a geometric mean titer (GMT) of 3,750. At 6 months, 96.3 % maintained protective titers (GMT = 1,039), and 96 % sustained protection at 36 months, with comparable antibody levels in elderly and young populations [77]. Safety data indicated good overall tolerability, but 1.5 % experienced severe, persistent adverse events resembling CHIKF. Based on these results, the FDA approved this vaccine in November 2023 for individuals aged 18–64 years. It is contraindicated for other age groups, pregnant women, immunodeficient / immunocompromised individuals, and chronic arthritis patients. Subsequent approvals were granted in Canada, the European Union, and the United Kingdom. However, due to severe adverse event reports during its use, the FDA restricted the administration of this vaccine to individuals aged 60 and above in May 2025, and subsequently suspended its marketing authorization in the United States on August 22.

VLP vaccine VIMKUNYATM

Developed by Danish biopharmaceutical company Bavarian Nordic A/S [78], this vaccine utilizes eukaryotic expression vectors to express structural proteins (C-E3-E2-6 K-E1) of the WA lineage strain 37,997 in 293 T/293F cells, enabling self-assembly into VLPs structurally resembling native virions (Fig. 2). Approved on February 14, 2025, it is the world's second licensed chikungunya vaccine and the only one indicated for adolescents aged 12–18 years.

Phase I/II clinical trial results demonstrated favorable safety of the candidate vaccine and induction of potent cross-neutralizing antibodies (covering WA/ECSA/Asian/IOL lineages) after the first booster immunization [79], [80], [81]; the alum-adjuvanted formulation elicited higher neutralizing antibody levels and longer immunogenicity persistence (> 6 months) compared to the non-adjuvanted group. Phase III trial results showed: in the 12–64 age group, GMT of serum neutralizing antibodies reached 1,618 at day 22 post single-dose vaccination (vs. 7.9 in placebo), with 86 % of participants maintaining neutralizing antibodies at 6 months (GMT declined to 337.73); in the elderly group (≥65 years), 87 % achieved seropositivity at day 22 (GMT = 723.93, lower than younger group), declining to 75.5 % at 6 months (GMT = 233.02). Regarding safety: in the 12–64 cohort, adverse events were reported in 45.1 % of vaccinees (vs. 34.7 % placebo), primarily mild-to-moderate reactions including injection-site pain (23.7 %), fatigue (19.9 %), headache (18.0 %), and myalgia (17.6 %), with Grade ≥ 3 adverse events in 0.1 % of vaccinees; the elderly group exhibited lower adverse event rates than the 12–64 group, with no severe vaccine-related adverse events [82].

Other candidate vaccines in development

Vaccine candidates for CHIKF currently in clinical trials or with significant progress are categorized by technological platforms: Inactivated vaccines, live-attenuated vaccines, recombinant subunit vaccines, viral vector vaccines, and messenger ribonucleic acid (mRNA) vaccines.

Inactivated vaccines

As a traditional and mature approach, inactivated vaccines were first explored for CHIKV in 1970. The most advanced candidate, BBV87, developed by India’s Bharat Biotech, uses an ECSA genotype strain (CHIK/03/06 isolate) inactivated with β-propiolactone and adjuvanted with alum [83]. Preclinical studies show it induces high-titer neutralizing antibodies and prevents viremia post-challenge. A phase I trial (2017) confirmed safety and immunogenicity, and it is now in phase II/III trials [84].

Live-attenuated vaccines

The first CHIKV live vaccine candidate to enter clinical trials was the passaged attenuated strain CHIKV 181/25 (also designated TSI-GSD-218), with development terminated in 1998 due to safety and stability concerns [85]. Beyond the licensed IXCHIQ®, which utilizes a 62-amino-acid deletion in the hypervariable region of the nsP3 protein, replaced by a short linker peptide, as its attenuation strategy, researchers are now pursuing novel genetic engineering approaches for next-generation vaccine development. The resulting recombinant strain CHIKV/IRES exhibits restricted replication in mammalian cells, no replication in mosquito cells (blocking transmission), and demonstrates high immunogenicity with complete challenge protection in mice and non-human primates (NHPs) [75,86]. The capsid gene-deleted attenuated strain ΔC-CHIKV, constructed by Chinese scientist Bo Zhang and colleagues, confers complete protection with a single-dose immunization in both Ifnar-/-deficient mice and wild-type C57BL/6 mice [87]. Their strategies aimed at more stringent control of replication or enabling a single-cycle infection are theoretically geared towards achieving a more favorable balance between immunogenicity and safety, though this awaits confirmation in human clinical trials.

Subunit vaccines (including VLP vaccines)

Subunit vaccines are prioritized as a core strategy for Chikungunya vaccine development due to their superior safety profile and lower biocontainment requirements during production; the critical technical challenge lies in antigen design: the major neutralizing antigen E2 protein exhibits insufficient immunogenicity when expressed alone due to structural and conformational instability, so antigen selection must involve either co-expression of all structural proteins or at minimum inclusion of E3, E2, and E1 proteins to enable formation of VLPs or native-like conformations. The most advanced candidate is the licensed VIMKUNYATM (described in Section 4.1), while other VLP vaccines utilizing diverse expression platforms are progressing rapidly. Although the E2-E1 fusion protein expressed by Chinese scientist X. Jin et al. [88] is less effective than VLPs in mimicking the native viral structure, its advantages lie in higher expression and purification efficiency, indicating promising application prospects in the future.

Viral vector vaccines

Multiple established viral vectors are currently utilized in Chikungunya vaccine development, with measles virus (MV) and simian adenovirus vectors advancing rapidly to clinical trials: a recombinant MV expressing all CHIKV structural protein genes induced high-level neutralizing antibodies against CHIKV in murine models and cynomolgus macaques; the phase I / II clinical trials reported no vaccine-related serious adverse events, achieved 100 % seroconversion after a prime-boost regimen (28-day interval), and demonstrated no interference from pre-existing MV immunity on vaccine reactivity [89]. A chimpanzee adenovirus vector candidate (ChAdOx1 Chik) expressing the full structural gene cassette C-E3-E2-6 K-E1 showed in phase I trials that it elicits cross-neutralizing responses against all three CHIKV genotypes (ECSA / Asian / IOL), exhibits favorable safety and tolerability after single-dose administration, and achieves 100 % seroconversion [90].

mRNA vaccines

The earliest-developed CHIKV mRNA vaccine candidate, mRNA-1388 (Moderna / Merck), utilizes mRNA-lipid nanoparticle (LNP) technology to encode the CHIKV structural polyprotein (C-E3-E2-6 K-E1), which self-assembles into VLPs post-translation to induce potent humoral and cellular immune responses. Phase I clinical trial results demonstrated favorable tolerability with no serious adverse events reported; regarding efficacy, the 100 μg dose group achieved 100 % seroconversion post-boost immunization, with neutralizing antibody titers remaining above protective thresholds at day 196 and superior to placebo at 1 year.

Future perspectives

The global epidemic trend of Chikungunya fever continues to intensify. This review systematically summarizes its etiological characteristics, epidemiological and evolutionary features, vaccine R&D progress, vector control strategies, and integrated prevention systems. Although two vaccines (IXCHIQ® and VIMKUNYATM) have been approved in Europe and the United States, challenges remain regarding vaccine accessibility and safety. Concurrently, the adaptive evolution of CHIKV in mosquitoes has enhanced transmission efficiency, posing threats to temperate regions, while traditional chemical vector control faces insecticide resistance challenges. Beyond insecticide resistance, the sustainability of vector control programmes is often constrained by challenges related to community participation, including inconsistent public engagement with long-term interventions and limited availability of technical personnel and equipment [91,92]. Multidimensional breakthroughs are urgently needed to address CHIKV outbreaks:

Vaccine development and deployment to protect pusceptible populations

Develop novel vaccine platforms (e.g., mRNA vaccines, virus-like particles) to improve safety and coverage for elderly, infant, and child populations; create broad-spectrum vaccines covering multiple genotypes to enhance universality. For countries without vaccine access, establish Emergency Use Authorization (EUA) and procurement agreements for low- and middle-income nations based on WHO's continuous evaluation of vaccine data.

Precision control of transmission vectors to block transmission routes

Integrating molecular surveillance techniques and biological control methods to address the challenge of increasing insecticide resistance in mosquito vectors; intensifying early warning in temperate regions (e.g., Europe and North America) to prevent further viral spread via Ae. albopictus, while enhancing public awareness through community-based education (e.g., eliminating breeding sites and applying repellents).

Optimized integrated control strategies to prevent importation and spread

Develop artificial intelligence (AI)-driven real-time surveillance systems integrating climate, travel, and genomic data to predict outbreak risks, while promoting international cooperation for cross-border joint prevention mechanisms. Strengthening surveillance systems at points of entry (airports, etc.) enables early detection of imported cases, as demonstrated in dengue programs in Singapore and China [93,94]. Rapid case detection, laboratory confirmation, and vector control around case residences, are critical to breaking transmission chains. Experiences from Zika virus control highlight the need for community-based breeding-sites reduction, particularly targeting peridomestic containers [95]. Furthermore, cross-sectoral collaboration, including public health, environmental, and municipal authorities, enhances sustainability of interventions. These approaches underscore the importance of integrated, multi-layered systems to prevent the importation and spread of CHIKV.
