The MC4-La1 cleavage module restricts plant virus infection by integrating R-motif–mediated defense mRNA translation
Authors: Qinglin Pi, Rujian Hu, Ning Yue, Zhihao Jiang, Jing Wei, Yanlin Chen, Meng Yang, Wen Song, Yongliang Zhang, Jinlong Wang, Dawei Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The plant cysteine protease metacaspases are structural homologs of animal caspases and play pivotal roles under biotic and abiotic stresses. However, whether metacaspases and purine-rich element (R-motif)–mediated defense messenger RNA translation are involved in plant antiviral immunity remains elusive. Here, we report that barley stripe mosaic virus (BSMV) infection activates metacaspase 4 (MC4) to cleave the RNA binding protein lupus autoantigen 1 (La1) behind residue arginine-448. The cleaved La11-448 version relocates to the cytoplasm and promotes the R-motif–mediated cap-independent defense mRNA translation. Moreover, MC4 overexpression restricts BSMV infection in tobacco (Nicotiana benthamiana), whereas knockout of MC4 and knockdown of La1 are more susceptible to diverse plant virus infection. To counteract plant defense, the BSMV γb protein directly interacts with and inhibits the self-processing of MC4 and subsequent La1 cleavage, thereby impairing La1-mediated immune responses. Collectively, our findings unveil a hitherto unknown defense mechanism whereby the MC4-La1 cleavage module inhibits virus infection by coordinating R-motif–mediated defense mRNA translation.

INTRODUCTION

Proteolytic enzymes play a pivotal role in diverse cellular and physiological processes across all kingdoms of life, modulating the activity of their substrates and governing the destiny and subcellular localization of proteins (1). In mammals, caspase family proteins are members of cysteine-dependent aspartate-specific proteases that serve as central mediators in the initiation and execution of apoptosis, pyroptosis, and inflammation (2). Metacaspases (MCs) constitute a family of cysteine proteases found in plants, fungi, and protozoa, which are structurally analogous to caspases (3). However, the amino acid sequence similarities between metacaspases and caspases are relatively low, ranging from 10 to 20%. Considerably different from caspases, metacaspases preferentially cleave substrates after a lysine (K) or an arginine (R) residue (4, 5).

Plant metacaspases are categorized into two types based on their sequence similarity and domain structures: type I has an N-terminal prodomain, whereas type II has a long linker instead that bridges p20 and p10 subunits (6). In Arabidopsis (Arabidopsis thaliana), MC1 to MC3 and MC4 to MC9 are grouped into type I and type II, respectively (7, 8). Metacaspases have been shown to participate in plant immunity (7, 9–12). Arabidopsis metacaspase 4 (AtMC4) undergoes self-processing in a Ca2+-dependent manner upon activation (13), while acting as a positive regulator in both biotic and abiotic stress-induced cell death (11). Meanwhile, damage-induced Ca2+ influx activates AtMC4, which is necessary and sufficient to release phytocytokine pep1 to transduce wound defense signals (10). Moreover, AtMC4 also plays an important role in defending against bacteria and fungi (11, 12, 14); however, the involvement of metacaspases in antiviral immunity remains unclarified.

In eukaryotes, most mRNAs are translated in a canonical cap-dependent manner, encompassing the processes of initiation, elongation, termination, and ribosome recycling (15). Translation initiation is key in regulating gene expression across multiple developmental programs and responding to various stresses. Recent studies have shown that global translational reprogramming is a fundamental layer to regulate immune responses in both pattern-triggered immunity (PTI) and effector-triggered immunity (16–20). Unlike the canonical translation mechanism, the cap-independent translation involves the use of internal ribosome entry sites (IRESs) in the 5′ leader sequence. These IRESs operate in a cap-like manner to recruit ribosomes and additional translational regulators to initiate translation (21). It is one of the critical importance mechanisms, especially when the global translation has been disturbed, but selective translation of adaptive mRNAs is required. For instance, while PTI-induced mRNA decapping inhibits cap-dependent translation, defense mRNAs with purine-rich elements (R-motif) are selectively translated in an IRES-dependent manner by recruiting poly(A)-binding proteins (PABPs) and other regulators (16, 22). Despite the well-established role of PABPs/R-motif as an initiation module for cap-independent translation in elf18-triggered PTI, the function of R-motif–mediated defense mRNA translation in antiviral immunity remains unknown.

Viruses hijack the host translation machinery to establish their replication and infection cycle, while plants repress global translation as an antiviral strategy (23, 24). The nuclear shuttle protein-interacting kinase 1 mediates the translocation of the ribosomal protein L10 to the nucleus where it interacts with L10-interacting MYB domain-containing protein (LIMYB) to down-regulate the transcription of ribosomal genes, which ultimately leads to global translational suppression and increased tolerance to geminivirus infection (24, 25). However, little research has been conducted on the translational control of host defense mRNAs in response to plant viral invasion.

Barley stripe mosaic virus (BSMV), the type member of the genus Hordeivirus, is a positive-strand RNA virus containing three genomic RNAs designated as RNAα, RNAβ, and RNAγ. BSMV genome encodes two replication-associated proteins (αa and γa), three Triple Gene Block proteins (TGB1, TGB2, and TGB3) for viral movement, a coat protein (CP), and a multifunctional γb protein (26–28). In addition to functioning in viral replication (29), movement (30), and virion morphogenesis (31), the γb protein counteracts plant defense responses by interacting with distinct host factors (27, 32–35). However, whether γb protein manipulates metacaspase-mediated defense remains largely unexplored.

In this study, we found that the MC4 is activated upon BSMV infection and cleaves the RNA binding protein lupus autoantigen 1 (La1) (36) behind residue R448. Subsequently, the cleaved La1 protein is relocalized to the cytoplasm, reinforcing the R-motif–mediated cap-independent defense mRNA translation. To counteract plant defense, the viral γb protein targets MC4 and inhibits its self-processing, thereby disrupting cleaved La1-mediated defense mRNA translation to facilitate BSMV infection. Our results unveil a previously unknown defense signaling pathway whereby the MC4-La1 cleavage module orchestrates R-motif–mediated defense mRNA translation to inhibit plant virus infection.

RESULTS

MC4 plays an antiviral role during BSMV infection

Metacaspases play important roles in biotic and abiotic stress (10, 37, 38); however, little is known about whether metacaspases regulate antiviral immunity. In our previous study, MC4 (Niben101Scf01376g04029.1) was identified as a potential BSMV γb interactor using a TurboID-based proximity labeling approach in tobacco (Nicotiana benthamiana) (39). To identify metacaspase family members in N. benthamiana, we used Arabidopsis metacaspases (4) as queries to search the Solanaceae Genomics Network (https://solgenomics.net/tools/blast) and identified six putative metacaspase paralogs (Table 1), each of which has a different number of isoforms. Phylogenetic analysis showed that NbMCA-Ia (NbMC1) to NbMCA-Id clustered within the type I metacaspase clade containing Arabidopsis orthologs, while NbMCA-IIa (NbMC4) and NbMCA-IIf (NbMC9) were classified as type II paralogs (fig. S1) (8).

As expected, a self-processing band was observed in MC4-3xFlag–overexpressing leaves but not in MC4C139A-3xFlag (Fig. 1A, middle). The accumulation of BSMV TGB1 was significantly reduced in the leaves overexpressing MC4-3xFlag compared with the control leaves expressing green fluorescent protein (GFP)–3xFlag (Fig. 1, A and B), indicating that MC4 restricts BSMV infection. In contrast, overexpression of MC4C139A-3xFlag had no effect on BSMV infection (Fig. 1, A and B), suggesting that the MC4 cleavage activity is essential for its antiviral function. To further confirm the antiviral role of MC4 in BSMV infection, we generated MC4 overexpression (MC4-OE) and MC4 knockout (MC4-KO) transgenic N. benthamiana plants (fig. S3, A and B). Both transgenic plants have no developmental phenotypes under normal conditions (fig. S3C). After agroinoculation of BSMV to the MC4-OE and MC4-KO plants, BSMV induced milder symptoms in MC4-OE plants but more severe symptoms in MC4-KO plants compared to the nontransgenic (NT) plants (Fig. 1F). Consistently, Western blot analysis revealed that the accumulation of viral protein was at a significantly lower level in MC4-OE plants, whereas it increased more than two times in MC4-KO plants (Fig. 1, G and H). Together, these results demonstrate the anti-BSMV role of MC4 in N. benthamiana.

Because MC4 undergoes self-processing for activation upon bacterial infection and damage treatment (10–12), we wondered whether virus infection could also activate MC4. To this end, an MC4-specific antibody that recognizes both the zymogen MC4 (zMC4) and the cleaved MC4 band (~25 kDa) was developed, and the ratio of cleaved MC4/zMC4 was used as a proxy for MC4 activation (10). First, we mimic the mechanical damage in the early stage of BSMV infection via the wounding procedure (41). The results showed that the cleaved MC4/zMC4 ratio was markedly increased upon damage stimuli, suggesting that damage activated MC4 in N. benthamiana (Fig. 1I), which is consistent with the previous investigations (10, 41). We next inoculated N. benthamiana leaves with BSMV and found that BSMV infection induced a much higher ratio of the cleaved MC4/zMC4 than the mock control (Fig. 1, J and K), demonstrating that MC4 can be activated and defend against plant virus infection. Moreover, treatment with LaCl3 (a Ca2+ antagonist) suppressed the BSMV-induced self-processing of MC4, implying that BSMV infection accompanied by an elevated Ca2+ concentration may activate MC4 self-cleavage (fig. S4).

MC4 interacts with and cleaves La1 after residue R448

Given the critical role of MC4 cleavage activity in antiviral defense (Fig. 1, A and B), we first tested whether MC4 directly cleaves BSMV-encoded proteins to inhibit BSMV infection. An in vitro cleavage assay was performed (12), and purified viral proteins and MC4-His from Escherichia coli were coincubated at room temperature for 10 min, followed by Western blot analyses. However, the results showed that none of the purified BSMV-encoded proteins were the substrate of MC4 (fig. S5), implying that MC4 may suppress virus infection through cleaving host factors.

The La1 protein (36) is an RNA binding factor involved in the RNA metabolism and regulation of cellular mRNA translation in an IRES-dependent manner (42). In Arabidopsis, AtMC4 regulates stem cell homeostasis by changing the subcellular localization of La1 in response to environmental hazards (43). Considering the important role of mammal La protein in programmed cell death 4 (PDCD4)-mediated apoptosis in response to inflammatory stimuli (44), we assume that La1 protein might participate in plant immunity. To investigate whether MC4 interacts with La1 in planta, we cloned N. benthamiana La1 (Niben101Scf02354g10002.1) and performed a bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assay. The results showed that the reconstructed yellow fluorescent protein (YFP) signal only appeared in the MC4-YFPn/MC4C139A-YFPn and YFPc-La1 coexpressing N. benthamiana leaves and not in the control leaves (Fig. 2A and fig. S6A). Furthermore, because MC4 undergoes self-cleavage and can be instantly activated upon extraction from tissues (10, 12), we transiently expressed GFP-tagged catalytically inactive mutant MC4C139A and Flag-tagged La1 protein in N. benthamiana leaves for a coimmunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assay. The results showed that 9xFlag-La1 immunoprecipitated with GFP-MC4C139A but not with GFP control (Fig. 2B). Based on these results, we conclude that MC4 interacts with La1 in N. benthamiana.

To test whether La1 is the substrate of MC4 in N. benthamiana, we used an in vivo digestion assay as described previously (10) and coexpressed 3xFlag-La1-3xHA with MC4-3xFlag or MC4C139A-3xFlag in N. benthamiana leaves. After 3 days postinfiltration (dpi), the leaves were harvested and treated with a wounding procedure, followed by incubation at room temperature for the indicated amount of time. Following this damage treatment, 3xFlag-La1-3xHA was processed by MC4-3xFlag, rather than MC4C139A-3xFlag, into ~70-kDa products, peaking at 5 min, followed by general protein degradation (Fig. 2C and fig. S6B). Furthermore, no obvious cleavage bands were detected in the leaves overexpressing CP-YFPn and MC4-3xFlag (Fig. 2D), demonstrating substrate specificity of MC4-mediated La1 cleavage in planta. In vitro cleavage assay confirmed that purified MC4, but not MC4C139A, was capable of directly cleaving recombinant La1 in a Ca2+-dependent manner (Fig. 2E), which was blocked by Z-VRPR-fmk, a specific inhibitor of metacaspase activity (Fig. 2E) (45). Furthermore, compared with NT plants, a higher amount of cleaved La1 version accumulated in MC4-OE transgenic plants, whereas no obvious cleaved La1 was detected in MC4-KO transgenic plants (Fig. 2F). Together, these results verified that La1 is the substrate of MC4.

To map the accurate MC4 cleavage site within La1, a series of truncated La1 mutants were constructed and coexpressed with GFP-3xFlag, MC4-3xFlag, or MC4C139A-3xFlag in N. benthamiana leaves, respectively. In vivo digestion assay showed that truncated mutant La11-444, but not La11-457, cannot be cleaved by MC4 (fig. S6C), narrowing the cleavage site down to amino acids 444 to 457. Because MC4 cleaves substrates after R or K residues (4) and there are only three R residues in this region, we further constructed La1R446E, La1R448E, and La1R455E single-point mutants. In vivo cleavage assay showed that MC4 failed to cleave La1R448E, suggesting that MC4 cleaves La1 after residue R448 (Fig. 2G and fig. S6D).

Considering that the cleavage site R448 of La1 resides in the C-terminal nuclear localization signal (NLS) region, we tested whether MC4-mediated cleavage alters the subcellular localization of La1. mCherry-tagged wild-type La1 and its cleavage variants were separately expressed in N. benthamiana leaves. Confocal microscopy observations showed that both mCherry-La1 and mCherry-La1R448E were colocalized with the nuclear marker histone 2B (H2B) protein in the nucleus; however, mCherry-La11-448 was mainly localized in the cytoplasm (Fig. 2H), suggesting that the MC4-mediated La1 cleavage changes its cellular localization. Notably, phylogenetic analysis showed that La1 as well as its R448 site are highly conserved across monocot and dicot plant species (Fig. 2I and fig. S6E), indicating that La1R448 cleavage leading to cytoplasmic relocalization may represent a conserved mechanism.

Cleavage of La1 is essential for its antiviral defense

To determine the functional role of La1 during viral infection, BSMV was inoculated onto the La1 down-regulated N. benthamiana plants using the RNAi approach (40). The immunoblotting analysis showed that knocking down of La1 increased accumulation of CP (Fig. 3, A and B). Because the dysfunction of Arabidopsis La1 (AtLa1) is lethal and arrests the development of embryo at an early stage (36), we generated La1 knockdown (La1-KD) transgenic N. benthamiana plants to further confirm its anti-BSMV role. After agroinfiltration to La1-KD plants, BSMV developed more severe symptoms with increased levels of viral proteins than NT plants (Fig. 3, C to E). These results demonstrate that La1 also has an antiviral role during BSMV infection. To examine whether La1-mediated antiviral defense depends on MC4, we overexpressed La1 in MC4-KO plants followed by challenge with BSMV. Compared to NT plants, the antiviral function of La1 was abolished under MC4-KO background (Fig. 3, F and G), indicating that the cleavage of La1 by MC4 is a prerequisite for the La1-mediated antiviral activity.

Next, to further evaluate the importance of cleavage on the activation of La1-mediated antiviral defense, we transiently overexpressed La1, La1R448E, and La11-448 in N. benthamiana followed by BSMV agroinfection. The immunoblot analysis showed that only the full-length La1 and the cleaved version La11-448 exhibited the antiviral activity, and, notably, overexpression of La11-448 confers a more robust resistance to BSMV infection (Fig. 3, H and I). To determine whether BSMV infection enhances the MC4-dependent cleavage of La1, we attempted to detect the endogenous La1 cleavage fragments in systemically BSMV-infected leaves. The results revealed that the accumulation of cleaved products was notably increased in BSMV-infected leaves but was almost diminished in BSMV-infected MC4-KO plants (fig. S7). Collectively, our findings suggest that La1-mediated antiviral defense depends on MC4 cleavage with the cleaved cytoplasm-localized La11-448, limiting BSMV infection.

To investigate whether BSMV infection alters La1 subcellular localization, we transiently overexpressed mCherry-La1 in BSMV-infected N. benthamiana leaves. Imaging analysis showed that the cytoplasm-localized mCherry-La1 signals were substantially enhanced in BSMV-infected leaves (Fig. 3J), indicating that BSMV triggers the relocalization of La1 from the nucleus to cytoplasm. The nuclear-cytoplasmic fractionation assay further confirmed the translocation of La1 and that BSMV infection greatly increases the accumulation of the truncated version of La1 in cytoplasm compared to the mock control (Fig. 3K). Following BSMV infection, no mCherry-La1 cytoplasm localization was imaged in MC4-KO transgenic plants compared to NT plants (Fig. 3L), and nuclear-cytoplasmic fractionation assay confirmed this result (Fig. 3M). These data suggest that BSMV-triggered cytoplasmic translocation of La1 depends on MC4.

Together, these results demonstrate that BSMV infection activates MC4 cleavage of La1 and La1 relocalization, which is essential for subsequent defense responses to BSMV infection.

Virus infection activates R-motif–mediated cap-independent defense mRNA translation

It has been reported that the R-motif in the 5′ leader sequence of defense mRNAs functions as an IRES to mediate cap-independent translation during PTI (16, 22). TL1-binding transcription factor 1 (TBF1), a key positive immune regulator, harbors three R-motifs and two upstream open reading frames (uORFs) in its mRNA 5′ leader sequence (LsTBF1) (Fig. 4A) (16, 22, 46). To investigate whether BSMV infection activates R-motif–mediated cap-independent defense mRNA translation, we first used the LsTBF1 from N. benthamiana to construct a translational reporter driven by either LsTBF1 or the R-motif mutant LsTBF1mR3 cassette after 35S promoter as described previously (22), using constitutively expressed Renilla luciferase (Rluc) as an internal control (Fig. 4B). After being transiently expressed in N. benthamiana leaves, we found that, compared to the mock, BSMV infection dramatically increased the reporter translation but not the reporter mRNA abundance (Fig. 4D and fig. S8A). Second, we made bicistronic reporters in which LsTBF1 or the R-motif mutant LsTBF1mR3 was inserted between the first cistron renilla luciferase (Rluc) and the second cistron firefly luciferase (Fluc) (Fig. 4C). The Fluc is expected to be translated only if LsTBF1 has an IRES while Rluc serves as an internal control. We found that the bicistronic reporter translational activities were doubled following BSMV infection (Fig. 4E). The BSMV-promoted translation was diminished after mutating the R-motif (Fig. 4, D and E). Together, these findings demonstrated that BSMV infection activates R-motif–mediated cap-independent translation.

To test the function of R-motif–mediated translation in BSMV infection, we silenced TBF1 in N. benthamiana using tobacco rattle virus (TRV)–induced gene silencing (47) and inoculated with BSMV. The results showed that BSMV accumulated remarkably higher in TBF1-silenced plants than in the TRV-GUS control (Fig. 4F). In contrast, the BSMV accumulation was barely detectable in the TBF1-3xFlag overexpressing leaves (Fig. 4G), indicating that R-motif–controlled mRNA translation plays a critical role in inhibiting BSMV infection.

To investigate whether R-motif–mediated translation is a general antiviral immune response in plants, we first transiently expressed the translational reporter in N. benthamiana leaves and infected them with diverse plant viruses, including the RNA virus beet black scorch virus (BBSV; from the family Tombusviridae) (40), cucumber mosaic virus (CMV; from the family Bromoviridae) (48), and DNA virus tomato yellow leaf curl virus (TYLCV; from the family Geminiviridae) (49). Compared with the mock, all tested viruses enhanced TBF1 reporter translational activity (Fig. 4, H to J) but not the reporter mRNA abundance (fig. S8, B to D). In addition, we performed virus challenge assays in TBF1-silencing N. benthamiana plants. Plants agroinfiltrated with BBSV, CMV-GFP, or TYLCV showed markedly higher viral accumulation in TBF1-silenced leaves compared to TRV-GUS controls (fig. S9, A to C). Conversely, plants that overexpressed TBF1-3xFlag exhibited markedly reduced viral titers when challenged with the same viruses (fig. S9, D to F). These results collectively demonstrated that R-motif–mediated defense mRNA translation is a previously unrecognized plant antiviral strategy.

Cleaved La1 enhances R-motif–mediated defense mRNA translation

Because R-motif is a cellular IRES (22) and La protein enhances IRES-dependent mRNA translation (50, 51), we reasoned that La1 may promote R-motif–mediated defense mRNA translation during virus infection. To test this hypothesis, we incubated the biotin-conjugated RNA probe of TBF1 R-motif 3 (5′-ACAGAGAAATACAGGGGGAAAA-3′; Fig. 4A) with Flag-La1 purified from mock- or BSMV-infected N. benthamiana leaves. Immunoblot analysis showed that the probe interacted with Flag-La1 protein, and the interaction was increased upon BSMV infection compared with the poly(A) control whose binding with Flag-La1 remains unchanged (Fig. 5A), indicating a dynamic regulation of La1 binding to the R-motif during BSMV infection. To test the impact of La1 on R-motif–mediated translation, we coexpressed Flag-La1 with TBF1 reporter containing either LsTBF1-Fluc or LsTBF1mR3-Fluc (Fig. 4A) in N. benthamiana leaves and found that, with comparable reporter mRNA abundance (fig. S10A), Flag-La1 significantly enhanced LsTBF1 but not LsTBF1mR3 reporter translation (Fig. 5B). To further confirm the role of La1 in R-motif–mediated translation genetically, we transiently expressed the dual luciferase reporter in La1-KD plants and found that the BSMV-induced reporter translation was greatly compromised (Fig. 5C). Moreover, similar results were obtained using bicistronic reporter (Fig. 4C), in which the reporter translation was diminished in La1-KD plants (Fig. 5D). Together, our data demonstrate that La1 dynamically binds to the R-motif and positively regulates R-motif–mediated defense mRNA translation during virus infection.

To confirm the interaction between BSMV γb and MC4 identified from proxitome profiling (39), we coexpressed MC4-YFPc with γb-YFPn or negative control Rubisco large subunit (RbcL)–YFPn in N. benthamiana epidermal cells. The BiFC results showed that γb was associated with MC4 in the cytosol (Fig. 6A). We also performed luciferase complementation imaging (LCI) assay, and the results indicated the association between γb and MC4 (Fig. 6B). Immunoblot analysis confirmed the protein expression of different BiFC and LCI constructs (fig. S11, A and B). Furthermore, we validated the MC4-γb interaction in planta by Co-IP assays. The MC4-3xFlag fusion protein was transiently coexpressed with γb-GFP in N. benthamiana leaves, and the Co-IP assay showed that MC4-3xFlag was precipitated with γb-GFP but not with GFP control (Fig. 6C). In addition, in γb-overexpressing transgenic N. benthamiana plants (29), endogenous MC4 was successfully precipitated with γb-3xFlag but not with the negative control Phosphoenolpyruvate carboxylase (PEPC) (fig. S14A). In vitro glutathione S-transferase (GST) pull-down assay further confirmed that MC4-His protein specifically bound to the GST-γb but not to the GST control (Fig. 6D). These results demonstrate that the BSMV γb protein directly interacts with MC4 in vivo and in vitro.

Because barley (Hordeum vulgare) is the natural host of BSMV, we further cloned and characterized the barley ortholog (HvMC4) of NbMC4. Sequence alignment showed that HvMC4 also has p20 and p10 domains and contains the conserved catalytic dyad (His and Cys) (fig. S2B). The BiFC assay showed the association of γb with HvMC4 (fig. S12, B and C). Overexpression of HvMC4, but not the catalytic inactive mutant HvMC4C140A, inhibits BSMV infection (fig. S12D). In addition, to validate the role of MC4-La1 pathway against BSMV in barley, we used BSMV-induced gene silencing to knock down barley MC4 and La1 (52). The results showed that systemic leaves of both HvMC4- and HvLa1-silenced plants exhibited exacerbated BSMV-induced chlorosis symptoms compared to the BSMV-alone controls (fig. S13, A to C). Consistently, immunoblot analysis further revealed higher viral accumulation in both HvMC4- and HvLa1-silenced plants (fig. S13, D and E). Conclusively, these results suggest that the evolutionarily conserved MC4-La1 module confers resistance to viral infection, while the γb-MC4 interaction promotes the infectivity of BSMV in both monocots and dicots.

Because γb-MC4 interaction facilitates BSMV infection, we hypothesized that γb may interfere with the antiviral function of MC4. To test this hypothesis, we first examined the effects of γb on the MC4-La1 cleavage by coexpressing 3xFlag-La1-3xHA with γb-GFP or γbE31A-GFP in MC4-OE plants. The results showed that γb, but not γbE31A, suppressed the MC4-mediated La1 cleavage (Fig. 7E), which was confirmed by in vitro cleavage assays (Fig. 7F). To investigate how γb inhibits MC4-mediated La1 cleavage, we detected the endogenous MC4 activation upon damage treatment in γb-overexpressing transgenic plants. The results revealed that the activation of MC4 was significantly compromised in the γb-overexpressing plants (fig. S14, B and C). To further analyze the dynamics of MC4 activation during BSMV infection, we inoculated BSMV or the γb-deficient mutant BSMVmγb (30) via agroinfiltration and monitored the activation of MC4 at different time points. Compared to the mock control, we found that the ratio of cleaved MC4/zMC4 increased over time during BSMV infection (fig. S15A), whereas the γb-deficient mutant BSMVmγb infection resulted in a considerably higher ratio of cleaved MC4/zMC4 over time compared to wild-type BSMV infection (fig. S15B). These findings demonstrate that BSMV γb directly binds to and inhibits the self-activation of MC4, thereby disrupting the MC4-mediated cleavage of La1 and repressing plant immunity.

MC4-La1 orchestrates defense mRNA translation to inhibit diverse viruses

Our results indicate that BSMV infection connects MC4-mediated La1 cleavage with the R-motif–dependent defense mRNA translation. To determine whether the MC4-La1 module represents a general strategy against various plant viruses, we inoculated two RNA viruses (CMV and BBSV) and a DNA virus (TYLCV) into the MC4-KO and La1-KD transgenic N. benthamiana plants. As expected, all the three viruses induced much severe symptoms in both the MC4-KO and La1-KD transgenic N. benthamiana plants than the NT control (Fig. 8, A, C, and E). Immunoblot analysis showed the higher viral accumulation in these transgenic plants (Fig. 8, B, D, and F). In summary, we revealed that the MC4-La1 module orchestrates broad-spectrum antiviral immunity by coordinating R-motif–dependent defense mRNA translation.

DISCUSSION

Although an increasing body of evidence highlights the important role of metacaspases in plant defense against nonviral pathogens (12, 14, 53), their role in plant virus infection remains unexplored. In addition, the current research on antiviral strategies in plants mainly focuses on the transcriptional level, while little is known about the regulation of host translation (54, 55). In this study, we found that BSMV infection activates MC4 (Fig. 1), which cleaves the RNA binding protein La1 after residue R448 (Fig. 2), leading to its translocation to cytoplasm (Fig. 3). The cytoplasmic La11-448 dynamically binds to the R-motif and promotes R-motif–mediated defense mRNA translation, thereby boosting plant immune responses (Figs. 4 and 5). To fight back, the viral γb protein targets MC4 to suppress its self-activation and La1 cleavage, impairing the La1 cytoplasmic translocation and R-motif–containing defense mRNA translation to optimize BSMV infection (Figs. 6 and 7). Our data unveil a hitherto unknown antiviral defense activation and signaling pathway, in which MC4-La1 cleavage module orchestrate host antiviral responses by R-motif–mediated defense mRNA translation (Fig. 8).

Because of the presence of a rigid cell wall surrounding the plant cell membrane, viruses need a wound for their initial entrance into a plant cell, a process that is associated with elevated Ca2+ flux (56). As an important signal molecule in plants, recent findings have indicated that the Ca2+signals are essential for antiviral immunity in plants (41, 57, 58). Our results demonstrate that a Ca2+-dependent protease, MC4, is activated during BSMV infection (Fig. 1, J and K, and fig. S4) and that the bioactive MC4 subsequently initiates plant antiviral responses. Thus, it is believed that BSMV infection accompanying elevated Ca2+ concentration promotes the self-cleavage of MC4. Nonetheless, the mechanisms by which plant Ca2+-related pathways sense viral infection, particularly when plant viruses are transmitted into adjacent cells, represent an unresolved question that warrants further investigation.

The La protein was first identified in human as an autoantigen in patients suffering from the rheumatic diseases systemic lupus erythematosus and Sjogren’s syndrome (59) and is ubiquitously found in almost all eukaryotes (42). Several studies have shown that the mammalian La protein is implicated in many aspects of RNA metabolism and IRES-mediated translation (42). In plants, AtLa1 regulates the stem cell homeostasis in response to environmental hazards (51); however, the function of the La1 protein during biotic stress remains unknown. Here, we demonstrated the importance of La1 in inhibiting plant virus infection and the mechanisms underlying how La1 contributes to antiviral responses. The antiviral function of La1 is dependent on MC4 cleavage (Fig. 3F), which extends the current understanding of La1 proteins in response to biotic stress and antiviral immunity in plants.

In mammalian cells, apoptosis and virus infection trigger La protein cleavage by protease such as caspase 3-like protease, resulting in the redistribution of La1 to the cytoplasm (60–62). Similarly, the AtLa1 protein also translocates to the cytoplasm under ultraviolet B irradiation (51); however, the subcellular localization pattern of plant La1 in response to biotic stress has not been characterized. Our data presented here indicate that a portion of La1 protein redistributes to the cytoplasm upon BSMV infection, which is controlled by cysteine protease MC4 (Fig. 3). These results suggest that the La1 regulatory module is conserved in plants and mammals.

Virus infection caused translational reprogramming in plant, remodeling the translation of specific mRNA sets (63, 64). However, the role of translational control of defense mRNAs in antiviral immunity remains unexplored. We found that R-motif–mediated defense mRNA translation is remarkably triggered by diverse plant viruses and functions as a previously unrecognized plant antiviral mechanism (Fig. 4). Furthermore, we provide evidence demonstrating that MC4-La1 module acts as a positive regulator in R-motif–controlled cap-independent translation; however, the molecular mechanism underlying the La1-mediated translational regulation needs to be further explored. Overall, our findings provide insights into how plants dynamically respond to virus invasion by rapidly facilitating IRES-dependent translation of defense proteins.

During the long-term arms race between host plants and pathogens, plants have coevolved sophisticated defense mechanisms against pathogen attack, while pathogens have developed various strategies to evade or manipulate the plant defense pathway for their successful infection (54, 55). So far, no pathogen effectors or viral proteins have been reported to directly target metacaspases. Here, our findings documented that the BSMV-encoded multifunctional γb protein directly targets MC4 and represses the MC4-mediated La1 cleavage (Figs. 6 and 7). These observations highlight a previously unknown counter-defense mechanism whereby viral protein manipulates MC4-La1 module-mediated antiviral responses to optimize viral infection. However, whether the MC4-La1 module can be targeted by nonviral effectors is worth investigating in future studies.

In summary, our findings establish a direct link between MC4-La1 cleavage module and R-motif–mediated defense mRNA translation in plant antiviral immunity. Furthermore, our investigations also provide targets for improving broad-spectrum resistance to distinct plant viruses through molecular breeding technology.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Plasmid construction

For transient expression in plants, full-length coding sequence of NbMC4 was amplified from the cDNA of N. benthamiana and cloned into the pMD19-T vector (Takara). The obtained plasmid was used to generate the MC4C139A mutant using polymerase chain reaction (PCR)–mediated site-directed mutagenesis. The MC4 and MC4C139A were inserted into the Spe I and Kpn I sites of pMDC32-3xFlag vector (65) to generate MC4-3xFlag and MC4C139A-3xFlag. For the construction of 9xFlag-La1, NbLa1 was amplified from the cDNA of N. benthamiana and cloned into the pGD-9xFlag vector (39, 66). The resultant plasmid was used to generate 9xFlag-La1R448E and 9xFlag-La11-448 mutants.

For BiFC assay, the MC4 and La1 were amplified, respectively, and cloned into Bam HI–digested pSPYNE-35S or pSPYCE-35S vectors (67). For split luciferase assay, the MC4 was inserted into Kpn I– and Sal I–digested pCAMBIA1300-nLUC or pCAMBIA1300-cLUC vectors (68). For subcellular localization assay, La1 and its derivatives were cloned into Sal I–digested pGD-mCherry vector, respectively, an updated version of the pGD vector (66) engineered with an N-terminal mCherry.

The NbTBF1 leader sequence was amplified from the cDNA of N. benthamiana, and the R-motif 3 mutant (mR3) in the leader sequence was constructed by reverse PCR method. For the dual luciferase reporter assay, we generated the 35S:Rluc/35S:MCS-Fluc vector by modifying the pGreenII 0800-LUC plasmid (69), in which a 35S promoter was inserted at the Kpn I and Spe I site. NbTBF1 5′ leader sequence or its mR3 mutant was inserted into the Spe I–digested vector to generate 35S:Rluc/35S:LsTBF1-Fluc and 35S:Rluc/35S:LsTBF1mR3-Fluc plasmids. For the bicistronic assay, we used reverse PCR method to reconstruct the pGreenII 0800-LUC (69) to generate a bicistronic construct. The NbTBF1 5′ leader sequence or mR3 mutant was cloned into the Kpn I and Nco I–digested bicistronic vector between Rluc and Fluc sequences.

To generate MC4-RNAi and La1-RNAi vectors, 300 nucleotides from MC4 cDNA and La1 cDNA were amplified and recombined into the pDONR207 vector (Invitrogen) by BP recombination followed by LR reaction to the destination vector pHELLSGATE 8 (70) to generate RNAi vectors. For TRV-mediated gene silencing, a 300-nucleotide fragment of TBF1 was amplified from N. benthamiana cDNA and inserted into the pYL156 vector (47).

For protein purification, the MC4 and MC4C139A mutant were amplified and cloned into Nde I– and Xho I–digested pET30a (+) vectors to express His-tagged fusion proteins. La1 was inserted into the pGEX-KG vector at Bam HI and Hind III sites to express GST-La1 protein.

All primers used for plasmid construction are listed in table S1, and the correctness of all mutants were confirmed by DNA sequencing.

Viral inoculation and detection

The BSMV strain ND18 infectious clones used in this study were described previously (71). The inoculation and its molecular detection of BSMV were performed as described previously (72).

Plant materials and growth conditions

N. benthamiana and transgenic plants were grown in a climate-controlled chamber at 23°C with a 15-hour/9-hour light/dark photoperiod. Barley (Yangfu 4056) was grown in the greenhouse at 21°C.

Protein extraction and immunoblotting

The leaf tissues were ground in liquid nitrogen to a fine powder, and total protein was extracted by immediately supplementing with approximately an equal volume of 2× SDS loading buffer [100 mM tris-HCl (pH 6.8), 20% glycerol, 4% SDS, 5% β-mercaptoethanol, and 0.2% bromophenol blue], followed by rapid and thorough vortexing and heating at 100°C for 10 min. The samples were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min, and the resulting supernatant was separated by SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes.

To generate anti-MC4 antibodies, the recombinant NbMC4C139A-His protein was purified from E. coli with Ni–nitrilotriacetic acid agarose (QIAGEN) and used to immunize rabbits to obtain polyclonal antibodies [Beijing Genomics Institution (BGI), China]. The anti-La1 antibody was produced using His-NbLa1 protein purified from E. coli as the antigen by BGI Group.

BiFC and LCI assays

Agrobacterium tumefaciens EHA105 containing different BiFC plasmids were mixed and infiltrated into 4-week-old N. benthamiana leaves. At 3 dpi, infiltrated leaves were observed with Zeiss LSM710 or Zeiss LSM880. Split-luciferase assays were performed as described previously (32, 68). Briefly, Agrobacterium harboring various constructs were mixed with a final optical density at 600 nm (OD600) = 0.5 and infiltrated into fully expanded N. benthamiana leaves for 3 days. The luciferase substrate was sprayed onto the surface of the leaves, and images were taken with a charge-coupled device (CCD) camera.

GST pull-down assays

In in vitro GST pull-down assays, ~5 μg of purified GST- and His-tagged proteins were incubated with 20 μl of Glutathione Sepharose 4 Fast Flow (GE Healthcare) in 600 μl of incubation buffer [20 mM tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 250 mM NaCl, 0.2% glycerol, 0.2% Triton X-100, 0.1% Tween 20, and 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride] for 4 hours at 4°C on a rocker platform. The GST beads were collected and washed six times with incubation buffer and detected by Western blot with anti-His (EASYBIO, catalog number BE7001; 1:5000) or anti-GST (Genscript, catalog number A00866, 1:5000) antibodies.

Co-IP assays

Co-IP assays were performed according to previously described method with minor modifications (73). Agrobacterium containing various constructs were agroinfiltrated into N. benthamiana leaves. At 3 dpi, 4 g of fresh leaves were harvested and ground in liquid nitrogen. The leaf powders were then transferred into a 50-ml tube, and 8 ml of extraction buffer [25 mM tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 1 mM EDTA, 150 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, 0.2% NP-40, 2% polyvinylpyrrolidone K40 (PVP-40), 10 mM dithiothreitol (DTT), and protease inhibitor cocktail] was added. After incubation in ice 45 min, crude extracts were centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 15 min at 4°C, and the supernatants were incubated with anti-Flag M2 Affinity Gel (Sigma-Aldrich) at 4°C for 4 hours on a rocker platform. The beads were washed six times with 1 ml of IP buffer [25 mM tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 1 mM EDTA, 150 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, and 0.2% NP-40] and analyzed by Western blot using anti-Flag (Sigma-Aldrich, catalog number F1804; 1:5000) and anti-GFP (EASYBIO, catalog number BE2002; 1:5000) antibodies.

Generation of transgenic N. Benthamiana plants

MC4-OE and La1-KD transgenic N. benthamiana plants were generated by Agrobacterium-mediated transformation (74) with pMDC32-MC4-3xFlag and pHELLSGATE 8-La1-RNAi plasmids, respectively. MC4-KO transgenic plants were obtained with BGK01-MC4. Guide RNA of MC4 was designed using CRISPR RGEN Tools (www.rgenome.net/cas-designer/). Western blots were used to screen the positive lines of MC4-OE and La1-KD transgenic plants using anti-Flag and anti-La1 antibodies, respectively. DNA fragments were amplified from genomic DNA of MC4-KO plants, and DNA sequencing was carried out to screen the positive lines.

RNA isolation and RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) was performed as described previously with minor modifications (75). Briefly, 3 μg of RNA was treated with deoxyribonuclease I (Takara) at 37°C for 30 min, and cDNA was synthesized with M-MLV reverse transcriptase (Promega). Subsequently, the cDNA was used as templates to carry out qPCR assays using 2× SsoFast EvaGreen Supermix (Bio-Rad) with CFX Manager (Bio-Rad). The N. benthamiana elongation factor 1α (EF1α) gene served as the endogenous control (47).

Nuclear-cytoplasmic fractionation assay

N. benthamiana leaves (1 g) were harvested, ground into powders in liquid nitrogen, and suspended in 2 ml of lysis buffer [20 mM tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 25% glycerol, 20 mM KCl, 2 mM EDTA, 2.5 mM MgCl2, 250 mM sucrose, 1 mM DTT, and 1× protease inhibitor cocktail]. The crude extracts were next passed through two layers of Miracloth (Sigma-Aldrich) followed by centrifugation at 1500g at 4°C for 15 min. The supernatant was used to prepare the nuclei-depleted (cytoplasm) fraction, and the resulting pellet was washed gently five times with 1 ml of nuclear resuspension buffer [20 mM tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 25% glycerol, 2.5 mM MgCl2, 0.2% Triton X-100, 1 mM DTT, and 1× protease inhibitor cocktail]. The pellet was solubilized with 2× SDS loading buffer and was taken as the final nuclei-enriched fraction. Total proteins, nuclei-depleted fraction, and nuclear-enriched fraction were subjected to immunoblot analysis with anti-La1, anti-PEPC, anti–histone 3 (H3), and anti-CP antibodies, respectively.

In vitro cleavage assay

The in vitro cleavage assay was performed as described previously with minor modifications (76). Briefly, 1 μg of purified GST-La1-3xHA protein was mixed with MC4-His or MC4C139A-His in 25 μl of reaction buffer [25 mM Hepes (pH 7.5), 250 mM NaCl, 20 mM CaCl2, and 0.1 mM DTT] at room temperature for 10 min. Reactions were stopped by the addition of 2× SDS loading buffer with 50 mM EGTA and analyzed by Western blot with anti-GST antibodies.

Luciferase reporter assay

Dual luciferase assay was performed as described previously (22). In brief, Agrobacterium containing the indicated construct was infiltrated into fully expanded N. benthamiana leaves at OD600 = 0.1. After 2 days, leaf discs were collected and ground in liquid nitrogen immediately, and dual luciferase assay was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Promega, E1910). For the bicistronic assay, Agrobacterium containing the bicistronic reporter (OD600 = 0.4) was infiltrated into N. benthamiana leaves. After 20 hours, leaf discs were collected and lysed with the passive lysis buffer (PLB) buffer (Promega, E1910). For testing the effect of La1 and truncated La11-448 on the TBF1 dual luciferase reporter translation, Agrobacterium harboring the dual luciferase reporter (OD600 = 0.1) was mixed with those carrying the indicated constructs or EV (OD600 = 0.3) and coinfiltrated into 4-week-old N. benthamiana leaves. After 2 days, leaf discs were harvested, ground in liquid nitrogen, and used to perform dual luciferase assay.

La1/R-motif binding assay

R-motif binding assay was performed as described previously with minor modifications (22). Agrobacterium containing the pGD-9xFlag-La1 plasmid was infiltrated into mock or BSMV-infected N. benthamiana leaves. Leaf tissues were collected 3 days later and ground in liquid nitrogen to leaf powders. Then, the leaf powders were incubated with extraction buffer [25 mM tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 1 mM EDTA, 150 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, 0.2% NP-40, 2% PVP-40, 10 mM DTT, and protease inhibitor cocktail] in ice for 45 min. After centrifuging at 8000 rpm for 15 min, the supernatants were incubated with anti-Flag M2 Affinity Gel (Sigma-Aldrich) at 4°C for 4 hours on a rocker platform. The precipitates were washed three times with IP buffer [25 mM tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 1 mM EDTA, 150 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, and 0.2% NP-40] before elution with 100 μl of 3xFlag-peptide (400 μg/ml) for 2 hours. Biotin-conjugated RNA probes (5′) were synthesized by AZENTA. For the R-motif binding assay, 10 μl of streptavidin beads (Invitrogen) were pretreated in 1 ml of washing buffer [50 mM Hepes (pH 7.5), 150 mM KCl, 1 mM EDTA, and 0.3% Triton X-100] containing 2% bovine serum albumin at 4°C for 2 hours. Subsequently, the beads were incubated with 0.3 nmol biotin-conjugated RNA probes for an additional 2 hours in the presence of ribonuclease inhibitors (200 U/ml; Takara) on a rocker platform. The resultant beads were washed three times with 1 ml of binding buffer [50 mM Hepes (pH 7.5), 150 mM KCl, 1 mM EDTA, 0.3% Triton X-100, 1 mM DTT, and protease inhibitor cocktail], followed by incubation overnight with 10 μg of plant-purified protein at 4°C on a rocker platform. The resulting beads were washed three times and analyzed by Western blot using anti-Flag antibodies.

Protein structure and interaction prediction

To predict potential γb-MC4 interaction sites, we first generated a fusion construct by connecting γb and MC4 with a flexible glycine-serine linker (GSGSGSGSGS). The chimeric protein sequence was then submitted to the AlphaFold2 online prediction site for structural modeling of the γb-MC4 complex. Subsequent analysis of the predicted models was performed using PyMOL (www.pymol.org) for structural visualization and interaction site mapping.

Statistical analysis

The intensity of the protein bands was measured and analyzed by ImageJ software. The data shown in this study were expressed as means ± SD from three independent experiments. Data plotting and statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8 with the Students’ t test or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA).
