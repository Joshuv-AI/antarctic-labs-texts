Oblique occipital sinus in neurosurgical practice: A retrospective analysis of imaging studies
Authors: Michał Senger, Tomasz Andrzej Dziedzic, Przemysław Kunert
Published: 2026

Introduction

The occipital sinus is one of smallest of the intracranial dural venous sinuses and contributes to intracranial venous drainage. It typically extends from the confluence of sinuses and terminates by draining into the marginal sinus and vertebral venous plexus at the level of the foramen magnum (Widjaja et al., 2004). In most cases, the occipital sinus is a single, or occasionally paired structure running along midline between the internal occipital crest and the falx cerebelli (Lang, 1991; Standring et al., 2005). Numerous anatomical variations of the occipital sinus have been described (Das et al., 1970; Dora et al., 1980; Kobayashi et al., 2003) among which the oblique occipital sinus (OOS) has a particular clinical relevance. In this variant, the sinus deviates from the midline and courses obliquely towards the sigmoid-jugular junction (Tubbs et al., 2011). Notably, the OOS often exhibits a wider lumen and may be accompanied by transverse and/or sigmoid sinuses hypoplasia (Shin et al., 2017), taking a primary role in intracranial venous outflow (Dora et al., 1980; Kobayashi et al., 2006).

The clinical significance of the OOS becomes evident during neurosurgical approaches to the posterior cranial fossa, such as retrosigmoid (Candanedo et al., 2020) or suboccipital craniotomy. Unrecognized OOS might be accidently injured during dural incision, potentially leading to severe and life-threatening complications, including massive hemorrhage or air embolism (Das et al., 2008). Therefore, preoperative awareness of this variant allows the surgeon to optimize the surgical strategy and reduce the risks.

Current literature indicates that vascular imaging modalities such as magnetic resonance venography (MRV) and computed tomography venography (CTV) provide reliable visualization of the OOS (Tubbs et al., 2011; Shin et al., 2017; Candanedo et al., 2020). However, many patients undergoing posterior fossa surgery - most often for tumor resection - are typically qualified based on routine magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) without venous specific sequences. Due to limited indications, procedural invasiveness and limitations of venographic imaging, preoperative venography is not routinely performed in this population.

The aim of the present retrospective study is to evaluate whether standard preoperative contrast-enhanced T1-weighted MRI sequences are sufficient for the detection of OOS in comparison to the digital subtraction angiography (DSA), which is the gold standard technique for intracranial vessels imaging. Ultimately, we seek to determine whether the omission of dedicated venographic imaging (such as DSA, MRV or CTV) could be justified, thereby minimizing patient burden and healthcare costs while maintaining the ability to reliably identify this clinically relevant venous variant.

Materials and methods

Following approval of the Bioethical Committee of the Medical University of Warsaw (AKBE/304/2025), we conducted a retrospective analysis of imaging studies of patients hospitalized in the Department of Neurosurgery. Patient data were retrieved from the hospital's electronic medical record system through the review from the oldest to latest available digital record. Eligible patients were ≥18 years old and had undergone both contrast-enhanced MRI with 3D high-resolution sequences and DSA during their hospital stay. The imaging studies were performed for many reasons, including diagnostic process of vascular malformations and aneurysms, preoperative assessment of meningioma vascularization, follow-up after aneurysm or vascular malformations treatment. The 3D high-resolution contrast-enhanced T1-weightened MRI (3DT1C-MRI) sequences and DSA images were reviewed independently by the primary and secondary researcher. On 3DT1C-MRI and DSA, the presence of an infratentorial obliquely running enlarged venous structure was evaluated. When identified, the OOS was further characterized with respect to the maximal lumen diameter and the site of connection with the transverse or sigmoid sinuses or jugular vein (unilateral or bilateral). We also evaluated the morphology of the transverse and sigmoid sinuses with particular attention to unilateral or bilateral hypoplasia presence (hypoplasia defined as the reduction of caliber compared to the contralateral sinus). In cases of disagreement between researchers, the final assessment was made by consensus. DSA considered the reference standard for cerebral vascular imaging (Harrigan et al., 2012; van Den Hauwe et al., 2020), served as the comparator for OOS visualization. All imaging analyses were performed using RadiAnt™ DICOM Viewer software (version 2025.2).

Results

A total of 100 patients met the inclusion criteria. Based on 3DT1C-MRI and DSA images review, the presence of an OOS was initially suspected in 7 cases (7%). Following consensus review, a true OOS was identified in 2 patients (2%) (Table 1). In the remaining 5 cases, the suspected findings were ultimately attributed to either enlarged outflow from developmental venous anomaly or an unusually prominent superficial vein with a slightly oblique course (Table 2). No instances were observed in which a venous structure was visualized on DSA but not on 3DT1C-MRI (i.e., no MRI false negatives). On the other hand, several venous structures apparent on MRI were not visualized on DSA, representing false-negative findings in DSA imaging.

Discussion

The prevalence of the OOS is estimated at 2–4% in published series (Das et al., 1970; Tubbs et al., 2011; Shin et al., 2017), though available data remain limited. In most cases, OOS is accompanied by transverse or sigmoid sinus hypoplasia, where it serves as the dominant venous outflow pathway from the posterior fossa (Dora et al., 1980; Shin et al., 2017; Kobayashi et al., 2006). When hypoplasia is absent, OOS may act as an additional or only drainage route, which can become particularly relevant in the setting of sinus thrombosis or its intraoperative damage (Shin et al., 2017).

For neurosurgical practice, preoperative identification of OOS is critical for planning posterior fossa approaches. Although DSA remains the gold standard for the evaluation of cerebral venous structures (Harrigan et al., 2012; van Den Hauwe et al., 2020), its use is limited by its invasiveness, radiation exposure, and procedure related risks, including vascular injury, hemorrhage, and ischemic events (Nam et al., 2022). Furthermore, as demonstrated in our study, DSA may not always show venous structures that are visualized by other modalities. Possible reasons for this include patient motion artifacts, suboptimal contrast delivery, or inappropriate projection angles unsuitable for venous structures visualization (Turski et al., 1983). These weaknesses limit use of DSA as a tool for routine preoperative assessment of OOS.

Alternatives such as MRV and CTV provide reliable evaluation of the cerebral venous system, with comparable diagnostic accuracy, at least in the context of cerebral venous thrombosis (Connor et al., 2002; Khandelwal et al., 2006). CTV requires iodinated contrast administration and involves exposure to ionizing radiation, whereas MRV can be performed with or without gadolinium-based contrast. Contrast-enhanced MRV offers shorter acquisition times and fewer artifacts, while non-contrast two-dimensional time-of-flight is considered the best non-invasive option overall, albeit more artifact prone (Mattle et al., 1991; Vogl et al., 1994). CTV remains favored in acute settings due to its speed and widespread availability (Casey et al., 1996).

In neurosurgical practice, however, patients are most often qualified for posterior fossa procedures based on routine contrast enhanced brain MRI, which rarely includes venography specific sequences. Although conventional contrast enhanced MRI is less sensitive than MRV for the diagnosis of dural sinus thrombosis, it does allow reliable visualization of the major venous pathways, including the OOS. Our findings indicate that 3DT1C-MRI could serve as a valuable, widely available tool for detecting the presence of OOS, reducing the need for additional imaging that may not be accessible, might be associated with increased invasiveness and that carries higher costs. In cases where precise depiction of the OOS course is essential, supplementary venographic imaging could be considered. However, in most situations, simple recognition of OOS on routine MRI should be sufficient for surgical planning and help in preventing serious intraoperative complications.

From a surgical point, the OOS is most likely to be encountered during suboccipital craniotomy, in which the dural incision is typically fashioned in a Y-shaped manner to expose the cerebellar hemispheres and cervical spinal cord or medulla oblongata. In this context, an unrecognized OOS coursing obliquely over the cerebellar hemisphere may be at risk of laceration during durotomy. Preoperative identification of OOS enables modification of the dural opening - such as performing a paramedian linear or unilateral curvilinear incision - to avoid sinus injury while maintaining adequate surgical exposure (Erol et al., 2024).

This study has several limitations. The most significant is the small sample size, which prevents quantitative analysis and definitive conclusions regarding the diagnostic adequacy of 3DT1C-MRI as a stand-alone tool. Further research in larger cohorts is needed to validate these findings and establish imaging guidelines for the preoperative assessment of OOS. In addition, the retrospective, single-center design may limit the generalizability and reproducibility of the findings. Finally, correlation of the identified OOS cases with advanced venographic sequences would have provided additional diagnostic value and strengthened the analysis.

Conclusion

Despite its rarity, the OOS represent a clinically significant variant that might be encountered during posterior fossa surgery. Our preliminary results suggest that routine high-resolution contrast enhanced T1-weighted MRI might not be inferior in terms of quality comparing to the gold standard DSA and could be a sufficient tool for preoperative OOS recognition and may aid optimization of surgical planning. Research on larger patient cohorts is necessary to confirm these observations and establish reproducibility of our results.
