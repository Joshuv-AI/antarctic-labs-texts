From vesicles to ventricles: pediatric group a streptococcal meningitis post-varicella infection – a case report
Authors: Dima Malhis, Sujood Saleh, Zeina Qadi, Zaid Khader, Haneen Zitawi, Lina J Yasin, Mohammad Hamdan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStreptococcus pyogenes meningitis is a rare but potentially life-threatening infection in infancy. It can occur as a primary infection or as a complication of viral illnesses such as varicella, which can increase the risk of invasive bacterial disease.Case presentationWe report a previously healthy 3-month-old boy who presented with fever, poor feeding, and a vesicular rash following varicella exposure. Despite outpatient acyclovir therapy, the patient developed dehydration and persistent fever. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis revealed markedly elevated white blood cell counts, elevated protein, and low glucose. CSF Gram stain demonstrated gram-positive cocci in pairs, and CSF culture subsequently confirmed Streptococcus pyogenes (Group A Streptococcus) that was susceptible to penicillin, vancomycin, amoxicillin/clavulanic acid, ceftriaxone, teicoplanin and cefotaxime. The patient received broad-spectrum antibiotics, antivirals, and supportive care. Despite escalation to a tertiary pediatric intensive care unit (PICU), he developed progressive cerebral edema, refractory seizures, and loss of brainstem reflexes. Brain death was confirmed by clinical examination, apnea tests, and electroencephalography. On the final day of hospitalization, the patient developed endotracheal bleeding with cardiovascular collapse and could not be resuscitated.ConclusionThis case highlights the fulminant course of invasive S. pyogenes infection in infants, particularly following varicella. These findings underscore the need for early recognition, aggressive management, and potential role of varicella vaccination in preventing secondary bacterial complications.Keywords: Group A Streptococcus, Pediatric meningitis, Varicella infection, Invasive bacterial infection, Cerebral complications

Background

Streptococcus pyogenes (Group A Streptococcus, GAS) is a pathogen commonly associated with pharyngitis, impetigo, otitis, sinusitis, mastoiditis, and skin infections. Invasive GAS (iGAS) infections, including bacteremia, necrotizing fasciitis, and meningitis, are rare, occurring in only 0.2–1% of cases [1]. GAS meningitis represents a particularly uncommon but severe manifestation, accounting for approximately 1% of iGAS cases overall and 2–10% in children under 18 years of age [2]. Despite its rarity, GAS meningitis is associated with high rates of intensive care admission and mortality, second only to septic shock.

Pathogenesis remains poorly understood. The proposed mechanisms include spread from contiguous infections of the upper respiratory tract, as well as risk factors such as neurosurgery, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leaks, skull fractures, and skin lesions such as varicella, erysipelas, and impetigo. Nevertheless, cases in previously healthy individuals with no identifiable risk factors have been reported [1, 3].

Recent epidemiological data suggest a rising incidence. A Danish surveillance study reported a 21-fold increase in cases between October 2022 and April 2023 [4]. Similarly, in the Netherlands, 19 cases were documented in 2022, and 10 more were documented in early 2023 than the long-term annual average of five [4].

We report the case of a 3-month-old boy who developed GAS meningitis following varicella infection, with a fatal outcome. This case highlights the diagnostic challenges of GAS meningitis in the post-varicella setting and emphasizes the preventive role of varicella vaccination.

Case presentation

Initial presentation

A previously healthy 3-month-old boy was brought to the pediatric emergency department with a 5-day history of generalized vesicular rash, as illustrated in Fig. 1, and a 3-day history of high-grade fever (39.5 °C rectally), decreased oral intake, and suboptimal activity. The infant had known household contact with varicella, as his 1-year-old brother had recently been diagnosed with chickenpox. He was initially managed at an outpatient clinic for varicella and received oral acyclovir (50 mg/kg/day for 3 days). During this period, he lost approximately 300 g (6.4 to 6.1 kg) and developed decreased urine output.

The patient was full term, with an uneventful pregnancy and no neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) admission, and he was fully immunized according to the Palestinian national immunization program, which does not include varicella vaccination.

Hospital course

The patient was admitted to the pediatric ward for further investigation and management. On admission, he was alert and reactive but ill-appeared and was moderately dehydrated. His vital signs were a blood pressure of 91/57 mmHg, heart rate of 121 bpm, and oxygen saturation of 98%. Skin examination revealed multiple vesicular and crusted lesions in different stages of healing across the body, with no mucous membrane involvement. A neurological exam revealed bulging anterior fontanelle.

In the pediatric ward, laboratory investigations revealed leukocytosis (16.2 × 10⁹/L; neutrophils 67%, lymphocytes 21%) and elevated C-reactive protein (CRP 77 mg/L). Renal and liver function tests, electrolytes, and chest radiographs were within normal limits. Procalcitonin was not measured during this admission, due to its un-availability within the treating institution. Then, intravenous isotonic saline was used as a replacement for dehydration, and intravenous (IV) acyclovir was initiated at 70 mg every 6 h IV (approximately 11.5 mg/kg/dose), which was below the recommended dose of 20 mg/kg/dose every 8 h.

On the 3rd day of hospitalization and despite his management, the patient remained febrile with increasing CRP (168 mg/L), although he was clinically active and no longer dehydrated. Therefore, the decision was to perform lumbar puncture (LP). The LP yielded turbid cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) with 800 WBCs/mm³ (405 lymphocytes), 478 mg/dL protein, 1 mmol/L glucose, and 50 RBCs/mm³. Gram staining revealed abundant gram-positive cocci in pairs. CSF culture subsequently confirmed Streptococcus pyogenes (Group A Streptococcus), which was susceptible to penicillin, vancomycin, amoxicillin/clavulanic acid, ceftriaxone, teicoplanin and cefotaxime; the complete culture and susceptibility report is shown in Table 1. Because the Gram stain demonstrated abundant gram-positive cocci in pairs, empirical treatment for bacterial meningitis was started promptly and broadened while awaiting culture identification and susceptibility results. Intravenous ceftriaxone was started empirically at 250 mg every 12 h (≈ 500 mg/day; ≈82 mg/kg/day) and then escalated to a meningitic dose of approximately 300 mg every 12 h (100 mg/kg/day divided every 12 h). Vancomycin was added empirically at 120 mg every 6 h (approximately 20 mg/kg/dose; ≈80 mg/kg/day), consistent with the high-dose regimen recommended for bacterial meningitis (60–80 mg/kg/day). Dexamethasone was given at 1 mg every 6 h (≈ 0.6 mg/kg/day). High-dose intravenous penicillin G, the agent of choice for confirmed GAS meningitis, was not available at the treating facility, so empirical ceftriaxone—an appropriate third-generation cephalosporin alternative—was continued. Intravenous acyclovir was adjusted to 120 mg every 8 h IV (approximately 20 mg/kg/dose every 8 h, consistent with standard dosing guidelines). The initial suboptimal acyclovir dose of 70 mg every 6 h was corrected at this point. That evening, he developed a focal convulsion and was aborted with diazepam. Two days later, despite improvement in fever, he became hypoactive with further CRP elevation (415 mg/L). Transfontanelle ultrasound revealed mild left ventricular dilatation without a midline shift or intracranial hemorrhage. He was transferred to the pediatric intensive care unit (PICU) for closer monitoring. Over the following 48 h, the patient remained lethargic but aroused, with CRP decreasing to 361 mg/L. CSF culture revealed Streptococcus pyogenes, while PCR for varicella-zoster virus was negative, confirming bacterial meningitis.

The patient subsequently developed multiple generalized seizures accompanied by anisocoria, bradycardia, and irregular respirations, indicating neurological deterioration. Seizures were treated with a loading dose of phenobarbital (90 mg). Owing to continued clinical decline, the patient was transferred to a tertiary hospital for advanced management.

Course at the tertiary hospital

Upon PICU admission, meningoencephalitis was suspected. The initial Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score was 14/15 with pinpoint pupils, but within 30 min, his condition deteriorated (GCS score 3/15), requiring urgent intubation. Brain CT revealed severe cerebral edema, white cerebellar signs, and possible left uncal herniation without acute hemorrhage, hydrocephalus, or venous sinus thrombosis, as demonstrated in Figs. 2, 3 and 4. Following neurosurgical consultation, osmotic therapy with mannitol (0.5 g/kg q6h) and hypertonic saline was initiated and later adjusted due to hypotension and hypernatremia.

At the tertiary hospital the patient received ampicillin 600 mg every 6 h (≈ 400 mg/kg/day) and cefotaxime 450 mg every 6 h (≈ 300 mg/kg/day), subsequently replaced with ceftriaxone 300 mg every 12 h (≈ 100 mg/kg/day), and 45 mg/day (≈ 7.5 mg/kg/day) gentamicin was added. The doses given at the tertiary hospital are reported as the weight-based doses specified by that institution’s protocol for severe infection, under which the patient was treated; individual administrations were not separately verified against the medication record available to the authors. Blood and urine cultures were obtained. In view of leukocytosis, marked thrombocytosis (platelets 1,600 × 10⁹/L), and clinical suspicion of superior sagittal sinus thrombosis, so anticoagulation therapy with aspirin and enoxaparin was initiated. Gentamicin was also added. Supportive therapy included packed red blood cells for anemia (Hb 7.9 g/dL), albumin for hypoalbuminemia, and vitamin K plus fresh frozen plasma for coagulopathy.

Later during the hospitalization course, PT and PTT became markedly elevated. Enoxaparin was subsequently held, and 1 mg intravenous vitamin K and 100 mL FFP were administered to reverse the coagulopathy. Although anticoagulation therapy can contribute to or exacerbate coagulopathy, its initiation was clinically warranted given the prothrombotic state. Laboratory parameters were monitored regularly, and upon deterioration, anticoagulants were promptly discontinued with reversal agents administered.

Neurologically, the patient deteriorated further: GCS score of 4T with fixed, nonreactive pupils, absence of corneal and gag reflexes, and gasping respiration. EEG demonstrated severe diffuse, nonreactive encephalopathic activity. Despite ongoing treatment with ceftriaxone, acyclovir, mannitol, and levetiracetam, there was no improvement over 5 days. Mannitol and levetiracetam were discontinued, and an evaluation for brain death was initiated. The findings included fixed dilated pupils, the absence of brainstem reflexes, and an apnea test with persistent hypercapnia (PaCO₂ increase from 60 to 104 mmHg). Brain death was confirmed by EEG.

On the final hospital day, the patient developed bleeding from the endotracheal tube with hemodynamic collapse. Cardiopulmonary resuscitation was performed for 10 cycles without return of spontaneous circulation, and death was pronounced.

Discussion

GAS meningitis is extremely rare but is associated with significant morbidity and mortality. GAS is a common cause of pharyngitis, impetigo, and skin infections, but invasive presentations such as meningitis, bacteremia, necrotizing fasciitis, pneumonia, peritonitis and osteomyelitis are uncommon [2]. Invasive GAS is defined as isolation from a normally sterile site and is associated with an increased risk of streptococcal toxic shock syndrome (STSS) and postinfectious complications such as acute poststreptococcal glomerulonephritis (APSGN), acute rheumatic fever (ARF), and rheumatic heart disease (RHD) [5]. The contribution of GAS to bacterial meningitis is minimal, representing approximately 0.8% of cases, whereas S. pneumoniae, N. meningitidis, and H. influenzae type B remain the predominant pathogens [6].

A systematic review revealed fewer than 150 pediatric cases worldwide over the past 30 years, mostly in infants and young children [2]. Mortality remains high, and survivors frequently suffer long-term sequelae, including epilepsy, hearing loss, and developmental delay [7].

Risk factors include neurosurgery, CSF leaks, skull fractures, cochlear implants, skin lesions, and systemic comorbidities such as HIV, cancer, diabetes, and alcoholism [8, 9]. Varicella has been repeatedly identified as a major facilitating factor. Our patient’s recent varicella infection and active vesicular lesions strongly suggest viral disruption of the skin barrier as the portal of entry. This finding reinforces the indirect benefits of varicella vaccination in preventing secondary bacterial infections.

The pathogenesis likely involves both direct invasion and the effects of GAS virulence factors, including superantigenic exotoxins, which trigger exaggerated host inflammatory responses and diffuse cerebral edema [5]. This mechanism may explain the disproportionate cerebral swelling observed in our patient relative to the CSF bacterial burden.

Diagnosis is challenging. CSF findings typically reveal lymphocytic pleocytosis, elevated protein, and low glucose, but Gram-stained morphology can mimic Streptococcus pneumoniae, the more common pediatric pathogen [10].

Following confirmation of invasive GAS (iGAS), the treatment of choice is high-dose intravenous penicillin G, given its reliable cerebrospinal fluid penetration and the continued universal susceptibility of GAS. Clindamycin, which effectively inhibits streptococcal toxin production, is also advised for both empiric and targeted therapy of iGAS. Clinical studies suggest that combining penicillin with clindamycin improves outcomes in severe cases [11, 12]. Third-generation cephalosporins are considered appropriate alternatives when penicillin cannot be used [13]. In our case, high-dose penicillin G was not available at the treating facility; therefore, the patient received empirical therapy with intravenous ceftriaxone (a third-generation cephalosporin and a beta-lactam) together with vancomycin and dexamethasone. Both ceftriaxone and vancomycin were begun empirically, immediately after the CSF Gram stain demonstrated gram-positive cocci in pairs and before culture and susceptibility results were available—that is, the beta-lactam was not withheld pending culture. Vancomycin was added empirically to cover the possibility of a resistant gram-positive organism (notably penicillin-/cephalosporin-non-susceptible S. pneumoniae, which the Gram stain morphology can resemble) while awaiting identification; the administered dose of 120 mg every 6 h (approximately 20 mg/kg/dose, ≈ 80 mg/kg/day) lies within the recommended high-dose range for bacterial meningitis (60–80 mg/kg/day) and is therefore appropriate. Once susceptibility confirmed a pan-susceptible GAS isolate, antimicrobial therapy was rationalized. Upon transfer to the tertiary hospital, treatment was modified to ampicillin and cefotaxime, which was subsequently switched back to ceftriaxone.

Regarding antiviral therapy, the recommended dose of intravenous acyclovir for severe varicella or disseminated infection in young infants is 20 mg/kg/dose every 8 h [14]. In this case, acyclovir was initially administered at a suboptimal dose of 70 mg every 6 h IV (approximately 11.5 mg/kg/dose) from Day 1 of admission. This was corrected on Day 3 to 120 mg every 8 h IV (approximately 20 mg/kg/dose), consistent with standard dosing. Although the VZV-PCR from CSF was ultimately negative — suggesting that varicella was not the direct cause of the CNS disease — adequate antiviral dosing remained important given the initial diagnostic uncertainty.

The decision to initiate anticoagulation with aspirin and enoxaparin was driven by marked thrombocytosis (platelets 1,600 × 10⁹/L) and clinical suspicion for superior sagittal sinus thrombosis. While anticoagulation is appropriate in confirmed cerebral sinus thrombosis, its use in the setting of severe meningoencephalitis carries a risk of worsening coagulopathy. In this patient, PT and PTT became markedly elevated on 4–5 August, necessitating enoxaparin discontinuation and reversal with vitamin K and FFP. This case highlights the difficult balance between thrombotic and hemorrhagic risk in critically ill patients with invasive GAS meningitis, and underscores the need for close monitoring of coagulation parameters when anticoagulation is used in this context.

A delay between hospital admission and lumbar puncture may have contributed to disease progression in this case. The patient remained febrile with rising inflammatory markers for the first two days of admission before meningitis was diagnosed on Day 3 and pathogen-directed antimicrobial therapy was initiated. It should be emphasized, however, that beta-lactam therapy was not delayed until culture results were available: empirical ceftriaxone was commenced as soon as the CSF Gram stain was obtained. Given the fulminant nature of invasive GAS meningitis and the severe cerebral edema observed, it is not possible to determine whether earlier lumbar puncture and antimicrobial treatment would have altered the outcome, as the disease itself is expected to follow an aggressive course.

Procalcitonin was not measured during this hospitalization, representing a limitation of this case report. Procalcitonin is increasingly recognized as a useful biomarker in distinguishing bacterial from viral meningitis and in monitoring treatment response. Its absence in this workup reflects resource limitations at the initial treating facility. Future cases of suspected invasive GAS should incorporate procalcitonin trending alongside CRP where available [15].

The literature reports remarkably high mortality rates and frequent cerebral complications associated with GAS meningitis in children. The major short-term cerebral complications described are cerebritis, cerebral edema, and intracranial abscess [16]. A Brazilian review analyzing 71 reported cases of pediatric GAS meningitis reported a mortality rate of 22% and an overall complication rate of 67%, with brain abscesses accounting for 8% of complications [17]. Another study reported only 13 documented cases of GAS meningitis in the pediatric population over a ten-year period (1996–2006). The outcomes ranged from full recovery to neurological delay, cranial nerve paresis, or death. Another review of 28 published pediatric cases reported a mortality rate of 11% and short-term cerebral complications—including brain abscess, CSF leakage, subdural effusions, and cavernous thrombosis—in 25% of cases. These findings are striking compared with other, more common causes of bacterial meningitis in children. A meta-analysis of 4,920 children with meningitis caused by S. pneumoniae, N. meningitidis, or H. influenzae reported mortality rates of 4–15% and no significant short-term cerebral complications. Cerebritis or brain abscess formation after meningitis caused by these pathogens is extremely rare [16]. Clinical improvement does not necessarily exclude cerebral complications, so some studies urge clinicians to perform routine MRI scans to detect cerebral complications [17].

Varicella infection is a recognized risk factor for invasive Streptococcus pyogenes (iGAS) disease, as vesicular skin lesions provide a portal for bacterial entry [18]. Routine varicella vaccination has been shown to reduce both the incidence of chickenpox and associated iGAS infections [19, 20]. Following the implementation of vaccination programs, pediatric GAS bacteremia has decreased from 2.43 to 1.30 cases per 100,000 children annually [18], with additional reports showing a 70% reduction in varicella-associated GAS cases in Canada [21] and a 25% decline in varicella-related GAS hospitalizations in the United States [22]. Systematic reviews and public health reports further confirm that vaccination lowers the risk of severe post-varicella bacterial complications, including meningitis and necrotizing fasciitis [23]. These findings highlight that varicella immunization protects not only against primary viral infection but also against life-threatening secondary bacterial infections.

Conclusion

GAS is a rare cause of bacterial meningitis in children and is associated with high mortality rates and rates of cerebral complications. Varicella infection has been identified as a preceding factor for invasive GAS infections; therefore, the implementation of universal vaccination programs helps protect against secondary bacterial infections. The diagnosis of GAS meningitis is challenging, and first-line treatment includes intravenous penicillin G. Antiviral dosing should be carefully verified and adjusted according to weight-based guidelines; suboptimal dosing, as occurred in this case, should be recognized and corrected promptly. Procalcitonin trending, where available, may aid in monitoring disease severity and response to treatment. When anticoagulation is indicated in critically ill patients with iGAS meningitis, coagulation parameters must be monitored closely given the risk of exacerbating coagulopathy. The high rate of cerebral complications should prompt clinicians to consider routine imaging, even when patients show clinical improvement.
