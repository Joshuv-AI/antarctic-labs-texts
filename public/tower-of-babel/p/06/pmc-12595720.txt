Association between physical activity patterns and health outcomes among older adults in India and China: a gendered perspective
Authors: Keying Song, Mengdan Zhao, Zijian Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPhysical activity (PA) is a significant predictor of physical and mental health, particularly among older adults. In India and China, gendered social norms shape physical patterns, and the intensity of androcentric settings is different. As a result, the gendered perspective of physical activity and its association with health outcomes could be different in China and India. Therefore, the study examines gender differences in physical activity and their impact on health outcomes.MethodsThis study utilizes nationally representative data from older adults aged 60 and above in India and China. Gender disaggregated physical activity is considered a key explanatory variable, and functional limitations (IADL/ADL), multimorbidity, and self-rated health are selected as outcome variables. Separate logistic regression analyses were conducted for males and females to examine the gender-specific associations between PA and health.ResultsIndian women show lower moderate PA (54.58%) than Chinese women (49.99%), with markedly fewer engaging in intense PA (12.35% vs. 24.01%). Health disparities are more severe in India: 56.15% report IADL limitations (China: 42.86%) and 25.2% ADL problems (China: 29.09%). While moderate PA strongly protects against poor SRH/ADL in both countries, Indian women face higher multimorbidity risks (aOR: 1.38 vs. China’s Non-Significant) when inactive.ConclusionThis comparative study reveals that Indian women face greater physical activity disparities and worse functional health outcomes than Chinese women, highlighting the urgent need for gender-sensitive health interventions to address these inequities.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25078-w.Keywords: Gender differences, Multimorbidity, Self-rated health, Activities of daily living, Instrumental activities of daily living, Older adults

Background

Population aging is a global trend, with the proportion of people aged 60 and above rising from 9.2% in 1990 to 12.4% in 2024 and projected to 21.1% by 2050 [1]. In line with this global transition, India and China share the largest global aging population. As a result, global academia and policymakers have been prioritizing research that focuses on contextualizing the healthy ageing population in China and India. In the context of a healthy ageing population, existing studies highlight the role of physical activities on health outcomes. Most of the studies clearly suggest that understanding how lifestyle factors influence health outcomes among older adults has become critically important [2]. Although both countries face a growing ageing population, the lifestyle patterns and gender settings are different. As a result, the lifestyle and gender background differentials between China and India could be a potential source of variation in ageing public health challenges [3]. Among lifestyle factors, physical activity is widely recognized as a critical factor in promoting healthy aging [4]. Physical activity is defined as any movement of the body produced by skeletal muscles that results in energy expenditure. This includes exercise, sports, and physical activities that are part of daily life, work, leisure, or active transportation. Whereas, physical function refers to an individual’s ability to carry out daily activities, encompassing motor function and control, physical fitness, and regular participation in physical activity [5]. Existing literature found that a positive association exists between physical activity and healthy ageing in both China and India, separately [6–11].

For instance, older adults engaging in moderate to vigorous physical activity experience lower rates of conditions such as hypertension, diabetes, and cardiovascular diseases in China [9]. In particular, a previous study found that physically active older adults had a 30% reduced risk of developing multimorbidity compared to their sedentary peers in China [10]. Similarly, in India, evidence suggests that engagement in physical activities like walking, yoga, and household chores contributes to better health outcomes for older adults with multiple chronic conditions [11]. A longitudinal study by Singh et al. (2024) reported that older adults who maintained an active lifestyle had significantly enhanced overall quality of life [12]. Additionally, physical activity has been associated with better mental health outcomes [8]. In particular, psychological benefits of physical activity include reduced symptoms of depression and anxiety and improved coping mechanisms for individuals managing multiple health conditions [13]. In China, Li et al. (2016) found that older adults who participated in community-based exercise programs positively impacted their ability to manage chronic conditions [9]. In India, yoga has gained popularity among older adults, with studies demonstrating its effectiveness in improving physical and mental health, thereby aiding in the management of multimorbidity [13]. Similar to objective health, a positive association between physical activity and subjective health is also well established [9]. Self-rated health (SRH), a widely used indicator of overall subjective health and well-being, is also significantly influenced by physical activity [14, 15]. In China, research indicates that regular moderate to vigorous exercise is associated with better SRH among older adults [9]. Evidence from China shows that older adults who engage in activities report higher levels of self-rated health [16]. Wang et al. (2017) demonstrated that Chinese older adults who regularly participated in physical activities reduced risks of chronic conditions like hypertension, diabetes, and cardiovascular diseases [10]. These health benefits translate into more favorable SRH [6, 17]. Concurrently, an identical result was also observed in India. Activities such as walking, yoga, and household chores are positively linked with SRH [4].

Similar to subjective health, a significant association between physical activity and functional limitations has also been observed in many previous empirical studies in China and India. Functional limitations, often defined as difficulties in performing Activities of Daily Living (ADLs) and Instrumental Activities of Daily Living (IADLs) [18]. An existing study in China highlights that older adults who were physically active had a lower risk of developing multimorbidity, which prevents functional decline [10]. A similar finding is also well-documented in India [4]. A study reported that regular engagement in physical activities such as walking not only improved physical strength but also enhanced the ability to perform essential tasks like bathing, dressing, and cooking [19]. Although life course theory highlights that physical and cognitive impairment cannot be checked at a later stage of life, life course regular physical activity, healthy diet, and healthy environment prevent the early onset of ageing physical and cognitive impairments [12].

Gender divide in physical activity and its impact on health outcomes is well established in the Asian continent [20, 21]. In the Asian continent, patriarchy is historically rooted in both Indian and Chinese social settings. As a result, the spatial mobility for recreation, economic, and physical mobility is limited among females compared to their male counterparts due to such prevailing gender norms [22]. Structural inequality in both countries further discourages women from engaging in public physical activities, thereby restricting their chances for health-enhancing exercise [23, 24]. Although the gender divides in physical activity are evident in both countries, explaining the factors of such gender gaps is not similar. For instance, India is a country with diverse religions, social groups, linguistic groups, and ethnic groups, whereas China is substantially less diverse than India in terms of such diversity, which could differ in the explaining factors of gender norms between India and China.

Based on these socio-cultural differentials, the degree of gender disparity in physical activities could vary significantly between India and China. Concurrently, the association between gender based physical activity variation and associated impact on health outcomes could also be varied. Further, the degree of urbanization in China is higher than in India, with female educational advancement and employment also higher in China than in India, which could potentially affect the progress in the normalization of gender equity and norms. Based on these assumptions, the present study is designed as a cross-sectional comparative study. Existing study shows that the gender disparity in physical activity is positively linked with differentials in health outcomes. Women experience higher rates of functional limitations and chronic diseases, such as arthritis and osteoporosis, which are found to be associated with lower levels of vigorous physical activity [25]. In contrast, men, who are commonly more inclined to participate in vigorous activities, report better health outcomes, including a reduced risk of metabolic syndrome and improved cardiovascular health [26].

Although existing evidence on associations between physical activity patterns and health outcomes among older adults in India and China using gendered analyses is substantial [26, 27], there is no prior study designed to conduct such a comparative study. Therefore, the present study is relevant to understanding the comparative scenario of the association between physical activity and health outcomes by highlighting the gender lens between China and India. The present study is conceptualized and framed using the following model (Fig. 1) to clearly understand the gender differences in physical activities and health outcomes at a later stage of life, based on existing literature [28–30]. The present study findings will be helpful to understand the role of physical activity and health outcomes among the expanding ageing population. In addition, the present study findings will fuel the gender debate in physical activity engagement and the health well-being gap.

Materials and methods

Data source

We used data from two harmonized, large-scale, nationally representative studies on ageing: the 2018 China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) and the 2017–2019 Longitudinal Ageing Study in India (LASI). Both CHARLS and LASI are nationally representative surveys designed to study ageing and health among adults aged 45 and above. They are part of the Health and Retirement Study (HRS) international network and follow harmonized protocols in sampling, questionnaire design, and data collection. This common framework enhances the comparability of data across countries.

By using the 2018 wave of CHARLS and the 2017–2018 wave of LASI-both of which represent the most recent, harmonized cross-sections available in the same time period - we ensure consistency in age range, variable definitions, and survey design, supporting the validity of our cross-national comparisons. Both surveys include extensive data on physical health, cognitive and emotional well-being, and socioeconomic variables, making them a robust source for analysing older adults’ health outcomes and behaviours. The baseline CHARLS survey in 2011 included 17,708 participants, with follow-up waves conducted in 2013, 2015, and 2018. In 2018, 16,973 participants were re-interviewed, and 2,843 new individuals were added, bringing the total sample to 19,816. The first wave of LASI (2017-18) involved a nationally representative sample of 72,250 older adults and their spouses [31–33]. The final analytical samples from CHARLS and LASI are 7,433 and 30,346, respectively. A flow chart for choosing the sample for the study is shown in Fig. 2.

Variable description

Outcome variables

The study included four outcome variables, which are multimorbidity, self-rated health, problems in ADL, and problems in IADL.

Multimorbidity

The LASI and CHARLS surveys enquired respondents about the presence of different chronic illnesses such as hypertension, chronic heart disease, stroke, any chronic lung disease, diabetes, cancer or malignant tumor, any bone/joint disease, any neurological/psychiatric disease, and high cholesterol/dyslipidaemia. They were asked, “Has any health professional ever diagnosed you with the following chronic conditions or diseases?“. Respondents with two or more chronic diseases were categorized as 1 “Yes”, else 0 “No” [34, 35].

Self-rated health

In CHARLS, the respondents were asked, “Would you say your health is very good, good, fair, poor or very poor?” The respondents were asked a similar question in LASI, also “Overall, how is your health in general?” and the responses were recorded as “very good, good, fair, poor, or very poor?“. The responses were then categorised as 0 “Good” and 1 “Poor” [36, 37].

ADL and IADL

Problems in ADL and IADL were evaluated through a set of several questions asked to respondents in CHARLS and LASI. For ADL, participants were asked if they faced any challenges with daily activities such as dressing, walking 100 yards, bathing, eating, getting in and out of bed, and using the toilet (including standing up and sitting down). For IADL, participants were asked about difficulties in tasks like preparing a hot meal, grocery shopping, making phone calls, taking medications, doing house or garden work, managing finances (e.g., paying bills or tracking expenses), and navigating unfamiliar places. These difficulties had persisted for over three months. Two dichotomous variables were then created: a score of “1” indicated participants reported any limitations, while “0” signified no limitations [38, 39].

Independent variables

The main independent variables included in this study were intensive physical activities and moderate physical activities. For intensive physical activities, the respondents were asked, How often do you engage in sports or activities such as running, jogging, swimming, going to a gym, cycling, heavy lifting, chopping, farm work, fast cycling, or digging with a spade or shovel?” For moderate activities, participants were asked, “How often do you engage in moderately energetic activities such as house cleaning, washing clothes by hand, fetching water or wood, drawing water from a well, gardening, regular-paced cycling, moderate-paced walking, dancing, or doing floor or stretching exercises?” The response options ranged from “every day” to “never,” and were later recoded into two categories: 0 for “No” and 1 for “Yes” [40].

Covariates

Based on the existing literature, the covariates included in this study were the age category of respondents (60–69, 70–79, and 80+), gender (male, female), place of residence (urban, rural), and current marital status (married/partnered, widowed, other), ever smoked (no, yes), and ever alcohol consumption (no, yes). The educational status was categorised as no education, low education (1–5 years of education), mid-education (6 years in CHARLS and 6–9 years in LASI), high education (7 + years in CHARLS, and 10 + years in LASI) [41]. Respondents in both surveys were asked whether, due to physical health problems, they had difficulty performing certain activities such as walking 100 yards, sitting for two or more hours, getting up from a chair, stooping, kneeling or crouching, reaching or extending arms above shoulder level (either arm), lifting or carrying weights over 5 kg, and picking up a coin from a table. Responses to these questions were combined to generate a dichotomous variable for mobility problems (no/yes) [39]. Respondents in both surveys were also asked, “Please think about your life as a whole. How satisfied are you with it?” and the response was categorised as satisfied, somewhat satisfied, or not satisfied. Respondents were asked about their feelings of loneliness, and responses were categorised as no, occasionally, or most of the time. Similarly, problems in sleeping were classified as no problems, occasionally, most of the time [42, 43].

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was conducted in two stages. First, descriptive statistics were used to summarize the demographic characteristics and key variables. This initial analysis provided an overview of the distribution and prevalence of relevant factors in the study population. Second, logistic regression analysis was employed to examine the association between intensive and moderate physical activities with health outcomes. Unadjusted and adjusted odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were estimated to assess the strength and direction of the associations.

Mental health and lifestyle factors further underscore gender disparities. Chinese females report higher rates of loneliness (20.7% feeling lonely most of the time) and sleep problems (30.9%) compared to males (11.3% and 19.2%, respectively), while in India, 15.1% of females feel lonely most of the time versus 11.6% of males. Gender differences in health outcomes

The results from Table 2 reveal significant gender-based differences in health outcomes among older adults in both China and India, highlighting a consistent pattern of higher health burden among women. In China, the prevalence of multimorbidity is higher among females (39.74%) compared to males (38.22%). Notably, a larger proportion of women reported poor self-rated health (31.31%) than men (26.31%), and this difference is significant (p < 0.001). The gender gap widens further when assessing functional limitations; problems in activities of daily living (ADL) are reported by 29.09% of women compared to 18.22% of men, while problems in instrumental activities of daily living (IADL) are observed by 42.86% of women compared to 27.29% of men. A similar pattern is also observed in India, though the overall prevalence of each indicator is lower than in China. Multimorbidity affects 25.36% of women and 22.16% of men. Poor self-rated health is also more common among women (15.8%) than men (14.18%). Functional difficulties are noticeably higher among Indian women, with 25.2% reporting ADL problems compared to 19.87% of men, and a noticeable 56.15% experiencing IADL limitations, compared to 37.88% of men.

Gender differences in health outcomes among older adults in China and India

Gender differences in physical activity

Figure 3 highlights a gender disparity in physical activity participation across China and India, particularly in both intensive and moderate physical activity (PA). In China, females exhibit higher engagement in moderate PA (49.99%) than males (41.92%), whereas males are more active in intensive PA (32.56%) compared to females (24.01%). A similar trend is observed in India, where the gender gap is even more pronounced; 54.58% of Indian females participate in moderate PA compared to 40.65% of males, while only 12.35% of females engage in intensive PA compared to 24.08% of males.

Gender differences in PA patterns in China and India

Physical activity and health outcomes

Figure 4 illustrates gender differences in health outcomes among older adults in China, by moderate and intensive physical activity (PA) levels. Within moderate PA, women exhibit a higher prevalence of multimorbidity (38.82%) than men (36.07%), yet report notably adverse outcomes in functional limitations, especially in instrumental activities of daily living (IADL), with 34.71% of women affected compared to only 21.13% of men. Additionally, women show a higher rate of poor self-rated health (25.4% vs. 21.12%) and ADL problems (21.68% vs. 13.09%). In contrast, under intensive PA, both genders demonstrate improved health, though disparities remain. Men engaging in intensive activities report lower levels of multimorbidity (32.49%) and significantly fewer ADL problems (12.27%) compared to women (23.35%). However, women show a better self-perception of health (26.63% poor SRH vs. 22.72% in men), but continue to face higher rates of IADL limitations (41% vs. 21.36%).

Prevalence of health outcomes by intense and moderate physical activity among older adults in China

Figure 5 highlights gender disparities in health outcomes among Indian older adults engaging in moderate and intensive physical activity. In moderate physical activity, women reported a higher burden across most health indicators compared to men. Specifically, women show a greater prevalence of multimorbidity (23.21% vs. 18.57%), poorer self-rated health (13.03% vs. 11.2%), and noticeably higher limitations in instrumental activities of daily living (IADL) at 53.06%, significantly more than the 31% reported by men. When it comes to intensive physical activity, the pattern remained largely consistent. Although both genders benefit from intensive PA, women still exhibit slightly worse health outcomes, with multimorbidity at 18.31% compared to 16.35% in men and substantially higher IADL limitations (50.21% vs. 30.52%). Interestingly, men engaging in intensive PA displayed the lowest rates of poor SRH and ADL problems.

Regression estimates of physical activity and health outcomes

The logistic regression estimates from China and India highlight notable gender-specific associations between physical activity and health outcomes among older adults (Tables 3 and 4). In China, men who do not engage in intense physical activity face significantly higher risks across all health outcomes, with adjusted odds ratios (aORs) ranging from 1.25 (SRH) to 1.57 (ADL problems), indicating a strong protective effect of intense activity against multimorbidity, poor self-rated health (SRH), and functional limitations (ADL and IADL). However, the absence of moderate physical activity shows no significant associations for men, suggesting that intense activity is particularly critical for their health. For Chinese women, the lack of intense activity significantly increases risks for poor SRH (aOR: 1.42; 95% CI, 1.15,1.76) and ADL problems (aOR: 1.3; 95% CI, 1.05,1.63), but not multimorbidity or IADL problems. In contrast, no moderate activity is strongly linked to poorer outcomes in SRH (aOR: 1.37; 95% CI, 1.14,1.66), ADL (aOR: 1.38; 95% CI, 1.14,1.67), and IADL (aOR: 1.35; 95% CI, 1.13,1.62), indicating that moderate activity plays a more substantial role for women than for men in China.

In India, gender differences are also pronounced but follow a different pattern. For men, the absence of intense physical activity significantly elevates risks for multimorbidity (aOR 1.41, 95% CI, 1.17,1.69) and SRH (aOR: 2.03; 95% CI, 1.70,2.43), but not ADL or IADL problems, while no moderate activity is associated with increased risks across all outcomes (aORs: 1.28–1.68), suggesting moderate activity is broadly protective. For Indian women, no intense activity strongly predicts poor SRH (aOR: 2.10; 95% CI, 1.58,2.78) and multimorbidity (aOR: 1.38; 95% CI, 1.04,1.85), but not ADL or IADL problems, similar to men. However, the lack of moderate activity significantly increases risks for multimorbidity (aOR 1.30; 95% CI, 1.11,1.53), SRH (aOR: 1.37; 95% CI, 1.18,1.59), and ADL problems (aOR: 1.66; 95% CI, 1.43,1.92), but not IADL. Compared to China, India shows stronger effect sizes, particularly for SRH in both genders, and moderate activity appears more consistently impactful for both men and women. These findings suggest that while intense activity is more critical for men in China, moderate activity is vital for women in both countries, with India demonstrating a broader protective effect of moderate activity across genders, potentially reflecting cultural or lifestyle differences in physical activity patterns. Discussion

Addressing gender disparities in physical activity and related health outcomes among older adults in China and India is vital for ensuring equitable health and enhancing the overall well-being of these ageing populations. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first comparative study utilizing nationally representative data from both countries to thoroughly examine these gender differences. Our analysis revealed three key findings. First, the present study observed that multimorbidity was more prevalent among females than males in both China and India. Second, the gender gap in the prevalence of ADL and IADL limitations was wider in India compared to China. Third, logistic regression results showed significant differences. While physical activity was significantly associated with multimorbidity, SRH, ADL, and IADL among males in both countries, the associations among females varied between India and China. In China, physical activity showed significant links only with SRH and ADL for females, whereas SRH and multimorbidity were found to be significantly associated in India.

Our study found that significant gender differences in multimorbidity among older adults in China and India are pronounced, with women generally experiencing a higher prevalence of multiple chronic conditions compared to men. This disparity can be attributed to a variety of factors, including biological, social, and behavioral differences. In both countries, women tend to live longer than men, which increases their exposure to chronic diseases typically associated with aging, such as arthritis, osteoporosis, and cardiovascular conditions [45, 46]. Additionally, women are more likely to report health issues due to greater health-seeking behaviors and a higher likelihood of suffering from non-fatal but disabling conditions, which contributes to the higher recorded prevalence of multimorbidity [47]. Socially, traditional gender roles often place women at a disadvantage regarding access to healthcare and nutritious diets, particularly in rural areas of India, where gender-based inequalities in healthcare access are prevalent [48]. Similarly, in China, older women face social and economic disadvantages that may increase their vulnerability to chronic diseases [49]. These gender disparities are further exacerbated by the fact that women often shoulder the burden of caregiving, which can limit their time and resources for self-care and physical activity, both critical for preventing and managing chronic conditions [50, 51].

We also found a wider gender gap in IADL problems in India compared to China, which aligns with findings from several previous studies [52, 53]. Numerous studies have explored and highlighted these differences, offering insights into why the gender gap in functional limitations, such as IADL problems, is more pronounced in India than in China [18, 39, 52]. One of the primary reasons for the wider gender gap in India is the rigid traditional gender roles that place a heavy caregiving and household responsibility burden on women throughout their lives [20, 54]. These roles often limit women’s opportunities to engage in paid work, education, and physical activities, all of which contribute to better health outcomes later in life [55]. In India, women are often expected to perform unpaid domestic work, including caregiving for children and elderly family members, which may lead to cumulative physical strain and exhaustion over time [56]. This long-term strain contributes to functional limitations, especially in older age, when women are more likely to suffer from IADL problems, such as difficulties managing household tasks or running errands independently [57]. In contrast, China has undergone significant social and economic transformations, including the development of healthcare infrastructure and social welfare programs that provide support to older adults, particularly women [9]. Additionally, China’s higher literacy and education rates among women have contributed to better health literacy and access to healthcare services, which in turn mitigate some of the gender disparities in functional limitations observed in India [58].

The present study found a substantial variation in adjusted association between physical activity and health outcomes among females between China and India. For instance, the adjusted association between physical activity and multimorbidity is found to be significant in India, but it is observed to be insignificant in China. This observed difference may be due to several factors. First, gender disparity in healthcare access, healthcare autonomy and finance is strongly prevalent in India than in China, which could be the coefficient effect of such significant association in India [59].

Historically, China’s health care system has been predominantly state-funded, ensuring broad coverage for its population. In contrast, India has relied more heavily on private insurance and out-of-pocket expenditures. However, recent shifts in China have led to a growing dependence on private health care payments, mirroring trends seen in India. While China has achieved greater success than India in enhancing population health outcomes, ongoing health care reforms have introduced significant challenges, undermining the efficiency and effectiveness of its health care system [60, 61]. Furthermore, Indian women often experience a higher prevalence of malnutrition and chronic diseases such as anemia and osteoporosis, conditions that can worsen physical function and lead to functional limitations at an early stage of the older period [62]. Contrary, the prevalence of these conditions among older women is lower, partly due to better healthcare access and nutrition, which reduces the overall burden of functional limitations in older age in China [6]. In addition, social stigma surrounding health in India, particularly for women, leads to underreporting of health issues among men and higher visibility of health problems among women.

Finally, the study reveals persistent gender gaps in physical activity participation in both countries, with women encountering greater barriers across all life stages. WHO data show Indian women report lower adherence to recommended activity levels than their Chinese counterparts. Furthermore, India demonstrates more pronounced cultural and societal constraints on female participation in sports and exercise compared to China [63]. Furthermore, individuals who did not engage in moderate or intensive physical activities reported more health issues compared to those who participated in such activities in both India and China. This finding aligns with the well-established relationship between physical activity and health outcomes in older adults [64]. Regular engagement in moderate to intensive physical activities, such as walking, cycling, or other forms of exercise, is known to improve cardiovascular health, maintain muscle strength, enhance joint flexibility, and reduce the risk of chronic diseases like diabetes, hypertension, and heart disease [27]. Those who are physically active tend to experience fewer functional limitations, such as difficulties in performing ADLs and IADLs, compared to sedentary individuals [52]. Individuals who remain inactive are more prone to multimorbidity, which refers to the coexistence of two or more chronic conditions, as well as mental health issues such as depression and anxiety [65, 66]. This pattern is consistent across various studies, which have shown that regular physical activity not only reduces the risk of developing health problems but also enhances overall quality of life by improving mental and physical well-being [9, 14]. Therefore, promoting physical activity among older adults in both countries is critical to addressing the growing burden of age-related health issues.

Strengths and limitations

One of the strengths of this study is its comparative analysis using nationally representative data from two of the most populous and rapidly ageing countries. This allows for a broader understanding of how gender differences in physical activity and its association with health outcomes vary between China and India. The study’s findings strengthen the gender debate on physical activity and association with health outcomes by covering the world’s two most populated ageing countries. Additionally, by analyzing both moderate and intensive PA patterns, the study provides a nuanced view of how different activity levels affect older adults’ health, particularly in terms of functional limitations and multimorbidity.

However, the study has certain limitations. First, the cross-sectional design makes it failed to establish causal relationships between physical activity and health outcomes. Second, self-reported data on physical activity may introduce recall bias, as older adults might either overestimate or underestimate their levels of activity. Cultural factors influencing physical activity reporting, especially for women’s domestic work, could also skew the data, particularly in India, where household chores are often not recognized as formal physical activity. Lastly, the study does not account for the potential influence of environmental factors, such as access to public spaces or community-based exercise programs, which could affect physical activity levels and health outcomes differently in urban and rural settings. Despite these limitations, the study offers valuable insights into the gendered dimensions of PA and health among older adults in both countries.

The present study discussion underlines that policy recommendations should focus on expanding community-based exercise programs, particularly for older women, to encourage participation in moderate and intensive physical activities. In rural areas where access to recreational facilities is limited, culturally appropriate interventions like promoting yoga, walking groups, and home-based exercises should be implemented. Additionally, public health campaigns need to raise awareness about the importance of regular PA for maintaining health and preventing functional limitations among older adults, with a particular focus on reaching women. Governments in India and China should also consider integrating PA into routine healthcare for older adults, emphasizing its role in managing chronic conditions and improving overall well-being. Finally, gender-inclusive urban planning that facilitates access to safe, outdoor spaces for exercise, particularly for older women, will be essential in addressing the disparities in PA and improving health outcomes across both nations.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study underscores the significant gender differences in physical activity patterns and their associated health outcomes among older adults in India and China. Women, especially in India, engage less in moderate to intensive physical activities, leading to a higher prevalence of functional limitations and chronic health conditions compared to men. The gender gap in physical activity not only contributes to poorer physical health but also limits the capacity of older women to maintain an active, independent lifestyle. These findings highlight the urgent need for gender-sensitive policies and programs to promote equitable access to physical activity opportunities for older adults in both countries.
