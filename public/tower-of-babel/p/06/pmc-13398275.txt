Comment on “A cost-utility analysis of pharmacopuncture versus physiotherapy for chronic low back pain: A multicenter, pragmatic randomized controlled trial”
Authors: Kanu Goyal, Riya Kalra, Ritika kashyap
Published: 2026

We read with great interest the article by Lee et al., entitled “A cost-utility analysis of pharmacopuncture versus physiotherapy for chronic low back pain: A multicentre, pragmatic randomized controlled trial”, recently published in Integrative Medicine Research.1 The authors should be commended for addressing an important gap in the economic evaluation of non-pharmacological interventions for chronic low back pain (cLBP), particularly by incorporating both healthcare system and societal perspectives.

The pragmatic multicenter design, use of validated outcome measures such as EQ-5D-5 L (EuroQol Five-Dimension Five-Level questionnaire) and SF-6D (Short Form Six-Dimension health utility measure), and adherence to the CHEERS (Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards) reporting standards enhance the methodological rigor and clinical relevance of the study. Notably, the inclusion of productivity loss using the Work Productivity and Activity Impairment questionnaire provides valuable insight into the broader economic implications of cLBP management, an aspect often overlooked in cost-effectiveness research.2,3 The finding that pharmacopuncture (PPT) was dominant from a societal perspective highlights its potential role as a cost-effective, opioid-sparing intervention.

Nevertheless, several points merit further discussion. First, the follow-up duration of 25 weeks, although adequate for short-term economic evaluation, may be insufficient to fully capture the long-term cost-utility of interventions for a chronic and recurrent condition such as CLBP.4 Longer follow-up periods are essential to determine whether the observed gains in quality-adjusted life years and reductions in productivity loss are sustained over time.

Second, while the pragmatic nature of the trial enhances external validity, the lack of standardized treatment protocols for both PPT and physiotherapy may introduce treatment heterogeneity, potentially influencing cost and outcome variability.5 Future studies may benefit from stratified or protocol-guided analyses to better isolate intervention-specific effects.

Finally, the study was conducted within the Korean healthcare system, where pharmacopuncture is well integrated into routine practice. This context may limit generalizability to countries where such interventions are less established or not reimbursed. Comparative economic evaluations across different healthcare systems would further strengthen the policy relevance of these findings.6

In conclusion, Lee et al. provide compelling evidence supporting the economic value of pharmacopuncture for cLBP, particularly from a societal perspective. We encourage further large-scale, long-term, and cross-cultural studies to confirm these findings and to guide informed decision-making in integrative pain management.
