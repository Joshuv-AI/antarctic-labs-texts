Combined effect of chronic inflammation and insulin resistance on stroke: a cohort study and mediation analysis
Authors: Yu Gao, Zhiping Duan, Jing Li, Sicheng Du, Lijun Song, Ran Zhao, Yan Hou, Yan Zhao, Ying Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStroke is a major public health challenge, and the combined effects of chronic inflammation and insulin resistance (IR) on stroke require further investigation. This study used high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP) and the metabolic score for insulin resistance (MetS-IR) to assess chronic inflammation and IR, respectively, and explored their combined effects on stroke in middle-aged and older Chinese adults.MethodsBased on the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study cohort, 7,422 participants ≥ 40 years old without stroke at baseline in 2011 were enrolled and followed up to 2020. MetS-IR was calculated from (ln 2 × fasting plasma glucose + triglyceride × body mass index)/ln high-density lipoprotein cholesterol. Participants were divided into four groups based on hsCRP (cut point 1 mg/L) and MetS-IR (cut point 34.4). Cox proportional risk modeling and mediation analysis were used to explore the effect of different inflammation and IR states on stroke.ResultsDuring an average follow-up period of 8.6 years, a total of 781 (10.5%) participants experienced a new stroke. Compared with participants with lower levels of both chronic inflammation and IR, the hazard ratio (HR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) for stroke in the higher chronic inflammation group was 1.64 (1.31–2.07), the HR and 95%CI for the higher IR group was 1.51 (1.19–1.90), and for the group with both higher levels of chronic inflammation and IR it was 1.95 (1.58–2.41). In addition, IR mediated 15.6% of the effect of chronic inflammation on stroke; chronic inflammation mediated 13.1% of the effect of IR on stroke.ConclusionsChronic inflammation and IR have independent and superimposed effects on new-onset stroke risk and mediate each other. In primary care and public health services, the use of hsCRP and MetS-IR to assess the body's chronic inflammation and IR status and further assess its risk of a new stroke is a simple and effective new approach.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-025-03375-9.Keywords: Chronic inflammation, Insulin resistance, High-sensitivity C-reactive protein, Metabolic score for insulin resistance, Stroke

Background

Stroke is the second leading cause of death and the third leading cause of disability in the world, with a global economic cost of more than $891 billion/year, accounting for approximately 0.66% of global GDP [1–3]. 87% of stroke deaths occur in low- and middle-income countries, and stroke has long been the first cause of death in China [3–5]. Early detection of stroke risk factors is expected to reduce the incidence of stroke.

Previous studies have found that chronic inflammation increases the risk of stroke by promoting the formation and maturation of atherosclerotic plaques [6, 7], and that it is also directly involved in the process of brain injury, increasing the size of cerebral infarcts and leading to neurological disability in patients [8, 9]. In addition, inflammatory factors can cause insulin resistance (IR) in fat, muscle, and liver by inhibiting insulin signaling [10]. IR further contributes to stroke by increasing the risk of diabetes, obesity, hypertension, and atherosclerosis [11–15].

Chronic inflammation and IR are interrelated [16–18], they may synergistically promote stroke [19]. Although previous studies have demonstrated the separate effects of chronic inflammation and IR each on stroke [7, 8, 14], further analysis is needed to determine their combined effects on stroke in middle-aged and older people. To this end, we used high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP) to detect chronic inflammation levels. Insulin resistance status was assessed using the metabolic score for insulin resistance (MetS-IR). We then used cohort studies and mediation effects analysis to explore the combined effect of these two metrics on stroke risk in a middle-aged and older population.

Methods

Study population

This study included participants who did not have stroke and were > 40 years of age from the 2011 baseline survey in China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), a representative survey programme for the middle-aged and older population in China that collects economic and health data on participants. CHARLS is a longitudinal survey with the baseline survey conducted in 2011, followed by follow-ups every 2–3 years, with the most recent follow-up conducted in 2020 [20].

In the baseline survey, we excluded those who (1) had an acute infection (hsCRP ≥ 10 mg/l) or missing hsCRP data (n = 6577), (2) missing MetS-IR data (n = 1821), (3) age < 40 years (n = 30), and (4) had a stroke (n = 229). We then further excluded participants who were lost to follow-up as of 2020 (n = 1626), and the final number of participants included in this study was 7422 (Fig. 1). Assessment of chronic inflammation and IR status

We assessed the body’s chronic inflammation status using hsCRP, a biomarker synthesized by the liver that responds to systemic inflammatory status [21]. Since hsCRP ≥ 10 mg/l may be associated with acute infections and acute inflammation, we excluded these individuals so that hsCRP would be able to reflect the body’s chronic inflammation status [21, 22]. The measurement of the hsCRP was conducted through the utilization of an immunoturbidimetric assay.

We used MetS-IR to assess IR status. MetS-IR is a surrogate for IR based on a combination of fasting glucose (FPG), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDLC), and body mass index (BMI), which permits assessment of IR status without the need for direct measurement of insulin levels and is significantly correlated with insulin levels and type 2 diabetes [23]. The determination of FPG, TG, and HDLC was conducted in a fasting state, employing the enzymatic colorimetric test method. The BMI was calculated using weight divided by height squared. Assessment of stroke

Whether participants had a stroke was determined using a questionnaire. At baseline and at each follow-up, participants were asked, ‘Have you been diagnosed with a stroke by a doctor?’ Those who answered ‘yes’ were defined as having a stroke. The time of stroke onset was defined as the time of the first ‘yes’ answer at follow-up, including ischemic and hemorrhagic stroke.

Covariates

The covariates in this study included demographic information, smoking status, alcohol consumption status, chronic disease status, and blood test indicators. Demographic information included gender, age, educational level, marital status, and place of residence. Chronic disease status included information on hypertension, diabetes, chronic lung disease, heart disease, kidney disease, and asthma. Blood test indicators included white blood cell count, total cholesterol, and glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c). Total cholesterol and HbA1c were measured using enzymatic colorimetric tests and boronate affinity HPLC, respectively.

Statistical analysis

Based on previous studies [21, 22], the cut point for hsCRP was set at 1 mg/L. hsCRP < 1 mg/L was considered a lower level of chronic inflammation, and hsCRP ≥ 1 mg/L was a relatively high level of chronic inflammation. The cut point for MetS-IR was set at a median of 34.4 for the population included in this study, with MetS-IR < 34.4 considered a lower IR status and MetS-IR ≥ 34.4 considered a higher IR status.

To analyze the combined effects of hsCRP and MetS-IR on stroke, we divided the participants into 4 groups. Group 1: hsCRP < 1 mg/L and MetS-IR < 34.4 (n = 2325). Group 2: hsCRP ≥ 1 mg/L and MetS-IR < 34.4 (n = 1376). Group 3: hsCRP < 1 mg/L and MetS-IR ≥ 34.4 (n = 1576). Group 4: hsCRP ≥ 1 mg/L and MetS-IR ≥ 34.4 (n = 2145).

Continuous variables following a normal distribution are represented by the mean and standard deviation, skewed continuous variables by the median and quartiles, and categorical variables by the number of cases and percentage. Analysis of ANOVA and Chi-square tests was used to compare baseline characteristics among participants.

Scatter plots and Spearman correlation analysis were used to assess the correlation between hsCRP and MetS-IR. Kaplan–Meier curves and log-rank tests were used to compare trends in stroke risk over time among participants with different hsCRP and MetS-IR levels. The Cox proportional hazards model was used to analyze the independent and combined effects of hsCRP and MetS-IR on stroke, and to estimate the hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The Schoenfeld residuals were used to test the proportional hazards assumption for all Cox models, with P values all > 0.05. Subgroup Cox models were used to validate the stability of the results across different age, gender, hypertension, and diabetes statuses. The mediating effects between hsCRP and MetS-IR were analyzed using a mediation model.

To gain a more detailed understanding of the combined effects of inflammation and different severities of IR on stroke, we regrouped participants based on the MetS-IR tertiles (31.8, 38.0) and quartiles (29.8, 34.4, 40.4). We maintained the cutoff point for hsCRP at 1 mg/L, dividing participants into 6 and 8 groups, and used Cox models to analyze stroke risk across different groups.

All statistical analyses were performed using Stata/MP version 18 and R version 4.4.1, with significance set at P < 0.05. Mediation analysis was performed using the “mediation” package in R, employing the Bootstrap method under a causal inference framework, repeated 1000 times.

Independent and combined association of chronic inflammation and IR on new-onset stroke

Figure 2a shows the distribution of hsCRP and MetS-IR, Spearman correlation analysis showed that hsCRP and MetS-IR were significantly weakly positively correlated (ρ = 0.279, p < 0.001). During an average follow-up period of 8.6 years, a total of 781 (10.5%) participants experienced a new stroke. The number of cases and incidence rates in group 1, group 2, group 3, and group 4 were 149 (6.4%), 159 (11.6%), 151 (9.6%), and 322 (15%), respectively (Fig. 2b).

When participants were regrouped according to the tertiles and quartiles of MetS-IR, the risk of stroke overall increased with rising levels of MetS-IR and hsCRP (Supplementary Tables 2–3).

Discussion

Using this cohort study with a mean follow-up of 8.6 years, we found a significant combined effect of chronic inflammation and IR on stroke in a middle-aged and older population in a Chinese community. Compared with those with lower levels of both chronic inflammation and IR, participants with higher levels of chronic inflammation only had a significant 64% increased risk of stroke, those with higher levels of IR only had a significant 51% increased risk of stroke, and those with higher levels of both chronic inflammation and IR had a significant 95% increased risk of stroke. In addition, IR mediated 15.6% of the effect of chronic inflammation on stroke; chronic inflammation mediated 13.1% of the effect of IR on stroke.

As a marker of the inflammatory state, hsCRP has been shown in numerous studies to be a predictor of stroke risk, functional prognosis, and mortality [8, 24–28]. MetS-IR is a new index for assessing the degree of insulin resistance, which is significantly correlated with visceral fat and fasting insulin levels and is effective in predicting the onset of type 2 diabetes mellitus and stroke [23, 29]. However, no studies have yet explored the combined effects of hsCRP and MetS-IR on stroke, and our study fills this gap.

Previous studies have explored the combined effects of other IR surrogate markers and chronic inflammation on stroke [30–33]. Cancan Cui et al. reported that when hsCRP ≥ 1 mg/L and triglyceride–glucose index (TyG) ≥ 8.6 (median level), the overall risk of stroke increased by 33.3%; however, when only one of the two was elevated, the risk of stroke did not increase [31]. Our study found that when either hsCRP or MetS-IR was elevated, the risk of stroke significantly increased, and the increase was most pronounced when both were elevated. Guijun Huo et al. found that high C-reactive protein–triglyceride–glucose index (CTI) levels were significantly associated with a 33% and 20% increase in stroke risk in people with normal glucose metabolism and prediabetes, respectively, but no such association was observed in people with diabetes [32]. Matsumoto K et al. found that the combination of the K index of short insulin tolerance test and hsCRP could not predict the occurrence of stroke in patients with type 2 diabetes [33]. We found that hsCRP and MetS-IR increase the risk of stroke in the general population and may be even more pronounced in people with diabetes. These differences in results may be due to the use of different assessment indicators for insulin resistance. Compared with TyG, CTI, and K index, MetS-IR incorporates more metabolism-related information, including FPG, TG, HDLC, and BMI. In summary, MetS-IR is a promising indicator for assessing the combined effects of chronic inflammation and insulin resistance on stroke risk.

Our mediated effects analysis revealed an interaction between hsCRP and MetS-IR in influencing stroke risk. This reflects a complex bidirectional pathologic link between inflammation and insulin resistance [16, 18]. It has been elucidated that obesity or a high-fat diet activates the IKKβ pathway in adipocytes, hepatocytes, and macrophages, inducing the expression of pro-inflammatory factors such as TNF-α and IL-6, which inhibit insulin signaling [34–36]. Inflammatory stimuli can activate the JNK pathway to phosphorylate the serine site of insulin receptor substrate-1, which interferes with the normal tyrosine phosphorylation of the insulin receptor and ultimately leads to insulin resistance [37–39]. At the same time, insulin resistance exacerbates inflammation through mechanisms such as immune cell activation, sustained inflammatory signaling pathways, and metabolic reprogramming, forming a vicious cycle [40–42]. Inflammation and IR lead to metabolic disorders and vascular damage, which in turn contribute to atherosclerosis and stroke [19, 43].

Our findings suggest that using hsCRP and MetS-IR as indicators of chronic inflammation and IR to jointly assess stroke risk is reasonable. Both indicators can be conveniently obtained through routine testing at primary care institutions, providing a simple and efficient new approach and practical tool for early screening of stroke risk in a broader population. This study also has some limitations. First, the study population was mainly Chinese community residents ≥ 40 years of age, and it would be prudent to generalize the findings to other regions, races, and younger populations, and future studies are needed to validate the findings. Second, the definition of stroke in this study was based on self-report rather than medical records or imaging, which may introduce potential recall bias. Moreover, no distinction was made between stroke subtypes (ischemic or hemorrhagic). Future studies should explore differences in the predictive efficacy of hsCRP and MetS-IR across different stroke subtypes. Third, our subgroup analysis showed that this combined effect was more pronounced in the diabetic population, but the confidence interval range was very wide. This may be due to the small number of diabetic patients, which reduced statistical power. Further research is needed to explore the differences in this combined association between diabetic and non-diabetic populations.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that chronic inflammation and IR have independent and superimposed effects on new-onset stroke risk and mediate each other. In primary care, the use of hsCRP and MetS-IR to assess the body's chronic inflammation and IR status and further assess its risk of a new stroke is a simple and effective new approach.
