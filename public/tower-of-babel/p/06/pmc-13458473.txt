Freestanding ferroelectric membranes via ionic unlocking van der Waals interface
Authors: Yang Liu, Xiwen Zhang, Zhang Zhang, Shuhao Wang, Jin Wang, Lisha Liu, Yaojin Wang, Yixin Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFreestanding oxide films have attracted significant interest for their potential applications in flexible electronics. However, the common use of sacrificial layers hinders the development of freestanding oxide films that meet industrial demands for larger area, device-scalable thickness and low cost. Here, we present a water-assisted ionic unlocking strategy that enables the rapid exfoliation of centimeter-scale, freestanding ferroelectric Pb(Zr,Ti)O3 membranes from Pb(Zr,Ti)O3/Pt/Mica. Leveraging density functional theory and ab initio molecular dynamics simulations, we identify water-induced potassium ion leaching from the Mica substrate as a key interfacial weakening pathway that substantially reduces the Pt/Mica interfacial binding energy, thereby enabling crack propagation driven by capillary forces along the weakened interface. The resulting 1×1 cm2 Pb(Zr,Ti)O3 membranes demonstrate a remarkable piezoelectric coefficient of 528 pC N−1, approximately twice that of their clamped counterparts on Mica. Moreover, by incorporating these membranes, we fabricate self-powered flexible acoustic sensors for high-fidelity sound recording and speech recognition, achieving a speech recognition accuracy exceeding 93%. This work represents a significant advance in both the scalable fabrication of freestanding ferroelectric membranes and the engineering of high-performance flexible sensors.Subject terms: Ferroelectrics and multiferroics, Electronic devices

Introduction

The integration of high-performance functional oxides with flexible electronic platforms is pivotal for advancing wearable devices and soft robotic systems1–3. Freestanding oxide membranes represent a key enabling technology, unlocking intrinsic physical properties by eliminating substrate-induced clamping while offering the mechanical compliance required for seamless integration with diverse substrates4. A prominent example is the record-high pyroelectric coefficient (1.76 × 10−2 Cm−2 K−1) achieved in freestanding PMN-PT ultrathin membranes, which enabled the development of high-performance, cooling-free infrared detectors5. High-performance flexible acoustic sensors, in particular, stand to benefit significantly from freestanding perovskite oxide membranes. These membranes can exhibit greater mechanical deformation under incident acoustic pressure, thereby improving electromechanical conversion efficiency and enabling the detection of low-intensity speech signals6. Commercial condenser microphones, despite their flat frequency response, are unsuitable for emerging wearable applications due to their low sensitivity, power dependency and rigid structure7. In contrast, self-powered flexible piezoelectric acoustic sensors (fPAS) present a compelling alternative, offering operation without external power and conformal mechanical attachment. Crucially, the performance of these fPAS devices is expected to increase by employing a freestanding membrane design8. However, a prevailing challenge in these applications is the difficulty in fabricating freestanding piezoelectric membranes that possess excellent piezoelectric properties, as these membranes are typically grown on rigid substrates with strong interfacial bonding.

To fabricate freestanding piezoelectric membranes, advanced exfoliation and transfer methodologies—predominantly relying on transferring as-grown films onto designated flexible substrates—have been proposed, enabling membrane thicknesses ranging from the nanometer to micrometer scale9,10. The transfer can be facilitated by a variety of physical or chemical methods, involving techniques such as laser lift-off11,12, mechanical exfoliation mediated by stressor layers13,14, mechanical thinning15,16, backside chemical etching of substrates17,18, and water-soluble sacrificial layer-assisted exfoliation19–21. However, these methods present several limitations: (i) the first three physical techniques often involve high operational costs, with laser lift-off in particular being restricted to optically transparent substrates such as sapphire; (ii) the last two chemical approaches frequently risk inflicting substantial damage22 or inducing fragmentation tendencies of the functional material and (iii) a major impediment shared by all existing techniques is their limited scalability and inability to support high-throughput, large-area manufacturing. Therefore, developing a cost-effective and scalable strategy for the high-throughput fabrication of high-quality freestanding piezoelectric membranes remains a significant challenge, hindering their widespread application in advanced fPAS devices23.

Recently, a van der Waals (vdW) exfoliation process utilizing natural water, without the need for any sacrificial layers, has been reported for the preparation of freestanding two-dimensional materials and oxide films24–26. However, a comprehensive mechanistic understanding of the exfoliation process remained elusive. Inspired by this, we report a cost-effective water-assisted vdW exfoliation process for preparing large-area freestanding Pb(Zr,Ti)O3 (PZT) membranes (1 × 1 cm2). Most importantly, an ionic-unlocking mechanism associated with water-induced potassium ion leaching was identified through density functional theory (DFT) and ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) calculations. The simulations indicate that potassium ion leaching from the Mica substrate weakens the Pt/Mica interfacial bonding, with the calculated binding energy decreasing by more than 50% after the introduction of water, thereby creating the weakened interface required for crack propagation driven by capillary forces. Due to reduced mechanical clamping from the substrate27, the freestanding films exhibit high piezoelectric performance with direct piezoelectric coefficient (d33,d) of ~528 pC N−1, approximately double that of PZT films clamped on Mica, showing great potential for high-performance devices. To demonstrate the practical viability of these membranes, the fPAS was fabricated by integrating freestanding PZT films with electrodes and a polymer substrate. The device exhibits an outstanding signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of 63 dBV and a sensitivity figure of merit (FOMSens.) of 45.21 mV Pa−1 mm−3. Additionally, optimized device architecture leads to a wide frequency response range from 100 Hz to 3600 Hz, which effectively encompasses the primary voice energy spectrum, enabling the device to perform high-fidelity sound recording, achieve accurate speech recognition, and facilitate intuitive human-machine interactions (HMI).

Results and discussion

Acquisition of freestanding PZT membranes

The freestanding PZT membranes were produced through the vdW exfoliation process, as shown schematically in Fig. 1a. The process initiates with the infiltration of water molecules at the Pt/Mica interface, leading to the formation of initial microcracks. The continuous penetration of water molecules facilitates the rapid propagation of these cracks along the interface, culminating in the integral delamination of the PZT/Pt film and the formation of a transferable, freestanding film. Figure 1b illustrates the underlying atomic-level mechanism, as revealed by a multiscale theoretical framework that combines static DFT calculations with AIMD simulations. Briefly, water molecules exhibit a strong interaction with the potassium ions on the Mica surface, inducing a pronounced solvation effect. The resulting solvation substantially diminishes the vdW forces and electrostatic interactions between Mica and Pt, thereby weakening the interface and facilitating exfoliation once water penetrates along the Pt/Mica interface.

To elucidate the water-assisted vdW exfoliation mechanism, DFT calculations were first performed to investigate the interfacial electronic charge redistribution between Mica and Pt, which governs the exfoliation behavior. The charge density difference (Δρ) was calculated by comparing two contrasting configurations, an ion-locked state (dry interface) and an ion-unlocked state (hydrated interface). The calculation models and details of Δρ are shown in Supplementary Fig. 1, Supplementary Note 1, respectively. Figure 1c, d show the charge density difference distribution at the Mica/Pt interface and its quantitative profile along the normal direction in the “ion-locked” and “ion-unlocked” states, respectively. In the absence of water, a significant accumulation of electron density exists between the Pt layer and the potassium ions on the Mica surface (Fig. 1c, left, red regions; Fig. 1d, red line), indicating strong vdW forces and electrostatic interactions. An electron enrichment concentrated between potassium ions and Pt reflects a local anchoring effect of the potassium ions, which stably “locks” the Pt film to the Mica substrate. Upon the introduction of water, the original electron accumulation between potassium ions and the Pt layer is significantly reduced, while a new region of electron enrichment emerges between Pt and the hydrated potassium ions (Fig. 1c, right; Fig. 1d, blue line). The altered charge distribution arises from the formation of stable solvation shells around the potassium ions, which screen the electrostatic interactions and weaken their function as bridges at the Mica/Pt interface. As a result, the interfacial constraint on the Pt film is reduced, effectively promoting its unlocking and water-assisted vdW exfoliation.

After ionic unlocking, successful peeling still requires that the interface be thermodynamically favorable for separation. EPt/Mica denotes the total interfacial energy of the fully relaxed Mica/Pt interface. Aint refers to the interfacial area. The numerator, EPt+EMica−EPt/Mica, defines the interfacial binding energy (Ebind), which directly determines Wad. A decrease in the Ebind, ΔE=Ebindwet−Ebinddry<0, is the thermodynamic requirement for water-assisted exfoliation, where Ebindwet and Ebinddry denote the interfacial binding energy at the wet and dry interfaces, respectively. To verify the thermodynamic weakening effect, DFT calculations were performed, showing that the introduction of water reduces the Pt/Mica interfacial binding energy by more than 50%, from approximately 1.24 eV to 0.60 eV per supercell (Supplementary Note 2, Supplementary Table 1). The substantial reduction confirms that water thermodynamically weakens the Pt/Mica interfacial binding strength. At the macroscopic level, the wettability contrast between Pt and Mica facilitates water access to the exposed Pt/Mica interface and its subsequent propagation along the progressively weakened interface, generating a capillary driving force for crack propagation, as indicated by contact angle measurements (Supplementary Note 3).

To validate the energetic profiles and microscopic mechanisms indicated by DFT, AIMD simulations were performed at 300 K. A two-stage simulation strategy was employed to deconstruct the ion-unlocking process, utilizing both a constrained model and a fully flexible model. The constrained model, in which all atoms, except for the potassium ions within the Mica structure and water molecules, were ideally fixed, demonstrates the fundamental ion-solvent interaction. As seen in Supplementary Fig. 2, Supplementary Video 1, the water-induced liberation of potassium ions from the Mica lattice initiates at t = 0.15 ps and concludes by t = 0.25 ps, confirming the intrinsic capacity of water to drive the ion-unlocking process. Subsequently, a more realistic simulation was performed where all atoms were allowed to move freely, accounting for the dynamic thermal vibrations of the entire system. AIMD simulations with a fully flexible model (Fig. 1e, Supplementary Video 2) capture the first 0–0.5 ps of the interaction between adsorbed potassium ions and incoming water molecules. Within only 0.12 ps after water introduction, a surface potassium ion starts to leave its lattice site; by 0.20 ps it is fully solvated and completely detached from the Mica surface. This ultrafast sequence shows that thermal fluctuations alone allow water to spontaneously solvate and mobilize potassium ions. To further validate environmental robustness, AIMD simulations at 330 K and exfoliation tests at 60 °C were performed, confirming that the ionic unlocking process remains kinetically favorable under thermal fluctuations (Supplementary Figs. 5, 6). Rapid solvation markedly weakens the electrostatic interactions between the cations and the substrate, thereby initiating vdW exfoliation of the Pt film. Finally, the dynamic AIMD results agree well with the static DFT calculations, further corroborating the mechanism of water-assisted ionic unlocking.

The vdW exfoliation process is experimentally illustrated in Fig. 1f–h, Supplementary Video 3. Initially, as depicted in Fig. 1f, the as-prepared film sample is positioned in close proximity to the water surface. Upon its gradual approach towards the water surface, capillary forces initiate the exfoliation process at one corner of the film. Subsequently, as illustrated in Fig. 1g, the advancing water front progressively infiltrates the film-substrate interface. The water-assisted exfoliation in the PDMS/PZT/Pt/Mica structure proceeds through a sequential cooperative mechanism. First, water accesses and penetrates the exposed edge of the Pt/Mica interface. During this process, water induces potassium ion leaching from the Mica substrate, thereby weakening the local Pt/Mica interfacial bonding and creating a weakened interface for subsequent capillary-assisted crack propagation. As water further advances along the weakened interface, the capillary driving force generated by the confined water wedge promotes crack propagation along the Pt/Mica interface. In contrast, the residual tensile stress stored in the sol-gel PZT film provides only a minor secondary contribution and is insufficient to drive exfoliation independently. A further quantitative comparison of these contributions is provided in Supplementary Note 4. After exfoliation, the freestanding film floats on the water surface, while the Mica substrate sinks (Fig. 1h), and the water surface serves as a non-constraining support that promotes stress relaxation and reduces handling-induced damage.

Microstructural characterization and macroscopic piezoelectric properties of PZT films

The successful fabrication of high-performance fPAS devices critically depends on preserving the structural integrity of PZT films throughout the exfoliation process. Accordingly, a comprehensive suite of characterizations was performed on the PZT film before and after the exfoliation process. The X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern, presented in Fig. 2a, confirms that the freestanding PZT film maintains a pure polycrystalline perovskite structure. Furthermore, comparative Raman spectra (Supplementary Fig. 8) exhibit consistent phonon modes and relative intensities, substantiating the preservation of the phase structure and the absence of transfer-induced phase transitions. Cross-sectional scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of the PZT film on Mica (Supplementary Fig. 9) and the freestanding PZT film (Fig. 2b) reveal a uniform film thickness of approximately 1.6 µm. The PZT films exhibit a columnar growth mode on both Mica and PET substrates, featuring a dense and crack-free morphology. Furthermore, atomic force microscopy (AFM) shows that the freestanding PZT film retains a smooth surface comparable to the PZT film on Mica (Supplementary Fig. 10). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) indicates no significant shifts in elemental binding energies, suggesting minimal chemical damage during the process (Supplementary Fig. 11). The observed film integrity further confirms that the exfoliation process induces negligible damage to the PZT film, which is essential for retaining its intrinsic electrical properties.

To evaluate the resulting electrical performance, the piezoelectric and ferroelectric properties of the freestanding PZT film were characterized and compared with the PZT film on Mica using piezoresponse force microscopy (PFM) and polarization-electric field (P-E) hysteresis loop measurements. The freestanding PZT film demonstrates excellent microscale ferroelectric switching (Supplementary Fig. 12) and a significantly stronger local piezoelectric effect, evidenced by the larger amplitude in its PFM butterfly loop (Fig. 2c). The enhancement is further confirmed on a macroscopic scale by P–E loop measurements (Fig. 2d), where the remanent polarization (Pr) increased from 31.1 μC cm−2 for the clamped film to 35.5 μC cm−2 for the freestanding film, with an analogous increase in saturation polarization (Ps). Frequency-dependent dielectric characterization was conducted under DC bias to investigate the physical origin of the piezoelectric performance improvement. The freestanding film exhibits substantially higher dielectric response in both low-field and high-field regimes relative to the substrate-clamped film (Supplementary Fig. 13). The enhanced low-field dielectric constant indicates increased extrinsic contributions arising from domain-wall motion after release of substrate constraint, while the higher high-field response suggests an increased intrinsic lattice contribution, thereby leading to the observed improved piezoelectric and ferroelectric performance.

Furthermore, a pronounced (100) crystallographic texturing of approximately 95% was observed in the PZT film both before and after exfoliation, as evidenced by the XRD patterns. The strong texturing is likely attributed to the favorable growth kinetics of the (100)-oriented crystallographic planes29. The preservation of this preferred orientation is further corroborated by transmission Kikuchi diffraction (TKD) analysis, which provides superior spatial resolution for thin-film characterization. The inverse pole figure (IPF) map (Fig. 2e) directly visualizes this predominant (100) grain orientation. Additional microstructural data, including grain boundary character, phase distribution, and pole figures, are provided in Supplementary Fig. 14. The combined assessments conclusively reveal a highly (100)-textured microstructure, a key factor for enhancing piezoelectric performance in PZT films near the morphotropic phase boundary (MPB). Such a strong (100) orientation facilitates non-180° domain wall motion, enabling more efficient electromechanical energy conversion30. Another contribution to the high piezoelectricity is the characterized MPB composition of the film. The compositional nature of the MPB is evidenced by the coexistence of tetragonal (T) and rhombohedral (R) domains observed in the local polar vector mapping extracted from atomic-resolution high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) analysis (Fig. 2f, Supplementary Fig. 15). T- and R-phase domains possess respective polar vectors close to <001> and <111> directions, as determined from the relative displacements of B-site cations (Zr and Ti) with respect to the center of the nearest A-site (Pb) cations, respectively (Fig. 2g). Moreover, a displacement amplitude histogram (Fig. 2h) indicates an average cation displacement within the PZT film of approximately 19.1 pm, consistent with previous reports31.

To elucidate the impact of substrate removal on piezoelectric performance, the dynamic response characteristics of PZT films on Mica were compared with those of freestanding PZT films using a custom-built testing platform32, as illustrated in Fig. 3a. The system integrates a piezoelectric actuator to generate a dynamic mechanical load (10 mN to 110 mN force at 1 Hz, with a representative profile shown in Supplementary Fig. 16), a calibrated force sensor for load measurement, a conductive ITO/PET electrode layer, and a rigid mounting stage for stable mechanical contact. Notably, the film was tested on a thick and rigid stage to ensure a quasi-uniaxial stress state and minimize contributions from the transverse piezoelectric effect (d31) that could arise from substrate bending. Dynamic tests involved measuring the output current for PZT films on Mica and freestanding PZT films (Fig. 3b). A near-linear increase in output current density with dynamic force up to 110 mN was observed, reaching approximately 5.75 nA cm−2 and 16.17 nA cm−2 for the clamped and freestanding PZT films, respectively. The approximately 2.8-fold increase in output current density for the freestanding film compared to the clamped film on Mica indicates a significant enhancement in electromechanical coupling. Moreover, charge can be calculated by integrating the current over time, and plotted as a function of applied force to extract piezoelectric coefficients d33,d via linear regression (Fig. 3c). The PZT film on Mica exhibited an average d33,d of (217.44 ± 11.59) pC N−1, consistent with reported values for PZT on rigid substrates (150–300 pC N−1)33,34. In contrast, the freestanding PZT film demonstrated a markedly higher d33,d of (527.88 ± 22.63) pC N−1, which we attribute to the significant reduction in substrate-induced mechanical clamping that enables more efficient strain-to-charge conversion under identical loading conditions. Furthermore, the PZT films demonstrate excellent stability, where the d33,d exhibits a slight enhancement to 559 pC N−1 at 60 °C due to increased domain-wall mobility, while remaining stable at 521 pC N−1 under 80% relative humidity (Supplementary Fig. 17). To further corroborate the direct piezoelectric measurement results, spatially resolved laser scanning vibrometry (LSV) was employed to evaluate the converse piezoelectric coefficient (d33,c). LSV is regarded as a reliable technique for piezoelectric films with substrate constraint because it can capture the top-surface displacement distribution over a large area, allowing bending-related deformation and edge effects to be identified and considered in the analysis35–37. According to the areal LSV methodology described in Supplementary Note 5, the top-surface response was corrected by considering the displacement dent caused by substrate or membrane bending. As shown in Fig. 3d, e, the PZT film on Mica exhibits a smooth and localized displacement distribution with no obvious edge effect, whereas the freestanding PZT film displays a much larger displacement together with a pronounced edge effect and a local displacement dent, indicating stronger membrane deformation under reduced mechanical constraint. The corresponding surface displacement curves extracted from the three-dimensional LSV profiles are provided in Supplementary Fig. 18. The corrected d33,c values are ∼225 pm V−1 for PZT film on Mica and 598 pm V−1 for the freestanding PZT film, consistent with the trend obtained from the direct piezoelectric measurements. The agreement between the d33,d obtained from direct piezoelectric measurements and d33,c obtained from LSV measurements provides an independent validation of the measured piezoelectric response and supports the reliability of the extracted coefficients. The obtained d33,d and d33,c of the freestanding PZT film surpasses that of previously reported flexible piezoelectric films (Fig. 3f)25,27,33,38–41.

Additionally, the output voltage was measured simultaneously. As with the current response, the freestanding PZT film demonstrated superior output voltage performance. Under an identical dynamic load of 110 mN, the freestanding film generated an output voltage of 0.67 V, substantially exceeding the 0.21 V produced by the PZT film on Mica substrate (Supplementary Fig. 19a, c). Both films also exhibited a highly linear output voltage response in the tested force range (Supplementary Fig. 19b, d). Consequently, these results indicate not only the enhanced piezoelectric performance of freestanding PZT film but also its consistency across a wide force range, confirming its superior sensitivity for acoustic sensing applications. To quantitatively elucidate the mechanical mechanisms underlying the observed performance enhancement, finite element method (FEM) analysis was conducted to evaluate the stress, strain, and electrical output parameters of the PZT films on Mica and freestanding PZT films. The simulation results revealed that freestanding PZT films exhibit significantly enhanced effective stress transmission and deformation compared with its substrate-clamped counterpart. The consequent augmented mechanical stimulus facilitates more efficient energy conversion, thereby generating a higher piezoelectric potential consistent with the experimental observations (detailed numerical comparisons are provided in Supplementary Fig. 20, Supplementary Table 4).

Acoustic sensing performance and speech recognition application of the fPAS device

Accurate speech recognition is contingent upon capturing the distinct phonemes that form the basis of speech. As these phonemes are characterized by specific frequency ranges (e.g., vowels ~2 kHz, consonants ~4 kHz), acoustic sensors must provide comprehensive spectral coverage (up to approximately 4 kHz). To characterize the resonant-mode behavior of the fPAS device, FEM simulations were performed, which revealed multiple distinct resonant modes ranging from the fundamental mode at 691 Hz up to 3096 Hz (Supplementary Fig. 21). The existence of these successive resonant modes suggests that the operational principle of the device provides sufficient spectral coverage for high-fidelity speech recognition. For experimental validation, the frequency was swept using the setup shown in Fig. 4a while maintaining a constant sound pressure level of 94 dB (equivalent to a pressure of 1 Pa). As depicted in Fig. 4b, the fPAS device maintained a consistent voltage output over a wide frequency spectrum from 100 Hz to 3600 Hz, with a pronounced sensitivity peak of 15.15 mV observed at 600 Hz, a frequency identified as the experimental resonance of fPAS. The experimental resonant frequencies were quantitatively compared with the simulated values to evaluate the model accuracy. The fundamental resonance measured at 600 Hz corresponds to the simulated first mode at 691 Hz, showing a relative deviation of approximately 13%. Furthermore, the higher-order experimental peaks observed at approximately 1.2 kHz, 2.0 kHz, and 2.8 kHz show good consistency with the simulated second (1249 Hz), third (1982 Hz), and fourth (3096 Hz) resonant modes, respectively. The relative deviations for these higher-order modes are maintained within the range of 5% to 10%. The observed frequency discrepancies are potentially associated with the idealization of boundary conditions as fully clamped, which may differ from the actual device damping. Comprehensive details regarding these simulation parameters have been provided in Supplementary Note 6, Supplementary Table 3. A fast Fourier transform (FFT)-based band-pass filter (590–610 Hz) was employed to effectively isolate the fundamental frequency and correct for signal asymmetry caused by harmonic distortions (Supplementary Fig. 22). Furthermore, the device also demonstrated excellent high-frequency fidelity, accurately reproducing sine waveforms up to 3000 Hz (Supplementary Fig. 23). The high-frequency capability, combined with the established operational bandwidth of 100–3600 Hz, confirms the suitability of the fPAS for covering the major frequency spectrum encountered in human speech environments.

The relationship between the output voltage of the fPAS device and the sound pressure level (SPL) was characterized at an incident frequency of 600 Hz. The output voltage of the fPAS device exhibits a distinct and positive correlation with SPL, increasing from 4.81 mV to 24.22 mV as the SPL rises from 70 dB to 100 dB (Fig. 4c). The observed direct correlation indicates that greater sound pressure effectively induces higher mechanical vibration amplitude in the PZT film, thereby generating a higher output voltage. The fPAS device exhibited an excellent frequency-resolving capability, distinguishing between frequencies just 1 Hz apart, as confirmed by FFT analysis (Fig. 4d). Beyond high-fidelity frequency detection, a critical attribute for practical acoustic sensing is the detection distance. Given that sound pressure attenuates with distance, which theoretically corresponds to a 6 dB SPL decrease for every doubling of the distance (Supplementary Note 7), a sensor requires sufficiently high sensitivity and SNR to detect weaker far-field signals. At its first resonance mode (600 Hz), the fPAS device exhibits an exceptional relative sensitivity of −39.39 dBV and an SNR of 63 dBV (Fig. 4e). The resulting performance metrics are not only high in absolute terms but also significantly outperform many commercial microelectromechanical system (MEMS) microphones, addressing common instability issues tied to noise amplification in such systems12. The practical benefit of the high sensitivity is evident in the far-field performance of the fPAS. With the sound source distance increasing from 5 cm to 25 cm, the SPL decreases from 94 dB to 80 dB, and the device maintains a robust output, with the RMS voltage decreasing from −39.39 dBV to −44.66 dBV, indicating a strong capability for far-field speech recognition (Fig. 4f).

The fPAS achieved an outstanding FOMSens. of 45.21 mV Pa−1·mm−3, placing it ahead of previously reported miniaturized piezoelectric sensors8,42–46, as seen in Fig. 4g. To assess the long-term operational stability of the fPAS, an accelerated aging test was performed at an SPL of 94 dB for 100 min. The excitation frequency was fixed at the fPAS resonant frequency of 600 Hz to induce maximal membrane vibration amplitude and mechanical stress. Based on acoustic intensity scaling, the applied high-intensity exposure corresponds, in terms of accumulated acoustic energy dose, to approximately 4200 h of continuous operation at a typical everyday SPL of 60 dB, as detailed in Supplementary Note 8. Stable performance was maintained throughout the testing period, as presented in Fig. 4h, thereby confirming device reliability for extended daily applications. In addition to acoustic durability, the structural integrity and operational stability were further evaluated using high-cycle vibration fatigue and thermal cycling tests (Supplementary Fig. 24). The consistent acoustic outputs and SNR recorded after the applied stress verify the robustness of the device against mechanical fatigue and environmental fluctuations.

The exceptional sensitivity and broad frequency coverage of the fPAS device make it ideal for advanced HMI systems. To realize a complete system for speech recording and recognition, the sensor was integrated with a deep-learning framework for speech recognition (Fig. 5a). Accurate speech recognition necessitates that the acoustic features captured by an sensor accurately replicate those of the raw audio signal. The recording fidelity of the fPAS was first validated by reproducing individually articulated letters, which demonstrated excellent performance (Supplementary Fig. 25). For a more quantitative assessment, a 5 s audio clip from the news broadcast was recorded with the fPAS. Analysis in both the time domain (Fig. 5b) and frequency domain (Fig. 5c) revealed a high degree of correspondence between the fPAS recording and the raw audio signal, confirming the excellent recording accuracy of the fPAS device. For integration with the deep-learning model, Mel-frequency cepstral coefficient (MFCC) features, which provide a perceptually relevant representation of sound5,47, were extracted from the raw audio and fPAS recording signals (Fig. 5d). A quantitative analysis confirmed that the time-varying characteristics of the MFCC features from the fPAS signal closely matched those from the raw audio, affirming the excellent performance of the device in speech recording. As seen in the processing pipeline of speech recognition (Fig. 5e), high-fidelity MFCC features were first extracted from the fPAS recording signal and then used as input for a convolutional neural network (CNN), which classifies the speech by integrating learned acoustic patterns.

To validate the feasibility of the fPAS device for speech-recognition applications, a deep learning model was developed using a dataset comprising eight distinct spoken words. The time-domain signals and corresponding acoustic feature maps of these words are presented in Supplementary Fig. 26. During 100 training epochs, the model exhibited stable learning behavior, as evidenced by the gradual increase in training and validation accuracies and the concurrent decrease in loss values (Fig. 5f). The confusion matrix in Fig. 5g shows that the CNN model achieved an overall classification accuracy of 93.3% for the eight-word recognition task. These results indicate that the fPAS-recorded signals contain distinguishable word-dependent acoustic features that can be learned by the CNN model. As a proof-of-concept demonstration, the integrated system successfully performed real-time speech-to-text conversion among the eight predefined words (Fig. 5h, Supplementary Video 4). The demonstrated recognition capability highlights the potential of the fPAS device for HMI applications, including wearable microphones, intelligent voice authentication, and acoustic sensing interfaces.

Here, we report a water-assisted vdW exfoliation strategy that enables the fabrication of high-performance, freestanding ferroelectric membranes for an advanced acoustic sensing system. The key contribution is the elucidation of an ‘ionic unlocking’ mechanism, wherein combined DFT and AIMD simulations reveal that the spontaneous solvation of potassium ions at the Mica/Pt interface disrupts the interfacial vdW forces and electrostatic interactions, thereby facilitating exfoliation. This mechanism provides a cost-effective and scalable strategy for producing high-quality freestanding PZT films while maintaining their structural integrity throughout the exfoliation process. Critically, the resulting freestanding PZT films exhibit a remarkably enhanced d33,d of ~528 pC N−1, more than double that of PZT films on Mica (~217 pC N−1), attributable to the elimination of the substrate clamping effect. The enhanced piezoelectricity endows the fPAS device with exceptional acoustic sensing capabilities, including high sensitivity, an outstanding SNR of 63 dB, and a broad frequency response range of 100–3600 Hz, effectively covering the primary human speech spectrum. Moreover, the successful application of these sensors in sound recording and high-accuracy speech recognition, achieving a 93.3% accuracy rate through deep learning–based speech processing, demonstrates their practical value in HMI scenarios.

Methods

Preparation of freestanding PZT films

The PZT films were prepared via a sol-gel method. The process began with the preparation of a 0.4 M precursor solution at a Zr/Ti molar ratio of 52:48. First, lead acetate trihydrate was dissolved in 2-methoxyethanol and magnetically stirred at 100 °C for 30 min, after which the solution was allowed to cool to room temperature. Subsequently, zirconium propoxide and titanium isopropoxide were incorporated into the lead-based solution. Formamide and acetic acid were then added as stabilizing and anti-cracking agents. After aging for 48 h, the final precursor solution was used for film deposition. The solution was spin-coated onto Mica substrates, with each layer undergoing a crystallization step via rapid thermal annealing (RTA). This deposition and annealing cycle was repeated until a final film thickness of approximately 1.6 µm was achieved.

To prepare the freestanding device, the as-grown PZT film was first encapsulated with a PDMS (Sylgard 184) protective layer, which was spin-coated and cured at 60 °C for 24 h. The vdW exfoliation process was then used to transfer the PDMS/PZT/Pt multilayer from the rigid Mica substrate by immersing the entire PDMS/PZT/Pt/Mica stack in deionized water. Following exfoliation, the freestanding film was dried in air at 60 °C for 60 min. The resulting freestanding film was then transferred onto a flexible PET substrate using an ultraviolet (UV)-curable adhesive to ensure conformal bonding. Finally, mechanical peeling was performed to remove the PDMS protective layer, which yielded the final PZT film on the flexible substrate.

Characterization of PZT films

The crystallographic phase structure of the PZT films was analyzed by XRD (Bruker D8) using Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å) over a 2θ range of 20°–60°. Surface morphology and microstructure were characterized using AFM (Bruker MultiMode 8) and field-emission SEM (Zeiss Merlin), respectively. For high-resolution analysis, TKD was performed in a Zeiss SEM equipped with a Nordlys EBSD camera (Oxford Instruments) at an accelerating voltage of 30 kV. Samples for TKD were prepared by focused ion beam (FIB) milling (Helios G4 PFIB CXe), which involved a stepwise reduction in ion beam current from 15 nA down to 0.1 nA at 30 kV. The resulting TKD patterns were processed using Oxford Instruments AZtec software. Atomic-resolution imaging and elemental analysis were conducted using STEM. Lamellae were extracted from the PZT films using a Zeiss Auriga FIB and investigated with a probe-aberration-corrected FEI Titan Cubed Themis G2 300 microscope operating at 300 kV. HAADF imaging was performed to obtain atomic number (Z) contrast, using a probe convergence angle of 25 mrad and collection angles from approximately 80–240 mrad.

For electrical measurements, circular Au top electrodes (150 µm diameter) were sputtered onto the PZT film surface. Ferroelectric hysteresis loops were measured at 1 Hz using a Radiant Technologies ferroelectric test system. Local piezoresponse was characterized using PFM mode on the Bruker Multimode 8 AFM. To measure the macroscopic piezoelectric performance, the PZT films were first poled under a 100 kV cm−1 electric field for 30 min using a high-voltage power supply (MPD-10 kV, Partulab Technology). The piezoelectric response was subsequently evaluated using a custom dynamic testing platform. Applied force was measured with a capacitive pressure sensor, while the resulting piezoelectric current (Isc) was recorded with a source meter (Keithley 2635A). Circular top electrodes with a diameter of 500 μm were defined to reduce substrate bending effects. Calculation methods

All first-principles calculations were performed using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP), within the framework of DFT49. The projector-augmented wave (PAW) method and the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) exchange-correlation functional were adopted50, with a plane-wave cutoff energy of 500 eV. Both lattice constants and atomic positions were fully optimized until the residual forces on each atom were less than 0.01 eV Å, and the total energy change between ionic steps was below 1 × 10−5 eV. To eliminate spurious interactions between periodic images, a vacuum layer of 20 Å was introduced along the out-of-plane direction. The vdW interactions were treated using the DFT-D3 method51. Because the hexagonal surface lattices of Mica (a = b = 5.33 Å; α = β = 90°, γ = 120°) and Pt(111) (a = b = 5.55 Å; α = β = 90°, γ = 120°) are nearly commensurate (mismatch ~ 4%), we built two interfacial models for detailed analysis (Supplementary Fig. 1). Such a modest misfit can be elastically accommodated by both lattices, making the models a realistic representation of the experimental interface. The Mica/Pt structure (Supplementary Fig. 1a), representing the relaxed interface prior to water adsorption, contains 35 atoms, including Pt (12), O (12), Si (3), Mg (3), Al (1), H (2), and K (2). The Mica/H2O/Pt structure (Supplementary Fig. 1b), representing the interface after water adsorption, contains 41 atoms, including Pt (12), O (14), Si (3), Mg (3), Al (1), H (6), and K (2). To investigate the vdW-driven interfacial delamination process, AIMD simulations52 were performed on a 3 × 3 × 1 Mica/H2O/Pt supercell consisting of 164 atoms. The simulations were performed using VASP under the Born–Oppenheimer approximation53, with a Langevin thermostat applied to maintain the temperature at 300 K. A time step of 0.5 fs was used for 1000 steps, corresponding to a total simulation time of 0.5 ps.

Measurement of the electrical signals

Acoustic signals were generated using the Audio Tester software to drive a commercial loudspeaker via a power amplifier (SAST, SA-9006). The SPL was calibrated using a commercial sound level meter (Smart Sensor, AR824). The resulting electrical signals from the fPAS device were captured using a National Instruments data acquisition card (NI USB-6210). A custom LabVIEW-based software platform was developed for real-time data acquisition and noise filtering. Subsequent FFT analysis and MFCC feature extraction were performed on the recorded audio using the librosa package in Python. Additionally, COMSOL Multiphysics 6.1 was employed to simulate the vibrational modes of the fPAS device.

Deep learning for speech recognition

For the speech recognition task, a dataset was created by recording eight distinct words with the fPAS device. With 100 samples collected for each word, the dataset comprised a total of 800 samples. The data were subsequently partitioned into training, validation, and testing sets. A CNN was trained for 100 epochs to perform the classification. Model performance was monitored throughout the training process by evaluating the classification accuracy and loss function on both the training and validation sets to assess effective learning and preventing overfitting.
