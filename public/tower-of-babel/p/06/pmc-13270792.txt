NSUN6 overexpression promotes malignant phenotypes and predicts poor prognosis in clear cell renal cell carcinoma
Authors: Can Chen, Shi-Yi Pan, Jia Xu, Xinyuan Cao, Wei-Ming Zhang, Hua-Guo Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundClear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) lacks early clinical diagnostic markers, and advanced ccRCC has a poor prognosis. NSUN6, as a key component of m5C methyl-modifying enzymes, is abnormally expressed in several tumours and correlates with the prognosis. However, the expression and functional role of NSUN6 in ccRCC remain unknown.MethodsNSUN6 expression was analysed using TCGA data, RT-qPCR, and Western blot. In vitro migration and invasion assays were used to assess the effect of NSUN6 on the malignant function of ccRCC cells. ssGSEA, ESTIMATE, CIBERDORT, and TIMER databases were used to study the immune cell infiltration of NSUN6 in ccRCC. TIDE database, IPS scores, and GDSC database were used to assess the different NSUN6-expressing groups’ drug therapeutic effects.ResultsBioinformatics and clinical experiments demonstrated that the mRNA and protein expression of NSUN6 was significantly higher in ccRCC than in normal tissues. NSUN6 was an independent prognostic factor for ccRCC patients, and its high expression was significantly correlated with overall survival and disease-specific survival. In vitro experiments showed that NSUN6 promotes migration and invasion of ccRCC cells. NSUN6 was associated with immune infiltration of ccRCC and might affect the efficacy of immune and targeted therapies in patients.ConclusionHigh levels of NSUN6 were associated with poor prognosis in ccRCC and might promote tumour cell invasion and migration. In addition, NSUN6 played an important role in influencing the efficacy of tumour therapy. NSUN6 might further serve as a potential prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12935-026-04259-6.Keywords: ccRCC, NSUN6, prognosis, immune infiltration, drug sensitivity

Introduction

Clear-cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) is a malignant tumour derived from renal tubular epithelial cell carcinoma with heterogeneous morphology. Its pathological features show that the tumour cells have mainly clear and occasionally eosinophilic cytoplasm, accompanied by a rich vascular network [1]. As the most common renal malignant tumour subtype, ccRCC accounts for 75% to 80% of renal cancer pathologic types [2]. Early-stage cancers have low malignancy and slow tumour growth, and patients have the highest early survival rate compared to other renal cell carcinomas. However, the lack of early clinical diagnostic markers and the low sensitivity of renal clear cell carcinoma to both chemotherapy and radiotherapy have prevented some patients from receiving early radical treatment [3, 4]. At the same time, there are not many targeted drugs for advanced tumours and fewer choices. For this reason, advanced renal clear cell carcinoma has a poor prognosis and is prone to metastasis and recurrence [5]. Therefore, the molecular mechanism of renal cancer progression has become a key issue to be solved in clinical diagnosis and treatment [6–8].

5-Methylcytosine (m5C) modifications are abundant and highly dynamic internal modifications in RNA. m5C methylation modification of RNA, which refers to the methylation of the fifth carbon atom of RNA cytosine, is a widespread RNA modification that can be detected in both mRNA and non-coding RNAs [9]. m5C modification is dynamically reversible, and the process involves three main types of effectors, including methyltransferases, methylated reading proteins, and demethylases [10]. m5C is involved in immunity, tumour proliferation and metastasis, stem cell renewal, and adipose differentiation by regulating the stability, expression, and translation of mRNAs [11, 12]. NSUN6, a methyltransferase primarily responsible for the methylation modification of mRNA m5C, is aberrantly expressed or mutated and leads to the development of many diseases and cancer generation [13, 14]. It has been shown that NSUN6 can inactivate the macrophage stimulating 1 (MST1) gene through m5C modification and is involved in the regulation of the Hippo-YAP pathway that triggers osteoclast differentiation and tumour bone metastasis [15]. In pancreatic cancer, NSUN6 inhibits tumour cell proliferation and differentiation both in vivo and ex vivo, and can be used as a predictor of tumour recurrence as well as a clinical characterization of patient survival [16]. However, studies of m5C in ccRCC have focused on the prognostic modelling of the entire m5C family in tumours, and the expression and prognosis of individual NSUN6 in ccRCC remained unknown.

In this study, we first determined that NSUN6 expression was significantly increased in ccRCC by The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database and related experiments and evaluated the diagnostic and prognostic value of NSUN6 in ccRCC to determine its correlation with clinicopathologic parameters. Second, we found that NSUN6 promoted migration and invasion of ccRCC cells. In addition, we analysed the correlation between NSUN6 and tumour immune infiltration as well as the responsiveness to immune and targeted therapies in patients with different GPR176 expression levels. In conclusion, we provided the basis for the potential mechanism of NSUN6’s role in the diagnosis and prognosis of ccRCC, and provided new potential tumour markers for future clinical treatment and prognostic diagnosis of ccRCC patients.

Methods and materials

Data collection and organization of public databases

The Cancer Genome Atlas Program (TCGA) database contains 535 ccRCC and 72 paraneoplastic tissue samples for which RNA-seq expression data and clinical information were obtained from the UCSC XENA website (https://xena.ucsc.edu/) [17]. Pan-cancer information analysis was obtained from the GEPIA2 online database (http://gepia2.cancer-pku.cn/#index) [18]. Additionally, by searching the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database for “clear cell renal cell carcinoma,” we obtained the gene expression profile dataset GSE29609 from patients with ccRCC. GSE29609 dataset includes sequencing data and clinical prognosis information for 39 ccRCC patients, and the microarray platform was GPL1708. The sequencing data was standardized using the R 4.4.3 software for subsequent analysis.

Cell culture and transfection

Human renal cortical proximal tubule epithelial cells HK-2, human renal clear cell adenocarcinoma cells 786-O, human renal cell adenocarcinoma cells 769-P, human renal clear cell carcinoma skin metastasis cells Caki-1, and human renal cell adenocarcinoma cells ACHN are kept in our laboratory. All cells were cultured using 10% cell culture complete medium in a cell culture incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Media used included 1640 (786-O, 769-P cells), McCoy’s 5 A (Caki-1 cells), DMEM/F-12 (HK-2 cells), and MEM (ACHN cells) media. Lipo2000 was used to transfect siRNA into cells.

Tumour pathology tissue samples

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital with Nanjing Medical University, and the inclusion criteria for clinical samples included: patients with primary renal clear cell carcinoma diagnosed at the beginning of the study, who had not been exposed to any radiotherapy or immunotherapy before surgery; pathologically confirmed diagnosis; and no history of other serious diseases, malignant tumours or infectious diseases. Eight samples of surgically resected renal clear cell carcinoma and para-cancerous tissues that met the inclusion criteria were collected, rapidly transported under cryogenic conditions and frozen in liquid nitrogen.

Immunohistochemistry

Paraffin-embedded samples were cured at 70 °C, dewaxed with xylene, and dehydrated through an ethanol gradient. Following antigen retrieval, sections were blocked with goat serum and incubated with NSUN6 primary antibody, followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibody. Staining was developed using DAB, with hematoxylin counterstaining. After final dehydration and mounting, samples were visualized by light microscopy.

Wound healing and transwell experiments

Scratch assay was used to detect cell migration. A sterile 200 µl pipette tip was used to scratch lines in the cell well plates of NSUN6-treated cells experiments and controls, and photographs were taken under microscopic observation for 24–48 h. Transwell assay was used to detect cell migration and invasion. 500 µl of medium for normal culture was placed in a 24-well plate, and 5 × 104 cells were resuspended in 200 µl of pure medium within the chamber, and after a period, the number of cells adhering to the bottom of the chamber was counted to reflect the migratory ability of tumour cells. Spread 60 µl of matrix gel (matrix gel: medium 1:8) in the chamber, and let it stand in the incubator for 1 h, after the gel solidified, resuspend 8 × 104 cells with 200 µl pure medium in the chamber, and after a period of time, count the number of cells adhering to the bottom of the chamber to reflect the invasive ability of the tumour cells.

Real‑time quantitative PCR

TRIZOL reagent (Invitrogen, USA) was used to extract RNA from renal cancer cell lines. HiScript III RT SuperMix (vazyme, China) was used to reverse transcribe RNA to form cDNA. ChamQ SYBR Color qPCR Master Mix was used to prepare the real-time quantitative reverse transcription. The mixture was used to determine the number of added components according to the manufacturer’s protocol for real-time fluorescent reverse transcription on an oche LightCycler 96 instrument. Primer sequences used in this study were NSUN6-F: ATGCCCCAGGAATTGTGTCAG; NSUN6-R: TCTTATGCCCATGCCTTTCAG; Actin-F: GAGCTACGAGCTGCCTGACG; Actin-R: GTAGTTTCGTGGATGCCACAG. Three replicates were performed for each sample.

Western blot

SDS, RIPA buffer, serine enzyme inhibitor, phosphatase inhibitor and protease inhibitor were mixed according to the reagent vendor’s ratio, and tissues and cells were added to the mixed protein lysate, after lysing the proteins on ice for 30 min and centrifuging at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C to collect the protein supernatant. After determining the protein concentration, samples were electrophoresed on a 10% SDS-PAGE gel and then electro-transferred to a 0.45 μm polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane (Millipore, USA). After the membranes were closed by shaking with 5% skimmed milk for 1 h at room temperature, the membranes were incubated with NSUN6 antibody (abcam, UK) and GAPDH antibody (beyotime, China) at 4 °C overnight. On the next day, after washing the membrane three times with TBST, the membrane was incubated with horseradish peroxidase-coupled secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. Proteins were colorimetrically imaged using chemiluminescent (Millipore, USA) substrates. Three replicates were performed for each sample.

Tumour immune infiltration analysis

Single sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) normalizes gene expression profiles within a sample and then calculates the corresponding ssGSEA score for each gene set [19, 20]. In this study, based on 29 immune gene sets including immune cell types, functions and pathways [21], we used the ssGSEA algorithm to quantify the relative abundance of immune-associated pathways and immune-infiltrating cell subsets for each tumour sample. Estimation of STromal and Immune cells in MAlignant Tumour tissues using Expression data (ESTIMATE) algorithm, which utilizes a pre-screened set of stromal and immune-related genes to predict stromal/immune cell infiltration in tumour tissues using gene expression data levels, which can generate three scores, including stroma score (to assess the level of stromal cell infiltration in tumour tissues), immune score (to assess the level of immune cell infiltration in tumour tissues), and ESTIMATE score (used to infer tumour purity) [22] .The CIBERSORT algorithm de-convolves the expression matrix of immune cell subtypes using the principle of linear support vector regression to estimate the abundance of immune cells [23]. The algorithm provides 22 common immune-infiltrating cell expression data LM22, including different cell types and functional states of immune cells, and the LM22 file is downloaded from the official website (https://cibersortx.stanford.edu/). The TIMER database (https://cistrome.shinyapps.io/timer/) was used to analyse and obtain scatter plots of correlation between NSUN6 expression and selected tumour-infiltrating immune cells [24].

Drug therapy response

The immunophenoscore (IPS) is a predictor of response to antibodies against cytotoxic T-lymphocyte antigen 4 (CTLA-4) and anti-programmed cell death protein 1 (anti-PD-1). The IPS consists of a four-component score: antigen presentation, effector cells, suppressor cells, and checkpoints. The higher the IPS, the greater the immunogenicity of the sample [25] .The Tumour Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE) database evaluates the likelihood of tumour immune escape based on the gene expression profiles of tumour samples, and effectively predicts the effect of immune checkpoint inhibition therapy by comprehensively analysing the immune escape mechanisms of immune dysfunction and rejection [26] .Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) database was used to predict the sensitivity and response to drugs in tumour patients [27]. Half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) was quantified by the pRRophetic package in R. The IC50 was utilized to measure sensitivity to drugs and to compare the differences in IC50 of chemotherapeutic drugs among different groups.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9 and SPSS 22.0, and t-tests were used to compare the experimental data between groups. The “ggpubr” R package was used to analyse NSUN6 expression in renal cell carcinoma, the “survival” and “survminer” R packages were used for visualization of survival analysis, the “pROC” R package performs univariate ROC analysis to assess the predictive accuracy of NSUN6 in distinguishing tumor from normal tissue, the “plyr”, “ggplot2 The “plyr”, “ggplot2” and “ggpubr” R packages analysed the correlation between NSUN6 and clinicopathological characteristics, and univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were used to assess the influence of single or multiple factors on patient survival. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. One asterisk denoting p < 0.05, two asterisks denoting p < 0.01, and three asterisks denoting p < 0.001.

Results

NSUN6 expression is increased in ccRCC

First, we analysed NSUN6 expression in pan-cancer, as seen in Fig. 1A, NSUN6 was significantly aberrantly expressed in several tumours, including Head and Neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSC), Kidney Chromophobe (KICH), Kidney renal clear cell carcinoma (KIRC), Kidney renal papillary cell carcinoma (KIRP), etc., which suggested that NSUN6 played a critical role in carcinogenesis. Among them, the expression level of NSUN6 in ccRCC tissues was significantly higher than that in normal tissues (P < 0.001, Fig. 1B). The analysis of the subjects’ working characteristic curve (ROC) showed that the area under the curve (AUC) of NSUN6 was 0.691, which indicated that it had good diagnostic effect in ccRCC and could accurately distinguish normal and tumour tissues (Fig. 1C).

To verify the expression of NSUN6, we examined the mRNA and protein expression levels of NSUN6 in normal kidney cells HK-2 and four different renal cell carcinoma cell lines, 786-O, 769-P, Caki-1 and ACHN, by RT-qPCR technique and Western blot. The results showed that the mRNA and protein expression levels of NSUN6 were significantly increased in renal cell carcinoma cells (Fig. 1D-F). Subsequently, we examined NSUN6 protein expression in four pairs of clinical renal clear cell carcinoma tissue samples collected. Again, we observed higher protein expression of NSUN6 in renal clear cell carcinoma tissues than in normal tissues (Fig. 1G-H). Then, we assessed NSUN6 expression in renal clear cell carcinoma tissues and adjacent tissues using immunohistochemistry. The results showed that NSUN6 was highly expressed in renal clear cell carcinoma tissues (Fig. 1I).

Correlation between NSUN6 and ccRCC clinicopathologic parameters

To investigate whether NSUN6 was associated with patient prognosis, we obtained patient survival data from the TCGA-KIRC database and performed Kaplan-Meier survival analysis. As can be seen from Fig. 2A-B, OS (Overall survival) and DSS (Disease special survival) were worse for high NSUN6 expression compared to low expression (p < 0.05). Furthermore, to further investigate clinical factors associated with survival in patients with ccRCC, subgroup survival analyses were performed, and the results were presented in Supplementary Figure S1. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis revealed that age (≥ 60 years), tumor grade, and TNM stage were significantly associated with overall survival (p < 0.05), whereas gender showed no significant association (p = 0.790).

Correlation between NSUN6 expression and tumor clinical indicators. A-B. Survival curves of patients with renal clear cell carcinoma with different NSUN6 expression levels, A: overall survival curve, B: disease-specific survival curve. C. Multifactorial Cox analysis demonstrating the ability to serve as an independent prognostic factor for ccRCC. D. nomogram visualized the extent to which different clinicopathological indicators affect patient survival. E. ROC analysis in the GSE29609 dataset

To further explore the prognostic value of NSUN6, univariate and multivariate Cox regression analysis were performed. Univariate Cox analysis determined that NSUN6, stage, grade, T-stage and M-stage were all significantly associated with patient survival (Table 1). Multivariate Cox analysis demonstrated that NSUN6, stage and grade were independent prognostic factors for ccRCC (Fig. 2C). In order to demonstrate more intuitively the effects of different clinical indicators on patients’ prognosis, we plotted column line graphs, using line segments with scales and plotted the lines on the same coordinate system according to a certain scale, to visualize the interrelationships among variables(Fig. 2D). In addition, we retrieved and downloaded the GSE29609 dataset, which includes sequencing data and clinical prognosis information from 39 ccRCC patients. In this study, GSE29609 was used as an independent validation cohort to assess the prognostic model, with results presented in Fig. 2E. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) values for 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year overall survival were 0.759, 0.853, and 0.751, respectively, demonstrating consistent predictive performance. In summary, increased expression of NSUN6 in ccRCC was associated with poor prognosis and could serve as a key biomarker for ccRCC patients.

NSUN6 enhances migration and invasion of ccRCC cells

To investigate whether NSUN6 is involved in regulating ccRCC progression, we verified its effect on ccRCC cell migration and invasion ability in vitro. Previous assays showed that NSUN6 expression was significantly increased in 786-O and Caki-1 (Fig. 1D-F). Wound healing and Transwell experiments demonstrated that when NSUN6 was knocked down, the migration and invasion abilities of ccRCC cells were decreased, and when NSUN6 was overexpressed, the migration and invasion abilities were increased (Fig. 3). The above results indicated that NSUN6 could promote the migration and invasion of ccRCC cells.

NSUN6 effects on the ccRCC immune microenvironment

To investigate the correlation between the expression level of NSUN6 and the immune microenvironment, we analysed the proportions of tumour immune cell subpopulations using the CIBERSORT algorithm and constructed a map of 22 immune cell types (Fig. 4A). CIBERSORT analysis showed that naive B cells and M0 macrophages infiltrated high in the low-NSUN6 expression group, while CD8 T cells and M1 macrophages infiltrated more in the high expression group (Fig. 4B). We then evaluated the potential relationship between NSUN6 and the expression levels of ccRCC tumour-infiltrating immune cells using the TIMER database. The results proved that the expression level of NSUN6 was significantly and positively correlated with CD4 + T cells (r = 0.329, P = 4.66e-13) and Neutrophil (r = 0.259, P = 1.78e-08) (Fig. 4C). The immunity scores of ccRCC samples were analysed by the Estimate algorithm, and we found that the low NSUN6-expressing group had higher stromal scores (Fig. 4D). By ssGSEA algorithm, we analysed ccRCC samples using a set of 29 immune genes representing tumour immunity. As can be seen in Fig. 4E, most immune cells and immune functions were significantly higher in the low expression group. These results suggest that the low NSUN6 expression group has a stronger immune microenvironment infiltration profile.

Prediction of response to drug therapy

Next, using the TIDE database, we evaluated the possible responses to immunotherapy in high and low NSUN6 expression groups. The results showed that the low NSUN6 expression group had higher T cell dysfunction scores and rejection scores (Fig. 5A-B). Moreover, we predicted immunotherapy response by IPS score. The results showed that the high NSUN6 group had higher immunogenicity to CTLA4 and PD1 immunotherapy (Fig. 5C). To validate the immunotherapy prediction results, we analyzed the expression differences of multiple immune checkpoint molecules between the high and low NSUN6 expression groups. It was clear that multiple immune checkpoint molecules including PD-1 and CTLA4 were more highly expressed in the high NSUN6 group (Fig. 5D-H). These results suggested that the high NSUN6 expression group had a lower tumor immune escape rate and a more optimistic outcome after receiving immunotherapy, while CTLA4 and PD-1 might be potential immunotherapy targets for patients with high NSUN6 expression. Subsequently, we used the GDSC database to analyze the sensitivity of NSUN6 to targeted drugs. As seen in Fig. 5I-L, the low NSUN6 expression group was more sensitive to Sorafenib, Pazopanib and Temsirolimus with smaller IC50 values, while the high NSUN6 expression group was more sensitive to Nilotinib treatment.

Discussion

Currently, studies have shown that NSUN6 is associated with the treatment and prognosis of tumors such as pancreatic cancer, triple-negative breast cancer, and glioblastoma. Among them, NSUN6 inhibits pancreatic cancer cell proliferation and tumor growth in vitro and in vivo, and its low expression is significantly associated with poor prognosis and tumor proliferation and metastasis in patients [16]. In lung cancer, NSUN6 expression is reduced in tumor cells, and its overexpression inhibits tumor cell proliferation, migration, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition [28]; in bladder cancer, the NSUN6 low-expression group has greater malignant potential, and NSUN6 enhances HDAC10 expression, inhibits macrophage-associated chemokine transcription, and inhibits M2 macrophage recruitment by mediating m5C methylation thereby improving patient survival [29]. These findings reveal a potentially important role for NSUN6 in cancer, suggesting that it may be able to promote malignant progression in a wide range of cancers, and side by side suggesting that NSUN6 may also have an irreplaceable role in ccRCC.

A previous study revealed the prognostic value of m5C effectors including the NSUN family in clear cell renal carcinoma (ccRCC), but did not analyse the potential biological effects of individual genes on tumours [30]. In this study, we first found that NSUN6 expression was significantly higher in renal clear cell carcinoma than in normal tissues by bioinformatics analysis and verified this finding in renal cancer cell lines and clinical renal clear cell carcinoma tissues. Correlation analysis of clinicopathologic parameters showed that high NSUN6 expression was associated with poorer patient survival. Univariate and multifactorial COX analyses demonstrated that NSUN6 could be an independent prognostic factor for ccRCC with good diagnostic and prognostic value. In addition, we plotted column line graphs of NSUN6 and clinicopathological parameters to visualize and demonstrate the prognostic performance of NSUN6 in predicting OS in ccRCC patients. Functional experiments demonstrated that NSUN6 could promote ccRCC cell migration and invasion. The above results suggest that NSUN6 may be a new potential biomarker for the diagnosis and prognosis of ccRCC and may be able to accelerate tumour development, especially tumour metastasis.

The tumour microenvironment is the internal environment in which tumour cells arise and live, including tumour cells and their surrounding fibroblasts, immune and inflammatory cells, and glial cells, as well as intercellular stroma, microvasculature, and biomolecules infiltrated therein, which play an important role in tumour development [31, 32]. In a previous study, we personalized the m5C modification patterns of individual ccRCC patients based on mRNA expression of m5C regulators and found that m5C methylation modifications are critical for regulating ccRCC immune infiltration. Evaluating the m5C modification patterns of individual ccRCC patients may help to fully understand the tumor immune profile and provide effective personalized immunotherapy strategies for clinical use [33]. However, the specific immune infiltration of NSUN6 in ccRCC has not been studied. In this study, results of CIBERSORT revealed that low NSUN6 groups were rich in immature/quiescent immune cells (Naive B cells, M0 macrophages), while high NSUN6 groups were enriched with effector/killer immune cells (CD8 + T cells, M1 macrophages), suggesting differences in the immune environment. Based on the TIMER database, our analysis showed that the expression level of NSUN6 was significantly positively correlated with the infiltration levels of CD4 + T cells and neutrophils. Although TIMER did not detect a statistically significant correlation between NSUN6 expression and bulk CD8 + T cell infiltration, this discrepancy was likely attributable to methodological factors: TIMER quantifies total CD8 + T cells without distinguishing functionally distinct subsets (e.g., activated, or memory phenotypes). Moreover, ssGSEA analysis suggested that the low NSUN6 expression group has a stronger immune microenvironment infiltration profile. Taken together, low NSUN6 expression was associated with broad but could phenotypically immature immune infiltration, whereas high NSUN6 expression correlates with a more cytotoxic tumor microenvironment. In addition, tumour immune microenvironment heterogeneity has been found to be one of the important reasons for drug resistance and recurrence of tumours, and precise elucidation of the heterogeneity of tumour immune microenvironment [34] will help to select tumour therapeutic regimens, monitor therapeutic efficacy and develop new targets for immunotherapy. We comprehensively analysed the effect of NSUN6 on the efficacy of multiple immunotherapeutic and targeted therapeutic agents in this study and found that patients in the high NSUN6 expression group showed higher immunogenicity to CTLA4 and PD1 immunotherapies and may have better efficacy. In contrast, patients in the low NSUN6 expression group were more sensitive to multiple targeted therapeutic agents. These findings provide insight into the role of NSUN6 in ccRCC and provide ideas for future studies.

Meanwhile, through immunological analysis, we found that high expression of NSUN6 is associated with increased infiltration of CD8⁺ T cells, but the prognosis is poor. This may be related to immune exhaustion. T cell exhaustion is a unique dysfunctional state formed under chronic antigen stimulation such as in tumors, leading to progressive loss of effector function, decreased proliferative capacity, and sustained high expression of various inhibitory receptors. This is a dynamic differentiation process, driven by transcriptional regulation, epigenetic modification, and microenvironment signals [35–37]. Therefore, we speculate that overexpression of NSUN6 may accelerate the formation of renal cancer, further leading to increased infiltration of CD8⁺ T cells in the body and accompanied by tumor cell exhaustion. This is an idea worthy of further exploration in future research.

This study convincingly demonstrates that NSUN6 promotes the migration and invasion of cancer cells, a critical finding for understanding its role in metastasis. However, the mechanistic link between NSUN6’s catalytic function and these aggressive phenotypes remain largely unexplored, representing a significant gap. To bridge this gap, future investigations should focus on elucidating the downstream pathways. For instance, given the established role of epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) in cell motility, analyzing the expression of key EMT markers (e.g., E-cadherin, N-cadherin, Vimentin) upon NSUN6 manipulation would be highly informative. Furthermore, the Hippo-YAP signaling pathway, a central regulator of cell proliferation and invasion, presents another compelling candidate, as its activity is often modulated by post-transcriptional mechanisms. Most directly, a comprehensive transcriptomic or epitranscriptomic analysis (e.g., RNA-seq, m5C-seq) comparing NSUN6-knockdown and control cells would provide crucial evidence. Such an approach could systematically identify the specific mRNA targets of NSUN6-mediated methylation whose altered expression or stability drives the observed phenotypic changes, thereby moving the field from correlation to causal biological interpretation.

However, our study preliminarily demonstrated that NSUN6 can be used as a potential clinical marker for ccRCC diagnosis, prognosis and prediction of therapeutic efficacy, but there are still some limitations of the current study. First, our clinical sample size was small, and a sufficiently large number of clinical samples is essential to better utilize the clinical value of NSUN6 in ccRCC. Second, liquid biopsy, as a biomarker detection technique that can be repeated, multiplexed, and non-invasive, plays an irreplaceable role in the field of tumour diagnosis. If some molecular biology techniques can be applied to detect and find differences in NSUN6 levels in body fluid samples, such as blood, urine, and cerebrospinal fluid, from patients and normal people, it can be widely used in the future for early diagnosis, treatment monitoring, and prognosis assessment of clinical ccRCC, which greatly improves the precision and convenience of tumour treatment.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we found that NSUN6 expression was upregulated in ccRCC, and its high expression led to poor patient prognosis and accelerated ccRCC cell migration and invasion. NSUN6 was closely associated with tumor immune infiltration and efficacy, showing potential as a biomarker for ccRCC and providing a new research direction for future clinical early diagnosis of ccRCC and prediction of treatment efficacy.
