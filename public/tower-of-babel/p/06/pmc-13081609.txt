Determinants of low birth weight in term singleton neonate delivered at public health facilities in Bahir Dar city, Ethiopia, an institution-based case-control study
Authors: Andualem Ashine Moges, Israel Woldeyohannes Yabago
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLow birth weight (LBW), defined as a birth weight of less than 2500 g, remains a major public health concern worldwide. Approximately 15–20% of all live births globally are affected, representing more than 20 million newborns each year, with the vast majority occurring in low- and middle-income countries. LBW is associated with increased risks of neonatal morbidity, mortality, impaired growth, and long-term developmental complications. Despite ongoing maternal and newborn health interventions in Ethiopia, determinants of LBW among term singleton neonates remain insufficiently explored. Therefore, this study aimed to identify factors associated with low birth weight among term singleton neonates delivered in public health facilities of Bahir Dar City, Ethiopia.MethodsAn institution-based unmatched case–control study was conducted from June 1 to August 30, 2020, in public health facilities of Bahir Dar city, Ethiopia. Cases were consecutive live-born term singleton neonates weighing less than 2500 g, while controls were the next two live-born term singleton neonates weighing 2500 g or more delivered at the same facilities. Data were collected using a structured interviewer-administered questionnaire and medical record review. Data were entered and analyzed using statistical software. Bivariate logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify candidate variables, and crude odds ratios (CORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were computed. Variables eligible for inclusion were subsequently entered into a multivariable logistic regression model to identify independent determinants of low birth weight, with adjusted odds ratios (AORs) and 95% CIs reported.ResultsMost study mothers (85.3%) were aged 21–34 years. In multivariable analysis, being a housewife (AOR = 3.87; 95% CI: 1.01–14.8), primiparity (AOR = 4.12; 95% CI: 1.87–9.05), pregnancy-induced hypertension (AOR = 4.69; 95% CI: 1.32–16.74), unwanted pregnancy (AOR = 3.80; 95% CI: 1.11–12.92), and not taking additional food during the current pregnancy (AOR = 3.09; 95% CI: 1.37–6.99) were significantly associated with low birth weight.ConclusionLow birth weight among term singleton neonates in Bahir Dar city was significantly associated with maternal, obstetric, and socioeconomic factors. Primiparity, pregnancy-induced hypertension, unwanted pregnancy, lack of additional dietary intake during pregnancy, and maternal occupation as a housewife were the main determinants. Strengthening antenatal care services with a focus on nutritional counseling, pregnancy planning, and early detection and management of hypertensive disorders may help reduce the burden of low birth weight.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-026-08886-9.Keywords: Low birth weight, Maternal risk factors, Pregnancy-induced hypertension, Socioeconomic factors

Introduction

The World Health Organization defines low birth weight (LBW) as a birth weight of less than 2500 g, regardless of gestational age. LBW remains a major global health challenge, particularly in low- and middle-income countries, where the burden is highest and contributes substantially to neonatal morbidity and mortality. Globally, an estimated 15–20% of all live births, representing more than 20 million infants annually, are born with low birth weight, with approximately 95.6% occurring in low- and middle-income countries [1]. The burden of LBW is disproportionately higher among vulnerable populations, particularly in South Asia (28%), sub-Saharan Africa (13%), and Latin America [2]. In Ethiopia, approximately 13% of children with reported birth weights are classified as LBW [3].

LBW encompasses a heterogeneous group of infants: some are preterm (born before 37 weeks of gestation), some are growth-restricted, and others are both preterm and growth-restricted. Birth weight is influenced by factors related to the infant, mother, and environment, and it plays a crucial role in determining both immediate and long-term health outcomes [4].

Multiple maternal, obstetric, and fetal factors contribute to the occurrence of LBW, many of which are biologically and socially interconnected and potentially modifiable. In sub-Saharan Africa, common contributing factors include maternal malnutrition and infectious diseases such as malaria, which also increases the risk of preterm delivery [5]. Other risk factors include advanced maternal age [6], low socioeconomic status, maternal anemia [7–9], primiparity, short maternal stature, and below-average maternal weight [10].

Low birth weight is a major predictor of neonatal and infant mortality and is associated with a wide range of adverse short- and long-term consequences. These include hypoxia, hypothermia, increased susceptibility to infections, respiratory distress, sudden infant death syndrome, impaired growth, delayed neurodevelopment, and an increased risk of chronic non-communicable diseases later in life [11]. Consequently, reducing the burden of LBW is a priority within global and national maternal and child health strategies, including improved antenatal care, nutritional support, prevention and management of maternal illnesses, and early identification of high-risk pregnancies.

In Ethiopia, previous studies have identified several factors associated with LBW, including maternal and environmental conditions [12], inadequate antenatal care (ANC) and pregnancy intention [13], urban residence, lack of additional dietary intake during pregnancy, multiple gestations, marital status, low household income, and maternal HIV infection [14]. However, the relative contribution of these factors varies across settings, and evidence from specific urban contexts remains limited.

Therefore, this study aimed to identify the determinants of low birth weight among term singleton neonates delivered at public health facilities in Bahir Dar city, Ethiopia. Generating context-specific evidence is essential to inform targeted interventions and strengthen maternal and newborn health programs at the local level.

Although low birth weight is not a rare outcome, an institution-based case–control design was employed because it allows efficient identification of multiple maternal, obstetric, and nutritional determinants within a relatively short study period and limited resources. This design is particularly useful for examining associations between several exposures and birth outcomes among facility-based deliveries.

Methods and materials

Study design, area, and period

An institution-based unmatched case–control study was conducted from June 1 to August 30, 2020, in public health facilities of Bahir Dar city, Ethiopia. The study was carried out in Tebebe Ghion Specialized Hospital, Felege Hiwot Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, Addis Alem Primary Hospital, and Vision Maternity Hospital.

Population

The source population comprised all mothers and their neonates who delivered in public health facilities of Bahir Dar city. The study population included mothers who delivered term singleton neonates in the selected public health facilities during the study period.

Eligibility criteria

Cases were mothers who delivered live-born term singleton neonates with a birth weight of less than 2500 g. Controls were mothers who delivered live-born term singleton neonates with a birth weight of 2500 g or more on the same day as the cases in the same health facility.

Newborns with major congenital anomalies incompatible with life, preterm births, multiple gestations, stillbirths, neonates with unknown vital status, and mothers who were unable or unwilling to participate were excluded from the study.

Sample size determination

The sample size was determined using the proportion difference approach for unmatched case–control studies, assuming a 95% confidence level (Zα/2 = 1.96), 80% power (Zβ = 0.84), and a case-to-control ratio of 1:2. The calculation was based on an odds ratio of 4 for lack of nutritional counseling, as reported in a previous study conducted in Nekemte. Using Epi Info version 7, the minimum required sample size was calculated to be 267. After adding a 10% non-response rate, the final sample size was set at 294 (98 cases and 196 controls).

Sampling technique and procedure

All public hospitals in Bahir Dar city were included in the study. Eligible cases were selected consecutively as they occurred until the required sample size was achieved. For each case, two consecutive eligible controls were selected from the same health facility. During the study period, a total of 294 mother-newborn pairs were approached, and 285 mothers gave consent to participate, resulting in a response rate of 97%.

Study variables

Dependent variable

Low birth weight.

Independent variables included

Socio-demographic factors: maternal age, residence, marital status, maternal occupation, maternal educational status, height, body mass index (BMI), and mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC).

Obstetric and clinical factors: history of abortion, hemoglobin level, antenatal care (ANC) attendance, pregnancy-induced hypertension, maternal anemia, parity, birth interval, and pregnancy intention.

Nutritional factors: meal frequency during the current pregnancy, additional food intake during pregnancy, and receipt of nutritional counseling.

Operational definition

Birth weight: the first weight of the newborn which is measured within 20 min of birth.

Low birth weight: neonate born with a birth weight of < 2500 g.

Anemia during pregnancy: a mother whose hemoglobin [15] level is < 11 g/dl during the current pregnancy.

Advanced maternal age: defined as the mother being aged 35 years or above at the time of birth.

Pregnancy-induced hypertension (PIH): A mother with high blood pressure (≥ 140/90mmHg) after 28 weeks of gestation of the current pregnancy and with or without proteinuria.

Incomplete data: Patient card lacking either one or more of the following (Age, sex, drug dose, duration of treatment, and diagnosis).

Additional foodduringpregnancy: food consumed in addition to main daily meals, e.g., snacks, dairy products, and fruits.

History of abortion: previous pregnancy loss before fetal viability, including both spontaneous and induced abortions.

Data collection tools and procedures

Data were collected using a structured interviewer-administered questionnaire adapted from the Ethiopian Demographic and Health Survey (EDHS) and relevant literature. The questionnaire was initially prepared in English, translated into Amharic, and back-translated into English to ensure consistency. The English version of the questionnaire is provided as Supplementary File 1.

Trained nurses and medical interns collected the data after receiving training on the study objectives, data collection tools, and procedures. Data were obtained through face-to-face interviews with mothers who provided informed consent, along with maternal anthropometric measurements. Maternal height was measured using a tape measure against a vertical wall, and MUAC was measured on the right arm. Clinical and obstetric information, including hemoglobin levels, was extracted from medical records.

Neonatal birth weight was measured within 20 min of birth using a calibrated weighing scale. Data completeness and consistency were checked daily by supervisors and the principal investigator.

Data management and analysis

Data were coded, entered into SPSS version 21, and cleaned prior to analysis. Bivariate logistic regression analysis was performed to identify candidate variables for multivariable analysis. Variables with a p-value less than 0.25 in the bivariate analysis were entered into the multivariable logistic regression model to identify independent determinants of low birth weight. Model fitness was assessed using appropriate goodness-of-fit tests. Adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported, and a p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics of study participants

A total of 285 mother–neonate pairs were included in the study, comprising 95 cases and 190 controls. The majority of mothers (85.3%) were aged 21–34 years. Urban residence was reported by 56.8% of cases and 68.4% of controls. Eighty-two mothers had no formal education, with a higher proportion observed among controls. Most mothers were married or cohabiting. Female neonates accounted for 54% of the total study population. The overall mean (± SD) birth weight was 2891.58 ± 583.69 g. Multivariable logistic regression analysis

In the multivariable logistic regression analysis, after adjusting for potential confounders, several factors remained independently associated with low birth weight. Maternal occupation was the only socio-economic factor significantly associated with low birth weight, with housewives having higher odds compared to employed mothers (AOR = 3.87; 95% CI: 1.01–14.8). Primiparity was associated with an increased likelihood of low birth weight (AOR = 4.12; 95% CI: 1.87–9.05). Mothers with pregnancy-induced hypertension had higher odds of delivering low-birth-weight neonates (AOR = 4.69; 95% CI: 1.32–16.74). Unwanted pregnancy was also significantly associated with low birth weight (AOR = 3.80; 95% CI: 1.11–12.92). Additionally, mothers who did not take additional food during pregnancy had increased odds of delivering low-birth-weight neonates (AOR = 3.09; 95% CI: 1.37–6.99) (Table 4).

Discussion

This study identified several maternal and socio-economic factors that were significantly associated with low birth weight among term singleton neonates delivered at public health facilities in Bahir Dar city. Maternal occupation, pregnancy-induced hypertension, parity, pregnancy intention, and lack of additional food intake during pregnancy were found to be independent determinants of low birth weight. In contrast, antenatal care follow-up, maternal height, and maternal anemia were not significantly associated with low birth weight in the multivariable analysis.

Maternal occupational status showed a significant association with low birth weight, with housewives having nearly four times higher odds of delivering low-birth-weight neonates compared to employed mothers. This finding is consistent with studies conducted in Nepal [16] and Southern Ethiopia [17]. Unemployment may reflect lower household income, reduced financial autonomy, and limited access to adequate nutrition during pregnancy. In addition, financial dependence and social stressors among unemployed mothers may increase psychological stress, which has been linked to adverse pregnancy outcomes, including low birth weight.

Pregnancy-induced hypertension was another strong determinant of low birth weight in this study. Mothers with pregnancy-induced hypertension had a substantially higher likelihood of delivering low-birth-weight infants compared to those without hypertension. This finding is in agreement with previous studies conducted in Ethiopia, like Addis Ababa [18], Bishoftu Town [19] and Tirunesh Beijing General Hospital [20] and from Brazil [21].This is due to compromised oxygen and nutrient supply through the placenta to the fetus. Vasoconstriction of blood vessel walls during hypertensive states can lead to LBW [22].

Parity was also significantly associated with low birth weight. Primiparous mothers were more likely to deliver low-birth-weight neonates compared to multiparous mothers. This finding is supported by a systematic review and meta-analysis by Shah et al [23] and by studies conducted in Kerala, Malabar Coast of India [24], Debremarkos [22] and North Wolo hospital [25], Ethiopia, shows same result. A proportion of primiparous mothers in this study were adolescents, a group known to be at increased risk of low birth weight due to ongoing maternal growth and competition for nutrients between the mother and the fetus [26]. Younger maternal age also a risk factor for LBW by itself due to biological immaturity and socioeconomic vulnerabilities, supported by a study in Northern Shewa Zone [27], Ethiopia. Additionally, primiparous women are more prone to pregnancy complications such as pregnancy-induced hypertension, which is itself strongly associated with low birth weight.

Pregnancy intention was significantly associated with neonatal birth weight in this study (AOR=3.80: (95% CI=1.11-12.92)). This result was supported by a study done at Adwa [28], Axum [13] and Northern Shewa Zone [27], Ethiopia. Although the exact mechanism remains unclear, unwanted pregnancy may influence birth weight through delayed initiation of antenatal care, poor maternal health-seeking behavior, reduced nutritional intake, and limited social and emotional support from partners or family members.

Maternal nutritional intake during pregnancy also played an important role in determining birth weight (AOR=3.09: (95% CI=1.37-6.99)). The study found not taking additional food during pregnancy increased the odds of delivering low-birth-weight infants which was similar with study done in other Ethiopia Towns , at Dassie [29], Nekemte [30] and Southern Ethiopia [17]. Pregnancy increases maternal requirements for energy, protein, and micronutrients, including iron and folate. Inadequate dietary intake during pregnancy may result in intrauterine growth restriction and subsequent low birth weight.

In contrast, some variables did not show a significant association with low birth weight in this study. Antenatal care attendance was not significantly associated with low birth weight, which differs from findings reported in Pakistan [31], in South Africa [32] and Bale Zone [12], Ethiopia. This discrepancy may be explained by differences in the definition and categorization of antenatal care utilization, as some studies classified ANC attendance based on adequacy rather than mere attendance. Similarly, maternal anemia was not significantly associated with low birth weight in this study, contrary to findings from Nekemte [30] and Dessie [29], Ethiopia. This may be due to the fact that anemia in the current study population was largely related to acute obstetric conditions rather than chronic nutritional deficiency.

Furthermore, maternal age, educational status, and area of residence were not significantly associated with low birth weight in this study. This finding aligns with reports from Eastern Nepal [16] regarding maternal age but contrasts with studies conducted in Bale Zone [12],Ethiopia, where maternal education and residence were identified as significant predictors. Variations in study design, population characteristics, and socioeconomic context may account for these differences.

The findings of this study may be generalizable to urban public health facility settings with similar socio-demographic and healthcare characteristics in Ethiopia and other low- and middle-income countries. However, caution should be exercised when extrapolating the results to rural populations or private health facilities, as differences in access to healthcare services, maternal nutritional status, and socio-economic conditions may influence birth outcomes. Despite these limitations, the study provides valuable context-specific evidence that can inform maternal and neonatal health interventions in comparable settings.

Clinical and public health implications

This study suggests potential mechanisms underlying low birth weight in this setting. Unwanted pregnancies may increase risk due to limited maternal education and reduced use of prenatal care. Housewives may experience psychosocial stress and limited economic support, contributing to adverse outcomes. Inadequate maternal nutrition, particularly the lack of additional food during pregnancy, may reflect limited nutritional knowledge or access.

Targeted interventions could include economic support programs for housewives, enhanced prenatal follow-up for primiparous mothers, school- and community-based health education on family planning, and nutrition counseling to ensure adequate maternal food intake. These strategies may help reduce the incidence of low birth weight in similar low-resource contexts, although further research is needed to confirm causal pathways.

Research implications

Future research is needed to explore the causal pathways linking unwanted pregnancies, maternal occupational status, psychosocial stress, and inadequate nutrition to low birth weight. Studies assessing maternal mental health, micronutrient deficiencies, and environmental exposures, as well as intervention trials targeting nutrition, prenatal care, and family planning, could help inform effective public health and clinical strategies in low-resource settings.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the data were collected five years prior to publication, which may limit direct applicability to current clinical practice due to potential changes in healthcare services and maternal health conditions over time. In addition, the institution-based design may limit generalizability to mothers delivering outside public health facilities. As with all case–control studies, recall bias and residual confounding cannot be completely excluded.

Despite these limitations, the study has important strengths. A high response rate (97%) minimized selection bias, and the case–control design allowed detailed assessment of obstetric, nutritional, and socio-demographic factors. The restriction to term singleton births enabled clearer identification of determinants independent of prematurity. Furthermore, the study provides additional evidence on underexplored factors such as primiparity, maternal occupational status, and pregnancy intention in a low-resource setting.

Conclusion

Low birth weight among term singleton neonates in Bahir Dar City was significantly associated with maternal occupational status, primiparity, pregnancy-induced hypertension, pregnancy intention, and maternal nutritional factors. These findings highlight the importance of strengthening maternal nutrition and antenatal care services, with particular attention to high-risk groups such as housewives, first-time mothers, and women with unintended pregnancies. Further studies are recommended to explore causal pathways and inform targeted interventions to improve neonatal health outcomes in Ethiopia.
