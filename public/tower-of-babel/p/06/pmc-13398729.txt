From sagittal to sprawled: Clavicular co‐option and the evolution of forelimb mechanics in talpines
Authors: Shin‐ichi Fujiwara, Hiroaki Saito
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe members of Eulipotyphla exhibit substantial ecological and locomotor diversity, yet forelimb skeletal correlates of these adaptations are not consistently shared across lineages. In particular, highly fossorial moles (Talpini and Scalopini) employ a distinctive lateral‐thrust digging mechanism unlike that of most other fossorial mammals. We tested whether such differences are associated with variation in forelimb posture and its functional consequences. Using 31 extant specimens representing 22 species, we quantified the orientations of the scapula, clavicle, and humerus, and estimated the compressive strengths of the clavicle and first true rib based on three‐dimensional models derived from computed tomography. Eulipotyphlans were divided into two groups based on humeral orientation: a “sagittal group” and a “sprawled group.” While the first true rib strength scaled consistently with body mass (BM) across taxa, clavicular strength differed markedly between groups, being substantially greater in the “sprawled group.” These results suggest that the first true rib plays a conserved role in transmitting vertical ground reaction forces, whereas the clavicle in talpines functions as a strut resisting medially directed forces under a sprawled posture. We further propose that increased clavicular strength preceded this postural transition and was subsequently co‐opted (exaptation) to support lateral‐thrust digging, functionally replacing the coracoid as a medial strut, as seen in monotremes.

INTRODUCTION

Eulipotyphla is a group of mammals comprising solenodons (Solenodontidae), hedgehogs (Erinaceidae), shrews (Soricidae), and moles (Talpidae), whose phylogenetic relationships have been explored using both morphological (Motokawa, 2004; Schwermann et al., 2019; Whidden, 2000) and molecular (Brace et al., 2016; Douady et al., 2002; Douady & Douzery, 2003; Dubey et al., 2007; Grenyer & Purvis, 2003; He et al., 2016, 2021; Huttler et al., 2025; Nikaido et al., 2003; Ohdachi et al., 2006; Sato et al., 2016; Shinohara et al., 2003; Willows‐Munro & Matthee, 2026) approaches (Figure 1a). Eulipotyphlans are well‐known for their remarkable diversification, encompassing taxa that range from fully terrestrial forms to species adapted to semi‐aquatic or subterranean lifestyles (Nowak, 1999).

(a) Phylogenetic relationships among studied eulipotyphlan species and (b) their ecological adaptation. The phylogeny is based on Ohdachi et al. (2006), Dubey et al. (2007), and Huttler et al. (2025) for non‐talpid eulipotyphlans and He et al. (2016) for Talpidae. The categories of ecological adaptations are based on Nowak (1999) and Shinohara et al. (2003). Surdisorex (Myosoricinae) was not included in these phylogenetic studies; therefore, its position in the tree is tentatively shown as sister to Myosorex (Myosoricinae), reflecting current morphology‐based hypotheses of relationships within Myosoricinae.

In response to life in subterranean and low‐light environments, previous studies have focused extensively on the evolution of inner ear morphology and echolocation abilities (Forsman & Malmquist, 1988; Gould et al., 1964; Koyabu et al., 2017; Mason, 2006; Tomasi, 1979), as well as on developmental processes underlying these sensory adaptations (Koyabu et al., 2011; Prochel, 2006). In addition, the highly specialized olfactory systems of eulipotyphlans have attracted considerable attention (Catania, 2019; Catania et al., 2008; Grassé, 1955). However, beyond sensory and developmental specializations, this group is also distinguished by the extensive diversification in locomotor function, reflecting a wide range of ecological niches and modes of substrate use (Singh et al., 2021).

Within Eulipotyphla, members of Soricidae exhibit considerable ecological diversification (Figure 1b: Woodman & Wilken, 2019). Although most members, such as Blarina (Soricidae), employ forelimb movements primarily in the parasagittal plane while supporting their body weight with strongly crouched joint angles (Riskin et al., 2016), locomotor strategies within the group are diverse, including ambulatory, semi‐aquatic, and semi‐fossorial forms, and many taxa show adaptations associated with more than one locomotor strategy (Nowak, 1999; Shinohara et al., 2003). Their locomotor functional adaptations have been evaluated based on whole‐body morphology (Woodman, 2023). Talpidae also exhibits comparable ecological diversity (Figure 1b). In addition to the ambulatory Uropsilinae, there are lineages adapted to semi‐aquatic and semi‐fossorial lifestyles (Nowak, 1999; Shinohara et al., 2003). Particularly noteworthy is the highly fossorial adaptation that is thought to have evolved independently in two lineages—Talpini and Scalopini—within Talpidae (He et al., 2016, 2021; Shinohara et al., 2003). In talpines, semi‐ and highly fossorial adaptations have been well‐documented in the humerus (e.g., Meier et al., 2013; Nakai & Yokohata, 2024; Piras et al., 2015; Sánchez‐Villagra et al., 2004; Sansalone et al., 2017) and forelimb musculature (Rose et al., 2013; Wada et al., 2021). Despite this body of functional morphological studies on locomotion in Eulipotyphla, no forelimb skeletal features associated with locomotor adaptation have been identified that are shared between Talpidae and other eulipotyphlan taxa, even though ambulatory, semi‐aquatic, and semi‐fossorial forms occur in both Soricidae and Talpidae.

Moreover, although highly fossorial adaptations are restricted to a subset of Talpinae within Eulipotyphla, their digging behavior is highly distinctive compared with that of other fossorial mammals in that they employ lateral‐thrust (humeral rotation) digging technique (Barnosky, 1982; Nakai & Fujiwara, 2023). Furthermore, although highly fossorial moles move their forelimbs alternately during surface locomotion (Wada et al., 2021), they often use bilaterally symmetrical, simultaneous strokes during digging (Lin et al., 2019a).

Why, despite exhibiting comparable ecological diversity, do Soricidae and Talpidae not share common morphological correlates of semi‐aquatic or semi‐fossorial adaptations? Moreover, why have highly fossorial moles evolved a digging strategy that is distinct from that of most other mammals? We hypothesize that these differences are attributable to fundamental differences in forelimb posture.

Most eulipotyphlans employ a sagittal forelimb posture, whereas many talpids adopt a sprawled (hyper‐abducted) forelimb posture (Fujiwara & Hutchinson, 2012). The evolutionary transition from a sagittal to a sprawled forelimb posture represents a highly unusual event in mammalian evolution.

This postural transition is expected to substantially alter the orientation of the ground reaction force (GRF). In a sagittal posture, the glenoid is positioned approximately above the manus, such that the GRF is dominated by a vertical component, with only a minor mediolateral component (e.g., Clayton & Hobbs, 2021; Corbee et al., 2014). In contrast, in a sprawled posture, the manus is placed lateral to the glenoid, resulting in a more pronounced medial component of the GRF (Chen et al., 2006). Among sprawled taxa, however, the magnitude of this medial component varies: in semi‐sprawled forms such as crocodilians, where the body is held well above the ground, the medial component is relatively limited (Arias & Azizi, 2023), whereas in taxa such as geckos that move with the body contacting the substrate, the mediolateral component of the GRF is strongly emphasized relative to the dorsoventral component (Wang et al., 2015, 2020).

These considerations suggest that, if the evolution of a sprawled forelimb posture in Talpidae was accompanied by increased demands to resist the medial component of the GRF, corresponding skeletal adaptations would have been required. This perspective may help explain both the absence of shared morphological indicators of semi‐aquatic or semi‐fossorial adaptations in the forelimb skeleton across Eulipotyphla and the evolution of the distinctive lateral‐thrust digging strategy in highly fossorial moles.

While several elements of the pectoral girdle and forelimb could, in principle, reflect these mechanical demands, the true ribs and clavicle provide particularly suitable structures for isolating distinct load‐bearing roles. Previous studies have shown that, in quadrupedal mammals, the vertical component of the GRF acting on the forelimbs is primarily transmitted to the vertebral column via the true ribs (Fujiwara, 2018; Fujiwara et al., 2009), and therefore well positioned to reflect the transmission of vertical forces to the axial skeleton. In contrast, the clavicle, when present, forms a strut‐like connection between the forelimb and the sternum and is therefore expected to resist mediolateral compression associated with medialward GRFs (Campbell, 1939), although its functional role has not been quantitatively evaluated.

By comparison, elements such as the humerus and scapula are subjected to more complex, multidirectional loading regimes and integrate multiple functional roles, making it more difficult to isolate specific directional components of loading. In addition, practical limitations in imaging resolution further constrain quantitative assessment of certain elements (e.g., scapula). For these reasons, the present study focuses on the clavicle and first true ribs as representative structures for mediolateral and vertical load transmission, respectively.

In this study, we examine extant eulipotyphlans to compare the functional properties of two skeletal elements: the true ribs, which transmit the vertical component of the GRF to the axial skeleton, and the clavicle, which potentially transmits the mediolateral component of the GRF to the trunk. We further aim to evaluate how these structures may have constrained the evolution of locomotor function across Eulipotyphla. To clarify the inferential framework of this study, we distinguish among anatomical observations of skeletal morphology and structural properties, functional interpretations regarding load transmission and mechanical roles, and evolutionary interpretations concerning the origin and modification of these traits, including potential cases of exaptation (Gould & Vrba, 1982; Pievani & Serrelli, 2011). Evolutionary interpretations such as exaptation are treated cautiously, as they rely on linking present‐day functional properties to historical transitions that cannot be directly observed.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Materials

Thirty‐one eulipotyphlan specimens were examined, representing 22 species and 19 genera, which were subdivided into 13 taxonomic groups for later analyses—Solenodontidae (SO: n = 1), Soricini (SR: n = 4), Blarinini (BL: n = 1), Nectogalini (NG: n = 4), Myosoricinae (MS: n = 2); Crocidurinae (CR: n = 5), Uropsilinae (UP: n = 2), Scalopini (SC: n = 1), Urotrichini (UT: n = 4), Neurotrichini (NT: n = 1), Condylurini (CN: n = 1), Desmanini (DS: n = 2), and Talpini (TA: n = 3) (Table 1, Table S1). Specimens consisted of unfixed carcasses and preserved materials, and their preservation condition varied (e.g., ethanol‐fixed, frozen and subsequently thawed prior to scanning, or unknown; see Table S1). We acknowledge that factors such as rigor mortis, postmortem relaxation, storage conditions, and handling procedures may influence preserved forelimb posture. However, because our analyses focus on comparative differences among taxa rather than absolute joint angles, and because specimens were processed using consistent procedures, we consider the observed differences to be biologically informative.

We followed the literature (Dubey et al., 2007; He et al., 2016; Huttler et al., 2025; Ohdachi et al., 2006) for the phylogenetic relationship among the taxa (Figure 1a) and the ecological adaptation (Figure 1b: Nowak, 1999; Shinohara et al., 2003) of each species. Among these, Chimarrogale, Neomys, Condylura, and Galemys are semi‐aquatic (Mendes‐Soares & Rychlik, 2009; Palmeirim & Hoffmann, 1983; Petersen & Yates, 1980; Saito et al., 2019); Sorex unguiculatus (Ohdachi, 1995), Surdisorex (Woodman & Stabile, 2015), Blarina (Hamilton, 1929), Dymecodon, Urotrichus, Neurotrichus (Carraway & Verts, 1991; Dalquest & Orcutt, 1942), and Condylura (Petersen & Yates, 1980) burrow through soft substrates; and Scalopus (Yates & Shmidly, 1978), Oreoscaptor (Sagara, 2009), and Mogera (Iwasa & Takahashi, 2021) burrow through tough substrates (Figure 1). Galemys are not typically fossorial—they usually form shallow trenches (Palmeirim & Hoffmann, 1983).

The image sequences of the X‐ray computed tomography (CT) scans were collected for the studied specimens. The image sequences of 20 out of 31 specimens were obtained using the scanner (inspeXio SMX‐225CT FPD HR, Shimadzu Co.) installed at Nagoya Municipal Industrial Research Institute, Nagoya, Japan; two were obtained using the scanner (Latheta LCT‐200, Hitachi Co. Ltd.) installed at the National Museum of Nature and Science, Tsukuba, Japan; and 11 were obtained from an online databank for 3D images of the specimens (MorphoSource, https://www.morphosource.org/: Table S1). The degree of epiphyseal fusion in the limb bones was assessed from CT image data, and all specimens used in this study were confirmed to represent skeletally mature individuals.

The specimens were handled carefully prior to CT scanning to avoid artificial repositioning of skeletal elements. In particular, the forelimb postures of the studied specimens were retained in an unposed condition. The specimens were CT‐scanned in postures that did not impose unnatural forces on the body. Therefore, the forelimb posture of the specimens observed in the CT images was assumed to fall within the natural range of forelimb motion.

The sequence of the CT‐scanned images was imported to a data visualization software, Avizo (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.) for each specimen. Thereafter, the entire body excluding the fur, which did not appear clearly on the CT images; ribcage; ossified nuchal ligament (if available); clavicles; scapulae; humeri; antebrachia; and manus were segmented out from the image sequence. The surface profile of each element was exported as 3D mesh (.STL file format: Figure S1). The 3D mesh (.STL) of the entire body was imported into software Voxelcon (Quint Co.) for structural analysis to estimate the body volume (BV [mm3]: Table 1).

Among the 62 forelimbs from 31 specimens, including both left and right sides, forelimbs with disarticulated joints or bone fractures on the first true rib and/or clavicle were excluded from the analysis of forelimb orientation, and forelimbs with broken bones were excluded from the strength analyses of the adjacent bone—that is, the right clavicle of a Uropsilus nivatus specimen (NSMT M55092) and the left first true rib of a Condylura cristata specimen (YPM M014548) were broken; the left shoulder joint of a Mogera imaizumii specimen (SIF 053) was disarticulated (Table 1). For all analyses other than body mass estimation, the statistical unit was the individual skeletal element; left and right sides were treated as separate observations when both were available. Accordingly, the number of forelimbs used to analyze the forelimb bone orientation is 59; the number used for the analysis of first true rib strength is 61; and that used for analyzing the clavicle strength is 61 (Table 1).

Body mass estimation

The body mass (BM [g]) was measured for the 18 specimens using an electronic balance (ViBRA AJII, Shinko Denshi: accuracy, 0.001 g), whereas BM measurement was not possible for the remaining 13 ethanol‐preserved or digitally sourced specimens (Table 1). We estimated the body mass from the body volume (BV [mm3]) for these 13 specimens using a regression line between the log (BM) and log (BV) obtained from the 18 specimens whose BM was available. This estimation assumes that the mass density of the entire body is shared among eulipotyphlan species.

The relationship between the log (BM) and log (BV) was obtained as the following equation for the 18 specimens (Figure S2): logBM=0.96102×logBV−2.9533. Thereafter, the BM of the other 13 specimens was estimated by substituting each BV value to the above equation (Table 1). The body weight (BW [N]) was calculated by multiplying gravitational acceleration (9.80665 [m/s2]) and the body mass (BM [g]) as follows: BWN=9.80665×BM/1000kg·m/s2. The BM [g] was used for analyzing the relationship with the strengths of first true ribs (RS [N]) and clavicles (CS [N]), which are explained later, and the BW [N] was used for standardizing and nondimensionalizing these strengths. To assess the plausibility of the estimated BM values, they were compared with published adult body mass ranges reported for wild individuals of each species (Table S1). Most estimated values fell within the reported ranges, supporting the validity of the estimation procedure.

Forelimb element orientations

The orientations of the scapula, clavicle, and humerus in relation to the ribcage were measured for both sides of the forelimbs. The 3D mesh (.STL) of the above skeletal elements was imported to Voxelcon. The dorsal margin of the first sternum was defined as a horizontal plane of the animal, which is parallel to the craniocaudal and mediolateral axes, and is perpendicular to the dorsoventral axis. The craniocaudal, mediolateral, and dorsoventral axes were aligned along the X, Y, and Z axes of the coordinate space in Voxelcon, where cranial, left, and dorsal orientations are positive, respectively (Figures 2, 3a and S1).

The scapular orientation was defined as the distalward unit vector along the line from the proximal end of the spine to the centroid of the glenoid (dark blue arrow along the scapula in Figure 2a,b,e,f). The clavicular orientation was defined as the distalward unit vector along the line through the centroids from the proximal to distal ends (dark green arrow along the clavicle in Figure 2a,b,e,f). The orientation of the humeral shaft (purple arrow along the humerus in Figure 2a,b,e,f) was defined as the distalward unit vector along the line from the center of the sphere that best fits the humeral head to the midpoint between ulnar and radial epicondyles (purple bar in the distal humerus in Figure 2a,b,e,f). The plane that passes through the center of the humeral head and the ulnar and radial epicondyles was also determined, because their orientational relationships with the humeral shaft indicate the angle of internal/external rotation of the humerus.

The unit vector coordinates of the scapular, clavicular, and humeral orientations were determined for each side of the forelimbs. The bone orientations were calculated by assigning the abduction angle of the bone to longitude and elevation/depression angle of the bone to latitude. For the scapula, we defined abduction as the condition in which the distal end points cranially. For the clavicle and humerus, we defined abduction as the condition in which the distal end points caudally. For the humerus, in addition to the abduction and elevation angles, we also calculated the internal/external rotation angle using Euler angles, with positive values indicating internal rotation, where the medial epicondyle is positioned above the lateral epicondyle (Table 1).

The orientations of the scapulae and clavicles, and the Euler angles of the humeri in relation to the sternum were projected onto a Schmidt net using a package RFOC (Lees, 2023) in a software for the statistical computing and data visualization, R version 3.2.5. (Comprehensive R Archive Network). The cranial side of the body was assigned to the top side of the diagram and the lateral side to the left, regardless of the sides of the limb. The upper and lower hemispheres of the Schmidt net were designated to represent the dorsal and ventral sides of the body, respectively.

The orientation vectors of the scapulae, clavicles, and humeral shafts were enclosed within a convex hull bounded by great circles for each taxonomic group on the Schmidt net. The orientation vectors on the Schmidt net were also enclosed by the convex hull categorized based on the humeral orientations (humeral abduction angle: <90°, sagittal; 90°<, hyper‐abducted).

Stress analyses

Finite element stress analyses (FEA) were conducted for the 3D models of clavicles and first true ribs imported into the Voxelcon (Figure 3a). We used material properties of compact bones (Young's modulus, 18,000 MPa; Poisson's ratio, 0.4) for the analyses (Vogel, 2003). The FEA in this study were designed to provide comparative estimates of relative structural strength rather than absolute mechanical performance. Although simplified material properties and loading conditions were applied, such approaches are widely used in comparative biomechanical studies to capture interspecific differences in load‐bearing capacity.

The fracture strength of clavicle (CS [N]) was estimated as follows. The proximal end of the 3D clavicle model was fixed toward all three axes for both right and left clavicles (Figure 3b). The orientation from the distal to the proximal ends of the clavicle was estimated for both right and left clavicles, and the proximalward load parallel to the orientation (1 [N]) was applied on the distal end of the clavicle. The clavicular strength (CS [N]) was calculated by dividing the yield stress of the compact bone (200 [N/mm2]: Vogel, 2003) by the resultant maximum von Mises stress [N/mm2] distributed within the clavicle (Figure 3c).

Likewise, the fracture strength of first true rib (RS [N]) was estimated as follows. The vertebral column of the 3D model was fixed toward all three axes, and the dorsalward surface load (1 [N]) was applied on the lateral surface of the right and left ribs, respectively (Figure 3d). The first true rib strength (RS [N]) was calculated by dividing the yield stress of the compact bone (200 [N/mm2]: Vogel, 2003) from the resultant maximum von Mises stress [N/mm2] distributed within the first true rib (Figure 3e).

For ribs posterior to the second ribs, we did not conduct stress analyses using the finite element method. This was because these ribs are relatively thin, making it difficult to obtain a voxel thickness sufficient for reliable finite element analysis. Instead, previous studies have suggested that the strength of a rib against vertical compression is inversely proportional to its degree of lateral expansion (Ando & Fujiwara, 2016; Fujiwara, 2018; Fujiwara et al., 2009). In contrast, the first true rib is expected to be narrower and mechanically more robust than the more posterior ribs. Moreover, it can be assumed to consistently serve as an origin for the m. serratus ventralis across all studied species. For these reasons, the first true rib was selected for analysis.

Although finite element analyses were not conducted for ribs posterior to the second true rib, for comparison purposes, we measured the maximum mediolateral width between each pair of left and right true ribs on the 3D skeletal model as a proxy for rib strength against vertical compression. Unlike the true ribs, the false and floating ribs do not have a firm ventral articulation with the sternum. Therefore, we judged that the width of the ribcage would not allow a straightforward comparison of rib strength, and we did not measure the width between the pair of ribs. Among the eight pairs of true ribs in the Solenodon specimen and the seven pairs in the non‐solenodontid eulipotyphlan specimens (Table 1), only the pairs without fractures or disarticulations were used for the measurements.

The relationships between BM and CS and between BM and RS were scatter‐plotted using R, respectively. Next, Spearman's rank correlation was used to assess the relationship between log BM and log CS. The same analysis was performed for the relationship between log BM and log RS.

The CS [N] and RS [N] were standardized by BW [N], respectively. Thereafter, the relationship between CS/BW [N/N] and RS/BW [N/N] was scatter‐plotted. A cluster analysis was performed using the average linkage method to examine the grouping of data points based on log CS/BW and log RS/BW, implemented in the R package cluster (Maechler et al., 2025).

To assess the phylogenetic signal in CS/BW [N/N] and RS/BW [N/N] across the studied species, we applied Abouheif's mean C‐statistics (hereinafter, C̄: Abouheif, 1999). This method evaluates phylogenetic autocorrelation based on tree topology and does not require branch length information (Münkemüller et al., 2012). The null hypothesis of no phylogenetic autocorrelation was rejected when p < 0.05 (Abouheif, 1999). Statistical tests were conducted using packages adephylo and ape (Jombart et al., 2010; Paradis et al., 2003) in R (R Core Team). Phylogenetic topology was derived from the literature (Figure 1a: Ohdachi et al., 2006; Dubey et al., 2007; He et al., 2016; Huttler et al., 2025), with Surdisorex tentatively treated as sister to Myosorex following morphology‐based hypotheses within Myosoricinae. For Abouheif's test, left and right asymmetry was not considered, and following Batalha et al. (2011), the values were averaged within each species as representative species‐level values.

RESULTS

Forelimb element orientations

Humeral orientations

The studied specimens were clearly divided into two groups based on the abduction angle of the humerus—the talpines employed the sprawled, hyper‐abducted humeral posture (hereinafter, “sprawled group”: interquartile range [IQR], 114.8°–135.1°), whereas the non‐talpine eulipotyphlans (Solenodontidae, Soricidae, and Uropsilinae) employed a more adducted, sagittal humeral posture (hereinafter, “sagittal group”: IQR, 20.0°–42.0°: Table 2, Figures 4c and 5). The abduction angle of the humeri was not associated with the range of elevation/depression (IQR: sagittal, 13.5°–49.2°; sprawled, 17.5°–42.2°). However, the sagittal group tended to exhibit a more externally rotated humerus (IQR: sagittal, −24.3°–16.0°), whereas the humerus of the sprawled group was relatively more internally rotated (IQR: 11.3°–33.4°: Table 2, Figure 4c).

The humeral abduction angle did not differ markedly among the three subcategories within the “sagittal group” (IQR: ambulatory, 20.0°–42.0°; semi‐aquatic, 19.6°–48.3°; semi‐fossorial, 23.7°–42.8°), and also among four subcategories within the “sprawled group” (IQR: ambulatory, 114.8°–135.1°; semi‐aquatic, 127.7°–135.3°; semi‐fossorial, 114.1°–139.2°; highly fossorial, 113.6°–130.7°) (Table 2, Figures 4c and 5). However, within the “sprawled group” the range of elevation angle was more restricted to horizontal orientations in highly fossorial taxa (IQR, −17.1°–16.0°) compared with the other three subcategories whose humeri were more elevated (Q1, >37.5°; Q3 >42.2°: Table 2, Figure 4c).

Clavicular orientations

There was little difference in the abduction angle of the clavicles between the “sagittal group” (IQR: 100.6°–128.2°) and “sprawled group” (IQR: 105.8°–131.0°) (Table 2, Figures 4b and 5). However, the range of elevation/depression angle was relatively large in the “sagittal group” (IQR: 17.1°–45.7°) compared with the “sprawled group” (IQR: 5.3°–16.9°) (Table 2, Figures 4b and 5).

Within the “sagittal group,” the abduction angles (IQR: ambulatory, 100.6°–128.2°; semi‐aquatic, 94.3°–120.5°; semi‐fossorial, 115.3°–127.1°) and the elevation angles (IQR: ambulatory, 17.1°–45.7°; semi‐aquatic, 11.8°–44.6°; semi‐fossorial, 14.5°–26.2°) of the clavicles did not differ greatly among the locomotor categories (Table 2).

Within the “sprawled group,” the abduction angles of the clavicles were relatively large in three out of the four locomotor categories (IQR: ambulatory, 121.1°–136.9°; semi‐aquatic, 133.3°–144.1°; semi‐fossorial, 119.8°–123.9°), whereas the angle was smaller in highly fossorial taxa (IQR: 80.3°–105.6°) (Table 2). There was little difference in the elevation angle of the clavicle among the four locomotor categories within “sprawled group” (IQR: ambulatory, 7.0°–19.0°; semi‐aquatic, 13.2°–19.3°; semi‐fossorial, 4.0°–16.3°; highly fossorial, 4.0°–8.1°) (Table 2). Accordingly, in ventral view, the sternum and clavicles together form a T‐shaped configuration in the highly fossorial “sprawled group,” whereas they form a “Y”‐shaped configuration in the remaining subcategories (Figure 5).

Scapular orientations

The scapulae oriented more transversely in “sagittal group” (IQR: 45.5°–73.6°) compared with “sprawled group” (IQR: −4.0°–9.6°), which aligned more sagittally (Tables 1 and 2, Figure 4a). The depression angle of the scapulae was similar between “sagittal group” (IQR: 55.2°–72.6°) and “sprawled group” (IQR: 47.6°–67.2°) (Table 2, Figure 4a).

Within the “sagittal group,” abduction angle did not vary greatly among the three subcategories (IQR: ambulatory, 45.5°–73.6°; semi‐aquatic, 61.9°–78.7°; semi‐fossorial, 50.3°–60.5°). The abduction angle of the scapulae did not vary greatly among the four subcategories within the “sprawled group” as well (IQR: ambulatory, −11.4°–7.6°; semi‐aquatic, −20.3°–5.2°; semi‐fossorial, −1.0°–12.2°; highly fossorial, 3.8°–9.4°) (Table 2).

The depression angle of scapulae did not vary substantially within the subcategories of “sagittal group” (IQR: ambulatory, 55.2°–72.6°; semi‐aquatic, 52.7°–78.1°; semi‐fossorial, 56.9°–71.2°) (Table 2). However, there was a difference in the depression angle of scapulae among subcategories within the “sprawled” group. The IQR was greatest in semi‐aquatic, some of which oriented nearly vertical (67.8°–75.3°); followed by ambulatory (58.3°–70.0°); semi‐fossorial (54.3°–63.6°); and highly fossorial, which aligned more horizontally among the others (41.3°–46.3°) (Table 2).

Correspondence in forelimb element orientations

In terms of the orientational correspondence among the forelimb elements, the scapulae, clavicles, and humeri exhibited different orientations in the “sagittal group” (Figures 4 and 5). However, in the “sprawled group,” the abduction angles of the humerus and clavicles were similar, although the scapular orientation did not align with these two elements (Figures 4 and 5).

The highly fossorial taxa of “sagittal group” were unique in that the clavicular and humeral orientations were similar to each other not only in abduction but also in the elevation angles—both oriented somewhat horizontally—while the scapulae oriented more cranially (Figure 4b,c, Table 2).

The clavicular ( CS ) and first true rib ( RS ) strengths

CS

The stress analysis on the clavicles revealed that von Mises stress was emphasized around the mid‐shaft of the clavicle (Figure S3). The clavicular strength (log CS [N]: Table 1) did not show a strong correlation with the body mass (log BM [g]) across all studied specimens (R 2 = 0.463: Table 3, Figure 6a). However, the log CS was strongly correlated with the log BM within the “sagittal group” (R 2 = 0.809) and moderately correlated within the “sprawled group” (R 2 = 0.603: Table 3, Figure 6a). The slopes were similar between groups (“sagittal group,” 0.700; “sprawled group,” 0.727), whereas the intercepts differed substantially (“sagittal group,” −0.549; “sprawled group,” 0.634: Table 3, Figure 6a). This suggests that the “sprawled group” exhibits higher clavicular strength relative to body size than the “sagittal group.”

RS

According to the stress analysis on the first true ribs, von Mises stress was emphasized around the middle of the rib curvature (Figure S3). The strength of the first true rib (log RS [N]: Table 1) was strongly correlated with the body mass (log BM [g]) for all studied specimens (R 2 = 0.778), “sagittal group” (R 2 = 0.826), and “sprawled group” (R 2 = 0.781: Figure 6b, Table 3). The slopes (0.656–0.783) and the intercepts (−0.0193–0.0397) of the regression line were similar among the “sagittal group,” “sprawled group,” and the entire studied specimens, which suggests that the relationship between log BM and log RS is shared among the studied eulipotyphlans (Table 3).

The RS standardized by body weight (RS/BW) showed limited variation across eulipotyphlans, though the value was slightly larger in the “sagittal group” (IQR: 39.2–81.9) compared with the “sprawled group” (IQR: 24.2–43.9) (Figure 7a, Table 2). Unlike the CS/BW, the trait RS/BW exhibited marginally significant phylogenetic signal, although the signal was relatively weak (Abouheif's test: C̄ = 0.198, p = 0.05).

Within the “sagittal group,” RS/BW tended to be lower in the semi‐aquatic (IQR: 37.8–57.5) and semi‐fossorial (IQR: 26.6–46.9) taxa, although the difference from ambulatory taxa (IQR: 39.2–81.9) was not clear (Figure 7a, Table 2). On the other hand, the semi‐aquatic “sprawled group” (Condylurini and Desmanini) showed markedly low value in RS/BW (IQR: 11.5–14.8) among eulipotyphlans (Figure 7a, Table 2). The difference in the value among the remaining subcategories was not clear within “sprawled group” (IQR: ambulatory, 19.2–54.8; semi‐fossorial, 26.5–59.1; highly fossorial, 26.0–34.6) (Figure 7a, Table 2).

Regarding the intercostal widths of the true ribs, the width between the first true ribs exhibited the narrowest, which progressively increased toward more posterior positions (Figure 8). True ribs of Soricidae exhibited a pronounced increase between the second and third true ribs, whereas those of Talpidae exhibited an approximately 50% increase between the first and second true ribs. Although such interclade differences were evident, the overall pattern was consistent across the studied specimens—the transverse expansion increased posteriorly, the width at the posterior‐most true ribs reaching roughly 2.5 times the width at the first true ribs. These results indicate that the resistance of the ribcage to vertical compression decreases substantially toward the posterior.

Relationship between CS / BW and RS / BW

The “sagittal group” and “sprawled group” were clearly separated in the scatterplot of log CS/BW and log RS/BW (Figure 7a). Cluster analysis of the scatterplot showed that the “sagittal group” and “sprawled group” were clearly separated into two groups based on log CS/BW and log RS/BW (Figure 7b).

Within “sprawled group,” the semi‐aquatic taxa, such as Condylura, Desmana, and Galemys formed a distinct subgroup separate from the others. The remainder of the “sprawled group” was broadly subdivided into urotrichinines, neurotrichinines, and highly fossorial taxa (talpinines and scalopinines: Figure 7b). The “sagittal group” was broadly subdivided into three subclusters, including one composed mainly of uropsilines and another including solenodontids and blarinins. Nevertheless, taxa other than those noted above were distributed across multiple subclusters, obscuring taxon‐specific clustering patterns (Figure 7b).

DISCUSSION

Functional integration of skeletal orientation and strength

The results of this study suggest that forelimb posture fundamentally influences the primary pathway of load transmission in eulipotyphlans, with sagittal taxa primarily transmitting vertical forces via the ribs, whereas sprawled taxa accommodate increased mediolateral forces through the clavicle.

The forelimbs of eulipotyphlans are clearly divided into sagittal (Figure 9b) and sprawled (Figure 9c,d) postures based on humeral orientation—the latter is employed by talpines and the former is employed by the rest. The results of this study demonstrate that orientations of the scapulae and clavicle vary according to the forelimb posture (sagittal or sprawled) as well as locomotor adaptation. This study also demonstrates that the strengths of the first true ribs (RS/BW) and clavicles (CS/BW), which were quantified from the skeletal morphology, strongly reflect locomotor function in eulipotyphlans.

Regression analyses further indicate that these functional differences are associated not only with differences in intercepts but also with modest differences in scaling relationships relative to body size (Figure 6, Table 3). Although the overall patterns are broadly consistent across taxa, the contribution of body size should therefore be considered when interpreting functional divergence between the sagittal and sprawled groups.

The cluster dendrogram based on the scatterplot of log RS/BW and log CS/BW (Figure 7b) primarily reflects functional clustering among eulipotyphlans based on forelimb posture and locomotor adaptation. However, Abouheif's tests indicate that phylogenetic structure also contributes to these patterns, particularly for CS/BW, which exhibited a significant phylogenetic signal. In contrast, RS/BW showed only a weak phylogenetic signal. These results suggest that clavicular robustness may have been more strongly influenced by lineage‐specific evolutionary history, whereas rib strength more consistently reflects mechanical loading associated with body support (Figure 6b).

Difference between sagittal and sprawled forelimb postures

The lack of alignment among the shafts of the scapula, clavicle, and humerus in “sagittal group” (Figures 4 and 9b) implies that the joints between them are angulated to some extent. This, in turn, indicates that GRFs on the forelimbs are not transmitted in a manner that axially compresses the bones, suggesting that the skeletal system is not adapted for purely axial loading, but rather the posture is maintained by a muscle‐driven lever system. Given that the clavicle is primarily orientated cranially, dorsally, and laterally (i.e., obliquely relative to the body axes: Figure 4b), the clavicle is likely to be rolled and pitched dorsally and yawed medially by the dorsalward GRF. A relatively long clavicle also contributes to increasing the moment arm of the GRF (Figure 9b, Figure S1). This rotation would either press the scapula against the ribcage or lift it dorsally relative to the trunk. The muscle system that counteracts this lift may involve the m. serratus ventralis and m. pectoralis; however, these muscles offer little resistance to medially directed forces. When the forelimbs are pressed medially to the ribcage, the reaction force from the ribcage would act to arrest its medial displacement. Under GRFs dominated by a vertical component (Figure 2a,b), the clavicle would not be subjected to axial compression, and the ribcage would be less likely to experience lateral compression. Therefore, the relatively low structural strength of the clavicle in the “sagittal group” does not contradict their postural maintenance strategy (Figure 6a).

In contrast, the “sprawled group” is characterized by the increasing correspondence between clavicular and humeral abduction angles (Figures 4b,c, 5, and 9c,d). In sprawled forelimb posture, the medial component of the GRF is expected to be substantial (Figure 2f,g). Moreover, due to the relatively short clavicle (Figure S1), the moment generated by the reaction force about the sternoclavicular joint is also expected to be limited. Taken together, the clavicle is likely to experience substantial axial compression. The function of the clavicle in true moles has been suggested in previous studies (Castiella et al., 1992), and the present study provides quantitative evidence supporting this interpretation. If the clavicle is interpreted as functioning like a strut that resists the medially directed force transmitted from the humerus to the shoulder joint via the humero‐clavicular joint connection acquired in talpines (Edwards, 1937; Freeman, 1886; Kistin, 1929), this is consistent with its relatively high structural strength (Figure 6a). Such function of clavicles in talpines parallels the role of the coracoid, which acts as a robust strut during forelimb downstrokes in birds (Akeda & Fujiwara, 2023).

Regardless of whether the forelimb posture is sagittal or sprawled, the vertical component of the GRF is transmitted to the vertebral column in proportion to body mass and gravitational acceleration of the center of mass associated with locomotion. The vertical force transmitted from the forelimb is conveyed to the true ribs either from the scapula via m. serratus ventralis, or from the clavicle via the sternum. The strong correlation between the body mass and strength of the first true rib against vertical compression among eulipotyphlans regardless of the forelimb posture (Figure 6b), as well as the fact that strengths of first true ribs likely exceed those of the more caudal ribs (Figure 8) suggests that the first true ribs serve as a key structure for transmitting the vertical component of GRFs from the forelimb element to the vertebral column. This trait is shared with other mammals, as some terrestrial carnivorans, proboscideans, and rodents, in which the few rows of cranial true ribs play a primary role in resisting vertically directed GRFs; but differs from ungulates such as perissodactyls and artiodactyls, which exhibit elevated strength in several rows of cranial ribs (Fujiwara, 2018; Fujiwara et al., 2009).

Although GRF orientations were not measured in this study, the inferred differences between sagittal and sprawled forelimb postures are consistent with previous experimental studies (e.g., Arias & Azizi, 2023; Chen et al., 2006; Wang et al., 2015, 2020). Therefore, the present functional interpretations should be regarded as biomechanically plausible scenarios rather than direct empirical demonstrations.

RS is therefore expected to serve as a useful proxy for estimating body mass in extinct eulipotyphlans (e.g., Barnosky, 1982; Sansalone et al., 2017) if their ribs are available. In addition, body mass and CS may serve as proxies for estimating their forelimb postures if clavicles are also preserved.

Difference among locomotor adaptations

Within the “sagittal group,” little difference in the orientation of the forelimb elements was detected among the locomotor categories (Figure 4, Table 2), and it was also difficult to identify differences based on log RS/BW and log CS/BW (Figure 7). These results indicate that, at least within the “sagittal group,” it cannot be concluded that the first true rib or the clavicle contributes functionally to ecological adaptations such as semi‐aquatic or semi‐fossorial lifestyles. On the other hand, it is noteworthy that Uropsilinae, which forms a sister group to Talpinae despite exhibiting a sagittal forelimb posture, shows relatively high log CS/BW values within this cluster. This may be attributed to the relatively straighter clavicles in Uropsilinae among the “sagittal group” (Figure S1).

Within the “sprawled group,” semi‐aquatic talpines (Condylura and Galemys) were characterized by having relatively low log RS/BW and log CS/BW relative to other taxa (Figure 7). The relatively low log CS/BW in these taxa may be attributed to their relatively slender clavicles among the “sprawled group” (Figure S1). The relatively low log RS/BW and log CS/BW in these taxa might be explained by differences in forelimb support requirements. However, this pattern is not evident among semi‐aquatic taxa within the “sagittal group” (Chimarrogale and Neomys: Figure 7). Given the limited sample size, further data are needed to clarify this pattern in the semi‐aquatic “sprawled group.”

It is noteworthy that highly fossorial members of the “sprawled group,” such as Scalopus and Mogera, which burrow complex tunnel systems in compact soils (Hisaw, 1923; Lin et al., 2016), exhibit exceptionally high log CS/BW compared with less fossorial taxa Urotrichini, Neurotrichini, Condylurini, and Desmanini (Figure 7) as well as more horizontally oriented scapulae and more laterally oriented clavicles and humeri (Figures 4 and 9d). This is consistent with the clavicles being short and robust among the “sprawled group” (Figure S1). This condition likely represents an additional reinforcement beyond that required for a general sprawled posture, representing an adaptation of the clavicle to withstand simultaneous lateral thrust loading transmitted to both forelimbs from the surrounding tunnel walls (Hisaw, 1923)—loads reportedly reaching up to 200 times body weight (Gambaryan et al., 2002). The clavicle functioning as a strut against the powerful bilateral contractions of the pectoralis musculature generated during the simultaneous forelimb “flapping” used in lateral‐thrust digging (Gaughran, 1954; Reed, 1951; Wada et al., 2021) parallels the role of the coracoid, which acts as a robust strut during synchronous forelimb downstrokes in birds (Akeda & Fujiwara, 2023).

Highly fossorial taxa exhibit strong constraints on the orientation of the forelimb elements (Figures 4 and 9d). In these forms, the scapula becomes more cranially oriented and inclines closer to the horizontal plane, while the clavicle also approaches a more horizontal orientation and is abducted to ~90° together with the humerus. This pattern contrasts with other sprawled taxa. The distinction between non‐fossorial and highly fossorial taxa may reflect differences in forelimb kinematics—namely, alternating versus bilaterally symmetrical forelimb strokes. Highly fossorial moles employ simultaneous, bilateral paddling of the forelimbs while digging and experience extremely large medially directed forces on both sides of the manus from the tunnel walls (Gambaryan et al., 2002; Hisaw, 1923; Lin et al., 2019a, 2019b). At the median plane, the left and right clavicles can contact via the sternum or directly contact one another at the proximodorsal corners, which may help counteract these medially directed reaction forces.

In this study, we did not evaluate scapular strength against the axial compression due to the resolution limits of the CT data for bone thickness. However, compared with non‐talpine eulipotyphlans, which typically possess a flat, thin, disc‐like scapula as in most mammals, talpines exhibit a conspicuously elongated, rod‐like scapula with a reduced scapular spine (Campbell, 1939; Gambaryan et al., 2002; Gaughran, 1954). This morphology suggests that the scapula in talpines may also exhibit markedly elevated resistance to compressive loading. In highly fossorial moles, this condition may represent an adaptation to the forces generated by the m. teres major used in burrowing (Gaughran, 1954; Reed, 1951; Shrivastava, 1963; Wada et al., 2021). Nevertheless, because even semi‐fossorial taxa such as Urotrichus possess a rod‐like scapula, it is possible that a sprawled forelimb posture may inherently produce greater compressive loading on the scapula than a sagittal posture.

The lateral‐thrust digging employed by true moles—used to maintain and extend tunnels—differs markedly from the scratch‐digging employed by golden moles (Chrysochloridae, Afrotheria) and marsupial moles (Notoryctidae, Marsupialia), which alternately stroke their forelimbs to move through loose substrate; and from the chisel‐tooth digging employed by mole rats (Spalacidae, Rodentia); although all of these taxa are highly fossorial (Gambaryan et al., 2005; Nakai & Fujiwara, 2023; Puttick & Jarvis, 1977; Warburton, 2006). Thus, their digging mechanisms diverge markedly, and the lateral‐thrust digging specifically employed by true moles is reflected in the structural robustness of the clavicle.

For Soricidae, locomotor functional adaptations have been evaluated based on whole‐body morphology (Woodman, 2023). In talpines, fossorial adaptations are well‐documented in the humerus (e.g., Meier et al., 2013; Nakai & Yokohata, 2024; Piras et al., 2015; Sánchez‐Villagra et al., 2004; Sansalone et al., 2017) and forelimb musculature (Rose et al., 2013). However, although earlier work (Kistin, 1929) suggested that clavicular morphology may represent an adaptation to lateral compressive forces, this hypothesis has not been quantitatively tested. The present study provides a quantitative evaluation of clavicular morphology in this context and further demonstrates that fossorial adaptations are also expressed in this bone and in other elements of the appendicular skeleton.

Co‐option of clavicles in talpine evolution

This study follows a functional morphological perspective in which anatomical form is interpreted in relation to mechanical function and biological role, while acknowledging that these relationships are not strictly one‐to‐one. Similar anatomical configurations may correspond to different functional outcomes depending on behavioral context, and similar functional demands may be met by alternative morphological solutions. Accordingly, the present interpretations are framed as biomechanically plausible scenarios rather than deterministic mappings from form to function (Bock & von Wahlert, 1965; Lauder, 1995; Rudwick, 1964; Vizcaíno et al., 2024).

In the evolutionary history of mammals, therians are considered to have shifted from an ancestral sprawled forelimb posture to a sagittal posture, while their outgroup, Monotremata, retain the sprawled forelimb posture (Figure 9a: Sereno & McKenna, 1995; Gambaryan & Kielan‐Jaworowska, 1997; Kielan‐Jaworowska & Hurum, 2006; Fujiwara & Hutchinson, 2012). In Monotremata, the clavicle and coracoid articulate firmly with the sternum along the median plane (Gambaryan et al., 2015; Luo, 2015), which can be interpreted as providing structural resistance to the medial component of GRF transmitted to the glenoid through the forelimb (Preuschoft et al., 2007, 2010, 2022; Preuschoft & Gudo, 2005).

In contrast, in therian mammals, including stem‐eulipotyphlans, the articulation between the sternum and the scapula via the coracoid was lost—as also indicated by developmental evidence indicating that this articulation is transiently present but lost during ontogeny in some taxa (Klima & Bangma, 1987)—and the degrees of freedom at the sternoclavicular and claviculoscapular joints increased (Figure 9b). In some lineages—such as perissodactyls, artiodactyls, and cursorial carnivorans—the clavicle is no longer ossified (Hildebrand & Goslow, 2001). This, in turn, enhances the mobility of the scapula relative to the ribcage, enabling an increase in forelimb stride length (Liem et al., 2000).

Thereafter, within therian mammals, including eulipotyphlans, which typically employ sagittal forelimb postures (Figure 9b), Talpinae appear to have secondarily evolved a sprawled forelimb posture with a less flexible sternoclavicular joint (Figure 9c,d). Uropsilinae, which forms the sister group of Talpinae (the “sprawled group”), exhibits relatively high clavicular strength among the “sagittal group” of eulipotyphlans (Figure 7a). The clavicle in Uropsilinae is unlikely to function as a structure resisting axial compression. However, if the stem‐Talpidae possessed relatively high clavicular strength (Figure 1), this preexisting structural condition, which originally did not serve a load‐bearing function, may have facilitated the evolution of Talpinae and was subsequently co‐opted (exaptation: Gould & Vrba, 1982; Pievani & Serrelli, 2011) for resisting axial compression during the transition from sagittal to sprawled forelimb postures. The phylogenetic signal detected in log CS/BW among eulipotyphlans further suggests that clavicular robustness is partly structured by shared ancestry, although this does not in itself demonstrate a specific sequence of trait evolution.

Although Talpinae secondarily evolved a sprawled forelimb posture, their relatively high clavicular strength may have been particularly effective in transmitting the medial component of the GRF acting on the glenoid to the sternum along the median plane. In this context, the clavicle of talpines may functionally parallel the coracoid of monotremes, both acting as structural struts resisting medially directed forces transmitted from the forelimb to the axial skeleton (Figure 9c,d).

Previous studies have suggested that the highly fossorial adaptations evolved independently in two lineages (Scalopini and Talpini) within talpines that employ a sprawled forelimb posture (He et al., 2016, 2021; Shinohara et al., 2003). In Eulipotyphla, both sagittal and sprawled forelimb postures occur across ambulatory, semi‐fossorial, and semi‐aquatic taxa (Figure 1), suggesting that posture alone does not strictly delimit ecological function. In this context, the relatively high clavicular strength observed in stem‐Talpinae (Figures 1 and 6a), which are inferred to exhibit a sprawled (i.e., laterally abducted) forelimb configuration, may represent a preexisting structural condition that is consistent with, and potentially relevant to, later functional shifts in highly fossorial lineages. Accordingly, the interpretation of co‐option (exaptation: Gould & Vrba, 1982; Pievani & Serrelli, 2011) should be regarded as a plausible evolutionary scenario rather than a directly demonstrated process. In derived talpines, this structural configuration may have been associated with the evolution of shorter, stouter, and more laterally oriented clavicles (Figure 9d), consistent with increased specialization for lateral‐thrust digging.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the relatively small sample size should be noted. In particular, it would have been desirable to include more data from semi‐aquatic species within the “sprawled group” and semi‐fossorial species within the “sagittal group.” Future data accumulation will help address this limitation. In addition, some of the available carcass specimens exhibited fractures of bones or joint disarticulation (Table 1), which further limited the number of samples available for analyses of skeletal orientation and strength. However, despite the limited sample size, the clear separation between sagittal and sprawled groups in both orientation and strength metrics suggests that the observed patterns are robust and reflect fundamental differences in forelimb functional organization.

Another limitation concerns that the strength of the scapula and sternum was not analyzed alongside that of the clavicle, although they are likely to play an important role in transmitting forces within the pectoral girdle. Including these elements would have allowed a more comprehensive evaluation of the forces resisted by acting on the entire pectoral girdle. However, as noted above, their structural strengths were not evaluated due to limitations in CT resolution, particularly for the thin, plate‐like scapulae of sagittal taxa. Future studies incorporating higher resolution imaging or alternative analytical approaches will be necessary to fully assess its mechanical contributions.

Studies of electromyography and GRFs during ambulatory locomotion in the “sprawled group,” such as Urotrichus and Neurotrichus, also remain scarce. The accumulation of such data would enable a more detailed functional interpretation of the functional division of labor between the clavicle and the first true rib in supporting body weight (Fujiwara, 2018; Fujiwara et al., 2009) in animals that employ a sprawled (hyper‐abducted) forelimb posture.

Body mass was estimated for part of the specimens using CT‐derived body volume. Therefore, some uncertainty should be acknowledged in the interpretation of allometric relationships and BW‐standardized variables such as CS/BW and RS/BW. Although most estimated values were consistent with published adult body mass ranges for each species (Table S1), the possibility of estimation error cannot be entirely excluded.

The finite element models presented in this study are based on simplified assumptions, including homogeneous material properties and idealized loading conditions, to enable controlled comparisons of structural performance across taxa. In particular, the clavicle model primarily evaluates resistance to axial compressive loading. However, in vivo locomotor behaviors such as humeral rotation digging and lateral‐thrust digging are likely to involve more complex loading regimes, including shear, bending, torsional, and rotational forces. Therefore, the present analyses should be interpreted as estimates of relative structural strength under specific, idealized loading conditions, rather than as reconstructions of the full spectrum of functional loads experienced in vivo.

Digging behaviors such as lateral‐thrust digging are also likely to be highly dynamic and phase‐dependent (Gambaryan et al., 2002; Lin et al., 2019a; Wada et al., 2021). During a single digging cycle, forelimb function likely alternates among thrust, stabilization, and recovery phases, accompanied by varying patterns of muscle activation, including concentric, eccentric, and isometric contractions. These behavioral and muscular dynamics are not explicitly incorporated into the present analyses and remain important topics for future investigation.

Although forelimb function involves the entire biokinematic chain, including distal elements and associated musculature, the present study focuses on the pectoral girdle to isolate primary load‐bearing pathways in a comparative framework.

Future perspectives

As a future direction, it would be important to consider that digging in subterranean environments involves not only peak force transmission but also repetitive mechanical work, metabolic cost, fatigue, ventilatory constraints, and spatially variable substrate properties. The orientation and mechanical role of the clavicle, first true rib, and ribcage may therefore be better understood as part of an integrated system that accommodates both force transmission and repetitive loading under physiological constraints. Comparative studies of fossorial mammals, including analyses of energetic cost and digging mechanics (e.g., Montoya‐Sanhueza et al., 2022, 2024) and forelimb inertial properties (e.g., Kilbourne & Hoffman, 2013), provide useful frameworks for future investigations.

CONCLUSION

The orientations of the scapula, clavicle, and humerus relative to the trunk, as well as the compressive strength of the first true rib and the clavicle along their long axes, were examined bilaterally in 31 extant eulipotyphlan specimens (22 species in 19 genera). Eulipotyphlans could be divided into a “sagittal group” and a “sprawled group” based on humeral orientation. In the former, the scapula is oriented primarily along the transverse plane, whereas in the latter, it is oriented along the parasagittal plane. In addition, the clavicle in the former exhibits a wide range of orientations, whereas in the latter, it is oriented mainly along the horizontal plane. The strength of the first true rib showed a strong correlation with body mass across eulipotyphlans regardless of forelimb posture, suggesting that it is consistent with a role in load transmission from the forelimb to the vertebral column within a broader locomotor context. Furthermore, the “sagittal group” and “sprawled group” differed in clavicular strength, being lower in the former and higher in the latter. This suggests that the clavicle in the “sprawled group” is consistent with a potential exaptation toward a strut‐like role resisting the medially directed component of GRFs transmitted from the manus under a sprawled posture. This tendency is even more pronounced in highly fossorial members of the “sprawled group,” which employ lateral‐thrust digging. These findings highlight that the evolution of locomotor function in eulipotyphlans is best understood not only in terms of morphology, but also in terms of the directional flow of forces through the musculoskeletal system.
