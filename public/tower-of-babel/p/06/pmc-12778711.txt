Can Aneurysm Wall Radiomics Help Predict Rupture Risk?
Authors: Robert W. Regenhardt, Eytan Raz
Published: 2023

Approximately 3% of people harbor unruptured intracranial aneurysms, 1 and incidental aneurysms are increasingly identified with advances and widespread availability of neuroimaging. Despite modern devices and advanced techniques, the treatment of intracranial aneurysms is not without risk; careful consideration of the natural history risk is essential to make a decision to treat an unruptured aneurysm. Risk factors for future rupture include history of subarachnoid hemorrhage, aneurysm size, aneurysm location, multiple aneurysms, sex, age, and smoking status. 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 Among the most used risk stratification scores to date, the Population, Hypertension, Age, Size of aneurysm, Earlier hemorrhage from another aneurysm, Site of aneurysm (PHASES) score was developed from a pooled analysis of 6 prospective cohort studies with individual patient data, especially weighted on International Study on Unruptured Intracranial Aneurysms (ISUIA) and Unruptured Cerebral Aneurysm Study (UCAS) populations. 3 , 7 , 8 However, no scoring system captures all relevant variables, and none adequately capture aneurysm morphology and dynamic changes. Furthermore, an apparent size–rupture paradox exists since available natural history data suggest a low rupture risk for small incidental aneurysms, but the median size of ruptured aneurysms is small. 9 As such, better indicators of rupture risk are needed.

Vessel wall imaging with black‐blood magnetic resonance imaging was first established as a method to evaluate carotid artery atherosclerotic plaque, but has grown in popularity over the past decade to include the evaluation of a multitude of intracranial and extracranial pathologies. 10 There are several technical considerations central to its implementation and interpretation: blood flow suppression, spatial resolution, cerebrospinal fluid suppression, and acquisition time. 10 , 11 Using gadolinium, this imaging approach allows the assessment of aneurysm wall enhancement. 12 , 13 Wall enhancement has previously been associated with unstable aneurysms prone to rupture, possibly related to inflammatory processes and proliferation of vasa vasorum. 14 , 15

Instead of treating medical images as pictures for manual interpretation, radiomics quantifies them for first‐, second‐, and higher‐order statistics. 16 For image interpretation, the tools of radiomics have much potential to aid standard approaches and augment clinical decision making. With growing clinical applications, radiomics is often conceptualized as the assessment of textures by analyzing signal intensities. 17 There are several groups in which radiomics features can be divided and defined: size, shape, statistical (such as intensity histogram), relationships between voxels (such as gray‐level co‐occurrence matrix and other derived textures), textures from filtered images, and fractal features. 18 The idea that “hidden” information within radiologic images can be unlocked using this approach motivates enthusiasm for the field. Serving as novel biomarkers, radiomics features and other clinical data can be correlated with diagnoses and prognoses.

In the accompanying article, 19 Veeturi et al analyzed 126 intracranial aneurysms in a retrospective, multicenter cohort analysis. All aneurysms underwent magnetic resonance imaging with and without contrast, and radiomics features were extracted from both. A random forest model was used to identify aneurysms with high risk on the basis of the 5‐year PHASES score. Seventy‐five radiomics features were different, comparing high‐ to low‐risk aneurysms. On a 3 Tesla data set, the radiomics model had good performance for predicting high risk, with 90% accuracy, 86% sensitivity, and 92% specificity. On an external 3 Tesla data set, it had moderate performance, with 88% accuracy, 50% sensitivity, and 95% specificity. A multivariable analysis identified the radiomics features with the greatest importance, such as neighboring gray tone difference matrix busyness and strength on contrast enhanced magnetic resonance imaging, neighboring gray tone difference matrix total energy on noncontrast magnetic resonance imaging, and total energy, median, and variance from the difference of with and without contrast. The authors concluded that radiomics could be used as a tool for quantifying aneurysm wall enhancement.

Radiomics seeks to improve upon and complement the current standard of interpreting aneurysm wall enhancement. While there have been recent advances in the 3‐dimensional morphological assessment of aneurysms and their wall enhancement, 20 , 21 the majority of routine clinical interpretation is through manual, qualitative, and subjective assessment. In contrast, radiomics is performed in an automated, quantitative, and objective fashion. 22 Given the particular subjectivity involved in wall enhancement interpretation, 23 it may be especially suited to the application of radiomics. Objectivity is a key strength of radiomics features, not biased by clinical history or experiences from clinical training and/or practice. Furthermore, the automation of radiomics also affords a decisive advantage. The manual implementation and interpretation of vessel wall imaging are involved processes, which can expend excessive resources and time. Quantitative data are essential for further research in aneurysm wall enhancement, allowing more granular data for sophisticated analyses. Ultimately, a more precise estimation of quantitative risk is the key goal for patient counseling.

The clinical translation of this approach may be more straightforward than one would think. Clinicians caring for patients with aneurysms could order vessel wall imaging as per existing processes. Then, software developed to segment the vessel wall and quantify radiomics features could be used. After clinicians input other known clinical variables related to aneurysm risk, the software could generate a report quantifying risk that could be used for patient counseling. This holds the promise of improving precision medicine for individual patients. Current estimates of risk are usually based on outdated scores that fail to capture all relevant variables and typically include only aneurysm size and location but no other important features determined by imaging.

While the accompanying article is promising, future research is needed before this technique reaches prime time. The authors used the PHASES score to define risk in a retrospective cohort of patients, which represents a significant limitation. While evaluating retrospectively the aneurysms that ruptured on follow‐up would be ideal for such a study, it is understandable that the rarity of these events would make this kind of analysis impossible to perform. In addition, another limitation is that the analysis relies on radiomics features extracted from manual segmentations of the aneurysm walls, which could result in bias and complicates the process of clinical translation. Further automation would have significant benefits to increase objectivity and reduce the time to interpretation.

A key next step will be evaluating the radiomics of aneurysm wall enhancement prospectively in large‐scale, longitudinal studies of patients without clearly high‐risk features in whom early treatment is not pursued. Important end points could include aneurysm growth on serial imaging and aneurysm rupture. Histological correlates would also help ensure radiomics features are biologically meaningful. This patient population stands to benefit most from this technique, since a better understanding of risk could inform more aggressive early treatment approaches or reassure more conservative monitoring with serial imaging.
