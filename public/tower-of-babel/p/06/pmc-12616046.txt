Microbial communities experienced during early development shape the host immune system and epigenome
Authors: Amy L. Tan, Stephanie F. Hendricks, Elliana E. Carter, Katherine M. Buckley, Marie E. Strader
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryShaping of the epigenomic landscape by local environments during early life stages could initiate adaptive developmental plasticity, allowing organisms to optimize their phenotypes for local conditions. Elevated seawater temperature can increase bacterial loads and pathogen spread, making it imperative to understand how physiological and molecular traits of marine organisms are impacted, especially the immune system. Experimentally manipulated temperatures and microbial environments during embryogenesis resulted in changes in larval morphology, immune cell development, and the epigenome of the purple sea urchin (Strongylocentrotus purpuratus). Development amid high microbial richness led to increased chromatin accessibility, predominantly in promoter regions, whereas elevated temperature altered very few chromatin regions but was associated with more differentially expressed genes. Larvae raised in high microbial richness also exhibited delayed immune system development. Overall, we show that early developmental environments strongly influence whole-organism and molecular phenotypes, with early exposure to microbes altering the host immune system and epigenome.

Introduction

Environmental conditions influence organismal morphology, behavior, and physiology across all life stages.1,2 In marine systems, temperature has a profound impact on ecosystem functioning, biogeochemical cycling, and organismal physiology, often mediated by microbial processes.3,4,5,6 Seawater microbial content varies in both species richness and community composition by latitude, such that annual sea surface temperature is the greatest predictor of taxonomic diversity.7,8 Intermediate water temperatures (15°C–20°C) typically harbor the greatest microbial richness,9,10 although distinct ecosystems also harbor unique microbiota.11,12 Localized regions are additionally subject to small-scale temporary environmental shifts such as upwelling and marine heatwaves (MHWs), which directly and indirectly impact both biotic and abiotic parameters of marine ecosystems.13,14,15,16,17,18 MHWs can create extensive variation in microbial communities, including increases in pathogen virulence, host disease susceptibility, and altered microbial communities.7,9,10,19,20,21 Growing evidence suggests that microbial cues orchestrate organismal development,6 metamorphosis,22,23,24,25 and epigenetics.26 Thus, there is a critical need to understand how temperatures associated with MHWs and changes in microbial communities interact to impact host phenotypes and responses to the environment.

The purple sea urchin, Strongylocentrotus purpuratus, is a marine invertebrate and basal deuterostome with a large population size spanning a broad latitudinal gradient. Despite its large geographic range, there is limited population structure due to long dispersal distances of its planktonic larvae that enable gene flow and high standing genetic variation on which selection can act.27,28 As a result, S. purpuratus shows evidence of local adaptation, as well as genetic variation for adaptive developmental plasticity in embryos and larvae.29,30,31 Larvae show a high degree of adaptive plasticity in response to both biotic and abiotic factors. When food is scarce, echinoderm larvae grow longer arms to facilitate feeding.30,31 Additionally, combinations of temperature and pH can lead to phenotypic plasticity in larval body size and skeletal structures.32,33,34,35,36 The microbial communities in seawater can also influence sea urchin larval development. Specifically, larvae of the green sea urchin, S. droebachiensis, exhibit different growth patterns when reared in seawater from different geographic locations.37 Developmental delays and reduced growth can also be caused by abiotic stressors, including temperature and pH, both directly increasing larval mortality and lengthening the duration of the vulnerable larval stage.31,38,39 Thus, both evolutionary processes and plasticity play a role in how sea urchins respond to environmental disturbances and cues.

Within marine ecosystems, microbes and their associated hosts form a variety of relationships ranging from pathogenic to symbiotic. Throughout development, S. purpuratus are directly exposed to microbes in the surrounding seawater. However, many foundational studies of larval development have been conducted in seawater sterilized with antibiotics, UV, and/or filters, thus eliminating potentially important influences of bacteria on larval growth. Recent studies indicate that, in addition to growth, environmental microbes may shape the development of larval immune systems.40,41,42 Considering the devastating ecological and economic effects wrought by marine pathogens,43,44 it is imperative to integrate microbial influences in our understanding of how organisms respond to the shifting global climate.

Here, we investigated the combined impact of elevated seawater temperature and environmental microbial content on S. purpuratus larval morphology, chromatin state, gene expression, and immune system development. We hypothesize that environmentally driven changes to the epigenomic landscape allow larvae to rapidly adjust their developmental trajectory and immune cell development. Larvae were raised in combinations of ambient (14°C) or elevated (18°C) temperatures in seawater containing either low or high microbial richness (LMR or HMR, respectively). Results indicated that pluteus larvae (5 days post-fertilization [dpf]) grown in LMR were larger than their counterparts cultured in HMR conditions. Temperature did not influence growth when larvae were grown in LMR seawater, but elevated seawater temperature counteracted the observed growth phenotype in HMR conditions (i.e., larvae grown at 18°C in HMR were larger than those cultured at 14°C in HMR). Analysis of ATAC-seq and RNA-seq data indicated that chromatin accessibility and gene expression, respectively, were altered by both temperature and environmental microbial richness, but in distinctly different ways. HMR conditions yielded larvae with increased open chromatin, whereas elevated temperature induced extensive changes in gene expression. For most genes, HMR seawater and elevated temperature differentially affected gene expression, indicating that the larval molecular landscape is intricately regulated at multiple levels in response to the environment during early development. Finally, larvae cultured in HMR or at the elevated temperature exhibited fewer immune cells, which could compromise pathogen response. Together, these results show that the environment experienced in early development alters chromatin accessibility and gene expression, resulting in variation in larval size and immune system maturity.

Results and discussion

Developmental temperature and microbial communities shape larval morphology

To determine if temperature and microbial richness synergistically impact larval development, S. purpuratus embryos were cultured at either ambient (14°C) or elevated (18°C) temperature in artificial seawater (ASW) that was either obtained from aquaria housing adult sea urchins (high microbial richness, HMR) or filtered (0.2 μm filter) to reduce microbial content (low microbial richness, LMR) (Figure 1A). Results indicate that HMR seawater had greater microbial richness, load, and diversity relative to LMR (Figures 1B–1E). Temperature did not significantly affect microbial diversity of the seawater samples (Figures 1B–1E). The microbial communities in all seawater samples were largely composed of common marine microbes distributed across 19 known classes and 98 known families (Table S1).47,48,49 Regardless of temperature, both LMR and HMR samples contained a high relative abundance of Alpha- and Gammaproteobacteria (Figure 1E). Other studies show that Gammaproteobacteria are highly prevalent in the gut microbiome of adult sea urchins, especially those fed solely on seaweed.50 Bacteroidia were also present in both LMR and HMR, although their relative abundance was greater in HMR seawater (Figure 1E). Fusobacteriia and Rhodothermia were present in HMR samples, but nearly absent from LMR (Figure 1E). Further, LMR and HMR samples each contained high levels of families Rhodobacteraceae, Alteromonadaceae, and Flavobacteriaceae (Figure S1D), each of which are present in the initial-stage core microbiome of echinoderm larvae,51,52,53 and are commonly found in seawater collected from wild sea urchin habitats.47,54 Finally, microbes in the LMR seawater exhibited greater relative abundances of family Sphingomonadaceae, whereas in HMR there were higher relative abundances of Cryomorphaceae and Franciscellaceae (Figure S1D).

Because the LMR seawater was filter-sterilized (0.2 μm) prior to use, the presence of microbes in these samples suggests that they are transferred from adult sea urchins during the spawning process. The tank water sourced for the HMR cultures contained recently wild-caught adult sea urchins, feces, and kelp provided as food, all of which serve as potential sources to seed these microbial communities. Together, these results demonstrate that the microbial communities within the LMR and HMR cultures were distinct and not significantly influenced by temperature.

The effects of temperature on S. purpuratus embryogenesis have been well-described: embryos develop more rapidly in warmer seawater.55,56 However, the influence of the microbial environment is less understood, especially since many studies of larval growth and development utilize sterilized seawater. Therefore, we measured body length and area of larvae (5 dpf) to determine how temperature and environmental microbial richness influence larval morphology. On average, larvae cultured in LMR seawater were 35 μm (18%) longer than those grown in HMR conditions (pANOVA < 0.001; Figures 2A and 2B). In the LMR condition, temperature did not affect larval body length (pANOVA = 1; Figures 2A and 2B). However, in HMR, larvae reared at 18°C were 17 μm longer (5%) than those grown at 14°C (pANOVA = 0.022; Figures 2A and 2B). Larval body area showed similar growth trends as larval length; larvae grown in LMR seawater had significantly larger body area than those cultured in HMR (pANOVA < 0.001; Figures 2A and 2C). Since microbial communities had a greater impact on larval size than seawater temperature, we used a machine-learning framework (Coracle57) to identify specific microbial taxa that correlate with larval morphology. This identified the presence of Sulfitobacter (family Rhodobacteraceae) as strongly associated with body length (p = 0.004, adj. R2 = 0.5300) (Figure 2D). This is consistent with previous reports that suggest Sulfitobacter spp. promote growth from embryos to juveniles in the sea cucumber Apostichopus japonicus.53

The smaller body size of larvae raised in HMR seawater may reflect a simple developmental delay such that only the rate of development is affected. If this is the case, we would expect that, given more time, these larvae would grow to reach the size of their counterparts cultured in LMR. Alternatively, this delay could be related to the overall increase in microbial biomass,32 since the total microbial load was higher in the HMR condition (Figure 1C). Finally, larvae cultured in HMR seawater may specifically allocate energy toward processes other than body size. Any of these hypotheses could potentially be induced in response to metabolites produced by various microbial taxa. For example, predicted functions of sea urchin microbiota revealed an abundance of KEGG Orthology categories related to metabolism, including carbohydrate, amino acid, lipid, and energy metabolism.47,49 Further, alterations in resource allocation, even to the extent of differential growth in arms vs. body size, are observed when larvae are raised with different food availability,58,59 so it is possible that the smaller overall body size in HMR larvae reflects energy usage elsewhere. Taken together, the bacterial taxa present in our samples reflect those found in the natural environment of purple sea urchins and may directly lead to the observed growth differences.

Microbial content causes extensive changes in chromatin state, whereas temperature alters gene expression

To identify the molecular mechanisms underlying these morphological changes, larvae (5 dpf) were collected for ATAC-seq and RNA-seq analysis to examine changes in chromatin accessibility and gene expression, respectively. Analysis of the ATAC-seq data indicated that chromatin accessibility was more strongly affected by environmental microbes than temperature (Figures 3A and 3B; Table S2), with 73% of the variance explained by experimental treatments (Figure 3C). We identified 4,816 regions that were differentially accessible in larvae cultured in HMR vs. LMR seawater (differentially accessible regions [DARs]; FDR/adjusted p-value ≤ 0.05; absolute log2 fold change ≥ 0.20). Notably, 97% (4,668) of the ATAC-seq peaks were more accessible in larvae grown in HMR conditions (Figure 3A). The increased chromatin accessibility of larvae grown in HMR seawater may represent a molecular landscape prepared for rapid response to the surrounding environment, as accessible chromatin can be transcribed without the need to first alter the chromatin configuration. In contrast, temperature significantly affected chromatin accessibility across only 134 regions (FDR/adjusted p-value ≤0.05; absolute log2 fold change ≥ 0.20, Figure 3B). Elevated temperature (18°C) correlated with primarily closed DARs (98% of DARs were closed compared to larvae grown at 14°C; Figure 3B). In contrast, temperature influenced gene expression more than the microbial environment. Analysis of RNA-seq data identified 5,607 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in larvae cultured at elevated (18°C) vs. ambient (14°C) temperature and only 2,678 DEGs between larvae grown in HMR vs. LMR (FDR/adjusted p-value ≤ 0.05; absolute log2 fold change ≥ 0.5; Figures 3D and 3E; Table S3). Principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) confirmed that most of the variation between samples (74%) was explained by experimental treatments (Figure 3F).

We also examined GO term enrichment of genes in which DARs specifically occurred within the promoter regions (≤ 1 kb from the transcription start site [TSS]). This allowed the examination of functional groupings of genes that experienced changes in chromatin accessibility based on environmental conditions. Here, significantly enriched GO terms were only detectable when comparing larvae grown in HMR vs. LMR (likely due to the low number of DARs regulated by temperature). GO term enrichment indicated that larvae cultured in HMR seawater exhibited less promoter accessibility in genes related to “DNA metabolic process (GO:0006259)” (Figure S4A); the same term was enriched in the RNA-seq dataset, associated with genes that had lower expression in larvae at 18°C but higher expression in larvae in HMR (Figure 4D). In contrast, there was higher accessibility in promoters of genes related to protein DNA complexes, structural constituents of chromatin, structural molecule activity, protein dimerization, and DNA binding. Several of these terms were also enriched in the DEGs. “Protein-DNA complex (GO:0000786; GO:0032993)” and “structural constituent of chromatin (GO:0030527; GO:0046982)” were enriched in the temperature-associated DEGs, particularly with genes exhibiting lower expression at 18°C. “Structural molecule activity (GO:0005198)” was associated with genes showing significantly lower expression in larvae at 18°C but higher expression in larvae in HMR (Figure 4D). The GO terms “protein dimerization activity (GO:0046983)” and “DNA binding (GO:0003677)” were enriched in the promoter accessibility dataset, but not in the DEG dataset. Although both chromatin state and gene expression were altered by temperature and microbial conditions, likely resulting in extensive downstream functional changes, we saw more extensive changes to chromatin accessibility due to environmental microbes, while temperature caused more effects on gene expression.

Chromatin accessibility does not predict differential gene expression levels

To determine if there was a relationship between chromatin accessibility and transcription, we compared the ATAC-seq and RNA-seq datasets. We examined gene expression changes based on chromatin accessibility near the transcription start site (TSS; defined as ≤ 1 kb from TSS). Open chromatin closer to the TSS was associated with higher base mean expression across all genes, regardless of whether they were differentially expressed (Figures 5A–5C). Narrowing in on the promoter regions of genes, we saw that the base mean expression of all genes was higher (1.2×) when the promoter was accessible, compared to genes with inaccessible promoters (pT-test < 0.001, Figure 5E). However, DEGs showed the opposite pattern. DEGs exhibited higher expression fold changes when the promoter was inaccessible (1.2× - 2.2× lower fold change in DEGs with accessible promoters; pT-test < 0.001; Figures 5F and 5G). Some DEGs had no accessible chromatin (i.e., had no peaks in the ATAC-seq dataset); these genes were transcribed at similar levels to those where only the promoter was inaccessible (Figure S4B). This indicates a general trend in which closed chromatin is associated with genes that have higher variability in expression. One possible explanation is that chromatin might be open in genes that require a more stable/constant level of expression, thus reducing how much change would be seen under different conditions. It should also be noted that promoter accessibility does not directly imply transcription factor binding.

Gene expression can also be influenced by chromatin accessibility in more distant genic regions.61 We therefore characterized the location of DARs within genes. The majority of DARs (47.5%) were within 1 kb of the TSS when larvae were grown in HMR (Figure 5D). In contrast, most DARs attributed to elevated temperature were located in either intronic or distal intergenic regions, accounting for 69.4% of all DARs, and only 10.5% were within 1 kb of the TSS (Figure 5H). Some genes contained multiple DARs. In larvae grown in HMR seawater, almost 70% of the DARs were in unique genes (genes with only one DAR). The remaining 30% of DARs were found in genes that had two or more DARs across different genomic regions. If a gene had multiple DARs, these regions were not necessarily uniformly altered in accessibility by the environmental treatments.

Transcription of genes associated with chromosome organization, chromosomal regions, and structural constituents of chromatin was altered by both temperature and microbial content (Figures 5I, S3A, and S3B). Similarly, both environmental temperature and microbes altered gene expression related to nucleic acid metabolism or transcription and translation (Figures 5J–5L, S3A, and S3B). In particular, many of the genes associated with these chromatin states and transcriptional functions had lower expression in larvae raised at 18°C, although it is unclear whether this is the cause or result of the growth differences between larvae at different temperatures.

Several factors may underlie the observed discrepancies between chromatin state and gene expression, including a mismatch in timing between chromatin state changes and mRNA persistence (i.e., inaccessible chromatin at the time of collection was previously accessible for the transcription of longer-lived mRNAs). Although closed chromatin may enable more precise transcriptional control,62 many complex factors influence gene expression.61,63 Changes to histone modifications and DNA-histone interactions can last from microseconds to days.64,65 Here, chromatin state did not completely explain gene expression levels, although genes with accessible promoters were expressed at higher base mean levels than those without (Figure 5E). It should be noted that the ATAC-seq and RNA-seq samples were generated from pooled, whole larval samples, and differences at the tissue level may not be detectable. It is also possible that the current chromatin state reflects genes that are to be imminently transcribed. Additionally, the relative accessibility of chromatin in the HMR larvae could represent an earlier developmental stage, as cells accumulate heterochromatin as differentiation progresses.66 The location of open chromatin and DARs in our data mirror what has been seen at earlier developmental stages in both S. purpuratus and Paracentrotus lividus (Figure S4C), but many of these regions are highly dynamic—only remaining accessible for one or two consecutive developmental stages67,68—and there may be a great deal of variation in the specific regions accessible at any given stage. Regardless, these results show a strong environmental contribution to chromatin accessibility, an interaction between the epigenome and environment that is not well-understood in all organisms.

Environmental conditions alter both development and metabolism, impacting larval growth

At 5 dpf, larvae raised in LMR were larger than their siblings in HMR conditions. Seawater temperature only influenced the growth of larvae in HMR, such that larvae were larger when reared at 18°C (Figures 2A–2C). These size differences could reflect a developmental delay induced by the environment or increased energy demands caused by certain conditions. The dGRN in S. purpuratus is a well-characterized network of 192 genes that encode transcription factors, signal transduction pathway components, and effectors with roles in early specification and development of the embryo.69,70 This framework provides a unique strategy to examine how developmental patterns may have been altered in response to environmental conditions. Of the 177 dGRN genes expressed in the larvae (5 dpf) collected here, 70 were differentially expressed based on environmental conditions (Figure 6A; Table S3). Differential expression was observed in 62 of the dGRN genes between larvae grown at 18°C vs. 14°C, the majority of which exhibited higher expression levels (44 genes) in elevated temperatures (Figure 6A). Several genes involved in mesodermal patterning, including members of the hedgehog signaling pathway (e.g., hh, smo (LOC576047), and ptch1 (LOC593924)71) and skeletogenesis (e.g., fgfr72) were expressed at higher levels in larvae grown at elevated temperature. The expression of these genes was not significantly altered by microbial conditions, despite microbial conditions having a larger impact on larval body size (Figures 2B and 2C). While the increase in overall larval length suggests a corresponding increase in length of both the body and arm skeletal rods, here we measured total larval length (apex to arm tip), which does not fully capture total variation in skeletal size/morphology. Increased expression levels of genes involved in patterning non-skeletogenic mesoderm was also observed in larvae cultured at 18°C, including the Notch signaling antagonist numb (LOC764088).73 Larvae reared at 18°C exhibited higher expression levels of several transcription factors that regulate endodermal development, including markers of the mid- and hindgut (blimp1/krox, gatae),74,75,76 as well as the oral region (gsc; gemin2 (sip1/LOC585551)).77,78

Only 18 dGRN genes were expressed at lower levels in larvae raised at 18°C (Figure 6A). These include tbr1 (LOC586389), a key skeletogenic gene, and hesC (LOC592057), the global repressor of skeletogenesis genes, including tbr1.79 Two transcription factors with key roles in pigment cell differentiation were expressed at lower levels in larvae grown at elevated temperature: gcm, which promotes pigment cell differentiation,80 and six1, which positively regulates gcm expression.81 Other transcripts present at lower levels include snail, which regulates epithelial-mesenchymal transitions and T4 hormone,82,83 and nanos2, which accumulates in primordial germ cells.84 Finally, the expression of genes associated with neuroectoderm and neural patterning (wnt8, eve, and foxq2)85,86 was lower in larvae raised at 18°C. Microbial exposure was associated with the differential expression of 29 dGRN genes (Figure 6A). Larvae raised in HMR conditions exhibited increased expression of delta (which regulates the expression of gatae and gcm) and hesC (LOC592057; which regulates the expression of delta).81,87

Metabolic activity and demand increase with larval size as development progresses.88 Therefore, we investigated expression levels of genes broadly associated with metabolism based on the manual curation of the S. purpuratus genome (1,792 genes total).60 Among these, 43% (767 DEGs) had altered expression based on temperature and microbial content (Figure 6B; Table S3). Temperature differences accounted for 60% of the DEGs, whereas microbial content affected 27%; the remaining 13% of DEGs associated with metabolism were altered by the combination of environmental conditions. Several ATP-processing related genes (pmca, serca) exhibited higher expression in larvae grown at 18°C but lower expression in larvae in HMR. The cytochrome P450 subunit cyp51 was among a handful of genes that showed lower expression in larvae grown at 18°C and in HMR seawater, although it is unclear whether CYP51 retains its functions of catalyzing sterol formation in echinoderms.89

ABC transporters have a variety of cellular functions, including signaling, maintaining mitochondrial and lysosomal homeostasis, and potassium channel regulation, as well as sensing and eliminating environmental toxins.90,91 abcb1, which is expressed earlier in embryonic development,90 was expressed at higher levels in larvae grown at 18°C but had lower expression in larvae cultured in HMR. On the other hand, abcc5a, which is expressed in mesodermal precursors later in development,90 had the opposite pattern of differential expression (i.e., lower in larvae at 18°C but higher in larvae in HMR). As body size and protein content of growing larvae increase, so do metabolic demands.88 However, differences in metabolic activity occur after feeding commences, with the expression of key metabolic genes remaining similar prior to feeding, regardless of size differences.92,93,94,95 Environmental conditions impose varying energy demands on organisms, with conditions such as high temperature, low pH, and other stressors generally increasing metabolism.93,96,97,98,99 The observed changes in metabolic gene expression may, therefore, reflect the energy required to maintain homeostasis and continue development as external temperatures and microbial content vary.

To link chromatin accessibility with transcriptional regulation, we identified DNA motifs that were enriched in open chromatin regions and compared these motifs to known transcription factor binding sites. In total, 49 unique sequence motifs were significantly enriched in open chromatin regions, 22% (11/49) of which were similar to known eukaryotic transcription factor binding sites (Figure 6C; Table S5). Sequence motifs that resembled Sox and Zinc-finger family transcription factor binding sites were enriched in accessible chromatin of larvae from all four treatments. At least seven classes of Sox genes have been identified in echinoderms that regulate a variety of developmental functions.100 Although there are over 300 Zinc-finger transcription factors identified in the S. purpuratus genome, their functions in echinoderms remain largely unknown.101 An enrichment of putative transcription factor binding sites for FIGLA and MSANTD was detected in open chromatin of larvae cultured at 14°C, however, sea urchins lack clear orthologs of these transcription factors. Accessible chromatin in larvae grown in 14°C LMR was enriched for sequence motifs corresponding to predicted binding sites of three transcription factor families: 1) Nuclear Factor I/A (Nfia), which is associated with nervous system development102; 2) Regulatory Factor X (Rfx), which regulates cilia development103; 3) the metal-responsive transcription factor Mtf, which regulates metal-related cell stress and interacts with NF-κB to mediate immune responses.104,105 Binding sites for Tcf, which is downstream of Wnt signaling, were enriched in the accessible chromatin of larvae in 14°C HMR, as were binding sites for the Krüppel-like factor (Klf) family. Klf, which is represented by eight paralogs in S. purpuratus and regulates a wide variety of cellular functions,106,107 also had enriched binding sites in the accessible chromatin of larvae in 18°C HMR.

The expression changes of genes in the dGRN, the accessibility of transcription factor binding sites, and the expression of transcription factor genes (Figure 2D) indicate that skeletogenesis, neurogenesis, and many regulatory signaling pathway patterns were altered by the environmental conditions. Temperature and microbial content also caused dramatic changes in larval metabolism and together, these dGRN and metabolism network changes likely result in the observed phenotypic differences.

Microbial exposure alters the development of the immune system without activating an immune response

Exposure to environmental microbes during early embryogenesis may influence the development of the larval immune system, but this is usually examined in the context of the microbiome.108,109 Less is known about whether or how the microbial environment influences embryogenesis prior to the onset of feeding and establishment of the gut microbiome or how the internal and external microbiota influence immune system development in invertebrates.110,111 Additionally, elevated temperatures often lead to organismal stress,38 which may further disrupt the proper development of the cellular immune system. To assess the potential impact of temperature and environmental microbial richness on larval immune system development, we quantified pigment cell numbers (Figures 7A and S5A). Pigment cells are a specialized population of cells with immunological functions, including the production of Echinochrome A, which has been shown to exhibit antimicrobial activity.112,113 We also measured the width of the midgut epithelium (Figures 7A and S5B), as S. purpuratus larvae exhibit a robust inflammatory response that includes the thickening of gut epithelial cells to constrict midgut volume in response to specific pathogenic bacteria.114 Larvae cultured in LMR had higher numbers of pigment cells than larvae in HMR when corrected for larval body size (pDunn’s < 0.01, Figure 7B). Smaller larvae generally had fewer pigment cells, even when the pigment cell counts were adjusted for body length. Accordingly, larvae raised in HMR at ambient temperature (14°C) were the smallest (Figures 2A–2C) and had the fewest pigment cells (Figure 7B). However, larvae in 18°C LMR had significantly fewer pigment cells than their siblings in 14°C LMR (pDunn’s = 0.02, Figure 7B), even though the larvae in 18°C had a slightly larger body size (when both length and area are considered). There was a corresponding decrease in the expression of many pigment cell markers and precursors in 18°C larvae (Figures 7F and 7G), although it is unclear whether these changes in gene expression are the cause or effect of the lowered pigment cell counts. It should be noted that the pigment cell counts were generated based on cell morphology and the presence of the characteristic red granules. The activation of the larval immune system also induces a stereotypic thickening of the gut epithelial cells. If the reduced number of pigment cells was due to basal immune activity, this would likely also be apparent within the gut. Therefore, we measured the thickness of the midgut epithelium in larvae (5 dpf) raised in all four treatments. Larvae raised in 14°C LMR had a thicker midgut epithelium (on average, 16% thicker) than the other three environmental treatments (corrected for body length; pDunn’s < 0.03, Figure 7D), correlating with the larger body size and higher pigment cell counts found in this group. Taken together, we speculate that the thicker midgut epithelium is unlikely to represent a unique immune response, but rather gut thickness is likely due to differences in growth/developmental rate.

Using the machine-learning framework Coracle,57 we investigated whether specific microbes within the environment correlated with the measured metrics of immune system development. There was a positive and significant correlation between pigment cell counts, midgut epithelial thickness, and the counts of Sulfitobacter (family Rhodobacteraceae) (Figures 7C and 7E). Similar to larval body size (Figure 2D), high counts of Sulfitobacter were associated with higher pigment cell counts (p = 0.006, adj. R2 = 0.5348) and thicker midgut epithelium (p = 0.021, adj. R2 = 0.4050) in the LMR larvae. Therefore, relative high abundances of Sulfitobacter in the environment may promote larval growth, including the development of the immune system. Members of Rhodobacteraceae are mainly photo- and chemoheterotrophs known for their involvement in various metabolic processes, including the production of secondary metabolites.116 Thus, these metabolic products may contribute to the observed growth differences in LMR and HMR larvae, although additional causal experiments would be necessary to validate the influence of either Sulfitobacter or Rhodobacteraceae in larval growth. However, overall HMR in the environment delays the development of the larval immune system.

In addition to morphological evidence of changes in immune system development, we observed changes in the expression of immune system genes. Examined genes (234 DEGs) included cell markers, receptors associated with pathogen recognition, inflammation markers, complement proteins, transcription factors, and other signal mediators (Table S3).60,117,118,119 The majority of these (167/234) exhibited altered expression due to temperature, with a smaller number of genes (42/234) affected only by microbial richness (Figure 7F; Table S3). This reflects the overall changes in patterns of gene expression changes such that the majority of DEGs were altered by temperature (Figures 3E, 4A, and 4B). Fewer immune genes responded to both temperature and microbial richness (25/234; Table S3). The expression of pigment cell precursors and markers was generally decreased in larvae grown at 18°C and in HMR, which correlates with the lower pigment cell counts in these larvae (Figures 7B and 7G). From these results, we see that elevated temperature and environmental microbial richness are associated with extensive shifts in immune system gene expression, including the down-regulation of some immune cell precursors. This, combined with the reduced pigment cell counts, indicates that elevated temperature and increased microbial richness negatively influence immune system development.

Overall, these findings are consistent with other recent work, which highlights the influence of microbes on the developing larval immune system. Rearing S. purpuratus embryos in microbially inoculated seawater attenuated subsequent larval immune responses to the pathogenic Vibrio lentus.40 Similar microbially driven differences in viral resistance were seen in the oyster, Crassostrea gigas, and it is possible that these immune advantages could be transferred transgenerationally. Immune benefits, as well as microbially-associated DNA methylation changes were conferred through the F2 generation.41,42 Marine microbial content may shape both an individual’s response to pathogens and also the epigenome,26 leading to immune system implications for future generations and presenting intriguing avenues for future work.

Limitations of the study

In this study, the primary influence on larval development was a microbially rich environment (Figures 2, 3, and 7). Consequently, one limitation of our study is that microbial communities were seeded from adult sea urchins maintained in artificial seawater, rather than a natural seawater environment. Although many of the microbes present in the HMR conditions reflect those found in environments native to purple sea urchins, this artificial setup likely reduces the overall complexity and may not fully recapitulate the influence of microbes on development. Additionally, the data collected here were samples of pooled, whole larvae, which may obscure the tissue-level resolution of chromatin structure and gene expression. The single time point of these samples also complicates disentangling effects caused by developmental asynchrony; future experiments should address the window of development in which microbial exposure shapes developmental timing. Finally, it is not clear if or how different numbers of pigment cells affect the subsequent larval immune responses. To address this open question, future studies should investigate how larvae grown in different environmental conditions respond to specific immune challenges.

Conclusions

There is an increasing understanding of the importance of how the holobiont shapes nearly all aspects of host biological function, from epigenetics and development to the function of organ systems.26,120 However, much of this work considers the microbiome that is directly associated, either internal or external, with the organism. This study investigated the effect of environmental microbial content, in which interactions may be transient but also influence the establishment of the internal microbiome. Taken as a whole, these molecular and morphological responses point toward a system-wide response to the early developmental environment and emphasize the importance of microbes even before the establishment of a stable microbiome.

Immune system priming through exposure to environmental microbes could be crucial for a rapid and effective response to pathogens.40,121,122 It is unclear how the stress caused by elevated temperature might affect infection outcomes. Developmental and phenotypic plasticity are crucial means for organisms to respond to local environmental conditions. The regulation of plasticity via the epigenome could help explain how rapid adjustment to immediate environmental conditions occurs. However, more work is needed to understand how the combined effects of elevated temperature and environmental microbial content impact survival from infection.

Experimental model and subject details

Strongylocentrotus purpuratus larvae

Adult purple sea urchins (Strongylocentrotus purpuratus) were collected via SCUBA in March 2023 from sites within the Santa Barbara Coastal Long Term Ecological Research (LTER) and shipped to Texas A&M University. Wild caught purple sea urchins (n ∼20) were housed together in a 75 gallon tank at ambient conditions (15°C and 32 ppt salinity) in artificial seawater (Instant Ocean; ASW) and fed frozen kelp (Macrocystis pyrifera) once per week until spawning. The sea urchins used for spawning were healthy adults which had not been used in any other procedures.

Method details

Larval culture conditions

Spawning was induced by injection of 0.53 M KCl into the coelom. Sperm was collected dry and stored on ice until fertilization. Eggs were collected into 14°C ASW sterilized with a 0.2 μm vacuum filter. Gamete compatibility was assessed through test fertilization crosses between each male and female and only crosses with >95% successful fertilization were used in the experiment. Eggs from two females and sperm from three males were pooled and fertilized in 14°C or 18°C filter-sterilized ASW and moved into their designated treatment conditions when >95% fertilization was confirmed. Embryos were cultured at a density of 10 larvae/mL in 3 L of ASW under one of the four treatment conditions in triplicate (Figure 1A). Developmental treatment conditions included combinations of two temperatures and two microbial treatments in full combinatorial design. Temperature conditions were 14°C or 18°C, which represent ambient and warm summer conditions, respectively (SBC LTER database: https://sbclter.msi.ucsb.edu/data/visuals/tsvisual/). Microbial conditions included ASW that was filtered with a 0.2 μm vacuum filter (low microbial richness, LMR) or ASW from the adult sea urchin tank coarsely filtered through a 40 μm mesh (high microbial richness, HMR) (Figure 1A). All conditions were maintained at 32 ppt salinity. A routine 1/3rd volume water change was performed every other day on each larval chamber, replacing the chamber water with either LMR or HMR, accordingly. Larvae were collected at the pluteus stage, 5 days post fertilization (dpf), for ATAC-seq, RNA-seq, and morphometrics, and microbes from the water were collected for 16S rRNA sequencing.

Characterization of microbial communities in each treatment

After larvae were collected at 5 dpf, DNA was isolated from 300 mL of water from each chamber (N = 12) and extracted using a modified protocol from the ZymoBIOMICS DNA Miniprep Kit. Following larval collection, the water was stored in temperature-controlled incubators until processing, which occurred within 24 h of the larval collections. The sampled water was vacuum filtered using a 0.45 μm filter in a Büchner funnel. The filter was divided in half and placed in two bead bashing tubes with lysis buffer. The microbial samples were removed from the filters and digested by bashing the bead tubes with a Bead Genie for 5 min at 4800 rpm, incubating for 30 min at 55°C, and bashing again for 5 min at 4800 rpm, before adding Proteinase K to the tubes and incubating for 2 h at 55°C. The ZymoBIOMICS default kit protocol was followed after this point and the supernatant from paired bead bashing tubes was recombined to a single sample at the DNA binding step. Library preparation and amplicon sequencing of the V3-V4 region was done by Novogene Corp. on a NovaSeq PE250 at 0.1 million reads per sample. The primers included a barcode sequence followed by the V3-V4 primer sequence: forward 5′-CCTAYGGGRBGCASCAG-3′, reverse 5′-GGACTACNNGGGTATCTAAT-3’.

For quantification of the microbial load in the artificial seawater of the larval cultures, microbial DNA samples were pooled by treatment (i.e., 14°C LMR, 14°C HMR, 18°C LMR, and 18°C HMR) for qPCR. Samples were standardized by input volume for this analysis. A standard curve was created with ZymoBIOMICS Microbial Community DNA Standard and the TaqMan 16S pan-bacterial assay (Ba0490791_s1, ThermoFisher) was run with TaqPath BactoPure MasterMix (ThermoFisher) on a BioRad CFX Opus 96. Each pooled sample was run in technical triplicate and average Ct values were compared against the standard curve to calculate absolute bacterial load, expressed as nanogram of bacterial DNA for the 16S rRNA gene per microliter of sample.

ATAC-seq and whole transcriptome RNA-seq

Larvae for nuclear DNA extraction for ATAC-seq (n = 500) were collected from each chamber (N = 12) at 5 dpf and placed on ice for immediate processing. Larvae were homogenized in 1 mL of lysis buffer from the Active Motif ATAC-seq kit using a Dounce homogenizer with a tight bore pestle. The resulting homogenate was poured through an EZ Strainer (40 μm, Greiner Bio-One) to remove cellular debris. The ATAC-Seq kit from Active Motif was used for tagmentation, purification, and PCR amplification (with index primers) according to the kit protocol. The individual barcoded libraries were analyzed using TapeStation and, for any libraries that showed high weight traces (>1000 bp), an additional SPRI bead clean-up was done. Following the instructions from the SPRIselect User Guide, a right side selection was conducted using a 0.6X bead concentration. After size selection was completed, the samples were eluted with 20 μL of elution buffer from the Active Motif ATAC-Seq kit. Fully size-selected and barcoded libraries were pooled and sent to Novogene Corp. for sequencing on a NovaSeq PE150 at 360 million reads (∼30 million reads per sample).

Larvae for RNA extraction (n = 5,000) were collected at 5 dpf from each chamber (N = 12) and flash-frozen in liquid N2. RNA was extracted by homogenizing the larvae with needle passes in TRIzol (Ambion), followed by the addition of chloroform for phase separation, and precipitation of RNA from the aqueous layer with isopropanol. RNA samples were sent to Novogene Corp. for library preparation and sequencing on a NovaSeq PE150 at 20 million reads per sample.

Morphology

Larvae (n = ∼200) were stored in Z-fix (Anatech) for microscopy. Z-stack brightfield images were taken on a Zeiss Axiophot and ImageJ/FIJI (v1.53t)141 was used to analyze images. Larval length was calculated by marking the position of the apical point and the tip of all four arms. The distance formula was used to calculate the length of each arm and the average of the four arms was used for the larval length based on the x, y, and z position information of each point provided by the “Cell Counter” plugin. Larval body area was measured using the “Area” tool to outline the larval body in a cross-section slice from the middle of the body. Pigment cells were counted using the “Cell Counter” plugin to mark all pigment cells throughout the larval Z-stack. Gut inflammation was quantified by using the “Line Tool” to take three measurements of the midgut epithelium, with the mean value of the three measurements used for gut thickness.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Sequencing analysis

ATAC-seq raw read quality was evaluated using MultiQC (v1.14)144 and trimmed to remove the NovaSeq PE adaptor with FastP (v0.20.0).134 Trimmed reads were aligned to the S. purpuratus genome version 5.0 from Echinobase123 using Bowtie2 (v2.4.4)126 with the preset for --very-fast-local to improve alignment. Samtools (v1.16.1)151 was used to sort the aligned reads by name (samtools sort -n), mate score (fixmate -m), sort by reference and position (samtools sort), mark and remove duplicates (samtools markdup -r), filter for reads with a Q score above 30 (samtools view -q 30), and remove reads mapping to the mitochondrial chromosome. Following quality filtering, an average of 87.8% of ATAC-seq reads mapped to the reference genome (Table S6). Peaks were called using MACS2 (v2.1.2.1),142 including settings for paired-end sequences (-f BAMPE) and an effective genome size of 921.8 Mb (-g 9.218e+08) to override the default human genome size setting in MACS2. Since N’s make up only around 0.06% of the S. purpuratus genome (version 5.0), the full genome size of 921.8 Mb was used as the effective genome size for alignment. Bedtools (v2.29.2)125 was used to merge overlapping peaks and only peaks with at least 25% reciprocal overlap between replicates were kept (merge and intersect functions). Differential accessibility was analyzed from normalized library sizes and peaks using DiffBind (v3.10.1)132 in R (v4.4.0).148 Read counts were normalized using DESeq2’s native method within the DiffBind package. The normalized counts were then analyzed for differential accessibility by contrasting 18°C vs. 14°C and HMR vs. LMR. ChIPseeker (v1.36.0)128 and metagene2 (v1.20.0)143 were used to determine the locations of peaks across genomic features and match peaks to annotations. Open chromatin was assessed for motif enrichment using XSTREME (v5.5.5) from MEME-Suite.153 FASTAs of the open chromatin for each condition (peaks with at least 25% reciprocity) were compared against a control of S. purpuratus promoter sequences (defined as 1 kb upstream to 500 bp downstream of the transcription start site) and examined for the enrichment of eukaryotic motifs 6–15 bp long and E-value ≤0.05.

RNA-seq raw read quality was evaluated using FastQC (v0.11.9)135 and MultiQC (v1.14)144 and trimmed to remove the NovaSeq PE adaptor with FastP (v0.20.0).134 Trimmed, paired-end reads were aligned to the S. purpuratus genome (version 5.0) using HISAT2 (v2.2.1).140 Aligned reads were sorted and filtered the same way as the ATAC-seq reads above using Samtools (v1.16.1).151 An average of 98.5% of filtered RNA-seq reads mapped to the reference genome (Table S6). Cufflinks (v2.2.1)129 was used to convert the Gff3 file from Echinobase to a GTF file and the new GTF was rearranged so that “gene_id” was available in the 9th column, as expected by counting programs. A read count matrix was generated with Subread (v2.0.3) featureCounts152 and differential expression was analyzed with DESeq2 (v1.40.2)131 in R (v4.4.0).148 Differential expression was calculated by the Wald Test, contrasting 18°C vs. 14°C and MMR vs. LMR, including an interaction term, and using adjusted p-value >0.05 and log2 fold change >0.5 as the significance cut-offs. Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was conducted on the Wald Stats using GO_MWU.136 Further ontological analysis was done by examining the fold change of genes that corresponded to functional classes as identified by Tu et al.60

Microbial 16S rRNA V3-V4 sequences were analyzed using Quantitative Insights Into Microbial Ecology (QIIME2) (v2024.10).146 Demultiplexed FASTQ files, with barcode and primer sequences removed, were imported into QIIME2. Sequences were quality filtered, denoised, merged, and chimeras removed using DADA2 (v1.30.0)130 with a truncation set at 0 for forward and reverse reads. Following QIIME2 filtering, there was an average of 112,488 reads per sample (Table S6). The feature table was filtered with a minimum total frequency of 10 for each feature to be retained and requiring each feature to appear in at least one sample. Taxonomic assignment was performed on sequences against the SILVA 138.145,46 reference database with RESCRIPt (REference Sequence annotation and CuRatIon Pipeline)149 using the V3-V4 region. Subsequent QIIME2 outputs were moved to RStudio (v4.4.0)148 and made into a “phyloseq” object (qiime2R, v0.99.6, function “qza_to_phyloseq”).147 Any Eukaryota, chloroplast, or mitochondria taxa were removed and then samples were rarefied to the lowest sequenced sample with a rarefaction depth of 35,532 (phyloseq, function “rarefy_even_depth”).145 Relative abundance was calculated for barplots (phyloseq, function “transform_sample_counts”).145 Data normality and homogeneity assumptions were checked using Shapiro-Wilk and Levene tests from the R packages stats (v4.4.0)148 and car (v3.1.2),127 respectively. Microbial alpha diversity was first calculated using Faith’s phylogenetic diversity (btools, v0.0.1, function “estimate_pd”).124 The two temperatures and two microbial communities in full combinatorial design (i.e., 14°C LMR, 14°C HMR, 18°C LMR, and 18°C HMR) were tested using an ANOVA, followed by a Tukey’s post hoc test. Shannon diversity was also calculated (phyloseq, v1.46.0, function “estimate_richness”).145 The two temperatures and two microbial communities in full combinatorial design (i.e., 14°C LMR, 14°C HMR, 18°C LMR, and 18°C HMR) were tested using an ANOVA, followed by a Tukey’s post hoc test. Additional alpha diversity measures (i.e., Observed, Chao1, Simpson) were calculated (phyloseq, function “estimate_richness”) and can be viewed in Figure S1. A principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) with weighted UniFrac distances was generated for visualization (phyloseq, function “distance” and “ordinate”).145 Taxonomy with count data from QIIME2 and morphological trait measurements (body size, immune cell counts (pigment cells), and midgut epithelium thickness) were analyzed with Coracle,57 an artificial intelligence framework to identify microbes associated with continuous variables. The package gvlma (v1.0.0.3)139 was used to check linear regression assumptions prior to running a linear model on the outputs from Coracle.

Morphological analysis

Statistical analysis on morphological samples was conducted using R (v4.4.0).148 Individual larvae that were outliers within any morphology metric were identified and removed from the dataset (rstatix, v0.7.1).150 One of the triplicate cultures of larvae from 18°C HMR was determined to be an outlier based on pigment cell counts and was consequently removed from all morphological analyses (the other two replicate chambers of larvae from this condition were used for analysis). Normality distributions and variance homogeneity were checked using Shapiro-Wilk and Levene tests. Based on the results from these tests, larval length and body area were assessed using an ANOVA with interactions between temperature and microbial richness. Pigment cell counts and midgut epithelium measurements were corrected for larval body length using the GroupStruct package (v0.1.0)137,138 and then tested using Dunn’s test (Benjamini-Hochberg adjustment) (dunn.test, v1.3.6),133 grouping by the full treatment terms to assess interactions (i.e., 14°C LMR, 14°C HMR, 18°C LMR, and 18°C HMR).
