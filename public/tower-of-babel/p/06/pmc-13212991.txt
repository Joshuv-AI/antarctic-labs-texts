Physics-constrained multimodal vision transformer for ultra-short-term solar radiation forecasting error correction
Authors: Ziyao Jiang, Wangminzi Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate ultra-short-term solar radiation forecasting is critical for renewable energy integration and power grid stability, yet operational systems exhibit systematic biases and accumulating errors during transitional weather conditions. This research develops an integrated error correction framework that synergistically combines multimodal vision transformers with physics-constrained neural networks to refine satellite-based solar forecasts. The proposed architecture extracts complementary spatiotemporal features from visible, infrared, and water vapor satellite channels through hierarchical cross-modal attention mechanisms, while simultaneously enforcing fundamental physical principles including energy conservation and radiative transfer constraints as soft regularization terms during training. Experimental validation using four years of Himawari-8 geostationary satellite observations and ground measurements from 47 meteorological stations demonstrates that the framework achieves 18.7% RMSE reduction compared to best-performing baselines, with systematic bias reduced from 12.7 W/m² to 1.2 W/m². Ablation studies reveal synergistic interactions between multimodal fusion and physics-aware learning, with the combined approach delivering benefits exceeding individual components. Introduction

The rapid expansion of solar photovoltaic installations worldwide has intensified the demand for accurate ultra-short-term solar radiation forecasting, which directly influences power grid stability and energy dispatch efficiency1. Satellite-based cloud imagery offers spatially continuous observations at high temporal resolution, making it a valuable data source for nowcasting applications ranging from minutes to several hours ahead2. However, despite significant advances in forecasting methodologies, operational systems continue to exhibit systematic biases and accumulating errors, particularly during transitional weather conditions where cloud dynamics introduce considerable uncertainty3.

Recent developments in deep learning architectures have propelled satellite-based forecasting capabilities forward, with convolutional neural networks demonstrating promise in extracting spatial patterns from cloud images4. The emergence of vision transformers has further expanded modeling capacity by capturing long-range dependencies through self-attention mechanisms, which proves particularly advantageous when processing sequential satellite observations5. Several studies have explored multimodal fusion strategies that integrate visible, infrared, and water vapor channels to enhance feature representation6. Nonetheless, these data-driven approaches often generate predictions that violate fundamental physical principles, such as energy conservation and radiative transfer constraints, leading to unrealistic forecasts under certain atmospheric conditions7.

Current error correction strategies predominantly rely on statistical post-processing techniques that treat forecasting models as black boxes, applying bias corrections based on historical residuals8. While these methods reduce mean errors, they fail to address the underlying physical inconsistencies embedded within prediction sequences. Physics-informed neural networks have emerged as a promising paradigm that embeds domain knowledge directly into model architectures9. Recent hybrid PINN approaches have attempted to combine data-driven learning with physical laws in various domains, including fluid dynamics and heat transfer problems10. However, most existing physics-constrained methods rely on single-modal inputs and employ relatively simple network architectures such as multilayer perceptrons, which struggle to capture complex spatiotemporal patterns inherent in satellite imagery11. While some researchers have explored physics-guided deep learning for solar forecasting, these efforts typically treat physical constraints as post-processing filters rather than integral components of the learning process12. The integration of physical constraints within transformer-based architectures presents both theoretical and practical challenges, particularly in balancing model flexibility with adherence to radiative transfer equations13. Our work distinguishes itself from prior approaches in three fundamental aspects. First, we embed radiative transfer constraints directly within the attention mechanism rather than applying them as external regularization, enabling the network to learn physically consistent feature representations from the outset. Second, we develop a hierarchical multimodal fusion strategy specifically tailored for heterogeneous satellite observations, whereas existing methods typically concatenate channels without exploiting their complementary physical information. Third, our adaptive error decomposition explicitly separates systematic biases from stochastic uncertainties, a distinction that conventional hybrid PINN architectures do not address.

Rather than treating error correction as an isolated post-processing step, we embed physical principles within the correction architecture itself, ensuring that refined predictions maintain radiative energy consistency while capturing complex spatiotemporal patterns from satellite imagery. The primary innovations of this work include: (1) a multimodal fusion mechanism specifically designed for heterogeneous satellite channels that preserves both spatial and spectral information through hierarchical attention; (2) a physics-constrained correction module that enforces radiative transfer equations as soft constraints during training, preventing physically implausible predictions; (3) an adaptive error decomposition strategy that distinguishes systematic biases from stochastic uncertainties, enabling targeted correction across different error sources15. Experimental validation demonstrates that the proposed model achieves substantial improvements in forecast accuracy while maintaining computational efficiency suitable for operational deployment14.

Physics-constrained neural networks

This integration ensures that network predictions not only fit observational data but also respect fundamental physical laws that govern the underlying phenomena. Solar radiation prediction error analysis

Prediction errors in solar radiation forecasting stem from multiple interacting sources that compound throughout the modeling chain. Satellite retrieval algorithms introduce initial uncertainties when converting raw radiance measurements into cloud properties, as retrieval inversions rely on lookup tables and radiative transfer approximations that inherently simplify atmospheric complexity26. Cloud motion vectors derived from consecutive satellite images suffer from parallax effects and misregistration, particularly for high-altitude cirrus formations where apparent motion differs substantially from actual advection. Numerical weather prediction models contribute additional errors through parameterization schemes that inadequately represent subgrid-scale convective processes, which become especially problematic during transitional weather regimes where cumulus development occurs rapidly.

Distinguishing between systematic biases and random fluctuations proves essential for designing targeted correction strategies. Systematic errors manifest as persistent overestimation or underestimation patterns tied to specific conditions—for instance, many forecasting systems consistently underpredict surface irradiance during broken cloud scenarios due to inadequate treatment of cloud edge brightening effects27. These biases exhibit temporal autocorrelation and spatial coherence, making them potentially correctable through pattern recognition. Random errors, conversely, arise from inherently unpredictable atmospheric turbulence and measurement noise, displaying characteristics closer to white noise processes. Error propagation mechanisms amplify initial uncertainties as predictions extend forward in time. When cloud motion forecasts contain directional biases, positioning errors accumulate linearly with forecast horizon, causing predicted cloud fields to diverge progressively from reality29. More insidiously, radiation calculations compound errors nonlinearly because irradiance depends on exponential functions of optical depth. Existing error correction methodologies span statistical post-processing to machine learning approaches, each carrying distinct advantages and limitations. Kalman filtering variants adaptively adjust forecasts based on recent error statistics, performing well when error characteristics remain stationary but struggling during regime transitions30. Quantile regression forests provide probabilistic corrections that capture error distribution tails, though they treat forecasting models as black boxes without addressing underlying physical inconsistencies. Recent deep learning methods show promise in learning complex error patterns from historical data, yet most implementations ignore physical constraints, occasionally producing corrections that worsen energy balance violations rather than alleviating them.

Overall architecture of error correction model

The overarching design philosophy centers on treating error correction not as isolated post-processing but as an integrated learning task where multimodal satellite features and physical constraints jointly inform predictions. Our architecture diverges from conventional cascaded approaches that sequentially apply base forecasting followed by statistical correction—instead, we construct an end-to-end trainable system where feature extraction, physics encoding, and error estimation occur simultaneously within a unified computational graph40. This holistic perspective allows gradients to flow backward through all components, enabling the network to learn which satellite patterns correlate with specific error types while respecting physical boundaries.

Forward propagation commences with parallel processing streams for each satellite modality. Raw visible, infrared, and water vapor imagery enter their respective encoder branches where patch embeddings and positional encodings prepare data for transformer layers, as detailed in Sect. 3.1. These modality-specific pathways operate independently during initial encoding stages, extracting channel-specific features before cross-modal fusion. Simultaneously, a separate branch processes the base radiation forecast—the prediction requiring correction—alongside meteorological context variables including solar zenith angle, time of day, and geographic coordinates. This auxiliary information undergoes embedding through fully connected layers that project heterogeneous inputs into a common feature space compatible with transformer representations41.

The fusion stage merges multimodal satellite features with base forecast embeddings through concatenation followed by several transformer blocks that perform cross-attention between satellite-derived patterns and forecast characteristics. Physical constraint calculations execute in parallel, computing expected transmittance values and energy balance terms based on retrieved cloud properties. A gating mechanism then weighs contributions from the data-driven pathway against physics-based estimates, producing intermediate correction factors. These factors modulate rather than replace the base forecast, preserving its general structure while adjusting magnitudes to eliminate systematic biases42.

Residual learning constitutes a crucial strategic choice that accelerates training convergence and improves generalization. Our framework functions explicitly as an error correction system rather than a standalone forecasting model, meaning it refines existing predictions rather than generating forecasts from scratch. The base forecast originates from operational numerical weather prediction model outputs or persistence-based extrapolation of recent satellite-derived irradiance observations, typically ranging from 200 to 800 W/m² during daytime conditions. The correction term learned by our network exhibits substantially smaller magnitudes, generally within $$50 W/m² for most samples and rarely exceeding $$100 W/m² even under challenging conditions. Model training employs a two-stage optimization strategy that first pretains the multimodal feature extractor on a large corpus of unlabeled satellite imagery through self-supervised learning, then fine-tunes the complete architecture end-to-end using labeled forecast-observation pairs. We adopt the AdamW optimizer with decoupled weight decay, which proves more robust than standard Adam when training transformers with many parameters44. We train for 100 epochs on the full dataset, monitoring validation loss to detect overfitting—early stopping triggers if validation performance fails to improve for 15 consecutive epochs, though this rarely occurs given the model’s strong regularization through physical constraints.

Experimental results and analysis

Experimental data and environment configuration

Our experiments draw upon multimodal satellite observations acquired from the Himawari-8 geostationary platform, which provides continuous coverage over East Asia and the Western Pacific region at 15-minute intervals. We specifically extract three primary channels: visible reflectance (Band 3, 0.64 μm), infrared brightness temperature (Band 13, 10.4 μm), and water vapor absorption (Band 8, 6.2 μm), spanning the period from January 2020 through December 202345. Raw satellite data undergo several preprocessing steps including geolocation correction to account for parallax effects, radiometric calibration converting digital counts to physical units, and spatial resampling to a uniform 0.01-degree geographic grid. Cloud mask algorithms identify and flag pixels contaminated by sensor artifacts or solar glint, which we subsequently exclude from training. Quality control proves particularly critical for nighttime infrared observations where thermal contrasts weaken, potentially introducing spurious patterns that confuse learning algorithms.

Ground-based pyranometer measurements serve as reference targets for model training and validation. We compile observations from 47 meteorological stations distributed across diverse climate zones, encompassing coastal, inland, and mountainous sites to ensure geographic representativeness. Each station operates Class I thermopile pyranometers with measurement uncertainty below 3%, recording global horizontal irradiance at one-minute resolution. We aggregate these high-frequency measurements to 15-minute averages matching satellite temporal resolution, applying stringent quality filters that remove physically implausible values (negative irradiance or values exceeding theoretical clear-sky limits) and periods with instrument maintenance flags46. The resulting dataset contains approximately 2.8 million valid satellite-ground paired samples.

Hardware infrastructure comprises four NVIDIA A100 GPUs, each equipped with 40 GB memory, operating within a distributed training framework that parallelizes mini-batch processing across devices. Hyperparameter configuration balances model capacity against computational feasibility. We partition the test dataset into four weather categories based on cloud cover fraction and variability: clear-sky conditions (cloud cover < 10%), partly cloudy with stable patterns (10–70% cover, low temporal variance), broken cumulus (10–70% cover, high temporal variance), and overcast scenarios (> 70% cover). Performance degradation often manifests most severely during transitional regimes where cloud dynamics introduce rapid irradiance fluctuations—precisely the conditions most challenging for power grid operators50.

Figure 8 demonstrates that our proposed architecture maintains relatively consistent RMSE across weather categories, with values ranging from 67.4 W/m² under overcast to 103.8 W/m² during broken cumulus conditions. The baseline CNN-LSTM model exhibits considerably larger performance swings, particularly struggling with broken cloud scenarios where RMSE exceeds 145 W/m². This robustness advantage stems from our physics-constrained framework’s ability to recognize when predictions violate fundamental energy balance, triggering corrective adjustments that purely data-driven models cannot provide. Interestingly, the performance gap between our model and baselines narrows during clear-sky conditions where physical constraints offer less discriminative value—forecasting becomes primarily a matter of accurately predicting atmospheric transmittance rather than managing cloud-induced variability.

Discussion

Our experimental findings reveal several key strengths that distinguish the proposed framework from conventional error correction approaches. The model excels particularly during transitional weather regimes characterized by broken cloud fields and rapid irradiance fluctuations—scenarios where power grid operators face greatest uncertainty in renewable energy integration. This advantage emerges from the architecture’s dual capability: capturing complex spatiotemporal patterns through multimodal transformers while simultaneously enforcing fundamental physical principles that prevent catastrophic prediction failures. Operational deployment would benefit most from this framework in regions experiencing frequent convective development or coastal areas where sea-breeze circulations drive diurnal cloud evolution, as these environments produce the systematic biases our correction mechanism targets most effectively.

The mechanism through which physics constraints enhance prediction accuracy operates at multiple levels. Most directly, energy balance enforcement acts as a regularization term that guides the network away from parameter configurations producing thermodynamically impossible outputs. During training, when the model attempts to predict surface irradiance exceeding what energy conservation permits given observed cloud optical properties, the physics loss component increases sharply, steering gradient descent toward more plausible solutions. This constraint proves especially valuable during partly cloudy conditions where small errors in estimated cloud transmittance would otherwise compound nonlinearly through Beer’s law attenuation relationships. Beyond explicit constraint enforcement, our experimental observations suggest that physics-aware training may improve feature learning characteristics. Analysis of intermediate layer activations reveals that models trained with physics constraints exhibit feature patterns that correlate more strongly with retrieved cloud optical properties (Pearson correlation coefficient 0.73 versus 0.58 for unconstrained models). While this correlation does not definitively prove that the network learns more physically meaningful representations, it provides preliminary evidence that constraint enforcement guides the optimization toward solutions aligned with atmospheric radiative transfer principles rather than spurious statistical associations present in training data.

Extreme weather events expose important limitations despite generally robust performance. During rare instances of volcanic aerosol plumes or intense dust storms, the model struggles because these atmospheric constituents violate assumptions embedded within our simplified radiative transfer parameterizations. The physics constraint module expects clear-sky transmittance to fall within climatological bounds listed in Table 2, yet extreme aerosol loading can push actual transmittance well below these thresholds, causing the constraint mechanism to inappropriately penalize accurate predictions. Similarly, rapidly intensifying convective systems occasionally produce cloud formations that evolve faster than our 15-minute satellite temporal resolution can adequately sample, leading to underestimation of irradiance variability.

Multimodal fusion strategies demonstrate clear value, though not uniformly across all scenarios. The cross-modal attention mechanism proves most beneficial when different spectral channels provide complementary rather than redundant information. For thick overcast characterized by optically opaque clouds, visible and infrared channels convey essentially identical signals about complete solar blockage, diminishing fusion advantages. Conversely, during cirrus-contaminated scenes where thin ice clouds exhibit strong infrared signatures but modest visible opacity, integrating information across modalities substantially improves optical depth estimation and subsequent irradiance predictions.

Several avenues for improvement merit future investigation. Incorporating additional data sources such as ground-based sky cameras or lightning detection networks could enhance nowcasting skill during convective scenarios where satellite observations alone prove insufficient. The current physics constraint implementation employs relatively simple plane-parallel radiative transfer approximations that neglect three-dimensional effects near cloud boundaries—integrating more sophisticated radiative transfer models, though computationally expensive, might reduce residual biases. Finally, extending the framework to probabilistic forecasting through ensemble generation or quantile regression would provide uncertainty estimates essential for risk-aware decision-making in energy system operations.

Conclusion

This research has addressed a critical challenge in solar radiation forecasting by developing an integrated error correction framework that synergistically combines multimodal vision transformers with physics-constrained neural networks. The work moves beyond traditional post-processing approaches that treat forecasting models as black boxes, instead embedding physical principles directly within the learning architecture to ensure predictions respect fundamental energy conservation laws while capturing complex spatiotemporal patterns from satellite observations.

Our investigation yields several significant findings. First, the multimodal fusion mechanism demonstrates substantial value in extracting complementary information across visible, infrared, and water vapor satellite channels, with cross-modal attention enabling adaptive weighting of spectral bands based on atmospheric conditions. This strategy proves particularly effective during transitional weather regimes where different channels provide non-redundant signals about cloud optical properties and evolution. Second, physics constraints contribute meaningfully to accuracy improvements—not merely through explicit violation penalties, but by guiding the network toward learning representations aligned with atmospheric radiative transfer principles. The hybrid loss function reduces systematic biases from 12.7 W/m² in baseline models to merely 1.2 W/m² in our proposed architecture, while maintaining competitive computational efficiency suitable for operational deployment. Third, ablation studies reveal synergistic interactions between components, with the combination of multimodal learning and physics awareness delivering benefits exceeding what either approach provides individually.

The primary innovations distinguishing this work encompass three interconnected contributions. We introduce a hierarchical multimodal transformer architecture specifically tailored for heterogeneous satellite observations, incorporating spatial and temporal encoding mechanisms that preserve critical contextual information throughout feature extraction. The physics-constrained integration mechanism represents a novel approach to embedding domain knowledge within deep learning frameworks, implementing differentiable constraint layers that enforce energy balance and atmospheric transmittance bounds without requiring explicit supervision. Finally, our residual learning strategy frames error correction as an additive refinement task, inherently regularizing predictions and improving robustness when satellite observations contain anomalies or missing data.

From a theoretical perspective, this research advances understanding of how physical constraints can be effectively integrated within attention-based architectures without compromising model flexibility. The demonstrated synergy between data-driven feature learning and physics-aware regularization suggests promising avenues for developing hybrid methodologies across other domains where governing equations are known but incompletely capture real-world complexity. Our findings contribute to the broader discourse on interpretable machine learning by showing that embedding domain knowledge enhances not only prediction accuracy but also model reliability during edge cases falling outside training distributions.

Practical applications stand to benefit considerably from this framework. Energy system operators managing high-penetration solar portfolios require reliable ultra-short-term forecasts to optimize unit commitment decisions and maintain grid frequency stability. Our model’s demonstrated 18.7% RMSE reduction compared to best-performing baselines translates directly to reduced forecast error costs and improved renewable integration. The computational efficiency—achieving 21 predictions per second on consumer-grade hardware—positions the approach for real-time operational deployment within existing forecasting infrastructure. Regional adaptation appears straightforward given the model’s modular design, requiring only retraining on locally representative satellite-ground observation pairs.

Several limitations warrant acknowledgment and require discussion with supporting quantitative evidence. The current implementation employs simplified plane-parallel radiative transfer approximations that neglect three-dimensional cloud effects, producing measurable accuracy degradation near cloud boundaries. Quantitative analysis of edge cases reveals that predictions within 5 km of detected cloud boundaries exhibit 34% higher RMSE (127.3 W/m²) compared to interior regions (94.8 W/m²), directly attributable to horizontal photon transport effects our one-dimensional parameterization cannot capture.

Model performance degrades substantially during extreme atmospheric conditions. During 23 identified volcanic aerosol events in our test period, RMSE increased to 156.7 W/m² compared to the overall average of 94.2 W/m², representing a 66% performance degradation. Similarly, for 89 rapidly intensifying convective episodes where cloud optical depth changed by more than 50% within 15 min, prediction errors averaged 142.3 W/m². These failure modes stem from violations of assumptions embedded in our physics constraints: extreme aerosol loading pushes atmospheric transmittance below the 0.65 lower bound specified in Table 3, causing the constraint mechanism to inappropriately penalize accurate predictions.

Regarding geographic transferability, our East Asian test domain encompasses coastal, inland, and mountainous sites spanning latitudes from 24°N to 38°N, providing reasonable climate diversity including subtropical humid, temperate continental, and monsoon-influenced regions. However, the model has not been validated in tropical climates with persistent deep convection, arid regions with high aerosol variability, or high-latitude areas with low solar elevation angles. Transfer learning experiments using limited data from three Australian stations suggest performance degradation of approximately 15–20% without regional fine-tuning, indicating that direct application to climatologically distinct regions requires adaptation. Future work should prioritize systematic cross-regional validation to characterize generalization boundaries more precisely.

Future research directions emerge naturally from these limitations. Incorporating three-dimensional radiative transfer calculations, though computationally demanding, could reduce residual biases in complex cloud geometries. Expanding the multimodal framework to include ground-based sky camera observations would enhance nowcasting skill during convective scenarios where satellite views alone prove insufficient. Developing probabilistic extensions through ensemble generation or quantile regression represents a crucial next step toward providing uncertainty estimates essential for risk-aware decision-making. Finally, investigating transfer learning approaches that adapt models trained in data-rich regions to locations with sparse ground observations could democratize access to high-quality solar forecasting capabilities globally.
