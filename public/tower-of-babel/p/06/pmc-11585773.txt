Receptor Kinase Signaling of BRI1 and SIRK1 Is Tightly Balanced by Their Interactomes as Revealed From Domain-Swap Chimaera in AE-MS Approaches
Authors: Lin Xi, Xuna Wu, Jiahui Wang, Zhaoxia Zhang, Mingjie He, Zeeshan Zeeshan, Thorsten Stefan, Waltraud X Schulze
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAt the plasma membrane, in response to biotic and abiotic cues, specific ligands initiate the formation of receptor kinase heterodimers, which regulate the activities of plasma membrane proteins and initiate signaling cascades to the nucleus. In this study, we utilized affinity enrichment mass spectrometry to investigate the stimulus-dependent interactomes of LRR receptor kinases in response to their respective ligands, with an emphasis on exploring structural influences and potential cross-talk events at the plasma membrane. BRI1 and SIRK1 were chosen as receptor kinases with distinct coreceptor preference. By using interactome characteristic of domain-swap chimera following a gradient boosting learning algorithm trained on SIRK1 and BRI1 interactomes, we attribute contributions of extracellular domain, transmembrane domain, juxtamembrane domain, and kinase domain of respective ligand-binding receptors to their interaction with their coreceptors and substrates. Our results revealed juxtamembrane domain as major structural element defining the specific substrate recruitment for BRI1 and extracellular domain for SIRK1. Furthermore, the learning algorithm enabled us to predict the phenotypic outcomes of chimeric receptors based on different domain combinations, which was verified by dedicated experiments. As a result, our work reveals a tightly controlled balance of signaling cascade activation dependent on ligand-binding receptors domains and the internal ligand status of the plant. Moreover, our study shows the robust utility of machine learning classification as a quantitative metric for studying dynamic interactomes, dissecting the contribution of specific domains and predicting their phenotypic outcome.Keywords: LRR-receptor kinase, domain-swap chimera, affinity enrichment mass-spectrometry (AE-MS), supervised machine learning, signaling specificity

Plants are firmly rooted at their site of growth and thus need to be able to rapidly adapt to altering environmental conditions throughout the diurnal and life cycle. This requires precise integration of extracellular information with intracellular metabolic signals. This integration of environmental and developmental signals in plants is often controlled by receptor kinases (RKs) which constitute the largest subclade within the kinome of plants (1). Plant RKs are involved in many developmental processes as well as in responses to different abiotic stresses (2). Signaling through RKs involves a complex sequence of protein–protein interactions at the plasma membrane: Receptor kinases usually function together with a so-called coreceptor which is characterized by a shorter extracellular domain and which interacts with the ligand-binding receptor (3). Structural analysis of receptor–coreceptor complexes proposes that coreceptors function as a molecular glue and contribute as a shape-complementary helper during ligand binding (4, 5, 6). Recent in vivo visualization of RKs and their signaling components suggest existence of pre-formed receptor/coreceptor modules which are stabilized and activated by the ligand (7, 8, 9). These preformed modules are likely re-arranged upon activation to reveal their full activity and interaction with their substrates (9, 10, 11). Specificity of signaling output may further depend on coreceptor phosphorylation, as has recently been shown for a tyrosine phosphorylation switch in BAK1/SERK3. In the phosphorylated state, it activates immune signaling and in dephosphorylated state feeds into other signaling pathways (12).

Brassinosteroid insensitive 1 (BRI1) is among the most well-characterized RKs in plants. BRI1 perceives the plant steroid brassinolide, it interacts with coreceptor BRI1-associated kinase (BAK1) and upon ligand binding orchestrates a signaling cascade which ultimately activates transcription factor brassinazole resistant 1 (BZR1). At the plasma membrane, besides receptor and coreceptor, another membrane protein, RLP44 was found to be part of the BRI1 receptor/coreceptor complex (7, 13) and seems to mediate interactions with substrate proteins. Besides triggering signaling cascades to the nucleus, BRI1 also is involved in regulatory processes directly at the plasma membrane, for example, in protein complexes with the plasma membrane proton ATPase AHA2 (14). Our group has in the past characterized sucrose-induced receptor kinase (SIRK1) (15), which was discovered in sucrose-stimulation phosphorylation profiling experiments (16). SIRK1 was shown to be involved in the regulation of aquaporins in response to osmotic changes of sucrose and its signaling is stabilized by coreceptor Qian Shou kinase 1 (QSK1) (17). Just recently, the small signaling peptide PEP7 was found as a ligand to SIRK1 (18).

Interestingly, RKs BRI1 and SIRK1, although both belonging to the large family of leucine-rich repeat (LRR) RKs, preferentially interact with different coreceptors. BAK1 is a well characterized coreceptor, which besides being involved in signaling with BRI1 (19, 20), interacts with several other LRR RKs, such as flagellin sensitive 2 (FLS2) (21), phytosulfokine kinase 1 (22), and many others (23). By contrast, SIRK1 was found to interact with and signal through coreceptor QSK1, which was found a highly abundant protein in the plant plasma membrane.

The structural features and mechanisms by which the coreceptor is defined for a given ligand-binding receptor system are still largely unknown. Coreceptor preference may be important in assisting ligand binding and substrate recruitment (23, 24). To explore this, we followed an approach of using chimeric domain-swap proteins of BRI1 and SIRK1. Such chimeric receptors were previously successfully used to confirm brassinosteroids as ligands to BRI1 (25) and to characterize the CrRLK1 receptor family consisting of FER, HERK, and ANX1 (26). Chimeric FLS2 receptors were used to elucidate flagellin-binding sites (27). Unravelling which domains of the RKs control coreceptor and substrate preference may contribute to our understanding of how specificity is generated in plant signaling pathways. Thus, in this work, we focus on studying the domains involved in coreceptor and interactome preferences of RKs BRI1 and SIRK1.

Experimental Design and Statistical Rationale

Affinity enrichment mass-spectrometry (AE-MS) experiments were conducted using different bait proteins from overexpressed Arabidopsis plants. The bait proteins included SIRK1, BRI1, and four types of chimeras (LSSB, LBSS, SBLS, and SSLB). The plants were cultivated using a hydroponic culture system. Five treatments were applied: sucrose starvation, stimulation by sucrose, brassinolide (BL), PEP7, and a combination treatment of BL and sucrose. Sucrose starvation treatment served as the control condition. For each condition and treatment, three to six independent replicates were sampled, with each replicate grown in a separate hydroponic culture. This resulted in two batches of samples: the first batch included 73 raw files for all baits under sucrose starvation, BL, sucrose, and BL and sucrose treatments as one batch, while the second batch comprised as 39 raw files for the PEP7 treatment and respective controls. The samples were processed on the mass spectrometer by batch, and the data were analyzed collectively. Bait protein abundance in each run was used as normalization factor to avoid batch bias. The same experiment setup was applied for phosphoproteomics. A separate phosphoproteomics experiment involved bri1-301 mutant and Col-0, with each subjected either starvation treatment (the control condition) or 10 nM BL. In this experiment, three to four biological replicates were included for each condition. Substrate selection focused on those that were insensitive in bri1-301 but sensitive in Col-0 after BL treatment, with the sensitivity threshold defined as the average fold change (BL/Starvation) ± SE.

Experimental Procedures

Plant Material

Arabidopsis WT (Col-0) seeds, sirk1 mutant (SALK_125543), and bri1-301 (28) were propagated in the greenhouse at University Hohenheim. The sirk1×bri1-301 double mutant was produced by crossing.

Construction of Domain Swap Chimera

The domain swap receptors were constructed by overlapping PCR (29). All the domain swap receptors along with SIRK1 and BRI1 were fused with GFP and cloned into pEZR(H)-LN (15) and are referred to as LSSB (Long-SIRK1 N-terminal domains fused with short-BRI1 intracellular domain), LBSS (Long-BRI1 N-terminal domains fused with short-SIRK1 intracellular domain), SBLS (Short- BRI1 N-terminal domains fused with long-SIRK1 intracellular domain), and SSLB (Short-SIRK1 N-terminal domains fused with long-BRI1 intracellular domain). The domain-swap receptors were overexpressed in Col-0, sirk1, bri1-301, and sirk1×bri1-301. Also, complementary DNAs of SIRK1 and BRI1 as well as complementary DNAs of domain swap receptors were cloned into split-YFP (rBiFC) plasmids for interaction analysis (30).

Hydroponic Culture

Surface sterilized seeds were transferred to the hydroponic culture system for cultivation (31). Approximate 20 mg Arabidopsis seeds were placed on the mesh in one glass jar filled with 150 ml ½ MS medium with 0.5% sucrose. Cultures were grown on the shaker in the growth chamber (16/8 h day/night, 22 °C, 120 ± 10 μE/s × m2). Twenty days days later, the cultivation solution was replaced by ½ MS without sucrose and seedlings were kept in the dark for 48 h for sucrose starvation. Sucrose was then resupplied to the growth solution to a final concentration of 30 mM (16). BL (Sigma-Aldrich) was supplied to a final concentration of 100 nM (32). PEP7 (Pepmic) was supplied with concentration of 1 μM (18). Root samples were harvested 5 min after treatment. Samples without sucrose resupply were also harvested for comparison. Two micromolars of brassinazole (BRZ) was added to the growth medium to inhibit internal brassinosteroid biosynthesis.

Split-YFC (rBiFC) and Confocal Laser Scanning Microscopy and Analysis

The rBiFC (ratiometric bimolecular fluorescence complementation) was applied to test in planta protein–protein interaction as described previously (30, 33). Data derive from four biological replicates with 30 different leaf sections each. Exemplary images were for define regions of interest for which mean fluorescence intensity ratios were calculated. Confocal imaging was performed at 20°C using a Zeiss LSM780 microscope and a × 20 (Plan-Apochromat, 0.3 N A). The laser settings are: GFP at 488-nm excitation (ex) and 490- to 520-nm emission (em); YFP at 514-nm ex and 520 to 560 nm em; and RFP/Mitotracker at 561-nm ex and 565 to 620-nm em. Single plane images were processed with ImageJ. The collected images were processed and both YFP and RFP intensity was measured using the FIJI software (34) and the YFP/RFP ratio was calculated. 10 to 30 different cells from leaves of the 2 plants were analyzed. Statistical significance was determined using Welch Two Sample t test. The plot was created by OriginPro software.

Microsomal Fraction Preparation

Microsomal fractions were enriched according to previous publication (35). Approximately 1 g of fresh root material was homogenized in a glass potter tissue grinder on ice with 10 ml isolation buffer (330 mM mannitol, 100 mM KCl, 1 mM EDTA and 50 mM Tris-MES, pH 7.5) and freshly added 5 μl/ml Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Sigma-Aldrich), 5 mM DTT, 1 mM Phenylmethylsulfonyl Fluoride (PMSF) and phosphatase inhibitors (25 mM NaF, 1 mM Na3VO4, 1 mM Benzamidin, 3 μM Leupeptin). The homogenate was centrifuged at 4 °C, 7500g for 15 min and the supernatant was centrifuged again at 48,000g for 80 min. The remaining microsomal fraction (MF) pellet was resuspended with 120 μl membrane resuspension buffer (330 mM mannitol, 25 mM Tris-MES, 0.5 mM DTT). The protein concentration was determined by BCA assay (Thermo Scientific).

Pull-Downs of GFP-Tagged Receptor Kinases

Root microsomal protein (150 μg) isolated as described above was incubated with 25 μl of anti-GFP agarose beads (Chromotek) for 2 h on a rotating wheel at 4 °C (35). After incubation, the beads were spun down at 2000g and removed the supernatant by pipetting, then washed two times with 500 μl wash buffer (10 mM Tris/HCl pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM EDTA). For AE-MS experiments, protein was eluted from the beads with 100 μl UTU (6 M urea, 2 M thiourea, pH 8), before in-solution tryptic digestion. For kinase activity assays, three more washing steps were carried out with one-time wash buffer (10 mM Tris/HCl pH 7.5, 300 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM EDTA) and two times kinase reaction buffer (40 mM Tris/HCl pH 7.5, 10 mM MgCl2, 0.1% BSA, 2 mM DTT).

Kinase Activity Assay

All the domain swap receptors along with SIRK1 and BRI1 GFP-fusion proteins were affinity purified over anti-GFP beads (see above). The washed beads with GFP-tagged proteins were incubated in 30 μl kinase reaction buffer with ATP and the generic kinase substrate myelin basic protein (40 mM Tris-HCl [pH 7.5], 10 mM MgCl2, 0.1% BSA, 2 mM DTT, 100 μM ATP, 0.4 μg μL−1 myelin basic protein). After 1 h, ADP-GLO Reagents (Promega) were added following the manufacturer's recommendations. Subsequently, kinase detection reagents were added and incubated for an additional hour. Luminescence, indicating the conversion of ATP to ADP, was measured using a luminometer (TecanM200Pro).

Polypeptide Isolation

Arabidopsis root materials were harvested and frozen in liquid N2. Approximately 1 g of root material was homogenized in a glass potter tissue grinder on ice with 10 ml 1% trifluoroacetic acid (TFA). The mixture was filtered through four layers of Miracloth (Merkmillipore). The filtrate was clarified by centrifugation at 10,000g for 20 min at 2 °C (36). The supernatant was adjusted to pH 4.0 with 0.05 M NH4HCO3 and loaded onto Macrosep Advance Centrifugal Devices with Omega Membrane for 10 kDa separation. Fractions were collected and lyophilized to dry by rotary evaporator. 40 μl UTU (6 M urea, 2 M thiourea) was used for resuspension of each sample. Bradford (BioRAD) measurement of protein concentration was carried out and 10 μg of protein from each sample was applied to proteomic analysis.

Protein Digestion, Peptide Clean-Up, and Phosphopeptide Enrichment

Trypsin digestion was conducted on the protein samples for mass spectrometry analysis (37). The digested peptides were subsequently desalted using a C18 column (38). For the phosphopeptide enrichment, 150 μg of microsomal proteins were subjected to trypsin digestion. The digested peptides were then resuspended in 1M glycolic acid (80% acetonitrile, 5% TFA). Phosphopeptides were enriched with TiO2 beads (Titansphere, 5 μm, GL Sciences) as described (39).

LC-MS/MS Analysis of Peptides

Peptide mixtures were analyzed by HPLC system nanoflow Easy-nLC (Thermo Scientific) and Orbitrap hybrid mass spectrometer (Q-Exactive, Thermo Scientific). Peptides were eluted from a 75 μm × 25 cm analytical C18 column (PepMan, Thermo Scientific) on a linear gradient running from 4% to 64% acetonitrile over 135 min or 70 min. Proteins were identified based on the data-dependent acquisition of fragmentation spectra of multiple charged peptides. Up to twelve data-dependent MS/MS spectra were acquired for each full-scan spectrum acquired at 70,000 full-width half-maximum (FWHM) resolution. Full scans (MS1) were acquired using an ion target of 1e6, ion acquisition time 50 ms, on a mass range between 300 to 1800 m/z. Fragment spectra (MS2) were acquired on a dynamic mass range using an ion target of 1e5, 50 ms acquisition time and a stepped collision energy of 28 eV. The same settings were used for whole proteome analysis as well as phosphoproteomics.

Protein Identification and Label Free Quantification of Protein Intensities

MaxQuant version 1.5.3.8 (40) was used for raw file peak extraction and protein identification against the Arabidopsis TAIR11 database (35,386 entries). The following parameters were applied: trypsin as cleaving enzyme; minimum peptide length of six amino acids; maximal two missed cleavages; carbamidomethylation of cysteine as a fixed modification; N-terminal protein acetylation, oxidation of methionine as variable modifications. For phosphopeptide identification also the phosphorylation of serine, threonine, and tyrosine was included as variable modifications. Peptide mass tolerance was set to 20 ppm and 0.5 Da was used as MS/MS tolerance. Further settings were: “label-free quantification” marked, multiplicity set to 1; “match between runs” marked with time window 2 min; peptide and protein false discovery rates (FDR) set to 0.01; common contaminants (trypsin, keratin, etc.) excluded. Phosphorylation sites were determined by the site-scanning algorithm search engine Andromeda (41). The mass spectrometry proteomics data have been deposited to the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the PRIDE (42) partner repository with the dataset identifier PXD035647 (AE-MS Analysis of BL and Sucrose Treatment) and PXD044354 (AE-MS Analysis of PEP7 treatment), as well as PXD029509 and PXD052599 (Identification of putative phosphorylation substrates for PEP7-SIRK1 and BL-BRI1, respectively). A list of identified and quantified protein groups can be found in Supplemental Table S1, identified phosphopeptides are listed in supplemental Table S2, and fragment spectra are available via MS-Viewer (https://msviewer.ucsf.edu/prospector/cgi-bin/msform.cgi?form=msviewer) with search key zsnzjjmdqg.

Quantitative Analysis of Mass Spectrometric Data

Label-free quantificationvalues delivered by MaxQuant (40) in the proteingroups.txt output was used as quantitative information. Bait protein abundance was used to normalize between different AE-MS experiments. The resulting data were then averaged within biological replicates and subjected to statistical analysis. To calculate the fold change between treatments and the control, the averages of biological replicates were used. The process of averaging per treatment and conducting statistical analysis was performed in Perseus (43). Proteins which were also identified in pull-down experiments with GFP only were excluded from further analysis. Later, a software (ST-Filter) written in Python (version 3.6) was developed to analyze workflow for interactor candidates (https://github.com/sysbiolinxi/S.T-Filter.git). Functional annotations were extracted from MapMan 3.6 (44). Subcellular locations were obtained from SUBA database (SUBA 4) (45, 46). Machine learning analysis was performed by data mining software Orange 3.33. Within, algorithms of Native Bayes, Random Forest, Gradient Boosting and Neural Network were applied for testing and scoring. Network analysis was performed by Cytoscape (3.10.0) and Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment was carried out by BINGO. In all data analysis procedures, multiple corrections was applied using a Benjamin–Hochberg corrected p value cut-off of 0.5.

Protoplast Swelling Assays

Surface sterilized seeds after 48h vernalization were germinated and grown vertically on sucrose starved medium (½ MS solid medium with 0.02% sucrose). Root from 10 to 20 seedlings were cut into small pieces and cell walls were digested in 300 mM mannitol, 10 mM MES-KOH pH 5.8, 10 mM CaCl2, 10 mM KCl containing 1% (w/v) cellulase Onozuka R10 (Duchefa) and 1% (w/v) macerozyme R10 (Duchefa). After 3 h dark incubation the protoplasts lysis went through a 50 μm nylon mesh. Then the filtered protoplasts were enriched by centrifugation at 80g at 4 °C for 10 min, and washed three times with ice-cold wash buffer (300 mM mannitol, 10 mM MES-KOH pH 5.8, 10 mM CaCl2, 10 mM KCl). Finally, the protoplasts were resuspended in 150 μl wash buffer and on ice for at least 30 min before an experiment was started. Protoplast swelling perfusion systems were set up according to previous work (18). The PEP7 and BL treatments were applied into buffers with 30 mM mannitol (supplemental Table S3). The diameter of the protoplasts was measured directly by using the Leica software LAS X Core, followed by the calculation of volume and surface area at each time point. A regression curve was fitted to the volume change and the maximal slope was obtained from the first derivative of the curve. The maximal water flux density that corresponds to aquaporin activity was determined by the maximal slope divided by the protoplast surface area at the corresponding time point. Curve fitting and derivative calculation were performed with OriginPro software.

Hypocotyl Length Measurement

Seeds were transferred to vertical ½MS plates with or without 100 nM brassinolide (BL) or 1 mM PEP7 and grown in darkness for 5 days. Plates were then photographed, and hypocotyl lengths were measured using ImageJ. For statistical analysis and graphical representation of the data, OriginPro software and Excel were used.

Results

Distinct and Common BRI1 and SIRK1 Interactome

In order to characterize the BRI1 and SIRK1 ligand-induced interactome, plants expressing p35S::BRI1-GFP or p35S::SIRK1-GFP were exposed to sucrose stimulation after sucrose starvation (as the original stimulus under which SIRK1 phosphorylation and complex formation was found), to brassinolide (BL) stimulation as a ligand to BRI1, or to PEP7 treatment as a ligand to SIRK1 (Fig. 1, A and B). In addition, a joint treatment of sucrose and BL was applied. The microsomal membrane fractions were extracted from the root tissues and the interactomes of both RKs under the different stimuli were compared using an AE-MS approach (47) (Fig. 1C). In all experiments, bait protein abundance was within highest 25% of the protein abundances of all protein identified in each AE-MS experiment (Fig. 1D). To compare the interactome of the same bait between different treatments, the variation of the bait abundance has to be considered. Therefore, bait protein abundances were used to normalize prey abundance and thus to allow comparison between treatments. After normalization, the bindomes under each condition showed a normal distribution and most proteins defined as interactors were of lesser abundance than the bait (values lower than zero) (supplemental Fig. S1A).

We refer to the “bindome” as the total set of proteins identified in the pulldown. Considering occasional missing values when comparing the AE-MS data sets from the microsomal fractions of different genotypes and treatments, we established the following criteria to determine the specific stimulus-induced interactome of each bait: Filter A consisted of proteins outside a two-standard-deviation range of the distribution of log2-fold change (treatment/control). Filter B described proteins with statistically different log2-fold change (treatment/control) based on pairwise t test (p < 0.05) and after Benjamini-Hochberg FDR multiple testing correction (supplemental Fig. S1B). There was a high overlap of proteins described by Filter A and B, and the respective proteins were considered as interactors of the bait under treatment condition in case of positive log2-fold change or as interactors of the bait under control condition in case of negative log2-fold change. Filter C refers to proteins which were quantified in the majority (>75%) of replicates under one condition, but were not identified or not quantified under the other condition. These proteins were considered as part of the interactome under the condition they were identified (supplemental Fig. S1B). Membrane proteins which were found in the bindome of the p35S::GFP control were excluded from the data interpretation (supplemental Table S4). Remaining proteins were considered as the interactors of the bait under respective conditions (supplemental Fig. S1C) and positive fold-change indicated that the interactor was assembled with the bait upon the treatment, and conversely, a negative fold-change suggested disassembly or reduced interaction.

In total, 3640 proteins were identified in the AE-MS experiments with SIRK1-GFP and BRI1-GFP, and in average about 16% of these were plasma membrane proteins (according to SUBA-database (45, 46)), and these were considered for detailed analysis. Within these 599 plasma membrane proteins, and after exclusion of proteins found in GFP-only pulldowns, we defined 278 proteins as conditional plasma membrane (PM) interactors for SIRK1 and 236 for BRI1. A total of 150 proteins were defined as interactors to both bait proteins, SIRK1 and BRI1 (supplemental Fig. S1C). Altogether, we defined 664 interaction pairs for both baits at the plasma membrane (supplemental Table S4).

Analysis of functional composition of both AE-MS interactomes using MAPMAN bin3.6 (44)revealed signaling components (bin 30) as highly enriched in both AE-MS interactome (supplemental Fig. S1D). Within bin 30.2, the subgroup of RKs was the top enrichment cluster, including the respective coreceptors BAK1 and QSK1. Besides, other basic signaling components were also strongly enriched in both interactomes, including RLCKs, RLPs, MAP-kinases, calcium signaling proteins, G-proteins and 14-3-3 proteins. A large proportion of these proteins was found among the 150 interactors identified with both bait receptors (supplemental Fig. S1C). On the other hand, some of the functional groups enriched differently in the SIRK1 and BRI1 interactome. For example, proteins from the RNA related functional cluster (bin 27) were more overrepresented in BRI1 interactome than SIRK1, while proteins of posttranslational modification cluster (bin 29.4) were more enriched in the interactome of SIRK1 compared to the BRI1 interactome. In addition, although SIRK1 has been reported to interact with aquaporins (bin 34.19.1) upon sucrose treatment and PEP7 treatment (15, 48) the overall interactome of BRI1 was even more enriched in proteins from the transport group (bin 34), covering also ABC transporters (bin 34.16) and amino acid transporters (bin 34.3) (supplemental Fig. S1D and supplemental Table S4). These results indicate that in general, SIRK1 and BRI1 interacted with similar signaling components and shared proteins in response to these treatments. However, the two RKs SIRK1 and BRI1 also showed clearly specific recruitment preferences towards other functional component at the plasma membrane.

To validate the identification of interactors, we compared the AE-MS pairs with STRING database. There were 35 interaction pairs published in STRING regarding SIRK1 and BRI1. In our AE-MS data, 28 of the published pairs were found, covering 80% of the published data (Fig. 2A and supplemental Table S5). For these 28 published pairs, the interaction was determined by experimental data (67%), co-expression (63%), text-mining (88%) and homology analysis (63%) and database searching (55%) in STRING. As we combined all the interaction pairs from STRING database with AE-MS data, we could see a highly connected interaction network between BRI1 and SIRK1, though the both bait receptors did not directly interact (supplemental Table S5). To identify pivotal proteins within this network, we calculated the betweenness centrality and topological coefficient for each protein node. These metrices assess a node's importance in network connectivity. Specifically, nodes with lower topological coefficients are crucial as topological bridges, while higher betweenness centrality values indicate nodes frequently traversed by the shortest paths within the network. Notably, the proteins within top 1% of highest betweenness centrality showed the topological coefficients less than 0.1 (Fig. 2B and supplemental Table S6). Due to the nature of the AE-MS experiment set-up, as expected, baits BRI1 and SIRK1 showed highest betweenness centrality and the lowest topological coefficient. Among the proteins with low topological coefficient and high betweenness centrality values we found proteins from the highest functionally enriched cluster, bin 30 and bin 30.2 (supplemental Table S6), among them also the coreceptors BAK1 and QSKs. This suggests that the coreceptors serve as key bridges connecting BRI1 and SIRK1 to the respective cellular network and to the cross-talk between the two signaling pathways.

We could not find evidence from AE-MS and STRING database for a direct interaction between SIRK1 and BRI1 (Fig. 2C). We used split-YFP (rBiFC) to further test the interaction between SIRK1 and BRI1, and to compare with established interactions of BRI1 and BAK1 or SIRK1 and QSK1 (3, 19). While BRI1 and SIRK1 showed strong interactions with their coreceptors BAK1 and QSK1, respectively, the fluorescence signal for BRI1 and SIRK1 was outside the range accepted as evidence for interaction based on respective controls (Fig. 2D). This suggests that BRI1 and SIRK1 do not form a stable direct interaction in planta and function as independent receptors, at least under the conditions studied here.

Ligand-Induced Kinase-target Network Topology Differs Between BRI1 and SIRK1

We analysed common and specific signalling events initiated by ligand-induced interactions by studying respective phosphorylation patterns. Phosphorylation induced by BRI1 and SIRK1 upon treatment with BL and PEP7 was derived by re-analysis of an existing phosphoproteomic data set of PEP7-induced SIRK1 signalling (48), and performance of a similar experiment to uncover potential BL-induced phosphorylation targets of BRI1. The selection criteria for ligand-induced phosphorylation sites (p-sites) included stability (no change) in the bri1-301 or sirk1 mutant and significant changes (beyond the mean ± SD) in the wild type upon BL or PEP7 treatment. Our analysis revealed a substantial (30% to 40%) overlap of phosphorylated proteins as putative substrates between the BL-BRI1 and PEP7-SIRK1 pathways (Fig. 3A), and the overlapping substrates were found with the same phosphosites. Specifically, 54 of 95 (56%) of the BL-induced phosphoproteins (BL-BRI1 substrates) were also found in the BL-induced BRI1 interactome, and 27 of 66 (40%) substrates for PEP7-induced phosphoproteins (PEP7-SIRK1 substrates) were found within the PEP7 or sucrose induced SIRK1 interactome (Fig. 3B and supplemental Table S7). This supports the robustness of PM AE-MS in obtaining direct or indirect phosphorylation targets as interaction partners to the bait. In the BRI1 interactome, BAK1 was observed under individual and combined treatments of BL and sucrose. In the SIRK1 interactome, QSK1 was consistently identified across all treatment modalities (Fig. 3B), highlighting differential impacts on ligand-induced heterodimer formation and the potential for cross-talk between these two pathways.

To further explore how treatments impact potential signalling pathways, we filtered the experimentally identified BL-BRI1 and PEP7-SIRK1 phosphorylation substrates for known kinase-target relationships based on PhosPhAt 4.0 (49) and then mapped the BRI1 and SIRK1 interactomes onto this network (Fig. 3, C and D). This analysis revealed distinct interaction patterns within the kinase-target network (Fig. 3E). Specifically, in the BRI1 interactome, BL and PEP7 treatments led to divergent kinase-substrate interactions, whereas within the SIRK1 interactome, the two treatments produced more uniform kinase-substrate interactions (Fig. 3E). The observed divergence or uniformity in the networks was demonstrated by the overlap of targets and the comparative analysis of network density, heterogeneity, and centralization. Specifically, the network heterogeneity of the BRI1 substrates activated by BL was 1.4, and 2.2 when activated by PEP7. In contrast, the BL-induced SIRK1 kinase-target network exhibited a heterogeneity of 1.3, and 1.4 upon PEP7 treatment (Fig. 3E).

Within the BRI1 interactome, BL treatment resulted in interaction with and phosphorylation of known BRI1 substrates, such as BAK1, BIR1 and BSKs, as well as H+-pump AHA2. Proteins like VAP27-1, ADL3, HIR2, and REM1.2, crucial for microdomain organization or organelle communication, interacted with BRI1 also in response to PEP7 treatment (supplemental Fig. S2A). By contrast, among the SIRK1 interactome, upon PEP7 treatment we found interaction with and phosphorylation of known SIRK1 substrates, such as QSKs and PIP2F, but also other proteins, such as ACA10 or PEN3. Interestingly, most of these known and putative SIRK1 substrate proteins were also found to interact with SIRK1 upon BL treatment (supplemental Fig. S2B).

These findings imply that the signalling pathways of PEP7 and BL are finely balanced through respective receptors SIRK1 and BRI1. However, there are clear indications of significant modulation of PEP7-SIRK1 ligand-induced interactions and pathway activations by phosphorylation through BL or the BL-BRI1 signalling pathway.

Domain-Swap Interactome and Unsupervised Classification Revealed Functional Component for Interaction Specificity

To dissect which parts of the ligand-binding receptors direct the different ligand-inducible interactomes, we created chimeric RKs between SIRK1 and BRI1, and compared their interactomes with the interactomes of native SIRK1 and BRI1 under different treatments. LSSB (long SIRK1-short BRI1), was composed of the extracellular domain (ECD), transmembrane domain (TM), and juxtamembrane domain (JM) of SIRK1, along with the kinase domain of BRI1 (KD). SSLB (short SIRK1-long BRI1) consisted of the ECD and TM domains of SIRK1, as well as the JM and KD of BRI1. LBSS (long BRI1-short SIRK1) consisted of ECD, TM, and JM of BRI1, along with the SIRK1 KD. SBLS (short BRI1-long SIRK1) was composed of the ECD and TM domains of BRI1, while including the JM and KD of SIRK1 (Fig. 4A).

All of the chimeric proteins were localized to the plasma membrane (supplemental Fig. S3A) and in all AE-MS experiments the bait proteins were quantified at top 25% within the bindome (supplemental Fig. S3B). As expected, the interactomes of the four domain-swap receptors showed a large overlap. Altogether, 2997 proteins, among them 373 PM proteins, were identified as interacting with at least one of the domain-swap RKs (supplemental Fig. S3C). The PM protein interactome of the domain-swap RKs overlapped by 81% with that of native SIRK1 and BRI1 receptors (Fig. 4B). Key PM signalling proteins such as coreceptors BAK1 and QSK1 were consistently identified across all chimeric interactomes. Additionally, BIR2, BSK1, and BSK7 were consistently present in all domain-swap RK interactomes. However, their interaction patterns differed with various stimuli. In the LSSB and LBSS constructs, the five common signalling components were recruited to the bait upon BL treatment. Conversely, in SBLS, these proteins were completely disassembled from the bait, and with SSLB, BAK1, QSK1, and BSK1 were retained, but BIR2 and BSK7 were disassembled (supplemental Table S8). Consequently, our results suggest that the dynamics of signalling complexes were influenced by the microenvironmental conditions created by various treatment and domain combinations at the plasma membrane. We propose that this dynamic might be moderated by a shared cluster of intermediate proteins that balances the signal transduction directions between distinct signalling pathways.

To quantify the contribution of RK domain elements to interaction preferences under the different treatments, in the following, we focused on the 309 proteins which were found to interact with the domain swap RKs as well as with the original RKs SIRK1 and BRI1. We then employed an unsupervised classification approach to quantify the impact of various treatments on the interactomes of the domain-swap RKs, SIRK1, and BRI1. The fold-change of interactor abundance upon a given treatment versus the control was used to calculate Euclidean distances for each bait-prey pair. We then applied k-means clustering to these distances. After determining the optimal number of clusters using silhouette scores, the bait-prey pairs were organized into four distinct clusters (supplemental Fig. S4, A and B). Hierarchical clustering analysis, based on the average Euclidean distances within these clusters, revealed distinct treatment effects: Clusters with PEP7 and sucrose treatments were grouped together, indicative of their role in activating SIRK1 (Fig. 4C). Clusters with BL treatments, both alone and combined with sucrose, formed separate groups, consistent with activation of a different signalling pathway by BL (Fig. 4C). Proteins in each cluster exhibited distinct treatment response patterns: Proteins in cluster 1 showed the interaction dynamics in response to BL treatment; proteins in cluster 2 showed highest responses to sucrose and PEP7; proteins in cluster 4 exhibited significant interaction dynamics under combined BL and sucrose treatment; proteins in the largest cluster 3 showed moderate response across all treatments.

To visualize these interactions, we constructed a network using the transformed Silhouette Coefficient factor (1-N) as the edge metric in a spring-embedded layout, highlighting the spatial dynamic clustering of bait-prey pairs (supplemental Fig. S4C). Cluster 3 encompassed many common prey proteins under different treatments, and BAK1 and QSK1 were found in this cluster. We therefore hypothesize that the interactors from cluster 3 are highly associated with particular domain structures of the respective baits in response to different ligands (Fig. 4D). A GO-term enrichment analysis of cluster 3 proteins revealed significant over-representation of protein kinase functions (e.g. coreceptors BAK1, QSK1), water channel activity (e.g. PIPs), ATPase activity (e.g. p-ATPases), and other transporters (Fig. 4E and supplemental Table S9).

As these proteins were found within the interactome of all the bait proteins, conducted a Pearson correlation analysis to assess the impact of domains structure on the recruitment of these substrates. Pearson correlation (PC) values were calculated between domain-swap kinase interactome and SIRK1 interactome based on treatment-induced log2-fold changes. We then decomposed the domain-swap RKs into three key components: the ECD, the JM, and the KD. Thereby individual domains of each chimera were associated with their corresponding Pearson correlation values of the respective bait protein (details see Supplementary Methods). We then added the Pearson correlation scores for each domain-prey combination. A higher summed Pearson correlation score suggested a stronger similarity in interaction partner recruitment between a given domain and the reference receptors SIRK1 or BRI1. Thus, the summed Pearson correlation scores were used to propose significant roles for respective domains in recruiting prey proteins, particularly coreceptors (Fig. 4F). The juxtamembrane domain of BRI1 was highly correlated with formation of heterodimers with BAK1, underscoring its critical role in this interaction. In contrast, the extracellular domain of BRI1 did not significantly correlate with QSK1 recruitment. Among the SIRK1 domains, the ECD showed highest correlation with recruitment of QSK1, outperforming the KD and JM domains. Notably, all domains of SIRK1 exhibited weak correlations with recruitment of BAK1 to respective bait proteins (Fig. 4F). This analysis supports the notion that ligand-induced heterodimer formation primarily occurs between BRI1 and BAK1, as well as between SIRK1 and QSK1. The potential affinity of different receptor domains for these interaction events varied between BRI1 and SIRK1.

Hierarchical clustering using Ward Linkage on Pearson correlation scores revealed eight interactor clusters with specific correlations to receptor domains: Prey proteins in clusters C7 (e.g. BAK1, BSK1) and C3 (e.g. QSK1, QSK2, PIPs) showed strong correlation to bait proteins containing the ECD of BRI1 or SIRK1, respectively. Clusters C4 (e.g. BAK1, BSK1, BSK2, AHA2) and C6 (e.g. FLAs, other RKs) contained prey proteins correlating with recruitment to bait proteins containing the JM domains of BRI1 or SIRK1. Clusters C5 (e.g. dynamins, SKU5, VAMP27–1) and C8 (e.g. RABs, secretory carriers, SYPs) contained proteins correlated with proteins containing the KD of BRI1 and SIRK1, respectively. The average correlation scores of these clusters exceeded 0.48. (Fig. 4G and supplemental Table S10). This analysis corroborates the hypothesis that ligand-induced heterodimers of RKs are influenced by their respective structural domains, which govern their predominant interactome at the plasma membrane. However, the analysis is based on correlations, which besides suggesting direct interactions also could include indirect secondary interactions, e.g. through recruited coreceptors. Nevertheless, these findings raise question of how in the chimeric receptors the composite domain structure impacts the activation of downstream signalling pathways and subsequently alters the phenotypic responses of plants expressing the chimera.

Classification of Chimeras RLK Interactome

To predict the direction of signalling activation in chimeric receptors, i.e. whether the signalling output is more SIRK1-like or more BRI1-like, we employed a supervised machine learning (ML) approach. This method assesses the similarity in interaction behaviour, which aids in predicting the affected signalling pathways under different conditions and in respective chimera. Firstly, we used the SIRK1 and BRI1 interactome datasets as training datasets to evaluate the performance of the learning algorithms. The learning features comprised a categorical feature, which involved bincode classification from MapMan 3.6, and numeric features representing the log2-fold change for each prey upon different treatments versus control. We compared the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves of several algorithms – Random Forest, Gradient Boosting (XBoost), Naive Bayes, and Neural Network – finding that Gradient Boosting exhibited the highest Area Under Curve (AUC) for interactors associated with both SIRK1 and BRI1 (Fig. 5A), leading us to further apply this method. The confusion matrix based on the Gradient Boosting algorithm demonstrated the sensitivity and specificity of the predictions, with 80.9% accuracy in predicting BRI1-like interactions and 71.6% accuracy in predicting SIRK1-like interactions (Fig. 5B).

Hence, this model enabled the classification of treatment-inducible interactions within the interactome of the domain-swap RKs. The model determined whether the respective interaction pairs are more similar to the interactome of SIRK1 or more similar to the interactome of BRI1 (supplemental Table S8, and supplemental Fig. S5A). We then performed a projection of informative components analysis from which two prominent projections emerged: the likelihood of the SIRK1 interactome as defined by the model, and fold-change abundance change of prey proteins induced by BL treatment (Fig. 5C). When distinct colours were assigned to the interactomes of various domain-swapped RKs, it became obvious that the interactomes for LSSB, LBSS, and SBLS grouped into distinct clusters. Notably, quadrant I highlighted interactions that were both induced by BL treatment and closely resembled recruitment characteristics of SIRK1 (Fig. 5C). In this quadrant I we found an enrichment predominantly of interactors to bait LSSB (red area). Consequently, the interactome of LSSB was more similarly to SIRK1 interactome (gradient boosting prediction likelihood to SIRK1 > 0.5), and its interactions were induced by BL treatment (highest density of red area at BL-induced log2-fold change >0). On the other hand, LBSS (blue area) had a higher likelihood of interaction patters similar to BRI1 (gradient boosting prediction likelihood to SIRK1 < 0.5), and its interactors were more likely to assemble upon BL treatment (highest density of blue area at BL-induced log2-fold change >0). By contrast, the majority of SBLS (green area) interactors showed disassembly upon BL treatment (BL-induced log2-fold change <0), and the density of interactors was higher when gradient boosting prediction likelihood to SIRK1 was low (Fig. 5C). The scatter plot further aimed to identify the positions of coreceptors. In the LSSB interactome (red area), recruitment of QSK1 was similar to SIRK1, but particularly in response to BL treatment. Recruitment of BAK1 to bait LSSB fell between the characteristic interactions of SIRK1 and BRI1. In the LBSS interactome (blue area), both BAK1 and QSK1 were recruited in a manner that resembled the interaction pattern of BRI1. The SBLS interactome (green area) showed a depletion of BAK1 upon BL treatment with a gradient boosting prediction score close to the interactome of SIRK1, whereas QSK1 was also depleted but exhibited prediction scores closer to the interactome of BRI1. Lastly, SSLB (orange area) recruited QSK1 in response to BL similarly to LBSS, and BAK1 in a manner akin to SBLS (Fig. 5C). The classification approach successfully dissected the interaction behaviour of the chimeric receptors, enabling the extraction of prominent contributions from specific treatments and domains. As a result, we can predict the potential of the chimeric receptors in terms of downstream signalling activation or phenotype complementation.

To further investigate the signalling pathways activated upon BL treatment in the chimeric receptor LSSB, we mapped the BL-BRI1 and PEP7-SIRK1 phosphorylation substrates onto the classification-derived likelihood interactome results of LSSB (Fig. 5D). Both under PEP7 and BL treatments, LSSB showed interactions with SIRK1 phosphorylation substrates, e.g. QSK1 and PIP2F, mirroring the behaviour of SIRK1 with considerable overlap in treatment effects. Under BL treatment specifically, LSSB also interacted with direct substrates of BRI1, namely BAK1 and BIR2, mimicking interaction patterns of BRI1. Additionally, PEP7 seemed to direct LSSB interactions towards microdomain (HIR2 and REM1.2) or membrane trafficking proteins (VAP27). This similar to known interactions observed for BRI1 (Fig. 5D and supplemental Figs. S3A, and S5). Plotting Gradient boosting models prediction likelihoods of LSSB interactions against log2-fold change upon PEP7 treatment again revealed high similarity to SIRK1 interactome. Interaction of LSSB with QSK1 was again found in quadrant I, with PIP2F closely aligned (supplemental Fig. S5B).

We therefore conclude that external BL may increase plant internal PEP7 abundance (Fig. 5E), leading to a heterodimer formation between LSSB and QSK1, and a signalling complex with PIP4F, potentially regulating aquaporin activity. Moreover, BL-induced PEP7 secretion may also activate LSSB towards BRI1 responsive pathways, as BAK1 can be recruited to LSSB via BL treatment, indicating phenotypic responses to PEP7 similar to SIRK1 and to BL similar to BRI1. In contrast, for LBSS, among the interactors more BRI1-substrates than SIRK1 substrates were found upon BL and PEP7 treatment, suggesting LBSS could activate a BRI1-responsive signalling pathway (supplemental Fig. S5C).

Validating Classification-Based Prediction of Chimeric Receptor Activity

To confirm the predictions above, we firstly conducted a kinase activity assay, to assess functionality of the chimeric receptors. We used the affinity purification method to isolate the kinase complex from Arabidopsis roots following various ligand treatments (supplemental Fig. S6). In the case of SIRK1, consistent with a previous study (48) the treatment with PEP7 significantly increased the kinase activity of SIRK1. BRI1 kinase activity was enhanced when treated with BL. As for the chimeric receptor LSSB, and in accordance with our predictions, both PEP7 and BL substantially induced kinase activity. Conversely, LBSS displayed no significant alteration in kinase activity compared to control condition when subjected to BL or PEP7 treatment, but kinase activity under BL treatment was slightly higher than under PEP7 treatment (supplemental Fig. S6A). To further assess the activation of these kinase complexes by PEP7, we conducted in vitro dose-response assay using SIRK1-GFP, BRI1-GFP, LSSB-GFP and LBSS-GFP, which were purified from transient expression in tobacco leaves. Notably, the kinase activity of SIRK1-GFP and LSSB-GFP exhibited dose-dependent kinase activity with increasing PEP7 concentrations. By contrast, both BRI1 and LBSS failed to demonstrate a dose-response relationship with PEP7 (supplemental Fig. S6B). Therefore, in line with our predictions, PEP7 can effectively activate the chimeric receptor LSSB in a manner that is dependent on the dosage of PEP7.

Following PEP7 induction, the SIRK1-QSK1 complex transmits phosphorylation signals to aquaporins, thereby controlling water influx into the protoplasts (Fig. 5E). Protoplast swelling assays were subsequently carried out to evaluate the phenotypic outcomes of both chimeric receptors and the proposed influence of BL on the PEP7-SIRK1 signalling pathway (Fig. 5E). In wild-type, both PEP7 and BL were able to induce water permeability in the protoplast. The sirk1 knock-out mutant showed insensitivity to PEP7 treatment, but its water flux density was increased by BL treatment. In bri1-301, both PEP7 and BL were able to increase the level of water permeability. However, BL treatment introduced significant data variation, suggesting potential indirect effects. The sirk1×bri1-301 double mutant was insensitive to PEP7 but showed increased water influx upon BL, again accompanied by considerable variation. When we overexpressed the different chimeric receptors in the double mutant background, as predicted, LSSB and SIRK1 mimicked wild-type responses in the protoplast swelling assay (Fig. 5F).

Given that chimeric receptor LSSB can also be activated by BL (supplemental Fig. S6A), we explored its ability to complement the bri1 mutant phenotype. We hypothesized that LSSB could correct BRI1-deficient phenotypes and tested this in a hypocotyl growth assay by overexpressing LSSB and LBSS in the bri1-301 mutant. Consistent with previous studies (28), wild type exhibited curly hypocotyl elongation under BL treatment, resulting in shorter lengths compared to bri1-301. BRI1 as well as LSSB, which contain the BRI1 kinase domain, successfully restored the mutant phenotype (supplemental Fig. S7, A and B). By contrast, SIRK1 and the chimeric receptor LBSS, both containing the SIRK1 kinase domain, failed to complement the BL-induced phenotype. This finding further supported our hypothesis that BL alters the balance towards PEP7 activation, which subsequently activated LSSB through the SIRK1 extracellular domain. The activation of LSSB, together with recruitment of BAK1 to the signaling complex, resulted in BRI1-like downstream signal transduction, able to compensate the bri1-301 phenotype. The application of external PEP7 activated LSSB directly via the SIRK1 extracellular domain, resulting in mildly curly hypocotyls similar as in BL-BRI1 response, while no such differences were observed with PEP7 induced LBSS (supplemental Fig. S7, C and D).

BR Affected PEP7 Production and PEP7-Induced Heterodimer Formation

The interactome and complementation properties of LSSB suggests that it activated by PEP7 as well as by BL. In fact, BL treatment induces QSK1 recruitment and LSSB interaction in a manner similar to SIRK1 (Fig. 5, C and D). While activation of LSSB by PEP7 was obvious via SIRK1-ECD in LSSB, we propose the activation of LSSB by BL was brought about by BL affecting internal PEP7 levels (Fig. 5E). To investigate this in detail, we analyzed PEP7 in apoplasmic extracts from roots under different treatments (Fig. 6A). Moreover, to assess the impact of reduced BL levels, we employed BRZ, a recognized inhibitor of BL biosynthesis (50). We found that BL, sucrose, or the combination of both treatments could enhance the abundance of PEP7 in apoplasmic fluids compared to sucrose starvation (Fig. 6B). Inhibition of BL biosynthesis by BRZ did not result in enhanced PEP7 secretion. In the sirk1xbri1-301 double mutant, a similar response pattern was observed, except that BL treatment did not, but sucrose did enhance PEP7 secretion. This suggests that both BL and BRI1 receptor signaling impacted PEP7 production (Fig. 6B).

To assess the impact of BR on SIRK1 signalling complex formation, we employed plants overexpressing the SIRK1-GFP fusion protein in a pep7 background. These plants were subjected to treatments involving BL, PEP7, and sucrose and the interaction SIRK1 with QSK1 and PIP2F was quantified by an AE-MS experiment. As expected, PEP7 treatment induced the interaction of SIRK1 with QSK1 and PIP2F (17, 48). However, neither BL nor sucrose treatment alone resulted in the assembly of the SIRK1 signalling complex in the pep7 mutant (Fig. 6C). When this experiment was repeated by expressing SIRK1-GFP in a sirk1 mutant background, treatments with BL, sucrose, and PEP7 induced interactions between SIRK1 and its phosphorylation targets QSK1 and PIP2F (Fig. 6C).

These results confirmed that internal levels of brassinosteroids and sucrose influence PEP7 production, which in turn affected the interactions within the SIRK1 signalling complex. Taken together, through domain-swapping and dynamic interactome analysis, combined with bioinformatic approaches we uncovered a complex balance among sucrose, brassinosteroids, and PEP7 signals. This balance operates through ligand-inducible heterodimer formation at the plasma membrane, thereby affecting the interaction with and signaling to respective substrates.

Discussion

Cell surface RKs are pivotal in enabling multicellular organisms to respond to external signals. This study aimed at understanding interaction dynamics of RKs at the plasma membrane, and it explicitly was not designed to unravel components of signaling cascades. We leveraged the AE-MS approach to efficiently identify interacting substrates or associated proteins for two receptors, BRI1 and SIRK1, and we applied unsupervised machine learning approaches and domain swapping to quantify the contribution of receptor domains to definition of respective interactomes.

Classification Applications and Receptor Engineering in Understanding Dynamic Interactomes

Machine learning has become increasingly popular in the field of proteomics research in recent years to gain statistically validated classifications and patterns. It is being used to tackle various challenges in the analysis of complex proteomic data, such as protein localization dynamics in spatial proteomics, biomarkers identification, or structural bioinformatics (51, 52, 53, 54). Various algorithms of machine learning assist the biologist in proposing mechanistic hypotheses, testing existing hypotheses or predicting behavior in different biological systems (55, 56). Unsupervised learning methods can reveal patterns or similarities from complex large datasets, while supervised learning methods can be adopted for classification or mapping of probability features to the unknown based on the known training data. These methods have been well applied for protein-protein interaction studies in the past (57).

In our study, we intentionally focused on distinct receptors with non-overlapping coreceptor preferences, namely PEP7-SIRK1-QSK1 and BL-BRI1-BAK1, but already the finding that SIRK1 and BRI1 ligand-inducible interactomes showed a large overlap suggested a strong cross-talk between the two signaling pathways. Domain swap engineering approaches in past research have proven to be powerful tools for dissecting the mechanisms of receptor activation and signal transduction (25, 26, 27, 58). Activation of chimeric receptors can lead to either phenotype complementation or the expected post-translational modification of downstream substrates, and thus helps to dissect the role of respective domains in that signaling process. Swapping domains between ligand-binding receptors that share the same coreceptors, such as the SERKs, has generally been shown to be feasible (25, 59). Similarly, swapping domains between different coreceptors (e.g BIR3 and BAK1), or their heterodimer partner RKs (e.g. BIR3 and SGN3) can also lead to the expected activation (58). However, the evolutionary position and structural conservation of different RK signaling pathways within the plant kingdom also play a role in functionality of chimeric receptors (60).

The unexpectedly strong cross-talk between the BRI1 and SIRK1 signaling pathways we encountered here, resulted in a mixed ligand-induced response of the chimeric receptor interactomes, especially regarding the chimeric receptor LSSB. In this context, machine learning, appropriately adapted to our needs, proves invaluable in distinguishing and weighting the features of the ligand-inducible interactome, along with the specificity of domains involved. At the same time, these predictions facilitated the formulation of new hypotheses, namely of BL-induced PEP7 generation and the responsiveness of the chimeric receptors under the respective treatments. These hypotheses were then confirmed experimentally. Therefore, applying machine learning in AE-MS proteomics shows the potential to greatly enhance the functional understanding of interactomes and their dynamics.

The Dynamic Interactome of Receptor Kinases at the Plasma Membrane

Our findings confirmed that SIRK1 and BRI1 did not directly interact, possibly due to their distinct microdomain locations, suggesting that their interactomes at the plasma membrane overlapped through common substrates rather than direct interaction. QSK1 was found as a common phosphorylation target upon both PEP7 and BR treatments and, besides being a direct interactor and substrate in the PEP7-SIRK1 signaling pathway (17, 48), we also found it associated with BRI1. Previous AP-MS experiments using BSK3, a component of the BRI1-signaling cascade as a bait, linked BSK3 also to QSK1 (61), (supplemental Table S7). Thus, coreceptors, BSKs, and key transporters such as PIPs or AHAs may be part of different regulatory complex in response to specific treatments, thereby modulating and balancing the signalling pathways induced by different RKs.

Mechanistic details of how specificity of LRR-RK signaling pathways is achieved are not yet fully elucidated, especially in view of certain coreceptors (e.g. BAK1) being recruited to multiple ligand-binding receptors and being part of different complexes in response to more than one stimulus. The efficiency with which common partners are recruited to respective complexes around distinct receptors in turn will regulates the responsive dose and phosphorylation efficiency of each receptor substrates, thereby directing signal transduction and impacting phenotypic performance. Such a concept of signalling balance was already described for competitive recruitment of coreceptor BAK1 to ligand-binding receptor FLS2 (upon pathogen attack) or ligand-binding receptor BRI1 (upon BL induction) (62). This, in general, is regulated at three levels: through the regulation of respective ligand homeostasis, dynamic interactions at the plasma membrane, and shared substrates or downstream signaling components. As a result, the capability of the plant to respond to different external cues is affected by the degree of occupation of common interaction partners in respective distinct signalling complexes.

We found that interactors of BRI1 and SIRK1 upon BL or PEP7 treatment contained a common set of proteins with polarized membrane location, including microdomain markers like HIR2 and REM1.2, and the TGN-associated protein VAP27 (63, 64). Also, there was a significant overlap among proteins affected by the Adaptor Protein (AP) complex suggesting their engagement in forming microdomains and recycling membrane proteins (65) (supplemental Table S8). Nanoscale mobility of BAK1 is influenced by peptide signaling and its interactions with various RKs (10). The molecular mechanisms of regulating and enabling within-membrane mobility, however, has yet to be resolved. Interestingly, proteins associated with microdomains were identified as putative phosphorylation substrates in the PEP7-SIRK1 and BL-BRI1 pathways, making them excellent candidates in regulating membrane positioning of the receptors.

Balance of Sucrose, BL, and PEP7 Signaling Pathways

Sucrose, a disaccharide composed of glucose and fructose subunits, has long been known to engage in cross-talk with BR signalling. It affects BR signalling by modulating the expression of BR homeostasis genes after sucrose starvation and regulating the accumulation of BR precursors in darkness, thereby influencing plant morphology under dark growth conditions (32, 66). At the plasma membrane, glucose plays a concentration-dependent role in the physical interactions and phosphorylation of BRI1 and BAK1, consequently affecting hypocotyl growth (67). BZR1 acts as a downstream substrate in the BRI1-BAK1 signalling pathway (67). In the dark, TOR inhibits BZR1 autophagy in response to sugar, promoting growth (32). Therefore, at low carbohydrate levels, sugar promotes BR signalling through various pathways.

Moreover, in our study, BL was found to impact the formation of the PEP7-SIRK1 signalling complex, leading to SIRK1-dependent aquaporin activity. However, it may affect water influx also through other unknown pathways, which were not BRI1- or SIRK1-dependent (Fig. 5F). Although we identified many PIPs in the BRI1 interactome, indirect effects and secondary interaction cannot be excluded.

The molecular mechanism by which sucrose regulates PEP7 levels remain still unknown. In our study, we observed that both sucrose and BR can induce PEP7 accumulation in the apoplasm under low carbohydrate levels. This suggests that either the allocation of sucrose or the osmotic change induced by sucrose uptake could trigger PEP7 production (48). Recently, it was discovered that BR reduces mechanical constraints imposed by the plant cell wall, generating mechanical pressure between different cell layers (68). Hence, it is plausible that sucrose and BR modulate protein-protein interactions through shared biological or biophysical pathways to promote PEP7 generation. Further research is needed to fully elucidate the mechanisms behind this phenomenon.

Conclusion

In this study, we have demonstrated the effectiveness of an integrated AE-MS workflow for investigating the dynamics of the SIRK1 and BRI1 interactome. By analyzing the ligand-inducible heterodimer formation pattern through domain-swap engineering and a machine learning approach, we successfully made validated predictions regarding structure-function relationships of RKs. Furthermore, our findings provided evidence that expanded our understanding of the intricate signaling balance between sucrose, BL, and PEP7. In conclusion, our study showed the power of the integrated AE-MS workflow in combination with learning algorithms in unraveling complex protein interactions and sheds light on the regulatory mechanisms underlying the interplay of different plant signaling components. However, despite these advancements, there is still much to learn about how signaling is balanced in plants.
