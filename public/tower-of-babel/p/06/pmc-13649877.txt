Association between preconception serum homocysteine levels and early pregnancy loss in women with recurrent pregnancy loss
Authors: Xiuli Tian, Xin Yang, Chen Wang, Xiaofeng Li, Fang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundElevated homocysteine (HCY) levels have been implicated in early pregnancy loss (EPL) but evidence remains inconclusive. The aim of this study was to investigate the association between preconception serum HCY levels and EPL in women who have experienced recurrent pregnancy loss (RPL).MethodsUsing data from a prospective dynamic pregnancy loss cohort established by our group, we screened 812 eligible patients with RPL, collected their medical records, and followed them for subsequent pregnancy outcomes. Multivariable logistic regression was used to assess the relationship between preconception serum HCY levels and the risk of subsequent EPL. Generalized additive models were also used to examine nonlinear associations between serum HCY levels and EPL. Subgroup analyses were further performed according to age, body mass index (BMI), and the number of previous pregnancy losses.ResultsThe proportion of subsequent EPL was 22.66% (184/812). Significant differences in the proportion of EPL were observed across HCY quartiles (Q1: 10.45%, Q2: 14.80%, Q3: 21.23%, Q4: 43.84%; P < 0.0001). After adjusting for confounders, elevated preconception serum HCY levels were found to be associated with an increased risk of EPL (adjusted OR = 1.27, 95% CI: 1.14–1.42). Participants in Q3 and Q4 had significantly higher odds of EPL than those in Q1, with adjusted ORs of 5.55 (95% CI: 1.01–30.49) for Q3 and 10.79 (95% CI: 1.87–62.37) for Q4. Additionally, the analyses demonstrated a nonlinear relationship between preconception serum HCY levels and EPL risk. In this cohort, when HCY levels were ≥ 10.71 μmol/L, the risk of EPL increased significantly (adjusted OR = 1.27; 95% CI: 1.03–1.58).ConclusionsElevated preconception serum HCY levels were associated with an increased risk of EPL in women with RPL, and the data-derived threshold of 10.71 μmol/L supports preconception risk stratification in this high-risk population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00404-026-08538-5.Keywords: Recurrent pregnancy loss, Homocysteine, Early pregnancy loss, Subsequent pregnancy, Pregnancy loss cohort

Background

Pregnancy loss (PL) is a serious adverse reproductive event that affects 10% to 15% of clinically recognized pregnancies [1]. Approximately 1%–3% of women experience two or more consecutive pregnancy losses, a condition known as recurrent pregnancy loss (RPL) [2]. Pregnancy loss occurring before 10 weeks of gestation is usually classified as early pregnancy loss (EPL) [1, 3]. RPL is a complex multifactorial condition with a variety of causes, including genetic and anatomical abnormalities, endocrine disorders, genital tract infection, thrombophilia, and environmental and psychological factors. Despite this, the pathogenesis of approximately 60% of PL cases remains elusive [4]. In addition, the risk of subsequent pregnancy loss has been reported to increase progressively among women with varying numbers of previous PLs [5, 6]. PL can alter a couple’s psychological status, with varying degrees of anxiety and depression [7], and also increase the risk of long-term cardiovascular disease compared to women without PL [8].

Our previous study found that homocysteine (HCY) was one of the key biomarkers associated with subsequent pregnancy outcomes in patients with RPL [9]. HCY is an intermediate metabolite of methionine. Under physiological conditions, HCY is maintained at low levels in the blood. HCY can be converted into glutathione and S-adenosylmethionine, both of which play important roles in normal physiological functions. However, elevated HCY is associated with the occurrence and development of many diseases, including cardiovascular diseases, renal dysfunction, and cognitive impairments [10–13]. Recent studies have shown that elevated HCY is associated with various pregnancy complications, including preeclampsia (PE), early pregnancy loss (EPL), placental abruption (PA), intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR), gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), and venous thrombosis [14, 15]. Approximately one third of PLs before 20 weeks’ gestation were associated with elevated HCY levels in a 2014 cross-sectional study [16]. In contrast, a 2013 Spanish cohort study found no association between high HCY levels and PL [17].

HCY levels are significantly higher in nonpregnant women than in pregnant women and decrease steadily with advancing gestational age [18]. However, most current studies on HCY and pregnancy complications have been conducted during pregnancy. Few studies have explored the association between preconception HCY levels and subsequent pregnancy outcomes, and the threshold at which preconception HCY affects subsequent pregnancy outcomes remains unclear. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the association between preconception HCY levels and subsequent EPL and to determine a data-derived threshold for preconception HCY.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study was based on a prospective dynamic pregnancy loss cohort conducted at the Second Hospital of Lanzhou University in China [19] and aimed to explore the association between elevated preconception HCY levels and recurrent EPL in patients with RPL.

Study population

This study recruited 1411 nonpregnant women with a history of RPL who presented to the Department of Reproductive Medicine, The Second Hospital of Lanzhou University from September 2019 to December 2023, and the follow-up period ended in August 2024. The inclusion criteria were: 1) having experienced at least two pregnancy losses that met the diagnostic criteria of the European Society of Human Reproduction and Embryology (ESHRE) [4]; and 2) age between 20 and 45 years. The exclusion criteria were as follows: 1) participants who were lost to follow-up or had not yet become pregnant by the end of the study; 2) participants whose subsequent pregnancy outcomes included ectopic pregnancy, hydatidiform mole, fetal developmental abnormalities, or ongoing pregnancy at < 10 weeks; 3) participants with subsequent pregnancies conceived through assisted reproductive technology or twin pregnancies; and 4) participants with missing serum HCY data. After applying these criteria, 812 women were selected for analysis to explore the relationship between preconception serum HCY levels and the risk of EPL in subsequent pregnancies. The participant selection process is detailed in Fig. 1. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Hospital of Lanzhou University(Ethical Approval Number: 2019A-231), and written informed consent was obtained from each woman before enrollment.

Data collection

We collected demographic information (including age, height, weight, education level, ethnicity, age at menarche, and menstrual cycle), reproductive history—including total pregnancy numbers (TPs), pregnancy loss numbers (PLs), prior induced abortion, and prior live births—from outpatient medical records. Height and weight were measured using standardized calibrated equipment, including wall-mounted stadiometers for height and calibrated digital scales for weight. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by the square of body height in meters.

Blood samples were collected in the morning after an overnight fast. A comprehensive panel of clinical indicators was tested, including serum HCY, D-dimer (D-D), fasting blood glucose (FBG), fasting insulin (FINS), cholesterol (CHO), triglyceride (TG), high-density lipoprotein (HDL), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), serum urea (SUR), serum creatinine (SCR), serum uric acid (SUA), folate, and vitamin B12. Blood measurements were performed during preconception visits, 1–12 months before pregnancy. These indicators were analyzed within 1 h at our hospital according to the manufacturers’ standard protocols. The homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) was calculated as FBG × FINS / 22.5. Subsequent pregnancy outcomes were obtained during follow-up.

Follow-up of patients

Participants were systematically followed up at 6-month intervals to monitor the outcomes of their subsequent pregnancies, with the most recent follow-up conducted in August 2024. For participants who became pregnant during follow-up, pregnancy outcomes were monitored according to the standardized prenatal examination schedule. The primary outcome was EPL in a subsequent pregnancy. EPL was defined as pregnancy loss before 10 weeks of gestation, including biochemical pregnancy loss. Biochemical pregnancy loss was defined as a pregnancy confirmed by a positive serum or urine β-hCG test, but without ultrasound or histopathological evidence of intrauterine or extrauterine gestation [20].

Statistical analysis

Normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and compared using the independent-samples t-test. Skewed continuous variables were expressed as medians and interquartile ranges (25th percentile, 75th percentile) and were compared using the Mann–Whitney–Wilcoxon test. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test. To compare differences in variables among multiple groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or the Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn’s post hoc test was performed, as appropriate.

Multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to assess the association between preconception serum HCY levels and subsequent EPL. Potential confounders were adjusted for in three models: an unadjusted model; Model I, which was adjusted for age and BMI; and Model II, which was further adjusted for clinically relevant variables selected based on the baseline characteristics of our cohort.

We further applied a two-piecewise linear regression model with a smoothing function to examine the threshold effect of HCY on subsequent EPL. The data-derived threshold was determined using a trial-and-error approach by testing candidate thresholds within a predefined interval and choosing the point that yielded the maximum model likelihood.

In secondary analyses, the association between baseline HCY and subsequent EPL was assessed in subgroups stratified by the number of previous PLs (2 and ≥ 3), age (< 30 and ≥ 30 years), and BMI (< 24 and ≥ 24 kg/m2), using unadjusted and fully adjusted models.

A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All data were analyzed using the statistical packages R (the R Foundation; http://www.r-project.org; version 4.4.1) and Empower Stats (www.empowerstats.com, X&Y solutions, Inc. Boston, Massachusetts).

Associations of preconception serum HCY with subsequent EPL

By the final follow-up, 184 of 812 patients (22.66%) had experienced EPL in their subsequent pregnancy. A significant increasing trend in the proportion of EPL was observed across serum HCY quartiles, with proportions of 10.45%, 14.80%, 21.23%, and 43.84% from Q1 to Q4, respectively (P < 0.001; Table 2).

As shown in Table 3, multivariable logistic regression analyses consistently showed that elevated baseline serum HCY levels were associated with an increased risk of EPL in subsequent pregnancies. This association was consistent across models; in the nonadjusted model, each 1 μmol/L increase in HCY was associated with a 27% increased risk of EPL (odds ratio [OR] = 1.27; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.20–1.35; P < 0.0001). The association remained significant in Model I, adjusted for age and BMI (OR = 1.28; 95% CI: 1.21–1.35; P < 0.001). In the fully adjusted Model II, which included additional confounding variables, the increased risk of EPL per unit increase in HCY was 27% (OR = 1.27; 95% CI: 1.14–1.42; P < 0.001). The associations across HCY quartiles also remained significant. In the unadjusted model, compared with Q1 as the reference group, the odds of EPL increased significantly in Q3 and Q4 (OR for Q3 = 2.31, 95% CI: 1.32–4.04; OR for Q4 = 6.69, 95% CI: 3.94–11.37; P for trend < 0.001). After further adjustment for age, BMI, and additional confounders in Model II, the increased risk persisted (OR for Q3 = 5.55, 95% CI: 1.01–30.49; OR for Q4 = 10.79, 95% CI: 1.87–62.37; P for trend < 0.001). These findings demonstrate that higher preconception serum HCY levels were associated with an increased risk of EPL. The consistent point estimate for continuous HCY across the nonadjusted model, Model I, and Model II supports the stability of this association after multivariable adjustment, and the quartile-based results demonstrate a graded increase in EPL risk across higher HCY categories.

Data-derived HCY threshold for subsequent EPL

As illustrated in Fig. 2A, our analysis revealed a nonlinear relationship between preconception serum HCY levels and the risk of EPL. A data-derived threshold was identified at an HCY level of 10.71 μmol/L in the current cohort. Below this threshold, increases in HCY levels did not significantly correlate with EPL risk (P > 0.05), suggesting a level of physiological tolerance or a threshold effect in the influence of HCY on pregnancy outcomes. However, once serum HCY levels reached or exceeded 10.71 μmol/L, the risk of EPL in subsequent pregnancies increased significantly. This increased risk persisted across analytical models; in the nonadjusted model, each 1 μmol/L increase in HCY levels above 10.71 μmol/L was associated with a 26% higher risk of EPL (OR = 1.26; 95% CI: 1.16–1.37; P < 0.0001). The association remained significant in Model I (adjusted for age and BMI) and Model II (further adjusted for additional confounders), with odds ratios of 1.26 (95% CI: 1.15–1.37; P < 0.0001) and 1.27 (95% CI: 1.03–1.58; P = 0.0281), respectively (Table 4). The analysis of EPL risk across HCY quartiles further supported these findings, as shown in Fig. 2B. The risk of EPL markedly increased with higher HCY quartiles, which supports the identified threshold effect.

Subgroup analysis and interaction test of the association between HCY levels and subsequent EPL

Subgroup analyses were performed according to age, BMI, and the number of previous PLs (Table 5). In the fully adjusted model, HCY as a continuous variable remained significantly associated with an increased risk of subsequent EPL among women aged ≥ 30 years (OR = 1.43; 95% CI: 1.16–1.76; P = 0.009), women with BMI < 24.0 kg/m2 (OR = 1.27; 95% CI: 1.06–1.53; P = 0.010), women with BMI ≥ 24.0 kg/m2 (OR = 1.47; 95% CI: 1.11–1.95; P = 0.007), women with two previous pregnancy losses (OR = 1.20; 95% CI: 1.01–1.43; P = 0.033), and women with three or more previous pregnancy losses (OR = 1.59; 95% CI: 1.16–2.18; P = 0.004). Among women aged < 30 years, the continuous HCY estimate after full adjustment remained positive but did not cross the conventional significance threshold (OR = 1.20; 95% CI: 0.98–1.48; P = 0.082). In the quartile-based analysis, Q4 HCY was significantly associated with an increased risk of EPL among women aged ≥ 30 years, both BMI subgroups, and both subgroups stratified by the number of previous PLs; in women aged < 30 years, the Q4 estimate remained directionally increased after full adjustment. The interaction tests were not statistically significant for age, BMI, or PL number, supporting the consistency of the association between HCY and subsequent EPL across these subgroups.

Discussion

This study shows that among patients with RPL, preconception serum HCY levels were associated with an increased risk of EPL, even after adjusting for age, BMI, and other clinically relevant confounders. Furthermore, using two-piecewise linear regression analyses, we identified a nonlinear association between preconception serum HCY concentrations and the likelihood of subsequent EPL. Notably, a data-derived preconception HCY threshold was identified at 10.71 μmol/L in the current cohort, defining a clear risk boundary for subsequent EPL in women with RPL. These findings have clinical relevance and provide a basis for prospective interventional studies evaluating targeted lowering of HCY to improve subsequent pregnancy outcomes in patients with RPL.

HCY is a crucial biomarker of overall health and is typically maintained at low concentrations in humans. However, a substantial prevalence of elevated HCY has been observed in specific populations, including pregnant women. This elevation is associated with racial, genetic, and lifestyle factors [10]. Beyond its established associations with cardiovascular, neurological, and chronic kidney diseases, emerging evidence has highlighted the adverse impact of elevated HCY on maternal and child health [14]. In this study, increased HCY levels were positively associated with the occurrence of EPL. Elevated HCY contributes to the development of various diseases through multiple mechanisms, including endothelial dysfunction, induced thrombophilia, epigenetic alterations, endoplasmic reticulum stress, and impaired folate metabolism [21–23]. However, there is no uniform clinical threshold value for HCY in clinical practice.

A long-term study in middle-aged and elderly cohorts revealed that all-cause mortality escalated significantly with HCY levels of 10.8 μmol/L or higher [24]. Similarly, a study in the Polish population indicated a marked increase in the relative risk of all-cause and cardiovascular disease (CVD) mortality when HCY levels exceeded 10.51 μmol/L [25]. In assisted reproductive technology, Ebisch et al. demonstrated that high concentrations of HCY (> 14.7 μmol/L) reduced embryo quality [26]. Ocal et al. found that the threshold value of HCY concentration in follicular fluid was 11.9 μmol/L, and that concentrations above this threshold were associated with a significantly increased risk of subsequent pregnancy failure in assisted reproductive technology [27]. In our study, we identified a data-derived threshold value for preconception serum HCY by analyzing the association between HCY and subsequent pregnancy outcomes in 812 patients with a history of RPL. This threshold has clinical implications for this high-risk population and provides a data-derived reference for preconception risk stratification. Notably, our study extends these observations to the preconception period in a well-characterized RPL cohort, providing a specific, clinically relevant threshold for this high-risk population.

In this study, we identified a 22.66% (184/812) prevalence of subsequent EPL among RPL patients. RPL is a risk factor for later cardiovascular disease (CVD), and the association between RPL and CVD risk increases with the number of previous PLs and is inversely related to maternal age. Potential mechanisms leading to RPL and an increased risk of CVD include endothelial dysfunction, a pro-inflammatory state, antiphospholipid syndrome, autoimmunity, and genetic predisposition [28, 29]. However, whether a single PL is associated with CVD remains under debate. Ranthe et al. reported that the association was positively correlated with the number of PL in a dose–response relationship, as each additional PL increased the rate of CVD by 9 to 20% [30]. By contrast, Maino et al. found that the adjusted odds ratio (OR) did not change significantly among women with one PL compared with controls or among women with three or more PLs. The risk of arterial thrombosis increased [31]. In our study, we found that patients with ≥ 3 PLs showed a stronger fully adjusted continuous association between HCY and EPL than patients with 2 PLs (OR = 1.59 vs. 1.20), while the interaction test showed no statistically significant modification by PL number. This finding indicates that HCY-related reproductive risk remains clinically relevant across different histories of previous PLs.

Studies have found that approximately 50% of clinical miscarriages occur in the first trimester and that the main cause of miscarriage is embryonic chromosomal abnormalities [32]. Abnormal fetal karyotype has been positively associated with parental age and increased maternal serum HCY levels [33]. This is because high concentrations of HCY may cause DNA damage and increase the risk of human genome instability [34]. Nevertheless, it is debatable whether abnormal karyotypes are related to the number of pregnancies lost. Ogasawara et al. believed that the incidence of abnormal embryo karyotypes decreased significantly with the number of previous miscarriages [35]. However, other researchers have found no correlation between the number of previous miscarriages and chromosomal aberration in aborted embryos [36]. This supports further investigation of the relationships among HCY, the number of previous PLs, and embryonic karyotype.

A retrospective single-center study found that sex, season, and serum folate levels had an effect on HCY levels, but age was not an influencing factor in the preconception population [37]. In the subgroup analysis of our study, HCY significantly increased the risk of EPL in patients aged ≥ 30 years, while the estimate among patients aged < 30 years remained positive after full adjustment. By contrast, Nasiri et al.’s study showed that high HCY levels were positively associated with RPL among patients older than 35 years, whereas no significant association was observed in patients younger than 35 years [16]. These findings indicate that age plays an important role in the association between HCY levels and RPL incidence, potentially through hormonal fluctuations and ovarian reserve function.

It has been reported that HCY levels are significantly increased in postmenopausal women and that circulating serum HCY is positively correlated with FSH and negatively correlated with estradiol in this population [38]. In addition, several studies have shown a higher incidence of diminished ovarian reserve (DOR) in RPL patients [39]. A clinical study found that the proportion of women with Anti-Müllerian Hormone (AMH) levels ≤ 1 ng/mL was significantly higher in the RPL group than in the control group (19.7% vs. 5.7%). The proportion of women with FSH levels ≥ 11 U/L was also significantly higher in the RPL group than in the control group (18.3% vs. 4.3%), suggesting that RPL may be associated with DOR [40, 41]. These studies indicate that the relationship between HCY and age in women of reproductive age is shaped by multiple reproductive and endocrine factors.

In addition, evidence on the relationship between BMI and HCY concentration remains conflicting. In polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) patients with RPL, HCY levels are significantly and positively correlated with BMI, and obesity may exacerbate the risk of miscarriage through metabolic abnormalities such as increased HCY levels [42]. A prospective community cohort study found no significant nonlinear relationship between BMI, waist circumference, and elevated HCY. However, the Cox regression model showed that low body weight, as measured by BMI, was negatively associated with the risk of elevated HCY after controlling for confounding variables [43]. Among middle-aged women, compared with nonobese women, the mean serum HCY concentration was significantly higher in waist circumference (WC)–based central obese women, but not in BMI-based general obese women [44]. The relationship between HCY and BMI may differ across diseases. Among both PCOS and non-PCOS patients younger than 35 years, serum HCY levels were higher in obese patients than in nonobese patients, and this difference was statistically significant in the control group [45]. Some studies found that women with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) had elevated HCY levels, which were correlated with higher HbA1c, longer T2DM duration, and hypertension, but not with BMI [46]. In schizophrenia, HCY levels and BMI are negatively correlated. In patients with bipolar disorder, HCY level was independently associated with high BMI and was positively correlated with BMI [47, 48], suggesting a complex relationship between HCY and BMI.

This study has several strengths. First, the prospective cohort design with a substantial sample size enhances the reliability of the findings. Second, the use of generalized additive models identified a clinically relevant nonlinear threshold effect (HCY ≥ 10.71 μmol/L) in the current cohort, providing a data-derived threshold for preconception risk stratification in this cohort. Third, comprehensive adjustment for confounders and subgroup analyses by age and BMI provide evidence for the independent association between HCY and EPL risk, offering potential clinical implications for preconception risk stratification and targeted interventions in RPL management.

This study also has several limitations. First, the timing of HCY measurement before conception was not standardized, with variations in the interval between blood sampling and subsequent pregnancy. This heterogeneity may influence HCY levels due to physiological fluctuations over time and represents an important source of variability. Second, we did not collect detailed dietary data (e.g., folate, vitamin B12, or methionine intake), which are known key modulators of HCY metabolism and could represent important residual confounding factors. Third, data on key comorbidities (e.g., PCOS, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, chronic kidney disease, or cardiovascular disease) were not systematically collected. These conditions are known to influence both HCY metabolism and pregnancy loss risk, potentially introducing unmeasured confounding. Fourth, the fully adjusted quartile-based analyses included multiple covariates relative to the number of events in selected subgroup cells, resulting in broader confidence intervals in higher HCY quartiles, particularly in the age < 30 years Q4 subgroup. Nevertheless, the continuous-variable analyses, threshold analysis, and quartile trend results consistently support a positive association between preconception HCY and subsequent EPL. Future studies should incorporate standardized preconception HCY testing windows, comprehensive dietary assessments, and comorbidity evaluations to improve accuracy.

Conclusion

Our findings identify preconception HCY ≥ 10.71 μmol/L as a data-derived, modifiable risk marker for EPL in patients with RPL. Using a prospective preconception cohort design, this study provides evidence to support preconception risk stratification in reproductive medicine. The HCY threshold identified in this study offers a valuable reference for optimizing preconception risk stratification strategies in patients with RPL. Future prospective controlled trials should further determine the extent to which preconception interventions aimed at lowering HCY levels reduce the risk of EPL and improve live birth rates in RPL patients.
