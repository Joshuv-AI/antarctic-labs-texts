Long-term observatory of groundwater, soil moisture and rainfall in a French urban catchment
Authors: Laetitia Pineau, Marie-Laure Mosini, Lauriane Ledieu, Jules Sekedoua Kouadio, Fabrice Rodriguez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This paper presents hydrological data for an urban catchment in Nantes (France), describing the hydrological behaviour of a neighbourhood over a long period (~15 years) by continuously monitoring rainfall, soil moisture and piezometric levels. Knowledge of this type of data is extremely important for a better understanding of water flows at the soil-atmosphere interface in urban areas and the role of permeable and vegetated areas, which are increasingly present in cities thanks to changes in urban stormwater management, encouraging infiltration and stormwater source control. Knowing the water status of the soil is vital to ensure that green spaces are preserved, even without irrigation. This data can be used to gain a better understanding of seasonal changes in groundwater levels in a west Europe oceanic climate, and to evaluate distributed hydrological models to improve the design of urban developments, particularly those that encourage the stormwater infiltration.

Background & Summary

Two out of three people are likely to live in cities or other urban centers by 2050, according to new data from the United Nations, and urbanization is continuing1. This increases the anthropogenic impact on receiving environments and in particular the distribution of water in the critical zone. Urban environments may be thus considered as “harbingers” of future global change2,3. The growth of urban areas raises questions about access to water resources, in a context of climate change and urban adaptation, particularly with regard to the development of urban vegetation.

The effect of urbanization on the water balance has been shown4, with a reduction in evapotranspiration and infiltration, and an increase in surface runoff with the rise in sealed surfaces. Other factors also affect the water balance, particularly in the urban subsoil: the presence of buried structures (networks, foundations), soil reworking and groundwater abstraction; all have an impact on soil moisture and saturation levels5. As a result, from a groundwater perspective, several effects of urbanization coexist and are documented and discussed by researchers6,7: reduction of recharge by limiting infiltration, soil drainage by buried structures, soil recharge by leakage from drinking water networks.

Furthermore, to mitigate the effects of urbanization on the water balance, strategies for infiltrating rainwater and even grey water are developing, particularly in Western cities; these strategies are often linked to the development of vegetation in cities, which is associated with the installation of Sustainable Urban Drainage Systems (SUDS). This makes access to water in the soil all the more important for cities, since vegetation maintenance is only possible if water resources are accessible.

For these reasons, knowledge of the hydrological and hydrogeological functioning of urban soils is a subject that needs to be consolidated8,9. Measurement is a relevant means of improving this knowledge, and the development of environmental and critical zone observatories is an interesting trajectory for providing quality data over the medium and long term10. In terms of urbanized areas, few critical zone sites exist11 and ONEVU (Observatoire Nantais des EnVironnements Urbains in french) is one of a number of observatory initiatives with a special measurement site in a well-instrumented urban district, to establish the hydrological balance of an urban area12,13. This site, located in a conurbation of 600,000 inhabitants in the west of France, with an oceanic climate, is a well-known and well-described study site on which long-term rainfall and soil water status chronicles are recorded.

The aim of this datapaper is to diffuse the data acquired as part of this observatory for characterizing the soil moisture and the spatial and temporal groundwater variability in an urban watershed, in relation to local rainfall. The data in this dataset are continuous, available over the long term and at a high temporal resolution.

The main reasons for setting up this observatory are as follows. On the one hand, these data can be used to establish a hydrological balance for urban areas, whereas most of the time, hydrological models are used14,15, and to link the evolution of the water table and soil moisture to rainfall conditions in the area studied, in the context of a west Europe oceanic climate. They can also be used to characterize changes in soil saturation over the long term, and to identify the possible acceleration of drought periods as a result of climate change. This information is useful not only for the maintenance of green spaces, but also for supporting the low-water flow of urban streams often impacted by pollution linked to urban rainwater discharges via storm overflows16. There are indeed clear relations between the groundwater level and the base flow in sewers, and consequently in rivers12,17. Groundwater data such as that presented here are highly relevant for highlighting and better estimating this phenomenon of parasitic water infiltration into buried networks. In the end, the evaluation of the future impacts of stormwater infiltration mentioned above is based on modelling, and the use of more integrated models coupling surface and subsurface processes is needed18. The development and assessment of these integrated hydrological modelling approaches requires detailed knowledge of the hydrological functioning of the subsoil, and data sets relating to the water status of the soil and the water table are still rare in urban environments. This dataset is designed to fill this gap.

Methods

This dataset includes the rainfall, groundwater levels and soil moisture monitored in an urban catchment area, named the Pin Sec catchment, located at the north-east of Nantes in France (Fig. 1). The study area is a gently sloping residential catchment covering a surface of 31 ha. Of this surface, ~45% is impervious and the remaining is green spaces, mainly individual gardens, hedgerows, shrubs and grass strips. The Pin Sec catchment is under an oceanic climate with frequent rainfalls. Stormwater is transferred through a 50 years old separate sewer network to the perennial Gohards river12. The geology of the study area is mainly composed of a mica-schists bedrock covered with altered mica-schists, silty eolian deposits and alluvial deposits12. The local groundwater is described as a hard-rock aquifer.Fig. 1Location of measurement points on the Pin sec catchment (latitude 47.24368, longitude -1.521212). Rainfall is recorded at 1-min interval by 3 tipping bucket rain gauges (3039, Précis Mécanique®) covering the area (Fig. 1) to obtain an accurate spatial understanding of the precipitation. Groundwater levels are measured in 10 simultaneous piezometers selected from 11 possible locations (Fig. 1), with pressure sensors at 20-min intervals since May 2006 using STS-DNL70 and Van Essen Diver sensors (Table 1). Three piezometers already existed (PZCI, PZGO and PZSD). The others were drilled between 4 m (PZUV, PZGH and PZCP) and 8 m (PZAF, PZCS, PZDU, PZPS and PSJV) deep for the observatory depending on the hardness of the geological layers12,19. All the piezometers have a 40 cm filter screen at the end. Being in urban green spaces maintained by the municipality, the drilled piezometers were hidden under manholes (Fig. 2a). The already existing piezometers protrude from the ground surface and are protected (Fig. 2b). Two types of pressure sensors were used. Initially, relative pressure sensors, providing the depth of the water level from the ground, were used. These were then gradually replaced by absolute pressure sensors estimating the water pressure above the sensor, which requires to have a reference atmospheric pressure measurement on site (Fig. 2c); two pressure sensors were used for this reference atmospheric data: PTB-110 Vaisala from January 2014 to August 2017 and Van Essen Diver since August 2017. Raw data from the pressure sensors is imported into a database, in which the altitudes of the sensors and the ground (indicated in the Table 1) are recorded for each piezometer. Soil moisture are measured using three dielectric permittivity sensors (CS-625) at 10-min intervals since 2010 (Table 1). Sensors were installed horizontally at a depth of 35 cm in the soil (Fig. 3). To do that, a pit about 50 cm deep was dug and sensors were inserted using a guide to keep the rods parallel. A second pit 2 m away contains the data acquisition unit. Sensors have never been removed from the ground since 2010. Sensors indirectly measure soil moisture (or water content) using time domain reflectometry (TDR) method. The TDR sensor measures the propagation time of a wave along two parallel rods, which is related to the soil dielectric constant, depending on the soil moisture, the type of soil and its density. The following calibration methodology was adopted. For each site, during installation, three soil samples were taken to determine the soil density with the cylinder method20. Along the soil preparation guidelines of the sensor21, field measurements were calibrated following a laboratory calibration. This was carried out with measurements at varying water contents in the soil sampled and reconstituted at the same density. Data Records

The raw data are available for all the measurement points via the metadata catalog of Geosuna IS, on the metadata sheet for the sensor concerned and listed in Table 1, and accessible via each doi link: Precipitation Rate data23–27, Water table depths data28–38, Soil moisture data39–41. Further information can be found on the ‘parent’ metadata sheets for the sites and measuring stations for each sensor.

The data provided in this data paper are those with a “good”, “corrected good” or “suspicious” quality code. Percentages of data provided for groundwater level and soil moisture are estimated according to the sensors and the years (Tables 3, 4). 2006 and 2010 are incomplete years because sensors were installed in May or June. In other years, most of the not supplied data for groundwater levels correspond to overflow in the piezometer during strong storm events, to manual pumping for control purposes or to periods when the piezometer is dry (mainly for PZGH, Table 3). Technical Validation

Validation of the rainfall

The rainfall measurement is annually checked by verifying the bucket volume using a constant flow calibration kit (R90000 Précis Mécanique®). In addition, during each data recovery (approximately every 3 weeks), the volume of water received in the container of the rain gauges is used to check the number of tipping and to compare it to the theoretical value. In case of a difference until 20%, rainfall is determined according to the number of tipping. In case of a higher difference, the quality code “suspicious” or “bad” is assigned to the data (Table 2). If the volume of water could not be measured (full or broken container, …), the theoretical value of 0.2 mm is applied to each tipping if the comparison with the data from nearby rain gauges is satisfactory based on both visual and statistical checking. By way of illustration, each rain gauge was compared with the Perray rain gauge (see locations in Fig. 1) which has the longest data history. The comparison of daily cumulative rainfall “two by two” is very satisfactory, with a determination coefficient R2 systematically larger with 0,95 (Table 5). Validation of the groundwater levels

The technical validation of the groundwater levels is carried out in two ways. Firstly, a comparison is made every two months between the on-site monitoring and a manual measurement (‘controlled’). The metrics chosen for this comparison shows a good agreement, with a Mean Absolute Error (MAE) smaller than 3 cm and a Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) smaller than 7 cm (Table 6), considering that the accuracy of the manual measurement may depend on the operator in charge of this control. No sensor drift was observed during the whole measurement period. Usage Notes

Most of the information needed to interpret and analyze the data is available in the metadata of the sensor records for each piece of data in the dataset (Table 1). Additionnal information is provided for a better use of this dataset in Supplementary Information: (i) the geological characteristics of the piezometric borehole profiles (SI Figure SI-1) and (ii) the properties of the soils in which the soil moisture probes are installed (SI Table SI-1). On sites where joint soil moisture and groundwater level data are available (as in the case of WCS and WPS), the time series can be used to analyse the hydrological processes on an urban green space and the water storage contained in the soil (Fig. 6), in relation to the catchment rainfall. Generally speaking, these data can be used to assess hydrological models on urban catchments, models able to simulate vadose zone behaviour, or for the more specific assessment of infiltration models. As an example, two years with contrasting meteorological characteristics are presented (Fig. 7) for the year 2020 (wet year, average rainfall 905 mm) and the year 2022 (dry year, average rainfall 699 mm) with regard to the minimum and maximum values recorded since 2006. For information, the average rainfall at the reference weather station in Nantes (Meteo France 44018001 Nantes-Bouguenais, latitude 47.15, longitude −1.608833) over the period 1990–2010 is 820 mm.
