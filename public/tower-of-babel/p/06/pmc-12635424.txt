Patient‐Controlled Sedation During Gynaecological Procedures—Aspects of Patient Satisfaction, Feasibility and Cost Per Patient: Empirical Research Quantitative

Introduction

Most women undergo gynaecological examinations during their lifetime for both checks and various interventions. Gynaecological visits are crucial to women's health, but many feel anxious about entering the exam room. A previous study found that up to 64% of women experience fear or anxiety before or during a pelvic exam, due to a cold speculum, embarrassment, or perceived pain or discomfort (O'Laughlin et al. 2021). Many of them experience the procedure as an intimate, vulnerable situation, where they are exposed both bodily and mentally (Grundström et al. 2011). A sense of control, comfort and respect for integrity have been shown to diminish the distress during examinations (Bates et al. 2011; Tancman et al. 2022). Gynaecological examination may lead to women failing to attend a planned visit due to a previous negative experience or may mean the examination needs to be carried out under anaesthesia in the operating theatre, which is resource‐intensive and allows low patient participation (Tancman et al. 2022). Local anaesthetics are widely used in minor gynaecological surgery as the procedures are often short (Latthe et al. 2007).

Recent international studies confirm that women in different cultural settings report similar experiences of fear, embarrassment and discomfort during pelvic examinations, underscoring the global relevance of patient‐centred strategies to improve the experience (Kapsner et al. 2022; Alpern et al. 2021). In particular, strategies that strengthen patient autonomy and reduce procedural anxiety, such as patient‐controlled sedation, have gained increased attention internationally (Leslie and Peyton 2020).

Background

The development of new methods, treatments and medicines changes the conditions for healthcare providers, whereby some minor operations no longer need to be performed or are carried out as day surgical procedures. In most of these procedures, local anaesthesia with light sedation has proven to be a superior strategy compared to general anaesthesia (Tancman et al. 2022). Patient‐controlled sedation (PCS) is a method of sedation in which the patient self‐administers a preselected drug to receive sedation to relieve discomfort and pain during procedures. The sedation method provides patients with the ability to control the sedation and to vary the degree of sedation depending on the environment and the stress of the procedure (Figure 1). PCS is well established within several contexts as is the use of the sedative drug propofol (Kreienbühl et al. 2018). The method and the properties of propofol have been shown to decrease the risk of complications in vital functions (Nilsson et al. 2012), to reduce the need for interventions compared to administration by staff (Kreienbühl et al. 2018) and to improve recovery without affecting the ease of the procedure (Lu et al. 2015) compared to other methods and drugs. PCS with propofol is appreciated by patients (Eknemar et al. 2022) and increases the patient's sense of well‐being (Höhener et al. 2008). In addition to patients preferring to administer the sedation themselves, there are also financial advantages to using PCS. A cost analysis demonstrates that propofol administered using PCS reduces the direct costs of procedures (Grossmann et al. 2020).

Closed‐loop sedation with patient‐controlled sedation where stimuli and patient preferences regarding satisfaction affect dose requirements leading to increased tolerance and comfort, and result in a satisfied/sedated patient or inability to require further doses. Reduced dose effect increases sensitivity to stimuli or alternatively increases patient discomfort, which affects the dose requirement.

International evidence supports these findings. Studies from both Europe and Asia show that PCS with propofol is safe, resource‐efficient and associated with high patient satisfaction in endoscopic and gynaecologic contexts (Goudra and Singh 2019; Chen et al. 2021). A recent systematic review further underlined its applicability outside the operating theatre, emphasising its role in outpatient and ambulatory procedures (Dumonceau et al. 2020).

Aim

The present study evaluated the first years of using PCS with propofol during various gynaecological procedures in an environment of non‐anesthesiologists, considering patient satisfaction, procedure feasibility and cost per patient for the procedure at both the gynaecological clinic (GC) and the daycare surgical unit (DSCU).

Methods

Design

This study used a descriptive and retrospective design to evaluate patient satisfaction, procedure feasibility and direct costs for PCS with propofol for outpatients with anxiety and fear of gynaecological outpatient procedures. Sedation was carried out without the presence of anesthesiologists. The study was approved by the Regional Ethics Review Board (2023–07495‐01). Reporting followed the STROBE checklist for cross‐sectional studies (Von Elm et al. 2007).

Data was collected from the medical records of all outpatients who completed a gynaecological procedure using PCS with propofol during the period February 2021 until July 2023 at the GC, Vrinnevi Hospital (Sweden). Data including patient age and evaluation of the procedure, and procedure‐related information describing the type of procedure, duration of the procedure, drugs used, vital signs, interventions and evaluations by staff was extracted from medical records. All data were decoded before being handed over to the researchers.

The procedures included hysteroscopy, intrauterine device or coil (IUD) insertion or extraction, cervix dilatation or colposcopy or the Loop Electrosurgical Excision Procedure (LEEP), vacuum aspiration, Hystero‐Salpingo‐Sonography (HSS) as well as minor vaginal, vulvar or dermatological procedures.

During a pre‐procedural visit to the clinic, the gynaecologist assessed patients' anxiety, fear and tolerance for the planned procedure to decide the appropriate level of sedation or anaesthesia. Based on this assessment, patients were divided into three clinical groups: those able to tolerate the procedure without sedation, those able to tolerate the procedure with PCS, and those requiring anaesthesia. This classification was primarily used as part of the clinical triage process and patient safety protocol, not for statistical comparison in the present analysis. Future studies may investigate potential differences between groups in relation to satisfaction, ease of procedure and pain.

The grouping procedure was guided by the Swedish Association for Anesthesiology and Intensive Care (2018) recommendations on patient selection for propofol sedation outside the operating room, as well as comparable international guidelines (Dumonceau et al. 2020).

All procedures were carried out at the GC except for patients in need of anaesthesia who were referred to the DSCU. In addition to the assessment, patient selection needed to comply with local and national recommendations regulating the use of propofol and PCS. A checklist with absolute contraindications and criteria for exclusion was used for the selection of patients: daily heartburn despite proton‐pump inhibitors, pregnancy, BMI ≥ 40/known obstructive sleep apnea, contraindication for the study drugs, functional disability, cognitive impairment, or language difficulties affecting PCS device operation. The checklist also included relative contraindications, based on criteria set by the American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) Physical Status, which states that an anaesthetist should be consulted before the initiation of sedation. The local recommendations also stated that an additional trained nurse should be appointed to support the patient and ensure compliance and safety during sedation with PCS. General nurses are locally trained before taking part in sedation with PCS. The training, given by anaesthetic staff, consists of theoretical education and practical training to handle sedation with PCS/propofol to fulfil the recommendations and requirements regarding procedure‐related propofol sedation outside the operating room issued by the Swedish Association for Anesthesiology and Intensive Care (SFAI) (2018).

Patients received verbal and written information regarding the procedure and sedation when scheduled for the procedure. The information was repeated during the day of the procedure. All patients needed to comply with the European anaesthetic guidelines for preoperative fasting. Patients received a peripheral venous catheter and an infusion of Ringer‐acetat. All patients received supplementary oxygen by nasal catheter upon procedure initiation. Administration of propofol was started by the patient 2–3 min before the start of the procedure (Propofol‐Lipuro 10 mg/mL, B. Braun Melsungen AG, Melsungen, Germany) using the PCS device (SyramedμSP6000, Arcomed AG, Kloten, Switzerland). Patients were encouraged to use the PCS device to achieve their desired level of relaxation. For each request from the PCS device 5 mg of propofol (0.5 mL) was administered without any lockout periods, giving a possible dose of 40 mg propofol per min.

Patients' vital signs, including oxygen saturation (SpO2), heart rate (HR), and arterial non‐invasive blood pressure (NIBP), were monitored during the whole procedure at five‐minute intervals. Hypoxemia was defined as SpO2 < 90%, hypotension as NIBP < 90 mmHg, bradycardia as HR < 40 beats/min and respiratory depression as RR < 8 breaths/min. Cardiorespiratory events were handled according to the local instructions from the anaesthetic clinic (Data S1 and S2).

After the procedure, both patients and gynaecologists evaluated key aspects using a study‐specific Likert scale instrument. This instrument has been applied in several earlier studies by our group (Nilsson et al. 2012; Eknemar et al. 2022), but has not been formally validated. Pain was measured as the perceived physical nociceptive sensation, whereas “discomfort” referred to psychological unease, anxiety, or distress experienced during the procedure.

The gynaecologist evaluated the procedure regarding the application of a paracervical block, the ease of the procedure, defensive reactions/patient movements and ease of sedation. The patient evaluated the procedure when recovered/fully awake regarding the degree of discomfort and pain during the procedure and their overall satisfaction with the procedure. Both evaluations used a Likert scale according to the following descriptions: Application of PCB/Ease of procedure/Ease of sedation (1 = ‘Easy’, 2 = ‘Some difficulties’, 3 = ‘Difficult’, 4 = ‘Very difficult’), Defensive reactions/Discomfort during procedure/Pain during the procedure (1 = ‘None’, 2 = ‘Minimal’, 3 = ‘Moderate’, 4 = ‘Significant’), and Overall satisfaction with the procedure (1 = ‘Very satisfied’, 2 = ‘Satisfied’, 3=‘Neither satisfied nor dissatisfied’, 4 = ‘Dissatisfied’).

The cost analysis was based on a hospital perspective for all direct costs involving IUD insertion (TLC00) for each patient, so‐called Cost Per Patient (CPP). This common procedure was selected based on the possibility of receiving reliable data from the Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP). CPP for the procedure when completed at the GC or DSCU included medications, material, medical services, staff and other costs. Medical services included costs for anaesthesia, surgery and postoperative care (for the DSCU) and laboratory medicine and other costs involved physical care rooms and equipment.

CPP described above for the procedure was identified and extracted for each year and a mean cost for 2021–23 was calculated. Currency was converted from Swedish krona (SEK) to Euro (€) using the annual average exchange rate for 2023 (11.51 SEK = € 1). Results were rounded to the nearest integer and converted to €.

Statistical Analysis

We used Microsoft Excel (version 16.78.3) to perform descriptive statistical analyses (mean, median, ranges and proportions). No inferential statistical tests were conducted, as the study design was descriptive and exploratory.

Discussion

Our study demonstrates that PCS with propofol is feasible, safe and associated with high patient satisfaction in a gynaecological outpatient setting. This is supported by our results showing that the majority of patients (73%–74%) reported none or mild discomfort/pain, and 86% were very satisfied with the procedure. These findings align with prior research in gastrointestinal and urological outpatient procedures, where PCS improved patient empowerment, reduced anxiety and provided cost efficiency compared to anesthesiologist‐administered sedation (Lu et al. 2015; Dumonceau et al. 2020; Amornyotin 2022).

Since 2021, PCS has been the primary sedation method at the studied GC, replacing oral diazepam for patients with high anxiety or PTSD. This approach is reflected in our results, where all procedures were successfully completed without sedation‐related cancellations, and transient cardiovascular events occurred only in a few cases, resolved spontaneously or with minor intervention. International guidelines support such pre‐procedural risk stratification and referral to dedicated sedation units when risk factors—such as BMI ≥ 35, obstructive sleep apnea, pregnancy, or ASA ≥ III status—are present (American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) 2018; Dossa et al. 2021; Rajan et al. 2021).

In addition to local guidelines at Vrinnevi Hospital the procedures are carried out according to SFAIs recommendation regarding procedure‐related propofol sedation outside the operating room (Swedish Association for Anesthesia and Intensive Care 2018). The recommendations outline that no additional analgesic or sedative drugs should be co‐administered with propofol and that the procedure duration should not exceed 45 min.

Wijma and Siwe (2002) state that gynaecological examinations are common and are estimated to be performed on approximately 1.5 million women in Sweden annually (Wijma and Siwe 2002). Although women in Sweden are generally positive about gynaecological examinations being performed, the same women often have negative feelings associated with them (Wijma and Siwe 2002, 2004). A survey with 6508 participants found that the examination was an embarrassing (47%), painful (35%) and traumatic (19%) event. Even though the patients felt uncomfortable, only 43% of the respondents stated they would notify the gynaecologist in case of discomfort or pain during the examination (Tancman et al. 2022). Women with negative feelings such as pain, aching, embarrassment and fear should be informed about methods for relaxation to reduce discomfort and pain (Teskereci et al. 2020). Previous negative experiences of suturing led to greater anxiety than for those who had a previous positive experience (Ohlsson and Wedenström 2011) which underlines the importance of increasing patient satisfaction. The intimate and vulnerable nature of gynaecological examination underlines the importance of choosing anaesthetic methods that alleviate unease and that are experienced as effective.

An earlier study showed that 64.7% of women undergoing vaginal examinations suffered from post‐traumatic stress disorder, and that physical, emotional and sexual violence were found to increase the possibility of this disorder. A history of PTSD increased the possibility of experiencing discomfort during vaginal examinations (Güneş and Karaçam 2017).

Using PCS in gynaecological interventions relieves the patient's discomfort and strengthens the patient's sense of control and autonomy in a stressful situation (Sheahan and Mathews 2014). To empower women and improve satisfaction have been cornerstones for the implementation of PCS at the GC, with a specific focus on the group of vulnerable women with negative experiences of earlier visits or previous PTSD.

Our results confirm that patient‐experienced discomfort and pain were largely minimal: 73% reported no defensive reactions, and 20% minimal, while only 7% had moderate movements. Similarly, the gynaecologists rated most procedures as easy or very easy, with only seven procedures classified as very difficult due to anatomical or technical issues. Despite occasional higher discomfort, all procedures were completed successfully, demonstrating that PCS provides effective procedural sedation while maintaining patient satisfaction.

In gynaecological procedures, single‐agent sedation with propofol is advantageously used because it provides a good effect and quick recovery. It also induces some amnesia and increases the patient's sense of well‐being (Höhener et al. 2008).

By allowing patients to self‐administer propofol, PCS enhances autonomy and empowerment, particularly in patients with prior negative experiences or PTSD. Our data indicate that even patients with higher baseline anxiety tolerated the procedure well and expressed high satisfaction, aligning with international evidence that PCS improves patient‐centred care outcomes (Kreienbühl et al. 2018; Dumonceau et al. 2020).

Results from this study support that no additional opioids were required for routine procedures. The gynaecologists were able to complete all planned procedures, except for nine cases where technical difficulties led to discontinuation. This confirms that PCS alone was sufficient for maintaining procedural feasibility and patient comfort in the majority of cases. The gynaecologists were able to perform and complete all planned procedures (except for those with technical difficulties) and no procedures needed to be interrupted or cancelled due to insufficient sedation. From a gynaecologist's perspective, the method in itself has been proved to be feasible for a variety of procedures, from vulvar excisions to hysteroscopic procedures without affecting the outcome.

For patients with a higher degree of discomfort or pain, the addition of intermittently administered bolus doses of an opioid, such as alfentanil, could be of interest (Grossmann and Nilsson 2022)., Earlier research has shown that combining an opioid with propofol improves sedation quality and patient tolerance, but it also increases the risk of desaturation (Yoon et al. 2011) and airway complications (Kreienbühl et al. 2018).

International research echoes these findings: PCS with propofol has been associated with improved patient empowerment, satisfaction and reduced anxiety across diverse outpatient settings, including gastroenterology, dentistry and gynaecology (Goudra and Singh 2019; Amornyotin 2022). Recent European consensus statements also emphasise the importance of integrating PCS into patient‐centred care models, highlighting its role in minimising inequities in access to safe sedation (Fabbri et al. 2023).

In Sweden, the anaesthetic propofol is handled by anaesthesia personnel (LIF 2020a). Propofol is often used in conjunction with sedation. During the last 15 years, the Anaesthesia Clinic at Vrinnevi Hospital has developed PCS with the aim of increasing the availability of good and patient‐safe sedation during extracorporeal shockwave lithotripsy (ESWL) and endoscopies. The advantage is that non‐anaesthetic staff can carry out the sedation independently at the outpatient clinic without anaesthetic staff being present. The decision to use non‐anaesthetic staff is based on the evidence presented by Kreienbühl et al. (2018) who conducted a meta‐analysis to clarify the difference between PCS and sedation administered by an anaesthetist, nurse anaesthetist, or nurse (Kreienbühl et al. 2018). The results showed that far fewer respiratory events occurred in PCS compared with sedation by staff.

By using this concept, many patients get access to good and resource‐efficient sedation. The transition to complete procedures at the GC reduces referrals to the DSCU whereby the clinic saves costs and time for the patient, while at the same time anaesthetic and surgery resources are optimised.

The economic evaluation demonstrates that PCS not only ensures patient comfort but also offers substantial cost savings. Our results showed that mean cost per procedure was reduced by 85% when conducted at the GC rather than the DSCU, resulting in a total reduction of €33,050 during the study period. This underscores the dual benefit of PCS: improved patient experience and resource‐efficient care delivery. A reduction of procedures at the DSCU increases the workload for the GC which could be an issue that needs to be resolved before the implementation of PCS.

Strengths of our study include the real‐world setting, a relatively large sample size, and the novel application of PCS in a vulnerable gynaecological population. Limitations, such as the retrospective design and incomplete patient evaluations (57% response rate), are acknowledged. Future research should validate patient‐reported outcome measures and explore subgroup differences, such as patients with higher procedural anxiety or previous negative experiences.

Introducing PCS as a method for sedation with a more liberal selection of patients, including women with no specific need, could increase patient satisfaction and quality for a broader group of patients such as postpartum obstetric lacerations, where this kind of anaesthesia may have the potential to considerably increase the patient‐experienced quality of care (Ohlsson and Wedenström 2011).

Our results indicate that PCS can contribute to the sustainable and efficient use of scarce resources within the healthcare.

Conclusion

The results of this study indicate that PCS used in a gynaecological outpatient setting for patients with anxiety and fear is a well‐tolerated method of sedation that gives high patient satisfaction and allows procedures to be completed in a high‐quality way with potential to reduce direct costs.
