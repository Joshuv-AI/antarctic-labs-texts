Speech Biomarkers for Quantifying Effects of Subthalamic Deep Brain Stimulation in Parkinson's Disease
Authors: Petr Krýže, Mário Sousa, Vojtěch Illner, Adriana Jorge, Matilde Castelli, Ines Debove, Andreia D Magalhães, Julia Waskönig, Lenard Lachenmayer, Joan Philipp Michelis, Gerd Tinkhauser, Deborah Amstutz, Marie Elise Maradan‐Gachet, Katrin Petermann, Claudio Pollo, Tobias Nef, Paul Krack, Jan Rusz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveDysarthria is one of the most common and disabling side effects of subthalamic nucleus deep brain stimulation (STN‐DBS) in Parkinson's disease (PD). Stimulation often exacerbates speech dysfunction beyond the effects of PD progression, likely because of current spread to structures surrounding the STN. This study aimed to develop speech biomarkers sensitive to DBS‐induced dysarthria by isolating stimulation side effects and mapping their emergence across incrementally increased amplitudes.MethodsTwenty‐four PD patients with bilateral STN‐DBS completed a standardized speech assessment in each hemisphere separately, including sustained phonations, rapid syllable repetitions, and reading passages across 7 increasing stimulation amplitudes defined relative to a clinically determined stimulation‐induced dysarthria threshold. A composite dysarthria index based on 7 key acoustic features, patient perceptual self‐ratings, and intelligibility scores were extracted.ResultsMore than 2,500 speech task recordings were analyzed. Both the composite dysarthria index and subjective self‐ratings worsened rapidly with increasing stimulation amplitude above a threshold (p < 0.001), whereas intelligibility scores varied markedly and did not reach significance. Among individual acoustic features, phonation duration, voice quality, and monopitch exhibited significant sensitivity to increasing stimulation amplitudes. Left‐sided stimulation induced greater speech deterioration than right‐sided stimulation.InterpretationWe systematically identified speech biomarkers that capture DBS‐induced dysarthria, characterized the progressive deterioration of speech with increasing amplitude, and highlighted the pivotal role of left basal ganglia circuitry in speech production. Our objective metric holds promise as safety outcome measure for surgical therapies, guidepost for initial and troubleshooting DBS programming, and input for adaptive, closed‐loop stimulation control. ANN NEUROL 2026;100:628–640

Bilateral deep brain stimulation (DBS) of the subthalamic nucleus (STN) is an established therapy for Parkinson's disease (PD), effectively alleviating the disorder's cardinal motor symptoms. However, improvements in overall quality of life can be tempered by mixed effects on patients' speech and other axial functions. 1

Although certain aspects of speech impairment may improve under DBS, other aspects may remain unchanged or even deteriorate. 2 , 3 , 4 The spread of current into areas surrounding the STN is suspected to be the primary cause for stimulation‐induced speech disorder, as this exceptionally dense region, containing functionally distinct zones and major cortico‐subcortical pathways, is highly vulnerable to unintended stimulation. 5 , 6 Between patients, the specific speech aspects affected can vary markedly because of individual differences in electrode placement, 7 , 8 , 9 adjacent structural connectivity, 10 stimulation direction, 11 and lead trajectory. 12 , 13 Collectively, these factors significantly influence the stimulation amplitude threshold at which side effects first emerge. Even minor adjustments can trigger side effects, as the stimulation current begins to interfere with the neighboring tracts. 14

Although acoustic speech biomarkers are well established in PD, 15 a comprehensive understanding of their relationship with increasing stimulation amplitude is essential to validate clinical applications in DBS, from perioperative optimization of electrode placement, 16 assistance in DBS programming, to monitoring stimulation side effects in future automated DBS control. 17 Several studies 7 , 9 , 18 , 19 have investigated the impact of stimulation amplitude changes on speech. However, these efforts have been constrained by small cohorts, coarse amplitude categories (eg, low–medium–high or simply low vs high), and a narrow focus on either overall intelligibility or a restricted set of acoustic metrics that fail to encompass all core aspects of hypokinetic dysarthria. To date, no study has identified which specific speech dimensions are affected or tried to systematically characterize the emergence of these side effects while accounting for variability between patients.

Therefore, in this study, we aimed to discover speech biomarkers sensitive to stimulation‐induced dysarthria by isolating and quantifying the specific influence of increasing stimulation amplitude on speech performance. To provide a thorough perspective, we used both objective and subjective markers of general speech deterioration alongside an inclusive set of discrete acoustic features that span all major dysarthria dimensions. We systematically assessed speech in 7 small amplitude increments, to capture subtle individual speech differences and unveil the emergence of stimulation‐induced side effects.

Methods

Study Design

This study was conducted in compliance with the ethical standards laid down in the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments and was approved by the local ethics committee (KEK 2021–01787), Bern, Switzerland. Each participant provided written informed consent to the clinical examinations and recording procedure. This study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (identifier: NCT05182892). A target of 30 patients was set for recruitment during standard clinical evaluations. Inclusion criteria were: (1) a clinical diagnosis of PD in accordance with Movement Disorder Society diagnostic criteria, (2) treatment with bilateral STN‐DBS, (3) being a minimum of 3 months post‐surgery to exclude acute lesional effects, (4) absence of dementia, (5) no recorded history of speech, language or hearing disorders unrelated to PD, and (6) being a native speaker of either Swiss German or Swiss French. In the chronic stimulation state and under standard medication, patients' cognitive functions were evaluated using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), and motor function was assessed using the Movement Disorder Society Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale (MDS‐UPDRS), part III.

All speech evaluations were performed with patients in the medication OFF condition, defined as at least 8 hours since the last levodopa intake and at least 48 hours without any dopamine agonist medication, to isolate the specific effects of DBS. All following assessments were conducted using each patient's chronic DBS settings for frequency, pulse width, and active contacts. Only ring level stimulation was used for the study experiment.

Individual Stimulation‐Induced Dysarthria Threshold Search

Each patient's amplitude threshold for stimulation‐induced dysarthria, defined as the lowest amplitude at which both patient and examiner perceived speech deterioration, was determined by a clinical neurologist experienced in movement disorders and speech (M.S.). The complete search protocol was conducted separately for the left and right stimulation sides, with contralateral stimulation amplitude set to 50% of the chronic level. The chronic stimulation level on each side was assumed to be optimized for motor performance and served as the reference for the experiment. To assess speech at every step of the threshold search, patients were asked to enumerate the months of the year and count from 1 to 10.

Starting with the tested side's stimulation turned off, the amplitude was increased in 0.5 mA increments until both the patient and examiner agreed that speech began to deteriorate. The amplitude was then decreased in 0.5 mA increments until speech improvement was observed. Finally, the amplitude increase sequence was repeated with 0.1 mA increments to precisely determine each patient's threshold. In this part of the protocol, patients were blinded to the stimulation amplitude level being administered; however, the direction (increase or decrease) was known at all times because of the structure of the threshold search. Blinding of the administering clinician was not feasible because of the nature of stimulation parameter adjustment.

Speech Assessment

At the beginning of the protocol, patients were evaluated in 2 chronic states: first with stimulation amplitude set to the chronic level on both sides, and then with 50% of the chronic level on the contralateral side. Afterward, a randomized series of 9 stimulation amplitude conditions was administered, during which the contralateral amplitude was, again, maintained at 50% of the chronic level to improve overall tolerability of the assessment protocol while sufficiently minimizing any impact on speech for the side not under test. These states included a stimulation OFF condition (with stimulation completely disabled on the tested side), an “on threshold” (OT) condition, and conditions where the amplitude was set to 0.3, 0.6, and 0.9 mA above (AT) and below (BT) the patient's stimulation‐induced dysarthria threshold. Assessment in the 0.3 BT condition was performed twice to assess consistency. A minimum washout period of 5 minutes was assured between the stimulation conditions, and patients were blinded to the order of conditions. Blinding of the clinician was not required, as no outcome measures were assessed by the clinician. The left and right leads were assessed on different days.

In each condition, patients completed: (1) 2 phonations of vowel /a/ in 1 breath for as long and steadily as possible, (2) 2 sequences of at least 12 /pa/−/ta/−/ka/ syllable repetitions, as quickly and accurately as possible, and (3) 1 reading of the standardized text “The North Wind and the Sun” in its German and French translations (Fig S1). We selected speaking tasks that provide the most relevant information for the objective description and interpretation of motor speech disorders. The mean duration of speech assessment based on these 3 tasks was 1 minute 51 seconds (standard deviation [SD], 31 seconds) per condition. All audio recordings were conducted in a quiet room with a minimal amount of ambient noise using a head‐mounted condenser microphone (Shure Beta 53; Shure, Niles, IL) placed approximately 5 cm from the subject's mouth. The monophonic speech recordings were sampled at a frequency of 48 kHz with 16‐bits of resolution. Furthermore, a subjective rating of speech impairment was obtained at each condition by having patients rate their own speech ability on a discrete scale from 0 (no impairment) to 10 (maximum conceivable impairment).

Acoustic Features

To provide a comprehensive description of hypokinetic dysarthria, we selected 7 acoustic features that represent its distinct aspects (Fig 1, Table S1). These features were chosen to align with the landmark perceptual description of main hypokinetic dysarthria patterns 20 and to corroborate findings from prior studies of dysarthria in PD patients treated with STN‐DBS. 3 , 7 , 18 , 19 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 All of the selected acoustic features are commonly used in speech biomarker studies in PD. 15

Acoustic features were computed using fully automatic algorithms in MATLAB R2024b (The MathWorks, Natick, MA) and Python (Whisper model large v3, language fine‐tuned). Comprehensive algorithmic details for the selected acoustic features have been reported previously. 30 The accuracy of the implemented algorithms has been validated in prior studies, including articulation rate, 31 harmonic‐to‐noise ratio (HNR), 32 long‐term average spectrum (LTAS), 27 speech fundamental frequency (F0 SD), 32 and voice onset time (VOT). 33 Intensity variability (speech intensity SD) and maximum phonation time (MPT) were derived using standard signal‐processing procedures based on amplitude envelope extraction and phonation duration, respectively. 34

Primary Endpoints

Composite Dysarthria Index

To account for the variable severity of dysarthria dimensions among patients, composite dysarthria index (CDI) was developed by integrating 7 acoustic features into a single global descriptor of dysarthria's impact on speech. A similar approach, using a CDI that captures the core aspects of hypokinetic dysarthria in PD, has previously been shown to be sufficiently sensitive to detect even subtle speech abnormalities in prodromal and early PD patients relative to healthy controls. 35 To minimize inter‐subject variability and isolate the specific effects of DBS on speech, all acoustic feature values at experimental amplitudes were normalized relative to each patient's side‐specific stimulation OFF condition. Each feature value in a given stimulation state was first offset by subtracting its corresponding OFF‐condition value, then divided by the absolute OFF‐condition value, and finally multiplied by 100 to yield the percentage deviation from the OFF‐condition. Subsequently, a sign inversion was applied to the relevant features to ensure that higher values consistently indicated more severe deterioration. Finally, because of the previous normalization, all acoustic feature values were simply averaged to obtain the CDI, which shows the percentage deviation from each patient's stimulation OFF condition.

Subjective Self‐Rating

A difference between the self‐assessed subjective speech impairment scores at the specific experimental amplitudes and the scores assessed at the stimulation OFF state was taken as a measure of subjective DBS‐induced change in speech impairment.

Intelligibility Score

To compare the sensitivity of the CDI against standard dysarthria evaluation in STN‐DBS, we used an instrumental intelligibility assessment based on Levenshtein‐distance comparisons between the original reading text and automatically transcribed recordings. (Table S1).

Statistical Analysis

The normality of data was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test (Tables S2 and S3). Differences between stimulation conditions were evaluated using a repeated measures analysis of covariance adjusted for language. Based on data normality, a post hoc analysis using 2‐tailed t test or a Wilcoxon signed‐rank test was performed to assess significant differences between the threshold state and 3 BT and 3 AT states. The 6 comparisons were adjusted using the Bonferroni correction. To assess associations between the primary endpoints, we used repeated measures correlation. 36 Single associations were assessed by Pearson and Spearman correlation coefficients for normally and non‐normally distributed data, respectively. A minimal level of statistical significance α = 0.05 was considered.

Results

Participants

A cohort of 24 STN‐DBS PD patients completed the study (Fig 2, Table 1, Fig S2). Six patients were unable to tolerate the experiment in medication OFF state and were, therefore, excluded. Most patients had no or only slight speech deficits preoperatively in the OFF‐medication state, with only 6 showing mild or moderate impairment (UPDRS‐III speech item: mean ± SD 1.14 ± 0.87; 16/24 ≤1). In the majority of cases, active DBS contacts were implanted in the motor region of the STN (Fig S2). There were no significant differences between the left and right electrodes in the posterior–anterior or inferior–superior axes (Fig S3). Two patients were unable to tolerate the protocol stimulation states on one of the sides (1 on the left, 1 on the right), and 6 patients could not tolerate the 0.9 mA AT condition on one of the sides (4 on the left, 2 on the right). Overall, 2,514 speech task audio files were analyzed.

Primary Endpoints and Individual Acoustic Features

All selected acoustic features across two 0.3 BT conditions exhibited a moderate to very strong stability (Fig S5).

We found significant differences between the stimulation conditions for all 3 primary outcomes including CDI (p < 0.001), subjective self‐rating (p < 0.001), and intelligibility score (p = 0.040) on the left side, but only for the CDI (p < 0.001) and subjective self‐rating (p < 0.001) on the right side (Fig 3, Table S4). Regarding the individual acoustic features, only the F0 SD displayed significance for both left (p = 0.001) and right side (p < 0.001). In contrast, the MPT was strongly significant only on the left side (p < 0.001), whereas the HNR and LTAS skewness reached significance only on the right side (p = 0.02 and p = 0.03, respectively) (Fig 4). Where applicable, results were adjusted to account for violations of the sphericity assumption (Table S5).

In the post‐hoc analysis of primary endpoints (Fig 3, Table S6), both the CDI and the subjective measure showed significantly worsened speech in the 0.6 AT state (only on the left side, p = 0.033 and p = 0.003, respectively) and in the 0.9 AT state (p < 0.001 on the left for both, and p = 0.005 and p < 0.001 on the right, respectively), whereas the intelligibility score did not demonstrate any significant changes.

Furthermore, varying sensitivity to increasing amplitude was observed in the individual acoustic features (Fig 4, Table S6). On the left side, significant deterioration above the dysarthria threshold was detected in the HNR (0.9 AT, p = 0.022), MPT (0.6 AT, p = 0.033; 0.9 AT, p = 0.006), and F0 SD (0.9 AT, p = 0.023) features. Notably, F0 SD was the only feature that displayed significant sensitivity to DBS induced deterioration bilaterally, with a significant decline on the right side at the 0.9 AT level (p = 0.013).

The CDI captured a significant, bilaterally worse performance in the stimulation OFF state, compared to a reference group of healthy controls (Table S7), whereas no significant difference was found in the intelligibility score. Likewise, the CDI and subjective self‐rating successfully differentiated between the ON and OFF stimulation conditions (p < 0.05 and p < 0.01, respectively) for both stimulation sides, whereas the intelligibility score did not (Table S8, Fig S6).

Moreover, a strong bilateral association was found between the CDI and the subjective self‐rating (p < 0.001), whereas the intelligibility score showed only a moderate correlation with these endpoints on the left side (p = 0.02) (Fig S7).

Correlations between Primary Endpoints and Patient Characteristics

Among all tested correlations, only the association between the CDI and the time elapsed since DBS surgery showed a consistent trend, exclusively for the left stimulation side. Beginning at the 0.3 BT level, the CDI correlated significantly with the postoperative interval (p < 0.05), and this relationship strengthened at higher amplitudes (p < 0.01), with moderately strong negative coefficients (Fig 5, Table S9).

Discussion

This article is the first to provide acoustic biomarkers that comprehensively characterize the side effects of increasing DBS amplitude on speech. CDI displayed objective and sensitive bilateral detection of stimulation‐induced side effects, which were more prominent for left‐sided stimulation, and was highly correlated with subjective self‐ratings of speech.

Below the clinically determined, stimulation‐induced dysarthria threshold, all primary outcomes on both sides remained stable, showing only marginal improvement relative to the stimulation OFF state. It remains possible that the beneficial speech effects of DBS reported in prior studies 16 , 23 , 26 , 37 could occur at stimulation amplitudes below the minimum level used in our protocol. Above the threshold, speech deteriorated rapidly with increasing amplitude, likely reflecting current expansion beyond the STN. Adverse effects on speech of some patients have been reported in several studies 3 , 7 , 9 , 18 , 38 and attributed to current spread into adjacent structures, 28 , 37 including the internal capsule, 4 , 5 cerebellothalamic tract, 28 , 38 , 39 and corticobulbar pathways. 4 , 22 , 28 Conflicting findings on DBS's impact on speech across literature 2 , 28 , 29 could be then, to some degree, explained by differences of electrode locations in studied cohorts, eliciting adverse effects on varied amplitudes as current approaches the STN's anatomical boundary in individuals that have active electrodes closer to the edge.

Our clinical determination of the individual dysarthria thresholds revealed a wide range of amplitudes at which side effects emerged, suggesting substantial heterogeneity of electrode locations and proximities to STN‐adjacent structures. Notably, on the left side approximately 25% of patients reached their dysarthria threshold at amplitudes below their chronic setting (Fig S4), implying that the chronic condition may, especially under bilateral stimulation, induce some level of dysarthria, which went undetected during routine programming. The latter highlights the potential higher sensitivity of acoustic speech biomarkers in detecting subtle speech changes compared to the clinician's perception, which is particularly important during individual dysarthria threshold evaluation in postoperative management. 40

Primary Endpoints Response

The CDI outperformed any single acoustic feature in detecting stimulation‐induced side effects, likely because averaging across features attenuated the variability introduced by different amounts of current spread to different structures across patients. Nonetheless, response variance remained high, and even at the highest stimulation amplitude, 25% of patients demonstrated improved speech relative to their individual stimulation OFF condition (Fig S8).

The observed speech deterioration was statistically significant for both the CDI and the subjective ratings, with the latter demonstrating slightly greater sensitivity because of reduced variability at higher stimulation levels. Although the CDI components were selected to encompass all principal dysarthria dimensions, subjective ratings inherently integrate a broader range of speech production aspects and may, therefore, detect changes beyond those captured by acoustic measures. These findings underscore the value of incorporating individual patient feedback through postoperative programming.

Currently, intelligibility remains a primary outcome in DBS‐related speech research. 28 However, our intelligibility measure did not achieve statistical significance, only suggesting a trend toward deterioration. Although we used a previously validated instrumental intelligibility measure, 41 the high transcription error rates associated with severely impaired speech, combined with the subtle changes introduced by emerging side effects, create a challenging environment for reliable tracking of stimulation effects using intelligibility alone. Yet, high variability of responses was also noted in a previous longitudinal study that used subjective intelligibility rating based on listener transcriptions. 38

Response of Individual Acoustic Features

Not all acoustic features describing hypokinetic dysarthria responded equally to the stimulation, with only those reflecting aerodynamic insufficiency (MPT), monopitch (F0 SD), and decreased voice quality (HNR) presented significant stimulation‐induced side effect sensitivity. This observation implies that speech disturbances caused by DBS cover only part of hypokinetic dysarthria signs typical of PD progression, as previously proposed in DBS‐related phenotype analyses. 4 , 10

For those acoustic features that showed significant deterioration under high stimulation, prior studies have reported inconsistent findings, 16 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 as some observed improvements 16 , 22 or no change 21 , 28 in maximum phonation time, while reports on monopitch range from improvement 16 , 24 to deterioration 23 to stability. 21 Similarly, voice quality outcomes displayed mixed effects. 16 , 25 These findings could be explained by the current spread into the corticobulbar and corticospinal tracts, which can induce contractions of the laryngeal, pharyngeal, and respiratory muscles, 40 thereby limiting respiratory volume, reducing control of glottal oscillations, and constricting the vocal tract. This directly affects phonation duration, fundamental frequency modulation range, and noise content during vowel production, as observed in our results.

Worsening trends in VOT and LTAS skewness were mitigated by substantially increased variance at high amplitudes (Fig S9). Previous studies on articulation have reported either detrimental effects, 18 no change, 19 , 28 or mixed responses 25 , 26 to DBS, and investigations using LTAS features have likewise yielded inconsistent findings. 16 , 25 , 27 Articulation rate exhibited only minimal changes, which is consistent with previous reports. 3 , 18 , 22 Likewise, the minimal alteration in monoloudness observed here aligns with earlier findings. 7 , 21 Side effects on loudness may be potentially offset by beneficial effects of stimulation inside the STN found in some studies, 22 , 38 , 42 counterbalancing impairments from current spread and yielding a net neutral outcome on average.

Lateralized Effects

We observed numerous lateral differences that support greater sensitivity of the left hemisphere to stimulation‐induced speech side effects. The CDI and subjective ratings both displayed significantly increased sensitivity to stimulation‐induced speech deterioration on the left, coupled with a slightly larger variance of outcomes when compared to the right side. This observation connects to the negative effects of left‐sided STN‐DBS on speech found by previous studies. 12 , 38 , 39 , 42 Interestingly, our finding agrees with a recent de novo, drug‐naive PD study 43 showing that lower left‐side dopamine transporter availability is linked to significantly more severe dysarthria than right‐side reductions.

Lateralization was also evident in several acoustic features, most notably phonation duration. On the left side, durations deteriorated significantly with increased stimulation, but improved at lower amplitudes, whereas on the right side, deterioration occurred in only a subset of patients, yielding highly variable outcomes at the highest amplitudes. Similarly, slight improvements in phonation duration with left‐sided stimulation and negligible change on the right have been documented. 42 Interestingly, this study 42 also found that right‐sided stimulation produced a significant increase in phonation intensity, whereas left‐sided stimulation led to a slight decline.

Increasing stimulation also produced a significantly greater deterioration of voice quality on the left side, accompanied by markedly higher variability than on the right. Although a previous study reported no overall stimulation effect on HNR when comparing preoperative and postoperative speech, they did identify a significant link between dysphonia severity and anterior–posterior electrode placement within the left STN. 8 This left‐sided, posterior‐related sensitivity may explain the elevated HNR variability we observed on the left, particularly given its slightly more anterior contact placement relative to the right (Fig S3).

In some of our patients, left‐sided stimulation provoked a severe decline in articulation captured by VOT. Previous study also reported articulation decline in a distinct subset of patients, although they did not examine lateral differences. 3 Likewise, a recent study of unilateral upper motor neuron dysarthria found that left‐hemisphere stroke lesions produced greater deterioration of articulatory features than right‐hemisphere lesions, linked to locations along the pyramidal tract. 44

Finally, the time elapsed since surgery showed a consistent, significant negative correlation emerging at higher amplitudes. This effect was observed exclusively in the CDI under left‐sided stimulation. This relationship suggests that as more time passes after DBS surgery, speech side effects from high‐amplitude stimulation become less severe. One potential explanation is a long‐term microlesion effect that persists beyond the 3‐month postoperative exclusion period, temporarily increasing vulnerability to current diffusion. A similar left‐sided microlesion effect has been reported for verbal fluency. 13 Lateralized effects of STN‐DBS on verbal fluency have also been observed in a recent unilateral study, in which verbal fluency deteriorated significantly under left‐sided DBS and improved slightly with right‐sided stimulation. 12 Alternatively, we may hypothesize that a neuroplasticity mechanism might underlie this trend, and over time, speech‐processing circuits could adapt to continuous low‐level chronic stimulation, yielding improved speech outcomes even under higher amplitudes. However, this finding should be considered with caution given the exploratory nature of this analysis and because of the well‐known worsening of speech with long‐term DBS. 1

Strengths and Limitations

The major advantage of the current approach lies in the use of a limited set of non‐overlapping speech features with well‐defined pathophysiological relevance that is distinctive for hypokinetic dysarthria. The proposed solution does not require large sample sizes to train language‐specific models and is, therefore, readily applicable as a potential screening tool in patients with DBS. The speech tasks themselves are easy to administer even by non‐professionals and require only approximately 2 minutes per stimulation state, further complemented by fully automated acoustic analyses.

We limited our investigation to amplitude adjustments, as this remains the dominant clinical programming parameter 45 despite emerging research on frequency 19 , 46 and pulse‐width effects. 19 , 47 Future studies should examine how variations in frequency and pulse‐width influence DBS‐induced speech side effects.

No systematic imaging or structural‐connectivity analysis was performed, limiting our ability to link speech deterioration directly to current spread into specific structures surrounding the STN. Although this was beyond the present scope, such analyses are essential and are planned for future work.

One potential limitation is that the current findings are based on speech from Swiss German‐ and Swiss French‐speaking patients. However, the expected direction of change in the investigated acoustic features, associated with progressive neurodegeneration, has been shown to be consistent across 5 Indo‐European languages, including English, French, Italian, Czech, and German. 35 Previous studies have generally reported largely similar profiles of hypokinetic dysarthria in PD across different languages. 48 , 49

Finally, the minimum post‐surgery inclusion threshold was 3 months, which may allow speech outcomes to be influenced by peri‐lead edema or microlesion effects. However, most patients exceeded this threshold (87.5% and 75% assessed at 6 and 12 months, respectively), and potential microlesion‐related bias is likely mitigated by the repeated‐measures design focusing on within‐subject differences. Although peri‐lead edema is observed in up to half of implanted electrodes and may rarely lead to substantial neurological complications, it is typically asymptomatic and resolves spontaneously or with minimal intervention within weeks after surgery. 50 Future longitudinal studies could assess these effects and their interaction with increasing stimulation amplitude.

Conclusion

Our study identified easily obtainable digital speech biomarkers that effectively assess stimulation‐induced side effects of STN‐DBS, making them strong candidates for safety outcome measures in surgical PD treatments. Given the critical need to manage speech outcomes post‐STN‐DBS, these findings offer practical guidance for stimulation programming aimed at mitigating dysarthria. Furthermore, they suggest that speech‐based metrics could serve as feedback signals in future adaptive, closed‐loop DBS systems.

Note the decreased subjective self‐rating correlation caused by discrete rating scale scoring. Additionally, several outliers were observed in the intelligibility score response.

Black stars indicate significant differences between the HC group and the group of the adjacent box, grey stars indicate significant differences between the CHRONIC and OFF groups. Chronic stimulation produced bilateral speech improvements in the CDI (p < 0.05) and subjective self‐rating (p < 0.01). Conversely, the CDI indicated significantly worse speech versus healthy controls in the OFF state (p < 0.01 bilaterally) and under chronic stimulation on the right side (p < 0.05). The intelligibility score did not show any significant effects. Significance: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001.

Figure S7. Repeated‐measures correlation of primary endpoints. Associations between endpoints were evaluated using repeated‐measures correlation [3], ensuring linearity, sphericity, and normality. A highly significant, moderate‐to‐strong correlation was observed between the CDI and subjective self‐rating on both sides, while the intelligibility score showed a significant but weaker correlation with the other endpoints, confined to the left side. Plot colors are used solely to differentiate between individual patients. Each plot contains the value of the repeated‐measures correlation coefficient (rrm) accompanied by its significance.

Figure S8. Primary endpoints parallel plots of individual patients' responses to stimulation. Colors of plots only differentiate between individual patients. Note the heterogeneity of responses that averages out to a consistent trend in the CDI and subjective self‐rating. The intelligibility measure suffered from high variability, especially on the left side.

Figure S9. Acoustic features parallel plots of individual patients' responses to stimulation. Colors of plots only differentiate between individual patients. Note the strong trend in maximum phonation time, limited to the left side. Voice F0 SD presented a bilaterally robust response to overstimulation. A consistent deterioration trend is shown in the LTAS skewness and voice onset time features, hindered by markedly high variance in the high stimulation amplitudes.

Table S1. Feature descriptions. List of primary outcomes and selected acoustic features representing distinct aspects of hypokinetic dysarthria with their pathophysiological interpretations. Phonation task amounted to a production of vowel “a” after a deep breath, for as long as possible. Syllables “pa‐ta‐ka” were produced consecutively for a minimum of 12 repetitions in the DDK task, as accurately and quickly as possible. Reading task used German and French translations of the standardized text paragraph “The North Wind and the Sun”, which patients read aloud in their habitual volume and speed. Articulation rate was calculated in Python via Whisper [7] (model large v3m, language fine‐tuned, OpenAI, Inc., San Francisco, CA) while the remaining features were computed using fully automatic algorithms in MATLAB (version R2024b, The MathWorks, Inc., Natick, MA). Detailed algorithmic information on feature extraction can be found via provided references.

Table S2. Feature normality across stimulation levels. The Shapiro–Wilk test was applied to all primary endpoints and acoustic features for each stimulation side and amplitude condition. The majority of data exhibited normality.

Table S3. Shapiro–Wilk normality test results for all primary endpoints and acoustic features in healthy controls, and in DBS patients at chronic and OFF stimulation states.

Table S4. RM‐ANCOVA results. Sensitivity of primary and secondary features to stimulation amplitude change was evaluated using a repeated measures analysis of covariance (RM‐ANCOVA) model. In this model, feature values served as the dependent variable, stimulation amplitude condition was treated as a 9‐level within subject factor (0.9BT, 0.6BT, 0.3BT, OT, 0.3AT, 0.6AT, 0.9AT), and language was included as a covariate. A Greenhouse–Geisser correction was applied in cases of sphericity violation, indicated by Mauchly's test. The homogeneity of slopes assumption was assessed and ensured in all instances. The interaction between stimulation level and the language covariate was assessed to ensure the homogeneity of slopes assumption. Effect size estimates are reported as partial eta‐squared (ηp 2 ) values.

Table S5. Sphericity assessment results. Mauchly's test was used to assess the sphericity assumption before RM‐ANCOVA.

Table S6. Post‐hoc analysis results. A post‐hoc analysis was performed to assess significant differences between the threshold and each of the BT and AT amplitudes, to determine improvements or deterioration in speech. The six comparisons per feature and side were conducted based on group normality: either a paired samples two‐tailed t‐test or a Wilcoxon signed‐rank test was used, and the results were adjusted for type I error using the Bonferroni correction.

Table S7. Healthy controls' demographic and clinical characteristics. Twenty‐four healthy controls from a prior multicenter study [9] were selected as a reference for stimulation effect in primary endpoints. The controls were language‐, gender‐, and age‐matched to the DBS group, and with no history of neurological or communication disorders. Motor performance and cognitive capabilities were assessed by the UPDRS III and MoCA scales, respectively. The DBS group exhibited significantly worse motor performance (Wilcoxon rank‐sum test; W = 814, p < 0.001), while cognition scores were on level with the control group (two‐sample two‐tailed t‐test; t = −1.68, p = 0.1).

Table S8. Group comparisons for all primary endpoints and acoustic features in healthy controls, and in DBS patients at chronic and OFF stimulation states. For paired comparisons, either a two‐tailed paired‐samples t‐test or a Wilcoxon signed‐rank test was employed; for independent comparisons, a two‐sample two‐tailed t‐test or a Wilcoxon rank‐sum test was used. All p‐values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Bonferroni correction.

Table S9. Primary endpoints associations with patient characteristics. The absolute amplitude at the stimulation‐induced dysarthria threshold, and a difference between the absolute amplitudes at the OT and chronic states were added to the available demographic data, clinical variables and chronic amplitudes for relationship assessment. Only the composited dysarthria index association with the postoperative interval exhibited a consistent, moderately strong, significant relationship trend under increasing stimulation amplitude.
