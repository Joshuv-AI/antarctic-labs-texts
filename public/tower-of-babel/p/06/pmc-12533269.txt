A replicating RNA vaccine protects against severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome virus infection in mice
Authors: Allie Stamper, Thomas Bisom, Kimberly Meade-White, Matthew Lewis, Shanna Leventhal, Chad Clancy, Troy Hinkley, Jesse Erasmus, Heinz Feldmann, David W. Hawman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
No approved vaccines are currently available for Severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome virus (SFTSV). SFTSV is a tick-borne RNA virus with a case fatality rate ranging from 5 to 30% and is considered by the WHO to be a priority pathogen. Here, we evaluated a replicating RNA (repRNA) vaccine for protection against lethal SFTSV challenge in mice. We found that a repRNA-expressed SFTSV nucleoprotein (repNP) or glycoprotein precursor (repGPC) could confer protection, identifying both as protective antigens for SFTSV vaccine development. Furthermore, protection by our repNP vaccine required the intracellular Fc receptor TRIM21 for optimal protection, expanding the role of anti-NP antibodies and TRIM21 in control of bunyaviruses. Our data support continued development of this vaccine for SFTSV.

Introduction

Severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome virus (SFTSV), taxonomically reclassified as Dabie bandavirus, is a tick-borne virus within the order Hareavirales in the class Bunyaviricetes1. SFTSV is endemic in Asia with serological evidence and case reports in China, Japan, South Korea, and other Asian countries2,3. Troublingly, the tick vector and reservoir of SFTSV, the Asian Longhorned tick (Haemaphysalis longicornis), is now an established invasive species in the United States4. SFTSV is closely related to Heartland virus, already found in the USA5. Similar to the distantly related but also tick-borne Crimean-Congo hemorrhagic fever virus (CCHFV), serological evidence of SFTSV infection in livestock and wildlife without apparent disease has been reported2,6, suggesting these animal species may serve as important amplifying hosts in the tick-viral lifecycle. Like other viral hemorrhagic fevers, the disease presents initially as a non-specific febrile illness before progressing to hemorrhagic manifestations, organ dysfunction, inflammatory immune responses, and in some cases, death2. SFTSV infection may also involve significant neurological complications in severe cases7. Currently, there are no approved vaccines or treatments for SFTSV, although pre-clinical evaluation of antivirals such as ribavirin and favipiravir has shown promise8,9. However, ribavirin treatment of SFTSV patients in China failed to show any improvement in outcome10.

Several vaccines against SFTSV have been evaluated in pre-clinical models2,11, including live-attenuated viruses, DNA and mRNA nucleic acid-based, subunit, and inactivated viruses. Most approaches have focused on the SFTSV glycoproteins Gn and Gc, as the Gn is the target of protective neutralizing antibodies12. Here, we utilized a replicating RNA vaccine platform based on an alphavirus replicon system in which the structural genes of Venezuelan Equine Encephalitis Virus were replaced with either the SFTSV nucleoprotein (NP) or glycoprotein precursor complex (GPC). The replicon retains the alphavirus replicase machinery, enabling dose sparing and increased potency compared to traditional mRNA vaccines while being incapable of spreading cell-to-cell, retaining a key safety margin13. Our replicon is delivered in vivo with a cationic nanocarrier named LIONTM, which shows restricted biodistribution and reduced reactogenicity compared to lipid nanoparticle delivery14,15. A similar vaccine approach has demonstrated pre-clinical efficacy against a variety of pathogens16–19, including against CCHFV20,21 and this vaccine against CCHFV has recently entered human clinical trials. A clinical stage repRNA/LION-based vaccine has received emergency use authorization for SARS-CoV-2 in India22–24, and clinical trials have shown the vaccine to elicit potent humoral and cellular immunity25,26. Here, we evaluated repNP or repGPC against lethal SFTSV challenge in mice. We found that either repNP or repGPC could confer protection against SFTSV. Furthermore, repNP was protective, in part, through the cytoplasmic Fc-receptor tripartite motif containing protein 21 (TRIM21).

Results

SFTSV YL-1 causes lethal disease in C57BL/6J mice with transient suppression of type I interferon (IFN)

We first established a lethal challenge model for SFTSV for the evaluation of candidate vaccines. SFTSV causes little-to-no disease in wild-type (WT) mice27, although mice deficient in type I IFN rapidly succumb27. However, to avoid the confounding factor of vaccinating mice with an absent type I IFN response, which can significantly confound immune responses, particularly to replicating vaccines28, we utilized WT mice treated with the type I IFN receptor blocking antibody, MAR1-5A3, which allows transient suppression of the type I IFN response. This model has previously been established for a Korean isolate of SFTSV29. To assess the pathogenicity and determine the median lethal dose (LD50) of a Chinese strain of SFTSV, YL-1, in C57BL/6J mice treated with MAR1-5A3, we inoculated mice with 0.1, 1, 10, 100, or 1000 TCID50 of virus. All groups of mice began to lose weight between days 2 and 4 post-infection (PI), and weight loss peaked at days 6–7 with slightly more than 20% of original body weight lost (Supplementary Fig. 1a). Mice that lost less than 25% of their body weight were generally able to recover, and the weight gain recovery period was from about day 6–12 PI. Generally, we found a correlation between viral dose and disease kinetics, with a higher challenge dose resulting in faster disease onset and a higher proportion of mice succumbing to the virus (Supplementary Fig. 1b). Euthanized mice typically exhibited an altered gait, sunken eyes, hunched posture, ruffled fur, and/or decreased responsiveness to touch. The LD50 was 1 TCID50. Together, these data establish a lethal model of SFTSV YL-1 infection in mice with transiently suppressed type I IFN responses, and we proceeded with studies to evaluate the efficacy of our repNP and repGPC vaccines.

RepRNA vaccination induces primarily humoral immunity with limited cellular immunity

Although previous vaccines for SFTSV have focused on the GPC, we have shown that our replicating RNA vaccine expressing the distantly related CCHFV NP could confer complete protection21. Therefore, we wanted to evaluate a repRNA vaccine expressing either the SFTSV GPC (repGPC) or NP (repNP). These antigens from the SFTSV strain HB29 were cloned into our replicon plasmid, and protein expression was confirmed via immunofluorescence assay using immune sera from mice that survived SFTSV infection (Fig. 1a). To assess the immune response produced by vaccination, groups of mice were vaccinated with a replicon expressing the irrelevant green fluorescent protein (sham vaccination), repNP, repGPC, or repNP + repGPC. Mice were vaccinated with 2.5 µg of each repRNA via IM injection on a prime-boost regimen at day -56 and day -28, respectively, relative to SFTSV challenge (Fig. 1b). Mice vaccinated with both repNP and repGPC received 5 µg of RNA in total. A subset of mice was euthanized at day 0 to assess B- and T cell responses induced by repRNA vaccination. Whole-virion ELISAs were conducted with sera from vaccinated mice to measure antibody response to SFTSV. Similar to what we have previously reported for CCHFV, mice vaccinated with repNP or repNP + repGPC showed robust humoral immunity to SFTSV (Fig. 1c). In contrast, the repGPC vaccine elicited a marginal antibody response, clearly weaker than repNP-vaccinated mice (Fig. 1c). This suggests that both repNP and repGPC elicit antibody responses against SFTSV, but responses elicited by repNP are more robust. To further investigate the response, we evaluated the sera against recombinant NP (rNP) and Gn (rGn). Consistent with our whole-virus ELISA, repNP-vaccinated mice had a strong response to rNP, while no response was measured against rGn in repGPC-vaccinated animals (Fig. 1d). Although little antibody was measured against rGn, which is the target of neutralizing antibodies, sera were assessed for SFTSV neutralization by microneutralization assay. To evaluate T cell activation in response to vaccination, splenocytes from vaccinated mice were stimulated with overlapping peptide pools against regions of SFTSV NP and GPC in an IFNγ ELIspot assay. Compared to sham vaccinated mice, repNP + repGPC-vaccinated mice showed responses to GPC peptide pool 1 (aa 1–131), while repGPC-vaccinated mice responded to GPC pools 1 and 7 (aa 717–843) (Fig. 1e, f). No significant responses were measured in repNP-only vaccinated mice (Fig. 1e). Overall, these data indicate that repNP elicits SFTSV-specific non-neutralizing antibody responses while repGPC elicits modest SFTSV-specific humoral and cellular immunity.

Vaccination with repNP, repGPC, or repNP + repGPC is protective against SFTSV challenge

We next evaluated if these responses were protective against lethal SFTSV challenge. Groups of vaccinated mice were treated with MAR1-5A3 and infected with 100 TCID50 of SFTSV strain YL-1 (100 LD50s). YL-1 and our SFTSV HB29 vaccine antigens differ by 2% in the GPC and are homologous in the NP, thus representing a largely homologous challenge between vaccine antigen and challenge virus. Mice that received the sham vaccine showed significant disease and all succumbed to the infection by day 7 PI (Fig. 2a). In contrast, all mice that were vaccinated against SFTSV survived (Fig. 2a). Mice receiving repNP or repGPC alone lost minimal weight (5–10%) around days 5 and 6 PI but returned to their starting weight between days 5 and 7 PI. No other clinical signs of disease were observed. In contrast to the single RNA vaccines, mice that were vaccinated with both repNP + repGPC did not exhibit any weight loss or clinical signs (Fig. 2a), suggesting synergistic protection in mice receiving both vaccines. To further evaluate control of SFTSV infection conferred by vaccination, groups of mice were euthanized at day 5 PI to assess viral loads in the liver, spleen, kidney, lung, and blood by qRT-PCR. Interestingly, despite eliciting modest humoral and cellular immunity, mice receiving the repGPC exhibited significantly reduced viral RNA in all tissues evaluated (Fig. 2b). Mice receiving the repNP vaccine alone or the repNP + repGPC combination had significantly reduced viral RNA in all tissues except the lung (Fig. 2b). We also measured anamnestic antibody responses in survivors at day 14 PI. Compared to antibody responses measured at time of SFTSV challenge, by day 14 PI, all surviving mice had significantly increased SFTSV-specific antibody as measured by whole-virus antigen ELISA (Fig. 2c). All vaccinated mice, including repNP-only vaccinated mice, developed modest neutralizing activity against SFTSV at study endpoint (Supplementary Fig. 2a) that correlated with detectable rGn binding antibodies by ELISA (Supplementary Fig. 2b). Further, repGPC-vaccinated mice also developed NP-specific responses after challenge (Supplementary Fig. 2b). These data suggest that in all groups, sufficient viral replication occurred to elicit anamnestic responses to vaccine encoded antigens along with de novo responses to viral antigens not present in their respective vaccines.

repRNA protects against tissue pathology in mice challenged with SFTSV

Since we were unable to detect the infectious virus and to further evaluate vaccine-mediated control, we also assessed whether vaccination protected against tissue pathology in challenged mice. In tissues collected from sham-vaccinated mice at day 5 PI, significant histopathologic lesions were primarily limited to the spleen, which has been observed previously by others with SFTSV30. A mild to moderate neutrophilic splenitis with follicular necrosis was observed in all (100%, n = 6/6) sham vaccinated, SFTSV challenged mice (Fig. 3a). Similar histopathologic changes were observed in all repNP-vaccinated mice (n = 6/6) but were graded minimal (n = 4/6) to mild (n = 2/6) (Fig. 3b). In repGPC mice, no changes were observed in 50% (n = 3/6) mice and only minimal (n = 2/6) to mild (n = 1/6) changes were observed in remaining mice (Fig. 3c). In-situ hybridization (ISH) was performed on a multi-tissue slide for each animal, which included section(s) of liver, kidney, and spleen. As expected, sham-vaccinated animals had labeling for viral RNA in all three organs. Viral RNA labeling was observed in hepatocytes, Kupffer cells, and cellular debris within foci of necrosis in the liver. In the kidney, RNA labeling was observed in peri-renal fat and within the renal interstitium (Supplementary Fig. 3). Finally, in the spleen, RNA labeling was observed in the peri-splenic mesentery as well as regions of both red pulp and white pulp (Fig. 3e, i and Supplementary Fig. 3). When evaluating for viral RNA in the repGPC-vaccinated cohort, limited viral RNA was detected in evaluated sections of liver and was limited to foci of hepatocellular necrosis (n = 2/6). Similarly, viral limited viral RNA labeling was detected in the kidney (n = 1/6) while peri-renal fat labeling was observed in 50% (n = 3/6) of repGPC-vaccinated mice (Supplementary Fig. 3). Evaluation of sections of spleen showed the most consistent viral RNA labeling in the repGPC cohort with viral RNA observed in at least one of the three micro-anatomic regions (mesentery, red pulp or white pulp) in almost all (n = 5/6) mice (Fig. 3g, k and Supplementary Fig. 3). In contrast, repNP-vaccinated mice challenged with SFTSV lacked viral RNA labeling in evaluated sections of liver and kidney with limited viral RNA labeling, predominantly in regions of white pulp within the spleen (n = 5/6; Fig. 3f, j and Supplementary Fig. 3). The addition of repGPC to the repNP vaccination showed minimal change to viral RNA distribution with only one (n = 1/6) section of liver showing viral RNA labeling in hepatocellular debris, no viral RNA labeling in evaluated sections of kidney, and limited viral RNA labeling within the white pulp (n = 5/6; Fig. 3h, l and Supplementary Fig. 3).

Cumulatively, our data show that both repNP and repGPC vaccination could confer protection against lethal challenge in mice, with mice exhibiting little-to-no signs of clinical disease, reduced viral RNA loads in several tissues, and minimal tissue pathology. Further, mice vaccinated with both antigens exhibited the greatest control of the infection, suggesting synergistic immunity between NP and GPC-specific responses.

A bivalent repRNA expressing both NP and GPC protects against SFTSV challenge

Our data showed that combined vaccination with both repNP and repGPC afforded the greatest protection against SFTSV challenge, with no weight loss or other clinical signs of disease after challenge. We therefore designed a bivalent repRNA expressing both the NP and GPC from a single RNA. Mice were then immunized with 5 µg of this bivalent vaccine, and for comparison, separate mice were vaccinated with combined repNP (2.5 µg) and repGPC (2.5 µg) or were sham vaccinated. Vaccinations and challenge were performed in a timeline identical to that shown in Fig. 1b. Similar to repGPC, bivalent vaccination appeared to be weakly immunogenic at day 0, suggesting inclusion of both antigens on a single RNA may have diminished NP-specific responses (Fig. 4a). This was an effect we did not observe with a similarly designed vaccine against CCHFV31. However, this immune response was sufficient for robust protection against clinical disease, and protection was equivalent between bivalent and repNP + repGPC groups (Fig. 4b). These data suggest the modest immune response elicited by our bivalent construct was still sufficient to confer protection. However, further optimization of the bivalent construct is likely needed to elicit similar immune responses as when the antigens are delivered on separate RNAs.

repNP protects through cytoplasmic Fc-receptor TRIM21

We have recently shown that our repRNA expressing the CCHFV NP (repNPCCHFV) protects through NP-specific antibodies and TRIM21, an intracellular Fc receptor involved in non-neutralizing antibody responses32. As CCHFV and SFTSV are distantly related viruses and our SFTSV repNP vaccine elicited primarily humoral immunity, we hypothesized that our repNP against SFTSV would similarly require TRIM21. We primed and boosted WT mice or mice deficient in TRIM21 (TRIM21−/−) four weeks apart. We also increased the vaccine dose to 5 µg of each RNA to evaluate whether an increased dose could improve the protection conferred by the individual repNP or repGPC vaccines. Four weeks after boosting, vaccinated WT and TRIM21−/− mice had equivalent humoral immunity against SFTSV (Fig. 5a). Mice were then MAR1-5A3-treated and challenged with SFTSV as before. All vaccinated WT mice survived challenge with minimal weight loss (<5%) and no other signs of clinical disease (Fig. 5b), suggesting the increased vaccine dose conferred greater protection. RepGPC-vaccinated TRIM21−/− mice were similarly protected as vaccinated WT mice, suggesting that repGPC and GPC-specific antibodies do not require TRIM21 for protection. In contrast, repNP-vaccinated TRIM21−/− mice showed severe disease, with mice losing >15% of their starting weight, and 3 of 8 mice succumbed to the infection (Fig. 5b). Discussion

Here, we report a promising vaccine platform against SFTSV and demonstrate that immunity directed against either SFTSV NP or GPC can confer robust protection against lethal challenge in mice. This is especially pressing considering no vaccines are currently available for SFTSV, and it is still considered by the WHO to be a priority pathogen. Our data demonstrate that host immunity directed against either the SFTSV NP or GPC can confer protection, with inclusion of both antigens further increasing protection.

The SFTSV glycoproteins encoded by the GPC are responsible for receptor binding and entry and are the target of protective neutralizing antibodies33,34. Thus, the glycoproteins have been the focus of most vaccine efforts for SFTSV11, and we show here that our repGPC can confer protection against SFTSV. As our repGPC elicited weak antibody but stronger cellular responses prior to infection, along with a rapid anamnestic antibody response, further work will be needed to dissect the contribution of humoral and cellular immunity in repGPC conferred protection. mRNA-based vaccines expressing the SFTSV GPC or just Gn elicited neutralizing antibodies after a prime-boost35–37, and thus, it is unclear why our replicon vaccine expressing similar antigens elicited weak humoral immunity against the SFTSV glycoproteins. Interestingly, Lu et al. observed that despite eliciting similar levels of glycoprotein binding antibodies in their 1 µg and 5 µg dose groups, only the 5 µg dose group had measurable neutralizing antibodies36, suggestive of dose-dependent qualitative differences in the antibody response to the SFTSV Gn. Indeed, we observed greater protection against clinical disease when we increased our vaccine dose from 2.5 to 5 µg. Interestingly, against CCHFV, our replicon expressing the CCHFV GPC conferred weak humoral immunity and poor protection on its own21. The distinct protection against CCHFV and SFTSV by respective repGPC candidates is likely due to the highly heterologous challenge model used in the CCHFV studies21. Here, our SFTSV vaccine antigens and challenge strain represent a largely homologous challenge with complete homology in the NP antigen and 98% homology in the GPC. For CCHFV, a mismatch between vaccine antigen and challenge virus can result in breakthrough infections38, and inclusion of the more conserved NP may confer broadly protective immunity. However, sequence variation in CCHFV (>25% difference in amino acids in GPC)39 is greater than that reported for SFTSV (5% in GPC)40,41, suggesting that vaccine breakthrough due to a mismatch between vaccine antigen and infecting strain of SFTSV may be less of a concern.

Extensive studies in mice and non-human primates with our repNPCCHFV have demonstrated that antibodies directed against the related CCHFV NP can confer robust protection20,21,32, and these antibodies protect through TRIM21-dependent and independent mechanisms32. A previous study of a vaccinia-based vaccine expressing the SFTSV NP found this vaccine could confer robust protection against SFTSV challenge, and protection was maintained in mice depleted of CD8 T-cells42. Similar to our CCHFV work, we found that vaccination with our repNP against SFTSV can also confer robust protection, in part, through TRIM21. CCHFV-challenge of repNPCCHFV -vaccinated TRIM21−/− mice was uniformly lethal in contrast to only partial lethality in our SFTSV challenge model. This suggests the TRIM21-dependency of NP-specific antibody against CCHFV and SFTSV may be distinct. The precise downstream mechanism of how NP-specific antibodies and TRIM21 restrict SFTSV will require further study. Upon binding of TRIM21 to antibody-coated antigen, ubiquitination of TRIM21 is initiated, resulting in proteasomal degradation of the TRIM21-antibody-antigen complex43,44. This can lead to direct inhibition of viral replication while also priming cellular immunity44,45. TRIM21 is an ubiquitously expressed protein, but it requires antibody-coordinated dimerization for activity, which favors activity against repetitive oligomeric targets46,47. Thus, viral nucleoproteins such as those encoded by SFTSV and CCHFV and potentially other negative-strand viruses may represent ideal targets for activation of TRIM21 due to the highly ordered arrangement of NP on coated viral genomes48. In SFTSV-infected individuals, the NP appears to be the immunodominant target of humoral immunity49, and failure to mount an NP-specific antibody response is a correlate of poor outcome in SFTSV-infected patients50,51, suggesting our findings may have relevance beyond vaccine-elicited immunity. Cumulatively, our findings for CCHFV and now SFTSV suggest that antibodies directed against the bunyaviral NP may be a broadly protective mechanism.

Our study has important limitations. First, we only evaluated prime-boost vaccination regimens, and an ideal vaccine for SFTSV would elicit protective immunity with minimal vaccinations. We have previously shown robust protection against CCHFV within two weeks of a single immunization21,31, and we have shown here that increasing vaccine dose could increase protection, suggesting that single-shot protection with our SFTSV vaccines may be possible. However, this will require further study. Second, although we showed that repNP-mediated protection against SFTSV is impaired in TRIM21-deficient mice, the precise mechanism by which our vaccines, antibody, and TRIM21 confer protection will require further study. Of critical importance, how NP-specific antibody accesses the viral NP is unknown for either SFTSV or CCHFV, and what mechanisms confer TRIM21-independent or -dependent restriction are also unknown. Further, after challenge, all surviving vaccinated groups had binding antibodies to both NP and Gn and developed modest neutralizing responses. Thus, further study will be needed to dissect the contribution to protection by the immune responses present prior to challenge and by the anamnestic and de novo responses that developed rapidly upon challenge. Nevertheless, it is clear from the distinct clinical outcome between sham- and SFTSV-vaccinated mice that vaccine-elicited responses present at the time of challenge were necessary for protection.

In summary, we have demonstrated the protective efficacy of repNP and repGPC against SFTSV. These vaccine candidates are well-positioned to utilize our building pre-clinical and IND-enabling data sets to address a critical need for vaccines against SFTSV. Our work also identifies the SFTSV NP in addition to the viral glycoproteins as a viable vaccine-expressed antigen for further SFTSV vaccine development. Our previous work with CCHFV and now SFTSV suggest protection mediated by NP-specific antibody and TRIM21 may be a broad bunyaviral phenomenon. Our data warrant investigation of this phenomenon against other bunyaviruses and other negative-strand viruses.

Methods

Animals, biosafety, and ethics

All infectious work with SFTSV was performed in the BSL3 or BSL4 containment laboratory in accordance with standard operating procedures approved by the Rocky Mountain Laboratories Institutional Biosafety Committee, Division of Intramural Research, National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases, National Institutes of Health (Hamilton, MT, USA). All animal work was performed in strict accordance with the recommendations described in the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of the Office of Animal Welfare, National Institutes of Health, and the Animal Welfare Act of the US Department of Agriculture, in an AAALAC-accredited facility and study protocol approved by the RML ACUC. Male and female C57BL6/J mice, 8 weeks of age, were purchased directly from Jackson and randomly assigned to study groups. TRIM21−/− mice were from an in-house colony established from Jackson # 010724. Animals were housed under controlled conditions of 30–35% humidity, 22 °C temperature, and light (12-h light/12-h dark cycles). Food and water were provided ad libitum. All procedures on mice were performed under anesthesia (Isoflurane to effect). Euthanasia was performed by exsanguination and cervical dislocation on heavily sedated animals. To transiently suppress type I IFN, mice were treated once with 2.5 mg of MAR1-5A3 (Leinco) via the IP route at the time of challenge. The LD50 was determined using the Reed and Muench method52.

Vaccine repRNAs expressing human codon-optimized sequences of the HB29 NP or GPC (accession numbers KP202165.1 and KP202164.1, respectively) were designed. Our bivalent construct expressed the NP, then the GPC from a single RNA separated by an additional subgenomic promoter31. RepRNAs were prepared as previously described17. Mice were vaccinated with a single intramuscular injection of 50 µL volume to the hind limb. Protein expression of the repRNAs was evaluated in vitro by immunofluorescence. BHK-T7 cells were transfected with repNP or repGPC RNAs using Transit LT1 (Mirus Bio), and empty transfections were performed as a negative control, also using Transit LT1(Mirus Bio). Cells were incubated with transfection complexes at 37 °C, 5% CO2 for ~48 h to allow complete transfection and expression of the repRNAs. Cells were then fixed with 10% formalin and incubated at 4 °C overnight. The following day, anamnestic sera from a mouse that survived a challenge dose of 100 TCID50 SFTSV were used as the primary antibodies for detecting NP or GPC expressed from the repRNAs. Cells incubated with this sera in permeabilization solution (PBS + 0.05% Saponin + 0.1% BSA) at a 1:500 dilution for ~50 min at room temperature. Cells were washed and incubated with AF488 anti-mouse (Invitrogen) in permeabilization solution at a 1:2000 dilution for 45 min at room temperature and washed with permeabilization solution. Cellular nuclei were then stained with Nucblue (Invitrogen). The cells were then washed again with permeabilization solution and visualized on an ECHO Revolve microscope (ECHO).

Viral stock

SFTSV strain YL-1 was kindly provided by the World Reference Center for Emerging Viruses and Arboviruses at the University of Texas Medical Branch. The lyophilized virus was resuspended in sterile water. The virus was initially grown on Vero cells to generate a working stock. Sequencing showed mycoplasma contamination in the resuspended virus and in working stocks. Viral supernatant was further passaged on Vero cells using a Mycoplasma-EX kit (PromoKine) and protocol. SFTSV was passaged once more on Huh7 cells and then sequenced and compared to the original stock sequencing. No mutations or contaminants were found. Final stocks were titered on Vero cells using the TCID50 assay. TCID50/ml was calculated using the Reed–Muench method. Mice were challenged with indicated doses of SFTSV via a single 100 µL IP injection.

Quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR)

RNA was extracted from liver, spleen, kidney, and lung tissues with Qiagen RNeasy Mini kits, and blood and tissue culture viral RNA was extracted using Qiagen Qiamp viral RNA-mini-isolation kit. The provided protocols and buffers were used for all RNA extractions. Viral RNA was quantified via qRT-PCR using Qiagen Quantifast one-step qRT-PCR master mix and primers and probe specific to SFTSV NP (Forward: 5′ CCCTGATGCCTTGACGATCT 3′ Reverse: 5′ TGATTGGGTGAGGGACACAAAGTT 3′ Probe: 5′ TTGCCTCGAGTCAGGGCAAAGACAA 3′). Primers and probes were purchased from IDT. Reactions were performed using QuantStudio 5 RT-PCR system (Thermo Fisher) and the following cycle conditions: initial hold of 50 °C 10 min, initial denaturation of 95 °C 5 min, and 40 cycles of 95 °C 10 s and 60 °C for 30 s. RNA standards were made in-house using the Invitrogen Zero Blunt® TOPO® PCR Cloning Kit and a DNA fragment of SFTSV NP made by IDT, followed by transcription using the Invitrogen Megascript Kit. RNA was diluted to known quantities in 10-fold increments to be run alongside samples for quantification. The limit of detection was calculated by determining the copy number given by our standard curve and a Ct value of 40.

ELIspot

ELIspots were performed with splenocytes taken from the immunology group mice at day 0. Whole spleens were collected into 5 ml of RPMI complete media (supplemented with 10% FBS, 0.01% benzonase, and 2.5% HEPES), and spleens were homogenized by passing through a 100 micron filter, which was washed with an additional 5 ml RPMI complete media. Cells were centrifuged for 3 min at 400 rpm to pellet, and then resuspended in 3 mL room temperature ACK lysis buffer for 5 min. Lysis was stopped by adding 10 ml FACS buffer (PBS w/o Ca or Mg + 2% FBS + 1 mM EDTA). Cells were pelleted again and resuspended in CTL media. ELIspots were completed using the Immunospot Murine IFN-γ Single-Color Enzymatic ELISPOT Assay kit and associated protocol. Cells were plated at 300,000 cells/well with pooled peptides at a final concentration of 1 μg/ml for each peptide. Peptides were synthesized (Genscript) and consisted of 15-mers overlapping by 11 amino acids spanning the SFTSV strain HB29 NP and GPC. Peptides were resuspended in DMSO (Hybrimax grade, Sigma) and pooled with 29–30 peptides in each pool.

ELISA

For the whole-virus SFTSV antigen ELISA, antigen was prepared by infecting Vero cells with SFTSV YL-1 and collecting supernatant at 5 days post-infection. The virus was then pelleted by ultracentrifugation through a 20% sucrose cushion. The viral pellet was resuspended in 4 ml PBS + 2% Triton-X 100 and irradiated with 10 megarad (Mrad) of γ-radiation by a JL Shepherd Model 484 irradiator with a cobalt cell. Mock antigen was prepared similarly from mock-infected Vero cells. SFTSV antigen was adsorbed at 1:500 and mock antigen at 1:1000 in PBS to Nunc Maxisorp plates overnight at 4 °C. For the recombinant SFTSV NP (Sino Biological 212-SFTSV0010) and recombinant SFTSV Gn (Acro Biosystems GNN-S52H4) ELISAs, 50 ng per well of antigen was adsorbed overnight at 4 °C. Plates were blocked with 5% skim milk in PBS + 0.05% Tween-20 and then washed with PBS + 0.05% Tween-20. Serial fourfold dilutions of mouse serum in blocking buffer (5% skimmed milk in PBS + 0.05% Tween-20) beginning with a 1:100 dilution were then applied to plates for 1 h incubation. Plates were washed thoroughly with PBS + 0.05% Tween-20, and then an anti-mouse IgG detection antibody (Southern Biotech) in blocking buffer was added to plates at a 1:2000 concentration for 1 h incubation. Plates were washed thoroughly before being developed with 2, 2′-azino-di(3-ethylbenzthiazoline-6-sulfonate) for 30 min. The reaction was terminated with 5% sodium dodecyl sulfate (Sigma) in water. Absorbance at 405 nm was measured. Data is shown as the area under the curve (AUC) calculated on log10-transformed X values in Prism.

Neutralization assays

Sera samples were diluted 1:10, 1:20, 1:40, 1:80, 1:160, 1:320, 1:640, 1:1280, 1:2560, 1:5120, 1:10240, and 1:20480, and 120 TCID50 of SFTSV YL-1 were incubated with each dilution for 1 h. Virus/sera dilutions were then added to Vero unc cells that had been plated into black-walled 96-well plates. The cells were then incubated at 37 °C, 5% CO2 for 3 days. Infectious virus was assessed using an immunofluorescence assay (IFA) as previously described53,54. Following the 3-day incubation, cells were fixed with 10% formalin for IFA. After washing with PBS, cells were incubated for ~50 min with primary antibody for SFTSV Gn (Creative Biolabs) diluted 1:500 in permeabilization solution (PBS + 0.05% Saponin + 0.1% BSA) for detection of internalized/infectious SFTSV. Cells were then washed 3× with permeabilization solution, and subsequently incubated in the dark for ~50 min with AlexaFluor 488 goat anti-human secondary antibody (Invitrogen) diluted 1:2000 in permeabilization solution. Cells were again washed 3× with permeabilization solution and visualized using the ZOE Fluorescent Cell Imager (BioRad).

Histology

Tissues were fixed in 10% Neutral Buffered Formalin (×2 changes) for a minimum of 7 days. Tissues were placed in cassettes and processed with a Sakura VIP-6 Tissue Tek, on a 12-h automated schedule, using a graded series of ethanol, xylene, and PureAffin. Embedded tissues are sectioned at 5 μm and dried overnight at 42 °C prior to staining. Tissues were fixed in 10% Neutral Buffered Formalin ×2 changes, for a minimum of 7 days. Tissues were placed in cassettes and processed with a Sakura VIP-6 Tissue Tek, on a 12-h automated schedule, using a graded series of ethanol, xylene, and PureAffin. Embedded tissues are sectioned at 5 μm and dried overnight at 42 °C prior to staining. Detection of mRNA was performed using the RNAscope 2.5 VS assay (Advanced Cell Diagnostics Inc.) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, tissue sections were deparaffinized and pretreated with heat and protease before hybridization with the target-specific probe RNAscope™ 2.5 VS Probe- V-SFTSV-NP (Cat. #449899). Peptidylprolyl isomerase B (PPIB) and the bacterial gene, dihydrodipicolinate reductase (dapB), were used as positive and negative controls, respectively. Tissue sections were stained using the Discovery ULTRA slide staining system (Roche Diagnostics).
