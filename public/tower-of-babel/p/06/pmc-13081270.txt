Indigenous water governance in a China-Vietnam borderland: ethnographic insights from Nung and Tay communities
Authors: Ly Viet Truong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study examines indigenous water governance among the Nung and Tay people in northern Vietnam, focusing on Lang Son Province, a China–Vietnam borderland adjacent to Guangxi. While earlier ethnographic studies have documented traditional irrigation techniques among Nung and Tay communities, less is known about how these systems are currently organized, negotiated, and rearticulated under changing environmental conditions and institutional frameworks.MethodsThe analysis draws on ethnographic fieldwork conducted between 2024 and 2025 in three localities of Lang Son Province, combining participant observation, in-depth interviews, and on-site surveys of irrigation infrastructure. Sixty-five Nung and Tay farmers were interviewed to document contemporary practices, decision-making processes, and local interpretations of water use and management. Ethnographic materials were thematically analyzed with attention to both technical arrangements and their social and ritual dimensions.ResultsThe study shows that water management among the Nung and Tay functions as a community-based governance system centered on “phai” (weirs), “muong” (canals), “lai”, and “lin”. New fieldwork observations indicate that water-sharing practices are actively renegotiated in response to increasingly irregular rainfall, shifting cropping calendars, and the implementation of state irrigation and land-use policies. Customary rules and ritual practices continue to legitimize water allocation and communal authority, while also providing flexible frameworks for adjusting labor coordination and managing emerging conflicts. Comparison with selected Zhuang communities in Guangxi reveals shared cosmological understandings of water alongside contemporary differences in governance mechanisms and modes of institutional integration across the border. These findings challenge static representations of indigenous irrigation as a harmonious and self-regulating tradition, revealing it instead as a negotiated and uneven governance system shaped by ecological stress and institutional asymmetries.ConclusionBy foregrounding recent ethnographic evidence, this study advances existing scholarship by demonstrating how indigenous irrigation systems among the Nung and Tay operate as adaptive, socially embedded governance arrangements rather than static technical traditions. The findings highlight the capacity of indigenous water governance to respond to climatic and policy pressures and underscore its relevance for culturally grounded and sustainable agricultural development in upland border regions.Keywords: Indigenous knowledge; Water management; Wet rice cultivation; Nung, tay; Lang son; Guangxi; Ecological anthropology

Introduction

In the Vietnam–China border region of mainland Southeast Asia, fragmented terrain and dispersed water sources have long shaped wet-rice agriculture. Among the Nung and Tay people of Lang Son, water management is embedded in an indigenous knowledge system associated with “na” and organized through structures such as “phai” (weirs) and “muong” (canals), customary rules of allocation, and ritual practices including Water God worship and seasonal dam-opening ceremonies. Together, these practices constitute what may be described as a form of water civilization. In this paper, the term water civilization is not used in the deterministic sense of hydraulic civilization theory, but rather to denote a cultural–ecological complex in which water-related technologies, social conventions, and ritual practices jointly organize human–environment relations. This usage deliberately departs from Wittfogel’s hydraulic determinism, emphasizing negotiated practice, localized authority, and community-based coordination rather than centralized control. In recent decades, however, agricultural modernization, hydropower development, and climate change have increasingly reshaped these arrangements, raising questions about how indigenous water systems operate under contemporary conditions.

Previous studies on Nung and Tay irrigation have primarily documented traditional hydraulic techniques and ritual beliefs, often treating them as relatively stable cultural forms. Far less attention has been paid to how water management is currently practiced, negotiated, and adjusted in response to climatic uncertainty, policy intervention, and socio-economic change. Moreover, existing research has rarely examined Nung and Tay water knowledge within a borderland context, nor has it systematically compared these practices with those of related Tai-speaking groups across the China–Vietnam border, such as the Zhuang1 in Guangxi.

This study addresses these gaps through ethnographic fieldwork conducted between 2024 and 2025 in Lang Son Province. It contributes to the literature by examining indigenous water governance as a contemporary, negotiated system rather than a static tradition; by analyzing the social and ritual mechanisms through which water allocation is coordinated and contested in everyday practice; and by situating Nung and Tay water management within a cross-border comparative perspective. In doing so, the paper advances understanding of indigenous water knowledge as an adaptive form of community-based governance shaped by environmental change and institutional pressures.

Literature review

Research on mountain communities has commonly employed the framework of Traditional Ecological Knowledge (TEK) within ecological anthropology and ethnobiology. Rather than viewing TEK as a mere accumulation of technical experience, this body of scholarship conceptualizes it as an integrated system linking empirical knowledge, customary practices, and belief structures that mediate human–environment relations [5, 25]. In studies of upland rice cultivation in Southeast Asia, TEK has been shown to operate as a form of community-based ecological governance, in which water management, labor organization, and ritual practices are closely intertwined [8]. In this study, TEK is used in a focused analytical sense, as a lens for examining how water is managed, allocated, and legitimized through everyday practices and rituals, rather than as a comprehensive theory of sustainability or resilience.

In the mountainous Vietnam–China border region, a substantial body of research has documented wet-rice agriculture and irrigation systems among the Nung and Tay in Vietnam. Studies by La Cong Y [12, 13], Hoang Nam [7], the Institute of Ethnology [9, 10, 11], Tran Binh [30], Hoang Hoa Toan [6], Nguyen Thi Dan [20] and provincial-level surveys [14] describe the “phai - muong - lai - lin” system as a communal arrangement combining small-scale hydraulic infrastructure with village conventions and ritual norms regulating water use. These works consistently emphasize the central role of water in agricultural production and ritual life, demonstrating that irrigation practices are embedded within broader social and spiritual frameworks.

Parallel research in Guangxi and neighboring regions of southwestern China has examined water culture among the Zhuang and related Tai-speaking groups. Scholars such as Liang Tingwang and Fu Guanghua conceptualize Zhuang water management as a culturally integrated system encompassing irrigation techniques, social conventions, and Water God worship [15, 4]. Ethnographic studies further show that water functions simultaneously as a material resource and a symbolic medium linking agricultural fertility, community cohesion, and the life cycle [16, 22, 27]. Comparative works suggest strong cultural continuities between Zhuang and Tay–Nung communities in their cosmological understandings of water and their reliance on village-based governance mechanisms.

While Ostrom’s (1990) work laid the foundation for understanding common-pool resource institutions, recent scholarship has significantly extended this framework [21]. Empirical studies highlight persistent mismatches between policy expectations and the actual capacities of collective management institutions [28]. At the same time, emerging governance models emphasize the importance of state-reinforced self-governance, in which community-based institutions operate in interaction with, rather than in isolation from, state actors [2]. Experimental reviews of CPR research further underscore that cooperation is shaped by evolving institutional configurations and behavioral dynamics, rather than static design principles [18].

Despite this extensive literature, two limitations remain. First, existing studies tend to treat Nung, Tay, and Zhuang water systems as relatively stable traditions, offering limited insight into how these practices are negotiated and reconfigured under contemporary pressures such as climate change and state policy interventions. Second, comparative analysis across the Vietnam–China border remains fragmentary, with few studies explicitly examining how shared cultural logics of water governance diverge under different institutional regimes.

Building on this literature, the present study adopts a focused ethnoecological perspective to examine indigenous water governance among the Nung and Tay as a contemporary, negotiated system. Rather than reiterating general TEK theory, it emphasizes empirical analysis of water-related practices, rituals, and social institutions, while situating these findings within a cross-border comparative context with selected Zhuang communities in Guangxi.

Research methods

Study area

This study was conducted in the northern mountainous region of Vietnam, with Lang Son Province selected as the primary research site. Lang Son is a border province directly adjacent to Guangxi, China, and constitutes a long-standing cultural–ecological corridor shared by Tai-speaking groups across the Vietnam–China frontier [14]. The province is characterized by fragmented terrain, small river systems, and a long tradition of wet-rice cultivation dependent on community-based water management (Fig. 1).

Lang Son Province on the map of northern Vietnam (Maison Office)

Fieldwork was carried out in three local communities representing different ecological and socio-spatial contexts. The first site was Na Lenh hamlet, Cao Loc commune, located approximately 13 km from Lang Son City and about 17 km from the Vietnam–China border. This village is inhabited by both Nung and Tay households and retains a relatively intact traditional irrigation system. The second site was Hai Yen hamlet, Ba Son commune, situated around 13 km from Lang Son City and 26 km from the international border; this community is composed exclusively of Nung households and lies in a border-adjacent upland setting. The third site was Son Hong hamlet, Ky Lua ward, approximately 12 km from Lang Son City and 27 km from the border, representing a peri-urban context where traditional agricultural practices coexist with expanding urban infrastructure. The selection of these three sites allows for comparative analysis across border-proximate rural areas and a transitional rural–urban setting, thereby capturing variations in water governance practices under different environmental and institutional conditions Figs. 2, 3, 4, 5.

The Nung and Tay communities

The research focuses on the Nung and Tay ethnic groups, two closely related Tai-speaking communities with long histories of co-residence in northern Vietnam. According to the 2019 Population and Housing Census of Vietnam, the Nung population numbers approximately 1,830,298 people, while the Tay population comprises about 1,845,492 people nationwide. The two groups share linguistic affinities, overlapping cultural practices, and similar livelihood strategies centered on wet-rice cultivation (Fig. 9).

Nung people in traditional attire. (Source: Ly Viet Truong)

In Lang Son Province, Nung and Tay communities often live side by side, jointly managing agricultural land, water sources, and ritual life. Through generations of interaction with mountainous environments, they have developed shared bodies of indigenous knowledge, particularly in relation to irrigation, water allocation, and the ritual regulation of water use. These shared practices form the empirical foundation for examining indigenous water governance as a culturally embedded and socially negotiated system [7, 9].

Data collection and analytical methods

Ethnographic fieldwork formed the core methodological approach of this study, enabling close examination of interactions among humans, water, and indigenous knowledge within their lived cultural contexts. Sustained fieldwork was conducted through multiple visits between December 2024 and September 2025 in Lang Son Province. The author’s positionality as a Nung researcher native to Lang Son facilitated language use (Nung and Tay), cultural familiarity, and the establishment of long-term rapport with local communities, while maintaining analytical reflexivity throughout the research process.

Data were collected through semi-structured, in-depth interviews with 65 Nung and Tay farmers directly engaged in wet-rice cultivation and water use. The sample comprised 38 men and 27 women, spanning a broad age range (20 years and above), and was distributed across three field sites: Na Lenh hamlet (25 informants), Hai Yen hamlet (20 informants), and Son Hong hamlet (20 informants). All informants provided informed consent to take part in the study (Table 1).

In addition to individual interviews, ten group discussions were conducted across the three communities, organized by age and gender cohorts. These discussions addressed key themes including water allocation, irrigation construction and maintenance, water-related rituals, and recent changes associated with infrastructure modernization.

Ethnographic data were further supplemented through participant observation, field notes, audio recordings (with prior consent), and photographic documentation of irrigation structures and ritual practices. Data collection took place alongside everyday agricultural activities, collective labor, and ritual events, allowing for triangulation between narratives and observed practices. All research activities followed standard ethnographic ethical principles, including informed consent, respect for privacy, and anonymization where appropriate.

For comparative purposes, the analysis also incorporates published ethnographic literature on Zhuang water culture in Guangxi, China, together with contextual observations derived from two short-term field visits to Zhuang communities in Daxin County (March 2023) and Ningming County (August 2023). These visits were conducted in the course of the author’s doctoral research in Guangxi and involved rapid observation of irrigation infrastructure, village-level water management arrangements, and informal conversations with local residents. Given their limited duration and exploratory character, these materials are treated as supplementary and illustrative rather than as part of the primary ethnographic dataset, which is analytically restricted to the Lang Son fieldwork period (December 2024–September 2025). They are used to provide contextual grounding for cross-border comparison, rather than to offer an exhaustive account of Zhuang water governance.

Analytically, the data were thematically coded with attention to irrigation technologies, water-sharing rules, labor organization, ritual practices, and perceptions of environmental and institutional change. This analysis was combined with cross-site comparison within Lang Son Province and a broader borderland perspective contrasting Nung-Tay communities in Vietnam with Zhuang communities in Guangxi.

Research results

The terms “muong” (Figs. 8, 9), “phai” (Figs. 6, 7), “lai”, and “lin” are lexical items belonging to the Nung and Tay languages, directly reflecting indigenous knowledge related to water management in agricultural production. From a historical-linguistic perspective, Nung and Tay in Vietnam belong to the Tai-Kadai language family; within this same family in Vietnam are groups such as the Thai, La Chi, La Ha, Co Lao, and Pu Peo, while in China the Zhuang people are likewise classified within the Tai-Kadai family [9, 10, 11]. This shared linguistic affiliation has produced a common technical vocabulary, clearly manifested in traditional irrigation terminology. In this system, “muong” refers to the canal network that conveys water from the dam to the fields; “phai” denotes a water-blocking dam; “lai” indicates the water embankment within a rice field used to retain and regulate water; and “lin” refers to a conduit made of bamboo or hollow plant stems that allows water to be carried across uneven or elevated terrain. These terms function not merely as labels, but encode technical experience, spatial classification of hydraulic features, and the operational logic of traditional farming systems. In this sense, language serves as a repository and a medium for the transmission of Traditional Ecological Knowledge (TEK), in which practical knowledge is embedded in vocabulary and reproduced through the everyday practices of the community (Fig. 10).

The role of water in mountainous rice cultivation: foundation of livelihood and social structure

In the livelihood systems of the Nung and Tay communities inhabiting valleys and low mountainous areas in northern Vietnam, water functions not only as an essential means of production but also as a structuring element shaping socio-economic life [7]. Ethnographic fieldwork conducted between December 2024 and September 2025 in Na Lenh hamlet (Cao Loc commune), Son Hong hamlet (Ky Lua ward), and Hai Yen hamlet (Ba Son commune), Lang Son Province, demonstrates that water operates simultaneously as an ecological condition and a social resource that organizes production, labor relations, and communal order. From an ecological anthropological perspective, water acquires social existence only when it is collectively organized, shared, and protected through interpersonal relations. Accordingly, the analytical focus shifts from water use to water governance, namely the social mechanisms through which communities regulate access to this shared resource.

Fragmented terrain, short streams, and pronounced seasonality make water a limiting factor in wet-rice cultivation in these mountainous settings. In response, the Nung and Tay have developed a socio-ecological strategy oriented toward regulating water rather than possessing it. Practices of retaining, channeling, and distributing water embody not only adaptive techniques but also ethical principles of fairness, reciprocity, and communal responsibility. In Son Hong hamlet, for example, irrigation water is regulated through a dense network of “pai na” (weirs), some connected to earthen canals and others to concrete-lined channels, reflecting different degrees of infrastructural intervention. As Gómez-Baggethun argues, the capacity to establish rules of sharing and cooperation forms the basis of community self-regulating ecological systems [5]. In Lang Son, collective construction and maintenance of irrigation works constitute a moral economy in which material benefits are inseparable from communal obligations.

From a rural sociological perspective, water functions as a catalyst for the formation of cooperative institutions. Through collective labor, negotiation, and water-sharing practices, villagers secure irrigation while simultaneously reinforcing trust and social cohesion. Implicit conventions operate as unwritten rules maintained through collective practice. These arrangements parallel Ostrom’s principles of common-pool resource governance, whereby sustainability depends on the simultaneous establishment of rights and obligations [21]. Water thus acts as a form of social glue binding households together within mountain villages.

Field observations in early 2025 illustrate how these principles are enacted in practice. In March 2025, prior to the Spring–Summer cropping season, households benefiting from the Pai Cam weir in Na Lenh hamlet convened an evening meeting at the house of Mr. Lut Van Nhi to plan repairs to the irrigation canal. Despite the canal having been concreted, seasonal landslides and structural damage caused water loss, requiring collective labor and financial contributions. According to Mr. Đuong Van Kich (born 1963, Tay, Na Lenh hamlet), holding meetings before each production season is necessary, because it clarifies each household’s responsibility; those who do not contribute labor will not be allowed to take water for irrigation. This rule, repeatedly observed during fieldwork, demonstrates how technical maintenance is inseparable from social regulation.

Nevertheless, water governance is not free of tension. Some households located at the downstream end or cultivating only small plots choose not to participate in meetings or maintenance, opting instead to shift to maize cultivation that requires less water. Similar tensions were reported in Son Hong hamlet during interviews conducted in June 2025. Mr. Hien Van Hoang (born 1980, Nung, head of Son Hong hamlet) explained that “meetings on irrigation repair sometimes last until midnight without reaching consensus, especially when disagreements arise over labor allocation, and these situations can create temporary social friction among households”. These cases indicate that indigenous water governance is not a static tradition but a negotiated process shaped by uneven benefits and changing livelihood strategies.

Despite such disagreements, group discussions across all three hamlets revealed strong consensus on the normative importance of customary water-sharing rules. The principle that water does not belong to any single household was consistently emphasized. Unauthorized diversion of water from another household’s field is publicly condemned, reinforcing communal control over the resource. Technical practices, such as directing water through small edge channels to prevent soil and fertilizer loss, further reflect the integration of ecological knowledge with ethical norms.

Beyond its material role, water also carries symbolic and spiritual significance. Seasonal practices observed in Son Hong hamlet in January 2025 show that irrigation channels are intentionally opened to drain water during the fallow period, allowing fields to dry before the next planting cycle. Villagers explained that this practice is necessary for resting the soil and that channels are rebuilt at the beginning of the new season. In another instance, observed in May 2025 at Chang Tong field (Na Lenh hamlet), bundles of cogon grass were tied at water diversion points to signal that a field had been fertilized, indicating that water should not be allowed to pass through. This long-standing convention among the Nung and Tay functions as a visible ecological marker regulating water flow through social agreement rather than formal enforcement [24].

Such practices illustrate that water in Nung and Tay societies constitutes a dual resource linking production, social regulation, and moral order. Indigenous knowledge of water therefore extends beyond technical irrigation skills, forming a socio-ecological governance model grounded in communal ethics and shared belief, which continues to structure the resilience and sustainability of mountain livelihood systems under contemporary conditions.

The irrigation system and the mechanisms of Management, Maintenance, and water distribution

In the context of steep terrain, short streams, and scattered water sources, the Nung and Tay communities in Na Lenh hamlet (Cao Loc commune), Son Hong hamlet (Ky Lua ward), and Hai Yen hamlet (Ba Son commune) have developed an indigenous irrigation system composed of four interrelated components: “phai - muong - lai - lin”. Ethnographic fieldwork conducted between December 2024 and September 2025 shows that this system functions not merely as a technical arrangement but as a socio-ecological governance mechanism in which local knowledge, collective labor, and moral norms are tightly integrated (Fig. 10).

From the perspective of Traditional Ecological Knowledge (TEK), these components form an adaptive technological complex. “Phai” (weirs) are constructed across small streams to elevate water levels; “muong” (canals) guide water along natural slopes; “lai” (diversion points) regulate distribution between paddy plots; and “lin” (aqueducts made of bamboo, rattan, or wood) carry water across uneven terrain. Each element reflects cumulative learning derived from long-term interaction with local hydrological and geomorphological conditions, consistent with Berkes’s view that indigenous knowledge integrates experience, practice, and cultural values [1].

Field observations in Son Hong hamlet illustrate this adaptive logic in concrete terms. During the 2024–2025 agricultural cycle, irrigation water in the area was regulated through a dense network of “pai na”, including Pai na Tau Hon, Pai na Coc Muong, Pai na Tam Pung, and Pai na Khuoi Cai, each supplying water to paddy clusters ranging from several “sao” to multiple “mau”. Some canals remain earthen, while others have been partially concreted, indicating selective infrastructural upgrading rather than wholesale replacement of traditional systems. Villagers emphasized that canal alignment and dam placement continue to rely on direct observation of slope gradient, flow direction, and seasonal variation, rather than standardized engineering designs. This practice aligns with earlier descriptions by La Cong Y regarding Tay and Nung irrigation in Viet Bac [12], while also confirming the continued relevance of gravity-based, locally adapted construction methods.

What distinguishes this system most clearly is its governance mechanism. At the beginning of each agricultural season, households sharing a water source convene meetings to assign labor for dredging and repairing “phai” and “muong”, and to negotiate water distribution schedules. In Na Lenh hamlet, such a meeting took place in March 2025 in preparation for the Spring–Summer crop, focusing on repairs to the canal linked to Pai Cam weir. Despite the canal having been concreted, landslides and structural cracks required collective maintenance. According to Mr. Đuong Van Kich (born 1963, Tay, Na Lenh hamlet), those who do not participate in repairing the canal are not allowed to take water, a rule widely acknowledged as both fair and necessary. This principle reflects Ostrom’s common-pool resource framework, in which sustainability depends on locally defined rights, obligations, and sanctions [21].

At the same time, governance involves negotiation and, at times, conflict. Interviews conducted in Son Hong hamlet in June 2025 revealed that disagreements frequently arise over labor allocation, particularly among households located at the downstream end of irrigation networks. Mr. Hien Van Hoang (born 1980, Nung, head of Son Hong hamlet) noted that meetings on canal repair could extend late into the night without consensus, occasionally generating tension among participants. These cases demonstrate that the “phai - muong - lai - lin” system operates not as a rigid institution but as a flexible arrangement continuously adjusted through social interaction.

In practice, water distribution relies heavily on trust and collective responsibility. Across all three research sites, households that fail to contribute labor or violate agreed schedules risk losing access to irrigation water. This mechanism, simultaneously disciplinary and ethical, supports Gómez-Baggethun et al.’s argument that TEK enables communities to maintain self-regulating socio-ecological systems [5]. Water knowledge thus functions not only as technical competence but also as a means of regulating social relations within village life.

From a biocultural heritage perspective, irrigation practices also embody the relationship between culture and nature. In Son Hong hamlet, field observations in January 2025 recorded the intentional release of water from the Tau Hon canal during fallow periods to dry fields and rest the soil; the canal is dismantled and later rebuilt at the onset of a new season. Villagers explain this not as pure technique but as a way to balance land and water, letting the soil breathe before the next cycle. At the Lai points, bundles of cogon grass tied into small knots signal fertilization and the direction of water flow; when observed in Chang Tong field (Na Lenh hamlet) in May 2025, these markings acted as shared cues that disciplined water use without formal enforcement. Such symbolic devices, when seen through the lens of ritual ecology, perform the kind of regulatory work that Rappaport identified in ritual: aligning individual action with collective ecological order and stabilizing the human–environment system [24].

The flexibility of this irrigation system underpins its capacity to respond to environmental variability. During dry periods, “phai” are raised or reinforced to retain more water; during heavy rains, “lai” are widened to release excess flow and prevent damage to fields. This elasticity exemplifies adaptive TEK systems, in which sustainability is achieved through self-adjustment rather than rigid control [26].

While earlier studies of irrigation in the Viet Bac region largely emphasized technical aspects, the present research demonstrates that the “phai - muong - lai - lin” system constitutes a comprehensive socio-ecological institution. It encompasses mechanisms of self-organization, collective monitoring, and knowledge reproduction embedded in everyday practice and seasonal routines. The key contribution of this study lies in showing how technology and belief coexist within this system: irrigation infrastructure is not separated from moral norms or ritual meanings but is continuously transformed into a cultural practice that sustains both agricultural production and communal life.

Classification of paddy fields and water use knowledge

The classification of paddy fields among the Nung and Tay is not merely a system of land naming but constitutes an indigenous body of knowledge integrating hydrology, soil properties, and agricultural ecology. Ethnographic data collected in 2024–2025 in Na Lenh hamlet (Cao Loc commune), Son Hong hamlet (Ky Lua ward), and Hai Yen hamlet (Ba Son commune), Lang Son Province, show that the naming and spatial differentiation of cultivation areas function as a vernacular encoding of environmental knowledge, through which experiential farming knowledge is organized into a shared cognitive framework.

The term “na” denotes paddy fields in general, yet it encompasses differentiated categories such as “na tong”, “na leng”, and “na luong”. These categories are defined by geomorphology, elevation, water availability, drainage capacity, and soil conditions. Such differentiation reflects a localized hydro-pedological mapping of the landscape, in which each field type corresponds to specific cultivation strategies and water management practices. This finding aligns with Rambo’s observation that local knowledge of terrain and water operates as a tool for organizing livelihood in upland Southeast Asian agroecosystems [23].

“Na tong” fields are typically located along streams or riverbanks, characterized by relatively flat terrain, fertile alluvial soils, and stable access to surface water. In Na Lenh hamlet, “na tong” fields in areas such as Chang Tong and Na Deng are supplied by large pai nà systems (e.g., Pai Cam), requiring coordinated water allocation among multiple households. Because these fields are highly productive and widely shared, water distribution is governed by stricter conventions, including rotational use and collective monitoring. As Ostrom notes, when a resource is both valuable and shared, communities tend to develop more robust institutions to prevent conflict [19]. In this context, water functions as a social intermediary that regulates both production and inter-household relations.

By contrast, “na leng” fields are situated at higher elevations with limited and unstable water supply, relying primarily on rainfall or long-distance canals. In Son Hong hamlet, several “na leng” plots depend on canals extending from upstream pai nà such as Pai na Khuoi Cai or Pai na Tau Hon, some of which remain earthen. These conditions have fostered adaptive technical solutions, including extended canals and small-scale water-lifting devices, illustrating what Dove describes as community-based indigenous science, where technology emerges from collective effort rather than formal design [3]. Mr. Hai Van Quang (born 1976, Nung, Son Hong hamlet, Ky Lua ward) explained that “fields located at higher elevations always lack water, so households must agree in advance on when to divert water; otherwise, no one can plant on time.” This statement highlights how water scarcity compels cooperation and reinforces communal governance. The “na leng” category further demonstrates a paradoxical relationship between scarcity and innovation. Limited water availability encourages the expansion of inter-household cooperation and refinement of technical knowledge, supporting Gómez-Baggethun et al.’s argument that TEK becomes most visible under conditions of resource scarcity [5]. Here, technical adaptation and social negotiation develop in tandem, reinforcing the resilience of the irrigation system.

In contrast, “na luong” fields are located in low-lying valley bottoms with excessive moisture and poor drainage, where waterlogging rather than scarcity poses the main challenge. In Hai Yen hamlet, farmers emphasized that managing “na luong” fields requires knowing how to let water out rather than bring it in. Drainage trenches and furrows are dug to regulate water levels, an empirical skill transmitted through observation and practice. As Reyes-García et al. note, such knowledge transmission occurs within a social learning environment, where skills are acquired through hands-on engagement rather than formal instruction [25]. Farmers’ ability to sense soil moisture and adjust drainage accordingly constitutes a form of embodied, sensory knowledge rooted in long-term interaction with land and water.

From a cultural ecological perspective, “na luong” reflects a yin–yang logic of water management: excess water must be released, while deficiency requires retention. This principle resonates with broader cosmological notions of balance between Heaven, Earth, and Humans that underpin Nung and Tay environmental ethics.

Taken together, the classification of “na” fields demonstrates that indigenous knowledge is inseparable from environmental context and organized according to an eco-social logic. This system corresponds closely to Fu Guanghua’s model of the Zhuang three-layered water culture, integrating hydraulic technology, social conventions, and belief [4]. By situating “na tong”, “na leng”, and “na luong” within this structure, field classification emerges as a dual system in which natural geomorphology and social order mutually reinforce one another.

The key contribution of this study lies in interpreting field classification not merely as an agronomic taxonomy but as a form of water governance knowledge. The identification of three field types corresponding to three hydrological states constitutes an indigenous cognitive model of the water cycle. Field classification thus operates as water management through language and practice, with each local term encoding a principle of interaction between humans and their ecological environment.

Social institutions and Indigenous knowledge in water management

Alongside irrigation infrastructure, the Nung and Tay communities in Lang Son Province maintain social institutions that regulate rights and responsibilities in water governance. Ethnographic fieldwork conducted between 2024 and 2025 in Na Lenh hamlet (Cao Loc commune), Son Hong hamlet (Ky Lua ward), and Hai Yen hamlet (Ba Son commune) shows that households sharing a common water source organize themselves into self-managed cooperative groups. Within these groups, access to irrigation water is inseparable from obligations of collective labor, so that each household functions simultaneously as a beneficiary and a caretaker of the system.

A core principle underpinning these arrangements is that households who do not contribute labor to the construction and maintenance of dams and canals are not entitled to use irrigation water. This norm operates as a locally recognized social contract, articulated clearly by community members themselves. As Ms. Nang Thi Dong (born 1965, Tay, Na Lenh hamlet) explained: “If a household does not participate in repairing and clearing the dam and canals, they are not allowed to use water from them. Water does not belong to any single family; it belongs to everyone who contributes labor.” Rather than being enforced through formal authority, this rule is maintained through shared understanding, peer pressure, and communal agreement.

Field evidence from Na Lenh hamlet illustrates how this principle is enacted in practice. During preparations for the Spring–Summer crop in March 2025, households benefiting from the Pai Cam weir convened seasonal meetings to allocate repair tasks and reaffirm labor obligations as a prerequisite for water access. As one Tay informant (male, born 1963, Na Lenh hamlet) noted, households that failed to participate in canal repair were excluded from irrigation for that season. Such practices demonstrate that indigenous water institutions rely on social consensus and collective monitoring rather than codified regulations.

From an analytical perspective, these labor-based rules closely resemble Ostrom’s model of common-pool resource governance, in which shared resources are sustained through clearly defined obligations, mutual trust, and community-level sanctions [21]. At the same time, they express a broader ecological ethic emphasizing restraint, equity, and respect for natural order. As Berkes argues, such unwritten conventions form the foundation of community-based governance by balancing individual interests with long-term ecological sustainability [1]. In this sense, indigenous water management among the Nung and Tay operates as a self-regulating socio-ecological system, where knowledge functions as a flexible, low-cost, and non-administrative mechanism for coordinating collective action, consistent with Sharifian’s observations on the dynamics of Traditional Ecological Knowledge [26].

Beliefs related to water

In the cosmology of the Nung and Tay, water is not merely a means of subsistence but a sacred entity symbolizing life, purity, and regeneration. This worldview underpins an indigenous ecological ethic in which natural elements are personified through the Water God, regarded as a guardian of harvests, health, and communal harmony.

Rituals such as New Year Water-God worship, rain-invoking ceremonies, and purification rites during funerals function not only as religious expressions but also as ecological practices mediating human–nature relations. As Rappaport argues, ritual performs an ecological function by restoring social order and environmental balance through collective action [24]. During fieldwork in Son Hong, Na Lenh, and Hai Yen hamlets in early 2025, villagers described the practice of fetching new water at the beginning of the lunar year to offer to ancestors, an act that transforms a material substance into a symbolic medium linking humans, ancestors, and the natural world.

Rain-invoking and thanksgiving ceremonies further reinforce communal cohesion through shared belief, illustrating Maffi’s concept of biocultural heritage, where ecological and cultural values are inseparably embedded in practice [19]. The use of water in funerary rituals similarly reflects a symbolic cycle of birth–death–return to nature, a key element of the mountain peoples’ cosmology. From an ecological anthropological perspective, such beliefs constitute a form of spiritual environmental governance, cultivating reverence, restraint, and moral responsibility toward water resources. This view resonates with recent anthropological arguments that recognize rivers and water bodies as co-participants in ecological relations rather than passive resources [28]. As Berkes notes, belief systems of this kind have historically enabled communities to sustain environmental balance and resilience over long periods [1].

Comparison of folk water knowledge among the Nung, Tay, and Zhuang peoples

A comparative examination of folk water knowledge among the Nung and Tay in northern Vietnam and the Zhuang in Guangxi (China) reveals both a shared transboundary water civilization and diverging contemporary configurations shaped by distinct institutional contexts. In this study, water civilization is understood as a cultural–ecological complex in which irrigation technology, social conventions, and ritual practices jointly structure human–water relations in mountainous valley environments. The Vietnamese case is documented through sustained ethnographic fieldwork conducted between December 2024 and September 2025, which constitutes the core empirical basis of this study. By contrast, the Guangxi comparison draws on a combination of published ethnographic literature and targeted observational insights derived from two short research visits to Daxin County (March 2023) and Ningming County (August 2023), undertaken in the course of the author’s doctoral research in Guangxi. These observations are accordingly treated as contextual and indicative material rather than as part of the primary fieldwork corpus, serving to illuminate cross-border institutional contrasts rather than to provide an exhaustive account of Zhuang water governance.

Across both sides of the Vietnam–China border, traditional irrigation structures reflect a common indigenous knowledge framework grounded in technical experience, collective labor, and moral regulation of water use. Fu Guanghua’s analysis of Zhuang water culture identifies three interrelated layers which closely correspond to the Nung–Tay system of irrigation and Water God worship [4]. From a comparative perspective, these similarities indicate a shared ecological–cultural principle that treats water as a living entity to be respected, used with restraint, and governed collectively.

Ritual practices further reinforce this shared symbolic foundation. Rain-invoking ceremonies, water festivals, New Year water-fetching customs, and Water God worship on both sides of the border articulate water as a symbol of purity, fertility, and renewal. Qiu Zhensheng’s observation that the Zhuang regard water as the soul of all things, endowed with both purifying and life-protecting functions, closely parallels Nung and Tay ritual practices associated with annual agricultural cycles [22]. This continuity suggests that water veneration constitutes a long-standing cultural constant in the borderlands, reproduced through ritual performance and everyday agricultural practice (Figs. 11, 12).

Despite these shared foundations, the contemporary organization of water knowledge has diverged significantly. In Guangxi, available evidence points to a pronounced transformation at both infrastructural and institutional levels. Traditional irrigation structures such as dams and canals have been largely concreted, with construction and major maintenance costs predominantly covered by state funding. In villages such as Hongjiang Town (Ningming County), irrigation systems along the Heba and Xiba rivers operate through a hybrid mechanism combining state investment with locally managed funds, to which households contribute for routine repairs.

Field observations conducted by the author in Ningming and Daxin counties indicate that irrigation infrastructure in these areas has, in most cases, been systematically concreted under state-led programs, with annual maintenance and repairs carried out through the direct involvement of local authorities. Interviews conducted in Hongjiang village further reveal that these maintenance campaigns are regularly organized in spring, mobilizing local Party branches, youth unions, and women’s associations (Figs. 11, 12). In an August 2023 conversation, Luong Haky (b. 1998), a Zhuang villager from Ningming County, noted that such collective repair activities have become a routine part of village governance. On the Vietnamese side, while irrigation structures such as phai (weirs) and muong (canals) remain largely community-managed in daily operation, their construction and upgrading are not entirely autonomous from the state. Local authorities commonly provide construction materials, particularly cement, while villagers contribute labor through collective work arrangements. This hybrid model reflects a form of co-production in which state support and community mobilization intersect, distinguishing Vietnam’s water governance from the more fully institutionalized and administratively embedded systems observed in Guangxi.

Local narratives from Hongjiang further indicate that this transformation is closely linked to shifts in livelihood structure. Residents recall that prior to the early 2000s, when rice cultivation dominated local agriculture, water scarcity and inter-household disputes over irrigation allocation were common. Over the past two decades, however, state-led policies promoting the conversion from rice to sugarcane, motivated both by higher economic returns and the crop’s lower water requirements, have substantially reduced pressure on water resources. Coupled with the concreting of dams and canals, these changes have resulted in a more stable and sufficient water supply for production, thereby diminishing everyday conflicts surrounding water use.

Comparable arrangements are observed in Tingliang Town (Ningming County) and in Longmen and Baoqiu villages (Daxin County), where water use continues to be guided by principles of equity and shared responsibility, yet is increasingly embedded within formal administrative frameworks. Taken together, these cases illustrate a broader process of technicization and institutional incorporation, whereby indigenous water knowledge is reconfigured and absorbed into state-led rural development and water governance programs.

In contrast, among the Nung and Tay in Vietnam, water governance remains largely informal and community-centered. Although some canals have been partially concreted, decisions regarding water distribution, maintenance labor, and sanctions continue to rely on customary norms, collective meetings, and ritual authority. Drawing on the frameworks of Berkes [1] and Ostrom [21], this contrast reflects two distinct modes of institutional adaptation: top-down integration in China and bottom-up communal resilience in Vietnam. While the Chinese trajectory emphasizes modernization and administrative efficiency, the Vietnamese trajectory foregrounds social negotiation, moral obligation, and ritual practice as core elements of water governance.

Viewed comparatively, the water civilization of the Vietnam–China borderlands thus emerges as a dual-polar knowledge system. Both sides originate from a shared foundation of Traditional Ecological Knowledge and ecological ethics, yet they diverge under different political, economic, and policy environments. This divergence does not imply the disappearance of indigenous knowledge in Guangxi, but rather its reconfiguration within state-led governance structures. Conversely, the Vietnamese case demonstrates the persistence of community-based water knowledge in the absence of strong institutional integration. Discussion

Based on the frameworks of Traditional Ecological Knowledge (TEK) and biocultural heritage, the folk knowledge of water among the Nung and Tay should be understood not as a harmonious or closed system, but as a dynamic and sometimes contested assemblage of knowledge, social relations, and belief. Ethnographic evidence from Lang Son shows that while TEK provides normative guidelines for cooperation and reciprocity, its actual operation is shaped by power asymmetries, environmental stress, and changing livelihood strategies. From a biocultural heritage perspective, water-related practices simultaneously embody productive, moral, and symbolic dimensions; however, these dimensions do not always align smoothly in practice. Indigenous water knowledge thus operates in the tension between ideals of communal ethics and the realities of scarcity, inequality, and social negotiation.

First, the relationship between folk knowledge and community governance demonstrates a capacity for ecological self-regulation, as described by Rappaport [24] and Sharifian [26], but this capacity is neither automatic nor conflict-free. Rules governing water sharing and the maintenance of “phai - muong - lin” structures reflect a locally negotiated order rather than a stable equilibrium. Fieldwork conducted in Na Lenh hamlet in August 2025 documented a dispute between two households in Tham Pat field during a period of water scarcity. The upstream household accused the downstream household of draining water excessively, thereby depriving its upper fields of irrigation, while the downstream household argued that its labor contribution to canal maintenance entitled it to water use. The conflict escalated into a breakdown of social relations, with both families temporarily severing contact. This case illustrates that TEK-based rules are vulnerable under conditions of environmental stress and that water governance is mediated by power relations linked to spatial position, rather than solely by shared norms.

Second, water governance among the Nung and Tay reveals forms of structural inequality embedded within ostensibly collective systems. Although households contribute labor to dam construction and canal dredging, access to water remains unevenly distributed. Fields closer to water sources consistently enjoy more reliable irrigation, while downstream plots face recurrent scarcity. In Chang Tong field, several households reported that despite years of participation in canal maintenance, they received insufficient water for rice cultivation; as a result, some shifted from rice to maize, thereby withdrawing both materially and socially from irrigation-related collective labor. Such dynamics indicate that inequality in water access can lead to disengagement from communal institutions, gradually eroding the foundations of collective governance. TEK, in this sense, does not eliminate inequality but may reproduce it along hydrological, spatial, and kinship lines.

Third, viewing water as a cultural–ecological symbol remains analytically valuable, yet this symbolic dimension should not be romanticized as uniformly integrative. Among the Nung and Tay, water embodies fertility and moral order, but ritual authority does not always translate into conflict resolution. Rituals such as Water-God worship and rain-invoking ceremonies articulate an ecological ethic of balance, yet they do not necessarily prevent disputes over allocation or labor obligations. Following Strang’s argument that rivers possess social agency [29], water may be seen as an actor that exposes social fault lines under scarcity. In this view, the sacred dimension of water functions less as a guarantee of harmony than as a moral reference point against which conflicts are interpreted, judged, and morally justified.

Fourth, these empirical tensions carry significant implications for sustainable development policy. While principles of co-management, co-benefit, and co-protection embedded in Nung and Tay water knowledge resonate with Ostrom’s [21] common-pool resource model and with UNESCO’s emphasis on locally grounded sustainability, current state policies often overlook the social and symbolic complexity of such systems. Irrigation modernization programs tend to prioritize technical efficiency while neglecting customary conventions, informal sanctions, and ritual practices that regulate water use. In Vietnam, key institutional barriers hinder the integration of Traditional Ecological Knowledge into water management. Governance remains largely technical and administrative, while customary village conventions regulating water use lack formal recognition. In addition, TEK is often viewed as informal or non-scientific, limiting its inclusion in modernization programs. As a result, infrastructure upgrades may weaken community self-governance by disrupting existing water-sharing hierarchies without providing alternative mechanisms for negotiation and conflict resolution.

Fifth, policy relevance can be strengthened by translating indigenous water knowledge into concrete, context-sensitive interventions. At the provincial level, village water conventions could be formally acknowledged and incorporated into local irrigation planning, rather than replaced by purely technical management schemes. In the Vietnam–China borderlands, comparative insights from Nung–Tay and Zhuang water systems suggest the potential for cross-border cultural–ecological exchange programs focusing on dam-building practices, canal maintenance rituals, and community-based water governance. At the community scale, integrating water-related knowledge into school curricula, heritage education, and small-scale community tourism could sustain intergenerational transmission without museumizing TEK. Such measures align with the Just Commons framework proposed by Luo and He [17], which emphasizes the integration of indigenous ethics, social justice, and environmental governance.

Ultimately, the water knowledge system of the Nung and Tay should be approached neither as an idealized relic nor as a fully self-regulating alternative to modern governance. Instead, it represents a historically grounded, yet fragile, socio-ecological system whose resilience depends on continuous negotiation among knowledge, power, environment, and policy. Recognizing both its strengths and its internal contradictions allows biocultural heritage thinking to move beyond celebration toward critical engagement, offering more realistic pathways for sustainable development in mountainous borderland regions, where water remains both a material necessity and a contested moral resource.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that the folk water knowledge of the Nung and Tay in northern mountainous Vietnam constitutes an integrated socio-ecological system rather than a collection of technical practices. The central contribution lies in showing that the “phai - muong - lai - lin” irrigation system operates as an indigenous governance model, in which experiential knowledge, customary norms, and sacred belief jointly regulate access to water and collective responsibility. By doing so, the study extends the concept of Traditional Ecological Knowledge beyond technical adaptation to include moral regulation and community-based institutional organization.

From a comparative borderland perspective, the study clarifies the concept of water civilization as a shared cultural–ecological complex structured around three inseparable elements: hydraulic technology, social conventions, and ritual practice. Within this shared framework, the Nung and Tay in Vietnam and the Zhuang in Guangxi exhibit divergent contemporary trajectories. In Vietnam, water knowledge continues to function primarily through informal community institutions and ritual authority. In contrast, in Guangxi, similar forms of knowledge have increasingly been incorporated into formal administrative and development frameworks. This divergence illustrates the flexibility of indigenous water knowledge when interacting with different governance regimes.

At the theoretical level, the research contributes to biocultural heritage studies by grounding abstract concepts in ethnographic evidence from a borderland context. It demonstrates that ecological, social, and sacred dimensions of water knowledge operate simultaneously in everyday practice rather than as separate analytical layers. At the practical level, the findings suggest that water knowledge should be recognized as a living knowledge resource, not only as intangible cultural heritage. Such recognition allows for its integration into ecological agriculture, community-based tourism, and environmental education in highland regions, consistent with arguments that community-based water tenure systems provide an equitable and resilient foundation for climate-adaptive governance [31].

More broadly, the analysis of water knowledge in the Vietnam–China borderlands deepens understanding of how Southeast Asian mountain communities respond to environmental uncertainty through locally grounded institutions. The study raises a critical question for future research and policy: how can indigenous water governance systems be sustained and adapted under conditions of market integration, state intervention, and climate change without eroding the social and moral foundations on which they depend?
