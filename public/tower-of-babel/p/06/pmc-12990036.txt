ABSTRACT
KRAS G12C is one of the most prevalent oncogenic mutations in nonsmall cell lung cancer. Herein we describe the discovery and optimization of divarasib (GDC-6036), an orally available, highly potent, and selective covalent KRAS G12C inhibitor. We demonstrate a significant noncovalent binding component of divarasib that contributes to its potency and rapid kinetics. Divarasib has greater potency and kinetics of alkylation compared with other KRAS G12C inhibitors in vitro and shows robust tumor growth inhibition in multiple KRAS G12C-positive cell lines.

Introduction

KRAS is one of the originally discovered oncogenes and is among the most frequently mutated genes in cancer. − Mutant KRAS is a potent oncoprotein that drives hyperactive signaling that promotes cancer growth and survival. Mutations in KRAS, particularly at codon 12, are prevalent in difficult to treat cancers such as lung, pancreatic ductal, and colorectal adenocarcinomas, making it an important therapeutic target for a critical unmet need. KRAS G12C is the most common KRAS mutation in lung adenocarcinoma, and is also found at lower frequency in colorectal adenocarcinoma and other tumor types, representing a significant patient population that could benefit from its therapeutic inhibition. ,

KRAS is a GTPase that essentially operates as a molecular switch that regulates critical signaling pathways in cells. In its quiescent or inactive state, KRAS is bound to nucleotide GDP. KRAS activation is initiated by upstream receptor signaling, which recruits and activates guanine nucleotide exchange factors (GEFs), such as Son of Sevenless (SOS). Upon recruitment to the membrane, SOS interacts with KRAS and promotes exchange of its nucleotide that is otherwise tightly bound, leading to the formation of GTP-bound KRAS due to the excess of GTP over GDP in cells. − In its active GTP-bound state, KRAS binds to critical downstream effectors such as RAF and PI3 kinase, initiating signaling cascades that promote cell growth and survival. KRAS is an inefficient enzyme on its own, requiring a GTPase activating protein (GAP) to promote GTP hydrolysis and convert KRAS back to its inactive GDP-bound state. In somatic cells, KRAS is tightly regulated and is activated only in pulses by cell surface receptors, reflecting a balance between positive regulation by GEFs and negative regulation by GAPs. Most oncogenic mutations of KRAS, including KRAS G12C, work by slowing GAP-mediated hydrolysis, thus disrupting normal down regulation of KRAS signaling and increasing the time that KRAS cycles through its active GTP-bound state. ,

Since its initial discovery as an oncogene over 40 years ago, KRAS has been considered a top target for drug development. However, for many years, it was also considered “undruggable” because its picomolar affinity for nucleotide combined with high GTP concentrations in cells made it difficult to design inhibitors that target its nucleotide pocket. , Furthermore, the development of allosteric inhibitors of KRAS had been challenging due to a paucity of targetable pockets, with the few identified only binding in shallow pockets with modest potency. − In 2013, Ostrem et al. discovered tool molecules that covalently reacted with KRAS G12C and bound in a proximal pocket that they called the switch-II pocket, due to its proximity to the switch-II helix. The switch-II helix is a region of KRAS long known to be important for its conformational sensitivity to KRAS nucleotide state and its importance for binding to SOS and other GEFs. , Rather than blocking signaling of active KRAS G12C, switch-II inhibitors work by binding to the inactive GDP-bound state of KRAS and promoting conformational changes in the switch-II helix that prevent its reactivation by SOS. While mutant KRAS oncoproteins, such as KRAS G12C, were generally thought to be trapped in their GTP-bound states due to a lack of GAP-mediated hydrolysis, it is now clear that KRAS G12C retains some intrinsic ability to slowly hydrolyze GTP in the absence of a GAP in vitro. Furthermore, recent studies suggest that a member of the regulator of G protein signaling family of proteins (RGS3), may also play a role in promoting GTP hydrolysis of KRAS G12C in cells. Thus, switch-II inhibitors can be effective inhibitors of KRAS G12C signaling in cells by kinetically trapping the oncoprotein as it transitions through its inactive GDP-bound state. ,

Several groups have successfully developed covalent KRAS G12C inhibitors targeting the switch-II pocket − and two, sotorasib − and adagrasib, , have been granted accelerated approval by the U.S. FDA for the treatment of adult patients with KRAS G12C-mutated locally advanced or metastatic nonsmall cell lung cancer (NSCLC) who have received at least one prior systemic therapy. While the availability of these therapies mark key milestones for the specific therapeutic inhibition of KRAS, clinical benefit with these therapies as single agents remains limited, , and acquired resistance has also been observed in clinical and nonclinical studies. − These observations leave open the possibility that more potent KRAS G12C inhibitors could have better efficacy in the clinic by achieving faster, stronger or more sustained inhibition or by being better suited for use in combination with other drugs, which ultimately may be required for better clinical outcomes. ,− Here we present the design and discovery of divarasib (GDC-6036), a next-generation inhibitor of KRAS G12C that is significantly more potent in vitro than the two clinically approved KRAS G12C inhibitors.

Results and Discussion

The strategy at the outset of our KRAS G12C inhibitor project was to optimize potency by enhancing interactions with the protein in the deepest region of the switch-II pocket, which we call the “back-pocket”, distal from Cys12 between the α2 (or switch-II) helix and the α3 helix. Previous KRAS G12C inhibitor studies suggested that the switch-II helix could potentially rotate to accommodate aromatic groups in the back-pocket and that Asp 69 was positioned to serve as a hydrogen bond acceptor. We set out to optimize hydrophobic interactions in the back-pocket while maintaining hydrogen bonding with Asp 69. Following an evaluation of a range of aromatic and partially saturated cores, we selected a quinazoline scaffold as the most promising starting point that best filled the switch-II pocket, while maintaining synthetic accessibility for rapid SAR development. Importantly, this core facilitated building toward Cys12 from the C4-position via a piperazine spacer connected to an acrylamide warhead, while containing a suitable vector to explore the back-pocket region proximal to Asp 69.

In an attempt to fill the lipophilic cavity of the back-pocket region, an isoquinoline moiety was attached at quinazoline-C7 1 (Table ). Incorporation of an amino group at isoquinoline-C3 was designed to simultaneously form hydrogen bonds with Asp69 and Glu63. Aminoisoquinoline 1 inhibited KRAS G12C with an IC50 of 41 nM in an assay that monitored SOS1-catalyzed nucleotide exchange, and had a potency of 590 nM in a cellular target engagement assay that monitored KRAS G12C alkylation in HCC1171 cells (Table ). Isoquinoline 1 also was shown to have antiproliferative activity in NCI-H358 cells, a KRAS G12C-dependent cell line, with an IC50 value of 480 nM (). The in vitro potency of this molecule compared favorably to indazole 2, a molecule that was used as a tool compound in previous studies, , and was an early indicator that there is space to accommodate large hydrophobic groups in the back-pocket.

To reduce aromatic ring count, the aminoisoquinoline was replaced with an aminopyridine featuring a methyl group, which was designed to fill the lipophilic cavity of this region while enforcing a desired dihedral angle about the biaryl bond. Aminopyridine 3 lost nearly 10-fold potency in the biochemical and cellular target engagement assays and was accompanied by a decrease in lipophilicity and LLE. , In order to more fully occupy the volume available in this pocket, the methyl group of pyridine 3 was expanded to trifluoromethylpyridine 4, which enhanced biochemical potency by nearly 20-fold and achieved a 13-fold improvement in cellular potency compared to methylpyridine 3. The increased potency of trifluoromethylpyridine 4 led to an improvement in LLE over both isoquinoline 1 and methylpyridine 3. Tetrasubstituted pyridine 5, which features an additional methyl group, was designed to further fill the accessible volume in the back-pocket region. Addition of the methyl group enhanced potency by 9-fold and 6-fold in the biochemical and cellular assays, respectively, and was accompanied by an additional improvement in the LLE.

The co-crystal structure of aminopyridine 4 (Figure a, , and ) confirmed that the amino group forms hydrogen bonds with the carboxylate of Asp69 and the backbone carbonyl of Glu63. Meanwhile, the pyridine nitrogen forms a water-mediated interaction with Arg68 and the main chain of Glu63. Additional hydrogen bonds and favorable van der Waals and π-stacking interactions are made by the quinazoline core and acrylamide of 4, similar to interactions that have been observed in crystal structures of several KRAS G12C inhibitors, ,,, including a previously published structure of indazole 2, where the interaction with Asp69 was made by an indazole N–H. The trifluoromethyl group of pyridine 4 is buried in the back-pocket, making van der Waals interactions with Gln99, His95, Ile100, Met72, Val9, Phe78, and Tyr96.

Having optimized the back-pocket moiety, we made efforts to identify further modifications that could enhance potency (Table ). Addition of a fluorine at quinazoline-C8 (i.e., R2) improved potency 5–7-fold (e.g., 2 to 6 and 4 to 7a), potentially due to restricting the torsion angle of the R1 substituent through introduction of axial chirality and/or improving van der Waals contacts in the back-pocket. Torsional control of the piperazine spacer through the introduction of a methyl group at the R3 position led to a modest increase in potency (e.g., 4 to 8). As expected, the inhibitor binding also shows an atropisomeric bias, favoring the orientation that maintains the amine hydrogen bond with Asp69 and the trifluoromethyl moiety projecting into the hydrophobic back-pocket (e.g., 7a vs 7b and 9a vs 9b). Incorporating all these changes in aminopyridine 9a led to subnanomolar potency in the biochemical assay and single-digit nanomolar potency in the cellular assays.

Further improvements in the potency of aminopyridine 9a were obtained by introducing substitution at quinazoline-C2 (i.e., R4). Contemporaneous internal work on a parallel series had identified prolinol substitution in this position to be particularly beneficial, and this finding has since been reported by others in the literature. , Gratifyingly, addition of prolinol functionality in this position, as in compound 10, led to substantial potency improvements over compound 9a and was selected as our lead candidate GDC-6036, which was subsequently named divarasib. The potency of 10 was beyond the limit of detection under our standard conditions for the SOS exchange assay. However, we were able to determine a Kd of 2.4 pM by fitting inhibition curves as a function of enzyme concentration to the Morrisson binding equation. Thus, the biochemical potency of 10 is nearly an 80-fold improvement over compound 9a.

The crystal structure of 10 (Figure b) compared to aminopyridine 4 suggests that both enthalpic and entropic components in multiple regions of the molecule might contribute to its improved potency. The addition of a methyl substituent to the aminopyridine shifts the rotamer of the adjacent trifluoromethyl group on the ring, and the rotational degrees of freedom of the pyridine are reduced by the introduction of fluorine at quinazoline-C8 (i.e., R2), which creates axial chirality. These changes optimize the fit of the substituted pyridine into the back-pocket, increasing van der Waals contacts, including the formation of an interface with Val103 via the added methyl substituent. Comparison of the molecular surfaces of 4 and 10 with the molecular surfaces of KRAS G12C in the structures of the complexes shows that the tetrasubstituted pyridine of 10 better fills and further expands the back-pocket region and that more extensive contacts are made between the protein and inhibitor (Figure c,d). The combination of these changes to the inhibitor structure results in its displacement within the binding pocket by ∼1 Å, approximately along a vector toward the switch-II helix. The introduction of methyl substitution on the piperazine shifts the twisted conformation of the ring seen in the structure of 4 to the chair conformation observed for 10 (see also ). This change also shifts the trajectory of piperazine and the covalent attachment to Cys12. The prolinol of 10 forms a salt bridge with Glu62, , which appears to in turn shift the position of the switch-II loop between Glu62 and Tyr64 by ∼1 Å and the adjacent helix by as much as 2.5 Å. During the preparation of this manuscript, a cocrystal structure of 10 in complex with KRAS G12C was reported that is consistent with the structure described herein.

As expected, based on its biochemical potency, 10 was the most potent compound tested in the cellular alkylation assay with an IC50 of 2.2 nM (Table ). However, while 10 improved by 79-fold over 9a in the SOS exchange assay, it was only 3-fold more potent in the alkylation assay. While potencies determined in the cellular alkylation and SOS exchange assays were highly correlated with each other across the entire set of molecules that were made, the two most potent compounds in the biochemical assay, including 10, had a significantly larger shift in potency between its biochemical and cellular potencies (Figure a). In contrast, a larger cell shift for these most potent compounds is not observed in the NCI-H358 proliferation assay (), where 10 had an IC50 of 0.21 nM, a 46-fold improvement over compound 9a, which is much closer to the potency improvement we predicted based on its potency in the biochemical assay.

The high potency of 10 in the proliferation assay suggests that it is unlikely that the large shift between the biochemical and cellular alkylation assays was caused by poor cell permeability. We speculated either that the most potent compounds were achieving better efficacy in proliferation with less target engagement or that there was an issue with the sensitivity of the cellular alkylation assay. To test the latter possibility, we adjusted the conditions of the alkylation assay to be more similar to those of the proliferation assay. In our proliferation assay, cells were grown as 3D cultures based on previous observations of higher KRAS dependency relative to cells attached to the plate as a monolayer (or 2D culture). , In contrast, our alkylation assay was run under 2D conditions. To see if this difference could account for the loss of potency in our alkylation assay, a subset of our molecules was tested for alkylation under 3D culture conditions as shown in the right-most column in Table and Table . While there was little difference between the 2D and 3D alkylation assays for our less potent compounds, the potency of 10 improved roughly 5-fold to 0.42 nM in this assay format (Table ), a result that is more reflective of its biochemical and proliferation potencies and likely to be a more accurate reflection of its potential for target engagement in cells than the 2D alkylation assay.

While the SOS exchange IC50 assay was useful in predicting cellular activity, it is a time-dependent parameter that on its own provides no information about the kinetics of the covalent reaction. Furthermore, since the IC50 value in this assay is dependent on the time the compound is preincubated with KRAS G12C prior to the initiation of SOS exchange, it makes it difficult to compare potencies in our studies with other studies that have also examined the potency of our molecules. In order to have a time-independent measure of alkylation kinetics, we developed an assay that monitors the kinetic displacement of a fluorescently labeled peptide known to have overlapping interaction in the switch-II pocket. , By fitting the displacement curves we are able to determine a k inact/KI (Figure b,c), which can be thought of as the catalytic efficiency of the covalent reaction, analogous to k cat/Km in enzyme kinetics. − The k inact/KI for all key molecules shown in Table and Table was strongly correlated with the potencies determined in the SOS exchange assay (Figure d), suggesting that this assay was accurately reflecting the potency of our molecules and that improvements in biochemical potency were driven by improvements in covalent catalytic efficiency. The k inact/KI results that we present here for 10, based on the peptide displacement assay, are similar values determined elsewhere for this molecule using different techniques. ,

Covalent inhibition is a two-step kinetic process, starting with the initial concentration-dependent formation of a noncovalent encounter complex (KI) and the subsequent concentration-independent rate of covalent bond formation (k inact) as shown in the model in Figure b. The KI and k inact values can be independently influenced by inhibitor properties, with KI reflecting the strength of the initial noncovalent binding to the switch-II pocket and k inact reflecting the reactivity of the acrylamide in the context of the geometry and local environment of the initial encounter complex. As the optimization of divarasib (10) involved enhancing hydrophobic interactions, we hypothesized that the significant improvements achieved in k inact/KI could be driven by improvements in the noncovalent KI. To understand whether our compounds could bind noncovalently to the switch-II pocket, acetamides 11–13 were made in which the covalently reactive acrylamides of key compounds 2, 5, and 10 were replaced with nonreactive acetamides. Using surface plasmon resonance (SPR), we measured the affinity of those compounds for KRAS G12C. While the Kd of acetamide 11, derived from 2, was beyond the detection limit of the assay (>500 μM), we observed a Kd of 84 μM for acetamide 12 derived from the pyridine analog 5 (Table ), the first molecule made with the same fully elaborated pyridine as in 10, although without purification of the active atropisomer. Although we could only put a lower limit of 6-fold improvement in Kd from compounds 11 to 12, this observation confirms that back-pocket modifications enhance noncovalent interactions with KRAS G12C, and tracks well with the 17-fold improvement in k inact/KI observed between 2 and 5. The KRAS G12C affinity of acetamide 13, derived from 10, was further improved by 130-fold over acetamide 12 to 0.63 μM, which is similar to the 290-fold improvement in the k inact/KI of their parent molecules 5 and 10.

The KRAS G12C Ki for compounds 11–13 will not be identical to the Kd of their acetamide equivalents since the alteration of the warheads could also contribute to noncovalent binding. Nevertheless, the fact that the affinity improvements that we observe in KRAS G12C Kd for our three acetamides molecules (11–13) are within 3-fold of the improvements in k inact/KI for their respective acrylamide parent molecules, 2, compound 5 and 10, strongly suggest that improvements in noncovalent affinity contributed significantly to the improvements in potency that were achieved in the development of 10. Another way to estimate the Ki for a covalent compound would be to measure the noncovalent affinity of the compound for the wild type KRAS in which there is no cysteine at position 12 for the compound to react with. Although we did not do this experiment in this study, another group recently observed that 10, referred to as "GDC6036" in thier paper, has a 3 μM Kd for WT KRAS. Although this value is higher than the Kd we measure for the acetamide 13 against KRAS G12C, we note that in the same study, the authors also measure 1,000 μM Kd for ARS-853, and earlier quinazoline based G12C inhibitor. We believe that this observation of dramatically improved noncovalent potency of 10 over earlier quinazoline based inhibitors is broadly consistent with our own observations in this study.

If we assume that the acetamide Kds are equivalent to the KIs, we can estimate a roughly 2-fold improvement in k inact for 10 (0.56 s–1) over 5 (0.26 s–1). This modest improvement in k inact was not driven by an increase in the reactivity of the acrylamide for cysteine, since this parameter does not alter by the same orders of magnitude as the kinact/KI and is poorly (and inversely) correlated with the potency of our molecules (Figure e and ), as has been observed in other studies of KRAS G12C inhibitors. A detailed kinetic study of acrylamide-based KRAS G12C inhibitors by Hansen et al. demonstrated that the acrylamide reactivity was enhanced dramatically when inhibitors are bound to KRAS G12C, due in part to the interaction of the acrylamide carbonyl oxygen with Lys 16, which catalyzes the covalent reaction with Cys12 by helping to position the acrylamide as well as increasing its electrophilicity. As expected, we see evidence of hydrogen bonding between the acrylamide carbonyl oxygen and Lys16 in our crystal structures of both compounds 4 and 10 (Figure ). As we noted earlier, the methylation of the piperazine ring in 10 alters the conformation of the piperazine ring as compared to 4 and thus the trajectory of the acrylamide toward Cys12. As the piperazine ring of 5 is also not methylated, it is possible that differences in the trajectory of the 10 acrylamide toward Cys12 contribute to the modest improvement we estimate in its k inact. It is notable that our estimated k inact for 10 is only 11-fold higher than the k inact determined for ARS-853 (0.05 s–1), a molecule that is more than 2600-fold less potent than 10 in our k inact/KI assay. So, while there may be some gains to further optimization of the acrylamide geometry, our data suggest that the substantial potency improvements that were achieved with 10 were obtained primarily by optimizing noncovalent binding to the switch-II pocket.

While our data and analysis strongly suggest that improvements in noncovalent KI, rather than k inact, were the key drivers for the potency improvements that were achieved with 10, covalency remains a critical determinant of the overall potency and selectivity of 10 for KRAS G12C. The contribution of covalency to potency is clearly demonstrated by 600,000-fold potency gain of the acrylamide 10 compared to its acetamide analog 13 in the SOS exchange assay (Tables ,). This multiple order of magnitude shift between noncovalent and covalent potency is likely a shared feature of covalent switch-II pocket inhibitors, reflecting the unique ability of the switch-II pocket to leverage relatively weak noncovalent interaction to catalyze covalent bond formation of acrylamide warheads to Cys12 in KRAS G12C. Nevertheless, our results suggest that substantial potency differences between noncovalent and covalent inhibition can be maintained, even as significant improvements are made in the noncovalent component of binding.

With our k inact/KI measurements providing time-independent measurement of potency, a literature comparison suggested that 10 was more potent in vitro than the two clinically approved KRAS G12C inhibitors. − To confirm this, 10 was directly compared with the approved inhibitors sotorasib and adagrasib in our panel of assays (see Table and Figure ). In our k inact/KI assay, 10 was 12-fold more potent than adagrasib and more than 100-fold more potent than sotorasib. The k inact/KI values measured in these assays were quite similar to the values published in the literature using other approaches, − ,, underscoring the value of this parameter for assessing relative in vitro potencies of KRAS G12C inhibitors across different laboratories.

Potencies were also compared in cells by measuring compound-dependent alkylation in a 3D culture (Table and Figure a). The potency shifts between 10 and adagrasib were similar to the shifts in the biochemical assays. However, sotorasib was 20-fold less potent than 10 in the alkylation assay compared to 100-fold in our biochemical assays, suggesting it has a lower potency shift in cells. As expected, based on the IC50 measurements, a representative Western blot of the 3D alkylation assay shown in Figure a demonstrates superior potency for 10 relative to the other tested inhibitors. For example, 10 shows significantly more alkylation of KRAS at compound concentrations of 3 nM or less than those of the other inhibitors (Figure a).

Determination of cellular IC50 values was performed at a fixed time point of 18 h, which provides no information on the kinetic nature of covalent bond formation in cells. As switch-II KRAS G12C inhibitors only react with KRAS G12C in its GDP state, the kinetics of alkylation in cells is limited by the cellular rate of conversion from GTP-bound to GDP-bound KRAS. , Hence, alkylation rates in the cell may differ from those observed in our biochemical assay kinetics of alkylation in the cellular assay, where GTP hydrolysis is not a factor since KRAS is preloaded in the GDP state. Furthermore, since tumor cells growing in vivo are not exposed to constant concentrations of compounds, the kinetics of alkylation may be an even more critical differentiator of compound efficacy.

To address the kinetics of cellular target engagement, alkylation rates were determined as a function of compound concentration in our Western blot assay (see for more detail). As in previous studies, the alkylation rate was plotted relative to compound concentration to generate a Michaelis Menton-like curve to determine EC50 (the concentration where the half maximal rate of alkylation is achieved) and the k max (the maximum rate of alkylation achievable in cells) (see Table ). The ratio between these two parameters, k max/EC50, represents the catalytic efficiency of alkylation of KRAS G12C in cells, analogous to k inact/KI in our biochemical assays. Consistent with other in vitro studies, the k max/EC50 for 10 was 12 and 15-fold higher than sotorasib and adagrasib, respectively. The value for k max varied by less than 2-fold among the molecules, which was expected since this parameter likely reflects the rate limiting step of conversion from KRAS-GTP to KRAS-GDP in cells. − Compound 10 was also significantly more potent in terms of EC50 than the two clinical comparators. The concentration dependence of alkylation rates shown in Figure b clearly reflects the ability of 10 to achieve much higher alkylation rates at lower concentrations than adagrasib or sotorasib, which are similar in terms of their cellular potency and kinetics in this assay.

Finally, we compared these compounds against a panel of KRAS G12C cell lines in a 7 day in vitro viability assay (Table , Figure c). While potencies against individual cell lines varied, 10 is more potent across the panel of KRAS G12C-driven cell lines than the other compounds tested (Figure c). The geometric mean of the IC50 across the panel of cell lines of 10 is 0.56 nM, which is 6-fold more potent than adagrasib and 12-fold more potent than sotorasib. As shown by others, the SW1573 line is generally insensitive to KRAS G12C inhibition, possibly partially due to a co-occurring PIK3CA mutation and thus was excluded from the analysis. 10 is 9200-fold selective against PC-9 WT KRAS cell line used in the panel and was more than 10-fold more selective as compared to adagrasib. For sotarasib, maximum viability was not achieved against PC-9 at the top concentration used (10 μM) so we can only say that its selectivity over PC-9 cells is greater than 1400-fold.

The lack of sensitivity of non-KRAS G12C driven cell lines left us with an incomplete picture of cellular selectivity. To further assess the cellular specificity of 10, a cysteine proteomics experiment was performed in the HCC1171 cell line (a KRAS G12C heterozygous cell line), to identify peptides that lose reactivity to an iodoacetamide probe after treatment with 10. These studies demonstrated that KRAS G12C was the only cysteine site whose reactivity was significantly blocked under the conditions of our experiment, suggesting that 10 is highly selective for KRAS G12C over the more than 11,000 other quantified reactive cysteines detected in the experiment (Figure ).

HCC-1171 cells were treated with 10 nM 10 for 4 h in a proteomics experiment monitoring loss of cysteine containing peptides. KRAS G12C was the only cysteine showing significant increased reactivity with the compound among the 11,042 quantified cysteine sites.

The physicochemical and pharmacokinetic properties of compound 10 are shown in Table . 10 is a highly soluble molecule with moderate permeability. It has a half-life of 55 min in a cysteine reactivity assay. In mice and cynomolgus monkeys, 10 has a low to moderate clearance (CL) relative to hepatic blood flow and a low to moderate volume of distribution (Vss) relative to total body water (1.7- to 2.8-fold total body water). Properties of 10

Having established its oral bioavailability, 10 was assessed in two human KRAS G12C mutant xenograft efficacy models in immune-compromised mice, including a highly responsive NCI-H358 model and a less responsive NCI-H2122 model (Figure ). Treatment with 10 led to regressions at doses as low as 5 mg/kg in the NCI-H358 KRAS G12C model, a model with known high-dependence on KRAS G12C. In the NCI-H2122 model, 10 required a higher dose, 100 mg/kg, to drive tumor-stasis, consistent with the lower sensitivity of this model. In spite of the difference in maximal response in these lines, when dose–response data was normalized to the maximal response in each line, the ED50 and ED90 were only moderately shifted (2.0 and 13 mg/kg for NCI-H358 and 6.3 and 21 mg/kg for NCI-H2122, respectively), suggesting that 10 has the potential to be effective against a range of KRAS G12C driven cancers at similar dose ranges.

Antitumor efficacy in two KRAS G12C-driven NSCLC tumor models: (a) NCI-H358 and (b) NCI-H2122. Although doses were not identical between the two experiments, similar doses are colored the same. All dosing was for PO and QD. All treatments in these studies were well tolerated.

Detailed pharmacokinetics/pharmacodynamics (PK/PD) studies were performed to connect antitumor activity with target engagement and RAS-MAPK signaling activity (Figure ). Mice were treated with 10 at the indicated dose levels followed by collection of plasma and tumors at 8 h postdose. Tumor samples were processed for mRNA by quantitative RT-PCR to assess a panel of MAPK target genes, including key transcripts DUSP6 and SPRY4 known to be highly responsive to downstream MAPK target genes. Tumor protein was also used to assess KRAS G12C alkylation by 10. Levels of compound 10 were assessed in plasma samples and were corrected for free drug concentrations. Both models demonstrated a strong correlation between DUSP6 and SPRY4 mRNA levels and KRAS G12C alkylation, establishing a dose-dependent relationship between 10 exposure, target engagement, and pathway suppression. The PK was similar between the two tumor models with the alkylation ED50, 6.6 mg/kg in NCI-H358 and 9.7 mg/kg in NCI-H2122, within 2-fold of each other, indicating similar levels of target engagement despite the discrepancy in underlying sensitivity. However, it is clear from a comparison of PK/PD data from the two models that there was deeper suppression of DUSP6 and SPRY4 at lower doses in the NCI-H358 model relative to NCI-H2122. For example, a 10 mg/kg dose in NCI-H358 achieves ∼95% suppression of DUSP6 and SPRY4 with around 75% alkylated KRAS G12C and 0.2 nM free drug (Figure a), whereas a 15 mg/kg dose in NCI-H2122 achieves only less than 75% suppression of DUSP6 and SPRY4 with similar levels of free drug and alkylated KRAS G12C (Figure b). This observation, combined with the 3-fold shift in ED50 observed in the efficacy experiment, suggests a difference in the inherent sensitivity of the two tumor models to inhibition of KRAS G12C. This type of biological variation is likely present in other tumor models as well as in patients. However, the fact that the ED50 shift is only 3-fold suggests that 10 could have a meaningful impact on KRAS G12C signaling and tumor growth in both sensitive and less sensitive KRAS G12C driven cancers. Further DMPK studies, including those to predict human PK parameters and anticipated efficacious human dose will be detailed in a future publication.

Conclusion

Herein we describe the discovery of the next-generation KRAS G12C inhibitor divarasib (10), and demonstrate that it has higher potency and more rapid alkylation kinetics than the current clinical inhibitors, sotarasib and adagrasib, both in biochemical and in cellular assays. We present evidence that the potency optimization was driven by a substantial improvement in KI, as demonstrated by gains in the affinity of acetamide derivatives of our top molecules to KRAS G12C. Largely, these gains in affinity and potency were derived from optimization of the back-pocket pyridine and through introduction of prolinol functionality at the C2 position of the quinazoline core. Our analysis suggests that the high affinity of divarasib for KRAS G12C and optimal positioning of its acrylamide in relation to Cys12 allows for efficient and rapid covalent bond formation within the switch-II pocket and that covalent bond formation is still a significant driver of potency.

Importantly, we show that divarasib can deliver robust responses in two KRAS G12C in vivo models that range in sensitivity. Our detailed PK/PD study suggests that differences in sensitivity among various cell lines are likely due to differences in the underlying innate sensitivity of each tumor line to KRAS G12C inhibition. Contributing factors to these observed differences may include varying rates of KRAS synthesis, different levels of upstream signaling activity that can shift KRAS from the sensitive GDP- to the insensitive GTP-state, and secondary driver mutations both within and outside the MAPK pathway that may contribute to intrinsic insensitivity to KRAS G12C inhibition. We show that divarasib has significantly faster cellular kinetics compared to the two clinically approved KRAS G12C inhibitors.

In a phase I clinical trial, divarasib has shown a manageable safety profile and promising antitumor activity in previously treated, metastatic solid tumors harboring the KRAS G12C mutation, including as a single agent (confirmed response rate of 53% in NSCLC and 29% in CRC) and in combination with the anti-EGFR therapy cetuximab (confirmed response rate of 62% in CRC). Divarasib is currently being investigated in multiple clinical studies as a single-agent or in combination with other anticancer therapies in patients with NSCLC, CRC, and other solid tumors harboring a KRAS G12C mutation (NCT04929223; NCT04589845; NCT03178552; NCT05789082; NCT04302025; NCT04449874; NCT06497556; NCT06793215).

Materials and Methods

Synthesis

The synthetic route used to access all quinazoline-containing compounds followed a sequence similar to that of divarasib (10). The synthesis of quinazolinone 13 has been previously disclosed, and it underwent smooth deoxychlorination and subsequent nucleophilic aromatic substitution with Boc-protected (S)-3-methylpiperazine to afford 2-chloroquinazoline 14 (Scheme ). Chloride displacement to 2-fluoroquinazoline 15 was affected with potassium fluoride at elevated temperature and served to mitigate undesired hydrolysis at quinazoline-C2 in downstream reactions. Aminopyridine functionality was introduced through Suzuki cross-coupling between the corresponding pyridyl boronic acid and bromide 15, yielding the biaryl linkage found within 16. Aminopyridine 16 was further functionalized via a two-step sequence involving nonstereoselective electrophilic halogenation to iodide 17 followed by a copper-mediated trifluoromethylation to furnish the elaborated aminopyridine 18. This mixture of diastereomeric atropisomers was carried forward through the synthesis until the final step. Reaction of 2-fluoroquinazoline 18 with prolinol under basic conditions gave C2-functionalized quinazoline 19, which, after global deprotection to piperazine 20, was acryloylated to yield divarasib as a mixture of diastereomers. Separation on the chiral phase gave the desired isomer 10 in isomerically pure form along with its atropisomer 21. This initial discovery route for the synthesis of parent divarasib (10) was optimized for process-scale production and has been recently disclosed.

Biochemical SOS Exchange Assay

HTRF Assay. Biotinylated-G12C KRas containing residues 2–188 of kRas with an N-terminal six histidine, tev cleavage, and avi-tag and the catalytic domain of SOS1 (residues 564–1049) with an N-terminal six histidine and tev cleavage sequence were expressed and purified from E. coli cells. 75 pM Kras G12C was incubated with 75 pM Tb-streptavidin (CisBIO) and 15 nM GDP for 1 h, added into 384-well plates containing varied concentrations of test compounds or DMSO, and then incubated for 18 h. All KRAS p.G12C and non-G12C cell lines used in the in vitro assay were sourced from the Genentech internal cell bank repository. Alkylation Electromobility Shift Assays

HCC1171 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 20,000 cells/well in 70 μL of RPMI1640 media (Invitrogen) + 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (Excell). Compound was added to the cells then the next day at varied concentrations with a final DMSO of 0.3%. After treatment of the cells for 18 h, cells were lysed by addition of 30 μL of RIPA buffer (Sigma) with 0.25% SDS (Sigma). Plates were shaken for 30 min at 500 rpm at 4 °C, mixed three times with a multichannel pipet, and spun at 2,000 rpm for 15 min. Lysate from each well (20 μL) was added to 10 μL of 4× loading buffer (Invitrogen) and 3 μL of 10x reducing agent (Invitrogen) and then boiled for 10 min at 95 °C. Fifteen μL of reduced/boiled lysate was loaded on a 4–20%, 15-well TRIS-Glycine gel and run for 165 min and 110 V and then transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane using iBlot Gell Tranfer Stacks (Invitrogen). The membrane was blocked for 1 h with blocking buffer at room temperature (Rockland), and then incubated at 4 °C overnight with KRAS antibody (Proteintech 12063-1-AP) diluted 1:1,000 in blocking buffer. The next day, after several washes in TBST (Sigma), the membrane was stained with fluorescent antirabbit antibody (LI-COR 926-32211) for 1 h at room temperature in a black box. Following washes in TBS-T and TBS (Sigma), the membrane is imaged using a LI-COR Odyssey at medium resolution. Quantitate fluorescence intensity (FI) of the upper (alkylated) KRAS band versus the lower (unmodified) KRAS band is determined by drawing a rectangle encompassing the individual bands. KRAS migrates around 21K, and the alkylated KRAS has a slightly higher molecular weight that is visible by an electrophoretic shift. The percentage of modification for each concentration of compounds is determined by the ratio of the intensity of the shifted band to the sum of both bands. IC50’s are determined by fitting the plot of the percent modification versus the compound concentration using Excel Fit (Microsoft).

The fluorescein-labeled KR-Pep2d peptide (Ac-RRRRCPLYISYDPVCRRRR-NH2, FL-peptide) was synthesized at Genentech. Binding assays were carried out in a 15 μL volume in a Proxiplate (PerkinElmer), and fluorescence polarization was measured using an Envision plate reader (PerkinElmer). Inhibitor titrations were dispensed to the assay plate using a D300 digital dispenser (Tecan) in a 300 nL total volume of inhibitor/DMSO (2% DMSO in the final assay volume). KRAS G12C was diluted to 50 nM in assay buffer (20 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 50 mM NaCl, 10 mM MgCl2, and 0.01% Tween-20) containing 2 nM FL-peptide and allowed to equilibrate for 30 min at room temperature. Following the equilibration period, the mixture of KRAS G12C and FL-peptide was added to the assay plate containing inhibitor/DMSO. Fluorescence polarization was monitored over an appropriate time course to capture the decrease in the intensity of the mP signal. Progress curve data was analyzed using DynaFit software (Biokin, Ltd.) to determine inhibitor k inact/KI with FL-peptide binding kinetics held constant at experimentally determined values of k on = 3.95 × 105 M–1 s–1 and k off = 0.0149 s–1. Protein Expression, Purification, and Crystallization

The N-terminal His-tagged KRAS G12C (1–169) constructs with and without cysteine mutations (C51S, C80L, C118S) were cloned into the pET-52b vector and transformed into BL21­(DE3) cells. Expression was induced by IPTG at 16 °C overnight, and cells were lysed by a microfluidizer in a buffer containing 50 mM Tris pH 8.0, 300 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 5% Glycerol, 1 mM TCEP, 1 mM PMSF, and EDTA-free protease inhibitors. The clarified lysate was incubated with NiNTA beads (Qiagen) and then washed with 10 mM and 20 mM imidazole, respectively, in the lysis buffer. Protein was eluted with 250 mM imidazole and then concentrated prior to running over a size-exclusion S75 16/60 (Cytiva) column in 20 mM Tris pH 8.0, 100 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 10 mM GDP, and 0.5 mM TCEP. Fractions containing KRAS were pooled and concentrated to 1 mg/mL before being flash frozen in liquid nitrogen. The protein was loaded with GDP and covalently modified by the inhibitors during an overnight incubation at 20 °C with 5 mM GDP, 20 mM EDTA and a 5× molar excess of compound. Complete covalent modification was confirmed by observing mass shift via LC/MS (Agilent). Excess compound was removed by a PD-10 desalting column (Cytiva), and the buffer was exchanged into 20 mM Tris pH 8.0, 100 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl2, and 0.5 mM TCEP.

KRAS G12C:GDP was concentrated to 10–20 mg/mL, and crystals of the aminopyridine 4 complex were grown at 19 °C from 1.0 μL+1.0 μL of vapor diffusion sitting drops against a grid of crystallization solutions including 100 mM Tris pH 8.3–8.7, 28%–32% Peg 3350, and 200 mM MgCl2 at 19 °C. Crystals of the divarasib (10) complex were grown from 20% v/v 2-Propanol, 100 mM MES pH 6.0, and 20% w/v PEG 2,000 MME. Crystals were transferred to crystallization solutions containing 10–20% and then were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen

Structure Determination

Diffraction data were collected at synchrotron X-ray sources SSRL 12-2 for aminopyridine 4 and SSRF BL18U for divarasib (10), on crystals of KRAS G12C:GDP complexes with aminopyridine 4 and divarasib (10), respectively. Data were processed and scaled with XDS, and the structures were determined by the molecular replacement method using PHASER. The models were built using Coot and refined () in PHENIX. The aminopyridine 4 complex crystal belonged to space group P1, with unit cell dimensions a = 34.754Å, b = 41.872Å, c = 64.423Å, α = 75.882°, β = 85.707°, and γ = 65.813°. The divarasib (10), KRAS G12C complex crystal belonged to space group I222, with unit cell dimensions a = 71.74 Å, b = 101.13 Å, c = 119.02 Å, α = β = γ = 90.0°. Both crystal systems had two protamers per asymmetric unit, and in each, full occupancies of GDP and inhibitor were observed. Covalent linkage between the inhibitors and the sulfur of the side chain of Cys12 were clearly observed in the electron density ().

SPR Methods

A series S SA (streptavidin) chip was inserted into a Biacore 8K or S200 instrument (Cytiva). The instrument was primed into running buffer [50 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, 0.2% (w/v) PEG 3350, 0.1% CM-Dextran, 10 mM MgCl2, 100 nM GDP, and 2% DMSO]. This SPR chip and buffer were used for all experiments with KRAS described in this paper. Data sets were collected at 20 °C. KRAS proteins were captured on FC2 to ∼600–1,000 RUs, and data were collected and all referenced to FC1 containing KRAS G12C with the switch-II pocket blocked by alkylation of Cysteine 12 with compound 5. For molecules with slow dissociations, data were acquired as a dose response experiment, injected from low to high with seven concentrations acquired with a single cycle kinetics format, with an association time of 120 s, a dissociation time of 3600 s, and a flow rate of 100 μL/min. For molecules with rapid kinetics, data were acquired as a dose response experiment, injected from low to high with ten concentrations acquired with a multi cycle kinetics format with an association time of 90 s and a dissociation time of 240 s with a flow rate of 100 μL/min. All data were analyzed using Biacore Insight evaluation software, using a simple 1:1 model. SPR data measuring low affinity molecules that did not saturate were fit to the theoretical Rmax (100% occupancy) using a simple 1:1 model.

LC-MS Methods and Data Analysis of G12C Alkylation of Divarasib

Cell Preparation and Cysteine Profiling with IodoTMT

Frozen HCC1171 cell pellets that were treated as previously described were thawed on ice and lysed with 200 μL of HENS buffer (Thermo Scientific). Lysis was performed by pipet mixing before sonicating in a cold water bath. After 10 min on ice, the protein lysate was obtained by high speed centrifugation, 14,000xg at 4 °C for 15 min, and separation of the protein solute from the pelleted cellular debris. Protein concentration was determined by BCA (Pierce) and 200 ug (2 mg/mL) was reduced with a final concentration of 10 mM TCEP for 1 h at 50 °C. IodoTMT reagent was prepared as per manufacturer’s recommendation (Thermo Scientific) and labeling was carried out for 1 h at 37 °C in the dark. Labeling was quenched with a final concentration of 20 mM DTT (Sigma), incubating at 37 °C in the dark for 15 min. Protein cleanup was performed via a methanol chloroform precipitation with 4 times (v/v) methanol and 1 times chloroform with an addition of 3 times water. High speed centrifugation at 21 000g for 2 min, removal of the nonprotein solutes, and rinsing with methanol 2 times resulted in a protein solid that was air-dried for 15 min. Subsequently, the pellet was dissolved with 2 M UREA (in 20 mM HEPES), sonicated before adding trypsin (Promega) at a 1:60 ratio of enzyme/substrate, and digested overnight at 37 °C. Following digestion, peptides were acidified with 1%TFA before desalting with a 3 cm3 C18 Sep-Pak columns (Waters, Milford, MA) eluting with 60% ACN/0.1% TFA. Dried eluents were fractionated using a high pH reverse phase liquid chromatography separation over an Agilent 1100 series offline HPLC system. Lyophilized peptides are resuspended in HPLC grade water and loaded onto an Agilent Zorbax 300 Extend C-18 analytical column with 2.1 mm × 150 mm dimensions and 3.5 μm particle size. Solvent A consisted of 5%ACN/50 mM ammonium bicarbonate while solvent B was 90%ACN/50 mM AMBIC. Peptide fractions were collected in 45 s intervals for a total of 63 min with a linear gradient of 15% to 60% B. Ninety six fractions were collected, and every seventh fraction pair was combined to form a final set of 6 distinct groups. Samples were lyophilized in preparation for enrichment.

Affinity Enrichment

Per sample fraction, 100 μL of anti-TMT resin slurry (Prod#90076, ThermoFisher Scientific) were rinsed 3 times with 200 μL of TBS. Peptides were then resuspended with 100 μL of TBS before addition to the rinsed resin. Enrichment was performed by incubating for 2 h at room temperature with end-overend mixing. The supernatant was removed, and the resin was washed 4 times with 100 μL of 2 M UREA in TBS, followed by 4 times with just TBS, and 3 times with water. Peptides were eluted off the beads with 50% ACN/0.5%TFA after incubation for 10 min with shaking, and elution was performed twice. All centrifugation steps were performed at 2,500g for 1 min at room temperature. After drying to completion, peptides were staged tipped using 5 μL of C18 Phytip (Biotage, Inc.) and injected for LC-MS/MS acquisition.

LC-MS/MS Analysis

Data was collected on an Orbitrap Fusion Lumos Tribrid (Thermo Fisher Scientific, San Jose, CA USA). Peptides were loaded onto a New Objective PicoFrit Acquity BEH130Å C18 column (1.7 μM, 100 μM × 250 mm) via a Nanospray Flex Ion-Source (Thermo Scientific) at a voltage of 1.9 kV with a flow rate of 0.7 μL/min in Solvent A (98% water/2% acetonitrile/0.1% formic acid). Separation was achieved at a flow rate of 0.5 μL/min with a linear gradient of 2% solvent B (98% acetonitrile/2% water/0.1% formic acid) to 35% solvent B over 158 min with a total run time of 180 min. Full ms scans were collected in the orbitrap at 120,000 resolution, scan ranging from 350 to 1350 m/z, automatic gain targeting (AGC) of 1 × 106, and a maximum injection time of 50 ms. MS2 ions were selected using a top speed data dependent mode and fragmented with a CID energy of 35, AGC of 2 × 104, and a maximum injection time of 100 ms. Utilizing the Orbitrap Fusion Tribrid’s SPS mode for isolating MS3 ions, the top 10 MS2 precursor ions are selected and fragmented via higher collision dissociation energy (HCD) of 55, AGC of 2 × 105, a maximum injection time of 150 ms, isolation width of 1.0 Da, and a resolution of 50,000 at 200 m/z. MS/MS spectra were searched using Mascot against a human proteome database alongside a concatenated targeted decoy database (Uniprot Augutst2017) with known contaminants. Search parameters included trypsin cleavage with allowance of up to 2 missed cleavage events, a precursor ion tolerance of 50 ppm, and a fragment ion tolerance of 0.8 Da. Searches permitted variable modifications of methionine oxidation (+15.9949 Da) and variable modifications of cysteine (+329.2266) for IodoTMT. Reporter ions produced by the IodoTMT tags were quantified with an in-house software package known as Mojave. Peptide spectra matches (PSMs) were filtered with a false discovery rate (FDR) of 0.97% on the peptide level and subsequently 4.3% on the protein level.

Liver Microsome Metabolic Stability Assay

Metabolic stability of divarasib was evaluated in pooled liver microsomes from mixed-sex humans (male and female), female CD-1 mice, male Sprague–Dawley rats, male beagle dogs, and male cynomolgus monkeys (Discovery Life Sciences, Woburn, MA). Incubations were carried out with microsomal protein (0.5 mg/mL) and divarasib (1.0 μM) in potassium phosphate buffer (100 mM, pH 7.4) in the presence or absence of NADPH (1.0 mM). The final reaction volume was 0.25 mL. Reactions were initiated by the addition of NADPH or a buffer. At 0, 20, 40, and 60 min, 50 μL aliquots were removed and quenched with 100 μL of acetonitrile containing propranolol (1 μM) and labetalol (200 nM) as internal standards. Samples were centrifuged at 3220g for 40 min, and the resulting supernatant was diluted with 2× volume of ultrapure water prior to analysis by LC-MS/MS.

gMDCK (MDR1 Knockout MDCK) Permeability Assay

Madin-Darby Kidney cells (MDCKI) were obtained from the ATCC, (Manassas, VA). CRISPR Cas9 was used to knockout the endogenous canine Mdr1 gene. Cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 1% pen-strep, and 5 μg/mL plasmocin before seeding on Millipore Millicell-24 well plates at 2.5 × 105 cells/mL and allowed to grow for 5 days. Prior to the permeability experiment, cell monolayers were equilibrated in transport buffer (Hank’s Balanced Salt Solution with 10 mM Hepes, pH 7.4) for 60 min at 37 °C with 5% CO2 and 95% relative humidity. Test compound dose solutions were prepared at 10 μM in transport buffer containing the monolayer integrity marker lucifer yellow (100 μM). The dose solutions were added to the donor chambers, and transport buffer was added to all receiver chambers. The permeability was examined in the apical to basolateral (A-B) and basolateral to apical (B:A) directions. The receiver chambers were sampled at 60, 120, and 180 min and were replenished with fresh transport buffer. Lucifer yellow was measured using a fluorescence plate reader (ex: 425 nm; em: 530 nm), and compound concentrations in the donor and receiving compartments were determined by LC-MS/MS analysis. The apparent permeability (Papp) in the A:B and B:A directions was calculated as follows: Papp = (dQ/dt)•(1/AC0), where dQ/dt = rate of compound appearance in the receiver compartment; A = surface area of the inset; and C0 = initial substrate concentration at time 0 min.

Cysteine Reactivity Assay

Incubation was carried out in 100 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) at 37 °C with gentle shaking. Test compound (1 μM), diphenhydramine (0.1 μM, preinternal standard), and cysteine (5 mM) were mixed sequentially to start the reaction. Aliquots were taken from 0, 30, 60, 90, 120, 150, and 180 min and quenched with a 1× volume of ice-cold acetonitrile containing propranolol (2 μM, postinternal standard). The resulting samples were quantified via LC-MS/MS. Percentage remaining was calculated using peak area ratios normalized to the 0 min time-point sample. Half-life and k react were then determined from fitting the data with one phase decay model ( %remaining=100×e−kreact×5mM×time ). Incubation without cysteine was also carried out to check the compound stability in buffer.

Pharmacokinetics (PK) Studies

For the mouse PK, 3 female CD-1 mice (Lingchang/Vital River Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd., Shanghai/Beijing, P.R. China) in each group were administered with a 1 mg/kg IV bolus or 3 mg/kg oral dose of divarasib (10). The administered dose volume was 5 mL/kg. The vehicles used for the IV and PO formulation were dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO)/polyethylene glycol 400 (PEG)/water (5:35:60; volume v/v/v) and ethanol/polyethylene glycol 400 (PEG) (20:80; volume v/v), respectively. Blood samples were collected via lateral tail into tubes containing K2EDTA (5:1 dilution) as an anticoagulant at 0.033 (IV group only), 0.083, 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, and 6 h postdose.

For the Rat PK, 3 male Sprague–Dawley rats (Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology, Beijing, P.R. China) in each group were administered with a 1 mg/kg IV bolus or 3 mg/kg oral dose of divarasib (10). The administered dose volumes were 1 and 2 mL/kg, respectively, for the IV and PO route. The vehicles used for the IV and PO formulation were dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO)/polyethylene glycol 400 (PEG)/water (10:60:30; volume v/v/v) and ethanol/polyethylene glycol 400 (PEG) (20:80; volume v/v), respectively. Blood samples were collected (implanted cannula in femoral artery) in tubes containing K2EDTA as the anticoagulant at 0.033 (IV group only), 0.083, 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, and 6 h post dose.

For the dog PK, 3 male beagle dogs (Marshall Bioresources, Beijing, P.R. China) in each group were administered with a 0.5 mg/kg IV bolus or 0.5 mg/kg oral dose of divarasib (10). The administered dose volume was 1 mL/kg for both groups. The vehicle used for the IV and PO formulation was dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO)/polyethylene glycol 400 (PEG)/water (10:60:30; volume v/v/v). Blood samples were collected in tubes containing K2EDTA as anticoagulant at 0.033 (IV group only), 0.083, 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, and 6 h post dose.

For the monkey PK, 3 male cynomolgus monkeys (Hainan Jingang Laboratory Animal Co. Ltd., Haikou Hainan Province, China) in each group were administered with a 0.5 mg/kg IV bolus or 0.5 mg/kg oral dose of divarasib (10). The administered dose volume was 1 mL/kg for both groups. The vehicle used for the IV and PO formulation was dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO)/poly­(ethylene glycol) 400 (PEG)/water (10:60:30; volume v/v/v). Blood samples were collected in tubes containing K2EDTA as anticoagulant at 0.033 (IV group only), 0.083, 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, and 6 h post dose.

For all the above studies, the concentration of divarasib (10) in blood was determined by a nonvalidated LC/MS/MS assay. PK parameters were determined by noncompartmental analysis (NCA) methods using IV bolus or extravascular input model (Model 200-202), Phoenix WinNonlinâ, Version 6.4 (Certara USA, Inc., Princeton, NJ). Oral bioavailability (F) was determined by dividing the dose-normalized area under the blood concentration–time curve, extrapolated to infinity (AUCinf) from each animal in the PO group by the dose-normalized mean AUCinf obtained from the IV group.

In Vivo Efficacy Studies

All animal studies were performed in an AAALAC-accredited facility, and animals were maintained in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (National Research Council 2011). All animal activities were performed in accordance with protocols approved by the facility’s Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC; ethical approval identification numbers: 16-2562 N, 16-2562 AE, 16-2562 AL, and 16-2562 AN).

Female C.B-17 SCID (Inbred) and Nu/Nu mice were obtained from Charles River Laboratories (Hollister, CA). Human NSCLC NCI-H358 and NCI-H2122 cells were cultured in vitro, harvested in log-phase growth, and resuspended in Hank’s Balanced Salt Solution containing Matrigel (Corning) at a 1:1 ratio. The cells were then inoculated subcutaneously in the right flank of the mice in a volume of 100 μL.

For the NCI-H358 xenograft study, C.B-17 SCID (Inbred) mice were subcutaneously inoculated with 5 × 106 NCI-H358 cells. Tumors for the efficacy study were monitored until they reached a mean tumor volume of 194–348 mm3. The mean tumor volume across all six groups was 209 ± 3 mm3 (mean ± SD) at the initiation of dosing. Mice were given 0 (vehicle −0.5% Methylcellulose) and 0.1, 1, 5, 15, or 50 mg/kg divarasib (10) oral gavage (PO) daily (QD) for 21 days in a volume of 6 mL/kg. For the NCI-H2122 study, Nu/Nu mice were inoculated with 10 × 106 NCI-H2122 cells. Tumors for the efficacy study were monitored until they reached a mean tumor volume of 180–235 mm3. The mean tumor volume across all seven groups was 266 ± 6 mm3 (mean ± SD) at the initiation of dosing. Mice were given 0 (vehicle -0.5% methylcellulose) and 0.7, 2.08, 6.25, 12.5, 25, or 100 mg/kg divarasib (10) oral gavage (PO) daily (QD) for 21 days in a volume of 6 mL/kg. For the pharmacodynamic and pharmacokinetic studies on both models, animals with tumor volume between 300 and 500 mm3 were dosed once orally, with tumors being collected 8 h after dose.

Tumor volumes were measured in two dimensions (length and width) using Ultra Cal-IV calipers (model 54-10-111; Fred V. Fowler Co.; Newton, MA). The tumor volume was calculated with the following formula: Tumor size (mm3) = (longer measurement × shorter measurement2)/2. Animal body weights were measured by using an Adventura Pro AV812 scale (Ohaus Corporation; Pine Brook, NJ). Percent weight change was calculated using the following formula: Group percent weight change = (new weight – initial weight)/initial weight) × 100.

RNA Isolation and RT-PCR Analysis

Total RNA was extracted from tumor tissue with an RNeasy Plus Mini kit (Qiagen) following the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA quantity was determined using a Nanodrop instrument (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA). Transcriptional readouts were assessed using a Fluidigm instrument according to manufacturer’s recommendations. RNA (100 ng) was subjected to cDNA synthesis/preamplification reactions using the Applied Biosystems High Capacity cDNA RT Kit and TaqMan PreAmp Master Mix per the manufacturer’s protocol (Thermo Fisher). Following amplification, samples were diluted 1:4 with Tris EDTA pH 8.0 and qPCR was conducted on Fluidigm 96.96 Dynamic Arrays using the BioMarkTM HD system according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Cycle threshold (Ct) values were converted to fold change or percentages in relative expression values (2̂-(ddCt)) by subtracting the mean of the housekeeping reference genes from the mean of each target gene, followed by subtraction of the mean vehicle dCt from the mean sample dCt.

Mass Spectrometry Target Engagement Assay for Quantitating KRAS G12C Alkylation

Details on the mass spectrometry experiments performed for KRAS G12C alkylation target engagement was described elsewhere (Analytical Chemistry 95 (11), 4834–4839). Briefly, powderized tumor samples were homogenized followed by detergent and salt removal using Thermo Fisher HiPPR detergent removal 96 well plates for 10 min at room temperature. Following centrifugation, the recovered samples were denatured with TFE followed by alkylation and tryptic digestion. After adding heavy isotopic peptides (final concentration 10 nM), the samples were desalted using a MCX μElution plate (Waters, Milford) and evaporated to dryness. An equivalent of 16 μg injection was performed on a HALO peptide ES-C18 column, 10 cm × 500 μm, 2.7 μm running a flow rate of 20 μL/min coupled to Sciex 6500 series QTrap mass spectrometer system in a 18 min gradient up to 80% B (0.2% formic acid in 3% DMSO + acetonitrile). The transitions for Cys12 Peptide (LVVVGACGV*GK) and Generic Peptide (SF*EDIHHYREQIK) were monitored.
