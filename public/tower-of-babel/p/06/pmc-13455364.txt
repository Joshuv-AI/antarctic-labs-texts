Nitrous oxide for pain and anxiety management in elderly patients after THA rehabilitation: a study protocol for a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Ziyang Wang, Yangming Hu, Cui Li, Renwei Zhang, Xiaochen Jiang, Weifeng Wang, Xueling Qiu, Ping Zhong, Lu Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWith the increasing incidence of femoral neck fracture and necrosis of the femoral head, total hip arthroplasty is the ideal treatment due to its advantages of small damage and quick recovery. Pain is a decisive factor in determining the early and active participation of patients in rehabilitation after total hip arthroplasty. Nitrous oxide with its rapid analgesic effect is often used to reduce pain in minor procedures. The purpose of this study was to determine if nitrous oxide reduces pain during rehabilitation after total hip arthroplasty.MethodsThis is a randomized, double-blind, controlled clinical study. Patients ≥ 65 years old undergoing rehabilitation training after total hip arthroplasty with acute pain (VAS ≥ 4) are included. Participants will be randomly assigned to nitrous oxide or placebo control group using randomization before rehabilitation training in a ratio of 1:1. Rehabilitation will be carried out by therapists. The two gases will be implemented by trained and qualified nurses. A total of 160 participants (80 per group) will be enrolled, accounting for an estimated 20% dropout rate. The primary endpoint is the between-group difference in pain intensity measured by Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) at T1 (during rehabilitation), with additional assessments at T0 (baseline) and T2 (post-rehabilitation). Longitudinal analyses using generalized estimating equations (GEE) will include T0, T1, and T2. Key secondary outcomes include anxiety score (VAS), Berg balance scale (BBS), functional ambulation category (FAC), side effects, satisfaction, and acceptance. Safety monitoring includes continuous assessment of oxygen saturation, vital signs, and predefined stopping criteria for adverse events.ConclusionThis study is designed to generate evidence regarding the effectiveness and safety of nitrous oxide for analgesia in patients undergoing rehabilitation after total hip arthroplasty. This intervention has the potential to provide more effective support options for pain management in rehabilitation training for elderly patients, potentially improving patient satisfaction and quality of life. If proven effective, this intervention may provide preliminary evidence for a new analgesic option in hospital and rehabilitation settings.Trial registrationThis trial was registered at the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR), on August 6, 2024 (registration number ChiCTR2400087866).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07689-z.Keywords: Nitrous oxide, Analgesia, Pain management, Rehabilitation, Total hip arthroplasty

Background

According to the World Health Organization (WHO), the population aged 60 years and over will increase from 1 billion in 2020 to 1.4 billion by 2030, with a particularly rapid increase in older adults at risk for hip joint pathology due to reduced physical function and osteoporosis [1, 2]. Hip fracture incidence will increase at a rate of 2.4% per year, and by 2050 it is projected that twice as many people will suffer from hip fractures as in 2015 [3]. With the increasing incidence of femoral neck fracture and femoral head necrosis, total hip arthroplasty (THA) is currently the ideal treatment method due to its advantages of less damage, faster recovery, and shorter hospitalization [4, 5]. It can effectively relieve patients’ pain, improve joint function, improve quality of life, and reduce the burden on the family [6–8], and the medium- and long- term therapeutic effects are satisfactory [9]. Nevertheless, even with improvements in surgical techniques and approaches to pain control, pain in elderly patients is often under-treated [10]. The final recovery outcome of joint replacement surgery depends on early, active post-surgical rehabilitation, and because the procedure is so invasive, it is difficult to ensure active early post-surgical functional exercise if severe pain during rehabilitation training cannot be effectively managed [11].

Several pharmacologic strategies are available for analgesia after THA, but each presents particular challenges for elderly patients [12]. Acetaminophen has minimal analgesic effects [13]. Nonselective NSAIDs carry risks of gastric, bleeding, and renal complications [14]. While COX-2-selective NSAIDs have a better gastrointestinal safety profile, concerns regarding renal impairment persist [15–17]. Opioids, though effective [18, 19], are associated with side effects like constipation, nausea, sedation, and respiratory depression [20–23]. Continuous femoral nerve block (CFNB) with local anesthetics techniques could provide superior analgesia and fewer side effects compared with systemic opioids. However, studies have demonstrated that it causes muscle weakness, increases fall risk, and may hinder active rehabilitation and delay functional recovery in total knee replacement [24]. These challenges are especially pertinent for managing pain specifically elicited by rehabilitation training.

Rehabilitation-induced pain is physiologically and clinically distinct from postoperative resting pain. While resting pain is relatively constant and can be managed with around-the-clock systemic analgesics, pain during rehabilitation is transient, movement-evoked, and requires rapid-onset, short-duration analgesia that does not impair motor function or cognition. Current systemic analgesic regimens often fail to provide adequate, timely relief for these transient pain flares, creating a significant barrier to optimal recovery. Effective management of this procedure-related pain is critical for enabling active participation in rehabilitation and achieving optimal functional recovery. Hence, the exploration of safer and more effective analgesics for pain induced by rehabilitation training after THA should be a priority.

Nitrous oxide (N2O) is an inhalational agent with analgesic, sedative, and anti-anxiety properties [25]. It has the advantages of simple handling, fast onset, and few side effects and has been shown to have good analgesic effects in various settings such as dental, emergency treatment, obstetrics, and pediatrics [26–30]. N2O usually produces effective and temporary analgesia without changing consciousness and cognition, so patients can control gas flow on themselves and cooperate with the treatment personnel soberly during the procedure. This pharmacological profile, characterized by rapid onset and offset along with preserved cognition, makes it a theoretically suitable option for managing procedure-related pain in elderly patients undergoing rehabilitation. It was observed that all side effects (dizziness, euphoria, nausea, vomiting, etc.) were temporary and disappeared within 5 min after stopping inhalation [31, 32]. A few studies reported its use in the rehabilitation training of the frozen shoulder, cerebral palsy, and knee replacement [33–35]. Evidence from studies on total knee arthroplasty (TKA) suggests potential benefits, but THA-specific data are lacking. There is an absence of a randomized control trial assessing the efficiency of N2O during rehabilitation training in elderly patients after THA. Therefore, this study will explore the pre-emptive analgesic effects of N2O on rehabilitation training pain in this patient population, aiming to improve postoperative outcomes and quality of life after THA. We hypothesize that N2O is a viable, safe, and effective analgesic agent for pain management during rehabilitation in this population.

Methods

Aims

This randomized controlled trial aims to assess whether inhalation of 65% N2O is superior to medical air (placebo) in reducing pain, and to evaluate its safety, during rehabilitation training in elderly patients after THA. The primary hypothesis is that 65% N2O reduces the VAS pain score at T1 (during rehabilitation) compared to medical air.

Study design

This is a prospective, single-center, double-blind, randomized, parallel-controlled trial with a 1:1 allocation ratio, designed following the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) 2013 guidelines [36] for protocol reporting (a completed SPIRIT checklist is provided as Additional File 1). The completed trial report will follow the CONSORT statement. It is registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry, https://www.chictr.org.cn, (ChiCTR2400087866). This is a phase II trial evaluating the efficacy and safety of N2O in this specific clinical context. Patients meeting the inclusion criteria will be randomly assigned to the premixed N2O or medical air (control) groups before the procedure by block randomization. Study setting

All assessments and treatments will take place at the Department of Rehabilitation Medicine, 960th PLA Hospital in China.

Participants and recruitment

The protocol has been approved by the Ethics Committee (2024085). We will protect the rights and safety of participants by fully complying with the Declaration of Helsinki and GCP guidelines. The number of patients screened and the reasons for exclusion will be documented in the CONSORT flow diagram (Fig. 1). Participants will be the elderly patients with acute pain caused by rehabilitation training after THA. Patients should meet the following inclusion criteria: (1) age ≥ 65 years old; (2) patients underwent primary THA between one week and one month prior to enrollment [37] ; (3) experience moderate to severe intensity of acute pain (caused by rehabilitation training), defined as a VAS score ≥ 4 assessed immediately after a standardized passive hip flexion exercise (performed by a trained therapist, moving the hip from neutral to the point of first pain, without forcing beyond the patient’s active range); (4) without postoperative complication; (5) no joint prosthesis loosening and dislocation; (6) American Society of Anesthesiologists scores of I-III; (7) voluntary participating in the trial and sign informed consent. Patients with the following criteria were excluded: (1) patients with contraindications to N2O, including severe pulmonary disease (e.g., chronic obstructive pulmonary disease with oxygen dependence), pneumothorax, bowel obstruction, or any condition requiring pure oxygen therapy; (2) compound injuries or multiple fractures; (3) mental disorder or cognitive impairment have difficulty in reporting pain; (4) with language dysfunction; (5) with pain unrelated to rehabilitation training; (6) any contraindication for exercise therapy as established by the treating physician; (7) drug dependence or abuse.

Flow diagram of the study

A researcher in the team will be responsible for patient recruitment. Eligible patients who provide informed consent will be enrolled. Participants will be provided with both gases free of charge. Throughout the study period, medical staff will provide specialized medical care and disease assessment to encourage completion of the study. Participants will receive compensation for time and travel expenses; no financial inducement for participation is provided.

Randomization, allocation, concealment and blinding

Prior to the start of recruitment, a statistician not involved in the trial will generate the randomization sequence using computer-generated randomization with varied block sizes (e.g., 4 or 6). The block sizes will not be disclosed to ensure allocation unpredictability. The allocation will be concealed using sequentially numbered, opaque, and tamper-evident envelopes, kept by a designated staff member not involved in recruitment or assessment. After a participant completes baseline assessments, the corresponding envelope will be opened to reveal the group assignment. The study employs a double-blind design. Participants, rehabilitation therapists, outcome assessors, and the data analyst will remain blinded to group allocation. Only the project manager (who handles the allocation process) and the staff member responsible for preparing the gas cylinders are unblinded. To protect blinding, all gas cylinders are identical in appearance and are labelled only as “A” or “B”. After the study concludes, we will record the guesses of patients, therapists, and outcome assessors regarding group assignment to statistically evaluate the success of blinding. The Bang Blinding Index will be calculated for each group; a value of 0 indicates perfect blinding, positive values indicate correct guessing (unblinding), and negative values indicate incorrect guessing (reverse blinding). The results will be reported descriptively and, where appropriate, analyzed using one-sample binomial tests to assess whether the proportion of correct guesses exceeds chance levels (50%).

For maintaining the overall quality and legitimacy of the study, code breaks should only occur in exceptional circumstances, such as severe side effects, whereas knowledge of the actual treatment is essential for further management of the patient. If unblinding is deemed necessary, researchers should use a contingency system for unblinding.

Interventions

Eligible participants will be randomized into intervention and control groups after being included in the study. All procedures will be conducted in the rehabilitation therapy hall. The main methods included manipulative massage, joint mobility training, hip adductor strengthening training, and isometric training (active ankle pumps, quadriceps sets, and heel slides) according to the patient’s limb function, with individualized intensity and duration guided by prespecified progression criteria based on pain levels and functional performance. Specifically, for resistance training, intensity progresses from low (10–15 repetitions per set) to moderate (8–12 repetitions) to high (5–6 repetitions) when the patient completes the current level with VAS < 4 and demonstrates good form. For balance training, progression across four phases (from toe/heel support to figure-of-8 walking) occurs when the patient completes each phase independently with operative limb muscle strength ≥ grade 3. Exercises are stopped if VAS ≥ 7, signs of distress, or gait instability. The rehabilitation typically ranging from 10 to 30 min per session, 2–5 times per week, over a 4–8 week period. Before and after the rehabilitation treatment, wax therapy and cold compress are applied as conventional therapy.

The rehabilitation protocol is standardized in content but allows for intensity progression based on the prespecified criteria described above. Detailed progression criteria for the rehabilitation exercises are provided in Additional File 2. To minimize inter-therapist variability, all therapists involved in the study received centralized training on the protocol prior to patient enrollment and follow a detailed manual specifying exercises, progression criteria, and safety precautions. Exercises are paused or modified if the patient’s pain VAS exceeds 7 or if they show signs of distress, ensuring consistency in safety decisions. Standardized verbal encouragement will be provided to all patients to minimize potential bias. Rehabilitation treatments in this study were administered by qualified therapists with at least two years of professional experience. Before each rehabilitation session, the therapist provides education to the patient.

Patients in the intervention group begin inhaling pre-mixed 65% N2O through a specially designed mask with a check valve two minutes before rehabilitation and continue throughout the session, while the control group inhales medical air under the same conditions. The 65% concentration was selected to optimize the balance between analgesic efficacy and safety. This concentration provides sufficient pain relief during rehabilitation, as supported by our feasibility study in a similar post-arthroplasty population [38], while minimizing the risk of adverse effects such as dizziness, nausea, and sedation. Lower concentrations (e.g., 50%) have shown limited analgesic effect in rehabilitation settings [39], whereas concentrations above 70% are associated with a substantial increase in side effects without proportional gain in analgesia [40]. We therefore hypothesize that 65% N2O provides optimal analgesia during rehabilitation while preserving cognitive and motor function.

The gas is self-administered by patients to allow observation of their state of consciousness. Participants are instructed that they may remove the mask to discontinue inhalation at any time if they feel uncomfortable; the time and reason for any such interruption will be recorded in the case report form. If oxygen saturation (SpO2) falls below 90% for more than 30 s, N2O will be discontinued and 100% oxygen will be administered via the same mask. The gases are administered by trained and qualified nurses. Adjuvant analgesics will be standardized as follows: scheduled acetaminophen (maximum 3 g/day) will be permitted as background analgesia. Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) or opioids will be allowed only as rescue medication, and their use (type, dose, frequency) will be recorded in the case report form. All participants have the right to withdraw from the study at any time. Reasons for withdrawal will be documented and analyzed by the data collector. Image and audio recordings will be separately coded for communication and data analysis. Adherence to the gas intervention will be monitored by recording the total inhalation time for each session. The target inhalation duration is the full rehabilitation session (10–30 min). Inhalation for less than 80% of the session duration will be recorded as incomplete adherence.

Outcomes

Baseline data

Demographic characteristics such as age, gender, body mass index (BMI), and country will be recorded at baseline (T0, 2 min before the intervention) on a self-designed form. Clinical data will include type of disease, comorbidities, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) class, background pain levels, anxiety levels, and postoperative duration. At baseline, vital signs including blood pressure (BP), heart rate (HR) and oxygen saturation (SpO2) will also be collected.

Primary outcome

The outcome measures were selected to form a core set of patient-centered endpoints that are clinically meaningful, widely validated, and feasible in a busy rehabilitation setting. The primary outcome is pain intensity assessed via VAS at T0 (baseline), T1 (5 min after intervention onset), and T2 (5 min post-intervention). The primary efficacy endpoint is the between-group difference in VAS score at T1, which corresponds to the expected peak analgesic effect of nitrous oxide during the rehabilitation session. The primary analysis will compare absolute VAS values at T1 between the two groups, with baseline VAS score (T0) included as a covariate. The T0 and T2 assessments will be used to characterize the temporal profile of pain and will be included in the longitudinal analysis using GEE. The VAS was chosen because of its simplicity and adaptability to a wide range of people, and its good visual properties. It is a horizontal line with no numerical or verbal descriptors at the midpoint, ranging from 0 to 10 cm [41]. The time points were determined based on the pharmacokinetics of N2O: its extremely low blood/gas partition coefficient (0.46) results in rapid onset of analgesia (5 min after inhalation) and rapid recovery (5 min after discontinuation) [42]. Standardized procedures, including fixed assessment time points and blinded assessors, were followed for all VAS assessments to minimize potential response bias.

Secondary outcome

Anxiety level, assessed concurrently with pain at T0, T1, and T2 using an identical VAS, is designated as the key secondary outcome, reflecting its critical role as a co-existing barrier to rehabilitation engagement. All other secondary outcomes are considered exploratory. The Berg balance scale (BBS) was used to evaluate the balance function of the patients at T0, T3 (after 2 weeks of treatment) and T4 (after four weeks of treatment) [43]: the BBS was divided into 14 evaluation items from easy to difficult, and each item was divided into 5 levels, with scores ranging from 0 to 4 from the lowest to the highest, and a total score of 56, with higher scores indicating better balance function. The functional ambulation category (FAC) was used to evaluate [44] the patients’ ability to use the lower limbs to walk at the same time points (T0, T3, T4), which was divided into 0–5 grades, and the higher the grade, the better the ability to walk, and the less help needed. In addition, types and severity of side effects, which corresponds to Grades 1–3 of the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) version 5.0 (Grade 1 = mild, Grade 2 = moderate, Grade 3 = severe). Vital signs (BP, HR, SpO2) of the participants will be observed and recorded at T0, T1 and T2. Physician and patient satisfaction will be collected at T2 (5 levels of satisfaction, 1 = very dissatisfied; 5 = very satisfied). Patients will also be asked about their acceptance (if they would like to receive the same gas for moderate to severe acute pain control, yes/no) and will be filled out at T2. Finally, the total inhalation time will also be recorded.

This combination ensures that the assessment encompasses both the sensory and emotional components of pain as well as the critical physical functional outcomes, providing a holistic evaluation of the intervention’s utility in facilitating active participation in rehabilitation.

Sample size estimates

Based on a two-group parallel superiority randomized controlled trial design, the sample size was calculated using PASS 2025 software (NCSS, LLC, Kaysville, Utah, USA). A two-sample t-test assuming unequal variances (Welch’s t-test) was employed. Using a conventional medium effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.5), with a two-tailed significance level (alpha) of 0.05 and 80% power (β = 0.20), the required sample size is 64 participants per group. Taking into account a 20% sample attrition rate, 80 participants per group are needed. Therefore, a total of 160 participants will be enrolled in this study. The sample size was calculated solely for the primary endpoint (between-group comparison of VAS pain score at T1); it does not account for the repeated measures design (GEE), which has greater statistical efficiency.

Data management and analysis

To improve the quality of data collection, all researchers will receive specialized training prior to the study. The data will be processed confidentially. Data collected from participants, such as personal information like name and gender information, will be replaced by codes throughout the study and will be kept in files with double passwords to maintain confidentiality at all times, and only the project administrator or the concerned physicians will know the personal information of the patients. The composition of the Data Monitoring Committee (DMC) and auditing system will be established after the start of the project to monitor data collection and management as well as patient safety throughout the study. Members will include four specialized therapists and nurses, a pain management specialist, and a statistician. A double data check will also be conducted for quality control. All data will be carefully and thoroughly documented according to the data collection methods and criteria established by the program director and professional staff to ensure the authenticity of the data. Missing data will be handled using multiple imputation, with complete-case analysis as a sensitivity analysis.

All collected data will be analyzed using the intention-to-treat principle by IBM SPSS Statistics 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, New York, USA). Continuous variables were described as mean±standard deviation (SD) if they obeyed normal distribution, and median (M) and interquartile range (IQR) if they were not normally distributed; categorical variables were expressed as frequency and percentage (%). Continuous variables were analyzed using the independent t-test or the Mann-Whitney U test, based on their distribution normality. Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate; categorical variables were analyzed by chi-square or Fisher’s exact probability method depending on the sample size or the number of observed frequencies.

To simultaneously analyze the effects of group, time, and their interaction on vital signs, we performed repeated measures analysis of variance on heart rate, systolic blood pressure, and diastolic blood pressure. Generalized estimating equations (GEE) were employed to determine the effects of group, time, and their interaction on pain, with baseline pain score (T0 VAS) included as a covariate to improve precision and account for any chance imbalances between groups. An unstructured covariance matrix will be specified to allow for free estimation of variances and correlations across time points. Model fit will be assessed using the Quasi-likelihood under Independence Criterion (QIC), with lower QIC values indicating better fit if alternative covariance structures are considered. Secondary outcomes are exploratory; no adjustment for multiple comparisons will be applied.

To aid in the clinical interpretation of the pain outcomes, the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) for the pain VAS will be estimated post hoc using distribution-based methods. This post-hoc MCID estimate will be used to facilitate clinical interpretation of the observed treatment effect; it will not be used to redefine study success, which is determined by the prespecified significance test (P < 0.05) for the primary outcome.

Safety and AE monitoring

The intervention in this study is a non-invasive analgesic, which can relieve the degree of acute pain. Possible side effects include nausea, vomiting, dizziness, headache, drowsiness, euphoria, or any other discomfort reactions. Vital signs (including SpO2) will be monitored continuously throughout the rehabilitation session.

In the context of this trial, a serious adverse event (SAE) is defined as any adverse reaction that is intolerable to the patient and leads to premature discontinuation of the rehabilitation session, including but not limited to severe nausea, persistent vomiting, or severe dizziness that prevents the patient from continuing rehabilitation. Any SAE will be immediately reported to the DMC within 24 h. All other adverse events (mild or moderate) will be recorded during the intervention and at the 24-hour follow-up assessment.

The following explicit stopping thresholds will be applied: (1) oxygen saturation (SpO2) < 90% for more than 30 s; (2) any moderate or severe adverse event (e.g., persistent nausea, vomiting, severe dizziness) that, in the judgment of the attending therapist or nurse, warrants discontinuation; (3) patient request to stop inhalation at any time for any reason. If any of the above situations occur, inhalation of the study gas will be immediately stopped and 100% oxygen will be administered via the same mask, and discomfort will quickly disappear within 5 min [31, 32].

A predefined monitoring rule will be implemented: if a participant experiences a severe adverse event (SAE) deemed related to the study gas, enrollment will be paused, and the event will be immediately reviewed by the study team and the DMC. In addition, if the same type of moderate adverse event occurs in more than 20% of participants in either group, the Data Monitoring Committee will review the safety data and consider whether protocol modification or trial suspension is warranted.

Quality control

At the start of this study, the project manager and team members developed a detailed project program and contingency plan to ensure the quality of the study. The study will be coordinated and supervised by pain management specialists, rehabilitation therapists, nurses, and statisticians. The DMC serves as an independent monitoring body, responsible for reviewing safety data and study conduct. Internal monitoring will be performed by the project team on a weekly basis. During the trial, researchers will receive a uniform and targeted training to ensure smooth implementation of the study. Audits will be utilized by the research funders to regularly check the sources of data and their accuracy, completeness, reliability, and security. The funders will have access to the data for auditing purposes (e.g., checking data accuracy, completeness, and reliability), but they will have no role in study design, data analysis, interpretation, or decision-making to avoid perceived bias.

Any modifications to the protocol that may result in changes to the conduct of the trial, potential benefits to patients, or safety, including changes to the study objectives, study design, patient population, sample size, or study procedures, will require formal amendments to the protocol. These modifications will be agreed upon by all members of the trial and approved by the Ethics Committee before implementation and notified to the trial registry, journals, and regulatory authorities. Regardless of the outcome, publication in a peer-reviewed journal is planned, and participants may be informed via hospital announcements or a summary report.

Discussion

Patients after THA often suffer from severe pain during rehabilitation training due to joint adhesion caused by postoperative immobilization [33], which makes it difficult to ensure successful implementation of functional exercises in the early postoperative period. This can ultimately lead to joint stiffness or thrombosis due to reduced postoperative activity, adversely affecting prognosis. Negative experiences such as anxiety and fear induced by rehabilitation training can also exacerbate pain, further hindering patients’ compliance with subsequent treatment and affecting surgical outcomes [45].

The distinct temporal profile of rehabilitation-induced pain, which is transient, movement-evoked, and procedure-bound, creates specific requirements for analgesia that are not fully addressed by conventional systemic regimens. N2O is particularly well-suited to this context due to its unique pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic profile. Several mechanisms underlie its analgesic effect: (1) N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) antagonism, (2) pain perception modulation at alpha-2 receptors in the dorsal horn of the spinal cord, and (3) release of endogenous opioids in the brain [42]. In addition, N2O may increase the pain threshold of patients. Unlike opioids, N2O does not bind to hemoglobin and has no depressive effects on respiratory or cardiovascular function [34]. It also does not cause respiratory depression or require prolonged monitoring, unlike midazolam or other sedatives [46]. These properties are particularly advantageous in elderly patients who are more susceptible to adverse drug reactions and make N2O suitable for repeated rehabilitation sessions.

Therefore, we designed this study to evaluate the efficacy of N2O for analgesia in elderly patients undergoing rehabilitation training after THA. This trial will be the first randomized controlled trial to assess N2O specifically in this context. If the findings demonstrate clinically meaningful benefits, this intervention may provide preliminary evidence for a new analgesic option for this population. Future research would then be needed to explore its broader implementation, cost-effectiveness, and impact on long-term functional outcomes.

Limitation

Firstly, this is a single-centre study conducted in a tertiary hospital setting. Future multi-centre studies are needed to increase the reliability of the findings and to evaluate the generalizability and feasibility of this intervention in broader rehabilitation contexts (e.g., community or primary care settings). Secondly, although allocation is double-blind, the characteristic odor or euphoric effects of N2O, which may lead to disinhibited behaviour, could potentially alert researchers or patients, thereby compromising blinding. Thirdly, the follow-up duration is limited to 4 weeks; longer-term effects on pain, function, and quality of life remain unknown. Fourthly, while our outcome measures cover key patient-centered domains using validated clinical scales, they do not include more objective, technology-driven assessments (e.g., wearable sensors for movement quality or physiological biomarkers for stress response). This choice prioritized clinical feasibility for this initial trial, but future studies incorporating such measures could provide deeper mechanistic insights. Fifthly, pain is inherently a subjective experience, and despite blinding, patients’ expectations about the intervention may influence their pain ratings (expectancy effects). We attempted to minimize this by using standardized instructions and keeping patients unaware of their previous scores, but some degree of expectancy bias cannot be entirely ruled out.

Conclusion

This study will be the first randomized controlled trial designed to evaluate the safety and efficacy of N2O for relieving acute pain and anxiety in rehabilitation patients with THA. If proven effective, this intervention may provide preliminary evidence for a new analgesic option for pain management in this population.
