The effect of lesion filling on brain age estimation in multiple sclerosis
Authors: Salem Hannoun, Grace Fayad, Nabil K El Ayoubi, Samia J Khoury
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBrain age estimation is an emerging biomarker for assessing neurodegeneration in multiple sclerosis (MS). However, MS-related lesions can distort structural measurements, potentially leading to inaccuracies in age prediction models. Lesion filling has been proposed as a corrective step, but its impact on brain age estimation and its associations with clinical and structural markers remains unclear.MethodsWe analyzed 571 relapsing-remitting MS patients using the BrainAgeR pipeline to estimate brain age from both non-lesion-filled and lesion-filled T1-weighted images. Bias correction was applied to remove age-related prediction bias. Brain Age Gap (BAG) was computed as the difference between corrected predicted brain age and chronological age. Multivariable linear regression models were used to assess associations between BAG and clinical outcomes (EDSS, 9HPT, SDMT, 25FWT) and volumetric measures.ResultsNon-lesion-filled and lesion-filled brain age estimates showed excellent agreement (r = 0.97; ICC = 0.962), with a mean difference of 1.23 years and slightly lower mean absolute error for lesion-filled predictions (8.12 vs. 9.40 years). Both BAG measures were significantly associated with EDSS, 9HPT, and SDMT, though effect sizes were modest. Lesion-filled BAG showed stronger and more consistent associations with gray matter, thalamic, and hippocampal volumes, and these associations remained significant after Bonferroni correction.ConclusionLesion filling modestly improves structural interpretability of brain age estimates in MS but has limited effect on clinical correlations. The high concordance between lesion-filled and non-lesion-filled estimates confirms the robustness of brain age as a biomarker, while supporting the use of lesion correction when structural precision is essential.Keywords: Multiple sclerosis, Magnetic resonance imaging, Brain age, Atrophy, Volumetry, Prediction

Introduction

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a chronic neuroinflammatory and neurodegenerative disease of the central nervous system. It is characterized by demyelination, axonal damage, and progressive neurodegeneration, leading to functional impairment and disability [1, 2]. The pathological mechanisms underlying MS contribute to accelerated brain atrophy and alterations in brain structure, which are measurable through neuroimaging biomarkers [3].Brain age estimation, derived from structural MRI data, has emerged as a promising biomarker for assessing neurological and cognitive health [4–6]. It provides an individualized measure of brain aging by comparing structural features of the brain to a normative population-based model [7]. An increased brain age relative to chronological age has been linked to various neurological and psychiatric disorders, including MS [5, 8, 9]. Studies have shown that a higher brain age gap (BAG) in MS patients is associated with greater disability and worse clinical outcomes [6, 9]. Thus, accurately estimating brain age could enhance disease monitoring, facilitate early detection of at-risk individuals, and aid in evaluating treatment responses [5, 10].

One potential concern in brain age estimation in MS is the presence of white matter lesions, which may affect tissue segmentation and MRI-derived biomarkers. Lesion filling, a preprocessing technique, addresses this by replacing lesioned areas with intensity estimates derived from surrounding normal-appearing tissue [10–12]. It has been shown to improve the accuracy and consistency of other MRI-derived structural measures, such as cortical thickness [13] and network connectivity metrics [14], by reducing segmentation errors in lesioned areas. Although these studies did not directly assess brain age, they support the broader rationale for applying lesion filling to enhance structural reliability in neuroimaging analyses. However, van der Weijden et al. (2022) noted that while lesion filling might reduce variability, it can also introduce significant artifacts and should be applied with caution [14].

Recent studies have begun to assess lesion filling’s direct effect on brain age estimation in MS. Notably, Cole et al. and La Rosa et al. concluded that lesion filling has a minimal impact on predicted brain age, suggesting that brain age may be robust to focal lesion pathology and primarily driven by diffuse or global degeneration [9, 10]. This resilience to focal damage has been proposed as a key strength of the brain age framework. However, because lesion filling demonstrably influences other imaging metrics, and since methodological differences across studies persist, additional comparative evaluations remain warranted, especially across varied pipelines and clinically relevant endpoints [12–16].

While some studies suggest that MS-related lesions have minimal influence on brain age predictions [9], others propose that lesion filling may enhance the interpretability of these estimates by reducing artifacts caused by segmentation errors in lesioned areas [12, 14]. In this study, we evaluate whether lesion-filled brain age estimates, and their corresponding BAG measures, exhibit stronger and more clinically relevant associations with brain structure and functional impairment. Using the BrainAgeR model [17, 18], we compare lesion-filled and non-lesion-filled predictions in a well-characterized RRMS cohort, incorporating agreement analyses (including ICC and Bland–Altman), and assessing associations with volumetric and clinical outcomes. This approach enables a more comprehensive understanding of whether lesion correction meaningfully enhances the utility of brain age as a neuroimaging biomarker in MS.

Materials and methods

Subjects

A total of 571 patients with clinically definite relapsing-remitting MS (RRMS), diagnosed according to the 2010 McDonald criteria [19], were identified from our institution’s longitudinal cohort database. Exclusion criteria included a history of intravenous steroid administration or MS relapse within the past four weeks, current pregnancy, major psychiatric or psychotic disorders, dementia, neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder, or any non-MS diagnosis. The chronological age of each patient was recorded, and clinical disability measures were extracted, including: the Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS), the 25-Foot Walk Test (25FWT), the 9-Hole Peg Test (9HPT), and the Symbol Digit Modalities Test (SDMT) (Table 1). Clinical scores were obtained retrospectively from patient medical records and matched to the closest available MRI scan date within a three-month window. This study was approved by the American University of Beirut Biomedical Institutional Review Board (IRB), which explicitly waived the requirement for written informed consent due to the retrospective nature of the study and minimal risk to participants. It was also conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and all relevant institutional and national regulations. All methods were performed in accordance with the applicable guidelines and regulations.

Image acquisition

Clinical MRI acquisitions were performed in two centers; the first (Center A) equipped with a 3-Tesla Philips Ingenia MR System (Philips Healthcare, Best, The Netherlands) and the second (Center B) with a 3-Tesla Siemens Magnetom Verio MR System (Siemens, Erlangen, Germany). A standardized conventional MRI protocol was used [20, 21]. For this study, the acquired 3D T1-weighted and 3D fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) images were used processed for analysis.

Preprocessing and lesion filling

An experienced operator (SH) first performed quality control to exclude images with major artifacts that could affect downstream analyses. Bias field correction of both T1-weighted and FLAIR images was applied using the N4 algorithm in 3D-Slicer (https://www.slicer.org/). Whole-brain extraction was then performed using the Brain Extraction Tool (BET) from the FMRIB Software Library (FSL 5.0) to measure intracranial volume (ICV) for normalization [22].

Lesion filling was performed using the Lesion Segmentation Toolbox (LST 3.0.0) (http://www.applied-statistics.de/lst.html, Technische Universität München, Munich, Germany), implemented in Statistical Parametric Mapping (SPM12) (http://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm, University College London, London, UK) [23, 24]. The lesion growth algorithm (LGA) was first applied to segment T2-hyperintense lesions by combining information from co-registered T1-weighted and FLAIR images. The resulting binary lesion mask was then used to fill lesions on the T1-weighted images.

The segmentation tool of SPM12 was then applied to the processed T1-weighted images using default settings to automatically obtain volumes for gray matter (GM), white matter (WM), and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) [24]. Subcortical gray matter structures, including the caudate nuclei, puttamen, globus pallidus, accumbens, amygdala, hypocampus, and thalamus, were segmented using FIRST (FSL’s model-based segmentation tool), which utilizes manually segmented templates to derive subcortical GM volume measurements [25]. All extracted masks were visually checked for accuracy, manually corrected if necessary, and had their volumes normalized by ICV.

Brain age estimation

Brain age estimation was performed using BrainAgeR, a validated machine learning-based tool for brain age prediction from structural T1-weighted MRI [17, 18]. Both non-lesion-filled and lesion-filled T1-weighted images underwent identical preprocessing steps, including bias field correction and skull stripping, prior to analysis. Two independent brain age estimation assessments were conducted using the non-lesion-filled T1-weighted images (referred to as non-lesion-filled Brain age), and the lesion-filled T1-weighted images (referred to as lesion-filled brain age). This approach allowed us to isolate the effect of lesion filling on brain age prediction while holding all other preprocessing variables constant. In addition to the direct brain age estimates, we computed BAG as the difference between predicted brain age and chronological age for both the non-lesion-filled and lesion-filled pipelines. These metrics were used to assess individual deviations from normative aging and their associations with brain volumetric and clinical outcomes.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Stata/MP v14.1. Brain age estimates were obtained from both non-lesion-filled and lesion-filled T1-weighted MRI scans. Agreement between non-lesion-filled and lesion-filled brain age estimates was assessed using Bland–Altman plots (Fig. 1) and the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC), computed using a two-way mixed-effects model with absolute agreement. Mean absolute error (MAE) between predicted and chronological age was calculated for both brain age models to assess overall model accuracy.

The Bland-Altman plot illustrates the agreement between non-lesion-filled and lesion-filled brain age estimates. Each point represents an individual subject, plotting the average of the two methods (x-axis) against their difference (y-axis). The solid horizontal line indicates the mean difference (1.23 years), and the dashed lines mark the 95% limits of agreement (− 6.20 to + 8.66 years). Positive values indicate that non-lesion-filled brain age exceeds lesion-filled age; negative values indicate the opposite

To account for the well-documented age bias in brain age predictions, where younger individuals tend to have overestimated brain age and older individuals underestimated brain age, we regressed the predicted brain age against chronological age and applied a residualized correction approach [10]. This method removes the linear dependency between chronological and predicted age, yielding a bias-corrected brain age estimate that more accurately reflects individual differences beyond normal aging effects. The residualized correction approach has been widely adopted in the literature for BAG studies [10, 26].Specifically, bias-corrected brain age was calculated by removing the linear age trend from predicted age estimates, and the Brain Age Gap (BAG) was computed as the difference between corrected brain age and chronological age [26]. This produced two bias-corrected BAG variables: one from the non-lesion-filled image model and one from the lesion-filled image model.

We conducted multivariable linear regression analyses to assess associations between corrected BAG and clinical outcomes (EDSS, 9HPT, SDMT, 25FWT), as well as global and subcortical brain volume measures. All models included sex and disease duration as covariates. Given the number of volumetric measures tested, we applied Bonferroni correction to account for multiple comparisons, using a significance threshold of p < 0.003125. Model performance was compared using adjusted R² and standardized beta coefficients. Variance inflation factors (VIF) were examined to assess multicollinearity in all regression models. Standardized β coefficients, standard errors, adjusted R2, and p-values were reported. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Demographic, clinical, and neuroimaging characteristics of the cohort are summarized in Table 1. A total of 571 MS patients were included (204 males, 367 females). The mean chronological age was 36.3 ± 11.7 years. The predicted brain age was 43.4 ± 14.7 years for the non-lesion-filled model and 42.1 ± 14.0 years for the lesion-filled model. The bias-corrected brain age gap (BAG) had a mean of 7.10 ± 10.69 years for the non-lesion-filled model and 5.87 ± 10.09 years for the lesion-filled model, suggesting slightly improved alignment with chronological age in the lesion-filled estimates.

The Bland-Altman plot (Fig. 1) illustrated the difference between non-lesion-filled and lesion-filled brain age estimates as a function of their average. The mean difference was 1.23 years, with 95% limits of agreement ranging from − 6.20 to + 8.66 years, indicating that non-lesion-filled brain age estimates are, on average, slightly higher than lesion-filled brain age estimates. This suggests a slight tendency of the non-lesion-filled model to overestimate brain age in some individuals. A scatter plot (Fig. 2) showed a strong correlation between non-lesion-filled and lesion-filled brain age (r = 0.97, p < 0.001), confirming high agreement between the two estimates. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), computed using a two-way mixed-effects model with absolute agreement, was 0.962 (95% CI: 0.947–0.971), indicating excellent reliability between the two methods.

Scatter plot showing a strong positive correlation (r = 0.97) between non-lesion-filled and lesion-filled brain age estimates, indicating high agreement between the two methods

The mean absolute error (MAE) between predicted and chronological age was 9.40 years for the non-lesion-filled model and 8.12 years for the lesion-filled model, indicating modest improvement in predictive accuracy with lesion correction. Variance inflation factors (VIF) in all regression models remained low (VIF = 1.1), indicating no multicollinearity concerns among predictors.

Associations between BAG and clinical outcomes

Table 2 presents the associations between bias-corrected BAG and clinical disability measures. Both non-lesion-filled and lesion-filled BAG were significantly associated with EDSS (β = 0.022 and 0.021, respectively, both p < 0.001), 9HPT (β = 0.178 for both, p < 0.001), and SDMT (β = −0.417 and − 0.383, p < 0.001). No significant associations were found with the 25FWT in either model. Model performance, based on adjusted R², was comparable across both BAG variants, though slightly higher for the non-lesion-filled model in SDMT.

Discussion

This study examined the effect of lesion filling on brain age estimation in patients with relapsing-remitting MS. Both non-lesion-filled and lesion-filled brain age estimates demonstrated high reliability, with a Pearson correlation of r = 0.97 and an ICC of 0.962. The mean difference of 1.23 years on Bland–Altman analysis, and a slightly lower MAE for lesion-filled brain age (8.12 vs. 9.40 years), suggest that while lesion filling may provide minor improvements in estimation accuracy, overall agreement remains strong.

Brain age estimation has emerged as a valuable neuroimaging biomarker for assessing neurodegeneration and predicting long-term cognitive and motor decline [27]. However, its application in MS has been challenging due to the presence of lesions that can affect tissue segmentation and brain volume measurements. Lesion-filling techniques, such as the one used in this study, aim to reduce this bias by normalizing lesioned regions and improving structural quantification [13–15]. In our analysis, bias-corrected lesion-filled BAG demonstrated stronger and more consistent associations with multiple volumetric outcomes, including gray matter, white matter, hippocampus, and thalamus, when compared to non-lesion-filled BAG. These associations remained statistically significant after Bonferroni correction. However, for clinical outcomes, both BAG estimates showed similar performance, with only minor differences across models. These findings emphasize that while lesion filling can modestly enhance the structural interpretability of brain age estimates in some cases, its added value in clinical prediction is limited.

La Rosa et al. (2024) found that lesion filling had minimal impact on age prediction in an MS-specific brain age model, suggesting robustness to lesion effects [10]. Our findings are largely in agreement, showing high consistency between the two estimates. However, we also observed that lesion-filled BAG was more closely aligned with several structural markers of neurodegeneration, suggesting modest benefits in anatomical specificity. Methodological differences—such as lesion-filling algorithms, modeling approaches, or population characteristics—likely account for variability across studies. These findings underscore the importance of harmonized preprocessing protocols and cross-cohort validation to determine when lesion correction adds meaningful value.

Consistent with prior work showing that lesion correction improves the accuracy of volumetric assessments in MS [12, 15, 16], our results extend this benefit to brain age models. Lesion-filled BAG was more strongly associated with global and regional volumes, suggesting that lesion correction can modestly increase the biological sensitivity of brain age pipelines, particularly for evaluating neuroanatomical integrity. However, it is important to note that the differences between models were modest. Both estimates were highly correlated, and the agreement metrics indicated that brain age is generally robust to lesion-induced variance. While lesion correction improved alignment with structural measures, its contribution to clinical prediction was limited.

Importantly, lesion-filling approaches are not without limitations. Prior work, including that by van der Weijden et al. (2022), cautions that lesion filling may introduce artifacts, especially in analyses of connectivity or network architecture [14]. Its use should therefore be guided by the specific analytical context. Although our cross-sectional design precludes predictive inferences, the observed enhancements in structural associations support further evaluation of lesion correction in longitudinal designs and clinical trials.

In our multivariable analysis, both non-lesion-filled and lesion-filled bias-corrected BAG measures showed significant associations with several clinical outcomes, including EDSS, SDMT, and the 9-hole peg test, although the strength of these associations was modest. No significant association was observed with the 25-foot walk test. These findings suggest that while brain age deviations may capture relevant aspects of clinical status in MS, their explanatory power is limited, likely due to the influence of compensatory mechanisms, variability in disease expression, and treatment effects [3, 28]. As such, brain age may serve better as a long-term prognostic marker rather than a direct reflection of current function [29]. Longitudinal studies will be needed to evaluate the utility of BAG trajectories in forecasting future disability and cognitive decline [5, 9].

We additionally explored the utility of the bias-corrected Brain Age Gap (BAG), defined as the difference between corrected predicted and chronological age, as a complementary metric of neurodegeneration. BAG has been proposed as a sensitive biomarker of pathological brain aging and has demonstrated prognostic value across neurological and psychiatric populations [5, 30]. In our study, lesion-filled BAG showed more consistent associations with gray matter and key subcortical volumes, suggesting enhanced biological relevance. These results align with recent MS-specific modeling efforts advocating for harmonized lesion correction and structural preprocessing [10]. Nonetheless, clinical correlations remained modest, reinforcing the known structure–function disconnect in MS [28, 29]. This disconnect may stem from disease-modifying therapy effects, individual variability in resilience, and the differing timescales of anatomical and functional change.

Despite these promising findings, several limitations warrant consideration. First, the study cohort consisted exclusively of RRMS patients from a single clinical center, which may limit the generalizability of our results to progressive MS subtypes or to broader populations with varying disease severity and treatment histories. Second, the absence of a healthy control group restricts our ability to directly quantify the degree of MS-related brain aging relative to normative trajectories. Third, while all MRI data were acquired using harmonized protocols across two scanners, we did not perform scanner-stratified analyses, and minor variability due to scanner effects cannot be entirely excluded. Another important consideration is the lesion-filling methodology. We used the LST toolbox in SPM, which is widely adopted; however, different lesion-filling algorithms, including advanced deep learning-based inpainting methods [31], may produce varying results. Future work should compare these techniques directly. Additionally, the cross-sectional nature of our study prevents inference on temporal dynamics or predictive performance. Longitudinal data will be essential to assess whether lesion-filled brain age more accurately tracks progression or treatment response than traditional volumetrics. Finally, both lesion-filled brain age and tissue volumetrics were derived from the same lesion-filled T1-weighted scans. This methodological overlap may introduce circularity and artificially inflate associations. Such partial dependence between predictor and outcome could account for the modest increases in explained variance and should be considered when interpreting results.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that lesion filling provides modest yet measurable improvements in brain age estimation among individuals with multiple sclerosis by mitigating lesion-induced biases in structural MRI. Lesion-filled brain age estimates showed slightly stronger and more consistent associations with key volumetric metrics compared to non-lesion-filled estimates, particularly for gray matter and subcortical structures. Despite these differences being small and not consistently observed across all outcomes, the two estimates were highly concordant, indicating that brain age models are generally robust to lesion-related variability. These findings support the careful incorporation of lesion correction in brain age pipelines for MS research, especially when precise structural alignment is crucial. Future longitudinal and multi-center studies are needed to further evaluate the predictive and clinical value of lesion-filled brain age in diverse MS populations.
