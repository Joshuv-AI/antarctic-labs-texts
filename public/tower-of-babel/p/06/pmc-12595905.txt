Preventable trauma deaths and health system strengthening recommendations: findings from a multi-disciplinary expert panel review of trauma deaths in the Western Cape, South Africa
Authors: Julia Dixon, Janette Verster, Heike Geduld, Hendrick Lategan, Shaheem de Vries, George Oosthuizen, Craig Wylie, Denise Lourens, Elmin Steyn, Lesley Hodsdon, Karlien Doubell, Mohammed Mayet, L’Oreal Snyders, Maria Kohlbrenner, Smitha Bhaumik, Chelsea Dymond, Ian Eisenhauer, Madeline Ross, Lani Finck, Maria D Rodriguez, Nee-Kofi Mould-Millman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInjuries rank among the top 5 leading causes of death worldwide with 90% of all injury-related deaths occurring in low- and middle-income countries. Up to 70% of injuries are potentially preventable. One approach to inform strategies to reduce post-injury mortality is expert-led trauma death reviews. The objective of this study is to present the results of an expert review of decedent cases and the associated recommendations for health system improvement.MethodsA cohort of trauma decedents from 2023 was identified from an ongoing study in the Western Cape Province of South Africa. Demographics, injury details, time and location of death, and postmortem findings were collected. In April 2024, a multi-disciplinary expert panel determined preventability for each death and made actionable recommendations for improvements. Analysis of preventable and non-preventable cases was performed using Chi-square, Fisher’s exact, and Wilcoxon signed rank tests. A rapid qualitative analysis of recommendations was completed and descriptively summarized.Results210 deaths were reviewed by 30 expert panelists. The largest proportion of deaths were from assaults (39%), followed by gunshot injuries (29%). Overall, 33% of deaths were classified as preventable or potentially preventable. Of the 161 in-hospital and ambulance deaths, 64 (39%) were preventable or potentially preventable. The largest proportion of preventable deaths were from haemorrhage (27, 39%) and multi-organ failure or sepsis (25, 36%). The majority (100, 62.1%) of deaths occurred in an emergency centre. Top recommendations from the panel were: development of protocols for diagnosis and management of severe head injuries, context appropriate and accessible trauma training for all frontline providers particularly junior physicians, improved access to diagnostic CT imaging and operating theatres at non-tertiary facilities, and community initiatives to reduce interpersonal violence.ConclusionOne-third of deaths were preventable or potentially preventable, demonstrating a decrease from a review of 2021 trauma deaths in the same system which found 45% to be preventable. Findings highlight a continued need to increase training and strategic utilization of limited existing resources, such as CT and operating theatres, to improve the quality of trauma care. Preventable trauma death reviews are a powerful methodology to inform actionable change.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25139-0.Keywords: Trauma, Inter-personal violence, Trauma outcomes, Preventable trauma death review, Mortality panel, Preventable death expert panel, Injury-related death, Health system improvement

Background

Injury is among the top 5 leading causes of death globally, and it is viewed as a potentially preventable phenomenon [1, 2]. The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that 3.1 million deaths were attributable to preventable injuries in 2019 alone [2, 3]. Despite being a global phenomenon, injuries and violence are not evenly distributed across geographic locations, economic landscapes, or even individual patient demographics. Men are twice as likely to die from trauma compared to women, and young persons aged 15 to 29 years are at greatest risk [2, 4]. Victims of injury are also at risk for long-term morbidity, including physical disability and development of mental health disorders, which can affect their socioeconomic prospects and household financial security [5].

Low- and middle-income countries (LMIC) bear a disproportionate amount of the burden, with 90% of injury-related deaths occurring in LMICs and the rate of disability-adjusted-life-years in LMICs more than triple that of high-income countries [2, 4]. Compared to global rates, South Africa has a comparatively high death rate due to injury, with most deaths occurring as a result of intentional interpersonal violence, followed by road traffic incidents [6–8]. Both road traffic and suicide death rates are fourfold higher in South Africa than the global average [8]. The social and economic impacts of preventing even a portion of these deaths would be immense. Importantly, the WHO also estimates that over half of deaths and over a third of disability in LMICs could be addressed by the implementation of effective emergency and critical care [2]. Multiple other actors and stakeholders have also urged immediate action to identify and intervene on preventable injury [7, 9–11].

A multifaceted approach is required to reduce preventable trauma mortality within LMIC health systems. This involves a detailed assessment of institutional processes and protocols to identify and mitigate common barriers in patient care. For example, health systems can engage in a systematic review of death cases to uncover gaps in care, infrastructure deficiencies, and systemic weaknesses [10, 12, 13]. This locally-driven approach ensures that interventions are tailored to the specific needs of the population, allowing for sustainable and impactful health system improvements.

Previously, in April 2022 a panel of local clinicians reviewed a cohort of 138 deaths due to injury in the Western Cape of South Africa and determined that 45% were preventable or potentially preventable [14]. We conducted a repeat appraisal of trauma deaths from 2023 and here we present results and accompanying health system priority recommendations from this interval, expert panel review of injury deaths.

Methods

Setting and population

The patient cases utilized in this preventable death review were identified based on an ongoing study in the Western Cape Province of South Africa, titled Epidemiology and Outcomes of Prolonged Trauma Care (EpiC). EpiC is a longitudinal, observational cohort study that enrolls adult (age ≥ 18) patients who experience moderate to severe trauma and receive care within 24 h of injury at one of six Western Cape Government Facilities or Western Cape Government Emergency Medical Services (EMS) all within the same trauma referral pathway [15]. Rural health facilities include primary level Ceres Hospital, and Worcester Regional Hospital. Urban health facilities within the Cape Town metro area include Site B Community Health Centre (CHC), Delft CHC, Khayelitsha District Hospital, and Tygerberg Tertiary Hospital. For all unnatural deaths in South Africa an autopsy is performed and a postmortem report prepared by Forensic and Pathology Services (FPS) [16]. Deaths from traumatic injuries between January 1-December 31 2023 and meeting EpiC study inclusion criteria were eligible for review [15]. EpiC excludes patients with injuries from toxicologic exposures, envenomations, drownings, and prisoners. Dead on scene, prior to any contact with the health system, cases were eligible for inclusion if an injury related death occurred within the EpiC catchment area. A convenience sample of dead on-scene cases was provided by FPS, as inclusion of dead on scene cases remains best practice for preventable mortality reviews by including all traumatic deaths, not only those that survived to receive care [12, 17].

Expert panel review process

In accordance with the WHO guidelines for preventable death reviews, an expert panel was convened to review included cases [12]. The expert panel consisted of 30 clinical providers from the Western Cape representing all EpiC facilities and a broad range of medical specialties including prehospital, family medicine, emergency medicine, intensivists, trauma surgery, orthopaedic surgery, neurosurgery and forensic pathologists. The clinical records including EMS, hospital clinical notes, radiology, surgical reports and postmortem reports were collated for each decedent. Panelists were each assigned 5–9 cases and given all available patient records to review prior to the panel convening. On April 17–18, 2024, the panelists were divided into three rooms, each with representation from both rural and urban facilities, a variety of specialists, forensic pathologists and prehospital practioners. A summary of each case was presented to the group, with clinical notes available if needed. After thorough review of case details, the expert panel engaged in a consensus discussion classify each case into one of the following categories: preventable; potentially preventable; non-preventable with improved care; non-preventable; or indeterminate. Definitions for the level of preventability were obtained from previously published preventable death reviews [12, 15, 18]. (Additional File 1) A case was considered preventable if the death could have been prevented within the existing health system with current resources and policies. As part of the review for each case, the panel discussed health system factors impacting care delivery and made recommendations for improvements in clinical care delivery. The recommendations from the expert panel were used in the study given the panel was comprised of key stakeholders, system managers, and clinical experts from the health system.

Data collection

Demographics, injury mechanism, physiologic cause of death and preventability were collected for all decedents. Physiologic cause of death as classified into the following categories: catastrophic tissue destruction (CTD), central nervous system (CNS), haemorrhage, multiple organ failure or sepsis (MOF + S), comorbidities and other. This classification system was previously developed and adapted through a Delphi process for this context and used in the inaugural review [14, 15, 19–21]. (Additional File 1) For decedents that received care within the health system, additional information on body regions injured, injury severity, time of injury, and time and health system location of death was collected. Data collectors captured physiologic cause of death, preventability and detailed notes from panelist discussions and recommendations for improvement for each case.

Data analysis

A multi method approach was utilized: (1) a quantitative analysis of decedent characteristics and preventability and (2) a rapid qualitative analysis of the recommendations for improvement from the panel. Decedent demographic and injury characteristics were analyzed quantitatively using descriptive statistics and underwent a comparative analysis, where injuries were categorized into preventable and potentially preventable compared to non-preventable with or without improved care or indeterminate. Wilcoxon rank sum and Chi-Square analysis were performed to compare preventability of all deaths. Significance was determined using a p-value of ≤ 0.05. We executed all analyses using SAS Software (Version 9.4, SAS Institute Inc).

The health system factors contributing to the death and panelist recommendations were analyzed qualitatively using descriptive rapid qualitative analysis [22]. Two authors (JD and MR) analyzed the notes from the expert panel review and each recommendation was categorized and mapped to a matrix that was comprised of levels within the health system and general categories for improvement such as education, protocols and resources. The qualitative matrix from the inaugural 2021 review was simplified for this review by collapsing thematic codes within categories while maintaining the overall structure to facilitate comparisons over time. (Additional File 2) Recommendations were coded according to the following levels and categories:

Codes were descriptively categorized into themes and grouped by sub-categories including preventable vs. non-preventable and physiologic mechanism of death.

Results

The consensus panel reviewed a total of 210 deaths, of which 49 (23%) deaths were pronounced on-scene and did not receive clinical care. An additional 47 deaths from the EpiC study were eligible for inclusion but not reviewed by the expert panel due to missing clinical records at the time of the review. The median age was 32 years, and the majority were male (181, 86%) (Table 1). Penetrating injuries were the dominant injury force type (97, 46%), followed by blunt force injuries (83, 40%). Stabs/strikes were the leading mechanism of injury representing 39% (n = 81) of deaths. Other leading mechanisms were firearms (61, 29%) and vehicular injuries (41, 20%), predominantly pedestrian versus auto (Table 1). Over two thirds (142, 68%) of deaths reviewed were from interpersonal violence, the combination of stab/struck and firearm injuries. Of the deaths reviewed, 60 (29%) cases occurred in the Cape Winelands (rural) and 150 (71%) occurred in the Cape Town metro (urban). Overall, there was no significant difference in demographics, injury force type, or mechanism between the preventable and potentially preventable death groups as compared to non-preventable and indeterminate. Only 1 (1%) dead on scene case was classified as indeterminate, due to insufficient information for the panel to determine preventability.

Preventability

Of all deaths reviewed, 23 (11%) were classified as preventable and an additional 47 (22%) as potentially preventable (Table 2). Of the 161 in-hospital and ambulance deaths, 64 (39%) were preventable or potentially preventable while 43 (88%) of the 49 dead-on-scene cases were non-preventable.

Preventability by health system and Dead-on scene cohorts

Among preventable and potentially preventable deaths, the most frequent panelist recommendations for priority action items included enhanced trauma care training for frontline providers with a focus on adequate early resuscitation, recognition of severe injuries, and ongoing monitoring of patients to identify signs of clinical deterioration. The panelists noted that within the health system, those most often providing initial resuscitation are junior doctors who have often not received formal training in trauma care and are working in clinical settings with limited access to critical resources such as blood products, diagnostic imaging including X-ray and Computed Tomography (CT), and operating theatres.

Panelist recommendations identified from review of cases determined to be non-preventable and non-preventable with improvements were predominately at the public health level, specifically a need for primary prevention of interpersonal violence through community-based interventions. The panelists also reflected on how community access to firearms and alcohol contribute to the high burden of injuries. For non-preventable cases that survived to the hospital, the panelists recommended development of system-wide palliation protocols, as well as additional clinical guidelines to inform both decision making and resource utilization.

Physiologic cause of death

Among all deaths reviewed, the leading direct physiologic cause of death was determined to be haemorrhage (57, 27%). The largest proportion of preventable deaths were from haemorrhage (27, 39%) and MOF + S (25, 36%). Non-preventable deaths were most frequently from CNS injuries including the brain and brain stem (46, 33%) and CTD (37, 26%) (Fig. 1). For those who survived to ambulance or hospital care, the leading physiologic cause of death was distributed across three major categories: CNS (45, 28%), haemorrhage (42, 26%), and MOF + S (35, 22%). Among those who died on scene, the leading physiologic causes of death were CTD (23, 47%) and haemorrhage (15, 31%). The other category includes airway, tension pneumothorax, comorbidities and sequalae of injuries as the most frequent physiologic cause of death.

Of those that died from CTD, the injured body region associated with death was most commonly the head (59%), followed by the thorax (34%). Among those that died from hemorrhage injuries were most frequently in the thorax (39%) and abdomen (27%) (Fig. 2). In decedents that died from MOF + S, injuries were more evenly distributed between abdominal (22%), head (20%) and external (19%) body regions. Thermal injuries are coded in AIS as external, or skin, injuries.

Body regions associated with injuries contributing to death, by physiologic cause of death

Among all deaths, the median time from injury to death was 7.8 h. The physiologic causes of death with the shortest times from injury to death were CTD (0.5 h) and hemorrhage (2.9 h) and the longest in decedents that died from MOF + S (200 h, 8.3 days) and CNS (20.8 h).

All CTD deaths were determined to be non-preventable, as defined in prior preventable death reviews. Panel recommendations for CTD deaths were largely focused on primary prevention of violence in the community. The panelists also discussed the need for increased safety for both pedestrians and motorists on roadways, noting that many pedestrians are killed along major roads and highways.

For preventable CNS and hemorrhage deaths panelist recommendations included increased access to CT at the district level to reduce the time to diagnosis and surgical intervention if needed. For both CNS and hemorrhage deaths, the panelists also identified a need for junior clinicians to have earlier access to senior and/or specialist providers to assist in transport and clinical management decision making which is particularly more challenging on nights and weekends when most trauma patients arrive in the emergency centre (EC). The panelists noted significant variability in management for patients who succumbed to CNS injury despite costly resource and time utilization to identify the nonsurvivable head injury. They recommended a system wide protocol for prognostication and palliation of head injured patients that could be implemented at all facilities.

The majority (26, 72%) of MOF + S deaths were determined to be preventable. Panel recommendations included the development and implementation of unit level protocols to (1) identify MOF and Sepsis in a timely manner and (2) guide administration of appropriate and early antibiotics. A frequent recommendation for MOF + S deaths was provider training in early-resuscitation and increased access to ICU beds and operating theatre (OT), to reduce the risk of delayed complications from infections or MOF.

Health system deaths

Among the 161 deaths reviewed that had contact with the health system, 43 (26.7%) received initial care at a Community Health Centre (CHC) or rural district facility with no access to CT or trauma OT and extremely limited or no access to blood products. 74 (24.3%) presented to an urban district or rural regional facility with CT access only on weekdays and limited OT and blood products. There was no significant difference in preventability by initial facility (Table 3).

Within the health system death cohort, 33% died in first six hours after injury, 32% between 6 and 48 h, 10% in 48–96 h, and 25% after 4 days. The median time from injury to death was 17.1 h (IQR: 98.6 h). Early deaths (< 6 h) were due to CTD (9%) and haemorrhage (26%). Late deaths (> 48 h) were most often due to MOF + S (22%) and CNS (28%). There is a higher proportion of non-preventable deaths among those with early deaths (i.e., < 6 h) compared to late deaths, with 53% of the deaths occurring after 24 h preventable compared to 21% of deaths that occurred in less than 6 h (Fig. 3). A second peak of preventable deaths occurred around 8 days (192 h).

The majority (100, 62.1%) of deaths occurred in the EC, which includes the initial EC and EC at the second facility if the decedent was transferred for a higher level of care. Among deaths that occurred more than 48 h after injury, 14 (8.7%) occurred in the EC (Fig. 4). The next most frequent unit of death was the ward (30, 18.6%) followed by the ICU or OT (29, 18.0%).

The most frequent panellist recommendations for the health system were trauma care education for frontline emergency care staff, increased capacity for CT, OT and monitoring of patients, and standardized health system protocols for EMS, including activation for critical inter-facility transfers, transport directly to a tertiary facility for critical injuries, and prioritization of trauma patients. A common theme among preventable deaths was inadequate initial resuscitation by junior clinicians, including poor airway management, delays to critical interventions and poor recognition of severe injuries. Cost-effective, context-appropriate trauma care education for all frontline providers, as all junior doctors work in ECs, was recommended in almost half of all cases.

Another theme among preventable deaths was patient decompensation over time that was unrecognized due to an inability to monitor patients, due to a lack of both nursing staff and equipment, especially in the EC while awaiting diagnostics. The panelists recommended designating high acuity care areas within hospital units and supplying those areas with staff and monitoring equipment. Almost half (30, 43%) of preventable deaths discussed the need for monitoring and/or high care units.

The panelists also identified the multi-factorial ways that delays to care contributed to preventable deaths. This includes delays due to needing EMS transport to higher level of care, limited access to CT at all levels within the health system and limited access to trauma OT. Delays were particularly noted as protracted waits for EMS transport to higher level of care, 66 (41%) of health system deaths included an inter-facility transfer (IFT). The need for system wide protocols for EMS transport of severe injuries directly to a tertiary facility and centralized EMS IFT coordination was identified. The panelists discussed the challenges with implementing EMS protocols including the relationship between EMS and facilities as well as the increased rates of violence against EMS by the community on scene calls.

Discussion

In April 2024, a multi-disciplinary expert panel of 30 physicians and prehospital professionals in Western Cape, South Africa convened to review 210 traumatic deaths. Among all deaths reviewed, 33% were deemed preventable or potentially preventable. The leading physiologic cause of death was hemorrhage from torso injuries, both overall and among preventable cases. Non-preventable deaths were more often caused by CNS and CTD. Penetrating injuries from interpersonal violence were the predominant injury type with stabs/strikes representing 39% of deaths followed by firearms and vehicular-associated injuries.

This review of deaths from 2023 showed an improvement in preventability to 33% from 45% previously reported in the inaugural 2021 review in the same health system [14]. In this review, among the 161 cases that received pre- or in-hospital care, 39% were deemed preventable or potentially preventable. The largest proportion of preventable deaths occurred at the tertiary urban hospital (31%), a decrease from 67% of preventable deaths at the tertiary facility in the 2021 review [23]. One explanation is the addition in 2023 of patients initially scene at a CHC that were transferred to either the urban district or tertiary facility. Among deaths that occurred within the health system, most occurred in the EC (60%) including the receiving EC after EMS transfer. This is an increase from the 2021 review where 46% of deaths occurred in the EC and may be due to the inclusion of CHCs in the 2023 review which increases the proportion of the patient cohort transferred to the tertiary facility [23]. It is well known that pre-hospital stabilization, and initial triage and intake into ECs are critical periods with the potential to influence patient outcomes after traumatic injury [24, 25]. Early recognition and management of critical injuries is associated with reduced morbidity and mortality, reduced complications (e.g. sepsis) and shortened hospital lengths of stay [26–28]. Timely access to surgical procedures was a key recommendation from the Lancet Commission on Global Surgery [28]. Despite this global mandate, a 2025 meta-analysis identified a paucity of implemented interventions, many of which focused on educational interventions alone [29].

While penetrating injury was the predominant injury type overall, among the preventable deaths almost half (49%) were due to blunt injury mechanisms. Hemorrhage was the leading physiologic cause of death both overall and in preventable cases – consistent with current literature which finds that hemorrhage accounts for 30–40% of global injury-related deaths and is the physiologic cause of death for almost half of prehospital deaths [30–32]. As compared to cases reviewed in 2021, the proportion of early deaths (< 6 h) was smaller, 33% in 2023 compared to 41% in 2021, with more deaths in 2023 occurring between 6 and 24 h (32% vs. 27%) [23]. With regards to physiologic mechanism of death, the proportion of preventable deaths from MOF + S (37%) and hemorrhage (26%) remained unchanged from the 2021 review [14]. Access to essential blood products, non-blood product medical treatments, specialty surgical consultants, and OTs is crucial in this early window to reduce preventable mortality [33, 34]. Patient monitoring, including hemodynamics and clinical status, is imperative for the identification of acute hemorrhage and to ensure timely intervention. Similar recommendations were made during the 2021 expert panel review and persist as core recommendations due to the lack of change in resource availability and training of providers within the health system.

The second most frequent preventable physiologic cause of death was multi-organ failure or sepsis (12%). These deaths were more likely to occur later in a patient’s clinical course, >48 h post-injury, most often in hospital wards and/or intensive care units. Major traumatic injuries requiring hospitalization are at heightened risk of secondary medical pathologies including organ dysfunction and healthcare associated infections due to multiple factors including post injury robust inflammatory response, bacterial contamination of wounds, and need for invasive therapeutic interventions [27, 35, 36]. Such complications are more likely with delays in early care, under-resuscitation, and prolonged times to definitive care [26, 27, 37]. Education on diagnosis and monitoring for these conditions is critical to reduce late trauma mortality. There have been multiple calls to action regarding the need for standardized, efficient, cost-effective, and high-quality trauma protocols worldwide particularly in areas with disproportionately high trauma burden such as South Africa [2, 26, 38, 39]. Yet, the impact of these interventions may be limited by staffing capacity, missing and/or poor adherence to wound care and antibiotic treatment protocols, and variable access to OTs to address wound contamination.

The largest proportion of non-preventable deaths were attributed to central nervous system injuries, such as exposed brain matter and brainstem herniation (22%). These deaths occurred a median of 20 h after injury and were noted to be resource intense for both diagnosis (e.g. IFT for CT imaging) and hospital admissions for palliation. Limited patient monitoring facilitates early identification of secondary injury, such as hypoxia and hypotension, known to contribute to brain injury mortality [40]. One review of TBI patients seen at a tertiary facility in Uganda found referral to be an independent risk factor for mortality likely due to secondary brain injuries while awaiting transfer [41]. The expert panelists in this review identified a need for system wide protocols to facilitate early diagnosis and prognostication of traumatic injuries. Griswold and Rubiano published similar recommendations for head injuries, noting a need for diagnostic protocols appropriate for both context and multiple levels of resources [42, 43].

Primary prevention strategies at the community level remain foundational to reducing morbidity and mortality from injury. As noted in the Western Cape Provincial strategic plan, the social determinants of health, including access to deadly weapons and controlled substances (alcohol and drugs) are inherently woven into the existence of community violence [44]. A community based intervention in Khayelitsha in the Western Cape found reduction in exposure to inter personal violence events after an urban upgrading intervention [45]. A 2024 analysis comparing the injury burden in South Africa in 2009 to 2017 showed that implementing policies and regulations targeting risk factors for injury-related deaths helped reduce road traffic mortality rates in South Africa [46]. One such risk factor is alcohol, with additional evidence from the alcohol sale bans during the COVID-19 lockdowns were associated with an over 50% decrease in the trauma burden at the tertiary facility, which explicitly links the consumption of alcohol and the incidence of injury [47]. There is work still to be done to reduce injury-related mortality. These data highlighted areas for improvement in local trauma system functions to prevent excess mortality and manage non-survivable injuries.

Limitations

The decedents reviewed by the expert panel do not represent all injury deaths within the Western Cape health system. Eligible cases were limited by EpiC study inclusion criteria and availability of clinical records. Cases with missing clinical records did not follow a pattern or represent a specific injury type. Review of paper records is challenging due to handwriting, poor documentation and the potential for missing parts of the record. The EpiC study staff and expert panelists are familiar with these paper records which were available during review days if additional clarification was needed. While provided at random, dead on-scene cases may not have been representative of all dead on-scene cases. Analysis of dead on-scene cases was limited to information available in the postmortem reports which do not include time of injury. Recommendations for improvement were identified through expert opinion and were limited to inuts from panelists available for the review process.

Conclusion

A multi-disciplinary expert panel in Western Cape, South Africa reviewed 210 traumatic deaths and assessed that 33% were preventable or potentially preventable. Key panelist recommendations for health system improvements include: (1) trauma care training for all frontline providers, (2) development of protocols for diagnosis and prognostication of head injuries inclusive of increased access to CT imaging at urban district and rural regional facilities (3) EMS transport of severely injured patients directly to the tertiary facility and reduced EMS waiting times for inter-facility transport (4) system wide protocols for palliation of non-survivable injuries. Local implementation of these recommendations may help avert future trauma deaths.
