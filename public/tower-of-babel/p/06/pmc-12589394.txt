Study on effective frequency range of wheel-rail excitation for elevated high-speed railway
Authors: Mengting Xing, Juxiang Zhu, Caiyou Zhao, Yu Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The main frequency bands typically considered in studies on the environmental vibration of high-speed railways often neglect high-frequency components, whereas a comprehensive and reliable excitation source is essential for accurately capturing the vibration characteristics of structural components. Using the CRTS III slab ballastless track and a simply supported box girder bridge as examples, this study develops a 3D slab track-viaduct frequency domain analysis model to investigate the vibration transfer characteristics of the track and substructure under wide frequency input excitations. The model is validated and its computational accuracy verified by comparing field test data with extracted simulation results. A method for determining the effective frequency range of wheel-rail excitations based on the statistical analysis of transferred energy is proposed, and the influence of varying fastener stiffness and vibration isolation pad stiffness is studied. The results show that the environmental vibration induced by the propagation of mid-to-high frequency energy in the bridge cannot be entirely disregarded. When evaluating high-speed railway environmental vibrations, the cutoff frequency band corresponding to a transmission energy threshold greater than 90% should be selected as the effective frequency range of wheel-rail excitations to enhance the accuracy and reliability of the results. Short wavelength excitation sources below 0.3 m remain significant in predicting high-speed railway environmental vibrations, and the potential vibration effects of broader frequency domain wheel-rail excitations, particularly those with wavelengths shorter than 0.07 m, on the bridge and substructure should also be considered.

Introduction

In China’s high-speed railway network, elevated structures are widely adopted owing to advantages including reduced construction cost, ease of implementation, and preservation of arable land1. In addition, for elevated high-speed railways, surface irregularities and impact effects at the wheel-rail interface produce a broad wavelength range of excitations, including short wavelength irregularities such as rail corrugation and uneven weld joints. The reduced contact time for trains passing over such irregularities results in wheel-rail excitations characterized by a broad frequency range and high energy content. These excitations propagate through the track structure and its subcomponents into the surrounding soil, potentially generating substantial environmental vibrations that may threaten the long-term safety and serviceability of nearby buildings and infrastructure, while also adversely affecting the quality of life of residents2–4.

In predicting and assessing environmental vibration induced by elevated rail transit, researchers have developed vertical dynamic interaction models of the train-slab track-bridge system based on vehicle-bridge coupled vibration theory5. Finite element methods have been applied to construct prediction models for vibrations generated by elevated railways6. By integrating multibody dynamics with finite element analysis, coupled vibration models for urban rail transit systems have been established7. Moreover, semi-analytical methods have been proposed that combine in-situ measurements of ground vibration responses, soil properties, and train loads8; while artificial neural networks have been introduced for dynamic modeling of bridges and underlying soil layers9. Field measurements conducted revealed short-wavelength effects and vibration amplification phenomena in ground vibrations induced by trains operating on elevated bridges10–12, whereas other studies analyzed vibration attenuation and spectral characteristics of high-speed railway bridge sections through long term monitoring13–15. Most existing studies adopt track irregularity spectra with wavelengths exceeding 1 m as input excitations, thereby focusing primarily on low-frequency structural responses. However, in addition to low-frequency components, mid- and high-frequency vibrations may also arise, which can contribute to component degradation and local modal resonance, and thus indirectly intensify vibration and noise problems that should not be overlooked.

Significant efforts have also been devoted to investigating the transmission characteristics of track-bridge systems16–20. Li et al. studied vibration transmission through the track and substructure based on field measurements of wheel-rail interactions during high speed train operation21. Liu et al. developed a vertical track-bridge coupled finite element model to analyze vibration transmission along the beam and validated the model through hammer tests on a simply supported steel concrete box girder22. Moliner et al. conducted a comparative analysis of vertical vibration transmission in typical elevated track structures using both theoretical modeling and mainline field testing, and examined the influence of different parameters on transmission characteristics11. While these studies analyzed the time frequency responses of track-bridge systems, few have addressed the vertical frequency domain transmission and attenuation behavior between structural components, and systematic quantitative evaluation from a vibration energy perspective remains rare.

To address this gap, this study proposes a method for identifying the effective frequency range of wheel-rail excitation based on a cumulative probability function model. Firstly, the frequency bands currently adopted in environmental vibration and noise research are summarized through a comprehensive literature review. Subsequently, the dynamic response amplitudes and frequency characteristics of structural components under broadband harmonic excitation (1–1500 Hz) are obtained using a 3D slab track-viaduct frequency domain analysis model. Based on the definition of transfer ratio between two points, the concept of cumulative transfer ratio is introduced to assess the correlation between input excitations and output responses. The proposed method for identifying the effective frequency range of wheel-rail excitation is then applied to determine the wavelength ranges of wheel-rail irregularities that should be prioritized in high-speed railway environmental vibration studies. The findings provide a reference for selecting wheel-rail interaction models and their component parameters in theoretical research, and offer theoretical guidance for managing ballastless track irregularities from the perspective of excitation source vibration reduction.

Frequency bands of interest in environmental vibration and noise

Most studies on railway environmental vibration induced by low speed train operations have primarily focused on low frequency excitations, such as quasi-static loads (0–20 Hz) and track geometric irregularities (1–80 Hz), which are typically regarded as the dominant sources of wheel-rail excitation. However, the increased operating speeds of high-speed railways have resulted in higher-frequency, broader-band, and larger-amplitude vibration characteristics of the wheel-rail system. The excitation associated with wheel-rail surface roughness can extend into the mid-to-high frequency range of 200–2000 Hz23. The frequency domain response and transmission characteristics of structures under such complex mid-to-high frequency excitations remain insufficiently understood and require further investigation.

The international standard ISO 263124 and the Chinese standard GB1007025 specify limits for frequencies between 1 and 80 Hz, considering the human body as the vibration receptor. Additionally, the Chinese standard JGJ/T17026 defines limits for vibration frequencies in the range of 4–200 Hz and for secondary radiated noise in the range of 16–200 Hz. Low frequency vibrations (1–80 Hz) that can be directly perceived by humans and higher frequency vibrations (16–200 Hz) that can be indirectly perceived through secondary noise are the two primary categories of railway environmental vibration problems23.

In summary, the increase in train running speed extends the excitation frequency range of wheel-rail irregularities from the low frequency band into the mid-to-high frequency bands. Accordingly, both long wavelength track geometric irregularities (> 1 m) and short wavelength irregularities (0.3–1 m) should be incorporated as input excitations when predicting and evaluating environmental vibrations induced by high-speed railways.

Ballastless track-viaduct system analysis model

Model development

Taking the CRTS III slab ballastless track and the simply supported box beam bridge as the research objects, a three-dimensional (3D) frequency domain analysis model is established using COMSOL based on vibration theory and finite element method, as shown in Fig. 1. The model considers only vertical vibrations, while lateral vibrations are neglected.Fig. 1Ballastless track-viaduct system frequency domain analysis model (a) overall view; (b) enlarged view.

Ballastless track-viaduct system frequency domain analysis model (a) overall view; (b) enlarged view.

The Euler–Bernoulli beam model for rails is applicable for studying the mechanical behavior of the track under vertical load excitation when the vibration frequency is below 500 Hz, whereas the Timoshenko beam model, which accounts for rail cross-section shear deformation, is suitable for track dynamic characteristics up to 1500 Hz27. Short wavelength irregularities at the wheel-rail interface can induce mid-to-high frequency vibrations of 500 Hz and above28. In this study, a 3D solid model of the track structure is developed, comprising rails, fasteners, track slabs, self-compacting concrete, isolation layers, and base slabs, wherein the track slabs and self-compacting concrete are combined into a single layer, as shown in Fig. 1b. The rails are modeled as Timoshenko beam elements corresponding to the standard CHN60 profile, and low reflection boundary conditions are applied at both rail ends to minimize the influence of elastic wave reflections on the numerical results. The fasteners and isolation layers are modeled using spring-damper elements to provide realistic vertical constraints and stiffness, which all other track components are represented by 3D solid elements. Model validation

The calculated rail vibration response under harmonic excitation is compared with field measurements of rail displacement admittance collected at a high-speed railway in China29. Under identical modeling conditions, the comparison between simulation results and field data for the rail head displacement admittance amplitude curve is shown in Fig. 3.Fig. 3Rail head displacement admittance amplitude.

Rail head displacement admittance amplitude.

As illustrated in Fig. 3, within the frequency range 1–1500 Hz, simulation results agree well with field data. The overall curve shapes align closely, with clear identification of the rail’s first-order vertical bending resonance peak (frequency fv) and the first-order vertical bending pinned–pinned resonance peak (frequency fp), showing minimal frequency deviation. The first-order vertical bending resonance occurs at 214 Hz, corresponding to the natural frequency of the rail-fastener system, during which the rail exhibits rigid body vibration above the fastener.

Overall, the frequency domain model of the ballastless track-viaduct system established herein effectively captures the vertical vibration transmission characteristics of the track system on the bridge.

Transmission characteristics between structural components

While continuous spectrum analysis reveals dominant frequency distribution and spectral peaks, single-frequency energy content is small and data variability is high. Therefore, one-third octave band analysis is conducted over center frequencies from 4 to 1250 Hz with an acceleration reference of 1 × 10−10 m/s2 to better quantify response amplitudes and frequency distribution. Note that the absolute acceleration magnitudes obtained are primarily used to explore transmission trends.

One-third octave band analysis results of rail, track slab, box girder, and bearing acceleration admittances, along with vibration transmission characteristics, are shown in Fig. 5.Fig. 5Vibration response of components (a) one-third octave band spectrum; (b) transmission characteristics.

Vibration response of components (a) one-third octave band spectrum; (b) transmission characteristics.

As Fig. 5a illustrates, vibration energy decays vertically from rail to bearing across the frequency spectrum, reflecting layered structural nature of the track. However, attenuation amplitudes and rates differ by frequency band, influenced by component modal properties and natural frequencies.

Figure 5b shows that the transfer ratio curve from rail to track slab via fasteners is relatively smooth; values range narrowly between 0.47 and 0.59 below 500 Hz, a 12% difference. Transfer ratios from track slab to bridge deck also remain stable, with maximum and minimum values of 0.95 and 0.70 across the frequency band. In contrast, transfer ratios from bridge deck to bearing vary markedly above 315 Hz, with a 53% difference between maximum (0.77) and minimum (0.24) values.

Hence, excitation energy transferred from rail to track slab predominantly spans 4–500 Hz and 800–1250 Hz bands, where the fastener system effectively reduces vibration, especially within the 500–800 Hz mid-to-high frequency range. Energy transmission from track slab to box girder covers the entire 4–1250 Hz range, while from box girder to bearing, it is limited to 4–315 Hz. This vertical stratification highlights the ballastless track-viaduct system’s significant vibration attenuation. A comprehensive vibration mitigation design should consider the main frequency bands of elastic elements between structural layers to minimize environmental vibration and noise transmitted to the substructure.

Figure 6 shows rail acceleration admittance decreases with increasing fastener stiffness. between 150–400 Hz, admittance grows with frequency until resonance frequency (denoted as fv), after which it declines. Increasing fastener stiffness shifts fv to higher frequencies and reduces admittance amplitude at fv. Bearing acceleration admittance below 100 Hz remains similar across stiffness values, but above 100 Hz it increases with stiffness, exhibiting larger fluctuations near fv.

Figure 7 reveals rail displacement admittance spectra vary slightly under different isolation layer stiffness, with a secondary peak frequency increasing with isolation stiffness (107, 132, 181 Hz for 200, 500, 1000 MPa respectively). Bearing acceleration admittance differs significantly above 100 Hz, increasing with isolation stiffness, especially within 100–250 Hz. These results indicate that reducing isolation layer stiffness or adding damping pads can effectively attenuate mid- to high-frequency vibration transmitted to the bridge.

Effective frequency range of wheel-rail excitation

Analysis of vertical vibration transmission indicates that mid-to-high frequency responses of the bridge contribute significantly to ground-borne vibration and noise in elevated railways. Accordingly, this section quantitatively investigates vibration response levels and frequency-domain features transmitted from rail to bearing via a statistical energy transmission method, providing clearer insight into effective excitation frequency ranges and excitation wavelengths relevant for environmental vibration studies.

Statistical energy transmission method

The statistical energy transmission method involves:

Step 1 Calculating the transfer function from rail to bearing as the acceleration level ratio at bearing and rail at each frequency band center.

Step 2 Evaluating the ‘cumulative transfer ratio (TRc)’ as the sum of transfer ratios over selected frequency bands.

Conclusions

In this study, a numerical model is established to investigate the vertical vibration transmission characteristics of the CRTSIII slab ballastless track-viaduct system and to determine the effective frequency band of wheel-rail excitations responsible for environmental vibration and noise. The model combines a 3D frequency domain analysis framework with a statistical energy transfer-based method to evaluate the correlation between input excitations and output responses, thereby proposing a criterion for defining the effective frequency range. Based on the simulation results, the main conclusions are as follows:As a typical vertically layered structure, the ballastless track-viaduct system exhibits significant vibration attenuation characteristics. However, the environmental effects caused by the propagation of mid-to-high frequency vibration energy from the bridge to the substructure and the surrounding ground cannot be entirely neglected.
