Longitudinal Evaluation of a Low Glycemic Load Dietary Intervention: A Multicentric Observational Study in India
Authors: Prabhat Kumar Agrawal, Ruchika Garg, Ashish Gautam, Nikhil Pursnani, Vineeta Dhyani, Prashant Tiwari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction:Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a chronic metabolic disorder marked by insulin resistance and progressive β-cell dysfunction. Medical Nutrition Therapy (MNT) plays a key role in improving glycemic control and reducing comorbid risks. This longitudinal, multicentric observational study assessed the impact of MNT using Diabexy Atta, a culturally tailored low glycemic load (GL) flour, on clinical and biochemical outcomes in real-world Indian settings.Materials and Methods:A trained team, supervised by a diabetes specialist, supported participants with home-based assessments and ongoing consultations. Recruitment began in July 2023, with the final visit in January 2025. Of 568 enrolled adults, 512 completed the study. Participants were followed quarterly to monitor glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c), FBG, PPBG, liver and kidney function, medication use, and diabetes-related complications. All received personalized diets with Diabexy Atta, and statistical analysis assessed clinical and biochemical changes.Results:Statistical analysis showed significant improvements in glycemic control. Mean HbA1c declined from 8.68% to 6.50% (P < 0.00001), with parallel reductions in FBG and PPBG. Medication use reduced from 59.6% to 41.4%, and complication rates from 28.5% to 12.0% in participant. There was no significant effect on kidney or liver function tests, except for a slight decrease in serum sodium among electrolytes.Discussion:This study suggests that incorporating Diabexy Atta, a culturally familiar low glycemic index food, into MNT is feasible and may improve glycemic and metabolic outcomes in adults with T2DM. Larger and longer studies are needed to confirm these preliminary findings and assess long-term effects.Conclusion:Incorporating Diabexy Atta, low-glycemic-load food, into structured medical nutrition therapy may improve glycemic and metabolic outcomes in adults with Type 2 Diabetes.

INTRODUCTION

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a chronic metabolic condition characterized by the loss of islet β-cell insulin secretion, typically occurring after the development of insulin resistance (IR). It accounts for 96% of all diabetes cases and is an important cause of detrimental health outcomes.[1] According to the International Diabetes Federation in 2025, the prevalence of diabetes was 589 million adults (20–79 years) globally, which can reach 853 million adults by 2050.[2] It leads to both macrovascular diseases, such as stroke, peripheral and coronary arterial disease, and microvascular complications, such as retinopathy, neuropathy, and diabetic nephropathy.[3] The glycemic burden over time is measured by glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c), which determines the risk of microvascular complications.[45] Moreover, individuals suffering from T2DM and their family members typically share a similar lifestyle, which not only predisposes the non-diabetic members to develop diabetes but also increases the risk of cardiovascular disease. Therefore, in T2DM management, family members may also be involved in the care of the individual with T2DM and should be evaluated for their own risk of developing diabetes.[6]

Conventionally, pharmacotherapy is the treatment of choice for T2DM; however, nutrition therapy is an important step of overall management following pharmacological interventions.[78] Healthcare professionals believe in or recommend medical nutrition therapy (MNT) to promote the quality of life of individuals with T2DM and prevent progression of diabetes and its chronic complications. MNT can improve glycemic control by lowering HbA1c level, and when combined with other aspects of diabetes care, it can significantly enhance clinical and metabolic outcomes, thereby reducing comorbid conditions and hospital admissions.[910]

India, with its rich medical and health legacy, has a well-established history of dietary principles and diet-based therapies for managing T2DM. However, over time, these concepts have become fragmented due to multiple studies and diverse clinical recommendations tailored for different regions and populations, a general lack of awareness among healthcare professionals, and a shortage of nutrition professionals familiar with MNT for T2DM. From the Indian point of view, dietary modification should involve a careful account of regional lifestyle factors, culinary variety, economic problems, and local agricultural practices to improve acceptance among T2DM individuals.[11] The MNT should focus on personalized diet plans that are not only clinically appropriate but also tailored to individuals’ needs.[12] Despite the data supporting MNT’s significance in T2DM therapy, there are still several studies that examine the impact of low glycemic flour mixture on both glycemic control and organ function.

Moreover, the long-term effect of MNT on liver and kidney biomarkers in type 2 diabetes is still not clear, particularly in real-world, community-based settings. The primary objective of the present study was to determine the efficacy of Diabexy Atta in improving glycemic indices, particularly fasting and postprandial blood glucose (PPBG), in people with type 2 diabetes. A secondary objective was to evaluate the effect on liver and kidney function indicators, offering more insight into systemic health implications beyond glucose management.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and objectives

This longitudinal, multicentric observational study without control group examined the effect of MNT on clinical, biochemical, and behavioral health outcomes in persons with type 2 diabetes. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee of Sarojini Naidu Medical College and Hospital, Agra (reference number: Care/EC/2023-09). Before initiation of the study, all eligible participants completed informed consent. For this study, a team of research assistants was trained to work under the supervision of diabetes specialist, who provided support to participants in the assessment of clinical and biochemical parameters at their respective locations. Participants were given unlimited access to their dedicated personnel via email and telephone, along with on-demand consultations throughout the study duration. Recruitment began in July 2023, with the last participant visit due in January 2025. The major goal was to monitor changes in glycemic indicators such as fasting blood glucose, postprandial glucose, and HbA1c levels across follow-up periods. Secondary goals include tracking changes in liver and kidney function tests, medication adherence, complication status, and general health behavior.

Study population

A total of 568 adult volunteers with type 2 diabetes were recruited from different geographical locations of India, such as Uttar Pradesh, Odisha, Haryana, Andaman and Nicobar Islands, Andhra Pradesh, Rajasthan, Assam, Karnataka, Bihar, Delhi, Gujarat, Telangana, Punjab, Jammu and Kashmir, Uttarakhand, and West Bengal. To be eligible, participants needed to be at least 18 years old, have a verified diagnosis of T2DM, be able to attend frequent follow-up appointments, and be willing to follow dietary guidance. The research excluded participants who had type 1 diabetes, were pregnant, had chronic liver or kidney disease, or were unable to provide informed consent.

Nutrition intervention and baseline dietary habits

All subjects received structured MNT from certified nutrition specialists. The dietary regimen used Diabexy Atta (Diabexy Atta nuts and seeds mix 40% (peanut, almond, flax, sesame, and pumpkin seed, coconut), fat-free soya flour, isolated soy and wheat protein, wheat flour, lentils, salt) a carbohydrate source with a reduced glycemic load (GL) that was adjusted to an individual’s dietary needs and preferences. Nutritional therapy consisted of meal planning, quantity control, and lifestyle adjustment teaching. Participants were urged to follow the prescribed food pattern during the research period. Baseline dietary habits and physical activity levels were documented. Participants were not required to follow structured exercise programs or specific dietary regimens such as intermittent fasting, although those already practicing them continued and were recorded. Only general advice on moderate daily activity was provided, and no additional weight loss interventions were introduced, allowing assessment of the isolated effect of MNT with Diabexy Atta.

Data collection and follow-ups

The data for the study were obtained at four points with a 3-month interval: Baseline (Visit 0), follow-up 1, follow-up 2, and follow-up 3. Each visit included a thorough evaluation to assess changes in clinical, biochemical, and demographic data. Demographic and anthropometric data, such as age, gender, body weight, and body mass index (BMI), were gathered to track physical health trends and changes in body composition. Clinical data included documentation of medication use (yes/no) and the presence or absence of diabetes-or digestion-related complications. At each follow-up, research assistants collected updated medical records and participant reports. Digestive issues assessed included bloating, abdominal discomfort, gas, constipation, irregular bowel habits, symptoms of gastroparesis (early satiety, nausea), gastroesophageal reflux, diarrhea related to dietary changes, food intolerances, and general indigestion. These parameters helped evaluate gastrointestinal tolerance, potential side effects, and adherence to the dietary intervention.

Biochemical assessments concentrated on essential metabolic and organ function markers. Glycemic control was measured using HbA1c, fasting blood glucose (FBG), and postprandial blood glucose (PPBG) levels, allowing for the study of both long-term and short-term glucose management. Serum values of Serum Glutamic Oxaloacetic Transaminase (SGOT), Serum Glutamic Pyruvate Transaminase (SGPT), and total bilirubin were used to monitor liver function, which helped to detect any hepatocellular abnormalities or stress caused by illness or intervention.

To assess renal function, urea, creatinine, and uric acid levels were assessed at each time point. In addition, electrolyte and mineral balance was investigated by the study of sodium, potassium, chloride, calcium, and phosphorus, since disturbances in these markers might suggest difficulties associated with diabetes or dietary treatments. All laboratory tests were carried out in certified laboratories using standardized, automated clinical chemistry analyzers to assure data correctness, consistency, and dependability throughout the trial.

RESULTS

Patient profile

Out of 568 adult participants enrolled in the study, 512 completed it. The remaining participants were lost to follow-up due to reasons such as being unreachable, out of station, loss of interest, or voluntary withdrawal. The subject group had a mean age of 45.83 years (±11.84), an average weight of 70.59 kg (±12.73), and a mean BMI of 25.54 kg/m² (±5.06), showing a predominance of overweight individuals [Table 1]. The cohort was predominantly males (81.8%). Concerning health outcomes, 28.5% of patients (n = 146) reported complications, whereas 71.5% (n = 366) did not. This distribution demonstrates a reasonably high complication-free rate in the study population. The results provide an in-depth overview of the group’s demographic and clinical features, indicating a middle-aged, predominantly male cohort with moderate body mass and a modest occurrence of comorbidities. This profile provides a context for interpreting treatment effects [Flowchart 1].

This means that the intervention caused changes that were reliable and clinically meaningful throughout the whole follow-up time.

Postprandial blood glucose (PPBG) results show a gradual and statistically significant decline in the measured parameter throughout the course of all follow-up periods [Table 5 and Figure 3]. An early improvement was shown by the mean decrease of 16.77 units (P < 0.00001) from baseline to follow-up 1. Between follow-ups 1 and 2 (P = 0.0009), there was a further decrease of 10.07 units, and between follow-ups 2 and 3 (P < 0.00001), there was a further decrease of 12.04 units. The null hypothesis was strongly refuted by the cumulative difference between baseline and follow-up 3, which showed a noteworthy drop of 38.88 units with a very significant P value (P < 0.00001). The low P values across comparisons and the steady downward trend indicate that the observed changes are unlikely to be the result of chance. These results confirm the efficacy of the intervention or the natural process that results in long-term, sustainable improvement. The comparatively high sample size and small SDs increase the trustworthiness of these findings.

Similarly, SGPT comparisons revealed negligible differences (0.51, 0.27, 1.19) and non-significant P values (0.76–0.48). Total bilirubin also remained stable, with minimal changes (0.01–0.03) and non-significant P values (0.92–0.84). Overall, the findings suggest liver enzymes and bilirubin levels were consistent over time, indicating no adverse hepatic effects or significant fluctuations across the follow-up periods.

DISCUSSION

The present study showed that Diabexy Atta, which contains low GL carbohydrates, may contribute to improved blood sugar control and overall metabolic health in people with type 2 diabetes. Throughout the study, there were clinically significant decreases in HbA1c, fasting blood glucose (FBG), and postprandial blood glucose (PPBG). The total HbA1c reduction of 0.90% observed in this study appears higher than reductions typically reported in diet and lifestyle-based treatment, suggesting a potential glycemic benefit from the intervention. A study carried out in 2019 revealed that LGL and dietary fiber therapies decreased HbA1c by approximately 0.58% in individuals with type 2 diabetes.[13] Similarly, LGL dietary patterns have been shown to produce modest but meaningful improvements in established targets of glycemic control. Similar observation was also made by Chiavaroli et al. against type 1 and type 2 diabetes in 2021.[14] A network meta-analysis also found that low-carbohydrate and LGL diets had the highest probability of improving HbA1c and fasting glucose in T2DM.[15] These findings are similar to the present study, where the use of a culturally acceptable LGL food like Diabexy Atta led to sustained glycemic improvements in a real-world setting.

Liver function parameters in T2DM subjects remained stable, suggesting good tolerability of dietary intervention. Pairwise comparisons showed minimal differences, and statistical analysis indicated no significant alterations. These results suggest that the dietary intervention was safe and well-tolerated. Overall, the findings support a neutral to mildly beneficial effect on liver health over time. These results may represent adaptive hepatic responses to food intervention rather than pathological liver damage. These findings are significant when considered in the context of LGL and regulated dietary therapy for diabetes management. Low glycemic index (GI) can lower hepatic fat mass and SGOT levels in people with Nonalcoholic Fatty Liver Disease.[1617] However, variability in liver enzyme changes has been reported[181920] which may be linked to adaptive responses or transient fluctuations associated with dietary modifications. Hence, long-term follow-up and mechanistic studies are warranted to better understand the hepatic impact of sustained LGL carbohydrate intake in diabetes management. A trend was previously recorded in several studies. According to some studies, sudden dietary changes, particularly those requiring extreme calorie or carbohydrate restriction, may cause temporary increases in liver enzymes without actually causing hepatocellular damage.[21] These observations emphasize the necessity of long-term follow-up and mechanistic research to assess the hepatic effects of sustained LGL carbohydrate intake in diabetes management.

Renal parameters, including urea, creatinine, and uric acid, remained within normal ranges, suggesting preserved kidney function and overall metabolic safety. Apart from a minor decrease in sodium levels, electrolyte profiles were stable. As Diabexy Atta is a plant-based intervention, evidence from previous studies suggests that a higher intake of plant-based protein may reduce the risk of chronic kidney disease in individuals with diabetes.[222324]

An encouraging observation from this study was the reduction in self-reported diabetes-related complications (from 28.5% to 12.0%) and decreased medication use (from 59.6% to 41.4%). The inclusion of Diabexy Atta, a low-GI Indian staple, most likely enhanced compliance, echoing findings by Viswanathan et al.,[13] who underlined the necessity of customizing MNT to regional eating patterns in India.[12] Further, Rachmah et al.[25] argue that culturally aligned nutrition interventions achieve better metabolic and behavioral outcomes due to higher acceptability and long-term adherence. This approach aligns with the American Diabetes Association and Indian guidelines recommending that MNT should be individualized based on cultural preferences, literacy, and socioeconomic conditions.[25]

The multi-organ effects seen in this trial, such as improved glycemia, renal and hepatic indicators, and electrolyte balance, support the systemic influence of organized MNT. Most research on MNT focuses solely on glycemic indicators. However, new research supports its broader physiological impacts. Thomas et al.[26] found that whole-food plant-based diets decrease inflammatory markers and improve endothelial function, indicating cardiometabolic advantages beyond glycemic management.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, this study indicates that incorporating Diabexy Atta, a culturally familiar low GL food, is practical and feasible for individuals with type 2 diabetes. The intervention was well tolerated and showed favorable trends in glycemic control with stable hepatic and renal function. These findings suggest that low-GL, culturally tailored diets may enhance adherence and metabolic health. The observed reduction in medication use further supports its potential role within structured MNT. However, larger and longer-term trials are needed to confirm these promising preliminary results.

The study’s observational design, absence of a control group, potential regression to the mean, reliance on self-reported complications and medication use, non-standardized lifestyle behaviors, and use of non-ideal statistical methods for repeated measures may have influenced the outcomes, and the follow-up duration may be insufficient for long-term assessment. Nonetheless, the results suggest that culturally familiar low glycemic load foods can enhance MNT effectiveness and adherence. Future work should include longer randomized trials with objective lifestyle monitoring and comparative dietary evaluations.
