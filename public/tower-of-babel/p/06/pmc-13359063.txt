Vigabatrin-related MRI changes and tapering strategy in hemispherotomy for infantile spasms: illustrative case

Infantile spasms originating from unilateral structural ­abnormalities constitute one of the most severe early-onset epileptic ­encephalopathies. These are marked by frequent seizures, rapid developmental regression, and a significant risk of enduring cognitive impairments. When the epileptogenic zone is restricted to a single dysfunctional hemisphere, hemispherotomy provides the most effective method for achieving long-term seizure control following the failure of medical treatment.1–4

Vigabatrin remains one of the preferred treatments for spasms of structural origin, including perinatal stroke, cortical malformations, and hemimegalencephaly.5–8 However, its use introduces a diagnostic challenge: vigabatrin can produce characteristic, reversible MRI abnormalities involving the basal ganglia, thalami, and brainstem.9,10

These findings reflect intramyelinic edema rather than true structural progression, yet they may be misinterpreted as disease progression and could delay surgical referral.

Accurate differentiation between vigabatrin-related imaging changes and genuine pathological evolution is therefore essential. This report illustrates these challenges and outlines a practical framework for interpreting MRI findings related to vigabatrin, determining surgical timing, and managing postoperative tapering to ensure accurate assessment of surgical outcomes.

Illustrative Case

Following the hemispherotomy, the patient remained seizure free and continued on vigabatrin despite persistent MRI abnormalities (Fig. 2E–H). One month later, the patient was admitted due to acute respiratory failure, hypokalemia, and hypothermia; during this episode, he required resuscitation. The MRI demonstrated ongoing signal changes indicative of vigabatrin toxicity; consequently, the medication was discontinued, alternative antiseizure therapy was initiated, and a ketogenic diet was commenced. At the 3-month follow-up, the patient remained free of seizures, and neurological examination showed improvements with no further staring spells. MRI showed regression of the previously noted signal alterations (Fig. 1I–L).

Observations

This illustrative case demonstrates how exposure to vigabatrin can complicate perioperative decision-making in infants undergoing hemispherotomy for drug-resistant spasms. The 8-month-old patient with a right perinatal stroke remained pharmacoresistant to vigabatrin and showed MRI changes typical of vigabatrin-related intramyelinic edema involving the basal ganglia, optic pathways, and hippocampus. As reported in prior studies,5,7,11 these findings may mimic structural progression yet are often reversible, requiring caution during surgical planning.

Despite persistent radiological abnormalities, hemispherotomy achieved immediate seizure freedom, although postoperative MRI continued to show drug-related changes without an electroclinical correlate. In the late postoperative period, systemic deterioration and ongoing MRI abnormalities raised concern for cumulative toxicity, prompting discontinuation of vigabatrin, initiation of alternative therapy, and a ketogenic diet.

Consistent with the literature, follow-up imaging showed regression of abnormalities after drug withdrawal, alongside sustained seizure freedom and neurological improvement. This case highlights three key management periods—preoperative pharmacoresistance, postoperative radiological ambiguity, and late postoperative toxicity—and underscores how timely adjustment of vigabatrin influences diagnostic clarity and clinical outcomes.

Lessons

This illustrative case underscores several essential lessons for determining the timing of hemispherotomy in infants treated with vigabatrin.

First, accurate interpretation of MRI findings related to vigabatrin is critical. Contemporary literature favors the term “vigabatrin-associated brain abnormalities on MRI” (VABAM) or “vigabatrin-associated transient MRI changes,” which more accurately describe the reversible, nonprogressive, and often asymptomatic nature of these findings.9,12,13

In our patient, the VABAM raised several important differential considerations, all of which were systematically excluded. Hypoxic-ischemic injury was unlikely given the absence of perinatal distress, cortical injury, or elevated lactate on MR spectroscopy.14 Postictal or status-related changes were also considered; however, the imaging abnormalities were symmetric and did not correspond to a focal seizure onset zone, making this explanation improbable.15 Deep venous sinus thrombosis with secondary thalamic congestion was ruled out through normal MR venography without evidence of venous flow abnormalities or hemorrhage.16 Metabolic and mitochondrial disorders were suspected because of the 16p13.11 microdeletion, but not confirmed.

Leigh syndrome and mitochondrial encephalomyopathy with lactic acidosis and stroke-like episodes were excluded based on the lack of progressive neurological decline and normal lactate levels.17

Additional metabolic encephalopathies, such as urea cycle defects, organic acidemias, and maple syrup urine disease, were not supported by the clinical presentation or laboratory evaluation.18 Infectious or inflammatory encephalitides were similarly unlikely, as the patient exhibited no fever, CSF abnormalities, or contrast enhancement.19 Taken together, the bilateral, symmetric, nonenhancing, and ultimately reversible nature of the lesions strongly supports VABAM as the most plausible explanation in this case.20

Vigabatrin was administered at an appropriate therapeutic dose (approximately 160 mg/kg/day) in our patient, without any clinical signs of toxicity. Follow-up MRI demonstrated improvement rather than progression of basal ganglia and thalamic hyperintensities—features consistent with VABAM rather than structural injury. Routine serum level monitoring was therefore deemed unnecessary. Attempts to taper vigabatrin prior to surgery resulted in a marked worsening of spasms, thereby confirming that the medication remained essential until definitive surgical intervention.

Second, this case underscores the importance of refraining from attributing excessive subsequent radiological deterioration to vigabatrin. The imaging changes that followed occurred amidst severe septic shock in a pediatric patient with a chromosomal abnormality and suspected mitochondrial dysfunction; both factors are well-documented contributors to basal ganglia vulnerability. A multidisciplinary review confirmed a multifactorial etiology, rather than attributing the changes solely to delayed vigabatrin injury. This highlights the necessity of considering systemic illness and metabolic fragility when interpreting radiological assessments.14–19

Third, the patient’s ongoing spasms despite an adequate trial of vigabatrin, coupled with a unilateral structural lesion, provided early and reliable evidence of pharmacoresistance. Postponing surgical intervention in such instances extends exposure to epileptic encephalopathy and adversely affects developmental outcomes.1,2,4,13 Early hemispherotomy continues to be regarded as the most effective intervention for disrupting pathological epileptic networks and preventing subsequent complications and regression.4,21

Even when bilateral radiological abnormalities are present, a unilateral approach is appropriate when the electroclinical onset is lateralized,22–24 provided that bilateral findings are considered drug-related rather than indicative of progressive disease.25,26 Surgical timing is consequently regarded as a neurodevelopmental priority, and strict adherence to established operative principles enhances safety and ensures beneficial long-term outcomes.27

Ultimately, the postoperative management in this case underscores the significance of tailored, physiology-informed therapy. Vigabatrin was promptly discontinued following hemispherotomy due to its lack of efficacy, the cessation of seizures postoperatively, and the necessity to eliminate confounding variables during the period of dynamic imaging changes. The recurrence of contralateral spasms was effectively controlled with topiramate and phenobarbital until the ketogenic diet could be commenced, whereas high-dose corticosteroids and adrenocorticotropic hormone were avoided owing to severe cardiomyopathy. The patient has remained free of seizures since the initiation of the ketogenic diet, and antiseizure medications are presently being gradually tapered.

Collectively, this case demonstrates the importance of precise terminology, careful differentiation between VABAM and true structural progression, early recognition of pharmacoresistance, and integration of metabolic and systemic factors into imaging interpretation. It reinforces that timely hemispherotomy, supported by judicious perioperative management of vigabatrin, offers the best opportunity to halt epileptic encephalopathy and optimize developmental outcomes. Although based on a single case, these observations align with existing evidence and highlight the need for larger prospective studies to validate this management framework.
