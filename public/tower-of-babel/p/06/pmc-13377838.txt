Psychological distress and functional disability in knee osteoarthritis: a cross-sectional analytical study
Authors: Siham Dkhissi, Brahim Demnati, Fahd Idarrha, Youness Abdelfettah, Anass Belbachir, Imad Abkari, Mohamed Amine Benhima
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionKnee osteoarthritis (KOA) is a prevalent chronic condition associated with pain, functional limitation, and reduced quality of life. Introduction

Knee osteoarthritis (KOA) is a common chronic degenerative joint disease and a leading cause of pain, functional disability, and reduced quality of life worldwide. Beyond joint damage, KOA is associated with a significant psychological burden that may influence symptom perception, coping strategies, and functional outcomes [1]. In Morocco and the broader North African region, KOA represents a growing public health concern, compounded by a high prevalence of obesity, type 2 diabetes, and limited access to multidisciplinary pain management and psychological care — factors that may amplify both the functional and psychological burden of the disease in this population.

Previous studies have reported a high prevalence of depressive symptoms among patients with KOA, with psychological distress closely related to pain intensity, activity limitation, and reduced social participation. This relationship is considered bidirectional, as psychological distress may both result from and exacerbate the clinical manifestations of KOA [2, 3].

Although psychological factors are increasingly recognized as important contributors to disease burden, their independent role in functional disability remains debated, particularly when major clinical determinants such as obesity and radiographic severity are taken into account [4, 5]. Clarifying this relationship is essential to inform comprehensive and patient-centered management strategies, particularly in low- and middle-income countries where psychological comorbidities in chronic musculoskeletal conditions remain largely understudied and where data from North African populations are virtually absent from the literature [6].

This study aimed to explore the association between psychological distress and functional disability in knee osteoarthritis after adjustment for major clinical confounders.

Methods

Study design and setting

Cross-sectional observational and analytical study conducted in the Department of Traumatology and Orthopedics B, Mohammed VI University Hospital of Marrakesh, Morocco.

Study population

Adults (≥ 18 years) with symptomatic knee osteoarthritis diagnosed on clinical and radiographic criteria were included. Patients with inflammatory rheumatic diseases, recent knee surgery, cognitive impairment, or unrelated psychiatric disorders were excluded.

Data collection

sociodemographic and clinical variables included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), comorbidities, disease duration, laterality, and Kellgren–Lawrence (KL) grade.

Psychological assessment and quality of life

Psychological distress was assessed using validated instruments. The SF-36 was used to evaluate health-related quality of life, with analysis of the Mental Component Summary (MCS). Depressive symptoms were assessed using the HADS-D and PHQ-9. Anxiety symptoms were additionally evaluated using the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 scale (GAD-7), reported descriptively to characterize the overall psychological burden of the cohort. Given its suitability for patients with somatic diseases, HADS-D was prioritized for inferential analyses, while other scales were used descriptively.

Ethical considerations

The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the University Hospital of Marrakesh. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to inclusion, and confidentiality and anonymity of the collected data were ensured.

Clinical trial number

Not applicable.

Outcomes

The primary functional outcome was the WOMAC function subscale. For analytical purposes, WOMAC subscale scores were coded in the opposite direction, such that higher values indicate better function and lower symptom severity. Psychological measures were considered explanatory variables. Pain was assessed using the WOMAC pain subscale.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software, version 4.5.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria, 2025). Quantitative variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range, according to their distribution, while qualitative variables were presented as frequencies and percentages.

The normality of continuous variables was assessed using graphical methods and the Shapiro–Wilk test. Group comparisons were conducted using the student’s t test or the Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate. Correlations between psychological measures and WOMAC scores were assessed using Pearson or Spearman correlation coefficients.

Multivariable linear regression analysis was performed to identify determinants of functional disability (WOMAC function subscale), adjusting for age, body mass index, and radiographic severity. Two complementary multivariable linear regression models were constructed. Model 1 adjusted for age, body mass index, and radiographic severity. Model 2 additionally included WOMAC pain intensity as a covariate to examine the extent to which pain accounted for the association between depressive symptoms and functional status. Variables included in the multivariable model were selected based on clinical relevance and prior evidence. A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Given the cross-sectional and observational nature of the study, a formal a priori sample size calculation was not performed. The study should therefore be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating. The sample size reflects the number of eligible patients recruited during the study period.

Results

Study population

Seventy-two patients were included (mean age 58.5 ± 12.9 years; 69.4% women). Mean BMI was 27.4 ± 2.7 kg/m². Most patients had KL grade III–IV disease (79.2%), and bilateral KOA was present in 59.7%.

Regarding comorbidities, type 2 diabetes mellitus was present in 44.4% of patients, and arterial hypertension was reported in 40.3%. The main demographic and clinical characteristics of the study population are summarized in Table 1.

Multivariable regression

Two complementary multivariable linear regression models were performed to examine the determinants of functional status, with the WOMAC function subscale as the dependent variable. As WOMAC scores were reverse-coded, higher values indicate better functional status throughout.

Model 1 (Table 5) adjusted for age, body mass index, and Kellgren–Lawrence grade, and examined the association between depressive symptoms and functional disability independently of pain intensity, given the conceptual overlap between the WOMAC pain and WOMAC function subscales, both derived from the same instrument. Depressive symptoms assessed by the HADS-D were significantly associated with functional disability (β = −1.14; 95% CI [− 1.95; −0.33]; p = 0.006). Age, BMI, and radiographic severity were not independently associated with functional status. The non-significant positive trend observed for KL grade IV likely reflects the wide confidence intervals associated with the limited sample size rather than a genuine clinical effect. Model 1 explained 15% of the variance in functional disability (R² = 0.15; adjusted R² = 0.08).

Discussion

The present study provides an integrated evaluation of psychological distress and its association with clinical severity in patients with knee osteoarthritis (KOA) within a Moroccan population. Our findings indicate a substantial psychological burden, with a remarkably high proportion of participants exhibiting clinically relevant depressive symptoms according to established screening thresholds. Rather than representing a peripheral comorbidity, depressive symptoms appear to constitute a prominent dimension of the disease experience in this cohort. The observation that all participants (100%) presented with HADS-D scores ≥ 8 warrants specific comment, as this prevalence is notably higher than rates reported in previous studies. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses have reported pooled prevalence rates of depressive symptoms in osteoarthritis ranging from approximately 20% to 34% depending on the population, screening instrument, and diagnostic threshold used [5, 7, 8]. A comparable Moroccan study using the same threshold (HADS-D ≥ 11) reported a prevalence of 31.7% among 410 patients with symptomatic KOA [9].

Several factors may account for the discrepancy observed in our cohort. First, recruitment in a tertiary orthopedic setting inherently selects patients with more severe and refractory disease, introducing referral bias toward individuals with greater overall symptom burden. Second, the advanced radiographic profile of the cohort (79.2% KL grade III–IV) and the long disease duration (median 11 years) reflect a population at high cumulative risk for psychological impact. Third, cultural and contextual factors specific to the Moroccan setting — including limited access to psychological screening and mental health services, cultural barriers to expressing psychological distress, and a high burden of metabolic comorbidities — may have contributed to an accumulation of unaddressed psychological burden.

Furthermore, it should be noted that HADS-D ≥ 8 represents the threshold for ‘possible’ rather than ‘probable’ depressive symptoms. The more stringent cutoff of ≥ 11 was met by 86.1% of participants, which, while still notably high, is more consistent with a clinically severe population. These findings should therefore be interpreted within the specific context of a hospital-based population and are not intended to reflect the general prevalence of depressive symptoms in KOA. Beyond depressive symptoms, the elevated GAD-7 scores observed in this cohort (mean 11.9 ± 3.6) suggest a concomitant anxiety burden, consistent with the frequent co-occurrence of anxiety and depression in chronic musculoskeletal conditions. Although anxiety was not the primary focus of the present analysis, these findings underscore the breadth of the psychological burden associated with KOA and warrant further investigation.

The markedly reduced SF-36 Mental Component Summary (MCS) scores further support the multidimensional impact of KOA on emotional well-being and health-related quality of life. These findings are consistent with a bidirectional model in which chronic pain, progressive functional limitation, and reduced autonomy cumulatively contribute to emotional distress, while depressive symptoms in turn lower pain thresholds, increase pain catastrophizing, and reduce engagement in physical activity — thereby exacerbating perceived disability. This perspective aligns with the growing recognition that KOA should not be conceptualized solely as a structural joint disorder but rather as a complex condition involving intertwined biological, psychological, and social processes [5, 7, 10–12].

Correlation analyses revealed significant associations between depressive symptoms (HADS-D) and both WOMAC pain and function subscales [13–18]. Given that WOMAC scores were reverse-coded in the present dataset, the observed negative correlations indicate that higher depressive symptom severity was associated with poorer functional status and greater pain intensity. These findings align with prior literature emphasizing the close interrelationship between psychological distress and symptom severity in KOA, in which persistent pain and progressive functional limitation may foster hopelessness and depressive cognitions, while depression may in turn amplify symptom perception and reduce functional engagement [19–26].

Two complementary regression models were used to examine the determinants of functional disability. In Model 1, adjusting for age, BMI, and radiographic severity, depressive symptoms assessed by the HADS-D were significantly associated with functional disability (β = −1.14; p = 0.006). However, this association was substantially attenuated in Model 2 following additional adjustment for pain intensity, and was no longer statistically significant (β = −0.42; p = 0.092), whereas pain emerged as the strongest independent predictor of functional status (β = 1.99; p < 0.001) [24, 27–31]. Taken together, these findings suggest that the association between depressive symptoms and functional impairment is largely accounted for by pain severity, rather than reflecting a fully independent effect of depression on function.

The high proportion of explained variance in Model 2 (R² = 0.71) should be interpreted with caution. This inflation primarily reflects the shared item variance between the WOMAC pain and WOMAC function subscales, both derived from the same instrument, and does not represent evidence of a true independent explanatory contribution of the covariates.

The observed attenuation of the HADS-D coefficient between Model 1 and Model 2 (from β = −1.14 to β = −0.42) is conceptually consistent with a potential role of pain in the relationship between depressive symptoms and functional impairment. However, given the cross-sectional design of this study and the absence of formal mediation analysis, this interpretation remains speculative and should not be overinterpreted. Longitudinal studies incorporating formal mediation testing are needed to clarify the directionality and mechanisms underlying this relationship. From a clinical perspective, these findings nonetheless underscore the central role of pain control in modifying disability, and highlight the potential indirect benefits of addressing psychological distress as part of a comprehensive management strategy.

Radiographic severity was not independently associated with functional status in adjusted models. This finding reinforces accumulating evidence that structural joint damage does not fully account for symptom burden in KOA [31–37]. The discordance between imaging findings and clinical presentation suggests that factors such as pain processing mechanisms, psychological context, and behavioral adaptation contribute substantially to functional outcomes. Thus, radiographic grading alone appears insufficient to explain interindividual variability in disability.

Taken together, our findings suggest a hierarchical relationship in which structural damage provides the anatomical substrate, pain represents the primary driver of disability, and depressive symptoms modulate pain perception and coping behaviors. The central role of pain in driving functional impairment underscores the importance of comprehensive pain management strategies that integrate physical rehabilitation and psychological assessment. Rather than viewing depression as an isolated comorbidity, clinicians should consider it part of an interactive symptom network influencing disease burden.

Limitations

Several limitations inherent to the study design and sample should be acknowledged when interpreting these findings. Its cross-sectional design precludes conclusions about causal relationships between psychological distress, pain, and functional status. Furthermore, as the study was conducted in a single tertiary center, the generalizability of the results to other settings or populations may be limited. The restriction of the sample to such a specialized setting, combined with the advanced disease profile of included patients, likely contributed to the extremely high prevalence of depressive symptoms observed (100% with HADS-D ≥ 8), which should be interpreted as reflecting a selected symptomatic population rather than the true prevalence in the broader KOA population. In addition, the absence of a control group without depressive symptoms limits the ability to fully characterize the range of psychological profiles in KOA. A further limitation concerns range restriction in the primary predictor variable: since all participants presented with HADS-D scores ≥ 8, the regression analyses capture gradations of depressive symptom severity within a pre-selected symptomatic group, rather than across the full spectrum of psychological status. This restriction may have attenuated the observed associations between depressive symptoms and functional outcomes, and limits the interpretability of regression coefficients to within-symptomatic-group variation, thereby threatening internal validity beyond mere generalizability. Studies including patients across the full range of psychological symptom severity are needed to confirm and extend these findings.

Psychological outcomes were evaluated using validated self-reported instruments, which are widely accepted in clinical research but remain subject to potential response bias, particularly in patients with chronic pain where negative affect may influence symptom reporting. The limited sample size relative to the number of predictors included in the regression model may have reduced statistical power to detect smaller associations, as reflected by the gap between R² and adjusted R² in the primary model, further supporting the exploratory and hypothesis-generating nature of this analysis. Residual confounding by unmeasured psychosocial or socioeconomic factors cannot be excluded.

Despite these limitations, the use of multiple validated instruments and a multivariable analytical framework strengthen the credibility of the findings, which provide meaningful insight into the relationship between psychological distress, pain, and functional impairment in knee osteoarthritis within this exploratory context.

Conclusion

Knee osteoarthritis is associated with a high psychological burden and functional impairment. Depressive symptoms were closely linked to pain and functional status; however, their association with functional limitation was largely attenuated after adjustment for pain intensity, suggesting that pain may play a central role in this relationship. These findings highlight the need for integrated management approaches targeting both pain control and psychological assessment, and underscore the importance of developing culturally adapted, multidisciplinary care pathways for patients with KOA in Morocco and similar low- and middle-income healthcare settings.
