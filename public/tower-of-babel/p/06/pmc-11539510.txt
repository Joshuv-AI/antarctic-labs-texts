ABSTRACT
AbstractIt is well known that seaplanes have twin floaters. Therefore, it is necessary to conduct a wind tunnel test that aims to determine the performance of twin floaters paired on a mock aircraft model. The twin floaters use the same scale as the mock aircraft, which is 1:6.3 with a length of 1555 mm and a distance between floaters of 668.8 mm or S/L of 0.43. The test plan uses a speed of 65 m s-1 and a Reynolds number 1.5 times the model configuration with flap angle variations of 0°, 10°, 18°, 30°, and 40°. The final test results showed that tabulations and graphs of lift coefficient (CL), drag coefficient (CD), and pitching moment coefficient (CM) with angle of attack (α) variations of −10° to 16°.Keywords: Twin floater, Flap, Lift coefficient, Drag coefficient, Wind tunnel test

Background

The main objective of this paper is to provide a collection of experimental data in the laboratory of wind tunnel tests regarding the characteristics of the twin floater of floatplane which include lift coefficient, drag coefficient, and pitching moment with zero-degree control surface, as well as their effect on the aircraft's glide ratio. The model in Fig. 3 is an existing normal aircraft model, making it easier and cheaper to modify into a seaplane by adding twin floater (catamaran). The results of this experiment provide relevance for further development regarding the production of a seaplane in Indonesia as an island country. The relevance of seaplane development to the development of marine tourism and the security of maritime boundaries. We hope that this data collection is useful for aircraft designers, engineers, pilots, and authority officials. Access to this data is intended to support exploration, analysis, monitoring, modelling, prediction, and decision-making across diverse maritime fields.

The manufacturing process of the twin floater of floatplane model scale 1:6.3 is made from a mild steel ladder box frame which is equipped with 12 ribs with a cross-section shape of the floater which is placed and arranged on a jig and fixture. Then the cavity that occurs between the ribs is filled with resin then the contour model is formed, finished, and painted. The floater model manufacturing process is presented in Fig. 4.

After the finishing process, each floater dimension is then measured, the dimensions are measured to the allowable precision, which is the result of measurements from a CMM with a tolerance of 100 µm as shown in Fig. 5. An Illustration of the model installation in the test section using wing strut suspension is presented in Fig. 6. The two floaters are integrated with the floatplane via the main floater strut and 4 ladders on the left, right, and front and rear as support between the fuselage and the floater, with moment reference point at 25 % MAC of wing model as presented in Fig. 7(a) and (b).

TEST conditions with fixed moment reference point

The aerodynamic test model used in this test is a model of an floatplane and pair of floaters with a scale of 1:6.3. The model was installed in the test section with the upside-down condition. In the model, Q-Flex is installed as a sensor for measuring the angle of attack which is connected to the outside with a sealed cable connected to the Conditioning Unit via an external balance with the measurement accuracy of Q-Flex being 0.001°. The measured values are not affected by the deformation of the suspension model due to aerodynamic loads. A preload weighing 180 kg is installed under the test section and connected using a preload wire to the nose of the test model as presented in Fig. 8(a) and also shows the fixed Moment Reference Point (MRP) position in the centre of the fuselage with wing MAC 25 %. After the model is installed properly, to ensure the position of the model is right in the middle of the test section and the angular position of the model is zero concerning the 2 rotational axes, levelling is carried out using a theodolite and inclinometer. In the test section, there are line marks that are used as reference directions and angles. Meanwhile, reference models usually use a model frame that is parallel to the longitudinal axis of the model. The test model that has been installed in the test section and is ready for testing can be seen in Fig. 8(b).

Data acquisition and data processing are controlled by a unified system which includes an instrumentation system, data acquisition software, and data processing software. During testing, data processing involves applying several corrections. First, a weight correction is made to adjust the model's weight and centre of gravity (CG) so that only aerodynamic forces are recorded. Zero corrections (first zero and second zero) are performed at the beginning and end of the test during offline correction (without wind blowing), each data point then adjusted based on the zero correction value.

Tunnel wall interference correction is used due to the presence of a wall, which causes the formation of a boundary layer. Consequently, the wind speed near the wall is lower than the free stream air velocity, and the blockage effect arises by both solid and wake blockages. Solid blockage occurs when an object obstructs the free stream airflow, while wake blockage is caused by the wake that forms behind the test object. Both of these blockages narrow down the area through which the free stream passes, resulting in a change in velocity according to the continuity equation and Bernoulli's principle. All corrections related the tunnel flow data are applied as correction factors, while tare-force correction is performed at the end of the test. The layout of data acquisition and data processing is shown in Fig. 9(a).

The Indonesian Low-Speed Tunnel (ILST), built in 1984 and operational since 1987, is currently under the auspices of BBTA3-BRIN (Aerodynamics, Aeroelastic, and Aeroacoustics Laboratory, National Research and Innovation Agency). An aerial photo of the ILST is presented in Fig. 9(b).

The wind Tunnel facility utilized for the aerodynamic test section has dimensions of 4 m in width, 3 m in height, and a maximum speed of 110 m s-1 with the overall model weight (floatplane, floater support, and floater) being 285 kg. The data was taken from the loadcell installed on the external balance with the measurement accuracy of each loadcell as presented in Table 2.

Fig. 12(a) shows that the CL, CD, and CM values tend to be constant at Reynolds numbers of 900,000. By repeating the data collection, it was confirmed that the data obtained are consistent with previous results, validating their accuracy. Fig. 12(b), presents a graph of the Reynolds number versus wind speed in a wind tunnel. For CM, the data aligns at Re 0.9 × 106 and above. The maximum Reynolds number achieved was 1.52×106, at 70 m s-1, as presented in Fig. 12(b), although Reynolds numbers ranging from 0.9 × 106 to 1.52×106 can be used, a wind speed of 65 m s-1 was chosen for this test. This decision was made because the large dimensions of the test model cause a significant blockage effect, resulting in vibrations at higher angle of attack.

The Reynolds number check was conducted with various speeds, V0, ranging from 10 m s-1 to 70 m s-1 in increments of 5 m s-1. The second dataset, referred to as Run 31, involved collecting data at various speeds between 45 m s-1 to 70 m s-1 with data collected 3 times at each point to validate the first dataset. The results of the test are to compare the characteristic that occurs between 3 configurations, namely Wing, Body, Tail (WBT), additional landing gear (WBTLG), and additional floater with strut (WBTP0). The lift coefficient graph as presented in Fig. 14(a) shows AoA (α) that the stall angle of the model is 15.53°, 14.72° and 14.69° respectively for the WBTP0, WBTLG, and WBT configurations. The CL shows 1.8391, 1.7209, and 1.7079 for WBTP0, WBTLG, and WBT respectively. When the AoA angle is high, the CL value for WBTP0 is highest because the wind impact on the floater's hull causes additional lift on the model. The drag coefficient shows that the minimum drag that occurs when the aircraft is in cruise condition occurs at angles of 0.22°, 0.24°, and 0.24° for WBTP0, WBTLG, and WBT respectively. Meanwhile, the CD values are 0.0963, 0.052, and 0.00416 for WBTP0, WBTLG, and WBT respectively. So it can be concluded that the WBTP0 configuration has the largest CD value as presented in the drag coefficient graph in Fig. 13(b).

The minimum pitching moment values that occur are at angles of 5.65°, 4.56° and 4.56° for WBTP0, WBTLG, and WBT respectively as shown in Fig. 14(a) where WBTP0 configuration it can be seen that when nose down or nose up, the aircraft is slower to recover at a neutral angle, when compared to other configurations. In the WBTLG configuration, there is not much significant difference in the WBT, but for the WBTP0 configuration, the angle of attack is slightly larger, which means the aircraft must cruise at an angle α of 5.65° with a CD value of 0.1164. The CL/CD graph is an additional graph to determine the glide ratio performance of the floater model (WBTP0) as presented in Fig. 14(b) which shows that the CL/CD value of the WBTP0 configuration is the lowest, namely 8529 and 15,035 for the WBT configuration.

Data from the configuration using flaps for take-off and landing requirements using angles of 10°, 18°, 30° and 40° using the WBTP0 configuration as presented in graphic Figs. 15(a), (b), and (c) for each CL, CD, and CM at varying angles of attack between −10° to 16° with an interval of 1°.

The lift coefficient from the test results using flaps is presented in Fig. 15(a) which shows that using a flap angle of 40 has the highest lift coefficient value of 2.6594 which occurs at an angle of attack of 15.37°, while the CL value for flap angles is 0°, 10°, 18° and 30° are 1.8391, 2.1416, 2.3370 and 2.4239, respectively.

Drag coefficient from test results using various flap angles as presented in Fig. 15(b) which shows that using a flap angle of 40° has the highest drag coefficient value of 0.1858 which occurs at an angle of attack of −5.62° (nose-down), while the CD value for flap angles of 0°, 10°, 18° and 30° are 0.0963, 0.1054, 0.1225, 0.1544 respectively. The results of experiments carried out on the drag coefficient values have the same pattern as the simulation results of other researchers [5,6].

The CM for flap angle variations is presented in Fig. 15(c). All flap angle configurations do not show significant differences in moment, especially when the pitching moment position is close to 0, almost all occur at an angle of attack of around 6°, but when the nose-down position is with a flap angle of 40°, the model with the WBTP0 configuration has a pitching moment value that is the smallest is (+) 0.2379, which means the plane is slower to recover to a neutral position. The configuration when the nose-up is at an angle of 15°, the WBTP0 configuration with a flap angle of 40° has a value of negative (-) 0.3476 and for a flap angle of 0° it has a Cm value of negative (-) 0.2255. When the nose-up condition, it tends to recover more quickly to the neutral position compared to other flap angle configurations, this is due to the drag force on the floater and the leverage moment on the wing due to the flap.

The repeatability graphs for CL and CD are presented in Fig. 16. The quality of the results and the measurement system as a whole can be assessed through short-repeat and long-repeat tests. Each run is a unit for each test point, and within a run series, there may be one or more polar data sets as needed. In a single run, polar numbers can include tasks such as repeating problematic data, checking repeatability, and other related activities.
