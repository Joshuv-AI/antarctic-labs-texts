Comparison of the processing of positive memories technique and supportive counseling for posttraumatic stress disorder: A randomized controlled trial
Authors: Ateka A Contractor, David Rosenfield, Isamar M Almeida, Siyuan Wang, Daniel J Taylor, Nicole H Weiss, M Tracie Shea
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractResearch suggests that positive memory processes influence maintenance, onset, and treatment of posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) symptoms. A novel PTSD intervention–Processing of Positive Memories Technique (PPMT)–addresses positive memory processes as an active treatment ingredient. This study compared PPMT to Supportive Counseling (SC) in a randomized controlled trial to examine their comparative effects on PTSD symptom severity and mechanistic targets (affect) underlying treatment effects. Seventy adults with PTSD (Mage = 31.31 [SD = 13.20]; 80% female participants) were randomized to five weekly PPMT or SC therapy sessions and were assessed at baseline, each weekly session, and 1-week and 3-month follow-up appointments. Measures included the PTSD Checklist for DSM-5 and Positive and Negative Affect Schedule. Mixed effects models indicated no significant overall differences between treatment groups on PCL-5 score improvement (p = .477). However, PCL-5 scores decreased significantly in both groups (ps < .001, d = 1.26 for SC, d = 1.42 for PPMT). Cross-lag mediation analyses indicated that neither PANAS-Positive (p = .735) nor PANAS-Negative (p = .224) scores mediated treatment group differences in PCL-5 scores over time. Post-hoc exploratory moderation analyses indicated that baseline PCL-5 severity moderated results of the primary analysis (p < .001), with participants having higher baseline PCL-5 scores (>31) showing significantly greater improvement in PCL-5 scores in PPMT than in SC (p = .028, d = .78); and PANAS-Positive scores significantly mediated treatment group differences in slopes of PCL-5 scores (p < .05). PPMT did not differ from SC in improving PTSD, although some preliminary evidence suggested that PPMT may be particularly beneficial for individuals with greater baseline PTSD severity, potentially through increasing positive affect.Clinical trials:NCT05523453.Keywords: Posttraumatic stress disorder, Processing of positive memories technique, Randomized controlled trial, Positive autobiographical memories, Affect

Introduction

Difficulties related to autobiographical memory (AM) processes, including encoding and retrieval, as well as beliefs about the purpose of AMs and what impacts their recall, influence the onset and maintenance of multiple psychological disorders, including posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD; Barry et al., 2021; Dalgleish & Hitchcock, 2023; Matsumoto & Watson, 2025). PTSD is characterized by several memory-related symptoms, such as intrusive and fragmented trauma AMs, flashbacks, difficulties recalling aspects of the trauma, a tendency to recall overgeneral and vague AMs, impaired working memory, and deficits in remembering spoken information (American Psychiatric Association, 2022; Honzel et al., 2014; Johnsen & Asbjornsen, 2008). Thus, several therapeutic approaches for PTSD address AM processes, with particular attention to negative AM processes, as direct treatment targets or as tools to enhance therapeutic work (Barry & Hallford, 2024). Examples of such approaches include Prolonged Exposure and Cognitive Processing Therapy (Brewin et al., 2025).

Emerging evidence suggests that positive AMs are important to address in PTSD therapeutic work. Theoretically, the scarring hypothesis suggests that individuals may begin to experience difficulties in positive AM processes (e.g., encoding, recall) after experiencing trauma and PTSD symptoms; whereas the vulnerability hypothesis suggests that difficulties in positive AM processes may contribute to distress after experiencing trauma (Williams et al., 2007). Intensive longitudinal studies have found that trauma survivors who experience more PTSD symptoms report less vivid and less accessible positive AMs in their daily lives (Contractor, Natesan Batley, et al., 2023; Contractor, Slavish, Wang, et al., 2024). Indeed, a scoping review has indicated that trauma survivors with more PTSD symptoms recalled fewer positive AMs (Contractor, Caldas, Dolan, et al., 2022). Critically, when trauma survivors recall positive AMs during times of heightened PTSD severity, they report maladaptive and avoidance-related reactions, including craving for substances, suppression, and engagement in risky behaviors (Contractor, Slavish, Straup, et al., 2024). Such reactions may be due to assumptions that recalling positive AMs may (1) lead to negative mood or fail to increase positive affect (i.e., anticipation of a mood contrast effect), (2) increase the discrepancy between a negatively-valenced current self-identity and a positively-valenced past self-identity represented in positive AMs, (3) be challenging due to an accumulation of and focus on negative experiences (mood-incongruent recall), or (4) result in emotional dysregulation (Matsumoto & Watson, 2025).

However, evidence suggests that repeated and systematic processing of positive AMs helps to improve well-being among trauma survivors (Miguel-Alvaro et al., 2021). For example, experimental studies have shown that participants who recall and narrate details of positive AMs repeatedly with experimenter guidance report less PTSD severity, improved affect and fewer posttrauma cognitions (Contractor et al., 2020; Contractor, Messman, et al., 2023). Further, a recent study on adult community participants with subthreshold or clinical PTSD symptoms found that writing about past positive or traumatic experiences equally contributed to reductions in PTSD symptoms, depression, anxiety and stress from baseline to follow-up (Dawson et al., 2024). Intervention studies similarly show beneficial effects of processing positive AMs. A recent study compared trauma-focused cognitive behavioral therapy with and without a training component to recall specific positive AMs among first responders, and found that participants receiving both treatments reported reductions in PTSD severity (Bryant et al., 2025). Another study examined the effects of a positive memory enhancement training intervention among 27 individuals with major depressive disorder, and found that the participants improved in their ability to relive positive AMs as well as in emotion regulation post-intervention (Arditte Hall et al., 2018). A different study examined the effects of focusing on AMs with positive self-representations (Positive Memory Training) among 100 participants with schizophrenia or schizoaffective disorder and mild depression. Compared to participants receiving treatment-as-usual, participants receiving positive AM training reported less depression pre-to-post intervention (Steel et al., 2015). Lastly, a randomized controlled trial (RCT) compared two traumafocused cognitive behavioral treatments (with and without a module focused on retrieving positive memories) among 91 female survivors of intimate partner violence, and found that participants in both conditions reported decreases in PTSD and depression severity and improvements in overall functioning pre-to post-intervention (Crespo et al., 2024).

Aligned with this empirical evidence and distinct from existing PTSD interventions, the Processing of Positive Memories Technique (PPMT) was developed to improve access to, and the phenomenological qualities of, salient and specific positive AMs (Contractor et al., 2018, 2021). Theoretically, PPMT’s focus on improving access to specific rather than overgeneral memories is informed by the capture and rumination, functional avoidance, and impaired executive capacity and control model (CaR-FA-X; Williams, 2006). This model identifies three mechanisms that contribute to overgeneral memories; these are relevant to trauma survivors and may reduce the retrieval of positive AMs. First, unconstructive rumination on primarily negative, abstract themes can limit access to specific positive AMs, especially among trauma survivors (Michael et al., 2007). Second, a focus on overgeneral memories may help to avoid distress triggered by recalling specific memories; this avoidance can extend from trauma AMs to other memories among trauma survivors (Williams, 1996; Williams et al., 2007). Lastly, deficits in executive functioning and working memory can impair the ability to store and retrieve specific and detailed AMs (Sumner, 2012); this process may be exacerbated by the experience of PTSD symptoms (Harvey et al., 1998). Further, PPMT’s focus on positive memories is informed by a positive psychology framework, which aims to enhance positive experiences rather than solely decreasing negative ones (Parks & Biswas-Diener, 2013); and by the Cognitive Triad model, which highlights the reciprocal relations between emotional experiences and cognitions (Beck, 1991).

In PPMT, the active ingredient is guiding clients to retrieve and narrate details of a highly salient and specific positive AM in each session and helping them focus on positive elements (e.g., thoughts, affect) associated with those AMs. Consequently, clients may begin to focus on positive events in their daily lives and encode additional positive AMs, consolidate and access prior positive AMs, emotionally connect with past positive experiences, identify ways to cope with trauma, and remember times when they have overcome adversities with coping skills that can help with current stressors (e.g., Barry & Hallford, 2024; Goddard et al., 2001). Further, the experience of positive emotions may help clients expand their thoughts and action options to build resources that support coping and better health (broaden-and-build theory of positive emotions; Fredrickson, 2001). By optimizing the processing of AMs and by improving affect and cognitions, these strategies may improve posttrauma well-being (Askelund et al., 2019; Barry & Hallford, 2024). Clients may then share positive AMs with others who they trust and value, which may bolster social support (Barry & Hallford, 2024) and may integrate trauma AMs with other AMs (Ehlers & Clark, 2000). Subsequently, positive AMs may start to become more integral to one’s identity and may help to generate a stronger positive self-view, aiding posttrauma recovery (Brewin et al., 2025). Repeated engagement with emotionally charged material (positive AMs in this case) reduces the dominance of negative AMs to foster adaptive changes in beliefs and schemas (Knowles & Tolin, 2022).

Open label pilot projects have supported the feasibility, acceptability, and therapeutic benefits of PPMT. For instance, one pilot study tested the effects of the 5-session PPMT among 12 trauma survivors who were seeking services at a University Psychology Clinic. Data were analyzed using the reliable change indices and clinically significant change score approach. After receiving PPMT, 67% of the participants reported less PTSD severity and negative affect; and 58% of the participants reported fewer posttrauma cognitions (Contractor, Jin, & Weiss, 2023). Building on this small study, a larger open label clinical study examined effects of a 4-session PPMT protocol among 70 trauma survivors. Pre-to post-intervention, participants reported decreases in PTSD severity (d = −.51), depression severity (d = −.22), negative affect (d = −.26), and posttrauma cognitions (d = −.17). This study also found that decreases in negative affect across sessions mediated effects of PPMT on PTSD severity (Contractor et al., 2025). Although prior pilot experimental and clinical studies have not found that increases in positive affect accounts for the beneficial effects of processing positive memories on PTSD severity (Contractor, Caldas, Banducci, et al., 2022; Contractor et al., 2025), substantial evidence suggests that processing positive memories increases positive affect, and that more positive affect improves posttrauma well-being (Craske et al., 2024; Williams et al., 2022). Given this literature along with the limitations of prior PPMT studies (e.g., no control condition), we cannot rule out the possibility that increases in positive affect may explain PPMT’s effects on reducing PTSD severity. Additionally, across both clinical studies, PPMT was perceived as acceptable, easy to learn and implement, and feasible by treatment recipients (Banducci et al., 2023; Rodenbaugh et al., 2025).

In summary, emerging evidence suggests that processing positive AMs may offer a novel and promising therapeutic approach for trauma survivors with PTSD. While the initial findings are encouraging, further research is needed to strengthen our understanding of PPMT’s effects and broaden its applicability. A key limitation of the current literature is that existing PPMT clinical studies have not used a RCT design with an active control condition, making it difficult to isolate intervention-specific effects from non-specific therapeutic factors or placebo effects (Mohr et al., 2009). Further, these previous studies have not included participants with clinician-determined diagnostic PTSD, and hence we cannot speak to PPMT’s applicability, clinical relevance, and utility for individuals in most need of PTSD interventions. Addressing these gaps, the current study, as an RCT, was designed to examine comparative effects of PPMT vs. Supportive Counseling (SC) among 70 individuals with PTSD. The present paper addresses the two primary aims of the study: (1) comparative effects of the two treatments on PTSD severity and (2) effects of mechanistic, affect-related targets on PTSD severity. Drawing from existing literature (e.g., Contractor et al., 2025; Contractor, Jin, & Weiss, 2023), we hypothesized that 1) the PPMT arm would report greater decreases in PTSD severity compared to the SC arm (and higher PTSD remission rates) as indicated by a Treatment Group × Time interaction, and 2) that improved affect, characterized by more positive affect and/or less negative affect, would mediate the treatment group differences in decreases in PTSD severity across time.

Method

Procedure and participants

The protocol paper by Contractor and colleagues (2024) outlines detailed information on the study design and procedure. The current study was approved by the Institutional Review Board and Institutional Biosafety Committee at the University of North Texas. Eligible participants were 18–65 years old; were able and willing to provide informed consent; had a PTSD diagnosis within the last month (assessed by the Clinician-Administered PTSD Scale for DSM-5 [CAPS-5]; Weathers et al., 2018); reported a working knowledge of English; did not report current or past 6-month suicidal intent, suicidal attempts, or psychosis (assessed by the Mini International Neuropsychiatric Interview [MINI]; Lecrubier et al., 1997; Sheehan et al., 1998); were not receiving psychotherapy and reported no changes in psychiatric medication dosage in the past 3 months; were not currently pregnant; and agreed to be video−/− audio-recorded during therapy.

The study had four phases (see Fig. 1 for the CONSORT diagram). In the Screening and Information Phase, interested individuals provided informed consent and then completed a screener to determine provisional eligibility. During the Intake Interview Phase, provisionally eligible individuals attended an in-person assessment session at the university’s Psychology Clinic to provide baseline data on psychological symptoms, affect, and cognitions and then to determine eligibility with the CAPS-5 and the MINI. In this parallel design, eligible participants were randomly assigned to either PPMT or SC using block randomization (block sizes of two), stratified by baseline PTSD severity (cut-off score of 45 on the PTSD Checklist for DSM-5) and biological sex. A research assistant who was not involved with any other study procedures, in consultation with the biostatistician, generated the random allocation sequence using the R software and randomized participants to the two treatment arms. Further, participants were randomly assigned to one of the 4 (02/2023–01/2024; 06/2024–10/2024) or 6 (01/2024–05/2024) study therapists, using block randomization for therapist assignment, with block sizes of 4 or 6 consecutively enrolled participants, respectively. In the Therapy Phase, participants attended five weekly PPMT or SC sessions and completed assessments on psychological symptoms, affect, and cognitions in each session. In the Post-Therapy Phase, participants attended 1-week and 3-month follow-up assessments, which included surveys on psychological symptoms, cognitions, and affect. Therapists and participants learned of their assigned condition at the start of the first therapy session. Follow-up assessments were conducted by different study personnel who were blinded to the participant’s treatment condition. Participants received up to $95 for the study: $10 for completing the Intake Interview; $5 for providing baseline saliva samples; $25 for completing all therapy assessments; $5 towards transportation costs to attend each session; and $20 and $10 for completing the 1-week and 3-month follow-up assessments, respectively.

Initial responses were received from 717 individuals, and the final sample included 70 participants. The first participant was enrolled in March 2023, and the last participant was enrolled in October 2024 (average enrollment/month = 3.5 eligible participants). The trial concluded once the target sample was enrolled. Of the 36 participants randomized to PPMT, 27 completed all sessions and follow-up assessments. Of the 34 participants randomized to SC, 32 completed all sessions and follow-up assessments. In total, 59 participants (84.3%) completed the entire protocol.

Primary outcome measures

PTSD Checklist for DSM-5.

Administered at all study timepoints, the 20-item self-report PTSD Checklist for DSM-5 (PCL-5; Weathers, Litz, et al., 2013) assessed past-week PTSD severity. The PCL-5 at the Intake Interview was cued to the index trauma endorsed on the Life Events Checklist for DSM-5 (Weathers, Blake, et al., 2013), which is a 17-item self-report measure of traumatic experiences. The other PCL-5 assessments were cued to the stressful event they considered the worst and bothered them the most at the time of the survey. Response options ranged from 0 (not at all) to 4 (extremely). PTSD remission was conceptualized as PCL-5 score <31 (Forkus et al., 2023). The PCL-5 scores have excellent psychometrics (Forkus et al., 2023). Cronbach’s alphas for the PCL-5 total score in the current study averaged .91 over the 8 assessments.

Positive and Negative Affect Schedule.

Administered at all timepoints, the 20-item Positive and Negative Affect Schedule (PANAS; Watson et al., 1988) examined past-week positive and negative affect levels. There were 10 items for each of the PANAS-Positive and PANAS-Negative subscales. Response options ranged from 1 (not at all/very slightly) to 5 (extremely). The PANAS scores have good psychometrics (Watson et al., 1988). Cronbach’s alphas for the PANAS-Positive and PANAS-Negative subscale scores in the current study averaged .89 and .87, respectively, over the 8 assessments.

Study treatments

PPMT, which is based on and integrates positive and symptom-focused treatment models, was administered as a 5-session protocol. Session 1 included a review of informed consent and confidentiality, assessment of symptoms, an overview of PPMT and psychoeducation on PTSD. As a core component of each session, participants were guided to identify one salient and specific positive AM. Positive AMs were defined as referencing important positive events personally experienced by participants, that made them feel good and brought up positive feelings and positive thoughts. Next, participants were asked to describe the event in detail using the present tense and by engaging all senses. To elicit values, affect, strengths, and thoughts (VAST) related to that positive AM, participants were provided with the following questions: 1) “What are the most important values that this positive memory has brought to mind,?” (2) “What are the most important feelings that this positive memory has brought to mind,?” (3) “What are the most important thoughts that this positive memory has brought to mind,?” and (4) “What are the most important personal strengths that this positive memory has brought to mind?” The recall and processing of positive AMs lasted for approximately 20–30 min of each session. Participants recalled details of different positive AMs in each session. Approximately 10 min of each session was devoted to assigning and discussing homework aligned with the study protocol (i.e., homework was not assigned according to the discretion of the study therapist). Homework tasks included listening to an audio-recording of the positive AM narrative daily, completing a VAST log with three-four entries for different positive AMs, and engaging in one behavioral activity that was informed by VAST elements discussed in session. These homework tasks were standardized. During Session 5, study therapists also reviewed progress made by the participant and discussed termination.

SC, adapted from Present-Centered Therapy (PCT; Shea et al., 2003), was administered as a 5-session protocol. Session 1 involved a review of informed consent and confidentiality, assessment of symptoms, an overview of SC, psychoeducation on PTSD, outlining of treatment goals, and introduction to the daily diary worksheet. The daily diary worksheet was assigned as homework every session, and participants were asked to provide information on significant daily activities, problems, stressful situations, or times that were distressing to them. In every session, participants discussed issues listed on the daily monitoring diary and explored any concerns they experienced between sessions. Most of each therapy session was devoted to these tasks. More details are provided in the protocol paper (Contractor, Blumenthal, et al., 2024).

Statistical analyses

Mixed effects multilevel models (MLM) were used to analyze Aims 1 and 2 because they are intent-to-treat analyses that include all 70 participants, regardless of missing data, and are the recommended analyses for longitudinal psychiatric clinical trials (Hamer & Simpson, 2009). We modeled the change over time for outcome over the 7 timepoints from Session 1 to the 3-month follow-up. For each MLM analysis, we first determined the optimal growth curve model (e.g., linear, quadratic, logarithmic, or piecewise) for the change in each outcome over time based on Akaike Information Criteria and Bayesian Information Criteria, since failure to correctly specify the growth curve model can lead to incorrect results (Liu et al., 2012). The error covariance matrix was modeled as unstructured. Each MLM analysis included Treatment Group, Time, and Treatment Group × Time as the primary predictors of outcome. To reduce variance, increase power, and account for the wide range of baseline PCL-5 scores (~11–62), we controlled for both the baseline PCL-5 severity score and the baseline PCL-5 × Time interaction (participants with high baseline PCL-5 scores improved more quickly [p < .001] than those with low baseline scores). PCL-5 baseline severity was defined as the average of Intake Interview and Session 1 assessments since they had different reference traumas. We then analyzed the proportion of participants who achieved reliable change (Jacobson & Truax, 1991) on the PCL-5 at the 3-month follow-up using a Fisher’s Exact Test and analyzed the growth curve of PCL-5 reliable change across the 7 timepoints using Generalized Linear Mixed Models (GLMM) with a logistic linking function.

For Aim 2, we performed within-subjects cross-lag longitudinal mediation analyses that investigated whether PANAS-Positive and/or PANAS-Negative scores mediated the changes in outcome (PCL-5 total score) over time (e.g., the mediators of the Treatment Group × Time interaction). The effect of the treatment group on the mediators was calculated using the same MLMs in Aim 1, but with the mediators as outcomes. Then we calculated the effect of the mediators on outcome by adding both potential mediators (PANAS-Positive and PANAS-Negative) to the MLM in Aim 1. To more accurately estimate the causal effect of each mediator on the outcome, we used a cross-lag mediation analysis, in which the mediator at time “t” predicted the outcome at the next assessment (“t+1”), controlling for the outcome at time “t”. As recommended by Wang and Maxwell (2015) and others, we disaggregated each mediator into its between-person and within-person components by calculating the mean of each mediator across assessments (the between-subjects component) and the deviation of the mediator, at each time point, from its mean across assessments (the within-person component). Significance of the mediated pathway was determined using RMediation (Tofighi & MacKinnon, 2011). Since the participant’s mean on a time-varying predictor is confounded with other between-person characteristics, the effect of the mean on the outcome is not considered causal and is not interpreted. However, the cross-lag effect of the deviations of each mediator at each session (time t) on the outcome at the next session (time t+1) is considered quasi causal when controlling for the outcome at time t. Thus, we only examined mediation through the deviations for each mediator.

P-levels for significance were set at p < .05. Effect sizes are reported for each significant effect. Cohen’s d is reported for the effect sizes for the MLMs, and Odds Ratios (ORs) are reported for the effect sizes for the GLMM. Cohen’s d for the effects involving slope over time in the MLMs were estimated following Feingold (2013).

Power calculations

For Aim 1, we used the MLM power analysis program RMASS3 (http://www.RMASS.org) to calculate the power for our primary outcome (PCL-5 total score). Based on prior PTSD treatment research (e.g., Garcia et al., 2011; Wortmann et al., 2016) and pilot PPMT results (Contractor et al., 2025; Contractor, Jin, & Weiss, 2023), we assumed a 10-point PCL-5 difference between PPMT and SC at the 3-month follow-up timepoint, with a baseline variance of 180. This Treatment Group × Time interaction would be equal to a Cohen’s d = .75. We have 7 potential assessments but assumed a dropout of 30% by the 3-month follow-up assessment. RMASS3 calculated that we would need a total baseline sample of n = 69 (less than our sample of 70) to achieve a power of .95 to detect our expected effect size. This power analysis for our primary aim (Aim 1) using the d = .75 estimate for the Treatment Group effect size formed the basis for our sample size (n = 70). The power analysis for Aim 2 was based on this sample size and was intended to inform the reader of the effect sizes we were able to detect for Aim 2. We conducted Monte Carlo Simulations to calculate power for mediation in Aim 2. Power was calculated using 500 simulations, each with 1000 bootstrap samples. Assuming a medium effect size (d = .5) for both the effect of the Treatment Group × Time interaction on the mediators, and for the effect of the mediators on outcome, we had >.80 power to detect mediation.

Transparency and openness

The study was preregistered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05523453). The protocol paper, which outlines all statistical details has been published by Contractor and colleagues (2024). Both treatment manuals, fidelity checklists, study measures, and data analytical code are available at Open Science Framework (https://osf.io/ef54z/).

Results

The randomized sample of 70 participants averaged 31.31 years (SD = 13.20); 80% (n = 56) identified as female participants; 31.43% (n =22) identified as Hispanic/Latinx; and 78.57% (n = 55) identified as white. While all participants met diagnostic criteria for PTSD on the CAPS-5, only 50% exceeded the clinical cut-off score of 31 on the PCL-5. See Supplemental Table 1 for information on sample characteristics (including the differences across arms on socio-demographic variables), Supplemental Table 2 for descriptive information on clinical variables and therapy attendance, and Supplemental Table 3 for descriptive information on trauma types.

Fidelity

Lead author (A.A.C) and co-author (M.T.S.) provided intensive initial and ongoing training and weekly supervision to study therapists and research assistants, while conducting random reliability checks throughout the study. Specific to the CAPS-5, therapists were trained by the lead author (A.A.C) and they completed the CAPS-5 training hosted by the Veterans Health Administration - Institute for Learning, Education and Development, which included simulation training cases (Veterans Health Administration - Institute for Learning, 2018). Therapists rated (1) the CAPS-5 administration by the lead author to a mock participant (percentage agreement with ratings by the lead author ranged from 83 to 100%), and (2) CAPS-5 administration to training cases as part of the training by the Veterans Health Administration - Institute for Learning, Education and Development (percentage agreement with the trainer scores ranged from 84% to 100%). Further, lead author (A.A.C) reviewed video-recorded CAPS-5 administrations for ~50% of the participants to provide feedback. See the protocol paper by Contractor and colleagues (2024) for more details.

Intake Interview.

Fidelity ratings were conducted on whether the primary components of the interview were covered by study assessors (e.g., reviewed informed consent, provided study information, administered all questions of the CAPS-5 and the MINI). Response options were yes, no, and not applicable. Video recordings of the interview (including administrations of the CAPS-5 and the MINI) for the first 36 participants were independently coded by trained research assistants for fidelity (32.73% of the total number of interviews). The average percent agreement between raters was 98.81% across all items (range = 77.10%-100%; 32 of 36 items had 100% agreement). Reasons for discrepancies were resolved between the raters (e.g., no shared understanding of certain questions). Subsequently, each of the two raters coded the remainder of the interviews for fidelity independently.

Therapy Sessions.

For PPMT, the fidelity rating sheet included prescribed unique and essential elements (e.g., conducted imaginal experiencing training, asked participants “what are the most important values/feelings/personal strengths/thoughts that this positive memory has brought to mind,” providing the homework assignment), and proscribed elements (e.g., encouraging participants to talk about trauma in detail) for each session. For SC, the fidelity rating sheet included prescribed unique and essential elements (e.g., reviewed daily diary sheets, discussed the topic that the participant wished to explore in session), and proscribed elements (e.g., encouraging participants to talk about trauma or positive memories in detail) for each session. Response options were yes, no, and not applicable.

Two trained research assistants independently coded nine PPMT Session 1 recordings and seven recordings for each of the PPMT Sessions 2 through 5. Average percent agreement for fidelity ratings across all items was 96.76%, 96.43%, 95.58%, 96.54%, and 95.78% for Sessions 1 to 5, respectively. For items with a lower-than-threshold agreement, there was an inaccurate understanding of the timeframe and parts of PPMT (e.g., did not consider the processing time for positive AMs when coding the item “provided ~20–30 min to narrate their positive memory”) or errors on the fidelity sheet (e.g., inconsistency in time for the imaginal experiencing training between the protocol and the fidelity sheet). Similarly, two research assistants independently coded seven recorded SC Sessions 1 through 5, with average percent agreement being 99.40%, 98.52%, 98.64%, 100%, and 92.51% for Sessions 1 to 5, respectively. The two items with a lower-than-threshold agreement (“discussed participants’ response to the course of SC and the therapeutic relationship,” and “reminded clients that they will be paid to complete the follow-up assessments”) were difficult to code reliably due to variable therapist practices and coder interpretations. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion with the lead author and study therapists, and fidelity documents were revised as needed. Thereafter, one rater independently coded the remaining therapy sessions for fidelity.

Treatment adherence

Therapist adherence to intervention components was monitored for ≥81% integrity (Borrelli, 2011). For the intake interviews conducted by the study therapists (including administrations of the CAPS-5 and MINI), the average adherence rating was 98.35%. For PPMT, the average treatment adherence for prescribed and proscribed elements across sessions was 94.53% and 99.81%, respectively. For SC, the average treatment adherence for prescribed and proscribed elements was 90.01% and 94.13%, respectively. The adherence ratings did not differ across therapy conditions, t(8) = 1.12, p = .294.

Aim 1

The difference between treatment groups in the slope of PCL-5 scores over time (Treatment Group × Time interaction) was not significant (p = .477), and there were no significant differences between treatment groups at the 3-month follow-up (p = .814, Fig. 2). Notably, there was a very wide range of baseline PCL-5 scores (~11–62), with 50% of participants endorsing a score of ≤31, a cutoff for “remission” according to the review by Forkus et al. (2023). Thus, we performed post-hoc exploratory moderation analyses to determine if the baseline PCL-5 score moderated the effects of treatment group on PCL-5 improvement over time. This analysis included the Treatment Group × Time × baseline PCL-5 triple interaction, and all the subcomponents of that interaction (the above analysis did not include baseline PCL-5 scores moderating any of the Treatment Group effects, but rather just controlled for baseline PCL-5 scores and the baseline PCL-5 × Time interaction). Results showed that treatment group differences in slopes over time were highly impacted by the baseline PCL-5 scores; b = 7.35, 95% CI: [3.67, 11.34], t(433) = 3.63, p <. 001, d = .41 for the baseline PCL-5 × Treatment Group × Time interaction. Simple slopes analyses showed that, for participants with higher baseline PCL-5 scores (>31), those in PPMT had significantly greater improvement in PCL-5 than those in SC (Treatment × Time interaction; b = 6.83, 95% CI: [.74, 12.93], t[433] = 2.20, p = .028, d = .78). Individuals in the PPMT arm also had significantly lower PCL-5 scores at the 3-month follow-up than those in SC; b = 8.65, 95% CI: [.31, 16.98], t[433] = 2.04, p = .042, d = .71 (Fig. 3a). On the other hand, for participants with baseline PCL-5 scores ≤31, there were no significant differences between treatment groups in slopes of improvement (p = .448) or in PCL-5 scores at the 3-month follow-up (p = .166, Fig. 3b).

The proportion of participants with missing data was somewhat higher in the PPMT arm (9 of 36, 25%) than in the SC arm (3 of 34, 9%), but this difference was not significant, Fisher Exact Test p = .112. Further, the proportion of participants who did not complete the final follow-up was significantly higher in PPMT than SC (9 of 36 [25%] in PPMT; 1 of 34 [3%] in SC, Fisher Exact Test p = .014). Therefore, we performed an analysis that did not assume that data were missing at random. Specifically, we performed a pattern mixture model (e.g., Hedeker & Gibbons, 2006), which is a missing not at random (MNAR) analysis. This pattern mixture model examined whether the results for our moderation analysis were different for participants with missing data (coded 1) vs. those with complete data (coded 0). This analysis adds interactions between the missing data code and all the other growth curve predictors in the exploratory moderation analyses. Results indicated that there were no significant effects of missing data on any of the growth curve parameters (p-values for interactions of each growth curve parameter and the missing data code ranged from p = .176 to p = .447). Further, the significant baseline PCL-5 × Treatment Group × Time interaction, and the significant Treatment Group × Time interaction, were significant for both those with missing data and those without missing data (ps < .005), suggesting that missing data were not responsible for the obtained effects.

To examine response rates, we calculated whether participants met criterion for reliable change (Jacobson & Truax, 1991). Using the formula in Jacobson and Truax (1991), we calculated the reliable change to be > 30.43 points on the PCL-5. Examining the proportion of participants who achieved reliable change at the 3-month follow-up, we found that a significantly greater proportion of participants achieved reliable change in PPMT (9 of 27; 33%) than in SC (3 out of 33; 9%), Fisher Exact Test p = .026. However, this analysis was only able to include those who completed the 3-month follow-up (n = 60). To include all 70 participants in a reliable change analysis, we calculated whether each participant achieved reliable change at each timepoint (yes or no) and analyzed the growth curve of this dichotomous reliable change variable using GLMM. The predictors in the GLMM were Treatment Group, Time, and Treatment Group × Time. Consistent with the results of the Fisher Exact Test performed on the completer sample, the intent-to-treat GLMM analysis found that the estimated proportion of participants with reliable change on the PCL-5 at the 3-month follow-up was significantly higher in PPMT (40%) than in SC (12%), b = −1.69, 95% CI: [−2.67, −.71], t(437) = −3.38, p < .001, OR = .19, 95% CI: [.07, .49].

Aim 2

Examining PANAS-Positive scores as a mediator in our primary analysis for Aim 1 (not the exploratory moderation analysis), we found that the “a” path in the mediation analysis (the Treatment Group × Time effect on PANAS-Positive) was significant, b = −3.23, 95% CI: [−5.63, −.84], t(375) = 2.65, p = .008, d = .66, but the “b” path in the analysis (the cross-lag relation between PANAS-Positive at time t with PCL-5 at time t+1) was not significant (p = .735). Thus, PANAS-Positive scores did not mediate treatment group differences in PCL-5 scores over time. For PANAS-Negative as a mediator, we found that neither the “a” (the Treatment Group × Time effect on PANAS-Negative; p = .770) nor the “b” (the cross-lag relation between PANAS-Negative at time t with PCL-5 at time t+1; p = .224) paths was significant. Therefore, the mediation by PANAS-Negative scores was not significant.

However, because the post-hoc exploratory moderation analyses showed treatment group differences in PCL-5 improvement for those with higher baseline PCL-5 scores, we explored whether the baseline PCL-5 scores moderated whether PANAS-Positive and/or PANAS-Negative scores were mediators of the treatment group differences in PCL-5 improvement (Fig. 4). In this cross-lag moderated mediation analysis, we found a significant treatment group difference in the slope of PANAS-Positive scores over time (the same finding as for the “a” path reported above); b = −3.23, 95% CI: [−5.63, −.84], t(375) = 2.65, p = .008, d = .66 (i.e., “a” path in Fig. 4). We also found that the “b” path in this mediation analysis (the cross-lag relation between PANAS-Positive scores at time t and PCL-5 scores at t+1, controlling for PCL-5 scores at time t), was moderated by baseline PCL-5 scores (Fig. 4). For participants with high baseline PCL-5 scores (>31), higher PANAS-Positive scores were significantly related to lower PCL-5 scores at the next assessment (the “b” path), b = −.28, 95% CI: [−.53, −.03], t(293) = −2.20, p = .028, d = .29. Hence, PANAS-Positive scores were a significant mediator of the treatment group difference in slopes of PCL-5 scores over time, a*b = .90, 95% CI: [.04, 2.18], p < .05 for participants with high baseline PCL-5 scores. However, this “b” path was not significant for participants with lower baseline PCL-5 scores (≤31), so PANAS-Positive scores did not mediate the effect of treatment group on change in PCL-5 scores for these participants. PANAS-Negative scores, on the other hand, were not a significant mediator of treatment group differences in the slopes of PCL-5 scores over time. The “a” path (the effect of Treatment Group × Time on PANAS-Negative score) was not significant (p = .770). And although the “b” path (the cross-lag effect of PANAS-Negative score on PCL-5 score) was moderated by baseline PCL-5, the “b” path was not significant for participants with either high or low baseline PCL-5 scores.

Additional analyses

Our study included weekly measurements of the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9; Kroenke & Spitzer, 2002) to monitor any rapid increases in depressive symptoms and suicidal ideation. Although analyses of PHQ-9 scores as an outcome or as a mediator was not the primary aim of the RCT (Contractor, Blumenthal, et al., 2024), we were asked to report changes in depressive symptoms and their relation with PCL-5 scores during the review process. To summarize the results of those analyses, MLM analyses indicated no treatment group differences in the change in PHQ-9 scores over the course of the study, either in the overall analysis (p = .901) or the analysis using baseline PCL-5 severity as a moderator (p = .939). However, on average, PHQ-9 scores did decrease significantly over time (p < .001), from an estimated 9.0 at baseline to about 5.7 at the 3-month follow-up. Finally, in a cross-lag mediation analysis, deviations in PHQ-9 scores were not significant predictors of the next-session PCL-5 scores either in the overall analysis (p = .422) or the moderated mediation analysis (p = .911).

Adverse events (AEs) and serious adverse events (SAEs)

The definition of AEs and SAEs as described by the U.S. Department of Health and Human Service was used for the current study (Office for Human Research Protections (OHRP), 2007). A total of 50 AEs occurred during the study; none of these were severe, and only two of them were related to study procedures. No modifications to the study protocol or interventions were required. The pre-determined stopping rules were if >15% of enrolled participants reported study-related AEs or >5% of enrolled participants reported study-related SAEs, which were not met. Discussion

The current RCT is the first to compare the effects of a novel 5-session Processing of Positive Memories Technique (PPMT) protocol to Supportive Counseling (SC) among individuals diagnosed with PTSD. Overall, participants in both conditions reported significant improvements in PTSD symptom severity from pre- to post-intervention, with large effect sizes, but there were no differences across conditions in PTSD symptom severity. The observed benefits of PPMT align with findings from prior open label pilot studies (Contractor et al., 2025; Contractor, Jin, & Weiss, 2023) and converge with the broader research highlighting the therapeutic value of recalling and processing positive AMs among individuals with mental health symptoms (Miguel-Alvaro et al., 2021). Within PPMT, as participants learn and practice retrieving and processing positive AMs throughout treatment, they may begin to shift their focus towards positive experiences, may emotionally connect with these positive AMs, and may integrate them more deeply into their sense of self. These factors may cumulatively contribute to improved coping and to better posttrauma adjustment (e.g., Askelund et al., 2019; Barry & Hallford, 2024; Brewin et al., 2025; Goddard et al., 2001). Further, the observed benefits for SC are consistent with prior studies supporting the effectiveness of PCT—from which SC was adapted—in improving PTSD and other posttrauma symptoms (Frost et al., 2014). Although SC did not include all the active ingredients of PCT (e.g., active problem-solving approaches), it provided psychoeducation on common responses to trauma and encouraged the exploration of present concerns through daily diary logs. These elements may have been sufficient to foster improvements in PTSD symptoms and affective experiences (e.g., McDonagh et al., 2005). Broadly, SC in the current study likely functioned as an active therapeutic intervention.

The lack of significant differences between the PPMT and SC arms is consistent with a broader pattern in PTSD treatment research showing that while many evidence-based treatments produce substantial improvement, differences between these treatments and active control treatment conditions or between evidence-based treatments are small or statistically non-significant (Benish et al., 2008; Frost et al., 2014). The role of common therapeutic factors shared across PTSD treatments (e.g., therapeutic alliance, empathy, validation) and common methodological components of RCTs such as structured and consistent therapeutic contact and discussion of personal life narratives in all treatment arms may account for such findings (e.g., Wampold, 2015). Moreover, PTSD is a heterogenous disorder, and variability in trauma history, comorbidities, socio-cultural characteristics and readiness for therapeutic work may account for differential responses to different treatments (e.g., Sripada et al., 2019). Thus, our study is consistent with the idea that multiple therapeutic pathways can lead to improvements in PTSD symptoms, and perhaps, personalization of PTSD treatments to individual and contextual characteristics may be important to maximize treatment effects (Herzog & Kaiser, 2022).

With the caveat of small sample sizes and being post-hoc exploratory analyses, our study findings indicated that PPMT demonstrated superior treatment outcomes relative to SC among participants with higher baseline PTSD severity (medium to large effect size). Results on PTSD remission rates showed the superior effectiveness of PPMT compared to SC. Thus, PPMT may be particularly helpful and effective for individuals who report more PTSD symptomatology prior to beginning treatment. Previous research suggests that these individuals experience greater difficulties retrieving detailed positive AMs and frequently experience distress when doing so (Contractor, Caldas, Dolan, et al., 2022; Contractor, Slavish, Wang, et al., 2024). For them, structured exercises in processing positive AMs as embedded in PPMT may address these barriers directly, thereby enhancing therapeutic gains. Additionally, our results suggested that for individuals with lower baseline PTSD severity, SC performed equally well as PPMT in improving PTSD symptoms (SC was not a control group in prior PPMT studies). Perhaps these individuals may already possess sufficient coping resources, such that the supportive structure of SC was adequate to achieve meaningful improvements in posttrauma symptoms. Nevertheless, these findings should be interpreted cautiously given the exploratory nature of the moderation analyses. Future research needs to examine the differential effects of PPMT among individuals with differing PTSD symptom levels and among those who do not respond well to existing trauma-focused treatments.

Additionally, study results only partially supported the proposed mediation hypotheses. Overall, in the full sample, improvements in affect did not mediate reductions in PTSD severity across treatment groups. However, among participants with higher baseline PTSD severity, improvements in positive affect levels accounted for improvements in PTSD severity in the PPMT condition compared to the SC arm. In contrast, participants did not report significant reductions in negative affect from pre-to post-intervention, and negative affect was not related to PCL-5 severity, regardless of treatment condition or baseline PCL-5 severity. These findings highlight the role of positive AM retrieval in affect regulation and how positive affect processes aid posttrauma recovery (Rusting & DeHart, 2000; Steptoe et al., 2009). Consistent with our primary results, we found that retrieving and processing positive AMs in PPMT contributed to more positive feelings, which may have helped to reduce PTSD severity among individuals who experienced relatively more severe PTSD symptoms prior to beginning treatment. These individuals may have been more likely to experience dysregulated affect, anhedonia, and negative affect in response to PPMT tasks (e.g., Contractor, Banducci, et al., 2022). Nonetheless, we did not account for other factors known to influence affect, such as dispositional traits (e.g., optimism) and supportive relationships among trauma survivors (Egan et al., 2024). Moreover, prior research shows that effects of positive AMs on affect are shaped by contextual and cognitive factors, including how individuals process AMs (e.g., concrete vs. abstract), their appraisal of positive emotions, and whether recalled AMs align with or conflict with their current identity (e.g., Bogaert et al., 2023; Dunn et al., 2018; Salmon et al., 2025). Taken together, these findings highlight the need to take a multidimensional approach to understanding affective mechanisms underlying PPMT’s effects on PTSD, including examining other potential moderators (e.g., cognitive abilities) and mediators (e.g., better positive emotion regulation) in this process.

We note that nine participants discontinued therapy in the PPMT arm (25%), and a total of 11 participants dropped out from the entire study (~16%). These dropout estimates align with and are lower than previously reported attrition rates in PTSD treatment trials (Varker et al., 2021). Our exploratory analyses, which assumed data were not missing at random, did not show evidence that missing data significantly affected our findings. Most participants who dropped out from the study cited reasons unrelated to the specific intervention they received, and which could apply across treatment arms, such as time constraints, difficulties or dissatisfaction with the amount of homework, an unexpected loss in the family, and medical issues. However, two participants identified PPMT-specific concerns when contacted via phone to discuss their decision to discontinue the study: one expressed dissatisfaction with the PPMT protocol, and another indicated that PPMT did not adequately address their trauma or complex PTSD experiences (also indicated additional reasons such as finding the recall of positive AMs to be unhelpful and frustrating). From a methodological perspective, while administration of the CAPS-5 was appropriately used to establish diagnostic eligibility for PTSD, it may have inadvertently contributed to participant distress and study attrition. To enhance retention, future PPMT trials should consider incorporating strategies, such as support after the assessment session, to help participants manage trauma-related distress experienced during initial diagnostic procedures. Further, eight participants who withdrew from the study declined to complete the post-intervention feedback survey on PPMT, limiting our knowledge on how PPMT might be adapted to better suit individuals who find PPMT distressing or not appropriate.

Some methodological considerations and limitations need to be noted for future RCT studies on PPMT. First, we recruited community participants, and despite efforts to enhance representation from diverse and underrepresented groups, most of the sample identified as white (78.6%), as female participants (80%), and as women (74.3%). It is important to examine the implementation and effects of PPMT within clinical settings and within diverse populations to address applicability and broader clinical relevance. Second, we did not measure beliefs about the nature and function of retrieving positive AMs from participants, which is an important area of future research (Matsumoto & Watson, 2025) and perhaps an additional component of PPMT. Third, we used a past-week timeframe for the PCL-5 at baseline, which was a statistical decision to allow modeling of symptom change over time using a consistent reference period. However, this timeframe differed from that of the CAPS-5, which assessed PTSD symptoms over the past month to determine study eligibility. Thus, many participants who met diagnostic criteria for PTSD on the CAPS-5 did not exceed the clinical cut-off score of 31 on the PCL-5, potentially underrepresenting symptom severity in the self-report data (due to the 1-week reference period for the PCL-5 vs. the 1-month reference period for the CAPS-5) and possibly reducing our ability to detect significant changes in this primary outcome measure across treatment arms over time. Relatedly, we note that the recommended clinical cut-off for the PCL-5 has varied across studies, even being as low as 22 corresponding to the CAPS-5 (reviewed in Forkus et al., 2023). Additionally, suggested clinical cut-off scores for the PCL-5 have referenced a past month timeframe (reviewed in Forkus et al., 2023), which calls into question the use of a past-week PCL-5 to align with PTSD assessed with the CAPS-5. Lastly, budget and time constraints of the trial prevented us from administering the CAPS-5 at follow-up timepoints, which meant that we could not do a pre-to post-treatment comparison on CAPS-5 scores for this study. Given these considerations, we defined PTSD remission as a reliable change pre-to-post intervention as described above. Future research would benefit from aligning the timeframes used in structured diagnostic interviews and corresponding self-report measures to ensure consistency in symptom assessment and to better capture clinically significant levels of distress, as well as from using a clinician-administered PTSD assessment as the primary outcome measure administered at pre- and post-intervention.

Our study findings have clinical and methodological implications. Given the limitations of existing evidence-based PTSD treatments such as lengthy protocols, requiring extensive training to ensure effectiveness, and high dropout rates coupled with the fact that not every treatment works for all individuals (e.g., Garcia et al., 2011; Lewis et al., 2020), we need to explore novel intervention targets for PTSD. PPMT can serve as a brief alternative or adjunctive 5-session intervention for trauma survivors who are experiencing significant PTSD symptom severity. We cannot speculate on its relative efficacy compared to current gold standard interventions such as Prolonged Exposure or Cognitive Processing Therapy since there are no RCTs comparing PPMT to those treatments. But even if PPMT is not as efficacious as a stand-alone treatment compared to the gold standard interventions, it may still be a desirable alternative for individuals who don’t have the time or resources for longer treatments, or for those who might drop out of trauma-focused treatments. It also could be employed alongside trauma-focused treatments. Due to its focus on positive memories and associated content, individuals receiving PPMT may access neglected positive beliefs and emotions and connect to a positive sense of self, which may help their recovery or may increase their readiness to engage in trauma-focused work (Contractor et al., 2018). Relatedly, it would be important to explore how and why PPMT works and for whom it does not work using a larger RCT. Further, study findings also suggest that supportive, non-trauma focused interventions can also reduce PTSD symptoms, even in shorter treatment formats. These findings add to many studies that have found supportive non-trauma focused treatments to be associated with reductions in posttrauma symptoms (Frost et al., 2014). Broadly, our study findings support the clinical utility of multiple non-trauma focused pathways for PTSD interventions.

Additionally, the current study was an RCT comparing PPMT to an active control condition, which was conducted with limited resources and informs decisions and logistical issues for larger PPMT RCTs (Teresi et al., 2022). Here, nine doctoral-level psychology students were trained in several aspects of this RCT, including administration of assessments and intervention protocols. Treatment fidelity and adherence indices suggested good metrics. Further, the study was conducted at a university-based psychology training clinic. Beyond supporting the use of a university structure for conducting studies on PPMT and SC, results suggest that PPMT and SC may be scalable and may be implemented by providers without extensive clinical experience once they are trained in PPMT. Additionally, our training protocols and fidelity checklists can be used in larger RCTs on PPMT and SC.

In conclusion, PPMT did not differ than SC in improving PTSD severity. Our exploratory findings suggested that individuals entering treatment with greater baseline PTSD severity experienced larger reductions in PTSD severity and more positive affect over time in the PPMT condition. Given the preliminary nature of these findings, RCTs with larger samples sizes are needed to formally examine PTSD severity as a potential moderator of treatment response when comparing PPMT to SC. Further research should also investigate other mechanisms underlying PPMT, such as decreases in posttrauma cognitions and improvements in emotion regulation capacities (Contractor, Banducci, et al., 2022), and impacts of PPMT and SC on other posttrauma health outcomes in one’s daily lives (e.g., sleep health, quality of life). Examining the feasibility of delivering PPMT and SC in different formats (e.g., self-help format, in groups) among diverse communities can speak to its scalability and clinical utility more comprehensively.
