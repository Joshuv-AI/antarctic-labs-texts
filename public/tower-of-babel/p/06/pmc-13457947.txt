Collaborations between healthcare and public health in rural settings
Authors: Casey P Balio, Rachel Dean, Kayla M Alvis, Benjamin Pelton, Haleigh M Leslie, Michael Meit, Stephanie M Mathis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionLocal health departments and healthcare organizations in rural areas face challenges, including workforce shortages and complex population health-related needs. While they share related roles in their communities, incentives to collaborate may be sparse.MethodsUsing a 2-tiered qualitative approach, we describe the purpose, motivations, benefits, and barriers to collaborations between local health departments and healthcare delivery organizations serving rural communities, with a focus on workforce factors. Twenty-four semi-structured interviews were conducted, including 18 interviews with individuals from local organizations and 6 interviews with representatives of membership groups that support these organizations. Interviews were analyzed using a deductive rapid coding process.ResultsWe find that collaborations are necessary, common, and often driven by workforce or other capacity limitations. However, potential competition for staff, differing structures, and a lack of familiarity with the role of public health were identified as barriers to robust and sustained collaborations.ConclusionsThere are potential benefits of collaborations between local health departments and healthcare organizations in rural areas, including for both the organizations and community. Developing and sustaining collaborations requires time, resources, and relationships. Inherent to collaborative work is a need for communication across professions, including the articulation of each organization's role, expertise, and value.Keywords: rural, public health, collaboration

Introduction

The health needs of populations across the United States are complex, ever changing, and differ across geographies and populations. For example, compared with urban areas, rural areas tend to face greater challenges related to health status and outcomes, social drivers of health, workforce, infrastructure, and funding.1-4 Local health departments (LHDs) and healthcare organizations in rural areas also face unique considerations and challenges in their delivery of health-related activities to meet the needs of their populations, often with more limited capacity.4-6 The complexity of addressing health needs in resource- constrained environments, such as rural settings, often requires a multisectoral network of organizations to contribute to improvements. Frequently, this includes governmental public health and healthcare organizations, focusing on various aspects of prevention, treatment, and health promotion efforts at the individual and population levels.7 This multifaceted approach is further formalized through Health in All Policies and Public Health 3.0, which recognize the health-related impacts of policy, activities across sectors, and the role and importance of governmental public health.8,9 While the roles of both public health and healthcare are to improve health, their work is often siloed, representing a missed opportunity for greater efficiency and impact.7,10,11 Collaborations between LHDs and local healthcare organizations are relatively common, with over half of LHDs reporting formal relationships with various types of local healthcare partners, including 75% of LHDs reporting formal relationships with hospitals.12 These collaborations have become more common since the passage of the Affordable Care Act, which requires non-profit hospitals to conduct community health needs assessments and design implementation strategies.10,13,14 While hospitals and LHDs may be primed for working together on needs assessments, there are many other areas for potential collaboration, such as data and resource sharing, emergency preparedness, substance use, maternal health, and infectious disease efforts.11,12,15,16 Further, evidence suggests that collaborations between public health and healthcare organizations have the potential to improve individual-level health behaviors, population health outcomes, and advance health equity.13,17,18 Although collaborations may be beneficial, barriers also exist. Competition for resources such as workforce and funding, difficulties sharing data, challenges understanding each organizations' roles, and the complexity of health and public health systems can hinder collaboration development.19-21

Some challenges faced by LHDs in rural settings may be more acutely felt than those in urban settings,6 and may affect collaborations. Additionally, the public health and healthcare landscapes have been evolving, especially since the COVID-19 pandemic and relevant funding such as the Public Health Infrastructure Grant and the recently established $50 billion Rural Health Transformation Program, both of which have a substantial focus on workforce.22,23 These considerations, among others, may impact the need for and nature of these collaborations within rural communities, potentially providing opportunities to strengthen systems and further partnerships.

The purpose of this qualitative study is to describe the rationale for, structure of, and factors influencing collaborations between rural-serving LHDs and local healthcare organizations. The findings may inform collaborative efforts among these organizations, as well as the work of federal and state organizations that administer or oversee programs involving such collaborations. Ultimately, strengthening these collaborations could maximize the impact of limited resources and improve the well-being of rural communities.

Data and methods

A multi-stage, qualitative approach was used to explore local public health and healthcare collaborations in rural settings. The East Tennessee State University Institutional Review Board determined that the study did not meet the definition of human subjects research.

Sample

Local healthcare organizations were defined as entities primarily providing direct clinical care services, such as hospitals, clinics, or emergency medical services providers. Local health departments included governmental public health agencies serving a county, city, or multi-county jurisdiction. Local health departments may provide clinical services as well, but as part of a wide array of activities and not their primary role. Eligible LHDs and local healthcare organizations served a largely or exclusively rural population. Rurality was defined broadly, allowing for consideration of multiple definitions, including Rural-Urban Continuum Codes, 2020 Census definition of rural,24 critical access hospital status, and in some instances, participant self-reported community-level rurality. Collaboration was broadly defined as working toward a shared purpose or goal; formal agreements were not required.

This study consisted of 2 stages of semi-structured interviews. Stage 1 included representatives of national, regional, or state organizations that either served or represented LHDs or local healthcare organizations. A purposive sample of organizations was identified, focused on membership organizations. Recruitment for interviews included email outreach to staff in leadership roles aligned with the study purpose.

Stage 2 included staff from LHDs or local healthcare organizations located in or serving rural areas. A multi-pronged, staggered recruitment approach using a combination of purposive and snowball sampling was applied. First, Stage 1 participants were asked to recommend individuals with experience collaborating between rural-serving LHDs and local healthcare organizations. Second, organizations eligible for Stage 1 interviews and other similar organizations were contacted to disseminate recruitment information via their professional networks. Third, outreach to individuals through the study team's professional networks was conducted to identify potential participants. For both stages, consideration was given to supporting balanced representation from local healthcare and public health organizations, as well as diverse geographical representation.

Data collection

Interviews were conducted virtually via Zoom using semi-structured guides tailored to each stage in February through May 2025. Interview questions were similar across stages, covering collaboration context, structure, motivators, benefits, facilitators, and challenges. Within relevant topics, interviews probed for considerations specific to the workforce. Stage 1 focused on the national or state landscape, while Stage 2 focused on the on-the-ground experiences of local organizations. Two study staff attended each interview. Interviewers generally did not have prior relationships with participants. Participants provided verbal consent, including for recording the interview. Interviews ranged from 28 to 59 minutes, with an average of 42 minutes. Interviews were professionally transcribed, and personally identifiable information was removed. Participants did not receive incentives for their participation.

Data analysis

Interview data were analyzed using a rapid coding process with a team of 3 trained researchers.25,26 Consistent with a largely deductive approach, an Excel template was developed to summarize interviews by organizing interview questions into overarching domains. Within each stage and participant type, a minimum of 20% of interviews were randomly selected and double-coded to make any necessary revisions to the coding template and reach consensus. The remaining interviews were then divided across coders for independent coding. Finally, domain summaries were developed across interviews and then collapsed into 3 themes.

Limitations

While this study advances understanding of local collaborations in rural settings, several limitations should be noted. Given the sample size, representation of select organization types and settings, the use of convenience sampling, and the broad definition of rurality, study findings may not be generalizable to all rural organizations and communities across the country. By design, Stage 1 participants had less direct, local collaborative experience. Therefore, findings from these interviews largely focused on national, state, or regional context for framing and sampling; a representative and generalizable sample of national, state, or regional organizations was not the focus. Given the more direct experience of local entities, we purposefully sought a larger sample of local organizations relative to national, state, or regional organizations. Additionally, local public health and healthcare organizations with a history of positive collaborations may have been overrepresented in the sample. Further, data were self-reported by participants.

Findings are organized into 3 themes: (1) collaboration context and rationale; (2) collaboration purpose and structure, and (3) factors related to collaboration formation, implementation, and maintenance, with several specific factors described as subthemes. In most cases, perceptions described were largely similar between public health and healthcare, unless otherwise noted.

Collaboration context and rationale

Participants characterized collaborations between LHDs and local healthcare organizations as common and essential, often formed out of “necessity” due to limited resources and infrastructure. Nearly all participants described a lack of access to healthcare providers and services and other workforce challenges in recruiting and retaining staff. The need for and frequency of collaborations were considered greater in rural areas as compared with urban areas.

In the more urban areas, the entities tend to be bigger. Your hospital health system is bigger, your health department tends to be bigger, and they just end up being more siloed. I mean, they have their own resources, they have their own people, they're doing their own work, so I don’t think there's as much of that collaboration aspect that tends to happen. Public Health, Stage 2 Interview #16

Given resource and infrastructure limitations in rural areas, participants commonly described collaboration as essential for addressing community needs or gaps in health-related services and maximizing impact. Collaborations provide a means of avoiding duplication and enhancing efficiency, effectiveness, or capacity, both in administrative and technical capabilities. Participants, for example, highlighted the need to pool or coordinate available staffing and other resources. They similarly alluded to “power in numbers,” recognizing that organizations are stronger together and that 1 organization often cannot accomplish goals alone in rural areas. The COVID-19 pandemic offered an illustration of this, with participants commonly indicating that the pandemic drove new partnerships or strengthened existing ones.

Necessity is the mother of all invention. … It's, “How do we do this? How do we work together?” If you don’t work together, A, it's going to be a poor product, because you don’t have the full picture, and [B] you don’t have the full resources. Each entity … has different resources they can pull from that each individual member doesn’t have. … It's just we have to [collaborate], because there aren’t enough of us to go around. Healthcare, Stage 2 Interview #22

In addition, some participants (especially from healthcare organizations) noted that collaborations may be driven indirectly by external requirements or expectations, such as requirements for or by federal or state emergencies, state mandates for community health assessments, regulations from governing or administrative bodies (eg, Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services and the Joint Commission), and specific grant funding requirements.

We [a healthcare organization] are also seeing, from [governing or administrative bodies], more of an expectation that we’re going to be taking care of population health. If you define “population health” as the health of the community, not healthcare for the community, … then we also, by regulation, need to at least look into that as to what we’re going to be doing. Healthcare, Stage 2 Interview #20

Lastly, participants indicated that anticipated benefits can be a rationale for collaboration development. Potential benefits vary based on the collaboration purpose, with benefits at various levels and to various entities highlighted, including to individual patients, partner organizations, and communities.

Collaboration purpose and structure

Participants identified a variety of collaboration purposes and topic areas. Example purposes included: grants, clinical rotations for students, behavioral health taskforces or coalitions, joint needs assessments, staffing, medical record access, messaging and communication, emergency preparedness and response, infectious disease surveillance and response, and referrals between organizations.

Most participants described a combination of formal and informal collaborations, with structures dependent on the type and purpose of the specific effort. While informal collaborations were often considered more common, especially for service delivery or programming, formal collaborations tended to occur when funding or shared staffing was involved.

Furthermore, participants commonly described additional collaborators, such as education, law enforcement, or nonprofits. Although considered important, a limited pool of community members and organizations for collaborative activities, especially those requiring volunteers, can constrain community support and engagement.

Factors related to collaboration formation, implementation, and maintenance

Participants identified numerous factors that may positively or negatively influence the formation, implementation, and sustainability of collaborations between LHDs and local healthcare organizations in rural areas. Common factors centered on: workforce; funding and other resources; and interorganizational alignment. Participants often described the details of these factors as being unique to rural settings.

Workforce

In contrast to the benefits from having flatter structures described above, participants explained that the limited number of staff, combined with fewer potential partner organizations, hinder robust collaborations. Many staff in rural areas are already managing multiple responsibilities and have limited capacity to assume additional roles or responsibilities related to new collaborative activities. Workforce challenges were also exacerbated by difficulties in recruiting and retaining staff and high levels of turnover. Several participants described limited applicant pools when filling vacancies and how individuals, especially clinicians, often leave an LHD for a position at a healthcare organization or vice versa.

Additionally, participants described the importance of strong and engaged leadership. In a few instances, collaborations formed because of an individual championing the effort or a clinician having an interest or additional training in public health. Similarly, 1 participant noted the advantage of a “flatter” leadership structure in rural organizations, where leaders are more closely tied to the direct activities, compared with urban organizations.

In the urban setting where you’ve got a large, maybe 700, 800, 900-bed [prospective payment system] hospital, the CEO is very far removed. You’ve got so many layers between executive management and those who are actually having conversations with public health, but some of that information loss happens between all those layers. I think that's an advantage as a rural organization when you’re having these conversations. You’ve got a flatter leadership structure, and so you can be more nimble and pivot more quickly to get things done. Healthcare, Stage 2 Interview #24

In addition to organizational leaders, participants described the broader influence of hospital boards or local boards of health, with these entities having the potential to support or hinder collaboration.

The head of public health is on our board of trustees at the hospital, she's on our quality committee. … The chair of public health is our retired chief nursing officer. Another one of the board members is our current chief nursing officer. Again, it sounds good, maybe it is or isn’t, but since there's only one healthcare system, and there's only one public health, you have the opportunity, but you also have the responsibility. Healthcare, Stage 2 Interview #20

Moreover, participants highlighted the role of interpersonal dynamics between staff across organizations. Establishing trust and regular, open communication were commonly seen as facilitators to collaboration, especially in identifying collaborative opportunities and maintaining communication in difficult times. Additional attributes such as flexibility, openness, and persistence were likewise considered beneficial. In contrast, differing personalities, egos, and a history of negative experiences were seen as barriers. Broader professional connections between organizations–shared staffing or settings, staff with work experience at both organizations, or staff with shared professional backgrounds or expertise–may help facilitate strong collaborations, mutual understanding, and mutual respect.

I’m a physician myself, so I think that helps because … the vast majority of my [public health] staff are not. I am able to pinch hit … because I understand … both public health and clinical. Public Health/Healthcare, Stage 2 Interview #19

Lastly, given the small and tight-knit nature of many rural communities, personal relationships may influence collaborations. Workers from both organizations often live locally, with overlapping social circles and communities. In general, this was seen as a facilitator, but in some instances, participants described negative personal relationships between staff and organizations impeding collaboration.

Funding and other resources

Participants indicated that funding models or mechanisms could support collaboration; however, in many cases, they are not structured to do so. For example, fee-for-service reimbursement was generally viewed as unsupportive of population health activities, which may be primed for collaboration. In contrast, value-based purchasing and alternative payment models were seen as more conducive to supporting population health initiatives and collaborative efforts. One healthcare participant further noted that while the payment structure for critical access hospitals is vital to their operations, it hinders their ability to work toward population activities and invest in related collaborations.

There are really no mechanisms of the healthcare delivery system funding public health, which I think is a true failure of our system in the US. For example, you have the poor little health department funding the healthcare delivery system partners in our county. We’re [the LHD] paying the rent, we’re paying providers to do free colonoscopies, we’re paying them to do mammograms, and so forth. There are a lot of things that we are paying the healthcare delivery system to do, yet we’re the local health department that's understaffed, under-resourced, and underfunded. Public Health, Stage 2 Interview #21

Interorganizational alignment

Participants indicated that the level of alignment, or lack thereof, between LHDs and local healthcare organizations can influence collaboration. While shared goals, mission, vision, or expertise may be beneficial, a shared understanding of the partner organization or of public and population health was lacking in many instances. This lack of understanding was most often described by public health participants in reference to their healthcare counterparts.

We do have, for instance, obstetricians who have a very, very medical model focus, and I’ve got a public health lens. Sometimes those are at odds. Not with all the obstetricians, but a few. An obstetrician may look at this issue and say, “Wait a minute, we just need to have obstetricians in a few hospitals across the state, and then all the pregnant women need to go to those hospitals.” What that means is closing down a lot of labor and delivery units. It does provide maybe quality care, but it reduces access to such a large amount … If you’ve got somebody with a very strong medical model focus and not the public health focus, there's a tension there because you’re looking at access vs quality of care. Public Health/Healthcare, Stage 2 Interview #19

Additionally, differences in organizational funding, size, capacity, and systems were reported as barriers. For example, shared electronic medical record access was challenging, though a few participants reported their organizations accomplishing this. Several other administrative and structural barriers were similarly identified, including liability and other legal concerns, information-sharing regulations, policies, and costs associated with shared staffing. These factors were often unique to specific collaboration types or purposes.

Lastly, participants described the potential for competition between LHDs and local healthcare organizations as a barrier. Organizations may compete for limited workforce, funding (including reimbursement), patients, service lines, and service areas. This was especially pertinent to direct services and staff with clinical training, particularly nurses. The salary difference for nurses working in healthcare vs public health settings was a frequently noted source of frustration for LHDs, resulting in challenges with recruitment and turnover.

There's competition for the resources. Also hospitals, they need to stay solvent, and in rural areas, that's a real risk. Things that impact their business model are challenges. Public Health, Stage 1 Interview #3

Discussion

Local public health and healthcare organizations may be natural partners given their overlapping missions, complementary expertise, and shared populations. However, the need for, organization of, and barriers to these collaborations may be unique in rural areas, where resources are more limited and population health needs tend to be greater. The current attention and resources for public health and rural health may provide a window of opportunity to invest in these collaborations, and the workforces needed to support them.

We find that collaborations are common and vital in rural areas due to resource constraints, limited capacity, and health needs. While essential, several factors may inhibit organizations' ability to partner, highlighting potential areas where targeted support or incentives may facilitate collaboration. Each of these are described below.

First, federal and state systems, infrastructure, and incentives are often not structured to support collaborations across public health and healthcare. For example, the largely independent federal healthcare and public health agency structures may preclude collaboration at a local level.7 Finances and reimbursement structures between public health and healthcare are often at odds, especially in many rural settings where movement toward value-based purchasing is more challenging. Additionally, rural healthcare providers and LHDs face financial challenges. Existing policies surrounding non-profit hospital community benefit and community health assessment requirements may be 1 avenue to support collaboration;14 however, these policies may affect rural providers differently than urban providers.15,27 Recognition by organizations and agencies at the national and state levels of the unique needs and impacts of policies and incentives on rural organizations may be helpful. Further, ensuring resources intended for rural healthcare and public health organizations can be accessed and used by such organizations is crucial, but not always the case.28-30 The recent RHTP funding may provide additional resources to support collaborations aligned with program goals,22 including workforce development and technological innovation, which were identified as ongoing challenges for participants in this study. While states are still in the early stages of their RHTP activities, states such as Colorado, Illinois, and Kansas have explicitly described efforts to strengthen connections between healthcare and public health partners in their state applications, particularly related to chronic disease.31-33

Next, workforce-related factors were identified as both collaboration challenges and facilitators. Workforce constraints across both organization types are a key factor driving collaboration to enhance capacity. However, competition for skilled workers may inhibit collaboration. Nurses in particular represent a large share of both workforces, are vital to both types of organizations, and were described as 1 area in which competition occurs. Previous evidence aligns with this, estimating that public health nurses earn significantly less than nurses in clinical settings.34 In addition, many participants described collaboration formation through happenstance, personal connections, or the interests of a single person. While valuable, this model is not sustainable, replicable, or reliable. Finally, both the public health and healthcare workforce are under pressure, understaffed, and facing increasing shortages, which may be especially pronounced in rural areas.35-38 While there are programmatic and funding efforts designed to support each workforce,23,39,40 challenges remain. Sustained, long-term investments in the workforce, potentially including specific opportunities for cross-training across healthcare and public health, are needed. Examples of cross-training for clinicians may include medical school or residency rotations at public health agencies, dual MD/MPH programs, public health-focused certification or credentialing programs, or incorporating public health training into clinical program curricula.41-46

Lastly, local public health roles and activities are highly varied across the country, which may muddy understanding of public health and opportunities for collaboration. The field has long faced challenges communicating its role and value to policymakers and the public and this challenge may extend to potential healthcare partners.47-49 Evidence suggests that public health can impact key population health outcomes, often with relative cost savings. For example, small amounts of public health spending are estimated to decrease infectious disease morbidity, premature mortality, salmonella cases, sexually transmitted infection rates, and increase the portion of the population in very good or excellent health.50-55 Improved efforts to communicate the role and impact of public health, including tailored messaging for local healthcare organizations, policymakers, and the public, may increase collaboration interest, activities, and investments, some of which are already available or underway.49,56,57

While this study was not designed to identify successful models, participants shared a variety of collaborative efforts which may serve as examples for others, each designed to meet the needs of their organizations, programs and communities. Examples include: co-located or closely located services or offices, shared electronic medical record access, regular communication between leadership (including sharing phone numbers), joint grant proposals, board membership from across organizations, and shared staffing arrangements. As described above, the formality and challenges to each of these vary, but were seen by participants as supporting their organizational efforts in a valuable way. Additional opportunities for shared learning may be beneficial for organizations working in similar contexts.

Conclusions

Collaborations between local public health and healthcare organizations serving rural populations are valuable to overcome resource limitations and address population health needs. However, challenges related to funding, aligning priorities, personnel, and siloed federal and state structures may inhibit their ability to develop and maintain collaborations.
