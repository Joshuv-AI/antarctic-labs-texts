Cochlear Implantation: Electrode Impedance in Users With Triphasic Pulse Pattern
Authors: Alhassan Algazlan, Fida Almuhawas, Sarah Alarifi, Yassin Abdelsamad, Maram Alkahtani, Isra Aljazeeri, Abdulrahman Alsanosi, Abdulrahman Hagr
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesThe triphasic pulse pattern (TPP) is one of the successful strategies for managing facial nerve stimulation (FNS) after cochlear implantation (CI). This study evaluates the electrode impedance (EI) changes for patients who were shifted from the biphasic pulse pattern (BPP) to TPP.Materials and MethodsThis retrospective study was conducted on the patients who experienced FNS post‐CI and were managed with a TPP strategy. The fitting parameters with TPP were compared to those with BPP, including electrode impedance, maximum comfortable levels (MCL), threshold levels (THR), and phase duration. .ResultsThis study included 25 implanted ears. The impedances of the electrode contacts significantly decreased when shifted to TPP at the first two apical contacts compared to the BPP (7.0 ± 2.7 vs. 7.8 ± 3.4 kΩ at the first contact and 6.5 ± 2.4 vs. 7.1 ± 2.9 kΩ at the second contact, respectively). The MCL showed a statistically significant increase with TPP compared to the BPP at the first 10 electrode contacts.ConclusionThe TPP of stimulation appears to be a successful strategy for managing FNS. Among the patients with FNS, there is a decrease in the most apical impedance values and an increase in MCL when using TPP. These findings suggest that TPP might be considered a routine and successful pattern for FNS patients. Future studies should investigate the impact of different stimulation patterns on CI outcomes.Level of Evidence3.

Introduction

Cochlear implantation is a life‐changing intervention for individuals with severe to profound sensorineural hearing loss (SNHL) who do not receive sufficient benefit from hearing aids [1, 2]. It involves surgically implanting a device that electrically stimulates the auditory nerve, restoring a sense of hearing. Facial nerve stimulation (FNS) is a minor complication of CI and presents a significant challenge during fitting sessions. The prevalence of FNS among CI patients is around 5.29%, with a greater prevalence associated with certain risk factors such as cochlear ossification, inner ear deformity, temporal bone fractures, meningitis, and otosclerosis [3]. It has also been theorized that FNS can be caused by a thin bony partition between the upper basal turn of the cochlea and the labyrinthine segment of the facial nerve, which is where the electrode and the facial nerve are at the least physical distance from each other [4, 5, 6, 7]. However, Gärtner et al. stated that the position of the electrode array may not be solely responsible for FNS [8].

FNS can restrict the electric dynamic range, particularly at higher current levels. To resolve the FNS effect, there has been a strategic pivot toward using the Triphasic Pulse Pattern (TPP) instead of the traditional Biphasic Pulse Pattern (BPP). TPP has a unique configuration of three distinct phases in each electrical pulse delivered to the auditory nerve [1, 9, 10]. The adoption of TPP in CI aims to refine neural stimulation precision and efficiency, thereby enhancing auditory perception and speech understanding [1, 11, 12, 13]. This adjustment aims to optimize the key fitting parameters, such as the maximum comfortable levels (MCL), thresholds (THR), and thus dynamic ranges (DR) for individuals with CI [2, 9].

Measuring the electrode impedance (EI), which represents the electrical resistance of the contacts along the electrode array, is crucial during and after the CI surgery. This measurement is part of a more comprehensive test named impedance field telemetry. This test is vital for assessing the impedance values, electrode contact status, ground path impedance, functionality, and integrity of the internal device [2]. Following CI, foreign body reactions within the cochlea lead to the adhesion of cells, proteins, and other materials around the electrodes. This biological response triggers the formation of a fibrotic tissue sheath around the electrode array, influenced by electrochemical processes at the electrode surface. Despite the clinical significance of intracochlear fibrosis, reliable methods for its assessment remain unclear. Nevertheless, routine EI measurements at the beginning of every fitting session have been suggested as predictive tools for evaluating intracochlear fibrosis [2, 11]. A lower impedance value is generally associated with better device performance [1, 2]. Electrode impedance plays a crucial role in the distribution of intracochlear electrical fields, influencing current spread and neural excitation patterns. Studies have demonstrated that impedance variations across electrode contacts can be linked to anatomical differences and tissue conductivities, possibly connected to unintended facial nerve stimulation in certain cochlear implant users [14].

Despite the proven efficacy of the TPP in resolving FNS in many patients, its impact on impedance levels is still unclear. Therefore, the primary objective of this study is to assess the electrode impedance in patients with TPP compared to BPP among the CI patients who experienced FNS. The secondary objective is to compare the main fitting parameters, such as MCL, THR, phase duration, central frequency, and stimulation rate in TPP with BPP.

Materials and Methods

Participants

This retrospective cohort study was conducted on all patients with SNHL who underwent cochlear implantation with the same implant (MED‐EL, Innsbruck, Austria) in a tertiary CI center between 2018 and 2024. The institutional review board approved this study, ethical approval number 240996/IRB, and the requirement for informed consent was waived due to its retrospective nature. This work included CI patients, whether they were implanted unilaterally or bilaterally; had the same electrode array design; full electrode insertion; and normal electrode position. Patients who were on BPP and shifted to TPP as part of the management of FNS were included. The exclusion criteria were patients with cochlear otosclerosis, temporal bone pathologies, extracochlear insertion, postmeningitis, otic‐capsule–involving temporal bone fracture, patients who had central nervous system abnormalities or intellectual disability, and patients who missed the postoperative audiological and speech assessment follow‐up.

Fitting Process

All participants encountered varying degrees of FNS during or following the fitting sessions while fitted with the BPP. Instances of FNS were either self‐reported by the patients or observed by the clinicians. In response, a series of fitting adjustments were made, including remeasuring MCL, reassessing THRs, adjusting sensitivity, extending phase duration, altering compression, and deactivating channels associated with FNS. Despite these interventions, satisfactory control of FNS was not reached until TPP was used as a basis for stimulation patterns instead of BPP.

Detailed fitting parameters were extracted from the programming software (MAESTRO 9) for each fitting session. Data from the final session in which the patient was fitted with BPP (before transitioning to TPP) and the most recent session using TPP were compiled into an Excel spreadsheet for further analysis.

Device Usage Quantification

Daily CI use was objectively monitored via the sound processors' datalogging function, with average daily usage calculated for the final month of stable BPP programming (pre‐TPP period) and the first month following TPP optimization (post‐TPP period). Data were retrieved using MAESTRO 9 software during routine clinical sessions.

Statistical Analysis

A comparative analysis between BPP and TPP regarding impedance measures as a primary outcome was performed. The same analysis was done for the fitting parameters, including MCL, THR, effective phase duration, minimum phase duration, center frequency, and stimulation rate. Paired t‐test or Wilcoxon signed rank test was applied according to the normality assumptions checked using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Means and standard deviations were used for descriptive statistics of quantitative variables, whereas counts and percentages were applied for categorical variables. All data were fed to the statistical analysis using R statistical software version 4.2.2, “Innocent and Trusting.” p value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Electrode Impedance Measures

Table 2 shows that the average impedance values significantly decreased using TPP only at the first two apical electrodes compared to the BPP (7.0 ± 2.7 vs. 7.8 ± 3.4 kΩ at the first electrode and 6.5 ± 2.4 vs. 7.1 ± 2.9 kΩ at the second electrode). However, the next two apical electrodes showed a slightly lower but nonsignificant impedance using the TPP compared to the BPP (6.0 ± 2.2 vs. 6.1 ± 2.1 kΩ at the third electrode and 5.8 ± 2.0 vs. 5.9 ± 1.8 kΩ for the fourth electrode). Moreover, all middle and basal electrodes showed nonsignificant differences in impedance values between TPP and BPP. A non‐significant increased impedance was observed using the triphasic pulse at the 7th and 11th electrodes (5.2 ± 2.0 and 7.9 ± 4.4 kΩ, respectively) compared to the biphasic values (5.0 ± 1.1 and 7.4 ± 2.7 kΩ, respectively) as shown in Figure 1. The mean ground path impedance (GPI) with TPP showed a statistically significant increase compared to the biphasic one (0.9 ± 0.3 vs. 0.8 ± 0.1, considering the presence of an outlier), as shown in Figure 2.

Maximum Comfortable and Threshold Levels

The maximum comfortable level (MCL) showed a statistically significant increase using TPP compared to the BPP at the first 10 electrodes, whereas a nonsignificant increase was found at the 11th and 12th electrodes. Furthermore, the threshold (THR) using TPP increased significantly at the four apical electrodes compared to the biphasic pattern. Meanwhile, THR showed a nonsignificant increase in the four middle electrodes. After switching to TPP, the 9th and 10th basal electrodes showed significantly increased THRs (5.1 ± 3.9 and 4.7 ± 3.3 vs. 3.6 ± 2.6 and 3.6 ± 2.5, respectively) but lost the significant difference by reaching the last two electrodes (Figure 3).

Effective and Minimum Phase Duration

The average effective triphasic phase duration showed an increase at all electrode contacts compared to the biphasic pattern; however, the detected increase was statistically significant at the first five electrodes only, followed by a nonsignificant increase at the sixth and seventh electrodes and a significant increase again at the eighth electrode. Furthermore, the four basal electrodes showed nonsignificant differences. The average minimum triphasic phase duration increased but nonsignificantly at all apical and middle electrodes compared to the biphasic pattern, in contrast to the 9th, 11th, and 12th basal electrodes that showed a nonsignificant decrease with the triphasic pattern (Figure 4).

Center Frequency, Stimulation Rate, and Compliance Level

The mean center frequencies showed nonsignificant differences using TPP at all electrode contacts compared to the BPP. Regarding the rate of stimulation, the triphasic pulse showed a statistically significant decrease in rate only at the four apical electrodes compared to the biphasic pattern (2836.7 ± 2570.8, 2825.1 ± 2517.3, 2800.2 ± 2536.6, and 2894.7 ± 2545.9, respectively, for triphasic vs. 4363.2 ± 3637.4 at the four apical electrodes in the biphasic pattern). Meanwhile, the middle and basal electrodes did not show significant differences in TPP and BPP stimulation rates. Regarding the compliance level, only the first two apical electrodes showed a statistically significant increase using TPP compared to BPP (1108.2 ± 506.1 and 1171.4 ± 500.5 vs. 1019.0 ± 456.7 and 1014.0 ± 441.9, respectively). However, the remaining electrodes showed nonsignificant differences between TPP and BPP (Figure 5).

Device Usage Patterns

CI datalogging revealed significantly increased daily device usage following TPP implementation. The average wearing time increased from 7.43 ± 3.60 h/day pre‐TPP to 10.09 ± 2.88 h/day post‐TPP (p = 0.0095, Figure 6). This 35.8% increase in device usage correlated with reduced FNS reports and occurred concurrently with observed impedance changes at apical electrodes.

Discussion

The results of the current study showed that the use of TPP led to a statistically significant decrease in electrode impedance for the first two apical electrodes compared to BPP. This decrease could be attributed to many factors; one might be the higher wearing time after managing the FNS with TPP. The significant reduction in apical electrode impedance corresponds temporally with the increases in device utilization. CI datalogging demonstrated a 35.8% increase in the wearing time post‐TPP (10.09 h/day vs. 7.43 h/day, p = 0.0095; Figure 6), suggesting that improved comfort with TPP enables longer device use. This extended stimulation exposure may facilitate electrochemical stabilization at the electrode‐tissue interface, and another factor could be the potential impact of the electric stimulation that resulted from increased MCL with TPP. This stimulation might improve neural excitation patterns from TPP's charge‐balanced design, enhancing perceptual clarity and auditory comfort Liepins et al. [13]. The resulting auditory benefit likely facilitates greater CI acceptance and usage.

The higher impedance levels in the more basal parts of the cochlea have also been consistently correlated with the higher amount of tissue growth in animal studies in these regions [15]. The prior evidence has led to the belief that intracochlear fibrosis is correlated with higher impedance. The measured impedance on the apical and basal electrodes demonstrated a high link with the tissue development surrounding the electrode array in terms of tissue growth [11]. Changes in the cochlea's tissue following surgery may be indicated by these increases in basal impedance [16]. TPP's three‐phase structure could result in more efficient charge distribution, potentially reducing the accumulation of charged particles at the electrode surface [9].

Furthermore, lower impedance has been associated with better structural and hearing preservation. Some authors have reported impedance as a biomarker for hearing preservation [17, 18]. Intraoperative impedance measurements and intraoperative electrocochleography are current trends for real‐time monitoring of the insertion process to decrease structural and residual hearing loss [19, 20]. Some authors have suggested that the impedance is also related to modiolar proximity [21, 22, 23]. It has been proposed that decreased intracochlear fibrosis and structural preservation are suggested to coincide with low impedance levels [1, 24].

The findings of the current study on the effects of TPP on electrode impedance may vary across different cochlear implant types and patient populations. Variations in electrode design, such as array length, shape, and material, can influence impedance measurements and fibrotic tissue formation [11]. Additionally, patient demographics, including age and underlying conditions, may lead to different healing responses and impedance profiles. For instance, patients with cochlear ossification or temporal bone abnormalities may experience altered impedance profiles due to unique anatomical challenges [1, 5]. Moreover, FNS is a complication in some patients, and it may necessitate a tailored approach to programming strategies, such as changing the pulse patterns. The effectiveness of TPP in managing FNS could vary based on individual anatomical differences and the underlying cause of hearing loss, further influencing impedance outcomes [3, 24].

Interestingly, although there were notable differences in impedance observed in the most apical electrodes, no significant changes in impedance were observed in the mid and basal electrodes. This finding suggests that the beneficial effects of triphasic pulses may be more noticeable in the impedance of the most apical region. The correlation between cochlear and facial nerve anatomy may explain the effectiveness of TPP in a specific region, along with the observed lower impedance in the apical electrode region and subsequent reduction in FNS. Previous studies have highlighted the proximity of the labyrinthine segment of the facial nerve to the superior segment of the basal turn of the cochlea, which likely contributes to the occurrence of FNS. However, other studies have stated that this proximity is not the only reason for FNS3. Nevertheless, the application of TPP addresses this anatomical relationship and significantly resolves FNS [3].

The role of electrical field distribution could also be influential. Conversely, the observed nonsignificant difference in impedance along many electrode contacts might have resulted from the extended period before the transition from BPP to TPP. These findings emphasize the importance of targeted electrode programming to optimize patient outcomes. Furthermore, using TPP resulted in higher MCL values for the first 10 electrodes and increased electric thresholds at the apical and basal electrodes. In general, the dynamic range was wider with TPP compared to BPP. This difference in the dynamic range could be attributed to the ability to increase the MCL when shifting from BPP to TPP, allowing patients to adequate loudness. In the case of FNS, increasing the MCL, even when required, could be difficult because every increase in the stimulation levels is most likely accompanied by increased severity and grade of FNS, leading to discomfort and dissatisfaction, which might result in nonuse in some cases. The current study's findings confirm that clinicians can go for higher stimulation values with TPP than with BPP without eliciting FNS. Several studies have found that a wider dynamic range correlates with improved speech perception and overall CI performance [25]. Improving hearing thresholds could lead to better speech recognition in noisy environments, a crucial measure of success for CI individuals [1, 12, 13, 24]. A previous review study showed improvements with TPP of up to 40% at 65 dB HL [24].

The current study also found that triphasic pulses resulted in a longer effective phase duration, especially in the first five electrodes. A decreased stimulation rate with TPP at the apical electrodes was also observed, which could result from increased MCL charges. In addition, a slight difference was found in the center frequency between TPP and BPP, which may be attributed to the reactivation of some electrode contacts after switching from BPP to TPP. However, the reasons for such findings and clinical implications require additional investigation, particularly regarding their impact on speech perception outcomes.

The implications of the findings of this study could be significant and wide‐ranging. From a clinical perspective, using TPP can offer improved customization of CI programming. This is particularly beneficial for patients who encounter discomfort, suboptimal performance, or FNS when using traditional BPP. By implementing triphasic stimulation, clinicians can better address individual patient needs and enhance overall outcomes. Although our study has yielded valuable insights, it is important to acknowledge its limitations. Although adequate for identifying significant changes, the sample size may not fully represent the diverse range of patient responses to TPP. Further randomized controlled research is necessary to explore these areas, as the long‐term effects and practical applications were not explicitly addressed. Prospective research could investigate the impact of TPP on speech comprehension in different listening environments and its long‐term effects on electrode integrity and cochlear functionality. Since TPP is available from one manufacturer only, further studies should also be conducted to investigate the impact of other pulse patterns on FNS.

Conclusion

In patients with FNS, TPP presents notable benefits compared to BPP, including decreased electrode impedance in the most apical electrodes, higher maximum stimulation levels, and wider electric dynamic ranges. These findings highlight the potential role of triphasic stimulation in enhancing cochlear implant performance and increasing patient satisfaction. Notably, TPP implementation was associated with a significant increase in daily device usage, reflecting improved patient tolerance and comfort. Future research should focus on the impacts of different pulse patterns on FNS management while observing patient outcomes and satisfaction levels.
