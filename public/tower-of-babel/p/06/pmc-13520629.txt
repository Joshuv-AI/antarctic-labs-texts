Aromatase inhibitor-induced psoriasiform dermatitis: A case report and literature review
Authors: Bram A. Klein, Anagha B. Thiagarajan, Ronald J. Barr, Briana R. Halle Brady, Michelle S. Min
Published: 2026

Introduction

Excluding skin cancer, breast cancer is the most common malignancy amongst women worldwide. Due to increasing global incidence, there remains a critical need for not only effective cancer treatments but also for management of therapy-related adverse events. Among breast cancers, estrogen receptor positive tumors account for most diagnoses in postmenopausal women, for which adjuvant endocrine therapy with aromatase inhibitors (AIs) remains a cornerstone of therapy.1 We present the case of a woman who developed a psoriasiform dermatitis induced by aromatase inhibitors. We discuss diagnostic features, immunologic mechanisms, and the role of estrogen in modulating psoriasis-related inflammation, as well as implications for clinical management.

Case report

A 68-year-old woman recently diagnosed with estrogen receptor-positive breast cancer presented with a mildly pruritic rash on her trunk, which began within 1 month of initiating letrozole. Her rash was limited when letrozole was held for 3 months during cyclophosphamide-docetaxel-pegfilgrastim chemotherapy. After chemotherapy, letrozole was resumed, with marked worsening of the rash over the next month. Subsequent transition to anastrozole, another AI, resulted in continued progression of the eruption, and her planned mastectomy was delayed due to concerns from surgical oncology about her rash.

Physical exam revealed thick pink plaques with silvery scale on the face, scalp, abdomen, lower back, and lower extremities, involving 15% of her body surface area (Fig 1). Skin biopsies from the posterior neck and back demonstrated hyperkeratosis, parakeratosis, hypogranulosis, and irregular psoriasiform hyperplasia (Fig 2). Given the clinicopathologic correlation and temporal correlation with her AI, a diagnosis of AI-induced psoriasis was made. After discussion with her oncologist, risankizumab was initiated given its efficacy, mechanism, and favorable safety profile. The patient experienced significant improvement in her psoriasiform eruption, and she safely proceeded with surgical management for her breast cancer.

Discussion

Medications may aggravate preexisting psoriasis, induce new lesions in clinically unaffected skin, or provoke de novo psoriasis in individuals with or without known risk factors.2 The temporal association with AI therapy, coupled with improvement during drug pauses, supports a causal relationship between aromatase inhibitor therapy and our patient’s psoriasis.

Aromatase is an enzyme responsible for the peripheral conversion of androgens to estrogens, and its inhibition results in marked suppression of circulating estrogen levels.3 AIs are widely used in adjuvant therapy for estrogen receptor-positive breast cancer, where they have been shown to reduce the risk of cancer recurrence and improve overall survival.1 There are 2 main classes of AIs. Nonsteroidal AIs include anastrazole and letrozole, both reversible inhibitors of the aromatase enzyme. Steroidal AIs include exemestane, and they irreversibly inactivate aromatase.4

Although generally well-tolerated, AIs have also been linked to several cutaneous reactions, often related to estrogen depletion. These reactions include erythema nodosum, vasculitis, subacute cutaneous lupus erythematosus, hypersensitivity reactions, erythema multiforme, systemic sclerosis, and cutaneous nodulosis.3,4 In one review, these reactions were noted to occur a median time of 2 months after initiation of drug therapy, with a range of 5 days to 6 months.3 The majority of cutaneous eruptions have been managed with topical and systemic steroids.3,4 In most cases, similar to our patient, switching medications within the drug class did not show benefit except in one case of erythema nodosum, though switching from a nonsteroidal to a steroidal AI did lead to improvement in several patients.4 Overall, AI-induced psoriasiform eruptions are not well-characterized in current literature, with only one documented case.5

Estrogen is believed to play a central immunoregulatory role in psoriasis. Experimental models demonstrate that estrogens suppress pro-inflammatory cytokine production, including interleukin (IL)-1β and IL-23, by neutrophils and dendritic cells.6 Thus, reduced estrogen may paradoxically enhance IL-23-driven responses and remove inhibitory control over the neutrophil- and dendritic cell–mediated IL-23/Th17 axis.7 Clinical studies support this mechanism, showing an inverse correlation between serum estrogen levels and psoriasis severity.8

Recent research also implicates poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase 2 (PARP2) as a contributor to psoriatic inflammation.9 PARP2 is implicated in keratinocyte proliferation and inflammatory regulation pathways, and aromatase function enhances PARP2’s anti-inflammatory effect in murine models. Inhibition of aromatase may therefore impair PARP2-mediated anti-inflammatory activity, suggesting a possible protective role of aromatase in the skin.9

Management of AI-induced psoriasis necessitates multidisciplinary care. When AI is required to address malignancy, we recommend treating through drug rashes when possible. Continuation of therapy should consider rash severity, impact on quality of life, and comorbidities. For mild psoriatic skin disease without psoriatic arthritis, topicals remain first line. For more significant disease, phototherapy may be considered, though efficacy is limited and frequent sessions may be burdensome to patients. Prior guidelines have advised acitretin, methotrexate, or apremilast as safe options for drug eruptions in the setting of malignancy, but these therapies are associated with lesser efficacy and increased side effects compared to several of our newer systemic agents. Immunosuppressive therapies, such as cyclosporine, are associated with increased malignancy risk and should be avoided.10

Recent studies have demonstrated the safety of IL-17 and IL-23 inhibitors in patients with malignancy.11 Given their speed of onset, superior efficacy, and safety profile, biologics should be considered for patients, though discussion with treating oncologists is warranted. In this case, our patient had severe skin disease, causing a delay in her surgical management. Thus, an IL-23 inhibitor was chosen, leading to rapid and robust improvement.

This case highlights the importance of recognizing AI-induced psoriasis as a potential complication of endocrine and oncologic therapy. Timely management with coordinated multidisciplinary care is imperative to ensure adherence to life-saving cancer treatment. We additionally highlight that our newer, targeted biologic agents may be considered as safe and effective treatment options for certain patients. This case represents an important addition to limited literature on AI-induced psoriasis, underscoring the essential role dermatologists play in recognizing and managing oncologic drug–related skin toxicities.
