Modular Biosurface Engineering of Magnetotactic Bacteria for Multimodal Synergistic Cancer Therapy
Authors: Qilong Li, Wangqing Li, Xuefei Sun, Feng Feng, Zhengdong Cheng, Wenchao Li, Li Yao, Xiuyu Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bacterial-mediated cancer therapy shows therapeutic potential yet remains constrained by bioavailability and biosafety challenges. We introduce a scalable, modular biosurface-engineering platform for functionalizing wild-type magnetotactic bacteria (Magnetospirillum magneticum, AMB-1), enabling the integration of diverse materials, including small molecules, polymers, nanoparticles, and metal–organic frameworks. As a proof-of-concept, AMB-1@Fe3+-PDA (poly dopamine) exemplifies a bacteria-mediated therapeutic strategy that merges tumor-targeted delivery, immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) reprogramming, and innate immune activation with photothermal-chemodynamic therapy. The Fe3+-PDA coating counteracts deep-tissue immunosuppression and primes adaptive immunity, while AMB-1 enhances penetration into resistant TME niches. These mechanisms synergistically suppress aggressive 4T1 breast tumor progression, metastasis, and recurrence while establishing immunological memory. In murine models, two treatment cycles prolonged median survival from 45 to 67 days and elicited systemic antitumor immunity, including abscopal effects targeting untreated distant tumors. This platform establishes a bridge between materials science and synthetic biology, providing a generalizable blueprint for advancing the clinical translation of engineered bacterial therapies.

Introduction

The unique anticancer mechanisms of bacterial therapies, which are beyond the reach of standard treatments, − are made possible by their ability to exploit key features of the tumor microenvironment (TME), namely hypoxia, immunosuppression, and nutrient abundance. − Typically, bacteria autonomously sense and respond to signals of hypoxic TME and preferentially migrate to hypoxic regions by self-propulsion. , Concurrently, the eutrophic tumor microenvironment amplifies the robust hemorrhage triggered by bacterially induced tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α). − This process establishes a feedback loop that facilitates deeper intratumoral penetration of the therapeutic bacteria. , Moreover, bacteria proliferate robustly in the TME due to its unique immunosuppressive environment. , Subsequent infection within the TME recruits innate immune cells, such as dendritic cells (DCs), neutrophils, and macrophages, into colonized tumors. , This recruitment elevates local chemokine and cytokine levels, orchestrating a potent antitumor immune response. , In this context, bacteria-mediated tumor therapy demonstrates critical advantages over tumor cell-specific therapies, as its inherent tumor microenvironment (TME) targeting capability and immune activation represent essential characteristics for optimized cancer treatment strategies. Over the past decade, bacterial genera such as Salmonella and Clostridium have demonstrated tumor growth suppression and survival prolongation in preclinical models. − Notably, magnetotactic bacteria (MTB), which exhibit geomagnetic field-guided locomotion (25–65 μT sensitivity), have emerged as an advanced bioplatform for simultaneous targeting, adapting to, and remodeling of TME. −

Similar to other immunostimulatory bacteria, magnetotactic bacteria produce various immune-stimulating substances, such as β-glucan, glycoproteins, and lipopolysaccharide, that can act as adjuvants to remodel the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME). − Distinct from many genera of bacteria, magnetotactic bacteria contain magnetic single domain particles in their magnetosome organelle. , These magnetic single-domain particles, with optimal sizes and shapes, and their intracellular linear assembly, provide a magnetic field-driven force that enhances tumor penetration and targeting. Additionally, as facultative anaerobes, MTB exhibit oxygen gradient-directed motility (aerotaxis), enabling efficient enrichment within hypoxic tumor niches (O2 < 0.5%). , This active targeting mechanism fundamentally differs from passive molecular diffusion (Fickian decay model, R2 < 0.8 beyond 100 μm vasculature), , instead employing a bistable redox-state switching system that integrates magnetic navigation with hypoxia-responsive chemotaxis. The resultant magneto-aerotactic cascade achieves 3.9-fold higher intratumoral accumulation than conventional nanoparticle delivery systems, as quantified by whole-body magnetic particle imaging tracking. Notably, the linear arrangement of magnetosomes, exhibiting high magnetic anisotropy (104–105 J m–3), significantly enhances proton spin relaxation through optimized dipole–dipole interactions. , This property enables precise T 2-weighted MRI detection with <1 mm spatial resolution, allowing noninvasive visualization of bacterial colonization patterns and real-time tracking of tumor infiltration dynamics. , Despite their inherent multifunctionality, magnetotactic bacteria (MTB) exhibit marked vulnerability to environmental stressorsincluding temperature fluctuations (±2 °C tolerance), oxygen concentration (1–3%) and metabolic perturbations (e.g., pH gradients >0.5 units)during critical stages of bioprocessing. − Thus, developing methods to functionalize MTB, enhancing their therapeutic potential while preserving their native activity, remains a critical yet difficult task.

In this study, we selected Magnetospirillum magneticum (AMB-1), a prototypical magnetotactic bacterium with a well-characterized magnetosome biogenesis pathway, as a scalable microbial model for biohybrid engineering. Through optimized electrostatic interactions, we established a high-throughput platform enabling three-dimensional functionalization of viable AMB-1 cells (Scheme ), achieving: (1) Modular Surface Engineering: A diverse range of functional coatings were successfully integrated, including small molecules (Figure S1), bioadhesive polymers­(Figure S2), magnetic nanoparticles (Figure S3), and Fe3+-coordinated metal–organic frameworks (Figure S4 and Figure ) with 85 ± 5% coating uniformity across individual bacteria; (2) Functional Synergy Preservation: Maintained >92% bacterial viability (live/dead assay, Figure S5) and magnetosome structural integrity after postfunctionalization (Figure S6a–S6d); (3) Clinical–Translational Scalability: Demonstrated GMP-compatible production of 1.2 × 1010 CFU mL–1 engineered AMB-1 per batch with <5% interbatch variability, meeting FDA guidelines for microbial therapeutics (CMC criteria, Figure S6e). Furthermore, as a proof-of-concept to demonstrate bacteria-mediated tumor therapy, we engineered Fe3+-coordinated polydopamine (Fe3+-PDA)-functionalized AMB-1 (designated APF) to exhibit synergistic antitumor efficacy (Figure S7). The AMB-1 moiety retains the native activity and functionality of wild-type AMB-1. In addition to promoting tumor penetration, AMB-1 provides intrinsic immune stimulation. The Fe3+-PDA coating moiety mainly endows APF with Fe3+-induced chemodynamic therapy and PDA-mediated photothermal therapy, which together promote tumor-cell damage and antigen release, especially after laser irradiation, thereby enhancing antitumor immunity. The dual immune activation strategies effectively reversed the tumor immunosuppressive microenvironment. Notably, the magnetosome’s T 2 imaging capability enabled the visualization of APF.

Experimental Section

Materials

All reagents and materials were used as obtained from commercial sources, without additional purification. Iron­(III) acetylacetonate (98%), oleic acid (90%), oleylamine (80–90%), 1-octadecene, 2,6-diaminopyridine, and dopamine hydrochloride were purchased from Aladdin (China) and used as received. Other analytical-grade solvents, including ethanol, tetrahydrofuran (THF), and cyclohexane, were obtained from Beijing Reagents Co. Distilled water was prepared using a Milli-Q system (Millipore, USA). The Magnetospirillum magneticum AMB-1 bacterial strain (ATCC 700264) was provided by S.T. (Institute of Electrical Engineering, Chinese Academy of Sciences).

Characterization

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) imaging was conducted by using a Hitachi HT7700 instrument (Japan), while high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) analysis was performed with a JEM-2100F microscope (Japan). Particle size distribution was assessed through dynamic light scattering (DLS) on a Nano ZS system (Malvern, UK). Magnetic properties were evaluated by using a superconducting quantum interference device (SQUID) magnetometer (MPMS-3, UK). For elemental analysis, the iron content in samples was quantified via inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS, Thermo iCAP RQ, USA). Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans were acquired with a 1 T MRI scanner (ASPECT M3, Israel). Fluorescence imaging was carried out using a laser confocal microscope (OLYMPUS FV1000-IX81, Japan).

MR Imaging in Vitro and in Vivo

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was used to track and evaluate the APF distribution both in vitro and in vivo. All MRI experiments were conducted on a 1.0 T MRI scanner (Aspect M3 system). For in vitro MRI, APF samples were prepared at a series of concentrations ranging from 0 (PBS only) up to 2.6 × 108 CFU mL–1. These samples were scanned to observe the MRI signal as a function of the bacterial concentration. For in vivo MRI, Balb/c mice bearing 4T1 tumor xenografts were used. Each mouse (n = 3) received an intravenous injection of 25 μL of APF (5 × 107 CFU mL–1). MRI scans of the mice were performed at designated time points after injection. Imaging was carried out using a fast spin–echo (FSE) sequence with the following parameters: repetition time (TR) = 4500 ms, echo time (TE) = 75 ms, field of view = 40 × 40 mm, matrix size = 256 × 256, number of slices = 14, and slice thickness = 1 mm. These MR images were analyzed to determine the localization and persistence of the APF in the tumor region over time.

Magnetic Field-Mediated Directional Control

A custom-built “magnetotaxis” platform was used to apply an external magnetic field to live animals for guiding the bacteria. Each mouse was placed at the center of the platform, which consisted of two parallel permanent magnets (dimensions 30 × 50 × 5 mm each). The magnets were oriented with opposite poles facing each other and spaced 5 cm apart, generating a directional magnetic field with a field gradient in the region of interest. During the experiment, this magnetic field was directed toward the tumor site on the mouse. The intention was to steer the Fe3O4-containing APF bacteria toward the tumor by magnetic attraction, thereby concentrating the bacteria in the tumor region for enhanced therapeutic effect.

Results and Discussion

The characterization of the AMB-1@Fe3+-PDA composite (designated APF) is presented in Figure and Figures S8–S9. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images reveal a distinct extra outer shell on the coated bacteria (Figure a,b), in stark contrast to the sharp edge of uncoated AMB-1 (Figure S8d). The homogeneous distribution of Fe and N dispersed on the bacterial surface was revealed by dark-field TEM (Figure c) and the corresponding energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping results (Figure d), further demonstrating the presence of a coating Fe3+-PDA film. Additionally, the negative zeta potential of AMB-1, which derived from the deprotonation of phosphate groups and carboxylic groups equipped on the bacteria, increased vastly from – 19.6 to 38.6 mV after coating with Fe3+-PDA (Figure e), due to the presence of positive charged Fe3+-PDA in the coating. The UV–visible spectra of AMB-1, PDA, AP, and APF also indicate that decorating individual bacteria with Fe3+-PDA coatings is successful (Figure f). As expected, the Fe3+-PDA coating moist rendered APF a higher degree of light absorption (300–900 nm), further supporting that the Fe-PDA moiety coat on the AMB-1 interface. Most importantly, compared with the magnetosomes produced by uncoated magnetospirillum, the highly ordered chain structure of magnetosomes in the APF remains unchanged, implying unaffected magnetotaxis-induced tumor-targeting capacity and intratumor penetration of APF. The arrangement of magnetosomes in chains maximizes its magnetic dipole, resulting in room-temperature ferromagnetism with the saturation magnetization of 40 emu g–1 (Figure g), suggesting that APF possesses excellent magnetotactic ability. The unique magnetic characteristics of intracellular magnetosomes efficiently shorten the T 2 relaxation time for MR imaging, enabling their use as vectors with real-time feedback functions to realize closed-loop transportation tracking.

The hypoxic, immunosuppressive, and eutrophic tumor microenvironment provides a survivable niche for the facultative AMB-1 moiety. Concurrently, the specific tumor microenvironment is accompanied by acidity and the overproduction of H2O2, a result of upregulated glycolytic metabolism. This disordered microenvironment is well-suitable to be exploited by the APF moiety to generate the hydroxyl radical (·OH) primarily for tumor chemodynamic therapy (CDT) through the Fenton reactions. As demonstrated in Figure a, APF dissolved redox-active ferric ions under the mildly acidic conditions (pH = 6.5) of the simulated TME due to the protonation of dopamine and initiated the Fenton reaction to H2O2 (100 μM), generating cytotoxic and nonspecific ·OH. The catalytic efficiency of ·OH generation was investigated by monitoring TMB degradation. With the presence of ·OH, colorless TMB could be oxidized and exhibited a time-dependent absorbance at 652 nm. The ·OH generated by APF catalysis was visualized by the colorimetric degradation of TMB (Figure b), providing a direct measure of its catalytic efficiency. In stark contrast to the negative controls (PBS, AMB-1, or AP), which showed no TMB degradation after 20 min in simulated TME buffer (100 μM H2O2, pH 6.5), the APF + H2O2 + H+ group produced a distinct color change. This change, consistent with the Fe3+ control, confirms the responsive Fe3+ release and catalytic ability of APF. The intracellular ROS generation by APF was further confirmed via confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) using the fluorescent probe DCFH-DA (Figure c). In contrast to PBS-treated controls, 4T1 cells exhibited green fluorescence within 30 min of simultaneous treatment with APF and H2O2 (100 μM) at pH 6.5. Fluorescence intensity increased dramatically with further incubation, indicating highly efficient generation of reactive hydroxyl radicals (·OH). Given that uncoated AMB-1 alone minimally enhanced intracellular ·OH levels, the ability of APF platforms to induce ·OH accumulation can be attributed to the PDA-Fe coating. Collectively, this APF will ensure normal tissue safety because the generation of highly active and lethal ·OH only occurs in TME and tumor cell.

Apart from the catalytic functions of APF, the APF also shows favorable absorbance features (Figure f) and excellent photothermal conversion efficiencies, implying that APF could be used as a photothermal agent simultaneously. As demonstrated in Figure d, compared with control experiment (PBS), the NIR light (808 nm) irradiation intensity of uncoated AMB is 1.0 W cm–2 increased the temperature from an initial temperature of 25 to 32 °C within 10 min. This result indicates that AMB-1 has a certain photothermal effect, though the temperature rise is not enough to reach the treatment window of photothermal therapy (PTT). As expected, the temperature increase (ΔT ≈ 20 °C) of APF was much higher than that of AMB-1 (ΔT ≈ 7 °C) after laser irradiation (808 nm, 1.0 W cm–2, 10 min) using the same concentration. The significant temperature increases clearly demonstrated the impact of the Fe-PDA coating moisty on the enhanced photothermal heating efficiency, which is almost thrice the level seen for AMB-1. The superior photothermal effect of APF is further visualized by thermal imaging in Figure e. Additionally, the photothermal effect exhibited a positive correlation with both APF concentration and laser power (Figure S10), demonstrating that heat generation can be precisely controlled. Given the TME-targeting characteristics of AMB-1, the APF could also be used as a photothermal agent for targeting PPT, further improving the efficacy of PTT.

Since the efficiency of Fenton or Fenton-like reactions in CDT can be enhanced by heat, we hypothesized that APF-mediated hyperthermia would improve CDT efficacy and thereby strengthen the antitumor effect. We first evaluated the cytotoxicity and biocompatibility of APF at the cellular level. As shown by the CCK-8 assay (Figure f), APF exhibited negligible cytotoxicity toward 4T1 cells at concentrations below 2 × 108 CFU mL–1, indicating good biocompatibility. Therefore, 5 × 107 CFU mL–1 was selected for subsequent experiments. At this concentration, CDT alone induced approximately 40% cell death, whereas the combination of CDT and PTT (APF + NIR, 1.0 W cm–2, 10 min) increased cell killing to about 60% (Figure g), demonstrating a synergistic therapeutic effect. This enhancement is likely due to the temperature increase under laser irradiation, which accelerates Fenton reaction kinetics and promotes ·OH generation.We next assessed the MRI capability of APF. As shown in Figure h and i, APF exhibited a concentration-dependent T2 signal enhancement owing to the presence of magnetosomes, indicating its potential for effective T2-weighted imaging and localization tracking.

We then investigated whether APF could modulate the tumor immune microenvironment. Because AMB-1 provides intrinsic immune stimulation, while Fe3+-PDA-mediated CDT/PTT promotes tumor-cell damage, immunogenic cell death (ICD), and antigen release after laser irradiation, APF was expected to activate both innate and adaptive antitumor immunity. To test this hypothesis, 4T1 tumor-bearing mice were peritumorally injected with PBS, AMB, APF, or APF + Laser, followed by flow-cytometric analysis of tumors and inguinal lymph nodes (Figure ). Compared with PBS, both AMB and APF significantly increased M1 macrophage polarization and dendritic-cell maturation, indicating that the bacterial component is mainly responsible for basal immune activation (Figure a, b). After laser irradiation, these effects were further enhanced: the proportion of M1 macrophages increased to 36.9%, and the proportion of mature DCs increased to 27.9%, consistent with amplified therapy-induced immune activation. In addition, tumor-specific antigen-presenting DCs (CD45+CD11c+CD103+MHC-II+) showed little change in the AMB and APF groups, but increased significantly to 9.54% in the APF + Laser group (Figure c), further supporting enhanced antigen presentation after combined treatment.We further analyzed T-cell responses. Both AMB and APF moderately increased the proportion of CD8+ cytotoxic T lymphocytes, whereas APF + Laser-induced a much stronger response, with CTL levels 1.4-fold higher than those in the control group (Figure d). By contrast, the proportion of CD4+ helper T cells remained largely unchanged. Consistently, cytokine analysis showed that TNF-α, IFN-γ, and CXCL10 were all markedly elevated in the APF + Laser group relative to the AMB and APF groups (Figure e–g). Together, these results demonstrate that APF and Laser effectively remodel the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment and promote antitumor immune activation.

Motivated by the TME-responsive properties and potent in vitro antitumor activity, we next evaluated the in vivo tumor inhibition efficacy of APF in a 4T1 breast cancer model (Figure a). Initially, we evaluated the photothermal capability and magnetic-field-enhanced tumor penetration of APF at the in vivo level. As shown in Figure b, mice treated with the AP + laser and APF + laser exhibited a significant temperature increase compared to the control group. And under identical laser irradiation conditions, APF-injected mice displayed the highest tumor temperature increase (ΔT = 18.1 °C). The intrinsic mineralized magnetosomes of AMB allowed us to visualize APF localization during tumor penetration via MRI T 2 imaging. As shown in Figure c, the peritumoral injection of APF exhibited significantly enhanced tumor penetration under magnetic field activation. We then assessed the tumor inhibition effect of APF on 4T1 tumors. As shown in Figure d–f and S11, bare AMB-1 exhibited moderate inhibitory effects compared to PBS or PBS + laser, and the tumor volumes were reduced by 30%, which might be attributed to the intrinsic tumor accumulation and intratumoral immune stimulation. Compared to AMB-1, APF showed an enhanced antitumor effect, indicating that Fe-PDA coating moiety triggers on-demand localization of Fenton reaction. In contrast, FeCl3 exhibited little inhibitory effect, probably because free Fe3+ was metabolized rapidly without activating CDT. Notably, tumors in the APF + laser group were nearly completely suppressed, likely due to the strong tumor ablation effect of PTT and the synergistic action of PTT, CDT, and immunotherapy. Meanwhile, compared to the control group or groups receiving monotherapies, the APF + laser group exhibited the longest survival time, with a 100% survival rate at the end of the treatment period (Figure h–g and Table S1). To investigate whether APF induces an immune memory response, the APF + laser-treated group with prolonged survival was rechallenged on day 60 to assess the new tumor formation on the opposite side (Figure i). After post-tumor cell injection in 10 days, the APF + laser group displayed suppressed tumor growth. While tumor growth was evident in control group and with continued growth into large tumors on day 15. These findings underscore the potential of the multifunctional APF platform for combined photothermal-chemical dynamic therapy and immune enhancement.

To further confirm whether APF induces an immune memory response, we evaluated the efficacy of APF (+) treatment against metastatic tumors using a dual 4T1 tumor model (Figure a). A bilateral 4T1 tumor model was established. Mice received a subcutaneous injection of 4T1 cells in the right flank to generate primary tumors, which were then treated locally with (1) PBS, (2) AMB, (3) APF, or (4) APF + laser for antitumor evaluation. On day 20 post-treatment, mice were rechallenged via left-flank injection of Luc-4T1 cells to model metastatic disease. Systemic antitumor efficacy was assessed by monitoring the growth of the untreated contralateral tumor. Metastatic progression was tracked using bioluminescence imaging at designated time points (days 28, 31, and 36; Figure b). Compared to the PBS control group, both AMB and APF demonstrated comparable or stronger inhibition of metastatic tumors, likely due to their immune-activating properties. Notably, combining APF with 808 nm laser irradiation produced the strongest antitumor effect among all groups, resulting in a marked suppression of metastatic tumor growth relative to that in the PBS control group (Figure c). Furthermore, no significant body weight loss was observed following any treatment (Figure d), further confirming the favorable biocompatibility of AMB-1 and its derived formulations in chemo-photothermal therapy. In addition to enhancing tumor-infiltrating CD3+CD8+ cytotoxic T cells, APF + Laser treatment markedly increased intratumoral CD62L+CD44+ central memory T cells, which were elevated 6.58-fold relative to PBS and 3.45-fold relative to APF alone (Figure e, f). Consequently, APF + Laser treatment effectively elicits immune memory, a critical component in preventing tumor recurrence and metastatic spread. These results indicate that ICD-associated antigen released from the CDT/PTT-killed tumor cells induces antigen-specific cytotoxic T cell infiltration and stimulates an antigen-spreading long-term immune memory.

Furthermore, as a bacteria-based therapeutic platform, there would be many misgivings concerning APF + Laser biosafety. In that case, a series of biosafety experiments including blood biochemistry, hematologic indexes, and immumo-histochemical staining were tested to evaluate the side effects of this treatment. Furthermore, comprehensive blood analysis showed no significant treatment-related alterations in key parameters: peripheral blood profiles (leukocytes, lymphocytes, granulocytes, platelets), liver enzymes (ALT, AST, GGT), and renal markers (urea) (Figure S11). Histological assessment via H&E staining further showed no appreciable morphological alterations in key organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney) after APF administration (Figure S12). These results demonstrate that APF treatment is well-tolerated and exhibits a favorable safety profile, supporting its potential for clinical translation and further development.

Conclusions

On the basis of the magneto-aerotactic migration behavior of magnetotactic bacteria, Magnetospirillum magneticum (AMB-1) have proven to be an effective bacterial model for bacteria-mediated cancer therapy. Despite their potential, the clinical translation of AMB-1 bioagents is hindered by significant challenges, primarily stemming from their inherent environmental vulnerability. Through optimized electrostatic interactions, we developed a modular surface engineering strategy that enables the creation of an engineered bacterial library in advance. As a proof-of-concept, APF serves as a TME-responsive, bacteria-mediated drug vector and “in situ vaccine” generator; it selectively kills cancer cells via photothermal-promoted CDT and concurrently enhances innate and adaptive immunity through bacterial immune stimulation and treatment-induced ICD, thereby enabling potent CDT/PTT/immunotherapy of primary and metastatic tumors. This work highlights that the controlled, targeted delivery of cytotoxic ICD inducers by engineered bacteria is a promising strategy for the efficient and synchronous therapy of primary and metastatic breast cancer. More importantly, modular surface engineering may reduce the production time and realize the bedside preparation of tumor therapeutic bioagents for individual patients in the future. These initial findings may influence not only specific engineering approaches and medical interventions but also open avenues for synthesizing new targeted bacterial agents for therapeutic, immunization, imaging, and diagnostic applications. While this study demonstrates the feasibility of modular biosurface engineering of magnetotactic bacteria, several challenges still remain for further development. In particular, the environmental sensitivity of living bacteria may affect their stability, activity, and reproducibility under complex in vivo conditions. Future optimization could focus on improving bacterial robustness, controllability, and biosafety through genetic engineering and more refined surface engineering. In addition, enhanced magnetic-guidance strategies and better control over in vivo distribution may further improve the therapeutic index and translational potential of this system.
