Micronutrient Deficiencies in Infants Following Gastrointinal Surgery at the National Children’s Hospital
Authors: Hang Nguyen Thi, Huong Le Thi, Thuy Hong Nguyen Thi, Tien Dat Tran, Le Tuan Anh Nguyen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo evaluate the micronutrient status of infants at 1 and 6 months after gastrointestinal surgery and identify the associated factors.MethodsThis prospective descriptive study was conducted between February 2023 and August 2024 at the Vietnam National Children’s Hospital. Infants who underwent gastrointestinal surgery were monitored for micronutrient deficiency. A total of 120 infants were enrolled, 60 of whom had complete data available for analysis. Among the remaining patients, 12 died from severe infections, and 38 discontinued participation for various reasons, including the high cost of re-examination, lack of time, and follow-up elsewhere. Additional cases were excluded owing to incomplete data for the 6-month follow-up period.ResultsThe study included 60 infants (58.3% male), all of whom underwent their first gastrointestinal surgery before 3 months of age. Over time, the prevalence of most micronutrient deficiencies decreased, except for zinc and iron deficiencies. One month post-surgery, anemia and vitamin D deficiency were the most common micronutrient deficiencies (55% and 57%, respectively). At 6 months post-surgery, zinc deficiency was the most prevalent (47%), followed by iron deficiency (33%). The 6-month prevalence of zinc deficiency was significantly higher in infants with intestinal failure than in those without it (p<0.008). The rate of hyponatremia was significantly higher in infants who underwent enterostomy than in those who did not (p<0.001).ConclusionInfants undergoing gastrointestinal surgery are at risk of persistent micronutrient deficiencies, particularly zinc, vitamin D, and iron deficiencies. Notably, zinc deficiency was more common in infants with intestinal failure than in those without.

INTRODUCTION

Infants who undergo intestinal resection often experience significant changes in their nutritional status, including alterations in diet, physical condition, and nutrient deficiencies owing to impaired absorption. In particular, children with short bowel syndrome (SBS) [1] after intestinal resection suffer from chronic malabsorption, which adversely affects growth and development and imposes a substantial economic burden on families because of prolonged hospitalization. The etiology of intestinal resection in pediatric patients is diverse; however, necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) has been consistently identified as the most common cause during the neonatal period. According to Feng et al. [2] NEC was the leading cause of intestinal resection (35.5%), followed by intestinal atresia (32.3%). Similarly, Quynh et al. [3] reported that NEC was the leading cause of pediatric intestinal resections (responsible for 42%).

Several studies have highlighted the prevalence of micronutrient deficiency in children with SBS. Feng et al. [2] reported that although the overall rate of micronutrient deficiency decreased from 96.8% during parenteral nutrition (PN) to 83.9% after transitioning to total enteral nutrition (EN), significant deficiencies persisted. Common micronutrient deficiencies post-transition to EN included zinc (51.6%), copper (38.7%), vitamin D (32.3%), and phosphorus (25.8%) deficiencies, with anemia present in 9.7% of cases. Less common micronutrient deficiencies were observed for vitamin B12 (9.7%), magnesium (9.7%), and iron (6.5%). A study by Ubesie et al. [4] found that 56% of children were deficient in at least one mineral and 35.4% were deficient in at least one vitamin during combined PN and EN, compared with 25% and 42.5%, respectively, during complete EN. Similarly, Yang et al. [5] found that one-third of children were deficient in at least one vitamin during PN, whereas 77% were deficient in at least one mineral.

Micronutrient deficiencies in children with SBS are influenced by several factors, including inadequate nutrient provision, increased nutrient loss because of malabsorption, and infections [6]. Micronutrient absorption may also be affected by the degree of intestinal adaptation. To date, most studies have focused on children with SBS or postoperative intestinal failure; however, limited information is available regarding the overall nutrient status of children following intestinal resection and the factors contributing to nutrient deficiencies. Therefore, in this study, we describe the micronutrient deficiencies in infants after gastrointestinal surgery, including intestinal resection or enterostomy, with the aim to contribute to the current understanding of nutritional challenges in this population.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Ethics approval

All guardians/parents of the children were informed about the purpose of the study and were required to sign a consent form. The children’s information was kept completely confidential and used for research purposes only. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of National Children’s Hospital (Approval No. 1807/BVNTW-HDDD).

Study population

This study included infants under 3 months of age who required gastrointestinal surgery (including small intestinal resection, colon resection, or enterostomies) at the Viet Nam National Children’s Hospital between 2023 and 2024. The inclusion criterion was intestinal resection because of various conditions. Patients with congenital malformations unrelated to the digestive tract (such as congenital heart disease or neurological malformations) or diagnosed malabsorption disorders were excluded. The infants indicated for enterostomy included those with suspected intestinal aganglionosis, colon aganglionosis, or severe NEC.

Infants younger than 3 months who underwent gastrointestinal surgery (including small intestinal resection, colonic resection, or enterostomy) at the Viet Nam National Children’s Hospital between 2/2023 and 8/2024 were eligible for inclusion. Infants who underwent small or large intestinal resection for various underlying conditions were included. Patients who had congenital malformations unrelated to the gastrointestinal tract (e.g., congenital heart disease and neurological malformations) and pre-existing primary malabsorption disorders were excluded. The indications for enterostomy included suspected intestinal aganglionosis, total colonic aganglionosis, and severe NEC.

A prospective longitudinal study was conducted, and the infants were continuously monitored for 6 months post-surgery. Medical histories and nutritional regimens were documented, and blood tests were performed to assess nutrient levels (including magnesium, iron, zinc, vitamin D, total calcium, ionic calcium, phosphorus, hemoglobin, potassium, sodium, and prothrombin time) at two time points: 1 month after the initial surgery (T1), when 26.7% of the infants were still receiving PN and EN, and 6 months after the initial surgery (T2), when all infants had transitioned to full EN. Patient selection began in February 2023 and endedin August 2024. A total of 120 infants were enrolled in the study, of whom 60 had complete research data; thus, the final study cohort comprised 60 infants (Fig. 1).

Pediatric patient enrollment process.

The reference intervals were specified as follows: Anemia when hemoglobin <10.5 g/dL; magnesium deficiency <0.6 mmoL/L; iron deficiency <7.2 µmoL/L; zinc deficiency <10 µmoL/L; vitamin D deficiency <50 nmoL/L; total calcium deficiency <2.1 mmoL/L; ionized calcium deficiency <1.1 mmoL/L; phosphorus deficiency <1.4 mmoL/L; hypokalemia <3.5 mmoL/L; hyperkalemia >5.6 mmoL/L; hyponatremia <134 mmoL/L; hypernatremia >142 mmoL/L; and prolonged prothrombin time >13 second.

Infants were diagnosed with intestinal dysfunction according to the Aspen criteria, and SBS was diagnosed according to the North American Society for Pediatric Gastroenterology, Hepatology, and Nutrition (NASPGHAN) definitions. According to the Aspen criteria, intestinal failure in children is characterized by a severe reduction in intestinal function below the level necessary to sustain life, resulting in dependence on PN for ≥60 days within a 74-day period [7]. NASPGHAN (2017) defines SBS in children as “the need for PN for more than 60 days after bowel resection or a bowel length less than 25% of the estimated length for gestational age [1].” Prematurity was defined as birth before 37 weeks of gestation.

Indications for enterostomy included suspected colonic or small intestinal aganglionosis during the neonatal period, severe NEC, type IIIB or IIIC intestinal atresia, bowel perforation with peritonitis, extensive bowel damage, and cases where immediate closure of both bowel ends after resection was not feasible. The criteria for enterostomy closure included a minimum interval of 2 months after the initial surgery and adequate clinical stability to tolerate the procedure.

All infants participating in the study received nutritional advice and were supplemented with micronutrients, including multivitamins (Hydrosol 20 mL contains the following: vitamin A, 50,000 UI; vitamin D2, 10,000 UI; vitamin E, 20 mg; vitamin B1, 20 mg; vitamin B2, 15 mg; vitamin B6, 20 mg; vitamin PP, 100 mg; vitamin C, 500 mg; vitamin B5, 40 mg), vitamin D (Dedrogyl: Calcifediol: 1.5 mg/10 mL), and zinc (Zinco: 15 mg elemental zinc/5 mL). The dosages were individualized based on each child’s clinical condition and blood test results. One treatment course consisted of a hydrosol dose of 10 drops/day for 30 days. Dedrogyl 4 drops/day for 30 days and zinc 2 mL/day for 30 days. The children were administered two additional courses of treatment at 1 and 6 months post-surgery. All (100.0%) the infants were supplemented with hydrosol; the remaining micronutrient supplementation rate was based on the deficiency rate of each deficient micronutrient. No children received iron supplementation. Micronutrient supplementation was prescribed by another doctor, by the author. Micronutrient supplementation was based on test results.

Statistical analyses

Data were analyzed using Stata version 16.0 (StataCorp LLC). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the demographic and clinical characteristics of the study population, including means and standard deviations for continuous variables and frequencies with percentages for categorical variables. Categorical variables were compared using Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate, to assess the association between nutritional deficiencies and factors such as intestinal dysfunction, birth status (preterm or full term), and the presence of enterostomy. For continuous variables, independent-samples t-tests or Mann–Whitney U-tests were used to compare the nutrient levels between groups. Statistical significance was set at p<0.05.

RESULTS

Patient characteristics

Table 1 presents the demographic and clinical characteristics of the 60 infants who underwent gastrointestinal surgery. Males constituted a slight majority (58.3%) of the sample. Six months postoperatively, the mean corrected age was 5.5 months. Most infants (71.7%) were full term at birth, and nearly half (48.3%) had undergone two or more surgeries. The proportion of infants who underwent enterostomy was 70% at 1 month post-surgery, which decreased to 33.3% at 6 months. Intestinal failure (IF) was observed in 26.7% of infants. Notably, 81.7% of surgeries involved the small intestine. Nutritional deficiencies were prevalent, with 92% of patients lacking at least one trace mineral at 1 month post-surgery, decreasing to 77% after 6 months. The causes of gastrointestinal surgery were fairly evenly distributed, with the most common being intestinal atresia (25.0%), followed by total colonic aganglionosis (21.7%), and NEC with peritonitis (18.3%) (Supplementary Table 1).

DISCUSSION

Nutrition plays a critical role in the growth and development of children, particularly during the first 2 years of life. Inadequate micronutrient intake can lead to illness, disability, and increased risk of morbidity and mortality [8910].

Postoperative gastrointestinal complications in children include anastomotic leakage, sepsis, surgical site infections, and postoperative intestinal adhesions. When these complications occur, the reported mortality rate ranges from 15% to 70% [11]. Despite supportive treatment with PN, growth retardation remains a significant issue in these children. Previous studies have shown that nearly 50% of children receiving long-term PN exhibit stunted growth, remain significantly underweight, and are shorter than their healthy peers [121314].

Therefore, monitoring and early detection of micronutrient deficiencies are crucial for supporting the optimal development of children during their first years of life. This study, conducted at the Viet Nam National Children’s Hospital from February 2023 to December 2024, aimed to describe micronutrient deficiencies in infants under three months of age who underwent their first gastrointestinal surgery and were assessed at 1 and 6 months post-surgery. The results indicated that the causes of gastrointestinal surgery were evenly distributed across the diagnostic groups, which differs from previous studies on SBS that primarily attributed the condition to NEC [23]. This difference may be explained by the inclusion of children without IF, whereas previous studies have focused exclusively on children with IF.

After surgery for severe gastrointestinal diseases, such as NEC, intestinal atresia, or total colonic aganglionosis, the absorptive capacity of infants is markedly impaired [15]. Resection of the ileum and loss of the ileocecal valve reduce the absorption of vitamin B12, bile salts, and fat-soluble vitamins [16]. Colon resection or creation of a stoma increases water and sodium loss through the stoma, leading to a heightened risk of dehydration and electrolyte disturbances [17]. Intestinal adaptation gradually improves the absorptive function; however, this process is slow and depends on the length of the remaining intestine [15]. The results revealed that some infants continued to experience micronutrient deficiencies at six months postoperatively. The prevalence of at least one micronutrient deficiency was 92% at one month post-surgery, decreasing to 77% at six months. Vitamin D deficiency was the most prevalent (57%), followed by anemia (55%), and zinc deficiency (47%). Infants with intestinal failure had a significantly higher prevalence of zinc deficiency than those without (p=0.008). Infants with enterostomies had a significantly higher prevalence of hyponatremia than those without enterostomies (p<0.001).

The findings of this study are consistent with those of previous studies demonstrating a high prevalence of micronutrient deficiency in children following gastrointestinal surgery. At 6 months post-surgery, 77% of the infants in our cohort had at least one micronutrient deficiency, which is comparable to that reported by Chatzidaki et al. (87.5%) [18]. However, the prevalence of individual micronutrient deficiencies in our study was generally lower than that reported in other studies [218]. This may reflect differences in patient characteristics, surgical indications, or postoperative nutritional management. Among the specific deficiencies, anemia, zinc deficiency, and vitamin D deficiency are the most common, a pattern that aligns with several studies on pediatric patients after gastrointestinal surgery [345]. These results underscore the need for targeted monitoring and supplementation strategies to address the most prevalent deficiencies in this vulnerable population.

A gradual decline in the prevalence of micronutrient deficiencies was observed over the follow-up period, consistent with the findings of previous studies [24]. By 6 months post-surgery, no deficiencies in magnesium or potassium were detected. In contrast, Zn and Fe deficiencies showed an increasing trend. This pattern may reflect a slower recovery of zinc and iron absorption than other micronutrients or an increased physiological demand for these nutrients during the postoperative recovery phase. The observation that zinc deficiency decreases only marginally or even increases when transitioning from combined PN and EN to full EN has been reported in multiple studies [245]. The observed increase in zinc deficiency may be attributable to the depletion of the body’s limited zinc stores. Furthermore, the infants in this study did not receive parenteral zinc supplementation but were supplemented exclusively via the oral route, while their intestinal absorption capacity remained compromised because of postoperative gastrointestinal injury. The infants in this study also did not receive iron supplementation from the immediate postoperative period up to 6 months after surgery, as oral iron preparations are commonly associated with diarrhea and intravenous iron formulations are not yet available in Vietnam.

One month post-surgery, most micronutrient deficiencies were observed in the NEC group, except for deficiencies in zinc, vitamin D, and vitamin K. This may be attributed to poorer intestinal absorption in this group than in the other groups. However, at 6 months post-surgery, the prevalence of most micronutrient deficiencies in this group was the lowest (except for zinc and vitamin K deficiencies), suggesting a rapid recovery of intestinal absorptive function. Magnesium, phosphorus, and calcium deficiencies remained low across all groups at both 1 and 6 months post-surgery, with prevalence rates below 10%. Compared to the IF group in the study by Ubesie et al. [4], the prevalence of magnesium and phosphorus deficiency was similarly low, approximately 10%.

Table 5 shows that differences in micronutrient deficiencies between the IF and non-IF groups were small. However, at 6 months post-surgery, the IF group had a significantly higher prevalence of zinc deficiency (p=0.008). This difference may be explained by the longer hospital stays and frequent readmissions because of dehydration and electrolyte imbalances in the IF group. During hospitalization, these children received supportive PN and intravenous micronutrient supplementation, which may not have accurately reflected micronutrient status at the time of blood sampling. Differences in micronutrient deficiencies between preterm and full-term infants were also minimal. Notably, 6 months post-surgery, all cases of hyponatremia occurred in the enterostomy group (p<0.001), while no cases of hypokalemia were observed in either group. These findings are consistent with clinical practice, as infants with an ostomy cannot absorb water and electrolytes in the colon, placing them at increased risk of dehydration and electrolyte loss. The limitations of this study include its relatively small sample size, which reduced the statistical power to detect an effect. In addition, the study population consisted exclusively of infants and, therefore, may not be representative of older children. Future studies with larger cohorts and longer follow-up periods are warranted to provide a more comprehensive evaluation of the developmental outcomes after gastrointestinal surgery.

In conclusion, even in the absence of postoperative gastrointestinal dysfunction, infants remain at risk of micronutrient deficiency for up to 6 months after surgery. Adequate nutrients are essential for infant growth and development. Therefore, it is imperative for clinicians to establish long-term monitoring strategies and provide appropriate nutritional support to this group of infants.
