Dentistry as a Test Case for Responsible Artificial Intelligence in Healthcare: Why External Validation Matters More Than Marketing
Authors: Owais A Farooqi
Published: 2025

Artificial intelligence (AI) is moving rapidly from research into clinical practice. In 2024, commercial dental AI systems that claim to detect caries and periodontal disease on radiographs are being marketed directly to clinicians and insurers. Accuracy figures are prominently displayed, yet most derive from developer-generated data sets with limited independent scrutiny. This reflects a broader challenge in digital health: marketing often advances faster than evidence. If AI struggles to demonstrate reliability in a structured field like dentistry, the risks for other disciplines are even greater.

Dentistry is a revealing test case for health care AI. Radiographic imaging is routine, diagnostic categories are standardized, and oral diseases remain among the most common chronic conditions globally, affecting 3.5 billion people.1 These features make dentistry well suited to pilot rigorous validation. Yet vendor-led studies dominate the literature,2 often reporting high accuracy without transparency about data set composition or representativeness. The risks are heightened in low-resource settings, where AI is marketed as a substitute for scarce expertise but may amplify inequities if unvalidated.

To address this gap, the US Veterans Health Administration launched the Validating Artificially Learned Intelligence in Dentistry (VALID) pilot in 2024.3 Conducted at the Greater Los Angeles Healthcare System, VALID was a retrospective study that analyzed existing radiographs, clinical examination findings, and follow-up documentation. By integrating clinical data with imaging, the pilot set a higher evidentiary bar than vendor reports based solely on radiographs. In an in-vitro setting, early observations indicated that measured performance differed from initial expectations, yet the tools continued to provide educational value, underscoring the importance of independent validation in responsible AI adoption.

Three lessons extend beyond dentistry. First, bias detection requires external testing. Developer datasets may underrepresent older adults, minority groups, or advanced disease. External validation across diverse settings reveals such blind spots. The disappointing performance of a widely implemented sepsis prediction model when externally validated illustrates this risk.4

Second, patient safety and trust depend on independent evidence. During the COVID-19 pandemic, hundreds of AI models for chest imaging were proposed, yet thorough assessments found that almost none were clinically useful, largely owing to methodological flaws and biased data sets.5 Dentistry is unlikely to differ unless standards change.

Third, system credibility is at stake. Health systems and regulators face reputational and legal risks when tools adopted on marketing claims fail in practice.6 Independent validation protects both patients and institutions, ensuring that innovation does not outpace accountability.

Clear steps are available. Regulators should require postmarket validation of AI tools, linking conditional approvals to real-world performance.7 Journals should enforce transparent reporting of data sources, demographic characteristics, and evaluation protocols.5 Health systems should adopt procurement standards that mandate independent validation before purchase.6 Educators should teach AI literacy so clinicians can critically appraise claims.8

The pilot study VALID aligns with wider policy attention, including the 2023 US Executive Order on trustworthy AI.9 The pilot demonstrates that independent evaluation is feasible within a public system and offers a model adaptable across disciplines. Comparable work in ophthalmology, using multiethnic data sets, has shown how such validation strengthens generalizability.10

Artificial intelligence will shape health care for decades, but responsible adoption requires evidence beyond developer claims. The VALID pilot, although retrospective and modest in scale, demonstrates how external validation can be achieved. If dentistry can take the lead to establish this standard, the benefits will extend far beyond oral health.
