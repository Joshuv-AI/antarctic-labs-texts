Transcriptome-based molecular subgroup identification and prognosis stratification in pediatric AML
Authors: YuWei Huang, MuYao Yang, JiaQi Hu, TingYuan Lang, JianWen Xiao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCurrent risk subgroups for acute myeloid leukemia (AML) rely primarily on molecular and cytogenetic markers. These subgroups have distinct clinicopathological and molecular characteristics and are the basis for classifying disease subgroups currently undergoing clinical trials and initial subgroup-oriented therapies. However, substantial biological heterogeneity and differences in survival are apparent within each subgroup, so transcriptome-based molecular subgroup identification and prognostic stratification in pediatric acute myeloid leukemia remain to be resolved. In this study, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of transcriptomic data for AML using data from public datasets and constructed a new prognostic prediction model. We first downloaded the transcriptome data of the GDC Data Portal and the gene set of MsigDB for survival and cluster analysis and reclassified AML into 8 different molecular subgroups. The expression profiles, changes in the immune microenvironment, biological functions and pathways, and clinical features among these subpopulations were further studied. The classification prediction model is then developed using four machine learning algorithms: RF, SVM, XGBoost, and DT. The XGBoost method showed the best performance. The vital feature variables in XGBoost suggest that HSD17B10, NDUFS8, ASCL5, FADS2, and COX8A were critical factors in identifying the prognosis of AML. We only found that NDUFS8 and FADS2 had been reported in AML, and the remaining three gene new prognostic markers for AML have not been reported before, but they have all been reported to be associated with cancer. we identified eight transcriptome-based molecular subgroups of AML and further assessed differences in the molecular landscape, immune networks, and signaling pathways underlying these subpopulations. Additionally, the prognostic models comprising 62 genes was proposed and demonstrated to have remarkable predictive value. Overall, we identified a new molecular subpopulation of AML, which improved disease risk stratification and established a prognostic model for AML, which demonstrated favorable performance in retrospective validation. Since this study relies on retrospective data, further prospective analysis is required to confirm its ability to accurately reflect patient prognosis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00277-025-06634-1.Keywords: Acute myeloid leukemia, RNA-seq, Prognostic prediction, Molecular subgroup, Machine learning

Background

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a heterogeneous disease characterized by the uncontrolled proliferation of clonal hematopoietic cells, accounting for 15–20% of all pediatric acute leukemia [1, 2]. Although approximately 80% of patients achieve complete remission after induction chemotherapy, refractory disease remains prevalent and around 40% of children experience relapse following remission, serving as the primary driver of treatment failure [3, 4].

Pediatric and adult AML patients are classified into distinct risk categories based on Molecular and cytogenetic markers. The ELN risk stratification currently delineates more than 20 genetic subgroups of AML, each assigned into different risk groups [2]. However, 20% of pediatric cases lack all known markers and 60% lack markers that can predict outcomes [5]. So, despite advancements in patient stratification, patient prognosis has not improved, and overall survival (OS) rates remain at about 75% [6]. Due to the heterogeneity of AML, the scarcity of targeted therapies, and the relatively slow development of immunotherapy, treatment outcomes remain lagging [7]. AML is surpassing acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) to become the primary cause of leukemia-related mortality in children [8, 9]. Data from 2020 indicates that childhood leukemia accounted for 33% of new cancer cases and 31% of cancer deaths among children aged 0 to 14 [10]. Among these, AML has a relatively high mortality rate [11]. This shift is partially attributed to current prognostic models misclassifying many children who will ultimately succumb to the disease as being at low or intermediate risk [12]. Hence, there is an urgent need to establish additional and robust risk classification systems to enhance existing risk-based treatment protocols and further refine therapeutic strategies for AML.

RNA-sequencing (RNA-Seq) provides comprehensive and multifaceted information, which can not only accurately measure the gene expression levels, but also identify structural alterations at the mRNA level, such as alternative splicing and chimeric gene fusions. Additionally, RNA-Seq also facilitates subgroup classification based on gene expression characteristics [13]. Regarding gene fusions, RNA-Seq is more effective in identifying novel fusion events compared to routine cytogenetic studies [12, 14]. The application of RNA-Seq in pediatric AML enables researchers to gain deeper insights into its unique molecular characteristics. Previous studies have reported that distinct subgroups of acute leukemias have been precisely identified in adult mixed phenotype acute leukemia and adult acute myeloid leukemia through extensive RNA-Seq data analysis. Furthermore, significant differences in survival have been observed among these subgroups [15, 16]. Although classification systems for adult AML have matured, they are not entirely applicable to children. Pediatric AML is a highly heterogeneous disease with diverse genetic abnormalities [17]. The genetic landscape of pediatric AML does not fully align with adult classification systems [18]. A previous study investigated the genomic landscape of pediatric AML using RNA sequencing and proposed a genomic framework based on driver gene alterations to improve classification and risk stratification [18]. RNA-Seq has helped highlight pediatric-specific alterations, including the identification of disease-causing gene rearrangements unique to children [19]. In terms of mutation profiles and fusion gene frequencies, pediatric AML exhibits distinct differences from adult AML. Pediatric AML patients demonstrate higher frequencies of structural variations [20] and higher frequencies of fusion genes [21], which are often products of chromosomal translocations and hold significant implications for disease diagnosis and risk stratification. Fundamental differences between pediatric and adult AML also encompass epigenetic and transcriptional profiles [22]. These differences mean that treatment regimens and risk stratification strategies for adult AML cannot be simply applied to pediatric patients. Therefore, conducting independent and in-depth research on pediatric AML and performing stratified analyses holds irreplaceable value for elucidating its unique pathogenesis, identifying novel prognostic markers, and developing targeted therapeutic strategies.

In this study, we conducted an integrated analysis of RNA expression profiles from public datasets to classify AML into eight molecular subgroups. Each subgroup exhibited distinct clinical and biological signatures. Additionally, we developed transcriptome-based machine learning models to predict the molecular subgroups.

Methods

Patient samples

The transcriptome expression profiles and clinical information associated with pediatric AML patients were downloaded from the GDC Data Portal (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). In this study, pediatric AML cases were defined as patients aged ≤ 18 years at diagnosis, meeting the criteria of the World Health Organization classification for childhood hematologic malignancies. The following quality control procedures were completed before data analysis: [1] Exclusion of low-quality samples with sequencing read lengths < 50 million, alignment rates < 70%, or Q30 base proportions < 80%; [2] Filtering of under-expressed genes with Transcripts Per Million (TPM) < 1 in over 50% of samples; [3] Exclusion of duplicate samples (based on sample ID and clinical information matching) and non-primary AML samples (including secondary AML, treatment-related AML, and relapsed AML); [4] Exclusion of samples or features with missing values > 20%.

Finally, this study encompassed a total of 1767 AML patients, with 1041 patients derived from The Children’s Oncology Group Trial COG-AAML1031, constituting the Discovery Cohort (No significant technical heterogeneity was observed). Within the Discovery Cohort, 70% of patients were randomly assigned to the Train Cohort, while the remaining 30% constituted the Test Cohort. Additionally, we obtained 726 cases from the following pediatric AML studies: AAML0531 (n = 598), AAML03P1 (n = 86), or CCG2961 (n = 42) to validate our findings.

Data preprocessing

The overall design process of this study is illustrated in Fig. 1. Since the samples originated from a single trial (AAML1031) with relatively uniform technical conditions, batch correction was not performed during pretreatment. To ensure better statistical analysis and modeling, the features of the discovery cohort underwent the following preprocessing steps: initial imputation of missing values using the KNN algorithm [23], followed by quantile normalization [24] to ensure comparability across different samples. Following this, the initial imputation values were removed, and a second round of missing value imputation was conducted. Normalization provided a more stable gene expression distribution, which improved interpolation accuracy and diminished the influence of original data heterogeneity on the results. Finally, logarithmic transformations and z-score scaling were applied to standardize gene expression values across various scales, facilitating model convergence during the modeling phase. No further processing was conducted on the Validation Cohort for result reliability and generalizability.

Survival-related features selection and ranking

The criterion for assessing prognostic quality in our study relies on the presence of significant differences in survival curves. We employed the following steps to evaluate and identify prognostic gene sets:

Initially, Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) [25] enrichment was conducted to evaluate the heterogeneity of a variety of biological processes and pathway activities using the “GSVA” R package on the Train Cohort(log2(RPKM + 1)). Briefly, Three gene sets ((Hallmark, h.all.v2023.1.Hs.symbols), (C2, c2. cp.kegg.v7.4. symbols), and (C5, c5.go.bp.v7.5.1.symbols)) downloaded from Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB, http://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb/index.jsp) were utilized as the input files for the subsequent GSVA analysis. Hallmark is a rigorously curated and integrated collection of core pathways that directly reflects the most critical biological processes in the occurrence and development of AML, particularly the changes in the immune microenvironment and the activation of oncogenic signaling pathways highlighted in this study. The C2 and C5 gene sets represent gene annotations from KEGG pathways and GO biological processes, respectively, forming complementary functional categories for immune cells across gene sets. The quartile was used as the dividing line between the high and low rating groups, and Kaplan-Meier analysis with log-rank test was employed to investigate the associations between each gene set and OS. The Benjamini-Hochberg method was employed for multiple testing correction. Prognostic gene sets were selected based on a corrected p-value < 0.05. Overlapping features within these prognostic gene sets were designated as Gene Set 1.

Univariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was performed on 103 genes. Genes significantly associated with OS (HR ≠ 1, adj. p < 0.05 after BH correction) were retained, while redundant genes without prognostic association were excluded. Among these, those augmenting prognostic hazard and those conferring protective benefits, direct ranking based on their Hazard Ratio (HR) was unfeasible. Notably, a feature with an HR of 1, regardless of its expression intensity, exhibited negligible influence on prognosis. Consequently, we standardized our approach by subtracting one from the HR values of these 143 features, computing their absolute differences, and thereby establishing a relative importance ranking of these OS-related features for subsequent in-depth analysis utilizing |HR−1| (Fig. 1B).

Molecular subgroups definition

After ranking the features according to their prognostic significance, our objective shifts towards identifying an optimal combination of features to define a molecular subpopulation that can effectively differentiate prognosis. First, the top-ranked core features were selected. Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression was used to analyze the dimensionality reduction of genes, and the optimal λ value was determined by 10-fold cross-validation to screen out the core prognostic genes. Based on the expression matrix of these core genes, K-means clustering algorithm was used for clustering. The initial cluster number (K) was chosen to be between 3 and 8, since higher K values might result in molecular subgroups that are overly extreme and not representative [25]. After obtaining sample labels through clustering, Kaplan-Meier survival analysis and log-rank tests were performed for each subgroup corresponding to a K value. The overall p-value for OS differences between subgroups was calculated (a BH-corrected p-value < 0.05 was used as the criterion for determining statistical significance of survival differences). When K = 8, survival differences between subgroups were most pronounced, and prognostic features within each subgroup were clearly distinguishable. The contour coefficients corresponding to each K value were then calculated. Values closer to 1 indicated higher similarity within the subgroup and poorer separation from other subgroups. Careful evaluation of the spatial distribution of the eight subgroups using contour coefficients demonstrated good independence and validity (Fig. 1B). After multiple rounds of screening, clustering was ultimately completed based on 62 high-predictive-value features, successfully identifying eight stable molecular subtypes (designated G1-G8) among the 728 samples in the training set for pediatric AML [15].

Differentially gene expression profiles of subgroups

Following the subdivision of the samples into eight distinct molecular subgroups, an in-depth analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was conducted to unveil the genetic distinctions existing among these molecular subgroups. DEGs were filtered using the limmaR package for microarray data comparison and normalization [26], with thresholds of BH-corrected p-values < 0.05 and |log2-fold change| >1. Volcano plots were constructed using the ggplot2 package to visualize the distribution of DEGs.

Immune infiltration based upon subgroups

We attempted to investigate the relationship between molecular subgroups and immune cells. Finally, the Cell-type Identification By Estimating Relative Subsets Of RNA Transcripts (CIBERSORT) algorithm [27] and the MCPcounter algorithm [28] were used to evaluate the abundance of immune cells in each sample based on the gene expression profile. The differences in immune cell abundances between distinct molecular subgroups were estimated using Kruskal-Wallis rank sum testing [29] and a value of p less than 0.05 was considered statistically different. Additionally, the Spearman correlation analysis was performed to investigate the relationship between immune cells and molecular subgroups [30].

Central gene set enrichment analysis of subgroups

To find gene sets significantly correlated to molecular subgroups, we utilized the R package “Weighted Correlation Network Analysis (WGCNA)” to analyze the gene expression matrix of the distinct subgroups, with the results visualized as a correlation between different subgroups and gene modules. Genes from modules highly associated with molecular subgroups (the maximum correlation coefficient and p < 0.05) were selected for further Gene ontology (GO) biological functions and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis using the R package of “clusterProfiler” [31, 32]. Statistical significance was determined by adjusting the p-values using the BH method, with a p. adjust value less than 0.05 considered as statistically significant.

Development of machine learning model for prognostic prediction

The establishment of stable and significant molecular subgroups plays a crucial role in predicting the prognosis of AML. Selected features were used to develop transcriptome-based machine learning (ML) models using four algorithms commonly used in the biomedical field including support vector machines (SVM) [33], random forests (RF), decision trees (DT) [34], and eXtreme gradient boosting (XGBoost).

Here, the different subgroups were utilized as the response variable, while the 62 features served as explanatory variables. Train Cohort was randomly divided into a training set (70%) and a testing set (30%), based on 10-fold cross-validated in the training set to explore the best set of hyper-parameters [35]. Then the model was trained using these selected the optimal hyper-parameters. The performance metrics such as accuracy, Kappa, recall, F1 score, and area under the receiving operating characteristic curve (AUC) were assessed and compared between the training and testing sets to evaluate the models’ effectiveness.

Model validation

A two-step validation process was undertaken to validate the prognostic prediction modeling methods proposed in this study. Initially, internal validation was conducted on the Discovery (Test) Cohort, followed by validation on an independent Validation Cohort. Due to substantial heterogeneity observed in the gene expression profiles between the Validation Cohort and the Discovery cohort (Fig. 1E) and differing preprocessing methods. The Validation Cohort can be considered as independent external data.

The Validation cohort, not subjected to the same preprocessing steps as the Train Cohort, required adjustments to address data dimensionality differences. The ComBat algorithm was employed to correct for batch effects between the training and validation sets, using trial origin as the batch variable and incorporating patient age as a covariate to minimize confounding. Feature standardization was performed on the validation set using the mean and standard deviation from the training cohort.

The Train Cohort and Test Cohort were derived by random selection, and there were no significant differences between cohorts with respect to patient clinical characteristics (p > 0.01, χ2 test; Table 1). Only previous cohort divisions were considered in the analysis.

Patient characteristics

Identification of molecular subgroups

We obtained three gene sets (Hallmark, C2, and C5) from MSigDB. The Kaplan-Meier algorithm was employed to identify 3,077 gene sets that exhibited a significant correlation with the prognosis of AML patients(Dataset S1). From these prognostic gene sets, 469 overlapping genes were extracted (Dataset S2) and further compared with 4833 OS-related genes from the Train Cohort (Dataset S3). Ultimately, 142 overlapping OS-related genes were identified (Dataset S4).

We successfully identified eight stable subgroups (G1-G8) (Fig. 2A) in AML through the clustering of transcriptome data (n = 728) based on 62 genes highly correlated with prognosis. The gene expression patterns exhibited significant variability among the eight subgroups (Fig. 2B). Survival analysis revealed distinct prognostic implications among these subgroups. G1 displayed a significantly better survival outcome compared to other subgroups, with G1 exhibiting survival rates exceeding 80% at three and five years. Conversely, subgroup G8 demonstrated the poorest prognosis, with three-year and five-year survival rates falling below 40% (Fig. 2C). A heatmap of p-values for survival curves among subgroups (Fig. 2D) demonstrated significant prognostic differences. This stark difference in outcomes emphasized the necessity for further refinement of subgroups in AML. For further clinical application, molecular subgroups with similar prognostic outcomes were combined, resulting in four distinct survival patterns (Figs. 2E, F).

Immune profiles of subgroups

By using CIBERSORT and MCPcounter algorithms, a notable heterogeneity in terms of immune cell infiltration was identified across the eight molecular subgroups(Fig. 4A, C, E). CIBERSORT analysis revealed abundant B cells, CD4 + T cells, and CD8 + T cells in G5. In contrast, the G7 subgroup exhibited higher numbers of monocytes and macrophages, particularly M2-type cells. MCP-counter analysis revealed both commonalities and differences (Fig. 4B, D, F). Results showed that endothelial cell infiltration abundance was significantly higher in the G8 subgroup compared to other subgroups.

Enrichment analysis of subgroups

Subsequently, the GO and KEGG analyses were performed to understand the differences in cell signaling pathways among the eight subgroups. The results of WGCNA as Fig. 5A. Functional enrichment analysis(Fig. 5B) revealed that the genes within G1 subgroup were primarily enriched in RNA splicing process, while genes in G2/4/6 subgroups were mainly related to RNA processing and DNA damage, G3 subgroup genes were predominantly involved in the translation process by regulating the ribosome, on the other hand, G5 subgroup genes were linked to lymphocyte differentiation and phosphotyrosine-based signaling mainly mediated by SH2 domains. Genes in the G7 subgroup were predominantly associated with immune responses, and those in the G8 subgroup enriched more platelet activation-related pathways.

GO and KEGG enrichment analysis of core gene in molecular subgroups (A) WGCNA analysis of molecular subgroups. (B, D) GO and KEGG enrichment analysis of core gene from WGCNA

Additionally, KEGG enrichment analysis (Fig. 5C) suggested that the genes in G1 affected processes such as Translation, Folding, sorting, and degradation of biological genetic information mainly through the pathways including Nucleocytoplasmic transport, Protein processing in endoplasmic reticulum, Spliceosome and Ubiquitin mediated proteolysis. The G5, G7, and G8 subgroups enriched more tumor microenvironment (TME)-related pathways such as T cell receptor signaling pathway, B cell receptor signaling pathway, hematopoietic cell lineage, Fc gamma R-mediated phagocytosis signaling pathways and T helper (TH) cell differentiation. Notably, the platelet activation pathways were upregulated in G8 subgroup in both the GO and KEGG enrichment analyses. These comprehensive functional analyses underscore the distinct molecular pathways present across the eight molecular subgroups of AML patients.

Development of machine learning model

To identify the most efficient machine learning model for molecular subgroups classification, we obtained 62 genes associated with OS and fit them into four machine learning algorithms. After obtaining the hyperparameter (Dataset S5), we estimated the ability of these machine-learning models to distinguish between eight molecular subgroups in the training set and the testing set. As shown in Fig. 7A, C. Firstly, this evaluation was conducted utilizing ROC curves in training set, XGBoost model displayed the highest AUC value (macro AUC = 0.999 and micro AUC = 1). Subsequently, we further validated the models by conducting ROC analysis on the testing set, where the XGBoost model once again demonstrated superior performance with the highest AUC (macro AUC = 0.999 and micro AUC = 0.989). Moreover, the XGBoost model also showed the best accuracy, Kappa, recall, F1 score, and precision both in the training set and the testing set (Fig. 7B, D, Dataset S6).

Furthermore, we conducted an analysis to assess the significance of feature variables using the XGBoost model. The feature importance score serves to indicate the relative importance of each feature employed in the XGBoost model for predicting the target variable. The importance ranking of feature variables within the XGBoost model was illustrated in Fig. 7E, revealing that the top five influential variables contributing to the model were identified as HSD17B10, NDUFS8, ASCL5, FADS2, and COX8A. After this feature importance analysis, we employed ROC curve analysis to evaluate the discriminatory capacity of these top five genes in distinguishing between subgroups within the testing set. The results showed that HSD17B10 and COX8A had significant prognostic efficiency in AML (Fig. 7F-J).

(F, G) Kaplan-Meier curves for OS of molecular subgroups in the test (F) and validation cohort (G). AUC, area under the dose response curve; tSNE, t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding. (H) Multivariable Cox analysis for OS in AML patients

To elucidate the independent prognostic value of these identified transcriptome-based subgroups, we conducted a multivariable Cox analysis in AML patients (Fig. 8H). Notably, the t (6;9) and t (9;11) chromosomal abnormalities emerged as unfavorable prognostic indicators. Furthermore, our findings underscored that the molecular subgroup classification independently predicted adverse OS outcomes even after adjusting for established clinical and molecular prognostic factors.

Discussion

AML is a highly heterogeneous hematological malignancy with a 75% OS rate but a recurrence rate of 25–40% [1]. Although widely implemented in clinical practice, studies have shown that existing risk stratification has insufficient information for predicting prognosis individually [2, 36–38]. With the advent of the era of precision medicine, more detailed and accurate molecular classification and risk stratification are essential for the treatment of AML. Therefore, the development of clinically useful biomarkers or features and the mining of new classifications of biologically significant AML subpopulations can help to enhance the accuracy of AML prognosis prediction.

RNA-Seq has proven to be a compelling NGS technique for detecting various genetic abnormalities in acute leukemia as well as discovering new mutations, and its potential to replace all genetic tests for classification for classification and risk assessment of AML [12, 13, 39]. Therefore, the exploration of transcriptome-related subgroups and relevant molecular landscapes may reveal AML heterogeneity, which may help to explain the survival heterogeneity among AML patients. Furthermore, a prognostic model constructed based on survival-related genes demonstrated excellent predictive performance, potentially offering new targets for therapeutic strategies. True validation requires model assessment using prospective data. Therefore, the use of retrospective cohorts in this study may have led to an overestimation of model performance. Future research must conduct prospective studies to fully validate the prognostic value of the model and support its potential clinical application.

TME has been shown to evaluate patient outcomes [40, 41]. The immune infiltration heterogeneity identified in this study not only explains the prognostic differences among AML subgroups but also directly points to potential directions for personalized immunotherapy. The high abundance of CD4+/CD8 + T cells in the G5 subgroup suggests that patients in this subgroup may be sensitive to immune checkpoint inhibitors or adoptive T cell therapy. Clinically, priority consideration should be given to conducting clinical trials of such immunotherapies in the G5 subgroup, or combining them with chemotherapy to enhance the tumor-killing effects of T cells. The high M2 macrophage infiltration in the G7 subgroup indicates a pronounced immunosuppressive microenvironment in this subgroup. Potential therapeutic strategies include evaluating the timing of CSF−1R inhibitor use based on CD206 expression levels—a marker of M2 macrophages—to eliminate M2 macrophages, or combining TLR agonists to reprogram M2 macrophages into pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages, thereby reversing immunosuppression. The enrichment characteristics of ECs in the G8 subtype may serve as potential biomarkers for microenvironment-targeted therapies. Treatment for this subtype requires VEGF inhibitors to block interactions between endothelial cells and leukemic cells, or the combination of CXCR4 antagonists to disrupt leukemic cell microenvironment colonization and enhance chemotherapy sensitivity. In this study, the subgroups with G7/G8 had many immune cell infiltration (mostly associated with poor AML outcomes). Additionally, we observed that G7/G8 was mainly enriched in immune response-related functions and pathways. This suggests that these subgroups are more likely to benefit from immunotherapy. Furthermore, our analysis revealed that subgroups with poorer prognoses were associated with standard risk, high risk, t (9; 11),11q23, FLT3-ITD, and age. Patients classified as standard risk under traditional standard risk stratification neither possess favorable genetic features associated with low risk nor carry unfavorable genetic features linked to high risk, yet they are grouped into the standard risk group and exhibit enrichment in numerous subgroups with poorer prognoses. However, this approach has significant Limitations, as approximately 60% of pediatric AML standard risk cases lack clear molecular or cytogenetic markers predictive of prognosis. These specific subgroups have previously been identified as bearing an unfavorable prognosis [42, 43], further reinforcing the prognostic significance of molecular subgroup classification. Importantly, multifactor Cox analysis demonstrated that these molecular subgroups could autonomously predict poor OS, underscoring their clinical relevance in prognostication.

In the subgroup differential gene expression analysis, HOXA family genes (e.g., HOXA9, HOXA10) and FLT3 expression were significantly higher in the G3-G7 subgroup compared to the G1-G2 subgroup (Fig. 3A, B). These genes are recognized as poor prognostic markers in adult AML [43], but pediatric AML exhibits distinct biological characteristics from its adult counterpart. In pediatric AML, HOXA family gene expression is strongly associated with MLL rearrangements and shows low overlap with NPM1 mutations. Therefore, in clinical practice, pediatric patients with high HOXA expression should prioritize FISH or RNA-seq testing for MLL rearrangements rather than focusing solely on DEGs, differing from the adult testing workflow. Additionally, only FLT3-ITD patients with AR > 0.5 in the G7 subgroup showed poor prognosis, suggesting that the prognostic value of FLT3-ITD in pediatric patients remains controversial. Therefore, future risk stratification for pediatric AML should integrate both classification subgroups and FLT3-ITD for comprehensive assessment. Furthermore, survival differences across other subgroups in this study were primarily concentrated within the standard risk stratification categories. Intermediate-risk cases represent the most heterogeneous group in AML risk stratification [38], characterized by the absence of favorable or unfavorable cytogenetic and molecular abnormalities, and exhibiting variable outcomes following standard therapy [44]. Consequently, this study underscores the need for enhanced prognostic classification in intermediate-risk AML patients [36].

Accurate early prediction of AML is needed in order to identify high-risk patients of AML in a timely manner so that prevention methods can be developed in a timely manner. Recently, due to their outstanding performance in clinical diagnosis, various machine learning algorithms have been widely used to predict new biomarkers and obtain new information about the pathogenesis of diseases [45, 46]. In our study, of the four machine learning algorithms were tested, we observed that the XGBoost method showed the best performance. The vital feature variables in XGBoost suggest that HSD17B10, NDUFS8, ASCL5, FADS2, and COX8A were critical factors in identifying the prognosis of AML. We only found that NDUFS8 and FADS2 had been reported in AML, and the remaining three gene new prognostic markers for AML have not been reported before, but they have all been reported to be associated with cancer. NDUFS8 also called TYKY, is a nuclear-encoded subunit of human mitochondrial complex I [47]. Studies have shown that the increased expression level of NDUFS8 was associated with poorer OS in AML [48]. FADS2 comes from the fatty acid desaturase family members (FADSs), and they are mainly responsible for lipid conversion to regulate the balance of lipid metabolism [49]. some studies indicate that High FADS2 expression in AML patients significantly correlates with poor clinical outcome [50]. HSD17B10(also termed SCHAD or ERAB) gene encodes the 17-beta-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type 10 (17-beta -HSD10) is a member of the short-chain dehydrogenase/reductase superfamily, HSD17B10 is found in osteosarcoma and might be a therapeutic target in conventional osteosarcoma [51]. ASCL5 is a member of the achuete-scute complex-like (ASCL) gene family, encoding basic helix-loop-helix transcription factors that control the development of the nervous system. ASCL5 was upregulated in lung cancer but downregulated in the majority of types of brain tumors, such as glioblastoma, anaplastic oligoastrocytoma, anaplastic oligodendroglioma and oligodendroglioma [44]. Cytochrome c oxidase (COX) is the terminal enzyme complex of the respiratory chain and catalyzes the transfer of electrons from cytochrome c to oxygen coupled to proton pumping from the mitochondrial matrix to the intermembrane space. The COX8A protein is the smallest subunit of the COX complex. The role of COX8A in cancer is unclear and there have been few preliminary studies [52]. The study found that COX8A is associated with the self-renewal ability of intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC) and promotes chemotherapy resistance of intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC) cancer cells [53]. There are few studies on the role and mechanism of these five genes in AML, and further exploration is needed.

Some limitations must be underscored in the current study. First, as this work was based on publicly available datasets, additional prospective samples for experimental evaluation are required for additional validation. Moreover, the subgroups should be further validated in a prospective study due to the retrospective nature of the current study. The samples employed for the identification of molecular subgroups or for the prediction of machine learning algorithms were somewhat tiny, necessitating a larger AML sample size for validation. Although we defined pediatric AML as age ≤ 18 years and implemented fundamental quality control measures (e.g., read length, mapping rate filtering), the discovery cohort underwent no batch correction (e.g., ComBat). The separation of AAML1031 from other cohorts in tSNE may partially stem from uncorrected batch effects. The subgroup stratification and gene selection in this study were primarily conducted using internal cohorts and were not validated in an independent, multicenter external cohort. This approach may introduce bias in conclusions due to sample selection bias or overfitting. Additionally, despite the XGBoost classifier’s strong performance in the discovery cohort, the design of the machine learning components still exhibits certain Limitations. This study employed a single 70%/30% random split to construct the training and testing sets for the discovery cohort, without incorporating stratified k-fold cross-validation or Bootstrap sampling for multi-round validation. Furthermore, no systematic consensus clustering was conducted to assess cluster stability, potentially leading to an overestimation of model performance. The eight molecular subgroups (G1-G8) exhibited significant sample size difference. The absence of algorithm optimization or metric adjustments for category imbalance may mask classification deficiencies in small-sample subgroups. Moreover, the 62 input genes used to construct the XGBoost model in this study were selected solely based on survival screening, without secondary optimization for molecular subgroup classification performance. This approach may reduce the model’s ability to distinguish subgroups with specificity. The data preprocessing stage employed a two-round KNN interpolation strategy. The initial interpolation aimed to support normalization, while the secondary interpolation sought to optimize accuracy, thereby enhancing data integrity. However, repeated interpolation operations may introduce risks of overfitting. In the future, more robust single-point interpolation methods (such as those combining multiple interpolation techniques or model-based interpolation) could be adopted to reduce value bias. This study employed a strategy of identifying prognostic gene sets via GSVA followed by extracting individual genes. However, since the translational rationale and selection criteria for integrating these sets into individual patients were not explicitly defined, the functional interpretive value of gene set analysis was not fully realized. Subsequent studies may establish explicit screening criteria, such as contribution scores within gene sets, and combine these with gene interaction network analysis. Simultaneously, deepening the exploration of pathway mechanisms at the gene set level can refine analytical logic and enhance the reliability of results.

Conclusions

In summary, we identified eight transcriptome-based molecular subgroups of AML and further assessed differences in the molecular landscape, immune networks, and signaling pathways underlying these subpopulations, which provides additional insights into the stratified treatment of AML. Additionally, the prognostic models comprising 62 genes was proposed and demonstrated to have remarkable predictive value. The promising prognostic accuracy of the model may facilitate individualized prognosis management and personalized therapeutic intervention.
