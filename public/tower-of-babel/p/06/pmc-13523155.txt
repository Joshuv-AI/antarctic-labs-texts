ABSTRACT
BackgroundApical right ventricular sequestration is a rare, nonobstructive variant of double-chamber right ventricle with complex anatomy that may be underrecognized.Case SummaryA 14-year-old female presented with palpitations, failure to thrive, and recurrent respiratory infections, with a history of cyanosis in early childhood. Evaluation demonstrated cardiomegaly, biatrial enlargement, severe atrioventricular valve regurgitation, and biventricular dysfunction. Multimodality assessment identified apical sequestration of the right ventricle as a variant of double-chamber right ventricle, associated with a large ventricular septal defect, reduced functional right ventricular volume, and severe pulmonary hypertension. Given advanced hemodynamic involvement, medical therapy was selected, resulting in clinical improvement.DiscussionApical right ventricular sequestration differs from the classic obstructive form of double-chamber right ventricle and poses diagnostic challenges.Take-Home MessagesApical right ventricular sequestration represents a rare variant of double-chamber right ventricle. History of Presentation

A 14-year-old female presented with heart failure symptoms, prompting comprehensive reassessment. Physical examination revealed an oxygen saturation of 90% and a blood pressure of 91/55 mm Hg. A systolic murmur was best heard at the mesocardium, and hepatomegaly was noted (4 cm below the costal margin), with preserved liver function (model for end-stage liver disease score: 8).

Past Medical History

The patient had no family history of congenital heart disease and an unremarkable prenatal history. Early infancy featured feeding intolerance, diaphoresis, and intermittent cyanosis, prompting clinical evaluation and leading to detection of a heart murmur. Echocardiography at that time suggested left ventricular hypertrabeculation, an apical ventricular septal defect, and a hypoplastic right ventricle. Despite the findings, further intervention was not pursued due to financial constraints and the challenges of living in a remote area, with poor adherence to follow-up; therefore, management was limited to medical therapy.

Differential Diagnosis

Initial differential diagnosis included complex congenital heart disease with single-ventricle physiology, left ventricular hypertrabeculation, isolated right ventricular hypoplasia, dilated, and pulmonary hypertension.

Investigations

Chest radiography demonstrated marked cardiomegaly (Figure 1A), and electrocardiography showed sinus rhythm with biatrial enlargement and right ventricular hypertrophy (Figure 1B). Management

Based on these findings, the heart team opted for medical management alone, as heart lung transplantation was not feasible.

Outcome

Medical therapy was optimized, and the patient was discharged after clinical improvement. At 1-year follow-up, the patient remained clinically stable.

Discussion

In its classic form, DCRV results from division of the RV into a proximal-inlet high-pressure chamber and a distal-outlet low-pressure chamber caused by 1 or more anomalous muscle bundles, typically a hypertrophied muscular annulus composed of the crista supraventricularis, trabecula septomarginalis, and moderator band, or by a muscular shelf. One of the earliest descriptions of this DCRV variant was provided by Restivo et al1 in 1984, who described separation of the apical trabecular component from the inflow and outflow portions, which together formed a tubular cavity connecting the tricuspid and pulmonary valves, while the apical trabecular portion remained isolated from the remainder of the RV. This distinctive anatomy supports the tripartite theory describing the ventricle as comprising inlet, apical trabecular, and outlet components.

The RV may be divided into 2 or more compartments by different structures through various mechanisms. Rarely, the apical trabecular component is sequestered from the remainder of the RV. In 1997, Yoo et al2 described 4 cases with different lesions sharing the common pathology of apical right ventricular sequestration. Case 3 closely resembles the present case, as it demonstrated a large defect in the trabecular septum. Because the defect involved the entire septal component related to the sequestered RV apex, the left ventricle (LV) together with the sequestered RV formed a boot-shaped chamber, and from a hemodynamic perspective, the muscular shelf functioned as an interventricular septum.2

The portion of the cavity projecting rightward corresponded to the sequestered RV, which communicated with the LV through a large apical muscular ventricular septal defect. Accordingly, as described by Yoo et al,2 the boundary between the anatomic RV and LV could be defined by the course of the anterior and posterior descending coronary arteries.

Several reports of DCRV evaluated by CMR imaging have described the classic form characterized by midventricular obstruction, rather than the rare subtype of apical sequestration.3, 4, 5, 6 Otto and Ferguson7 reported a unique case of apical right ventricular sequestration, detailing the characteristic CMR findings of this uncommon variant. We present a similar variant of DCRV with apical sequestration, assessed using a comprehensive multimodality imaging approach.

Consistent with the report by Otto et al,7 there was no flow obstruction between the inlet and outlet portions of the right ventricle, which were connected by a tubular channel between the tricuspid and pulmonary valves, resulting in a reduced functional RV volume due to apical sequestration. While transthoracic echocardiography identified initial abnormalities, it did not fully characterize the complex ventricular anatomy. Diagnostic catheterization further delineated the complex ventricular anatomy.

CMR was performed on a 1.5-T system using a comprehensive protocol with morphological, functional, and dynamic perfusion sequences with gadolinium. Imaging demonstrated a small RV with trabecular division by a muscular shelf continuous with the interventricular septum, with the pulmonary artery arising from the functional RV outlet. The LV assumed a distinctive “Christmas hat” configuration due to its communication with the sequestered RV apex. LV hypertrabeculation was evident on cine and T2-weighted sequences. Additional findings included bilateral atrioventricular valve dysplasia with severe regurgitation, leading to marked biatrial dilation. Four-dimensional flow imaging demonstrated shunting between the sequestered apical chamber and the functional RV, as well as severe mitral and tricuspid regurgitation. CCT further identified multiple communications between the sequestered RV apex and the functional RV cavity.

Although a well-defined moderator band could not be clearly identified within the sequestered trabecular component, additional anatomical landmarks allowed identification of ventricular morphology. The presence of a subpulmonary infundibulum, apical displacement of the tricuspid valve, and the spatial distribution of the coronary arteries supported the identification of the morphologic right ventricle.

The tricuspid subvalvular apparatus appeared dysplastic, with chordal attachments extending toward the interventricular septum. The LV also demonstrated a dysplastic subvalvular apparatus, with small papillary muscles located in the anterosuperior and lateral walls.

In our patient, identification of the apical chamber as a morphologic RV relied primarily on coronary artery anatomy. CCT demonstrated that the anterior and posterior descending coronary arteries followed their normal course along the interventricular septum and converged at the true apex, thereby defining the boundary between the RV and LV. This finding supports the interpretation that the apical chamber corresponds to a sequestered portion of the RV rather than a displaced LV, as described in hypoplastic right ventricular variants.

Prior reports suggest that apical right ventricular sequestration may result from abnormal development or partitioning of the trabecular component of the right ventricle. This aligns with the tripartite model of ventricular development (inlet, trabecular, and outlet components). Abnormal persistence, malposition, or hypertrophy of trabecular structures, such as the septomarginal trabeculation or moderator band, may lead to separation of the apical component. In addition, disruptions in molecular pathways involved in atrioventricular canal and trabecular development (e.g., NKX2-5, Notch, and Wnt signaling) may contribute to related congenital cardiac anomalies.8,9

This case, like that reported by Otto et al,7 underscores the central role of multimodal imaging in the evaluation of DCRV. Recognition of the apical right ventricular sequestration subtype is essential for accurate diagnosis and appropriate management.

Conclusions

Apical right ventricular sequestration is a rare variant of DCRV in which a multimodal imaging is essential for accurate anatomic characterization and appropriate management.
