Effect of epigenetic modulation on metabolites from endophytes isolated from Embelia ribes
Authors: Ajinkya Terkar, Aditya Raut, Jayant Kulkarni, Vitthal T. Barvkar, Mahesh Borde
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionFungal endophytes share a symbiotic relationship with the host plants. Endophytes from medicinal plants produce metabolites similar to plants as well as some new metabolites, which serve as a promising medicinal source with, significant potential in the field of biomedicine. Epigenetic modifiers, such as DNA methyltransferase and histone deacetylase inhibitors, activate cryptic biosynthesis gene clusters, resulting in a significant increase in cryptic metabolite production. This study elucidated the alteration in the metabolite profiles of two endophytes isolated from the medicinal plant Embelia ribes after treatment with two epigenetic modulators.Materials and methodsThis study assessed the effect of epigenetic modifiers—Azacitidine (AZ) and Sodium butyrate (SB)—on the metabolite profiles of Phomopsis azadirachtae and Diaporthe phaseolorum. Different concentrations of AZ and SB (1, 10, 50, 100, and 500 mM) were employed to assess their impact on the fungal endophyte cultures. Metabolome analysis was performed to observe the alteration of metabolites.ResultsLC–MS analysis revealed 47 targeted metabolites in the AZ-treated P. azadirachtae culture. Treatment with AZ significantly affected the production of metabolites compared with the control. AZ treatment also altered the production of nine silent metabolites; namely dicerandrol B, phomosine A, epiepoxydon, taxol, cladosporine, phomonaphthalenone A, phomophyllin A, 3-indolepropionic acid (3-IPA) and ergosterol in P. azadirachtae culture. Two metabolites enhanced their production compared to the control. A total of 47 metabolites were identified in P. azadirachtae culture treated with SB, which also altered 11 silent metabolites and enhanced production of six metabolites; cytosporone B, phomophyllin A, phomosine A, phomosin B, laiolactol A, and ergosterol P by logarithmic analysis. Similarly, 41 metabolites were identified in D. phaseolorum culture treated with various concentrations of AZ. In D. phaseolorum culture treated with AZ, an epigenetic modification activated 11 silent metabolites—Cytochalasin N, bostrycoidin, phomonaphthalenone, phomopsterone, dicerandrol A, pinselin, indole-3-acetic acid, betulinic acid, phomophyllin A, dalienxanthone B and phomopoxide A. Two metabolites, phomosine A and zeatin riboside, were enhanced in majority of the AZ treatments compared to control by logarithmic analysis. SB treatment significantly modulated the metabolite profile of D. phaseolorum, with LC–MS analysis detecting 46 targeted compounds across different concentrations. The treatment activated 11 previously silent bioactive metabolites, including Ganodermaside D, lithocarpinol A, dalienxanthone B, cladospirone, dicerandrol B, libertellenone, phomonaphthalenone A, phomopoxide A, phomopsichin B, phomopsterone B, and cladospirone. Three metabolites, pinselin, dicerandrol A, and phmosine A was significantly enhanced in most of the SB treatments compared to control.ConclusionAZ treatment induced significant, concentration, dependent alterations in the metabolite profile of P. azadirachtae, with the most pronounced effects observed at the P1AZ concentration. Multivariate and clustering analyses revealed clear metabolic differentiation between treated and control cultures. A total of 47 targeted metabolites were detected under AZ treatment, including nine previously silent metabolites consistently induced across all concentrations. Notably, AZ exposure enhanced the production of phomophyllin A and phaseolorine, indicating the selective activation of cryptic biosynthetic pathways in P. azadirachtae. SB treatment significantly altered the secondary metabolite profile of P. azadirachtae in a dose-dependent manner. Metabolomic analysis detected 47 compounds in SB-treated cultures, with the most pronounced metabolic changes observed at the P50SB and P500SB concentrations. SB exposure activated a previously silent biosynthetic gene cluster responsible for the production of 11 metabolites. Furthermore, log fold-change analysis demonstrated significant and consistent upregulation of six metabolites across most SB treatments, highlighting SB’s effectiveness in activating cryptic secondary metabolism in P. azadirachtae. In AZ-treated D. phaseolorum cultures, epigenetic alteration triggered 11 metabolites. Log fold change analysis reported significant upregulation of two metabolites. In D. phaseolorum, SB treatments detected 46 targeted compounds across different concentrations. The treatment activated 11 previously silent bioactive metabolites. and significantly increased the levels of three metabolites compared with controls. These findings demonstrate that the epigenetic modulators AZ and SB altered secondary metabolite profiles in fungal endophytes, indicating their potential to activate silent biosynthetic pathways. These findings support their use as exploratory tools for metabolite discovery, while highlighting the need for multi-omics and structural validation in future work.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10123-026-00796-2.

Introduction

Endophytic fungi are believed to be the goldmine of secondary metabolites (Strobel and Daisy 2003) and can produce compounds potentially useful for pharmaceutical and commercial applications (White et al. 2019). Various studies have reported the isolation of endophytic fungi that produce taxol from Taxomyces andreanae (Stierle et al. 1993), the endophyte Fusarium solani that produces camptothecin (Shweta et al. 2010), and Eupenicillium parvum that produces azadirachtin (Kusari et al. 2012). A growing number of studies on endophyte discovery and the synthesis of secondary metabolites from them underscore their promising potential as a rich and dependable natural resource (Strobel, 2003).

In fungi, biosynthetic gene clusters, often found in distal telomeric regions of the chromosomes (Shwab et al. 2007; Williams et al. 2008), are transcriptionally controlled by epigenetic regulations, specifically histone acetylation and methylation (Brakhage 2013; Pfannenstiel and Keller 2019). Fungal DNA arranged into histone proteins is transcriptionally inactive, whereas acetylation is believed to be associated with the transcriptional activity of DNA (Okada and Seyedsayamdost 2017; Cichewicz 2010). Epigenetic processes can activate silent or lowly expressed BGCs by modifying chromatin shape and DNA accessibility, resulting in increased synthesis or the production of new secondary metabolites. Treating the culture of fungal endophytes with epigenetic modifiers such as histone deacetylase inhibitors (HDACi) and DNA methyltransferase inhibitors (DNMTi) has been reported as a promising strategy to induce cryptic gene expression (Toghueo et al. 2020; Gupta et al. 2020). Shwab et al. (2007) reported that the treatment of A. alternata and P. expansum with HDACi resulted in the overproduction of several metabolites, suggesting HDAC repression of some secondary metabolite gene clusters as a conserved mechanism for metabolite production. Furthermore, studies have mostly used epigenetic modifiers such as DNMTi and HDACi (Cichewicz 2010; Brakhage 2013; Lim and Keller 2014; Guo et al. 2020; Zhu et al. 2019). A novel compound, spergillusidone G, was obtained from endophytes of the strain Aspergillus ungus DLEP2008001 by supplementing the culture medium with 1 mM procaine (a DNMTi) (Yang et al. 2018). Yu et al. (2019) observed that Neosartorya udagawae HDN13-313 grown in the culture medium supplemented with 5-azacytidine led to the production of two novel polyketide compounds. Another study reported that the addition of 5-azacytidine enhanced the production of alkaloid camptothecin in Botryosphaeria rhodina (Botryosphaeriaceae) culture (Vasanthakumari et al. 2015). Treatments of Diaporthe sp. culture with HDACi valproic acid and suberohydroxamic acid enhanced metabolite production (Jasim et al. 2019).

In the present study, five different concentrations of two epigenetic modifiers, namely 5-azacitidine (AZ) and sodium butyrate (SB), were added to the culture media of endophytes isolated from Embelia ribes, namely Phomopsis azadirachtae and Diaporthe phaseolorum, to investigate their effect on the metabolite profile of these fungal endophytes. The main objective of the present work was to assess the effects of epigenetic modulators on secondary metabolite production from P. azadirachtae and D. phaseolorum.

Materials and methods

Fungal endophytes

Two fungal endophytes, namely P. azadirachtae and D. phaseolorum, were isolated from the leaves of E. ribes. DNA extraction from the mycelia of the two endophytes was performed as described by Aamir et al. (2015). The internal transcribed spacer region of the ribosomal DNA (rDNA) gene was amplified from the DNA using the primers described by White et al. (1990). The PCR-amplified samples were sent to Apical Scientific Sdn Bhd, Malaysia, for Sanger sequencing. The isolation and identification of fungal endophytes from Embelia ribes were reported by Terkar et al. (2023). The cultures of D. phaseolorum and P. azadirachtae have been deposited in the National Fungal Culture Collection, Agharkar Research Institute, Pune, India, with the accession numbers NFCCI5390 and NFCCI5391, respectively.

Chemicals and solvents

The culture media consisted of potato dextrose broth (PDB). The epigenetic modifiers used were AZ and SB.

Treatments

Each endophyte was treated with a control and 1 µM, 10 µM, 50 µM, 100 µM, and 500 µM concentrations of AZ and SB. So, the treatments of P. azadirachtae for AZ and SB were PCAZ, P1AZ, P10AZ, P50AZ, P100AZ, P500AZ, PCSB, P1SB, P10SB, P100SB, and P500SB. The treatments of D. phaseolorum for AZ and SB were DCAZ, D1AZ, D10AZ, D100AZ, D500AZ, DCSB, D1SB, D10SB, D10SB, D500SB, respectively.

Fermentation conditions

The two endophyte cultures were grown on potato dextrose agar for 7 days prior to inoculation of the mother culture containing 100 mL of PDB. The cultures were incubated at 27 °C. After 3 days, 250 mL culture bottles containing 100 mL of PDB were inoculated with four cubes of seed culture and treated with the five concentrations of corresponding epigenetic modulators (AZA and SB) dissolved in DMSO. Fermentation were carried out over 2 weeks at 27 °C under shaking conditions at 150 rpm. All treatments were prepared in triplicate, including control.

Sample extraction and preparation

Triplicates of each treatment were used for metabolites extraction. After 2 weeks, whole culture broths (including mycelium) for each treatment were extracted with 50 mL of ethyl acetate by using a pestle and mortar, followed by sonication for 60 min. Mycelia were removed from the culture supernatant through filtration with glass cotton. Subsequently, the extracts were filtered and reduced by evaporation drying. A small portion of each dry residue was suspended in 1,000 µL methanol. For liquid chromatography-quadrupole-time-of-flight-mass spectrometry (LC-Q-ToF-MS) analysis, the samples were syringe filtered by using a 0.45-µm filter, and 20 µL aliquot of each sample was used for the analysis.

LC-QTOF-MS analysis

The analysis of extracts were conducted on the Agilent HPLC Prime Infinity II 1260 system (800 bar) combined with the Agilent 6530 Q-TOF (Agilent, USA) mass spectrometer with, a dual electrospray ionization (ESI) source. The separation was carried out using Hypersil GOLD C18 column (2.1 × 150 mm2, 1.9 μm particle size, Thermo Scientific, USA), with the following gradient elution programme: 0–0.3 min, 2% B; 0.3–2.0 min, 30% B; 2.0–7.0 min, 45% B; 7–16 min, 98% B by using mobile phase A (0.1% formic acid in water) and mobile phase B (0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile). The flow rate was adjusted to 0.3 mL/min. For mass spectrometry analysis of compounds, an MS acquisition method was used to acquire the positive-ion ESI mass spectra mode. To induce the fragmentation of parent ions, collision induced dissociation (CID-MSn) was applied. The molecular ions and their fragments were analyzed using tandem mass spectrometry. Two masses (120.0508 and 1225.9906) were used for real-time mass correction. The area under the peak of metabolites was calculated by employing Agilent Mass hunter Qualitative Navigator B.08.00. The peak areas of metabolites from different treatments were used for data analysis.

LC–MS data analysis

Statistical and data analyses were performed using the MetaboAnalyst platform version 5.0 (https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/) (Pang et al. 2022). Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed, followed by partial least-squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) and sparse partial least-squares discriminant analysis (sPLS-DA). Additionally, a PLS-DA-VIP score plot was constructed to identify differentially abundant metabolites. Cluster analysis was performed using MetaboAnalyst 5.0 to evaluate the relative abundance and relation among all metabolites by constructing a heat map. Venn analysis was performed using the InteractiveVenn (http://www.interactivenn.net/) tool to study metabolite distribution. Changes in the level of metabolites between the control and different treatment groups were calculated using the logarithmic fold change in MS Excel. In addition, Student’s t-test was performed in MS Excel to test whether differences across the groups were significant.

Results

Effect of epigenetic modulators on metabolite production in P. azadirachtae culture

Treatment of P. azadirachtae with different concentrations of AZ

Through LC-MS analysis of metabolites from P. azadirachtae culture treated with AZ, 47 targeted metabolites were detected through LC–MS analyzed for metabolites alteration. PCA and PLS-DA showed that treatment of P. azadirachtae with AZ significantly changed the metabolite profile compared to the control. The PCA of the metabolite profile demonstrated distinct score plots for the AZ treatment. Metabolites under the PCAZ treatment formed a separate cluster, those under the P10AZ and P50AZ treatments formed one cluster, and those under the P1AZ, P100AZ, and P500AZ treatments formed significantly distinct clusters compared to the other treatments (Figure S1A). In line with the PCA results, PLS-DA for metabolite profiling showed that the PCAZ treatment formed a separate cluster from the other treatments (Figure S1B). Score plot analysis of AZ treatment showed that PCAZ and P1AZ metabolites formed a separate cluster, whereas P10AZ, P50AZ, P100AZ, and P500AZ metabolites formed a single cluster. (Fig. 1A). PLS-DA was performed on 10 metabolites, of which 6 exhibited significant alterations, with high VIP scores (Fig. 1B). To gain further insight into the enhancement of metabolite production resulting from the treatment of P. azadirachtae with five different concentrations of AZ, the metabolite profiles of the five treatments, the control, and the 47 compounds in P. azadirachtae, were visualized using hierarchical clustering and a heatmap. The relative abundance levels within a metabolite are represented using dark brown, white, and blue colors to indicate above-average, average, and below-average values, respectively. The heatmap is divided into two major clusters, namely cluster 1 and cluster 2, with cluster 2 further divided into three subclusters (2 − 1, 2–2, and 2–3) (Fig. 1C). PC1AZ treatment resulted in the alteration of a higher number of metabolites compared with the other treatments.

Discussion

The epigenetic modulators AZ (a DNMTi) and SB (an HAT inhibitor) have been utilized individually or in combination to enhance metabolic pathways and promote the production of otherwise undetectable compounds in microbial cultures. These modulators effectively activate cryptic or silent genes that remain dormant under normal growth conditions. Focusing on their impacts on the metabolic profiles of fungal endophytes, this study applied different concentrations of SB and AZA to P. azadirachtae and D. phaseolorum cultures.

Analysis of 47 targeted metabolites was performed through LC–MS of P. azadirachtae culture. PCA, PLSDA, and sPLS-DA indicated that the metabolic profile of P. azadirachtae was significantly altered under AZ treatments compared with the control. Venn diagram shows that AZ significantly altered some metabolites; five metabolites, namely dicerandrol B, Phomosine A, Epiepoxydon, Taxol, and Cladospirone exhibited significant alterations in all AZ treatment compared with the control (PCAZ). Venn diagram shows that AZ significantly activated metabolites in P. azadirachtae cultures. For example, dicerandrol B was altered only in the P500AZ treatment. Conclusions

In this study, the effects of two epigenetic modulators, AZ and SB, on the metabolite profiles of two fungal endophytes, P. azadirachtae and D. phaseolorum, were investigated using different concentrations of AZ and SB. AZ treatment—particularly at the P1AZ concentration—induced pronounced, concentration, dependent alterations in the metabolite profile of P. azadirachtae, as evidenced by multivariate and clustering analyses. A total of 47 targeted compounds were detected in P. azadirachtae culture treated with AZ. Of these, nine silent metabolites were induced in all AZ treatments compared with the control. Under AZ treatment, Phomophyllin A and Phaseolorine showed increased concentrations, indicating a selective induction of the cryptic biosynthetic pathway in P. azadirachtae. SB treatment effectively altered the metabolites in P. azadirachtae, with treated samples clearly separated from the control and P500SB treatment exhibited the most pronounced effect. A total of 47 compounds were detected in the culture of P. azadirachtae treated with SB. Strong, dose-dependent alteration in P. azadirachtae’s metabolite profile was brought on by SB treatment; the most significant alterations were seen in P50SB and P500SB. In P. azadirachtae culture, SB treatment induced the activation of a silent biosynthesis gene cluster encoding 11 metabolites. Log fold change analysis indicated that SB treatment significantly enhanced the production of six metabolites: cytosporone B, phomophyllin A, phomosine A, phomosin B, laiolactol A, and ergosterol P. exhibited consistent upregulation over the majority of treatments. In D. phaseolorum culture treated with different concentrations of AZ, 41 targeted compounds were detected through LC–MS analysis. In D. phaseolorum culture, epigenetic modification caused by AZ activated 11 silent metabolites. In addition, AZ treatment considerably increased the log fold chain production of two metabolites—phomosine A, and zeatin riboside—compared with the control. In D. phaseolorum culture treated with different concentrations of SB, LC–MS identified 46 targeted compounds. In D. phaseolorum culture, SB treatments generated 11 bioactive silent metabolites. Additionally, in D. phaseolorum culture, SB treatments led to a positive and significant alteration of metabolites such as three metabolites, Pinselin, Dicerandrol A, and Phmosine A- compared with the control environment by logarithmic analysis These findings highlight the potential roles of AZ and SB in the activation of a wide range of silent metabolites in fungal endophyte cultures, such as P. azadirachtae and D. phaseolorum. However, the findings are based on targeted LC–MS profiling and relative abundance analysis and therefore do not provide direct evidence of de novo biosynthesis, pathway activation at the transcriptional level, or absolute increases in metabolite production. Additionally, structural confirmation and functional characterization of the induced metabolites were beyond the scope of this work. Despite these limitations, the findings provide evidence that epigenetic modulators can alter secondary metabolism in fungal endophytes and support their use as exploratory tools for metabolite discovery. Future studies integrating transcriptomic, epigenomic, and metabolomic approaches, along with structural validation and bioactivity assays, will be necessary to confirm the mechanisms underlying metabolite induction and to evaluate the functional significance of the compounds produced.
