Women in chemistry: Q&A with Dr Janelle Sauvageau
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Dr Janelle Sauvageau is a carbohydrate chemist within the Human Health Therapeutics Research Centre at the National Research Council of Canada (NRC).

Her career began at the Université Laval in 2001, achieving a B.Sc. and M.Sc. in Chemistry. Before her Ph.D., she worked in Wellington, New Zealand as a Researcher for Industrial Research Limited. Dr Sauvageau stayed in New Zealand to pursue her Ph.D. at Victoria University of Wellington graduating in 2013. She was brought back to Canada by the NRC in late 2014 to work in the field of vaccines and immunomodulators. At the NRC, Dr Sauvageau’s interest switched to a new Pathogen Associated Molecular Patterns (PAMPs) molecule excreted by a gram-negative bacteria and its impact on the immune system.Janelle Sauvageau

She also contributed to the development of a vaccine against Pseudomonas aeruginosa to prevent infections in cystic fibrosis patients and other immunocompromised individuals. Her study of the surface glycans of pathogens also forms part of the Canadian national efforts against COVID-19. Recently the assays she developed, simplified and sped-up the analyses of glycans on SARS-CoV-2 glycoprotein vaccine antigens. When time is of importance, for example such as in a pandemic, those assays will be critical to develop vaccines faster as current analyses are so time consuming that they slowed down the glycoprotein vaccine development process during the COVID-19 pandemic.

Dr Sauvageau is also engaged in mentoring activities, and the promotion of policies for equity in science careers. She recognized the need for young researchers to have their own support network, so she worked within the NRC to establish the Early Career Network. She is also actively involved in supporting equity diversity in inclusion by performing research on differential access to resources for equity-deserving groups. Her work informed policies within the whole NRC.

Why did you choose to be a scientist?

Since I was young, I have always loved science in general. I remember at the age of 7 or 8, my dad bought me an edition of a science journal, and I tried to understand what the science meant. I realized that I understood so little and that a whole new world was opening up to me. Around the same time, I decided to go to a science camp. As a young woman in Quebec, being interested in science and going to that camp was something different and exciting for me, and it hooked me entirely on science.

I decided to pursue chemistry after watching a movie in high school, The Saint. This movie taught me that chemistry can change the world in a good way. Today, I know that chemistry can change the world in both good and bad ways, but as chemists, I am confident that we can be a part of many solutions to improve our world, whether through vaccines to prevent diseases, new drugs, or green chemistry.

What scientific development are you currently most excited about?

Right now, I am most excited about the scientific developments in nanopore sensing of glycans and carbohydrates. RNA and DNA sequencing have allowed the scientific community to achieve great things. For example, mRNA vaccines would not have been possible without this advancement. However, it is not yet possible to sequence glycans and carbohydrates. We are still using the same methods of analysis as originally developed because carbohydrates are extremely complex and difficult to analyze as they are branched, not linear. I wonder what answers we will be able to discover once we can sequence them. That said, the thing I am most excited about changes relatively often - so you can ask me again in a year.

What direction do you think your research field should go in?

I think my research field is currently moving in the right direction. We can visualize glycans on the surface of organisms, we are working toward their sensing, and we can see that their sequencing is on the horizon. We are also working toward automating their synthesis. Once we can do both, I believe our field will flourish and help us to better understand our world. Achieving carbohydrate sequencing will likely uncover an unexpected mine of data to explore and enhance our understanding.

In the broader field of chemistry, I hope that we can make improvements to sustainability. Carbohydrate synthesis requires protecting groups and large amounts of solvents, and I hope we can develop more methods that are water-based to reduce the use of environmentally damaging solvents.

What do you most (and least) enjoy about being a scientific researcher?

What I enjoy most about being a researcher is being the first person in the world to know something—whether it’s creating a compound for the first time or discovering what this compound does in a biological system. This is the most exciting aspect for me. I also love the learning. There isn’t a single day where I don’t learn something new. To me, learning is the best gift anyone can give me. My grandfather always said that what is in your head, nobody can ever take away from you. Additionally, the highs of success, the happiness one feels when a grant is obtained after multiple applications, and when a manuscript is published, are very rewarding.

What I like least about being a researcher is the politics involved within the field. There tends to be a focus on past achievements, rather than on giving every researcher an equal chance to succeed. Alongside this are the lows of disappointment, such as when a manuscript is rejected or when a grant is not awarded.

What action(s) do you feel employers in chemical research should take to make a difference for women scientists?

This is a very complex question, and I think the answer could vary for different women, employers, and even countries. Within one employer, the situation can vary between different departments as well. What I wish is for employers to listen to women and understand the challenges specific to their institutions, and to be more willing to listen to women’s perspectives on these challenges. It’s important to avoid generic solutions such as simply providing more mentorship or networking opportunities.

For example, in a government research institute, research funding is provided by the employer and researchers are usually not eligible for other government funding. In contrast, researchers in academia often have to seek their own funding, and those in industry face different situations as well. Additionally, the challenges can vary within institutions. For instance, in some departments, a discrepancy in research funding allocation can be the main issue, while in another department, access to equipment may be the primary concern.

In this diverse landscape, institutions like my employer, the National Research Council of Canada, play a crucial role. Recognizing the importance of fostering inclusivity in leadership roles within the organization, efforts are made to support diverse talents and perspectives. While I appreciate the strides being made, I believe there are still opportunities to enhance inclusion within research activities. I am grateful to work alongside many talented colleagues, including women who share my passion for science.

In my experience, such institutional efforts underscore the need for employers to take broader responsibility in addressing challenges with an intersectional approach. At my institution, I have been fortunate to have my concerns heard and addressed regarding project resourcing. However, advocating for these changes required significant time and energy. This highlights the importance for individuals within large organizations to prepare for the effort required to effectively voice concerns and effect change.

What steps do you feel are needed to retain more women in chemistry, for example from PhD level to Professorship?

I think that allocating more resources for women to succeed is important, and thinking outside of the box is also crucial. For example, if a woman is pregnant and cannot work as a professor for a while, could an institution allocate her a post-doctoral fellow to continue her work so there is no delay in her career? I believe that thinking creatively to ensure that existing gaps are filled is important. Could there also be remediation systems? For example, understanding the statistics on funding allocation and actively remediating this with observed biases.
