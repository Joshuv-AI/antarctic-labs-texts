Correlates of virological non-suppression among children and adolescents aged 5–19 years on Dolutegravir-based regimens attending Gulu regional referral hospital, Northern Uganda: a mixed methods cross-sectional study
Authors: Vincent Okech Moro, John Mbaziira Ssenkusu, Flavia Matovu Kiweewa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundViral suppression has been sub-optimal among children and adolescent in Uganda, despite a near-universal transition to the much effective Dolutegravir-based regimens. Therefore this study was aimed at determining the prevalence of virological non-suppression and its associated correlates among children and adolescents aged 5–19 years on Dolutegravir-based regimens.MethodsA mixed methods cross-sectional study was conducted among 303 children and adolescents aged 5–19 years on Dolutegravir-based regimens from January 2021 to December 2022. The chi-square likelihood ratio test and Modified Poisson regression analyses were used to identify correlates significantly associated with virological non-suppression. Purposive and convenient sampling were done and interviews analyzed using manual thematic content analysis.ResultsVirological non-suppression prevalence was at 12.2% (95% CI: 8.5% − 16.0%). Parental status (being single orphan, adjusted Prevalence Ratio (aPR: 5.4 [95% CI: 1.81–15.90], p = < 0.001), (double orphan, aPR: 3.3 [95% CI: 1.07–10.02], p = 0.04), clinical stage (WHO clinical stage II on Dolutegravir-based anti-retroviral therapy initiation (aPR: 3.4 [95% CI: 1.55–7.62], p = < 0.001), and malnutrition status (moderate acute malnutrition (aPR: 2.3 [95% CI: 1.16–4.76], p = 0.02) were significantly associated with virological non-suppression. Findings also indicated that differentiated service delivery models, multi-month drug dispensing, tolerability and palatability of Dolutegravir, adolescent child friendly services, awareness and stigma reduction programs, having a caregiver and having a caregiver who is on anti-retroviral therapy emerged as important deterrents to virological non-suppression. However, HIV status non-disclosure, food unavailability, deprivation of family and social support, secondary school age, poor adherence, malnutrition, depression, tuberculosis co-infection, disease progression to stage II and above, drug holidays and fatigue, transitioning from child to adolescent age and information gap on treatment were potential enablers of virological non-suppression.ConclusionThe prevalence of virological non-suppression among children and adolescents aged 5–19 years on Dolutegravir-based regimens was 12.2%. This was relatively low in comparison to other studies conducted in Uganda and other Sub-Saharan African countries but short of the UNAIDS third 95% target for viral suppression.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11647-2.

Background

The United Nations Joint Program on HIV/AIDS (UNAIDS) set targets referred to as the 95-95-95 aimed at epidemic control by 2030 and therefore this translated into ensuring that; 95% of all HIV positive individuals knew their HIV status, 95% of those who knew their HIV status, should be on antiretroviral therapy (ART) and of those on ART, 95% should be virally suppressed hence reducing the risk of transmission, improving the wellbeing of infected persons and reducing mother- to - child transmission (MTCT) rates [1].

Despite the growing ART coverage among people living with HIV (PLWH), virological suppression was still uneven across regions and populations with prevalence of 59% to > 95% in Asia and the Pacific, 47–83% in Central and Western Africa, 73% to > 95% in Eastern and Southern Africa and consequently, children and adolescent population have the lowest viral suppression (VS) rate compared to the adult population [2]. This is because only 53% of Children and Adolescents Living With Human Immuno-deficiency Virus and Acquired Immune Deficiency Syndrome (CALWH) globally are receiving lifesaving ART compared to 68% of adults [3]. In Uganda, children CALWH aged 5–19 years have historically had lower viral load (VL) suppression rates than adults and this is partly due to sub-optimal ART regimens [4].

Consequently, the World Health Organization (WHO) recommended the use of an Integrase Strand Transferase Inhibitor (INSTI), Dolutegravir (DTG) as an anchor drug for first line, second and third line HIV treatment for all populations including children and adolescents. The premise of recommending DTG in children was based on previous formulations that were either Non-Nucleoside Reverse Transcriptase Inhibitor (NNRTI) based and had become sub-optimal due to high levels of pre-treatment HIV drug resistance (HIVDR) to NNRTIs or formulations were not child-friendly, most especially protease inhibitors (PIs) leading to sub-optimal adherence and virological non-suppression (VNS) [5–7].

Following WHO recommendations, Uganda adopted DTG-based ART as first line regimen for treatment of HIV and the United States President’s Emergency Plan for AIDS Relief (PEPFAR), as the predominant HIV donor included DTG rollout in its annual program targets for national HIV response [8, 9]. Evaluation of program data for HIV national response in Uganda, showed that 52.2% of children were still on NNRTIs despite a policy shift from NNRTIs to paediatric DTG prompting the Ministry of Health (MOH), Uganda and its partners to design an optimization plan for CALWH hence the “Rapid Paediatric and Adolescent ART Optimization” in Uganda [4].

Subsequent to DTG optimization among children and adolescents, substantial VS was realized among CALWH in Uganda with a viral suppression estimate of 78% in December 2021 from 70% in June 2019 among the 0–9 age category and a 82% suppression rate in 2021 from 73% in 2019 in the 10–19 age category, despite falling short of the UNAIDS third 95% target for viral suppression [4]. Substantial efficacy and safety has been appreciated in the use of DTG for the treatment and management of CALWH in SSA with a documented post DTG VL suppression rate of 93.4% [10]. Just like other low and middle income countries, Uganda still faces a challenge with information on VS among CALWH and this is because data for adolescents aged 10–15 years is lamped under the age disaggregation of 0–14 years while adolescents aged 16–19 years are considered under the age disaggregation of adults making it difficult to ascertain virological outcomes of CALWH [10, 11]. However several studies in Uganda have reported suboptimal VS among CALWH age 0–19 years [11–14].

As much as several studies in SSA and Uganda in particular have documented correlates associated with virological non-suppression (VNS) among children and adolescents [1, 15–17], there is limited evidence on VNS among children and adolescents on DTG-based treatment in Uganda. Thus this study was aimed at determining the prevalence of VNS and its associated correlates among children and adolescent on DTG-based regimens in Gulu regional referral hospital, Northern Uganda.

Methods

Study design

We utilized a sequential explanatory mixed methods study design. A quantitative cross-sectional study was followed by a qualitative study combining in-depth (ID) and key informant (KI) interviews.

Study setting

The study was conducted in Gulu regional referral hospital, located in Gulu district, Northern Uganda. The hospital is in the central part of Gulu, approximately 333 km (207 miles), by road, North of Uganda’s capital city, Kampala. The coordinates of the district are, near the city of Gulu, Latitude: 2.830556 and Longitude: 32.320278 [18]. This study included all CALWH on DTG-based regimens attending the Gulu regional referral hospital ART clinic between January 2021 and December 2022. The target population included all CALWH on DTG-based regimens aged 5–19 years for at least 6 months between January 2021 and December 2022.

Quantitative component

Study participants

The study also targeted key informants who manage children and adolescent HIV/AIDS care and treatment services in Gulu regional referral hospital and in-depth interview participants who are adolescents of consenting age in care, parents and caregivers of children and adolescents. The study focussed on CALWH aged 5–19 years on DTG-based regimens, primarily enrolled and active on treatment in Gulu regional referral hospital from January 2021 and December 2022. The study included all CALWH aged 5–19 years on DTG-based regimens with at least one documented viral load result following 6 months of DTG treatment. This was ideal because viral load monitoring is done at least twice a year among CALWH. This study strictly considered and ensured that only those who gave consent participated in the study. The study excluded those with interrupted DTG treatment as a result of contraindication or treatment ineligibility, Lost to follow-up (LTFU) and lost to follow (LTF). Absence of viral load test results after 6 months of DTG hindered the ascertainment of the primary outcome and so it was a key exclusion criteria.

Data collection

A cross-sectional review of 423 patient files of CALWH aged 5–19 years on DTG-based regimens between January 2021 and December 2022. Closed ended questionnaire was used to collect information on predictors of virological non-suppression (Supplementary file 1).

Variables

Outcome

The primary outcome variable was VNS after six months following DTG initiation or after DTG optimization defined as viral load of at least 1000 copies of viral ribonucleic acid (RNA) per ml of blood or plasma, coded as 1 for non-suppressed and 2 for suppressed. Data on virological outcome was extracted from the patient files considering the Uganda Health Management Information System (HMIS 103) or ART care card as the secondary data collection tool. Verification of the data was done using the HMIS 002-laboratory viral load register, and the Uganda Electronic Medical Record (Uganda EMR). Virological outcome was categorized into two; virological non-suppression and virological suppression. In this study, virological non-suppression was considered as viral load of ≥ 1000 copies per millilitre of blood or plasma while virological suppression was considered as viral load of < 1000 copies per millilitre of blood or plasma. For a patient with virological non-suppression, the data was captured as “YES” while virological suppression was captured as “NO” and coded as 1 and 2 respectively in the data analysis. A cut off of 1000 copies per millilitre was used to define virological non-suppression, based on several studies on virological non-suppression [1, 15, 17, 19–21] and the consolidated guidelines on treatment and management of HIV in Uganda [22, 23].

Independent variable

We examined three categories of independent variables and these were socio-demographic characteristics, clinical related correlates and health facility related correlates. The patient correlates examined were socio-demographic characteristic; Age of child or adolescent, age at ART initiation, education level, residence, religious denomination, HIV status of parent or caregiver, parental status and the clinical related characteristic; duration on ART, ART adherence level, ARV exposure, WHO clinical stage, CD4 count on ART initiation, multi-month dispensing, history of TB infection, current line of treatment and nutritional status (Table 1). Description of the study variables, categories and measurement of the variable categories

The health facility-related characteristics examined included; health worker attitude, distance to the health facility, children and adolescent friendly services, paediatric and adolescent DTG availability, waiting time, paediatric and adolescent ART management knowledge and skills and retention in care and treatment. Duration on ART was ascertained using patient record documented as date of enrolment. Information on adherence was collected from patient files as percentage adherence (Number of pills taken/Expected number of pills to be taken) × 100 documented as good (≥ 95%), fair (85–94%), and poor (< 85%). History of TB was collected as YES or NO from patient files using the TB status column of ART card (HMIS 003) highlighting the date TB treatment started and stopped. Information on ARV exposure was collected as YES or NO following review of patient file for history transition from mother-baby care clinic and date of ART initiation. Multi-month dispensing data was collected as 3 and 6 months ART refills, current line of ART was collected as first, second and third line ART regimen and this data was ascertained from patient file using the ART card (HMIS 003) on ARV drugs column (Table 1).

WHO clinical stage on DTG initiation was collected as Stage I, II, III and IV from patient files recorded in the WHO clinical stage column of ART card (HMIS 003). CD4 count was collected as absolute figures and categorized as ≥ 250 and < 250 cells/mm3. Malnutrition status was collected as normal coded as green (≥ 14.5 cm for 5 to < 10 years, ≥ 18.5 cm for 10 to < 15 years, ≥ 21.0 cm for 15 to < 18 years and > 22.0 cm for 18-19years), moderate acute malnutrition (MAM) coded as yellow (≥ 13.5 to < 14.5 cm for 5 to < 10 years, ≥ 16.0 to < 18.5 cm for 10 to < 15 years, ≥ 18.5 to < 21.0 cm for 15 to < 18 years and < 22.0 cm for 18 to 19 years) and severe acute malnutrition (SAM) coded as red (< 13.5 cm for 5 - <10 years, < 16.0 cm for 10-<15 years, 18.5 for 15 - <18 years and < 19.0 cm for 18–19 years) from the middle upper arm circumference (MUAC) measurements guided in the Uganda HIV ART card [22] (Table 1).

Qualitative component

Population and sample

We targeted targeted key informants who manage children and adolescent HIV/AIDS care and treatment services in Gulu regional referral hospital and in-depth interview participants were adolescents of age 18–19, parents and caregivers of children and adolescents. The Ugandan and study definition of adolescent was adopted from the WHO [24] which defines adolescents as individuals between the ages of 10–19 years. For the purpose of this study, we interviewed adolescents of age 18–19 years assuming that these adolescents were of secondary school going and capable of expressing themselves regarding perspectives on issues related to virological non-suppression.

Sample size

The Kish-Leslie formula (1965) was used to calculate the sample size determine to determine the prevalence and factors associated with virological non-suppression among children and adolescents, 5–19 years. A minimum of 303 participants was determined using confidence level of 95%, error margin of 5%, non-response of 10.2% and expected prevalence of 23% [15]. For the qualitative aspect of the study, sample size was not predetermined, but rather data saturation was used to determine how many interviewees would be included into the study, during the data collection process [25]. Therefore, data saturation was a point at which further interviews could not yield new response [26, 27] and this informed how many key informants and in-depth interview participants who were sampled and interviewed. Consequently, 6 key informants and 9 in-depth interview participants were interviewed.

Data processing and analysis

Microsoft Excel version 2013 and STATA software version 14 were used for data entry and analysis respectively. Descriptive analysis in the form of mean, median, mode standard deviation and frequency were used to summarize socio-demographic and clinical characteristics of the participants. Categorical variables were also presented in the form of absolute numbers and relative frequencies. Bivariate analysis was the first level of inferential analysis done using cross tabulation and Pearson’s chi-square likelihood ratio of sociodemographic, and clinical correlates and virological non-suppression were determined and this was to purposely predict any correlates associated with virological non-suppression at p ≤ 0.05. Modified Poisson regression was used to estimate the associations between socio-demographic and clinical correlates with virological non-suppression. The choice for using the modified Poisson regression is because the outcome of interest is not a rare outcome of HIV and it approximates the prevalence ratio better as in cross-sectional studies involving binary outcome analysis than the log binomial logistic model which overestimates the risk and the confidence intervals. Variables that were found to have a p-value of ≤ 0.20 at bivariate level of Poisson regression analysis were included into the multivariate model and adjusted for confounders to determine which of them had an independent significant association with the virological non-suppression. The predictor variables significantly associated with virological non-suppression after multivariate analysis were reported in terms of prevalence ratio (PR) at a significance level of 0.05 at 95% confidence interval. The Akaike Inclusion Criteria or model (AIC) was used to determine the goodness of fit of the model at p > 0.05 to ensure that the expected values based on the model were close to the observed values or the sample was representative of the whole population.

Qualitative component

Population and sample

We targeted adolescents 18–19 years who were on DTG-based regimens, parents, caregivers and health workers. For the purpose of this study, we interviewed adolescents 18–19 years assuming that these adolescents were capable of expressing their views on issues relating to DTG treatment experience and challenges attributed to virological non-suppression. The sample size for the interviews were not pre-determined but rather data saturation was used to determine how many interviewees were included into the study [24]. Therefore, data saturation was a point at which further interviews could not yield new responses [26, 27] and this informed how many key informants and in-depth interview participants who were sampled and interviewed. Consequently, 6 key informants and 9 in-depth interview participants were interviewed.

Data collection

Qualitative data was collected through key informant and In-depth interview guides with specific themes which aided the interviews (Supplementary files 2 and 3), translated in Acholi dialect. The responses were captured manually by direct transcription. The key informants comprised of health workers who provide child and adolescent ART services while in-depth interview participants included children and adolescent caregivers and adolescents of 18 years consenting age. Both interviews were open ended, allowing for flexible deliberation on a given issue. The interviews were administered in a face to face, straight-forward and direct approach of interviewing while probing for clarity and feelings on research questions and concerns. The interviews ended once saturation was achieved for the topic under discussion. To ensure data quality, we used three approaches; credibility, conformability, and reflexivity. Credibility was assured by establishing that each of the questions posed to the key informants were comprehended by them, so that they can be in position to provide appropriate and valid responses. In addition, prolong engagement of each interviewee and their persistent observation was taken into account [28]. Conformability was observed by providing a recap of the responses provided by each of the informants to confirm that the information captured was their viewpoint. As for reflexivity, the researcher ensured imposition of perspectives on the key informants during the interview was avoided but rather their personal views was unabated, only coming into a probe.

Data analysis

Qualitative data collected from the key informants and in-depth interview participants were analysed using the manual thematic analysis approach suggested by Braun and Clarke [29]. This approach included six steps, following transcription of the data. The steps involved becoming familiar with the data, generating initial codes, searching for themes, reviewing themes, defining themes to form categories, and providing narratives for each theme and category developed.

Ethical statement

The study sought ethical clearance from Makerere University School of Public health Research and Ethics Committee (MakSPH-REC − 308), the institutional review board (IRB) MakSPH and from the Gulu Regional Referral Hospital Research and Ethics Committee (Gulu RRH-REC-RH/2023/11/04) prior to commencement of the study, consent was sought from consenting participants aged 18 years and caregivers for those aged 5–17 years while ensuring that they have signed consent forms before participating in the study. Consent was also sought from in-depth and key informant interview participants for audio voice recordings. The adolescents and caregivers were informed of their voluntary nature of participation and free withdrawal from the study using a consistent dialect of Acholi/Luo. The adolescents and caregivers were further informed that their individual medical records would be reviewed and information extracted with utmost confidentiality. Research assistants received extensive training on data management protocol to avoid breach of confidentiality. The study participants were assured of anonymity since no existing personal identifiers such as ART clinic numbers were used and data was not accessible by any other third parties, except the study investigator and study team with the principal investigator’s approval.

There were 423 patients aged 5–19 years initially initiated on DTG based regimens in the period of January 2021 to December 2022 (Fig. 1). Sixty eight (16%) were not eligible for the study, Twenty five due to missing viral load results (37%) and forty three (63%) did not meet the study inclusion criteria. Three hundred and fifty five (84%) were eligible for the study based on inclusion criteria and availability of viral load test results. However three hundred and three (85%) of the eligible were enrolled and analyzed. There were fifty two participants (15%) who were not enrolled and analyzed. The reasons for non-enrollment were incomplete information on variables, non-consent and opting out of the study as seen in the flow chart in Figure 1.

Prevalence of virological non-suppression

The overall prevalence of virological non-suppression among children and adolescents aged 5–19 years on DTG-based regimens who had a non-suppressed viral load between July 2021 and December 2022 was 12.2% (95% CI: 8.5–16.0), (n = 37/303). Prevalence of VNS was higher among males (70.3%, 26/37) than females (29.7%, 11/37). Enablers of virological non-suppression

Non-disclosure of HIV status outside the biological family

Both children and adolescents, caregivers and health workers revealed the “challenges” surrounding HIV status disclosure, with on a few biological family members and relatives involved. This was indicated by the “challenges of disclosure to peers, children and among parents”. The following excerpts indicate some of the related themes:

“….As a peer adolescent, most times, we have not embraced the information around HIV care and treatment. Most times we are faced with challenges of disclosure among our peers and this is normally when we are in school”. (P (6), Adolescent, 18 years).

“….As a peer adolescent, most times, we have not embraced the information around HIV care and treatment. Most times we are faced with challenges of disclosure among our peers and this is normally when we are in school”. (P (6), Adolescent, 18 years).

“Disclosure among parents has always been a challenge and therefore children and adolescent face the same problem. Most times we as clinicians take it upon us to facilitate disclosure among children and adolescents”. (KI (4), Nursing officer, 37 years).

“Disclosure among parents has always been a challenge and therefore children and adolescent face the same problem. Most times we as clinicians take it upon us to facilitate disclosure among children and adolescents”. (KI (4), Nursing officer, 37 years).

Taking DTG without food

Challenge of lack of food at moments when children and adolescents are to be taking their medication emerged as a finding and discussion among the in-depth and key informant participants admitting that most times medications are taken at the most convenient time when food is available with some adolescents admitting that medications were not convenient during school hours as food may not be timely and this affects the right time of the doses and subsequently affecting adherence. These were their responses;

“Without food, taking drugs becomes a challenge but also there are times when the appetite for a particular food is poor. There have been days when I have had to take my medicines without eating food and this makes me feel so weak. I have resorted to taking my medicine early in the day before I feel hungry because I am not sure whether I will eat in the course of the day since everyday has its own difficulty”. (P (2), Adolescent, 18 years)

Deprivation of family and social support

Lack of family support has been reported to be a major driver to VNS among children and adolescents on DTG, most especially the orphaned children and adolescents. Health workers have most times linked these vulnerable children and adolescents to partner organizations for social support. Health workers have also noted that children and adolescents’ psychosocial issues are rooted into lack of social support.

“…I think if my father was alive, it could have been much better. Some of my peers are lucky to have sponsorship and I think that is good enough for them. Of course there are many of my peers or even young children who are suffering and some are even not studying and this affects their treatment”. (P (8), Adolescent, 19 years)

“…I think if my father was alive, it could have been much better. Some of my peers are lucky to have sponsorship and I think that is good enough for them. “It has not been easy. My support has always come from relatives and when they can’t provide, I live with what I have. Yes at times you feel stressed most especially at school but with the friends I have, they really support me too”. (P (2), Adolescent, 18 years)

Children and adolescents of secondary school age level

This study found out that secondary school education level was associated with VNS and they have been the target for DTG transition and optimization given that they are also soon transitioning from adolescent to adult care + and are already getting independent in terms of managing their treatment. “Those above 10 years are of major concern and in secondary since they are more of independent in care and treatment and pose a major risk to HIV transmission once not suppressed on ART”. (KI (1), Clinical officer, 34 years)

Missing DTG doses as a result of medication fatigue, drug holiday and pill burden

This study revealed major reasons for missing doses as fatigue, drug holiday, forgetfulness and pill burden among the children and adolescents on DTG. The adolescents further admit that the reasons for high viral load among some of them is attributed to missing the pills or not taking them as instructed. This has subsequently led to VNS.

“.There has also been that feeling of tiredness. The reason why I am saying this is because some of us have taken drugs for a very long time. I have always felt tired of my medications and if it wasn’t for the health workers supporting me, I think I would have given up but however their motivation and the need to keep my viral load count low has been a very big push factor”. (P (2), Adolescent, 18 years)

Poor adherence spanning a long time

Key informants revealed in their responses, the issues relating and contributing to adherence among children and adolescents such as stigma, loss of a parent, lack of food as psychosocial issues that over time affect adherence leading to VNS. Key informants further noted that poor viral outcomes have been a predisposition to poor adherence.

“Major reasons for poor viral outcomes has been due to missed adherence and poor adherence since caregivers fail to bring children in time for appointments citing high transports costs and long distances.” (KI (4), Nursing officer, 37 years)

“Major reasons for poor viral outcomes has been due to missed adherence and poor adherence since caregivers fail to bring children in time for appointments citing high transports costs and long distances.” (KI (4), Nursing officer, 37 years)

This study found that depressive states have been identified among children and adolescents impacting on treatment and leading to high viral load as reported by caregivers. They indicated that these mood swings have prevailed since starting the medication. This is what the caregivers had to say;

“Even we adults get depressed. Same with these young ones and for one who keeps wondering why they keep taking drugs while other children don’t, they really get stressed and can even stop taking their medication. There was a time the boy refused to take medicine until I talked to one of the health workers who talked to him often. Unfortunately his viral count had gone high.” (P (4), Caregiver, 47 years)

“Yes. My child always has mood swings. In the morning she can be fine then all of a sudden the mood changes.” (P (9), Caregiver, 40 years)

Information gap on treatment

Both children and adolescents have decried lack of information regarding their treatment from the health workers given the busy clinic appointment schedules. Even parents and caregivers have noted the lack of interface for information sharing and as noted by one of the key informants, this ultimately has a bearing on treatment outcomes as well as leading to VNS. This is what they had to say;

“…As a peer adolescent, most times, I think we have not embraced the information around HIV care and treatment. Most times we are faced with challenges of disclosure among our peers and this is normally when we are in schools. (P (6), Adolescent, 18 years)

“…As a peer adolescent, most times, I think we have not embraced the information around HIV care and treatment. Most times we are faced with challenges of disclosure among our peers and this is normally when we are in schools. (P (6), Adolescent, 18 years)

“…Most times patients expect attention and therefore any sort of missing information or guidance is considered a poor attitude. However this in turn affects their clinical outcome.” (KI (6), Pharmacist, 48 years)

“…Most times patients expect attention and therefore any sort of missing information or guidance is considered a poor attitude. However this in turn affects their clinical outcome.” (KI (6), Pharmacist, 48 years)

“.HIV information should be passed easily in schools since children and adolescents spend most of their time at school and it’s where they interact with their peers.” (KI (6), Pharmacist, 48 years)

Malnutrition

Nutrition is a boost to therapeutic management of HIV and it improves the quality of life and given poor nutrition, it compromises the care and treatment of children and adolescents. This study found lack of access to food and poor feeding as one of the issues surrounding malnutrition with majority of virologically non-suppressed children and adolescents presenting with moderate acute malnutrition.

“.Malnutrition status is highly evident among children with non-suppression.” (KI (2), Medical officer, 45 years)

“.Malnutrition status is highly evident among children with non-suppression.” (KI (2), Medical officer, 45 years)

“Moderate acute malnutrition is a common nutrition status mostly among children less than 10 years. Whether suppressed or non-suppressed, most children and adolescents do not eat a variety of foods and this has been a major cause of malnutrition.” (KI (4), Nursing officer, 37 years.

“Moderate acute malnutrition is a common nutrition status mostly among children less than 10 years. Whether suppressed or non-suppressed, most children and adolescents do not eat a variety of foods and this has been a major cause of malnutrition.” (KI (4), Nursing officer, 37 years.

“Screening for malnutrition is a priority for children and adolescents to identify those likely to be malnourished and this is because over time viral non-suppression is likely because of the body demands for food nutrients.” (KI (3), Enrolled nurse, 28 years)

TB co-infection

Key informants frequently mentioned the pill burden associated with management of TB co-infection in children and adolescents given that the once daily pill dose of DTG will be double coupled with the introduction of anti-TB medications leading to poor adherence hence compromising viral suppression among the children and adolescents. It is also noted that with introduction of anti-TB medication, directly observed therapy is necessary.

“TB co-infection affects both children and adolescents with viral non-suppression given that DTG dose is doubled or put on hold until TB is successfully treated. Most times some of them fail to thrive following TB infection affecting viral suppression.” (KI (1), Clinical officer, 34 years)

“TB co-infection affects both children and adolescents with viral non-suppression given that DTG dose is doubled or put on hold until TB is successfully treated. Most times some of them fail to thrive following TB infection affecting viral suppression.” (KI (1), Clinical officer, 34 years)

“Yes the interaction between DTG and rifampicin has to be put into context and therefore to avoid TB advancing we double the dose of DTG in morning and evening which will surely cause pill burden for some of them hence affecting viral suppression leading non-suppression.” (KI (6), Pharmacist, 48 years)

“Yes the interaction between DTG and rifampicin has to be put into context and therefore to avoid TB advancing we double the dose of DTG in morning and evening which will surely cause pill burden for some of them hence affecting viral suppression leading non-suppression.” (KI (6), Pharmacist, 48 years)

Disease progression to stage II and above

Clinician in ART clinics reported that the paediatric and adolescent ART optimization program started with a number of children having progressed to stage II HIV disease and with some of them already failing on the traditional regimens. They also noted that as children and adolescents advance from stage I to stage II HIV disease, they are likely to present with virological non-suppression. From the perspective of the clinicians, most children and adolescents with virological non-suppression present with signs and symptoms of advanced HIV disease.

“.It should also be noted that by the time children and adolescents were transitioned to DTG, majority of those failing on ART had already progressed to stage II disease with no advanced HIV disease and after 6 months with prolonged with prolonged high viral load, it would be possible for one to advance to stage III or even IV. That is why it is important to put a patient on effective ART regimen earlier.” (KI (2), Medical officer, 45 years)

“.It should also be noted that by the time children and adolescents were transitioned to DTG, majority of those failing on ART had already progressed to stage II disease with no advanced HIV disease and after 6 months with prolonged with prolonged high viral load, it would be possible for one to advance to stage III or even IV. That is why it is important to put a patient on effective ART regimen earlier.” (KI (2), Medical officer, 45 years)

“.Majority of the children and adolescents we have in the clinic presenting with viral non-suppression have been those with advanced HIV disease with repeated high viral load and therefore when assessed clinically, they present with WHO clinical stage II mild symptomatic features such as weight loss, otitis media and pharyngitis.” (KI (3), Enrolled nurse, 28 years)

Discussion

Proportion of virological non-suppression among children and adolescents on DTG-based regimens

This study found that 12 out of 100 children and adolescents were virologically non-suppressed. A lower prevalence compared to earlier studies conducted in Uganda by Maena [30] at 31.4% and Nabukeera [15] at 23% respectively. However this finding shows a gap in achieving the third 95% UNAIDS target towards viral suppression. This relatively low prevalence compared to other studies conducted in Uganda can be attributed to rapid transition from NNRTI-based regimens to INSTI based regimens such as DTG among children and adolescents specifically those aged 5–19 years over the last two years. Other probable reasons are the early adoption of the “Test and Treat” policy of 2013 among children and adolescents in Uganda and enhanced facility models such as facility-based individual management (FBIM), fast track drug refill (FTDR), facility based groups (FBG) and community care and treatment models; Community Client-Led ART Delivery (CCLAD) and Community Drug Distribution Point (CDDP), complimented by Multi-month drug dispensing (MMD) which have enabled early ART initiation, adherence and retention in care and treatment [21, 31].

Our finding is consistent with studies conducted by Fokam [32] in Cameroon which found a 8.2% virological non-suppression and 91.8% viral suppression among children and Nyakato [33] who found a virological non-suppression of 15.5% in South Africa. From a regional viewpoint, the VNS rate is much lower than that of a study done in Tanzania which reported a prevalence of 32.8% among children and adolescents [1].

In comparison to other Sub-Saharan African countries with similar pediatric and adolescent HIV burden, a study done in Ghana found a considerably higher virological non-suppression rate of 38.4% and a precisely similar 12% proportion of adolescents were virally non-suppressed in a study conducted in Namibia [17, 34]. Possible reasons for this variation in proportion of virological non-suppression rates are; differences in HIV management guidelines that also explains variations based on standard copies per milliliter per country as threshold of viral non-suppression and study designs employed in the different studies. These variations can further be explained by the differences in age disaggregation in SSA region constrained by age definition of a child or adolescent and varying data collection modalities as in Uganda where information and data regarding VS outcome among adolescents aged 10–15 years is lamped under the age disaggregation of 0–14 years while adolescents aged 16–19 years are considered under the age disaggregation of adults prompting a challenge in ascertaining comparative virological outcomes of CALWH among different countries [10, 11]. This study found that most patient viral load results still use a threshold of 1000 copies per ml of viral RNA copies for both whole blood and plasma as lower limit for non-suppression contrary to WHO recommendation of 1000 copies per ml when using plasma and 5000 copies per ml when using whole blood or dry blood spot [35, 36]. However some Ugandan studies have assessed viral suppression based on much lower cut-offs than 1000 [37, 38]. This constitutes a policy gap in standardizing non-suppression thresholds for different populations of HIV patients such as children and adolescents since they pose differences in risk of transmission and treatment failure that affects treatment outcomes and epidemic control.

The viral suppression rate of 87.8% in this study is below the UNAIDS 95% target for viral suppression on ART [3]. Despite a relatively low virological non-suppression rate of 12.2% among the children and adolescents, sub-group analysis showed a higher variation in proportion by gender, age group, and education level prompting enhanced efforts towards averting virological non-suppression in the identified groups.

Most notable observation in this study was the inverse linear relationship between proportion of virological non-suppression and months of drug dispensing (multi-month dispensing). Proportion of virological non-suppression decreased with increasing months of drug dispensing. However this was not subjected to significant testing but gives insights to the role of spaced months of dispensing in achieving treatment success among stable children and adolescents. This is consistent with a study in Tanzania which indicated increased likelihood of viral suppression among those who received multi-month prescriptions compared to those who did not receive multi-month prescriptions [39].

This study also found a high proportion of virological non-suppression among those who had disclosed their HIV status compared to those who had not. This is similar to the high proportion reported by Orji [40] in Nigeria. Qualitative findings in this study already highlighted perceived challenges concerning disclosure by caregivers but also those who have fully disclosed their HIV status still show high viral non-suppression rates prompting a possible gap in disclosure mechanisms. According to Orji [40], late disclosure is one of the possible explanations to this phenomena. According to Sisay [41], early disclosure of HIV status to an infected child builds and maintains trust between the child and caregiver further assuring protection against virological non-suppression [42]. This study however doesn’t provide details as to whether the disclosure was assisted or non-assisted or if it was early or late disclosure. Nevertheless the need for disclosure guidelines and mechanisms among caregivers and health workers is justified and it’s also cautious that more studies are done to understand why children and adolescents known to have fully disclosed their HIV status are showing trends of VNS.

Sociodemographic correlates associated with virological non-suppression among children and adolescents on DTG-based regimens

This study further showed that children and adolescents who were single orphans were 5.4 times more likely to be virologically non-suppressed and similarly double orphans were 3.3 times likely to be non-suppressed compared to those who had both parents alive. This is in consistency with a study in South Africa by Hendrickson [43]. However a Ugandan study by Gordon [11] found no statistical association despite a high proportion of adolescents in the study being reported as orphans. Nevertheless, most studies attest that being an orphan is significantly associated to virological failure and this is because orphans are at high risk of non-adherence to ART [44–46]. This is similar to what a respondent reported in this study that as a result of the loss of his parents, his adherence to ART was altered severely. In this study, children and adolescents who testified loss of a parent reported feelings of depression with significant effect on viral suppression. The unmet emotional and psychosocial needs of children and adolescents living with HIV due to death of one or both parents may be the best explanation for this finding. Explanations from interviews further highlighted deprivation of family and social support as a major enabler of VNS as a result of orphan status. Therefore social protection referral systems can be the best probable option in the ART clinics to address social needs of the children and adolescents through mapping and networking the social services referral network of partners, donors and other non-governmental organizations providing social protection services such as household livelihood programs and socio-economic empowerment programs.

Clinical correlates associated with virological non-suppression among children and adolescents on DTG-based regimens

Being on WHO clinical stage II was significantly associated with virological non-suppression. Those in WHO clinical stage II were 1.61 times more likely to be virologically non-suppressed. This is in consistence with earlier studies conducted in Uganda [15, 30]. This is likely because with advancing secondary infections, there is rapid depletion of CD4, impairing the immune system which enables viral replication and high viral load leading to virological non-suppression. This therefore evokes the need for routine screening and diagnosis for advanced HIV disease. An analogous study in Ethiopia also found a significant association between being in clinical stage II and virological non-suppression [47]. However in a study conducted in Uganda by Natukunda [14], there was no association between clinical stage and virological non-suppression. In contrast to this study and studies done in Nigeria [48] and Swaziland [49] that found association between clinical stage III and IV with virological non-suppression, this study had a higher proportion of children and adolescents in clinical stage II and therefore the magnitude of statistical association was not evident in clinical stage III and IV but its logical reasoning that advancing HIV disease is associated with higher odds of non-suppression and treatment failure. In support of findings in the quantitative analysis, key informants confirmed that disease progression was a limiting factor to viral suppression and as in the case of children and adolescents aged 5–19 years on DTG, they acknowledged that majority of those who were optimized or transitioned to DTG were already in stage II disease and switched to second line regimens. Consequently it is highly recommended that children and adolescents should be prioritized just as adult population whenever new interventions are rolled out to maximize benefits towards better viral outcomes. This could be the reason why children and adolescent population lag behind in achieving UNAIDS global targets.

Furthermore, Moderate acute malnutrition (MAM) was associated with virological non-suppression. This is because malnutrition increases the risk of HIV pathogenesis, while HIV in turn induces malnutrition by reducing nutrient intake, absorption and metabolism which negatively affects the immune system [50–52]. Previous studies have also identified malnutrition as a risk factor for attrition from care and treatment among children on ART [53–55]. The finding is unswerving with that in Ethiopia that reported malnourished children were less likely to achieve viral suppression [56]. A Ugandan study reported that not being diagnosed with malnutrition was significantly associated with viral suppression [11]. Similarly in Tanzania, Maghembe [39] in consistency with Gordon [11], also stated that children and adolescents on DTG-based regimens with severe malnutrition were less likely to achieve viral suppression.

Medication fatigue, pill burden, and drug holiday leading to missed doses and non-adherence were potential enablers to VNS according to the respondents despite trying to maintain adherent behavior. Studies in Uganda and SSA have linked pill burden to three dimensions which include quantity, taste and challenges of taking pills at different social settings [57–59]. However in our study, respondents spoke to the challenge of not having food and among those on advanced HIV disease management such as TB which requires doubling the dosage of DTG medication. This eventually leads to drug holidays and significantly increases the risk of developing drug resistance to the ART regimen [58]. Efforts are in play to address these challenges in Uganda with a wide range of biomedical interventions under pilot study such as reduction in number of pills and frequency of doses which is now evident with DTG-based regimens and long acting injectable under clinical trials [60]. This may however take long to be approved and recommended among children and adolescents as already evidenced with previous interventions [58].

Poor adherence was exacerbated by the impact of several barriers. According to respondents, poor adherence is complicated by social challenges and non-disclosure among the children and adolescents. For example several of the aforementioned studies have attributed not having a biological parent or caregiver and children whose caregiver was the only one who knew their HIV status were more likely to report poor adherence [45, 59, 61]. As much as lack of food is the most predominant psychosocial barrier surrounding poor adherence, health workers still have a significant role in averting non-adherence through timely intensive adherence counseling sessions and bridging the information gap between the clinician and the caregiver. In pursuit of improved adherence, interventions such as intensive home based case management model [62], linkage to social services and retention in care [63], community base adherence support [64], and adolescent peer support [65], have proven to be effective. While key informants did not explicitly raise the role of caregivers in adherence support, the few who did emphasized the need to equip caregivers with much information regarding pediatric and adolescent-based HIV care and treatment.

Key informants noted how TB co-infection progressively leads to VNS. They acknowledge that with increased frequency of DTG doses when on intermittent preventive therapy (IPT) and when on active TB treatment there is a high likelihood of VNS among children and adolescents. This is consistent with studies that have shown HIV/TB co-infected individuals to have a high likelihood of low CD4 count, advanced clinical stage and poor treatment outcome [66, 67]. The increased pill burden further leads to drug-drug interaction hence affecting efficacy, safety and adherence [68]. However, a Kenyan study by Kadima [69] reported that children and adolescents with a history of TB co-infection had a better viral outcome. The best possible reason for this could be the directly observed therapy that is associated with TB treatment and management, high frequency of clinical visits as pre-requisite to follow-up at intensive phase of treatment and intensive adherence support. Despite this study not finding any statistical association between TB co-infection and VNS, several studies in SSA found a close link and association between TB co-infection and VNS among children and adolescents [17, 70]. This non-statistical association in this study could be a result of the low proportion of children and adolescents who reported having been TB co-infected and virally non-suppressed.

Last but not least, interviews identified depression to be a significant challenge leading to VNS among children and adolescents with caregivers and adolescents reporting having had moments of depression that affected their treatment routine. Dires, Manyazewal and Charlotte [20] found a high level of VNS among sufficiently adhered adolescents, acknowledging the presence of independent risk correlates to adherence such as depression. Several studies have also found depression to be a predictor of non-adherence facilitated by suicidal ideation and despair [71–74]. In reference to the RV254/SEARCH 010 Research Team [75] in evaluating neuropsychiatric outcomes before and after switch to DTG, no major depressive symptoms and neuropsychiatric adverse events were reported. In contradiction to the RV254/SEARCH 010 Research Team [75], a recent study by Turkova [76] found that more participants had psychiatric events and reported suicidal ideations in the DTG group than the standard of care group and recommended clinicians and policy makers to consider suicidality screening among children and adolescents on DTG-based regimens. In contrast to findings by Turkova [76], very few studies have evaluated the neuropsychiatric symptoms among children and adolescents on DTG in clinical practice. Nonetheless, children and adolescents with perinatal HIV infection are more likely to have neuropsychiatric symptoms influenced by long term chronic disease management, psychosocial stressors, stigma and neurocognitive impact of HIV with most common disorders including anxiety, depression, behavioral disorders, learning disorders and attention deficit hyperactivity disorder [77]. Therefore, it is overriding for healthcare workers to be trained on integrating psychiatric screening into HIV care services and referrals to appropriate services where available in order to improve adherence, engagement in care, neurocognitive development and social relationships.

Health facility-related correlates of virological non-suppression among children and adolescents on DTG-based regimens

The study findings showed that pediatric and adolescent ART management is contextual and reliant on the health facility’s strategy of achieving successful treatment outcomes in such a way that the health workers’ efforts can be easily translated into the treatment outcomes. It emerged that the facility in its processes can be deterrent and enabling towards VNS. Training and capacity building on pediatric and adolescent ART management, availability of adolescent and child friendly services, health worker attitude, retention in care, waiting time and long distances all emerged as important attributes to VNS.

The study found that training and capacity building contributed to the downward trend in non-suppression among children and adolescents which implies that with more on-site mentorships, health workers will be in position to address challenges associated with pediatric and adolescent ART management. Wagner [78] assessed the impact of systems analysis and improvement approach through training on pediatric and adolescents HIV care cascade and concluded that this training strategy was more on health system performance indicators than HIV care and treatment services. However, a study in Uganda suggested that training of health workers in adolescent health service delivery may address the existing challenges among this age group [14].

In identifying the role of adolescent and child friendly services, the study found that adolescent and child friendly services are an attraction to both children and adolescent in care and those not yet enrolled in care but also closed the information gap as well as retention in care in regard to HIV among this age group. As such, adult clinics do not offer services that are as friendly as pediatric and adolescent clinics since children and adolescents are not yet ready to be responsible of their own health [79–82]. This finding is consistent with studies that showed; scaling up child and adolescent friendly services have a greater impact on uptake of health services among children and adolescents [62, 83, 84].

Furthermore health worker attitude plays a significant role in health outcomes of children and adolescents. Studies have attributed stigma and discrimination among HIV patients as a result of health worker attitude, behavior and lack of knowledge [85, 86]. The best possible explanation to this challenge may be lack of supervision structures and client satisfactory surveys to bridge the gap between the health workers and patients.

Additionally, results indubitably reveal the need for scaling up retention efforts among children and adolescents. Adolescents and caregivers attest that clinic reminders and follow-ups were not done routinely. Meanwhile this may be as result of financial constraints on the side of the implementing mechanisms as highlighted by the key informants. In Uganda, regional referral hospitals have transitioned into HIV care programs christened G2G or Government to Government funded by USAID and therefore budget limitations were noted in the first phase of the transition. The findings of this study is in congruence to a study in South Africa which revealed that the odds of viral suppression was significantly lower among those not retained in care [87]. Similarly, another study in South Africa by Van Liere [18] also found virological non - suppression to be associated with a high loss to care. It is therefore imperative that HIV programs at local or donor funded mechanisms allocate substantive resources towards retention activities such as follow-ups, appointment tracking, home visits for the emaciated, loss to follow (Lost for 30 days), Lost to follow –up (Lost for 90 days) and many others. This is because retention in care is a precursor to better adherence to ART and achieving viral suppression while reducing VNS [88].

We also found long distance to health facility to be a facilitator of VNS. Findings from interviews perceived long distances and poor retention to facilitate poor adherence among children and adolescents. Adolescents and caregivers on the other hand decry walking long distances to the health facility but also coupled with long waiting hours in the clinic. They further admit that missing clinic appointments is attributed to transport challenges long waiting times at the clinic in a bid to drop in on a non-clinic day which subsequently affects adherence and leads to viral non-suppression. Studies have shown that long travel distance to the health facility and lack of transport money have an effect on clinic appointments and subsequently affect adherence [89, 90]. However, a study in Tanzania did not find an association between distance to health facilities and virological non-suppression [70]. Nevertheless a similar study in Kenya, found a significant difference in viral suppression by distance to health facility with virological non-suppression associated with longer distance [91]. As suggested by the key informants, there is a need for scaling up and improving uptake to differentiated service delivery approaches such as Community Drug Distribution Points (CDDP) and Community Client Led ART Distribution (CCLAD) and multi-month dispensing for clinically stable children and adolescents to bring services closer to the patient.

Lastly, the results also emphasize the need for the facility to improve on its clinic waiting time and consequently patient satisfaction, clinic appointments and comprehensive care. The in-depth and key informant interviews reported long waiting time as a result of delays in clinic processes that affects the need for one to follow their specific appointment dates and would rather come to the clinic on days they suspect no queues in the clinic. This finding is consistent with a study in Uganda by Oryokot [92] who found prolonged facility waiting time as a barrier to viral suppression. However this finding is in contradiction with a finding by Brown, Malagala and Bajunirwe [13] who suggested that longer waiting time is more likely to facilitate viral suppression compared to those who waited for a shorter time possibly due to chances of interactions with peers and friends, calling for more research. The patient load due to congestion arises from the inflexibility of health workers to utilize the differentiated models of care and treatment but also due to non-randomization of clinic appointments. Earlier studies in Bauchi, Nigeria by Umar, Hajara and Khalifa [93] and three HIV clinics in Uganda by Wanyenze [94] attributed patient-doctor ratio to long waiting times and therefore recommended task shifting to be the best possible approach towards addressing issues related to few health workers such as doctors and nurses in a clinic. Contextual to Uganda, Wanyenze [94], recommended effective triage and long visiting intervals as remedies that could improve patient flow. Considering these recommendations highlighted above and those proposed by key informants such as MMD, community models and facility fast track, they provide indubitably practicable strategies towards reducing clinic congestions and long waiting time in ART clinics.

Strength and limitations

The major strength of this study was adequacy of its sample size, a pivot to generalizability of its findings. Furthermore, the logical approach to this study and its setting adds to its generalizability in resource limited settings given that the study setting manages an immense majority of children and adolescents on DTG-based regimens in the region. The use of clinical and programmatic data in the quantitative phase provided credible and reliable information since it revealed the true state of affairs on ground. In addition, the cross-sectional nature of the study provided robustness in the undertaking with qualitative and quantitative components complementing each other, hence overall credibility of the findings.

We did not consider those who were lost to follow-up while on DTG given their interruption in care and treatment and therefore it could have biased the primary outcome since treatment interruption is known to obviously cause unprecedented rise in HIV viral copies and then subsequently bias the study outcome. The study addresses the critical issues relating to protecting DTG based regimens from primary resistance associated with VNS given the paucity in pediatric and adolescents ART regimens, reaching the 95% UNAIDS target of viral suppression and epidemic control among the 0–19 years since they are a high risk population transitioning from the child and adolescent sub-group to adult age category and without viral suppression as prevention towards transmission, the significant gain in epidemic control among the adult population will not be sustained if children and adolescents transitioning to adult care are virologically non-suppressed.

This study relied on self-reported data that may have been affected by recall and social desirability bias [95]. Recall bias was however ameliorated by triangulation of data from different sources. The cross sectional nature of the study limited the founding of causal relationships of correlates associated with virological non-suppression (Antecedent-consequent bias). Given the nature of mixed methods sequential explanatory design, findings from the qualitative data does not provide for statistical generalization but sets ground for further studies. Furthermore, the study limited its understanding of correlates of virological non-suppression among children and adolescents on DTG-based regimens to its context, potentially limit its applicability to other settings. The study didn’t consider ascertaining statistical association between health facility correlates and virologically non-suppression but examined the correlates qualitatively given that it was limited to Gulu regional referral and didn’t consider other health facilities for comparability of health facility correlates and generalizability of findings. It was therefore recommended that the study findings are replicated in other health facilities.

The key informants and in-depth interview participants declined to consent for voice recording and therefore the qualitative data was manually collected by documenting responses. This significantly affected the time taken to conduct the qualitative interviews but also for ascertaining clarity during analysis. Missing data variables for exposures and outcome were frequently realized leading to exclusion of CALWH into the study due to incompleteness of critical information. However validation using secondary tools such as electronic Uganda EMR (Electronic Medical Records) and making phone calls to care givers was sufficient coupled with the already established non-response of 10.3%, this didn’t affect the sample size and recruitment into the study as subsequent files were reviewed to meet the study sample size.

Finally this study did not consider virological non-suppression by DTG fixed dose combination regimen in the quantitative analysis which could have been an important consideration for bias in the form of a confounder or effect modifier given that the child or adolescent’s viral outcome may be predicted by the DTG fixed dose combinations.

Implications

The implication of virological non-suppression among children and adolescents aged 5–19 years on DTG-based regimens is linked to the existent paucity of pediatric and adolescent ART regiments as a result of primary drug resistance which might limit the progress towards achieving the third 95% UNAIDS target for viral suppression given that this a population transitioning to adult care and therefore the already significant milestone in viral suppression and epidemic control in the adult population may not be sustained. Systematic reviews show that despite DTG being commended as highly effective and safe among CALWH [10, 96–98], available data indicates the emergence of resistance to HIV-1 integrase inhibitors specifically to DTG treatment in pediatric population [99–101]. It is also evident that integrase resistance can emerge in patients treated with co-formulated DTG/lamivudine (3TC) and therefore there is need to monitor drug to drug interaction to avoid virological failure as a result of genotypic resistance [102]. Cognizant with other causes of virological non-suppression, there is need for concerted efforts towards averting virological non-suppression in this population.

The following recommendation could help in averting virological non-suppression among children and adolescents in the advent of DTG-based regimens; Establish accelerated social support-based programs and mechanisms to avert stigma and discrimination among orphaned children and adolescents. Ensure children and adolescent HIV/AIDS programs are strengthened through sustainable food security and food support interventions at clinic and household levels, so as to alleviate malnutrition which is a modifiable risk factor associated with virological non-suppression. HIV/TB intervention studies and roll-out should prioritize children and adolescents alongside adult population to maximize benefit towards children and adolescents as in the case of DTG transition where children and adolescents lagged behind adults with majority already in clinical stage II disease. This could be the reason why children and adolescent population holdup behind in achieving UNAIDS global targets since most interventions take priority in the adult population on inception.

Determining and standardizing non-suppression thresholds for different sample methods and population groups such as children and adolescents in contrast to WHO recommendations due to differences in sensitivity of testing methods, risk of transmission, treatment failure trends and detectable viraemia (Low –level viraemia and viral blips) among children and adolescents will ensure comparability in viral suppression outcomes among age categories of 0–9, 10–14 and 15–19. The need for HIV disclosure guidelines and mechanisms will ensure supported and full HIV status disclosure and limit virological non-suppression among those who have disclosed and believed to be living positively with zero stigma.

Strengthening sustainable food security and food support interventions at clinic and household levels will alleviate malnutrition which is a modifiable risk factor among children and adolescents with VNS. HIV/TB intervention studies and roll-out should prioritize children and adolescents alongside adult population to maximize benefit towards children and adolescents as in the case of DTG transition where children and adolescents lagged behind adults with majority already in clinical stage II disease. This could be the reason why children and adolescent population holdup behind in achieving UNAIDS global targets since most interventions take priority in the adult population on inception.

Establish and document national HIV disclosure guidelines and mechanisms to realize supported and full disclosure in order to limit VNS among those who have disclosed and believed to be living positively with zero stigma. A time series analysis (survival analysis) to establish time duration to virological non-suppression among children and adolescents on integrase inhibitor based regimens such as DTG even if it has been dubbed a “forgiving drug”. This will guide and provide justification to health workers on timeliness of interventions among this age group such as IAC, screening for advanced HIV disease (AHD), timely bleeding and retrieving of results for HIV viral load, adherence monitoring and follow-up efforts for those lost to care at 30 and 90 days. It would be worthwhile to replicate this study in other high volume settings.

Conclusion

The prevalence of virological non-suppression among children and adolescents aged 5–19 years on Dolutegravir-based regimens was 12.2%. This was relatively lower in comparison to other studies conducted in Uganda and other Sub-Saharan African countries but still translates to a 12.2% shortfall in achieving the UNAIDS third 95% target for viral suppression among CALWH aged 5–19 years. This calls for concerted efforts towards achieving the third 95% UNAIDS target among this young and high-risk population with subsequent epidemic control by 2030, given that universal transition and viral suppression to Dolutegravir-based regimens is sustained.
