Dynamics of bioactive compounds and antioxidant activity of Guazuma ulmifolia Lam. fruits and pulp rheology throughout development
Authors: Gilson Gustavo Lucinda Machado, Amanda Aparecida de Lima Santos, Natália de Oliveira Souza, Elda Eller, Carlos Henrique Milagres Ribeiro, Ana Cristina Freitas de Oliveira Meira, Jaime Vilela de Resende, Elisângela Elena Nunes Carvalho, Eduardo Valério de Barros Vilas Boas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGuazuma ulmifolia Lam. (Malvaceae) is widely found throughout Latin America, especially in Brazil and Mexico, and its fruit, popularly known as mutamba, exhibit high bioactive potential. This study aimed to investigate the physicochemical, bioactive compounds, and antioxidant activity changes of G. ulmifolia fruit throughout their development, as well as the rheological properties of the hydro-homogenate of their pulp. For this purpose, fruit at beginning of development, immature-green, mature-green, and ripe stages were analyzed for pH, titratable acidity, soluble solids, total phenolics (Folin–Ciocalteu, Fast-Blue), flavonoids, antioxidant activity (phosphomolybdenum, ABTS, and β-carotene), and profiles of phenolic and alkaloids compounds. A hydro-homogenate of the pulp was prepared, and its properties were analyzed using a viscometer and Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR). The results indicated significant changes during fruit development. There was an increase in titratable acidity and soluble solids, accompanied by a reduction in pH, while levels of total phenolic compounds, flavonoids, and antioxidant activity decreased, possibly due to their conversion into other secondary metabolites. Chromatographic analysis revealed the presence of 11 compounds, with trigonelline, gallic acid, and resveratrol standing out, highlighting the functional potential of these fruits. Furthermore, the fluids obtained after pulp homogenization in water exhibited pseudoplastic behavior, with decreasing viscosity as the shear rate and temperature increased, reflecting structural changes in the cell walls. Rheological models and FTIR analyses confirmed alterations in polysaccharides, while multivariate analysis emphasized significant correlations between phenolic compounds, antioxidant activity, and viscosity. These findings suggest the potential of G. ulmifolia Lam. fruit for food and pharmaceutical applications.Keywords: Mutamba, Bioactive compounds, Brazilian fruit, Malvaceae, Phenolic compoundsSubject terms: Biochemistry, Chemistry, Plant sciences

Introduction

Guazuma ulmifolia Lam., popularly known as embireira and mutambeira1 belongs to the family Malvaceae, which includes 250 genera with pantropical distribution and is found predominantly in Latin America, especially in Brazil and Mexico2. According to these authors, this plant is widely used in traditional medicine by various cultures and tribes in countries such as Mexico, Brazil, Bolivia, Cuba, Honduras, Ecuador, and Guatemala.

The plant may take on herbaceous, shrubby, or arboreal forms, with leaves arranged alternately, which can be simple or compound and are accompanied by stipules3. According to the same authors, its flowers are small or showy, arranged in a dichasial pattern, cyclic, hermaphroditic, pentamerous, and generally exhibit radial symmetry. The fruit, known as mutambo, mutamba, fruit of the monkey, and embira, are dry, dehiscent and spherical, with a diameter that usually does not exceed three centimeters. The epicarp is leathery and irregular, full of eruptions that can reach up to 1 mm in height, displaying a green color in young fruits and a dark color in ripe ones. The mesocarp is also leathery and thin, measuring approximately 1 mm thick. The endocarp, which proportionally represents the largest volume of the fruit, is firm, light-colored, and filled with locules where dozens of seeds are formed.

The proximate composition of G. ulmifolia fruits has already been reported in the literature, with ranges varying from 11 to 18.6% for moisture, 3.18 to 8.4% for proteins, 1.3 to 2.6% for lipids, 2.7 to 15.9% for ash, 26.9 to 49.9% for fiber, and 23.6 to 55.7% for non-fiber carbohydrates4–6. The fruit has been identified as a source of bioactive compounds such as proanthocyanidins and flavonoids2. Fruit composition depends on the translocation of photoassimilates from the leaves and changes throughout its development, which can be summarized as formation, growth, maturation, and senescence. Thus, changes in fruit composition, such as those reported above by the different authors, may be attributed to the developmental stage of the fruits, as well as to genotypic effects and the different edaphoclimatic conditions to which the plants were exposed.

In Latin American populations, the decoction of the stem bark, fruits, and leaves of G. ulmifolia is traditionally used to treat gastrointestinal and cardiovascular disorders2. According to these authors, this practice, rooted in traditional knowledge, is supported by in vitro and in vivo studies that demonstrate the species’ antimicrobial, antioxidant, antiprotozoal, antidiarrheal, and cardioprotective activities.

Although the proven bioactive effects of mutamba fruits are mainly associated with phenolic compounds7, current literature is still limited regarding the evolution of these secondary metabolites during fruit development. In addition, no studies exist on the rheological properties of homogenized pulps, which are important for the potential use of the fruits by the food and pharmaceutical industries. Given these gaps, the objective of this study was to investigate the physicochemical changes, bioactive compounds, and antioxidant activity, as well as the profile of phenolic compounds and alkaloids in G. ulmifolia fruits, in addition to the rheological properties of the fluid obtained by homogenizing the pulp in water throughout its development.

Materials and methods

Experimental design

The experiment was conducted in a completely randomized design consisting of 4 treatments (developmental stages) and 6 replications.

Plant material

G. ulmifolia Lam. (Malvaceae) fruits were harvested from wild specimens located at the Federal University of Lavras (UFLA), Minas Gerais, Brazil (latitude 21° 14’ 43” S, longitude 44° 59’ 59” W, altitude 919 m). The species was identified by Antônio Massensini Júnior, Vice-curator of the ESAL Herbarium (Biologist - CRBio 93965/04-D, MSc. in Plant Biology). A voucher specimen was deposited in the ESAL Herbarium under the identification number ESAL 34,041.

Fruits were collected in June 2023 in the municipality of Lavras, Minas Gerais, Brazil. The harvest was conducted in a single period to minimize environmental variability (seasonality, precipitation, and temperature), taking advantage that G. ulmifolia simultaneously presents flowers and fruits at different developmental stages on the same tree8. The classification of developmental stages was performed based on size, peel color, dimensions, firmness to the touch, and physicochemical variables (soluble solids, pH, and titratable acidity, presented in Table 1). To ensure representativeness, six trees were selected, each one considered a biological replication.

The fruits were categorized into four distinct developmental stages, designated as S1 (at beginning of development), S2 (immature-green), S3 (mature-green), and S4 (ripe) (Table 1; Fig. 1). Approximately 600 g of fruits were collected from different positions on each tree. Fruits showing signs or indications of pathogens, pests, or defects were discarded. Analyses

pH, titratable acidity (TA), and soluble solids (SS)

Samples were homogenized in water at a ratio of 1:10 (m/v), filtered through organza, and the filtrate was used for pH, TA, and SS determinations. The pH was measured using a TECNAL® pH meter previously calibrated with buffer solutions (pH 4.0 and 7.0). TA was determined by titration with 0.1 N sodium hydroxide (NaOH) solution, using phenolphthalein as an indicator, according to AOAC9. The results were expressed as mg of citric acid 100 g− 1 sample. Soluble solids were determined using an ATAGO PR-100 digital refractometer (Tokyo, Japan) with automatic temperature compensation, and the results were expressed as %, as described by AOAC9.

Total phenolic compounds and antioxidant activity

Preparation of extracts

This procedure was carried out as described by Waterhouse10, and the extracts obtained were used for the analyses of total phenolics and antioxidant activity.

Total phenolics

Folin-Ciocalteu method

The total phenolic content was determined using the Folin-Ciocalteu method11, and the results were expressed as mg GAE 100 g− 1 fresh sample.

Fast blue method

Performed as described by Medina12, and the results were expressed as mg GAE 100 g− 1 fresh sample.

Antioxidant activity

Phosphomolybdenum complex

The analysis was performed as described by Prieto, Pineda, and Aguilar13, with adaptations to the volumes of reagent and sample used in the reaction. The results were expressed as mg of ascorbic acid 100 g− 1 fresh sample.

ABTS

Conducted as described by Auzanneau et al.14, and the results were expressed as % ABTS reduction, using Eq. 1.

β-Carotene/linoleic acid

Determined as described by Rufino et al.15, with modifications to reagent and sample volumes, adapted for use in microplates. Results were expressed as the percentage of oxidation inhibition.

Flavonoids

Flavonoid content was determined using the method adapted from Arvouet-Grand et al.16, and the results were expressed as mg quercetin 100 g− 1 sample.

Phenolic and alkaloid profile

Phenolic compounds were extracted from 2.5 g of sample homogenized in 20 mL of 70% methanol (v/v), HPLC grade, and kept in an ultrasonic bath at room temperature for 1 h. The resulting extract was then centrifuged at 8832 g for 15 min at 4 °C, and subsequently filtered through quantitative filter paper with 25 μm pore size. Before injection, the extracts were filtered again using a 13 mm diameter, 0.22 μm pore membrane filter. The identification and quantification of phenolics were carried out at the Chemical Analysis and Prospecting Center of the Federal University of Lavras using a high-performance liquid chromatograph (HPLC-DAD-UV-Vis), Shimadzu model, composed of a quaternary pump LC-20AT, degasser DGU-20A5 (serial no. L20244808404), injector SIL-20 A (serial no. L20164503197), controller CBM-20 A (serial no. L20234505269), oven CTO-20AC (serial no. L20214503287), detector SPDM-20 A (serial no. L20154503047), detector RID-10 A (serial no. C20934806770), and fraction collector FRC-10 A (serial no. C20374504580). The analysis parameters were: oven temperature 35 °C; Shim-pack VP-ODS column (250 mm × 4.6 mm × 5 μm); and Shim-pack GVP-ODS pre-column (10 mm × 4.6 mm × 5 μm). The mobile phase consisted of 2% (v/v) glacial acetic acid in type 1.5.10.2 water (mobile phase A), and 70:28:2% (v/v) methanol/water/acetic acid (mobile phase B), at a flow rate of 1.0 mL min⁻¹, using a 65-minute gradient elution program. Injection volume was 20 µL, and phenolic compounds were detected at 280 nm. Standard solutions were prepared by diluting trigonelline, theobromine, catechin, vanillin, resveratrol, and the following acids: gallic, chlorogenic, caffeic, syringic, p-, o-, and m-coumaric, ferulic, rosmarinic, and trans-cinnamic, in methanol. Calibration curves were obtained from injections of ten different concentrations of each standard, in duplicate. Phenolic compounds were identified by comparing the retention times of sample peaks with those of the available standards. Results were expressed as mg 100 g− 1 fresh sample.

Viscosity analysis of the fluid obtained from mutamba pulp

The rheological behavior of mutamba pulp hydro-homogenates was evaluated at different ripening stages and at two temperatures (5 °C and 25 °C). For this purpose, five grams of pulp were homogenized in 45 mL of distilled water using a Polytron-type tissue homogenizer. The homogenate was centrifuged at 5300 g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was used for viscosity analysis. Part of the supernatant was lyophilized and used for Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy analysis.

Viscosity was measured five times per replication using a concentric tube rotational viscometer (Brookfield DVIII Ultra, Brookfield Engineering Laboratories, USA). Spindle SC4-31 and an increasing ramp with a shear rate ranging from 5.1 s− 1 to 71.40 s− 1 were used for the analyses. The Power Law model (Eq. 2) was fitted to the shear stress and shear rate data to determine the flow behavior of the fluids17.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy

The hydro-homogenate obtained was frozen at − 75 °C for 24 h in an ultra-freezer (Coldlab CL 120–86 V, Brazil) and dried in an Edwards freeze dryer (L4KR, Brazil) at − 30 °C under a vacuum pressure of 0.998 mbar for 72 h. The samples were analyzed by Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy using the attenuated total reflectance (ATR) technique on a Bruker FTIR spectrophotometer (Vertex 70, USA). The infrared region analyzed ranged from 4000 cm− 1 to 500 cm− 1, with 64 scans and a resolution of 4 cm− 1.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses of the evaluated variables were performed using the SISVAR software18. The results were subjected to analysis of variance, considering the F-test at a 5% significance level. When significant differences were detected, the means were compared using Tukey’s test, also at the 5% significance level.

The results of the physicochemical analyses of the mutamba pulp fluids were autoscaled and subjected to principal component analysis (PCA) and hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA). The Chemoface software (version 1.61, Brazil) was used to perform these analyses19. The figures were edited using Inkscape software (version 0.92.4, USA). Pearson correlations (p < 0.05) among the parameters were estimated with Jamovi software (version 2.3.17).

Results and discussion

Physicochemical variables, total phenolics, and antioxidant activity

An increase in soluble solids (SS) and titratable acidity (TA) was observed, from 8 to 32% and from 5.78 to 11.53 mg 100 g− 1, respectively, along with a reduction in pH from 6.08 to 5.00 during fruit development (p < 0.05, Fig. 2a–c). The SS and TA levels remained stable in the fruits during the first two developmental stages (S1 and S2), when fruits were immature, and increased significantly from S3 onward, when fruits entered the ripening phase, with the most pronounced rise occurring during maturation (S4). On the other hand, pH decreased between S1 and S2 and between S3 and S4, remaining stable between S2 and S3.

The increase in SS and TA, more pronounced in the final developmental stage (S4), can be related to the increase in sugars and organic acids, and consequently, the respective sweetness and acidity of the fruit during ripening. The accumulation of sugars may occur directly through translocation from the leaves to the fruits and their interconversion, as well as through the hydrolysis of starch possibly accumulated in the early stages of development. Famiani et al.20, in their review on organic acid accumulation in fruits, corroborate their synthesis during development and point to the Krebs cycle as the main formation pathway. The increase observed in TA was accompanied by decreasing in pH, although the values point to a low-acid fruit.

Additionally, these variables are strongly influenced by water loss, as the fruit becomes increasingly dry21. This intense dehydration (from 71.14% to 14.11%) leads to the concentration of solutes and organic acids, resulting in a reduction in pH. This loss of moisture is a determining factor for the fruit pulp’s rheological properties; the concentration of mucilaginous polysaccharides and structural fibers during natural drying explains the observed viscosity transition, giving the fruit pulp the characteristics of a dense and stable matrix. These changes directly affect palatability and reveal the nutritional relevance of the mutamba. The high SS content in stage S4 indicates a high energy density, which is uncommon for many wild fruits, suggesting its potential for the development of functional ingredients, such as concentrated syrups. Although Vasconcelos et al.21 reported distinct variations in absolute terms for pH (4.98 to 4.70) and TA (5.42 to 16.99 mg 100 g-¹), the behavioral trend is similar, with the divergences likely associated with genetic and edaphoclimatic factors.

The total phenolic content, determined by the Folin-Ciocalteu and Fast Blue methods, decreased throughout fruit development by approximately 63% and 75%, respectively (p < 0.05; Fig. 2d-e). This reduction suggests the participation of these compounds as precursors in the synthesis of secondary metabolites derived from phenylpropanoids22. Physiologically, this decrease may be associated with the hardening of the mutamba’s pericarp during maturation, a process in which phenolics are polymerized to provide mechanical protection or become covalently bound to the cell wall. These interactions occur through ester and ether bonds to insoluble macromolecules, such as cellulose, hemicellulose, structural proteins, and pectin23.

In ripe fruits, the average total phenolic content determined by the Folin-Ciocalteu and Fast Blue methods was 410.04 and 1230.39 mg GAE 100 g− 1 (Fig. 2d-e), respectively. The higher averages observed across the four developmental stages when using the Fast Blue method can be explained by its greater specificity compared to the Folin-Ciocalteu method. Vasconcelos et al.21 recorded a total phenolic content of 645.14 mg 100 g− 1 using the Folin-Ciocalteu method, a higher average than that observed in the present study for the same determination method. Factors such as genotype, edaphoclimatic conditions, the actual developmental stage, and the execution of the analytical method may justify these discrepancies. However, from a nutritional standpoint, maintaining significant phenolic levels even at the ripe stage (S4) is a relevant finding. These compounds are widely recognized for their antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, as well as their potential in modulating metabolic diseases within the context of human health7.

Despite the reduction observed during ripening, mutamba fruits exhibit total phenolic levels higher than those of red-skin peanuts, which have values of 325.87 and 1121.67 mg GAE 100 g− 1 using the Folin and Fast methods, respectively24. These levels also exceed those found in several Cerrado fruits analyzed by the Folin method, such as buriti (90.24 mg GAE 100 g− 1), cagaita (143.81 mg GAE 100 g− 1), cajá (58.27 mg GAE 100 g− 1), and coquinho (173.49 mg GAE 100 g− 1)25. This quantitative superiority positions the mutamba as a promising source of bioactive compounds, with potential for exploration in functional applications within the food industry, aiming the increase of antioxidant capacity of processed products7.

Similar to what was observed for total phenolics, a significant reduction in total flavonoid content was found throughout fruit development, at approximately 50.26% (p < 0.05, Fig. 2f). This pattern is similar to those described for other fruits such as blueberry (Vaccinium corymbosum L.), strawberry guava (Psidium cattleianum Sabine), gabiroba (Campomanesia xanthocarpa O. Berg.), and uvaia (Eugenia uvalha C.)26,27. Flavonoids are phenolic compounds consisting of two aromatic rings with a C6C3C6 structure. While the C6C3 structure is derived from the shikimate pathway via the phenylpropanoid route, the second ring originates from the condensation of three malonyl-CoA molecules. Physiologically, the reduction of flavonoids during maturation suggests their metabolic redirection, for example toward the synthesis of condensed tannins, which play crucial roles in defending against pathogens in the final stages of development22,28. On the other hand, this reduction may also be associated with the degradation of anthocyanins. However, the presence of tanins and anthocyanins was not studied in this work.

Furthermore, the retention of about half of the initial flavonoid content in the ripe fruit holds significant nutritional relevance. These compounds are known for their high free-radical scavenging capacity, contributing to the functional value of mutamba7. From an industrial standpoint, the presence of these residual flavonoids can assist in the oxidative stability of derived products, such as flours or extracts, reinforcing the potential of mutamba as a functional ingredient, resilient to the maturation process6.

Bioactive compounds, such as phenolics, act as antioxidants by scavenging free radicals and combating cellular stress and premature aging. Therefore, the quantification of phenolics provides an indication of the antioxidant potential of plant materials. However, there are specific methods to determine antioxidant activity, the responses of which depend on the composition of the plant matrix. Ideally, antioxidant activity should be evaluated by at least three methodologies, as performed in this study, to elicit a broader view of the antioxidant potential of the product evaluated.

An increase was noted in the antioxidant activity measured by the Phosphomolybdenum and β-carotene/linoleic acid methods between S1 and S2, followed by a decrease in S3 and stability until S4 (p < 0.05; Fig. 2g, i). On the other hand, the antioxidant activity measured by the ABTS method remained stable between S1 and S2, dropping in the subsequent stages (p < 0.05; Fig. 2h). Regardless of the method used, a decrease in antioxidant activity was observed as the fruits entered the maturation stage, coinciding with the transition from S2 to S3. This reduction parallels the drop in total phenolic concentration (Fig. 2d, e). Similar behavior between phenolics and antioxidant activity is commonly observed, as these compounds are the primary phytochemicals responsible for antioxidant activity in fruits and vegetables29. This suggests that the consumption or polymerization of these compounds during maturation reduces the availability of free hydroxyl groups to react with radicals. Physiologically, a higher antioxidant activity in the early stages (S1 and S2) may be associated with a need for greater protection of young tissues against oxidative damage and environmental stresses during the phase of intense cell division7.

Despite the downward trend in the antioxidant activity of G. ulmifolia Lam. fruits throughout development, the averages obtained in ripe fruits are still relevant, indicating that the mutamba retains a robust antioxidant matrix even after tissue senescence. For example, the antioxidant activity by the Phosphomolybdenum method was equivalent to 654.66 mg 100 g− 1 of ascorbic acid, a value significantly higher than that found in ripe passion fruit pulp (Passiflora edulis Sims) (119.22 ± 1.76 mg 100 g− 1 ascorbic acid)30. Meanwhile, the antioxidant activity of approximately 30% in ripe mutamba, measured by the ABTS and β-carotene/linoleic acid methods, is higher than that observed in araticum (17%), cagaita (16.4%), and tucum (19.9%) by the β-carotene/linoleic acid method31.

Phenolic and alkaloid profile

Using high-performance liquid chromatography, 11 compounds were identified: 2 alkaloids and 9 phenolics, all of which were present in the four developmental stages of mutamba (Table 2). The alkaloid trigonelline stood out as the major compound in the fruit, surpassing the predominant phenolic gallic acid, with overall means of 191.31 mg 100 g− 1 and 15.49 mg 100 g− 1, respectively. Both compounds, along with catechin and trans-cinnamic acid, showed no significant changes throughout development, unlike the other identified compounds (p < 0.05). Resveratrol was the second most abundant phenolic compound, reaching an average of 2.29 mg 100 g− 1 in ripe fruit (Table 2). Except for trigonelline, gallic acid, and resveratrol, the remaining compounds were quantified at concentrations below 1 mg 100 g− 1, regardless of developmental stage. The p- and m-coumaric acids were the least abundant, with concentrations around 0.03 mg 100 g− 1.

The trigonelline levels identified in the mutamba are higher than those reported forpassion fruit (21.45 ± 1.47 mg 100 g− 1), pequi (Caryocar brasiliense Camb) (77.51 ± 2.91 mg 100 g− 1), and marolo (Annona crassiflora Mart.) (61.43 ± 0.93 mg 100 g− 1) at different developmental stages30,32,33. Although the literature indicates that trigonelline levels are generally higher in green fruits due to its role in osmoregulation and nitrogen storage during cell growth34,35, the stability observed during the development of G. ulmifolia fruits suggests a mechanism of continuous synthesis or efficient metabolic retention in this species.

From the perspective of human health, the robust presence of trigonelline positions the mutamba as a promising matrix for the development of functional foods aimed at preventing chronic diseases. In addition to its proven role in plant physiology, trigonelline is widely described as an anti-inflammatory and antioxidant agent, with beneficial effects on various organs and tissues. The study by Nguyen et al.36 demonstrates its efficacy in the metabolic modulation of glucose and lipids, which, combined with protection against ischemic brain damage and diabetic neuropathy, confers high nutraceutical value to the fruit. These properties are particularly relevant when considering the industrial application of fruit for the extraction of neuroprotective and cardioprotective compounds.

Just like trigonelline, the alkaloid theobromine was also identified in G. ulmifolia fruits. This compound, belonging to the xanthine group, is found in high concentrations in cocoa37. A significant increase in theobromine concentration was observed as the fruit reached maturity (S3 and S4), which may indicate a redirection of alkaloid biosynthetic pathways during the final phase of senescence (Table 2; p < 0.05). Although the concentrations are considered low (0.34 mg 100 g− 1 in ripe fruits), they still exceed the values reported for Annona crassiflora Mart. (0.03 to 0.1 mg 100 g− 1)33. Nutritionally, even in low doses, the presence of theobromine in conjunction with trigonelline may generate a synergistic effect on the regulation of immune function and lipid metabolism38, reinforcing the importance of mutamba as a previously underutilized dietary source of bioactive alkaloids.

Catechins are flavonoids from the flavan-3-ol group, characterized by a C6C3C6 structure originating from the phenylpropanoid and malonyl-CoA pathways. They are widely found in various plants, most notably in tea leaves, where they predominantly exhibit antioxidant activities39. The average catechin content quantified in the analyzed fruits throughout development was 0.97 mg 100 g− 1 (p < 0.05, Table 2), a value higher than those described for passion fruit pulp, which ranged from 0.87 to 0.60 mg 100 g− 1 in immature green and ripe fruits, respectively30. Unlike the constancy of catechins throughout mutamba development, a pattern of increasing concentration for this flavonoid was observed in Annona crassiflora Mart. fruits during development33, indicating significant metabolic differences between these fruit species.

From a human health standpoint, the sustained presence of catechins until the ripe stage (S4) is of great nutritional relevance. Catechins are recognized for their anti-carcinogenic, anti-obesity, and cardioprotective properties, acting on the modulation of cell signaling pathways and free radical scavenging40. Therefore, the maintenance of these compounds in the ripe fruit reinforces the potential of mutamba as a functional ingredient capable of maintaining its biological value even after technological processing or full ripening.

Resveratrol, like catechins, originates from the phenylpropanoid and malonyl-CoA pathways, though it has a C6C2C6 structure and is classified as a stilbene. This polyphenol is recognized as one of the most relevant secondary metabolites in plants, with a marked presence in grapes, peanuts, and sprouts41. In G. ulmifolia fruits, the highest concentrations were observed at stages S1 and S3, while the ripe fruits (S4) showed the lowest content, at 2.29 ± 0.15 mg 100 g− 1 (p < 0.05, Table 2).

Physiologically, the oscillation in resveratrol levels during mutamba development may be associated with its role as a phytoalexin42. The peak observed in S3 suggests an intensified defensive response during the transition to ripening, aimed at protecting the tissue against pathogens and abiotic stresses43 before the fruit’s final desiccation. Similar behavior was reported for C. brasiliense (pequi) fruits throughout development. However, the resveratrol content found in ripe mutamba was approximately twice as high as that observed in ripe pequi32.

The retention of significant levels of resveratrol in the ripe stage (S4) gives mutamba high nutritional and functional relevance, especially when compared to other wild matrices. Among the primary properties of resveratrol, its potent antioxidant activity stands out, which is fundamental in combating pathologies associated with an excess of reactive oxygen species (ROS). The profile identified in this study reinforces the potential of mutamba as a strategic dietary source for the modulation of chronic diseases, including tumors, inflammatory processes, cardiovascular diseases, diabetes mellitus, and neurodegenerative disorders44. Furthermore, from a technological perspective, the presence of this stilbene in dry fruits suggests chemical stability that favors the extraction and incorporation of mutamba flour into high-value-added processed foods.

Phenolic acids, intermediates of the phenylpropanoid pathway, attract much attention due to their relatively high concentrations in foods and beverages, their strong antioxidant activity, and easy intestinal absorption45. Seven of them were found in G. ulmifolia fruits, with gallic acid (3,4,5-trihydroxybenzoic acid, GA) being the major phenolic compound; it remained stable throughout development with concentrations fluctuating around 15.5 mg 100 g− 1 (p < 0.05; Table 2). The other phenolic acids were quantified in concentrations lower than 1 mg 100 g− 1 (Table 2). Physiologically, the stability of gallic acid, a universally occurring metabolite formed from 3-dehydroshikimic acid46, suggests that mutamba maintains a constant basal antioxidant defense system from early stages until senescence. Unlike other compounds that decline with ripening, the persistence of GA indicates that it may act as a continuous precursor for the synthesis of hydrolyzable tannins or as a persistent chemical barrier against lipid oxidation in the fruit tissues47.

The average content of this phenolic acid in the fruits under study was approximately eight times higher than that reported by Pereira et al.7 for fruits of the same species. This divergence in levels can be explained by the influence of biotic and abiotic factors, such as UV radiation, microbial infections, and other environmental stresses. Additionally, the extraction method used significantly impacts the amount of GA obtained48. From a nutritional and public health perspective, the high concentration of gallic acid in mutamba is promising, as this compound has proven antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, antiviral, and antitumor properties48. Its high intestinal bioavailability, as previously mentioned, reinforces the potential of mutamba flour as an efficient vehicle for delivering antioxidants in the human diet, contributing to the functional value of the developed product.

Although found in low concentrations, the presence of chlorogenic, p-coumaric, and m-coumaric acids is noteworthy, as their concentrations varied throughout fruit development (p < 0.05).

Syringic acid is a phenolic derivative originating from a branch of the phenylpropanoid pathway, produced following sinapyl alcohol, one of the fundamental precursors of lignins49. Biochemically, it can be synthesized directly in the phenylpropanoid pathway or result from the oxidative depolymerization of lignins present in the plant cell wall. The lowest content of syringic acid was observed in fruits at S2, compared to S1, S3, and S4, which did not differ from one another (Table 2; p < 0.05). This oscillation suggests dynamism in cell wall metabolism during the transition from initial growth to ripening to ensure the structural integrity of the fruit.

Similarly, in an analysis of C. brasiliense (pequi) fruits at different developmental stages, da Costa et al.32 reported the presence of the same compound. However, the compound was no longer identified in ripe pequis, unlike in mutamba, which presented 0.108 mg 100 g− 1. This continuous detection in ripe mutamba is an important technological differentiator, indicating that the fruit maintains its bioactive potential even after harvest. Syringic acid possesses antioxidant, antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and anti-endotoxic activities, and can be used as a therapeutic agent in controlling diseases such as diabetes, cardiovascular issues, cancer, cerebral ischemia, and neurological and liver damage50.

Rosmarinic acid is a natural phenolic compound resulting from the esterification of caffeic acid with 3,4-dihydroxyphenyllactic acid51. The rosmarinic acid content decreased significantly across the first three stages of development (S1–S3), from 0.49 to 0.35 mg 100 g− 1, increasing to 0.37 mg 100 g− 1 in ripe fruits (S4) (p < 0.05; Table 2). This trend of initial reduction followed by stabilization at maturity suggests that rosmarinic acid may act as a primary chemical defense in young tissues52, being partially consumed or redirected to other branches of the phenylpropanoid pathway during fruit expansion.

In a similar manner, in fruits of A. crassiflora (marolo), the concentration of rosmarinic acid varied with fruit development; however, its presence was not detected in ripe and overripe fruits33. The persistence of rosmarinic acid in ripe mutamba represents a relevant biological differentiator, indicating that the fruit retains a complex antioxidant profile until the time of consumption. This compound has recognized anticarcinogenic, antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, antidepressant, and anti-aging potential53. Thus, maintaining measurable levels of rosmarinic acid reinforces the functional value of mutamba.

Trans-cinnamic acid, or simply cinnamic acid, is synthesized from the aromatic amino acid L-phenylalanine in a reaction catalyzed by phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL). Subsequently, it converts into coumaric, caffeic, ferulic, sinapic, and syringic acids, which give rise to other phenolics. The concentrations of trans-cinnamic acid remained stable throughout fruit development (p < 0.05), with a general average of 0.16 mg 100 g− 1 (Table 2). Despite being present in small amounts in the analyzed fruits, its content was higher than that reported for passion fruit peel, pulp, and seed30. Among the various properties of trans-cinnamic acid, its antioxidant and antibacterial activities stand out, and beneficial effects in combating metabolic syndrome in rats have been reported54.

Rheology of mutamba pulps

The rheology of mutamba pulps at different maturation stages was investigated at 5 °C and 25 °C. The rheograms showed that as the applied shear rate increased, the apparent viscosity of the samples decreased (Fig. 3a), while the shear stress increased (Fig. 3b). This behavior is characteristic of non-Newtonian, pseudoplastic fluids.

Rheograms of mutamba pulp (G. ulmifolia Lam.) at different maturation stages, analyzed at different temperatures. Relationship between (a) apparent viscosity and shear rate, and (b) shear stress and shear rate.

The flow curves of the fluid indicate that, as the maturation stage of the mutamba progresses, the pulps exhibit a significant reduction in viscosity. At the same shear rate (35.7 s− 1) and temperature (5 °C), for example, it is possible to observe apparent viscosities of 126.5 mPa s, 49.0 mPa s, 21.9 mPa s, and 8.5 mPa s for pulps at stages S1, S2, S3, and S4, respectively.This drastic drop in consistency is likely related to intense cell wall metabolism and the increase in soluble solids (from 8% to 32%, Fig. 2a) and titratable acidity (from 5.78 to 11.53 mg 100 g− 1 of citric acid). Physiologically, although the severe dehydration of the fruit tends to concentrate solutes, viscosity is governed by the quantity of structural polymers and not merely by the concentration of the matrix. During ripening, the activation of hydrolytic enzymes such as pectin methylesterase and polygalacturonase promotes the depolymerization of structural polysaccharides, like pectins and hemicelluloses. This chemical modification causes the softening and loss of texture in the plant tissue55. This disruption of the cellular matrix also explains the decrease in total phenolic and flavonoid contents observed in the fruit, as the rupturing of membranes facilitates contact between vacuolar phenolic compounds and oxidative enzymes, such as polyphenol oxidase.

In addition to biological factors, increasing the temperature resulted in a slight reduction in viscosity (Fig. 3a). This occurs because intermolecular forces decrease due to the higher kinetic energy generated by heating56. The rheological parameters, detailed in Table 3, show that the Power Law was the model that best fitted the data (R² > 0.986 and low root mean square error values, RMSE ≤ 0.063). The values of the flow behavior index (n) confirm the pseudoplastic (shear-thinning) behavior of the analyzed fluids, as the values were less than 1. As the mutamba maturation stage progressed, there was a significant increase (p < 0.05) in n, indicating that the pseudoplasticity of the pulps became less pronounced. This phenomenon confirms the transition from a complex and fibrous matrix in immature fruits to a solution richer in simple sugars and fragmented polymers in ripe fruits, resulting in a fluid with lower internal resistance.

The consistency index (k) of the pulps also exhibited a significant difference (p < 0.05), with the highest values observed for pulps prepared with mutambas in the early stages of maturation. This index corroborates the viscosity results found in the rheograms (Fig. 3a).

While viscosity and total phenolics decline, compounds such as gallic acid, trigonelline, and trans-cinnamic acid remain stable, suggesting that the mutamba maintains a basal defense system and a nutraceutical value resilient to senescence. On the other hand, the increase in compounds like syringic acid in S4, in parallel with the drop in viscosity, suggests a metabolic redirection: while the internal pulp liquefies due to pectin hydrolysis, free phenolics are polymerized to harden the pericarp or are converted into secondary defense metabolites.Thus, the decrease in antioxidant activity (ABTS and β-carotene) observed from S3 onwards is a direct reflection of the lower availability of free hydroxyl groups, which are consumed or incorporated into the rigid structural matrix of the dried fruit.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) of mutamba pulps

Mutamba pulp samples at different stages of maturation were analyzed by FTIR (Fig. 4). The spectra showed a peak near 3400 cm− 1, associated with the OH bond present in water molecules and carboxylic acids in the samples57. The peak at 1252 cm− 1 and two peaks below 940 cm− 1 (namely 819 cm− 1 and 780 cm− 1) can be attributed to C-H and C-O stretching in the carbohydrate structures present in the fruit, and their changes throughout the maturation stages indicate alterations in the sugar fraction bonds of this food58. The band at 1252 cm− 1 may also be associated with citric acid59.

Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) of mutamba pulp (G. ulmifolia Lam.) at four different developmental stages.

Changes in the pectin chains present in mutamba pulps at different maturation stages were also indicated by FTIR analysis (Fig. 4). Two bands were observed at approximately 1732 cm− 1 and 1620 cm− 1, which can be attributed to the carbonyl stretching vibration in COOH and COOCH₃ groups and the symmetric stretching vibration of the -COO group, respectively60. As the mutamba fruits progress through maturation, a reduction in these two bands can be observed, and consequently, a change in the proportion between the areas of these peaks occurs. This indicates that during fruit ripening, pectin undergoes de-esterification and possibly partial depolymerization60,61.

Peaks near 1370 cm− 1 and 1055 cm− 1 were also observed, associated with C-H bending and C-O, C-C, and O-C-H vibrations present in hemicellulose and cellulose molecules, respectively62. These peaks showed changes due to significant modifications in the structure and composition of these polysaccharides that form the cell wall during fruit ripening60, and the results observed in the FTIR spectra corroborate the rheological behavior presented by the samples.

Principal component analysis (PCA) and hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA)

The results of physicochemical analyses, total phenolic concentrations, antioxidant activity, phenolic and alkaloid profiles, as well as the rheological data of mutamba fruits at four developmental stages, were subjected to multivariate analysis. PCA showed that PC1 and PC2 cumulatively explained 83.42% of the total data variation, with 54% and 29.42%, respectively (Fig. 5a). Considering a loading of 0.822 (Table 4), the variables responsible for the separation along PC1 (Fig. 5a) were total soluble solids, titratable acidity, and catechin, which were positively correlated with each other and negatively correlated with pH, Fast Blue, Folin, flavonoids, phosphomolybdenum, gallic acid, chlorogenic acid, syringic acid, p-coumaric acid, m-coumaric acid, resveratrol, rosmarinic acid, and viscosity at 5 °C and 25 °C, allowing the distinction of mutamba at stage 4 from the others. Indeed, from the visual analysis of Fig. 5a, total soluble solids, titratable acidity, and catechin cluster together in the PC1 space near mutamba at developmental stage 4 (S4), in a position diametrically opposed to ABTS, Fast Blue, Folin, and resveratrol. On the other hand, PC2 (Fig. 5a; Table 4) indicates that the lower the content of total phenolics determined by the Fast Blue and Folin methods, antioxidant activity by β-carotene, ABTS, and phosphomolybdenum assays, and the levels of p-coumaric acid, gallic acid, m-coumaric acid, and resveratrol, the higher the concentrations of catechin, total soluble solids, titratable acidity, pH, trans-cinnamic acid, trigonelline, rosmarinic acid, syringic acid, chlorogenic acid, theobromine, flavonoids, and viscosity at 5 °C and 25 °C, correlations associated with fruits at initial development (S1), immature green (S2), mature green (S3), and ripe (S4) stages.

A strong positive correlation was observed between antioxidant activity measured by the β-carotene/linoleic acid and ABTS methods (R2 = 0.963, Table 5). In addition, antioxidant activity evaluated by the ABTS method showed a significant correlation with total phenolic content determined by the Fast method, and with catechins (R² = 0.993 and 0.969, respectively, Table 5). These results are consistent with those reported by Machado et al.24, who analyzed peanuts with different test colors and also identified a high positive correlation between the β-carotene/linoleic acid and ABTS methods.

Catechins also showed a strong correlation with total phenolics determined by the Folin–Ciocalteu method, as well as with viscosity at 5 °C and 25 °C (R² = 0.975, 0.970, and 0.981, respectively, Table 5). Similarly, flavonoids exhibited a positive correlation with gallic acid and with viscosity at these temperatures (R² = 0.968, 1.00, and 0.998, respectively, Table 5). This positive correlation between total phenolic compounds and flavonoids with viscosity may be related to the presence of condensed tannins, which interact with proteins and polysaccharides in the food matrix, contributing to increased viscosity63.

Finally, gallic acid showed a positive correlation with trans-cinnamic acid and p-coumaric acid (R² = 0.977 for both, Table 5). In fact, phenolic compounds, due to the presence of hydroxyl groups in their structure, play a fundamental role in antioxidant activity by scavenging free radicals in cells, which explains the results obtained24.

Conclusion

The development of G. ulmifolia Lam. fruits revealed significant changes in physicochemical parameters, with an increase in soluble solids (8% to 32%) and titratable acidity (5.78 to 11.53 mg 100 g− 1), accompanied by a reduction in pH. A decline in total phenolic content, flavonoids, and antioxidant activity (ABTS and β-carotene/linoleic acid) was also observed throughout maturation. HPLC analysis identified 11 compounds, showing an increase in trigonelline (the major compound), stability in gallic acid, catechins, and trans-cinnamic acid, and a reduction in p-coumaric, m-coumaric, and rosmarinic acids in the ripe fruits. The pulp exhibited non-Newtonian pseudoplastic behavior across all stages, with a reduction in apparent viscosity and the consistency index (k), and an increase in the flow behavior index (n) during maturation. The variations observed in the FTIR spectra indicate modifications in the bands associated with the functional groups of the polysaccharide matrix. Multivariate analyses (PCA, HCA, and Pearson’s correlation) demonstrated an association between rheological parameters, total phenolic content, and antioxidant activity. Collectively, these results describe quantitative shifts in the chemical composition, antioxidant activity, and rheological properties of the mutamba fruits throughout their development.
