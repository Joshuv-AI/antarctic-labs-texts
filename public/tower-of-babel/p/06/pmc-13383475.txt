Development of a deep-learning model for classification of intensivists' extubation decisions using ventilator graphic monitor images: a prospective observational study
Authors: Tatsuya Hayasaka, Masaki Nakane, Yuka Fujiwara, Yoko Yuzawa, Ryuto Yokoyama, Kiyotaka Soekawa, Masahiro Kuroki, Kenya Yarimizu, Yu Onodera, Tadanori Fukami, Hiroaki Toyama

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTimely extubation requires advanced expertise and extensive clinical experience; however, intensive care specialists are scarce globally, and interpreting ventilator graphic monitor waveforms can be challenging for less experienced healthcare providers. Given these challenges, we aimed to develop a deep-learning model to classify extubation decisions by intensivists based on ventilator graphic monitor images obtained at the time of the spontaneous breathing trial (SBT) outcome determination.MethodsThis single-center prospective observational study was conducted in the Intensive Care Unit of Yamagata University Hospital between August 2023 and July 2025. Adult patients aged ≥ 20 years who received mechanical ventilation and underwent SAT and SBT using a Puritan Bennett™ 980 ventilator were included. Ventilator graphic monitor images were acquired at the time of SBT outcome determination using the screen capture function. Four convolutional neural network architectures pretrained on ImageNet (DenseNet121, EfficientNet-B0, MobileNetV3-Large, and ResNet18) were evaluated using transfer learning. This study was divided into four periods, with a threefold cross-validation performed on the first three periods and the fourth period used as an independent test set. Gradient-weighted class activation mapping (Grad-CAM) was performed to visualize the basis for the model decisions.ResultsA total of 191 patients were included in the final analysis: 160 (83.8%) in the extubated group and 31 (16.2%) in the non-extubated group. All four architectures exhibited consistently high performance (AUC: 0.827–0.900). The Grad-CAM analysis suggested that the model tended to focus on the entire waveform, including pressure, flow, and volume, in the extubated group while focusing primarily on the flow waveform in the non-extubated group.ConclusionsThis study developed a deep-learning model capable of classifying intensivists’ extubation decisions from ventilator graphic monitor images with good classification performance (AUC 0.900). As a proof of concept, this approach suggests that waveform-based components of intensivists’ decision-making may be approximated from image data, although further validation with larger multicenter datasets is required.Trial registration: The study was registered in the UMIN Clinical Trials Registry (UMIN000051938).Keywords: Artificial intelligence, Deep learning, Extubation, Mechanical ventilation, Ventilator graphics, Spontaneous breathing trial, Convolutional neural network, Intensive care unit

Background

Weaning from mechanical ventilation (extubation) is one of the most critical clinical decisions in intensive care. Extubation failure considerably worsens patient prognosis and increases the risk of serious complications, including hypoxemia, hypotension, and aspiration, which can lead to prolonged hospital stays and higher healthcare costs [1]. Extubation failure rates of 10–20% have been reported, with mortality rates in patients requiring reintubation approximately eight times higher than those of patients with successful extubation cases [2–5]. Conversely, prolonged mechanical ventilation due to overly cautious extubation decisions increases the risk of complications, such as muscle weakness. Therefore, timely extubation is crucial for optimizing patient outcomes.

In Japan, intensive care specialists are scarce, and several healthcare facilities have no or severely limited access to these specialists. As of 2021, there were approximately 2,000 certified intensive care specialists nationwide, significantly fewer per capita than in Western countries [6]. In regional areas and small-to-medium-sized hospitals, mechanical ventilation management is often performed in environments, where intensive care specialists are almost or entirely absent, and inexperienced physicians may assume responsibility for ventilator management and extubation decisions [7]. Therefore, determining the appropriate timing of extubation becomes difficult, raising concerns regarding the risk of complications from delayed or inappropriately early extubation.

Conventionally, extubation decisions employ a combination of a spontaneous awakening trial (SAT) and a spontaneous breathing trial (SBT) [8]. During SBT, spontaneous breathing is observed for a certain period (typically 30–120 min), and extubation readiness is determined by comprehensively evaluating multiple parameters, including respiratory rate, tidal volume, rapid shallow breathing index (RSBI), blood gas analysis, and hemodynamics. However, these evaluations require advanced specialized knowledge and extensive clinical experience, and the quality of judgment varies in environments without specialists [9, 10]. Moreover, qualitative assessment of breathing patterns, which cannot be captured by numerical data alone, remains essential but relies heavily on subjective clinical experience.

Intensive care specialists assess patient breathing effort, airway resistance, and lung compliance through a comprehensive evaluation of the pressure, flow, and volume waveforms displayed on ventilator graphic monitors [11]. Experienced specialists can interpret these graphic waveforms to evaluate detailed respiratory status and make informed extubation decisions [12]. However, waveform interpretation requires specialized training, and understanding these graphics is challenging for less experienced healthcare providers. Visual patterns in flow and pressure provide additional information regarding the changes in lung function, aiding physicians in making comprehensive judgments, assessing lung conditions and dyspnea, and adjusting ventilator settings. Therefore, visual monitoring plays a crucial role in improving the quality of care in intensive care units (ICUs) as physicians must respond quickly to patient needs. However, few clinicians can fully understand and analyze such visual information [13, 14].

Recently, artificial intelligence (AI) technology—particularly deep-learning-based image recognition technology using convolutional neural networks—has been applied in medicine, with various innovative applications reported in medical imaging. Studies have demonstrated diagnostic accuracies matching or exceeding those of specialists in chest radiography and computed tomography interpretation, pathology, and diabetic retinopathy screening using fundus photographs [15–19]. Research on AI for ventilator weaning has increased; however, most studies rely on numerical parameters, such as respiratory rate, RSBI, partial pressure of arterial oxygen/fraction of inspired oxygen (PaO2/FIO2, P/F) ratio, as inputs [20]. However, no prospective study has attempted to use ventilator graphic monitor images directly as input to approximate intensivists’ visual assessment of waveforms.

This study aimed to develop a deep-learning-based model to classify intensivists’ extubation decisions using ventilator graphic monitor images collected at the time of SBT outcome determination, as a proof of concept to investigate whether the waveform-based component of clinical decision-making can be approximated from image data alone. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to classify intensivists’ extubation decisions in actual patients using images from ventilator graphic monitors.

Methods

Study design and ethical approval

This single-center, prospective observational study was conducted at Yamagata University Hospital between August 2023 and July 2025. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Yamagata University Faculty of Medicine (Approval number: 2022-217) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was prospectively registered in the UMIN Clinical Trials Registry (UMIN000051938) prior to patient enrollment.

We explained the study to all patients or their authorized representatives and obtained written consent.

Study population

The study included adult patients aged ≥ 20 years who received mechanical ventilation with a Puritan Bennett™ 980 ventilator (Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN, USA) in the ICU and underwent SAT and SBT. Patients < 20 years were excluded. For patients with multiple ICU admissions during the study period, only data from the initial admission were used. Patients with incomplete graphic or laboratory data on the day of extubation evaluation were also excluded.

SAT/SBT protocol and extubation decision

The sedation depth and level of consciousness were evaluated using SAT to determine the appropriateness of sedation reduction or discontinuation. SBT was performed at a pressure support of 5 cmH2O and a positive end-expiratory pressure of 5 cmH2O for ≥ 30 min. During SBT, the intensivist monitored patients and discontinued the trial if standard failure criteria were met, including decreased peripheral oxygen saturation, increased respiratory rate, increased heart rate, or increased blood pressure changes; arrhythmia; dyspnea; or respiratory distress. At the end of SBT, arterial blood gas analysis was performed to assess pH, PaO2, partial pressure of arterial carbon dioxide (PaCO2), HCO3−, base excess, and lactic acid levels.

After SBT, intensive care physicians assessed circulatory stability, oxygenation (PaO2/FIO2 ratio), ventilation (PaCO2), respiratory pattern, cough reflex, consciousness, and blood gas results to comprehensively evaluate extubation readiness. The final extubation decision was made in all cases by or under the direct supervision of a board-certified intensivist. Fellows were not permitted to make extubation decisions independently. SAT and SBT were performed according to a standardized protocol; however, the final determination of extubation readiness was based on the intensivist’s overall clinical judgment, incorporating SBT results, level of consciousness, hemodynamic status, and other clinical factors. Patients who were extubated were classified as the "extubated" group, whereas those who were not extubated were classified as the "non-extubated" group. Among the extubated group, patients who required reintubation within 48 h after extubation were to be reclassified into the non-extubated group; however, no such cases occurred during the study period. Although patients who met the predefined SBT failure criteria during the trial were classified as SBT failures, the final extubation decision incorporated additional clinical factors beyond SAT and SBT performance, including consciousness level, fluid balance, surgical context, and other safety considerations. Therefore, patients who passed SBT could still be deferred from extubation based on the overall clinical judgment of the attending intensivist, in collaboration with the primary care team when appropriate.

Data acquisition and image processing

Ventilator graphic monitor images were acquired by intensivists at the time when the final determination regarding extubation readiness was made following SBT evaluation, using the screen capture function, and were saved in PNG format. This timing was consistent across all cases. The display on the screen was cropped using the machine’s screen capture function to extract only the waveform portion and remove the numerical data and alarm displays. Respiratory graphic display settings were airway pressure −20 to 40 cmH2O, flow rate −80 to 80 L/min, tidal volume 0–1000 mL, and a display period of 12 s. Cropped waveform images were saved in PNG format for analysis (Fig. 1).

Dataset split and validation method

The study period was divided into four periods of approximately 6 months each: Period 1 (August 2023–January 2024), Period 2 (February 2024–July 2024), Period 3 (August 2024–January 2025), and Period 4 (February 2025–July 2025). Data from Period 4 were reserved as an independent test dataset, and threefold cross-validation was performed using Periods 1–3. Specifically, Periods 1 and 2 were used for training and Period 3 for validation in fold 1, Periods 1 and 3 for training and Period 2 for validation in fold 2, and Periods 2 and 3 for training and Period 1 for validation in fold 3 (Fig. 2). Training images from the non-extubated group were augmented fivefold by horizontal shifting within ± 20% to address class imbalance. Data augmentation was applied exclusively to the training data; no augmentation was applied to the validation or test data. The dataset was split based on temporal periods, ensuring no overlap between training, validation, and test sets. Therefore, model performance was evaluated using original, unaugmented images only.

Dataset split strategy and threefold cross-validation. The study period was divided into four 6-month periods: Period 1 (August 2023–January 2024), Period 2 (February 2024–July 2024), Period 3 (August 2024–January 2025), and Period 4 (February 2025–July 2025). Period 4 was reserved as an independent test dataset. Threefold cross-validation was performed using Periods 1–3, with each fold using two periods for training and one for validation

Deep-learning model development

Using transfer learning, deep-learning models pretrained on ImageNet were adapted for this task. Input images were resized to 224 × 224 pixels. Four architectures namely, DenseNet121, EfficientNet-B0, MobileNetV3-Large, and ResNet18, were compared. The feature extraction layers of each model were frozen, and only the final classification layer was replaced for binary classification (extubated vs. non-extubated group). Each patient contributed a single ventilator graphic monitor image, ensuring strict one-patient–one-image correspondence throughout the dataset. No patient contributed multiple images to the dataset.

Model training was performed using categorical cross-entropy as the loss function and the Adam optimizer (learning rate 1e-5). The batch size was set to eight, and the maximum number of epochs was set to 50. Early stopping was implemented if the validation data loss did not improve over seven epochs. PyTorch was used as the deep-learning framework, and the analysis was performed on an Intel Core i9 CPU, NVIDIA RTX A6000 48GB GPU, and on Windows 11.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as median (interquartile range), and the Shapiro–Wilk test was used for normality testing. Given that normality was rejected for most continuous variables, the Mann–Whitney U test was used for comparisons between the two groups (extubated and non-extubated), considering the consistency of the analysis. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies (percentages) and compared using Fisher's exact test. EZR version 1.68 was used for the statistical analysis [21].

The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve and area under the curve (AUC) were used for model performance evaluation. The 95% confidence intervals for the AUC were calculated using the DeLong method [22]. For performance evaluation, the non-extubated group was defined as the positive class (label 1) and the extubated group as the negative class (label 0). Accordingly, sensitivity represents the proportion of non-extubated patients correctly classified by the model, and specificity represents the proportion of extubated patients correctly classified. The sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy were calculated, and the classification performance was assessed using a confusion matrix. The optimal cutoff value was determined based on the shortest distance from the ROC curve to the ideal point (sensitivity, 1; specificity, 1). Among the three models from threefold cross-validation, the model that demonstrated the best AUC on the validation data was selected as the final model, and performance evaluation was conducted on the independent test dataset (Period 4). The AUC of the test data was used as the final value. The diagnostic capability of the model was considered significant if AUC > 0.700.

To visualize the basis for model decisions, gradient-weighted class activation mapping (Grad-CAM) was performed to evaluate the waveform regions on which the model focused. Python 3.10 was used for model construction. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Patient characteristics and study population

A total of 198 patients were enrolled in the study. After excluding two duplicate data entries due to readmission, three patients with missing graphic images at the time of SBT, and two patients with missing blood test data, 191 patients were included in the final analysis (Fig. 3). The mean age of all patients was 69 years (range, 61.5–74.5), and 122 (63.9%) were male. The length of hospital stay from intubation to discharge was 22 days (range, 17–29), and 43 patients (22.5%) required emergency surgery or intubation.

Flowchart of the patient selection process. During the study period, 198 patients were included. After excluding duplicate admissions to the intensive care unit (ICU) (n = 2), missing graphic data at the spontaneous breathing trial (n = 3), and missing blood test data (n = 2), a total of 191 graphics from 191 patients were included in the final analysis

Extubation outcomes

Among the 191 patients, 160 (83.8%) were extubated (extubated group), and 31 (16.2%) were not extubated (non-extubated group). At the time of image acquisition, 164 patients passed SBT (extubated group: 160, non-extubated group: 4) and 27 patients failed SBT (all in the non-extubated group). The four patients who passed SBT but were not extubated were judged to be unready for extubation based on clinical considerations beyond the SBT criteria: impaired consciousness (n = 2) and concerns regarding post-extubation safety due to ongoing fluid management issues that did not meet SBT failure criteria (n = 2; one with substantial drain output following emergency surgery for which continued observation was judged preferable after a discussion between the attending intensivist and the primary care team and another with fluid overload for which additional diuresis was judged necessary after a similar discussion). In both of the latter cases, hemodynamic parameters during the SBT remained within the criteria for SBT success, but extubation was deferred in consideration of the overall clinical context. The 27 patients who failed the SBT were naturally placed in the non-extubated group. No patients required reintubation within 48 h after extubation. Five patients required reintubation more than 48 h after extubation: three in the extubated group (gastrointestinal hemorrhage, reoperation, and fatal arrhythmia, all non-respiratory causes) and two in the non-extubated group (reoperation and ARDS), who were not extubated at the time of the study evaluation but were extubated on a subsequent occasion during their ICU stay and later required reintubation. These cases were not included in the classification labels, as they occurred beyond the 48-h threshold defined a priori based on previous studies.

Given the class imbalance between the extubated (n = 160) and non-extubated groups (n = 31) (ratio approximately 5:1), data augmentation was performed only on the training data to achieve comparable sample sizes between the two classes. Specifically, images from the non-extubated group were augmented to five times the original number by horizontal shifting within a range of ± 20%. No data augmentation was applied to the validation or test data, and the original images were used to appropriately evaluate the generalization performance of the model.

Deep-learning model performance

The results of evaluating the four deep-learning architectures (DenseNet121, EfficientNet-B0, MobileNetV3-Large, and ResNet18) are shown in Fig. 4. The AUC calculated from the ROC curve of each model was 0.886 (95% confidence interval [CI] 0.758–1.000) for DenseNet121, 0.827 (95% CI 0.642–1.000) for EfficientNet-B0, 0.900 (95% CI 0.807–0.993) for MobileNetV3-Large, and 0.891 (95% CI 0.757–1.000) for ResNet18. MobileNetV3-Large demonstrated the highest AUC and was selected as the final model.

Final model performance and validation

The performance evaluation of the MobileNetV3-Large model on the independent test set (Period 4 data) achieved an AUC of 0.900 (95% CI 0.807–0.993) (Fig. 4). The confusion matrix is shown in Fig. 5. The final model had a sensitivity of 0.900, specificity of 0.727, and accuracy of 0.759. Additional performance metrics of this model in the independent test set were as follows: precision (= positive predictive value, PPV) = 0.429, recall (= sensitivity, reported above) = 0.900, F1-score = 0.581, and negative predictive value (NPV) = 0.970. There were 12 false-positive patients (classified as non-extubated but actually extubated) and one false-negative patient (classified as extubated but actually not extubated) (Fig. 5).

Discussion

This study developed a deep-learning model to classify intensivists’ extubation decisions from ventilator graphic monitor images obtained at the time of SBT outcome determination, achieving high classification performance with an AUC of 0.900. This model was developed as a proof of concept to investigate whether the waveform-based component of intensivists’ clinical decision-making can be approximated from image data alone. Grad-CAM analysis suggests that the model learned clinically valid decision patterns, comprehensively evaluating the entire waveform in patients in the extubated group while emphasizing abnormalities in the flow waveform in patients in the non-extubated group. In this study, the non-extubation rate was 16.2% (31/191), within the range of non-extubation rates (10–20%) reported in previous studies [23]. This suggests that our study population had characteristics comparable to those reported in previous studies.

A distinctive feature of the present study is that the ground truth was defined by intensivists’ extubation decisions rather than by clinical outcomes, such as reintubation. This was a deliberate design choice, consistent with the proof-of-concept aim of approximating the experience-dependent, waveform-based component of intensivists’ clinical reasoning. Under this design, the model’s performance reflects the degree of agreement with intensivists’ decisions rather than the ability to predict post-extubation outcomes directly, which positions the model as a potential aid for waveform interpretation rather than as a predictor of reintubation. At the same time, this labeling approach involves several considerations that should be acknowledged. Even when SAT and SBT are conducted according to a standardized protocol, the final extubation decision incorporates clinical judgment regarding factors not fully captured by numerical criteria, and a degree of variability among intensivists is, therefore, inherent to this type of label. Although extubation decisions in our institution were made through team-based discussion among board-certified intensivists, formal inter-observer agreement assessment with independent evaluation of the same cases by multiple specialists was not performed in the present study, and the possibility that the model has partly learned decision patterns specific to our institution’s clinical culture and patient population cannot be excluded. These considerations should be borne in mind when interpreting the model’s results.

Several prediction models using numerical parameters related to ventilator weaning have been reported in AI research. Hsieh et al. used an artificial neural network to predict successful extubation from 37 clinical risk factors and reported an AUC of 0.85 [24]. In conventional SBT evaluations, the RSBI is widely used as an indicator for predicting weaning, and a meta-analysis by Jia et al. reported an AUC of 0.81 [25]. In contrast, our objective was not to predict post-extubation clinical outcomes or to replace existing SBT protocols, but to classify intensivists’ extubation decisions directly from ventilator graphic monitor images. To the best of our knowledge, this represents the first such attempt. The AUC of 0.900 obtained in this study suggests that this new approach warrants further investigation.

The clinical importance of ventilator graphic waveforms has been extensively reported. Hamahata et al. demonstrated that flow curves provide information on respiratory mechanics, patient effort, and patient–ventilator interaction, and that proper evaluation serves as a useful diagnostic and therapeutic tool at the bedside, as worsening of respiratory effort and asynchrony can occur when ventilator settings are not synchronized with the patient's breathing pattern [26]. Costa et al. also reported that the identification and correction of patient–ventilator asynchrony are important for critically ill patient management [27]. The novelty of this study lies in the creation of a model that uses AI technology to objectively classify clinical decisions regarding ventilator graphic waveforms, which previously relied on the experience and intuition of physicians.

In this study, consistently high performance (AUC 0.827–0.900) was obtained across four architectures: DenseNet121, EfficientNet-B0, MobileNetV3-Large, and ResNet18. This result suggests that the learned features are generalizable and independent of the model architecture. In addition, the methodology of this study, which evaluated the most recent period as an independent test set through time-series-based dataset splitting, represents a validation method that assumes real-world clinical use. Major et al. reported that in medical AI, time-series splitting reflects real-world clinical performance more accurately than random splitting [28]. The fact that our model maintained high performance even after time-series splitting is an important strength for clinical implementation.

The Grad-CAM analysis suggested a tendency to focus on the entire pressure, flow, and volume waveforms in the extubated group while focusing primarily on the flow waveform in the non-extubated group. This finding is physiologically valid from the respiratory perspective. In patients classified as non-extubated (Fig. 7b: true positive, Fig. 7c: false positive), shortened respiratory cycles, indicating increased respiratory rate and steep fluctuations and irregularities in the flow waveform, were suggested to be important decision factors. Albani et al. reported that changes in the shape of the inspiratory flow waveform during pressure-support ventilation are independently associated with the patient’s inspiratory effort [29]. The model’s attention to the flow waveform region in patients classified as non-extubated is consistent with, but does not confirm, a physiological interpretation related to changes in respiratory effort. Grad-CAM visualizes pixel-level attention and does not directly indicate physiological mechanisms; therefore, these interpretations remain speculative. Notably, among the false-positive cases (Fig. 7c), two distinct attention patterns were observed. In some cases, the model’s attention was located near the waveform region, suggesting that the model may have responded to flow waveform patterns or waveform morphology but arrived at an incorrect classification. In other cases, the attention was directed toward blank areas devoid of waveforms, indicating that the model may have responded to image features without clinical significance. The latter pattern represents an important limitation regarding the reliability of the model’s decision-making. The interpretability and reliability of the model’s decisions require further verification with larger datasets.

The one false-negative case (classified as extubated but not actually extubated) is important from the perspective of model safety. However, owing to the limited sample size, large-scale validation is necessary. The 12 false-positive cases (classified as non-extubated but actually extubated) indicated an overly cautious judgment, which may lead to prolonged mechanical ventilation. This relatively low precision (0.429) of the model in this study on the independent test set is consistent with the high number of false-positive cases, indicating that the model tends to classify some extubatable patients as non-extubated. In contrast, the high NPV (0.970) suggests that cases classified as extubated were rarely misclassified, which may be a favorable characteristic from a safety perspective. However, given the limited sample size of the test set, particularly the small number of non-extubated cases (n = 10), these metrics should be interpreted with caution and require validation in larger datasets. The final extubation decision should be made comprehensively by clinicians using AI classification as a reference.

In clinical practice, passing SBT does not automatically lead to extubation; the responsible clinician still needs to make a final judgment that incorporates qualitative interpretation of ventilator waveforms together with other clinical information, such as blood gas analysis, level of consciousness, and hemodynamic status. This waveform interpretation is experience-dependent and can be challenging for less experienced clinicians. The proposed AI model is positioned to support this final judgment step by providing a classification that approximates how an experienced intensivist would interpret the waveforms. The model is not intended to replace SBT or to provide an independent extubation decision; rather, it is intended as a supplementary reference at the moment when the responsible clinician makes the final extubation decision after SBT. The final decision always remains with the clinician, and any clinical deployment in this role would require validation against patient-centered outcomes in larger multicenter studies.

The shortage of intensive care specialists is not only a problem in Japan but also an international issue [30]. Particularly during nights and weekends, inexperienced physicians may be responsible for extubation decisions [31]. The AI model in this study can make classifications simply by acquiring images directly from the ventilator monitor screen without requiring additional tests or complex calculations, which may offer a potential advantage for future clinical application. Lilly et al. reported reduced ICU mortality and shortened hospital stays with the introduction of remote intensive care physician programs [32], and in Japan, Watanabe et al. reported reduced ICU mortality and decreased workload for on-site physicians after tele-ICU implementation [33]. If decision-classification tools, such as our AI model, are further validated and extended to predict clinical outcomes, they may in the future assist in prioritizing consultations with specialists within tele-ICU systems. However, such applications represent a potential future direction and require substantial further validation beyond the scope of the current study. In addition, the visualization function using Grad-CAM may potentially be utilized as a training tool for waveform interpretation by junior physicians and respiratory therapists, although further validation is required.

Limitations

This study had some limitations. First, this was a single-center prospective observational study, and external validity was limited; only the Puritan Bennett™ 980 was used, and validation with different ventilator models is necessary. A large-scale validation through multicenter collaborative research is a future challenge. Second, the non-extubated group was limited to 31 patients, and the existence of one false-negative case requires cautious interpretation from the perspective of statistical reliability. In addition, although some patients were not extubated, no patients were reintubated after extubation. Third, owing to the prospective nature of this study, only patients who consented to participate were included, and there was a possibility of selection bias. In addition, blinding was not achieved, because the evaluation was performed with the knowledge of the timing of image acquisition. Furthermore, formal inter-observer agreement assessment was not performed. Although extubation decisions were made through team-based discussion among intensivists, the model may have learned institution-specific decision-making patterns, and validation through multicenter studies is necessary to assess generalizability. Fourth, the model in this study used only images as input and did not consider other information that is important in real clinical practice, such as blood gas analysis, patient consciousness level, and the amount of airway secretions. The development of a multimodal model that integrates this information is expected to further improve predictive accuracy. Fifth, because this study was designed as a proof of concept to classify intensivists’ overall extubation decisions, subgroup analysis stratified by SBT outcome and direct comparison of predictive performance among SBT alone, the AI model alone, and a combined approach were outside the scope of the present study. No patients in the extubated group who passed SBT required reintubation within 48 h after extubation, precluding evaluation of the clinically important subgroup who pass SBT but subsequently fail extubation. Whether AI can provide incremental value beyond SBT, particularly in identifying post-extubation failure, should be addressed in future multicenter studies with clinical outcome-based labeling and larger sample sizes. Sixth, the non-extubated group comprised only 31 patients, and although data augmentation by horizontal shifting was applied to the training data, this technique does not generate novel respiratory physiology but merely creates modified versions of the same underlying waveforms. Despite the use of early stopping and temporal dataset splitting, the risk of overfitting cannot be excluded. The reported AUC may overestimate the model’s true generalization ability, and validation with larger sample sizes is required. Furthermore, the independent test set was small (N = 54, non-extubated group: 10), which further limits the reliability of the reported performance metrics. Seventh, the data used in this study did not necessarily correspond to the first SBT for each patient. In clinical practice, patients often undergo multiple SBTs during their ICU stay, and it was not always feasible to consistently retain only the first SBT data. This study focused on cases, where the timing of SBT outcome determination could be clearly identified.

Conclusions

In this study, we developed a deep-learning model to classify intensivists’ extubation decisions from ventilator graphic monitor images obtained at the time of SBT outcome determination, achieving an AUC of 0.900. The Grad-CAM analysis suggested that the model learned decision patterns consistent with clinical reasoning. As a proof of concept, this approach demonstrates that waveform-based components of intensivists’ decision-making can be approximated from image data, and may warrant further investigation for potential clinical applications.
