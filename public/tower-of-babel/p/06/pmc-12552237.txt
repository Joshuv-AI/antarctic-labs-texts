T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia: therapeutic outcomes in adolescents and young adults
Authors: Emna Azza, Aya Ben Othmen, Maroua Bahri, Roua Hsasna, Raihane Ben Lakhel, Yosr Ben Abdennebi, Lamia Aissaoui
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractT-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (T-ALL) in adolescents and young adults (AYA) poses distinct clinical challenges. This study evaluates the effectiveness and tolerability of a pediatric-inspired regimen in this specific age group, focusing on response rates, survival outcomes, and prognostic indicators. We retrospectively analyzed AYA patients (15–29 years) diagnosed with T-ALL between 2010 and 2020 at Aziza Othmana Hospital, Tunis. All patients received treatment per the EORTC 58,951 protocol. Clinical, cytological, and immunophenotypic data were collected, with special attention to treatment response and minimal residual disease (MRD). Survival outcomes were assessed using Kaplan–Meier estimates, and prognostic factors were examined via uni- and multivariate analyses. Thirty-two patients were included (median age: 20 years; male: female ratio 2.3:1). Complete remission after induction was achieved in 84.4% of cases, with MRD negativity (< 10⁻⁴ at day 35) observed in 42% of patients. At a median follow-up of 62 months, the 5-year overall survival (OS), event-free survival (EFS), and relapse-free survival (RFS) were 62.2%, 62.5%, and 70.8%, respectively. Chemotherapy response at day 19 and MRD negativity were associated with better outcomes, although only relapse occurrence remained independently predictive of OS. Induction mortality reached 9.4%. Relapse occurred in 32.1% of patients, underscoring the need for improved risk stratification. Our findings support the efficacy of pediatric-based protocols in treating AYA with T-ALL. However, high relapse rates and early induction mortality highlight the importance of integrating MRD-guided decisions and enhancing supportive care. Future strategies should incorporate targeted and immune-based therapies to improve long-term outcomes in high-risk subgroups.Keywords: T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia, Adolescents and young adults, Pediatric-inspired treatment, Minimal residual disease, Relapse, Survival outcomes

Introduction

T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (T-ALL) is a malignant clonal proliferation of lymphoid progenitor cells arrested at an early stage of differentiation [1]. It accounts for approximately 10–15% of pediatric ALL and 25% of adult cases. A significant proportion of cases occurs in adolescents and young adults (AYA), a distinct population with unique clinical, biological, and psychosocial characteristics [2, 3].

The definition of AYA age range varies across studies and geographic regions, influenced by socioeconomic and physiological considerations. Adolescence is commonly defined as ranging from 15 to 19 years, while adulthood begins at 20. The upper age limit for AYA ranges from 24 to 39 years in different frameworks [4].

AYA treated with pediatric-inspired regimens have demonstrated superior outcomes compared to those receiving adult protocols, with complete remission rates approaching 90% and overall survival rates around 70% [5]. Despite these advances, outcomes remain suboptimal due to relapse, therapy resistance, or treatment-related toxicities [6, 7].

To evaluate the efficacy and tolerability of pediatric-based therapy in this population, we conducted a retrospective study analyzing clinical and therapeutic outcomes in newly diagnosed AYA patients with T-ALL treated according to the EORTC 58,951 protocol.

Patients and methods

This retrospective, descriptive, single-center study was conducted at the Department of Hematology of Aziza Othmana Hospital, Tunis, and included AYA diagnosed with T-ALL between January 2010 and December 2020.

For each patient, demographic, clinical, laboratory, prognostic, and therapeutic data were collected. The diagnosis of T-ALL was established by bone marrow cytology showing ≥ 20% blasts and confirmed by flow cytometry immunophenotyping. A five-color FC500 cytometer was used. T-ALL subtypes were defined according to the European Group for the Immunological Classification of Leukemias (EGIL) criteria [8, 9] from 2010 to 2015 and the WHO classification [10] thereafter.

Minimal residual disease (MRD) was assessed by multiparametric flow cytometry on bone-marrow samples collected at the end of induction (day 35). The same FC500 five-color cytometer was used and the primary antibody panel included: CD3, CD4, CD5, CD7, CD8, CD16, CD34, CD38, CD45 and CD56; when needed an extended panel (CD1a, CD2, CD11b, CD13, CD33, CD117, TdT) was employed to increase discriminative power. The analytical sensitivity of the assay was approximately 10⁻⁴; however, for risk-adapted treatment decisions we used the EORTC 58,951 clinical threshold (MRD ≥ 10⁻² at day 35) to reassign patients to the Very High Risk (VHR) arm.

Cytogenetic analysis was performed using conventional karyotyping. Molecular analyses, including NOTCH1 and FBXW7 mutation testing, were not performed, as these investigations are not currently available in our setting in Tunisia. Lumbar puncture for CNS evaluation was performed prior to treatment initiation or after cytoreduction in cases of hyperleukocytosis. All male patients underwent testicular ultrasonography to assess for extramedullary involvement.

Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) is defined biologically as a systemic activation of coagulation, leading to excessive thrombin generation, consumption of clotting factors and platelets, and elevated fibrin degradation products [11]. Clinically, DIC presents with simultaneous bleeding and thrombosis. In this study, DIC was classified according to the ISTH scoring system, with a score ≥ 5 indicating overt DIC.

Patients were stratified according to the EORTC 58,951 risk groups using morphologic, clinical, and early-response criteria [12]. Under this protocol, all patients with T-cell ALL are classified as at least MR2. In our cohort, this classification was reinforced by age > 10 years in all cases.

Induction therapy followed the MR2/very high-risk (VHR) protocol arm included Dexamethasone, Vincristine, Daunorubicin, Methotrexate, Cyclophosphamide and L-asparaginase (LASPA), combined with intrathecal chemotherapy (Fig. 1). Patients who developed hypersensitivity to LASPA were switched to Erwinase. The therapeutic agents used in the MR 2 and VHR arms of the EORTC 58,951 protocol are summarized in Tables 1 and 2.

A summary of the therapeutic response evaluations performed during induction therapy according to the EORTC 58,951 protocol is presented in Table 3. Additional assessments (Day 19 bone marrow evaluations, MRD) were implemented to improve risk stratification. Complete remission at the end of induction required combined clinical, hematologic, cytologic, and molecular criteria.

Patients achieving CR underwent consolidation and maintenance therapy according to the protocol. Patients meeting one or more of the following criteria: poor response to prephase, poor chemotherapy response on day 19, failure to achieve CR or MRD ≥ 10⁻² on day 35, were switched to the VHR treatment regimen. All VHR patients, except those with a poor response to chemotherapy, were eligible for allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT) if they had an HLA-identical donor.

Relapse was defined as disease recurrence following CR. Early relapse was defined as a medullary relapse within 36 months of diagnosis or an isolated extramedullary relapse within 18 months.

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 26.0. Survival curves were generated using the Kaplan–Meier method. Univariate analysis was conducted using the log-rank test, and multivariate analysis was performed using the Cox model. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Variables with p ≤ 0.20 in univariate analysis were included in the multivariate model.

Overall survival (OS) was defined as the time from diagnosis to death from any cause or last follow-up. Event-free survival (EFS) was defined as the time from diagnosis to the first documented event, including induction failure, relapse, progression, or death from any cause. Relapse-free survival (RFS) was calculated from the date of complete remission to the date of relapse or death in remission. Patients without the event at last follow-up were censored. The 5-year EFS estimate was slightly higher than OS. This was explained by the event definitions and the small sample size. Differences in censoring may have contributed to this variation.

The following prognostic factors were evaluated : gender, age, tumor burden, initial CNS involvement, WBC at diagnosis, cytogenetic profile, response to pephase and chemotherapy, CR, MRD at D35, treatment arm, allo-HSCT in first CR, and relapse characteristics (occurrence and timing).

The study was conducted in accordance with institutional ethical standards the study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Local Ethics Committee of Aziza Othmana Hospital, Tunis, Tunisia. Due to the retrospective design and use of anonymized data from patient records, the committee waived the requirement for individual informed consent. No conflicts of interest were declared. The authors received no specific funding for this work.

Results

Patient characteristics at diagnosis

Among the 67 AYA patients treated with a pediatric-inspired protocol during the study period, 32 (47.7%) were classified as T-cell ALL. The median age at diagnosis was 20 years [15–29], with a male predominance of 70%.​.

The most common presenting feature was a high tumor burden, observed in 51.6% of patients. Peripheral lymphadenopathy was reported in 20 patients, splenomegaly in 5, hepatomegaly in three and signs of superior vena cava syndrome in 3. Clinical examination revealed infectious symptoms in 9 patients, anemia in 18, and bleeding manifestations in 5.​.

Extra medullary involvement was observed in five patients (15.6%), including 4 with CNS infiltration and 1 with Testicular involvement. Mediastinal widening was detected in 3 patients (9.4%).The median white blood cell (WBC) count at diagnosis was 49 600/mm3 (range: 4300–286000). Hyperleukocytosis (WBC ≥ 100 000/mm3) was present in 11 patients (34.4%). The median hemoglobin level was 9.8 g/dL (range: 5.5–15.3), and the median platelet count was 61,500/mm3 (range: 3000–502000).

Bone marrow aspiration was performed in all patients, with a median blast percentage of 90% (range: 30–100%). Immunophenotyping revealed: 1 patient with T-ALL type I (3.1%), 10 with type II (31.2%), 15 with type III (46.9%), and 6 with type IV (18.7%).

Cytogenetic analysis showed a normal karyotype in 14 patients (43.8%), pathological abnormalities in 13 (40.6%), and failure of karyotyping in 5 (15.6%).Abnormalities included 11q23 deletion (n = 1), complex karyotypes (n = 5), and other abnormalities with no known prognostic impact (n = 7).

Thirteen patients met the laboratory criteria for tumor lysis syndrome; of whom two also exhibited clinical criteria (renal insufficiency). Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) was diagnosed based on biological criteria in seven patients, and both clinical and biological criteria in two patients.

At diagnosis, 29 patients (90%) were classified into the MR2 risk arm, and 3 patients (10%) into the VHR arm.

Therapeutic outcomes

All patients received the same induction chemotherapy regimen, based on the MR2/VHR protocol. Three patients (9.4%) died during induction: one from septic shock, one from cerebral hemorrhage, and one from hepatic failure.

Prephase response was assessed in all patients; 23 (71.9%) were classified as good responders. On day 19, among 31 evaluable patients, 23 (74.2%) had a good chemotherapy response.​.

At the end of induction, 27 patients (84.4%) achieved CR. MRD < 10⁻⁴ was detected in 12 patients. Following re-stratification: 16 patients (55.2%) remained into the MR2 arm and 13 (44.8%) were reassigned to the VHR arm. Of the two patients who failed to achieve CR, one attained remission after a second induction (Ib’); the other died during induction failure.

Allo-HSCT was indicated in first CR for 7 patients. Among them, 4 patients did not have a suitable donor and subsequently experienced disease relapse and death. The remaining 3 patients had HLA-matched sibling donors. Two of these patients underwent allo-HSCT and remained in continuous remission, while one patient relapsed before transplantation could be performed.

At final follow-up, 9 patients (32.1%) had relapsed. Six relapses were early (≤ 36 months), and three occurred later. The median time to relapse was 13 months (range: 8–72). Sites of relapse included bone marrow (n = 5), CNS (n = 3), and combined marrow and CNS (n = 1).

Univariate analysis identified chemotherapy response, relapse occurrence, and timing of relapse as significant factors influencing OS. OS rate was 73.4% in early chemotherapy responders, compared to 37.5% in poor responders (p = 0.039; Fig. 3A).OS was 94.7% in patients without relapse, vs. 22.2% in those who relapsed (p < 0.0001; Fig. 3B). Timing of relapse further affected OS: 33.3% for late relapse vs. 0% for early relapse (p = 0.01; Fig. 3C). Although MRD < 10⁻⁴ was associated with improved OS (90% vs. 50%), the difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.06). We may not found statistical significance due to the small number of patients. In multivariate analysis, relapse occurrence remained the only significant factor impacting OS (p = 0.03; 95% CI 34.7–54.5).

Discussion

Distinct clinical and biological features of AYA with T-ALL

Adolescents and young adults (AYA) with T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (T-ALL) represent a unique patient population with distinct clinical and biological characteristics. Numerous studies have demonstrated that AYA patients Benefit from pediatric-inspired treatment protocols, with reported 5-year overall survival (OS) rates ranging between 60% and 75% [13, 14].

To our knowledge, this is the first national-level study in Tunisia specifically focusing on AYA with T-ALL. In the absence of prior national data, our findings provide an initial benchmark for future research and underscore the urgent need for establishing a national registry and conducting multicenter studies to enhance knowledge and optimize patient care in this setting.

High induction mortality: challenges and mitigation strategies

The induction mortality rate observed in our cohort was notably high at 9.4%, substantially exceeding rates reported in comparable international studies, which are typically below 3% [15]. This discrepancy may be attributed to several factors, including limited access to optimal supportive care, a high incidence of treatment-related complications such as infections and tumor lysis syndrome, and possible delays in their management. Socioeconomic constraints and systemic healthcare limitations likely contribute as well.

To reduce induction mortality, it is imperative to improve early detection and management of infections, implement standardized protocols for tumor lysis syndrome prophylaxis, and strengthen sup0portive care infrastructure, including availability of intensive care units and appropriate antimicrobial therapies. Treatment modifications tailored to high-risk patients to reduce toxicity, alongside enhanced training of healthcare personnel for early recognition of complications, are critical. Addressing these areas is essential to decrease early mortality and approach survival outcomes observed in higher-resource settings.

Comparative outcomes with international trials

Despite heterogeneity in age definitions and follow-up durations across studies, our results are largely consistent with the published literature. For example, the GMALL trial involving 1,529 patients aged 15–35 years reported 5-year OS rates of 73%, 69%, and 60% in the 15–17, 18–25, and 26–35 age groups, respectively [16]. Similarly, the NOPHO ALL 2008 protocol demonstrated a 5-year event-free survival (EFS) of 74% in patients aged 1–45 years, albeit with increased toxicity in older patients [17].

Major international trials such as CALGB 10,403 and GRAALL further provide valuable benchmarks for AYA T-ALL outcomes. The CALGB 10,403 trial, which enrolled 295 patients aged 16–39 years treated with a pediatric-inspired regimen, reported 3-year OS and EFS rates of 73% and 59%, respectively [18]. GRAALL studies have similarly reported 5-year OS rates ranging from 65 to 70% in adult and AYA T-ALL patients receiving pediatric-based protocols [19, 20].

Our cohort’s 5-year OS of 62.2% and EFS of 62.5% are slightly lower but remain within a comparable range. This may reflect differences in patient demographics, healthcare resource availability, supportive care quality, and notably the higher induction mortality rate in our setting. Additionally, the lower rate of MRD negativity (42%) compared to pediatric and Western cohorts may indicate more aggressive disease biology or challenges in treatment delivery in our context.

Collectively, these findings highlight both the efficacy of pediatric-inspired protocols across diverse environments and the critical need to optimize supportive care and integrate MRD-guided risk stratification to improve outcomes further.

Prognostic value of MRD and implications for treatment stratification

Minimal residual disease (MRD) emerged as a key prognostic marker. Only 42% of patients achieved MRD negativity post-induction, a rate lower than that reported in pediatric populations. This likely reflects more aggressive disease biology and reduced treatment sensitivity in the AYA group. Patients with MRD < 10⁻⁴ at day 35 exhibited significantly better 5-year relapse-free survival (RFS) (91.7% vs. 46.2%, p = 0.038). Similar observations have been reported in the GIMEMA LAL-1308 trial [21] and GRAALL studies [19, 20], which also emphasized MRD’s critical role in guiding hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) decisions.

Hematopoietic stem cell transplantation indications based on MRD status

Only two patients in our cohort underwent allogeneic HSCT in first remission. While pediatric-inspired protocols generally achieve favorable outcomes without routine upfront transplantation, MRD status has become pivotal in refining HSCT indications. Patients with high MRD levels post-induction (commonly defined as ≥ 10⁻³) are considered at elevated relapse risk and may benefit from early HSCT to improve long-term survival. Conversely, those achieving deep MRD negativity (< 10⁻⁴) typically have excellent prognoses and may avoid transplantation-related risks. This risk-adapted approach, increasingly adopted in recent trials such as GRAALL and French studies [22], aims to balance treatment efficacy with toxicity.

Our findings align with this paradigm, suggesting that HSCT mitigates poor prognosis in patients with persistent MRD, whereas its benefit in MRD-negative patients remains uncertain. Establishing clearer national guidelines and conducting prospective studies are warranted to optimize HSCT indications in AYA with T-ALL.

Intermediate prognosis and management of relapsed disease

AYA patients represent an intermediate prognostic group between children and older adults. The relapse rate in our cohort (30%) reflects this, compared to less than 20% in pediatric populations and over 40% in older adults. Post-relapse outcomes remain poor, with survival rarely exceeding 25%, largely due to limited efficacy of salvage regimens.

No standard treatment exists for relapsed T-ALL. Novel therapeutic approaches are under investigation. Nelarabine, a purine nucleoside analogue, has shown promising activity, especially in AYA patients [23]. It is approved for relapsed/refractory T-ALL and increasingly incorporated into frontline protocols. Reinduction regimens including Nelarabine have demonstrated second complete remission (CR2) rates around 40% in first relapse settings [24]. Proteasome inhibitors such as Bortezomib, combined with chemotherapy, may enhance chemosensitivity, particularly in resistant disease [25]. The phase II COG AALL07P1 trial reported a CR2 rate of 68% in 22 relapsed T-ALL patients aged 1–31 years treated with Bortezomib-based therapy [26].

Emerging CAR-T cell therapies targeting CD7 offer a promising new frontier. Early-phase clinical trials have shown encouraging response rates, suggesting potential curative benefits for high-risk AYA patients. However, access is limited and long-term efficacy remains under evaluation [27].

These novel therapies represent important avenues for future clinical trials and hope for improved outcomes in high-risk or relapsed patients. Until broader availability, allogeneic HSCT remains the only potentially curative option for relapsed T-ALL in AYA.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations. It was conducted at a single center, and the sample size was relatively small, which may limit the generalizability of the findings.

Advanced molecular diagnostics were not available, preventing genetic characterization of T-ALL subtypes. This may have influenced risk classification and treatment choices.

MRD was assessed in all patients at the end of induction but not after consolidation. This limited our ability to track disease evolution and adjust post-remission strategies such as HSCT.

These limitations should be considered when interpreting the results and highlight the need to strengthen diagnostic and monitoring tools in our setting.

Conclusion

This study suggests that pediatric-inspired treatment protocols are feasible and effective in AYA with T-ALL in our setting. Initial remission and survival rates were encouraging, but outcomes in relapsed patients remained poor. This highlights the need for refined risk stratification and early identification of high-risk cases.

In our cohort, MRD was a strong prognostic marker and should guide post-induction therapy and HSCT decisions. Enhanced supportive care during induction may help reduce early mortality.

To improve outcomes in relapsed or refractory T-ALL, future clinical trials should evaluate novel approaches, including Nelarabine, proteasome inhibitors, and CAR T-cell therapies. Until such therapies become accessible, allo-HSCT remains the only potentially curative option for these patients.
