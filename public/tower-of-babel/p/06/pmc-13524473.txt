Practice habits in board-eligible shoulder and elbow orthopedic surgeons
Authors: Bhargavi Maheshwer, Molly M Piper, Lucas R Haase, Robert Gillespie, Raymond Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and PurposeThere is little data regarding newly practicing orthopedic surgeons and their surgical preferences during the 6-month case collection for the American Board of Orthopaedic Surgery Part-II. The purpose of this study was to conduct a comprehensive survey analysis regarding the practice habits of board-eligible orthopedic surgeons specializing in shoulder and elbow surgery. The survey was sent to all surgeons who graduated from an American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons accredited shoulder/elbow fellowship from 2017 to 2021 in order to capture surgeons who recently applied for American Board of Orthopaedic Surgery Part-II. Respondents were able to choose from the following answer choices for each procedure question: “No indication for this procedure in my practice,” “I would not do this during boards collection but otherwise would do it,” or “I would do this during boards collection.”ResultsA total of 54 board eligible orthopedic surgeons completed the survey. Furthermore, over half of respondents (53.7%) had cases meeting their surgical indications that they did not do during the 6-month board collections period that they otherwise would have performed. Regarding specific survey questions and procedures, there was a majority of surgeons stating they would do the indicated procedure during boards collection for universally adapted procedures such as anatomic total shoulder arthroplasty with stemless humeral head for primary osteoarthritis (98.1%), patient-specific instrumentation for anatomic total shoulder arthroplasty or reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (rTSA; 92.3%), rTSA for primary osteoarthritis (90.7%), rTSA with augmented baseplate (96.3%), and rTSA for massive irreparable rotator cuff tear (98.1%). The American Board of Orthopaedic Surgery (ABOS) is one of the 24 separate boards that make up the American Board of Medical Specialties.8 Of the 24 boards, 14 require an oral examination. Upon completion of an approved residency program, applicants for board certification must pass a comprehensive written examination (ABOS Part-I). The applicants must then satisfactorily complete a thorough credentialing process and pass an oral examination (ABOS Part-II) that is based on all the candidate's operative cases in a predetermined, consecutive six month period, commonly known as the boards collection period.8 All cases where the candidate is the primary surgeon during this six month period are eligible for possible selection as part of the ABOS Part-II formal examination, which consists of 12 cases. This period of time can be stressful for the young surgeon and, anecdotally, many surgeons are very cautious about surgical indications during this time period. In fact, the scrutiny placed on the candidate surgeon during this time is often so great that it may potentially alter the surgeon's practice habits.

There has been a growing trend for orthopedic surgeons to pursue fellowship training, with approximately 90% of current orthopedic surgery trainees planning to complete at least 1 fellowship. Some trainees consider completing 2 fellowships, commonly in related specialties such as hand surgery and shoulder and elbow surgery, or orthopedic sports medicine and shoulder and elbow surgery.7,10 Notably, the shoulder and elbow subspecialty is one of the newer fellowship opportunities, with only 34 American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons accredited orthopedic fellowship programs. However, this number continues to grow as the subspecialty increases in popularity.6 As the subspecialty continues to grow, there are more young shoulder and elbow surgeons in the field than ever before. There are rapid advancements in shoulder arthroplasty and arthroscopic shoulder surgery that make this subspecialty prone to changes in surgeon practice.

There is little data regarding the habits of new practicing orthopedic surgeons and their surgical preferences during the boards collection period for the ABOS Part-II. As such, the purpose of our study was to conduct a comprehensive survey analysis regarding the practice habits of board-eligible orthopedic surgeons specializing in shoulder and elbow surgery. We hypothesize that surgeons would opt out of most complex surgical cases and controversial treatment options during the time of boards case collection.

Materials and methods

Survey distribution and design

An anonymous 27-question survey was created through Microsoft Forms and distributed to all board-eligible orthopedic surgeons through the American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons (a total of 470 surgeons). One reminder e-mail was sent to all eligible participants 60 days after initiation of survey distribution. After 120 days, the survey was closed. Responses from participants were included if they answered all 27 questions. Incomplete surveys were excluded from analysis.

Survey questions

A complete list of survey questions can be viewed in Supplemental Table 1. Respondents were able to choose from the following answer choices for each procedure question: “No indication for this procedure in my practice,” “I would not do this during boards collection but otherwise would do it,” or “I would do this during boards collection.” Participants were only allowed to select one answer per question. Questions were written to specifically target aspects of shoulder surgery that were perceived to be controversial or utilizing recent innovations.

Data analysis

Survey responses were collected through the included organizations online services. Similar to previously published survey studies,3,13,17 general statistical analyses were not performed. However, to handle the issue of multiple comparisons when attempting to compare private-practice surgeons to academic or hospital-employed surgeons, the Benjamini-Hochberg False Discovery Rate correction was utilized. Survey respondents were subsequently split by response by 1 researcher. Questions were then grouped by subject of interest, as seen in Supplemental Table 1.

Results

Respondent demographics

A total of 54 board eligible orthopedic surgeons completed the survey. A majority of respondents stated they were in private practice (44.4%) (Fig. 1). The 2 respondents who selected “Other” identified as a health system employee and military orthopedic surgeon, respectively. Between 2017 to 2022, 32 respondents (59.3%) submitted cases for ABOS part II with 41% in the process of submitting cases (Fig. 2). Over half of respondents (63%) were more aggressive with non-operative management during their board collections period. Furthermore, over half of respondents (53.7%) had cases meeting their surgical indications that they did not do during the 6-month board collections period that they otherwise would have performed. Nearly 40% of respondents performed a surgery booked under another primary surgeon during board collections to avoid collecting the case of interest. When comparing overall responses between private practice surgeons to all other surgeons, there were no significant differences observed after Benjamini-Hochberg False Discovery Rate correction.

Type of practice for all survey respondents. ∗Other = health system employee, military.

Responses to general questions. ABOS, American Board of Orthopaedic Surgery.

Procedure preferences

Glenohumeral osteoarthritis

Regarding primary glenohumeral osteoarthritis (OA), nearly all (98%) of respondents stated they would perform an anatomic total shoulder arthroplasty (aTSA) with a stemless humeral head for primary OA during boards collection (Fig. 3). Additionally, 49 (90.7%) respondents said they would also do a reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (rTSA) for primary OA during boards collection. When specifically asked about performing an aTSA with inlay glenoid, this percentage decreased to 33 (61%) respondents. When asked further regarding performing an aTSA with convertible metal backed glenoid for primary OA, only 10 (18.5%) respondents stated they would do this procedure during boards collection. There were heterogeneous responses regarding arthroscopic débridement and ream and run procedures for OA.

Responses to questions pertaining to primary glenohumeral osteoarthritis. aTSA, anatomic total shoulder arthroplasty; rTSA, reverse total shoulder arthroplasty.

Reverse total shoulder arthroplasty

When evaluating responses to rTSA procedures, 52 (96.3%) respondents said they would perform a rTSA with an augmented baseplate during boards collection (Fig. 4). There was further consensus, with 50 (92.3%) respondents stating they would use patient specific instrumentation for anatomic or rTSA and 45 (83.3%) respondents stating they would use a custom-made baseplate for rTSA. Regarding indications for rTSA during boards collection, 100% respondents would perform a rTSA for a proximal humerus fracture and 98% would perform a rTSA for a massive, irreparable rotator cuff tear. With regards to performing a single stage revision for low virulence shoulder arthroplasty prosthetic joint infection, over 50%stated that they would perform this procedure during boards collection.

Responses to questions pertaining to reverse total shoulder arthroplasty. PSI, patient specific instrumentation; aTSA, anatomic total shoulder arthroplasty; rTSA, reverse total shoulder arthroplasty; RCT, rotator cuff tear; PHF, proximal humerus fracture; PJI, prosthetic joint infection.

Rotator cuff tears

When looking at specific questions regarding surgical management of massive irreparable rotator cuff tears, nearly half (48%) of respondents stated that there was no indication for a superior capsular reconstruction (SCR) in their practice (Fig. 5). Similarly, 46.3% of respondents stated there was no indication for a subacromial balloon spacer procedure in their practice. On the other hand, 37 (68.5%) respondents stated they would perform a tendon transfer (such as lower trapezius) during boards collection and 70% stated they would perform an arthroscopic débridement.

Responses to questions pertaining to rotator cuff tears. RCT, rotator cuff tear; SCR, superior capsular reconstruction; SBS, subacromial balloon spacer.

Shoulder instability

For anterior instability, 52 (96%) respondents stated they would perform a remplissage in addition to a Bankart repair during boards collection (Fig. 6). In the setting of significant bone loss, 61% of respondents would perform an arthroscopic allograft bone block (eg, distal tibia) during boards collection. On the contrary, over half (59.3%) of respondents had no indication in their practice for performing an arthroscopic Latarjet in the setting of significant bone loss.

Responses to questions pertaining to shoulder instability.

Biologics

For specific questions regarding biologics in the setting of shoulder surgery, 23 (42.6%) of respondents stated they would not use a bioinductive patch (such as Regeneten) for partial thickness rotator cuff tears during boards collection but otherwise would use it outside of the boards collection period (Fig. 7). Regarding the use of a bioinductive patch or dermal allograft augmentation for full thickness rotator cuff tear, 26 (48.1%) of respondents stated they would do this procedure during boards collection. Thirty-five (64.8%) of respondents stated they had no indication for the use of biologic injectable augmentation (eg, platelet rich plasma) in their practice.

Responses to questions pertaining to biologics. RCR, rotator cuff repair; RCT, rotator cuff tear.

Discussion

This unique survey of board-eligible orthopedic shoulder and elbow surgeons illustrates a variety of practice patterns and how surgeon procedure preference and surgical indications may differ during this specific time in one's practice. Notably, the results from our survey illustrate over half of respondents were more aggressive with non-operative management during their board collections period. Furthermore, over half of respondents had cases meeting their surgical indications that they did not do during the 6-month board collections period that they otherwise would have performed. With regards to specific surgical procedures, there was a variety of surgeon preference based if a procedure was considered universally adapted versus more controversial.

Notable findings from this survey include that over half of respondents had cases meeting their surgical indications that they did not perform, and nearly 40% of surgeons booked a case with another surgeon or senior faculty in order to circumnavigate collecting the case for boards collection. Shoulder and elbow is a unique field that is rapidly advancing in technology and surgical techniques, creating an ever-changing landscape in clinical practice.2,14 The apparent hesitancy in performing certain cases during the boards collection period ultimately may not be in the patient's best interest. The survey findings are a signal that the certification process should account for such technological advancements and growth in surgical techniques. Furthermore, there appears to be a significant number of surgeons who book surgeries with other partners specifically to avoid collection during the boards process. There is a potential ethical consideration to the idea of “circumnavigating” cases to senior partners as this could be considered a barrier to care for patients or delay their treatment. However, this could also be considered a safety for patients as their treatment is being “deferred” to a more experienced surgeon. Although not queried in the survey, these are also likely situations where there is a complex patient scenario or a non-established surgical technique is being used, which may be cases to involve senior partners regardless of if a surgeon is in boards collection or not. These findings signify that the cases collected during the boards collection period are likely not an accurate depiction of a young shoulder and elbow surgeon's future clinical practice. Further modifications of the boards process to better identify which surgeons are practicing safely and appropriately is required.

The survey questions also intentionally highlighted newer and potentially more controversial surgical techniques. The survey responses shed light on which of these procedures have become more or less widely adapted. For all questions regarding management of glenohumeral arthritis and rTSA, there was more consensus for surgeons as to whether they would do the procedure during boards collection, and fewer respondents in the category of not doing the procedure during boards collection specifically. Clear consensus was achieved for procedures such as aTSA with stemless humeral head for primary OA, patient-specific instrumentation for anatomic or rTSA, rTSA for primary OA, rTSA with augmented baseplate, rTSA for massive irreparable rotator cuff tear and rTSA for proximal humerus fractures. All of these are well established in the literature as indications for the described procedure. As a result, a majority of young surgeons are confident in their decision to perform the procedure and be able to justify their reasonings in the setting of ABOS Part II.

When evaluating responses to practice habits for massive rotator cuff tears, there remains a lack of consensus on procedure preferences. Nearly 50% of respondents stated they had no indication for s SCR in their practice, while 40% stated they would do this procedure during boards collection. In a survey study performed by Hankins et al9 evaluating surgeon preferences for graft use, including biologic augmentation and SCR associated with surgical treatment of rotator cuff repair, 51% of surgeons reported a decrease in the volume of SCR use in the past 5 years. In another survey study, Vaudreuil et al15 found that over 75% of polled surgeons performed 0-5 SCR procedures in a 6-month period. For the use of subacromial balloon spacer, 46% of survey respondents stated they had no indication for this procedure in their practice. A recent meta-analysis of 19 studies investigating the subacromial balloon spacer had a high risk of bias, lack of appropriate control, and low levels of evidence.11 These findings illustrate there continues to be no consensus on the appropriate surgical strategy for patients suffering from massive, irreparable rotator cuff tears.

It is worth noting that in the setting of shoulder instability with significant bone loss, 61% of respondents would perform an arthroscopic allograft bone block (eg, distal tibia) during boards collection while over half (59.3%) of respondents had no indication in their practice for performing an arthroscopic Latarjet in the setting of significant bone loss. This could be attributed to the lack of experience or training during fellowship in these advanced arthroscopic stabilization procedures. Many of the current studies discussing arthroscopic Latarjet have come out of Canada and Europe.1,5,12 The all-arthroscopic Latarjet is a reliable but difficult technique, with a steep learning curve and not without complications. Given these limitations, this could preclude young shoulder and elbow surgeons who completed their subspecialized training in the United States to indicate patients for an arthroscopic Latarjet in their practice.

For procedures involving biologics, there was more variability in surgeon response and more surgeons stating that they would not perform the procedure during boards collection or that there was no indication for the procedure altogether in their practice. This could be attributed to the lack of robust clinical evidence to support some of the uses of certain biologics. Autologous biologic agents vary in ease of acquisition, in composition, and likely in effectiveness for treating a variety of shoulder pathologies. Platelet-rich plasma is relatively easy to harvest, with heterogeneous clinical results despite strong basic science support for its use. Cell therapy approaches, including use of bone marrow aspirate concentrate and adipose-derived cells, also have promising basic science evidence but few clinical studies to support their regular use.4,16 Orthopedic surgeons must be well informed early on in their practice when discussing biologic agents with patients. The combination of aggressive marketing and patient demand has led to the indiscriminate use of cell therapy for a wide range of musculoskeletal conditions in the United States. It is paramount that orthopedic surgeons work toward the development of universal practice guidelines and policies for the use of biologic agents.

This study must be interpreted in the context of the following limitations. Given the overall smaller subspecialty and subsequently small number of surgeons who are in boards collection, there were only 54 respondents to the survey. Consequently, there was limited ability to perform statistical analyses on the results or perform sub-group analyses or obtain any statistically significant results. Ultimately, the results from the survey must be interpreted with this in mind as the views of these surgeons may not be representative of all shoulder and elbow orthopedic surgeons. Additionally, the direct relationship and impact of cases done in fellowship with respect to early practice patterns needs further research and is outside the scope of this study. This warrants further investigation as surgeons with sports fellowship training versus shoulder and elbow fellowship training may have differing indications and practice habits that affect their respective ABOS Part II case collections.

Conclusion

This unique survey of board-eligible orthopedic shoulder and elbow surgeons illustrates a variety of practice patterns and how surgeon procedure preference and surgical indications may differ during this s period in one's practice. Notably, over half of respondents were more aggressive with non-operative management during their board collections period. Over half of respondents also had cases meeting their surgical indications that they did not do during the 6-month board collections period that they otherwise would have performed. Our study shows that indications for surgery are adjusted during the monitored board collection period, perhaps indicating a need for further adjustments to the process in the future. Future research in the form of larger-scale surveys and qualitative studies should be performed to further corroborate these findings and justify potential future changes in the boards collection process.
