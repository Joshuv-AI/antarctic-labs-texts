Glucuronoyl Esterase Expressed in Aspen Xylem Affects γ‐Ester Linkages Between Lignin and Glucuronoxylan Reducing Recalcitrance and Accelerating Growth
Authors: Marta Derba‐Maceluch, Laura García Romañach, Mattias Hedenström, Madhusree Mitra, Evgeniy N Donev, János Urbancsok, Zakiya Yassin, Madhavi L Gandla, Pramod Sivan, Jan Šimura, Gerhard Scheepers, Leif J Jönsson, Francisco Vilaplana, Ewa J Mellerowicz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWood is the most abundant renewable natural resource composed of different polysaccharides and lignin, but its utilisation is hampered by intermolecular linkages between these components forming lignin‐carbohydrate complexes (LCCs) causing recalcitrance. The links between glucuronoxylan and the γ‐C of lignin (γ‐ester linkages) are thought to contribute to one‐third of LCCs, but direct evidence for their natural occurrence and their role in recalcitrance has been scarce so far. To address these issues, Phanerochaete carnosa glucuronoyl esterase (PcGCE), hydrolysing γ‐ester linkages, was expressed in cell walls of developing wood in hybrid aspen ( Populus tremula L. × tremuloides Michx.). The enzyme reduced HSQC 2D NMR signals corresponding to the γ‐esters and xylan in dioxane‐extracted LCCs without altering glucuronoxylan content or structure. This increased acid solubility of lignin and decreased lignin content. Reduced wood recalcitrance was shown by increased sugar yields and glucose production rates (by approx. 20%) in saccharification without pretreatment and increased xylan extractability by subcritical water (by approx. 70%). Moreover, trees expressing PcGCE exhibited greater primary and secondary growth. Transcriptomics and metabolomics analyses in developing wood suggested that growth could have been induced by a higher transcription of SMR2 and RPOTmp, which was likely triggered by the secondary cell wall integrity signalling. The results provide evidence for the natural existence of LCC γ‐esters and their significant contribution to lignocellulose recalcitrance. Furthermore, they show that reducing γ‐ester linkages could increase plant productivity.Keywords: ce15, glucuronoxylan, glucuronoyl esterase, hardwood genetic engineering, lignin‐carbohydrate complexes, lignocellulose, secondary cell wall

Introduction

Wood biomass is a versatile renewable resource that can be used to produce biofuels, bioplastics and lignocellulosic bioproducts, but the technological processes to extract different wood components are still costly due to their complexity and inter‐molecular cross‐linking (Simmons et al. 2010; Martín et al. 2022). Bonds between cell wall polysaccharides and lignin, which contribute to the formation of lignin‐carbohydrate complexes (LCCs), are considered a major factor causing the recalcitrance of wood‐derived lignocellulose (Min, Li, et al. 2014; Min, Yang, et al. 2014). Three types of LCCs have been reported in the wood of conifers (softwoods) and dicotyledons (hardwoods): phenyl‐glycosides (PGs) between lignin hydroxyl groups and the anomeric carbon in polysaccharides, benzyl ethers (BEs) between lignin hydroxyls and C‐2, C‐3 or C‐6 carbons in sugar rings of cell wall polysaccharides, and γ‐esters (GEs) formed between a γ‐OH groups of lignin and the carboxylic acid functional group in the 4‐O‐methyl glucuronic acid (MeGlcA) decorations in glucuronoxylan (Tarasov et al. 2018; Giummarella et al. 2019; Terrett and Dupree 2019). Characterisation of bonds in LCCs has been difficult due to their relatively low abundance, a need for harsh analytical procedures for LCC isolation such as ball milling, and the overlapping NMR signals from these bonds and other bonds occurring in the wood. Nevertheless, several papers reported quantification of the different LCC linkages based on different NMR analyses (Watanabe and Koshijima 1988; Balakshin et al. 2011; Yuan et al. 2011; Min, Li, et al. 2014; Min, Yang, et al. 2014; Giummarella and Lawoko 2016) and the α‐ether link to O6 in mannose has recently been established unequivocally (Nishimura et al. 2018).

Most authors agree that while BE and PG linkages are well‐supported by NMR evidence, the identification of GE linkages and their quantification is more difficult (Giummarella et al. 2019; Terrett and Dupree 2019). However, there is a considerable body of indirect evidence supporting the natural occurrence of GE bonds. First, the biosynthesis of MeGlcA side chains was shown to contribute to lignocellulose recalcitrance, as mutants lacking these side chains have increased xylan extractability and glucose (Glu) and xylose (Xyl) yields in enzymatic saccharification (Mortimer et al. 2010; Lyczakowski et al. 2017). Interestingly, the post‐synthetic removal of MeGlcA decorations from glucuronoxylan residing in the cell wall did not increase enzymatic Glc or Xyl yields or affect GE bonds in LCCs, indicating that their formation likely occurs prior to the deposition of glucuronoxylan in the cell wall (Derba‐Maceluch et al. 2023). Second, many wood‐decomposing microbes have glucuronoyl esterases (GCEs) classified in the esterase family ce15 that hydrolyse GE bonds between glucuronoxylan and lignin (Špániková and Biely 2006; Biely et al. 2015; Biely 2016; Hüttner et al. 2017; Ernst et al. 2020). When applied to LCC fractions from spruce or birch, the recombinant GCEs originating from different fungi have been shown to generate carboxylic acid residues with a reduction in the molecular size of LCCs (Arnling Bååth et al. 2016) and to greatly improve the release of MeGlcA by α‐glucuronidases (Raji et al. 2021). These analyses estimate that approximately 30%–40% of MeGlcA side chains of glucuronoxylan could be GE‐bonded to lignin in hardwoods.

Based on these results, expression of GCEs in planta was considered a strategy allowing more efficient lignocellulose processing. First attempts to apply this strategy in Arabidopsis thaliana , using Phanerochaete carnosa GCE (PcGCE) under control of the 35S CaMV promoter, indeed increased xylan extractability while reducing cell wall cross‐linking (Tsai et al. 2012). A similar strategy applied in a hardwood species, hybrid aspen ( Populus tremula L. × tremuloides Michx.) did improve glucan conversion (Gandla et al. 2015). However, in both cases the constitutive expression of PcGCE led to activation of plant defence responses and premature leaf senescence that ultimately affected plant growth (Tsai et al. 2017; Donev et al. 2021). Interestingly, these adverse effects were not dependent on the enzymatic activity of PcGCE, and they could be completely avoided by rather expressing the enzyme during secondary wall deposition using a wood‐specific promoter (WP) (Ratke et al. 2015) than ectopically (Donev et al. 2021). These findings make GCEs an interesting target for expression in planta provided that the positive effects on reduction of wood recalcitrance could be demonstrated in WP:PcGCE transgenic plants. Here, we have analysed the effects of expressing PcGCE in the wood‐forming tissues of hybrid aspen by investigating wood biomass composition and recalcitrance, plant growth, transcriptional changes in the cambial region, hormone and metabolite contents, to evaluate this transgenic modification in detail. These analyses provided evidence for the natural occurrence of GE bonds in LCCs and their important role in mediating wood recalcitrance in a hardwood species.

Results

Transgenic Lines Carrying WP : PcGCE Construct Express the Transgene Specifically in Secondary‐Walled Developing Xylem

PcGCE under control of the WP (WP:PcGCE) was introduced to hybrid aspen as described in the accompanying paper (Donev et al. 2021). Its transcripts were confirmed in the developing xylem cells depositing secondary wall (subsequently denoted “xylem”) of three selected lines, 8, 9 and 14, where line 8 had the highest expression according to the RT‐PCR analysis (Figure 1a). Expression in the primary‐walled developing wood tissues (denoted “cambium”) isolated from the inner surface of the peeled bark and containing mostly cambium, some secondary phloem and expanding xylem cells was negligible in the transgenic lines, and the presence of PcGCE transcripts in the wild type (WT) samples of either tissue was not detected. RNA‐sequencing in lines 8, 14 and WT confirmed these results (Figure S1). GCE activity in cell wall‐bound protein fraction using methyl 4‐O‐methyl‐D‐glucopyranuronate substrate of GCEs (Duranová et al. 2009), mimicking the GE‐linked glucuronoxylan in LCCs, was detected in the xylem of all three transgenic lines, and no activity was observed in the WT (Figure 1b). This proves that active PcGCE protein has been targeted to cell walls of transgenic plants, specifically in cells forming secondary walls.

Wood Chemical Analyses Reveal Increases in Lignin Solubility and Cellulose Content and Decreases in S‐Lignin and (Me)GlcA Contents in PcGCE‐Expressing Lines

In order to assess changes in major cell wall components, the sequential extraction of wood powder following the protocol illustrated in Figure 2a was performed. The fraction of extractives and hemicelluloses determined by weight (Gandla et al. 2015) was not affected by the expression of PcGCE (Figure 2b). Moreover, the matrix sugar composition analysed by methanolysis‐TMS derivatization in the extractive‐free, de‐starched wood did not indicate any change in Xyl content (Figure 2c), indicating that GCE did not affect wood glucuronoxylan content. There was, however, a 14% increase in Glc content, which could represent amorphous cellulose, and small decreases in glucuronic acid (GlcA), MeGlcA and mannose, indicating some changes in the acid‐soluble matrix representing mostly wood hemicelluloses in the transgenic lines. Also, the amounts of acid‐resistant lignin (Klason), acid‐soluble lignin, total lignin and crystalline cellulose (Updegraff) determined in extractive‐free wood by spectrometry were altered (Figure 2b). Transgenic lines presented an average increase of 20% (of WT content) in acid‐soluble lignin, an average decrease of 33% in Klason lignin, and an average decrease of 28% in total lignin. Thus, the fraction of acid‐soluble lignin increased by 69% in transgenic lines on average, indicating increased lignin solubility. Updegraff cellulose content increased by 12% (of WT content) in transgenic lines. The recovery of the measured wood components was 106% on average for all genotypes, and it was not affected by the transgenic modification. Pyrolysis‐GC/MS (Py‐GC/MS) analysis (Gerber et al. 2012) showed decreases in S‐lignin content in transgenic lines compared to WT only in the extractive‐free wood and in the residue after hemicellulose removal, but not in the wood samples containing extractives (Figure S2). This indicates that it was the polymeric S‐lignin content that decreased in transgenic lines.

Since wood chemistry in hardwoods is highly dependent on the fraction of tension wood (TW) (Mellerowicz and Gorshkova 2012) and knowing that the greenhouse‐grown trees always produce some TW even when grown upright, we analysed the occurrence of TW in transgenic and WT samples by near‐infrared (NIR)–based modelling (Renström et al. 2025) supplemented by measurements of cellulose microfibril angle (MFA) and the longitudinal modulus of elasticity (MOE) using SilviScan. Representative images of TW distribution in the samples suggested an increased occurrence of TW in transgenic samples (Figure 2d), which was supported by a decreased cellulose MFA and increased MOE (Table S1). Other measured SilviScan traits were not altered in transgenic lines.

PcGCE Increases Xylan Extractability Without Affecting Its Structure

Since PcGCE is expected to hydrolyse GE linkages between glucuronoxylan and lignin, we tested if the extractability of these components from lignocellulose was changed in the transgenic lines. Subcritical water extraction analysis that maintains ester linkages and allows isolation of native polymers (Sivan et al. 2023) showed that the transgenic samples yielded substantially more glucuronoxylan compared to WT, with gains over 90% and over 70% in 20‐ and 30‐min extracts, respectively (Figure 2e). The OLIMP profiling of extracted xylan did not show any significant differences between transgenic and WT samples (Figure S3). Thus, PcGCE greatly increased the extractability of glucuronoxylan without affecting its structure.

PcGCE Alters Lignocellulose Decomposition by DFRC Treatment

To study the fine changes in lignin structure caused by PcGCE, we studied differences in monomers released from the extractive‐free wood between transgenic and WT samples by the derivatization followed by reductive cleavage (DFRC) treatment, which liberates ether‐bound monomers from lignin and carbohydrates while conserving the ester bonds (Regner et al. 2018; Younes and Grasset 2020). Although no (Me)GlcA ester‐bound lignin monomers could be detected by DFRC, similarly to previous reports, we observed considerable differences in the spectrum of liberated monomers between transgenic and WT samples. Out of 69 identified compounds, the content of 49 compounds significantly differed in abundance between the genotypes (Figure 3; Table S2). Among them, several lignin and phenylpropanoid‐related monomers were more abundant in the transgenic lines, indicating increased accessibility or increased content of cleavable ether bonds in transgenic lines. Some sugar‐related compounds, including arabino‐hexose, ribopyranoside and D‐glucal, were more abundant in WT, while others (D‐galactopyranoside, xylopyranose and xylopyranoside) were more abundant in the WP:PcGCE lines. The higher release of Xyl and derivatives from transgenic samples is in line with increased xylan extractability demonstrated by subcritical water extraction (Figure 2e). We also detected two saturated fatty acids, C16:0 and C18:0, which were less abundant in transgenic lines (Figure 3b). These decreases likely correspond to decreased (Me)GlcA content (Figure 2c) since this xylan substituent determines the fractions of wall‐bound lipids in the wood (Derba‐Maceluch et al. 2023). Overall, the DFRC data indicate that the presence of PcGCE in the wood increases the susceptibility of lignin to decomposition.

PcGCE Affects LCC Composition and γ‐Ester NMR Signals

To test if the activity of PcGCE affected LCCs in the wood of transgenic plants, we isolated the LCC fraction from extractive‐free wood powder using dioxane (Giummarella et al. 2019) and analysed it using heteronuclear single quantum coherence (HSQC) two‐dimensional nuclear magnetic resonance (2D‐NMR). In the spectral region of GE, three peaks were detected (Figure 4a), of which one was annotated as acetylated or p‐hydroxybenzoylated lignin units (Yuan et al. 2011; Balakshin et al. 2011) overlapping with triglycerides (TAGs; Stark et al. 2010; Derba‐Maceluch et al. 2023) and cinnamyl alcohol (Kim and Ralph 2010) denoted as “GEs/TAG/CA”, one corresponded to acetylated or p‐hydroxybenzoylated lignin units overlapping with TAGs denoted as ‘GEs/TAG’, and the third peak partially overlapping with GEs/TAG corresponded to LCC GE signal (Balakshin et al. 2011). The integral of this peak could nevertheless be determined and it followed the same trend as the MeGlcA peak, which supports its assignment. NMR spectra corresponding to lignin, carbohydrates (including GE) and lipids are shown in Figure 4b. Integrated peaks normalised to total lignin signals (Figure 4c) show that the transgenic LCC fraction was depleted in xylan, as supported by the decrease in several glucuronoxylan‐related peaks (MeGlcA, Xyl2,3, Xyl2, Xyl3, Xyl, and acetate), and in LCC GE, as supported by decreases in LCC GE peaks. Moreover, the lignin composition was altered in the LCC fraction of transgenic plants, as evidenced by a decrease in S units, which reflected a decrease in S‐lignin units in the wood detected by the Py‐GC/MS (Figure S2), and an increase in G units, resulting in a decreased S/G ratio.

PcGCE Affects Saccharification

To study if a decrease in GE bonds in LCCs and an increased lignocellulose extractability influenced the release of sugars from the lignocellulosic biomass, the ground wood was subjected to enzymatic saccharification either without any pretreatment or after a dilute acid pretreatment (Gandla et al. 2015). The principal component analysis (PCA) of all analysed saccharification traits, including the yields of different sugars and glucose production rates, revealed a clear separation of transgenic and WT samples in saccharification without pretreatment along the PC1 component that covered over 80% of total variation among the samples (Figure 5a). A less clear separation of the transgenic and WT samples was observed for traits characterising saccharification with acid pretreatment when considering the two main components covering approximately 80% of variation. This indicates that PcGCE has a strong effect on the recalcitrance of untreated biomass, but acid pretreatment removes this effect. Based on the Glc production rate and yield without pretreatment, this effect was positive with average gains of over 20% and 15%, respectively (Figure 5b,c). The yields of other sugars were also increased in transgenic lines with gains of 16% for Xyl, 22% for galactose (Gal), 28% for arabinose (Ara) and over 40% for mannose (Man). In saccharification with acid pretreatment, the ester linkages are expectedly hydrolysed by the harsh pretreatment conditions and therefore no benefit of PcGCE was expected. There were, however, some minor changes noted in transgenic plants, including decreased Glc production rate and increased Man yield in pretreatment liquid.

Increased Growth of Transgenic WP : PcGCE Lines Is Associated With Changes in Gene Expression, Hormones and Metabolites in Wood‐Forming Tissues

Previous studies with transgenic aspen expressing PcGCE showed that this protein induces stress responses, slow secondary growth and accelerated leaf senescence when expressed ectopically, and that these negative effects are eliminated when the enzyme is expressed under the control of the WP promoter active in developing wood during secondary wall deposition (Gandla et al. 2015; Donev et al. 2021). Moreover, the lines carrying WP:PcGCE had slightly increased height (Donev et al. 2021; Urbancsok et al. 2025). Considering the potential biotechnological value of WP:PcGCE plants, we have analysed their primary and secondary growth in more detail. The greenhouse‐grown transgenic plants exhibited not only the height and internode length increase compared to WT, indicating a higher rate of primary shoot growth, but also increased basal stem diameter, indicating greater secondary growth (Figure 6a). The resulting stem volume was increased by 30% on average in the three analysed transgenic lines. WP:PcGCE transgenic plants had also increased total root biomass. The greenhouse‐grown transgenic plants were healthy, and no sign of premature leaf senescence or stress responses was ever evident (Figure 6b). This was consistently observed in three independent greenhouse experiments. Inspection of stem transverse sections revealed normal wood anatomy and an increased xylem cell production by the vascular cambium (Figure 6c,d).

To have more insights into the mechanism of increased wood production by transgenic lines, we have carried out transcriptomics, hormonomics and metabolomics analyses in the primary‐ (‘cambium’) and secondary‐walled developing wood (‘xylem’) tissues of the two most highly expressing lines, 8 and 14. In line 8, only seven genes were differentially expressed in the cambium and the same number was differentially expressed in the xylem considering p adj < 0.05 and log2fold change > |0.5| (Figure 7a; Table S3). Considerably more genes were found to be differentially expressed in line 14, 52 in the cambium and 1591 in the xylem, in agreement with the stronger growth phenotype of this line. Consequently, only nine genes were found to be differentially expressed in common in the two transgenic lines considering both tissues. Six of them were upregulated by PcGCE and three were downregulated (Figure 7b; Table S4). Two of the upregulated genes are annotated as non‐coding RNA, and their function is unknown. Both these genes were expressed only in transgenic lines, in both cambium and xylem tissues, with almost no expression in the WT. The third upregulated gene, found in the cambium only, was PSBA encoding a photosystem II reaction centre protein A. Noteworthy, the cambium samples included cells with green chloroplasts. Nuclear‐encoded organellar RNA polymerase RPOTmp was also upregulated in transgenic lines but only in the xylem. The transgenic lines also showed a higher expression of TRICHOME BIREFRINGENCE‐LIKE 10 (TBL10) in both tissues, encoding a Golgi‐localised acetyl‐transferase involved in rhamnogalacturonan I and homogalacturonan acetylation (Stranne et al. 2018; Zhong et al. 2024). The sixth upregulated gene (in the xylem) encoded an ankyrin repeat protein of unknown function. Among the three downregulated genes, there was a homologue of Arabidopsis MILDEW RESISTANCE LOCUS O 5 (MLO5) involved in Ca2+ transmembrane transport and signalling (Gao et al. 2022) that was highly downregulated in both cambium and xylem of transgenic lines, two genes downregulated in the xylem only, a homologue of cationic amino acid transporter CAT9 and a gene with high similarity to SIAMESE‐RELATED 2 (SMR2) encoding a cyclin‐dependent kinase inhibitor (Yamada et al. 2022).

According to the hormonomics analysis (Šimura et al. 2018), only a few hormones were affected in transgenic lines (Figure 7c). In the cambium, the concentration of cis‐12‐oxo‐phytodienoic acid (cis‐OPDA), a precursor of jasmonic acid (JA), was increased. In the xylem, the concentrations of auxins, IAA and its oxidised form (oxIAA), were increased, whereas that of cis‐zeatin (cZ), an active cytokinin, was decreased in transgenic lines compared to WT.

Among the metabolites affected in transgenic lines (Figure 7d), there were secondary metabolites (tremulacin and quinate) and myo‐inositol, which increased in abundance in the xylem of transgenic lines compared to WT.

Discussion

The study of wood in hybrid aspen expressing PcGCE under control of WP provided valuable insights on the impact of GCE on LCCs, wood cell walls, lignocellulose properties and plant growth. The HSQC 2D‐NMR analysis of dioxane‐isolated lignin (Figure 4) provided support for the natural occurrence of GE bonds in LCCs based on the cleavage of these bonds by PcGCE in vivo. These results fully confirm the previous findings on the action of recombinant fungal CGEs produced in bacteria on isolated LCC fractions (Arnling Bååth et al. 2016; Raji et al. 2021) and extend them, demonstrating that PcGCE reduces GE LCC bonds existing already within native plant cell walls without altering glucuronoxylan content or structure. Reduction of GE bonds by secondary wall‐targeted PcGCE was accompanied by reduced xylan content in LCCs (Figure 4c) and reduced xylan‐lignin cross‐linking as revealed by the DFRC treatment (Figure 3a,c). These changes correlated with and likely caused an increased lignin solubility in acid (Figure 2b), increased xylan extractability by subcritical water (Figure 2e) and decreased S‐lignin subunits content that was only detected after extractives removal (Figure S2), thus indicating that PcGCE affected the incorporation of S‐lignin units into the supramolecular lignin‐polysaccharide structure of the cell wall. All these changes in lignocellulose properties are expected as a direct consequence of PcGCE enzymatic activity in wood cell walls, and they provide support for the important role of GE bonds in lignocellulose integrity. They also prove that substantial and desirable alterations in lignocellulose properties can be achieved with minor changes in wood cell wall composition (Figure 2b,c; Figure S2), by targeting specific key linkages responsible for cell wall integrity.

Effects of PcGCE controlled by WP on wood chemistry were very different from those of ectopically expressed enzyme by 35S CaMV promoter. Ectopic expression caused a substantial increase in Klason lignin, concerning both S and G units, no change in acid‐soluble lignin, a decrease in the extractives and a decrease in cellulose contents (Gandla et al. 2015). In contrast, the wood of WP:PcGCE lines had a reduced content of Klason lignin with a reduction in S lignin units, an unaltered content of extractives and increased contents of acid‐soluble lignin and crystalline cellulose (Figure 2b). The increased lignin content in 35S:PcGCE lines was attributed to the massive induction of immune stress responses observed in these lines (Donev et al. 2021). The matrix sugar composition, on the other hand, was similarly affected in WP:PcGCE and 35S:PcGCE plants, showing a small decrease in (Me)GlcA in extractive‐free wood (Figure 2c; Gandla et al. 2015). This could be a direct effect of PcGCE activity as reduced cross‐linking in the transgenic plants could expose (Me)GlcA to degradation (likely non‐enzymatic), or an indirect effect mediated by cell wall integrity sensing and regulation of cell wall biosynthesis. Although we did not observe any decrease in transcripts of aspen homologues to Arabidopsis main secondary wall xylan glucuronyltransferases (AtGUX1 and AtGUX2) (Lee et al. 2012; Bromley et al. 2013), line 14 did exhibit a strong downregulation of a homologue of AtGUX4 encoding a xylan glucuronyltransferase (Rennie et al. 2012), PtGUX4‐A, seen also in other xylan‐modified transgenic lines (Table S4), which could support an indirect effect of PcGCE expression on xylan glucuronidation.

The positive effect of PcGCE (expressed by WP) on wood properties is further supported by improved saccharification without pretreatment (Figure 5). This could allow for reducing the combined severity of the acid pretreatment, leading to a reduction of environmental hazards as well as costs in lignocellulose processing, making the entire saccharification process more affordable. We suggest the wood of WP:PcGCE plants could be well suited for biorefinery applications targeting intact hemicellulose, pure lignin, and crystalline cellulose due to reduced lignin‐hemicellulose cross‐linking and increased crystalline (Updegraff) cellulose content in the transgenic lines (Figures 2, 3, 4). The latter is likely related to an increased tendency for TW formation (Figure 2d, Table S1), which was also observed in other xylan‐modified lines with increased growth and is attributed to indirect transgene effects (Urbancsok et al. 2025). Improved saccharification in WP:PcGCE lines is largely attributed to their lower lignin content. The decrease in lignin could be a direct result of a reduction in the number of GE bonds in transgenic lines or an indirect effect involving TW induction.

Increased productivity is an important goal of crop improvement programmes; therefore, better primary and secondary growth of greenhouse‐grown WP:PcGCE trees (Figure 6) is promising from a biotechnological perspective, but the performance of such transgenic lines needs to be verified by field trials. Greenhouse‐grown WP:PcGCE lines never showed any undesirable effects on growth and development, although some mild stress could be experienced in these trees based on their slightly increased DAB staining in the leaves indicating elevated ROS content (Donev et al. 2021) and increased amounts of secondary metabolites such as tremulacin and quinate in developing wood (Figure 7). It is therefore possible that WP‐driven PcGCE leads to priming to other stresses—a hypothesis that should be further tested. This is in a strong contrast to the detrimental effects caused by the same transgene expressed ectopically, which induces the activation of defence responses and premature senescence (Tsai et al. 2017; Donev et al. 2021). These responses are independent of the enzymatic activity of PcGCE but likely caused by the unfolded protein response (UPR) stress since several UPR marker genes were upregulated in the transgenic plants (Donev et al. 2021), opening a window for testing other GCE enzymes.

The basis for the increased growth of trees carrying WP:PcGCE is not yet understood, but we and others previously observed that the xylan modifications induced during secondary wall formation sometimes enhance growth (Biswal et al. 2015; Derba‐Maceluch et al. 2015; Ratke et al. 2018; Urbancsok et al. 2025). This reaction could be linked to increased stem swaying, as a moderate stem shaking was shown to accelerate plant growth (Urbancsok et al. 2023). However, there was very little overlap in transcriptomic changes between shaken and WP:PcGCE plants (< 1% of DE genes affected in WP:PcGCE line 14; Table S5) suggesting that a different signalling pathways could be responsible for growth stimulation in these cases. Among a few genes affected in the developing wood of WP:PcGCE lines, SMR2 and RPOTmp could be related to their increased xylem production (Figure 6d). SMR2 regulates cell cycle progression by inhibiting the G2‐M transition (Yamada et al. 2022). Its downregulation in transgenic lines (Figure 7b, Table S4) could therefore shorten the cell cycle duration, increasing xylem production. SMR2 was also downregulated in xylan‐modified lines with increased growth (Urbancsok et al. 2025). It was also downregulated in aspen lines expressing a GH10 endoxylanase under the control of WP, which showed increased secondary phloem production despite decreased xylem production and inhibited primary stem growth (Sivan et al. 2025) (Table S4). This suggests that SMR2 could regulate both xylem and phloem production. Moreover, downregulation of a closely related gene, SMR3, in line 14 xylem (Table S4), which was also observed in GH10‐expressing lines, concomitant with upregulation of a gene encoding CYCB1;2—a target of SMRs—strengthens the conclusion that a shortened cell cycle could be responsible for increased cambial growth in WP:PcGCE lines. RPOTmp is another gene known to affect growth (Baba et al. 2004; Gorbenko et al. 2024). It was upregulated in the xylem of WP:PcGCE lines but not altered in other xylan‐modified lines (Figure 7b, Table S4) pointing to its specific involvement in the response to PcGCE. This gene encodes a dual‐targeted (mitochondrial and chloroplastic) nuclear‐encoded RNA polymerase, involved in organellar transcription, that stimulates primary growth in Arabidopsis thaliana . Revealing its role in secondary growth awaits further studies. These data as well as a large overlap of transcriptomic changes between WP:PcGCE line 14 and other xylan‐modified lines studied before (Table S4) support an indirect effect of PcGCE activating secondary wall integrity signalling (Sivan et al. 2025; Urbancsok et al. 2025). Among the signalling‐related genes that could be involved in such a response, MLO5, which was affected in both WP:PcGCE lines, encodes a Ca2+ channel and mediates calcium signalling during fertilisation and stress (Gao et al. 2022) is particularly intriguing since it was also downregulated in the WP:GH67 line with increased growth (Urbancsok et al. 2025). GH67 encodes an α‐glucuronidase that strips (Me)GlcA side chains from xylan, affecting lignin‐associated suberin‐like lipids without affecting GE bonds to lignin (Derba‐Maceluch et al. 2023). Although PcGCE does not hydrolyse xylan (Me)GlcA side chains, the WP:PcGCE lines had reduced matrix (Me)GlcA, possibly through an indirect effect on GUX4, as discussed above. Concomitantly, cell wall‐associated fatty acids (C16:0 and C18:0) were reduced in WP:PcGCE lines (Figures 2c, 3b). MLO5 could therefore be specifically responsible for the signalling of secondary wall defects related to the integrity of cell wall lipids. The support for the activation of the secondary wall integrity pathway in WP:PcGCE lines is also provided by the observed increase in cytokinins consistent with higher mitotic activity. The increase in cytokinins was also observed in other lines with different xylan defects in secondary walls (Sivan et al. 2025; Urbancsok et al. 2025).

Conclusions

This work demonstrated that fungal GCEs from family ce15 expressed specifically during secondary wall formation and targeted to the apoplast reduced GE bonds in LCCs. Reduced GE bonds substantially increased xylan and lignin extractability and reduced xylan–lignin cross‐linking. Wood biomass enzymatic saccharification without pretreatment was also improved. This demonstrates the natural occurrence of GE bonds in LCCs and their importance for wood recalcitrance in a hardwood species.

Secondary walls‐targeted GCE induced small changes in wood composition while preserving the xylan content, and it had positive effects on plant primary and secondary growth, increasing wood production. Some of these effects could be mediated through an altered expression of genes involved in growth and Ca2+ signalling, supporting the concept of secondary wall integrity signalling.

Materials and Methods

Generation of Transgenic Aspen Lines and Growth Conditions

Phanareochete carnosa Burt glucuronoyl esterase (PcGCE) cDNA (NCBI accession: JQ972915; Tsai et al. 2012), with its native signal peptide replaced by the signal peptide of PttCel9B3 (GenBank accession AY660968.1) as described previously (Gandla et al. 2015), was used for the cloning of the WP:PcGCE construct. The transgene in the pENTR/D‐TOPO vector (pENTR Directional TOPO Cloning Kit, Invitrogen/Thermo Fisher Scientific, Carlsbad, USA) was subcloned into the vector pK‐pGT43B‐GW7 (Ratke et al. 2015) containing the WP promoter, and this vector was used to transform hybrid aspen, Populus tremula L. × tremuloides Michx., clone T89 as described by (Donev et al. 2021). Three highly expressing lines were selected for detailed characterisation.

Plant Phenotyping at the WIWAM Platform

Clonally propagated saplings were planted in soil (K‐jord, Hasselfors Garden AB, Örebro, Sweden) in 7 L plastic pots, watered to 25%–30% (v:v) soil moisture content, covered with transparent 8 L plastic bags, and grown for 9 weeks in the phenotyping platform (WIWAM Conveyor, custom designed by SMO, Eeklo, Belgium) as described by Wang et al. (2022) under an 18 h/6 h (day/night) light regime with 160–230 μmol/m2/s light intensity during the day, 22°C/18°C temperature, and the average air relative humidity of 60%. White light (FL300 LED Sunlight v1.1) and far‐red light (FL100 LED custom‐made, 725–735 nm) lamps from Senmatic A/S (Søndersø, Denmark) were used for illumination. After 2 weeks, the bags were removed, and plants were watered automatically based on weight, and their height was automatically measured.

At the end of the 3‐month experiment, stem diameters at the stem base and aboveground fresh weights were recorded. Belowground biomass was determined by weighing cleaned and air‐dried roots. A 30 cm‐long stem segment above internode 37 was debarked, frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −70°C for RNA, metabolomics and hormonomics analyses. The stem below was used for determining internode length. The 38th and 39th internodes were used for microscopy analyses. The four‐cm‐long bottom segment was used for SilviScan analysis, and the remaining stem was debarked and freeze‐dried for 48 h for wood chemistry analyses.

Transgene Expression

Developing xylem tissues scrapped from the stems after bark peeling were used for RNA extractions as described in (Ratke et al. 2015) using three biological replicates. cDNA was synthesised using the Bio‐Rad iScript cDNA Synthesis kit. Quantitative polymerase chain reactions (qPCRs) were performed using LightCycler 480 SYBR Green I (Roche, Indianapolis, USA) with a 10 L reaction volume in a C1000 Touch thermal cycler (Bio‐Rad Laboratories, Hercules, USA). The PCR programme was 95°C for 5 min, then 50 cycles of 95°C for 30 s, 60°C for 15 s, and 72°C for 30 s. UBQ‐L (Potri.005G198700) and CYP (Potri.004G168800) were selected as reference genes chosen from four different genes based on GeNorm (Vandesompele et al. 2002). Primers are listed in Table S6. The relative expression level was calculated according to Pfaffl (2001).

Glucuronoyl Esterase Activity

Wall‐bound proteins were extracted from frozen, scraped developing xylem and analysed as previously described by Gandla et al. (2015) except that the protein extract was desalted and concentrated using a column (Pall Nanosep 30 k Omega) against 50 mM sodium phosphate buffer according to the manufacturer's instructions. Also, 5 mM methyl 4‐O‐methyl‐D‐glucopyranuronate was used as substrate (kindly provided by Prof. Peter Biely, Slovak Academy of Sciences), and 5 μg of desalted protein extract was used per reaction. Remaining ester bonds in the reaction mixture were quantified according to Hestrin (1949).

Preparation of Wood Powder

Internode 40–60 was debarked and freeze‐dried (36 h) and ground to a rough powder using an SM 300 Cutting Mill with a 2 mm sieve (Retsch, Haan, Germany). The rough powder was then sieved using an AS 200 vibratory sieve shaker (Retsch) and divided into the following particle size fractions: < 50, 50–100, 100–500 and > 500 μm.

Pretreatment and Enzymatic Saccharification

Wood powder with a particle size of 100–500 μm from three different trees was pooled as one biological replicate. The number of biological replicates (pooled samples) was 7, 5, 4 and 4 for WT, line 8, line 9 and line 14, respectively. Saccharification with and without pretreatment was performed in technical duplicates using 50 mg of milled wood according to Gandla et al. (2015).

Wood Chemical Analysis

Extractive‐free wood was prepared according to Ona et al. (1995) starting with 150 mg of wood powder by sequential extraction in a Soxhlet apparatus with 900 mL of ethanol: toluene mixture (1:2; v:v) for 6 h, then 800 mL of 96% (v:v) ethanol for 4 h, and finally with 800 mL of distilled water for 2 h. The extracted wood powder was dried at 103°C overnight, and three technical replicates of each sample were used to analyse hemicellulose, lignin and cellulose contents, whereas four technical replicates were used for matrix monosaccharide composition analysis.

A portion (75 mg) of extractive‐free wood from each biological replicate was subjected to sequential hemicellulose extraction with 4% and 24% KOH at 4°C as described by Gandla et al. (2015), using 0.5 mL of solvent per 10 mg of material. The pellet was washed with water, then with 30 mM sodium acetate buffer, and finally with absolute ethanol, and dried in a desiccator overnight. Hemicellulose content was determined by weighing the residue.

Acid‐soluble and acid‐insoluble lignin (Klason lignin) content was determined according to Theander and Westerlund (1986), and crystalline cellulose was quantified according to Updegraff (1969), where the glucan content was determined with the anthrone method (Scott Jr. and Melvin 1953).

Monosaccharide composition by methanolysis and trimethylsilyl (TMS) derivatization was performed using 500 μg of de‐starched with α‐amylase (Roche 10 102 814 001 α‐amylase) extractive‐free wood and 10 μg of inositol (internal standard) as described in Gandla et al. 2015. Silylated monosaccharides were separated on a J&W DB‐5MS column (30 m length, 0.25 mm diameter, 0.25 μm film thickness) (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, California, USA) with the oven programme: 80°C followed by a temperature increase of 20°C/min to 140°C for 2 min, then 2°C/min to 200°C for 5 min, then 30°C/min to 250°C for 5 min.

Pyrolysis Coupled to Gas Chromatography/Mass Spectrometry (Py‐GC/MS)

In this study, 21, 15, 14 and 14 trees were analysed in the WT, line 8, line 9 and line14, respectively, using 60 (±10 μg) of extractive‐free wood powder (50–100 μm) in a pyrolyser with an autosampler (Y‐2020iD and AS‐1020E, FrontierLab, Japan) coupled to a GC/MS (7890A/5975C; Agilent Technologies AB, Sweden). The pyrolyzate was analysed according to Gerber et al. (2012).

Subcritical Water Extraction of Xylan and Its Structural Analysis

Wood powder with a particle size of 100–500 μm from eight trees was pooled to obtain 2 g of material for each sample: one sample of line 9, one of line 14, and two samples of WT. The sequential subcritical water extraction procedure was described in detail in Sivan et al. (2023). The amount of recovered xylan in each extract was determined based on triplicate quantifications of Xyl in the original material and in each extract by methanolysis‐TMS.

Xylan structure from 30‐min extracts was analysed by oligosaccharide mass profiling (OLIMP) following depolymerisation with GH30 endo β‐(1,4) glucuronoxylanase (kindly provided by Franz J. St. John, US Forest Service). Details of enzymatic digestion and oligosaccharide detection by electrospray ionisation mass spectrometry (ESI‐MS) are described in Sivan et al. (2024).

LCC Isolation

Wood powder (particle size 100–500 μm) from 15 trees per line was pooled and five technical replicates of 4.2 g were prepared per line. Extractives were removed, as described above. Extractive‐free wood was ball milled using 10 ZrO2 balls at 500 rpm for 10 min, followed by a 10 min break, for five cycles in Pulverisette 7 (Fritsch, Germany). LCCs were isolated from this material by extraction with dioxane: water (96:4; v:v) for 24 h at room temperature, using 10 mL of solvent per g of material following the protocol by Min, Li, et al. (2014). The samples were centrifuged (2500 g for 10 min) and the supernatant was collected, whereas the pellet was extracted two more times using a fresh portion of the same solvent. The extracts were combined, filtered with a glass filter, and mixed with 2.5 volumes of water, and the lignin was precipitated overnight at 4°C, and then continued to precipitate in a rotary evaporator under reduced pressure at 40°C. When the solvent was almost completely evaporated, a portion of 10 mL of water was added, and the evaporation followed by the addition of 10 mL of water was repeated two more times. The final almost dry precipitate was collected and freeze‐dried to give LCCs, which were analysed using NMR spectroscopy.

NMR

For the analysis, 10–15 mg of the LCC samples were transferred to 5 mm NMR tubes and dissolved in 600 μL dimethyl sulfoxide‐D6 before analysis. 2D 1H‐13C heteronuclear single quantum coherence (HSQC) experiments were acquired at 298 K on a Bruker 600 MHz Avance III spectrometer equipped with a 5 mm BBO cryo‐probe. A pulse programme employing adiabatic 13C refocusing and inversion pulses was used (hsqcetgpsisp2.2). Sweep widths of 8.1 ppm in the 1H dimension and 140 ppm in the 13C dimension were used. Sixteen scans were recorded for each of the 256 t 1 increments and the spectra were calibrated using the residual dimethyl sulfoxide peak (δH 2.49 ppm and δH 39.5 ppm). A Gaussian window function was used in F1 and a 90° shifted square sine bell window function was applied in F2. Processing and peak volume integration were performed in Topspin 3.6 (Bruker Biospin, Germany). Peak assignments are based on literature (Kim et al. 2008; Kim and Ralph 2010; Balakshin et al. 2011; Shakeri Yekta et al. 2019; Correia et al. 2020).

Derivatization Followed by Reductive Cleavage (DFRC)

Extractives were removed from 100 mg of wood powder samples (pooled as described above) using a Dionex 200 Accelerated Solvent Extractor (Sunnyvale, California, USA) and a mixture of toluene: ethanol (2:1; v:v), followed by ethanol and water. The solvent‐free wood residues were vacuum‐dried and stored in sealed plastic bags at ambient temperature for later use.

For DFRC analysis (Regner et al. 2018), 10 mg of extractive‐free wood powder was incubated at 50°C for 2 h in a solution made of 0.8 volumes of ~99% acetyl bromide and 9.2 volumes of glacial acetic acid. Solvent was removed using nitrogen stream evaporation and the solids were re‐dissolved in a solvent system composed of dioxane, acetic acid, and water (5:4:1; v:v:v), followed by the addition of 50 mg of powdered zinc. After stirring for 40 min at room temperature, the contents were transferred to a separatory funnel along with 10 mL dichloromethane and 10 mL saturated ammonium chloride. The pH of the mixture was adjusted to < 3.0 using 3% HCl and shaken vigorously to facilitate phase separation. The aqueous layer was extracted three times with 5 mL dichloromethane, and the pooled organic layers were dried using anhydrous Na2SO4 and concentrated via nitrogen evaporation. The resulting material was acetylated using 0.2 mL pyridine and 0.2 mL acetic anhydride in 1.1 mL dichloromethane for 1 h. The sample was dried as above, dissolved in heptane, and analysed by gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS).

GC–MS analysis was performed using a LECO HT GCTOFMS 2010 system (LecoCorp., St Joseph, MI, USA) equipped with a DB‐5MS capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm i.d., 0.25 μm film thickness) (J&W Scientific, Folsom, CA, USA). The oven temperature was programmed as follows: 70°C for 2 min, then increased to 320°C at 20°C/min and held for 8 min. The injector and transfer line were set at 260°C and 250°C, respectively. Helium served as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 1 mL/min. Raw GC–MS data were converted to net CDF format and processed using the SMC‐RDA software (https://www.swedishmetabolomicscentre.se/). Compound identification was achieved by comparing mass spectra and retention indices to standards available in SMC (https://www.swedishmetabolomicscentre.se/), NIST (http://www.nist.gov/srd/nist1a.cfm), MassBank (https://massbank.eu/MassBank/), MoNA (https://mona.fiehnlab.ucdavis.edu/), Golm GMD (http://gmd.mpimp‐golm.mpg.de), and Fiehn (http://fiehnlab.ucdavis.edu/db) libraries, using the NIST MS Search 2.3 software (http://www.nist.gov/srd/nist1a.cfm).

Transcriptomics

RNA was extracted and purified as described by Urbancsok et al. (2023) from primary‐ and secondary‐walled developing xylem of five trees of each line. RNA samples having RNA integrity number (RIN) ≥ 8 were used for cDNA preparation and sequencing at Novogene Co. Ltd. (Cambridge, UK). Quality control and mapping to the Populus trichocarpa (v3.1) transcriptome were carried out by Novogene. Raw counts were used for differential expression analysis in R (v3.4.0) with the Bioconductor (v.3.4) DESeq2 package (v.1.16.1), as previously detailed (Kumar et al. 2019). The best BLAST hits were identified in Arabidopsis thaliana (v11.0).

Metabolomics

Non‐targeted metabolomics analysis was performed at the Swedish Metabolomics Centre using methods described in Urbancsok et al. (2025).

Hormonomics

Samples were extracted, purified, and analysed as described in Šimura et al. (2018). Mass spectrometry analysis of targeted compounds was performed by an UHPLC‐ESI‐MS/MS system comprising a 1290 Infinity Binary LC System coupled to a 6490 Triple Quad LC/MS System with Jet Stream and Dual Ion Funnel technologies (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). The quantification was carried out in Agilent MassHunter Workstation Software Quantitative (Agilent Technologies). The list of all detected hormones is shown in Table S8.

NIR and SilviScan

NIR and SilviScan analyses were performed as described in detail by Donev et al. (2023) and Renström et al. (2025), using six individual trees for each line.

Statistical Analyses

Data were analysed by the analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Dunnett's or Tukey's post hoc tests and contrast analyses comparing all transgenic lines versus WT. For the volcano plots, two‐sided t‐tests were performed comparing transgenic versus WT samples. The analyses were executed in RStudio 4.1.2 (2021‐11‐01), JMP (student edition 18.2.0; http://jmp.com) or Excel.
