Low-beta frequency band neurofeedback training: effects on attentional orientation, executive control, and underlying neural mechanisms
Authors: Meng Zhang, Chengcheng Wei, Can Ding, Jiayi Zhao, Jiahe Sun, Liyue Lin, Yishu Chen, Min li, Xiaokai Li, Ming-Yang Cheng, Jie Zhuang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Neurofeedback Training (NFT) employs real-time feedback on brain activity to empower participants to self-regulate cognitive functions within specific brain regions. Understanding the neurophysiological correlates of NFT is critically important, as these remain largely unclear, especially regard to their neural underpinnings. This study aims to investigate whether and to what extent NFT enhances attentional orientation and executive control in healthy adults, and to explore the potential electrophysiological features associated with these behavioral changes. Participants were divided into two groups: the neurofeedback group (NF, n = 19), who received real-time EEG signal feedback from the F3 electrode with training in the beta1 band (15–18 Hz); and the sham group (sham, n = 18), who watched pre-recorded videos unrelated to brain activity. Both groups underwent pre- and post-intervention assessments, which encompassed attention tasks and EEG data collection. Behavioral results demonstrated that the NF group achieved improvements in attention performance compared to the sham group. These findings were corroborated by event-related potential (ERP) data: post-intervention, the NF group exhibited enhanced N1 amplitudes during attention-orienting tasks and increased N2 amplitudes during executive control tasks. Furthermore, the NF group showed a phased increase in beta1 power during training, reaching statistical significance by the third session, whereas the sham group remained stable. These findings suggest that NFT may, to some extent, modulate attention-related neural activity, thereby potentially contributing to the enhancement of attentional task performance in healthy adults.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12993-026-00351-z.

Introduction

Attention is a fundamental cognitive function, conventionally delineated into three subsystems: alerting, orienting, and executive control. Orienting and executive control facilitate the efficient filtering of distracting information in complex and noisy environments, enabling individuals to focus on goal-relevant stimuli [41]. Studies indicate that attention orienting is primarily associated with activity in the parietal and frontal lobes, whereas the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) are integral components of the executive control network [18, 40]. A pertinent inquiry arises: Could attentional control capacities be substantially enhanced by self-regulating neural activities of these relevant brain regions, particularly via neurofeedback training (NFT). However, the extent to which, and indeed whether, NFT can improve attentional control capacities remains a largely uncharted territory.

Beyond regional brain activity, attention-related event-related potentials (ERPs) also provide crucial physiological indices for understanding cognitive processing. Among these, the N1 component is an ERP elicited during early sensory processing of exogenous stimuli, typically occurring approximately 100 milliseconds after stimulus onset. It reflects early perceptual processing and is also closely associated with perceptual expectation and spatial orienting in attentional processes [33, 37]. Research indicates that N1 amplitudes are significantly enhanced under high perceptual load conditions, such as in the absence of spatial cues, compared to low-load conditions. This modulation suggests that the N1 component serves as a neural index of attentional resource allocation during the orienting process, becoming particularly pronounced when increased target localization demands require more refined sensory gating. The N2 component, which emerges between 200 and 350 milliseconds post-stimulus, is primarily associated with attentional state and cognitive control. Studies have shown that as the probability of No-go stimuli decreases, the amplitude of the N2 increases, with the most pronounced effects observed in the frontal cortex [12]. Additionally, research using visual search paradigms has found that N2 is more sensitive to novel and mismatching stimuli, indicating its involvement in executive control processes and the suppression of irrelevant information during attentional tasks [35].

The beta-band power within the PFC has demonstrated a significant correlation with attentional processes, notably encompassing orienting and executive control mechanisms [5, 6, 29, 42]. Research findings underscore the selective modulation of exogenous selection mechanisms by the beta-band, particularly under circumstances of cognitive conflict [13]. As a result, beta-band activity in the PFC is postulated to play a pivotal role in orchestrating top-down attentional control [2, 46]. Furthermore, investigations involving healthy individuals reveal a robust association between heightened local beta-band amplitude and superior performance in attentional tasks [6, 52].

The beta-band spectrum can be further delineated into two distinct frequency ranges: beta1 (15–18 Hz) and beta2 (12–22 Hz). Notably, the beta1 band, also referred to as the low beta-band, has been specifically implicated in attentional enhancement phenomena as observed in neurofeedback studies [14, 15, 31, 45]. Emerging evidence suggests that augmenting beta1 activity can bolster inhibitory control mechanisms within the realm of attention [23]. Research on alertness in attention has found that activity in the beta1 frequency band of the left prefrontal cortex is significantly associated with changes in attentional alertness, suggesting that beta1 activity in the left prefrontal cortex plays a critical role in the maintenance and control of attention [30]. Moreover, the low beta-band is intricately linked to generalized cortical activation, and targeted training within this frequency range has been demonstrated to mitigate errors in attentional tasks, expedite reaction times, and amplify P300 amplitude [14]. While previous research has established that occipital-parietal alpha oscillations act primarily as a sensory gating mechanism to inhibit task-irrelevant regions, and frontal theta oscillations function mainly as a signal for conflict detection, detection alone is insufficient. Beta oscillations—particularly within frontal regions—play a unique role in the implementation of actual executive control. Research findings underscore the selective modulation of exogenous mechanisms by the beta band, particularly under conditions of cognitive conflict [13]. Consequently, beta-band activity in the PFC is postulated to play a pivotal role in orchestrating top-down attentional control [2, 46]. Specifically, according to the theory proposed by Engel and Fries [16], beta activity is responsible for maintaining the current cognitive set, thereby actively inhibiting distracting stimuli and incorrect response tendencies. Studies involving healthy individuals further support this view, revealing a robust association between enhanced local beta-band amplitude and superior performance in attentional tasks [6, 52]. Therefore, we selected the beta band as our target because our intervention aims to strengthen the participants’ capacity to actively maintain attentional states and resolve conflict, rather than merely enhancing early conflict detection (theta) or passive sensory inhibition (alpha).NFT stands out as a highly effective methodology for augmenting attentional capabilities [14, 34, 43, 50, 51]. Within the NFT paradigm, participants are furnished with real-time feedback on their cerebral oscillatory activity through a computerized interface, empowering them to exert conscious control over specific Electroencephalogram (EEG) rhythms or signal amplitudes within the cerebral cortex [17, 43, 49]. Empirical investigations involving healthy individuals have unveiled a robust positive correlation between heightened beta-band power and superior performance in attentional tasks, with evidence suggesting that increased beta amplitude in targeted brain regions correlates with enhanced task ACC [3, 6, 14, 48, 52]. Nevertheless, the majority of existing research endeavors have predominantly concentrated on specific beta-band frequency ranges (12–15 Hz) or the ratios of beta to alpha and gamma bands, with relatively scant attention directed towards exploring other beta sub-bands and conducting comprehensive analyses of the physiological processes underpinning this phenomenon [22, 24]. Consequently, further inquiry into the effects of NFT on alternative beta-band frequencies is imperative to unravel the neural intricacies of attention and to identify evidence-based strategies for optimizing attentional performance.

The objective of this study is to enhance attentional orienting and executive control capacities through the implementation of NFT. To this end, healthy college students were randomly allocated to one of two experimental conditions: a genuine NF group or a sham neurofeedback control group. Participants in both groups participated in an EEG-based NFT protocol specifically tailored to promote self-regulation of beta1 power within the prefrontal cortex. Following the training, offline analyses were conducted on datasets acquired prior to, during, and subsequent to the intervention. The primary purpose of these analyses was to assess the impact of the NFT intervention on attentional performance and to attempt to explore the potential electrophysiological signatures associated with these behavioral changes. This study hypothesized that neurofeedback training targeting a specific prefrontal frequency band (beta1) would induce the autonomous regulation of endogenous neural activity. Such neurophysiological changes were expected to further optimize the efficiency of neural resource allocation during cognitive processing, thereby leading to significant behavioral improvements in both attentional orientation and executive control performance.

Methods

Participants

Sample size was estimated using G*Power 3.1 [19] Based on prior neurofeedback studies involving healthy adults, we assumed a large effect size (f = 0.5); these studies consistently reported robust physiological and behavioral effects with similar sample sizes [15, 31, 44]. For a repeated-measures ANOVA with two groups and five measurements, assuming an alpha level of 0.05 and a power of 0.80, the required sample size was calculated to be 17 participants per group. A total of 37 healthy participants (18 females, aged 19–25 years; mean age = 20.6) took part in this study. They were right-handed college students with normal or corrected-to-normal vision. None of them had a history of neurological or psychiatric disorders. All participants provided written informed consent and were compensated for their time. All experimental procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee for Scientific Research of Shanghai University of Sport. Participants were randomly assigned to one of two groups: the genuine NF group (n = 19) or the sham training group (n = 18). They remained blinded to their group allocation and whether they received real beta1 feedback. The NF group received real-time neurofeedback based on their own beta1 neural activity recorded at the F3 electrode site, whereas the sham group was presented with pre-recorded, random signals unrelated to their actual cortical activity.

Experimental design and procedure

Attention task

The experiment was conducted on a desktop computer, with stimuli presented on a 24-inch LCD monitor at a resolution of 1920 × 1080 pixels. Participants were seated at a distance of 60 cm from the screen, with their gaze fixed. E-prime 3.0 software was used for stimulus presentation and to record participants’ reaction time (RT) and accuracy (ACC).

In this experiment, the stimulus materials were generated using the ImgInGrid-v1.3-release software, with each image sized at 650 × 900 pixels against a white background. In the center of each image, there was a 4 × 6 matrix of blue butterflies, blue ducks, and red butterflies, with an 80 mm gap between each two adjacent animal objects. The blue butterflies serve as target stimuli, the blue ducks as non-target stimuli, and the red butterflies as distractor stimuli.

The experiment consisted of 608 trials in total, divided equally into four test sessions, with each session comprising 152 trials. Within each session, 104 trials with a red butterfly were categorized under the interference conditions, and the rest 48 trials without a red butterfly were allocated to the non-interference conditions. An equal number of 76 trials were allocated to the directed and undirected conditions, respectively. In directed conditions, there was an up or down arrow cue in the center of each stimulus picture, indicating participants to focus on only half of the animal objects above or below the cue only. In contrast, there was only a cross “+” in the center of each undirected trial, therefore, participants had to search across all animal objects before making a response. The sequence of trial presentation within each session was pseudo-randomized to guarantee a randomized and unbiased order. The cueing conditions were designed to assess participants’ attentional orienting abilities, while the interference conditions were specifically tailored to evaluate their executive control of attention.

For each trial, a cue (a cross or up/down arrow) were first presented for 1000ms, followed by a stimulus picture for 80ms, and a blank (inter-trial interval, ITI) lasting for a randomized duration between 2000ms to 3200ms. The directional cues (up/down arrows) served to indicate the spatial location (either at the top or bottom) of the forthcoming target stimulus. Once the stimulus appeared, participants were required to identify the target’s location (left or right) and respond by clicking the corresponding mouse button (left button for left, right button for right) as fast as possible. The flow of the experimental stimuli is shown in Fig. 1.

Neurofeedback protocol

NFT was carried out using a neurofeedback system developed by Thought Technology. This system employs an infinite impulse response (IIR) filter to extract frequency bands from raw EEG signals, while the brainwave activity data were amplified via the ProComp5 InFinity amplifier. Real-time feedback was presented to participants through a program, developed with the BioGraph InFinity software, which was employed to continuously display the participants’ brainwave activity in a dynamic and visually engaging format. In terms of electrode placement, the device was configured with three precision electrodes. One electrode was placed over the target brain region to capture the specific neural activity of interest, and the other two reference electrodes were positioned on the ears, ensuring accurate signal referencing and reducing potential noise interference. Before each session, electrode-scalp impedance was carefully adjusted and reduced to a level below 5 kΩ to ensure signal quality and reliability.

Participants were randomly assigned to either the NF group or the sham group. Both groups completed a one-week training program, consisting of five sessions. The key difference between the groups was that the NF group received real-time neurofeedback based on their own beta1 signal from the F3 electrode, whereas the sham group received randomly generated signals unrelated to actual EEG activity. Crucially, to ensure equivalent task engagement, participants in both groups received identical instructions. They were unaware of their group assignment and were equally instructed to employ mental strategies to visualize the bar graph rising above the threshold. At the start of the training, participants familiarized themselves with the NFT program. Subsequently, they completed an attention task, during which the beta1 power at the F3 electrode was recorded and averaged value was calculated (beta1_mean). The individualized training target was set at beta1_mean + SD. At the initial stage of training, the BioGraph InFinity software automatically set the training threshold at 80% of the baseline target power. Once the participant achieved a success rate of 80% in the first session—defined as the proportion of time during which the power exceeded the training threshold—the threshold for subsequent sessions was adjusted based on the individual’s performance. Specifically, the threshold was modified within a range of 80% to 100% of the baseline. For example, if the participant reached an 80% success rate in the first session, the threshold for the next session was increased to 90% of the baseline, and so forth, until the participant reached a 100% success rate. If the success rate was lower than 80%, the baseline threshold remained unchanged [8]. Given that participants generally improve their ability to regulate the target frequency band over time, such threshold adjustments continued dynamically throughout the training period [9, 44].

Each training session lasted approximately 30 min, with a 24-hour interval between consecutive sessions. Each session consisted of four rounds, each lasting 7 min and 40 s and comprising 20 trials. Within each trial, three distinct phases were incorporated. It commenced with a 2-second fixation screen, featuring a green circle that served as a visual prompt for participants to concentrate their attention. The subsequent 20-second feedback screen presented a purple bar graph, which visually represented the participant’s beta1 brainwave power. A yellow line on this graph denoted the training threshold. Participants were tasked with the responsibility of self-regulating their brainwave activity, aiming to elevate the bar graph above the established threshold. When successful regulation was achieved, a green light illuminated, accompanied by an auditory cue transmitted through headphones, signaling their accomplishment. The trial concluded with a 2-second blank screen, offering participants a brief respite before the commencement of the next trial. To alleviate potential fatigue, During the training process, participants were explicitly instructed to remain physically relaxed and to minimize any facial movements, such as jaw clenching, particularly during the feedback sessions. Additionally, participants were provided with a 3 to 5-minute break between sequences of trials (see Fig. 2).

Pre-/post EEG recordings and processing

Participants first completed a preliminary behavioral task followed by EEG data collection. The pre-test consisted of two sequences from the attention task, each lasting approximately 8 min, for a total duration of 16 min. After being fitted with EEG equipment, participants performed the attention task while their EEG signals were recorded in real time. A rest period was provided between the two sequences to minimize fatigue. Following the pre-test, participants underwent neurofeedback training. The post-test, conducted after the final training session, was identical to the pre-test.

EEG data were recorded using the BrainAmp Standard system (Brain Products GmbH, Germany) in conjunction with Brain Vision Recorder 2.0 software. EEG signals were acquired from 32 Ag/AgCl electrodes positioned according to the international 10–20 system, with an online sampling rate of 1000 Hz. The FCz electrode served as the online reference point, while the AFz electrode functioned as the ground. Vertical electrooculogram (VEOG) electrodes were placed 1 cm below the left eye, and horizontal electrooculogram (HEOG) electrodes were positioned 1 cm lateral to the outer canthus of the right eye. To ensure signal quality, electrode-scalp impedance was maintained below 10 kΩ prior to data collection.

Data analysis

Behavior Data. Key performance indicators included ACC and RT. Non-responses and extreme values—defined as RT shorter than 300 ms or exceeding the mean by more than three SD—were excluded from the analysis. ACC and RT were calculated for each participant.

NFT Data. Data preprocessing was carried out using the built-in system of the NFT program, during which training data exceeding ± 25 µV were excluded. Complete training data from each session for all participants were exported. To assess changes throughout the training process, we extracted the beta1 band (15–18 Hz) amplitude data from the F3 electrode across the four segments of each training session and calculated the average value to represent the data at each time point.

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS 24. A 2 (Group: NF, sham) × 2 (Time: baseline, T5) repeated measures ANOVA was performed to examine differences in training effects between the two groups.

EEG Data. EEG data were preprocessed using Matlab (2019b, MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA) and the EEGLAB 13.6 toolbox. Preprocessing steps included electrode localization and the removal of unused electrodes. Data were re-referenced offline to the bilateral mastoids and filtered with a low-pass filter at 30 Hz and a high-pass filter at 0.1 Hz, with 50 Hz line noise removed. Eyeblink and movement artifacts were corrected using independent component analysis (ICA), and additional artifacts, such as those from head movements, were thoroughly excluded. The data were segmented using a 200 ms pre-stimulus baseline, followed by baseline correction. The analysis epoch extended to 1000 ms post-stimulus, based on participants’ average RT. Trials with incorrect responses or amplitudes exceeding ± 100 µV were excluded from further analysis. The study strictly followed the standard VSAD protocol [28], utilizing 104 interference and 48 no-interference trials to maintain conflict expectancy. Despite this inequality, the 48 trials in the no-interference condition exceeded the recommended threshold (30–40 trials) for stable N1/N2 quantification (Luck, 2014), thereby ensuring a sufficient signal-to-noise ratio.

Based on a review of the literature, we focused on analyzing the N1 and N2 components. The N1 component reflects the rapid, intuitive response following stimulus presentation, whereas the N2 component is associated with conflict inhibition processing, predominantly observed in the frontal regions [27, 32]. Guided by topographical maps and previous research, we defined the N1 time window as 100–120ms post-stimulus onset and analyzed the average amplitude at electrodes F3 and F4 [36]. Similarly, the N2 time window was set at 250–300ms post-stimulus onset, and the average amplitude at electrodes F3 and F4 was examined [21].

Time-frequency analysis preprocessing was performed using the EEGLAB toolbox (based on MATLAB R2019b, MathWorks), with a workflow consistent with ERP preprocessing. Data were epoched into 1500 ms segments (-500 to 1000 ms). Time-frequency representations were computed using Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT) with a 0.2 s window, covering 1–30 Hz. Power values were baseline-corrected by subtracting the mean power from the − 400 to -100 ms interval. The resulting time-frequency matrices (time × frequency) visualize power via color intensity.

Results

Behavioral results

A 2 (Group: NF, sham)×2 (Time: pre, post)×2 (Interference: no-interference, interference) repeated measures ANOVA was conducted on ACC. There was a significant interaction between time and group (F (1,35) = 11.917, p = .001, η2p = 0.254). Further simple effects analysis revealed that the ACC in the NF group was significantly higher in the post-test compared to the pre-test (F (1, 35) = 29.94, p < .001, η2p = 0.461), while no significant difference was found in the ACC between the pre-test and post-test in the sham group (F (1, 35) = 0.068, p = .795, η2p = 0.002). Similarly, a 2 (Group: NF, sham) × 2 (Time: pre, post) × 2 (Direction: directed, nondirected) three-way repeated-measures ANOVA was conducted on ACC. The analysis revealed a significant interaction between measurement time and group (F (1,35) = 7.456, p = .010, η2p = 0.176). Further simple effects analysis indicated that in the NF group, post-test ACC was significantly higher than pre-test ACC (F (1, 35) = 22.97, p < .001, η2p = .396). while no significant difference was found in the ACC between the pre-test and post-test in the sham group (F (1, 35) = 0.73, p = .399, η2p = 0.020; Fig. 3).

The NFT effect

A 6 (Time: baseline–T5) × 2 (Group: NF, Sham) repeated-measures ANOVA on beta 1 power revealed a significant Group × Time interaction (F (2.67, 93.46) = 3.606, p = .020, η2p = 0.093). Simple effect analysis further confirmed a significant and progressive learning effect within the experimental group (F (5, 31) = 5.144, p = .002, η2p = 0.453). Compared to the baseline, the beta 1 power of the experimental group increased significantly at T3 (t (36) = -2.726, p = .010, Cohen’s d = 0.45), T4 (t (36) = -2.525, p = .016, Cohen’s d = 0.42), and T5 (t (36) = -2.983, p = .005, Cohen’s d = 0.49). In contrast, the beta 1 power of the sham group remained remarkably stable throughout the entire training period (F (5, 31) = 0.504, p = .771, η2p = 0.075), with no significant differences observed between any two time points (Fig. 5).

ERP analysis

N1

The mean amplitudes of the N1 component at the F3 and F4 prefrontal sites were extracted and analyzed using a 2 (Group: NF, sham) × 2 (Time: pre-test, post-test) × 2 (Interference: no-interference, interference) repeated measures ANOVA. The main effects of group (F (1, 35) = 0.019, p < .890), η2p = 0.001), time ( F (1, 35) = 0.061, p < .807, η2p = 0.002), and interference (F (1, 35) = 3.589, p < .066, η2p = 0.093) were not significant, and no significant interactions were observed (Figs. 6a and 8).

A 2 (Group: NF, sham) × 2 (Time: pre-test, post-test) × 2 (Direction: directed, nondirected) repeated measures ANOVA indicated that the interaction between time, group, and direction was significant (F (1, 35) = 6.731, p = .014, η2p = 0.161). Further simple effects analysis revealed that under the nondirected condition, the mean N1 amplitude in the NF group was significantly increased in the post-test compared to the pre-test (F (1, 35) = 4.729, p = .014, η2p = 0.119), while the sham group showed no significant change (F (1, 35) = 0.009, p = .925, η2p = 0.000) Under the directed condition, there were no significant differences in N1 amplitude between pre- and post-test for either the NF group (F (1, 35) = 3.880, p = .057, η2p = 0.100) or the sham group (F (1, 35) = 0.250, p = .620, η2p = 0.007) group (Figs. 6b and 8).

N2

The mean amplitude of the N2 component at the frontal F3 and F4 was analyzed using a 2 (Group: NF, sham) × 2 (Time: pre-test, post-test) × 2 (Interference: no-interference, interference) repeated measures ANOVA. There was a significant interaction among time, group, and interference (F (1, 35) = 4.347, p = .044, η2p = 0.110). Further simple effects analysis revealed that under the interference condition, the post-test N2 mean amplitude in NF group was significantly increased than in the pre-test (F (1, 35) = 16.983, p < .001, η2p = 0.327) whereas no significant change was observed in the sham group (F (1, 35) = 0.324, p = .573, η2p = 0.009). Under the no-interference condition, neither the NF group nor (F (1, 35) = 3.192, p = .083, η2p = 0.084) the sham group (F (1, 35) = 0.324, p = .573, η2p = 0.009) exhibited significant pre-to-post changes (Figs. 7a and 8).

A 2 (Group: NF, sham) × 2 (Time: pre-test, post-test) × 2 (Direction: directed, nondirected) repeated measures ANOVA indicated that the main effect of time was significant (F (1, 35) = 6.648, p = .014, η2p = 0.160). The time × group interaction demonstrated a marginal significance (F (1, 35) = 4.093, p = .051, η2p = 0.105). Further simple effects analysis indicated that under the nondirected condition, the post-test N2 mean amplitude in the NF group was significantly increased than in the pre-test (F (1, 35) = 11.401, p = .002, η2p = 0.246)), whereas no significant change was observed in the sham group (F (1, 35) = 0.531, p = .471, η2p = 0.015). Similarly, under the directed condition, the post-test N2 mean amplitude in the NF group was significantly increased than in the pre-test (F (1, 35) = 6.941, p = .012, η2p = 0.1166), while the sham group showed no significant differences (F (1, 35) = 0.002, p = .961, η2p = 0.000) (Figs. 7b and 8).

Discussion

This study examined the efficacy of NFT targeting the low-beta frequency band in enhancing attentional orienting and executive control in healthy adults, as well as the underlying Neurophysiological correlates. Our findings support the initial hypothesis that NFT enhances individual performance on attentional tasks, as evidenced by improvements in both behavioral ACC and a range of neurophysiological indices.

Behavioral performance enhancements

Behavioral results confirmed that Beta 1 NFT significantly enhanced attentional performance in healthy adults. While the NF group exhibited a significant increase in ACC across all experimental conditions, no such improvement was observed in the Sham group. Although both groups showed reduced RT due to the practice effect, t-test analyses of the inter-group differences revealed that the NF group achieved a significantly greater reduction in RT compared to the Sham group, particularly in high-difficulty tasks involving interference and non-directional cues. These findings suggest that the observed behavioral enhancements were driven by the NFT intervention rather than baseline discrepancies.

These findings align with existing empirical evidence. Egner and Gruzelier, [15] observed that Beta 1 training effectively shortened reaction times and improved target recognition ACC. This is further corroborated by Vernon et al. [47], who reported specific enhancements in attentional ACC among individuals undergoing Beta 1 training compared to a pseudo-training control group. Taken together, these behavioral gains underscore the efficacy of Beta 1 NFT in strengthening attentional performance. Although research into Beta 1-mediated attentional enhancement in healthy populations remains an emerging field with limited evidence, our results provide further empirical support for the association between increased Beta power and improved attentional outcomes.

Neurophysiological correlates of attentional enhancement

Sustained gains in beta power

Beyond behavioral improvements, the experimental group exhibited significant neurophysiological shifts. Within the training data, the NF group demonstrated a progressive increase in beta 1 power, with a significant divergence from baseline emerging by the third session. In contrast, the sham group showed no directional fluctuations in power over the same duration.

From a temporal perspective, this sustained increase in power aligns with established learning effect models in the field of neurofeedback. As synthesized by Gruzelier [24], effective neurofeedback interventions must transcend placebo effects by demonstrating cumulative shifts in EEG patterns over time. The absence of significant beta 1 power changes in the sham group underscores the potential specificity of authentic neurofeedback in modulating endogenous brain rhythms. The observed power gains from baseline to the final training phase likely reflect both the acquisition of task-specific skills and a functional recalibration of frontal cortical activity in response to specialized cognitive demands.

Attentional selection is posited to involve complex interactions among prefrontal neurons. Within the PFC, cue-induced local field potentials in the beta band have been shown to reflect exogenous selection processes, characterizing bottom-up modulation [6, 20]. Conversely, post-cue beta-band activity is associated with endogenous selection and the suppression of sensory inputs, marking top-down regulation [1, 5]. These findings suggest that beta activity plays a critical role in both exogenous and endogenous attentional selection. While direct investigations remain sparse, prior research has reported that increased prefrontal beta power following NFT is accompanied by enhanced performance in sequence learning tasks, illustrating the impact of beta enhancement on attentional control [25]. Our study extends this evidence base, demonstrating that increased beta 1 power modulates both exogenous attentional orientation and endogenous executive control.

ERP modulations

Significant changes in ERPs were also observed following NFT. Post-training, participants in the NF group exhibited increased N1 amplitudes under undirected conditions and enhanced N2 amplitudes under interference conditions. These findings suggest that neurofeedback training facilitates both early attentional processing, as indexed by N1, and conflict monitoring and inhibitory control, as indexed by N2.

Previous ERP research has consistently demonstrated that selective spatial attention modulates early sensory processing in the visual cortex, particularly influencing the P1 (70–130ms) and N1 (150–200ms) components [11, 27] In the present study, the undirected condition—which imposed a higher perceptual load—elicited significantly larger N1 amplitudes compared to the directed condition. This result highlights N1 as a marker of attentional orienting capacity, as greater attentional resources were required to process unexpected stimuli in the absence of a cue.

The N2 component, typically occurring between 200–350ms post-stimulus, is widely recognized for its role in executive functions such as cognitive control and inhibition [12, 21, 38, 39]. Specifically, N2 is sensitive to interference, stimulus complexity, and the proportion of non-target stimuli, reflecting processes related to conflict detection and inhibitory control. Research has demonstrated that frontal N2 activity, particularly during no-go trials, may serve as a neural marker of conflict monitoring or pre-motor inhibition at the response selection stage [12]. In the present study, N2 amplitudes increased under high-demand stimulus conditions (i.e., interference conditions), highlighting its sensitivity to cognitive conflict. Notably, participants in the NF group demonstrated a significant reduction in N2 amplitude from pre- to post-test, whereas no such change was observed in the sham group. This reduction suggests NFT enhanced the participants’ ability to monitor and resolve cognitive conflicts more efficiently, thereby improving executive control performance. Supporting evidence from prior research, such as studies involving children with ADHD, also shows that theta/beta NFT interventions lead to significantly larger N2 amplitudes during no-go tasks in NF groups compared to controls [4, 26], further underscoring N2 as a robust neural marker of executive function enhancement. Our findings align with these prior studies, as N2 amplitudes in the NF group were significantly reduced under interference conditions post-training, indicating enhanced executive control. This effect was absent in the sham group, highlighting the specificity of NFT-induced improvements.

Time-frequency power distribution of EEG activity during the attention task

In the present study, a three-way repeated measures ANOVA was conducted to examine changes in beta 1 power at the F3 electrode from pre- to post-training. The analysis yielded no significant main effects or interactions across any of the experimental conditions. These results indicate that while the NF group exhibited a phased increase in beta 1 power during the training sessions themselves, this gain did not translate into statistically robust enhancements within the task-specific window (150–400ms). This discrepancy may reflect a divergence in training-induced neurophysiological changes between resting and task-engaged states. Furthermore, the findings may be influenced by population-specific factors. Unlike clinical or aging populations—such as those with ADHD or older adults (Campos Da [7])—who often demonstrate higher intervention sensitivity, healthy young adults typically possess highly optimized baseline brain function. Consequently, reduced cortical plasticity and potential “ceiling effects” in this demographic present a greater challenge for observing significant gains in neurophysiological indices.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations of the present study warrant consideration. First, the abbreviated training duration (five sessions within one week) may have constrained the depth of observable neurophysiological changes. Existing literature suggests that more extensive protocols, typically ranging from 10 to 30 sessions, are often required to elicit robust electrophysiological shifts [7, 10, 29]. Second, the single-electrode (F3) configuration may not fully capture the spatial complexity of prefrontal activity. Given that NFT effects are likely distributed across broader networks, future studies should employ multi-electrode arrays to provide a more comprehensive mapping of these neural modulations.

Regarding signal specificity, we acknowledge the inherent challenges of isolating subtle electromyographic (EMG) artifacts from neural activity using a single-channel setup. However, the neural validity of the observed beta1 enhancement is supported by several factors: the power gains were localized to the beta1 band—lacking the broadband characteristics typical of EMG noise—and were further safeguarded by rigorous experimental controls, including low-impedance maintenance (5KΩ) and strict instructions to minimize facial movements. Nonetheless, the absence of multi-channel data precluded the use of advanced spatial filtering or Independent Component Analysis (ICA).

To further investigate the underlying mechanisms linking brain activity and behavior, correlation analyses were conducted between pre-to-post changes in ERP components (ΔN1, ΔN2) and behavioral performance metrics (ΔRT, ΔACC) in the NF group. Nevertheless, no statistically significant linear correlations were observed (all ps > 0.05). This absence of direct individual-level brain-behavior coupling is not unprecedented in neurofeedback research employing healthy samples e.g., [43]. It may stem from a potential ceiling effect coupled with the high homogeneity of baseline cognitive function among young adults, which limits the statistical variance required to detect linear associations. Additionally, the brief 5-session training paradigm may only trigger early-stage, macro-level cortical resource reallocation, without consolidating stable individual-level brain-behavior linkages. Future studies adopting extended longitudinal training protocols and larger sample sizes are warranted to further clarify the dynamic integration between neural dynamics and behavioral improvements. Apart from the brain-behavior association deficiency, the sham control design also has inherent shortcomings. Although participants were blinded to the condition to account for non-specific factors such as expectancy and cognitive effort, the use of random feedback did not strictly equate the reinforcement rates between groups. Future research could benefit from a yoked control design—whereby control participants view the playback of a successful session from a matched experimental counterpart—to better standardize reward frequency and isolate the specific effects of neural self-regulation.

In summary, constrained by the brief intervention period and single-electrode recording, the neurophysiological gains in the task-engaged state did not reach statistical significance. Future investigations should extend the training duration and incorporate multi-electrode recordings alongside yoked control designs to further elucidate the long-term plasticity induced by neurofeedback.

Conclusion

The results of this study suggest that NFT targeting the beta1 frequency band may enhance attentional control in healthy adults to some extent, with corresponding changes observed at both behavioral and neurophysiological levels. Participants who received real-time beta1 feedback showed moderate improvements in attentional orienting and executive control, including increased ACC, reduced reaction times, and modulation of event-related potential (ERP) components (N1 and N2). Changes in the N1 and N2 components may indicate that NFT is associated not only with early perceptual processing of task-relevant stimuli but also, to some degree, with conflict monitoring and inhibitory control under conditions of increased cognitive demand. However, these findings require further verification in future studies. In addition, the behavioral and electrophysiological changes were accompanied by increased beta1 power over the left prefrontal cortex, which is broadly consistent with previous findings suggesting a role of beta-band oscillations in top-down attentional modulation.

Overall, the present findings provide preliminary evidence for a relationship between beta1-based NFT and attentional control, suggesting that such training may involve both exogenous and endogenous components of attention. Nevertheless, given the sample size and aspects of the study design, these conclusions should be interpreted with caution. From an applied perspective, beta1 NFT may represent a potentially useful non-pharmacological approach for cognitive enhancement, although the stability and generalizability of its effects remain to be established. Future studies with larger samples, optimized training protocols, and multimodal neuroimaging may help clarify the underlying neural mechanisms.
