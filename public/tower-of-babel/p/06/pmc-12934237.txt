Evaluating a Pragmatic Strength Alternative for Frailty Measurement and Assessing Its Predictive Capacity Against Established Frailty Instruments in Rheumatoid Arthritis
Authors: Kylie E. Riggles, Hannah F. Brubeck, Adrienne D. Tanus, Courtney N. Loecker, Punyasha Roul, Bryant R. England, Elizabeth R. Wahl, James S. Andrews, Namrata Singh, Joshua F. Baker, Patricia P. Katz, Dolores M. Shoback, Jose M. Garcia, Ariela R. Orkaby, Katherine D. Wysham
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveFrailty occurs prematurely in rheumatoid arthritis (RA) and is associated with poor health outcomes. We compared the performance of four frailty instruments, including a pragmatic alternative measure using chair sit‐to‐stand (STS), and evaluated their abilities to predict poor health outcomes.MethodsFrailty was measured at baseline using four instruments: the Fried Frailty Phenotype with STS (Fried‐STS), the Fried Frailty Phenotype with hand grip strength (Fried‐HGS), the Veterans Affairs Frailty Index (VA‐FI), and the FRAIL Scale. Outcomes collected at the one‐year follow‐up included category of falls (none, one, more than one), category of days hospitalized (none, one to three, more than three), and a composite outcome of fall, hospitalization, or death. Ordinal logistic or logistic regression models, adjusted for age and sex, explored the association of frailty and each outcome.ResultsA total of 143 participants were included (mean ± SD age 64.5 ± 11.7 years, 73% male, and 69% White). Categorization as frail differed by instrument: Fried‐STS, 17%; Fried‐HGS, 15%; VA‐FI, 36%; and FRAIL Scale, 20%. There was poor agreement between frailty instruments (k = 0.07–0.31) except for the Fried‐STS and Fried‐HGS (k = 0.62). Frailty by the Fried‐STS, Fried‐HGS, and FRAIL Scale was associated with falls (adjusted odds ratios [aORs] 3.83–9.54, P < 0.05). Frailty by the VA‐FI was associated with days hospitalized (aOR 5.21, P = 0.017). Frailty by the Fried‐STS, VA‐FI, and FRAIL Scale was associated with higher odds of the composite measure of incident fall, hospitalization, or death (aORs 2.93–7.25, P < 0.05).ConclusionEach frailty measure predicted adverse health outcomes, with phenotypic and patient‐reported measures predicting falls and the deficit accumulation model predicting hospitalization days. Being frail by the Fried‐HGS did not predict poor outcomes as well as the other frailty instruments, including the Fried‐STS.

INTRODUCTION

Frailty is a state of diminished physiologic reserve that confers increased vulnerability to adverse health outcomes, including falls, fractures, hospitalization, and mortality, in the general population. 1 , 2 Frailty occurs at a younger age and has higher prevalence in individuals with rheumatoid arthritis (RA) when compared to the general population. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 The burden of frailty in RA increases with age and multimorbidity, 7 , 8 making promotion of healthy aging critical for adults with RA. Despite the recent advances of frailty research in RA, it is unknown whether frailty tools derived in the general population perform similarly in RA. 3 , 9

Most frailty assessments are guided by two frameworks: phenotypic and deficit accumulation models. 10 , 11 Phenotypic models conceptualize frailty as a physical syndrome reflecting decreased biologic reserve and resilience. 12 In contrast, the deficit accumulation approach views frailty as a cumulative state resulting from the burden of health deficits. 10 , 13 RA may impact frailty ascertainment regardless of the model used. RA disease activity often results in pain and fatigue, 14 which may confound phenotypic frailty assessment. On the other hand, deficit accumulation methods can be impacted by the accrual of comorbidities directly due to RA and secondary effects from RA treatments. 7 , 8 Importantly, hand grip strength (HGS), the strength measure used in the Fried Frailty Phenotype to estimate whole‐body strength, may not be an accurate measure of whole‐body strength in RA due to hand deformities and pain. 15 Additionally, HGS requires a hand dynamometer to obtain readings, which is not widely available in routine clinical care. Chair sit‐to‐stand (STS) is a valid measure of lower body strength 16 that has been used to measure frailty in the general population 17 and has been validated as an alternative strength measure to measure phenotypic frailty in other disease states 18 , 19 ; however, this has yet to be explored in RA.

We aimed to evaluate the use of STS as a pragmatic alternative to HGS for phenotypic frailty ascertainment by comparing it with, and evaluating its ability to predict poor health outcomes against, three validated frailty instruments: a phenotypic model, the Fried Frailty Phenotype with HGS (Fried‐HGS) 1 ; a deficit accumulation model, the Veterans Affairs Frailty Index (VA‐FI) 20 ; and the patient‐reported FRAIL Scale. 21 We hypothesized that these frailty measures, which are based on different conceptual frameworks, would demonstrate poor agreement in frailty classification, yet all would equally predict adverse outcomes.

METHODS

Participants

This study analyzed data from the VA Rheumatoid Arthritis Frailty and Osteoporosis (VA‐FROst) study, an ongoing single‐center, longitudinal cohort study of US Veterans with RA at the VA Puget Sound Health Care System (VAPSHCS). Participants were required to have a clinical RA diagnosis from a board‐certified rheumatologist and meet the 2010 American College of Rheumatology/EULAR classification criteria for RA. 22 Eligibility criteria for the primary study focused on osteoporosis, with a minimum age of 35 years to reflect achievement of peak bone mass, and also excluded those with other forms of inflammatory arthritis, medical conditions affecting bone health, or diseases impairing muscle function. Additional exclusions for the parent study are detailed in Supplemental Figure 1. The study was sanctioned by the VAPSHCS Institutional Review Board (IRB no. 1872726), and all participants provided written informed consent.

Study design

All participants completed a baseline in‐person visit that included frailty measurement, collection of RA disease characteristics, and other assessments. For this analysis, participants without three years of clinical data within the VA were excluded because it prevented the accurate calculation of the VA‐FI (n = 2). At the time of this analysis, all but seven participants had either completed their one‐year follow‐up visit or had died, allowing for contribution of health outcome data. The remaining participants had either withdrawn from the study before follow‐up (n = 5) or had not yet completed their follow‐up visit (n = 2) and were therefore excluded from our longitudinal analyses evaluating poor health outcomes (Supplemental Figure 1).

Outcome measures

The outcome measures were number of falls, days spent hospitalized, and a composite of any fall, all‐cause nonelective hospitalization, or death. Falls and hospitalizations were recalled over six‐month time intervals and self‐reported at a follow‐up six‐month phone call and one‐year in‐person follow‐up visit (Supplemental Figure 1). Falls were categorized as none, one, or multiple (more than one) falls. Number of days hospitalized were confirmed by chart review. Hospitalization days were categorized based on severity of hospitalization as zero days, one to three days, and more than three days. 23 Death occurrence was determined through chart review or by report from the next‐of‐kin during follow‐up contact attempts by the study team. There is no missingness in the death data due to the nature of the study design requiring consistent follow‐up contacts.

Predictors: Frailty measures

Fried‐HGS and Fried‐STS

The Fried‐HGS assesses phenotypic frailty by measuring exhaustion, physical activity levels, weight changes, walking speed, and HGS (Supplemental Table 1). 1 Self‐reported exhaustion was assessed using the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES‐D). 24 Participants completed the International Physical Activity Questionnaire to quantify physical activity. 25 Weight loss was defined as >5% over the past year and was ascertained using historical medical record weight (one year before baseline) and weight directly measured in study visits. 1 Fifteen‐feet walk speed was measured by one trial. 1 Both HGS and walk speed were assessed at research visits and categorized based on existing cutoffs. 1 HGS was measured by three trials on each hand, and the highest value was used for analysis. 1 Participants earned a point for each subcategory that met the deficit threshold based on their respective cutoffs, and points were summed to assign a score between 0 and 5 for each participant. Based on total deficit score, individuals were categorized as robust (0), prefrail (1–2), or frail (3–5). 1 Chair STS was substituted for HGS when calculating the Fried‐STS. Cutoffs for STS were based on a validated cutoff for sarcopenia of >15 seconds to complete five chair stands. 26

VA‐FI

The VA‐FI assesses frailty through a deficit accumulation model using over 6,000 administrative claim codes from VA administrative data. These are based on International Classification of Disease, Ninth Revision (ICD‐9), ICD‐10, Current Procedural Terminology, and Healthcare Common Procedure Coding System codes and grouped into five domains: morbidities, cognitive and mood disorders, sensory deficits, functional impairments, and other geriatric syndromes 20 (Supplemental Table 2). VA‐FI scores were calculated as the ratio of deficits present in an individual out of the total possible deficits (n/31) that were accumulated over a three‐year look‐back period from the baseline visit date and resulted in a range of 0 to 1. These scores were then placed into frailty categories derived based on previously defined cutoffs: robust (≤0.1), prefrail (>0.1–0.20), and frail (>0.2). 20

FRAIL Scale

The FRAIL Scale is a patient‐reported measure that was constructed to be an overlapping instrument, mixing features of phenotypic and deficit accumulation models. The FRAIL Scale queries fatigue, resistance, ambulation, number of chronic illnesses, and weight loss over the last year 21 (Supplemental Table 3). Points were assigned for each deficit based on existing cutoffs 21 and summed for a total score 0 to 5. Participants were categorized based on their point totals (robust = 0, prefrail = 1–2, frail = 3–5). 21

Other measures

Sociodemographic data were obtained at baseline through participant self‐report and included age, sex, race, ethnicity, and smoking history. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated using height and weight measurements collected during the baseline visit. RA disease duration was derived from the participant‐reported date of RA diagnosis. Multidimensional Health Assessment Questionnaire (MDHAQ), 27 pain scores (0–10), and joint replacements data were also obtained by self‐report. Anti–cyclic citrullinated peptide positivity was extracted from laboratory records from the electronic medical record. The Disease Activity Score in 28 joints using the erythrocyte sedimentation rate (DAS28‐ESR) and DAS28 using c‐reactive protein (DAS28‐CRP) were collected by a board‐certified rheumatologist, participant self‐report, and laboratory records and was calculated based on an existing equation on a scale of 0 to 9.4. 28 Presence of deformities was also determined by a board‐certified rheumatologist at the baseline visit.

Statistical analysis

The participants’ characteristics were described at baseline as well as at the one‐year follow‐up for the subcohort who contributed follow‐up data. Frailty categorization between frailty instruments was visualized by chord diagrams. Frailty instrument components were visualized using pie charts. For the Fried‐STS, Fried‐HGS, and FRAIL Scale, these were described as n participants (percentage) because each component contributes points equally. For the VA‐FI, because each category contains unequal numbers of deficits, these were described as the mean number of deficits in the category (percentage contribution of deficits in the category to the total mean score). Kappa testing was used to assess the agreement between the three frailty levels of the Fried‐STS, Fried‐HGS, VA‐FI, and FRAIL Scale. To ensure that greater BMI did not negatively impact the STS measure, Pearson's correlation test was used to assess whether there was a relationship between STS completion time and BMI.

We explored the associations between three‐level frailty measured by instrument (Fried‐STS, Fried‐HGS, VA‐FI, and FRAIL Scale) and poor health outcomes for those who completed the one‐year visit. Ordinal logistic regressions were used to evaluate the association between frailty level and the category of falls and category of cumulative days of hospitalization and were reported as odds ratios (ORs). We tested the assumption of the proportion odds using the parallel lines proportion test. Logistic regressions evaluated the association between frailty category and the composite outcome of occurrence of a fall, hospitalization, or death. The robust frailty group was the reference group in all models. Models were performed unadjusted and adjusted for age and sex. Additional covariates were not included in the primary model due to the number of outcomes. Because RA disease activity can influence frailty as well as numerous poor health outcomes, sensitivity analyses were performed to include RA disease activity, measured by DAS28‐CRP, as a covariate. Significance level was set at P < 0.05, and analyses were performed using STATA version 18.0. 29

RESULTS

Cohort characteristics

Baseline data were collected from 143 participants with an average age of 64.5 ± 11.7 years (Table 1); 73% were male, 69% were White, 13% were Black, and 12% reported multiple races. Across the survey instruments, the VA‐FI classified the highest proportion of veterans as frail (n = 51, 36%), followed by the FRAIL Scale (n = 28, 20%), the Fried‐STS (n = 24, 17%), and the Fried‐HGS (n = 22, 15%). People who were frail, regardless of the measure used, were older (range of means 67.5–68.5 years), had more pain (range of means 5.0–6.3), and had higher MDHAQ (range of means 0.8–1.1) and DAS28‐ESR (range of means 4.0–4.7) scores than the entire cohort. Compared to those classified as frail by the Fried‐HGS, participants who were frail by the Fried‐STS were less likely to be male (79% vs 92%) and had a shorter disease duration (mean ± SD 13.6 ± 13.2 vs 15.4 ± 15.9 years). In contrast to those classified as frail by the Fried‐HGS, those classified as frail by the VA‐FI had relatively lower MDHAQ (mean ± SD 0.8 ± 0.4 vs 1.1 ± 0.4), DAS28‐ESR (mean ± SD 4.0 ± 1.2 vs 4.6 ± 1.3), and pain scores (mean ± SD 5.0 ± 2.4 vs 6.0 ± 2.6). Individuals classified as frail by the FRAIL Scale had longer disease duration (mean ± SD 17.3 ± 17.7 vs 15.4 ± 15.9 years) than those classified as frail by the Fried‐HGS. Those classified as frail by the Fried‐HGS had more deformities (n = 9, 41%) than those classified as frail by other measures (Table 1).

Frailty and adverse health outcomes

One hundred thirty‐six individuals had either completed their one‐year visit or died, allowing them to contributed follow‐up data. Similar demographics and clinical characteristics were observed in the cohort that contributed follow‐up data compared to the entire cohort at baseline (Table 2). Sixty‐five participants experienced a poor health outcome (fall, hospitalization, or death) and were all included in the composite outcome. Of those, 43 fell but were not hospitalized, 10 were hospitalized but did not fall, and 12 both fell and were hospitalized. Two people died, both of whom also fell and were hospitalized before death.

Being prefrail by all measures was associated with an increased category of falls (adjusted ORs [aORs] 3.81–4.23, P < 0.01), except for the VA‐FI (Figure 4A, Supplemental Table 5). Prefrailty was not associated with an increased category of cumulative hospitalization days in any measure (Figure 4B, Supplementary Table 5) but was associated with increased odds of the composite outcome of fall, hospitalization, or death incidence using all frailty measures (aORs 4.41–5.41, P < 0.01), except for the VA‐FI (Figure 4C, Supplemental Table 5). Being characterized as frail was associated with higher category of falls (aORs 3.83–9.54, P < 0.05) in all frailty measures except the VA‐FI. The point estimates between the Fried‐HGS‐measured prefrail and frail groups, however, did not differ (prefrail aOR 3.81 [95% confidence interval (CI) 1.52–9.54]; frail aOR 3.83 [95% CI 1.14–12.94]). However, frailty was only associated with higher category of hospitalization days using the VA‐FI and none of the other measures (aOR 5.21, P = 0.017). Being frail was also associated with increased odds of the composite incidence outcome in all frailty measures (aORs 2.93–7.25, P < 0.05) except for the Fried‐HGS (aOR 2.57, P = 0.118). The sensitivity analyses including RA disease activity as a covariate demonstrated similar findings to the primary models, with the exception of the Fried‐HGS and VA‐FI no longer having statistically significant associations with falls and the composite outcome, respectively (Supplemental Table 6).

DISCUSSION

Frailty was common and predicted poor health outcomes in our cohort of US veterans with RA. Consistent with other studies, the VA‐FI deficit accumulation model categorized more participants as frail than the other three instruments. 3 , 5 , 11 The phenotypic instruments (Fried‐STS and Fried‐HGS) and the mixed patient‐reported FRAIL Scale categorized a similar number of individuals as frail. Although frailty instruments based on different conceptual models had poor agreement, our exploratory analyses found that the Fried‐STS and FRAIL Scale similarly predicted falls and the composite outcome, whereas the VA‐FI predicted hospitalizations and the composite. 11 Being frail by the traditional Fried‐HGS did predict falls; however, the point estimate was similar to that of the prefrail group, unlike the other frailty measures, which showed a stepwise increase between the prefrail and frail groups. The Fried‐STS also did not significantly predict days spent hospitalized; however, its ability to predict falls and the composite outcome better than the Fried‐HGS suggests that the Fried‐STS may be a pragmatic alternative to the Fried‐HGS for measuring phenotypic frailty in RA.

Previous studies have validated chair STS as a strength measure in frailty ascertainment in both the general population 17 and some specific disease states, including liver and kidney transplantation allocation, 18 , 19 but our study is the first to explore this in RA. We found that when using chair STS in place of HGS to measure strength for phenotypic frailty ascertainment, frailty was significantly associated with falls and our composite outcome variable (incidence of fall, hospitalization, or death), aligning with frailty measured by the FRAIL Scale. However, frailty measured by the Fried‐HGS only demonstrated a significant association with category of falls in this cohort, and the point estimates between the prefrail and frail groups were comparable, suggesting poor discrimination between frailty levels, unlike the Fried‐STS. Although our small sample size may preclude statistically significant associations in those categorized as frail by the Fried‐HGS with the composite outcome, the comparably sized subgroups of those identified as frail by the Fried‐STS suggest that the Fried‐STS may be a more accurate measure of frailty in RA.

It is known that although RA is a multisystemic disease, it often has a disproportionate impact on the joints of the hand, resulting in pain and deformities that can cause altered hand strength and function. 30 Prior work by our group has demonstrated a strong relationship between RA disease activity and frailty, though etiologies of each are difficult to disentangle due to overlapping frailty features such as exhaustion and HGS. 31 In our cohort, those classified as frail using HGS had longer disease duration and more deformities, suggesting HGS may not be an accurate measurement of generalized strength in similar individuals. Our sensitivity analysis revealed that the Fried‐HGS lost its only significant association with the poor health outcomes after adjusting for RA disease activity, whereas the Fried‐STS's associations, though attenuated, retained their significance, suggesting that the Fried‐STS may be less impacted by RA disease activity than the Fried‐HGS. Though work has been done to adjust HGS strength‐based cutoffs to account for aspects of RA disease, 32 the measurement of HGS may not be feasible in many clinical settings because it requires a specialized hand dynamometer. For this reason, we decided to explore STS as a pragmatic alternative for frailty measurement because it only requires a chair. These findings need to be interpreted in the context of the exploratory nature of our study due to the small number of participants and outcomes available for analysis. We also acknowledge that HGS may be the most appropriate tool to measure strength in individuals with RA who have preserved hand function but have lower‐extremity functional limitations, such as advanced knee osteoarthritis or conditions that primarily impact lower‐extremity muscle function (eg, stroke or those who are wheelchair bound for other reasons). Future studies are needed to explore the relationship of the Fried‐STS with poor health outcomes in a larger RA cohort with more diverse health outcomes available.

Prior studies comparing frailty instruments in RA showed moderate to high agreement 33 , 34 ; however, these studies used frailty instruments with highly overlapping constructs. The predictive ability of multiple frailty instruments on health outcomes in RA has been examined in one previous study 35 , 36 of two separate RA cohorts. In this study, Hanlon et al used a frailty index and an adapted phenotypic instrument, both of which predicted death and hospitalization. 36 Adding to this prior work, we compared four unique instruments: two phenotypic instruments (Fried‐STS and Fried‐HGS), one deficit accumulation model (VA‐FI), and one patient‐reported mixed model (FRAIL Scale). Although the FRAIL Scale was designed to have construct overlap with phenotypic frailty measures as well as deficit accumulation models, 37 we found low agreement by kappa testing between instruments in our cohort. Despite poor overall agreement between instruments of different conceptual frameworks, we found that each instrument predicted poor health outcomes to varying degrees. Frailty by the VA‐FI, driven by morbidities, was associated with hospitalizations, whereas frailty by Fried and FRAIL Scale measures, driven by exhaustion and physical function deficits, was associated with falls. Despite these differences, all measures, except the Fried‐HGS, were associated with the composite outcome. Notably, the FRAIL Scale was able to predict outcomes similar to the objectively measured Fried instruments, further validating the utility of patient‐reported outcomes. Although exploratory, our findings are the first to our knowledge to use three validated frailty instruments, as well as a novel pragmatic alternative, when evaluating adverse health outcomes in RA.

The primary limitation of this study is the size of the cohort, resulting in a limited number of health outcomes for our exploratory analysis (eg, only two deaths during follow‐up). It is also possible that the Fried‐STS predicted falls because STS reflects lower‐extremity function. It is notable that the Fried‐STS maintained its significant association with the composite outcome; however, falls contributed to the majority of the composite outcome. We recognize that self‐reported falls will be subject to some level of recall bias. However, we collected data on falls from participants on a six‐month interval basis to minimize that bias. Our study required in‐person visits and a life expectancy greater than two years to allow for follow‐up, which may have limited our ability to recruit the frailest participants. Lastly, our cohort is from a single center with a high proportion of men with established RA disease, which limits generalizability to all individuals with RA. Due to our single‐center design, limited follow‐up data, and small sample size, we acknowledge the importance of replicating these results to further investigate the use of the Fried‐STS in larger external RA cohorts. This study had many strengths. The same two rheumatologists and study staff conducted all research examinations, amplifying precision and limiting variability. The male predominance adds important knowledge as male patient are underrepresented in RA research. Additionally, the veteran‐only population allows us to address unique health concerns of veterans and provides us with comprehensive data from VA electronic health records and administrative data. Our cohort, although smaller, is focused on frailty, so we have comprehensive data to directly compare three validated, unique frailty instruments against a novel pragmatic alternative that uses a strength that has been validated for frailty measurement in other disease states but has not yet been used in RA. 3 , 9

As expected, the four frailty instruments (Fried‐STS, Fried‐HGS, VA‐FI, and FRAIL Scale) demonstrated low agreement in classifying frailty in our cohort. Each instrument was able to predict poor health outcomes, with phenotypic and patient‐reported instruments predicting falls and the composite outcome and the deficit accumulation model predicting hospitalizations and the composite measure. The Fried‐HGS demonstrated the lowest precision in its ability to predict poor health outcomes among the frailty instruments evaluated. Additionally, our data suggest that special consideration should be given to the use of HGS as a measurement of whole‐body strength in RA, particularly in those with long‐standing disease and deformities. Overall, our findings support the idea that the best frailty measure is the one available, but the individual's health conditions and the outcome of interest should be considered when deciding on which instrument to use. 38 Further investigation of frailty instruments in larger RA cohorts is needed, particularly to evaluate a wider array of health outcomes while also accounting for the impacts of RA disease.
