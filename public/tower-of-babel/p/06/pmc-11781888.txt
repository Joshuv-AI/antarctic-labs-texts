Reply to the Editor—What should we expect from Omnipolar mapping technology during cardioneuroablation?
Authors: Andrea Giomi, Andrea Bernardini, Alessandro Paoletti Perini
Published: 2024

We would like to thank Dr Aksu for his interest in our case report. It is noteworthy that different research groups have explored the same method to achieve high accuracy in targeting ganglionated plexi.1,2 Cardioneuroablation allows electrophysiologists to face a futuristic challenge—understanding the extracardiac neural network through endocardial electrical activity. Basic physiological research and technological advancements are both fundamental elements in this challenge. As electrophysiologists, we are used to considering the electrical activity of myocardium as our target, directly linked to the arrhythmic disorder we want to cure. However, when we approach the cardiac autonomic nervous system for cardioneuroablation, we deal with an invisible structure. Endocardial electrical activity serves only as an indirect marker of its presence. Electrogram frequency and fragmentation are well-known features related to local innervation. In the early phase of cardioneuroablation, Pachón and colleagues3 used spectral analysis to differentiate between compact myocardium (low and uniform frequencies) and fibrillary myocardium (high and wide frequencies), postulating that the latter was the result of the interposition of nervous fibers and myocytes. Aksu and colleagues4 spread the concept of fragmentation as a more reproducible method to localize ganglionated plexi. The greatest advantage of fragmentation over spectral analysis is that it allows automatic acquisition with modern high-density mapping systems. Abbott’s Omnipolar Technology Near Field algorithm enables automatic high-density mapping with a frequency-colored scale. This tool has proven to be useful in the setting of cardioneuroablation,1,2 where the optimization of ablation area is a primary goal. The Omnipolar Technology Near Field algorithm currently appears to be the most versatile and reliable tool for acquiring frequency information on a high-density map. In both articles, a mismatch between areas of high fragmentation and high frequency was observed in identifying the ganglionated plexi; this suggests that fragmentation and high frequency may not serve as direct surrogates and have a more complex correlation. Further research is required to better understand the relationship between these functional aspects and the cardiac innervation.
