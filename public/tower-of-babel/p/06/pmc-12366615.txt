Evaluating pharmacist prescribing education programmes: a realist synthesis protocol
Authors: Kristin Xenos, Charlotte E Rees, Ian Murray Heslop, Shalom I Benrimoj, Sarah Dineen-Griffin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPharmacist prescribing has evolved to meet healthcare system needs, but the effectiveness, mechanisms and contextual factors influencing education programmes remain poorly understood. Realist approaches are fairly novel in pharmacy practice research. This realist synthesis aims to answer the question: to what extent do pharmacy prescribing education programs work (or not), for whom and under what circumstances, and why?Methods and analysisA realist methodology (realist synthesis) will be used to review the outcomes of programmes. Pawson’s key stages will be followed: (1) clarifying the scope; (2) determining the search strategy; (3) study selection; (4) extracting and analysing data; and (5) synthesising findings and drawing conclusions. The synthesis will follow Realist And Meta-narrative Evidence Syntheses–Evolving Standards publication guidelines. Data extracted will include the study characteristics, alongside the contexts, mechanisms and outcomes of varied pharmacy prescribing education programmes. The search strategy will include searching PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science and CINAHL Complete. An initial programme theory will use selected grey literature. Context-mechanism-outcome configurations will be identified, and recurring patterns will be synthesised to refine the initial programme theory.Ethics and disseminationEthics approval is not required. Dissemination will be sought via peer-reviewed academic conferences and journals.PROSPERO registration numberCRD420251056576.

Introduction

Globally, the role of pharmacists is expanding to include prescribing to optimise healthcare services for accessibility, effectiveness and efficiency.1 2 The drivers of governmental policies include rising demands for primary healthcare services, increases in emergency department visits and shortages of medical practitioners.3 4 While studies have explored prescribing education programmes, limited literature exists evaluating pharmacy prescribing programmes. Employing a realist approach to evaluate non-medical prescribing education programmes as complex interventions, this realist synthesis addresses a pressing gap in the literature to identify to what extent pharmacy prescribing education programmes work (or fail to work), for whom and under what circumstances, and why. Such work is critical in improving non-medical prescribing programmes to optimise outcomes for pharmacy students and pharmacists going forward.

Non-medical prescribing

In 2003, the UK introduced supplementary prescribing, which was expanded in 2006 to facilitate independent pharmacist prescribing.5 The jurisdictions of England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland have sought to facilitate the implementation of pharmacist prescribing in various ways.69 From December 2026, all graduates in the UK will be able to practise as pharmacist prescribers if they meet the Standards for the initial education and training of pharmacists, pass the General Pharmaceutical Council’s registration assessment and their criteria for registration.10 11 For pharmacists graduating previously to this, to become prescribers they must complete an accredited independent prescribing course.10 There are over 50 such courses available in the UK for prescribing. These courses can be specific to pharmacists or can be open to other non-medical disciplines such as podiatrists, radiographers, paramedics, physiotherapists or nurses; usually being described as non-medical prescribing courses.

Since 2007, Canadian pharmacists have had prescribing authority, which varies by jurisdiction, with most allowing them to renew or extend prescriptions for continuity of care.1214 Pharmacy education has evolved in Canada, with the Association of Faculties of Pharmacy of Canada and the Association of Deans of Pharmacy of Canada agreeing in 2010 that all baccalaureate pharmacy curricula would be replaced with a comprehensive doctor of pharmacy curricula by 2020.15 Currently in Canada, there are no specific postgraduate prescribing courses as their regulatory bodies regard a certain level of prescribing as a standard qualification for graduates of pharmacy programmes.16 Yet in some provinces, there are requirements for continuing education modules to provide prescribing for minor ailments that may include ethical, legal, professional or clinical aspects.17

In Australasia, collaborative prescribing was introduced in 2013 for pharmacists in New Zealand.1 18 19 In New Zealand, approved postgraduate prescribing courses exist that pharmacists must undertake before they can prescribe medications.20 While in Australia, the first instance of pharmacist prescribing was in the Urinary Tract Infection Pharmacy Pilot in Queensland in 2020.21 Other states and territories in Australia have begun to conduct similar pilots or trials, such as the prescribing trials in New South Wales.22 In Australia, the education offerings are still rapidly evolving, being guided by the Australian Pharmacy Council Accreditation Standards for Pharmacist Prescribers education programmes, published in late 2023.23

Non-medical prescribing education programmes

Given these developments in various countries, literature on pharmacy prescribing is rapidly expanding, covering topics such as the perceptions of medical prescribers and the community, outcomes (eg, clinical and cost effectiveness), comparisons with other medical prescribers, and implementation barriers of pharmacy prescribing.2427 However, there is currently a lack of published research examining pharmacy prescribing education, the educational approaches employed and their impacts.28 29 Education is a key consideration to support pharmacists during significant practice changes such as the introduction of prescribing.13

Regulatory reform alone is insufficient for successfully implementing pharmacist prescribing. Legislative frameworks must be accompanied by robust educational supports to drive meaningful practice change.30 Education plays a critical role in equipping pharmacists with the knowledge, skills and confidence required to undertake prescribing.30 By prioritising education alongside policy reform, the development and optimisation of pharmacist prescribing can be better supported across diverse healthcare settings. Given this, evaluating the effectiveness of pharmacy prescribing education programmes is essential, so that programmes can be contextually optimised.

Previous studies have explored the effectiveness of prescribing education programmes for medical students,31 but there is limited literature focusing specifically on non-medical prescribers, such as pharmacists.32 A rapid review by Omer et al on prescribing education programmes included 22 studies (18 of which had only medical students as participants, the other 4 had non-medical prescribers, ie, pharmacy or nursing students).32 One study in the Omer rapid review involved third-year pharmacy students participating in a simulation-based Interprofessional Education activity, which included interactions with patient actors and small-group discussions at specific points during the sessions.33 This study’s results revealed that the pharmacy students developed a significantly improved understanding of the roles of their other healthcare profession peers in the prescribing process and the importance of collaboration.33

Realist approaches to evaluating complex educational programmes

Given the complexity of educational programmes,34 and the growing interest in realist approaches to evaluate complex interventions,34 35 a realist synthesis of the pharmacy prescribing literature is suited to examine pharmacy prescribing education programmes. Realist approaches differ from more traditional scientific methodologies that might evaluate clinical treatments to see if they are effective or not.36 Conventional review methods such as systematic reviews concentrate on measuring the effectiveness of an intervention, but the heterogeneity of data can mean that conclusions are difficult to draw.37 Such methods have been described as suboptimal as they fail to explain why programmes work or do not work.37 Realist approaches are fairly novel in pharmacy practice research, with some examples of realist synthesis and realist evaluations.3840 Education outcomes are rarely generated by single factors and are triggered by context; indeed, the same programme may produce different results in different settings.41 Similarly, realist methodology acknowledges that programmes will not work in the same way for all participants or for the same reasons.42

Realist syntheses (also known as realist reviews) are a methodology driven by theory and the realist philosophy of science.43 Realism asserts that the social world exists independently of our understanding and is shaped by underlying mechanisms.34 This is a different ontology to the positivist, constructivist and pragmatist paradigms.44 The realist approach considers generative causation to explain how programmes can generate outcomes.45 46 Generative causation rejects that causation is deterministic and instead asserts that mechanisms are context-sensitive causal forces with outcomes being generated not just observed.47

Realist approaches facilitate the understanding of complex programmes by examining the interaction of various components.48 Realist approaches explore causal explanations through developing programme theory about how programmes work (or not), for whom and under what circumstances and why, informed by the context-mechanism-outcome configuration (CMOC) heuristic49 (table 1). Realist approaches investigate the underlying mechanisms (causal processes) that shape social reality and outcomes, as well as how these mechanisms are activated by the specific contexts in which they operate.34 46 50 The same mechanism can exist everywhere but may or may not be activated in different contexts.51 Therefore, these mechanisms may or may not be transferrable, enabling learnings elsewhere.52 Nevertheless, by identifying important features of programmes (alongside their associated mechanisms), guidance can be provided to those seeking to adapt programmes to their own contexts.

Study aims and research question

The aim of this realist synthesis is to evaluate pharmacist prescribing education programmes (including their content, pedagogy and delivery), as well as any underpinning theories, models or frameworks. The realist synthesis will consider diverse pharmacy prescribing education programmes and their implementation to address the following research question: To what extent do pharmacy prescribing education programmes work (or not), for whom and under what circumstances, and why?

Methods

This realist synthesis has been registered with the International Prospective Register for Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO)—CRD420251056576. PROSPERO was registered on 21 May 2025, at which time articles were in the process of being screened. The anticipated end date for the realist synthesis is December 2025.

Study design: realist synthesis

This realist synthesis will be guided by the RAMESES I (Realist And Meta-narrative Evidence Syntheses–Evolving Standards) publication guidelines: realist syntheses42 and by Pawson’s five key steps of realist synthesis: (1) clarify the scope of the review; (2) outlining the search strategy; (3) study selection; (4) data extraction and analysis; and (5) synthesising evidence and drawing conclusions.37 It is noted that these steps are iterative and potentially overlapping.

Realist syntheses typically begin with identifying programme theories, examining existing research, extracting warranted assertions and using the assertions to evaluate these theories.53 This is sometimes referred to as theory gleaning.54 An initial programme theory was developed based on a grey literature search,55 56 which identified a rapid synthesis by Bhuiya et al.28 This rapid synthesis reported that comprehensive education approaches combining learning resources with interprofessional simulation training, conferences, online learning activities and/or peer-led workshops yielded improved confidence, knowledge and self-efficacy of pharmacist learners, as well as decreased inappropriate prescribing behaviours in hospital environments.28 Our initial programme theory, therefore, provisionally suggests that for pharmacy students and qualified pharmacists at undergraduate, postgraduate or continuing professional development levels (contexts) prescribing education programmes (interventions) could lead to increased confidence, knowledge and self-efficacy (outcomes) through mixed pedagogies serving to facilitate experiential, reflective and social learning (mechanisms).

Clarifying the scope with realist synthesis

Primary and secondary literature (ie, systematic reviews) on pharmacy prescribing education will be identified to develop appropriate search terms, possible synonyms and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms to facilitate a comprehensive search. Pilot searches to test search terms, Boolean operators (ie, “AND”, “OR”), truncations and asterisks will be conducted for four databases (see below). This scoping will optimise the search, so that all relevant literature will be identified including pharmacy prescribing education programmes. The search will be focused on pharmacy prescribing specifically rather than non-medical prescribing broadly as the implementation of pharmacist prescribing is likely to unfold in very specific contexts. Furthermore, a broad search for non-medical prescribing education could potentially be too unwieldy to synthesise. Studies focused on non-medical prescribing, including pharmacy participants, will however be included.

Search strategy

As mentioned above, an initial exploration of existing literature (including the grey literature) was conducted to develop the initial programme theory for our realist review. However, this grey literature (eg, General Pharmaceutical Council accreditation reports) will be purposely excluded from search strategies for this review as it lacked in richness for a realist synthesis, so the planned search will focus on peer-reviewed journal articles only.

PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science and Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL) Complete (EBSCO host) databases will be searched for peer-reviewed articles. These are deemed the most relevant databases for the topic of enquiry. Citation searching will be conducted to identify further peer-reviewed literature. Search parameters will not be limited by date, to be maximally inclusive given recent developments in pharmacist prescribing (as described above). Study selection

Search results will be managed using Endnote and Covidence. Titles and abstracts will be screened by one reviewer to identify relevance to pharmacy prescribing education. A second reviewer will independently check 25% of these titles and abstracts, with any disagreements being resolved through discussion. There is no minimum percentage specified by the RAMESES I standards for double-checking but our plan exceeds the percentages for double-checking (ie, 5%–20%) reported in other realist syntheses protocols.36 48

Articles will be included for full-text review if they: (a) focus on pharmacy prescribing education or are about non-medical prescribing education programmes including pharmacists; (b) are in English; (c) are published in any country; (d) are a primary study; and (e) use any methodology. No time limits are planned to be placed on the search (as described above). Articles will be excluded if they are: secondary literature; news articles; book chapters; letters/opinion pieces/editorials; dissertations; or conference presentations/abstracts. Authors of the studies will be contacted if it is unclear whether pharmacists are included in the participants.

Realist syntheses emphasise whether individual findings within an article can support the inferences drawn by the review team.53 Full texts will be screened for realist relevance, richness and rigour.49 This can be further explained as the extent to which the results/discussion sections contain CMOCs able to inform the development and refinement of our initial programme theory. Specifically, relevance considers whether the article can contribute insights to the programme theory development.49 Rigour can be considered as the trustworthiness of data, whether it is coherent with the programme theory, commensurate with the context of the study type, and inferences made are supported by the methodology.45 48 49 Evaluating the rigour in realist reviews is not carried out through the formal use of standardised critical appraisal tools or checklists.57 Instead, richness is determined by how dense the article is for data contributing to the initial programme theory.49

Data extraction and analysis

Excel spreadsheets will be employed to document extracted data from identified articles. One spreadsheet will contain details of study characteristics: (a) author; (b) date; (c) country; (d) title; (e) methodology (ie, qualitative, quantitative or mixed methods); (f) methods (ie, study design, sampling, data collection and analysis); (g) participants; and (h) details of programme (eg, postgraduate certificate, short course, doctorate of pharmacy). A second spreadsheet will document full CMOCs, as well as partial CMOCs (eg, an outcome and context; however, the mechanism remains unclear).

The process for coding CMOCs will be a collaborative approach. The first reviewer will conduct the primary coding of articles for full and partial CMOCs. A third reviewer will double-check a minimum of 25% of these articles. Other realist synthesis protocols have reported double-checking of 10% of articles at this stage.36 This joint process will facilitate in-depth discussion and clarification, particularly around more complex or ambiguous coding decisions. Iterative dialogue and negotiation will facilitate a shared understanding of the coding approach. This calibration between the two reviewers will enable the first reviewer to code the papers in a robust manner. The first reviewer will engage in periodic consultation with reviewer 3 for specific sections of additional articles when further guidance is needed. Any persisting disagreements will be discussed and negotiated among the wider research team.

Synthesising findings and drawing conclusions

CMOCs will be analysed to find recurring patterns (demi-regularities), which will help to refine the initial programme theory. The developing programme theory will give explanatory insights into how the interventions being evaluated are working. It is expected that the programmes under evaluation will be diverse, so they may be classified into groups based on the nature of the programme as other realist syntheses have done.58 59 Contradictory evidence will be closely examined to consider why it does not support the refined theory.60 The demi-regularities will be presented to the broader authorship team for sense-checking, feedback and refinement. The refined programme theory will be reviewed by the research team and a stakeholder group for feedback, refinement and validation.45 Ultimately, the propositions articulated in our initial programme theory will be reviewed alongside the extracted CMOCs, to consider where there is alignment, misalignment or other factors that were not considered initially.54

Stakeholders will be engaged to inform how the programme theory (or theories) develops during the synthesis, as is advocated for realist syntheses.61 Stakeholders will include individuals with professional experience, such as healthcare professionals, or individuals with policy or research experience. This group will be consulted during the data synthesis stage of the realist review and their opinions sought for directions for future research. The RAMESES I reporting standards are not prescriptive about how stakeholder advisory groups should be organised; rather, the standards emphasise optimising the relevance for end-users.62

Patient and public involvement

Since this paper outlines a protocol for a realist synthesis focused on pharmacy prescribing education programmes, patients and members of the public were not engaged in the design process, and they will not participate in the implementation or dissemination of the synthesis.

Methodological strengths and challenges

Only studies published in English will be included due to pragmatic constraints, meaning that contexts such as the UK, Canada and New Zealand are likely to be well represented, but other countries less so. This is due to the challenges involved in accessing studies in any language and resources required for translation.63 To our knowledge, there has been a lack of realist approaches employed to evaluate pharmacy prescribing education programmes, so while this study is innovative, numerous partial CMOCs are expected to be identified in non-realist articles, as has been found in other realist syntheses.58 As others have found, it may therefore be difficult to tease out contexts, particularly within CMOCs.58 And therefore, an attempt to contact the study authors will be made to elucidate further information where possible. Furthermore, it is anticipated that diverse pharmacy prescribing programmes will be identified with varied durations, pedagogies, learning outcomes and learners. While this diversity will bring comprehensiveness to the synthesis, it will be challenging to synthesise demi-regularities for different programmes, so similar programmes may need to be clustered together. Studies where pharmacists are part of non-medical programme cohorts will be included. Excluding such studies would potentially limit our sample, thereby overlooking relevant contextual insights. The prescribing context for pharmacists may differ from that of other professionals, yet in mixed-cohort programmes, curricula are typically designed to ensure relevance and applicability across the included professional groups, including pharmacists

Ethics and dissemination

Ethics approval is not required as the study synthesises evidence already in the public domain. Findings will be presented in accordance with RAMESES I publication standards for realist synthesis.42 Dissemination will be sought via a peer-reviewed academic conference presentations, as well as a journal articles in the field of health professions education and/or pharmacy education. The findings will inform best practice for pharmacy prescribing education programs at undergraduate level with pharmacy students, and at postgraduate and continuing professional development levels for practising pharmacists.
