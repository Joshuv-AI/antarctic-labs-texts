Performance of five large language models in oral and maxillofacial surgery exam questions: a comparative study
Authors: Lisi Liu, Ke Ma, Yansong Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLarge language models (LLMs) have shown promise in medical education and assessment, but their performance on highly specialized, context-specific exams remains under-explored. This study aimed to evaluate the accuracy of five advanced LLMs – Qwen3, DeepSeek-R1-0528, ChatGPT o3-pro, Gemini 2.5 pro, and Claude 4.0 – in answering oral and maxillofacial surgery multiple-choice questions, and compare their performance.MethodsA total of 110 oral and maxillofacial surgery questions were collected from the 2013–2024 Chinese National Medical Licensing Examination (CNMLE), including 80 specialized knowledge questions and 30 case analysis questions. All questions were presented in Chinese to each LLM, and the correctness of each model’s first answer was recorded. We also attempted to verify the authenticity of any references provided in the models’ answers. Performance differences among models were analyzed with a significance level of P < 0.05.ResultsAll models achieved higher accuracy on knowledge-based questions than on case analysis questions. Qwen3 and DeepSeek showed no significant within-model accuracy difference between question types, whereas ChatGPT, Gemini, and Claude had significantly lower accuracy on case-based questions (P < 0.05). Across questions on tooth extraction, maxillofacial trauma/infection, and maxillofacial tumors, the models performed well (overall accuracy 87.3%–95.5%) with no significant inter-model differences. Qwen3 achieved the highest overall accuracy (95.5%), followed by DeepSeek (94.5%), ChatGPT (89.1%), and Gemini and Claude (87.3% each). However, 37.7%–43.9% of the references provided by the models were not verifiable, with no significant difference among the models.ConclusionLLMs demonstrated high accuracy on oral and maxillofacial surgery exam questions, indicating great potential as assistive tools in medical education and examinations. Nevertheless, their performance in complex case reasoning is notably lower and a substantial portion of their cited references are spurious. These findings highlight the need for further improvements in LLMs’ clinical reasoning capabilities and for reducing reference hallucinations before they can be reliably used in unsupervised clinical decision-making.Keywords: Large language models, Artificial intelligence, Medical licensing examination

Background

Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery (OMFS) is an interdisciplinary field that lies at the intersection of dentistry and surgery, encompassing a diverse range of diseases and complex pathological mechanisms. The diagnosis and treatment of these conditions often necessitate the integration of multidisciplinary knowledge [1]. The CNMLE, aimed at dentists with bachelor’s degrees, serves as a critical benchmark for assessing whether candidates possess the essential foundational professional knowledge required for medical practice [2]. In the CNMLE’s dentistry category, questions related to OMFS typically present a high cognitive load and are particularly challenging, reflecting the depth of knowledge and reasoning abilities required in clinical practice [3–5].

In recent years, LLMs have demonstrated significant capabilities in natural language processing and have begun to be applied in medical education and examination assessments [6]. The GPT series of models has achieved remarkable results in the United States Medical Licensing Examination (USMLE), showcasing their potential to aid in medical learning and knowledge retrieval [7, 8]. However, most studies to date have concentrated on general medical knowledge or comprehensive licensing exams, and systematic evaluations of LLM performance in highly specialized, detail-intensive fields-particularly within the Chinese context-are lacking. Although preliminary research has investigated LLM performance in certain specialty domains [9–11], OMFS is highly complex and specialized, presenting unique challenges for artificial intelligence. Currently, there is a notable absence of evaluation studies specifically focused on this field.

The OMFS questions in the Chinese licensing exam closely adhere to domestic clinical guidelines and practical experience, necessitating a high degree of accuracy concerning the disease spectrum, treatment protocols, and references to authoritative literature [12, 13]. These questions encompass core topics, including pathological diagnosis, imaging interpretation, multidisciplinary treatment planning, and complication management. This poses a significant challenge to a model’s accuracy in specialized knowledge, logical reasoning ability, and comprehension of subtle differences in terminology [11]. Notably, LLMs pose a risk of generating “hallucinated” information in medical Q&A contexts [14, 15]. In a field such as OMFS, where absolute accuracy is paramount, any misinformation could lead to serious consequences.

To address this gap, our study evaluates the performance of five leading LLMs on OMFS questions from the 2013–2024 CNMLE in the dentistry category. This includes two domestic models, Qwen3 and DeepSeek-R1-0528, as well as three international models, ChatGPT o3-pro, Gemini 2.5 pro, and Claude 4.0. Unlike previous studies that focused solely on overall accuracy, we further analyze the differences in model performance across various question types, specifically basic knowledge questions versus case analysis questions, and examine the authenticity of the reference citations provided by these models. Through an in-depth analysis, we reveal the strengths and weaknesses of each model in terms of specialized knowledge and clinical reasoning, thereby providing guidance for the safe and effective future application of LLMs in specialized teaching, intelligent question banks, and clinical decision support.

Methods

Question sources and classification

We collected all oral and maxillofacial surgery multiple-choice questions from the CNMLE(dentistry category) between 2013 and 2024, totaling 110 questions. The questions provided by Beijing Medical Examination Assistance Technology Co., Ltd [16] are accompanied by reference answers that have been verified by two dentists holding advanced professional titles. These questions are publicly accessible on the software platform. The experimental process is shown in Fig. 1. Among these, 80 questions were knowledge-based, focusing on fundamental disease knowledge and key diagnostic points, while 30 were case analysis questions that provided patient histories or clinical scenarios requiring diagnosis or management decisions. Within our software platform, these two distinct question formats have been systematically distinguished. Given that the open-source models Qwen3 and DeepSeek-R1-0528 are unimodal, whereas ChatGPT o3-pro, Gemini 2.5 pro, and Claude 4.0 are multimodal models, we removed image-related questions to ensure fairness. Each question had an official correct answer and explanation, supported by documented reference sources (textbooks/guidelines). Questions were categorized by primary topic into three disease categories: tooth extraction, maxillofacial trauma/infection, or maxillofacial tumors. As this study did not involve human or animal subjects, institutional ethics approval was not required.

Settings of LLMs

To ensure session independence, we employ distinct approaches for online and offline models.

For proprietary online models, such as ChatGPT o3-pro, Gemini 2.5 pro, and Claude 4.0, we enable the ‘Disable Memory’ or ‘Memory Retention Off’ setting. This ensures that each session is isolated and prevents cross-context interference.

For open-source offline models, like Qwen3 and DeepSeek-R1-0528, which typically lack a dedicated memory toggle, we achieve session isolation by re-establishing a new API connection for each interaction. Evaluation procedure

Following a standardized protocol, on June 5, 2025, a researcher individually entered all 110 Chinese questions into five LLMs and recorded their responses (Figs. 2 and 3). Generation parameters were fixed for open-source models (Qwen3 and DeepSeek-R1-0528: temperature = 0.7, top_k = 40, top_p = 0.9 [default]); proprietary models used default configurations. Each query initiated a new session to avoid any carryover context between questions. Two other researchers immediately evaluated the responses against the official answer key, categorizing each as “correct” or “incorrect,” and documented the results. Before proceeding to the next question, the reference answer was provided to the model and followed by a session reset, ensuring that each question was answered independently. If any program failure occurs during operation, the affected queries are put on hold.

Due to the propensity of LLMs to generate hallucinated bibliographic references, all references cited within the contextual responses of each model were documented and systematically searched across designated databases. Chinese references were verified using databases including CNKI and Wanfang, while English references were searched in PubMed (National Institutes of Health), Web of Science (Clarivate), and Google Scholar, respectively. References located within these databases and pertinent to the query content were classified as “verified”, those unavailable or irrelevant were classified as “unverified”.

Data analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS 27.0. The comparison of categorical variables was performed using Fisher’s exact probability test and Chi-square tests to assess differences in accuracy across models categorized by question types and categories. Two-tailed P < 0.05 defined statistical significance. For post-hoc analyses, the Bonferroni correction was utilized to adjust the P-values.

Results

Overall accuracy

All five LLMs achieved an overall accuracy ranging from 87.3% to 95.5% on the 110 oral and maxillofacial surgery questions (Table 1). Qwen3 and DeepSeek-R1-0528 attained the highest overall accuracies, at 95.5% and 94.5% respectively, representing 105/110 and 104/110 questions correct responses. ChatGPT o3-pro was next with 89.1% (98/110), and Gemini 2.5 pro and Claude 4.0 were lowest at 87.3% each (96/110). Fisher’s exact test showed that the differences in overall accuracy among the models did not reach statistical significance (χ² = 8.548, P = 0.070), as the value fell within the 95% confidence interval. In general, the domestic models (Qwen3 and DeepSeek-R1-0528) performed slightly better than the international models (ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude), but this advantage was not statistically significant.

Performance by question type

We next compared performance between knowledge-based questions (n = 80) and case-based questions (n = 30) (Tables 2 and 3). All models had a higher average accuracy on the knowledge-based questions than on the case-based questions, and there were no statistically significant differences between the models on either question type. Within each individual model, Qwen3 and DeepSeek-R1-0528 showed no significant accuracy difference between the two question types (P > 0.05), whereas ChatGPT o3-pro, Gemini 2.5 pro, and Claude 4.0 each performed significantly better on knowledge questions than on case analysis questions (P < 0.05). Specifically, Qwen3 and DeepSeek-R1-0528 each answered 78 out of 80 knowledge questions correctly (97.5%), and their own results on the case analysis questions. ChatGPT o3-pro answered 75 knowledge questions correctly (93.8%) versus 23 case questions (76.7%); Gemini 2.5 pro achieved 91.3% (73/80) on knowledge questions and 76.7% (23/30) on case questions; and Claude 4.0 scored 92.5% (74/80) on knowledge questions versus 73.3% (22/30) on case questions. These findings indicate that all models exhibited reduced accuracy in clinical reasoning tasks compared to factual recall, though inter-model performance variation within question types was not statistically significant.

Performance by disease category

Across the three major disease-related question categories (tooth extraction, maxillofacial trauma/infection, and maxillofacial tumors), all models demonstrated high accuracy in correct response identification (Table 4). In tooth extraction questions (n = 39), accuracy ranged from 87.2% to 94.9%. Qwen3 and DeepSeek-R1-0528 performed the best in this category (94.9% each, 37/39 correct), followed by ChatGPT o3-pro (89.7%, 35/39), and Gemini 2.5 pro and Claude 4.0 (87.2%, 34/39 each). For maxillofacial trauma/infection questions (n = 33), overall accuracy was the highest among the categories; Qwen3 achieved a perfect score of 100% (33/33) on these questions, DeepSeek-R1-0528 scored 97.0% (32/33), Gemini 2.5 pro 90.9% (30/33), and ChatGPT o3-pro and Claude 4.0 each scored 87.9% (29/33). The maxillofacial tumor questions (n = 38) were the most challenging, with model accuracies between 84.2% and 92.1%. Qwen3 and DeepSeek-R1-0528 still attained a relatively high 92.1% (35/38 each) on the tumor-related questions, Claude 4.0 achieved 86.8% (33/38), and ChatGPT o3-pro and Gemini 2.5 pro scored 84.2% (32/38) and 86.8% (33/38), respectively. There were no statistically significant performance differences among the models within any of these three disease categories. It is noteworthy, however, that the error rates were higher on the tumor diagnosis questions compared to the other categories. Gemini 2.5 pro, for instance, demonstrated a 15.8% error rate in this category. Some models exhibited a tendency to misclassify malignant tumors as benign lesions or inflammatory conditions, potentially leading to significant diagnostic inaccuracies.

Reference authenticity

We investigated the authenticity of reference citations provided in the models’ answers. The results showed that all models frequently generated reference hallucinations, occurring at similarly high rates across all models. Overall, only 56.1%–62.3% of references were verifiable in scholarly databases, whereas the remaining 37.7%–43.9% were fabricated or non-existent. Inter-model differences in reference authenticity rates were not statistically significant (χ² = 0.750, P = 0.945). In detail, Qwen3 had the highest proportion of genuine references (62.3%, 43/69 verifiable), followed by Claude 4.0 (61.9%, 39/63), ChatGPT o3-pro (59.3%, 32/54), Gemini 2.5 pro (58.0%, 29/50), and DeepSeek-R1-0528 (56.1%, 37/66). These findings indicate the prevalent fabrication of references by current LLMs in medical Q&A, suggesting clinicians should verify all citations prior to clinical application (Table 5).

Discussion

The present study offers the first systematic evaluation of five leading LLMs in relation to questions from the Chinese oral and maxillofacial surgery licensing examination. Overall, we observed minor differences in accuracy among the models: Qwen3 and DeepSeek-R1-0528 achieved slightly higher scores, while ChatGPT o3-pro demonstrated intermediate performance; Gemini 2.5 Pro and Claude 4.0 scored slightly lower. However, none of these differences were statistically significant. Given the absence of significant disparities, any ranking of model performance should be interpreted with caution. Without clear effect size measures, minor differences in accuracy may not translate into meaningful performance gaps. In essence, current advanced LLMs appear to exhibit a uniform ability to handle specialized medical questions. This observation is consistent with the findings of Wu et al. [9], who reported that ChatGPT-4.0 and DeepSeek-R1-0528 achieved closely matched accuracies (87.2% vs. 92.0%) on a similar Chinese national exam. Although the two domestic models in our study demonstrated a slight numerical advantage, this difference was not statistically significant, indicating that their performance was essentially comparable to that of the international models. Yılmaz et al. [17] also found the same problem in the study aimed at evaluating whether large language models (LLMs) can emulate the clinical history-taking process and diagnostic reasoning of oral and maxillofacial surgeons.

A notable finding from this study was the performance disparity between straightforward knowledge-recall questions and more complex case-based questions. All models demonstrated higher accuracy on knowledge-focused items, achieving an overall correct rate of approximately 94.5%, with Qwen3 and DeepSeek each attaining 97.5%. However, their accuracy significantly declined on clinical case analysis questions, averaging around 80.7%. This reduction in performance was particularly evident in certain models, notably ChatGPT o3-pro, Gemini 2.5 Pro, and Claude 4.0, while Qwen3 and DeepSeek exhibited a more consistent level of accuracy across both question types. These results suggest that while current LLMs excel at retrieving and regurgitating factual medical knowledge, they struggle with the contextual reasoning and integrative thinking required for complex clinical scenarios. In our dataset, the models’ errors on case-based questions often stemmed from overlooking critical clinical clues or suggesting inappropriate management plans. For instance, several models failed to recognize that a presentation of “progressive pain with trismus” indicates the progression of a malignant tumor, while one model even recommended only palliative care for what was actually an early-stage malignancy. Such examples illustrate that although LLMs can memorize and recall information, their ability to apply knowledge in nuanced clinical contexts remains limited.

Our findings regarding the differential performance by question type align with reports from other medical domains. For instance, Hussain et al. [10] found that when several LLMs were tested on ophthalmology case vignettes, one model slightly surpassed human experts on certain straightforward glaucoma cases; however, all models underperformed compared to experts on more complex corneal cases. Similarly, Yılmaz et al. [11] reported in their study of 100 oral pathology questions that while AI models achieved high scores on basic knowledge questions, they struggled with case-based questions, reflecting the performance gap we observed. Other evaluations have also noted variability among models. In one analysis of dental prosthetics queries, ChatGPT-based models produced the most accurate and readable answers among the evaluated LLMs [18]. Another study found that in a Polish infectious diseases exam, both GPT-4.0 and DeepSeek-R1-0528 achieved nearly identical scores, with accuracy rates ranging from 42% to 44%. This indicates a negligible difference between an advanced international model and a domestic model [19]. Furthermore, some researchers suggest that utilizing the most advanced or updated model versions can yield better results on complex questions, implying that the evolution of models is continually narrowing these performance gaps [20, 21].

Another critical issue identified is the prevalence of “reference hallucinations” in answers generated by LLMs. Across all models, only approximately 60% of the cited references could be verified as authentic, indicating that around 40% were spurious. No model demonstrated a clear advantage in this aspect; even the highest-performing model produced a significant number of false references. Instances were observed where LLMs cited entirely nonexistent literature, such as the fabricated “Chinese Oral Tumor Treatment Guidelines, 2026 edition,” or invented journal articles with plausible-sounding details. This behavior undermines the rigor expected in medical scholarship and could mislead users if not addressed. Similar issues with unreliable references have been documented in other studies. Uldin et al. [22] noted that LLMs often generate fabricated citations in the context of musculoskeletal radiology queries. Similarly, Agarwal et al. [14] and Kaygısız et al. [15] reported elevated rates of false references in various medical question-answering scenarios. These findings highlight that despite the extensive domain knowledge acquired by modern medical LLMs, they cannot consistently retrieve and cite authoritative sources [23]. Consequently, medical educators and clinicians should approach any references provided by an LLM with skepticism and ensure independent verification. Moving forward, it will be crucial to integrate fact-checking mechanisms or limit the automatic generation of references to mitigate the spread of misinformation.

Our study also compared Chinese-developed LLMs with international counterparts, revealing only marginal differences. The two domestic models, Qwen3 and DeepSeek-R1-0528, exhibited a slight trend towards higher accuracy on certain questions, particularly those closely associated with Chinese clinical guidelines or local epidemiology. For example, when asked about the most prevalent oral malignancy in China, both Qwen3 and DeepSeek provided the expected answer, which aligns with local consensus, more readily than the international models. However, this domestic advantage was minor and not statistically significant overall. Furthermore, it did not translate into improved reference accuracy. In essence, while training on region-specific medical data enhanced certain aspects of factual recall, it did not render the models more reliable in sourcing information.

Evidence from various studies indicates that localization provides certain advantages without fundamentally altering overall performance. For instance, Sallam et al. [24] demonstrated that Chinese LLMs such as DeepSeek and Qwen either matched or even surpassed ChatGPT-4.0 in addressing ophthalmology-related inquiries. Conversely, Luo et al. [25] observed that DeepSeek’s superiority was apparent only in responses to prostate radiotherapy questions in Chinese, while this advantage diminished in English responses. Additionally, Ibrahim et al. [26] reported that a Chinese LLM outperformed an earlier generation of the ChatGPT model in a gastroenterology board examination. Collectively, these findings suggest that although models optimized for specific languages or domains can excel within those contexts, contemporary domestic LLMs do not exhibit any significant overall superiority compared to well-established international models. Ongoing enhancements through domain-specific training will be essential to fully leverage any potential localized advantages.

Despite providing valuable insights, this study has several limitations that warrant acknowledgment. First, our evaluation was confined to a single specialty, oral and maxillofacial surgery, with a relatively small sample size of 110 questions. These constraints may restrict the generalizability of our findings to other fields or question formats. Additionally, we did not include other dental or medical disciplines, and we excluded image-based questions to accommodate the unimodal nature of most models. Consequently, the results may not be applicable to multimodal clinical reasoning tasks. Second, the versions of the LLMs we evaluated were those available as of early 2025. Given the rapid pace of AI development, newer model updates or versions released after our study period may exhibit different performance characteristics; thus, our results represent a time-bound snapshot. Furthermore, our evaluation metrics primarily focused on quantitative accuracy and reference authenticity, and we did not conduct a detailed qualitative analysis of the models’ errors or reasoning processes. This limitation hinders our ability to ascertain whether the observed mistakes stemmed from knowledge gaps, flawed reasoning, or misinterpretation of the questions. Addressing these limitations in future research would yield a more comprehensive understanding of the capabilities and shortcomings of LLMs.

In conclusion, our comparative study demonstrated that current LLMs possess a broad and largely accurate knowledge base in oral and maxillofacial surgery, in some cases approaching the performance of experienced clinicians on standard examination questions. This finding highlights the considerable potential of these models to assist in dental education and examination preparation. However, their performance significantly declines when faced with complex case-based reasoning tasks, and occurrences of “reference hallucinations” are common. Consequently, at their current stage of development, LLMs should not be relied upon for unsupervised clinical decision-making or high-stakes clinical judgments. Nevertheless, there are practical approaches to harness their benefits while mitigating associated risks. Medical educators, for instance, could utilize LLMs as intelligent tutoring aids or to generate draft questions and explanations for students [27]. However, it is essential that they cross-verify the content and teach learners to critically appraise AI-generated answers. Clinicians may leverage LLMs as a convenient reference for general knowledge or as a second-opinion tool for routine inquiries; nevertheless, they must rigorously validate any recommendations against trusted clinical guidelines before implementation. Policymakers and healthcare institutions should consider establishing guidelines for the responsible use of LLMs, promoting their integration into training programs and clinical workflows in a supervised manner. For instance, LLMs can assist with preliminary information gathering and decision support; however, their outputs must always be verified for factual accuracy and proper sourcing. Looking ahead, ongoing research and development are essential to address the current limitations. Advances such as targeted domain-specific training, integrated reasoning and fact-checking modules, and multimodal information processing are expected to enhance the reliability and reasoning capabilities of future LLMs. With these improvements, we anticipate that next-generation LLMs could evolve from being merely impressive knowledge aggregators to becoming truly trustworthy clinical reasoning partners that enrich medical education and practice.
