Associations between triglyceride-glucose-related indices and all-cause and cardiovascular mortality among individuals with atrial fibrillation and metabolic syndrome: a cohort study of the UK biobank
Authors: Lei Ding, Hongda Zhang, Yuandong Liu, Zihan Jiang, Fengyuan Yu, Yingjie Qi, Bin Zhou, Yujing Shen, Min Tang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe triglyceride-glucose (TyG) related indices are simple biomarkers of insulin resistance and are associated with metabolic syndrome (MetS) and atrial fibrillation (AF). However, whether TyG-related indices are linked to all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in individuals with coexisting AF and MetS remains unclear.MethodsWe included 2535 participants with both AF and MetS from the UK Biobank. TyG-related indices, including TyG, TyG combined with body mass index (TyG-BMI), TyG combined with waist circumference (TyG-WC), and TyG combined with the waist-to-height ratio (TyG-WHtR), were calculated. The study endpoints were all-cause and cardiovascular mortality. Participants were followed until death or December 31, 2023, whichever occurred first. Associations between TyG-related indices and mortality outcomes were assessed using Cox proportional hazards models and restricted cubic spline (RCS) analyses.ResultsOver a mean follow-up period of 12.7 years (IQR: 11.8‒15.2), 956 all-cause deaths and 385 cardiovascular deaths were documented. Kaplan‒Meier survival analyses revealed the highest incidence of all-cause mortality in the fourth quartile of TyG (log-rank P < 0.001). Compared with individuals in the second quartile, individuals in the highest quartile had a significantly increased risk of all-cause mortality (TyG: hazard ratio (HR) 1.36, 95% confidence interval (CI): (1.14‒1.64); TyG-BMI: 1.31, 1.10‒1.56; TyG-WC: 1.41, 1.18‒1.68; TyG-WHtR: 1.56, 1.3‒-1.86). Moreover, TyG, TyG-BMI, and TyG-WHtR were significantly associated with cardiovascular mortality (TyG: HR 1.47, 95% CI 1.10‒1.95; TyG-WC: 1.41, 1.03‒1.93; TyG‒WHtR: 1.35, 1.01‒1.80). RCS analyses revealed nonlinear association between TyG and mortality and between TyG-BMI and all-cause mortality (all P < 0.05). The results of the sensitivity and subgroup analyses were consistent with those of the primary analyses.ConclusionsThis study demonstrateed the prognostic value of TyG-related indices in individuals with AF and MetS. Background

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is among the most common cardiovascular disorders and is associated with severe complications, including heart failure and stroke, which substantially impair patients’ quality of life [1]. In 2021 alone, AF or atrial flutter accounted for 4.48 million incident cases, 8.36 million disability-adjusted life years and 0.34 million deaths worldwide [2]. Metabolic syndrome (MetS) represents another major global public health challenge. It is characterized by a cluster of cardiometabolic and vascular risk factors [3] and is diagnosed when at least three of the following criteria are present: central obesity, elevated blood pressure, elevated glucose, and elevated triglycerides, or reduced high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol [4]. Importantly, MetS is a key risk factor for the onset and progression of AF because it adversely affects cardiovascular hemodynamics, cardiac structure, and function [5, 6]. Over the past three decades, the global burden of AF attributable to metabolic risk factors has markedly increased, resulting in 137,179 deaths and 4,099,146 disability-adjusted life years worldwide [7]. These trends underscore the need to identify reliable biomarkers for predicting mortality risk in patients with AF and MetS, thereby informing effective prevention strategies.

Insulin resistance (IR), a hallmark of MetS, refers to a reduced sensitivity and responsiveness to insulin in promoting glucose utilization, which contributes to both metabolic and cardiovascular disorders [8–10]. Experimental and clinical evidence suggests that IR promotes atrial remodeling and abnormal intracellular calcium homeostasis, thereby increasing AF susceptibility and worsening outcomes [9]. Given its central role in MetS, early detection of IR is critical for assessing cardiovascular disease and mortality risk. The triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index, as well as its combinations with adiposity markers (body mass index[BMI], waist circumference [WC], and waist-to-height ratio [WHtR]), have been proposed as reliable surrogate markers for IR [11]. Previous studies have demonstrated significant associations between TyG-related indices and a higher incidence of AF [12], as well as higher mortality in critically ill patients [13] and those with AF during sepsis [14]. Moreover, TyG-related indices have been linked to adverse outcomes in populations with hypertension [15], diabetes [16], and MetS [10]. However, most of these studies were conducted in U.S. populations, and prospective cohort data on the prognostic value of TyG-related indices in patients with coexisting AF and MetS remain scarce.

Given that the prevalence of AF and MetS continues to increase globally, clarifying the prognostic significance of TyG-related indices in this high-risk population may provide important insights for risk stratification and primary prevention. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the associations of TyG-related indices with all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in individuals with AF and MetS on the basis of data from a large-scale, population-based UK Biobank cohort.

Methods

Data sources

Data for this study were drawn from the UK Biobank (application number 217390), a large-scale epidemiological cohort that includes more than 500,000 adults aged 37–73 years from across the United Kingdom. Between 2006 and 2010, participants completed touchscreen questionnaires and interviews and underwent anthropometric measurements and biological assessments at 22 assessment centers in England, Scotland, and Wales. Ethical approval for the UK Biobank was granted by the North West Multicenter Research Ethics Committee (reference 21/NW/0157), and all participants provided informed consent. Further details on the study design and measurements are available on the UK Biobank website (https://www.ukbiobank.ac.uk; ethics information: https://www.ukbiobank.ac.uk/learn-more-about-uk-biobank/governance/ethics-advisory-committee).

Study population

We evaluated the associations between TyG-related indices and all-cause and cardiovascular mortality among participants with AF and MetS. Eligible individuals were those with both AF and MetS. AF was identified using the International Classification of Disease, Tenth Revision (ICD)-10 codes. MetS was defined on the basis of the 2009 Harmonized Criteria [3]; participants who met at least three of the following cruterua were classified as having MetS: (1) elevated fasting blood glucose level (≥ 100 mg/dL or ≥ 5.6 mmol/L) or glucose-lowering medication; (2) elevated blood pressure (≥ 130 mmHg systolic blood pressure and/or ≥ 85 mmHg diastolic blood pressure) or use of antihypertensive medication; (3) elevated triglyceride [TG, (≥ 150 mg/dL)] or lipid-modifying therapy; (4) reduced HDL cholesterol (< 40 mg/dL in men; < 50 mg/dL in women); and (5) abdominal obesity (larger waist circumference: ≥ 102 cm in men; ≥ 88 cm in women) (Supplementary Table 1). From 502,137 UK Biobank participants, we excluded those without a diagnosis of AF or MetS (n = 499,440), or with missing data on covariates (n = 118), or missing TyG-related indices (n = 44), yielding 2535 participants with AF and MetS for the final analysis (Fig. 1).

Calculation of TyG-related indices

We computed four TyG-related indices—TyG, TyG-BMI, TyG-WC, and TyG-WHtR—following established definitions [17]. All the logs use the natural logarithm, and the units are specified in brackets.

TyG = Ln [TG (mg/dl) * glucose (mg/dL) / 2]

TyG-BMI = Ln [TG (mg/dl) * glucose (mg/dL) / 2] * [weight (kg) / height2 (m2)]

TyG-BMI = Ln [TG (mg/dl) * glucose (mg/dL) / 2] * [weight (kg) / height2 (m2)]

TyG-WC = Ln [TG (mg/dl) * glucose (mg/dL) / 2] * WC (cm).

TyG-WC = Ln [TG (mg/dl) * glucose (mg/dL) / 2] * WC (cm).

TyG-WHtR = Ln [TG (mg/dl) * glucose (mg/dL) / 2] * [WC (cm) / height (cm)]

TyG-WHtR = Ln [TG (mg/dl) * glucose (mg/dL) / 2] * [WC (cm) / height (cm)]

Participants with AF and MetS were categorized into quartiles according to each index (TyG, TyG-BMI, TyG-WC, TyG-WHtR). The second quartile served as the reference group.

Assessment of mortality

The primary outcome was all-cause mortality during follow-up; the secondary outcome was cardiovascular mortality. Cause-specific death was defined using ICD-10 codes recorded as the primary cause of death in national death registries (ICD-10 codes for cardiovascular mortality are listed in Supplementary Table 2). Participants were followed from the date of the baseline assessment until the date of death or 31 December 2023, whichever occurred first; survivors were censored at that date. The dates of new-onset incident cases were identified through linkage with hospital inpatient data and death register records.

Covariates

Covariates included factors considered potential confounders in the relationship between the TyG-related indices and mortality. Age (years) was calculated as the interval between the date of birth and the date the of baseline assessment. Sex (males and females) were obtained from the National Health Service and/or self-reported data. The Townsend deprivation index was used as a proxy for socioeconomic deprivation and as a continuous variable. Self-reported ethnicity (Asian, Black, White, Mixed, Other), education level (less than or above the college/university degree), smoking status (never and current/former), drinking status (never and current/former), and sleep duration per day (< 6 h, 6–8 h, or > 8 h) were measured via touchscreen questionnaires. The diagnosis of comorbidities was ascertained from hospital records using ICD-10 codes (Supplementary Table 3).

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized by the occurrence of death and, additionally, by quartiles of the TyG index. Continuous variables are reported as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Two-group comparisons were performed using Student’s t-test or the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, and multigroup comparisons were performed using ANOVA or the Kruskal–Wallis test. Categorical variables are presented as counts (percentages) and were compared using Pearson’s chi-square test.

The incidence rates for all-cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality were computed during follow-up. Kaplan–Meier (K–M) curves stratified by quartiles of TyG-related indices were compared using the log-rank test. Cox proportional hazards models were fitted to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association between TyG-related indices and (i) all-cause and (ii) cardiovascular mortality among participants with AF and MetS. The univariable model included no covariates. The Multivariable model was adjusted for age, sex, ethnicity, Townsend deprivation index, education level, smoking status, drinking status, and coronary heart disease. We reported multivariable-adjusted HRs for each quartile of the TyG-related indices versus the second quartile (reference) and per 1-standard deviation (SD) increase in each index. To characterize the shape of the exposure-outcome, we used a restricted cubic spline (RCS) with four knots within Cox models, adjusting for the same covariates. When nonlinearity was detected, we identified the threshold (inflection) value by maximizing the model likelihood and then fitted a two-segmented (piecewise) Cox proportional hazards model on either side of the inflection point to investigate the associations between the TyG-related indices and mortality.

Robustness was assessed through several sensitivity analyses. First, we repeated all analyses after multiple imputation of missing covariates using the chained equation method. Second, to reduce potential reverse causality, we excluded participants who died within the first 2 years of follow-up. Third, we excluded participants who had a history of diabetes mellitus at baseline. We also conducted subgroup analyses for all-cause and cardiovascular mortality stratified by age (< 60 years vs ≥ 60 years), sex (male vs female), smoking status (never vs current/former), drinking status (never vs current/former), and sleep duration (< 6 h, 6–8 h, >8 h per day). Two-sided P < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. All analyses were performed in R software (version 4.3.2).

We additionally compared baseline characteristics across quartiles of the TyG index (Supplementary Table 4). Compared with participants in the lowest quartile, participants in the highest quartile were more often male, had greater adiposity, had a higher prevalence of diabetes and heart failure, and were, on average, younger.

Associations of TyG-related indices with all-cause and cardiovascular mortality

During a mean follow-up period of 12.7 years (IQR: 11.8–15.2), 956 all-cause deaths were recorded, corresponding to an all-cause mortality rate of 29.67 per 1000 person-years. There were 385 cardiovascular deaths, yielding a cardiovascular mortality rate of 11.95 per 1000 person-years. K–M curves stratified by quartiles of the TyG-related index revealed higher all-cause mortality in the highest quartiles, with log-rank P values < 0.05 for all comparisons (Fig. 2). Table 2 shows the Cox regression models used to evaluate the correlation between TyG-related indices and all-cause and cardiovascular mortality. In multivariable models, each 1-SD increase in the TyG was associated with an 11% higher risk of all-cause mortality (HR 1.11, 95% CI 1.04–1.18), whereas the association with cardiovascular mortality was not statistically significant. When the second quartile (Q2) was used as the reference, the multivariable-adjusted HRs (95% CIs) for the highest quartile (Q4) of TyG were 1.40 (1.17–1.69) for all-cause mortality and 1.47 (1.10–1.95) for cardiovascular mortality (Table 2). Consistent patterns were observed for TyG-BMI, TyG-WC, and TyG-WHtR. In the multivariable-adjusted models, the HRs (95% CIs) for all-cause mortality associate with a 1-SD increase were 1.24 (1.15–1.34) for TyG-BMI, 1.25 (1.16–1.35) for TyG-WC, and 1.30 (1.21–1.40) for TyG-WHtR. Additionally, the HRs (95% CIs) for cardiovascular mortality associated with a 1-SD increase in TyG-BMI, TyG-WC, and TyG-WHtR were 1.18 (1.03–1.34), 1.19 (1.06–1.35), and 1.24 (1.10–1.39), respectively (Table 2).

Dose–response associations between the TyG-related indices and mortality

The multivariate-adjusted RCS dose–response associations between TyG-related indices and mortality are shown in Fig. 3. With respect to the TyG index, we observed a J-shaped association with all-cause mortality (P for nonlinearity = 0.006; Fig. 3A) and a U-shaped association with cardiovascular mortality (P for nonlinearity = 0.009; Fig. 3A). For the TyG-BMI, a nonlinear relationship with all-cause mortality was also evident (P for nonlinearity = 0.023; Fig. 3B). In contrast, the multivariable-adjusted RCS models revealed a linear dose–response association between TyG-WC and TyG-WHtR and between all-cause and cardiovascular mortality (all P values for nonlinearity > 0.05; Fig. 3C–D).

Threshold effects of TyG-related indices on all-cause and cardiovascular mortality

To further examine the nonlinear association of TyG with all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, as well as TyG-BMI with all-cause mortality, we fitted two-segment Cox proportional hazard models on either side of the identified inflection points. When TyG values were below the inflection point, no significant associations were observed between TyG and either all-cause or cardiovascular mortality. However, above the inflection point, higher TyG levels were associated with increased risks of both outcomes [HR 1.476, 95% CI (1.235, 1.764) for all-cause mortality and; HR 3.087, 95% CI (1.679, 5.676) for cardiovascular mortality (Table 3)]. Similarly, the risk of all-cause mortality increased significantly after the inflection point of the TyG-BMI, with an HR (95% CI) of 1.004 (1.003–1.005) (Table 3).

Sensitivity and subgroup analysis

To ensure the robustness of our findings, additional sensitivity and subgroup analyses were performed. In the sensitivity analyses, comparable results were observed after imputing missing covariate data using the multiple-chain imputation method (Supplementary Table 5), after excluding participants who died within the first two years of follow-up (Supplementary Table 6), and after excluding individuals with low or normal BMIs (Supplementary Table 7). Furthermore, we examined the associations of TyG (Table 4), TyG-BMI (Supplementary Table 8), TyG-WC (Supplementary Table 9), and TyG-WHtR (Supplementary Table 10) with mortality across different subgroups defined by age (< 60 years vs ≥ 60 years), sex (male vs female), smoking status (never vs current/former), drinking status (never vs current/former), and sleep duration (< 6 h, 6–8 h, >8 h per day). These results were consistent with those of the primary analyses, reinforcing the stability of our conclusions.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate the association between TyG-related indices and both all-cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality in individuals with coexisting AF and MetS. The main findings can be summarized as follows: (1) all TyG-related indices (TyG, TyG-BMI, TyG-WC, and TyG-WHtR) were positively associated with all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, even after adjusting for potential confounders; (2) individuals in the highest quartile of TyG-related indices had a 1.40–1.66-fold higher risk of mortality than those in the second quartile; and (3) RCS analyses revealed a clear dose–response relationship between TyG-related indices and both all-cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality.

Traditionally, homeostatic model assessment (HOMA) has been regarded as the “gold standard” for evaluating insulin sensitivity. However, its clinical application is limited because it is expensive and time-consuming [18]. In contrast, TyG has emerged as a simple and reliable surrogate marker for IR. Previous studies have demonstrated that TyG predicts the incidence and mortality of AF in critically ill patients [13], in patients with sepsis [14], and in the general population without cardiovascular disease [12]. Moreover, in a study of 2242 AF patients who underwent radiofrequency catheter ablation, both TyG and TyG-BMI were independently associated with AF recurrence, underscoring the prognostic value of TyG-related indices for ablation outcomes [19]. A meta-analysis included identified studies and included 6,171 participants [20]. The results demonstrated that the TyG index was higher in patients with AF than in non-AF individuals. Importantly, AF is not merely an atrial disorder but is closely linked to systemic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and metabolic disturbances [21]. Metabolic abnormalities may further impair atrial function through dysregulation of glucose and lipid metabolism, mitochondrial dysfunction, and disruption of myofibrillar energetics [22]. In line with this, Kornej et al. [1] reported that metabolic disorders such as hypertension, diabetes, and obesity adversely affect AF patients. Additionally, many meta-analyses have investigated different cardiometabolic diseases. Nineteen studies included 44,275 heart failure patients, and a significant association was found between an increase in the TyG index and an increase in the risk of all-cause death [23]. Similarly, the TyG index was also confirmed to be a reliable prognostic marker of major adverse cardiovascular events in the hypertensive population in a meta-analysis [24]. In the diabetes population, the TyG index is valuable as N-terminal pro b-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) and troponin-T (TnT) for predicting cardiovascular disease risk [25]. Collectively, these findings support the utility of TyG-related indices as clinically relevant biomarkers in AF patients with MetS.

Although previous studies have examined AF or MetS separately, few studies have addressed the prognostic value of TyG-related indices in patients with both conditions. In our study, TyG-related indices were significantly associated with all-cause and cardiovascular mortality among individuals with AF and MetS, which is consistent with previous findings in other populations [26–28]. For example, Zhou et al. [26] reported the prognostic value of TyG in 1329 critically ill patients with chronic heart failure, while another study linked TyG with in-hospital (HR 1.371, 95% CI 1.053–1.784, P = 0.013) and ICU (HR 1.653, 95% CI 1.244–2.197, P = 0.001) ischemic stroke patients [27]. Similarly, TyG was shown to predict adverse outcomes in patients with cardiovascular–kidney–metabolic syndrome stages 0–3 [28]. Given that obesity is an important risk factor for both MetS and AF, combining TyG with adiposity indicators (TyG-BMI, TyG-WC, and TyG-WHtR) has been proposed. BMI, although widely used, cannot be used to differentiate body fat distribution or muscle from fat. In contrast, WC and WHtR better capture central obesity, which has a stronger predictive value for cardiometabolic risk. In our study, the TyG-WHtR was most strongly associated with both all-cause (HR 1.30, 95% CI 1.21–1.40) and cardiovascular mortality (HR 1.24, 95% CI 1.10–1.39). These findings align with those of the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES)-based studies: in 9,432 hypertensive participants, the TyG-WHtR had the strongest association, with each 1-unit increase linked to a 41.7% and 48.1% higher risk of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, respectively [29]. In another study cohort of 11,937 adults, the TyG-WHtR predicted cardiovascular mortality (HR 2.52, 95% CI 1.21–2.29) and myocardial infarction (OR 2.24, 95%CI 1.45–3.44) [17]. Furthermore, a meta-analysis revealed that WHtR outperformed BMI and WC in predicting metabolic syndrome [30]. The superior performance of WHtR may be explained by the higher metabolic and inflammatory activity of visceral fat depots than of subcutaneous fat [31].

Our study also revealed nonlinear associations. Specifically, TyG or TyG-BMI exhibited a J-shaped relationship with all-cause mortality, whereas TyG showed a U-shaped association with cardiovascular mortality. Conversely, TyG-WC and TyG-WHtR demonstrated positive linear associations with mortality. These results are consistent with those of studies in AF or MetS populations. For example, Ding et al. [13] reported a positive linear association between TyG and mortality risk at 30, 90, and 365 days in critically ill patients, with AF, whereas other studies demonstrated a link between TyG and short-term mortality patients with AF (7 days, 15 days, and 30 days) [32]. The same association was found in individuals with cardiometabolic disease [33] or simple MetS [34]. Several mechanisms may explain these associations: (1) higher TyG-related indices indicate greater IR and metabolic dysregulation, leading to lipotoxicity, oxidative stress and multiorgan injury [35]; (2) IR promotes endothelial dysfunction, chronic inflammation, atherosclerosis, and platelet activation, contributing to cardiovascular disease, such as hypertension, heart failure, and myocardial infarction [36, 37]; and (3) elevated TyG-related indices are associated with hypercoagulability, predisposing patients to thrombotic events [38, 39]. Collectively, these mechanisms highlight the clinical importance of maintaining TyG-related indices within an optimal range, as both extremely high values may result in adverse outcomes.

From a clinical perspective, our findings highlight the utility of TyG-related indices as practical and low-cost tools for risk stratification specifically in patients with AF and MetS. This high-risk population often experiences worse outcomes because of the combined burden of arrhythmia and metabolic abnormalities, but currently lacks simple prognostic markers in daily practice. Because TyG-related indices are derived from routinely available laboratory and anthropometric measurements, they can be readily implemented in real-world settings without additional testing burden. Incorporating these indices into clinical assessments may help to identify AF patients with MetS who are at particularly high risk and who could benefit from intensified lifestyle interventions, tighter metabolic control, or closer follow-up. This approach could complement existing risk scores and potentially improve individualized prevention strategies in this vulnerable population.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although we adjusted for multiple potential confounders, residual confounding from unmeasured or unknown factors cannot be completely excluded. Second, this was an observational cohort study in a UK population at a certain time; therefore, prospective studies in diverse populations are warranted to validate our findings and to further elucidate the underlying mechanisms. Third, TyG-related indices were assessed only at baseline, which precluded the evaluation of longitudinal changes and their potential impact on outcomes. Fourth, our study was conducted using UK Biobank data, which are subject to specific limitations, including “healthy volunteer” selection bias, the predominant European ancestry of participants, and certain standardized protocols that may not fully reflect real-world clinical diversity. These features have been widely discussed in recent critiques of UK Biobank research, and we have explicitly acknowledged them here to enhance transparency. Future replication in independent cohorts with broader ethnic and clinical representations will be essential to confirm our findings.

Conclusions

In the present study, we demonstrated a significant association between TyG-related indices and both all-cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality in individuals with AF and MetS. Specifically, TyG showed a J-shaped association with all-cause mortality and a U-shaped association with cardiovascular mortality. Moreover, compared with TyG alone, TyG-BMI, TyG-WC, and TyG-WHtR exhibited stronger correlations with mortality risk. These findings highlight the clinical utility of TyG and TyG-related indices as simple and efficient markers for evaluating mortality risk in populations with AF and MetS.
