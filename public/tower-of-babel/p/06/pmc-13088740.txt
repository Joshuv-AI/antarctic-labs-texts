Does plant form recapitulate its therapeutic function? a test for red sap plants
Authors: Andrew Gerren, Michael A. Coe, Kowiyou Yessoufou, Orou G. Gaoue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The Doctrine of signature suggests there are parallels between a plant’s appearance, structure, or therapeutic functions that it may have on the corporal systems of the human body as well as the disease it treats. This concept, often considered more of a traditional belief than an established scientific theory has been dismissed as primitive and unscientific or as a mnemonic aid. However, direct tests of these theoretical predictions are rare, suggesting most of these criticisms are premature. We tested one of the most popular doctrine of signatures that red sap plants treat blood disorders. We systematically reviewed the literature for a comprehensive list of red sap-containing plant species across the world, randomly selected 200 other plant species without red sap in the same regions, and recorded the medicinal uses of all species. We demonstrate that species containing red sap were three times more likely to be used in the treatment of blood-related disorders. In addition, similarly to plant species used in the treatment of blood disorders, species with red sap were also phylogenetically close. This strong relationship is most likely due to red quinones often present in plants with red sap which are known for their efficacy in wound healing. Our straightforward approach can be used by ethnobiologists for more tests of other doctrines of signatures. It is important to establish a mechanistic understanding of the links between signature, plant secondary chemistry, and therapeutic efficacy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13002-026-00880-x.

Introduction

Understanding how people learn about and select wild plants for medicinal uses is at the core of ethnobiology [1–3] since its inception. The Doctrine of Signatures (DOS), also known as the Law of Correspondence [4], is a theoretical framework that proposed one of the ways by which human societies discover medicinal properties of wild plants [5]. For centuries, DOS has been employed by human societies around the world to identify therapeutic functions of various plant species [4, 6]. While the theoretical origins of along with philosophical discussions surrounding the DOS are debated in the literature, scholars indicate the DOS dates back to as early as ancient Egypt, ancient Rome, pre-Columbian America, ancient China or medieval Europe. Across its historical foundations and in contemporary ethnobotanical contexts, the DOS predicts parallels between a plants’ appearance, structure, biological behavior and the symptomatic disease expressions of the human body [7]. Hence, DOS predicts that the appearance of a given plant’s organ recapitulates its therapeutic function [4, 8–11]. For example, according to the Eurocentric or Western perspective, the common fern Asplenium scolopendrium L. has sori on its fronds that have been speculated to resemble the shape of the human spleen. Therefore, this DOS is thought to be imbued with a sense of knowledge corresponding with the use of this plant and its therapeutic efficacy in treating illnesses associated with the spleen [9, 12, 13]. Similarly, plants with white milky latex are often expected to be a galactagogue within traditional ethnomedicine [10, 14]. Further, plants which exude a red sap, resin or red latex, or plants that are red in color have been useful for treating hemorrhages, menstrual induction, circulatory ailments or increasing blood supply to the body [8, 14, 15]. In addition to plant form, plant organoleptic properties may reflect its therapeutic functions within a given ethnomedicine [11, 14].

The use of DOS has a long history among traditional cultures and is widespread throughout the world. Surprisingly, scholars have previously dismissed DOS as a primitive or prescientific idea [6]. As a result, the DOS is rarely tested [6, 7, 11, 16]. However, developing such a mechanistic understanding of indigenous and local knowledge and use of organisms is an important goal of ethnobiology. This theory-inspired and hypothesis driven approach is required to advance our ability to identify areas of convergence between local knowledge and science. Failure to rigorously test existing ethnobiological hypotheses grounded in local knowledge before dismissing them as irrelevant can hinder the growth of the discipline and our in-depth understanding of traditional ethnomedicine. Direct tests of the theoretical predictions of DOS are rare, suggesting that most of these criticisms are premature. Contemporary studies of the DOS concluded that it is an important tool utilized as a mnemonic aid, not merely a coincidence, yet rather, facilitates a way local people remember useful plants and their therapeutic function [6, 11]. However, the rare direct test of the DOS presents major limitations with respect to the interpretation of the signature itself from an emic perspective.

Bennett [6] provided a rare direct test of the central prediction of the DOS. The author tested the DOS that plant species with heart-shaped leaves will be used to treat cardiovascular disease. In this study, Bennett [6] researched six specific epithets through the International Plant Name Index, involving the Latin root cord, including cordata, cordatum, cordatus, cordifolia, cordifolium, cordifolius. In this approach, the Latin root cord is expected to yield plants whose leaves are heart shaped. This search yielded 2,584 plant species. Bennett [6] then referenced the Journal of Ethnopharmacology, The Healing Forest, and Native American Ethnobotany to screen 80 plant species that were randomly selected from this list of plant species with heart-shaped leaves to elucidate species’ medicinal values. Out of the 80 plant species, 21 species were used as medicine and three were used in cardiac medicine. On this basis, Bennett [6] concluded that there was no support for the DOS and suggested that DOS is rather used as a mnemonic aid. However, using the Latin root cord to identify species with heart or cordate shaped leaves may not include plants whose leaves anatomically resemble a human heart (which is not heart-shaped) from an emic perspective. Therefore, it is quite unlikely that local people see a human heart in the form that resembles a valentine’s-shaped heart. Further, the sole use of Latin etymology to infer plant species’ morphology that resembles therapeutic functions within traditional ethnomedicine may yield misleading results given that species’ binomial nomenclature tends to be determined by botanists or taxonomists which often fail to include local and indigenous perceptions of plant form and interpretations of related therapeutic function when naming taxa. One of the significant challenges in testing the DOS is in understanding the emic perspective of the signature and local nomenclature or ethnotaxonomy. One must carefully understand how local people perceive the shape, color, or taste of the plant organ instead of the etic and subjective perspective of the researcher.

In this study, we provide an alternative method to test the central prediction for the DOS. We tested one of the most widely known DOS linked to the use of plants with red latex, sap, or resin to treat blood-related disorders (i.e., wound healing, menstrual problems, or anemia). For example, the red sap of the bloodroot (Sanguinaria canadensis L.) is used historically in Iroquois ethnomedicine for menstrual issues, gastrointestinal bleeding [17], and it is speculated to cure blood diseases [6]. Prior studies also demonstrated the use of red exudates as indicative of having therapeutic functions used to treat blood-related disorders [15, 18, 19]. We conducted a global literature search to identify plant species with red sap, resin, or red latex across the world. We then randomly selected other plant species without red sap, resin, or latex in the same regions and recorded the medicinal uses of all species. We used Bayesian modeling approach to test whether plant species with red sap, resin, or latex were more likely to be used to treat hematologic disease than not. We explicitly included in the model the presence of (non-red) resin as a predictor to investigate if resins are the underlying driver for red sap efficacy on blood disorder. We then used phylogenetic comparative methods to test if species with red sap used to treat blood disorders are phylogenetically close. Further, we discussed the biochemical underpinnings for our results and the use of DOS as a mnemonic.

Methods

Data compilation

We conducted a global literature search to identify plant species with red sap, resin, or red latex across the world. We searched the literature using the keyword “red latex”, “red sap” and “red resin”, AND “plant” in Google Scholar, Academic OneFile, Academic Search Complete, OneSearch Mānoa, PubMed, ScienceDirect, Duke’s Phytochemical and Ethnobotanical Databases, and the Native American Ethnobotanical Database. We then referenced the Journal of Applied Biology and Pharmaceutical Ethnobiology and Ethnomedicine, Molecules, Pharmaceutical Biology, Research in Ayurveda, Separation Science, Structure and Function, The Analyst, The Brazilian Chemical Society and Useful Tropical Plants for the same keywords. Lists of plant species with red sap, resin or red latex were also obtained from the website of globally recognized organizations such as the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations, South African National Biodiversity Institute, World Health Organization, Kew Botanical Gardens, and the International Union for the Conservation of Nature Red List. Additionally, we searched for red sap or latex plant species from books and websites including the South Africa Directorate of Plant Production [20], Graves [18], Pardo De Tavera [21], Aboriginal medicine (http://www.wanowandthen.com/), Always Ayurveda (http://alwaysayurveda.com/), Amusing planet (http://www.amusingplanet.com/), Healing secrets of aboriginal bush medicine (http://www.bri.net.au/).

All species cited to contain red sap, resin or red latex (hereafter red sap) were recorded. We found 27 plant species with red sap. For each species, we noted the part of the plant which is used for medicine as reported in the literature (see above and in the Supporting Information, Table S1), we determined the plant family, and Latin binomial. We also recorded the different plant parts for each species that are reported to be medicinally used, presence of signature (for plants that produce it) and geographic location (see Supporting information, Table S1). Most of these species were found in Australia, Africa, Asia, South and Central America, Mexico, and India. For each geographical location where red sap containing species were found, we randomly selected 226 other plant species without red sap to create a total list of 253 species, including the 27 plant species with red sap previously identified. Although several comparative species have a diversified distribution range, species were selected around the locations in which red sap plants are known. Subsequent research was then conducted on these species to determine whether they were used to treat hematologic disease (see source in Supporting information, Table S2). We did not consider symptoms such as high blood pressure, cardiovascular disease, and painful menstruation as hematologic disease because they are related to pain and cardiac issues which are part of other type of DOS [6]. We also recorded if any of these species produced resin but that is not of a red color. Prior to the analyses, we standardized the plant species’ taxonomy on the World Flora Online using the R package WorldFlora [22]. This led to the change in 13 out of the 108 old families (112 new families now) and 35 out of the 253 species names. The data compiled is freely available on data dryad.

Phylogenetic comparative methods

A phylogeny of all plant species was assembled as implemented in the R library V.PhyloMaker [26]. First, a species list was formed, and then the R function phylo.maker was used was used to assembly the phylogeny using the megatree “GBOTB. extended.tre” which includes over 70 000 species and all families of extant vascular plants, as the backbone. Our database also contains information about (i) species red sap status (presence/absence of red sap), (ii) blood disorder treatment (yes/no) and (iii) presence or absence of non-red resin, (iv) the part of the plant that is used for medicinal purpose (Appendix 1, Table S1). We used this database and our phylogeny to test if species with a red sap signature and species used in the treatment of blood disorder were randomly distributed along the phylogeny. A non-random distribution of the signature or its therapeutic use will indicate consistency and convergence in use of the signature as proxy for therapeutic value. We tested for such phylogenetic signals using D test [27] implemented in the R package Caper [28]. All statistical analyses were conducted in R 3.4.3 [29].

Results

Plant species with red sap signature were more likely to be used to treat blood disorders than those without such signature (βred sap = 3.6; 90% credible intervals [2.6; 4.7], Figs. 1 and 2). This therapeutic function was not due to the presence of resin in the plant species. We found no support for a significant association between the presence of resin in a plant and its use for blood disorder (βresin = − 0.6; 90% CI [-1.7; 0.5], Figs. 1 and 2). Most of the plant species that lacked a red sap signature were not used to treat blood disorders. Out of the total number of plant species in our sample size, approximately 82% of the 27 plant species with red sap signature were used to treat blood disorder. Surprisingly, although species in the Myrtaceae family have the red sap, 3 out of 5 species in this family were not used to treat blood disorder. Similarly, 11 out of 20 species with red sap in the Fabaceae family were not used to treat blood disorder. Interestingly, plant species with red sap were more phylogenetically close than not (D = − 0.72, p < 0.001; Fig. 3a), similarly to species used to treat blood disorder, although the latter was only marginally clustered (D = 0.83, p = 0.06; Fig. 3b). The phylogenetic signal in red sap is driven by the following families Arecaceae (e.g., Daemonorops), Dracaenaceae (e.g., Dracaena), Euphorbiaceae (e.g., Croton), and Fabaceae (e.g., Pterocarpus). The marginally significant signal found in blood disorder treatment is also unsurprisingly driven by the same families that drive the signal in red sap (support of DOS), but also by additional families, including Amaranthaceae, Apocynaceae, Asteraceae, Lamiaceae, Moraceae, and Plantaginaceae.

Discussion

Most studies to date simply dismissed DOS or solely suggested it was an important tool utilized as a mnemonic aid for knowledge acquisition on the use of medicinal plant species [6, 7]. In contrast, our study provides a direct test of the DOS hypothesis. Plant species with red sap were more likely to be used for the treatment of blood disorder than other plant species. Among the seven different plant families represented in this study, five of them belong to distinct genera associated with red sap commonly known as dragon’s blood, a vernacular term used ubiquitously on the herbal market on a global scale to distinguish numerous plant derived red saps [30–33]. These plant genera include Croton (Euphorbiaceae), Dracaena (Asparagaceae), Daemonorops (Areaceae), and Pterocarpus (Fabaceae) [30, 31].

The historical origin and identity of dragon’s blood remains unclear due to its widespread use by human societies worldwide. However, the term dragon’s blood is speculated to stem from the species Dracaena cinnabari Balf.f (Asparagaceae) and to originate from the Indian Ocean island of Socotra [30]. The remaining three plant families not associated with the vernacular term dragon’s blood contain species that are heterogenous in their spatial, temporal, and geographic regions. Examples include Virola sebifera Aubl. (Myristicaceae) [34, 35], Sanguinaria canadensis L. (Papaveraceae) [36–39] and Corymbia calophylla (R.Br. ex Lindl.) K.D.Hill & L.A.S.Johnson (Myrtaceae) [40–44]. These species secrete red sap after anthropogenic harvest due to oxidation and are often used in traditional ethnomedicine to treat blood related disorders.

Human societies across the world are known to widely use red sap containing species to treat blood related disorders [30, 31, 45–47]. Given the widespread use of red sap containing species to treat blood related disorders and their association with distinct genera, we ask, “Are plant species that produce red sap, latex or resin often used to treat blood related disorders more likely to share species’ evolutionary relatedness?” Our analyses provide support for this prediction, as we found that plants containing red sap are more phylogenetically closely related than expected at random. This finding suggests that plants with the red sap are not random subsets of the floristic composition of our sample, perhaps pointing to similar bioactivity due to shared evolutionary traits such as plant secondary chemistry, which may be a global signature with respect to their use in traditional ethnomedicine. This is further supported by our finding that species used to treat blood disorders are also phylogenetically structured, suggesting not only a shared evolutionary history but also a nonrandom selection of plants to treat blood disorders.

To better understand why human societies around the world have selected for and used red sap containing plant species to treat blood related disorders, it is important to consider the biochemical underpinnings of therapeutic efficacy. Plant secondary metabolites such as alkaloids, terpenes, phenols, flavonoids are known for their bioactivity and therapeutic applications [14, 31, 48–50]. Notable biochemical and therapeutic activities associated with red sap, commonly known as dragon’s blood, include anti-inflammatory, antiviral, antifungal, antimicrobial, anticancer, antihemorrhagic, immunomodulatory, antiulcer, antidiarrheal, analgesic, antioxidant, and wound healing activity [30, 32, 33, 51–54]. These therapeutic properties associated with dragon’s blood containing species demonstrates a biological basis for why human societies worldwide may have selected for these plants and their therapeutic efficacy in traditional ethnomedicine.

The production of latex occurs in approximately 10% of all flowering plant species representing 20 plant families [55]. Red latex or resin producing species are restricted to seven plant genera [30, 34–36, 40, 47] considered in our study. Among dragon’s blood containing species, the signature or red color of the sap is a result of the presence of red quinones, a class of phenolic compounds along with the alkaloid Taspine often associated with wound healing, antimicrobial, and hemostatic activity [30–32, 56–59]. This biochemistry is consistent among all red latex and resin producing species where the characteristic color of red latex or resin is due to enzymatic activity associated with polyphenol oxidase (PPO), oxygen-dependent oxidizing enzymes, that convert phenols within the latex or resin to reactive quinones in response to oxidation [60]. Quite clearly, our results suggest that the therapeutic value of plants with red sap is not due to the simple presence of resins but only if these resins are red colored. Similar pharmacological activity has been noted in other species containing white or yellow latex [61, 62] resulting in slight to significant color changes of resin or latex. However, it is reasonable to expect red latex or resin to be significantly rich in polyphenols by comparison due to the color of the latex. Finally, the pharmacological activity and plant secondary chemistry associated with red sap containing species provide strong support for why human societies worldwide may associate the red sap signature and therapeutic efficacy to treat blood related disorders in traditional ethnomedicine. It is noteworthy to clarify that the goal of this study was to investigate whether or not the data provide support for DOS. Our objective was not to provide scientific evidence that red sap indeed contains bioactive molecules that treat a range of blood-related diseases. Thus, our reference to red quinone is simply speculation rather than scientific evidence. Such inference will require additional experimental analyses beyond the scope of this study. We recommend that future research and clinical trials investigating the mode of action and pharmacokinetics of redsap plant secondary chemistry use in human subjects is warranted and is expected to yield informative results on the therapeutic potential of red sap in contemporary medicine.

Ethnotaxonomies have often been investigated to better understand how human societies organize their knowledge and orientate their cosmologies pertaining to the natural world [63, 64]. Vernacular terminology associated with a given medicinal plant species has, in many cases, been attributed to a given therapeutic function within traditional ethnomedicine, suggesting support for the use of DOS as a mnemonic aid for the transfer of local ecological knowledge. The widespread use of red sap to treat blood related disorders, when one considers the identifying signature, vernacular terminology associated with a given species and their uses in traditional ethnomedicine, may imply there is direct support for signature as mnemonic aid for the transfer of medicinal plant knowledge. However, the perceived relationship between these factors alone does not provide unequivocal support for the use of DOS as a mnemonic aid. It is possible that by observing plant species exuding a red sap similar in color to human blood, human societies were inspired to experiment with them to treat blood disorders. That is, humans used observations to develop hypotheses on the therapeutic efficacy of these plant species to treating blood disorders which they later tested through long-term experimentation. After finding support for this hypothesis, it is possible the signature of red sap was then used as a mnemonic aid. We argue the use of DOS as a mnemonic aid does not preclude it from being a source of inspiration for humans to develop testable hypotheses about the link between a given plant’s form and therapeutic function.

We suggest further research is needed to provide several independent tests of the DOS to enable future meta-analysis. Any attempt to generalize our findings that support the red sap DOS and blood disorder treatments must be done with caution. The support demonstrated in our study is possibly due to the nature of the signature itself. Therefore, the findings in our study may not be generalized. However, our study does provide insights into how to test other DOS to identify in which cases a given plant form might recapitulate its therapeutic function. This might be likely when the signature trait (which can be a shape) also correlates with taste or odor that indicates therapeutic activity. In addition, support for DOS might be more likely when it comes to signatures that are related to sap or resinand color in specific cases. Further, one of the central goals of contemporary ethnobiology is to acknowledge the value of indigenousor local knowledge and practices while developing novel ways to test major predictions surrounding them. This is the essence of our work. To further our understanding of the Doctrine of Signature, ethnobiologists aiming to test the hypothesis must also focus on the mechanistic understanding of the links between the identified signature, plant secondary chemistry and therapeutic efficacy from both emic and etic perspectives.
