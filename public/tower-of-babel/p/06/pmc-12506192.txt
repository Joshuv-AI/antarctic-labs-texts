Effects of compression therapy on the upper limb of patients with stroke: a systematic review protocol
Authors: Jia Min Lee, Siaw Chui Chai, Dongdong Shi, Kok Beng Gan, Shin Ying Chu, Kuicheng Li, Ai Lian Lim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractIntroductionStroke is a leading cause of disability and mortality worldwide, with upper limb dysfunction being among its most common effects. Compression therapy has recently gained growing interest as an adjunct to other rehabilitation interventions for managing upper limb dysfunction among stroke survivors. However, the evidence for its effectiveness remains inconsistent and equivocal. Therefore, this systematic review aims to assess the effectiveness of compression therapy in managing upper limb dysfunction in patients with stroke.MethodA systematic search of databases (PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, CINAHL, Cochrane Library, Embase, PEDro, OTseeker, ClinicalTrials.gov, and the WHO International Clinical Trials Registry Platform) will be conducted to identify peer-reviewed studies and relevant grey literature on compression therapy on upper limb dysfunction after stroke. Two reviewers will independently screen, select and extract data, with discrepancies resolved through discussion or involvement of a third reviewer. Outcomes of interest include clinical measures of upper limb function, activity, participation and safety (ie, adverse effects). Risk of bias will be assessed using the Cochrane RoB 2 for randomised studies, the Risk Of Bias In Non-randomised Studies of Interventions for non-randomised studies. The certainty of evidence across types of compression therapy and outcomes will be evaluated using the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluation approach. Data will be narratively synthesised, and a meta-analysis will be performed if feasible. The review will be reported following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines and will be conducted from September 2025 to December 2025.Ethics and disseminationThis systematic review does not require ethical approval as only secondary data will be used. This review will synthesise the body of evidence on the effectiveness of compression therapy in managing poststroke upper limb dysfunction. Results will then be disseminated through a peer-reviewed publication to inform research and clinical practice.PROSPERO registration numberCRD42024625815.Keywords: REHABILITATION MEDICINE, Systematic Review, Stroke

Introduction

Globally, stroke is the third-leading cause of disability, with over 101 million individuals living with a history of stroke.1 Also known as a cerebrovascular accident, stroke occurs due to a disruption of cerebral blood flow caused by either an obstruction or rupture of a cerebral blood vessel (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention 2024). Economically, the global average cost of stroke from 2012 to 2021 reached up to €220 822.45.2 In stroke survivors, the effects of stroke differ based on its aetiology, severity and the site of the lesion.3 The recovery trajectories also tend to evolve across different phases of stroke (ie, acute, subacute, chronic).4 5 For instance, some impairments improve with spontaneous recovery in early rehabilitation. In contrast, other deficits (eg, spasticity or contractures) may persist or emerge later, potentially influencing the timing and effectiveness of interventions. One of the most prominent and prevalent impacts of stroke is upper limb dysfunction, with approximately 70% of stroke survivors reporting impaired arm and hand function, which persists long term in about 40% of cases.6

Upper limb dysfunction after stroke encompasses a wide range of motor, sensory and functional deficits. According to the International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health model (ICF), these impairments can be categorised into impairments of body function and impairments of body structures.7,9 Impairments of body functions include neuromusculoskeletal and movement-related function (ie, limited joint mobility,10 11 weakened muscle power,11,13 imbalanced muscle tone11 12 14 15), as well as sensory function (ie, sensory disturbances) and pain.10 Impairments of body structures involve changes in structures related to movement, such as oedema10 16 and contractures.12 17 Following the ICF model, these upper limb impairments caused by stroke are closely related to limitations in activities and participation, which is also one of the components in the model.8 9 Impaired upper limb functions significantly impact basic activities of daily living and instrumental activities of daily living,18,20 thereby affecting quality of life.21,24 Hence, management of upper limb dysfunction has become one of the fundamental aspects of stroke rehabilitation, aiming to maximise patients’ abilities and minimise disability.

Therapeutic interventions such as mirror therapy,25 26 constraint-induced movement therapy27 28 and robot-assisted therapy29 30 have shown promising results in improving upper limb function after stroke. Compression therapy has been implemented in clinical practice as an adjunct to other rehabilitation approaches (eg, usual care, physiotherapy, occupational therapy). It serves different purposes, including oedema31 and sensory management.32 33 This term encompasses a wide range of compression therapeutic devices or approaches, which include pressure garments,34 compression bandages35 36 and intermittent pneumatic compression devices.32 33 37 Other than stroke rehabilitation, compression therapy has been widely used in different medical conditions such as hypertrophic burn scar management,38 39 lymphoedema40 41 and neurological conditions such as cerebral palsy.42 43 Its mechanism was believed to apply or exert pressure to the intact skin to improve circulation,44 45 increase tissue pressure44 45 and provide stabilisation to underlying tissues.46

Despite its growing use in stroke rehabilitation, the evidence regarding the effectiveness of compression therapy, particularly for managing upper-limb dysfunction after stroke (eg, spasticity, oedema, pain), remains equivocal. Some studies suggested that applying pressure garments47 and functional bandages35 reduced spasticity in the upper limb and improved range of motion. Similarly, Kabbash et al37 supported the positive impacts of compression therapy, highlighting that intermittent pneumatic compression may enhance upper limb functional abilities and somatosensory functions. However, Watson et al48 found that the effects of pressure garments on upper limb functions remain unclear. Moreover, conflicting findings have been reported, with some studies indicating that compression therapy had no impact on controlling spasticity,34 decreasing oedema49 and even bringing adverse effects.50

Compression therapy has been studied for its effects on various medical conditions such as hypertrophic burn scars,51 orthostatic hypotension52 and ankle fracture.53 In stroke cases, one systematic review examined intermittent pneumatic compression applied to the lower limb and found that intermittent pneumatic compression significantly reduces the risk of deep vein thrombosis and improves survival in immobile post-stroke patients.54 These findings support that compression therapy can have significant physiological and clinical effects in patients with stroke, which provides a foundation for investigating its potential benefits for the upper limb. However, to date, no systematic review has specifically examined the use of compression therapy for upper limb outcomes in people with stroke. Given the inconclusive findings regarding the effects of compression therapy on the upper limbs of patients with stroke, and as prior reviews focused on lower-limb vascular outcomes rather than upper-limb functional outcomes, there is a critical need for a systematic and comprehensive review of the existing evidence to evaluate its effectiveness for upper-limb rehabilitation. Therefore, the objectives of this systematic review are:

Identify the types and delivery parameters of compression therapy interventions used to manage upper limb dysfunction in patients with stroke.

Method

Protocol and registration

The systematic review protocol is reported following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) for Protocols 201555 (online supplemental material 1).55 The protocol has been registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under CRD42024625815. This review is planned to be conducted from September 2025 to December 2025.

Electronic search

Extensive literature searches will be conducted using the following databases: PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science (WoS), the Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL), Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, Embase, Physiotherapy Evidence Database (PEDro) and Occupational Therapy Systematic Evaluation of Evidence (OTseeker). Keyword searches will be performed in these selected databases to identify eligible studies. We will also search trial registries (ClinicalTrials.gov and WHO International Clinical Trials Registry Platform) for ongoing or unpublished trials, and screen reference lists of included studies, relevant reviews, or the recommended articles sections in different databases. Grey literature and relevant studies will be identified by manually searching using Google Scholar. English language limits will be applied when screening studies.

Eligibility

The systematic review question in this study is framed using the PICO (Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome) framework. Studies will be selected according to the criteria outlined below.

Types of studies

This systematic review will include randomised controlled trials, non-randomised controlled trials, controlled before-and-after or uncontrolled before-and-after studies, and observational studies (prospective and retrospective cohort studies or case–control studies) which have a minimum of two times of assessment (ie, baseline/ pre-intervention and post-intervention). We will include pilot and feasibility studies, case reports and case series reporting relevant outcomes or safety/adverse-event data to capture the compression therapy safety profile comprehensively.

Participants

Studies that include adults (≥18 years) with a diagnosis of stroke regardless of the stroke types and duration, receiving hospital-based in or outpatient care will be included.

Intervention

Studies will be included if the compression therapy (experimental or standard care intervention) aims to manage upper limb dysfunction in patients with stroke. Compression therapy is defined for this review as interventions that apply sustained pressure to targeted body parts, such as pressure garments and intermittent pneumatic compression. Interventions primarily provide other mechanisms of action, such as kinesiotaping, which lifts the skin, and rigid shoulder strapping, which offers mechanical support without sustained pressure, will be excluded. Since compression therapy is commonly delivered in clinical practice as an adjunct to other rehabilitation interventions, studies in which compression therapy is provided alongside cointerventions (eg, usual care, physiotherapy, occupational therapy) will be eligible, provided the role of compression can be reasonably ascertained (eg, compression therapy combined with usual care vs usual care alone). Detailed information on cointerventions will be extracted to enable subgroup and sensitivity analyses. As this review focuses on compression therapy for the upper limb, studies that address only lower limb outcomes or do not include any form of compression therapy will be excluded.

Comparisons or control

No restrictions on the comparison or control group for the studies included.

Outcome measures

The outcome of interest is the upper limb functions, measured using appropriate instruments. All clinical measures used to assess upper limb dysfunction, including but not limited to pain, oedema, range of motion and spasticity across various time points, will be investigated. Outcome measures will be combined into domains, if feasible. Adverse effects related to compression therapy such as skin irritation and circulatory issues will also be considered.

Study selection

EndNote V.21 will be used to manage the references. Two reviewers (JML and DS) will independently perform deduplication, screen titles and abstracts, focusing on articles mentioning both upper limb dysfunction after stroke and compression therapy. Articles unrelated will be excluded. Eligibility assessment will involve a thorough review of the full texts of the remaining records and will be performed independently by the same two reviewers. All decisions will be recorded in a prepopulated Microsoft Excel spreadsheet. In the event of disagreements regarding eligibility, the two reviewers will resolve the issue through discussion and by referring again to the inclusion criteria. A third reviewer (SCC) will make the final decision if a consensus cannot be reached. Subsequently, all included studies will be carefully reviewed to assess their methodological properties and extract relevant data. Following PRISMA guidelines, the screening process results will be reported in detail.

Risk of Bias Assessment

The risk of bias of the included studies will be evaluated using the Cochrane risk-of-bias tool for randomised trials (RoB 2)56 for randomised controlled trials, and the Risk Of Bias In Non-randomised Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I)57 for non-randomised intervention studies.

For randomised controlled trials, RoB 2 assesses the risk of bias of the studies across five domains (ie, the randomisation process, deviations from intended intervention, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome, selection of the reported result). Each signalling question within these domains will be answered as ‘yes’, ‘potentially yes’, ‘potentially no’ or ‘no’, leading to the risk of bias in each domain and the overall risk of bias rated as ‘low risk’, ‘some concerns’ or ‘high risk’. For non-randomised intervention studies, ROBINS-I evaluates the following domains: confounding, selection of participants into the study, classification of interventions, deviations from intended interventions, missing data, measurement of outcomes and selection of the reported results. After responding to the signalling questions for each domain, the risk of bias will be judged as ‘low’, ‘moderate’, ‘serious’ or ‘critical’.

Results of the risk-of-bias assessments will be presented using ‘traffic light’ plots or summary plots. Two reviewers will independently perform the assessments, and any discrepancies will be resolved through discussion or by a third reviewer (SCC). These findings will be used to inform sensitivity analysis and the assessment of the certainty of evidence.

Two independent reviewers will extract the data using a customised data extraction form. Any disagreements will be solved by discussion among the two reviewers. The third reviewer (SCC) will extract the data if unresolved. Data extracted will cover the following:

Intervention characteristics: type of compression therapy, duration, frequency, control group (if applicable), aims of the therapy, and details of any cointerventions (type, frequency/intensity, timing). We will record whether compression therapy was delivered as adjunctive therapy or as a standalone.

We will record whether compression therapy was delivered as adjunctive therapy or as a standalone.

Data synthesis

A variation of study designs and heterogeneous compression therapy interventions is expected to be found. The included studies will be divided into types of study design and subdivided by type of intervention (eg, pressure garment, multilayer bandages and intermittent pneumatic compression). A narrative synthesis of all included studies will be provided by type of outcomes, divided in terms of study design and subdivided by type of intervention, describing study characteristics, participants’ demographics, interventions, outcomes, results and conclusion by the authors. The authors of the studies in this review will be contacted to retrieve any missing data needed.

When clinically and methodologically comparable studies report the same outcome using the same or comparable instruments, a random-effects model meta-analysis will be conducted using Review Manager V.5.4 (RevMan), following the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions guidelines.58 Effect measures for continuous outcomes will be reported as mean differences (MD) or standardised MDs with 95% CIs, and dichotomous outcomes will be reported as risk ratios or ORs with 95% CIs as well. Heterogeneity will be assessed by visual inspection of the forest plots and calculated using the I2 statistic, where I2 will be interpreted as low (0%–30%), moderate (30%–60%), substantial (60%–75%) and considerable heterogeneity following Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions guidelines.59 Two reviewers will discuss the inclusion of studies in the meta-analysis. Studies will be pooled in the meta-analysis when reviewers reach consensus that they are comparable in the following domains:

Population: Similar stroke duration (eg, acute/subacute vs chronic).

Population: Similar stroke duration (eg, acute/subacute vs chronic).

Intervention: Same or comparable in terms of type, duration and frequency of compression therapy.

Intervention: Same or comparable in terms of type, duration and frequency of compression therapy.

Outcomes: Same outcome domain measured with the same or conceptually similar outcome measures and at comparable follow-up time points.

Outcomes: Same outcome domain measured with the same or conceptually similar outcome measures and at comparable follow-up time points.

Studies must also provide sufficient outcome data (eg, means and SD) to calculate effect estimates. The reasons and rationale for excluding any study from meta-analysis will be recorded in a Word document. Disagreements about eligibility for meta-analysis will be resolved through discussion, with unresolved disputes adjudicated by a third reviewer (SCC) if necessary.

Meta-regression will be considered to explore sources of heterogeneity (eg, intervention duration and frequency) if at least 10 studies are available for an outcome. To reduce the risk of false-positive (type I error) findings, we will prespecify only three covariates for meta-regression (ie, type, duration and frequency of compression therapy) and interpret any meta-regression results as exploratory. The Instrument to assess the Credibility of Effect Modification Analyses checklist60 will be used to evaluate the credibility of any statistically significant meta-regression findings.

Subgroup analyses will be conducted where appropriate and possible. This might consist of subgroups of type of compression therapy, stroke duration (acute/subacute vs chronic) and presence versus absence of cointerventions. Sensitivity analyses will be performed by removing studies with a high risk of bias and comparing fixed vs random effects. Publication bias will be assessed using visual inspection of funnel plots if at least 10 studies are available, which allows for any between-group effect size calculations.

The certainty of the body evidence for each type of compression therapy with respective outcome domains will be determined using the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE).61 Using the GRADE, the certainty of evidence will be evaluated based on eight criteria: risk of bias, inconsistency of results, indirectness of evidence, imprecision, large magnitude of effect, confounding effects, presence of a dose–response relationship and publication bias. The quality of evidence will then be categorised as high, moderate, low or very low. Two reviewers will perform the assessment independently, and discrepancies will be resolved by discussion or by a third reviewer (SCC) if necessary.

Ethics, amendments and dissemination

As this systematic review only involves secondary data to address the research questions, no ethical approval is required. Any protocol amendments will be documented and tracked via the PROSPERO record and captured in the final publication. The completed review will be submitted for publication in peer-reviewed journals.

Discussion

This systematic review aims to compare the effectiveness of compression therapy interventions in managing upper limb dysfunction among patients with stroke. The methods and results of the review will be reported in adherence to the PRISMA statement 2020.62 However, certain limitations are acknowledged. There may be missing data that will affect data collation and pooled analysis. In addition, the language of the included studies is limited to English though there are no geographical restrictions.

By categorising the interventions, this review will identify the effects of each type of compression therapy on the upper limbs of patients with stroke and compare their effectiveness. The findings will help consolidate existing literature and provide insights for practitioners to make informed decisions about the most suitable compression therapy options to achieve their targeted outcomes for patients with stroke. By examining the potential adverse effects of compression therapy, practitioners can mitigate the potential harms while optimising the benefits of the compression therapy for patients with stroke. It is also hoped that this review will address the current uncovered gaps in research or practice, informing or generating the development of novel approaches or refined application strategies for using compression therapy in the management of upper limb dysfunction in patients with stroke in the future.
