Teams perform worse when newcomers hold critical information than when original group members do
Authors: Sydney E. Phlegar, Kareena S. del Rosario, Oana D. Dumitru, Tessa V. West
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In this study, two new acquaintances joined an online chatroom, spent five minutes discussing the qualities of a job candidate, then a newcomer joined the chat. The three group members (Ntriads = 370; Nparticipants = 1110) engaged in the Hidden Profile Task during which they chose the best job candidate among four to hire. Before they began, all group members received common information and one group member—either one of the initial members or the newcomer—received critical, unshared information about one candidate that the team needed to make the correct decision. Teams in which the newcomer held critical information were less likely to make the correct decision than teams in which one of the initial members held it. Newcomers with critical information showed weaker linguistic coordination to group members—a measure of social alignment that captures the degree to which group members mimic each other’s language style—and reported greater perceived task conflict than newcomers who did not have critical information. Lastly, we tested whether a manipulation of prestige-based status—giving false feedback on a test for skills ostensibly relevant to decision making—could overpower newcomer status; however, we did not find credible evidence that it did so. These findings illustrate the power of brief interactions for shaping biases against newcomers.

Introduction

From lab-based science and technology development to healthcare and public policy, organizations depend on teamwork to bring together different sources of expertise1. Today’s teams are dynamic in nature, with new members cycling in at different stages2,3. For example, advisors join early-stage ventures to help establish a business model4, team rotation programs introduce new members to increase learning across departments5, and influxes in hiring can lead to constant changes in team composition6.

Although newcomers are often brought on because they have expertise and unique insights that can help groups make smarter decisions, teams often fail to incorporate their perspectives into their decisions7–9. This may be because changes in group composition can disrupt established communication dynamics among team members9–13.

Yet in newly formed groups, where such dynamics have had little time to take hold, do newcomers still face these challenges in influencing the group? In the present research, we test whether newcomers can influence newly formed teams to incorporate new information the team needs to make the right decision, and explore whether prestige-based status, operationalized as being relatively more competent at decision-making tasks than others in the group, can offset the costs of being new to a group.

Even when created on the basis of minimal information, newly formed teams quickly show bias against non-team members14, suggesting that an “us versus them” mentality can emerge rapidly and that group members may begin differentiating themselves from newcomers almost instantly. In addition, a large body of work on interpersonal perception has shown that people develop communication patterns early in interactions, often within minutes, that set the stage for later ones12,15–24. These patterns often emerge without conscious awareness, yet across settings—from getting-acquainted conversations to negotiations—team members pick up on subtle behavioral cues, which they then use to coordinate with one another and communicate. For example, coordination of nonverbal behaviors (e.g., body movements, eye gaze) is associated with rapport-building, social alignment, and interpersonal connectedness25,26. Although people are often unaware that these early dynamics are unfolding, they can influence team outcomes such as the likelihood of reaching the correct decision in a decision-making task20,27–34. These early forms of interpersonal communication are also fragile. For example, a short time delay in a video-mediated interaction can disrupt rapport building35, and video-manipulated smiling can enhance perceived rapport between two new-acquaintances36. We argue that the disruption caused by newcomers who try to bring novel information into a team discussion may occur very early in the team formation process.

The present study examines interactions among newly formed teams after which a third person joins the team. Two new acquaintances met in a chatroom during which they discussed the traits of a good job applicant for five minutes. A newcomer joined the chatroom and worked with the dyad on a new, yet related, task of deciding who to hire among a set of job candidates for ten minutes. At this stage, one person—either the newcomer or an initial group member—is given unique, critical information, providing them with the “expertise” the group needs to make the correct decision. At the end of the triadic interaction we measured group performance (whether the team made the correct or incorrect decision) and perceptions of team conflict from each team member. During the interaction, we measured a form of interpersonal synchrony between each pair of team members that emerges early in team interactions and can be observed through patterns of text: linguistic coordination.

Linguistic coordination measures the degree to which team members mimic each other’s language style, which includes their use of function words37, pronouns38, and sentence length39. Coordination can be analyzed turn-by-turn in the chat text. It captures alignment in how group members express their thoughts28, not in what they say. People show stronger coordination when they are motivated to attend to a partner or group member, such as when highly engaged in a conversation40,41. This attentiveness matters for group outcomes because effective information exchange relies on members being attentive to others’ contributions42,43. Coordination offers insight into whether people are indeed engaged and is linked to better team performance28,44 and more cooperative and less conflictual group interactions45. Moreover, coordination emerges very early in social interactions, often before teams are aware of more explicit communication dynamics that develop over time (e.g., rules around who should speak up and when), making it an ideal measure of communication for studies on early team development27,46.

In addition to measuring conversation dynamics during the interaction, we also measure perceptions of task conflict at the end of it, or the degree to which each team member perceives conflict during the decision-making process47–49. Task conflict is not a shared group property; team members often disagree on how much conflict they perceive, reflecting the fact that people can experience the same decision-making process differently as a function of their role on the team. Individual perceptions of task conflict are strong predictors of productive teamwork across a variety of contexts48, making it an ideal subjective measure for this study.

We hypothesized that newcomers who are uniquely positioned to help the team (i.e., those given critical information) would experience weaker linguistic coordination to their team members during a team interaction and report more task conflict at the end of it, relative to newcomers who are not in this position to help the team (i.e., newcomers not given critical information), and to original group members. We also hypothesize that teams in which newcomers are given critical information the team needs to succeed will be less successful (i.e., less likely to make the correct decision) than teams in which original group members are given critical information. In other words, teams as a whole will suffer when they rely on newcomers to bring to light novel information compared to when they rely on an original group member.

Lastly, we tested the question: can prestige-based status overpower the newcomer label and enable newcomers to influence groups? Most groups are organized into status hierarchies, which influence group processes and performance50,51. Introducing a high-status newcomer creates a paradox: “newcomer” is a low-influence position, whereas prestige-based status, which is conferred based on individuals’ relevant skills, attributes, and knowledge, is a high-influence position that affects how much respect, admiration, and informal influence people have in groups52–54. Newcomers disrupt the group makeup, are seen as less trustworthy, and are often relegated to a low-power position because of their tenure status55. Adding a newcomer with “expertise” may resolve this ambiguity by making it clear that they should be higher in the hierarchy. Therefore, assigning status pits these two group-value positions against each other to test whether being high-status can override the disadvantages of being a newcomer.

In the present research, we manipulate prestige-based status by providing participants with false information regarding their own and their team members’ scores on a test that ostensibly measures a skill—competence at decision-making—relevant to the hiring task56. After completing the test, they are told that they score relatively higher or lower than other group members57. This method has been used to manipulate prestige-based status in multiple studies58,59. We test the hypothesis that for newcomers, being relatively higher in status coming into the group will alleviate or override the negative effects of being a newcomer. There is some evidence that newcomers with expertise are more likely to influence groups than those without it60, but this effect is not consistent. For example, it varies as a function of other factors, such as how strongly identified the newcomer is with the group61, and whether they engage in different entry behaviors into the group than low-status ones62. We randomly assigned newcomers to either be relatively high or low in prestige-based status coming into the triadic interaction. The original group had one high and one low status member; thus, the newcomer was the same status as one of the original group members, which allows us to compare high versus low prestige-based status newcomers. If prestige-based status overrides the effects of being a newcomer, then only relatively low-status newcomers with critical information should show weaker linguistic coordination to the group and higher task conflict at the end of the discussion. In addition, when high-status newcomers hold the critical information, they will have an easier time influencing the group to come to the correct decision relative to when low-status newcomers have this information.

Methods

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at New York University (IRB-FY2020-3706). Participants provided informed consent before participation, and studies were conducted in compliance with the institution’s ethical guidelines. Participants were compensated $5 for their time. The hypotheses, design, and analyses were not preregistered. Analyses were guided by theoretical predictions.

Power analysis and statistical assumptions

We conducted an a priori power analysis using G*Power for an ANOVA to estimate the minimum number of groups needed to model group-level outcomes (e.g., group performance). We specified 1 numerator degree of freedom and 4 groups (groups where the information belonged to (1) low-status newcomers, (2) high-status newcomers, (3) low-status initial members, and (4) high-status initial members). Based on the median effect sizes reported in social psychology, we estimated a Cohen’s f of 0.263–67, indicating a recommended total sample size of 199 groups. To account for potential attrition and data loss, we recruited a larger number of groups than indicated by the power analysis.

Participants

We initially recruited 443 triads (Nparticipants = 1254) through Prolific Academic. Participants must have had a Prolific approval rating of 100%, which suggests that they had not been rejected from prior studies due to low quality data (e.g., failed attention checks). Additionally, participants were required to speak English as their first language and be a United States citizen. Seventy-three triads were excluded from the analysis for three main reasons: (1) at least one participant in the triad indicated that they suspected the manipulation was not real; (2) at least one participant in the triad did not type anything in the group chat, and (3) due to technical difficulties internet connection was lost for at least one of the participants, resulting in 370 triads (Nparticipants = 1110). Participants were between 17 and 79 years old (Mage = 35, SDage = 12.5). Participants self-reported their gender identity as follows: Men (40.8%), Women (56.8%), Transgender Men (0.4%), Transgender Women (0.3%), gender-queer/gender non-conforming (1.2%), preferred not to say (0.5%), and 0.3% did not indicate their gender identity (see Supple. Methods 1 for more information on participant demographics).

Procedure

We used SMARTRIQS68 to match participants into triads. Upon entering the study, participants were placed in a virtual waiting room while they waited to be matched. After matching, participants were evaluated on their ability to read other people using a “Person Insight” test and were told that they either fell in the 80th percentile (high status) or 40th percentile (low status). Next, two of the participants were presented with the other’s status before entering a chat room where they discussed the characteristics that would make someone a good candidate for the job of an airline pilot for five minutes. The third participant, who would become the newcomer, watched a five-minute nature video during that time. At the end of the five minutes, the initial participants were told that they would be interacting with a third person (newcomer) on a group decision-making task. Participants were then reminded of their own scores on the Person Insight test as well as the other two participants’ scores to ensure that status in the group was salient. For the group decision-making task, participants were told that they would select one job candidate from a pool of four applications to be a pilot for an international airline69. Participants were all provided with positive and negative information about each of the candidates, which presented one of the candidates (Candidate C) as the least attractive candidate. One participant, however, was given critical additional information that made Candidate C clearly the best suited for the job. Pilot testing with 159 participants confirmed that when individuals were provided with the full set of information (i.e., given “critical information”), Candidate C was seen as the best-suited option 75% of the time (for more information on pilot testing, see Suppl. Methods 2).

Participants first reviewed the applications on their own and then had 10 minutes to discuss them as a group. They then completed a final questionnaire. Measures

Status manipulation

To manipulate group member status, we adapted a version of the Meaning Insight Test53,57,70, called the Person Insight Test, in which participants are presented with a series of photos and asked to rate how conscientious they believe the individual in the photo to be based on their appearance. They are told that their scores represent their ability to accurately evaluate others and that these scores are predictive of success in decision-making tasks, to make it clear that their scores were directly relevant to the personnel selection task. In reality, participants were randomly assigned to score in the 40th percentile (low status) or 80th percentile (high status). The initial dyad members were always cross-status, such that one initial member in the group was always high status and the other was always low status, and newcomers could be either low or high status.

Self-report measures

Manipulation checks

We conducted two manipulation checks for status58. First, before each interaction, participants were asked to recall both their own and their partner(s)’ Person Insight scores by identifying the percentile rank (ranging between the 20th to the 90th percentile) to ensure that they remembered each group member’s assigned status. Before the dyadic task, only initial group members completed this check, whereas before the triadic task, all members (including newcomers) completed it.

Ratings of task conflict

After the triad interactions, participants responded to four items relating to how much group conflict there was when trying to reach a decision on a 1 (Strongly agree) to 7 (Strongly disagree) scale48. These items were combined: “There were differences of opinions about task-related issues,” “We disagreed about task-related information,” “We sometimes disagreed about the process to get the task done,” and “We agreed about the way to do things” (reverse-scored; alpha = .82 for triadic task conflict).

Behaviors

Group performance

We evaluated group-level performance based on whether they chose the correct job applicant (Candidate C). Performance was coded as a binary variable such that 1 = correct decision and 0 = incorrect decision.

Linguistic coordination to group members

We measured linguistic coordination using the ConvoKit package in Python71 to analyze conversational data from the chat interactions. For each speaker, ConvoKit estimates the baseline usage of eight function word categories (articles, auxiliary verbs, conjunctions, high-frequency adverbs, impersonal pronouns, personal pronouns, prepositions, and quantifiers) and computes the degree to which one speaker’s use of a specific class of function words triggers the other speakers’ usage of the same word class relative to their own baseline. For example, a coordination score of .10 could mean that the speaker uses articles 10 percentage points more often when their partner has just used them, relative to the speaker’s typical rate. Coordination scores can be negative (speaker uses function word less often after partner) or positive (speaker uses function word more often after partner), and these values tend to cluster around zero. We computed average coordination scores to the other two group members for each participant during the triad hidden profile task.

Results

For our analyses, we used a Bayesian approach with weakly informative priors. We specified normal priors centered at zero for all regression coefficients (Normal(0,1)), while model intercepts were given a slightly wider prior (Normal(0,2.5)) to allow greater flexibility in baseline outcome levels. The Bayesian framework allows us to describe the uncertainty surrounding an effect by estimating a full range of plausible values based on the data. Rather than p-values, we present 95% credible intervals, which show the range of effect sizes best supported by the data. If the credible interval does not include zero, then the data provide strong evidence that the effect is reliably above or below zero. We performed these analyses in R using the brm() function within the brms package72. Binary predictors (role, who had critical information, and status) were effect-coded (-1,1) for multilevel model analyses and dummy-coded (0,1) for logistic regressions. We also performed these analyses using frequentist techniques and obtained nearly identical results (see Suppl. Note 1).

For all analyses, we first ran models that included the main effect of status, and interactions of status with role, who had critical information, and the three-way interaction between role and who had critical information. No credible interactions were found with status, and we report the results of these models in Suppl. Note 2. For parsimony, in the main text, we only report the analyses with the main effect of status. Results are consistent across analyses.

Manipulation checks for status

Because response options were ordinal percentiles (20th to 90th percentile), we tested whether participant responses were systematically closer to their assigned status using a Bayesian ordinal regression. We tested this twice: once prior to the dyadic interaction and once prior to the triadic interaction. For both, we found a credible effect of status such that high-status participants were more likely to report higher percentile ranks than low-status participants, (prior to dyadic interaction; β = 3.55, 95% CrI [3.15, 3.97) and prior to triadic interaction; β = 4.05, 95% CrI [3.65, 4.49]).

As with task conflict, we tested whether linguistic coordination to one’s partners varied by role (initial member vs newcomer) and who held the critical information, adjusting for the main effect of status (low vs high). Discussion

In the present research, we examined whether newcomers who have joined newly formed groups—those whose members have been working together for five minutes—face challenges that those who join established groups face. Consistent with prior work documenting biases against newcomers in decision-making teams—including those who are experts brought on to teams for their unique perspectives9—groups of three were less likely to make the correct decision when a newcomer held critical information than when an initial group member did. In addition, we found that compared to newcomers who did not hold critical information coming into the group, those that did hold critical information reported greater task conflict and showed weaker linguistic coordination to team members—an interpersonal process that underlies coordination in teams.

These findings illustrate that not all newcomers face a “newcomer hump” when joining teams; in fact, those not tasked with sharing critical information showed positive linguistic coordination to their team members. It may be the case that these newcomers did not need to disrupt communication dynamics, so they could prioritize the goal to socialize with the group in an effort to gain acceptance73, which is reflected in their coordination scores. Future work could test this possibility by directly measuring affiliative versus persuasive behaviors (e.g., signaling warmth versus expertise) or experimentally manipulating whether newcomers are incentivized to prioritize being liked by the group or having influence over the group. In addition, such studies should measure repeated interactions among such groups to understand the point at which newcomers lose their newcomer status. It remains to be seen whether there is a critical point at which affiliative goals are no longer relevant, especially in group decision-making tasks where having influence is critical for avoiding big mistakes.

In our research, we also focused on perceptions of task conflict, which were made about the group in general. Consistent with prior work showing that teams often lack consensus in judgments of conflict because it’s subjective and does not reflect a shared property of the group74,75, we found variability in perceptions of conflict, with newcomers who held critical information perceiving more task conflict than others overall. These newcomers’ perceptions of relatively high conflict may reflect their subjective experience of communication difficulties when attempting to introduce critical information. However, other scholars have found that this pattern, in which the majority of the team perceived low conflict and the minority perceived relatively high conflict, positively impacts performance. For example, in some teams, high-conflict minorities express divergent views in a careful and cooperative manner, and low-conflict majorities respond by engaging in open and productive task discussions and reflective communication. This pattern is associated with better performance74,76.

In those studies, however, minority members who perceived high conflict were accepted by team members, and their dissenting views contributed to productive group discussions. Our findings suggest a different process for when the high-conflict minorities are newcomers. When newcomers struggle to get the team to consider their views, their perceptions of conflict may reflect their frustration with not being able to influence the team. The majority, however, may have been unaware of the newcomer’s experience, and the interaction may have then felt smooth because the discussion focused on common information.

Limitations and future directions

There are several limitations that future research should address. First, by using an online chat, we restricted the available cues team members could use to perceive one another and build communication in teams. Although this methodological choice enables us to isolate the effect of being a newcomer independent of other social identity characteristics, it deprived team members of nonverbal and verbal behaviors (e.g., eye contact, speech length, interruptions, back channels), which not only play a critical role in team decision making77, but are the primary channels through which status cues are displayed78–80. Prestige-based status in particular is displayed through a distinct set of cues—upward head tilt, symmetrical smile, subtle chest expansion—which our participants did not have a chance to express or perceive in others81. The absence of these cues might explain in part why the status manipulation did not override newcomer status.

We also note that when manipulating prestige-based status, the realism in how this form of status is naturally communicated gets a bit lost. When prestige-based status is earned (rather than manipulated), people have knowledge of each other’s past experiences and successes, which can be made explicit during team interactions, especially if they are pertinent to the team decision. To this end, prestige-based status can be communicated explicitly, like our manipulation. However, giving people explicit status rankings does not mirror how earned prestige-based status is typically conveyed. This information is inferred by the group during the status conferral process. Finding naturalistic ways of manipulating status rankings is always a challenge, but future work should test whether other manipulations, including those that subtly remind people of their prestige-based status in person82 are powerful enough to interact with newcomer status, and potentially overpower it. We did find one effect of status: that consistent with prior work on the effects of status and power on nonverbal synchrony39,83,84, linguistic coordination scores were lower among high-status group members than low-status group members, suggesting that the manipulation influenced one subtle process, but it did not overpower the effect of who held critical information—the newcomer or an original group member-–on the team decision making process.

Second, it should be noted that the effects on linguistic coordination were small, such that high-status members were about half a percentage point less likely to coordinate with the group, and newcomers with critical information were about two percentage points less likely to coordinate with the group. The small effect sizes are consistent with how we conceptualize linguistic coordination as a subtle and unconscious process. By extending the present paradigm to include in-person or video interactions, we could also test the conditions under which the social identities of team members interact with the variables we manipulated. When newcomers are socially similar to initial group members (e.g., in terms of race or gender), teams expect them to agree with the collective. Often, those newcomers unwittingly stick to the ideas of the group85. What happens when socially similar newcomers have unique insight and need to go against the group? One possibility is that they face immediate backlash, experiencing even more resistance than newcomers who are socially dissimilar to initial group members.

Additionally, it will be important to study the dynamic interplay between team members’ nonverbal behaviors in tandem with linguistic coordination, which, like other measures of interpersonal synchrony, does not reflect one single psychological process. Linguistic coordination can reflect the motivation to persuade86 and to build rapport27, and yet in contexts like group decision-making, the goal to build rapport is often in conflict with the goal to persuade87,88. Thus, studying other dynamic measures that co-occur with linguistic coordination is critical for narrowing down which process is occurring. For example, for some team members linguistic coordination may co-occur with rapport-building behaviors89,90, whereas for others, it co-occurs with those related to persuasion91.

Lastly, this study was designed such that there would only be one newcomer and two initial members in each group. It is possible that some of our findings may be attributed to the fact that the newcomer had “solo status,” meaning they were uniquely distinct from the rest of the group92,93. Solo status makes newcomers’ adjustment to new groups more difficult than joining groups at the same time as other newcomers14. Therefore, another avenue for future research entails investigating whether being one of several newcomers leads to different outcomes than being the only newcomer in the group. Additionally, the study used a dyad-to-triad structure, which may introduce qualities unique to dyads. Dyadic bonds tend to be rooted in rapport and positive affect, whereas bonds in larger groups are often driven by uncertainty reduction94. Dyadic bonds may therefore be more difficult for newcomers to break through compared to larger initial groups where bonds may be more diffuse. Still, our findings mirror research on newcomers joining larger teams, where teams often fail to utilize newcomer insights9,95, suggesting that the newcomer penalty is not an artifact of using dyads for the initial groups. Future work could directly examine whether these findings extend to larger starting groups.

Overall, the results of this study demonstrate that the identity of “newcomer” can form rapidly, and even short interactions between newly acquainted dyads can create a strong advantage for initial members. This has important implications for interpersonal interactions because it suggests that a mere five minutes of interaction between two or more people can automatically make it more difficult for a new person to join the group, even if they bring unique perspectives and critical information.
