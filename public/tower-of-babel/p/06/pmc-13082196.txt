Exercise training induces mitochondrial biogenesis, while high‐fat diet increases the ability of mitochondria to use long and short‐chain fatty acids
Authors: Jessica L Dowling, Rachel M Handy, Nicole M Notaro, Sara M Frangos, David J Dyck, Graham P Holloway
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAbstractShort‐chain fatty acids (SCFAs), derived from peroxisomal metabolism and the gut microbiota, have been proposed as key substrates to support mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) in extrahepatic tissues such as skeletal muscle. However, the extent to which mitochondria can oxidize SCFAs (acetate, propionate and butyrate) and the ability of exercise training and a high‐fat diet (HFD) to modulate this process remains unclear. Here, we show that SCFA‐supported respiration in skeletal muscle is relatively limited (18 ± 6 nmol min−1 mg−1), accounting for only ∼7% of maximal carbohydrate (pyruvate: 252 ± 41 nmol min−1 mg−1) and ∼14% of LCFA (palmitoylcarnitine)‐linked respiration. Despite this low capacity, the intrinsic mitochondrial ability to oxidize palmitoylcarnitine, acetate and butyrate increased (P < 0.05: +50%) following HFD consumption, suggesting HFD rewires mitochondria to optimize lipid oxidation. By contrast, exercise training prevented these HFD‐induced intrinsic mitochondrial responses. Although intrinsic changes are biologically relevant, skeletal muscle adaptation to metabolic stress also involves mitochondrial biogenesis and an expansion of the mitochondrial proteome. Proteomic analysis and citrate synthase activity revealed that, although HFD independently did not alter mitochondrial protein abundance, exercise training increased mitochondrial proteins, a response amplified in the presence of a HFD. Consequently, although exercise did not directly enhance mitochondrial SCFA‐supported respiration, the combined effect of HFD and exercise predicted a greater overall capacity for SCFA oxidation because of increased mitochondrial abundance. Collectively, although SCFAs contribute minimally to mitochondrial respiration in skeletal muscle, combined HFD and exercise synergistically enhance overall OXPHOS capacity across diverse substrates, including SCFAs, primarily through increased mitochondrial protein abundance rather than intrinsic mitochondrial remodelling. Key points Peroxisome and gut derived short‐chain fatty acids (SCFA) have been proposed as an alternative metabolic fuel source to support skeletal muscle oxidative phosphorylation.The capacity and adaptability of mitochondrial SCFA oxidation remains unknown.SCFA‐supported mitochondrial respiration is limited (<15%) compared to carbohydrate (pyruvate) and long‐chain fatty acid linked substrates.High‐fat feeding increased the intrinsic capacity of mitochondria to utilize palmitoylcarnitine, acetate and butyrate− effects prevented by 4 weeks of exercise training.Combined high‐fat diet and exercise training increased skeletal muscle mitochondrial protein content in an additive manner, increasing oxidative capacity and ability to utilize both long‐ and SCFAs as a fuel source. Keywords: exercise, high‐fat diet, mitochondria, short‐chain fatty acids, skeletal muscle

Introduction

The content of mitochondrial proteins within skeletal muscle, particularly those involved in oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), display an impressive ability to respond to changes in energetic supply and demand (Erecińska & Wilson, 1982; van Loon et al., 2001). A key adaptation to exercise training is the induction of mitochondrial biogenesis, triggered by the rapid activation of the calcium‐AMPK signalling axis following a single bout of exercise, initiating a co‐ordinated transcriptional and translational response involving both the nuclear and mitochondrial genomes (Gollnick et al., 1973; Holloszy, 1967; Perry et al., 2010). Similar to exercise, high‐fat feeding‐induced redox signalling chronically activates calcium‐dependent pathways and increases skeletal muscle mitochondrial content and respiratory capacity (Gurr et al., 1989; Hancock et al., 2008; Jain et al., 2014). Given the conserved mechanisms by which exercise and high‐fat diet (HFD)‐induced signalling increase mitochondrial content, this evidence raises the possibility that their combination may additively affect mitochondrial protein accumulation and the oxidative capacity of muscle.

Given their abundance in the circulation and within skeletal muscle, carbohydrates and long‐chain fatty acids (LCFA) are key substrates readily available to support OXPHOS at rest and during exercise (Holloway & Spriet, 2012). In addition to carbohydrates and LCFA, short‐chain fatty acids (SCFA), primarily produced by the gut microbiota and peroxisomal fatty acid oxidation, have emerged as important metabolic regulators and may provide additional substrates to support OXPHOS within muscle (Frampton et al., 2020; Gao et al., 2009; Hong et al., 2016; Lhiri et al., 2019; Schönfeld & Wojtczak, 2016). Although colonocytes metabolize a substantial portion of gut‐derived SCFAs, the remainder enters the systemic circulation for uptake by peripheral tissues (Bloemen et al., 2009). Plasma concentrations of the SCFAs, acetate, propionate and butyrate are reported to be 100–200 µM, 1.7–3.6 µM and 1.0–7.5 µM, respectively, which are substantially lower than circulating LCFAs (0.22–0.45 mm) (Arabi et al., 2019; Bloemen et al., 2009; Tangerman et al., 1983). Despite their low concentrations in circulation, arteriovenous measurements across skeletal muscles suggest that SCFAs are transported into muscle and can be oxidized to support ATP production within mitochondria (Steffen et al., 1982; van Hall et al., 2002). However, these measurements do not consider the intracellular supply of SCFAs via peroxisomal very‐long‐chain (VLCFA) and branched‐chain (BCFA) fatty acid oxidation which can in turn be transported into mitochondria for oxidation (Jakobs & Wanders, 1995; Lodhi & Semenkovich, 2014; Trefely et al., 2020). This pathway appears particularly relevant in skeletal muscle where both HFDs and exercise stimulate peroxisomal biogenesis and increase VLCFA oxidation (Huang et al., 2017, 2019). Thus, direct assessments of SCFA‐supported mitochondrial bioenergetics may provide a more precise assessment of the capacity of muscle to utilize all available SCFAs.

By contrast to the highly regulated carnitine palmitoyl‐transferase (CPT) system necessary for the uptake and oxidation of LCFAs within mitochondria (Bremer, 1962, 1983), SCFA uptake is CPT independent (Roe et al., 2002). However, the abundance of short‐chain acyl‐CoA synthetase (ACSS) enzymes necessary for the activation and subsequent oxidation of SCFAs is low, suggesting the capacity for SCFAs to support OXPHOS is probably minimal compared to LCFAs (Robitaille et al., 1993; Szutowicz et al., 1981). Nevertheless, although HFD and exercise‐induced mitochondrial adaptations may preferentially optimize pathways specific to carbohydrate and LCFA oxidation, the low starting capacity for SCFA oxidation within skeletal muscle mitochondria may provide a larger reserve for adaptation. Given the low abundance of ACSS in muscle and the ATP requirement for this reaction to occur, SCFAs would be expected to contribute to ATP production under conditions of low energetic demand and high substrate availability. A HFD is expected to raise plasma SCFA levels via increased gut production and decreased liver metabolism (Dankert et al., 1981), whereas high lipid availability during recovery from exercise may stimulate peroxisomal fatty acid oxidation (Huang et al., 2017, 2019). Therefore, it is hypothesized that the capacity for SCFAs to support mitochondrial OXPHOS will be lower than carbohydrate‐ and LCFA‐linked substrates in muscle, but that HFD and exercise training will independently and additively increase the capacity of skeletal muscle to utilize SCFA. In the present study, incorporating mitochondrial bioenergetic and quantitative proteomic analyses revealed that high‐fat feeding and exercise training uniquely affect skeletal muscle metabolism, as the combined intervention prevented the typical HFD response in intrinsic mitochondrial fatty acid utilization yet additively increased the content of mitochondrial proteins to enhance overall respiratory capacity supported by SCFAs.

Methods

Animals

Five‐week‐old male Sprague–Dawley rats were obtained from Charles River Laboratories (Wilmington, MA, USA) and group‐housed (four rats per cage) under a 12:12 h reverse light/dark photocycle at room temperature (25°C).

Experimental design

Rats were randomized to consume either a low‐fat (LFD) or high‐fat (HFD) diet for 6 weeks when remaining sedentary (Sed) or exposed to exercise training (Ex) for the final 4 weeks (n = 8–9 per group; LFD‐Sed, LFD‐Ex, HFD‐Sed, HFD‐Ex). After 6 weeks, rats were anaesthetized with sodium pentobarbital (6 mg per 100 mg of body weight), skeletal muscles were excised for further analysis and the heart was excised to kill the animal. Terminal surgery for tissue collection was randomized within and across experimental days (two animals per day). The experiments were approved by the University of Guelph's Animal Care Committee (AUP No. 4933) and conform to the animal ethical principles outlined by The Journal of Physiology (Grundy, 2015)).

Diets

The animal diets, sourced from Research Diets Inc. (New Brunswick, NJ, USA), were sucrose‐matched, purified ingredient diets consisting of 10% fat for the LFD group (Product #D12450J) and 60% fat for the HFD group (Product No. D12492). The rats had ad libitum access to both food and water.

Exercise

The exercised rats were familiarized to treadmill running at low speeds prior to starting the exercise protocol. They then completed 1 h of high‐intensity treadmill running 5 days per week, starting at 10 m min−1 with a 0% incline. Running speed was increased 1 m min−1 day−1, whereas the treadmill incline was increased 5% each week until 25 m min−1 and a 15% incline was reached. Rats maintained this exercise intensity for the remainder of the training protocol with an additional bout of 6 × 1 min sprints (35–40 m min−1, 10% incline) during the final week. All rats completed their final training session 48 h prior to tissue collection.

Tissue collection

A small portion of the red quadriceps (RQ) muscle from each animal was excised and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen for western blotting, citrate synthase activity and proteomic analysis. The remaining RQ was placed in isolation buffer (100 mm sucrose, 100 mm KCl, 50 mm Tris‐HCl, 1 mm KH2PO4, 0.1 mm EGTA, 0.2% bovine serum albumin and 1 mm ATP, pH 7.2) for mitochondrial isolation. The RQ was selected because of its mixed fibre type composition which, similar to human vastus lateralis muscle, contains ∼50% type 1 and type 2 fibers and demonstrates robust adaptive responses to treadmill training (Delp & Duan, 1996; Holloszy & Coyle, 1984).

Liquid chromatography‐mass spectrometry

Liquid chromatography‐mass spectrometry (LC‐MS) using data‐dependent acquisition was used to quantify proteins (proteomic analysis) in whole muscle homogenates. RQ muscle of six animals from each group was homogenized in lysis buffer with protease inhibitor and phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride. The Bradford assay was used to determine the protein concentration of samples. Aliquots containing 25 µg of protein per lysate were diluted at a 1:10 ratio with denaturation buffer (6 m urea/2 m thiourea) for efficient digestion. Samples were subsequently reduced with 10 mm DTT at room temperature for 30 min, followed by alkylation with 20 mm iodoacetamide at room temperature for 30 min in the dark. Samples were then precipitated by adding cold acetone (6:1 v/v) and kept at –80°C for 60 min. Pellets were collected following centrifugation at 12,000 g for 10 min at 4°C and allowed to air dry for 10–30 min to remove any remaining acetone. Afterwards, samples were resuspended in 50 mm ammonium bicarbonate and incubated with MD‐grade trypsin (catalog. no. 90057; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) at a ratio of 1:50 protease to protein overnight at 37°C. Following digestion, samples were resuspended in 50 mm ammonium bicarbonate and dried by vacuum centrifugation. Pierce C18 Spin columns (catalog. no. 89873; Thermo Fisher Scientific) were utilized to purify samples where dried pellets were resuspended in 0.1% formic acid.

The Vanquish Neo UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was combined with an Orbitrap Exploris 240 mass spectrometer using the Easy‐Spray source (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for nanoLC‐MS‐based protein identification. This system operated in a trap‐and‐elute configuration, where peptides were first retained and washed on a PepMap Neo C18 trap column (Thermo Fisher Scientific) (5 µm particle size, 300 µm × 5 mm dimensions) before being separated on an EASY‐Spray column (75 µm inner diameter, 50 cm length) under a maximum pressure of 1200 bar. The nanoLC‐MS workflow was managed using the Standard Instrument Integration (SII) for Xcalibur software (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The mobile phase A and weak wash solution consisted of water with 0.1% formic acid, whereas mobile phase B and the strong wash solution contained 80% acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid. The gradient program involved the following steps: 4–19% mobile phase B over 72 min, 19–29% B over 28 min, 29–45% B over 20 min, followed by a 14.5 min wash at 100% B. The flow rate was maintained at 300 nL min−1. System temperatures were maintained (autosampler at 7°C and the column at 45°C). Sample injection was performed with the ‘Fast Loading’ function enabled, using pressure control at 500 bar. The column's ‘Fast Equilibration’ feature was also enabled, with pressure control set to 800 bar and an equilibration factor of 3. The autosampler was configured to detect vial bottoms.

Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on the proteome data to visualize sample clustering and identify potential outliers before statistical analyses were conducted. Protein identification was established using the false discovery rate, whereas label‐free quantification (LFQ) was used to determine the relative abundance of proteins across samples. A t test was used to identify differentially expressed proteins between LFD‐Sed and HFD‐Ex which were subsequently used for pathway analysis via Gene Ontology (GO) (http://geneontology.org). Total mitochondrial protein abundance in each sample was determined by calculating the sum of all mitochondrial (MitoCarta: https://www.broadinstitute.org/mitocarta/mitocarta30‐inventory‐mammalian‐mitochondrial‐proteins‐and‐pathways) proteins that were detected across all samples.

Citrate synthase activity

Citrate synthase activity was measured in RQ muscle homogenates as described previously (Holloway et al., 2009a; Perry et al., 2010). Briefly, a portion of the muscle (∼10 mg) was homogenized in 100 vol/wt of a 100 mm potassium phosphate buffer and CS activity was assayed spectrophotometrically at 37°C by measuring the absorption at 412 nm (2) in the presence of acetyl‐CoA and oxaloacetate as originally reported (Srere, 1969).

Isolation of mitochondria

Differential centrifugation at 4°C was performed to isolate intact mitochondria, containing both subsarcolemmal (SS) and intermyofibrillar (IMF) fractions, from RQ muscle as previously described (Frangos et al., 2023; Holloway et al., 2009a, 2009b). After tissue collection, RQ samples were trimmed of visible fat and connective tissue, weighed and minced in isolation buffer. The minced tissue was then homogenized and subjected to centrifugation at 800 g for 10 min to collect the supernatant containing SS mitochondria. The remaining pellet was resuspended in 4 mL of isolation buffer and treated with protease for 5 min to denature contractile proteins. To terminate the reaction, 10 mL of isolation buffer was added to the protease‐treated homogenate, followed by centrifugation at 5000 g for 5 min. The supernatant was discarded and the pellet was resuspended in 4 mL of isolation buffer and centrifuged again at 800 g for 10 min. The supernatant, now containing IMF mitochondria, was collected. Both SS and IMF mitochondrial fractions were subsequently centrifuged at 10,000 g for 10 min, after which the supernatant was discarded. The resulting pellets were resuspended in 0.5 mL of isolation buffer and pooled, followed by two additional centrifugations at 10,000 g for 10 min. The final pellet, containing both SS and IMF mitochondria, was resuspended in 300 µL of Mg2 +‐absent MiR05 (pH 7.2). Throughout the isolation process, samples were kept on ice. The mitochondrial protein content was determined using the Bradford assay prior to conducting mitochondrial respiration experiments.

Percoll gradient and western blotting

Isolated mitochondria were processed through a Percoll (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA) density gradient before western blot analysis, as previously described (Holloway et al., 2009a, 2009b). Briefly, RQ mitochondrial samples were placed on top of 1 mL of 60% Percoll solution (composed of 1.336 mL 5 × SMEA, 4 mL of Percoll and 1.334 mL of dH2O; density 1.08–1.12 g mL−1) and centrifuged at 20,000 g for 5 h at 4°C. The mitochondrial layer was identified as a dark and consistent band traversing the Eppendorf around the top one‐third of the column. The resulting mitochondrial layer was carefully extracted, suspended in 1 mL of isolation buffer and centrifuged again at 12,000 g for 10 min at 4°C to remove any residual Percoll. The mitochondrial pellet was then resuspended in isolation buffer and prepared for western blotting along with homogenized whole RQ samples. After protein quantification using the Bradford assay, samples were diluted with water and Laemmli buffer to achieve a concentration of 1 µg µL−1. Equal amounts of each sample (10 µg) were loaded and separated by SDS‐PAGE, transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride membranes, and incubated with blocking solutions and appropriate primary and secondary antibodies. Commercially available antibodies were used to detect electron transport chain complex IV (COXIV; A21347; dilution 1:30,000; Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA), superoxide dismutase 2 (SOD2; Ab13533; dilution 1:5,000; Abcam, Cambridge, UK), pyruvate dehydrogenase subunit E1 (PDHE1; 459400; dilution 1:1,000; Invitrogen), beta‐hydroxyacyl‐CoA dehydrogenase (ß‐HAD; Ab55231; dilution 1:1,000; Abcam), adenine nucleotide transporter (ANT2; AP1057; dilution 1:1,000; Merck Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany), caveolin 1 (CAV1; 610406; dilution 1:1,000; BD Biosciences), glucose transporter 4 (GLUT4; ab654, dilution 1:2,500; Abcam), sarcoplasmic/endoplasmic reticulum calcium ATPase (SERCA2; Ab2861; dilution 1:1,000; Abcam), calnexin (C4731; Sigma‐Aldrich, St Louis, MO, USA) and total protein kinase B (AKT; ab4691L; dilution 1:1,000; Abcam). The proteins of interest were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (FluorChem HD; Alpha Innotech, Kasendorf, Germany).

Mitochondrial respiration

High‐resolution respirometry experiments on isolated mitochondria were conducted using the Oroboros‐2k system (Oroboros Instruments, Innsbruck, Austria) at 37°C, with constant stirring and room air saturation, as previously described (Frangos et al., 2023). To determine ADP/O ratios and maximal complex I‐ and II‐supported respiration, 30 µg of rat RQ isolated mitochondrial protein was added to chambers containing 2 mL of Mg2+‐absent MiR05 (pH 7.2). ADP/O ratios were calculated by measuring the change in oxygen content following the addition of 200 nmol of ADP in the presence of 5 mm pyruvate and 2 mm malate. Maximal respiration was quantified after the subsequent addition of 5 mm ADP, 10 mm glutamate and 20 mm succinate. SCFA state 3 respiration (2 mm SCFA, 2 mm malate, 5 mm ADP) was determined using 50 µg of mitochondrial protein from rat RQ muscle in the absence and presence of 2 mm potassium bicarbonate (KHCO3 −). The final concentration of SCFA (2 mm) was selected after performing titrations to ensure maximal respiration was achieved and based on a previous report highlighting the ability of KHCO3 − to stimulate respiration (Goldberg et al., 2019). To confirm the coupling efficiency of mitochondria was maintained with the addition of KHCO3 −, ADP/O ratio experiments with and without KHCO3 − was performed. Maximal SCFA‐supported respiration was carried out using 50 µg of rat RQ isolated mitochondrial protein in the presence of 2 mm KHCO3, 2 mm SCFA (acetate, propionate or butyrate), 2 mm malate and 5 mm ADP. Experiments were completed with the addition of either 5 mm pyruvate or 25 µM palmitoylcarnitine. Mitochondrial respiration experiments were conducted in duplicate. Pyruvate‐ and palmitoylcarnitine‐supported respiration values were averaged across SCFA experiments. Acetate‐, propionate‐ and butyrate‐supported respiration was determined by averaging state 3 respiration across carbohydrate and lipid experiments. Outliers, defined as more than 2 SD away from the mean, were removed from the final analysis. This included three values in palmitoylcarnitine, two values in pyruvate and propionate, and one value in acetate and butyrate experiments.

Estimated muscle oxidative capacity

Mitochondrial protein abundance in whole RQ muscle homogenates was quantified from label‐free quantitative LC‐MS. LFQ values for each individual protein (138 in total) were expressed relative to LFD‐Sed defined as a value of 1 and summed (LFD‐SED = 138). This approach equally weights changes in individual proteins, as opposed to relying on summing raw LFQ values, comprising an approach that would emphasize changes in more abundant proteins which are probably not rate‐limiting. The calculated mitochondrial abundance was then multiplied by isolated mitochondrial state 3 respiration (expressed as pmol min−1 mg−1) to estimate muscle oxidative capacity for each substrate (pyruvate, palmitoylcarnitine, acetate, propionate, butyrate).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using RStudio (Boston, MA, USA) and Prism (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). The effect of HCO3 − on SCFA‐supported mitochondrial respiration was assessed using a one‐tailed paired Student's t test. Two‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to determine the interaction between diet and exercise and a least significant difference post hoc test was performed when an interaction (P < 0.05) was observed. Main effects were reported when a column and/or row factor (P < 0.05) was present in the absence of an interaction. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All data are reported as the mean ± SD.

Results

SCFA oxidation in RQ mitochondria is enhanced by the addition of bicarbonate

We first aimed to evaluate the ability of SCFAs, specifically acetate, propionate and butyrate, to stimulate respiration in isolated mitochondria. Isolated mitochondria were highly purified (content of mitochondrial proteins) (Fig. 1A ), well‐coupled (P/O ratios >2.5) (Fig. 1B–D ) and displayed robust responses to ADP (RCR > 8) and complex I and II linked substrates (Fig. 1D and E ). Although ADP stimulated respiration in the presence of SCFAs (Fig. 1F ), the drive was relatively small. We therefore examined the possibility of HCO3 − increasing SCFA‐supported respiration (Goldberg et al., 2019) because propionate requires HCO3 − for oxidation (Fig. 1G ) and HCO3 − has been shown to increase plasma membrane transport, raising the potential for a conserved effect for SCFA transport across mitochondrial membranes (Mascolo et al., 1991; Reynolds et al., 1993; Umesaki et al., 1979). In support, the addition of HCO3 − significantly increased SCFA‐supported respiration (P = 0.0273, acetate; P = 0.0335, propionate; P = 0.0022, butyrate) without impairing mitochondrial coupling efficiency (P = 0.303) (Fig. 1H ). However, maximal respiration supported by acetate, propionate and butyrate remained ∼7% of pyruvate (Pyr) (Fig. 1I ) and ∼14% of palmitoylcarnitine (PC) (Fig. 1J ). Combined, these data highlight the minimal capacity for SCFAs to support OXPHOS in muscle.

Exercise and high‐fat feeding remodel the mitochondrial proteome, enhancing fatty acid oxidation pathways in skeletal muscle

Given the minimal respiratory drive with SCFAs, we investigated whether high fat‐feeding and/or exercise training could possibly stimulate/increase the capacity of skeletal muscle mitochondria to utilize SCFAs (Fig. 2A ). Predictably, a HFD increased body weight (P = 0.0736) (Fig. 2B ) and fasting blood glucose (main effect, P = 0.0015) (Fig. 2C ), responses effectively prevented with exercise training (Fig. 2B and C ). LFQ mass spectrometry‐based proteomics identified ∼2750 total proteins, with 455 proteins consistently detected across samples, which were used to examine the impact of HFD and exercise on proteins influencing SCFA metabolism. PCA suggested combined HFD‐Ex displayed a unique proteomic profile (Fig. 2D ), supported by the apparent protein distribution visualized in a volcano plot (Fig. 2E ). The HFD proteomic signature was relatively similar to LFD‐Sed controls with almost equal abundance of proteins up‐and‐down regulated and very few displaying >log2(1)‐fold change. By contrast, exercise altered the proteomic signature regardless of diet, with combined HFD‐Ex displaying marked differences, including 223 up‐regulated proteins compared to LFD‐Sed animals (Fig. 2E ). GO Biological Process analysis of these proteins revealed differential expression of several pathways involved in mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation (Fig. 2F ). Thus, to further investigate differences in the mitochondrial proteome, we utilized MitoCarta, a comprehensive database of mitochondrial proteins, to investigate specific mitochondrial changes between groups. Of the 455 conserved proteins detected across samples, 138 were contained within MitoCarta (Fig. 2G ). The sum of these 138 proteins were used as a measure of the mitochondrial protein abundance of each sample. The content of these MitoCarta proteins suggested that exercise induced the accumulation of mitochondrial proteins indicative of mitochondrial biogenesis (main effect of exercise, P = 0.0017) (Fig. 2H ), with an additive response in animals consuming a HFD (P = 0.0302) (Fig. 2H , inset). We evaluated these findings by assessing citrate synthase (CS) activity (Fig. 2I ), a well‐established marker of mitochondrial content (Larsen et al., 2012). Importantly, CS activity showed an identical pattern to the proteomic data where exercise increased CS activity (main effect of exercise, P = 0.0005) (Fig. 2I ), with an additive response in animals consuming a HFD (P = 0.0388) (Fig. 2I , inset). Given the apparent Ex‐mediated accumulation of mitochondrial proteins, we analyzed total SCFA oxidation protein abundance (Fig. 2J ), which predictably increased with exercise training (P = 0.049). We next normalized SCFA protein abundance to total mitochondrial protein abundance to investigate the potential for HFD‐Ex to concentrate enzymes involved in SCFA oxidation within mitochondria (Fig. 2K ). Although the enzyme ACSS2, comprising the mitochondrial isoform critical for acetate oxidation, was not detected, most enzymes involved in propionate and butyrate oxidation were identified in all samples (Fig. 2L ). Although the relative abundance of cumulative mitochondrial SCFA (P = 0.441) (Fig. 2K ) and several specific SCFA proteins were not affected by HFD or exercise (P = 0.751; ACADS; P = 0.559, PCC; P = 0.783, ECHM; P = 0.327, HCDH) (Fig. 2M–P ), methylmalonyl‐CoA epimerase (MCE) was concentrated following exercise (main effect of exercise, P = 0.046) (Fig. 2Q ), whereas exercise and HFD decreased the abundance of acetyl‐CoA acetyltransferase (THIL) additively (main effect of diet, P = 0.0028; main effect of exercise, P < 0.0001; P = 0.0055, LFD‐Ex vs. HFD‐Ex) (Fig. 2R and S ). Combined, these proteomic profiles raise the possibility that HFD and exercise uniquely affect SCFA oxidation proteins.

The effects of diet and exercise on the capacity of mitochondria to utilize carbohydrate and fatty acid substrates in skeletal muscle

Given the divergent responses in proteins involved in SCFA oxidation, we next directly investigated mitochondrial OXPHOS in the presence of various substrates, including SCFA in the presence of HCO3 −. Although pyruvate supported respiration was not affected by diet or exercise (P = 0.527) (Fig. 3A and B ), HFD increased the capacity for mitochondria to utilize LCFA (P = 0.0413) (Fig. 3C and D ), a response not observed in exercise trained animals (Fig. 3D ). With the clear impact of diet and exercise on mitochondrial LCFA utilization, we sought to determine whether these effects extended to the SCFAs. High‐fat feeding increased acetate (P = 0.0457) and butyrate (main effect, P = 0.0145) but not propionate (P = 0.637) respiration (Fig. 3E–G ). Although exercise did not affect either propionate or butyrate supported respiration (Fig. 3F and G), similar to palmitoylcarnitine‐supported respiration, exercise mitigated the HFD‐induced increase in acetate‐supported OXPHOS (Fig. 3E ). Altogether, these data suggest that, in the absence of exercise, HFD increased the relative abundance of enzymes and the functional capacity of mitochondria to utilize both LCFA and SCFA, which was not observed with pyruvate or propionate (Fig. 3H ).

The combination of exercise and high‐fat feeding increases carbohydrate, LCFA and SCFA supported respiratory capacity in skeletal muscle

Although exercise prevented the HFD‐induced changes to palmitoylcarnitine and acetate supported respiration in isolated mitochondria, it is important to note that the canonical induction of mitochondrial biogenesis following exercise training is not captured with the use of this model (Fig. 4A ). Therefore, to gain a more complete understanding of the impact of HFD and exercise on skeletal muscle fuel utilization, we estimated the oxidative capacity of muscle by factoring the total abundance of mitochondrial proteins relative to the LFD‐Sed condition (obtained from LFQ‐based LC‐MS) with the intrinsic capacity of mitochondria to utilize substrates to support OXPHOS (obtained from state 3 respiration with each substrate in isolated mitochondria). Using this approach, we found that exercise training increased the capacity of muscle to utilize all substrates, a response potentially potentiated in rats consuming a HFD (pyruvate: interaction, P = 0.0911; palmitoylcarnitine: main effect of diet, P < 0.0001 and main effect of exercise, P < 0.0001; acetate: main effect of diet, P = 0.0003 and main effect of exercise, P = 0.0042; propionate: interaction, P = 0.0257; Butyrate: interaction, P = 0.0641) (Fig. 4B–F ).

Discussion

The aim of the present study was (i) to investigate the capacity of mitochondria to utilize SCFAs to support OXPHOS and (ii) to determine the ability of high fat feeding and exercise to potentially enhance SCFA‐supported respiration in skeletal muscle. Combining measurements of mitochondrial respiration in isolated mitochondria with whole muscle proteomic analysis, we show that: (i) the capacity for mitochondrial SCFA‐supported respiration is minimal in comparison to carbohydrate and LCFA‐linked substrates; (ii) exercise training prevented high fat feeding‐induced increases in intrinsic mitochondrial palmitoylcarnitine (LCFA), acetate and butyrate supported respiration; (iii) exercise training increased the abundance of mitochondrial proteins, with an additive response in animals consuming a HFD; and as a result (iv) exercise training combined with high‐fat feeding additively increased the estimated capacity of muscle to utilize SCFAs. The present study further provides evidence that exercise and high fat feeding impose unique metabolic stimuli because their combination prevented the typical HFD‐induced adaptations in intrinsic mitochondrial fatty acid‐supported respiration yet additively increased the total content of mitochondrial proteins.

SCFAs as an oxidizable substrate in skeletal muscle mitochondria

The present data reveals that, although SCFAs can drive mitochondrial respiration in vitro, the maximal rates of SCFA‐supported respiration are substantially lower than those supported by carbohydrate‐ and LCFA‐linked substrates. Interestingly, the addition of HCO3 − significantly increased SCFA‐supported respiration in vitro. This aligns with previous work in heart isolated mitochondria where HCO3 − increased branched‐chain α‐ketoacid (BCKA)‐supported respiration (Goldberg et al., 2019). Although both BCKAs and propionate require HCO3 − in the reaction of propionyl‐CoA to methylmalonyl‐CoA (Fig 1G ), we showed HCO3 − can increase respiration supported by all SCFAs. It is unclear how HCO3 − enhances SCFA‐supported respiration; however, it probably involves an indirect mechanism affecting transport across the inner mitochondrial membrane. Nevertheless, the minimal capacity of SCFA to support OXPHOS is consistent with previous studies in Langendorff‐perfused hearts, which demonstrate that perfusion with propionate is insufficient to meet ATP demands (Peuhkurinen et al., 1982). Furthermore, recent work by our group has shown that short‐chain monocarboxylic acids provide minimal respiratory drive in both heart and muscle isolated mitochondria (Petrick et al., 2019). Specifically, ketone bodies (acetoacetate and β‐hydroxybutyrate) generated maximal respiratory rates ∼4‐fold lower than pyruvate in left ventricle and red gastrocnemius isolated mitochondria of rats. In comparison, the present study reveals SCFA‐supported respiration in RQ muscle of rats to be ∼4‐fold lower than palmitoylcarnitine, with a marked ∼10‐fold reduction relative to pyruvate. Additionally, Goldberg et al. (2019) reported that respiration supported by the branched‐chain keto acid, ketomethylvalerate (KMV), which converges with propionate oxidation, accounted for only ∼3% of maximal respiratory flux in heart isolated mitochondria from mice. The minimal respiration driven by KMV aligns with our findings with SCFAs, where SCFA‐supported respiration represented ∼3.7% of maximal respiratory flux in skeletal muscle isolated mitochondria. The limited capacity for SCFA‐supported respiration could be a result of the low activity of SCFA activating enzymes within mitochondria and/or their lower sensitivity to CoA (Robitaille et al., 1993; Szutowicz et al., 1981), a supposition supported by the finding that combined HFD+Ex did not increase the abundance of specific enzymes involved in SCFA metabolism or respiratory capacity. Although these findings suggest SCFA cannot in isolation support bioenergetics in a contracting muscle, these substrates could contribute substantially to basal metabolism, especially in the context of either HFD or post‐exercise recovery because of increased enzyme capacity and substrate availability.

Ability of HFD and exercise to alter SCFA‐supported respiratory capacity in muscle

The present study demonstrates that HFD and exercise provide unique adaptive stimuli to affect mitochondrial respiratory function. Specifically, we found that HFD increased the intrinsic capacity of isolated mitochondria to utilize fatty acids specifically (JO2 per mg of mitochondrial protein), whereas exercise training primarily induced mitochondrial biogenesis, a response amplified by consuming HFD, without affecting the intrinsic capacity of mitochondria. Although our investigation of mitochondrial SCFA oxidation in the present study is relatively novel, the broader interpretation that HFD remodels mitochondria and exercise training induces mitochondria biogenesis aligns with previous findings examining medium‐chain fatty acid utilization (Batterson et al., 2023; Huang et al., 2021). Furthermore, our data support prior evidence suggesting that exercise training mitigates HFD‐induced alterations in the mitochondria lipid oxidation capacity (Batterson et al., 2023; Huang et al., 2021). Despite growing evidence that exercise counters HFD‐driven mitochondrial metabolic remodeling, the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. One possibility is that HFD and exercise training induce unique and competing stress‐signalling events. Intriguingly, emerging evidence suggests that excess fatty acids may increase nuclear acyl‐CoA abundance, acting as a novel transcriptional regulator that warrants further investigation in the context of the observed HFD responses (Trefely et al., 2020). Alternatively, because both metabolic stimuli have been reported to induce overlapping calcium‐mediated signalling events (Jain et al., 2014; Wu et al., 2002), rather than competing stress responses, exercise and excess fatty acids may act along a continuum of stress‐signalling response, with exercise representing a more potent metabolic challenge. In this context, modest calcium‐induced transcription of mitochondrial lipid metabolism genes under HFD conditions could lead to accumulation of lipid metabolism enzymes within existing mitochondria, enhancing the intrinsic capacity for LCFA and SCFA oxidation. Conversely, the stronger stimulus of exercise probably induces broad transcription of mitochondrial genes, increasing overall protein accumulation within existing mitochondria, and ultimately necessitating fission events and the clear induction of mitochondrial biogenesis. In this context, if all mitochondrial proteins increase proportionately following exercise training, the intrinsic capacity of mitochondria would not be expected to change. Supporting this interpretation, in the present study, LC‐MS based proteomics revealed total muscle SCFA protein enrichment following exercise training was proportional to all mitochondrial proteins. By contrast, combined HFD‐Ex decreased the mitochondrial abundance of THIL, comprising the last enzyme in β‐oxidation of palmitoylcarnitine and butyrate, which may reflect the absence of exercise‐induced gene transcription and effectively the dilution of this protein during fission events/mitochondrial biogenesis. Collectively, these findings highlight that HFD and exercise exert distinct yet overlapping influences on mitochondrial adaptation, reflecting a continuum of metabolic signalling, with exercise training representing a more robust metabolic stress.

At the whole muscle level, we found that HFD‐Ex increased mitochondrial protein content indicative of mitochondrial biogenesis, resulting in a predictive increase in estimated muscle oxidative capacity when accounting for differences in mitochondrial content between groups. This effect was additive because mitochondrial protein and OXPHOS capacity in skeletal muscle of HFD‐Ex animals were significantly greater compared to LFD‐Ex and HFD‐Sed rats. Although this finding is supported by Huang et al. (2021), a mechanistic explanation is currently unclear. Nonetheless, considering the conserved adaptive processes that occur following a single bout of exercise and in response to high‐fat‐feeding with respect to reactive oxygen species, free ADP and calcium, these factors may converge to amplify similar signalling pathways to induce mitochondrial biogenesis (Silveira et al., 2006; Winder et al., 2000; Wu et al., 2002). Future research should aim to uncover the mechanisms underlying the additive induction of mitochondrial biogenesis with combined HFD and exercise because this will provide valuable insights for developing new interventions and pharmaceuticals to treat metabolic diseases.

Study considerations

It is important to note that the results of the present study primarily reflect the metabolic capacity to utilize SCFAs derived from the gut microbiota and liver, as opposed to intramuscular peroxisomal SCFA production, which is expected to generate SCFA‐carnitine esters because of the high abundance of carnitine acetyltransferase (Jakobs & Wanders, 1995). Future research should therefore examine the capacity of short‐chain acylcarnitines relative to their non‐esterified counterparts (acetate, propionate and butyrate) and investigate the potential for peroxisomal–mitochondrial communication, as well as the relative capacity to utilize peroxisome and gut/liver‐derived SCFAs. This information could be important for advancing our understanding of molecular and metabolic responses to situations when fatty acids are highly available to support OXPHOS, and the low energetic demand of skeletal muscle could rely on substrates with negligible metabolic capacity, including but not limited to HFD, fasting and post‐exercise recovery.

Summary

Our findings demonstrate that, in RQ muscle of male rats, the capacity of mitochondria to utilize SCFAs (acetate, propionate and butyrate) is very low. We further show that HFD increases the intrinsic capacity of mitochondria to utilize palmitoylcarnitine, acetate and butyrate, comprising an effect that is prevented by exercise training, whereas neither diet nor exercise affect pyruvate or propionate supported respiration. Furthermore, we show that exercise training in the presence of a HFD additively increased mitochondrial protein abundance, which resulted in a predicted synergistic effect of HFD and exercise to enhance respiratory capacity supported by carbohydrates, LCFA and SCFA substrates in muscle. Our findings highlight the potential for SCFAs to support basal metabolism in the context of a high lipid environment and underscore exercise and high fat feeding as distinct metabolic stimuli regulating intrinsic mitochondrial fatty acid utilization, while together synergistically inducing mitochondrial biogenesis.
