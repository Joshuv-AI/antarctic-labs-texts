Late initiation of antenatal care and its associated factors among pregnant women in Benin: a facility based cross-sectional study
Authors: Eyram Maria Concheta Tchibozo, Thalouth Abodunrin Alamou Gado, Yessito Corine Houehanou Sonou, Salmane Ariyoh Amidou, Philippe Lacroix, Dismand Stephan Houinato, Holy Bezanahary
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInitiation of Antenatal Care (ANC) after 16th gestational weeks (GW) influences the course of pregnancy and childbirth. It is a risk factor for maternal, perinatal morbidity and mortality in Sub-Saharan Africa. The objective was to investigate the rate of late initiation of ANC and to document its associated factors in a Beninese pregnant women population.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted in 30 Beninese maternities from January 2020 to November 2021. All pregnant women ≥ 15 years old who attended ANC contact were included. Data were collected through structured face-to-face interviews, review of medical records and maternal cards. Binary and multivariable logistic regression analysis were performed by using 95% Confidence Interval (CI) and significance was declared at P < 0.05.ResultsThe sample consisted of 3,528 pregnant women, of whom 1,469 (41.6%) initiated their first antenatal care (ANC) visit after the 16thGW. Factors significantly associated with late ANC initiation included having no formal education compared to having at least one level of education (Odd ratio (OR) = 1.6; 95%IC) [1.3–3.0]), and belonging to the Otamari or Adja ethnic groups compared to the Fon ethnic group (OR = 1.7; 95% CI [1.1–2.4] and OR = 1.5; 95% CI [1.1–1.9], respectively). Women practicing a religion other than Christianity or Islam had a significantly higher likelihood of late ANC initiation compared to Christians (OR = 7.0; 95% CI [4.4–11.5]). Parity of one or more was also associated with late ANC use compared to nulliparous women (OR = 1.3; 95% CI [1.1–1.6]), as was having pre-existing diabetes before the index pregnancy compared to non-diabetic women (OR = 2.8; 95% CI [1.7–4.7]). Among women with previous pregnancies, a history of fetal loss was associated with a lower likelihood of late ANC initiation compared to those without such a history (OR = 0.7; 95% CI [0.6–0.9]). In contrast, a history of gestational diabetes (OR = 4.2; 95% CI [2.2–8.8]) and a history of preeclampsia (OR = 1.7; 95% CI [1.1–2.5]) were both associated with increased odds of initiating ANC late.ConclusionA high rate of late initiation of ANC in this population was documented. In addition to socio-demographic risk factors, largely described in the literature, chronic disease like diabetes or previous pregnancy complications could positively or negatively influence initiation of ANC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-025-08170-2.

Background

Antenatal care (ANC), can be defined as the care provided by qualified health professionals to pregnant women and adolescents with the aim of ensuring optimal health conditions for both the mother and the child during pregnancy. This care includes the identification of risk factors, the prevention and management of pregnancy-related or coexisting conditions, as well as health education and health promotion [1–3]. Early initiation of ANC is indeed necessary, to prevent the occurrence of obstetric complications and improve pregnancy outcomes [4]. WHO recommends initiating the first ANC visit before the 12th GW, and in Benin before the 16th GW [1, 5]. Initiating antenatal care late is associated with a higher risk of adverse maternal outcomes, including increased maternal mortality [6]. ANC is critical in reducing pregnancy-related complications. However, late initiation of ANC remains prevalent in low-income countries, leading to increased maternal and neonatal mortality [7]. In Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), the prevalence of late ANC initiation ranges from 42 to 56% [8, 9]. In 2018, late ANC initiation was reported in 74.8% of pregnant women in Nigeria [10] and 51% in Benin in 2017 [11]. The WHO guidelines recommend eight ANC visits for optimal pregnancy outcomes [1]. While previous studies in Benin have focused primarily on the total number of ANC visits, data remain scarce regarding the timing of the first visit and the socio-demographic, clinical, and health-system factors associated with late initiation of antenatal care. Identifying these determinants is essential for designing targeted and context-specific interventions aimed at promoting timely ANC attendance, which is a critical step toward improving both maternal and neonatal health outcomes in low-resource settings [12].

The objective of the study was to determine the rate of late ANC and its associated factors in Benin.

Methods

Study design & setting

In Benin, ANC contacts are provided in various types of health facilities, including isolated maternity units, maternity units in hospitals, public health centers, confessional facilities, and private institutions. A cross-sectional study was conducted from January 2020 to November 2021 in 30 randomly selected maternity clinics, distributed across rural and urban districts.

This study used a three-stage random sampling. The randomization was performed using a random sampling procedure implemented in R software First, half of the districts in the country were randomly selected. Then, within each selected district, maternity clinics were chosen based on the acceptability of the study by the clinic heads and local health authorities. Finally, an exhaustive and consecutive recruitment of all pregnant women attending antenatal care during the study period was conducted in the selected clinics, resulting in a total of 30 participating facilities. All pregnant women attending the selected clinics during the study period were consecutively enrolled.

Sampling

The sample size was calculated based on an expected late ANC rate of 57.7% in Ghana in 2019 rate [13]. Selecting a 2% precision level, 5% type I error level, and non-response rate (participants who declined or were unable to complete the study) of 20%, a sample size of 2814 subjects was found to be mandatory. In total, 3533 participants were recruited for the study. The rural–urban distribution of the sample reflects the distribution of maternity clinics in the selected districts.

Inclusion/Exclusion criteria

All women aged ≥ 15 years who attended ANC contacts on the day of data collection were included.

Women who were deaf, mute, or had physical disabilities preventing them from answering the questionnaire were excluded which may have introduced a selection bias.

Data collection

Data collection was conducted by trained interviewers and midwives, who administered structured questionnaires and reviewed patient medical records. In addition, patient medical records were reviewed.

Sociodemographic and economic data collected included: age, marital status, ethnic group, religion, education level, monthly income and residency (urban or rurals per INSAE classification [14]).Obstetrical and clinical data included: gravidity, parity, past medical history of hypertension, diabetes and fetal loss, gestational hypertension, pre-eclampsia (PE), gestational diabetes, macrosomia, gestational age estimation was based on the last normal menstrual period (LNMP).

The gestational age was either confirmed by the midwife or was written on the medical record. In cases where the gestational age reported by the woman based on her LNMP differed from that recorded in the medical file or estimated by the midwife, the latter was used as the reference, as it was based on clinical assessment and/or available ultrasound data. The outcome variable was the timing of ANC initiation, categorized as early or late. Initiation was considered late if the first ANC visit occurred after the 16th GW.

Data analysis

The data were entered on tablets using the KoboCollect application. The results were presented as number and percentage for categorical variable. “Non-response rate” refers to participants who declined or could not complete the questionnaire. The proportion of missing data was very low (0.1%). These missing cases were excluded from the analysis. Given the minimal extent of missingness and its random nature, no imputation method or sensitivity analysis was deemed necessary.

Multicollinearity diagnostics were performed using generalized variance inflation factors (GVIF), which are appropriate for variables with multiple degrees of freedom. The adjusted GVIF values GVIF1/(2×Df) for all variables included in the model ranged from 1.02 to 1.11, well below the commonly accepted critical thresholds of 5 or 10. These results indicate no problematic multicollinearity among the explanatory variables.

The analysis was conducted using the R software (R Core Team, 2023). Quantitative variables were described using medians and quartiles. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess the normality of the distribution of quantitative variables. Chi-square test or the Fisher test were used for proportion analysis. A p value of less than 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant. Then a multivariate logistic regression was conducted in order to document the factors associated with a late ANC. Records with missing information on the timing of the first antenatal care visit and key covariates were not included in the models. An analysis comparing sociodemographic and clinical characteristics of individuals retained in the models versus those deleted data was conducted in order to avoid biases (see Supplementary Tables 1 and Table 2 Additional File 1). Interaction between religion and ethnic group, that had theoretical or empirical relevance based on prior literature, was tested. The threshold for variable retention in models was set at 20% following the recommendation by Hosmer and Lemeshow. Models’ validation was performed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test. Model performance was assessed using both the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test and the area under the ROC curve (AUC), (see Supplementary figure S1 and figure S2, Additional File 1). Odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were used as measures of association, with a significance level set at a p < 0.05. In the multivariable analysis, odds ratios were adjusted for age (aOR). The analysis was conducted first on the population excluding factors related to previous pregnancies (history of gestational diabetes or hypertension, preeclampsia, fetal loss). Given that multigravida women may perceive themselves as more experienced and potentially delay care, we conducted a second analyses for this group, taking into account previous pregnancy history and complications. The analysis and presentation of results of statistical analysis are in accord with the STROBE recommendations.

Results

Overall, 3,533 pregnant women were screened. Of these, five (0.1%) were excluded due to missing data on the obstetrical term. The analysis was conducted on the 3,528 subjects included. The first ANC contact was before the 16th WG in 2059 cases (58.4%) and after in 1469 cases (41.6%). As shown in Fig. 1, (n = 2,059, 58.4%) of women attended their first ANC visit before the 16th GW, while (n = 1,469, 41.6%) initiated ANC late. Among late ANC initiators, multigravida women, most sought care between 16 and 25 weeks (n = 716, 27,0%), with only (n = 81, 3,1%) attending after the 32nd week.

Factors associated to late initiation of antenatal care among pregnant women

In bivariate analysis, several sociodemographic factors were associated with late ANC initiation, including age, education level, ethnicity, and religion. However, in the multivariate model, only age, education, parity, ethnicity, and history of diabetes remained significant predictors (Table 1).

The factors associated, adjusted to age in the multivariate analysis with late ANC contacts are showed in Table 1. Identifying with an indigenous religion conferred the highest risk (7-fold increase), (aOR: 7, 95% CI: 4.4–11.5), whereas the protective effect of age 25–34 (OR 0.8) is modest (aOR: 0.8, 95% CI: 0.6–0.9). Conversely few ethnic origins were associated with a higher risk (Bariba, Otomari). Women of Otamari ethnicity were 1.7 times more likely to delay ANC than Fon women (aOR = 1.7, 95% CI: 1.1–2.4). Women with no formal education were 1.6 times more likely to initiate late ANC compared to those who had at least one level of formal education. (aOR:1.6, 95% IC:1.3–1.9). A parity ≥ 1 was associated with a late ANC. Women with one previous birth were 1.3 times more likely to initiate ANC late compared to nulliparous women (aOR:1.3, 95% IC: 1.1–1.6). Although diabetes carried an OR 2.8, its wide CI (1.7–4.7) indicates uncertainty about the true magnitude (aOR: 2.8, 95% CI: 1.7–4.7).

Discussion

In this study, a large gap between WHO guidelines and the number of women attending recommended ANC protocols was documented. A rate up to 41.6% of late ANC was found. Our data were close to that described in Ethiopia (46.9%) in 2023 [15] and in Cameroun (44%) in 2017 [6]. In South Africa in 2018, a rate of 40% for late ANC attendance was found in rural and peri-urban areas, with a threshold of 20 weeks of gestation [16]. However higher rates were published in various African populations with ANC close to 88% in Uganda [17], 82% in Nigeria [18] or 61% and 56 in Rwanda [19] and Gambia [20] respectively. Overall in the analysis of the literature conducted by Jiwani [21], the timely ANC initiation was 40.8% in SSA. The variability in late ANC initiation across SSA may be explained by differences in healthcare infrastructure, accessibility, cultural norms, and health system policies. For instance, the higher rates of late ANC in Uganda and Nigeria could reflect limited healthcare access in rural areas or financial barriers, while the relatively lower rates in South Africa may result from better infrastructure and ANC promotion.” From the Demographic and Health Surveys data, recommended ANC attendance ranged from 14.5–68.7% in SSA [22]. Results were far better in high income countries HIC up to 81.9% [23].

The factors associated with late ANC in SSA were previously documented in different settings and with different methods. In the Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) program which collect information through questionnaire administration [24], maternal health data were analyzed. Regarding SSA [22], women aged 24–35 years and above, with education, living in richest households, media exposure and considering that distance from health facilities in not a problem were more likely to initiate ANC timely. Conversely women who wanted their pregnancy later or with unwanted pregnancies and living in rural area were more prone to late ANC initiation. Most of our results are in accordance with the latest systemic review [25] regarding the influence of age, education, residency, incomes, primiparity and planned pregnancy.

The influence of parity is a matter of debate. In our Beninise study parity ≥ 1 was associated with a lower ANC attendance. In the Jiwani’s [21] analysis the ANC visit of women at their first child and with birth order between 2 and 4 was more timely. Being 1 st birth order was associated with an early initiation in the last SSA DHS data [26]. Considering these contradictive results women, who have conducted previous pregnancy may consider themselves at low risk of adverse outcomes [27–29] and may postpone the ANC. However, the awareness of being pregnant is increased with age which may facilitate an early attendance. Multiparous women may perceive themselves as more experienced and less in need of early care, particularly if they had uneventful previous pregnancies. In contrast, nulliparous women may seek earlier ANC due to concerns about first-time pregnancy and unknown risks. Previous qualitative work in Ghana found that women with ≥ 1 prior live birth often describe a sense of mastery over pregnancy, reducing perceived need for early ANC [27].

We found in our study, risk factors not usually described, and which deserve to be highlighted. While many studies in SSA have focused on sociodemographic factors, this study is among the first to document the strong association between pre-existing chronic conditions (e.g., diabetes) and late ANC initiation, highlighting the need for integrated care strategies Interestingly, we found that women with pre-existing diabetes were more likely to delay ANC initiation. This may be due to a perception that diabetes management is a priority over early pregnancy monitoring, or due to inadequate integration of chronic disease management and maternal care services in this population. Future studies should explore whether these women face specific barriers in accessing both types of care. Such a behavior seems unusual however, Shewane et al. results were consistent with ours: women with known chronic illness were described with a 2.3-fold increased risk of late ANC [28]. Several mechanisms may underlie the delayed initiation of antenatal care observed in our study. These include competing clinic schedules, fragmented care pathways, and stigma related to chronic care management, which can all hinder access and continuity of care.

If the previous pregnancy was complicated by gestational diabetes or hypertensive disorders (PE), this appears to be a risk factor for ANC late initiation. In this group it might be relevant to document the influence of the previous handling given by the healthcare providers and even if it seems non significative in our study the influence of educational and poverty levels. On the contrary history of fetal loss was protective which might be related to the willingness of birth and motherhood in this population. In a study conducted in Ethiopia [30] history of abortion was associated with an early ANC booking.

It seems meaningful to focus on the ethnic and religious influence. Women from Otomani or Adja origin and without Christian or Muslim religion were at higher risk. The strong association between indigenous religion and late ANC initiation (OR = 7) may reflect cultural norms that prioritize traditional care over medical interventions, or potential mistrust of formal healthcare systems. This finding highlights the need for culturally tailored health promotion programs. Such characteristics were not previously documented. However, in Ghana [31] Christian religion was associated with early ANC contact. Religions are regarded as a network where social value might be shared and they tend to become an important level for health promotion. The impact of socio-cultural belief and pregnancy perception were suggested in few analyses. In Ethiopia pregnant women [28] are advised to hide the pregnancy in the early months. In Ghana [29, 32], Zimbabwe [33] and South Africa [34], qualitative studies have shown that fear of witchcraft or societal stigma surrounding pregnancy disclosure may contribute to delayed ANC. This might partially explain the high rate of late ANC initiation we observed among certain ethnic groups, such as the Otamari and Adja, who may hold similar beliefs. This implies to understand local perception in order to improve early ANC initiation.

Strength and limits

To our knowledge, this is the first Beninese study to document the paradoxical association between pre-existing diabetes and delayed ANC initiation, underscoring the urgent need for integrated antenatal and chronic-disease services in low-resource settings. Findings from the study are representative of 30 maternities all over the country. The data were collected by investigators during the ANC with the same protocol and questionnaires and data were validated by the midwifes and the physicians. Our study has several limitations. The cross-sectional design limits our ability to infer causality. Given that the study was conducted in selected maternity clinics in Benin, caution should be exercised when generalizing the findings to other settings. Differences in health system structures, cultural norms surrounding maternal care, and socioeconomic conditions may affect the applicability of our results in different contexts. The exclusion of deaf/mute and physically disabled women may have introduced selection bias. Future research could consider alternative approaches, such as involving caregivers as proxies, to ensure their inclusion. The data collection was not exhaustive for all the subjects, but we did not document any difference in the general characteristics between the subjects excluded and those included in the multivariate analysis (supplementary material). The cutoff for a late first ANC was 16th GW according to the Beninese guidelines. The WHO recommends a first visit within the first 12 GW. In some other studies the cutoff was the 16th GW or the first trimester. So, the comparison might be difficult and our results worse with a shorter period for the cutoff. Although our study used the national definition of late antenatal care initiation, future research should consider using the WHO-recommended 12-week threshold to allow for better comparability across international studies. However, in our study the gestational age was assessed by the midwifes, in other studies it was by the mother. Some variables (ethnicity, religion, educational level, monthly income) were based on self-report by the women, which may subject to recall-bias. Our study was unable to explore all factors which could explain late initiation of the first ANC. Specifically, sociocultural factors and factors related to the healthcare system were not fully investigated. Sociocultural factors such as societal perceptions, delayed recognition of pregnancy, lack of support from the husband or partner, and unmarried or unwanted pregnancies can hinder early initiation of the first ANC. Similarly, the patient-provider relationship (perceived poor quality of care) and the distance between health centers and the residence of pregnant women are also factors that can lead pregnant women to seek ANC late. Future mixed-methods studies should incorporate GPS-based travel-time mapping and in-depth interviews on cultural beliefs to untangle these influences.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates that 41.6% of pregnant women in Benin initiate antenatal care (ANC) after the recommended gestational period. Late initiation was independently associated with indigenous religious affiliation, non-Fon ethnicity, no formal schooling, and chronic diabetes. In addition to commonly recognized determinants like education and socioeconomic status, our findings reveal that cultural and medical factors, such as indigenous religious practices and chronic conditions like diabetes, play a significant role in late ANC initiation. Considering the influence of these variables, additional qualitative studies on ANC perceptions might be useful.

Based on these findings, we recommend several actionable strategies to improve early antenatal care uptake: partnering with faith and community leaders to co-develop culturally sensitive outreach programs; integrating chronic disease screening into routine antenatal visits to ensure seamless care; and implementing targeted follow-up by community health workers for high-risk populations.

Future research should focus on cluster randomized trials evaluating culturally tailored health education interventions, qualitative investigations into pregnancy-related beliefs among Otamari and Adja women, and longitudinal cohort studies to elucidate the causal pathways between diabetes management and timing of antenatal care initiation. By illuminating these cultural and clinical barriers, our study lays the groundwork for targeted interventions to ensure timely, equitable ANC access and ultimately improve maternal and neonatal outcomes in Benin.
