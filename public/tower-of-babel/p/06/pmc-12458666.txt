Effect of deep brain stimulation on patients with Parkinson’s disease with excessive daytime sleepiness
Authors: Sunny Vansdadia, Pooja Dhupati, Priya Ramaiah, Robert W Bina
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundExcessive daytime sleepiness (EDS) is a prevalent non-motor symptom in Parkinson’s disease (PD), significantly impairing quality of life. While deep brain stimulation (DBS) effectively improves motor symptoms, its impact on EDS remains unclear.ObjectiveThis systematic review aims to evaluate the effects of DBS on EDS in patients with PD.MethodsFollowing Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines, a systematic search of PubMed, Scopus, Embase and PsycInfo databases published by 2024) was conducted using terms related to DBS, EDS and PD. 20 studies met the inclusion criteria. Data on demographic characteristics, DBS parameters, sleep assessment scales and Levodopa-equivalent daily dose (LEDD) changes were extracted and analysed.ResultsAmong the 20 studies, 9 reported improvements in EDS post-DBS, with a mean Epworth Sleep Scale (ESS) reduction of 26.8%. However, 11 studies found no significant change. All studies reported LEDD reductions (mean 47.7%), but only two demonstrated a significant correlation between LEDD reduction and ESS improvement. Notably, some studies revealed discrepancies between subjective improvements in sleep quality and objective measures.ConclusionThe effect of DBS on EDS in PD remains inconclusive, with mixed findings across studies. While DBS consistently reduces LEDD and improves overall sleep quality, its direct impact on EDS varies. Further research with larger cohorts, objective sleep assessments and focus on confounders is necessary to elucidate DBS’s role in managing EDS in patients with PD.Keywords: NEUROSURGERY, PARKINSON'S DISEASE, CLINICAL NEUROLOGY

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is the second most common neurodegenerative disorder, following Alzheimer’s disease, and its prevalence is expected to rise alongside an ageing population.1 Recent literature indicates that the prodromal phase of PD may encompass a range of non-motor symptoms (NMSs), including sleep disorders, olfactory deficits, constipation and depression.2 Sleep disorders are one of the most common NMS that ail patients with PD. Their prevalence in the literature ranges from 50% to 80% of all patients with PD.3 The most common sleep disorders include excessive daytime sleepiness (EDS), REM sleep behaviour disorder, insomnia, restless leg syndrome, sleep disordered breathing disorders, disturbances of Circadian rhythm, etc.1

Deep brain stimulation (DBS) is well established as an effective intervention for managing the motor symptoms of PD. While there is general agreement that DBS improves motor symptoms, particularly when targeting the subthalamic nucleus (STN-DBS), its effects on NMSs, such as sleep disorders, are less clear. Deuschl et al4 demonstrated that, compared with medication alone, neuromodulation significantly increased the duration of uninterrupted sleep. Some other studies indicate that DBS may alleviate various NMSs, while others suggest no significant improvement or even a worsening of certain symptoms.5 6 This variability may stem from differences in the specific NMS evaluated.5 Despite the high prevalence and impact of these issues, there is limited understanding regarding effective management strategies for sleep-related problems in PD, with only a few studies examining the outcomes of various therapeutic options aimed at improving sleep quality.

EDS warrants particular attention due to its profound impact on quality of life, increased morbidity and heightened accident risk, along with limited treatment options. The Epworth Sleep Scale (ESS) and multiple sleep latency test (MSLT) are the most used measures to diagnose EDS. ESS is a self-administered subjective measure of EDS, while MSLT is an objective sleep instrument which measures mean sleep latency which is the average time it takes a person to fall asleep. A mean sleep latency of less than 8 min can represent sleep pathology.5 The reported prevalence of EDS in patients with PD ranges widely, from 20% to 60%. EDS is characterised by a failure to maintain alertness or wakefulness during the day, leading to an overwhelming need for sleep or episodes of unintentional sleeping.7 Given the substantial burden of sleep disorders in PD, there is an urgent need for further research to enhance our understanding and management of these NMSs, especially in conjunction with advanced therapies such as DBS. This systematic review aims to consolidate existing evidence evaluating the effects of DBS on EDS in patients with PD. By analysing the methodological constraints of existing studies, this analysis aims to inform future research on the pathophysiology of sleep disorders to optimise clinical benefit from DBS on sleep-related Quality of Life.

Methods

This systematic review was conducted according to guidelines from the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews (and Meta-Analysis statement)8 and the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews.9 An electronic search of the National Library of Medicine (PubMed), Scopus, Embase and PsycInfo were performed for articles published by 2024. The search strategy was generated using the following terms: “Deep Brain Stimulation”, “Parkinson’s Disease” and “Excessive daytime sleepiness”. Further articles were added from the references of the relevant review to ensure the inclusion of articles that could have not been captured in the search terms. Three authors independently reviewed the search entries using strict inclusion and exclusion criteria defined below. After removing duplicates, articles were first screened by title and abstract using the web application Rayyan to determine eligibility under blind review, followed by a full-text review. Discrepancies between reviewers were noted through the Rayyan system and subsequently re-reviewed and resolved by consensus. Figure 1 shows the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses flowchart for the study. Ultimately, after a comprehensive review, 20 studies were included in the final analysis of the systematic review. Due to substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity among the included studies, particularly in outcome measures and follow-up durations, a meta-analysis was deemed neither feasible nor informative. Therefore, a qualitative synthesis was conducted to summarise the available evidence. Weighted averages of the available evidence were included in the study, but these should be interpreted with caution as the only adjustment made for these calculations was sample sizes that contributed to the datapoint.

Inclusion/exclusion criteria

Original science studies that compared preoperative and postoperative sleep parameters and EDS outcomes after DBS implantation were included in the study. Review articles such as systematic reviews, meta-analyses, narrative reviews and scoping reviews were excluded. Original science studies with no DBS implantation or outcomes, wrong study design, no diagnosis of EDS, and animal studies were excluded from the study.

Data extraction

Demographic information such as age, number of subjects, mean disease duration, subjective sleep instrument measures such as ESS or Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), and any quality-of-life measurements were extracted. DBS surgery characteristics such as laterality and Nucleus target were also collected. Subgroup analysis of unilateral versus bilateral and STN versus other targets was not conducted due to most studies targeted STN and placed bilateral DBS electrodes. Online supplemental table 1 provides a brief description of each test and scale that was measured in the included studies. For studies that reported an improvement in EDS following DBS, weighted means were computed for ESS, Parkinson’s Disease Sleep Scale (PDSS) and PSQI. Additionally, a weighted average reduction in Levodopa-equivalent daily dose (LEDD) was calculated across all studies that reported this outcome.

ROBINS-I criteria

Risk of Bias in the studies was assessed by the ROBINS-I (Risk of Bias in Non-Randomised Studies—of Interventions) by Cochrane. ROBINS-I consists of seven domains: confounding, classification of interventions, selection of participants, deviations from intended interventions, missing data, outcome measurement and selection of the reported result. Judgments were categorised as low, moderate, serious or critical risk of bias.10 Table 2 summarises the assessment of the studies according to the guideline.

Study demographics

A total of 610 patients were included across the 20 studies in the review, with an overall mean disease duration of 12.6±5.7 years. Only two of the 20 studies included control groups consisting of patients who did not receive DBS implantation. Except for one study that implanted DBS into the Globus pallidus interna and the pedunculopontine tegmental nucleus (PPT), all other studies targeted the subthalamic nucleus (STN). Peppe et al11 compared the effects of targeting PPT and STN. All studies employed bilateral implantations, with Chahine et al12 also including some patients with unilateral implants. The follow-up range for the studies was anywhere from 1 week postoperatively to 3 years. Some studies only had one follow-up while other studies had multiple follow ups after implantation. The ESS was the sole test used to measure the impact of DBS and diagnose EDS in patients with PD. MSLT was not employed by any study for the diagnosis of EDS. Various other sleep instruments and other scales used in the studies are outlined in online supplemental table 2.

EDS improvement

A reduction in ESS score is seen as an improvement in symptoms of EDS. Among the 20 studies that used ESS to evaluate EDS, nine reported a reduction in ESS scores at follow-up after DBS implantation, indicating an improvement in EDS. The mean percentage decrease in ESS scores across these studies was 28.3 (range 21.3% to 44.8%). In addition to ESS, PDSS was used in eight studies to assess various sleep parameters, all of which demonstrated improvements, with an average increase in PDSS scores of 25% (range 9.1% to 45%). The weighted average percent changes and corresponding SD for ESS, PSQI, PDSS, Parkinson’s Disease Questionnaire-39 (PDQ-39) and LEDD are summarised in table 4. It is important to note that these weighted averages do not account for variation in follow-up durations across studies and include only those that reported a postoperative change in the respective scores. Deli et al13 employed the PDSS-2, a scale more specifically designed to assess EDS, and reported a substantial 58.3% reduction in EDS. Notably, the score scale was reversed in the updated PDSS-2 version.

However, 11 studies reported no significant change in ESS scores following DBS. While the improvement in EDS was variable, most studies observed benefits in overall sleep quality post-implantation. Only two studies, by Torun et al14 and Dulski et al,15 reported a deterioration in sleep parameters after DBS, despite a perceived improvement in sleep quality by patients. These studies found no objective improvement, and in some cases, a decline in sleep quality as measured by polysomnography (PSG).

LEDD reduction

All studies that assessed and compared preoperative and postoperative LEDD levels observed a reduction following DBS implantation, with an average decrease of 47.5% (range 15.6% to 88.4%). Among the 10 studies that explored the correlation between LEDD reduction and EDS improvement, only two identified a significant relationship. Tolleson et al16 reported a Pearson correlation coefficient of −0.60 between LEDD reduction and ESS score improvement, indicating a moderate negative correlation. Kharkar et al17 observed a weak association between ESS improvement and LEDD reduction at 6 months follow-up, but this relationship did not persist at 12 months. Although half of the studies included assessed the correlation between LEDD and EDS, most did not perform further statistical analyses to account for the potential confounding effect of LEDD reduction and its effects on EDS. It is important to note that these studies had varying follow-up times, and this was not accounted for in the calculation of the weighted average reduction in LEDD.

Discussion

The goal of this study was to elucidate the impact of DBS on NMSs, particularly EDS. Addressing EDS in PD is a high priority due to its high prevalence and adverse effects, including increased mortality, higher rates of accidents and overall reduced quality of life.18 The existing literature on the impact of DBS on EDS is conflicting. While DBS has been shown to improve NMSs such as sleep quality, olfaction and urinary symptoms,6 18 19 the evidence for its effect on EDS appears sparse. Of the studies included in this review, fewer than half reported a positive impact of DBS on EDS.

Several factors might contribute to the perceived improvement of EDS following DBS. EDS is multifactorial, resulting from a complex interplay of various variables and disease variability. Deuschl et al reported subjective improvements in sleep among patients undergoing neuromodulation. Although these improvements were self-reported, an increase in the average sleep duration was noted in patient diaries compared with the medication only group. Another significant factor is the poor quality of nighttime sleep caused by PD-related motor symptoms, such as dystonia, akinesia, rigidity and pain, which lead to unfulfilling sleep and increased daytime sleepiness, ultimately contributing to pathologic EDS.20,22 Theoretically, if DBS improves motor symptoms, sleep quality should also improve. This nocturnal improvement in motor symptoms is thought to be due to the modulation of basal ganglia-thalamo-cortical loops by STN-DBS. Direct neuromodulation of arousal circuits by the DBS electrodes could also potentially play a role in improving these symptoms. Anatomically the STN has reciprocal connections to the PPT and mesencephalic reticular formation and sends projection to areas like the thalamus, hypothalamus and amygdala which are strongly involved in the sleep-wake regulation. Chronic high frequency stimulation of the STN could potentially modulate these arousal promoting centres leading to improvement in EDS symptoms.20 21 23

Many studies reported that patients experienced improved sleep quality following DBS, as evidenced by improvements in subjective sleep scales such as the PDSS and PSQI. Of the 10 studies using these scales, nine demonstrated symptomatic improvement, with mean changes of 19.8% in PDSS and 22.9% in PSQI scores. These subjective improvements were variably supported by objective sleep assessments using PSG. For instance, Baumann-Vogel et al observed increased sleep efficiency, enhanced deep sleep and greater accumulation of slow-wave activity throughout the night. Similarly, Oner et al reported improvements in overall nocturnal sleep quality and architecture. In contrast, Torun et al did not find significant changes in objective sleep parameters, while Dulski et al reported a deterioration in objective sleep quality post-DBS. These findings suggest that, despite inconsistent results in objective sleep measures, patients subjectively perceived an improvement in sleep quality following DBS. This discrepancy may be influenced by potential confounding factors such as fatigue and depression, which were not consistently controlled for across studies. Although the PDSS and PSQI do not directly assess EDS, these scales were included to evaluate the effects of DBS on nocturnal sleep, which may indirectly affect EDS due to its multifactorial nature.

Fatigue as a potential confounder for EDS was not assessed in many studies and may have indirectly influenced the subjective perception of sleep quality and EDS following DBS, as previously discussed. Changes in fatigue symptoms were infrequently evaluated, and the findings were mixed. Chou et al 24 reported no significant change in fatigue levels post-DBS, whereas Jost et al23 observed significant improvements in both sleep and fatigue symptoms, as measured by the NMSs, following DBS. These inconsistencies highlight the need for further investigation, as improvements in fatigue may confound the interpretation of ESS score changes and the overall assessment of EDS symptomatology.

Another potential contributor to increased EDS in patients with PD is the somnolent effect of dopaminergic medications, such as pramipexole, ropinirole and pergolide.22 25 26 Dopaminergic agonists are more likely to cause EDS compared with Levodopa, with a dose-dependent relationship. Patients on both Levodopa and dopaminergic agonists are at the highest risk of developing EDS.25 27 28 If dopaminergic medications significantly contribute to EDS, a reduction in LEDD following DBS should correlate with improvements in EDS. This review found that LEDD reduction was consistently noted across all studies that measured it, with reductions ranging from 15.6% to 88.1%. Of the 10 studies that examined the relationship between improvement in EDS and reduction in LEDD, only Tolleson et al reported a significant and strong correlation between LEDD reduction and ESS scores, despite limited statistical power due to a small sample size. They noted that although this association reached statistical significance, the magnitude of change in ESS scores was likely insufficient to confer any meaningful clinical relevance. Barigotas et al reported that the reduction in EDS remained significant even after adjusting for the reduction in LEDD within their cohort. Similar findings were observed by Choi et al29 and Oner et al,30 who also noted a change in EDS independent of decrease in LEDD post-DBS. Given the mixed effects of DBS on EDS, despite a consistent reduction in LEDD, it is likely that dopaminergic agonists and Levodopa play a role, but not a major one.

Most studies reported significant motor improvement and reduced medication use after DBS, yet EDS outcomes remained variable. This suggests that neurodegeneration beyond the basal ganglia, particularly in brainstem arousal nuclei and thalamo-cortical pathways, contributes to EDS and is not reversed by DBS.4 29 31 Wakefulness relies on an ascending arousal network that degenerates in PD, leading to daytime somnolence.32 Animal models show loss of orexin neurons in the lateral hypothalamus linked to sleep-wake disturbances,25 and multiple arousal-related neurotransmitter systems are also affected.33 These widespread changes may limit the impact of STN DBS on arousal, explaining the mixed EDS outcomes. Future studies should explore alternative targets, though this was not possible here due to small sample size.

This review is subject to some limitations related to the included studies. Most studies relied on subjective measures such as the ESS and PSQI, which, while easy to administer, are influenced by mood symptoms like depression and anxiety.34 These tools capture patient perception rather than physiological sleepiness and may not accurately reflect changes following interventions. For instance, Dulski et al15 and Torun et al14 reported improved subjective sleep scores despite no objective improvement or even worsening of sleep parameters post-DBS.

Sample sizes were generally small, ranging from 15 to 89 participants, with only four studies enrolling more than 40 patients. This limited statistical power and generalisability. Moreover, dopaminergic medications were commonly reported as total LEDD, without distinguishing dopamine agonists from levodopa. Given that dopamine agonists are more strongly linked to EDS, this lack of differentiation may obscure medication-specific effects.

Risk of bias assessment using the ROBINS-I tool indicated that the majority of studies (17 out of 20) were at serious risk of bias. This was primarily due to a failure to statistically account for key confounding variables that may influence EDS outcomes. Only three studies assessed the effect of LEDD reduction as a confounder in relation to changes in EDS scores. Most studies did not adjust for additional potential confounders such as depression or fatigue contributing to serious confounding bias.

The reliance on subjective sleepiness scales such as the ESS introduced a moderate risk of bias, as no study employed objective measures like the MSLT. This likely reflects limitations related to cost, accessibility and ease of administration. Additional moderate bias was introduced by the lack of standardised protocols for medication tapering across participants in most studies. Furthermore, the small sample sizes of many included studies made them particularly vulnerable to the effects of loss to follow-up, contributing to a moderate risk of bias in approximately half of the studies assessed.

This review highlights several key limitations in the current literature that should inform future investigations into the pathophysiology of sleep disorders in PD and the optimisation of DBS effects on sleep-related quality of life. Notably, objective physiological tools such as the MSLT and the Maintainence of Wakefulness Test (MWT) were absent from the methodologies of the studies reviewed. While these tests offer standardised assessments of sleepiness, their utility is often limited in clinical research due to high costs, time demands and the requirement for overnight PSG followed by multiple daytime sessions. Furthermore, these tools may lack the sensitivity to detect subtle improvements in EDS following DBS, which could result in underestimation of clinical benefit in cases where changes are not robust.

A significant limitation across studies was the underexplored impact of common comorbidities. Psychiatric conditions (such as depression), autonomic dysfunction and structural sleep disorders like obstructive sleep apnoea are prevalent in PD and can independently contribute to EDS. Although many studies collected data on these comorbidities, few performed detailed analyses to examine their confounding effects on EDS outcomes. This complicates interpretation, as it becomes difficult to isolate the contribution of DBS from these confounding factors. Future research should prioritise the systematic evaluation of confounders, especially depression and fatigue, and assess how these conditions influence patient-reported EDS. Additionally, the establishment of a standardised protocol for LEDD reduction post-DBS may help delineate the extent to which medication changes contribute to alterations in sleepiness. Together, these refinements will improve the accuracy and interpretability of studies assessing the impact of DBS on sleep and EDS in PD.

In summary, this review highlights the inconsistent effects of DBS on EDS in PD. While DBS consistently improved motor symptoms and subjective sleep quality across studies, its impact on EDS was variable and often limited. These mixed findings likely reflect the multifactorial etiology of EDS, including disease-related neurodegeneration in arousal pathways, medication effects and comorbid conditions such as depression and fatigue which were not consistently controlled for in the studies. Furthermore, most studies relied on subjective assessments of sleepiness and only had small sample sizes, contributing to limitations. Future research should prioritise the use of objective sleep assessments, such as the MSLT, and incorporate standardised protocols for dopaminergic medication tapering post-DBS. Additionally, studies should rigorously control for potential confounders, especially fatigue and mood disorders. Larger, well-powered prospective studies with standardised outcome measures are necessary to clarify the independent effects of DBS on EDS and to guide more targeted interventions for sleep-wake disturbances in PD.
