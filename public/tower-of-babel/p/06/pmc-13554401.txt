AI-driven radiomics and functional imaging integration for intelligent identification of coronary high-risk plaques and prediction of cardiovascular events
Authors: Lei Yu, Ying Cao, Xinyue Li, Manli Zhang, Chunpeng Yang, Junzhi Sang, Yuguang Wang, Tan Xue, Kexin Pang, Guangwei Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAccurate identification of high-risk coronary plaques and prediction of cardiovascular events are essential for improving the management of coronary artery disease (CAD). Although coronary computed tomography angiography (CTA) is widely used in clinical practice, conventional assessment methods remain limited by subjective interpretation and suboptimal prognostic accuracy. This study aimed to develop and validate a deep learning (DL)-based radiomics model integrating coronary CTA and computed tomography-derived fractional flow reserve (CT-FFR) for automated identification of high-risk coronary plaques and prediction of cardiovascular events.MethodsIn this retrospective study, 800 coronary CTA scans from patients with stable plaques, vulnerable plaques, and acute coronary syndrome (ACS) were analyzed. Clinical characteristics and follow-up outcome labels were incorporated for annotation of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) during follow-up. After image preprocessing, coronary arteries were automatically segmented using a U-Net-based DL model. Radiomics features describing plaque morphology, texture, and attenuation, including surrogates for fibrous cap integrity, were extracted and combined with CT-FFR to incorporate functional hemodynamic information. A multimodal fusion model integrating convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and Transformer architecture was developed and internally validated using cross-validation.ResultsIn the independent test cohort, the multimodal model achieved an accuracy of 90.8%, sensitivity of 91.2%, specificity of 89.4%, F1-score of 0.91, and area under the curve (AUC) of 0.927 [95% confidence interval (CI): 0.902–0.948], outperforming the structural-only model (AUC =0.864) and functional-only model (AUC =0.878). Feature importance analysis indicated that the radiomics-derived fibrous cap surrogate, plaque attenuation, and CT-FFR were among the most influential predictors of adverse cardiovascular outcomes.ConclusionsThis study presents an interpretable multimodal framework for quantitative assessment of high-risk coronary plaques and cardiovascular risk prediction using CTA. The proposed approach improves risk stratification and supports the integration of artificial intelligence (AI) into clinical management of CAD.

Key findings

• A multimodal deep learning framework integrating coronary computed tomography angiography (CTA) radiomics and computed tomography-derived fractional flow reserve (CT-FFR) was developed for automated identification of high-risk coronary plaques and prediction of cardiovascular events.

What is known and what is new?

• Identification of high-risk coronary plaques is critical for preventing acute coronary events. Coronary CTA provides valuable anatomical information but remains limited by subjective interpretation and insufficient functional assessment.

• This study introduces a multimodal artificial intelligence (AI) framework that integrates radiomics-based structural plaque features with CT-FFR-derived functional information, enabling more comprehensive and interpretable risk prediction.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• The proposed model provides an objective and efficient tool for automated identification of vulnerable coronary plaques.

• Integration of structural radiomics and functional hemodynamic features may enhance clinical risk stratification and support AI-assisted decision-making in coronary artery disease management.

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) has remained the leading cause of global mortality for decades, imposing a substantial burden on public health systems and socioeconomic development (1). Among various CVDs, coronary artery disease (CAD) represents the most prevalent form (2). Pathologically, CAD is characterized by the formation, progression, and rupture of atherosclerotic plaques (3,4). Plaque instability, particularly the presence of high-risk plaques (e.g., vulnerable plaques) (5-7), substantially increases the likelihood of adverse cardiovascular events, including acute coronary syndrome (ACS) (Figure S1). Therefore, accurate identification of high-risk coronary plaques is essential for early CAD diagnosis, optimal therapeutic intervention, and personalized treatment strategies (8,9). However, reliable detection and risk stratification of high-risk plaques remain major challenges in current clinical practice.

Coronary computed tomography angiography (CTA) has emerged as a noninvasive, high-resolution imaging modality widely used for evaluating coronary anatomy and atherosclerotic plaques (10). Its advanced visualization capabilities provide rich imaging information for assessing vascular stenosis severity, plaque morphology, and calcification burden (11,12). Particularly for patients unsuitable for invasive procedures, CTA serves as a vital tool for initial screening and risk assessment (13,14). However, conventional visual or semi-quantitative interpretation methods remain highly subjective, show limited interobserver reproducibility, and provide insufficient evaluation of plaque composition and functional status (15). These limitations hinder the objective and standardized identification of high-risk plaques required for precision medicine (16) (Figure S2). Therefore, developing more objective and automated imaging analysis strategies is essential to enhance the clinical value of coronary CTA.

In recent years, advances in the understanding of high-risk plaque pathophysiology have enabled the identification of several characteristic imaging features associated with plaque instability, including low-attenuation cores, positive remodeling, spotty calcification, plaque surface irregularity, and thin fibrous caps (17). Furthermore, quantitative imaging metrics such as plaque volume, density distribution, and plaque burden have demonstrated significant prognostic value (18). However, structural imaging features alone often fail to fully capture the hemodynamic impact of coronary plaques. Computed tomography-derived fractional flow reserve (CT-FFR) addresses this limitation by simulating coronary blood flow and integrating anatomical and functional assessments. Nevertheless, the combined evaluation requires complex simulations and intensive computation, which limits its widespread clinical implementation. A more efficient and scalable approach is to integrate structural and functional information within a unified artificial intelligence (AI) framework for risk prediction using machine learning techniques (Figure S3).

Against this backdrop, AI, particularly deep learning (DL), has demonstrated substantial potential in medical image analysis. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have been widely applied to image segmentation, classification, and detection tasks, achieving remarkable progress in automated cardiac anatomy recognition, coronary artery segmentation, and plaque detection. Recently, Transformer architectures have shown strong capability in modeling long-range dependencies, making them well-suited for feature extraction from complex and high-dimensional imaging data. Meanwhile, radiomics technology has expanded traditional image analysis by enabling quantitative extraction of high-dimensional plaque characteristics (texture, shape, density, etc.) for model construction. The integration of these technologies provides a robust foundation for constructing an intelligent coronary CTA system for high-risk plaque identification and offers a promising pathway toward accurate and interpretable prediction of cardiovascular events (Figure S4).

To address current technical bottlenecks and clinical challenges, this study aims to develop a DL and radiomics-based model for the intelligent identification of high-risk plaques and the prediction of cardiovascular events in coronary CTA. Coronary CTA images from patients with stable plaques, high-risk vulnerable plaques, and ACS were systematically collected and annotated. These imaging data were integrated with clinical variables and follow-up outcomes to construct a multimodal feature space. Automatic coronary artery segmentation was performed using a U-Net architecture. CNN and Transformer frameworks were subsequently integrated to learn high-dimensional structural and functional imaging features, while radiomics analysis was applied to extract key plaque characteristics, including texture, morphology, and CT-FFR–related parameters. The predictive model was then trained and validated to evaluate its accuracy, sensitivity, and generalizability. This framework provides an integrated and efficient strategy for automated identification of high-risk coronary plaques and offers clinicians an objective decision-support tool for cardiovascular risk assessment. The proposed approach holds significant scientific and clinical value for advancing precision medicine in CAD management and may facilitate improved early screening, intervention decision-making, and prognostic evaluation. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD+AI reporting checklist (available at https://cdt.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/cdt-2025-1-653/rc).

Methods

Data sources and sample selection

The coronary CTA data used in this study were strictly curated from specific cardiac-focused subsets within The Cancer Imaging Archive (TCIA) (specifically the CAD and coronary CTA collections) and other verified open-access cardiac datasets. General neuroimaging repositories [e.g., the Open Access Series of Imaging Studies (OASIS)] were excluded to ensure anatomical relevance. To guarantee suitability for high-precision radiomics analysis, strict inclusion criteria were applied: (I) electrocardiogram (ECG)-gated acquisition protocols to minimize motion artifacts; (II) slice thickness ≤0.75 mm; and (III) contrast-enhanced sequences covering the entire coronary artery tree. A total of 1,240 candidate scans were initially screened, and 440 were excluded due to non-standardized reconstruction kernels or insufficient image quality, yielding a high-quality baseline dataset. Since public repositories typically lack standardized voxel-wise plaque annotations, a rigorous ‘Human-in-the-Loop’ annotation protocol involving three experienced radiologists was established to generate ground truth masks for these cases (detailed in the “Ground Truth Annotation and Expert Consensus” section). In addition to the public datasets, a structured in-house coronary CTA database was incorporated, containing raw CTA scans, vessel segmentation masks, three-dimensional (3D) plaque masks, and plaque-type annotations. This dataset was used to construct an independent testing cohort under unified methodological standards. Furthermore, real-world CTA cases obtained from our institution and approved by the institutional ethics committee were included as an external test set to evaluate model generalizability across heterogeneous clinical data domains. All data were fully de-identified and complied with ethical and privacy regulations, and no additional human experimentation was involved.

Sample selection followed predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Inclusion criteria were: (I) age ≥18 years with coronary atherosclerotic plaques visible on CTA; (II) clearly defined diagnostic labels (stable plaques, vulnerable plaques, or ACS); (III) acceptable image quality without severe motion artifacts or truncation; and (IV) availability of basic clinical variables (e.g., blood lipid levels, glucose, hypertension status, and inflammatory markers) together with follow-up information. Exclusion criteria included: (I) incomplete image acquisition; (II) congenital cardiac abnormalities or other comorbidities that could interfere with analysis; and (III) absence of reliable annotation information. This retrospective study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. Ethical approval was waived by the Ethics Committee of The Second Affiliated Hospital of Qiqihar Medical University because this study used retrospective and de-identified coronary CTA imaging data and did not involve any additional intervention or collection of identifiable private information. The requirement for individual informed consent was also waived by the Ethics Committee of The Second Affiliated Hospital of Qiqihar Medical University due to the retrospective nature of the study.

To ensure rigorous evaluation and prevent data leakage, dataset partitioning was performed strictly at the patient level, ensuring that no slices or plaque images from the same patient appeared in both training and test cohorts. The final allocation ratio was 7:1.5:1.5 for the training, validation, and internal test sets, respectively, with balanced distributions of demographic characteristics and disease severity. Patient-level splitting was conducted using unique patient identifiers prior to any slice- or plaque-level processing, guaranteeing that each patient contributed data to only one cohort. Together, these cohorts constituted the “public-dataset baseline evaluation framework” for model development. Our structured coronary CTA dataset (System-CTA set, n=45) was excluded from all training and hyperparameter tuning and served as Independent Test Set 1, allowing evaluation of performance under conditions with complete annotations and standardized data layers. In addition, CTA scans exported from our institutional PACS (Hospital-CTA set, n=38) were used as External Test Set 2 to assess model robustness and practical applicability under heterogeneous acquisition protocols, scanner characteristics, and patient distributions. None of the test cohorts contributed to any stage of model training or validation. All test sets included complete CTA series, plaque- or vessel-level labels, and coronary branch information. Detailed dataset distributions are summarized in Table S1.

Clinical endpoint definition and follow-up adjudication

The primary clinical endpoint was defined as the occurrence of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) within a median follow-up period of 24 months (IQR: 18–36 months). MACE was defined as a composite of: (I) cardiac death; (II) non-fatal myocardial infarction (defined by the Fourth Universal Definition); and (III) unstable angina requiring unplanned revascularization. All clinical events were retrospectively adjudicated by an independent Clinical Events Committee (CEC) consisting of two cardiologists who were blinded to imaging data and AI predictions. Any disagreements were resolved by consensus. Patients without documented events by the end of follow-up were censored as event-free controls.

Standardized plaque-level integration enabling quantitative analysis and function-oriented interpretation

In this study, four complementary data layers were systematically integrated and aligned at the case level: raw coronary CTA images, coronary vessel segmentation masks, plaque-specific 3D masks, and plaque-type annotations. The representative case demonstrated right-dominant coronary anatomy, with the left main coronary artery (LM) and right coronary artery (RCA) originating from the left and right coronary sinuses, respectively. Mixed plaques located in the proximal-mid left anterior descending artery (LAD) caused 70–99% luminal stenosis and were accompanied by an overlying myocardial bridge. The distal LAD showed mild calcification (25–49%). The mid-distal left circumflex artery (LCX) exhibited moderate calcification (50–69%), whereas the proximal-mid RCA contained 25–49% calcified or mixed plaques and the distal RCA showed minimal calcification (1–24%). Based on the aggregated imaging assessment, the case was classified as Coronary Artery Disease-Reporting and Data System (CAD-RADS) 4A, and the LAD myocardial bridge was identified as an important anatomical modifier. To maintain methodological consistency with the overall framework, all images underwent isotropic resampling and standardized window-width/level normalization. Vessel masks were subsequently applied to define the region of interest (ROI) and suppress nonvascular tissue. Case-level anatomical orientation and dominance assignment are illustrated in Figure 2.

Handling of myocardial bridging (MB): to mitigate the confounding effects of MB on radiomics and functional assessment, a rigorous screening protocol was implemented. MB segments were identified by evaluating multiphase reconstructions for the characteristic ‘milking effect’ (dynamic systolic compression).

Segments exhibiting significant systolic compression (>50%) or deep intramural courses were excluded from the plaque-vulnerability training cohort to prevent the model from learning features associated with extrinsic compression rather than intrinsic plaque morphology. For cases with superficial MB, CT-FFR analysis was restricted to diastolic-phase datasets (70–80% of the R-R interval), when myocardial relaxation minimizes compressive artifacts. This approach ensured that functional parameters primarily reflected the hemodynamic impact of fixed atherosclerotic stenosis rather than transient dynamic obstruction.

Plaque-level quantification and phenotyping followed a “segmentation-first, label-guided, vessel-constrained” workflow. All 3D plaque masks were mapped onto the vessel centerline coordinate system, enabling calculation of proximal and distal reference diameters, minimum lumen area (MLA), and a continuous stenosis profile along the vessel trajectory. Stenosis severity was categorized into four standardized grades: 1–24%, 25–49%, 50–69%, and 70–99%. Plaque phenotypes were classified as calcified, non-calcified, or mixed. Mixed plaques were allowed to share voxels with calcified components but were assigned a dominant category for statistical analysis. To mitigate phase-dependent overestimation caused by MB, the tunneled LAD segment underwent systolic-phase masking and segmental attenuation correction during functional surrogate estimation. Outputs generated at both case and plaque levels were aligned with CAD-RADS annotations and formatted into clinically interpretable reports, including radial cross-sectional views, longitudinal stenosis curves, and plaque-type heatmaps anchored to Figure 2.

Quality assurance procedures ensured reproducibility and traceability of all outputs. Three consistency checks were performed during data import: (I) plaque masks were required to be fully enclosed within vessel masks; (II) stenosis-category labels had to correspond with centerline-based quantitative measurements; and (III) myocardial bridge annotations had to coincide with intramural vessel trajectories. Any discrepancies triggered a semi-automated correction process (connected-component refinement and morphological closing), followed by slice-level manual verification. The processing pipeline also generated three standardized visualization libraries for primary and supplementary figures: (I) anatomical overview and coronary dominance determination (Figure 2); (II) plaque-level radial profiles with stenosis spectra; and (III) plaque-type heatmaps with surrogate fibrous-cap visualization. These outputs enabled fully standardized plaque-wise structural quantification and function-oriented interpretation, providing stable spatial references and high-quality inputs for downstream multimodal modeling and cardiovascular risk stratification.

Ground truth annotation and expert consensus

Since public repositories typically lack standardized voxel-wise plaque annotations, a rigorous ‘Human-in-the-Loop’ annotation protocol was established to generate ground truth. The annotation team consisted of three radiologists with 5–10 years of experience in cardiovascular imaging. An initial coarse segmentation generated by a pre-trained baseline model was used as the starting point. Experts then manually refined the 3D boundaries of coronary vessel walls and plaque components, including calcified, non-calcified, and mixed plaques, using ITK-SNAP software.

To ensure reliability, 20% of the cases were randomly selected for independent blind annotation by all three experts. Inter-observer agreement was assessed using the Dice similarity coefficient (DSC) for segmentation masks and Cohen’s Kappa for plaque classification. The analysis yielded a mean DSC of 0.84±0.05 and a Kappa value of 0.89, indicating high inter-observer consistency. Ambiguous cases were further reviewed by a senior cardiologist with more than 15 years of experience in cardiovascular imaging to determine the final ground truth.

Plaque radiomics feature extraction

For each annotated plaque region, high-dimensional radiomics features were extracted and grouped into three categories: geometric, density, and texture features. Geometric features included plaque volume, maximum diameter, surface area, and wall thickness. A radiomics-derived fibrous cap surrogate was additionally calculated based on the density gradient at the lumen-plaque interface, providing an indirect indicator of cap integrity given the spatial resolution limitations of coronary CTA. Density features focused on CT value distribution, including mean density, maximum and minimum values, low-density core ratio [<30 Hounsfield unit (HU)], and calcified area proportion (>130 HU).

Texture features were derived using the gray-level co-occurrence matrix (GLCM), gray-level run-length matrix (GLRLM), local binary pattern (LBP), and Gabor filters, capturing spatial heterogeneity within plaque regions. All radiomics features were extracted using 3D Slicer and PyRadiomics, with batch processing implemented through Python scripts. The resulting feature matrices were exported in structured comma-separated values (CSV) format for downstream modeling and analysis (Figure 3).

Functional imaging integration and multimodal construction

This study incorporated functional hemodynamic parameters to construct a multimodal feature space by integrating structural and functional characteristics. CT-FFR, a key indicator of flow limitation caused by coronary stenosis, was used as a complementary feature for risk prediction. To ensure methodological consistency, a standardized DL-based CT-FFR estimation pipeline based on the VesselNet architecture was applied across the entire cohort, minimizing variability introduced by heterogeneous data sources. The reliability of AI-derived CT-FFR was evaluated in a subset of 45 patients with available invasive fractional flow reserve (FFR) measurements. Estimated CT-FFR values showed a strong correlation with invasive FFR (r=0.82, P<0.001) and achieved a diagnostic accuracy of 86.7% for detecting ischemia (FFR <0.80), supporting its validity as a robust functional input feature.

Three key functional parameters were derived: (I) trans-lesional FFR gradient (ΔFFR), defined as the difference between proximal and distal FFR values across the lesion; (II) nadir FFR value, representing the minimum FFR along the vessel and indicating maximal hemodynamic significance; and (III) volumetric ischemia burden, calculated as the ratio of vessel volume with FFR <0.80 to the total vessel volume. These metrics quantitatively reflect the hemodynamic impact of coronary plaques and complement structural CTA-derived features.

To achieve feature-level fusion, functional data were spatially aligned to structural plaque ROIs via centerline interpolation. In the fusion stage, we explored multiple strategies to integrate these distinct physical semantics, including early fusion (simple concatenation), weighted fusion (assigning learnable weights), and attention-based fusion (dynamic weighting) (Figure 4).

Feature dimensionality reduction and variable selection

To optimize the model input structure and reduce the risk of overfitting, systematic dimensionality reduction and feature selection were performed. First, principal component analysis (PCA) was applied to both radiomics and functional features for linear transformation, retaining principal components with a cumulative explained variance greater than 90% to remove redundant information.

Subsequently, nonlinear dimensionality reduction methods, including t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) and uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP), were used for two-dimensional visualization, enabling assessment of clustering patterns and separability among different plaque types. These methods were used solely for visualization, while the original features were retained for model development.

Feature selection was subsequently performed using the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) for regularized regression with λ tuning to enforce sparsity. In parallel, the Boruta algorithm was applied to identify features with the highest importance. After cross-validation, a stable feature subset with an average dimensionality of 30–50 variables was obtained. All selected variables were standardized using Z-score normalization to ensure balanced contributions during model training. Model performance evaluation and interpretability analysis

Model performance on the independent test set was evaluated using multiple metrics, including accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and F1-score. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated to assess overall predictive performance. Differences in AUC between models were statistically compared using DeLong’s test.

To improve model interpretability, gradient-weighted class activation mapping (Grad-CAM) was applied to generate heatmaps highlighting image regions contributing most strongly to the model’s predictions. In addition, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis was performed to quantify the contribution of individual features. The analysis identified several key predictors, including the radiomics-derived fibrous cap surrogate, low-attenuation core ratio, and minimum FFR value.

Model predictions were further compared with expert annotations to assess consistency and discriminative performance across plaque categories (stable versus high-risk plaques), thereby evaluating the feasibility and limitations of AI-assisted coronary CTA interpretation in clinical workflows.

Statistical analysis

Model performance on the independent test set was evaluated using accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, F1-score, and AUC. ROC curves were generated to assess discrimination performance. Differences in AUC between models were compared using DeLong’s test. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. A two-sided P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study sample characteristics and imaging data quality assurance

This study included 800 CTA scans from patients with clinically confirmed CAD. All imaging data were obtained from public medical repositories or multicenter real-world datasets with ethical approval. According to the predefined protocol, samples were randomly divided into training (n=560), validation (n=120), and independent test (n=120) sets using a 7:1.5:1.5 ratio to ensure consistent distribution across cohorts. Plaque phenotypes included stable plaques (n=350), vulnerable plaques (n=280), and ACS-associated plaques (n=170). In addition, a structured coronary CTA cohort (System-CTA set, n=45) was used as Independent Test Set 1. These cases contained four fully aligned data layers—raw CTA images, vessel segmentation masks, plaque-level 3D masks, and plaque-type annotations—organized using standardized file structures consistent with the original DICOM acquisition series (including 2.25.* identifiers). This dataset enabled direct evaluation of model interoperability in the intended deployment format. An additional 38 real-world CTA cases from our institution formed External Test Set 2 (Hospital-CTA set), which was used to evaluate model robustness under heterogeneous imaging protocols, disease spectra, and image quality conditions. The mean patient age was 63.4±8.7 years, with a male predominance (male:female ratio =2.1:1). No significant differences were observed in age or sex distribution across cohorts (P>0.05). However, ACS cases exhibited significantly higher high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) levels (P<0.01), indicating a higher cardiovascular risk profile (Table S2).

During image preprocessing, all CTA datasets were converted to NIfTI format, and spatial resolution was uniformly resampled to 0.5 mm3 to reduce inter-scanner variability. Non-local means filtering and linear interpolation were applied to correct artifact-affected regions. After standardizing window settings (350/50 HU), 3D ROI cropping isolated the LM to LAD and LCX branches. Automatic coronary segmentation was performed using a U-Net-based DL model (Figure 7A). The model achieved mean DSC of 0.872 in the training set and 0.861 in the validation set, with an intersection-over-union (IoU) of 0.801, indicating high spatial segmentation accuracy (Figure 7B).

Segmentation outputs were further refined using maximum connected-component extraction and morphological closing, effectively removing background artifacts and nonvascular noise. Visual inspection of the independent test set demonstrated that reconstructed vascular structures preserved high anatomical continuity in the LAD and LCX branches, with no major discontinuities or structural deviations. These results provided reliable vascular boundaries for subsequent plaque localization and feature extraction.

Approximately 3.4% of cases exhibited ambiguous segmentation boundaries due to blurred myocardium–vessel interfaces or imaging artifacts. These cases underwent expert review and manual correction to ensure accurate ROI delineation. All processed datasets passed strict quality-control procedures before inclusion in model training. When the trained model was applied to the System-CTA structured cohort, the presence of pre-generated vessel and plaque masks allowed seamless integration with the segmentation-recognition pipeline, with the mean Dice coefficient remaining approximately 0.86, indicating strong adaptability to the standardized data structure. In the external institutional cohort (Hospital-CTA set), despite variability in acquisition protocols and occasional anatomical factors such as MB or focal calcification, the continuity of major coronary branches was preserved. The completeness of LAD and LCX segmentation exceeded 95%, and only ~3.8% of cases required slice-level manual review due to boundary ambiguity or streak artifacts—comparable to the 3.4% issue rate observed in public datasets. Collectively, the incorporation of the two additional test cohorts did not alter the overall quality-control assessment but strengthened the evidence for the model’s robustness and practical applicability in heterogeneous real-world imaging environments.

In-depth characterization of plaque radiomics and functional features

To comprehensively characterize the microstructural and hemodynamic properties of coronary plaques, a high-dimensional radiomics feature set (>300 features) was constructed based on preprocessed and segmented coronary CTA images. Extracted features were categorized into three groups: (I) geometric features (plaque volume, maximum diameter, and lumen stenosis ratio); (II) density metrics (mean CT attenuation, low-attenuation core ratio <30 HU, and calcified plaque volume >130 HU); and (III) texture features derived from the GLCM, LBP, and Gabor filters, capturing spatial heterogeneity and microstructural variation within plaque tissues.

Comparative analysis revealed that high-risk plaques had significantly larger low-density core ratios than stable plaques (35.1%±12.8% vs. 14.7%±8.4%, P<0.001), indicating lipid-rich necrotic cores prone to fibrous cap rupture. The ACS group also showed significantly greater calcified plaque proportions, suggesting more advanced calcification and reduced vascular compliance. Texture analysis further revealed significantly elevated GLCM entropy, LBP contrast, and Gabor response heterogeneity in high-risk plaque (P<0.001), reflecting complex microstructural organization and increased plaque instability.

Corresponding texture heatmaps showed blurred plaque boundaries, heterogeneous grayscale distribution, and mixed granular patterns characteristic of vulnerable plaques (Figure 8).

Functional imaging analysis based on CT-FFR demonstrated that ACS cases had significantly lower minimum FFR values compared with stable plaques (0.71±0.06 vs. 0.85±0.04, P<0.001), indicating substantial flow limitation. In addition, the proportion of vessel segments with FFR <0.80 was markedly higher in ACS patients (78.2% vs. 23.5% in stable cases). These findings suggest that while structural features describe plaque morphology, functional parameters provide complementary insights into hemodynamic significance and risk stratification.

To integrate structural radiomics and functional parameters, an early fusion strategy was applied to concatenate all features into a unified multimodal representation. To mitigate redundancy associated with high-dimensional data, PCA was performed, retaining approximately 40 principal components that explained 90% of cumulative variance. Further feature refinement using LASSO regression and the Boruta algorithm identified 42 core variables for model training, including structural, density, texture, and hemodynamic features.

Nonlinear dimensionality reduction using UMAP revealed clear clustering patterns among plaque categories, with a distinct separation between high-risk and stable plaques (Silhouette score =0.67), supporting the discriminative capability of multimodal features. Feature importance analysis consistently ranked the radiomics-derived fibrous cap surrogate, minimum FFR value, plaque volume, and texture heterogeneity metrics among the strongest predictors across multiple selection methods (Figure 9).

Construction and performance evaluation of multimodal DL model

To enable accurate automatic identification and risk prediction of high-risk plaques in coronary CTA, a multimodal DL framework integrating structural imaging and functional hemodynamic information was developed. The model adopted a dual-stream architecture: the structural pathway employed a ResNet50 network to extract plaque texture, morphological, and edge features, while the functional pathway incorporated Vision Transformer (ViT) modules to capture long-range dependencies among CT-FFR-derived functional signals. The outputs of the two streams were integrated through a multi-head attention fusion module, generating a unified risk prediction output.

Model training used a joint optimization strategy combining cross-entropy loss and Dice loss to balance classification performance and spatial feature learning. Training was conducted using the Adam optimizer on NVIDIA A100 GPU servers with five-fold cross-validation, ensuring convergence stability and generalization capability. Data augmentation strategies, including translation, rotation, mirroring and brightness variation, were applied to improve robustness across heterogeneous imaging conditions.

On the independent test set, the multimodal model achieved strong predictive performance, with an accuracy of 90.8%, sensitivity of 91.2%, specificity of 89.4%, F1-score of 0.91, and AUC of 0.927 [95% confidence interval (CI): 0.902–0.948]. Sensitivity analysis demonstrated that the structural-only model (CNN) achieved an AUC of 0.864, while the functional-only model (ViT) achieved 0.878. In comparison, the multimodal fusion model significantly improved performance (AUC =0.927, P<0.01), confirming the incremental value of integrating structural and functional information while maintaining robust baseline performance even without CT-FFR inputs (Figure 10).

From a pathological classification perspective, the fusion model demonstrated particularly strong performance in detecting high-risk plaques, achieving an accuracy of 94.1% with a misclassification rate of 5.9%, significantly higher than the 87.2% accuracy observed for stable plaques (P<0.01). These results indicate enhanced sensitivity to structural and functional signatures associated with plaque vulnerability.

External subgroup analyses further evaluated model robustness in patients with hypertension (n=132) and diabetes (n=105). In both cohorts, the model maintained high predictive performance (AUC =0.914 and 0.917, respectively). Although the sample sizes were limited, these findings suggest preliminary evidence of transferability to heterogeneous clinical populations. Data augmentation significantly improved model robustness, increasing AUC from 0.903 to 0.927 (P=0.01), highlighting the importance of diversified training samples in complex imaging environments (Figure 11).

To assess the effectiveness of the proposed fusion strategy, the attention-based fusion module was compared with two alternative approaches: early fusion (direct feature concatenation) and weighted fusion (learnable scalar weighting of modalities). The attention-based method achieved the highest predictive performance (AUC =0.927), significantly outperforming early fusion (AUC =0.895, 95% CI: 0.871–0.919, P=0.02) and weighted fusion (AUC =0.908, 95% CI: 0.885–0.931, P=0.04). These findings indicate that attention-based fusion more effectively captures complementary relationships between structural and hemodynamic features, leading to improved prediction performance.

Interpretability analysis supports clinical credibility of the model

To evaluate the transparency and clinical credibility of the proposed model, interpretability analyses were performed at both the spatial localization and feature contribution levels. At the spatial level, Grad-CAM was applied to generate heatmaps highlighting regions contributing to model predictions. Visualization results showed that the model primarily focused on plaque cores, low-attenuation regions, and proximal stenosis entry points, which are well-recognized markers of plaque vulnerability. Comparison with expert-defined ROIs demonstrated strong concordance, with a mean IoU of 0.81 between Grad-CAM heatmaps and manual annotations, indicating clinically relevant spatial attention.

Feature-level interpretability was further assessed using SHAP analysis. The top five contributors to model predictions were the radiomics-derived fibrous cap surrogate, minimum FFR value, low-attenuation core ratio, texture heterogeneity index, and total plaque volume. Among these, the fibrous cap surrogate exhibited the highest mean SHAP value (0.341). Notably, this feature represents a radiomics-derived pattern reflecting density transition at the lumen-plaque interface rather than a direct micrometric measurement, suggesting that the model captures vulnerability-related imaging signatures beyond conventional visual resolution.

To evaluate prognostic relevance, model predictions were compared with 6-month follow-up clinical outcomes. The model correctly identified 88.5% of cases that subsequently developed cardiovascular events, indicating high predictive sensitivity. In addition, Kaplan-Meier survival analysis demonstrated that patients classified as high-risk by the model at baseline had a significantly higher cumulative incidence of MACE over the 24-month follow-up period compared with the low-risk group (log-rank test, P<0.001).

Clinical consistency was further assessed by comparing model predictions with independent interpretations from two senior cardiovascular imaging specialists (>10 years of experience). Agreement between AI predictions and expert readings was high (κ=0.81). Misclassification rates were comparable between the AI model and expert assessment (8.1% vs. 10.3%, P=0.19). Combined with an inference time of approximately 1.8 seconds per case, these results highlight the model’s potential for rapid and reliable clinical deployment (Figure 12).

Discussion

Coronary atherosclerotic heart disease remains a leading contributor to global cardiovascular mortality, with ischemic heart disease accounting for a substantial proportion of cardiovascular deaths worldwide (19). The most catastrophic clinical manifestations are frequently triggered by thrombosis originating from high-risk (vulnerable) coronary plaques, typically associated with plaque disruption such as rupture (11,12). Despite advances in cardiovascular imaging, accurate identification of high-risk plaques remains a major challenge in routine clinical practice (20,21). Against this backdrop, the present study integrates DL and radiomics to develop a multimodal coronary CTA-based framework for high-risk plaque identification and cardiovascular event prediction. By combining anatomical, functional, and textural imaging information, the proposed model provides an integrated pathway from noninvasive imaging to quantitative risk assessment. Compared with previous studies relying on conventional quantitative indices or single-modality AI methods, this approach demonstrates improved predictive performance, enhanced interpretability, and greater potential for clinical translation.

In plaque structural analysis, our study systematically extracted over 300 radiomic features encompassing three major categories: geometric morphology, density distribution, and textural complexity. The results demonstrated that high-risk plaques exhibited significantly higher low-attenuation core ratios than stable plaques (35.1%±12.8% vs. 14.7%±8.4%, P<0.001) despite comparable plaque volumes. In addition, texture metrics such as GLCM entropy and Gabor-based heterogeneity were markedly elevated in unstable plaques, reflecting greater microstructural irregularity. These high-dimensional radiomic features, which are often difficult to detect through conventional visual assessment, enabled quantitative characterization of subtle imaging patterns associated with plaque vulnerability, thereby improving the model’s discriminative capability.

Beyond structural characterization, the integration of CT-FFR parameters allowed the model to incorporate functional hemodynamic information. The ACS group exhibited significantly lower minimum FFR values than the stable group (0.71±0.06 vs. 0.85±0.04, P<0.001), with 78.2% of plaque regions showing FFR <0.80, compared with 23.5% in stable plaques. These findings indicate that structural imaging alone may not fully capture the hemodynamic significance of coronary lesions. By incorporating CT-FFR as a complementary dimension, the model achieved improved identification of functionally significant plaque instability. The application of an early fusion strategy to integrate structural and functional features further enhanced predictive performance, supporting the feasibility and value of multimodal imaging integration.

From the perspective of model architecture, a dual-path deep neural network combining ResNet50 and ViT was developed to capture complementary imaging representations, including local plaque textures and long-range feature dependencies. These two streams were integrated through an attention-based fusion mechanism, enabling effective multimodal feature interaction. The final fused model achieved strong predictive performance on the independent test set, with an accuracy of 90.8%, sensitivity of 91.2%, specificity of 89.4%, and an AUC of 0.927 (95% CI: 0.902–0.948). This performance significantly exceeded that of the standalone CNN (AUC =0.864) and ViT models (AUC =0.878), highlighting the synergistic benefit of jointly modeling structural and functional information for plaque risk identification.

The model also demonstrated differential discriminative performance across plaque types. In particular, identification of high-risk plaques achieved an accuracy of 94.1% with a misclassification rate of 5.9%, outperforming the 87.2% accuracy observed for stable plaques. This finding suggests that vulnerable plaques exhibit more distinct structural and functional imaging signatures that can be effectively captured by the multimodal network. Consistently, UMAP-based two-dimensional feature visualization revealed clear clustering patterns among plaque categories (Silhouette coefficient =0.67), supporting the strong discriminative capability and biological relevance of the extracted multimodal features.

Model interpretability is essential for clinical adoption. In this study, Grad-CAM was used to visualize the model’s attention regions. The heatmaps showed that the model primarily focused on low-attenuation cores, fibrous cap regions, and proximal stenosis sites, which are recognized markers of plaque vulnerability. The overlap between Grad-CAM maps and expert annotations achieved a mean IoU of 0.81, indicating strong spatial agreement with clinical assessment. Feature-level interpretation using SHAP identified the radiomics-derived fibrous cap surrogate (SHAP value =0.341), minimum FFR value, low-attenuation core ratio, and texture heterogeneity index as the most influential predictors. These results are consistent with current clinical understanding of high-risk coronary plaques and support the biological plausibility of the model’s predictions.

To enhance model generalizability and clinical applicability, the training and validation datasets were constructed from multicenter cohorts with heterogeneous scanner types and imaging conditions. In high-risk subgroups, including patients with hypertension and diabetes, the model maintained strong predictive performance with AUC values exceeding 0.91, indicating robust applicability across diverse CAD populations. In addition, data augmentation strategies (e.g., rotation and brightness adjustment) significantly improved model robustness, increasing the test-set AUC from 0.903 to 0.927 (P=0.01) and reducing dependence on specific training datasets.

Despite these strengths, the model showed limited accuracy in a small proportion of cases (~4.3%), primarily involving small plaques with weak textural expression, intermediate attenuation, or borderline functional parameters. These plaques often lack typical imaging features and are also subject to inter-observer variability in manual interpretation. Future work may incorporate dynamic perfusion parameters, temporal texture variations, or multiphase CTA imaging to improve sensitivity for such borderline lesions, together with refined attention mechanisms to better capture subtle boundary features.

For clinical application, our AI model provides crucial decision support, enabling the real-time identification of high-risk plaques (inference time ~1.8 s/case) during routine CTA scans and predicting future cardiovascular events (pre-identification rate: 88.5%). Compared with expert annotations, the AI system achieved excellent agreement with two senior cardiologists (Kappa =0.81), with no statistically significant difference in misclassification rates (P=0.19). Kaplan-Meier analysis confirmed significantly higher event rates in model-stratified high-risk groups (log-rank P<0.001), demonstrating potential for risk stratification and guidance on intervention timing.

Despite these promising findings, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the external validation cohort from our institution was relatively small (n=38), and larger multicenter studies are needed to further confirm the model’s generalizability across diverse scanners and patient populations. Second, the spatial resolution of conventional coronary CTA (≈0.5 mm) limits direct visualization of microstructures such as thin fibrous caps (<65 µm). Therefore, the fibrous-cap feature identified by the model should be interpreted as a radiomics-derived surrogate reflecting attenuation gradients and textural heterogeneity associated with cap thinning, rather than a direct anatomical measurement comparable to optical coherence tomography (OCT) or intravascular ultrasound (IVUS). Future studies incorporating photon-counting CT may provide higher spatial resolution for validating these radiomic signatures. Finally, prospective clinical trials are required to determine whether the implementation of this model can improve treatment decision-making and patient outcomes.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we developed an interpretable multimodal DL framework for identifying high-risk coronary plaques and predicting cardiovascular events using coronary CTA. By integrating radiomics-derived structural features with functional hemodynamic information through a dual-stream network architecture, the model achieved improved predictive accuracy and enhanced clinical interpretability. This approach provides a practical tool for risk stratification in CAD and highlights the potential of multimodal AI in advancing precision cardiovascular medicine. Future work will focus on incorporating dynamic imaging data, longitudinal plaque tracking, and large-scale real-world validation to further strengthen the reliability and clinical applicability of AI-driven cardiovascular diagnostic systems.
