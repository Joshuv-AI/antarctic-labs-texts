Does Conventional Radiography Overrepresent Joint Space Narrowing in Hallux Rigidus? A Comparative Study With Weightbearing CT Imaging
Authors: Ahmed Saleh, Vineel Mallavarapu, Nacime Salomao Barbachan Mansur, Aly M. Fayed, Cesar de Cesar Netto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Conventional weightbearing radiographs are the gold standard for grading hallux rigidus (HR), yet clinical symptoms often do not correlate with radiographic severity. This study compared 2-dimensional (2D) weightbearing radiographs with weightbearing computed tomography (WBCT) to evaluate if traditional imaging overrepresents joint degeneration.Methods:A retrospective study of 21 patients (13 males; average age 55.9 years) with HR was conducted. All patients had both weightbearing radiographs and WBCT. Two fellowship-trained orthopaedic surgeons measured the first metatarsophalangeal joint (first MTPJ) space, dorsal osteophyte height, and dorsal narrowing percentage. Inter-rater reliability was assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs).Results:Joint space width was significantly larger on WBCT (1.9 ± 0.44 mm) compared to radiographs (0.92 ± 0.53 mm; P < .001). WBCT also showed significantly less narrowing of the dorsal articular surface (22.78% ± 8.71% vs 46.57% ± 18.8%, P < .001). Inter-rater reliability for joint space narrowing was excellent for WBCT (ICC = 0.91) but moderate for radiographs (ICC = 0.67; P < .001). WBCT was more sensitive in detecting loose bodies and sesamoid changes.Conclusion:WBCT demonstrated consistently larger joint-space measurements and less dorsal joint-space narrowing compared with conventional radiographs, along with substantially higher inter-rater reliability for joint space narrowing. These findings indicate a systematic measurement difference between modalities, with radiographs tending to overrepresent joint-space loss. This discrepancy may help explain why joint-preserving procedures such as cheilectomy can succeed even when radiographs appear advanced.Level of Evidence:Level III, retrospective comparative study.

Introduction

Hallux rigidus (HR), also referred to as hallux limitus (HL), is defined as a chronic and progressive degenerative condition primarily affecting the first metatarsophalangeal joint (first MTPJ). Symptoms commonly associated with HR include pain with joint motion, intolerance to shoe wear, stiffness, and functional limitation. 1 It is estimated to affect a significant portion of the adult population, increasing in prevalence with age. 2 The impact of HR can be substantial, with affected individuals reporting lower scores on health-related quality of life measures. 3

To evaluate the severity and guide treatment for HR, classification systems have been developed over time.4-8 The Coughlin and Shurnas classification, which integrates clinical findings with radiographic criteria, has gained popularity and is now widely used. 7 Such grading systems typically imply that as radiographic changes of the degenerative joint disease progress, the associated clinical symptoms will concurrently increase in severity. 7

However, Nixon et al 9 stated that the correlation between the conventional radiographic severity and patient symptoms in patients with HR may be discordant. They have found no significant correlation between Coughlin and Shurnas clinical-radiographic grade and Foot and Ankle Ability Measure activities of daily living (FAAM ADL) scores, and no direct correlation between radiologic findings and clinical signs and symptoms, as well as a weak predictive value regarding intraoperative cartilage loss. Furthermore, several investigations have addressed the effectiveness of cheilectomy in advanced Coughlin classification grades (grade 3), indicating that traditional radiography might overestimate joint deterioration.10-13

The lack of a strong, consistent correlation between the conventional standing radiographic grading and clinical symptoms indicates that 2D radiographic appearance alone may not fully capture the patient’s experience of pain and functional limitation, as well as the intraoperative joint status, indicating the necessity for a more precise method like weightbearing computed tomography (WBCT), which has been proven recently to assess the true joint status more precisely. 14

The aim of the study is to compare the radiographic staging of HR using traditional weightbearing 2D conventional radiography and 3D WBCT imaging. We hypothesized that first MTPJ joint space width measurements would be larger on WBCT compared with radiographs, and that WBCT would demonstrate superior inter-rater reliability when assessing joint space width (JSW).

Methods

This was a retrospective study that was conducted at a single academic institute. The study was approved by the institutional review board (IRB) with IRB number (201904825). All patients with clinical diagnosis of HR who had both conventional weightbearing radiographs and WBCT of the affected foot (between 2021 and 2022) were consecutively screened and evaluated to minimize selection bias. Patients who had previous hallux surgeries or those with associated severe hallux valgus (hallux valgus angle > 40°) were excluded. The complete patient screening, exclusion, and final enrollment pathway is systematically detailed in the flow diagram (Figure 1). Statistical Analysis

The primary outcome measures of this study were the first MTPJ space width and the corresponding inter-rater reliability. Evaluations concerning dorsal spur length, the detection of intra-articular loose bodies, and sesamoid degenerative changes were defined as secondary, exploratory analyses. Given the exploratory nature of these secondary comparisons aimed at generating hypotheses for future research, no multiplicity adjustments for alpha inflation were performed. Descriptive statistics were calculated for all study variables. Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Between-group comparisons were performed using the independent samples t test for parametric data and the Mann-Whitney U test for nonparametric data. Statistical significance was defined as P <.05. To compare interobserver reliability between weightbearing radiographs and WBCT, intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) were calculated separately for each imaging modality using a 2‑way random effects model with absolute agreement. Differences in ICCs between modalities were assessed using Fisher’s z‑transformation, which allows statistical comparison of correlation coefficients by converting ICC values to normally distributed z‑scores. This approach enabled evaluation of whether agreement between readers differed significantly across the 2 imaging techniques. P values lower than .05 were considered significant. Additionally, a post hoc power analysis was performed for the primary outcome measure (first MTPJ space width) using G*Power software (v3.1.9.7) to verify the adequacy of our sample size. The computation was based on a 2-tailed paired t test with a significance level (alpha) of .05.

Results

The study included 21 patients; 13 were males (61.9%). The average age was 55.9 ± 12.5 and body mass index (BMI) 28.9 ± 6.5. The patient cohort was distributed across the Coughlin and Shurnas clinical-radiographic grading system as follows: grade 2 (n = 10, 47.6%) and grade 3 (n = 11, 52.4%). The average joint first MTPJ space width was significantly larger on WBCT (1.9 ± 0.44 mm) than in conventional radiographs (0.92 ± 0.53 mm; P < .001). WBCT also showed less narrowing of the dorsal articular surface (22.78% ± 8.71%) vs (46.57% ± 18.8%). No difference in the length of dorsal spurs was noted (4.57 ± 1.49 mm vs 4.77 ± 1.56 mm; P = .788) (Table 1). WBCT detected loose bodies in 9 patients whereas radiographs detected it only in 6 patients. Sesamoid changes were appreciated on the WBCT image of all patients.

A post hoc power analysis demonstrated that given our sample size of 21 patients, the observed substantial effect size (Cohen d approx. 2.0) for the primary outcome of joint space width provided an achieved statistical power of approximately 100% (beta = 1.00), supporting the conclusion that the study was fully and adequately powered to detect the reported significant measurement differences (P < .001) despite the modest cohort size.

The comparative analysis of inter-rater reliability between radiographic and WBCT modalities for evaluating the first MTPJ reveals consistently higher agreement with WBCT across all assessed parameters. For joint space narrowing, WBCT demonstrated excellent reliability (ICC = 0.910), markedly surpassing the moderate reliability observed with radiographs (ICC = 0.671). Similarly, assessments of dorsal joint space narrowing yielded high ICCs for both modalities, although WBCT (ICC = 0.884) showed slightly lower precision than radiographs (ICC = 0.901), potentially reflecting differences in image interpretation or measurement standardization. Evaluation of dorsal spur size also favored WBCT (ICC = 0.883) over radiographs (ICC = 0.811), reinforcing the modality’s reproducibility in capturing osseous detail. All comparisons reached statistical significance (P < .001; Table 2).

Discussion

The most important finding of the current study was that, when assessing patients with HR using conventional radiographs and WBCT imaging, radiographs showed significantly smaller joint space measurements, which could negatively affect surgeons’ treatment decisions for HR patients. WBCT also allowed better assessment of the metatarso-sesamoid arthritic degeneration as well as the presence of intra-articular loose bodies. The current study also demonstrated that WBCT yielded higher interobserver reliability than conventional radiographs for the assessment of overall joint space narrowing, consistent with our hypothesis. Although WBCT measurements generally showed less joint space narrowing of the first MTPJ than those obtained from radiographs, WBCT showed a slightly lower ICC for evaluating the percentage of dorsal narrowing on the sagittal plane. This is likely attributable to variability between readers in selecting the exact midsagittal slice used for percentage narrowing measurements. Despite this minor reduction in ICC, the magnitude of narrowing measured on WBCT remained lower than that observed on radiographs.

The Coughlin and Shurnas classification system is the gold standard for grading the severity of HR. Developed in 2003, its appeal stems from its comprehensive nature, which incorporates a combination of clinical findings, radiographic criteria, and passive range of motion to categorize the condition into 5 distinct grades (0 through 4). 7

A review by Beeson et al 8 evaluated 14 different classification systems for HR and found that only 3, including the Coughlin and Shurnas system, met the criteria for being “robust.” The primary critique by Beeson et al was that the system was developed using retrospective data rather than prospective trials that would allow for a formal assessment of validity. Additionally, certain boundary criteria (eg, “mild” vs “moderate” osteophyte production) are inherently subjective, posing a danger of inconsistent classification in borderline cases.

A study by Nixon et al 9 investigated whether the Coughlin and Shurnas grade correlated with patient-reported function using the validated FAAM ADL score. The study found no relationship between the clinical-radiographic grade and the FAAM ADL score. This suggests a disconnect between the classification’s severity grade and the patient’s functional disability.

Similarly, a prospective, randomized study by Baumhauer et al 15 found no correlation between the Coughlin and Shurnas grade and key clinical factors such as active peak dorsiflexion or visual analog scale (VAS) pain scores. Although they found a statistically significant correlation between a higher grade and less remaining cartilage, the correlation was weak, limiting its predictive value.

This discrepancy underscores a fundamental challenge in orthopaedic diagnostics: a primary limitation of conventional radiographs is the frequent disconnect between radiographic findings and the patient’s clinical presentation. The severity of arthritis seen on radiographs often does not align with the intensity of the patient’s symptoms or their functional disability.9,16,17

Similarly, a good or excellent surgical outcome often does not correlate with the radiographic appearance of the joint at final follow-up. This lack of correlation suggests that relying solely on radiographs can be misleading when assessing the true impact of the disease on a patient’s life.1,8,18

Long-term observational data support this clinical-radiographic paradox. For instance, radiographic deterioration over time does not necessarily mean a patient’s symptoms will worsen. 19 One long-term study of nonoperatively treated patients found that although 67% had radiographic deterioration, only 1 patient reported that their pain had worsened. 19

Cheilectomy is a well-known surgical intervention for HR. It is described as one of the most common and reproducible procedures for early to mid-stage HR.17,20,21

One of the most interesting findings in the literature is the debate around the effectiveness of cheilectomy for advanced stages of HR. Some authors considered cheilectomy for high-grade HR (grade III and IV) as a viable option that deviates from the traditional treatment algorithm. Doğan et al 13 specifically analyzed patients with grade 3 and 4 HR who underwent cheilectomy because they declined arthrodesis. At a mean follow-up of 26.2 months, 19 of 21 feet (90%) achieved good to excellent results based on American Orthopaedic Foot & Ankle Society Score (AOFAS) scores, with significant improvements in dorsiflexion and a significant decrease in VAS pain scores.

Another study by Maes et al 22 prospectively evaluated 105 feet, of which 37.6% were high-grade (defined as modified Coughlin and Shurnas classification grade 3 or 4) managed with cheilectomy and phalangeal osteotomy (Moberg/Moberg-Akin). At final follow-up, there were no significant differences in functional outcomes (AOFAS), quality of life (36-Item Short Form Health Survey), or pain scores (VAS) between the low-grade and high-grade groups.

Similarly, O’Malley et al 12 reported on 81 patients with advanced Hattrup and Johnson grade III HR (severe narrowing with loss of more than 50% of the joint space) managed with cheilectomy and great toe proximal phalangeal extension osteotomy. At a mean follow-up of 4.3 years, 85.2% of patients were satisfied, mean dorsiflexion improved by 27 degrees, and only 4.9% required subsequent arthrodesis. 12

These favorable clinical outcomes are further corroborated by a systematic review by Arceri et al, 23 who reported that cheilectomy in advanced stages (grade III/IV) provides pain reduction and some functional improvement with careful patient selection that depends on clinical signs (absence of mid-range pain) rather than radiology.

The efficacy of cheilectomy in advanced stages of HR may indicate the inadequacies of standard radiography in accurately depicting the condition of the first MTPJ.

WBCT may offer measurement advantages over standard 2D weight-bearing radiographs for evaluating foot alignment and rotation, given that it provides 3-dimensional (3D) reconstructions that reduce measurement variability caused by projection angles and bone overlap inherent in plain films.1,2,14

WBCT can better delineate subtle findings such as subchondral cysts in the M1 head and P1, thereby allowing better characterization of the extent of hallux and sesamoid osteoarthritis. This detailed view can more accurately reflect the true stage of the disease.24,25 Cheung et al 14 reported excellent intraobserver reliability (ICC ≥ 0.93) for all measured parameters, including metatarsal declination ratios, lengths, and angles. They also found that WBCT demonstrated good to excellent interobserver reliability (ICC ≥ 0.85) for these measurements, reinforcing the consistency of this imaging modality across different evaluators.

This study represents the first direct comparison between conventional 2D radiography and WBCT specifically for the assessment of HR. Although this comparative analysis offers a novel perspective on diagnostic imaging, the findings must be interpreted within the context of several inherent limitations, including the study’s retrospective design, small sample size, and the current lack of a standardized midsagittal slice for measuring dorsal narrowing on WBCT, and the absence of a formally validated method for ensuring anatomical correspondence of the 6 measurement points between the radiographic and WBCT modalities.

Although a retrospective design carries potential biases, it permitted the analysis of existing clinical data without subjecting patients to unnecessary radiation exposure or the additional costs associated with prospective WBCT protocols. Furthermore, although the cohort was relatively small, our post hoc power analysis confirmed that the study achieved a statistical power of approximately 100% because of the high effect size observed between the 2 modalities. This indicates that the measurement discrepancies between conventional 2D radiographs and WBCT are highly robust and mathematically reproducible, minimizing the risk of a type II error. Nevertheless, future larger, multicenter prospective studies are strongly warranted to confirm these results and further expand their clinical utility.

Regarding the lack of a specific midsagittal measurement standard, this is largely a reflection of the nascent state of WBCT protocols in foot and ankle surgery. As a universal consensus on anatomical landmarks for HR has yet to be established, our approach reflects the current challenges of clinical implementation. To address this, we propose a standardized technique to identify the true mid-joint axis by locating the center of the first MTPJ articular surface on both sagittal and coronal views, which mathematically yields the longest definitive articular distance. Consequently, we strongly encourage future investigators and multicenter collaborative groups to develop a unified, standardized consensus protocol based on these anatomical landmarks. Establishing such a baseline across international registries will be vital to optimize inter-rater reliability globally, minimize measurement variability, and enable direct comparability of diagnostic parameters in upcoming clinical trials.

Another limitation of this study is the lack of correlation with direct clinical data, patient-reported outcome measures (eg, VAS pain or FAAM ADL scores), and intraoperative cartilage grading, which were beyond the scope of this study.

Conclusion

The current study suggests that WBCT demonstrates consistently larger joint-space measurements and less dorsal joint-space narrowing compared with conventional radiographs, along with substantially higher inter-rater reliability for joint space narrowing. These findings indicate a systematic measurement difference between modalities, with radiographs tending to overrepresent joint-space loss. This discrepancy may help explain why joint-preserving procedures such as cheilectomy can succeed even when radiographs appear advanced.
