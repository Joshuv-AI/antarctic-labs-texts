Elucidating activity-stability trade-offs in nano-fingerprint carbon anchoring single atoms and clusters in oxygen reduction reaction
Authors: Feng Li, Qikang Wu, Yan Zhou, Shanshan Lv, Jing Meng, Yongning Zhang, Sixin Dou, Yanyang Cheng, Chang Chen, Wenming Sun, Zheng Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHybrid sites (M1+x) that incorporate single atoms (M1) with clusters (Mx) hold great potential for overcoming linear scaling relations in electrocatalysis, but spatial decoupling of M1 from Mx fundamentally hampers intercomponent synergy. Here, we report a composite electrocatalyst (Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC) featuring Fex clusters encapsulated within nano-fingerprint carbon layers and surrounded by densely dispersed Fe/Zn-N4 atomic sites. Decoupling experiments revealed that Fe1/Zn1 atomic sites mainly initiate catalytic activity, while Fex clusters further boost intrinsic activity, whereas nano-fingerprint carbon facilitates the confinement and stabilization of M1+x. The Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC demonstrates competitive oxygen reduction reaction performance, achieving a half-wave potential of 0.93 V and stability over 50 h. Zinc-air batteries with Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC air cathode exhibit a peak power density of 263.82 mW·cm−2 and robust stability exceeding 2200 h at 10 mA cm−2. Density functional theory calculations reveal that the atomic Zn sites, Fex clusters and nano-fingerprint carbon layers jointly enhance the catalytic activity and stability by modulating the electronic structure of Fe-N4 sites and optimizing the adsorption energy of the key intermediate, especially OH*.Subject terms: Electrocatalysis, Batteries, Electrocatalysis, Carbon nanotubes and fullerenes

Introduction

The energy crisis and the environmental pollution caused by using traditional fossil fuel promote the development of other renewable energy technology such as the application of fuel cell and metal-air battery1,2. However, the cathodic oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) slow sluggish kinetics for multi-step electron and proton transfer process3–5. The platinum-based catalysts are the most commonly used and effective ORR catalysts today especially in proton exchange membrane fuel cells6, however, the inferior activity and stability of Pt in alkaline electrolytes limit its application in anion-exchange membrane fuel cells and metal-air batteries7,8. Moreover, the high cost and low storage capacity of Pt greatly limit large-scale commercialization, thereby low cost, high efficiency and strong stability of non-noble metal-based ORR electrocatalyst with high efficiency and stability is desirable to replace Pt-based catalysts9,10. Single atom catalysts (SAC) containing metal-nitrogen sites (M-N-C) has demonstrated comparable or even favorable activity to Pt-based catalysts especially in alkaline ORR11,12, which is benefited from its distinct coordinative and electronic state13. The M-N-C configuration has attracted attention because it has an incomplete d orbital and is easier to accept electrons provided by ORR intermediates14–17. Among which atomically dispersed Fe-N-C catalysts with Fe-N4 sites are most widely studied and considered as one of the most potential candidates due to their adjustable sites, convenience of synthesis and low cost18,19.

However, Fe-N4 has a highly symmetrical electronic structure distribution and the adsorption of ORR intermediates (O* and OH*) on FeN4 sites is still too strong compared to Pt20,21, which slows down the reaction kinetics and limits the overall ORR activity. In addition, the migration and aggregation of single atomic sites under continuous operating conditions, leading to a decline in activity and stability, present a significant hurdle that must be overcome for their potential commercialization22. To this end, an effective method is to introduce exotic metal sites or form metal clusters for synergistic catalysis23. This method regulates the coordination and electronic structure through pairing or long-range coupling of adjacent exotic metal sites and clusters, thereby adjusts the adsorption intensity of Fe-N4 active sites for ORR intermediates24. Another effective approach is adjustment of support for carbon-hosted Fe-N4 sites25, in which the spatial and geometric structure of the supporting carbon framework of SAC is crucial in regulating the metal-support interaction and the structure of metal sites26. Compared with a flat surface without strain, a curved carbon support facilitates the induction of intrinsic micro-strain, which affects the d-orbitals and electronic structure of metal atoms27. In addition, highly curved nanostructures can regulate the electric field around the catalyst surface and adjust the microenvironment at the reaction sites, thereby high activity and stability can be achieved simultaneously in ORR28. The above regulation means of electronic or orbital structure of Fe-N4 are usually separate, and the cooperative regulation of more factors are rarely studied but instrumental for the intricate environment of Fe-N4 embedded in carbon matrices29,30.

Herein, a composite catalyst containing Fex clusters encapsulated within nano-fingerprint carbon layers and surrounded by densely dispersed Fe/Zn-N4 sites (denoted as Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC) was obtained. A series of decoupled control samples, including those with dual Fe/Zn single-atom sites anchored on nano-fingerprint carbon (denoted as Zn1Fe1/f-NC), dual Fe/Zn single-atom sites co-anchored with Fex sites on planar carbon (denoted as Zn1Fe1+x/NC), and dual Fe/Zn single-atom sites on planar carbon (denoted as Zn1Fe1/NC), were also prepared. Decoupling experiments revealed that Fex clusters promoted the high activity of Fe/Zn-N4 atomic sites, while the nano-fingerprint carbon layers facilitate the confinement and stabilization of hybrid (M1+x) sites. In 0.1 M KOH electrolyte, the prepared Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC displayed competitive ORR performance with E1/2 = 0.93 V, surpassing that of Zn1Fe1/f-NC (E1/2 = 0.904 V), Zn1Fe1/NC (E1/2 = 0.89 V), Zn1Fe1+x/NC (E1/2 = 0.925 V) and Pt/C catalyst. After 50 h of stability testing, Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC and Zn1Fe1/f-NC exhibited less attenuation than Zn1Fe1+x/NC and Zn1Fe1/NC, demonstrating the crucial role of nano-fingerprint carbon in enhancing stability The Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC catalyst was further evaluated as the cathode by assembling zinc-air battery (ZAB). The Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC air cathode showed a high power density (263.82 mW·cm−2) in ZAB, which is much higher than Pt/C (145.22 mW·cm−2). The Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC-based ZAB exhibits high stability for over 2200 h and good resistance to deformation. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations have demonstrated that introducing each site (single atom Zn, Fe clusters or nano-fingerprint carbon layers) in the composite catalyst can optimize the adsorption of key intermediate OH* on Fe-N4. Therefore, single atom Zn/Fe, Fex clusters and nano-fingerprint carbon layer jointly enhanced the catalytic performance in ORR through the multivariate regulation of electronic structure.

Results

Synthesis and characterization

The utilization of ZIF-8 as a precursor for coordinating metal ions via high-temperature pyrolysis has emerged as a prevalent approach for synthesizing nitrogen-doped carbon-anchored transition metal catalysts. Typically, single-atom metal catalysts can be obtained by removing metal salts that are weakly bound to ZIF-8. Figure 1a outlines the synthetic process of Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC and Zn1Fe1/NC catalysts. Initially, the synthesized ZIF-8 undergoes stirring to adsorb Fe(acac)3 and subsequent centrifugation without washing, resulting in supersaturated adsorption of Fe salts (denoted as Fe(acac)3@ZIF-8). After calcination at 1000 °C, Fe(acac)3@ZIF-8 evolves into a catalyst comprising Fe/Zn single-atom, and Fex clusters anchored on nano-fingerprint nitrogen-doped carbon, which is labeled as Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC. The solid obtained by centrifugation and washing after ZIF-8 adsorbing Fe(acac)3 (denoted as Fe(acac)3-ZIF-8), and then calcined at 1000 °C, will yield a catalyst containing only single atom Fe and Zn without Fex clusters and fingerprint carbon layers, which is labeled as Zn1Fe1/NC. It should be noted that based on the synthetic pathways of these two fundamental catalysts, we also prepared two more comparative samples for decoupling experiments, including Zn1Fe1+x/NC and Zn1Fe1/f-NC (where “M1”, “Mx”, and “f” represent “single-atom site”, “clusters”, and “fingerprint carbon layers”, respectively; more details see the part of Methods).

The X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of the precursors Fe(acac)3@ZIF-8 and Fe(acac)3-ZIF-8 show obvious reduction in peaks intensity compared with ZIF-8 (Figure S1), indicating that increasing the amount of Fe salt loaded onto ZIF-8 increasingly perturbs its crystallinity, while not destroying the overall structure. The scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of the precursors Fe(acac)3@ZIF-8 and Fe(acac)3-ZIF-8 also prove that the introduction of Fe salt to ZIF-8 didn’t change the morphology (Figure S2–S4). After pyrolysis at high temperature, the XRD patterns of Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC and Zn1Fe1/NC show that there are no characteristic diffraction peaks of metal and metal oxides except two broad peaks are observed at ≈24o and 44o corresponding to the (002) and (010) crystal facets of graphitic carbon (Figure S5). The Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC and Zn1Fe1/NC is basically maintaining a morphology similar to Fe(acac)3@ZIF-8 and Fe(acac)3-ZIF-8 with slightly smaller size due to shrinkage (Fig. 1b and S6). It was determined by inductively coupled plasma-optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES) measurement that the Fe content in Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC was 0.90 wt%. The Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) analysis revealed that the surface area of Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC (958.7 m2 g−1) is slightly smaller than Zn1Fe1/NC (1140.6 m2 g−1) (Figure S7), and both achieved a high surface area.

As shown by SEM and transmission electron microscope (TEM) images in Fig. 1b, c, S6, and S8, the morphology of Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC and Zn1Fe1/NC is similar to ZIF-8 with polyhedral size of about 250 nm. The element mapping images of the relevant area shows that Fe, Zn, C and N elements are uniformly distributed on Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC (Fig. 1d). The high-resolution transmission electron microscope (HRTEM) image reveals that an obvious multi-layer carbon like fingerprints with a diameter of 8 nm were observed on the surface of the polyhedral particles in Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC (Fig. 1e), and this type of nano-fingerprint carbon layer is widely present (Figure S9). In Zn1Fe1/NC (Figures S10–S12) and sample obtained by Fe(acac)3@ZIF-8 annealed at 900 °C (Figure S13 and S14), no multilayer carbon is observed. These indicate that supersaturated adsorption of Fe(acac)3 and pyrolysis at 1000 °C are the key factors that induce the formation of particular nano-fingerprint carbons layer structures. Recent studies have shown that adjusting the concentration of iron metal salt can increase the curling degree of graphitic carbon layer31–33.

The high-angle annular bright-field scanning transmission electron microscope (HAABF-STEM) image shows more clearly that the layer spacing of nano-fingerprint carbon layer is about 0.353 nm (Fig. 1f), which is slightly larger than the spacing of some common nitrogen-doped graphitized carbon layers34. The high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscope (HAADF-STEM) image (Fig. 1g) shown that a large number of atomically dispersed bright spots can be clearly identified on the carbon support, which are the single Fe and Zn atoms. The Fe clusters (marked by red circles) surrounded with Fe/Zn single-atoms (marked by orange circles) were also observed as multiple bright spots gathered together on the nano-fingerprint carbon layer, and the size of the Fe clusters are about 1 nm. It has been reported that when the spatial distance between the cluster and the single atom is sub-nanometer, there is a strong electronic coupling effect35–37. In Figure S15, the number of Zn/Fe single atoms within 1.0 nm range around the cluster was counted, and calculated the average distance distribution from single-atom to cluster. As shown in Figure S12, the HAADF-STEM image of Zn1Fe1/NC shows that a large number of single atoms (marked by blue circles) are evenly distributed on the carbon support without nano-fingerprint carbon layer and also lacking clusters. Subsequently, we investigated the possible reasons for the generation of the nano-fingerprint carbon structure. Figure S16 shows HRTEM image of the prepared “Zn1/NC” catalyst from direct calcination of ZIF-8, but no curled nano-fingerprint carbon layer appears. “Zn1Fe1+x/NC” catalyst was prepared by impregnating the obtained Zn1Fe1/NC sample with an excess of Fe precursor, followed by calcination. The resulting Zn1Fe1+x/NC catalyst also lacks the fingerprint-like carbon layer structure, demonstrating that the formation of nano-fingerprint carbon layer is a self-catalyzed, in-situ process requiring an appropriate amount of Fe precursor at a suitable calcination temperature. In contrast, the nano-fingerprint carbon layers structure cannot be obtained by re-introducing Fe precursors into the pre-graphitized carbon substrate (Figure S17).

The X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was used to characterize the electronic structure of Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC and Zn1Fe1/NC. The measured full spectrum of Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC illustrates the presence of Zn, Fe, C and N signals (Figure S18). The high-resolution N 1 s spectrum of Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC, Zn1Fe1/NC and Zn1/NC (Figure S19a) can be deconvoluted into pyridinic-N (398.3 eV), metal-N (M-Nx, 399.1 eV), pyrrolic-N (400.6 eV), graphitic-N (401.3 eV) and N-oxide- (403.2 eV), respectively. The peak area of M-Nx in Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC and Zn1Fe1/NC is larger than that in Zn1/NC, indicating that there are more pyridinic N coordinated with more single atom metal species. The high-resolution C 1 s spectrum (Figure S19b) of Zn1/NC, Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC and Zn1Fe1/NC can be deconvoluted into four peaks of C-C (284.8 eV), C-N (285.8 eV), C-O (287.0 eV) and C = O (289.7 eV), respectively. The Fe 2p fitting curves in Figure S20 show the presence of positively charged iron species of Fe2+ and Fe3+. Table S1 shows the proportions of the two valence states of Fe species in Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC (Fe2+ content 68 %, Fe3+ content 32 %) and Zn1Fe1/NC (Fe2+ content 57 %, Fe3+ content 43 %). Mössbauer deconvolution of the transmission profiles reveals that D1, D2 and D3 doublets are the dominant Fe sites in both Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC and Zn1Fe1/NC (Figs. 2a, b). D1 has been observed in a large number of Fe-N-C catalysts and is usually attributed to low-spin Fe(II)-N4 or high-spin Fe(III)-N4 moiety (with O2 adsorbed), which have been identified as highly active species for ORR in contrast to the D2 sites (D2 sites represent medium-spin Fe(II)-N4)35,38. On the contrary, the D1 sites exhibit less stable compared to D2 sites39. The percentages of D1 and D2 are 58.1% and 33.2% for Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC, respectively, and 60.4% and 19.9% for Zn1Fe1/NC, respectively (Table S2). These findings indicate that compared to Zn1Fe1/NC, Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC shows a better balance of activity and stability regulation. In particular, D3 in Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC is a minor, high s-electron-density species interpreted as Fe-C coupled (metal-like/carbide-like or encapsulated Fe) because of its negative IS and relatively small QS40. Overall, the Mössbauer spectroscopy show that nano-fingerprint carbon layers increases local s-electron density at a subset of Fe sites and changes local symmetry41. Raman spectra (Figure S21) shows the ID/IG ratio of Zn1Fe1/NC and Zn1/NC are 0.99, and the value of Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC is 1.04, which indicates that the nano-fingerprint carbon layer structure destroys the graphitized carbon lattice and thereby produce the defects synchronously.

As shown in the X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) spectra (Fig. 2c), the Fe K-edge of Zn1Fe1+x/NC is located between Fe foil and Fe2O3, indicating that the oxidation state of Fe is between 0 and +3. Compared with the Zn1Fe1/NC, the absorption edge energy of Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC is slightly lower, which is because of the existence of Fe cluster. Figure 2d is the Fourier transform extended X-ray absorption fine structure (FT-EXAFS) spectra of the samples, Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC and Zn1Fe1/NC have a prominent peak at about 1.5 Å, indicating Fe-N scattering. There is another smaller peak at 2.3 Å, which is attributed to the Fe-Fe bonds in Fe clusters. As shown in Figure S22, the valence state of Zn in Zn1Fe1+x/NC and Zn1Fe1/NC is between 0 and +2. The FT-EXAFS spectra of Zn K-edge demonstrates that Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC and Zn1Fe1/NC only exist Zn-N bonds and while no Zn-Zn or Zn-O bonds could be found (Fig. 2e), indicating the atomically dispersed Zn on the NC support. In the wavelet transform (WT) analysis (Fig. 2f), Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC and Zn1Fe1/NC have the highest intensity scattering path at about 4.5 Å, which can be inferred that the Fe atoms of Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC and Zn1Fe1/NC are typical Fe-N coordination. In addition, Fe-Fe scattering paths were observed at about 5.5 Å for Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC, indicating that the material contains Fe clusters. In the WT analysis of Zn (Figure S23), there is only one intensity maximum in both samples, proving that Zn is atomically dispersed in both Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC and Zn1Fe1/NC. Figure 2g, h and Table S3 and S4 shows the best fitting curves for Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC, the fitting result of Zn1Fe1/NC sample revealed that the Fe-N4 with Fe-N bond length of 1.85 Å is the main structural features. The Zn is coordinated with four nitrogen atoms with Zn-N bond length of 1.88 Å for both Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC and Zn1Fe1/NC samples (Table S4)42,43. Figure 2i shows the XANES spectra of the C K- edge in Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC and Zn1Fe1/NC. The p* and s* intensities of Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC are lower than those of Zn1Fe1/NC, which is due to the fact that the curved structure of the nano-fingerprint carbon layer destroys the growth order of sp2 carbon. Although the Raman spectra of Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC (ID/IG = 1.04) indicate a high defect density, the low s* intensity indicates that the defect type is edge-defect rather than sp3 hybrid carbon. This is consistent with the phenomenon that most of the curved carbon layers appear at the edges of the support as shown in HRTEM. Therefore, the curved structure of the nano-fingerprint carbon layer leads to uneven electron distribution of the carbon matrix, which weakens the p* and s* peak intensities of Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC44,45. The three peaks at 286.1 eV (peak a), 289.2 eV (peak b1), and 293.1 eV (peak c) are assigned to p*C = C, p*C-N-M, and s*C-C antibonding states in both Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC and Zn1Fe1/NC. The peak at 287.5 eV (peak b2) represents the emergence of a new p*C-N-M type, which may be due to the distinct interaction between the Fex clusters and the support differed to single atom metal species.

Catalytic activity testing and decoupling experiments

Electrocatalytic ORR activities of the Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC and comparison samples were assessed by the linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) curves in 0.1 M KOH solution, as shown in Fig. 3a and Figure S24. The activity of Zn1/NC was the lowest, and while the Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC showed the highest ORR activity, with the onset potential (Eonset) up to 1.05 V and the half-wave potential (E1/2) is 0.93 V. The Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC is also higher than catalysts obtained by pyrolysis of Fe(acac)3@ZIF-8 at 800 °C (Eonset = 0.93 V, E1/2 = 0.83 V) and 900 °C (Eonset = 0.95 V, E1/2 = 0.88 V) (Figure S25). To determine the optimal loading amount of the Fe precursor, we also investigated the ORR performance under different Fe contents before obtaining the Zn1Fe1+x/ f-NC. As shown in Figure S26a, when the precursor loading was 1.5 mmol (the sample of Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC), the half-wave potential is the highest (E1/2 = 0.93 V), higher than at 0.5 mmol (E1/2 = 0.88 V), 1.0 mmol (E1/2 = 0.90 V) and 2.0 mmol (E1/2 = 0.92 V). Figures S26b–d show that the Tafel slope, kinetic current densities and fitted double-layer capacitance (Cdl) increase with Fe precursor concentration up to 1.5 mmol but decrease at 2.0 mmol (Figures S27), consistent with Fe cluster aggregation into zero-valent nanoparticles and a reduced effective active area. The XPS results (Figure S28a) shown that the relative proportion of Fe2+ gradually increased while that of Fe3+ decreased as the Fe precursor loading increased from 0.5 mmol to 2.0 mmol (Table S5). A small zero-valent Fe peak (706.2 eV) of Fe-NC can be seen in the Fe 2p spectrum when the Fe precursor reaching 2.0 mmol, and Fe nanoparticles (diameter exceeding 2 nm) can also be observed (Figure S28b), which means the Fe metal possibly have a large volume and break through the definition of clusters46.

Figure 3b shows the Tafel slopes of these catalysts, which can be used to evaluate the ORR kinetics for these electrocatalysts. Compared with other samples, Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC has a smaller Tafel slope (46.51 mV dec−1), which can prove the Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC has higher ORR kinetic character. At 0.93 V, the kinetic current (jk) of Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC reaches 5.8 mA/cm−2, which was 4.8 times that of commercial Pt/C (Fig. 3c), and the jk of Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC is as high as 43.7 mA/cm−2 at 0.90 V, which was 16.2 times that of commercial Pt/C. From Figure S29 and S30, it can be found that electrochemical double-layer Cdl of Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC is 32.3 mF cm−2, which is larger than other contrast samples, indicating that Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC has a higher electrochemically active area. According to the slope of Koutecky-Levich (K-L) diagram (Fig. 3d), it can be known that the ORR process of Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC followed a 4-electron pathway (Fig. 3d and Figure S31). Meanwhile, the H2O2 selectivity is maintained at a low level within full potential range. In addition, we also evaluated the catalytic performance of the Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC catalyst by using a flowing reactor (configuration was shown in Figure S32). Figure S33 shows the flow-cell test results, indicating that Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC does not produce H2O2 at potentials above 0.4 V and generates only trace amounts H2O2 at potential below 0.3 V. Therefore, under typical device operating potentials (which are generally higher), the Fenton reaction which causes iron dissolution and device destabilization is almost unlikely to occur. The electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy was used to monitor the in-situ-generated OH* during ORR in real time at the gas-diffusion-electrode level for Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC, employing 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline N-oxide (DMPO) as the OH* scavenger (quasi-in-situ testing). As shown in Figure S34, compared with the curve without applied potential, the test results at 0.4−0.9 V are approximately linear, indicating that the OH* radical is not detected. Weak free-radical signals appear at 0.3 V and 0.2 V, but the OH* signal curve cannot be reliably fitted, indicating that the amount of OH* generated is small. The resistance of Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC to methanol poisoning was tested (Figure S35). After injecting methanol into the electrolyte (0.1 M KOH and 0.5 M CH3OH), the current of Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC changed slightly, while the current of commercial Pt/C fluctuated significantly. The LSV curve of Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC changes slightly before and after injection methanol, while the Pt/C catalyst generates a positive current peak after the addition of methanol, indicating that Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC has high resistance to methanol poisoning. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy was evaluated to reveal the electron transfer characteristics of these electrocatalysts, and the results showed that Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC had the smallest electrochemical transfer resistance (Figure S36).

To explore the main roles of single-atom sites, Fex clusters and nano-fingerprint carbon layers in the composite catalysts, decoupling experiments were performed in depth. We prepared a total of four decoupled samples, including Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC, Zn1Fe1+x/NC, Zn1Fe1/f-NC and Zn1Fe1/NC, where “M1”, “Mx”, and “f” represent “single-atom site”, “clusters”, and “fingerprint carbon layers”, respectively. Firstly, we analyzed the appearance and species characteristics of the decoupled samples. As shown in Figure S37, Zn1Fe1/f-NC exhibits nano-fingerprint carbon layers in the absence of Fe clusters, whereas Zn1Fe1+x/NC possesses Fe clusters but lacks fingerprint carbon structures. Furthermore, magnetic hysteresis loops of catalysts at room temperature (25 °C) in Figure S38 provide additional validation, Zn1Fe1+x/NC exhibits clear S-shaped hysteresis and non-zero remanent, indicating the presence of magnetic Fe clusters. Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC shows a similar, but much weaker non-linear response, aroused by a smaller amount or smaller-size clusters. By contrast, Zn1Fe1/f-NC and Zn1Fe1/NC display near-linear M-H behavior with negligible coercivity and remanence, which is aroused by predominantly atomically dispersed metal sites. As shown in Figure S39a, the BET surface area of the Zn1Fe1/f-NC is 976.5 m2 g−1, comparable to the surface Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC, indicating that acid washing does not significantly affect the catalyst structure. Figure S39b shows the Raman results for Zn1Fe1/f-NC, with an ID/IG value of 1.05 compared to with 1.04 for Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC and this means that the degree of graphitization does not change significantly. After characterizing and confirming the species characteristics of the decoupled samples, the next step is to decouple the activity and stability attribution of its species. LSV curves indicated that compared with Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC, the half-wave potential of Zn1Fe1/f-NC decreased by 26 mV (E1/2 = 0.904 V, Figs. 3a and e). The E1/2 of Zn1Fe1+x/NC is 0.925 V, which is close to Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC. This result indicates that the activity is significantly impaired upon removal of Fe clusters. As shown in Fig. 3g, a comparison of the residual activity after 50 h i-t tests for Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC (94%), Zn1Fe1/f-NC (93%), Zn1Fe1+x/NC (89%), and Zn1Fe1/NC (87%) reveals that the nano-fingerprint carbon layers are mainly enhancing the catalyst stability. Specifically, Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC manifests superior stability with negligible activity decay following 10,000 continuous potential cycles. HAADF-STEM analysis after the durability tests of Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC (Figure S40a) and Zn1Fe1/f-NC (Figure S40b) shows that within regions protected by fingerprint carbon layer, single-atom sites maintain better dispersion, and the clusters do not have obvious aggregation. Whereas the single-atom sites in Zn1Fe1/NC (Figure S40c) show partial agglomeration after long-term stability testing. This indicates that the fingerprint carbon layer can prevent the large-scale aggregation of Zn/Fe active sites surrounding Fe clusters. In summary, the decoupling experiments indicate that Fe clusters primarily promote activity, while the coiled, nano-fingerprint carbon layer mainly contributes to enhance stability (Fig. 3e). The ORR activity of Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC in 0.1 M KOH is better than the other contrast samples and most non-noble metal electrocatalysts reported in the literatures (Fig. 3f and Table S6). As shown in the Fig. 3h, it can obtain gram-scale catalyst yields in a single batch. Simultaneously, we tested the ORR performance of this batch of samples and found that its performance remains reliable and reproducible (Fig. 3h, E1/2 = 0.93 V).

In view of the competitive ORR activity of Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC in 0.1 M KOH, we further studied its ORR activity in the acidic electrolyte of 0.1 M HClO4. As shown in Figure S41, Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC (Eonset = 0.91 V, E1/2 = 0.81 V) delivers an activity slightly inferior to that of commercial Pt/C (Eonset of 0.99 V, E1/2 = 0.86 V), while is much higher than the Zn1Fe1/NC (Eonset = 0.82 V, E1/2 = 0.67 V) and Zn1/NC (Eonset = 0.63 V, E1/2 = 0.43 V). It is worth noting that, as shown in Figure S42, the Tafel slope of Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC (61.42 mV dec−1) is lower than Pt/C (76.77 mV dec−1), which indicated Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC has faster ORR kinetics. The Cdl value of Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC is also the highest among all comparison samples (Figure S43 and S44), which means that it has the largest electrochemical active surface area. Rotating disk electrode (RDE) test result shows that the electron transfer number of Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC is 3.85, which is close to the theoretical value of the 4-electron transfer ORR pathway (Figure S45). In addition, Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC also shows high stability in acidic electrolytes with a potential decay of ~33 mV after 10,000 repeated cycles (Figure S46), and high tolerance to methanol in acidic electrolyte (Figure S47). In summary, Pt/C is the state-of-the-art catalyst for ORR in acidic electrolytes own to its high dissociation efficiency, low adsorption energy barriers, and favorable mass-transfer characteristics (Figure S48 and S49). Nevertheless, the ORR activity of Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC in 0.1 M HClO4 is comparatively high among reported acidic non-precious-metal catalysts (Table S7).

DFT calculation

To clarify the distinct roles of carbon support curvature and co-catalytic effects in ORR performance, we conducted systematic DFT calculations on FeN4 sites embedded in carbon substrates with varying curvatures: graphene, (10,10)-CNT, (6,6)-CNT, and C60. The ORR mechanism involves sequential adsorption of *OOH, *O, and OH intermediates, modeled as the thermodynamic rate-determining step under our DFT conditions (1.23 V). According to the Sabatier principle, excessive adsorption of intermediates, particularly *OH, can hinder desorption and reduce activity. Systematic calculations reveal that both curvature and co-catalytic effects significantly enhance ORR activity. Increasing the curvature of the carbon support substantially reduces the *OH desorption energy, from 1.40 eV in planar Fe1/NC to 0.80 eV in Fe1/(10,10)-CNT and further to 0.66 eV in Fe1/(6,6)-CNT (Fig. 4a), this trend is consistent across other models (Fe1-Zn1/NC and Fe1-Zn1/(10,10)-CNT) (Figure S50–S53), consistent with the interpretation that curvature-induced strain modifies the electronic structure of the Fe active site47. Integrated crystal orbital Hamilton populations (iCOHP) value below Fermi level for Fe-O bonds in Fe1/NC, Fe1/(10,10)-CNT and Fe1/(6,6)-CNT were −0.094, −0.081 and −0.062, respectively. The integration of -COHP from the occupied states till Fermi level quantifies the total bonding contribution. A larger integrated value corresponds to stronger overall bonding interaction, which is directly correlated with Fe-O bonding strength (Figure S54)48,49. Interestingly, when we investigated the correlation between *OH desorption energy and polarization parameters of Fe-site in Fe1/NC, Fe1/(10,10)-CNT and Fe1/(6,6)-CNT, a strong negative correlation emerges between the Fe-site total polarization (total spin-up electron minus total spin-down electron) and OH* desorption energy (with a Pearson correlation coefficient of −0.988) (Figure S55)). As substrate curvature increases, both polarization and adsorption energy decrease synchronously. This suggests that curvature-induced strain reduces the spin polarization of Fe centers, promoting d-electron delocalization and thereby weakening Fe–OH bonding. However, this trend is not observed for the d-polarization parameters (total spin-up d orbital electron minus total spin-down d orbital electron), indicating that the polarization changes also involve non-d electrons (s and p orbitals). It should be noted that the statistical significance of this correlation is limited by the small sample size, but the directional consistency supports the mechanistic interpretation that reduced spin polarization facilitates OH* desorption in curved Fe-N₄ catalysts. Meanwhile, co-catalytic modifications also improve performance: introducing a Zn single atom (Fe1-Zn1/NC) lowers the *OH desorption energy to 0.89 eV, while adding an Fe cluster (Fe1-Zn1-Fe4/NC) further reduces the barrier to 0.85 eV, suggesting possible electronic modulation and synergy (Fig. 4b). Projected density of states (PDOS) analysis reveals that the d-band centers of Fe, particularly the spin-down dz2 and dxz orbitals (dz2-down and dxz-down), correlate strongly with the reaction energy barrier (Fig. 4d and S56–S59). A regression equation (rds-height = 1.009 − 0.118 × dz2-down − 0.107 × dxz-down, R2 = 0.9939) indicates that both curvature and co-catalysis may tune orbital energies to enhance overlap with adsorbate orbitals. Curvature introduces intermediate states near the Fermi level mainly from dxz-down, while Zn incorporation shifts both dz2 and dxz centers downward (Table S8, S9 and Figure S60). A regression equation (rds-height = 1.009 − 0.118 × dz2-down − 0.107 × dxz-down, R2 = 0.9939) indicates that both curvature and co-catalysis could tune orbital energies to enhance overlap with adsorbate orbitals. Electron density difference analysis further confirms that curvature and Zn incorporation (Figure S61) efficiently redistributes electron density around the Fe-N4 region (Fig. 4c)47,50,51. Hence, both carbon support curvature and co-catalytic sites optimize ORR performance by modulating the electronic structure of Fe active centers via dz2 and dxz orbital regulation52. Coupling nano-fingerprint carbon supports with dual single-atom sites and clusters may offer a promising design strategy53,54.

Zn-air batteries test

As shown in Figure S62, the practical application of the Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC material as ZAB cathode catalysts was tested compared with Pt/C and Zn1Fe1/NC. This ZAB based on Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC exhibits a peak density of 263.82 mW cm−2 (Fig. 5a), outperforming that of Pt/C-based ZAB (145.22 mW cm−2) and Zn1Fe1/NC-based ZAB (196.77 mW cm−2). In order to test the practical application value of Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC electrocatalyst, a rechargeable and dischargeable ZAB was prepared by using Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC to form a composite catalyst, and a long-term durability test was carried out. The overall test uses constant current charge and discharge cycles, and each cycle lasts for 60 min (30 min charging and 30 min discharging). The ZAB cycle test demonstrated robust durability of over 2200 h under 10 mA cm−2 (Fig. 5b). The stability of Pt/C + RuO2 significantly declined after 200 h and Zn1Fe1/NC significantly declined after 100 h (Fig. 5c and Figure S63). Figure 5d and Table S10 and S11 show the competitive long-term stability compared to other reported electrocatalysts55–64. The charge and discharge voltages of the Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC-assembled ZAB after 50 h of continuous operation were 1.93 V and 1.16 V, which have a high round-trip efficiency. After 1000 h of continuous operation, the charge and discharge voltages were 1.94 V and 1.15 V, with a voltage difference of 0.79 V. Even after 2200 h of testing, the charge and discharge voltage difference remain almost unchanged from the value before 2200 h of testing (insert Fig. 5b, the charge and discharge voltages are 1.95 V and 1.14 V, and the voltage difference is 0.81 V). We further characterized the air cathode catalyst after the long life cycling with HAADF-STEM. As evident from Figure S64, not only the nano-fingerprint carbon structure remains clearly defined, but also the numerous atomically dispersed single atom active sites onto clusters are also clearly visible. However, due to the prolonged stability testing, a slight aggregation of some atomic sites forming particles ~2 nm in size. This is also the reason for the minor decline in stability and activity after extended cycling. The test results show that Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC catalyst not only has high stability in the RDE half-cell test, but also has super strong stable cycle characteristics in practical applications. We also assembled button cells and flexible solid-state ZABs. As shown in Fig. 5e, f, the resulting button cell ZAB could run stably for 1000 min. In addition, compared with quasi-solid-state battery shows good flexibility and stability. As shown in Fig. 5g, whether it’s flexible or flat, the discharge-charge curve hardly changes, indicating its high anti-deformation performance. Further research into the potential improvements for current rechargeable ZABs devices was conducted. Using button cells as a model, we disassembled batteries before and after charge-discharge cycling and observed that the electrolyte in the separator had almost evaporated after cycling, accompanied by the formation of significant precipitate on both the separator and the zinc electrode (Figure S65). Based on these findings, we postulate that in the future commercialization of the rechargeable ZABs, the development of air cathode catalysts should be complemented by the creation of highly efficient and durable electrolytes. This latter aspect represents a significant challenge and is a primary focus of ongoing research efforts65,66.

Discussion

In summary, by pyrolysis of Fe(acac)3 oversaturated adsorption on ZIF-8 at 1000 °C, a composite site catalyst was generated with nano-fingerprint carbon layer encapsulating Fe clusters, single-atom Zn and single-atom Fe. The composite catalyst exhibits competitive ORR activity and stability in alkaline electrolytes, which performs better than dual single-atom Zn&Fe on nitrogen-doped carbon and commercial Pt/C catalysts. Comprehensive experimental and theoretical studies have confirmed that the introduction of single-atom Zn site and Fe cluster weaken the adsorption of OH* intermediates, and the nano-fingerprint carbon layers structure enhances the intrinsic activity and stability of the Fe-N4 site by adjusting the electronic structure of the Fe centers. The composite catalyst was further evaluated as the cathode by assembling Zn-air batteries, which achieve a much higher power density, energy density and more than 2200 h stability compared to Pt/C. The jointly optimization of Zn/Fe single-atom, Fex clusters and nano-fingerprint carbon layer has been demonstrated to make the ORR activity of the composite catalyst in alkaline reach a top performance. Therefore, multivariate modulation not only provides direction for the design of high-performance ORR catalysts but also holds great promise for practical applications in energy conversion devices.

Methods

Materials

Zinc nitrate hexahydrate, sulfuric acid (96% ~ 98%), perchloric acid (70% ~ 72%), anhydrous methanol, sodium hydroxide, and anhydrous ethanol were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). DMPO, 2-methylimidazole, and iron acetylacetonate were purchased from Aladdin Co. Ltd. Pt/C (20 wt%) were supplied from Innochem Technology (Beijing, China). Nafion solution (5 wt%) were supplied from Alfa Aesar. Nitrogen and Oxygen ( ≥ 99.999%) was purchased from Liyuan Gas. The water used in all experiments was ultrapure water (18.25 MΩ.cm−1). All reagents were used without further purification.

Catalyst synthesis

Synthesis of ZIF-8

In a typical synthesis, 11.9 g of Zn(NO)3.6H2O were dissolved in 300 mL of methanol to form a clear solution. It was subsequently introduced into 300 mL of containing 13.14 g of 2-methylimidazole under vigorous stirring for 12 h at room temperature (25 °C). The as-obtained precipitates were centrifuged, washed with ethanol, and dried in vacuum at 50 °C.

Synthesis of Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC

In a typical synthesis, 800 mg of as-synthesized ZIF-8 and 1.5 mmol of iron acetylacetonate were dispersed in 60 ml of ethanol under vigorous stirring for 24 h at 60 °C in oil bath. Subsequently the mixture was centrifugal separation of suspension and solid before being ground to give a fine powder (Fe(acac)3@ZIF-8). Finally, the product Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC was thermally treated at 1000 °C under an argon atmosphere for 2 h with ramping rate of 3 °C min −1. The Fe loadings in the catalyst were determined to be 0.90 wt %.

Synthesis of Zn1Fe1/NC

The synthetic method of Zn1Fe1/NC was similar to that of Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC, except mixture was washed with ethanol for 3 times before being ground to give a fine powder (Fe(acac)3-ZIF-8). The Fe loading in the catalyst was measured to be 0.14 wt %.

Synthesis of Zn1/NC

The synthetic method of Zn1/NC was similar to that of Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC, except ZIF-8 was used without the use of iron acetylacetonate.

Synthesis of Zn1Fe1/f-NC

Zn1Fe1/f-NC was prepared by treating 400 mg of Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC with dilute H2SO4 under stirring for 24 h to remove the Fe clusters. The product was then collected by centrifugation, washed thoroughly with deionized water until the supernatant reached neutral pH, and finally dried in a vacuum oven at 60 °C.

Synthesis of Zn1Fe1+x/NC

Zn1Fe1+x/NC was prepared by loading Zn1Fe1/NC with Fe(acac)3 again, specifically by loading 400 mg of Zn1Fe1/NC with 1.5 mmol of iron acetylacetonate dispersed in 60 ml of ethanol under vigorous stirring for 24 h at 60 °C in oil bath. Subsequently the mixture was centrifugal separation of suspension and solid before being ground to give a fine powder (Fe(acac)3@Zn1Fe1/NC), Finally, the product Zn1Fe1+x/NC was thermally treated again at 1000 °C under an argon atmosphere for 2 h with ramping rate of 3 °C min −1.

Electrochemical measurements

Electrochemical ORR measurements were implemented with a CHI 760E electrochemical workstation (CH Instruments, Inc., Shanghai) connected to rotation-control equipment (Pine Instrument Company, USA) with a standard three-electrode system in 0.1 M KOH and 0.1 M HClO4. Rotating-disk electrode (RDE, d = 5 mm, the geometric surface area of the RDE electrode is 0.196 cm−2, Pine Instrument Company, USA) or rotating-disk electrode (RRDE, 5.61 mm in disk outer diameter, the geometric surface area of the RDE electrode is 0.247 cm−2, Pine Instrument Company, USA) was used as a working electrode. Ag/AgCl electrode and graphite rod were applied as reference electrode and counter electrode, respectively. All potentials were referenced to a reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE). All electrochemical measurements were conducted in a thermostated water bath at 25 °C.

10 mg of electrocatalyst was mixed with 500 μL of ethanol, 470 μL ultrapure water, and 30 μL of Nafion solution (5 wt%) by ultrasonication to form a homogeneous catalyst ink. An aliquot of 10 μL of the as-obtained electrocatalyst ink was pipetted onto the working electrode surface with a mass loading of 0.51 mg cm−2. The 0.1 M KOH electrolyte used for electrochemical measurements was freshly prepared by dissolving 2.81 g of KOH in 500 mL of deionized water, and the solution was maintained at 25 °C under dry, ventilated conditions prior to use. The actual pH of the 0.1 M KOH electrolyte was measured to be 12.90 ± 0.15. The five-port electrolysis cell has a volume of 150 mL. ~100 mL of 0.1 M KOH is used for each test. The 0.1 M HClO4 solution used for electrochemical tests was prepared by dissolving 4.25 mL of concentrated perchloric acid in 500 mL of deionized water, the actual pH was 1.1 ± 0.15. ~100 mL of solution was used per test. The electrolyte was purged by a specific gas for at least 30 min before the measurements and the gas flow was maintained during the experiments. Before the ORR measurements, cyclic voltammograms were performed at 50 mV s−1 for at least 20 cycles to clean and stabilized the electrocatalyst surface. LSV measurements were recorded at a scan rate of 10 mV s−1. The double-layer capacitance (Cdl) of electrocatalyst was derived from CV curves in the non-Faradic potential region with different scan rates from 5 to 30 mV s−1. Electrochemical impedance spectroscope (EIS) measurements were conducted in the frequency range from 106 Hz to 1 Hz with a voltage amplitude of −3 mV. The solution resistance (Rsol) was obtained by measuring EIS at the open-circuit potential in the electrolyte (Rsol = 35 ± 1.5 Ω in 0.1 M KOH and Rsol = 16 ± 1.5 Ω in 0.1 M HClO4). The polarization curves of all studied catalysts (without iR-corrected) are shown in Figures S66 and S67.

The selectivity to methanol solution (0.5 M L−1) was conducted at 0.816 V vs RHE by chronoamperometry. The selectivity of electrocatalyst during ORR was calculated by measuring LSV curves on RDE with different rotating speeds (400, 900, 1225, 1600, and 2025 rpm). Bulk electrolysis in flow cell

The cathode catalyst slurries were prepared by sonicating 20 mg catalyst, 1 mL ethanol, 1 mL H2O and 60 μL 5 wt% Nafion suspension in a bath sonicator for at least an hour. The ink was air-brushed onto air-brushed onto a carbon paper until the cathode catalyst loading reached 0.5 mg cm−2.

EPR measurements

The catalyst spray-coated onto carbon paper (prepared in the flow-cell tests) was used as the cathode in the flow cell for EPR measurements to quantify OH* generated during the ORR. The catholyte (30 mL), containing 0.1 M KOH and 0.42 M DMPO, was continuously supplied to the cathode liquid channel at 10 mL min⁻¹ using a peristaltic pump. O₂ was fed to the gas chamber at 30 mL min⁻¹. The anode was a commercial Pt foil (0.1 mm thick, 20 mm × 20 mm), and 0.1 M KOH was supplied to the anode at the same flow rate. Tests were conducted at room temperature (25 °C). The flow cell was operated under i-t conditions over the range 0.2 - 0.9 V, with each potential held for 10 min. Immediately after each i-t measurement, a 10 μL aliquot was taken from the catholyte reservoir; the sampling and analysis was completed within 3 min.

Zinc-Air Battery assembly and test

The home-made zinc-air battery was manufactured. A 0.2 mm thick zinc foil was pre-polished with sandpaper as the anode. Catalysts were loaded on carbon fiber paper (1 cm2) as the air cathode. The electrochemical cell was filled with 6.0 M KOH and 0.2 M zinc acetate solution as the electrolyte. The Zn1Fe1+x/f-NC catalyst loading is 0.4 mg cm−2, and the Zn1Fe1/NC and commercial catalysts’ loading are the same. Button-cell ZABs were assembled with a positive electrode, carbon paper (diameter: 16 mm) loaded with catalyst ink, electrolyte, zinc plate, spring plate, and negative electrode. The catalyst loading is 0.4 mg cm−2. The flexible solid-state ZABs were assembled with a zinc plate, modified PVA hydrogel, and carbon cloth loaded with catalyst ink (catalyst loading: 0.4 mg cm−2). The measurements of ZABs (including home-made ZABs, Button-cell ZABs, and flexible solid-state ZABs) were performed at room temperature with a battery test workstation (Neware battery testing system). All the tests were under the air environment (the climate temperature ranged from 20 °C to 30 °C).

Theoretical Calculations

Spin-polarized density functional theory calculations were carried out via the Quantum Espresso suit67–71 in the framework of GGA-PBEsol72. PBEsol functionals has been developed as a modification of the widely used PBE73 functional to improve lattice constants and surface energies. SSSP pseudopotential libraries (precision version)74 was selected as suggested by N. Marzari et al.75. Kinetic energy and charge density cutoffs cutoff of 30 Ry and 360 Ry with a Marzari–Vanderbilt cold smearing76 width of 0.002 eV were selected in the geometrical optimization. The van der Waals effects were considered by using DFT-D3 scheme77. According to experimental data, models except for Fe1/f-NC were constructed based on 2D graphene slab models. To prevent erroneous interactions between neighboring periodic images, a vacuum layer of 20 Å along the z-axis is added. The periodic cell is 17.089 × 17.265 × 20.000 Å3. Monkhorst–Pack k-point grids were 2 × 2 × 1 in geometrical optimization and self-consistent field (SCF) calculations for flat models. Isolated C60 in a periodic cell (15.000 × 15.000 × 15.000 Å3) was selected as one bent carbon model. It seems that the curvature varies too drastically from flat carbon (graphene model) to the bent carbon (C60 model) to investigate the general function of carrier materials. Hence, we added two substrate models (single wall carbon nanotube with different curvatures) among them to reveal the general role of carrier materials from flat to bent. Herein, (6,6)-CNT and (10,10)-CNT were selected, while similar construction proposal was adopted as in graphene model to embed FeN4 moiety on them. The periodic cell is 31.483 × 31.483 × 14.757 Å3 and 36.907 × 36.709 × 14.757 Å3 for (6,6)-CNT and (10,10)-CNT based models, respectively. Monkhorst–Pack k-point grids were 1 × 1 × 2 in geometrical optimization and self-consistent field (SCF) calculations for CNT based models. Geometry optimization was performed until the maximum residual force was 0.03 eV/Å. The convergence requirements were chosen at 1 × 10−7 eV atom−1 for SCF iterations. For non-self-consistent field (NSCF) calculations k-point grids was 3 × 3 × 1 and 1 × 1 × 3. Considering the delocalization of Fe-3d and Zn-3d states, DFT + U approach78 was employed to overcome its self-interaction error79. U3d values of 3.29 and 4.12 eV for Fe and Zn80 were adopted, respectively. The Gibbs energies were evaluated by including zero-point energy correction and entropy contribution terms in the framework of computational hydrogen electrode approach developed by Norskov and co-works81,82: ΔG = ΔH + ΔEZPE – TΔS where ΔH is obtained from calculated DFT total energies, ΔEZPE is the zero point energy correction which is evaluated in a harmonic fashion of intermediates. The entropic contribution term is the change of entropy due to the reaction (Supplementary Data 1).
