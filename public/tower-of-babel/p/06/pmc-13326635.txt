De novo direct sequencing of small therapeutic RNAs by layer-by-layer intensity-resolved mass spectrometry
Authors: Shangsi Lin, Sophia Jiang, Lin Tang, Sateesh Kumar Kumbhakonam, Justin C Dingman, Jung Yeon Lee, Ruixin Yang, Tony Frudakis, Michele Kirchner, Sihang Xu, Chuanjuan Tao, Xuanting Wang, James J Russo, Xudong Zhang, Qi Chen, Shenglong Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe rapid growth of RNA-based therapeutics demands accurate sequencing of all RNA species, including minor and modified variants. Conventional LC-MS/MS typically confirms only a predefined target sequence rather than determining RNA sequences de novo from the analyzed sample, thereby overlooking coexisting impurities and modifications. Here, we present 3D NGMS-Seq, a three-dimensional next-generation mass spectrometry-based sequencing platform for de novo direct sequencing of mixed RNA samples with essentially 100% sequence accuracy. This method incorporates MS intensity into traditional 2D mass–retention time (tR) analysis and introduces a nested algorithm that aligns ladder fragment intensities with parent RNA abundances for computational separation. Controlled acid hydrolysis produces RNA ladder fragments, which are segregated into mass-intensity-tR layers. Within each layer, short reads are generated de novo by sequentially base-calling each nucleotide, canonical or modified, from mass differences between adjacent ladder fragments and subsequently assembled into full-length RNA sequences. Guided by hydrolysis kinetics and statistical modeling, 3D NGMS-Seq accurately sequences synthetic siRNA, miRNA, and CRISPR/Cas9 sgRNAs, reveals unexpected low-abundance RNA impurities, and resolves subtle methylation ambiguities (Um versus mU; Am versus mA), while providing a quantitative profile of each RNA’s relative abundance and site-specific modifications. By enabling direct, unbiased sequencing of heterogeneous RNAs without prior sequence knowledge, 3D NGMS-Seq addresses key limitations of current RNA analysis and provides a powerful tool to aid small RNA drug development, quality control, and regulatory validation.

Introduction

The rising prevalence of RNA-based drugs and vaccines highlights the imperative need for comprehensive detection and sequencing of all RNA species, including modified variants and low-abundance impurities [1–4]. Just as impurities in small-molecule drugs can pose serious safety risks, unidentified RNA impurities or modifications in therapeutic RNA reagents may compromise clinical outcomes and lead to misinterpreted findings [5]. Therefore, it is essential to have the capability to comprehensively sequence mixed RNA samples, as therapeutic RNA reagents rarely achieve 100% purity, e.g. due to limitations in current phosphoramidite synthesis and subsequent purification. In fact, achieving a purity level exceeding 98.5% for RNA drugs, as required by U.S. regulatory standards for small-molecule drugs [6], remains a substantial challenge. Inefficiencies in RNA synthesis result in samples containing a variety of RNA molecules of different lengths, particularly those longer than 20 nucleotides (nt), highlighting the critical need for direct, holistic RNA sequencing approaches that can sequence both target RNAs and coexisting impurities.

Furthermore, therapeutic RNAs often contain chemical modifications that complicate their detection and sequencing. These modifications are crucial for enhancing the stability, efficacy, and specificity of RNA vaccines and therapeutics, which are increasingly used in disease prevention and treatment [7]. Numerous biopharma companies are currently developing a wide range of RNA-based therapeutics and vaccines, including miRNA and siRNA-based therapeutics, mRNA vaccines [8], and CRISPR-based gene editing immunotherapies [9]. Single guide RNA (sgRNA) in the CRISPR/Cas9 system, for instance, is essential for guiding Cas9 nuclease to recognize specific DNA sequences before gene editing takes place. sgRNA usually consists of ~100 nucleotides and often includes modifications to improve stability and editing efficiency. Due to inefficiencies in RNA synthesis, sgRNA samples typically contain multiple RNA molecules, each with unique sequences or modifications, demanding precise sequencing for both patient safety and regulatory compliance.

Despite its wide applications, next-generation sequencing (NGS)-based RNA sequencing, reliant on cDNA synthesis and either ignoring RNA modifications altogether or selectively focusing on certain RNA modifications, falls short in providing a comprehensive sequence and modification profile. Traditional mass spectrometry (MS) techniques such as liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS), the gold standard for RNA modification analysis, promise unbiased analysis and have been used to verify therapeutic RNA sequences of interest [10–12]. However, these methods typically confirm the presence of a predefined target RNA sequence rather than determining it de novo from the analyzed sample. Sequence information is inferred from prior knowledge rather than read directly from mass spectrometric data [13]. As a result, they do not provide clarity on the sample’s purity or identify coexisting RNA impurities [1, 14]. Moreover, the complexity of MS/MS spectra makes it too difficult to determine an RNA sequence de novo and typically limits its application to analysis of relatively short RNA oligonucleotides [15]. For RNAs longer than 20 nt, these methods require additional enzymatic fragmentation into short segments (∼17 nt), followed by meticulous LC separation for MS/MS analysis. This bottom-up strategy complicates analysis and reduces throughput.

To address these challenges, we have created a next-generation mass spectrometry-based sequencing (NGMS-Seq) platform, a top-down MS method for de novo sequencing of full-length RNAs and their modifications [16–18]. Unlike LC-MS/MS, NGMS-Seq employs controlled formic acid hydrolysis to produce 5′ and 3′ ladder fragments without nuclease or MS/MS fragmentation. Each RNA molecule is cleaved ideally once, yielding two ladder pools—one retaining the original 5′ end and the other the 3′ end [19]. When measured by LC-MS, each fragment forms a rung of the sequencing ladder. The mass difference between successive ladder fragments reflects the exact mass of the RNA nucleotide at that position, modified or not. To distinguish ladder fragments from spurious MS signals, a second “dimension,” the retention time (tR) of each RNA fragment measured in the liquid chromatography step of LC-MS, is included in data analysis. Lower mass RNA fragments typically elute from the LC column earlier (shorter tR), while higher mass RNA fragments elute later (longer tR), forming a characteristic sigmoidal curve in a 2D tR versus mass plot. The 2D NGMS-Seq enables direct sequencing of cellular tRNAs (∼80 nt) and site-specific quantification of the stoichiometry of partial nucleotide modifications without bias [20]. While the 2D method has been used in de novo MS sequencing of highly purified short synthetic RNAs (∼20 nt) [19], it struggles with complex samples, requiring an additional hydrophobic end labeling step (HELS) for mixed RNA samples [16], complicating the sequencing process.

Here, we present 3D NGMS-Seq, a fast, direct method that eliminates HELS and enables complete de novo sequencing of every RNA nucleotide and modification in mixed RNA samples (Fig. 1). We found that the MS intensity ranking of 5′ or 3′ ladder fragments with identical length generally reflects the abundance of their parent RNAs in the pre-hydrolysis sample, enabling computational separation of distinct RNA populations. By adding a third dimension—MS intensity—recorded simultaneously with mass and tR for each hydrolysis-produced fragment during LC-MS measurement, base-calling accuracy is improved by prioritizing high-intensity ladder fragments, transforming 2D sequencing into a more powerful 3D mass-tR-intensity platform for sequencing heterogeneous RNA samples. To automatically interpret complex LC-MS data, we developed a nested algorithm that segregates hydrolyzed RNA ladder fragments into layers by intensity, as well as by mass and tR, enabling stepwise sequencing from the most to the least abundant RNA species. Within each layer, short sequences are generated de novo by sequentially base-calling nucleotides, modified or not, from mass differences between adjacent ladder fragments, and then assembled into full-length RNA sequences. This approach uses mathematical modeling to simulate RNA hydrolysis kinetics in silico, allowing the quantification of diverse hydrolyzed RNA fragments—including even internal fragments that lack original 5′ and 3′ ends and complicate data analysis (e.g. see Fig. 2) [16]. Together, 3D NGMS-Seq can accurately sequence full-length therapeutic RNAs of interest, resolve unexpected low-abundance RNA impurities, and distinguish subtle methylation ambiguities (Um versus mU; Am versus mA), providing a comprehensive quantitative profile of each RNA’s relative abundance and site-specific modifications. This method addresses a critical gap in current RNA drug analysis, with the potential to become an industry standard for rapid, accurate sequencing of small RNAs. It thus holds the promise to serve as a reliable quality control and regulatory framework for verification of synthetic therapeutic RNA reagents to ensure patient safety.

Materials and methods

Reagents

LC-MS grade methanol, triethylamine (TEA), diisopropylamine (DIPA), 1,1,1,3,3,3-hexafluoro-2-propanol (HFIP), and analytical grade formic acid were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA). HPLC-grade water was purchased from Sigma–Aldrich (Burlington, MA, USA). Synthetic RNAs (~10 nmol) were purchased from Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT) (Coralville, Iowa, USA).

Control runs and optimization

To optimize data quality, three LC-MS runs are performed. Control Run 1 measures 5% of the intact sample without acid hydrolysis. Control Run 2 analyzes 5% of the sample after acid hydrolysis for 5 min at 40°C. The remaining 90% of the sample was used for the third run to ensure strong fragment signal intensities.

For Control Run 1 (Intact Run), synthetic RNAs (1–5 pmol) were vortexed in RNase-free water and transferred to 0.2 ml Eppendorf tubes for LC-MS measurement. For Control Run 2 (Standard Hydrolysis Run), the hydrolysis reaction was performed for 5 min at either 40°C or 70°C prior to LC-MS measurement. Preliminary data from these runs, combined with in silico simulation based on RNA hydrolysis kinetics, guided the optimization of acid hydrolysis conditions to maximize RNA ladder fragments while minimizing undesired internal fragments.

The optimal hydrolysis time was determined using the average hydrolysis ratio per site, rav, calculated using Equation (5). The theoretical optimal rav for a ladder fragment with length k is 1/k. Since rav is directly proportional to hydrolysis time, a small rav (<0.05) enables proportional estimation of the optimal hydrolysis duration.

Controlled acid hydrolysis

Synthetic RNAs (5–100 pmol) were vortexed with 10 µl of RNase-free water and transferred to 0.5 ml Eppendorf tubes. The RNA mixture was transferred to a thermal cycler and heated at 70°C for 15 min for denaturation. Subsequently, 10 µl of formic acid solution was combined with the RNA mixture before it was transferred back to the thermal cycler and then kept at either 40°C if using 50% formic acid solution or 70°C if using 1% formic acid solution for a range of 5 to 20 min before stopping the reaction on dry ice. The sample was then dried down using an Eppendorf Vacufuge Plus instrument (Eppendorf, Enfield, CT, US) to remove formic acid. Finally, the dried sample was dissolved in 20 µl of RNase-free water in preparation for LC-MS.

LC-MS measurement

A DNAPac RP column (4 µm, 2.1 × 100 mm) was purchased from Thermo Fisher (Waltham, MA, USA) and installed in a Vanquish Horizon UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA). Mobile phase A was prepared with 15 mM DIPA and 35 mM HFIP buffers in aqueous solution. Mobile phase B was prepared at the ratio of 50:50 water/methanol with 7.5 mM TEA buffer and 25 mM HFIP or 15 mM DIPA and 35 mM HFIP. During LC analysis, the column temperature was maintained at 60°C and the flow rate was set at 0.25 ml/min. The composition of mobile-phase B started at 1% and increased to 35% over 17 min. Then it was increased to 95% over 17.5 min before dropping back to 1% over 18 min. 20 microliters of RNA solutions were injected by the autosampler without dilution.

A heated electrospray ionization (HESI) probe was installed in an Orbitrap Exploris 240 mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA) coupled to LC. The capillary voltage was set at 3.4 kV in negative mode. Sheath and aux gas were set to 45 arb and 10 arb, respectively. The temperatures of the ion transfer tube and vaporizer were 320°C and 275°C, respectively. Other parameters were set as follows: 500 ms scan time, standard/300% normalized AGC target, 2/3 microscans, 70% RF lens, and 200/300 ms maximum injection time. Full scans were performed in the m/z 450–2000/600–2000 ranges and at 120 000 resolution.

LC-MS software and data processing

The coupled LC and Orbitrap instruments were controlled by Chromeleon 7.1 and Xcalibur 4.2, respectively. TIC and MS raw data obtained from LC-MS analysis were processed by BioPharma Finder 5.0/5.2 and deconvoluted by the embedded Xtract algorithm. The parameters used for BioPharma Finder were as follows: Sensitivity: High; Target Avg Spectrum Width: 0.12 min; % Offset: 25; Merge Tolerance: 10.0 ppm; Min. Number of Detected Intervals: 3; S/N Threshold: 2.00; Min. Num Detected Charge: 2.

Nested algorithm and read generation

Raw data from Biopharma Finder 5.0/5.2 were exported as .xlsx files and processed using an in-house nested algorithm implemented in R (v4.4.1) and Python 3.11. Prior to ladder generation, data points were sorted in descending order by monoisotopic mass. In each iteration, the algorithm selects the data point with the highest intensity as a “seed” and searches downward for candidate fragment data points with mass differences matching known nucleotide unit masses from a nucleotide building-block library that includes canonical and a few common modified nucleotides. When multiple matches occur, the most intense match is selected as the next seed, and the process continues until no further matches are found. The algorithm then returns to the original seed and searches upward in mass. The resulting matches from both directions are combined into a single RNA sequencing read, and all associated data points are removed from the dataset. This process repeats iteratively until all remaining data points fall below a user-defined intensity threshold (∼1% of the most abundant RNA). Additional details are provided in the Supplementary Information (SI).

To generate sequencing reads from the first ladder layer, the algorithm identifies the most intense fragment (data point A) with a mass below that of the intact RNA, likely representing a ladder fragment from the most abundant RNA species. Starting from A, the algorithm searches for the next ladder fragment (data point B) with a mass difference corresponding to a single nucleotide unit. This process continues iteratively to build a sequencing read. Ladder fragment selection is guided by four criteria: (i) mass differences matching canonical or modified nucleotide units in the nucleotide building-block library; (ii) retention time (tR) consistency with a 2D tR–mass sigmoidal curve; (iii) selecting the match with the highest intensity in cases of multiple matches; and (iv) consistent intensity across consecutive fragments. See SI for further details.

Site-specific stoichiometric determination

Site-specific modification stoichiometries at defined sgRNA nucleotide positions were determined by extracted ion chromatogram (EIC) integration of ladder fragments differing only in modification state at the position of interest. This well-established approach enables direct comparison of coexisting isoforms and has been used successfully for quantitative analysis of partial RNA modifications [16, 20, 21]. For each site, stoichiometry was calculated as the EIC peak area of the modified fragment divided by the sum of modified and unmodified counterpart peak areas. This method provides position-resolved, site-specific stoichiometry and is distinct from global nucleoside-level measurements after complete enzymatic digestion, which report modification frequencies normalized to the total nucleoside pool and may be influenced by sequence- and modification-dependent enzymatic biases [22].

AdductFinder algorithm

After obtaining the de novo sequencing results using the Nested Algorithm, the AdductFinder algorithm is applied to identify non-native RNA ladder fragments that lack consecutive neighboring fragments and are therefore missed by the nested algorithm. Using the sequencing results and common adduct masses (Supplementary Table S4), the algorithm calculates the theoretical masses of potential adducted ladder fragments. It then searches the experimental dataset for these masses and reports any matching non-native fragments to the user. This algorithm can increase both ladder coverage and sequencing depth.

Basic statistics

All experiments were performed in ≥3 replicates. No sequence information was provided to the algorithm or analysts prior to blind sequencing. Base calls required mass differences within 10 ppm unless otherwise specified. Reads <3 nt were excluded as unreliable. Additional reliability criteria are provided in SI Section IV.

Results

Overview of 3D nested algorithm-based NGMS-Seq to sequence mixed RNAs

The workflow of 3D NGMS-Seq (Fig. 1) consists of three key steps: (i) LC-MS measurement of intact and acid-hydrolyzed RNA samples to acquire data for MS sequencing, (ii) de novo base calling to generate short RNA sequencing reads in a “layer by layer” fashion, and (iii) assembly of these reads to full-length RNA sequences with modifications (if there are any). Each “layer” represents a subgroup of RNA ladder fragments derived from the same parent RNA and sharing similar MS intensities (typically within one order of magnitude). Each layer therefore represents an abundance-defined tier of RNA-derived fragments that are clustered by mass, intensity, and tR and ranked from the most to the least abundant. Layers may vary in length and appear in native and non-native (e.g. Na+, K+, and DIPA adducts; dehydrated) forms, depending on the sample composition and LC-MS operating conditions.

The method design was guided by both empirical observations and theoretical models of RNA acid hydrolysis. Key features include: (i) the average hydrolysis ratio per nucleotide (rav), which defines hydrolyzed fragment types and proportions (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. S1); (ii) the MS intensity ratio of internal versus ladder fragments, which approximates the rav value and is tunable by hydrolysis conditions (Supplementary Fig. S1 and Supplementary Table S1); and (iii) the MS intensity ranking of 5′ or 3′ ladder fragments of identical length, which was found to reflect their parent RNA abundance in the pre-hydrolysis sample, providing a basis for computational separation of different RNA species and their post-hydrolysis ladder fragments (Fig. 3). To maximize ladder fragment yield while suppressing internal fragments, two control runs (intact run and standard hydrolysis run) are performed to determine rav and to optimize RNA hydrolysis and LC-MS conditions through in silico simulations that are based on RNA hydrolysis kinetics (Fig. 2; Supplementary Fig. S1; and Supplementary Table S1).

Adding MS intensity as a third dimension transforms the previous 2D NGMS-Seq into a more powerful 3D mass-tR-intensity platform. By prioritizing ladder fragments with intensities within the same order of magnitude, the 3D method reduces outlier miscalls and markedly improves sequencing accuracy in complex RNA mixtures.

Nested algorithm to automatically de novo read short RNA sequences

Inspired by a “matryoshka-doll” structure, the nested algorithm was developed to exploit the intrinsic 3D relationship among mass, intensity, and tR of each ladder fragment hydrolyzed from parent RNAs and recorded during LC-MS. This algorithm segregates data points from consecutive ladder fragments into 3D-mass-tR-intensity layers, sorted by the parent RNA abundance. Within each layer, short RNA sequences are read out de novo by base-calling each individual nucleotide, determined from mass differences between two consecutive ladder fragments that match unit masses of canonical or modified nucleotides in the nucleotide building-block library (Fig. 1b).

To automatically read RNA sequences, the algorithm begins with the most intense fragment (data point A), likely from the most abundant RNA, and searches for adjacent fragments (data point B) differing by a nucleotide-unit mass (Fig. 1b). Iterative base-calling proceeds in both increasing and decreasing mass directions using four criteria (Fig. 1d). Once no further ladder fragments are found in one direction, the algorithm either reverses direction or initiates a new layer. The order of layers reflects the original parent RNA abundance, enabling accurate separation of RNA species and orderly de novo readout of short RNA sequences (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Fig. S2).

Short sequences often result from the limited nucleotides in the nucleotide building-block library, which includes only canonical and a few common modified nucleotides. In complex samples, segments of the same RNA may be independently sequenced across different layers and forms (native, adducted, and dehydrated) (Fig. 3c).

Sequence assembly to reconstruct full-length RNA sequences

To construct full-length RNA sequences, short reads are aligned and assembled based on nucleotide positions and mass differences corresponding to one or more nucleotide units. NGMS-Seq data inherently carry positional information: fragment length can be approximated by dividing the monoisotopic mass by ∼320 Da, the average mass of a ribonucleotide unit.

Sequence assembly begins with the readout from layer 1, which includes the highest intensity data points, and aligns it with its parallel sequences—often arising from partial modifications or metal adducts—showing constant mass differences at each nucleotide position (e.g. +14 Da for a partial methylation and +22 Da for a Na+ adduct). Bidirectional reads (5′ → 3′ and 3′ → 5′) [19] and the MassSum algorithm [20] further aid assembly by pairing 5′ and 3′ ladder fragments from the same parent RNA. Prior knowledge of target RNA sequences can assist but is not required. Sequence “gaps” caused by rare modifications not in the nucleotide building-block library (e.g. cmnm5ges2U) are filled during assembly.

Optimal hydrolysis time

Guided by preliminary data from the two control runs and in silico simulation (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. S1), we optimized acid hydrolysis conditions, primarily hydrolysis time in this study, to maximize the yield of desired RNA ladder fragments while minimizing internal fragments. Since the value of rav is directly proportional to hydrolysis time, a small rav (<0.05) allows proportional estimation of the optimal hydrolysis time.

MS intensity order of same-length ladder fragments reflects parent RNA abundance

Under optimized conditions, we observed that the MS intensities of the same-length 5′ or 3′ ladder fragments reliably reflect the abundance of their parent RNAs in the pre-hydrolysis sample. This trend is consistent with the first-order kinetics for the hydrolysis of RNA's P-O bonds [Equation (2)], allowing estimation of fragment formation probability based on the initial parent RNA concentrations.

This model is validated experimentally. As shown in Fig. 3b, three synthetic RNAs mixed at 9:3:1 molar ratio produced the same-length ladder fragments that preserved this intensity order. For instance, the 10-nt 5′ and 3′ ladder fragments from each RNA retained the 9:3:1 ratio, reflecting their initial distribution. By contrast, the internal fragments were of much lower intensity and largely undetectable, likely due to limited hydrolysis time. This predictable RNA-hydrolyzed fragmentation pattern enables precise 3D data separation of RNA ladder fragments by intensity, mass, and retention time, supporting reliable MS sequencing of each parental RNA in the mixture (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Table S2).

System for evaluating de novo sequencing quality

To assess the quality of 3D NGMS-Seq results, we established evaluation criteria tailored to our LC-MS datasets, which differ fundamentally from conventional sequencing data. These datasets consist of digitized numeric values exported from deconvoluted Thermo Fisher RAW files using BioPharma Finder 5.0 software (see the “Materials and methods” section).

Sequencing data quality classification

High-quality data meet one of the following criteria:

Either the 5′ or 3′ ladder independently contains fragments spanning the entire RNA sequence from the first to the last nucleotide, providing 100% sequence coverage alone.

Either the 5′ or 3′ ladder independently contains fragments spanning the entire RNA sequence from the first to the last nucleotide, providing 100% sequence coverage alone.

If neither ladder alone spans the full sequence, at least one ladder (5′ or 3′) must cover more than half of the RNA length in opposite directions (5′→3′ and 3′→5′), together yielding 100% bidirectional coverage.

If neither ladder alone spans the full sequence, at least one ladder (5′ or 3′) must cover more than half of the RNA length in opposite directions (5′→3′ and 3′→5′), together yielding 100% bidirectional coverage.

Ladder fragments may exist in native or non-native forms (e.g. Na⁺, K⁺ adducts; dehydrated), depending on the RNA sample composition and LC-MS conditions.

High-quality data typically yield 100% accuracy with zero error, as each nucleotide is confirmed multiple times independently via overlapping 5′ and 3′ ladder fragments. These fragments, whether native or non-native, support confident base calling regardless of RNA abundance or modification status. Following the 3D NGMS-Seq, MassSum [20] is further applied to identify paired 5′ and 3′ ladder fragments derived from the same parent RNA, validating that the RNA sequence is present and consistent across MS data for both intact and fragmented RNA. This cross-layer verification reinforces sequence fidelity and strengthens confidence in de novo sequencing results. To further enhance ladder coverage and sequencing depth, the AdductFinder algorithm identifies non-native RNA fragments missed by the nested algorithm through calculating theoretical adducted masses and matching them to experimental data (see the “Materials and methods” section).

Evaluating de novo sequencing results from low-quality data

For low-quality datasets that do not meet the above high-quality criteria, often resulting from poor sample quality, low RNA abundance, and/or non-optimized hydrolysis, it is challenging to achieve complete sequence coverage. Nevertheless, the nested algorithm can still generate short reads de novo from individual layers, and overlapping reads may support partial sequence assembly.

However, full verification becomes difficult when coverage falls below 100%. In particular, incomplete sequences cannot be fully validated by MassSum because it requires the presence of paired 5′/3′ fragments. These fragments, produced by single phosphodiester bond cleavages during acid-mediated RNA hydrolysis [20], may be absent in low-quality datasets. To address this limitation, a probability score was introduced to assess sequencing reliability by quantifying the likelihood of false-positives at each position based on the size of the nucleotide pool (modified or not) and random A/C/G/U combinations (Supplementary Table S5). Further details are provided in SI Section IV.

Testing 3D NGMS-Seq on a mixture of siRNA/miRNA mimics

Small interfering RNAs (siRNAs, 21–27 nts) and microRNAs (miRNAs, 21–23 nts) are key regulators of gene expression with significant therapeutic potential [23]. To mimic these RNA samples, we prepared a mixture of three synthetic 20-nt RNAs at an input molar ratio of RNA-A:RNA-B:RNA-C = 9:3:1. MS intensities observed at the intact level prior to acid hydrolysis closely reflected this input ratio (Fig. 3b).

Without any prior sequence input, 3D NGMS-Seq with the nested algorithm achieved 100% accurate de novo sequencing of all three RNAs. The nested algorithm automatically generated short reads by sequentially base-calling each individual nucleotide from ladder fragments in native or non-native forms across intensity-ranked layers. Native ladder fragments were detected in layers 1, 2, 6, 7, 10, 11, and 15, respectively, for three RNAs (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Table S3), and the intensities of the same-length ladder fragments scaled with their parent RNA abundances. Bidirectional readout sequences (reading in either 5′ → 3′ or 3′ → 5′ direction) further facilitated sequence confirmation [19] and complementation [20].

Full-length RNA sequences were assembled by aligning short reads according to nucleotide positions and mass differences. For example, RNA-A assembly began with a 19-nt read from layer 1 (a native 3′ ladder with sequence AAACC GUUAC CAUUA CUGA), which aligned with its parallel sequences in K+ form (+38 Da) (Fig. 3c). Similarly, a native 5′ ladder in layer 6—covering 18 nt from A3 to G20—aligns with dehydrated counterparts (−18 Da). Ladder fragment masses indicate nucleotide positions. For instance, the 1005.2 Da ladder fragment in layer 6 and its dehydrated form with 987.2 Da in layer 4 corresponded to position 3, consistent with ∼320 Da per ribonucleotide. For clarity, 3′ fragments were converted to their corresponding 5′ counterparts based on the MassSum principle [20], and sequences are presented in the conventional 5′ → 3′ direction.

RNA-A, the most abundant, yielded the strongest ladder intensities and sequencing depth, followed by RNA-B and RNA-C (Fig. 3d). Sequencing depth, coverage, and accuracy were enhanced by incorporating multiple layers of native and non-native ladders, including sodium/potassium adducts and dehydrated forms, particularly for the most abundant RNA-A (layers 3, 4, 8, 9, 12, 13, and 17) (Fig. 3c).

In contrast, RNA-C, which comprised only 6.71% of RNA-A’s abundance in the pre-hydrolysis mixture, was sequenced only from native 3′ and 5′ ladders in layers 10, 11, and 15. Its two 5′ terminal nucleotides were initially missed due to weak signals, leaving a 635.078 Da gap consistent with a U and A combination without definitive order (Supplementary Fig. S5). However, this ambiguity was resolved by integrating bidirectional readouts with additional data from a replicate run, resulting in 100% sequence coverage.

De novo sequencing of full-length CRISPR/Cas9 sgRNA with 100% sequence accuracy

After validating the method by sequencing a mixture of 20-nt miRNA/siRNA mimics, we applied the 3D nested algorithm-based NGMS-Seq to de novo sequence two CRISPR/Cas9 100-nt sgRNA samples separately (referred to as sgRNA2 and sgRNA1) (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Fig. S3). sgRNA1 contains only canonical nucleotides, while sgRNA2 incorporates 2′-O-methylated nucleotides. The two sgRNAs were largely identical except for three nucleotides at positions 2–4 (with 2UCA4 in sgRNA1 and 2AUC4 in sgRNA2) and two modifications: Am at position 7 and Um at position 90 (Fig. 4b).

Without any prior sequence input, NGMS-Seq successfully sequenced both sgRNA1 and sgRNA2 de novo, achieving 100% sequencing coverage and accuracy when benchmarked against vendor-provided reference sequences. The nested algorithm systematically identified short reads from consecutive ladder fragments, starting from the most intense datapoints and tracing adjacent ladder fragments across intensity-ranked layers—native or non-native 3′ and 5′ ladders—to generate short reads, which were then assembled stepwise into the full-length sequences (Fig. 4).

High-confidence base-calls were obtained from native 3′ and 5′ ladders in the top two layers of each sgRNA (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Fig. S3), where mass differences between adjacent ladder fragments matched known canonical or modified nucleotide unit masses (Fig. 4f). Overlap of the native 3′ and 5′ ladders ensured complete coverage through bidirectional sequencing (Fig. 4b and c) [19], while non-native ladders further increased sequencing depth and validated the sequencing assignments, including those for the modified sgRNA2 (Fig. 4f). 2′-O-methylations were identified by missing ladder fragments at modified sites, as formic acid hydrolysis of the phosphodiester bond was blocked at the 2´-O- methylated sites (Fig. 4b–f). A mass gap of 625.0821 Da, corresponding to a C (unit mass: 305.0413 Da) plus a Um (unit mass: 320.0410 Da), confirmed the presence of a 2′-O-methylated U at the specific position (Fig. 4e).

3D NGMS-Seq identified sgRNA impurities and resolved methylation ambiguity (um versus mU; Am versus mA)

To our surprise, de novo sequencing of the sgRNA samples revealed a substantial fraction of RNA impurities coexisting with the target sgRNA, which remains the main RNA species in the sample (71.8%) (Fig. 4g). The major impurity (20.8%) was a 93-nt truncated RNA lacking the first seven nucleotides (1GAUCGGAm7) of the sgRNA2. In the LC-MS profile, this truncated species appeared as a shoulder peak preceding the major sgRNA peak (Fig. 4a), consistent with a synthetic failure during incorporation of the modified nucleotide Am at position 7 (corresponding to nucleotide 93 in the 3′→5′ synthesis direction). In addition, we also detected minor isomers of the full-length 100-nt target sgRNA2, featuring unexpected nucleobase methylations: mU replacing the intended Um at position 90 and mA replacing the planned Am at position 7 (Fig. 4b–d).

To resolve the ambiguity between 2′-O-methylation (Um) and nucleobase methylation (mU) at position U90 of the sgRNA, we examined the ladder fragmentation pattern produced by controlled formic acid hydrolysis. If methylation occurs exclusively (100%) at the 2′-O position of U90 in the sgRNA, a mass gap would appear at this site in both 3′ and 5′ ladders. This results from the absence of the 3′ and 5′-ladder fragment at the modified site, since the 2′-O-methyl group blocks formic acid hydrolysis of the sgRNA between positions 90 and 91 (Fig. 4e). This phenomenon has been previously documented when sequencing Cm at position 32 of yeast tRNA-Phe and m5Um at position 54 of mouse liver tRNA-Glu [20]. In contrast, if methylation occurs in the nucleobase (mU), which does not block the hydrolysis, no mass gap should be present, and the ladder fragment at U90 should show normal intensity relative to adjacent fragments.

In our data, we detected a 3′ ladder fragment corresponding to methylated U at position 90, but with 94% decreased intensity (Fig. 4c). This suggests the coexistence of two different methylated forms, with stoichiometry of 94% Um and 6% mU, rather than a single methylation at position 90. We further confirmed that this observation was not attributable to any random masses, e.g. internal fragments or other impurities.

Similarly, at position 7, we observed coexisting modifications with stoichiometry of 94% Am and 6% mA (Fig. 4b and c). These minor nucleobase methylations likely originated during the synthesis of the 2′-O-methylated nucleoside phosphoramidites used for the synthesis of 100-nt sgRNAs, as introducing a methyl group at the 2′-O-position of the nucleoside can also cause it to attach to other positions in the nucleobase if they are not fully protected. For example, although the adenine N6 position is typically protected (e.g. benzyl-type protection) prior to 2′-O-methylation during phosphoramidite synthesis, such protection is often incomplete, leaving a small fraction (∼5%–6%) of adenine unprotected [24]. Under 2′-O-methylation conditions, the unprotected exocyclic N6 amine is the most nucleophilic and is therefore preferentially methylated. The resultant m⁶A can interconvert with m¹A through Dimroth rearrangement in aqueous solution, establishing a dynamic equilibrium between these two species [25]. Based on these known reaction pathways (Supplementary Fig. S7) [24, 26], we further propose the minor mU modification corresponding to N3-methyl-U (m3U) (Fig. 4d) and the minor mA to N6-methyl-A (m6A) or N1-methyl-A (m1A) (Fig. 5f and Supplementary Fig. S7).

To confirm these assignments, we performed two orthogonal validations: AlkB demethylase treatment (which selectively demethylates m1A but not m6A) [27–29] and exhaustive nucleoside digestion followed by LC-MS/MS [30]. AlkB treatment of the sgRNA2 produced a 14 Da decrease in intact mass (32 313.306→32 299.295 Da) and complete conversion of the minor m¹A to canonical A at position 7, as resolved by 3D NGMS-Seq (Supplementary Fig. S8). Complementary nucleoside LC-MS/MS independently confirmed the presence of low-abundance m¹A/m⁶A and m³U impurities coexisting with Am and Um, respectively, in the sgRNA sample (Supplementary Figs S9–S11). Such methylation ambiguity has historically been difficult to resolve, but our 3D NGMS-Seq approach clearly distinguished it (Fig. 4g). Importantly, these results demonstrate that 3D NGMS-Seq not only enables full-length sequencing of major target RNAs of interest but also resolves low-abundance sgRNA impurities and subtle methylation ambiguities that remain inaccessible to conventional methods.

3D NGMS-Seq of mixed samples reveals full-length sequences of distinct sgRNA populations and RNA impurities from partial modifications

A persistent challenge in sgRNA synthesis is incomplete incorporation of modified nucleotides at specific positions, resulting in heterogeneous mixtures of canonical and modified RNA species. Misincorporation of canonical nucleotides can also occur due to inefficient synthesis or capping. To assess 3D NGMS-Seq’s capability in resolving such RNA complexity, we sequenced five RNA mixtures with varying known ratios of modified sgRNA2 and non-modified sgRNA1. Sample identities remained blinded until sequencing analysis was completed.

3D NGMS-Seq accurately reconstructed the full-length sequences of major RNA species while detecting and quantifying low-abundance impurities (Fig. 5). For example, in a mixture of 70% modified sgRNA2 and 30% unmodified sgRNAs (input molar ratio), both sgRNAs were identified with correct intact masses in native and non-native forms (Fig. 5a and b). The measured abundance of non-modified sgRNA1 (31.4%) closely matched its input (30%). By contrast, sgRNA2 accounted for only 50.8%, significantly lower than expected due to sgRNA2-associated impurities. The major impurity (12.6%) was a 93-nt truncated RNA lacking the first seven nucleotides (1GAUCGGAm7), while additional impurities (5.2%) were isomers of the 100-nt sgRNA2, with mU replacing Um at position 90 and mA replacing Am at position 7 (Fig. 5h), consistent with sgRNA2-alone sequencing results (Fig. 4).

Beyond quantification, the 3D nested algorithm-based NGMS-Seq enabled sequencing of both major and minor RNAs de novo within mixtures (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Fig. S6). 3D mass-tR-intensity plots show clear separation of two sgRNA ladder fragment datapoints as the sample ratio changes (Supplementary Fig. S4), allowing independent sequencing of each RNA in the mixed sample. Native 3′ and 5′ ladders provided full bidirectional coverage of minor sgRNA1, while non-native ladders increased depth and validated assignments (Fig. 5c, d, and g). For sgRNA2, the 5′ ladder fragment at position 7 and the 3′ ladder fragment at position 91 were not completely absent as expected for 2′-O-methylation but appeared with 94% decreased intensity (Fig. 5e and f), indicating again coexisting isomers from different methylations—94% Um with 6% mU and 94% Am with 6% mA.

Thus, even in complex mixtures, 3D NGMS-Seq achieves full-length sequencing of target sgRNAs, resolves minor RNA species of varying lengths and isoforms, and distinguishes subtle methylation ambiguities. By leveraging MS intensity with mass and tR, this approach provides quantitative impurity profiles (i.e. impurity RNA relative abundance and site-specific nucleotide stoichiometry) alongside target therapeutic RNA sequencing data, offering a powerful platform for RNA drug development, quality control, and regulatory validation.

Discussion

Traditional MS has been widely used for RNA analysis, but primarily to confirm a known target sequence supplied by the manufacturer or inferred from orthogonal methods. In these conventional workflows, sequence information relies heavily on prior knowledge rather than direct interpretation of mass spectrometric data from the sample itself. Consequently, MS has rarely been applied to de novo RNA sequencing, in which the order of nucleotides—whether modified or unmodified—is determined directly from the analyzed sample.

In contrast, our 3D nested algorithm–based NGMS-Seq enables true de novo sequencing of RNA directly from complex mixtures, determining the full sequence—including modifications—without prior sequence knowledge. Unlike traditional bottom-up LC-MS/MS, which requires highly purified RNA and prior sequence input, the 3D NGMS-Seq method relies on controlled hydrolysis and employs a mathematically robust nested algorithm to accurately sequence mixed RNA samples. We demonstrated its power for sequencing synthetic RNAs ranging from 20-nt miRNA/siRNA mimics to 100-nt CRISPR/Cas9 sgRNAs, resolving RNA sample heterogeneity, and distinguishing subtle methylation ambiguities (Um versus mU and Am versus mA), which may carry distinct functional biological or therapeutic consequences. Their site-specific stoichiometries were determined by EIC integration of chemically matched oligonucleotide ladder fragments that differed only at the modification site, thereby providing position-resolved quantification of partial modifications at defined nucleotide positions [16, 20, 21]. In contrast, Supplementary Figs S9 and S11 present bulk nucleoside abundances measured after enzymatic digestion of heterogeneous RNA mixtures and were included solely to confirm the presence of base-methylated species, not to determine site-specific stoichiometries. Because these two approaches quantify different entities and use different denominators, they are not expected to yield numerically equivalent values.

A key discovery was that the MS intensity ranking of same-length 5′ or 3′ ladder fragments reflects the abundance of their parent RNAs in pre-hydrolysis samples. Integrating intensity into conventional mass–tR analysis enabled computational separation of RNA species, reducing the need for physical purification and improving accuracy by prioritizing ladder fragments with similar intensities while reducing base miscalling from outliers. Clear intensity separation of sgRNA ladders (Supplementary Fig. S4) further demonstrated the ability of this 3D approach to sequence mixed RNA populations.

Currently, the nested algorithm can effectively read out short sequences in each layer of ladder fragments that originated from the same parent RNA, but assembly is required to reconstruct complete sequences. This is partly due to the restricted set of modified nucleotides in the library of nucleotide building blocks used by the nested algorithm. Expanding the library would increase computational complexity and risk higher error rates, necessitating careful management of modification inclusion, data filtering, and assembly strategies. More discussion can be found in SI Section IV.

Sequencing depth and accuracy improve by integrating short sequences from non-native ladder forms (e.g. dehydrated and/or metal adducted). Dehydrated 5′ ladders, intermediates formed during RNA acid hydrolysis (Fig. 2), and other non-native fragments help bridge gaps caused by missing native ladder fragments during layer-by-layer sequencing. These additional forms not only enhance sequencing coverage but may also provide insight into RNA stability, ion interactions [31], and small-molecule binding, e.g. to RNA aptamers, potentially advancing RNA-targeted drug discovery [32].

The performance of NGMS-Seq depends critically on generating well-defined ladder fragments. In practice, any RNA species with a relative abundance above the exhaustive level (∼1% of the most abundant RNA) can be reliably sequenced, while it may be a struggle to generate complete ladders from extremely low-abundance RNAs (<1%). Nonetheless, sequencing remains feasible when coexisting isomers provide complementary ladders [20], as demonstrated for sgRNA2 impurities (Figs 4 and 5). Reliable results for low-abundance RNAs can be further enhanced by tailoring hydrolysis and acquisition conditions to the sample’s complexity, guided by control LC–MS runs and in silico simulations, with consistent fragment intensity improving both coverage and accuracy. Although exhaustive sequencing of every RNA in a complex mixture remains challenging, advances in MS sensitivity and algorithm development—particularly those leveraging both ladder and internal fragments—are expected to lower the effective abundance threshold toward 1% or below.

Looking forward, 3D nested algorithm-based NGMS-Seq has the potential to extend beyond short synthetic RNAs. We are actively testing the method for sequencing cellular RNAs, including complex cellular tRNAs and small RNAs (<200 nt). While NGMS-Seq is a top-down method that does not require nuclease digestion when sequencing 100 nt sgRNA, incorporating nuclease digestion into our NGMS-Seq platform could extend its applicability to longer RNAs such as synthetic therapeutics, mRNA vaccines, and cellular rRNA (>2000 nt).

With further workflow optimization of 3D NGMS-Seq, particularly in hydrolysis control, LC-MS instrumentation, and data processing, sequencing results could be delivered within a few minutes once the deconvoluted MS data files are available. The method can reduce the overall sequencing time to <1 h/RNA sample, making it ideal for real-time quality control of therapeutic RNA products. This NGMS-Seq approach provides a much-needed tool for direct and comprehensive sequencing of therapeutic RNAs as well as their modifications and impurities.

Conclusions

We discovered that the MS intensity order of same-length ladder fragments mirrors the pre-hydrolysis abundance of their parent RNAs, enabling computational separation of mixed RNA species and their ladder fragments by intensity. Building on this principle, our 3D nested algorithm-based NGMS-Seq approach integrates mass intensity into 2D mass-tR analysis, achieving accurate de novo sequencing of complex RNA mixtures without prior sequence input. 3D NGMS-Seq resolves RNA heterogeneity and methylation ambiguity (Um versus mU and Am versus mA), identifies coexisting RNA impurities, and quantifies their relative abundances. Bypassing nuclease digestion and MS/MS fragmentation, the method delivers comprehensive RNA and modification profiles, addressing a critical gap in RNA drug analysis. Its high accuracy, efficiency, and ease of use position it as a potential industry standard for rapid small RNA sequencing and as a reliable tool for quality control and regulatory validation—ultimately supporting the highest safety standards for patients.
