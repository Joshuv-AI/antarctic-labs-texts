Advances in biomaterials and technologies applied in craniofacial regeneration
Authors: Farah Asa’ad
Published: 2026

In this editorial, we present a Journal of Materials Science: Materials in Medicine Collection highlighting advances in biomaterials, bioactive systems, and manufacturing strategies for craniofacial regeneration. The included studies span sterilization effects, scaffold design, composite biomaterials, angiogenesis, soft tissue regeneration, and precision manufacturing, reflecting interdisciplinary progress in translational craniofacial research.

Craniofacial regeneration represents a rapidly evolving field at the intersection of biomaterials science, tissue engineering, and clinical translation. Over the past decade, significant progress has been achieved through the development of advanced biomaterials, biologically active systems, and precision manufacturing technologies. These innovations collectively aim to restore both function and aesthetics in complex craniofacial defects, ultimately improving patient outcomes [1]. The present Collection brings together six contributions that reflect this multidisciplinary progress, spanning fundamental material characterization to preclinical and translational applications.

A key aspect of biomaterials research lies in understanding how post-fabrication processing and sterilization influence material properties relevant to biological performance. In this context, Kunrath et al. [2] demonstrated in vitro that sterilization methods can significantly affect the physicochemical properties of modified dental implant surfaces. While surface morphology and roughness parameters remained unaltered across all sterilization modalities, significant chemical changes were observed following autoclaving and UVC exposure. In addition, hydrophilic implant surfaces were negatively affected by autoclaving, and their functional characteristics were shown to be sensitive to atmospheric air exposure after package opening, independently of the sterilization method. Their findings emphasize the importance of selecting appropriate sterilization protocols in order to preserve functional biomaterial properties intended for clinical or experimental use. This work highlights a critical but often overlooked step in the translational pipeline from material design to biological application.

Beyond sterilization-related effects on biomaterial properties, scaffold architecture plays an important role in guided bone regeneration strategies. Yanagisawa et al. [3] introduced bioresorbable mesh domes fabricated from Vicryl mesh (woven and knitted types) combined with carbonate apatite granules for alveolar ridge augmentation. In a rabbit calvarial model, different mesh architectures were evaluated, demonstrating that constructs incorporating knitted mesh on the outer (soft tissue) side promoted enhanced appositional bone formation. This effect was attributed to reduced fibrous tissue infiltration compared with woven configurations. In contrast, woven mesh tended to permit earlier material degradation and increased fibrous invasion, limiting new bone formation. Overall, the study highlights that mesh architecture within bioresorbable constructs can regulate tissue infiltration and spatial stability of graft granules, thereby influencing regenerative outcomes.

Advances in biomaterial composition further extend into the development of load-bearing structures for cranial reconstruction. Daoush et al. [4] investigated alumina-reinforced Ti-12Mo-6Zr composites fabricated via spark plasma sintering. Their results showed that increasing alumina content enhanced nanomechanical properties, including hardness and Young’s modulus, as determined by nanoindentation, suggesting potential suitability for cranial reconstruction applications. This contribution highlights the growing role of hybrid ceramic-metal systems in achieving mechanically robust cranial reconstruction materials.

While hard tissue regeneration remains central to craniofacial reconstruction, soft tissue regeneration is equally critical for aesthetic and functional outcomes. Yu et al. [5] developed an injectable biomaterial based on the self-assembly and self-crosslinking of activated plasma albumin gel (APAG) with concentrated growth factors (CGF) for gingival black triangle reconstruction. The system exhibited a hierarchical microstructure with uniform porosity, nanofibrous features, controlled biodegradability, and growth factor enrichment, collectively generating a biomimetic microenvironment. In vitro, this matrix enabled sustained growth factor release that enhanced the viability, proliferation, and migration of human periodontal ligament stem cells (PDLSCs) for at least 14 days. Clinically, injection into gingival black triangle regions resulted in successful papilla reconstruction with stable outcomes maintained over a 6-month follow-up period. This work exemplifies the shift toward minimally invasive, biologically active injectable systems for soft tissue regeneration.

Angiogenesis represents a fundamental biological process that is tightly coupled to osteogenesis during successful tissue regeneration. Wang et al. [6] investigated nano-dicalcium silicate using an in vitro human umbilical vein endothelial cell (HUVEC) co-culture model and in vivo bone defect models. Their findings demonstrated that nano-dicalcium silicate enhanced endothelial proliferation, migration, and tube formation, confirming pro-angiogenic activity. RNA sequencing further revealed upregulation of MAPK and PI3K/Akt signaling pathways, which are critically involved in the regulation of cell proliferation, migration, and angiogenesis. Importantly, in vivo implantation of nano-dicalcium silicate scaffolds promoted neovascularization within bone defect sites, supporting the concept that vascular induction is a prerequisite for effective bone regeneration through angiogenesis-osteogenesis coupling [7].

Finally, advances in manufacturing technologies are enabling the fabrication of patient-specific cranial implants with improved precision and functionality. Choudhary et al. [8] investigated heat-assisted single point incremental forming (SPIF) of perforated AZ31B magnesium alloy for cranial applications. Their hybrid experimental-numerical study demonstrated that warm forming at elevated temperature (400 °C) significantly improves material formability in a system with limited room-temperature ductility. Using optimized tool geometry and process parameters, greater forming depth and reduced surface roughness were achieved, enabling fabrication of geometrically complex cranial implants. Finite element modelling provided additional insight into stress, strain, and deformation behavior during forming. This contribution reflects the increasing importance of advanced manufacturing strategies in producing high-precision cranial implants.

Collectively, the contributions in this Collection highlight the convergence of multiple disciplines driving innovation in craniofacial regeneration. From biomaterial sterilization effects and associated physicochemical stability considerations [2], to scaffold architectural control [3], advanced composite systems for cranial reconstruction [4], bioactive injectable soft tissue systems [5], angiogenesis-driven bone regeneration mechanisms [6], and precision manufacturing approaches [8], the field is clearly advancing toward integrated, multifunctional solutions.

A unifying theme emerging from these studies is the transition from passive biomaterials to biointeractive and functionally engineered systems. Modern craniofacial regeneration increasingly relies not only on structural support but also on active modulation of biological processes such as angiogenesis, cellular migration, and tissue-specific regeneration [9]. In particular, the close interdependence between vascularization and new bone formation highlights the importance of angiogenesis–osteogenesis coupling as a central design principle in regenerative strategies. Moreover, the integration of advanced manufacturing techniques enables patient-specific solutions, further aligning the field with the principles of personalized medicine [1].

Future progress will likely depend on deeper integration between materials science, cellular biology, and digital manufacturing technologies. Bridging the gap between preclinical optimization and predictable clinical outcomes remains a central challenge [10]. The studies presented in this Collection collectively contribute to this evolving landscape and emphasize the multidisciplinary direction of craniofacial regenerative research.
