Is there an Association between Trauma and Musculoskeletal Tumours? A Review of the Literature
Authors: Efstratios D. Athanaselis, Alexandros Koskiniotis, Aikaterini Sourmenidi, Efstathios Konstantinou, Konstantina Zacharouli, Michael Hantes, Theofilos Karachalios, Sokratis Varitimidis
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThere is a relatively high incidence of patients recalling an injury before the appearance of a musculoskeletal (MSK) mass, especially in the hand and upper limb. Despite numerous reports of such cases, no clear pathogenetic mechanism has been elucidated to explain this. A tumour may be pre-existing, with the injury bringing the patient's attention to it, or develop coincidently following trauma. Alternatively, injury or the resultant reparative mechanisms may initiate or accelerate tumour formation. Following such a case presenting to our unit, a narrative review of the literature was conducted to determine what evidence existed to support or refute these hypotheses. This is presented along with the case. Though trauma as a cause of MSK tumours cannot be excluded, there is a lack of scientific evidence to support this theory, and further research in the field of pathophysiology and molecular biology is necessary. It is important that clinicians caring for trauma patients maintain a high index of suspicion for alternate diagnoses. A local injury may mask tumour development and lead to the incorrect conclusion that persistent symptoms are the result of failure to recover from the injury, rather than having a more sinister explanation.How to cite this articleAthanaselis ED, Koskiniotis A, Sourmenidi A, et al. Introduction

Neoplasia of the musculoskeletal (MSK) system includes benign, intermediate, and malignant bone and soft tissue tumours, with the latter representing approximately 4% of tumours occurring in adults annually. Incidence is higher in patients under 30 years of age.1 Although MSK tumours represent a relatively small proportion of all human neoplasms, incidence has been increasing recently, potentially related to environmental and lifestyle factors alongside improved diagnostic yield with modern imaging techniques.

The aetiology of MSK tumours remains poorly understood. It is, however, a relatively consistent experience that patients presenting with these lesions report an injury preceding the appearance of the pathological lesion. Whether this association is real or represents a form of recall bias, given that injuries are extremely common, is unclear. We recently experienced a 14-year-old presenting acutely with a mass just above the popliteal fossa following blunt trauma 15 days previously. This prompted a review of the literature, investigating whether there is a connection between trauma and oncogenesis in the musculoskeletal system, which is reported here alongside the case.

Materials and Methods

Search Strategy

A narrative review of the literature was conducted. Keywords and their combinations were utilised to search the PubMed database and identify relevant articles exploring association between trauma and musculoskeletal oncogenesis. The keywords employed included “post-traumatic tumour”, “musculoskeletal tumour”, “orthopaedic tumour”, “orthopedic tumour”, “soft tissue tumour”, “hand tumour”, “upper limb tumour”, and “trauma”. No specific inclusion or exclusion criteria were applied due to the limited number of published articles on the topic.

Data Extraction

A total of 848 articles were identified and screened during the literature review by two independent reviewers. After the screening process, 25 articles were ultimately included in the study. The study selection and data extraction process are summarised in the flowchart, which encompasses the research process conducted between December 2022 and March 2023 (Flowchart 1).

The included articles consist of 9 case series, 11 case reports, 1 case-control study, 1 laboratory study, and 3 literature reviews. The most studied tumours associated with a history of injury in the region prior to their occurrence were lipomas and sarcomas. The anatomical locations where such lesions were predominantly observed were the upper and lower limbs, followed by the trunk and head (Table 1). Most of the cases involved primary tumours, with the exception of one case of a metastatic lesion in the skull, originating from lung cancer.

Case Report

A 14-year-old patient presented to our department with a large subcutaneous mass in the popliteal fossa of the left knee. She related this to a low-energy blunt injury, having been hit by a swing at the site 15 days before. The mass was painful, making walking difficult, with loss of the active and passive extension of the knee (Fig. 1). There was no history of swelling or pain at the site before the injury. The mass was initially diagnosed as a haematoma, and instructions were given for limited weight-bearing, ice, and rest. As the symptoms failed to resolve, the patient was admitted for further evaluation. A differential diagnosis of haematoma, pseudoaneurysm of the popliteal artery, and soft tissue neoplasm was made. After the vascular surgeon's examination, CT angiography and ultrasound triplex of the lower extremity, haematoma and pseudoaneurysm have been excluded. MRI scan of the knee revealed a possible soft tissue sarcoma (Fig. 2), and open biopsy of the popliteal fossa mass was undertaken. Histopathological examination revealed mitotic activity, solid areas of uniform, undifferentiated round cells with scant cytoplasm and indistinct cell borders, and partially middle-sized cells with moderate nuclear pleomorphism, occasionally with prominent nucleoli and eosinophilic cytoplasm, establishing a diagnosis of Ewing's sarcoma (Fig. 3). Consequently, the patient was referred to a specialist paediatric oncology centre for further treatment.

Discussion

Oncogenesis is recognised to be a multifactorial process, associated with genetic and environmental factors, including specific gene mutations, inherited genetic traits, chronic infections, as well as exposure to certain chemicals, radiation, and pharmacological agents. Despite intensive research, including advancing molecular biology techniques, the aetiology of MSK tumours remains largely undetermined.2 Previous local trauma has been proposed as a potential cause, and there is a relatively high incidence of patients recalling injury before the appearance of a MSK mass, especially in the hand and upper limb. It is unclear whether trauma might trigger oncogenesis, accelerate it, or simply draw the patient's attention to an existing abnormality. This issue has been considered in the scientific literature for more than 100 years. An article published in the Journal of the American Medical Association (JAMA) in September 1923 examined the association between trauma and the formation of bone lesions.3 In 1943, Shields Warren described certain criteria for a tumour to be defined as post-traumatic, including a mechanism of injury with enough energy to cause pathologic reparative proliferation of cells at the site of trauma.4

Several studies suggest an association between local trauma and the development of musculoskeletal tumours, with soft tissue sarcomas and lipomas being the most reported. A case-control study from Sweden suggests that surgery and high-energy injuries to any part of the body may increase the risk of leiomyosarcoma and liposarcoma when compared to population rates.5 Montgomery et al. presented a series of six post-traumatic tumours from 501 cases of bone or soft tissue sarcoma treated in their institution between 2004 and 2014.6 All the patients included in the study presented with a significant injury requiring at least one operation before the formation of the tumour, with the average time from trauma to the development of the sarcoma being 19 years. The authors suggest an association between referred traumatic injury and post-traumatic sarcoma development on this basis. Any link with injury remains controversial, and the importance of trauma as a potential causative factor remains unclear, particularly given the low rates of post-traumatic tumours in their series overall. This is particularly striking when one considers how commonly injuries occur in the whole population. It would appear unlikely on this basis that trauma alone is a causative factor for oncogenesis. Another series reports a higher rate of preceding trauma in a series of 51 children presenting with osteosarcoma, with 17 cases (33%) recalling an antecedent injury at the site. There were no significant differences related to patient demographics or other characteristics between those with a history of trauma and those without. The only reported difference was that those with a trauma history requested medical advice earlier, with a mean duration of symptoms of 20 days vs 62 days of those without such history (p = 0.025).7 Post-traumatic osteoid osteoma formation has also been reported. Bednar et al. reported 11 of 46 cases, and Baron et al. 15 patients developing osteoid osteoma between one and thirty-six months after local injury or surgery.8–10 Osteoid osteoma, and osteoblastoma (a benign tumour which is very similar to osteoid osteoma) development in united fracture sites between 2 and 12 years post-injury have also been reported.11–13 The formation of tumours at sites of surgery, which is also a form of trauma, has similarly been reported in literature. Cohen et al. report a case of a 27-year-old woman with a large subcutaneous mass in her back, 3 years after a C6 vertebrae fracture due to a traffic accident. The neoplasm was identified as a desmoid tumour by core needle biopsy, and it was surgically removed. Whilst no operations had been carried out in this case, the group referenced other cases of post-traumatic lipoma and three cases of centroblastic lymphomas associated with sites of previous surgery with metallic implants.14,15 A chronic inflammatory reaction to metallic implants at surgical sites has also been proposed as a potential oncogenic stimulus.

A small number of case series investigate the pathophysiology of a possible link between trauma and the formation of lipomas or pseudolipomas. Pseudolipoma is defined as a prolapse of adipose tissue through a tear in the overlying fascia due to some form of trauma. This phenomenon was first described by Brooke et al. in 1969, with Rozner et al. presenting a series of 24 post-traumatic pseudolipomas 8 years after the injury.16,17 Aust et al. presented a series of 170 patients with lipomas, 34 of which were reported as post-traumatic. The mean time elapsed between trauma and lipoma formation was about 2 years. The authors describe two possible mechanisms that could explain the connection between regional trauma and these benign tumours, either by the formation of “pseudolipomas” created by herniated fat tissue through fascia due to compression or by preadipocyte differentiation and proliferation as a result of cytokine release following tissue injury and haematoma.18 The former mechanism is not true neoplasia but rather a physical occurrence, and it is unclear which predominates.

Krebs and Olsen in 1963 investigated the role of trauma in the development of malignant bone tumours. As with other groups, they observed that a significant proportion of their patients reported an injury in the area before they noticed skeletal abnormality. They concluded these tumours probably pre-existed the injury, estimating no difference in the incidence of malignancy between patients with trauma when compared to the general population. The authors concluded that trauma may allow or stimulate neoplastic growth by destroying the surrounding capsule, producing haematoma, or consuming local immune defence mechanisms, but that there was unlikely to be a direct oncogenic mechanism based on their findings. Considering that musculoskeletal tumours usually require months to grow to a size which is clinically apparent, it is difficult to understand a mechanism by which they might appear very early after injury. Again, the authors conclude that pre-existing malignant cell growth may be accelerated by trauma and the mass brought to the patients attention by the injury.19

Despite the number of cases reported, no clear pathogenic mechanism has been identified for tumour development following trauma. Indeed, it remains unclear that such a link exists at all; the association may simply represent reporting bias in patients with tumours or the high frequency of injuries in general, meaning that recollection of pre-existing injury is common. Alternatively, trauma might initiate tumour development or accelerate the growth of a pre-existing lesion at the site via pathways leading to tissue growth as part of the reparative response. Repetitive injury is another consideration. Particularly in the upper limb, and especially the hand, soft tissue tumours, including fibromas and giant cell tumours of the tendon sheath, may be provoked by repetitive trauma during daily or labour activities. It would be difficult to conduct prospective observational studies to try and better elucidate a true link due to the high frequency of injury in the population and the heterogeneity and low incidence of musculoskeletal tumours. Laboratory studies may be more fruitful. Research regarding the basis of oncogenesis in general concludes that usually a combination of multiple environmental and host factors leads to tumour pathogenesis. The frequency of reference to post-traumatic musculoskeletal tumours means that tissue injury should probably not be entirely excluded.20 More recently, advances in molecular biology have greatly increased understanding of tumour development. In a laboratory study published in 2018, Van Mater et al., based on the results of a previously published study by Young et al., compared the oncogenic potential of acute injury to that of a well-known oncogene (KRAS) in sarcoma genesis. They found that reproducing an acute muscle injury by an injection of cardiotoxin in combination with systemic deletion of the p53 tumour suppressor gene led to sarcoma formation in a mouse model.21,22 Trauma might therefore initiate sarcoma formation in patients with a particular genetic susceptibility. The mechanism of injury-induced sarcoma formation may also depend on the timing of the gene mutations as well, according to previous studies from the same authors.23

Intramuscular haematomas and muscle tears have been associated with the development of metastatic tumours months and years after injury, though both primary and metastatic tumours of skeletal muscle are rare.24 Multiple factors have been proposed to explain this. Muscle contractility may result in mechanical tumour destruction under normal circumstances. The intramuscular metabolic environment (pH, metabolites, blood pressure, and temperature) seems to prevent malignancy growth. The ability of skeletal muscles to remove tumour-produced lactic acid impedes tumour neo-angiogenesis.25–29 Changes in traumatised muscle physiology may impair these protective mechanisms, increasing the survival of metastatic cancer cells. Post-traumatic local hyperaemia may increase the risk of circulating malignant cells seeding while haematoma formation alters mechanisms of their removal.24 Moreover, authors have concluded that though the presence of micro-metastases is a prerequisite for disease recurrence, trauma may trigger tumour growth-promoting neo-angiogenesis and local release of cytokines and growth factors.30 In 2005, El Saghir et al. presented a case of a patient who was successfully treated for an isolated lung tumour with surgery and chemotherapy. At the time there was no evidence of metastasis on a brain and skull CT scan. Two years later the patient presented with a mass growing rapidly at the lateral side of his skull after a minor blunt injury, and isolated tumour recurrence in the skull was diagnosed; this was treated with radiotherapy.31

Tumours and other post-traumatic lesions are not easily distinguished and differentiated; however, accurate diagnosis is critical. Though patients with a history of trauma have been found to seek medical advice earlier than those without, delayed diagnosis is just as common.7 It may be that the trauma history distracts clinicians from the potential pathology, and appropriate investigation is not initiated as rapidly as in those without an injury. In lesions considered to be post-traumatic that do not respond to usual treatment, increase in size, or have sinister features such as an association with non-mechanical pain, the possibility of malignancy must always be considered. Giant cell tumour may be confused with several sports-related injuries. Osteosarcoma may appear similar to fracture callus radiographically, and tumours may present as pathologic fractures, sometimes difficult to differentiate from non-pathological lesions on post-injury imaging. Local scarring may be mistaken for neoplastic growth. Lesions including chondroblastoma, osteoid osteoma, Ewing's sarcoma, soft tissue sarcomas, and synovial sarcomas are malignancies that must be excluded in such circumstances. Haemorrhagic soft tissue sarcoma mimics haematoma and should be considered in cases of a spontaneous bleeding with no trauma mechanism or associated bleeding disorder that do not respond to usual treatment.10,32 Post-traumatic and postoperative neuromas are relatively common. Rarely, more worrying lesions may present in a similar way. Kos et al. published in 2013 a case of a patient with breast cancer and previous history of lumbar spine surgery who presented with a rapid growth of a nodule at the site of lumbar surgery scar. After biopsy, the lesion was defined as a malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumour. The authors conclude that this alternative diagnosis to benign post-traumatic neuroma should be considered in cases of a painful, rapidly growing mass and further diagnostic tests undertaken to exclude malignancy.33 Brust et al. published in 2021 the case of a 19-year-old patient presenting with a non-healing injury after surgery performed on the middle ear, lateral skull base, and neck for skull base trauma. There were no pathologic findings in initial histology samples. Despite this, the patient was finally diagnosed with angiosarcoma at the site after a subsequent biopsy when the lesion continued to grow. The authors highlight how the history of trauma and absence of usual signs masked the diagnosis and conclude that one can only postulate as to whether the trauma and chronic delayed wound healing initiated or accelerated tumour growth or whether the two events were unconnected.34 Injury is a common occurrence, and it is quite easy for a patient to associate a current lesion with a traumatic event, especially in more vulnerable parts of the body, including the limbs. The consequences of delayed diagnosis can be devastating. This highlights the need for careful consideration and comprehensive evaluation when assessing the potential relationship between trauma and the development of subsequent lesions and emphasises the importance of distinguishing between true causation and coincidental association. Furthermore, high clinical suspicion for a concealed tumour is appropriate, and early imaging or biopsy, as indicated by site and local protocols for diagnosis of neoplasia, is critical for successful management.

Conclusion

The current literature is insufficient for trauma to be placed on the causative pathway of musculoskeletal tumours. However, there appears to be sufficient evidence of association that further research is indicated. Given the rarity of these tumours, it would seem most likely that laboratory studies investigating relevant molecular biology would be most helpful in establishing this link should it exist. It is important for clinicians managing trauma to be aware that injury may at times mask regional tumour development, making early and accurate diagnosis challenging. Maintaining a high index of suspicion where patients report unusual symptoms or fail to recover as expected may prove lifesaving in cases of malignancy.
