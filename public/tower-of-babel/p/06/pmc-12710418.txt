A retrograde, non-canonical integrated stress response cascade maintains synaptic strength under amino acid deprivation
Authors: Megumi Mori, Grant Kauwe, Ammar Aly, Edward H Liao, Gary Scott, A Pejmun Haghighi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYNeuronal response to changes in nutrient availability is critical for maintaining metabolic homeostasis and organismal survival. Nevertheless, we know little about the molecular players that regulate and maintain neurotransmission under nutritional stress. We demonstrate that, under acute amino acid restriction, the maintenance of normal synaptic strength at the Drosophila larval neuromuscular junction critically depends on the integrated stress response (ISR) machinery. Our findings indicate that amino acid restriction triggers a non-canonical ISR cascade in muscle via GCN2 and eIF2α phosphorylation but independently of ATF4. We have identified Still life (Sif), an ortholog of human TIAM1, as a translational target of the ISR and show that it is required in muscle for mediating the action of the ISR. Our results reveal an intricate non-canonical ISR signaling cascade at the synapse and offer a new framework to separate the role of the ISR in proteostasis from its synaptic actions.

INTRODUCTION

Many nutrient-sensing mechanisms work in concert to allow for appropriate cellular processes as organisms are faced with conditions of food scarcity or abundance.1 In higher eukaryotes with more complex nervous systems, it has been appreciated that neuronal control plays an important role in sensing changes in nutrient availability as well as in orchestrating an organismal wide response to these changes.2,3 At the cellular level, inhibition of the target of rapamycin (TOR in flies, mTOR in mammals) and activation of the integrated stress response (ISR) and the subsequent phosphorylation of initiation factor 2 alpha (eIF2α) by general control non-derepressible 2 (GCN2) are primary mechanisms triggered in response to amino acid (AA) scarcity, conserved from yeast to mammals. Both of these mechanisms lead to a general inhibition of protein synthesis and, interestingly, both have been implicated in the regulation of synaptic plasticity.4–10 However, existing knowledge lacks many details about whether and how these pathways control basal synaptic activity in response to acute changes in nutrient availability. Previous work has demonstrated that mTOR participates in the retrograde enhancement of presynaptic function, a process critical for presynaptic homeostatic plasticity both at the Drosophila neuromuscular junction (NMJ)8,9 and in mammalian cultured hippocampal neurons.11,12 At the Drosophila larval NMJ, acute starvation blocks the ability of mTOR to trigger its retrograde control of presynaptic function via a FOXO and 4E-BP-dependent pathway in postsynaptic muscle, presumably by suppressing mTOR-dependent translational enhancement.9 On the other hand, we know little about ISR activation and synaptic regulation under AA restriction.

The ISR is an intricate molecular mechanism tasked with cellular protection when organisms are faced with nutrient deprivation, proteostasis disruption, viral infection, or oxidative stress.4,13 A critical step in the ISR is the phosphorylation of eIF2α, a response that is conserved in organisms from yeast to mammals. Four eIF2α kinases phosphorylate eIF2α in mammals: GCN2, in response to AA deprivation; PERK, in response to endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress and accumulation of unfolded proteins; PKR, in response to viral infection; and HRI, in response to heme deficiency in mammals. A key action of the ISR is the regulation of the ternary complex. The ternary complex is formed by coassembly of three eukaryotic translation initiation factors, eIF2α, eIF2β, and eIF2γ, together with guanosine 5′-triphosphate (GTP) and charged methionyl-initiator tRNA (Met-tRNAi).14 Once phosphorylated, eIF2α curtails the formation of the ternary complex, leading to a slowing of the rate of translation.15 Despite this general inhibition, translation of a small number of mRNAs, of which activating transcription factor 4 (ATF4) is the archetype, is enhanced because of features in their 5′ UTRs.2,14,16 ATF4 plays a critical role in mediating the downstream effect of eIF2α phosphorylation by transcriptionally activating a number of stress response genes.17 While the ISR has been implicated in synaptic plasticity previously,5,6,18,19 we lack molecular insights into how ISR activation, under metabolic stress caused by AA deficiency, is linked to the regulation of neurotransmitter release.

We have taken advantage of the powerful genetic approaches in Drosophila and the amenability of the Drosophila larval NMJ to investigate the role of activation of the ISR in the regulation of synaptic strength under AA restriction. Our findings indicate that, under AA restriction, GCN2 becomes critical in postsynaptic muscles, triggering a retrograde cascade that is required for maintaining normal presynaptic release. We further show that, as expected, eIF2α phosphorylation is essential; however, our genetic analysis indicates that ATF4 is dispensable. This surprising discovery prompted us to search for other potential translational targets. Our genetic and biochemical interrogation identified Still life (Sif), a guanine-exchange factor, as a translational target of GCN2/eIF2α in postsynaptic muscles. We show that Sif is required for the retrograde regulation of synaptic strength under AA restriction. These findings, together, point to an alternative non-canonical mode of ISR at the NMJ that is critical for the maintenance of synaptic strength as part of an organism-wide response under metabolic stress.

RESULTS

Synaptic strength critically depends on GCN2 under AA deprivation

Glutamatergic synapses at the Drosophila larval NMJ employ a wide number of mechanisms to tightly regulate synaptic strength. The powerful genetic tools and the accessible anatomy of the larval neuromuscular innervation allows for genetic interrogation of regulators of synaptic activity at a single identifiable NMJ. Our work and that of others have demonstrated that synaptic function and homeostatic regulation of synaptic strength are not only tightly regulated20 but are also sensitive to environmental stress.8,9 Drosophila larvae are tasked with foraging and eating during the short few days between hatching of the egg and the formation of the pupa, and availability of nutrients is a critical determinant of this process.2 We therefore set out to examine how acute activation of the ISR, in response to AA restriction, would influence the basal synaptic transmission at the NMJ. We found that NMJ synapses have a remarkable capacity for maintaining basal synaptic strength under AA restriction. When larvae were acutely (6 h) fed on AA-restricted food, the amplitudes of the miniature excitatory postsynaptic currents (mEPSCs) and the evoked postsynaptic currents (EPSCs) and the quantal content (QC) remained unaffected (Figures 1A–1C).

AA deprivation triggers the ISR through activation of GCN2 and, at the same time, inhibits mTOR, leading to a profound suppression of protein synthesis.4,21–23 We have previously studied the role of mTOR in the establishment of the retrograde signaling at the NMJ that underlies the ability of the NMJ to undergo homeostatic potentiation; however, inhibition of mTOR either pre- or postsynaptically has a negligible effect on the baseline synaptic transmission.8 We, therefore, investigated the role of GCN2 in the maintenance of basal synaptic strength at the NMJ. Knockdown of GCN2 using an RNAi transgene (UAS-GCN2-RNAi) was effective at reducing GCN2 transcript level by nearly 50% and dampening phosphorylation of eIF2α (Figures S1A and S1B). When larvae were raised on normal food (fed), transgenic knockdown of GCN2 in either postsynaptic muscles (Figures 1A and 1B) or presynaptic motoneurons (Figures S1B and S1C) had no effect on baseline electrophysiological measurements (Figures 1A, 1B, 1E, and 1F). However, when larvae were transferred on yeast-free food (AA restricted) for 6 h, transgenic knockdown of GCN2 in postsynaptic muscles (Figures 1C and 1D), but not presynaptic motoneurons (Figures S1B and S1C), led to a severe reduction in synaptic strength, such that the baseline EPSCs and QC were reduced by more than 40%. We found that the requirement for postsynaptic GCN2 remained under more prolonged (24 h) AA scarcity, suggesting that no adaptation had occurred at least for 24 h (Figures 1D and 1E). In other words, in order to maintain normal release in presynaptic motoneurons under AA restriction, the NMJ requires a sustained retrograde signal that is dependent on the availability of GCN2 in postsynaptic muscles.

AA restriction did not affect the average amplitude of mEPSCs, suggesting that the changes in QC are most likely due to presynaptic changes in neurotransmitter release. Furthermore, to rule out the possibility that GCN2 knockdown could have had a significant effect on synaptic structures and NMJ growth, we examined NMJ morphology. Postsynaptic knockdown of GCN2 did not affect the gross morphology of the NMJ or the number of synaptic boutons (Figures S1D and S1E). Together, these results demonstrate a critical role for GCN2 in the maintenance of synaptic strength at the NMJ under conditions of AA restriction and indicate that GCN2 exerts its function by initiating a retrograde cascade of signaling from muscle to motoneuron.

eIF2α phosphorylation is critical for the maintenance of synaptic strength under AA deprivation

Under conditions of AA scarcity, GCN2 phosphorylates its target eIF2α and thereby curtails the formation of the ternary complex, leading to a slowing of the rate of translation initiation.13,14 Consistent with activation of GCN2 under AA-deprived conditions, we detected a strong increase in eIF2α phosphorylation in muscle preparations from larvae feeding on AA-restricted food compared to normal food, as evidenced by western blot analysis of larval muscle extracts (Figures 2A and 2B). GCN2 phosphorylates eIF2α at the serine residue at position 51 in mammals and yeast24 and at position 50 in Drosophila.25 If increased phosphorylation of eIF2α were required for the maintenance of the baseline synaptic function under AA restriction, then preventing eIF2α phosphorylation should mimic the effect of GCN2 knockdown. To test the relevance of eIF2α phosphorylation, we generated transgenes containing the complete eIF2α coding sequence (with S50 intact or substituted by an alanine, eIF2αWT and eIF2αS50A) and the upstream region of the transcriptional start site (see STAR Methods) to capture endogenous expression of eIF2α (Figure 2C). We combined either transgene with eIF2α mutants, eIF2αG0272, which are otherwise adult homozygous lethal.8 Both transgenes rescued lethality in homozygous females, suggesting that adult lethality related to this allele is due to loss of eIF2α, and our pseudo-genomic rescue transgenes were acting as a good substitute for the wild-type copy of the gene (Figure S2A). Previously, we have reported that loss of function of eIF2α led to a significant reduction in the size of mEPSCs.8 We tested the ability of the eIF2αWT and eIF2αS50A transgenes to restore normal mEPSC amplitude; indeed, both transgenes were able to rescue the reduction in the amplitude of mEPSCs in hemizygous male or homozygous female eIF2αG0272 mutant larvae such that the average mEPSC amplitudes were indistinguishable from those of control larvae under normal food conditions (Figures S2B–S2D).

Having verified that these transgenes were capable of rescuing lethality in eIF2a mutants and restore synaptic defects associated with loss of eIF2a, we set out to test the relevance of S50 phosphorylation for the ability of the NMJ to maintain synaptic strength under AA restriction. Homozygous females or hemizygous male eIF2α mutant larvae that carried the eIF2αWT insert, like wild-type larvae, were able to maintain synaptic strength under AA restriction. These larvae would have preserved the ability of eIF2α to be phosphorylated. On the other hand, eIF2α mutant larvae carrying the non-phosphorylatable eIF2αS50A insert showed a more than 40% drop in average EPSC amplitude and QC (Figures 2D–2G). These findings demonstrate, as our model would predict, that phosphorylation of eIF2α downstream of GCN2 is essential for the ability of larval NMJ to maintain its synaptic strength under AA restriction.

ATF4 is not required for the maintenance of synaptic function under AA restriction

ATF4 acts as a master regulator orchestrating the cellular response when the ISR is activated.17,26,27 Therefore, we hypothesized that ATF4 is required downstream of GCN2 activation and phosphorylation of eIF2α to initiate the retrograde signaling mechanism required for the maintenance of NMJ synaptic strength under AA-restricted conditions. To test this hypothesis, we transgenically knocked down ATF4 (the Drosophila homolog of ATF4 is also known as cryptocephal, Crc) in postsynaptic muscles. Knockdown of ATF4, using a muscle-specific driver, MHC-Gal4, was highly efficient and reduced its transcript level by more than 90% (Figure S3A). Surprisingly, however, this did not replicate the effect of GCN2 knockdown or inhibition of eIF2α phosphorylation under AA restriction (Figures 3A and 3B). Spontaneous and evoked synaptic responses remained unchanged in response to postsynaptic knockdown of ATF4 under normal food or AA-restricted conditions (Figure 3B). To further assess the role of ATF4 in the retrograde control of synaptic strength under AA restriction, we examined a previously described ATF4 mutant combination (crc1/Df(2L)Excel7081) that lives to late larval stages.28 Consistent with what we observed with transgenic knockdown experiments, synaptic strength in this mutant combination was not affected after 6 h of AA restriction (Figure 3C). We have not directly assessed activation of ATF4 in postsynaptic muscles as a result of AA restriction; nevertheless, these results indicated that the maintenance of synaptic activity under AA-restricted conditions does not require the function of ATF4, pointing to the presence of alternative translational targets mediating the effect of GCN2/p-eIF2α at the NMJ.

Sif is required for the ISR response to AA restriction at the NMJ

Phosphorylation of eIF2α by GCN2 curtails the formation of the ternary complex, leading to a slowing of the rate of translation initiation.13,14 Paradoxically, a small number of mRNAs with highly structured 5′ UTRs, of which ATF4 is the archetype, are better translated when the overall rate of translation initiation is slowed in response to eIF2α phosphorylation.29 What explains the paradoxical increase in ATF4 during the ISR is the presence of a number of upstream open reading frames (uORFs) in its 5′ UTR, of which one overlaps with the main ORF (mORF).16 The surprising discovery that ATF4 is dispensable for the regulation of synaptic strength downstream of GCN2/eIF2α prompted us to set out to identify alternative potential candidates.

The complexity of the secondary structure of 5′ UTRs is an important determinant of the rate of translation.14,30 Highly complex 5′ UTRs require unwinding before the nascent ribosome can begin to scan the start codon, and the energy required for this unwinding can slow down the rate of translation initiation.31 To screen for mRNAs with complex 5′ UTRs in Drosophila, we ranked all fly 5′ UTRs based on their predicted thermal stability (ΔG) using RNAfold (ViennaRNA). We then categorized mRNAs based on the number and type of uORFs using the uORFlight database.32 uORFlight categorizes uORFs into three groups: type 1 located before the mORF, type 2 located within the mORF, and type 3, shared with the mORF. The length of 5′ UTR correlates positively with both the thermal stability (ΔG) and the number of ORFs in the 5′ UTR; however, the number of type2 ORFs shows little correlation with the length of 5′ UTR, indicating that this feature may have evolved separately (Figures S4A–S4C). Once we narrowed down the list of ranked 5′ UTRs with Gene Ontology (GO) terms relevant to synaptic function, we focused on the top 50 transcripts and selected those containing type 2 uORFs, similar to ATF4. This resulted in a list of 11 transcripts corresponding to six genes: dunce (dnc), misfire (mfr), mangetout (mtt), Rab26, Rabphilin (Rph), and still life (sif). We then tested whether knockdown of each individual gene in muscle would phenocopy GCN2 knockdown and reduce evoked synaptic currents under AA restriction. Our electrophysiological analysis identified Sif as the only one among the six whose knockdown phenocopied GCN2 (Figure 4A), high-lighting Sif as a possible mediator of the effect of GCN2/p-eIF2α.

Sif is a Rac1/Cdc42 guanine nucleotide-exchange factor (GEF) with orthologs in mammals.33,34 Indeed, a recent article reported that some of the neurological phenotypes associated with loss of sif could be rescued by transgenic expression of the human ortholog, TIAM1.34 At the fly NMJ, Sif has been reported to localize to the peri-active zones and participate in the regulation of synaptic growth and the larval NMJ. However, loss-of-function mutant combinations of sif exhibit only mild functional defects at the NMJ.33,35 Consistently, we found that postsynaptic knockdown of Sif, which was effective in reducing sif mRNA by 75% (Figure S4D), had little effect on baseline synaptic function under fed conditions (Figure 4B). On the other hand, based on our findings, Sif becomes critical under AA restriction: muscle knockdown of Sif (6 h AA restriction) greatly compromised evoked synaptic currents without affecting mEPSCs, indicating a more than 40% decrease in QC (Figures 4C and 4D). Similarly to the case of GCN2 knockdown, we saw no compensation up to 24 h of AA restriction; larvae with Sif knockdown in muscle were not able to maintain normal synaptic strength and showed more than 40% reduction in QC (Figure 4E). These results suggest that, like GCN2, Sif is required in the postsynaptic muscles to ensure normal presynaptic release when AAs are scarce.

Sif is a translational target of GCN2/eIF2α and its overexpression induces a retrograde enhancement in presynaptic release

Our bioinformatic analysis suggests that Sif is a likely candidate for translational regulation by ISR activation under AA restriction. To address this, we generated in vivo and in vitro reporters to assess translational regulation conferred by the Sif 5′ UTR. We expressed a transgene comprising the 5′ UTR of Sif upstream of eGFP and compared the fluorescence signal in muscle under fed conditions or AA restriction. Strikingly, we found a significant increase (15%, p < 0.01) in reporter activity based on eGFP signal in postsynaptic muscles following 6 h AA restriction (Figures 5A and 5B). Next, we conducted in vitro luciferase assays, where we generated luciferase reporters carrying 500 bp of the C-terminal region of Sif’s 5′ UTR, including multiple uORFs (Sif-5′UTRWT-Luc) or a reporter where we removed the start codon for the type 2 uORF (uORF7) by a single nucleotide mutation (Sif-5′UTRΔuORF7-Luc) (Figure 5C). We found that the translational activity of Sif-5′UTRWT-Luc was increased by severalfold when we destroyed the type 2 overlapping uORF7 (Figure 5D). Then, we tested the response of the two Sif-5′UTR reporters to AA restriction in HEK293T cells. Based on the behavior of type 2 uORFs, we predicted that Sif-5′UTRΔuORF7-Luc should exhibit an increase in luciferase signal under AA restriction. Indeed, our findings supported this prediction. Consistent with our in vivo reporter, in spite of the expected decrease in global translation, we measured a significant increase in luciferase activity in cells expressing Sif-5′UTRWT-Luc under AA restriction compared to Sif-5′UTRΔuORF7-Luc (Figure 5E). These findings together support a model in which translation of sif mRNA, because of the unique features in its 5′ UTR, shows a paradoxical enhancement in response to AA restriction.

To directly test whether endogenous sif mRNA is translationally regulated by AA restriction, we turned to polysome fractionation experiments using larval muscle extracts. As an expected consequence of activation of ISR, the polysome profiles showed a clear reduction in overall total protein synthesis after 6 h of AA restriction as indicated by a reduction of the peaks associated with polysomes (Figure 5F). Genomic annotation of sif (flybase.org) predicts the presence of a number of isoforms with 5′ UTRs of varying lengths; a large majority of these 5′ UTRs contain a type 2 uORF, making them prone to translational regulatio n according to our model. To test translational regulation of endogenous sif mRNA, we took advantage of polysome fractionation followed by RT-qPCR using primers that recognize the majority of sif transcripts. We pooled the RNA from fractions associated with ribosomal 60S and 80S peaks and pooled the polysome fractions into three groups of early, middle, and late polysomes. Our quantitative analysis of sif transcripts showed that, compared to actin, sif transcripts exhibited proportionally higher association with polysome fractions under AA restriction, indicating an enhancement in its translation (Figures 5G and 5H). These results together indicate that AA restriction translationally regulates the sif transcript, at least in part, through features in its 5′ UTR, identifying Sif as a target of ISR activation at the synapse.

Our findings thus far support a model in which, under AA restriction, a cascade initiated in postsynaptic muscles under the control of GCN2/p-eIF2α and mediated by Sif becomes critical for maintaining presynaptic release in motoneurons. Furthermore, our findings suggest that a critical step in triggering this retrograde enhancement of release is the translational regulation of Sif. The muscle senses the decrease in AAs, GCN2 is activated, and Sif is translationally upregulated. This model, therefore, predicts that, under normal physiological conditions, overexpression of Sif in postsynaptic muscles may be sufficient to enhance presynaptic release in motoneurons without general activation of ISR. Taking advantage of a transgene carrying only the coding sequence of Sif, we examined the consequence of Sif overexpression in postsynaptic muscles. Indeed, as our model would predict, Sif overexpression in muscles led to a more than 35% increase in the average EPSC amplitude without affecting mEPSCs, resulting in a similar increase in QC (Figures 5I and 5J). These results indicate that, while ISR activation is necessary for the fidelity of neurotransmitter release under stressful conditions, chronic activation of ISR can dysregulate synaptic function and lead to abnormal upregulation of release and potential excitotoxicity. In addition, these results suggest that, in this non-canonical cascade, the effect of the ISR on the regulation of proteostasis can be separated from its effect on synaptic function by targeting a specific translational target.

DISCUSSION

During the few days of their life, Drosophila larvae have a singular goal of eating and rapidly growing before they pupate on their way to metamorphosing into adult flies. This high rate of metabolism36 necessitates coordination between neuronal control of movement and feeding behavior to ensure metabolic goals are reached. Our multidisciplinary dataset uncovers the existence of an ISR signaling cascade acting retrogradely from muscle to motoneuron that ensures the fidelity of presynaptic release under conditions of AA scarcity in these fast-growing larvae. Genetic and electrophysiological analyses at the NMJ indicate that activation of GCN2 and the subsequent phosphorylation of eIF2α are essential for this process, but the canonical ISR transcription factor, ATF4, is dispensable. Through further biochemical and genetic interrogation, we identified Still life (Sif), a GEF, as a translational target of the ISR in muscle, which mediates the retrograde regulation of synaptic transmission at the NMJ under AA-deprived conditions. The requirement for this cascade to rise to action appears to be on the scale of hours; when GCN2 or Sif is transgenically knocked down in muscle or eIF2α phosphorylation is disrupted, within 6 h of AA deprivation the presynaptic release fails to reach the normal set point. Our findings together provide a strong body of evidence that AA deficiency is sensed by the muscle, as a metabolic hub in the organism, which then engages the ISR machinery to trigger a retrograde signaling cascade that ultimately converges on presynaptic release. We demonstrate that disruption of this non-canonical ISR pathway, while dispensable under nutrient-rich conditions, can severely compromise presynaptic release under AA-deficient conditions. An intriguing question to address in the future is how Sif sets in motion a retrograde cascade that ultimately enhances presynaptic release in a set of experiments. We speculate that Sif, as a GEF, could conceivably activate small GTPases involved in cytoskeletal and trafficking pathways, leading to the secretion of a potential retrograde signal at the NMJ. Alternatively, since Sif has been suggested to interact with the adhesion molecule Fasiculin II (FasII)37 at the NMJ at peri-active zones,38 one can envision a similar interaction between Sif and FasII or other adhesion molecules postsynaptically, which could influence the retrograde regulation of synaptic release. Future experiments will have to examine these possibilities to determine the molecular steps through which Sif regulates presynaptic function in response to activation of the ISR.

Scientists have grappled with the fundamental understanding of how neurons control their neurotransmitter output for decades,39 leading to the discovery of an intricate and vast network of proteins that act as gatekeepers and modulators of neurotransmission. The mechanistic insight into the retrograde action of the non-canonical ISR cascade through GCN2/p-eIF2α/Sif extends our current understanding of how neurotransmission maintains its fidelity when organisms are faced with metabolic stress caused by AA restriction. On the one hand, our findings reveal the role of the ISR as an endogenous protective measure; on the other hand, we demonstrate that muscle overexpression of Sif, as the downstream mediator of the ISR, alone is sufficient to retrogradely enhance presynaptic release. The ability of the gain of function of the cascade to retrogradely enhance presynaptic release under normal conditions is reminiscent of the action of the gain of function of mTOR8 and the pathogenic gain of function of the Parkinson’s disease-related gene LRRK240 at the NMJ. This further suggests that chronic stimulation of the ISR can lead to abnormal enhancement of release and potential disruption of circuit activity, which has been observed in early stages of neurodegenerative diseases.41–45 As the critical role of the ISR in health and disease from cancer to neurodegeneration is being recognized, our findings will be foundational for understanding the complex biology underlying the action of the ISR in linking metabolic stress to the regulation of neuronal function.

A major role of the ISR is to orchestrate a cellular response to disruptions in normal proteostasis; however, the ISR may have a variety of functions in the nervous system independent of its role in proteostasis.4,19 As such, strategies to target the ISR in the nervous system face the inherent challenge of causing unwanted effects. Our work offers an alternative approach by identifying a non-canonical target of GCN2/eIF2α that brings specificity to ways in which the ISR can be manipulated in the nervous system, in particular in response to metabolic stress. Interestingly, the human ortholog of Sif, TIAM1,34 appears to have a complex 5′ UTR similar to that of sif mRNA: it contains multiple uORFs, of which one is a type 2 uORF. This suggests that TIAM1 translation may be regulated in a similar manner compared with Sif, indicating functional conservation from flies to humans. Mounting evidence suggests that tissue and cell specificity may underlie the seemingly counterintuitive and contradictory outcomes of ISR activation and/or inhibition in different animal models of disease.19 Our discovery that a GEF, as a potential conserved translational target of the ISR, can mediate the downstream effect of ISR activation in response to metabolic stress holds great promise for allowing the targeting of the ISR’s synaptic function independent of its effects on proteostasis, opening new possibilities for future, alternative therapeutic approaches.

Limitations of the study

While our study clearly shows that activation of the ISR in postsynaptic muscles is required for the maintenance of presynaptic release at the NMJ under AA restriction, it does not examine the role of specific AAs in this process. It would be interesting to know which AAs, if restricted, would compromise the QC at the NMJ under a genetic block of the ISR (e.g., GCN2 knockdown). And intriguingly, there could be a scenario in which restriction of an individual or a group of AAs would be sufficient to activate the ISR but not enough to compromise presynaptic release. The current study does not delve into these questions. Similarly, while we speculate how muscle overexpression of Sif may trigger a retrograde enhancement in presynaptic release, no experiments in this study directly examine the action of Sif at the NMJ or the genetic interaction between GCN2 and Sif. Finally, previous work by our group and other laboratories has shown that activation of mTOR-dependent mechanisms in postsynaptic muscles can lead to a retrograde enhancement in release. The current study does not examine whether the underlying mechanism of mTOR-dependent enhancement and the enhancement triggered by the activation of the ISR (e.g., Sif gain of function) are distinct or overlapping. The study was not powered to determine sex effects.

METHOD DETAILS

Construction of eIF2αWT and eIF2αS50A rescue transgenes

Rescue transgenes eIF2αWT and eIF2αS50A were generated by inserting eIF2alpha promoter amplified with PCR, eIF2α coding region and 3xHA from pTHW into pUAST vector, replacing the UAS region with the eIF2α promoter. Mutation in eIF2α coding was generated with standard PCR mutagenesis. The eIF2α promoter region was amplified with primers: CGG GAT CCC CGA TTC AAG CTA CAT GTC ATT ACA TAT TTA ATG G and CCT CTA G G TT A AAG TCT GTG GTC TAT GGC CAA AAA ATG and cloned upstream of the start site. For genomic eIF2αS50A, standard mutagenesis techniques were used to mutate serine at position 50 to alanine. Clones were verified by sequencing and transgenes were again verified for the presence of the S50A mutation. Both transgenes were capable of rescuing lethality in homozygous or hemizygous eIF2a mutant flies.

Construction of 5’ UTR-Sif reporters

In vitro reporters:

Two 5’UTR-Sif reporter luciferase constructs were generated, one carrying the 500 base pairs immediately upstream of the ATG start site of Sif cloned upstream of the luciferase cassette Sif-5’UTRWT-luciferase and another where the start site of the type2 uORF, uORF7, was mutated, Sif-5’ UTRDORF7. Fragments were purchased (Integrated DNA Technologies) and were cloned upstream of the start site in the luciferase plasmid.

In vivo reporter:

Sif 5’UTR was amplified with primers CGG CTA GCA GTC AGA ACG GGA ACG TCG and CGT CTA GAT GTC TCC TCA TCG CGA GC from cDNA and inserted into the XbaI sites of pUAST-eGFP.

Luciferase assay

Luciferase assays were conducted following standard protocol as previously described8,40.

Amino acid deprivation assays: 24 hours after transfecting the HEK293T cells with the equal DNA amounts of luciferase reporter plasmids, amino acid deprivation was achieved by placing the cells in amino acid free media supplemented with 5% media containing 10% fetal bovine serum. Following 30 minutes of exposure to the amino acid limited media cells were harvested and examined for luciferase expression. HEK293T cells were not authenticated for this study.

Acute amino acid restriction (AA restriction) food and procedure

Third instar larvae (72–84 hr post hatching) were transferred to control food (1L water, 13.8g bacteriological agar, 50g sucrose, 80g corn flour, 24g brewer’s yeast, 0.5g methylparaben, 5mL EtOH, and 3mL propionic acid) or amino acid restricted food, same as above without Yeast, for the specified period of time (6 hr or 24 hr). The larval transfer time was staggered to account for the recording time between larvae. Third instar larvae were visually verified, and only mobile larvae were chosen for recording, and recordings were rejected if muscle 6 input resistance was below 4 MΩ, membrane resting potential was more positive than −60 mV.

Western blot analysis

Wandering third instar larvae were dissected in cold PBS and the muscles were collected. 10 larvae were used per biological replicate for a total of 3 biological replicates. Western blot was performed according to standard protocols. The following primary antibodies and concentrations were used: anti-phospho-eIF2α 1:1000 (S51, 119A11, Cell Signaling), anti-non-phospho-eIF2α 1:1000 (EIF2S1, ab26197, Abcam), anti-Actin 1:5000 (C4, MAB1501, Sigma-Aldrich). The following secondary antibodies and concentrations were used: HRP-conjugated anti-mouse IgG, IgM 1:2000 (A-10677, Invitrogen), HRP-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG, IgM 1:2000 (G21234, Invitrogen).

Quantitative PCR

Total RNA was extracted from muscle fillets from 10 wandering third instar larvae with standard Trizol extraction. cDNA was produced using the BioRad iScript cDNA synthesis kit (Cat No. 170-8891). qPCR data was generated using the BioRad iTaq Universal SYBR Green Supermix on the BioRad CFX96 detection system. Primers used for qPCR are indicated in Table S2.

Confocal imaging and image analysis

Muscle 6/7 from segment A3 of third instar larvae were dissected as previously described35. Briefly, third instar larvae were dissected in cold HL3 and fixed with 4% Paraformaldehyde in PBS (Phosphate buffered saline) for 10min. Larval fillets were then washed with PBS three times, permeabilized and blocked with 5% Normal Goat Serum (NGS) in PBT (PBS with 0.1% Triton X-100) for 1hr and placed in primary antibody overnight at 4°C. The larvae were then washed three times for 15min in PBT, placed in secondary antibody for 2 hr, washed three times for 15min with PBT and mounted in Vectashield (Vector labs). The following antibodies and concentrations were used for quantifying the boutons at the NMJ: anti-Dlg 1:500 (4F3, Developmental Studies Hybridoma Bank), Cy3-conjugated goat anti-HRP 1:250 (123-165-021, Jackson ImmunoResearch), Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-mouse 1:500 (115-545-166, Life Technologies). The number of boutons were quantified using confocal images acquired on LSM700 with 40x objectives.

Polysome profiling

Sucrose gradient was created by dissolving 20%, 30%, 40%, 50%, and 60% w/v sucrose (S7903, Sigma) in polysome lysis buffer (20mM Trizma Base, 10mM magnesium chloride, and 250mM sodium chloride, pH adjusted to 7.2–7.5 with hydrochloric acid and filtered using 0.22 μm vacuum filter) in ultracentrifuge tubes. The gradients were frozen at −80°C for at least 16 hours. On the day of the experiment, the gradients were thawed at RT.

Early 3rd instar larvae muscles were dissected. A total of 80 larvae were used per genotype per condition. The muscle preps were lysed in polysome lysis buffer 1x EDTA free cOmplete Tablet (04 693 159 001, Roche), 100μg/mL cycloheximide, and 0.4% NP40 using a 10mL dounce (Wheaton) on ice. The lysate was centrifuged at 4500rpm for 5 minutes and supernatant collected for further analysis.

Relative RNA concentration was measured using NanoDrop 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Volume of the lysates were normalized using the absorbance at 260 nm. The lysates were loaded to the top of the sucrose gradient and centrifuged at 38,000 rpm at 4°C for 1 hour 50 minutes using the SW41-Ti rotor in Beckman Optima™ XL-100k Ultracentrifuge (Beckman).

Fractions were collected using Biocomp Gradient Master™ (Model 108, Biocomp), Piston Gradient Fractionator (Model 152, Biocomp), Gradient Station (Model 153, Biocomp), and Model EM-1 Econo UV Monitor (731-8160, Bio-Rad). The data was recorded using Gradient Profiler 1.58 (Biocomp). The fractions were stored at −80°C until further analysis.

The polysome profile was plotted using RStudio for presentation in the figures.

RT-PCR

RNA was extracted using Trizol LS Reagent (10296028, Invitrogen) and chloroform (472476, Sigma-Aldrich) according to the manufacture’s protocols. 200 proof pure ethanol (V1001, Koptec) and 2-Propanol (I9516, Sigma) were used for RNA precipitation. RNA was reconstituted in non DEPC treated nuclease-free water (AM9938, Life Technologies). RNA concentration was measured using NanoDrop 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). 1000ng of total RNA and 120ng of RNA from each fraction was used for cDNA was synthesis from each fraction. cDNA synthesis was performed with iScript cDNA synthesis kit (1708891, Bio-Rad) according to the manufacturer’s protocols using C1000 Touch Thermal Cycler (Bio-Rad). PCR was performed using GoTaq Green 2x Supermix (M7823, Promega). 50ng of total cDNA or 5ng of cDNA from each fraction was used per PCR reaction. PCR products were visualized on 1.5% agarose gel (20–101, Genesee) in TAE stained with GelRed (41003, Biotium) imaged with Bio-Rad ChemiDoc system (Bio-Rad).

Sif transcripts were amplified with the following primers: TGGTAAAGGATCTGCGGCAA and GCCCACAACCTGAACGAACT. Actin transcripts were amplified with the following primers: TGTGTGGATACTCCTCCCGA and CACATTTTGTAAGATTTGGTGTGTT.

Electrophysiology

Wandering third instar larvae were dissected in cold HL3 solution without Ca2+ following standard protocol37,48. The spontaneous (mEPSC) and evoked (EPSC) synaptic currents were recorded from muscle 6 in abdominal segment A3 with standard two-electrode voltage-clamp (TEVC) technique49. All recordings were performed at room temperature in HL3 solution containing 0.5 mM Ca2+ for standard recording unless otherwise noted. The calcium chloride solution was added to the HL3 solution fresh on the day of the experiment. The current recordings were performed using AxoClamp 900A amplifier and Axon Digidata 1550 (Molecular Devices). The suction electrode and recording electrodes were made from borosilicate glass capillaries (1B100F-4, World Precision Instruments). The recording electrodes were filled with 3M potassium chloride solutions (see Reagent Recipes). Nerve stimulation was delivered through a suction electrode connected to ISO-Flex (A.M.P.I.). All records were subjected to 1kHz low-pass filtering during acquisition, and a Boxcar Lowpass Filter with a kernel of 11 was applied before analysis. For each NMJ, mEPSCs were recorded continuously for 3 minutes and EPSCs were recorded with 40 pulses at 0.5–1Hz. For both mEPSC and EPSC recordings, the holding potential was −80mV.

Amplitudes of mEPSCs were measured using Mini Analysis 6.0.7 software (Synaptosoft) or SimplyFire, an inhouse software47, and EJCs were measured using Clampfit 11.1 (pClamp). QC was calculated by dividing the mean EPSC amplitude by the mean mEPSC amplitude for a given NMJ.

QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

Values of n and p values are reported in the figures and figure legends, * is p < 0.05, ** is p < 0.01 and *** is p < 0.001. For all electrophysiological quantifications, we have normalized the data to the control in each set and used the geometric means followed by Welch’s t-tests, which does not assume equal variance between groups and does not require equal sample size. In all electrophysiology bar graphs, error bars represent ± 95% Confidence Intervals (CI). For all other quantifications, we have used Welch’s t-tests for pairwise comparisons and One-Way or Two-Way ANOVA with appropriate posthoc tests for 2+ comparisons.
