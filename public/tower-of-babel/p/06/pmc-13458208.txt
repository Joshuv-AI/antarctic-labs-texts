Continuous positive airway pressure usage before and after issue of an urgent product defect correction notice following a global Philips recall
Authors: Kirra Liu, Christopher Worsnop, Julie Tolson, Thomas Churchward
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFollowing the global recall of three to four million Philips continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) devices in 2021 due to emissions of harmful products, the Therapeutic Goods Administration (TGA) issued guidance recommending continuing usage, stating discontinuing treatment could be immediately harmful. At the time of the recall, Philips devices were exclusively utilised in the subsidised CPAP programme at Austin Health.AimThis study aims to evaluate the mean nightly usage of affected Philips CPAP devices before and after the TGA notice. (Hypothesis: Our hypothesis is that mean nightly usage of affected Philips CPAP devices decreased after the TGA notice.)MethodsAutomatically uploaded nightly CPAP usage data from a single centre 6 months prior to the TGA notice was compared with the data 6 months after using a paired t‐test analysis.ResultsThere were 629 Philips pumps issued in the period before the TGA notice. Usage data were available from 368 devices. Of these, 30 devices had no usage data after the notice. The average Apnoea‐Hypopnea Index was 47.7 (±25.7). Mean usage over the 6 months prior to the notice decreased by 1.33 h (±2.14) from 5.56 (±2.45) h/night to 4.23 (±3.08) with a 95% confidence interval (CI) (1.11, 1.55), P < 0.001. There was a large effect size (Cohen's d = 2.14).ConclusionThe TGA notice was associated with decreased mean nightly CPAP usage. Possibly due to concerns regarding inhalation of toxic compounds or coincidental reasons. Decreased use has implications for participant health, with function normalisation predicted with increased duration of use.

Introduction

Obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA) is a prevalent respiratory condition that carries both significant health and economic costs, affecting nearly one billion people worldwide. 1 Undiagnosed and untreated OSA in America in 2015 was estimated to cost $149.6 billion 2 and in Australia 2004, the indirect cost of sleep disorders was $7348 million. 3 The first‐line treatment for OSA is continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) therapy, which has been shown to reduce apnoeic events and improve symptoms, 4 motor vehicle accidents, 5 , 6 cardiometabolic disease 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 and cardiovascular mortality, 12 , 13 as well as improve mood and quality of life. 11 , 14 , 15 , 16 Despite these benefits, the effectiveness of CPAP is often limited by suboptimal usage. Research suggests that consistent use of over 4 h per night is necessary to achieve meaningful benefits, 17 , 18 , 19 with some studies indicating a dose‐dependent relationship exists between CPAP usage and overall health improvements. 16 To mitigate the adverse effects and reduce the broader economic impact of poorly managed OSA, it is essential for patients to use their CPAP devices consistently, with an emphasis on longer duration of use.

A significant disruption to CPAP therapy occurred on 14 June 2021, when Philips Respironics, a major CPAP manufacturer with a 42% market share, announced a voluntary recall and field safety notice internationally of three to four million devices due to potential adverse effects linked to the degradation of polyester‐based polyurethane (PE‐PUR) foam in some models. 20 The foam was reported to degrade into particulate matter and volatile organic compounds that could be inhaled, increasing the risk of respiratory issues, headaches, irritation and potential toxic and carcinogenic effects. 21 The impact of this was a global shortage of devices, 22 significantly affecting the availability of therapy for patients reliant on these devices.

On 2 July 2021 the Therapeutic Goods Administration (TGA), the Australian regulatory agency for licensing medical and therapeutic products, issued a notice to address this, with a recommendation that sudden cessation of treatment could have an immediate detrimental effect on patient health, advising patients to consult with their healthcare providers before changing their CPAP therapy. Clinicians were asked to determine whether the risks of continued use of an affected device outweighed the risks of discontinuing treatment altogether. Despite this official guidance, media coverage of the recall was often alarmist and there was a delay in access to healthcare services in the setting of a global pandemic, so it is likely that some patients made decisions to cease CPAP use without consulting their physicians.

Research has focused on investigating general CPAP device usage, 23 , 24 the negative health effects of inhalation of foam breakdown products 25 and Apnea‐Hypopnoea Index (AHI) after affected Philips device replacement to a Resmed device. 26 Only one other study to our knowledge has investigated the effect of the global upheaval in Philips devices on recalled device usage. This study surveyed sleep medicine clinicians from the American Academy of Sleep Medicine, finding almost a third of patients ceased use (26.5%) despite only 9.3% of patients being advised to do so, and 62.4% patients planned to continue usage, with 59.9% demonstrating continued treatment. 27 However, no literature to date has assessed mean nightly Philip CPAP usage before and after the TGA notice. Suboptimal CPAP use has a high burden on the patient, healthcare system and society. Therefore, it is essential to determine whether there were any changes to usage patterns after the TGA notice. Further, patterns of usage provide information on the efficacy of efforts from the manufacturers and health professionals in relaying recommendations to participants, informing future participant information dissemination. The question this project seeks to address is: Did mean nightly CPAP usage of affected Philips CPAP devices change after the TGA notice?

Our hypothesis is that mean nightly usage of affected Philips CPAP devices decreased after the TGA notice.

Materials and methods

Austin Health Funded Program for financially disadvantaged participants were allocated a subsidised Philips CPAP device. This centre's programme exclusively utilised Philips devices at the time of the recall, and participants were not in a position to purchase or hire a replacement. Once it was set up, CPAP usage data were collected every 24 h and automatically uploaded and stored in a cloud‐based database. These data are based on the pressure in the mask, so they reflect real usage of the device, not just whether or not it was switched on. The upload of data requires 3G reception and for the machine to be turned on. However, backdating of data occurs when these conditions are next met. A period of 6 months was chosen as patterns of usage are generally determined in the first weeks of CPAP uptake 28 ; thus, usage is likely to be established by this time and changes not attributable to issues that arise in the initial stages of use. Inclusion criteria: device usage data from participants with affected CPAP devices supplied by the Austin Health Funded Program in the 6 months before and after the TGA Notice. Exclusion criteria: device usage data from participants with CPAP devices supplied by the Austin Health Funded Program who were not affected by the TGA notice or who had an affected device but for whom usage data were missing in the 6 months before the TGA notice, or after, if verification of missing CPAP usage data was unable to be determined as cessation of use, or participants who had died during the pre‐ or post‐TGA notice period. Deaths from any cause were flagged for lack of CPAP usage data and confirmed by follow‐up phone calls to the participant or next of kin. For those with CPAP usage data prior to the TGA notice but without usage data afterwards, cessation of use was verified by confirming issuance with a refurbished device assigned in Care Orchestra. If there was zero usage after the date it was assigned, we concluded the participant had ceased use.

Participants were initially sent a letter as a TGA requirement. However, due to confusion regarding the guidelines, participants were called to confirm registration of their device and to determine requirements for consultation regarding their device. These phone calls did not specifically enquire into current and intended use; however, notes regarding participant registration and pertinent information were recorded at the time. Average CPAP nightly usage data were retrieved from the cloud‐based database by individually searching a participant name, navigating to the therapy data tab and creating a sleep summary report with custom data ranges. In this way, two reports for each participant were generated, with data from 2 January 2021–2 July 2021 and 3 July 2021–3 January 2022. The average usage (all days) of CPAP device and demographic information was then collated. Participant information was de‐identified and stored in a different location. Those who had a CPAP start date after the allotted 2 January 2021 had their CPAP use averaged over the period of their start date to the end date of the 6‐month period before the TGA notice. These data were then analysed in SPSS version 28.0.0.1.(14) with a paired t‐test statistical analysis to assess mean difference of nightly CPAP usage before and after the TGA notice. The effect size (Cohen's d) was calculated. All other assumptions of the paired t‐test were satisfied.

In an analysis of CPAP nightly usage 6 months before and after the TGA notice, 1.1% made no changes to their use (n = 4), 23.0% increased their use (n = 84), 76.1% decreased usage (n = 280), and 6.1% of those participants with decreased use ceased use altogether (n = 17). The results of the paired t‐test revealed that the mean CPAP nightly usage decreased after the TGA notice (x̄ = 4.23, s = ±3.08) in comparison to before (x̄ = 5.56, s = ±2.45). An overview of each participant's mean nightly 6‐month usage before and after the TGA notice can be seen in Figure 2, with the difference in participants' mean nightly usage seen in Figure 3. The 95% confidence interval (CI) for the mean difference was (1.11, 1.55), indicating that there was a difference in means between the two time points. A paired sample t‐test confirmed that the difference (x̄ d = 1.33, s d = 2.14, P < 0.001) in CPAP nightly usage was statistically significant, t(367) = 11.94, P = <0.001, and Cohen's d (d = 2.14) showed that this effect size was large. Discussion

The statistical analysis revealed a significant decrease in mean nightly CPAP usage of recalled devices following the TGA notice, despite recommendations to continue use and prior research highlighting only a small proportion of participants were advised by a clinician to discontinue. 27 This suggests that, at some level, there may have been insufficient or ineffective dissemination of health information to participants or a lack of uptake of this information, affecting adherence.

CPAP usage after the TGA notice

In one study, it was noted that most participants were first notified of the recall from media outlets. 27 Given the extended waitlist times experienced during COVID‐19 and disruption to access to healthcare services, it is likely that for a lot of participants, initial decisions about device usage were based on publicly available information in the lay media, without input from a healthcare professional. A plausible explanation for the observed decline in recalled device usage after the TGA notice is that participants, concerned about the potential adverse health effects of inhaling PE‐PUR products, chose to reduce or cease device usage independently as they did not have the opportunity to consult with a respiratory clinician. During follow‐up phone calls, many participants expressed significant anxiety about the potential health risks associated with recalled devices, with some choosing to discontinue use as a precaution. Further, many participants expressed preference for advice in consultation with a clinician, suggestive of a desire for additional information to guide ongoing device usage. These calls, while helpful in providing some insight into participant concerns, were limited to confirming registration and were not structured to comprehensively assess CPAP usage patterns. While some participants acknowledged the guidelines directed by the TGA notice and indicated they would continue use of their device, there were no corresponding data to confirm this behaviour, suggesting that many did not adhere to the recommendation despite their initial intent. This is consistent with results from one survey, where 9.3% of participants were advised to discontinue use of their affected device; however, a much higher proportion of 26.5% participants were recorded to have ceased usage. 27

A 6‐month pre–TGA notice period was analysed to capture established CPAP usage patterns; however, several methodological and practical factors may have influenced post‐recall usage data. Disruptions such as hospitalisation, travel, relocation to areas with limited reception, device misplacement or intentional shutdown may have reduced recorded use. In some cases, usage data were unavailable due to failed or delayed uploads related to 3G connectivity issues, prolonged device inactivity, batch registration delays or modem malfunction. Additionally, some participants could not be contacted for device registration, with missing data potentially reflecting relocation overseas, travel or unconfirmed death. Distinct demographic patterns were observed in usage following the TGA notice, suggesting use was influenced by both disease severity and participant characteristics. Participants who decreased or ceased CPAP use were generally older and had lower baseline body mass index (BMI) and AHI, consistent with milder OSA and a lower perceived risk associated with treatment interruption. This is consistent with prior findings of decreased usage patterns in those with milder OSA. 25 , 28 These participants may have been advised to temporarily suspend therapy while awaiting replacement given short‐term discontinuation was unlikely to result in significant adverse outcomes. Other possible influences include a heightened risk perception surrounding device safety following the recall among older participants. Socio‐economic factors may have further influenced usage patterns, as participants with greater financial means may have accessed alternative devices, leading to reduced recorded use of recalled Philips devices or missing usage data altogether. In contrast, those with increased CPAP use were younger and demonstrated higher BMI and AHI, suggesting more severe disease and a greater perceived need to maintain therapy despite potential risks. Previous research identified younger age as a negative predictor of CPAP adherence. 25 , 28 The difference in our findings may be because younger participants with higher severity OSA were more willing to accept potential risks to mitigate morbidity and may have been better informed about TGA recommendations through greater media engagement. Notably, sex distribution and ESS did not appear to strongly influence changes in CPAP use. Overall, objective measures of disease risk and severity and some demographic factors played a more prominent role than subjective symptoms in shaping patient decision‐making following the recall.

Implications for clinical practice

Frequent and sustained CPAP use, particularly in the initial stages of uptake, is a significant predictor of therapeutic success. 29 The global Philips recall and TGA notice offer important insights into how large‐scale disruptions of essential medical equipment can influence participant adherence, even when established usage habits are in place. To date, there has been one other study that has quantified the effect of the worldwide Philips recall and TGA notice on recalled device usage. 27 The findings from this study indicate a decrease in recalled device usage out of proportion to recommendations to cease usage. 27 However, they did not quantify the change in usage before and after the global upheaval. In our study, while both time points surpassed the 4‐h threshold, which is generally considered adequate for therapeutic efficacy, evidence suggests that longer and more consistent use is associated with function normalisation and better clinical outcomes. 16 , 30 Every additional hour of CPAP use, up to 9 h, has been shown to reduce hospitalisations (by 5.4%–9.7%) and emergency visits (by 5.1%–7.4%). 31 Even a single night without CPAP can reverse the positive effects on sleepiness. 32 The exact timeframe to optimise functioning is likely to be dependent on the outcome measured; nevertheless, the implication is that increased duration and frequency of use is necessary to achieve the best outcomes. 16 While we cannot say whether CPAP usage decreased as a result of the TGA notice, we can conclude that there was a significant reduction in recalled device use after the TGA notice in this clinical cohort. Given the significant health and financial costs associated with suboptimal CPAP use, this study serves as an important foundation for future research on the impact of product recalls and defect notices on CPAP usage patterns.

Limitations

This single‐centre study, despite its broad catchment, has limitations on generalisability to other populations. Interpretation of subgroup analyses due to unequal group sizes is limited, particularly among participants with unchanged or ceased CPAP use. Data upload was inconsistent and, in some instances, incomplete and unable to be verified. CPAP devices automatically upload usage data in real time once activated; therefore, for most participants with no data after the TGA notice, it is reasonable to assume that their devices were no longer in use. To distinguish true cessation from data unavailability, participants with pre‐ but no post‐TGA notice data were cross‐referenced with Care Orchestra records – if a refurbished replacement device had been issued without subsequent recorded use, cessation of use was inferred. However, the precision of this process was complicated by the batch upload of serial numbers during the Philips device rectification period. Cessation status could not be conclusively determined for 30 of 47 participants due to the absence of a registered replacement device, failure of patient linkage to a device, modem malfunction or technical errors preventing data upload, and they were excluded. To assess the impact of attributing these as missing data on the primary findings, a double analysis of data extremes – treating missing data as ‘missing’ or as ‘zero’ – was completed with comparable results, providing reassurance that any bias related to data classification was unlikely to have affected the study conclusions. Nevertheless, these limitations highlight the challenges of interpreting real‐time device usage data during periods of large‐scale device disruption. Ultimately, it is not possible to determine the precise reason(s) for the decrease in CPAP usage due to the TGA notice, as the data collected were quantitative reports of usage directly uploaded from CPAP devices. While the follow‐up phone calls provide some insight into the intentions of participants at the time, they do not provide specific information regarding CPAP usage and explanations for changes in use. A follow‐up survey of participants without post‐TGA notice data could provide further insights into their CPAP usage patterns.

Future outlook

Further research is needed to explore the reasons behind the cessation of CPAP usage after an initial period of consolidated use. This would help clarify whether the observed decrease in usage following the TGA notice is directly attributable to the recall or if other factors played a role. Such research could provide valuable insights into participant perspectives and inform future strategies for the dissemination of product safety information. Additionally, further investigation of demographic predictors of CPAP adherence could help identify high‐risk groups that require more targeted interventions to improve adherence.

Conclusion

This study highlights the significant impact of the global Philips device recall and TGA notice on participant adherence to CPAP therapy. Many participants with recalled devices did not follow the national TGA guidelines to continue usage of their recalled device as per prior usage habits, suggesting that concerns over potential health risks, limited access to timely medical advice and other external factors played a role in this decline. Given the dose‐dependent therapeutic benefit of device usage, the decrease in usage after the TGA notice could have important implications for participant health and the broader economic burden of OSA. It is therefore imperative to optimise strategies to provide timely, accessible medical information to participants to mitigate the adverse effects of suboptimal OSA treatment.
