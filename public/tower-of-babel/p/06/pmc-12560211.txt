From Human Child to Grey Parrot: Exploring a Common Model of Word Meaning Extension Across Species
Authors: Michal Fishkin, Shereen Chang, Yang Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWord meaning extension refers to the process by which a single word form develops multiple related meanings. Prior studies demonstrate that meaning extension at diverse timescales, from decades‐long historical change and to month‐long changes in child overextension, is accounted for by models grounded in conceptual relations across knowledge types. Whether this framework generalizes to other species remains an open question. We address this question with a probabilistic model of overextension based on various knowledge types to predict word choice of nonhuman animals. As a starting point, we compared cases of overextension from Apollo — a grey parrot who has acquired some English words — to the cases of overextension documented in child language acquisition. We apply an established model of child overextension to a novel parrot dataset of over 200 referent‐utterance pairs (e.g., bead‐“ball”) collected from Apollo's YouTube channel and test whether the child model can predict parrot word choice. Our results show that Apollo's overextension can be predicted by the multimodal model of child overextension better than baseline models that rely on frequency or sound similarity. We also find independent evidence supporting the role of different knowledge types from Alex, a grey parrot, who features prominently in prior research on animal acquisition of human language. Our findings suggest that a common model might account for the cognitive ability of word overextension identifiable in a species that diverged from humans about 320 million years ago. We discuss potential limitations and future research directions that may further strengthen the current findings.Keywords: Word meaning extension, Overextension, Evolution, Comparative cognition, Animal semantics, Chaining, Multimodal model

Introduction

A key function of the human lexicon is to support the expression of emerging or novel ideas within a limited vocabulary. One solution is the development of multiple meanings in a single word form. For example, the word “face” originally denoting a body part was extended to express both a visage and the side of a cube.

How words develop new meanings is typically not random, and often these meanings are related to one another through a process known as word meaning extension (abbreviated as WME) (Ramiro, Srinivasan, Malt, & Xu, 2018; Brochhagen, Boleda, Gualdoni, & Xu, 2023; Bréal, 1897). Existing research has shown that the extension of word meanings attested in the historical development of English and across languages tends to operate by relating similar items or based on semantic or conceptual relatedness (Brochhagen et al., 2023; Ramiro et al., 2018; Xu, Duong, Malt, Jiang, & Srinivasan, 2020).

Word meaning extension occurs not only in historical periods but also in shorter time frames through child language development. In particular, overextension is a well‐documented phenomenon observed in young children between the ages of 1 and 2. Overextension refers to “the use of a word for a broader range of referents than is conventional in adult usage” (Rescorla, 1980). Similarly to historical meaning extension, overextension is often understood as involving conceptual knowledge (Rescorla, 1980), although instances of overextension tend to be more spontaneous and transient. An example of overextension would be a child using the word “cat” (the utterance) to refer to all four‐legged furry mammals (the referents). Recent work suggests that products of word meaning extension from history and child development are derived from a common core, namely, a shared foundation of conceptual knowledge. Importantly, a common model that draws on multifaceted knowledge is shown to explain a wide range of meaning extension patterns in historical language change, as well as word choices of child overextension in language development (Brochhagen et al., 2023).

However, humans are not alone in our ability to refer to things with different vocalizations, as demonstrated by previous research on dolphins, elephants, and monkeys (Janik, Sayigh, & Wells, 2006; Oren et al., 2024; Pardo et al., 2024; Seyfarth, Cheney, & Marler, 1980). Similarly, a preference for conceptual relatedness has also been found in nonhuman animals: Chemla et al. (2019) found that baboons learned rules quicker when there was some degree of connectedness in the stimuli, suggesting the importance of conceptual relatedness as a prelinguistic cognitive ability inherited from our evolutionary ancestors. These studies allude to human‐like cognitive and linguistic capabilities in nonhumans.

Inspired by these prior studies, we ask whether humans and nonhumans might have a shared ability for word meaning extension. To our knowledge, this question has been underexplored in previous work. We approach this question by exploring the possibility of a common model to account for word choice in overextension across species, hence extending the recent framework that explores a shared model of word meaning extension in both children and adult populations (Brochhagen et al., 2023).

Our initial objective is to identify cases of word‐meaning extension that permit cross‐species comparison. Historical records of meaning extension typically document conventionalized uses shaped by (generations of) adult humans, which makes them difficult to compare with the more individualized and spontaneous word use observed in nonhuman animals. In contrast, children are a population whose overextensions tend to be transient and are often recorded on an individual basis. For these reasons, children may be a suitable reference for comparison with other species. We, therefore, focus on an initial study drawing on word overextension in human children, as well as in a nonhuman species.

Overextension can be distinguished from other types of word production such as sound error, misspeaking, or babbling, because, as Clark asserted, children could comprehend the correct referent labels despite not using them in production (Clark, 1978). This production‐comprehension asymmetry is an indication that the child is actively using their conceptual knowledge of the world to attribute multiple meanings to a specific word. Related to this idea, Vygotsky, an early observer of child overextension, proposed the notion of a chain complex, in which a single word is used to refer to different concepts that are linked together in a logical manner within conceptual space. (Vygotsky, 1986). In a similar vein, Rescorla (1980) identified three main knowledge modalities that typically underlie the conceptual relationships observed in child overextension:

We build on these empirical observations and examine whether a model drawing on similar modalities might also account for word choices related to overextension by nonhuman animals.

Research framework

Previous work has developed probabilistic multimodal models that draw on the three types of knowledge described to predict word‐referent pairings attested in child overextension (Ferreira Pinto & Xu, 2021). In that study, each “modality” represents a type of knowledge described in Rescorla (1980). More recently, a similar approach has been taken showing that a multimodal framework like that for child overextension also accounts for meaning extensions attested in historical and cross‐linguistic settings (Brochhagen et al., 2023). This work suggests that meaning extensions occurring at societal timescales and those occurring at shorter timescales in child language development (specifically, overextension) rely on a shared cognitive foundation of conceptual relations that are grounded in different knowledge types. However, such a modeling framework has not been used to predict overextensional behavior of nonhuman species.

In our study, we compare potential cases of overextension in animals with overextension in human children by applying a formal computational framework previously used to analyze child overextension (Ferreira Pinto & Xu, 2021). To carry out our analysis, we implement the established child overextension model from Ferreira Pinto and & Xu (2021). This model approximates the knowledge types described by Rescorla with categorical information drawn from WordNet (Miller, 1994), visual‐analogical information from ImageNet (Deng et al., 2009), and predicate information from a word association dataset called the Small World of Words (SWoW) (De Deyne, Navarro, Perfors, Brysbaert, & Storms, 2018). This model was trained on child overextension data and was, therefore, optimized to predict how children overextend words.

Knowing whether the child overextension model can predict animal overextension offers us a starting point to understand the evolutionary trace of word meaning extension. If a nonhuman agent were only babbling, or trying their luck at picking out the correct words based on sound similarity, our application of a child overextension model drawing on multiple (semantic) knowledge types should yield poor model performance; if the animal were extending a word to a related concept in a manner similar to how children do so, our model should reasonably predict some of the word choices. Moreover, if the model relies on some knowledge types as better predictors of meaning extension than others, we might learn how similar the animal's conceptual map is to that of human children in these well‐predicting knowledge types.

There are a few common pitfalls in animal studies that we want to acknowledge and avoid. The first and most important is the differentiation between meaningful word use compared to operant conditioning or babble‐luck. Operant conditioning occurs when an animal learns to associate an action, such as the production of a word, with a prompt, such as a presented object, usually by reinforcement with rewards (Staddon & Cerutti, 2003). One way to rule out that a behavior is caused by operant conditioning is by prompting the agent under varying conditions or with novel stimuli. For example, if an agent is trained to use “red” by labeling a red bucket, and if they understand the meaning of that color word, then presenting a red teddy bear should result in them also labeling it “red.” Otherwise, the agent was conditioned to say “red” only in the presence of a red bucket (the operant), and may not be internalizing the concept.

Babble‐luck occurs when the agent chooses a word at random until they receive an award or a response from the trainer (Thorndike, 1911). To rule out the possibility of babble‐luck, we would require that the agent's mistakes during normal training are not random but conceptually consistent. That is, if the random model or frequency and sound baselines for word production outperform all of the knowledge types reported to underlie overextension, we can attribute the parrot's successes to babble‐luck rather than to meaning extension.

Additionally, we face the problem of seeing overextensions where there are none. Even if an agent labels an unfamiliar object in a conceptually appropriate way, the possibility of babble‐luck remains. Consider some classic examples from Washoe the chimpanzee, who used “water bird” to label a swan and “rock berry” for Brazil nuts (Fouts & Mills, 1997). Without more information, it is difficult to determine whether these are cases of innovative word use in composite word formation, similar to that in children, or whether Washoe merely signed individual words in sequence, for example, “water” and then “bird,” without combining them into a novel concept. The poor control of experiments, cherry‐picking of data, and subsequent overinterpretation of animal abilities have caused a serious decline in animal intelligence studies in the past (Pepperberg, 2016).

To minimize these pitfalls, we consider Pepperberg's research into parrot word use as a basis for our study. Her studies have established symbolic communication, concept transfer along several dimensions—color, material, shape (Pepperberg, 2002)—and even neologisms (Chang, 2025; Pepperberg, 2009) to fall into the capability of Psittacus erithacus, the grey parrot. Overextension among the analogical axis is, therefore, at least possible, meaning that the data we collect and analyze, even if subject to selection bias, could still be a genuine case of meaning extension.

Our primary choice of agent is Apollo, an African Grey featured in his owners' YouTube Channel “Apollo and Frens” (Lacey Mason & Mason, 2024). The channel contains years worth of videos of Apollo's training, self‐babbling, and linguistic development. He is trained to identify various properties, and the prompt objects are frequently changed to prevent operant conditioning.

In our analysis, we apply a child overextension model to the data of Apollo the grey parrot. We compare the model performance and dominant knowledge types (learned from the model) between the animal and human agents and suggest that there is evidence for child‐like overextension capabilities in parrots.

We hypothesize that a unified model, drawing on the three knowledge types described by Rescorla and trained solely on child overextension data as in Ferreira Pinto and & Xu (2021), can predict the alleged cases of overextension observed in a grey parrot. All codes and data are made available online. 1

Materials and methods

Prior models of child overextension

Existing models of word meaning extension build off the idea of semantic chaining to predict historical patterns of meaning extension (Ramiro et al., 2018). This approach also has roots in the chain complex as described by Vygotsky (1965) in the context of overextension, wherein a child learns the word “quah” for a duck, and then extends “quah” to the water in which a duck floats, and then to any liquid including the milk in his bottle. The way the child attributes more meanings to the word “quah” is done by the chaining of one related concept to the next. This chaining is operationalized into a probabilistic multimodal model that integrates different knowledge types as in Ferreira Pinto and & Xu (2021), upon whose work we build. By integrating semantic similarity (or semantic distance) into the computational model, we allow for a multidimensional semantic space to capture the conceptual relations of children and, theoretically, parrots. The overextension model from Ferreira Pinto and & Xu (2021) was not only accurate at predicting child overextensions but also replicated Clark's production‐comprehension asymmetry, making it a reliable model for the empirical observations.

Probabilistic inference for nonhuman communication has been explored before, as in Franke et al. (2024). In that study, researchers used Bayesian probabilistic inference to construct a matrix that mapped intent to gesture to action in orangutans. The difference in our study is that we use a probabilistic framework to model parrot's word production, without considering the “signaling game” associated with multiparty communication. In the next section, we formalize the model and operationalize the knowledge types that it incorporates in computational terms.

Model specification

The input to the model is a string that indicates the referent concept. The output of the model L={a0,a1,…,am} is a set of descending ranked lists, one for each modality in Table 1. Each element of am=(wm,0,wm,1,…wm,n) is a word from Apollo's vocabulary. They are arranged in decreasing probability for the given referent and modality, that is, p(wm,k|cref)≥p(wm,k+1|cref). Note that while the number of words is n, the number of rankings is nr≤n, since candidates can be equidistant from the referent concept. For example, suppose that the categorical distances between candidates PIG and DOG from referent COW are identical, as both are nonbovine mammals. This would result in a tie in the categorical ranking of the words when the referent word is “cow.” This ranking of the word, rw, corresponds to its probability, not its index in the output list.

The sensitivity parameter, h, controls the rate of overextension (Ferreira Pinto & Xu, 2021). Intuitively, a large h would cause a larger similarity between any two concepts, which would correspond to an agent using a single cognitively “easy” word (e.g., a frequent word or one easy to produce) to refer to many different concepts, and thereby increase the rate of overextension; a small h would, by the opposite mechanism, correspond to a decreased rate of overextension, where the conceptual difference between referents is too great to be captured by the easiest word to produce. Baselines

We consider two types of baselines that predict word choice using strategies different from the multimodal overextension model. The first type of baseline postulates that the agent makes use of sampling‐based strategies in word production toward a referent. Specifically, we consider two common ways of sampling: random or uniform sampling, and frequency‐based sampling, where the agent is more likely to utter a frequent word than a less frequent word. The second type of baseline model postulates that the agent produces noisy word choices, particularly due to sound or phonetic confusion, which is attested in parrot (and child) word production. These baselines differ from the overextension model we described in that they do not depend on the multimodal space of conceptual relations in word choice prediction.

For the random baseline, the expected ranking of a given word is halfway down the ranked list, giving Rrandom=0.5.

For the frequency baseline, we extract a vocabulary V from the utterances in the data and estimate each word's relative frequency. The frequency baseline ranking metric, Rfreq(w), for any vocabulary entry w, corresponds to the rank of the word when V is sorted by relative frequency.

For the sound model, we used the Carnegie Mellon University Pronouncing Dictionary (2015) to look up a phonetic ARPABET representation of all the words in V. We then construct a matrix that records the Levenshtein Distance between any two words in V (Levenshtein, 1965). For a given referent c, the sound baseline ranking metric of any candidate word w is given by Rsoundc(w), where Rsoundc is the ranking of words by their pronunciation Levenshtein distance from c. As an example with the toy vocabulary V={crack,plant,block,black}, Rsoundplant(block)=0, Rsoundcrack(block)=0.5, and Rsoundblack(block)=1.

We should expect the sound baseline to be a strong predictor in cases where the agent made an error in production, such as mixing up production mechanisms for similar consonants. In contrast, assuming the agent engages in word‐meaning extension in a manner comparable to that of children, the multimodal model should outperform baseline models when the intended referent is not part of the agent's vocabulary. In the next section, we describe how the semantic space comprised of different knowledge types is constructed.

Semantic space construction

We operationalize the semantic space by considering the three knowledge types described in Rescorla (1980) and Ferreira Pinto and & Xu (2021), as follows:

Categorical. Rescorla (1980) gives no specific categorization framework, so we replicate (Ferreira Pinto & Xu, 2021) by using WordNet's hierarchical synset structure. The categorical distance between two words is given by the WordNet synset‐to‐synset Wu‐Palmer Similarity (wup_similarity).

Analogical → Visual. Due to technological limitations, distance in the analogical dimension is simplified to only account for visual factors. The analogical modality is impoverished in our model, as both children and parrots would have other cues upon which to draw (e.g., tactile, auditory). Using the images in each word's ImageNet2012 synset folder, we retrieve a visual embedding for each concept using the output of the first ReLU layer of the VGG19 convolutional model (Ferreira Pinto & Xu, 2021).

Predicate‐based → Associative. We approximate predicate‐based distance between concepts using a word‐association distance measure. This approach simplifies the account given by Rescorla (1980), who examined a wide variety of relationship types when identifying predicate connections between concepts, and who also accounted for individual idiosyncrasies in his analysis. For example, while the general public would not associate the word “doll” with “house,” a child who is playing with a dollhouse might use the former to refer to the latter. The basis of our metric is the Small World of Words English dataset (De Deyne et al., 2018). We build a graph of word associations and run a decaying random walk to approximate the associative similarity of two concept words.

Parrot overextension

Pepperberg has done extensive studies of parrot word learning. Her work with an African grey parrot named Alex spanned several years and provided support for complex cognitive abilities in P. erithacus. Among other tasks, Pepperberg was able to train Alex to vocally identify objects by their features. For example, when shown a green wooden triangle, Alex would respond to the prompt “What is this?” with the label “green three‐corner wood.” Importantly, he was also able to accurately respond to categorical questions about the objects presented—questions like “what color?” or “what shape?” (Pepperberg, 2002). The ability to extract and identify features of an object is fundamental to categorical and analogical overextension, making Alex a promising agent to analyze for our purposes. However, many of the stimuli to which Alex was exposed occurred in the laboratory setting, which may present differences from those of a comparable home environment in which children overextend. Additionally, there is limited data exemplifying Alex's capacity for word meaning extension.

Due to these limitations, we pursued a different data source. Our pick as agent, Apollo the grey parrot, was raised in a home environment with his two handlers and has a large online repository of information regarding his labeling. A parrot raised in a home may be a better point of comparison to children, especially if the parrot was also exposed to unfamiliar objects whose appropriate labels were outside of his vocabulary.

Apollo then produces a word from his vocabulary (utterance). We define an agent's vocabulary as the set of words the agent is able to produce, and we define the lexicon as the set of distinct items in the agent's conceptual space. We assume each distinct item in conceptual space has a corresponding word in linguistic space, which may or may not be in the agent's vocabulary. For example, Apollo might know the word for “ball” but not for “apple.” In this case, “apple” is in his lexicon but not in his vocabulary. There is some uncertainty as to when parrots make distinctions between concepts, and this is an area that would require further investigation. For this study, we assume all distinct words map to distinct concepts in the agent's conceptual space.

We abbreviate “referent‐utterance pair” as RUP, and we collected 290 RUPs (the raw set) from YouTube Shorts featuring Apollo labeling objects. Based on personal communication with Apollo's owners, the published videos are typically an accurate representation of Apollo's answering when he was focused or “locked in.” 2 The editing only requires a reordering of the question sequence for viewer retention, removing duplicated questions, and removing the constant restating of questions remaining; that is, “Okay Apollo, three/two/one more question(s) to earn a pistash[io].” On average, the owners gave Apollo two attempts at each question before moving on to the next.

We begin our analysis by showing two examples of the child‐overextension model prediction based on Apollo's data, as visualized in Figs. 3 and 4. Fig. 3 visualizes ranked model output given the input RUP bead,ball, as seen in Table 2. The visual knowledge type is dominant in this case, ranking “ball” in first place. All other knowledge types performed worse than the frequency baseline, and the categorical type performed worse than chance. The model indicates that if Apollo is extending the word “ball” to the concept BEAD, it is because of a perceived visual similarity.

Fig. 4 visualizes the ranked model output when given the RUP book,bowl. This is an instance in which Apollo made a mistake during training, that is, his trainer presented him with an object for which the correct label was in Apollo's vocabulary, but Apollo uttered the incorrect word. The notable difference between this case and that seen in Fig. 3 shows that the former mistake is attributed to sound‐based error that is dissociated with semantic information. Importantly, this distinction is also reflected in the poor model performance of the different knowledge types when compared to the sound and frequency baselines. Since “ball” and “bowl” are phonetically close, only changing one vowel, the excellent performance of the sound prediction is expected; likewise, the poor semantic performance is due to BOOK and BOWL being distant concepts in semantic space. This contrast suggests that he is not extending the word “bowl” to the concept BOOK in a semantically meaningful way.

Model performance by data type

We want to verify that the model, which is trained on child overextension data, can predict the word choice of Apollo on a broad scale. To evaluate the model across the RUPs we collected, we calculate the Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR) across all the target referents. MRR is a common metric for evaluating the effectiveness of a ranking algorithm, typically used to optimize search engines. We require that the model does not result in high MRR scores for arbitrary RUPs, that is, the model can discriminate between randomly assigned referent‐utterance pairs and pairs that have the potential to be instances of word overextension. To verify the robustness of the model, we permute the mappings between referents and utterance entries and assign them randomly to each other, avoiding self‐referral so that no utterance refers to the referent concept. We expect the model performance, measured by MRR, on the permuted dataset would be significantly poorer than its performance on the original dataset, which contains the in‐vocabulary cases of overextension.

To ensure that our models are capturing patterns of overextension and not any arbitrary or noisy patterns of word choice, we conducted the permutation test where word‐referent pairings were randomly shuffled to create a hypothetical or null condition. We compared the model performance in the original data (without permutation) to that in the permuted trials (1000 of them) in a standard permutation test, and we found that the multimodal model in the original data to be significantly better in MRR compared to itself in the permuted trials (p<.005 for datasets All, OoV, and iV). This parallels the children's cases, which are dominantly predicted by semantic knowledge types as opposed to sound. Additional analysis of Alex

So far, our analysis has focused on a single parrot, and it is unclear whether overextension may be present in other parrots or whether the modalities identified in the overextension model may be generalizable.

To probe this issue, here we analyze an independent dataset from Pepperberg's research with Alex (Pepperberg, 2002). The dataset was made available in Table 13.1 of Pepperberg (2002), based on a labeling experiment that Pepperberg conducted with Alex. Similar to the iV cases of Apollo, Alex was trained to learn referential mapping between words and nonfood items in this task where the goal of the study had been to investigate whether Alex's sound play may be transformed into meaningful vocalizations (see Chapter 13 of Pepperberg, 2002).

As an example, in this dataset, Alex was attested to extend the word “water” to express the referent concept of shower (and he did so four times, suggesting that this is not by accident), presumably due to an association between water and shower. As another example, the dataset also includes cases where Alex made an apparent sound‐based error, such as using the word “chalk” to express truck. Unlike the experiments with Apollo, Pepperberg used stimuli confined to a lab setting and allowed for many repetitions with Alex in this task when the parrot was prompted to produce a word for each target referent. For example, for the referent of shower, Alex was prompted 32 times. In most of the cases, Alex was able to produce the correct word (e.g., he got 28 correct responses in 32 repetitions, the remaining four being the overextensional instances of “water”). Across all the referent stimuli, Pepperberg reported that Alex achieved an accuracy in the range of 66.7–90.3%.

We applied the overextension model that we used for Apollo on Alex, based on a truncated version of Table 13.1. We focused on the data that Pepperberg reported where Alex did not produce the correct word for a given referent. In particular, we assess whether Alex exhibits similar use of the three knowledge types in his RUPs, and we compare them to sound‐based error through the child overextension model.

Alex's raw lexicon based on the data we had access to consists of 22 words. We were able to use 17 of these which have appropriate ImageNet and WordNet synsets. In Alex's case, we assume that his vocabulary is the same as his lexicon, and we obtained 29 RUPs from Table 13.1 of Pepperberg (2002). Of these, 21 are RUPs where both the referent and the utterance are in the lexicon, that is, they have ImageNet and WordNet synsets. We also removed any RUPs where either the referent or the production is a adjective‐noun pair, such as “grey wood,” as opposed to a single word because the resources we worked with do not contain compositional concepts.

Fig. 6 summarizes the dominant knowledge types as output from the child overextension model. We observed that similar to the iV case of Apollo, Alex's case involves the three knowledge types 67% of the time, in comparison to cases of sound error, which takes up 33%. These results are analogous to those of Apollo's iV case, where the knowledge types accounted for 74%, compared to sound which explained 26%.

Together, these results show that a common model of overextension reveals similar meaning extension patterns in both Apollo and Alex, and the three knowledge types play an important role in their word choice.

Discussion

We introduce a common model of word choice in overextension and a novel dataset that contains the use of human language pertaining to overextension by a grey parrot named Apollo. We collected the data from the YouTube channel of Apollo's trainers and ran the collected referent‐utterance pairs through a child overextension model which ranked the words in Apollo's vocabulary from most to least likely according to the probabilistic model used for predicting child overextension.

The performance of the model indicates that while the frequency baseline is a decent predictor of parrot utterance, the modalities that involve different types of knowledge (multimodal, including categorical, visual, and associative) are consistently good predictors. In the attested overextensions, in which the referent words are out‐of‐vocabulary, the multimodal model is dominant. This result shows that the processes giving rise to Apollo's overextensional word choices may parallel those of human children, at least when he chooses a word to refer to an object for which he has no in‐vocabulary label.

Sound error is more prominent in the iV cases, but not in the OoV cases. This outcome indicates that some of Apollo's alleged overextensions were made because he tried to produce one word but mixed up the sounds that constituted his desired utterance; an example is shown in Fig. 4. Although sound error might be potentially thought of as a kind of overextension, it has little relevance to conceptual relation per se and instead reflects phonetic confusion among the words that Apollo has acquired. Our assessment of overextension focuses on concepts that are grounded in a semantic space, and we do not consider these sound‐based errors as demonstrative cases that draw on (meaningful) conceptual relations.

It is important to note that we do not claim that the conceptual representations we used for modeling parrot overextension necessarily reflect the veridical representations, but they may be approximations as in the case of modeling child overextension. The multimodal semantic space we construct is a proxy for validating the probabilistic word choice model, which is used to explain word choice behavior in both children and parrots. We do not believe that the set of modalities we examined is exhaustive or fully representative of the “true” conceptual representations of parrots, and we acknowledge that assessing the veridicality of the conceptual representations is challenging and outside the scope of the current study.

Limitations and possible extensions

Our study is limited in that it is difficult to assess the degree of bias in the data sample of Apollo collected from YouTube. It is conceivable that the data we analyzed reflect the “interesting” cases of Apollo and are, therefore, not entirely representative of Apollo's overextension behavior. We should be cautious about how rigorous the conclusions drawn from YouTube videos could be. The incentive for Shorts is to be entertaining and interesting, and the content on Apollo n Frens is inevitably curated and edited. Furthermore, the OoV overextensions we have collected are second‐hand attestations by Apollo's trainer, which in itself suggests that a round of selection bias likely occurred before the data was even recorded. It is worth noting that such problems are also present with existing studies in which a caregiver is asked to record their child's linguistic use. However, in addition to our analysis of Apollo, our analysis of Alex provides independent assessment and converging evidence for our hypothesis. Nevertheless, we believe that a more controlled and experiment‐based study on parrot overextension would help consolidate the current findings.

Related to this issue, another limitation for our analysis is the lack of comprehensive word usage data from grey parrots, especially in the realm restricted to word overextension. More data would allow us to perform rigorous statistical testing, which would help differentiate operant conditioning and babble‐luck from overextension. Additionally, more data could help us build an accurate vocabulary frequency, which would more accurately describe Apollo's word choice process. As the Apollo n Frens YouTube channel continues to post more videos, the potential dataset keeps growing. Follow‐up work can then be conducted on a larger dataset.

Apart from the data issues, one of the difficulties of asserting that there is genuine or creative overextension in Apollo's case is the lack of word comprehension data. As previously found, Clark (1978) noted that the production‐comprehension asymmetry indicates intentional overextension. A child who identifies a “goat” when asked to point to one, but refers to it with the overextended label “dog,” is aware of the correct label and chooses to overextend. There are a few videos showing Apollo engaged in comprehension tasks, such as @ApolloandFrens (2024), but not in a controlled setting with predetermined objects. Future work could focus on whether Apollo also displays production‐comprehension asymmetry, which would lend credit to the idea that he may be intentional in his innovative use of words.

Conclusion

We present evidence that a probabilistic multimodal model trained on child overextension performs well on predicting parrot word production in a word‐referent task. Our analysis supports the applicability of a common model of word meaning extension to humans and a nonhuman species. While the categorical, visual, and associative knowledge of parrots is undoubtedly different from that of humans, the fact that the model predicts parrot utterances without further training suggests a shared ability for word meaning extension in a species 320 million years apart.
