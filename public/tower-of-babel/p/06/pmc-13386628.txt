Impact of curriculum reform and mentorship on career willingness among undergraduate psychiatry students: a mediation analysis
Authors: Xiaolu Chen, Xiaoli Cheng, Jingxin Wang, Ying Lu, Yu Tian, Xiao Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe objective of this study was to investigate the satisfaction levels of undergraduate students majoring in psychiatric medicine at Chongqing Medical University. Additionally, it aimed to examine the differences in satisfaction among students of different grades to assess the impact of curriculum reforms implemented since 2017.MethodsA self-designed questionnaire, validated by the Delphi method (CVI = 0.90), was utilized to survey undergraduate students from the 2016 to 2019 grades. A comparative analysis was conducted between the pre-reform cohort (2016 grade) and post-reform cohorts (2017–2019 grades). Multivariate logistic regression and mediation analysis were employed to identify factors associated with career intentions.ResultsA total of 284 valid questionnaires were collected. The overall satisfaction of students in the 2017–2019 cohorts was significantly higher than that of the 2016 cohort (P < 0.001). The post-reform cohorts showed significantly higher satisfaction with mentorship (P = 0.004) and clinical practice (P = 0.003). Multivariate analysis indicated that the curriculum reform was independently associated with willingness to pursue a career in psychiatry (OR = 2.28). Mediation analysis suggested that improved satisfaction with mentorship partially accounted for the association between the reform and career intentions.ConclusionThe curriculum reform and mentorship appear to have strengthened professional education, enhanced student satisfaction, and were associated with greater willingness to pursue psychiatric careers.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09529-5.Keywords: Psychiatric Medicine, Major Satisfaction, Undergraduate Students, Curriculum Reform, Mentorship

Introduction

With the rapid advancement of social technology and the accelerated pace of modern life, significant changes are occurring in the medical field. The traditional medical model, which has been dominated by biological factors, is gradually shifting toward a focus on social and psychological factors [1]. Globally, the disease burden of mental disorders is equally severe. According to the Global Burden of Disease Study 2019, mental disorders accounted for as high as 4.9% of global disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), with depression and anxiety ranking among the top ten leading causes of non-fatal health loss [2]. However, the current state of mental health services in China is concerning. Not only is the quantity of mental health service resources severely limited, but there is also a significant imbalance in their geographical distribution [3]. Specifically, A 2024 scoping review by Sun et al. published in Lancet Regional Health-Western Pacific noted that although the number of psychiatrists in China increased by 139% from 2000 to 2020, challenges such as uneven geographical distribution and an unreasonable composition of professional personnel remain severe [4]. As of 2020, China’s psychiatrist-to-population ratio stood at approximately 2.1 per 100,000 population, far below the global average of 5.9 per 100,000 and the average of 12.9 per 100,000 observed in high-income countries [5–6]. In response to this situation, the Chinese government has issued a series of policies aimed at ensuring the training and retention of psychiatrists, and has set a target of reaching 4.0 psychiatrists per 100,000 population by 2025, which, even if achieved, would remain considerably below current high-income country levels [7–8]. These data suggest that expanding the training and supply of mental health professionals is a key strategy for improving public health [4]. Therefore, medical education, especially undergraduate education as the starting point for cultivating future psychiatrists, has received increasing attention for its role in shaping professional identity and career choices [5].

In terms of training models, quite a number of western countries represented by Canada, psychiatry is often arranged as a postgraduate specialization that students can choose after completing general medical education and obtaining a basic medical degree [9]. By comparison, some medical universities in China have set up a distinctive five-year undergraduate program in Psychiatric Medicine. Students are admitted straight from high school candidates mainly according to their comprehensive performance in the National College Entrance Examination (NCEE). Throughout the five-year undergraduate period, students take systematic coursework that combines foundational general clinical medicine knowledge with targeted specialized training in psychiatry. After completing all required courses and clinical practice, graduates can obtain a bachelor’s degree with a concentration in psychiatric medicine. This educational pathway is conducive to shaping students’ professional awareness of psychiatry at an early learning stage, and may also support the steady cultivation and talent reserve of mental health professionals in China [9–10].

This five-year undergraduate program is unique internationally. In a comparison of psychiatric education between China and Canada, Yu et al. found that the Western “4 + 4” model (in which students select psychiatry after completing general medical education) may lead to insufficient early professional exposure, leaving many medical students with limited understanding of psychiatry before entering postgraduate training [9]. In contrast, China’s five-year continuous program places students on a professional track from matriculation. Through five consecutive years of psychiatric coursework and clinical training, it helps foster a stronger professional identity. However, the effectiveness of this educational model, particularly its long-term impact on students’ professional satisfaction and career willingness, has not been fully evaluated previously. In 2018, Jing et al. reported initial positive outcomes of this major setup but still called for more longitudinal empirical studies [10].

Chongqing Medical University, located in the municipality of Chongqing in southwestern China, is one of the earliest medical universities approved by the Ministry of Education to establish this distinctive five-year undergraduate program. As a key medical institution serving a vast regional population of over 300 million, it provides a representative setting for examining the early outcomes of China’s psychiatry undergraduate education model. This university began admitting undergraduate students majoring in psychiatric medicine in 2016 and has since recruited eight grades of undergraduates. We previously conducted a survey on the professional satisfaction of the first cohort (2016 grade) [11], which identified several issues such as insufficient specialized training and weak mentorship. These findings led to significant adjustments in the training program starting with the 2017 grade. The current study aims to: (1) investigate the satisfaction levels of students from 2016 to 2019 grades; (2) objectively evaluate the effectiveness of the curriculum reform by comparing the pre-reform (2016) and post-reform (2017–2019) cohorts; and (3) explore the mechanisms underlying the observed changes in students' career intentions.

Materials and methods

General information

From November 2020 to January 2024, we conducted a survey of undergraduate students from the 2016 to 2019 grades majoring in psychiatry at Chongqing Medical University. All participants were surveyed at the same stage of their training, specifically, during their final year before graduation, regardless of the calendar year in which they completed that stage. Thus, although the overall data collection spanned several calendar years, each student contributed responses at the equivalent academic phase.

The Tencent Questionnaire link (an online web survey tool) was distributed to all enrolled students in the 2016–2019 cohorts via class QQ groups (an instant messaging platform). A total of 324 students were invited and surveyed, and 284 valid questionnaires were returned, resulting in an effective response rate of 87.7%. Due to the anonymous nature of the survey, information on students who declined or did not complete the questionnaire was not collected. Written informed consent was obtained electronically, stating that participation was voluntary and would not affect academic evaluation. Although some authors are faculty members in the same institution, the survey was conducted online and anonymously, and no identifying information was collected. Students were assured that their responses would not affect their course evaluations or academic standing. The study protocol was approved by the Human Research and Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University.

Survey methodology

The questionnaire was developed based on existing literature and validated using the Delphi method. Three rounds of anonymous feedback were conducted with 5 experts (2 psychiatric education professors and 3 senior clinical psychiatrists). After 3 rounds, 14 items were retained from an initial 20, and the content validity index (CVI) was 0.90, indicating good validity. A pilot test with 30 students confirmed item clarity. The survey was conducted online, and detailed explanations were provided to ensure understanding.

To ensure data quality, the survey was designed to be submitted only after all required questions were completed. IP address checking was used to prevent duplicate submissions. Two researchers independently screened the returned questionnaires, and those with a completion time of less than 120 s or with logically contradictory responses were excluded.

Statistical analysis

Data were collected through the “Tencent questionnaire” and analyzed with SPSS 26.0 statistical software. Categorical data are presented as percentages (%). Intergroup comparisons of qualitative data were performed using the Chi-square test or Mann-Whitney U test. The sample size was based on the total number of eligible students enrolled in the 2016–2019 cohorts at our institution, as this study aimed to survey the entire target population rather than a sample drawn from a larger population. Eligible students were invited to participate. To provide a more robust analysis, multivariate logistic regression was performed to determine factors independently associated with career willingness, adjusting for gender and enrollment motivation [12–14]. Furthermore, mediation analysis was conducted to explore whether satisfaction with mentorship accounted for part of the association between curriculum reform and career intentions. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Curriculum reform overview

To address the feedback from the 2016 cohort regarding insufficient specialized training, the curriculum underwent a comprehensive restructuring for the 2017 grade. As detailed in Table 2, while the core clinical courses (e.g., Psychiatry, Neurology) were maintained, significant adjustments were made to the structure of professional courses. The most notable reform was the introduction of new specialized modules, including Psychosomatic Medicine (40 h), Child Psychiatry (24 h), Geriatric Psychiatry (24 h), and Community and Forensic Neurology (24 h). These additions aimed to broaden students’ clinical horizons. Conversely, some theoretical psychology courses (e.g., Social Psychology) were removed or integrated. Consequently, the total professional training hours increased, shifting the focus from general theory to sub-specialty clinical practice.

Comparison of satisfaction and career intentions

The comparative analysis between the pre- and post-reform cohorts is presented in Table 3. Regarding enrollment motivation, students in the 2017–2019 cohorts were significantly more likely to have chosen the major due to personal interest (66.3% vs. 45.9%, P = 0.004). Overall satisfaction with the major showed a substantial increase, rising from 61.2% in the 2016 cohort to 90.4% in the post-reform cohorts (P < 0.001). In terms of specific training components, significant improvements were observed in satisfaction with undergraduate mentorship (P = 0.004) and clinical practice training (P = 0.003). However, no significant difference was found in satisfaction with theoretical course content (P = 0.19). Consequently, the proportion of students intending to pursue a career in psychiatry significantly increased from 44.7% to 59.6% (P < 0.001), while the proportion of those unlikely to pursue the career dropped to 10.6%.

Factors influencing career willingness

To control for potential confounding factors such as gender and initial interest, we performed a multivariate logistic regression analysis (Table 4). The results indicated that the Curriculum Reform (Cohort 2017–2019) was independently associated with willingness to pursue a psychiatric career (OR = 2.28, 95% CI: 1.18–4.36, P = 0.013). Additionally, personal interest in the major was independently associated with career willingness (OR = 3.12, P < 0.001).

Mechanism of improvement: mediation analysis

We further explored the mechanism by which the reform was associated with career intentions. Mediation analysis (Table 5) revealed that satisfaction with mentorship played a significant mediating role. The indirect effect was significant (β = 0.08, 95% CI: 0.05–0.17), accounting for approximately 32.1% of the total effect. This suggests that the association between the reform and students' willingness to become psychiatrists was partially explained by enhancing their satisfaction with the mentorship program.

Discussion

Based on a survey conducted among four graduating classes, this study revealed that the overall satisfaction of students with their major was relatively high. Following revisions to the syllabus and training program informed by previous survey findings, students in the 2017–2019 cohorts exhibited significantly increased satisfaction compared to the 2016 cohort. The present study yielded several significant positive findings: overall major satisfaction increased from 61.2% to 90.4% (P < 0.001), the proportion of students intending to pursue a psychiatric career rose from 44.7% to 59.6% (P < 0.001), and significant improvements were observed in satisfaction with mentorship (from 54.1% to 70.2%, P = 0.004) and clinical practice training (from 50.6% to 69.2%, P = 0.003). Notably, our multivariate logistic regression analysis confirmed that the curriculum reform was independently associated with students’ willingness to pursue a career in psychiatry, suggesting that the observed positive outcomes occurred alongside the educational improvements rather than being explained by demographic differences alone.

The structural optimization of the curriculum was a central element of this educational change. Initially, the 2016 program focused primarily on general psychiatry and psychopharmacology, which led to student feedback regarding insufficient specialized knowledge. In response, the 2017 reform maintained the core courses while introducing specific modules such as Psychosomatic Medicine, Child Psychiatry, and Geriatric Psychiatry. These additions aligned well with the students’ desire for practical, specialized skills. Consequently, the proportion of students in the post-reform cohorts requesting “more psychiatry expertise” significantly decreased(from 72.9% to 62.5%, P = 0.008), suggesting that the new curriculum was associated with a partial reduction in the gap between student expectations and professional training requirements, although a substantial demand for further specialized knowledge remained.

Despite the curriculum expansion, a portion of students still called for greater emphasis on psychological fundamentals. This may be because the current psychology curriculum is theoretical rather than clinical-oriented [15]. This persistent demand may reflect not merely a need for more instructional hours, but a gap between the theoretical nature of current psychology courses and students’ expectations for clinically applicable skills. This interpretation aligns with prior research showing that medical students often perceive their psychology curriculum as overly theoretical and lacking practical relevance to clinical practice [16]. Such perceptions have been found to contribute to negative attitudes toward the subject and calls for more clinically grounded instruction. To address this, future reforms should prioritize enhancing the quality of teaching and reforming instructional methods rather than simply increasing teaching hours [17]. For instance, organizing multidisciplinary teaching teams comprising psychotherapists, psychiatrists, and counselors could facilitate the sharing of diverse expertise [18]. Furthermore, incorporating experiential teaching methods—such as in-class assessments using tools like the SCL-90 (Symptom Checklist 90), SDS (Self-Rating Depression Scale), and SAS (Self-Rating Anxiety Scale) [19–21]—could enhance students’ understanding of operational procedures. Such practical exposure is crucial for bridging the gap between psychological theory and clinical application, thereby increasing student satisfaction with this aspect of the curriculum.

A key finding of this study is the exploration of factors related to the observed increase in career willingness. Our mediation analysis indicated that satisfaction with mentorship accounted for a significant portion of this relationship. Previous studies have noted that undergraduate mentorship often suffers from issues like poor communication or busy mentors [22–27]. Our reform addressed this by establishing a comprehensive selection and exit mechanism for mentors. For the post-reform cohorts (2017–2019 grades), a structured mentorship program was implemented. According to the program design, mentors were responsible for: (1) fostering students’ medical ethics and professionalism; (2) providing academic guidance, including helping students understand the training curriculum, developing effective study habits, and enhancing learning motivation; (3) supporting research and innovation activities, such as literature review, academic competitions, and scientific projects; and (4) offering career planning advice, including preparation for postgraduate entrance examinations and employment.

In terms of specific work requirements, mentors were required to meet with their mentees in group sessions at least three times per semester, supplemented by individual consultations as needed. Mentors were required to document each meeting using a standardized record form. Mentors also guided students in academic competitions, helped cultivate good learning habits, and provided timely feedback on student progress. At the end of each academic year, students provided anonymous evaluations of their mentors, and mentors with persistently low ratings could be replaced. These structured elements were gradually introduced from the 2017 cohort onward, based on feedback from the 2016 cohort, and remained largely in place for the 2017–2019 cohorts. This structural change was associated with higher mentorship satisfaction, which in turn was related to students’ professional identity and commitment to the field.

The mediating role of mentorship satisfaction may be understood through several potential mechanisms. First, mentors in psychiatric settings serve as professional role models, allowing students to visualize their future careers through direct observation of clinical work and doctor-patient interactions. Second, structured mentorship provides individualized guidance that helps students navigate the transition from theoretical learning to clinical practice, thereby reducing professional uncertainty. Third, consistent mentor feedback and encouragement can enhance students’ professional self-efficacy—the belief in their capacity to succeed as psychiatrists—a factor that has been linked to career planning and specialty commitment in medical education contexts [25–26]. These observations suggest that the mentorship reform may have contributed to a deeper process of professional identity formation, rather than merely improving satisfaction as an end in itself. This may help to explain the more deliberate and committed career planning observed in the post-reform cohorts.

The positive associations observed were reflected in students’ career planning. We observed a shift from “passive assignment” to “active choice” regarding the major. Students in the post-reform cohorts were significantly more optimistic about employment prospects and more determined to become psychiatrists. This aligns with previous findings that a positive professional attitude predicts career choice [28].While the present study focused on educational factors such as mentorship and curriculum, we acknowledge that broader systemic factors—including income prospects and social recognition of the psychiatric profession—may also influence career decisions and warrant investigation in future studies [29]. Early clinical exposure and strong mentorship remain essential in the undergraduate phase to help students develop a deeper understanding of the profession and cultivate a resilient professional identity [30–32].

Limitations

Several limitations of our current study should be noted. First, the sample size is confined to students from a single university, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other institutions. Second, this study adopted a census sampling strategy that included all eligible students in the target population, so no prior sample size calculation was performed, which may lead to insufficient evaluation of statistical efficiency. Third, the data collection spanned from November 2020 to January 2024, and such a long timeframe may involve potential confounding factors such as changes in teaching arrangements and external environment, which could slightly affect the consistency of the results. Furthermore, the study focuses primarily on satisfaction levels and attitudes but does not delve deeply into other potential factors influencing career choices, such as economic factors. Finally, the observational design precludes causal inferences; the reported associations should be interpreted with caution. Future studies could utilize longitudinal designs to track the long-term career trajectories of these graduates.

Conclusion

This study indicates that the comprehensive curriculum reform—characterized by optimized course structures and an enhanced mentorship system—is associated with higher student satisfaction and career willingness in psychiatric medicine. To further address the shortage of psychiatrists in China, it is recommended to continue strengthening professional sub-specialty education and optimizing the mentorship system to cultivate high-quality mental health professionals. Future studies could adopt longitudinal designs to track graduates’ actual career trajectories and examine whether the enhanced career willingness observed at graduation translates into sustained workforce participation in psychiatry.
