Increasing detection of individuals with tuberculosis through engagement of informal health care providers in Northeastern Nigeria
Authors: Stephen John, Md. Toufiq Rahman, Suraj Abdulkarim, Robert Stevens, Beatrice Kirubi, Tushar Garg, Joseph Kuye, Emperor Ubochioma, Jacob Creswell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPatent and proprietary medicine vendors (PPMVs) and traditional healers (THs) are informal healthcare providers with a significant presence in Nigeria and many parts of Africa. They have established partnerships and trust with community stakeholders and are often the first point of contact for health-seeking, especially in rural communities. Disease programs like malaria have engaged PPMVs, but documentation on efforts to engage these informal providers for tuberculosis (TB) care in Africa is limited.MethodsWe evaluated the impact of an intervention engaging informal health providers to increase linkages of people with presumptive TB to testing and treatment in Adamawa and Yobe States between October 2020 and December 2022. Four local government areas (LGAs) were included as intervention areas and four others as control areas. PPMVs and THs’ leadership were engaged, and providers were mapped. Community health volunteers were linked to PPMVs and TH and supported the intervention. TB screening was conducted among clients of PPMVs and THs, and people with presumptive TB had sputum samples collected and transported for diagnostic testing and treatment.ResultsIn the intervention area, 344 PPMVs and 142 THs were mapped, and 120 PPMVs (35%) and 60 THs (42%) were successfully engaged. Informal providers identified 7,553 individuals with presumptive TB and linked 6,920 (92%) to testing. A total of 1,014 individuals with TB (926 with bacteriological confirmation) were detected. All forms of TB notifications increased by 16.5% (13% in control areas) while bacteriologically confirmed TB increased by 16.7% (14.3% in control areas) compared to the baseline, fuelled by a 97.1% increase in diagnostic testing.ConclusionA substantial proportion of people with TB seek care from informal providers like PPMVs and THs in Nigeria. Properly engaging these providers at scale could improve TB detection.

Background

Tuberculosis (TB) is a significant public health concern in Nigeria. Globally, Nigeria is home to the sixth highest number of people developing TB every year (510,000), and the highest in Africa, leading to an estimated 56,000 deaths [1]. The main reason for the high number of deaths is that Nigeria has the second-highest number of people with TB who remain undetected in Africa, 108,000, and is eighth globally [1]. One of the main reasons for the high numbers of people with TB who are missed in Nigeria is the lack of access to and trust in primary care services, where TB can be diagnosed and treated [2].

Unlike many countries in Asia, where most people with TB may be treated in the private sector [3, 4], in Nigeria, most people with TB are notified and treated by the public sector. Nevertheless, private healthcare providers are essential in delivering healthcare services in Nigeria. Non-public service providers accounted for up to 84% of first consultations in the country, and the private sector accounted for more than 60% of healthcare delivery [5, 6]. After doubling in the last few years, TB notifications from the private sector accounted for 24% of national numbers in 2022, but given the gap between estimated incidence and TB notifications, more engagement efforts are needed.

In rural and economically disadvantaged regions of Nigeria, formal healthcare options are limited, people often seek care from patent and proprietary medicine vendors (PPMV) and traditional healers (TH) because they are located within communities and are more easily accessible and affordable [7]. A study from Tanzania found that almost 60% of people with TB first visited a TH, while in Mozambique, 62% of people newly diagnosed with HIV first sought care from a TH [8, 9]. Similar rates of initial care seeking were documented among drug sellers and traditional medicine practitioners in Cameroon [10]. PPMVs are drug retail stores run by proprietors and staffed by people without formal pharmacy training. These establishments primarily engage in the retail sale of traditional pharmaceutical items for profit. Estimates of the number of PPMV in Nigeria are up to 200,000 nationally [6, 11].

The term "traditional healer" refers to individuals who provide health and healing services and may be known by different designations, including herbalists, or faith-based practitioners [12]. Although a large proportion of the population in the African region relies on TH for healthcare [13], THs have been largely absent from strategies by the TB community as part of PPM efforts. Small studies have shown willingness on their part, but also a lack of general knowledge about how to manage someone with presumptive TB in Nigeria [14]. In addition, multiple studies have documented that seeking care with traditional healers can lead to a delayed TB diagnosis [10, 15], further highlighting the need for interventions that engage them.

Potentially due to the large number and informal nature of these providers, efforts to engage them in the TB response have been minimal [11]. A systematic review documented that most efforts to engage PPMV in providing health services have been focused on adults for combating malaria, and the TB-related interventions showed low rates of referral for testing [16]. A 2021 study from Nigeria showed inadequate and delayed referral for TB care from PPMV [17].

While Nigeria's health policies and strategic plans acknowledge the role of informal healthcare providers to some extent, there is a lack of clear channels for their integration into the National health system [14]. Given the low treatment coverage for TB, abundance of informal health providers, and dearth of evidence around efforts to engage them in the TB response, a consortium of two local non-governmental organizations (NGO), SUFABEL Community Development Initiative (SCDI) and Janna Health Foundation (JHF), collaborated with State authorities on a two-year intervention in two states in Northeast Nigeria. The primary objective was to enhance TB detection by connecting people seeking care from informal providers (IP) to diagnostic and treatment services through community linkages. Here, we aim to document the outcomes of an intervention engaging informal health-care providers to identify people with TB and notification trends in Northeastern Nigeria.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a controlled intervention study from October 2020 until December 2022 (nine quarters) in Yobe and Adamawa states, comprising 38 Local Government Areas (LGAs), which are similar to districts. In each state, two LGAs were selected as intervention areas because of more rural demographics, and two LGAs were selected as controls based on similar population and TB service availability (Fig. 1). The four selected intervention LGAs (Bade, Nguru, Ganye, and Shelleng) served a population of approximately 940,000 people, while the control LGAs (Michika, Gombi, Nangere, and Geidam) served a population of approximately 854,000. The intervention area was served by 27 TB diagnostic sites (three with rapid molecular (Xpert) testing), while the control area had 25 TB diagnostic sites (three with Xpert testing). The intervention and control LGAs did not share geographic borders. Mapping informal provider engagement

All PPMVs and THs in the intervention area were mapped by teams from JHF and SCDI using several data sources. The inventory list of the National Association of Patent and Proprietary Medicine Dealers branches in Adamawa and Yobe States was collected. In addition, we used a list of officially registered members of The Association of Traditional Medicines. These lists were supplemented by state health ministries’ databases, and finally, we surveyed each LGA with local supervisors to confirm the initial results.

Following the mapping phase, JHF and SCDI engaged PPMVs and TH to participate in the intervention. Engagement criteria included the location of service delivery point of the IPs (rural areas with limited public-sector coverage), an average daily clientele of at least 15 clients, and recognized influence among peers as determined by local-authority consultation and willingness to participate in project activities with a goal of equal numbers of PPMV (30) and TH (15) in each of the four LGAs. Engaged providers received a three-day TB training, conducted by the NGOs, State, and LGA TB programs, covering intervention procedures and operations as well as general TB guidance. In addition to the informal providers, the intervention contracted 40 community health volunteers (CHV) to support the documentation during the study, transport samples, and return results.

TB screening process

For the intervention, PPMV and TH screened all clients seeking services verbally for symptoms (cough ≥ 2 weeks, night sweats, fever, or recent weight loss) and recorded data on gender and age using paper forms. Individuals with any symptom were deemed to have presumptive TB, and a sputum sample was requested. Forty local individuals were identified as CHVs by JHF and SCDI, and played a critical role in the screening. Each CHV supported up to three PPMVs and two THs, depending on geographic proximity and workload. CHVs collected sputum samples from provider sites, transported them to one of the 27 Xpert testing facilities in the intervention LGAs, retrieved results, and facilitated linkage to care for individuals diagnosed with TB. CHVs provided additional support for children (< 15 years) and their parents, such as accompanying them to treatment centers. CHVs submitted weekly data on screening and testing to LGA TB supervisors, who compiled monthly reports for the monitoring and evaluation officer. To encourage participation, CHVs received a monthly stipend of USD 34 (approximately N13,600 at N400/USD in 2020–2022), slightly above the minimum wage (USD 20 or N8,000). Additionally, CHVs earned an incentive of N500 (USD 1.25) per sputum sample successfully transported and tested, capped at 20 samples per month to ensure equitable workload distribution. PPMVs and THs also received N1,000 (USD 2.5) for referring and linking each individual with confirmed TB to treatment. These incentives were funded by the TB REACH grant and disbursed monthly via mobile money transfers after verification by LGA TB supervisors.

Data analysis

We documented the TB screening cascade [18] for both PPMV and TH by gender, which included the number of people who were screened, individuals with presumptive TB, the number who were tested, people diagnosed with TB, and those enrolled on treatment (both bacteriologically confirmed and all forms). We also collected data on age and sex for all people screened, as well as the HIV status of people diagnosed with TB. PPMVs and THs were considered ‘active’ if they referred at least one person for testing monthly.

A framework for assessing the impact of TB interventions to improve case notifications is used for many TB REACH projects and employed by the WHO [19, 20]. TB notification data from all intervention and control LGAs were collected for 27 months before the intervention and then prospectively until the end of the intervention.

Changes in TB case notification trends for bacteriologically-confirmed and all forms of TB, including those clinically diagnosed, were calculated from the difference between case notifications during baseline, intervention, and post-intervention periods. We fitted a controlled, interrupted time-series model to quarterly TB case-notification data using negative-binomial regression with Newey-West standard errors. The specification included time, post-intervention, group, and all two- and three-way interactions, yielding notification-rate ratios for the immediate and trend effects of the intervention.

Approvals and ethics

Authorization from the TB control office of Adamawa and Yobe States was granted. Support and approval were also acquired from the chairpersons and state executives of the coordinating bodies that represent the PPMV and TH in each state. Approval was also obtained from the chairpersons of Primary Health Authorities within the study and the local government in each region. Ethical approval was granted by the Scientific and Research Ethics Commissions in the Gombe State Ministry of Health and Adamawa State Ministry of Health. Written consent was provided by all participants. The study adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

The mapping operation identified 344 PPMV and 142 TH within the four intervention LGAs. We engaged 120 PPMV and 60 TH, divided evenly among each LGA through discussions with local authorities. Each engaged provider signed a memorandum of understanding to codify their participation. Of the 120 PPMV, 102 were male and 18 were female. Among the 60 THs, all but three were female. Among the 40 CHVs selected, ten were female. Over the course of the intervention, the majority of the providers stayed actively engaged (defined as referring at least one person with presumptive TB per quarter). Overall, 73% of engaged providers were actively referring people with presumptive TB, while TH had higher levels of activity for PPMV (80% vs. 70%). The proportion of active providers ranged from 67% in Ganye to 78% in Bade and Shelleng. During the intervention, we documented 38,140 people attended to by informal providers who were verbally screened for TB symptoms. Among those screened, 7,553 (19.8%) were found to have presumptive TB, and 6,920 (92% of people with presumptive TB) had their sputum collected, transported, and tested with Xpert. Laboratory testing identified 926 people (13.4% of those tested) with TB. In addition, 88 individuals were diagnosed clinically, for a total of 1,014 people with TB. Among those with diagnosed TB, 89 (8.8%) were children (< 15 years), of whom 31 (35%) were bacteriologically confirmed. Thirteen individuals (1.4% of bacteriologically confirmed) had rifampicin resistance detected by Xpert. Of the 926 individuals with bacteriologically confirmed TB, 923 (99.7%) were started on treatment, while 1,003 (98.9%) of the 1,014 people diagnosed with TB were enrolled and notified. Impact on TB notifications

In the nine quarters preceding the intervention, the four intervention LGAs notified 8,366 B + TB cases and 9,544 all forms of TB cases. During the informal provider engagement intervention, notifications in these LGAs increased to 9,760 B + cases (+ 16.7%) and 11,114 all forms cases (+ 16.5%). During the baseline period (testing data were available for 8 quarters), 7,814 individuals were tested. In the implementation period (9 quarters), total testing increased to 17,330 (10,410 from routine testing and 6,920 from the intervention). Multiple comparison approaches were used to assess changes in TB testing between the baseline and implementation periods and engaging informal providers led to substantial increases in testing. Using quarterly averages from all available quarters, testing increased by 97.1%. Weighted averages yielded a slightly lower but large increase in testing of 78.0%.

Discussion

In areas where IP’s dispense medical care to large portions of the population, engaging them in TB screening and referral supported by community health workers can increase the number of people detected with TB and started on treatment. A study in urban India documented a 30% improvement in TB notifications when informal providers were engaged [21]. Another intervention from India in Telangana State documented similar numbers of people with presumptive TB and people with confirmed TB diagnosed through the engagement of over 500 unqualified providers who used non-laboratory tests to make a large proportion of the diagnoses and did not document the impact on TB notifications [22]. However, few examples in TB come from African settings where the scope of engaging the private sector in the TB response is less developed.

Our results add to this limited body of evidence documenting improved notifications for IP engagement. A systematic review from 2021 found 13 articles on informal providers engaged in the TB response – and only six published between 2010–2019 [23]. A high-level review of 11 countries’ private sector engagement programs supported by Global Fund documented more than 24,000 private providers engaged in Nigeria supporting the large gains in PPM notifications nationally [5]. However, the study did not provide details on what types of engagement were used, and whether different providers were actively referring or detecting people with TB. Our study, albeit much smaller in scope, documents the TB screening cascade in two district groups of informal providers to report on the impact this intervention had on TB detection at the local level.

Several countries in Africa have documented results from engaging the private sector but not IP, at any scale [24]. There are many thousands of TH and PPMV in Nigeria and engagement efforts could be scaled. Compared to many Asian countries, it is unlikely that many people will be receiving TB treatment outside of designated TB treatment sites [3], but a study from Lagos and Kebbi states in Nigeria showed a high proportion of PPMV selling loose anti-TB drugs [25]. Engaging IPs could improve the time to treatment for people who seek care outside of the public system. A review of reasons for diagnostic delay found that seeking care from IPs had higher odds of delayed TB diagnosis, presumably because of the historical lack of engagement with TB programs [12]. We did not measure diagnostic delay in this study, but future research could document how expanded engagement of IPs in Nigeria and other African settings where they play a large role in rural health care could affect the time to treatment.

We documented the screening cascade for both PPMV and TH, finding that both groups had similar rates of identifying presumptive TB among people screened (19.4% vs 20.4%), while people were more likely to test positive for TB among those referred by PPMV than TH (14.5% vs 11.8%). TH had slightly higher retention among people with presumptive TB getting tested (93% vs 90.7%) and treatment initiation (98.3% vs 100%) so differences were minimal. This study used CHVs to help collect and transport sputum samples to GeneXpert sites, which resulted in a lower rate of pre-diagnostic loss to follow-up. According to a study by Vyas et al., deploying community-based sputum collection and transport methods allows more people to be tested for TB than just referring patients with TB symptoms to laboratories [26]. We could not document any studies that showed the effect of engaging PPMV to increase TB notifications and so this study begins to build this evidence base. A handful of studies have documented the results of engaging TH for TB prevention and care. In the Gambia a small intervention from 20 years ago engaged 24 TH and identified eight people with TB [27]. A small cluster randomized trial in Uganda demonstrated significantly higher HIV testing rates using TH [28]. We could not locate results of studies engaging multiple IP for TB care in the same intervention.

TB programs have been working to engage different private sector providers for the last 25–30 years [29]. While many different models of private sector engagement have been used over the years, with IP, engagement with professional organizations played a critical role in this intervention. Active involvement of PPMV and TH associations from the outset of the intervention facilitated training programs to a broader cohort of association members. Local PPMV associations have the potential to accelerate the expansion of supervision and improve TB care delivery in rural areas beyond the study timelines.

Our intervention's findings demonstrated a significant increase in the frequency of presumptive and TB notifications within the evaluation LGAs. Similar approaches have provided empirical support for the idea that community-based interventions aiming at actively detecting people with TB improve notifications [20, 21] and increased diagnostic testing is a critical component of almost any intervention to increase TB notifications.

The proportion of all TB notifications that were first identified by informal providers was 9%. While not a huge proportion, it is similar to other programmatic interventions around contact investigation for example [30], and much higher than focused research studies on the same topic, which several interventions that use contact investigation have found. Nonetheless, if the Stop TB Partnership’s Global Plan and WHO’s End TB Strategy goals are to be fulfilled, all people with TB must be reached and a large proportion of people with TB symptoms and TB are seeking care through IP [31, 32].

The high and sustained engagement of PPMVs and THs during the intervention suggests strong potential for long-term integration into community-based TB activities. Given their embedded position within underserved communities, these providers could be further trained and aligned with NTP structures to support ongoing symptom screening and referral, similar to CHVs. Formal integration, supported by appropriate supervision and policy guidance, could enhance sustainability of active case-finding efforts beyond project funding.

There are several limitations of our study. While we documented an increase in TB notifications during the intervention period compared to the baseline period, we cannot be sure this was related to the intervention itself, nor can we assume that similar findings would be generalizable to other parts of Nigeria due to our study design. Nevertheless, the IP in our intervention identified almost 10% of the people with TB notified in the intervention areas, and their referrals were responsible for 40% of the laboratory testing which also increased by 122% Many, but not all, contemporary interventions that engage the private sector have shown a positive impact on notifications suggesting there is more scope for TB engagement efforts [33–36]. Although we used control LGAs to document the impact of the intervention, we did not randomize the selection of either intervention or control areas although we tried to match on population and access to Xpert testing to adjust for secular trends in TB notification and to provide a contextual counterfactual for interpreting intervention effects. During the intervention, both intervention and control LGAs showed notification increases, though gains were slightly higher in intervention areas (16.7% vs 14.3%). The modest difference likely reflects the spill-over of good practices and broader programmatic improvements across both regions. We were not able to document any effect of the intervention after it concluded, as other projects began to work in the areas in 2023 making attribution of TB detection and notifications difficult for any post-intervention analysis. The long-term effect of interventions that improve TB detection once they end requires more evaluation.

Conclusions

Despite the prevalent global emphasis on private sector engagement, a considerable proportion of individuals affected by TB opt to access care from informal providers in Nigeria who are not engaged by the national program. These informal providers often need a more supportive engagement than larger private clinics or hospitals. Engaging informal service providers can contribute to TB notifications and early diagnosis, and such approaches should be considered by TB programs where they deliver care to large parts of the population.
