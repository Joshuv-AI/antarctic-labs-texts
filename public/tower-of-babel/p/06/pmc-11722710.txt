A case of mesalamine potentiating hypoprothrombinemic effect of vitamin K antagonist (VKA) therapy resulting in life-threatening bleeding
Authors: RS Prabhu, SN Rahmathullah, J Ahammad
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA 76-year-old male patient, who underwent a post-aortic valve replacement with a mechanical valve in 2006, was on oral anticoagulant therapy with warfarin, maintaining a stable therapeutic level of anticoagulation until 2022. He had a new diagnosis of ulcerative colitis in 2022, following which he was started on mesalamine. He had been having a supratherapeutic level of anticoagulation, as evidenced by an international normalized ratio (INR) of 12 to 14 on multiple occasions since 2022, leading to gastrointestinal bleeding, necessitating multiple packed red cell transfusions. He presented to us in August 2024 with severe anemia due to gastrointestinal bleeding. The evaluation revealed severe depletion of vitamin K-dependent coagulation factors. In this report, we discuss the possible drug–drug interactions between mesalamine and vitamin K antagonists, which went unrecognized leading to life-threatening gastrointestinal bleeding.

Introduction

Before the advent of direct oral anticoagulants, vitamin K antagonists (VKA) s were the most widely used oral anticoagulants. For certain indications, such as thromboprophylaxis post-mechanical heart valves and antiphospholipid syndrome (APS)-associated thrombosis, they are still the preferred oral anticoagulants. As VKAs are metabolized primarily by the CYP2C9 complex and to a lesser extent by CYP3A4 and CYP1A2 complexes, they are prone to significant drug–drug interactions.[1] In this report, we highlight the case of an elderly man who was on a stable therapeutic level of anticoagulation with VKA therapy, developing life-threatening gastrointestinal bleeding following supratherapeutic INR after the addition of mesalamine for ulcerative colitis, which was not recognized for years.

Case Report

A 76-year-old male patient, who had undergone an aortic valve replacement (AVR) with a metallic valve in 2006, and required an automatic implantable cardioverter defibrillator implantation for cardiac arrhythmias, presented to us in August 2024 with a history of recurrent episodes of melena, requiring fresh frozen plasma and packed red cell transfusions multiple times during the last 1 year. Following AVR, he was started on oral anticoagulation with warfarin, a VKA drug. On a warfarin dose of 4 mg per day, he maintained the international normalized ratio (INR). In 2022, he developed abdominal pain and multiple episodes of bloody diarrhea and was diagnosed with ulcerative colitis following colonoscopy and biopsy. A few weeks after the diagnosis of ulcerative colitis, his cardiologist noted supratherapeutic INR in the range of 12 to 14 on multiple occasions, leading to melena. Warfarin was changed to another VKA, acenocoumarol. He had been on a small dose of the drug, 0.5 mg per day. Even with this drug, his INR was unusually high. Hence, he was referred to the hematology clinic.

There was no history of bleeding symptoms before 2022, and he did not have any excess bleeding-related issues during his aortic valve surgery in 2006. There was also no history of bleeding symptoms among his family members.

On presentation to us, he had discontinued acenocoumarol for the previous 2 weeks due to gastrointestinal bleeding. On examination, he was pale. There were no enlarged lymph nodes or organomegaly. Laboratory parameters showed hemoglobin (Hb) 6.4 g/dL, white blood cells (WBCs) 9930/μL, and platelet count 341000/μL. Prothrombin time (PT) was grossly prolonged at >120 s and an INR of 14. Activated partial thromboplastin time was also prolonged at 65.8 s (control 32 s). He was detected to have deranged renal functions with raised creatinine of 2.01 mg/dL (creatinine clearance–29 mL/min).

In view of the grossly deranged coagulation screen, a detailed coagulation workup was conducted [Table 1]. Factor II, VII, IX, and X were grossly depleted – 3.1%, 4.1%, 3%, and 4.1%, respectively (normal range 50–150%). Mixing studies with pooled normal plasma (PNP) ruled out acquired factor inhibitors. Factor VIII and von Willebrand factor (antigen) were elevated. Fibrinogen, factor V and factor XI were normal.

He was diagnosed to have acquired a deficiency of vitamin K-dependent factors such as VKA. VKA inhibits the vitamin K epoxide reductase complex 1(VKORC1) enzyme, which prevents the body from activating vitamin K (which is necessary for coagulation). A review of his drugs revealed he has been on mesalamine delayed released tablets 1200 mg twice daily since 2022, prescribed for ulcerative colitis. Mesalamine, particularly in the setting of decreased renal function, can increase the acenocoumarol effect. He was advised to stop mesalamine and follow up with PT/INR results and restart at a lower dose of acenocoumarol once INR comes down to the therapeutic range.

Discussion

Since the introduction of dicoumarol in the early 1940s and later warfarin in 1954,[2] VKAs were the major oral anticoagulants for various indications such as treatment of deep venous thrombosis, thromboprophylaxis in atrial fibrillation and following mechanical heart valves. With the advent of direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) in 2008 and the need for routine monitoring for drug effects no longer required, DOACs have slowly supplanted VKAs for most indications.[3] For certain clinical scenarios, such as APS-associated thrombosis and thromboprophylaxis post-mechanical heart valves, VKAs are still considered superior to DOACs. VKAs have significant interactions with multiple drugs, which often implies close monitoring for their anticoagulant effect, to prevent supratherapeutic/subtherapeutic effects, especially in elderly persons with comorbidities, requiring multiple medications.

In this case report, we describe an elderly person who is on VKA following aortic valve replacement with a mechanical valve and has been maintaining a therapeutic INR on a stable dose of warfarin for many years. Years later, he developed a significantly supratherapeutic level of INR with VKA. This coincided with a diagnosis of ulcerative colitis, for which he was prescribed mesalamine.

Mesalamine (5-amino salicylic acid) is a drug used in inflammatory bowel disorders, such as Crohn’s disease and ulcerative colitis, acts by interfering with the local mediators of an inflammatory response, especially leukotrienes, though the exact mechanism of action is not known. The major route of elimination following oral administration is via renal excretion.[4]

Acenocoumarol, a vitamin K antagonist, acts by inhibiting VKORC1. VKORC1 is required for reactivating the “inactive” form of vitamin K, which is formed after the gamma-carboxylation of the key glutamic acid residues of coagulation factors II, V, IX, and X. The gamma-carboxylation of glutamic acid residues of above coagulation factors in the presence of “active” vitamin K activates these factors. Thus, in the presence of therapeutic concentrations of acenocoumarol, “active” vitamin K is depleted and thus the above coagulation factors cannot be activated. Acenocoumarol is metabolized in the liver, primarily by CYP2C9 complex and the major route of excretion of metabolites is renal. A minimal amount is excreted in urine as an unchanged drug.

Mesalamine is a drug known to increase the VKA effect though the exact mechanism is not known.[5] There are reports that it can also decrease the drug effect.[67] In our case, there is a strong temporal association of supratherapeutic INR after the addition of mesalamine to a stable therapeutic range VKA therapy. The decreased renal function may have exacerbated this interaction between these agents as both are primarily excreted via the renal route, thus leading to prolonged depletion of vitamin K-dependent coagulation factors.

Our case brings to light a drug interaction between two agents that can have potentially fatal consequences, if not recognized and monitored for closely. While prescribing a new medicine for patients on chronic anticoagulation with vitamin K antagonist (VKA) therapy, potential drug interactions should be checked. Mesalamine can potentiate the effect of VKA, especially in elderly patients with reduced renal function. This can result in life-threatening bleeding if not recognized.
