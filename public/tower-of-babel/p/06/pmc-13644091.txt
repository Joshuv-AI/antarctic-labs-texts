Chronic lower back pain and its impact on health-related quality of life among undergraduates of Gulu University, Uganda: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Martha Lucky Tino, Ivaan Pitua, Monica Lillian Nakyeyune, Jerom Okot, Hellen Patricia Ssegawa, Daniel S Ebbs, Felix Bongomin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChronic lower back pain (CLBP) is a common health problem affecting individuals worldwide. However, studies concerning the prevalence of CLBP among undergraduates are scarce. University students represent a unique population because they are at a formative stage of life with academic demands, prolonged sitting during lectures and study sessions, and lifestyle factors that may increase risk. Additionally, CLBP in this population can impact performance, career trajectory, and long-term health outcomes, making early identification and intervention valuable. This study aimed to determine the prevalence and factors associated with CLBP and its impact on health-related quality of life (HRQoL) among undergraduates at Gulu University, Uganda.MethodsBetween November 1 and November 30, 2023, a cross-sectional study was conducted using self-administered questionnaires. CLBP was defined as non-specific lower back pain lasting 12 weeks or more. The Oswestry Disability Index was used to evaluate disability associated with CLBP, and the EuroQoL-5D-5 L was used to evaluate HRQoL (higher scores indicate better HRQoL). Data were analysed using Mann-Whitney U test for non-normally distributed continuous variables and chi-square test for categorical variables.ResultsWe enrolled 394 participants, 79.2% (n = 312) were males, with a median age of 23 (IQR: 22–24) years. The prevalence of CLBP was 20.6% (n = 81). With respect to HRQoL scores (median (IQR)), both overall health (0.83 (0.75–0.9) versus 0.67 (0.61–0.78), p < 0.001) and self-reported health (80 (65–90) versus 65 (50–80), p < 0.001) were worse in participants with CLBP than in those without CLBP. Among participants with CLBP, 45.7% (n = 37) reported minimal disability, 34.6% (n = 28) reported moderate disability, and 19.7% (n = 16) reported severe disability on the Oswestry Disability Index.ConclusionsCLBP affects one in five (20.6%) undergraduates at Gulu University, with 54.3% experiencing moderate to severe disability. Both overall health scores (median 0.67 vs. 0.83, p < 0.001) and self-reported health (median 65 vs. 80, p < 0.001) were significantly worse in those with CLBP. These findings highlight the urgent need for university-based screening programmes, ergonomic interventions, and accessible rehabilitation services to address this highly prevalent condition and its substantial impact on students’ quality of life and academic performance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12891-026-10212-5.

Background

Chronic lower back pain (CLBP) is a widespread musculoskeletal condition defined as non-specific pain in the posterior lower aspect of the body, from the lower margin of the twelfth rib to the lower gluteal folds, with or without referred pain to one or both lower limbs, persisting for 12 weeks or more [1, 2]. According to the most recent Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 analysis, approximately 619 million people worldwide were affected by low back pain (LBP) in 2020, with projections indicating that this figure will rise to 843 million by 2050, driven primarily by population growth and ageing. LBP remains the leading cause of years lived with disability (YLDs) globally, with an age-standardised YLD rate of 832 per 100,000 population in 2020, showing its immense public health significance [1].

CLBP is among the foremost musculoskeletal conditions contributing to global impairment and reduced quality of life. Musculoskeletal disorders collectively affected an estimated 1.71 billion people worldwide in 2019, with LBP being the most prevalent condition in 134 of the 204 countries analysed [3]. Among musculoskeletal conditions, CLBP stands out as the most prevalent issue in high-income settings and a leading cause of functional incapacity [4]. The associated disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) from musculoskeletal disorders vary across regions, with occupational ergonomic factors, high body mass index (BMI), and smoking identified as the primary attributable risk factors for LBP globally [5].

Importantly, CLBP is no longer considered exclusively a condition of elderly populations. A systematic review and meta-analysis reported that the overall pooled prevalence of chronic pain among young adults aged 15 to 34 years was 11.6%, with LBP representing one of the most common pain sites in this demographic [6]. Additionally, the GBD 2021 analysis of adolescents aged 10 to 19 years identified approximately 19.2 million prevalent cases of LBP globally in 2021, highlighting that the burden of this condition begins early in life [7]. These data collectively reinforce that LBP is a significant health concern across the entire age spectrum, including university-age populations.

The economic impact of CLBP on healthcare systems is substantial. In high-income countries, average annual direct and indirect costs per population for LBP range from €2.3 billion to over $8 billion, encompassing hospitalisation rates of approximately 7% to 9% among affected individuals [8]. In low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), a separate systematic review found that total annual costs attributable to LBP can reach $2.2 billion per population and $1,226 per patient, with hospitalisation rates ranging from 13.4% to 18.7% [9]. Despite this significant burden, retrievable evidence on the economic cost of CLBP in Africa remains scarce, and the financial impact is presumed to be enormous given the continent’s fragile health systems, limited infrastructure, and the dual burden of infectious and non-communicable diseases competing for restricted resources [9, 10].

University students represent a unique and important population for CLBP research. Studies from diverse geographical settings have reported varying LBP prevalence rates in this demographic, ranging from approximately 20% to over 68% depending on the definition used and the population studied [11, 12]. For instance, a cross-sectional study of Serbian medical students reported a 12-month LBP prevalence of 62.1% [13], while studies from Saudi Arabia, Malaysia, and Tunisia have reported comparable findings among healthcare and non-healthcare student populations [12, 14, 15]. University students face unique risk factors including prolonged sitting during lectures and study sessions, frequent use of electronic devices in suboptimal postures, high levels of academic stress, and the physical demands of clinical training in health-related programmes [16, 17]. These sedentary behaviours and postural demands can lead to sustained mechanical loading of the lumbar spine, disc degeneration, muscle fatigue, and altered pain perception through neuroendocrine stress pathways [18].

Despite the growing body of literature on LBP in university students globally, data from sub-Saharan Africa remain limited. In Nigeria, the most populous African country, studies among physiotherapy students and adolescents have reported lifetime LBP prevalence rates of 45.5% and variable period prevalence rates [19, 20]. However, there is a conspicuous paucity of data on CLBP prevalence among university students in Uganda and the broader East African region. Understanding CLBP in the Ugandan context is particularly important given that the country’s higher education infrastructure differs from that of high-income countries, with potential variations in classroom ergonomics, student-to-faculty ratios, and resource availability that may influence CLBP risk. Additionally, Ugandan students may face unique challenges including limited access to healthcare services, financial constraints affecting nutrition and physical activity, and cultural attitudes toward pain reporting that differ from those in populations where most CLBP research has been conducted. Therefore, this study aimed to determine the prevalence, associated factors, and impact of CLBP on health-related quality of life (HRQoL) among undergraduates at Gulu University, Uganda.

Methods

Design

A cross-sectional study involving undergraduates of Gulu University, Uganda. Data were collected between November 1 and November 30, 2023.

Study setting and participant recruitment

The study population comprised undergraduates enrolled in various programmes at Gulu University, located in Gulu City, Northern Uganda. Gulu University has an approximate enrollment of 5,000 students across six faculties: Education and Humanities, Medicine, Science, Law, Business and Development Studies, and Agriculture and Environment. Sample size was calculated using the Kish-Leslie formula for cross-sectional studies: n = Z²P(1-P)/d², where Z = 1.96 (95% confidence level), P = 0.455 (expected prevalence based on previous studies among university students [19]), and d = 0.05 (desired precision). This calculation yielded: n = (1.96) ² × 0.455 × (1 − 0.455) / (0.05) ² = 3.8416 × 0.455 × 0.545 / 0.0025 = 381 participants. To account for potential non-response and incomplete questionnaires, we recruited 394 participants for the final analysis.

The participants were selected via a combination of cluster and systematic sampling. The university’s six faculties served as clusters, and every 15th student on the faculty lists (obtained from the academic registrar’s office) was selected until the desired sample size was reached. The 15th student was chosen based on the sampling interval calculated from total population (5000) and desired sample (374), giving 13.37, since systematic sampling requires a whole number, the interval was rounded up to a convenient number, 15 in this case.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: all undergraduates of any age, sex, and year of study who provided written informed consent. The exclusion criteria included students who were not present during data collection, those with physical injuries (e.g., from road accidents), congenital deformities, mental incapacities, pregnancy, or who did not consent to participate.

Data were collected via a self-administered questionnaire and screening tool. Trained research staff guided participants in completing the questionnaires, addressing any questions to provide clarification. The completed questionnaires were stored securely to maintain confidentiality. The complete data collection questionnaire is provided in additional files (Supplementary File 1).

Variables

The primary outcome variable was the presence of CLBP, defined as non-specific lower back pain lasting 12 weeks or more. Secondary outcome variables included disability level (measured by Oswestry Disability Index) and health-related quality of life (measured by EQ-5D-5 L and EQ-VAS). Independent variables included sociodemographic characteristics (age, sex, year of study, faculty), lifestyle factors (physical activity level, sitting hours per day), and academic factors (study hours per day).

Assessment of CLBP

Chronic lower back pain was assessed using a screening questionnaire that identified participants who experienced non-specific lower back pain lasting 12 weeks or more, as defined by the Global Burden of Disease studies (1). Participants were asked: ‘Have you experienced pain in your lower back (from the lower margin of the twelfth rib to the lower gluteal folds) that has lasted 12 weeks or more?’ Those who answered ‘yes’ were classified as having CLBP and subsequently completed the Oswestry Disability Index and health-related quality of life questionnaires.

The Oswestry Disability Index is a validated instrument used to assess disability related to lower back pain. The questionnaire consists of 10 sections: pain intensity, personal care, lifting, walking, sitting, standing, sleeping, sex life, social life, and travelling. Each section is scored from 0 to 5, with higher scores indicating greater disability. The total score is expressed as a percentage, with 0–20% representing minimal disability, 21–40% moderate disability, 41–60% severe disability, 61–80% crippled, and 81–100% bed-bound or exaggerating symptoms. The ODI has demonstrated excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.87–0.95) and test-retest reliability (ICC = 0.83–0.99) in diverse populations, including young adults.

The EQ-5D-5 L is a standardized instrument for measuring health-related quality of life. It comprises five dimensions: mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression. Each dimension has five levels: no problems, slight problems, moderate problems, severe problems, and extreme problems. Responses are converted into a single index value ranging from 0 (death) to 1 (perfect health) using established value sets. The EQ-VAS is a vertical visual analogue scale ranging from 0 (worst imaginable health) to 100 (best imaginable health), where participants rate their overall health on the day of assessment. The EQ-5D-5 L has demonstrated good validity, reliability (test-retest ICC = 0.78–0.91), and responsiveness in various populations, including young adults [21, 22].

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using SPSS version 26 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test and visual inspection of Q-Q plots. Normally distributed continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation and were compared using independent samples t-tests (for two groups) and one-way ANOVA (for more than two groups). Non-normally distributed continuous variables are presented as median (interquartile range) and were compared using Mann-Whitney U test (for two groups) and Kruskal-Wallis test (for more than two groups). Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages and were compared using chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test (when expected cell counts were < 5). Bivariate analysis was performed to examine associations between CLBP and sociodemographic, lifestyle, and academic factors. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 for all analyses. Missing data were minimal (< 5% for any variable). Complete case analysis was used, with participants excluded from specific analyses if data were missing for the variables under examination.

Ethical considerations

The research proposal was approved by the Gulu University Research Ethics Committee under approval number GUREC-2023-627. All participants provided informed consent before participating in the study. The study adhered to ethical guidelines for research involving human participants, ensuring confidentiality, voluntary participation, and the right to withdraw at any time without penalty.

Discussion

Prevalence of CLBP among university students

This study found that CLBP affected 20.6% of undergraduate students at Gulu University, Uganda. This prevalence is consistent with the pooled estimate reported in the systematic review which found an overall chronic pain prevalence of 11.6% in young adults aged 15 to 34 years, although that estimate encompassed all chronic pain types, not exclusively LBP [6]. When compared to studies that specifically assessed LBP in university student populations, our finding aligns with the lower end of reported ranges. A cross-sectional study among Serbian medical students reported a 12-month LBP prevalence of 62.1% [13], a Malaysian study using the Oswestry Disability Index found that 53% of university students had LBP [12], and studies from Saudi Arabia have reported prevalence rates ranging from 33.5% to 68.2% [11, 14]. The relatively lower prevalence in our study may be attributable to the use of a more restrictive definition requiring pain duration of at least 12 weeks, whereas many studies assessed LBP using shorter timeframes such as one-month or 12-month period prevalence.

Our finding is comparable to data from other African studies. In Nigeria, the 12-month prevalence of LBP among physiotherapy students was 32.5% [19]. The variation in prevalence across studies may stem from differences in study methodology, pain definitions, cultural practices, and the physical demands associated with specific academic programmes. For instance, students in physically demanding programmes such as medicine, nursing, and physiotherapy consistently report higher LBP prevalence than those in purely lecture-based programmes [15, 17]. Nevertheless, the 20.6% prevalence observed in our multi-faculty sample, which included students from six diverse faculties, shows that CLBP is a significant health issue affecting approximately one in five university students in this setting.

Factors associated with CLBP

Our study identified overall health (EQ-5D-5 L index score) and self-reported health status (EQ-VAS) as the only factors significantly associated with CLBP. Students with CLBP had significantly lower median overall health index scores (0.67 versus 0.83, p < 0.001) and lower self-reported health scores (65 versus 80, p < 0.001) compared to those without CLBP. These findings are consistent with existing evidence demonstrating that chronic LBP is associated with significant reductions in HRQoL. A study demonstrated longitudinal relationships between pain severity, disability, and reduced HRQoL among chronic LBP patients, supporting the notion that CLBP negatively impacts multiple domains of physical and psychological functioning [2]. Similarly, studies among university students in South Korea have reported that pain significantly interferes with daily activities, academic performance, and general well-being [23].

In contrast to several other studies [11, 24, 25], age was not significantly associated with CLBP in our population. This may reflect the relatively narrow age range of university students in our sample, where the median age was 23 years with a limited interquartile range. Similarly, sex was not significantly associated with CLBP in our study, despite a higher absolute prevalence observed among females (25.6%). This compares with findings from the GBD 2021 analysis, which consistently reports higher age-standardised LBP prevalence rates in females globally [1, 26]. In Saudi Arabia, female sex was significantly associated with LBP in both general university and medical student populations [11, 14]. The absence of a sex difference in our study may be related to the predominantly male composition of our sample (79.2%) and the limited statistical power to detect sex-specific associations.

Disability profile and functional impact

Among participants with CLBP, the majority (45.7%) reported minimal disability on the Oswestry Disability Index, while 34.6% reported moderate disability and 19.7% reported severe disability. These findings indicate that while most affected students had manageable functional limitations, over half (54.3%) experienced moderate to severe disability, warranting clinical attention. The predominance of mild to moderate disability in our sample is consistent with findings from the Malaysian study which reported that the majority of university students with LBP had minimal to moderate disability on the ODI [12]. Similarly, a study from Nigeria reported that most young adults with LBP experienced mild to moderate functional impairment [19].

Sitting, lifting, and personal care were the most affected domains on the ODI in our study, while social life, standing, and travelling were less impacted. These domain-specific findings are clinically relevant for university students, as sitting is the predominant posture during lectures, study sessions, and examinations. Previous studies have identified prolonged sitting as one of the strongest risk factors for LBP among university students [11, 27]. The COVID-19 pandemic further exacerbated sedentary behaviour among university students, with a study from Italy demonstrating significant increases in musculoskeletal pain associated with reduced physical activity during the pandemic [27]. Additionally, the lifting domain may be particularly relevant for students in agriculture, science, and medicine faculties who may be exposed to physical tasks during practical sessions.

Impact of CLBP on health-related quality of life

The finding that CLBP was significantly associated with reduced HRQoL in both overall health index scores and self-reported health scores has important implications. The EQ-5D-5 L index score difference (median 0.67 versus 0.83) represents a clinically meaningful reduction in health status among affected students. A study demonstrated that pain severity and disability are independently and longitudinally associated with poorer HRQoL and higher healthcare costs among chronic LBP patients, suggesting that the quality-of-life deficits observed in our student population may persist and potentially worsen without intervention [2]. Pain interference with daily activities has been documented among university students in diverse settings, including South Korea, where pain significantly impaired academic functioning and social participation [23]. These findings collectively highlight that CLBP in university students is not merely an inconvenience but a condition with measurable impact on multiple dimensions of health and well-being.

Strengths and contextual considerations

This study contributes to the limited body of evidence on CLBP among university students in East Africa. By using validated instruments (the ODI and EQ-5D-5 L) and a standardised definition of CLBP consistent with the Global Burden of Disease framework [1], our findings are directly comparable with international literature. Furthermore, the inclusion of students from all six faculties at Gulu University strengthens the generalisability of the findings within the university context. Studies from Canadian, Ethiopian, and Malaysian university settings have similarly employed multi-faculty samples, enhancing the cross-cultural relevance of the observed prevalence estimates [12, 24, 28]. The identification of modifiable risk factors and the characterisation of disability profiles provide actionable data for university health services and policymakers in similar resource-limited settings across sub-Saharan Africa.

Clinical and policy implications

Our findings have several important implications for clinical practice, education policy, and public health. First, the 20.6% prevalence of CLBP among university students highlights the need for routine musculoskeletal screening programmes in higher education institutions, particularly for students in physically demanding programmes. University health services should incorporate musculoskeletal assessments into student health evaluations and provide early intervention services. Second, education policymakers should consider implementing ergonomic improvements in lecture halls and study spaces, including adjustable seating, regular breaks during long lectures, and access to standing desks. Previous studies have demonstrated that prolonged sitting in uncomfortable lecture hall seating significantly increases LBP risk [11]. Third, universities should develop comprehensive prevention programmes that combine health education, physical activity promotion, and stress management, as physical inactivity has been consistently associated with higher LBP prevalence among students compared to physically active counterparts [29]. Fourth, given the significant impact on HRQoL, universities should provide accessible rehabilitation services, including physiotherapy and pain management support, to affected students. Finally, the economic implications of CLBP in university students, although less quantified than in working populations [8, 30], may include decreased learning capacity, reduced academic performance, and long-term effects on workforce readiness.

Future research directions

While this study provides important insights into CLBP among university students in Uganda, several areas warrant further investigation. First, longitudinal studies are needed to track the natural history of CLBP in this population and identify predictors of persistence or resolution. Second, intervention studies evaluating the effectiveness of prevention programmes, ergonomic modifications, and treatment approaches specifically tailored to university students would be valuable. Third, future research should explore the role of psychological factors, such as stress, anxiety, and depression, in the development and maintenance of CLBP in students, as psychological distress has been shown to significantly predict LBP in medical student populations [15]. Fourth, studies investigating the impact of CLBP on academic performance outcomes would strengthen the evidence base for university-level interventions. Finally, cost-effectiveness analyses of screening and intervention programmes would help guide resource allocation decisions in resource-limited settings.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference; we can identify associations but cannot determine whether CLBP causes reduced HRQoL or whether other factors influence both outcomes. Second, CLBP and disability were assessed using self-reported questionnaires rather than clinical examination, which may introduce recall bias and lacks confirmation of specific diagnoses or pathology. Third, we did not assess psychological comorbidities (depression, anxiety) or previous treatment history, which may confound the relationship between CLBP and HRQoL. Fourth, the sample was drawn from a single university, which may limit generalisability to students at other institutions with different academic demands, infrastructure, or student populations. Fifth, the sample size, while adequate for prevalence estimation, may have limited statistical power to detect associations within subgroups, particularly sex-specific differences given the predominantly male sample. Sixth, we did not collect detailed information on the types, severity, or duration of physical activities, which could provide more nuanced understanding of risk factors. Finally, selection bias may have occurred if students with severe CLBP were less likely to attend classes and thus less likely to be sampled.

Conclusions

In conclusion, chronic low back pain affects one in five undergraduate students at Gulu University, with over half of those affected experiencing moderate to severe disability. The condition significantly impairs health-related quality of life, highlighting an urgent need for comprehensive university-based interventions. We recommend that universities implement routine musculoskeletal screening programmes during student health assessments, develop ergonomic improvements in learning spaces, and establish accessible physiotherapy and rehabilitation services. Prevention strategies should focus on health education about proper posture and lifting techniques, promotion of regular physical activity and exercise, and stress management programmes. Early identification through screening, combined with timely intervention, may prevent progression to chronic disability and mitigate the substantial negative impact on students’ academic performance and quality of life. Future longitudinal research should evaluate the effectiveness of these interventions in reducing CLBP prevalence and improving outcomes in this population.
