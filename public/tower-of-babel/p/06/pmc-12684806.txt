Admixture Mapping of Lipid Traits in Hispanic Americans
Authors: Keyue Ding, Angad S Johar, Arslan Zaidi, Paul K Crane, Digna R Velez Edwards, Gail P Jarvik, Elizabeth W Karlson, Elisabeth A Rosenthal, Ian B Stanaway, Chunhua Weng, Daniel J Schaid, Iftikhar J Kullo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdmixture mapping can be leveraged to identify genomic loci associated with disease traits in admixed individuals such as Hispanic Americans.Methods and ResultsWe characterized fine‐scale population structure of participants (n=105 108) in the multiethnic eMERGE‐III (electronic Medical Records and Genomics) cohort, using reference panels from the 1000 Genomes Project and a Native American cohort. We defined “genetically admixed Hispanic Americans” as those having ≥10% Native American genetic ancestry (n=5025), in addition to European (specifically, Iberian) and African genetic ancestry. This population descriptor was subsequently applied to 3 other cohorts: the MESA (Multi‐Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis; n=1176), WHI (Women’s Health Initiative; n=2873), and the HCHS/SOL (Hispanic Community Health Study/Study of Latinos; n=7804). We inferred local ancestry along the genome in genetically admixed Hispanic Americans in each cohort using RFMix2 with phased haplotypes from SHAPEIT2. Local ancestry proportions were used for admixture mapping of lipid traits (low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, total cholesterol, and triglycerides) using extended linear mixed models that incorporated multiple or single ancestries. After accounting for fixed‐effect covariates and random effects, ancestry proportions at the following loci were associated with lipid traits in the HCHS/SOL cohort: 11q12.2 (P=6.8×10−7) with low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, 2p23.3 (P=5.2×10−7) with total cholesterol, and 11q23.3 (P=7.3×10−12) with triglycerides. The LDL cholesterol and triglyceride loci replicated in at least one independent cohort, whereas the total cholesterol locus showed suggestive evidence for replication in the eMERGE cohort (P=0.073). Native American (NAMR) ancestry appeared to drive the LDL‐C association, whereas both NAMR and Iberian population in Spain (IBS) ancestries contributed to the triglyceride signal.ConclusionsAdmixture mapping in genetically admixed Hispanic Americans identified genomic loci influencing lipid traits and the different ancestral origins driving these associations.Keywords: admixture mapping, genetically admixed Hispanic Americans, local ancestry, Native AmericansSubject Categories: Genetic, Association Studies

A “Hispanic” or “Latino” American, as defined by the Census Bureau (2010), refers to an individual with cultural or ancestral ties to Cuba, Mexico, Puerto Rico, South or Central America, or other Spanish origins, regardless of their self‐identified racial and ethnic background. Hispanic/Latino individuals (or Latin Americans) have 3 main sources of genetic ancestry (source populations), that is, European (especially from the Iberian Peninsula), Native American (indigenous peoples of the Americas or American Indians), and African (predominantly from the Atlantic slave trade in West Africa). 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 Relative to non‐Hispanic White people, Hispanic/Latino individuals have a higher risk of prediabetes and type 2 diabetes, alongside higher levels of triglycerides and lower levels of high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C). 5 , 6 A risk haplotype in SLC16A11, prevalent in Native Americans (50%) and East Asians (10%) but rare in Europeans and Africans, was associated with type 2 diabetes in a cohort from Mexico. 7 , 8 In addition, several loci have been associated with triglyceride levels in participants recruited in Mexico. 9 , 10 , 11 For example, a cross‐population allele screen identified several previously unreported triglyceride genes, notably RORA and SIK3, harboring variants exclusive to a cohort of individuals from Mexico and absent in Europeans. 10

Several genome‐wide association studies (GWASs) of lipid traits have been performed in Hispanic/Latino cohorts from different geographic regions. The PAGE (Population Architecture Using Genomics and Epidemiology) study identified 49 variants for low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C), HDL‐C, and triglycerides, many of which were replicated in a cohort of ≈2500 Mexican American individuals. 12 Below et al 13 discovered 5 novel regions associated with lipid traits through genome‐wide meta‐analysis of 12 259 Hispanic individuals. Further, Graff et al 14 revealed 8 new loci in a cohort of 12 800 participants from the HCHS/SOL (Hispanic Community Health Study/Study of Latinos) study. Additionally, a multiancestry GWAS 15 of 17 708 Hispanic and 3652 Brazilian participants identified 18 novel loci. Despite these advances, the use of fine‐scale local ancestry patterns in admixture mapping for lipid traits in Hispanic American individuals has not been previously reported.

Admixture mapping can identify genomic regions associated with a trait in admixed populations, where the trait has different distributions in the source populations. 16 , 17 The core principle underlying admixture mapping is that differences in local ancestry (ie, the array of ancestral segments along the genome 18 ) may explain differences in trait prevalence/distribution. Such an approach led to the discovery of novel Native American (NAMR) ‐specific loci associated with Alzheimer disease in Caribbean Hispanic populations. 19 , 20 , 21 Recently, admixture mapping of 640 circulating metabolites in the HCHS/SOL cohort (n=3887) revealed 2 NAMR ancestry–enriched loci (FADS1/FADS2 and LIPC) that were associated with levels of long‐chain polyunsaturated fatty acid metabolites. 22 However, whether local ancestry can be incorporated for admixture mapping of lipid traits in Hispanic/Latino individuals from diverse regions in the United States and whether the proportion of NAMR ancestry at trait‐associated loci influences lipid levels in Hispanics/Latino individuals is not known.

Reliable inference of local ancestry is important for assessing demographic history, conducting admixture mapping, and ultimately identifying genetic variants associated with disease traits in an admixed population such as Hispanic/Latino. These methods have been previously implemented in the HCHS/SOL cohort, a community‐based study of genetic epidemiology and diverse health outcomes among Hispanic/Latino populations in the United States. 23 In the present study, we ascertained “genetically admixed Hispanic Americans” by delineating the fine‐scale genetic ancestry patterns in participants from several multiethnic cohorts, regardless of self‐reported ethnicity. Subsequently, we conducted admixture mapping of 4 lipid traits in these individuals to identify genomic regions where differences in local ancestry proportions are associated with a lipid trait.

Methods

Population Descriptors

The use of population labels such as race, ethnicity, or ancestry may not accurately reflect the genetic diversity present in admixed populations. We obtained “race” or “ethnicity” data from the electronic health record or study databases, where the ethnicity label is usually classified as “self‐reported Hispanic or Latino,” “not self‐reported Hispanic or Latino,” “not assessed,” and “unknown.” However, such labels used in a cultural or social context may not correspond to the genetic ancestry of admixed individuals. To harmonize analyses across cohorts, we defined genetically admixed Hispanic Americans as individuals possessing NAMR proportion ≥10%, in addition to European (specifically, from the Iberian populations in Spain [IBS]), and African ancestries acknowledging the historical interactions among indigenous NAMRs (the first inhabitants of the Americas), Iberians (colonizers), and Africans (originating from the Atlantic slave trade). This genetic ancestry‐based population descriptor considers the complex demographic history involving these source populations and was applied to the 4 diverse cohorts.

Overall Study Design

The conceptual framework of the study is shown in Figure 1. We assembled harmonized genotype data from diverse cohorts and reference panels including the 1000 Genome Project (1KGP, GRCh38) and an NAMR population. We ascertained genetically admixed Hispanic Americans as those with NAMR ancestry proportion ≥10% based on global ancestry estimated from a scalable population structure inference tool (SCOPE). 24 Subsequently, we used RFMix2 25 with phased haplotypes from ShapeIT2 26 to infer local ancestry. Finally, we performed admixture mapping of lipid traits incorporating local ancestry dosage using GENESIS (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). 27

Study Cohorts and Reference Panels

The eMERGE‐III (electronic Medical Records and Genomics) cohort includes 105 108 individuals of diverse genetic ancestry from 12 sites across the United States, 28 and genotype data are publicly available in the database of Genotypes and Phenotypes repository under phs001616.v2.p2. Data from 3 additional cohorts, the MESA (Multi‐Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis), 29 WHI (Women’s Health Initiative), 30 and Hispanic Community Health Study/Study of Latinos (HCHS/SOL), 31 were obtained from the database of Genotypes and Phenotypes under phs000209.v13.p3, phs001077.v1.p1, and phs000810.v1.p1, respectively. For reference panels, we used whole‐genome sequence data from the 1KGP (n=2548) that includes 5 major continental populations (ie, Africans, admixed Americans, East Asians, Europeans and South Asians). 32 In addition, genotype data of an NAMR cohort (n=401) from the Native Mexican Diversity Panel and Mexican Genome Diversity Project 2 were included (available from the original authors).

We downloaded genotyping data for each target cohort from the database of Genotypes and Phenotypes. Table S1 lists genotyping platforms for these cohorts. To create a harmonized genotype data set, we non genotyped variants were imputed using Michigan Imputation Server (https://imputationserver.sph.umich.edu/) (eMERGE‐III and HCHS/SOL) or TOPMed imputation server (https://imputation.biodatacatalyst.nhlbi.nih.gov/) (WHI and MESA). Since the resolution for genotyping data for the NAMR reference panel was low (n=372 692 intersecting variants from different Affymetrix platforms), 2 we identified ≈340 000 markers that were shared between NAMR cohorts, reference panels from the 1KGP, and target cohorts (Table 1).

Quality Control and Harmonized Genotyping Data

We did not prune variants based on linkage disequilibrium due to the limited number of genotyped markers in the NAMR cohort (n=372 692, global affy.unrel.release). 2 Additionally, the lack of a whole genome sequenced reference panel containing homogeneous NAMR samples precluded genotype imputation. To align our genotype data set with the 1KGP reference panel and to address potential strand‐related issues arising from A‐T and C‐G variants, we employed GenotypeHarmonizer version 1.4.20 33 for the NAMR cohort to obtain a refined set of genotypes, encompassing ≈340 000 shared variants across both the cohorts and reference panels, for further genetic analyses. We did not use a Hardy‐Weinberg equilibrium filter due to the presence of structure in the multi‐ethnic sample.

Ascertaining Genetically Admixed Hispanic Americans

We employed SCOPE 24 to estimate global ancestry proportions, given the high computational burden of analyzing hundreds of thousands of individuals and variants in model‐based algorithms such as ADMIXTURE. 34 SCOPE implements an enhanced principal component analysis for inferring population structure, balancing processing speed and accuracy, with a greater efficiency than model‐based and likelihood‐driven methods. 24 Running ADMIXTURE 34 to infer global ancestral proportions in eMERGE‐III under K=4 on an HPC server equipped with 36 central processing units required >15 days of computation. Additionally, ADMIXTURE struggled to distinguish NAMRs from East Asians under K=4, the large sample size possibly affecting statistical inference. In contrast, the implementation of randomized linear algebra in the eigen‐decomposition process in SCOPE permitted a more thorough exploration of the state space. This allowed SCOPE to detect subtle allele frequency variations between NAMRs and East Asians that ADMIXTURE missed. We set a convergence threshold at 1×10−6 and carried out 105 iterations using the alternating least squares algorithm in running SCOPE.

We subsequently defined genetically admixed Hispanic Americans as individuals having an NAMR ancestry proportion ≥10%. This definition was based on the distribution of individuals observed at varying cutoffs of NAMR ancestry proportion (see Results), as well as prior categorization of individuals with >90% NAMR genetic ancestry as indigenous NAMRs. 35 , 36 Self‐reported Hispanic/Latino individuals with an NAMR ancestry proportion <10% or those with exclusively European and African admixture were not included in further analyses. EAS ancestry was not considered a core component of Hispanic American admixture, and individuals with ≥80% EAS ancestry were excluded to avoid misclassification.

Demographic History of Hispanic Americans and Local Ancestry Inference

While SCOPE offers estimates of global ancestry proportions for admixed individuals, it cannot determine the source populations on the basis of reference panels, due to substantial substructure in European and African populations. To clarify the source populations for genetically admixed Hispanic Americans, we used “qpAdm” from ADMIXTOOLS2 (uqrmaie1.github.io/admixtools/index.html) to identify the best‐fitting model. We sourced genotype data from the Allen Ancient DNA resource version 54.1 37 and considered Chimpanzee, Neanderthal, Denisovan, and East African populations as outgroups. In addition, we evaluated whether the NAMR cohort 2 was an appropriate representation of the NAMR ancestral component within the genetically admixed Hispanic Americans ascertained in this study. Ancient DNA samples from San Nicolas Island, Chumash from California, Southwestern Ontario and Zapotec from Oaxaca, Southern Mexico, as the representatives of NAMR populations present in those regions during the Pre‐Columbian era, were used in qpAdm analyses.

Local ancestry refers to an individual’s genetic composition at a particular chromosome location, with the number of alleles derived from distinct ancestral populations at a given locus. 38 Accurate local ancestry inference requires homogeneous reference panels for the source populations inferred from the best‐fitting model. To fulfill this requirement, we included individuals from the Yoruba population in Ibadan, Nigeria (YRI; n=106) and the IBS (n=97) sourced from 1KGP. Additionally, we incorporated 18 groups with predominant NAMR ancestry (>99% [n=97]) from the Native Mexican Diversity Panel and Mexican Genome Diversity Project data sets, based on ADMIXTURE analysis of the reference panels from the 1000 Genomes Project, NMDP and MGDP datasets. 2 The inclusion of NAMR groups mitigated potential inaccuracies that could arise by using admixed American populations from 1KGP. Variants with MAF > 0.005 were included in the analysis, with 337,618 variants in eMERGE, 337,410 in MESA, 336,137 in WHI, and 333,914 in HCHS/SOL, respectively. Sample sizes from the source populations were similar to minimize bias in local ancestry inference. Variants with MAF > 0.005 were included in the analysis, with 337618 variants in eMERGE, 337410 in MESA, 336137 in WHI, and 333914 in HCHS/SOL, respectively. We applied SHAPEIT2 26 to phase the target genomes alongside the reference panels and the genetic recombination map (http://bochet.gcc.biostat.washington.edu/beagle/genetic_maps/). Multiple algorithms for local ancestry inference based on hidden Markov models have been developed, for example, PCAdmix, 39 RFMix, 25 and ELAI. 40 RFMix uses a conditional random field parameterized by random forests trained on reference panels. We implemented RFMix2, which involves iterative runs to optimize the model through expectation–maximization. The run spanned a maximum of 3 iterations and 20 generations, acknowledging the historic contact of source populations composing genetically admixed Hispanic Americans. We used the default output from the “msp.tsv” files (ie, the most probable ancestry call) to assign local ancestry for each individual for further admixture mapping involving 3 ancestries.

Ascertaining Lipid Traits From Electronic Health Records

In the eMERGE‐III cohort, an electronic health record–based algorithm was devised to extract values associated with various “MEASUREMENT_CONCEPT_ID” codes relating to 4 plasma lipid traits (ie, LDL‐C, HDL‐C, total cholesterol, and triglycerides). When multiple longitudinal measurements were available, we used the measurement closest to enrollment. To ascertain age, we calculated the temporal gap between the date of the recorded value and the date of birth. We also defined a binary “statin use” covariate, signifying whether the date of statin use fell on or before the date of the lipid trait measurement. For the MESA, WHI, and HCHS/SOL cohorts, baseline values for lipid traits and corresponding covariates were accessed from the database of Genotypes and Phenotypes.

Estimates of Genetic Relatedness

We constructed a genetic relatedness matrix using both “pcair” and “pcrelate” functions within the R GENESIS package. 27 In brief, the pcair function performs a principal components analysis to detect population structure while accounting for sample relatedness derived from the kinship coefficient estimated through KING‐robust. 41 Using the principal components estimated by pcair, pcrelate can provide accurate estimates of relatedness due only to recent structure. 42 Consequently, the output of pcrelate led to the derivation of a genetic relatedness matrix containing pairwise kinship coefficient estimates, which were used as ϕ in the model described below.

Extended Linear Mixed Models for Admixture Mapping

We performed admixture mapping using linear mixed models implemented in the “fitNullModel” and “admixmap” functions in the R GENESIS package. 27 Three ancestry groups—IBS, NAMR, and YRI—were tested simultaneously in the joint admixture mapping test. 43 The full admixture mapping linear mixed model was defined as:

where Y is the vector of quantitative trait for N individuals; X is the vector of covariates including sex, age, statin use, and global ancestry proportions (derived from SCOPE.); α is the vector of fixed effects of covariates including an intercept; Aj represents a N×(K−1) matrix of the local ancestry dosages at locus j from the K−1 source populations (K=3), and the vector βj is the corresponding effect size. We assumed random effects for the N individuals (g), as g∼N0σα2ϕ, where σα2 is the additive genetic variance, ϕ is the genetic relatedness matrix (see above), and ϵ is the residual effect. The null model of no effect of local ancestry dosage was fitted by multiple fixed‐effect covariates, and then the null hypothesis of βj=0 was assessed by a multivariate Wald test (ie, χ2 statistic, degrees of freedom=2). The joint P value derived from the χ2 statistic indicates the significance levels of the relationship between local ancestry proportions and the phenotype. To identify the ancestry driving the association signal, we also performed single ancestry admixture mapping. Under this scenario, Aj in the model (Eq. 1) represents a N×1 matrix of the local ancestry dosages at locus j from 1 of the 3 source ancestries.

We used P value of 4.5×10−6 as the genome‐wide significance threshold for admixture mapping studies in Hispanic/Latino cohorts. This threshold was proposed by Grinde et al 44 on the basis of an analytical framework that accounted for the correlation of local ancestry and test statistics.

Overlap of the Significant Loci Identified by Admixture Mapping With Loci Known to Be Associated With Lipid Traits

We assessed whether loci identified by admixture mapping were listed in the GWAS catalog (ebi.ac.uk/gwas). The experimental factor ontology (EFO) codes for LDL‐C (EFO_0004611), HDL‐C (EFO_0004612), total cholesterol (EFO_0004574), and triglycerides (EFO_0004530) were used to retrieve the previously reported loci associated with lipid traits.

Results

Ascertainment of Genetically Admixed Hispanic Americans

We characterized fine population structure in 105 108 participants, using the multiethnic eMERGE‐III cohort as a model. Principal component analysis of the reference panels (Figure S1A) and eMERGE‐III participants (Figure S1B and S1C) recapitulated admixed individuals in this cohort. Assuming 4 ancestral clusters (K=4), SCOPE distinguished NAMR genetic ancestry from the African, European, and East Asian genetic ancestries and their clustering patterns remained consistent across various K values; however, the European cluster broke down into distinct groups when K≥5, reflecting European substructure (Figure S2).

Individuals varied in the proportions of NAMR ancestry, indicating diverse ethnic identities and self‐classification as Hispanic/Latino (Figure 2A). To increase power for identifying genetic signal specific to NAMR ancestry, we analyzed individuals with NAMR (≥10%) and European ancestry (particularly IBS), regardless of presence of African ancestry. In addition, individuals with ≥80% East Asian genetic ancestry were excluded. This approach identified 5025 genetically admixed Hispanic Americans, including 1044 who did not self‐report as Hispanic/Latino (Figure 2B and 2C). To validate the designation ‘genetically admixed Hispanic Americans’, we showed that IBS was the most representative European source, in self‐reported Latino and non‐Latino individuals (Figure S3, upper panel). Nevertheless, at a cutoff of 5% NAMR ancestry proportion, none of the models were plausible (Figure S3, lower panel). Notably, genetically admixed Hispanic Americans exhibited a significantly higher mean proportion of NAMR ancestry (29.7%±19.3%) compared with those who self‐identified as Hispanic/Latino (25.2%±20.9%; P<2.2×10−16, Wilcoxon test; Figure 2D).

A significant number of genetically admixed Hispanic Americans in MESA, WHI, and HCHS/SOL cohorts were similarly ascertained (Table 1). Nearly 80% of the HCHS/SOL participants were classified as genetically admixed Hispanic Americans.

Variable Ancestry Proportions in Genetically Admixed Hispanic Americans

Hispanic Americans from different geographic regions had varying global ancestral proportions. The MESA and HCHS/SOL cohorts had a relatively higher proportion of NAMR ancestry (39.2%), compared with 34.8% and 30.3% in the WHI and eMERGE‐III, respectively (Figure 3A). Of 5025 genetically admixed Hispanic Americans in eMERGE‐III recruited from 12 sites in 4 regions, most were from the western (n=1028) and northeastern (n=3385) United States. Notably, the average NAMR proportion in the West (46.7%) was much higher than that in the Northeast (25.2%; P<2.2×10−16, Wilcoxon test), consistent with a west‐to‐east gradient of NAMR ancestry in the United States. 45 Conversely, the average African ancestry proportion in the West (5.7%) was much lower than that in the Northeast (18.1%; Figure 3B). These differences underscore the variable ancestry proportions among genetically admixed Hispanic Americans residing in different geographic regions of the United States.

To ensure precise local ancestry inference, we determined the best‐fitting demographic model for genetically admixed Hispanic Americans identified in eMERGE‐III. The best‐fitting source populations were IBS for European ancestry, YRI for African ancestry, NAMR, and Han Chinese in Beijing, China for East Asian ancestry, respectively (Figure 3C). Consequently, the most appropriate model highlights the predominant source ancestries as NAMR, IBS, and YRI. The qpAdm results, based on ancient pre‐Columbian American groups as ancestral populations, aligned closely with the SCOPE estimates and supported the NAMR cohort 2 as a fitting source population for genetically admixed Hispanic Americans ascertained in our study. The mixing of separate lineages of NAMR ancestry (initially diverging 14 000–17 000 years ago) in the western and eastern regions of North America 46 may have influenced this pattern.

We further inferred local ancestry along each chromosome using the best‐fitted source populations. The distribution of the length of local ancestry haplotypes is shown in Figure S4. The global ancestry proportions computed from the genome‐wide average of local ancestry assessments via RFMix2 and with those derived from SCOPE, were nearly identical (Pearson’s r>0.99) (Figure 3D). Painted karyograms of local ancestry revealed impressive variation in admixture contributions across chromosomes, emphasizing differences in NAMR ancestry proportions in admixed individuals (Figure S5).

Replication and Meta‐Analysis of Admixture Mapping Findings

To replicate the significant findings identified in the discovery cohort (ie, 3 loci in the HCSH/SOL cohort), we tested for associations in the remaining 3 cohorts (Figure 5A). After adjusting for multiple testing (Bonferroni correction), the 11q12.2 locus, associated with LDL‐C levels, was replicated in eMERGE‐III (P=0.013) and WHI (P=8.9×10−3). In addition, the 11q23.3 locus associated with natural log‐transformed triglycerides was replicated in MESA (P=3.8×10−4) and WHI (P=3.0×10−4). The 2p23.3 locus, associated with total cholesterol, marginally replicated in the eMERGE‐III cohort (P=0.073). Of note, the direction of the effect sizes for the NAMR ancestry, relative to the YRI reference ancestry, remained consistent between the discovery and replication cohorts for the 11q12.2 and 11q23.3 locus. Furthermore, random‐effects meta‐analysis for single ancestry admixture mapping showed that both NAMR and IBS ancestries were significantly associated with LDL‐C and triglyceride loci at 11q12.2 and 11q23.3, respectively (Figure 5B).

Based on locus annotation and the GWAS catalog, the 11q12.2 locus encompasses a gene cluster of the fatty acid desaturase (including FADS1, FADS2, and FADS3) (Figure S9A). Discussion

We ascertained genetically admixed Hispanic Americans from 4 different cohorts across the United States and conducted admixture mapping of lipid traits by incorporating local ancestry dosage. Homogeneous reference panels for estimating both global and local ancestry enabled an accurate inference of genetic ancestry proportions in admixed individuals. Joint and single admixture mapping revealed the influence of local ancestry on lipid traits, offering insights into the interactions between the locus and ancestral backgrounds. Ancestry proportions at 3 genomic loci in genetically admixed Hispanic Americans were associated with lipid traits, and different source ancestries influenced the associations. NAMR ancestry appeared to drive the LDL‐C association, whereas both NAMR and IBS ancestries contributed to the triglyceride signal.

Use of an NAMR ancestry proportion threshold to define genetically admixed Hispanic Americans allowed consistent selection of such individuals across diverse cohorts. In the eMERGE‐III cohort, we identified 5025 individuals with varying admixture proportions who met this criterion (Figure 2). Self‐reported and genetically admixed Hispanic Americans were mostly concordant, but 1044 individuals with >10% NAMR ancestry did not identify themselves as Hispanic/Latino individuals (Figure 2B). Such discrepancies shed light on the self‐perception of identity among Hispanics/Latino individuals, reflecting the diversity/complexity of their origin and the limitations of trying to characterize complex genetic ancestry with a single question about Hispanic/Latino ethnicity. In the HCHS/SOL cohort, where participants were exclusively recruited from Hispanic/Latino communities, ≈80% had an NAMR ancestry proportion of ≥10%. By incorporating reference panels, such as from descendants of pre‐Columbian Mexican civilizations who have predominant NAMR ancestry, 2 we were able to achieve accurate inference of genetic ancestry in admixed individuals from large cohorts, as confirmed by qpAdm‐based analysis for inferring population ancestry.

Admixture mapping leverages the genetic contributions from multiple ancestral populations and their potential interactions, to identify genomic loci where diseases or traits exhibit differential risk by source ancestry in recently admixed (within the past 20 generations) populations. 16 , 64 A simulation study demonstrated that combining admixture mapping with single‐variant association testing within regions identified by admixture mapping offers greater statistical power than standard GWAS, particularly when the difference in effect allele frequency between ancestral populations is ≥0.4. 65 Moreover, joint admixture mapping can also reduce false positives common in single‐ancestry analyses by incorporating the complexities and interactions of genetic variants from different ancestral backgrounds. In addition, the distinct ancestry backgrounds across different regions of the United States among Hispanic American individuals (eg, a relatively higher NAMR ancestry in the West–Pacific and YRI ancestry in the North–East regions), must be considered to accurately capture the genetic landscape for admixture. For example, YRI ancestry may have driven the association signal at the GCKR locus (Figure 4E). However, variations in ancestry proportions among different cohorts (Figure 3A) and their effects in different directions may weaken association signals, as illustrated by the lack of replication in some cohorts (Figure 5A). Although some participants also had EAS ancestry proportion, it was not considered as a defining source ancestry.

An allele that has experienced positive selection and influences a trait in a source population can exert its influence on the trait after admixture, which is relevant to admixture mapping. This phenomenon was evident when using single‐ancestry admixture mapping, where the ancestral component linked to the trait can be identified, and this component may correspond to signs of positive selection (ie, an increased allele frequency) in the original ancestral population. For example, consider the FADS gene cluster: It evolved in Greenlandic Inuit as an adaptation to a diet rich in long‐chain polyunsaturated fatty acids 66 and among Africans by enhancing the synthesis of polyunsaturated fatty acids. 67 In a previous study, 68 we noted significant differentiation among global populations at this locus. Another example is GCKR, where Puerto Rican individuals, who possess ≈20% West African genetic ancestry, exhibit a significantly lower frequency of a protective (eg, favorable lipid levels) minor allele than non‐Hispanic White individuals. 69 A signature of positive selection has also been identified in APOA5 in Mexican Huichol individuals. 10 , 70

Strengths of our study include the use of a consistent population descriptor, specifically “genetically admixed Hispanic Americans,” in the admixture mapping analyses. We used contemporary population genetic analysis methods (eg, qpAdm) to determine the best‐fitted source populations for Hispanic Americans. This step is essential to accurately infer local ancestry proportions and increase the statistical power of admixture mapping. Furthermore, integrating both joint and single ancestry admixture mapping aids in identifying which source ancestry is driving signals associated with a trait. Such a comprehensive approach can enhance our understanding of the influence of local ancestry on biomedically relevant traits in admixed populations.

We acknowledge several limitations of the present study. First, a relatively low marker density limits the precision of local ancestry inference. We used ≈340 000 markers that were common between source and target populations (with imputed data) due to the limited genotype data available in the NAMR cohort. 2 Using the admixed Americans panel from 1KGP for imputation may not accurately predict unobserved genotypes in NAMR. We also employed this panel in GenotypeHarmonizer to ensure consistent allele alignment and reference matching in the NAMR cohort. To achieve a similar imputation performance as observed in Europeans, at least 3000 NAMR samples were required. 71 Second, the effect of Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium on local ancestry inference warrants further investigation, which is beyond the scope of the present study. Third, fine mapping for significant loci is needed since association signals obtained from admixture mapping span a large genomic region (Figure S9), and confirming the effect alleles within such regions is challenging. 65 , 72 Admixture mapping and conventional GWASs assess distinct null hypotheses, the former testing the association between ancestry proportions (as opposed to specific alleles) and traits and lacking the resolution to pinpoint the effect alleles directly. 21 However, within the genomic loci identified by our admixture mapping, previous GWASs of lipid traits in Hispanic/Latino individuals have shown several variants to be associated with such traits. Finally, validation of genetic mapping findings in admixed populations is hindered by the absence of genetically similar populations for replication. 21 For example, our analysis revealed substantial variability in ancestry proportions across different cohorts (Figure 2A through 2C).

In conclusion, in genetically admixed Hispanic Americans (defined as individuals having ≥10% NAMR genetic ancestry), admixture mapping identified 3 genomic loci where ancestral proportions were significantly associated with lipid traits. Our approach was distinct from conventional association studies, which test different null hypotheses. However, the results were similar in that the regions we identified had been previously reported to be associated with lipid traits. Different ancestral origins may have driven these associations, especially, NAMR ancestry at 1 LDL‐C locus and NAMR and IBS ancestries at 1 triglyceride locus. Our results suggest that admixture mapping can identify the source ancestry driving association of loci with biomedical traits in admixed individuals such as Hispanic Americans, an understudied population with high prevalence of dyslipidemia.
