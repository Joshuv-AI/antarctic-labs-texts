Proteomic landscape of colorectal cancer liver metastasis: molecular signatures and novel therapeutic targets
Authors: Hongyuan Shen, Jia Li, Guoqing Wang, Xiangyu Su, Zhigang Zhang, Jinjun Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLiver metastasis accounts for most deaths in colorectal cancer patients, but we still lack a clear understanding of how this devastating process unfolds at the molecular level. Identifying these mechanisms could lead to better ways to predict and treat metastatic disease.MethodsWe performed proteomic analysis on 38 tissue samples, including benign adenomas, primary colorectal tumors, and liver metastases, to map the molecular changes that drive cancer spread to the liver.ResultsWe found striking differences in protein expression patterns across tissue types. Primary tumors showed massive protein changes compared to normal tissue (nearly 1,900 alterations), but liver metastases were surprisingly selective in their modifications (just 89 unique changes from primary tumors). This specificity indicates that liver colonization depends on precise molecular adjustments rather than random cellular disruption. The proteins involved in metastasis fell into three main categories: tissue restructuring to support tumor growth, mechanisms to avoid immune detection, and blood clotting factors that help cancer cells establish themselves in the liver. We validated three biomarkers with clinical potential: HDHD3 shows promise for detecting existing metastases, while OLFM4 and ZNF34 can help identify primary tumors at high risk for spreading.ConclusionsOur work reveals that colorectal cancer liver metastasis involves targeted molecular changes rather than widespread cellular chaos. The biomarkers we identified could improve early detection of metastatic risk and guide more personalized treatment decisions for patients.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) stands as the third most diagnosed malignancy worldwide and represents the second most common cause of cancer death globally, with approximately 1.9 million new cases and 935,000 deaths reported annually. Despite significant advances in screening programs and therapeutic interventions, the prognosis for CRC patients remains heavily dependent on the stage at diagnosis and the presence of distant metastases. The liver emerges as the predominant site of hematogenous metastasis in CRC, with colorectal cancer liver metastases (CCLM) occurring in approximately 50%–60% of patients. This predilection for hepatic involvement stems from the portal venous circulation, which directly channels blood from the intestinal tract to the liver, creating an anatomical highway for circulating tumor cells. Tragically, CCLM represents the leading cause of death in CRC patients, fundamentally altering treatment paradigms and clinical outcomes, thereby posing one of the most formidable challenges in contemporary oncological practice (Lieberman et al., 2025; Eng et al., 2022; Abedizadeh et al., 2024; Canellas-Socias, Sancho & Batlle, 2024).

The evolutionary trajectory of CRC follows a well-characterized adenoma-carcinoma sequence, representing a paradigmatic example of multistep carcinogenesis that unfolds over several years to decades. This progressive transformation initiates with the development of benign adenomatous polyps from normal colonic epithelium, subsequently advancing through intermediate stages of increasing dysplasia—including low-grade and high-grade dysplastic changes—before culminating in invasive carcinoma with metastatic potential (Moorman et al., 2025; Al Bitar et al., 2023). While this morphological progression has been extensively documented through histopathological studies, the intricate molecular choreography orchestrating each transition remains incompletely elucidated. The complexity is further amplified when considering the additional molecular events required for metastatic dissemination, including epithelial-mesenchymal transition, invasion through basement membranes, intravasation into the circulation, survival in the bloodstream, extravasation at distant sites, and successful colonization of the hepatic microenvironment (Bertocchi et al., 2021; Li et al., 2020).

Contemporary understanding emphasizes that CRC progression and metastasis are driven by an intricate interplay of sequential genetic mutations, epigenetic modifications, and proteomic alterations that collectively transform normal epithelial cells into highly aggressive malignant tissues. The canonical molecular pathway involves initial mutations in tumor suppressor genes such as APC and TP53, followed by activation of oncogenes including KRAS and PIK3CA, ultimately resulting in the acquisition of hallmark cancer capabilities (Canellas-Socias et al., 2022). However, the proteome—representing the dynamic functional output of the genome—remains significantly understudied in the context of CRC metastasis, despite its direct relevance to cellular phenotype and therapeutic targeting. Unlike the relatively static nature of genetic alterations, proteomic landscapes undergo continuous remodeling in response to microenvironmental pressures, making them particularly valuable for understanding the adaptive mechanisms underlying metastatic colonization.

The clinical imperative for identifying reliable biomarkers capable of predicting metastatic potential and guiding therapeutic decision-making has never been more pressing.

Current staging systems, while valuable, often fail to capture the biological heterogeneity that determines individual patient outcomes. Furthermore, the emergence of precision medicine approaches necessitates the identification of organ-specific molecular signatures that can inform targeted therapeutic strategies and predict treatment responses. The liver microenvironment presents unique metabolic and immunological challenges that metastatic cells must overcome, suggesting that successful hepatic colonization requires distinct proteomic adaptations that differ from primary tumor biology.

To address these critical knowledge gaps, the present study was designed to comprehensively characterize the proteomic landscape across the CRC continuum, from benign precursor lesions to established liver metastases. We hypothesized that systematic proteomic profiling would reveal both conserved molecular programs driving CRC progression and distinct signatures associated with successful hepatic metastasis. Through comparative proteomic analyses of formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissues from three clinically relevant groups—patients with benign adenomatous polyps (control, CONT), primary CRC (PCRC), and CCLM (HM)—we sought to decode the molecular evolution underlying this devastating disease process.

This comprehensive proteomic investigation aimed to accomplish several critical objectives: first, to elucidate the proteomic homogeneity and heterogeneity patterns among polyps, primary tumors, and liver metastases, thereby providing unprecedented insights into CRC pathogenesis and the molecular determinants of metastatic success; second, to identify high-specificity and high-sensitivity proteomic biomarkers with potential clinical utility for risk stratification, early detection of metastatic disease, and therapeutic target discovery; and third, to establish a robust foundation for the development of personalized treatment strategies by revealing organ-specific molecular vulnerabilities that could be exploited for therapeutic intervention. Through this multifaceted approach, we anticipated that our findings would not only advance fundamental understanding of CRC biology but also provide tangible clinical benefits for the management of this challenging malignancy.

Materials and Methods

Study design and participants

This study was approved by the IEC for Clinical Research of Zhongda Hospital, Affiliated to Southeast University (Approval No.: (2022ZDSYLL021-P01)) and had obtained a waiver of informed consent from the participants. A total of 38 colorectal tissue samples were collected, with the following distribution for different analyses: 30 samples were allocated for data-independent acquisition (DIA) proteomic analysis, including 10 benign adenomas (designated as the control group, CONT), 10 primary colorectal cancer (PCRC) cases, and 10 colorectal cancer liver metastasis (HM) cases. All these 30 samples were processed using standard formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue preparation techniques to investigate protein abundance changes across the three lesion types. The remaining eight samples (four PCRC cases and four HM cases, also FFPE-processed) were used for parallel reaction monitoring (PRM) validation to verify the abundance levels of selected proteins identified from the DIA analysis.

Proteomic analysis

Total proteins were extracted from formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue samples. Paraffin slides were first deparaffinized by sequential treatment with xylene and graded ethanol solutions, followed by rehydration. The deparaffinized tissue sections were then transferred into microcentrifuge tubes and homogenized in an appropriate volume of SDT lysis buffer (4% SDS, 100 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.6, 0.1 M DTT) using a tissue homogenizer. The lysate was incubated in a boiling water bath for 3 min, followed by ultrasonication for 2 min to ensure complete tissue disruption. After centrifugation at 16,000× g for 20 min at 4 °C, the supernatant was collected, and protein concentrations were determined using the BCA assay (Pierce Biotechnology, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

Following trypsin digestion, peptides were desalted and subjected to liquid chromatography coupled with tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis. Protein extraction, quantification, and mass spectrometry analysis were performed by Shanghai Bioprofile Biotechnology. Data-independent acquisition (DIA) mass spectrometry was conducted using a Vanquish Neo UHPLC system coupled with an Orbitrap Astral mass spectrometer. Mass spectrometry data processing and protein quantification were performed using DIA-NN software, with database searching against the UniProt Human proteome (207,981 entries, downloaded July 17, 2023).

Quality control parameters included: Q-value ≤ 0.01 for peptide and protein identification, with precursor and fragment ion mass tolerances automatically optimized by DIA-NN. Label-free quantification was performed using extracted ion chromatograms. For statistical analysis, proteins detected in fewer than 50% of samples within any experimental group were excluded to ensure data reliability.

Statistical data analysis

Differential protein abundance analysis was conducted, and clustering, principal component analysis (PCA), and other statistical methods were applied to identify significant differences among the three groups. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis, and protein-protein interaction (PPI) network construction were performed to elucidate the functional implications of the proteomic changes; for GO and KEGG enrichment analyses, P-value < 0.05 was considered indicative of significant enrichment. Selected differentially abundant proteins were further validated using parallel reaction monitoring (PRM) techniques. Differential protein abundance was analyzed using a two-tailed Student’s t-test combined with fold change (FC) analysis. The proteins with a P-value < 0.05 and FC ≥ 3 or ≤ 1/3 were considered significantly differentially abundant proteins (DAPs).

Results

Patient characteristics

The baseline characteristics of 38 enrolled patients are summarized in Table 1, including 10 patients with benign adenomatous polyps (CONT), 14 with primary colorectal cancer (PCRC), and 14 with colorectal cancer liver metastasis (HM). Age and sex distributions were comparable across groups (mean age: CONT 49.80 ± 14.57 years, PCRC 53.64 ± 13.88 years, HM 57.21 ± 14.94 years, P = 0.470; male patients: 60.00%, 57.14%, and 64.29%, respectively, P = 1.000). No significant differences were observed in alcohol consumption or smoking history among groups.

Lesion location differed significantly (P = 0.011). Sigmoid colon predominated in CONT (60.00%), while PCRC showed heterogeneous distribution with descending colon most common (35.71%). In HM, the rectum was the predominant site (42.86%). TNM staging analysis was limited in some HM patients due to incomplete clinical information from pathology consultations, extensive metastatic disease with early postoperative death, or biopsy-only procedures. Among evaluable cases, T stage distribution was similar between PCRC and HM (P = 0.762), with T3 predominating (78.57% vs. 66.67%). However, the N stage differed significantly (P < 0.001): 85.71% of PCRC patients presented with N0 disease, whereas 60.00% of evaluable HM patients had N2 disease. All PCRC patients were M0, and all HM patients were M1 (P < 0.001). Most HM patients had synchronous liver metastases (71.43%).

Treatment strategies differed significantly between groups (P = 0.023). Combined surgery and systemic therapy predominated in PCRC (64.29%), while HM patients received more diverse regimens reflecting their heterogeneous clinical presentations.

Proteomic profiling

The proteomic analysis conducted in this study identified a total of 10,472 protein groups and 62,364 unique peptides across the benign, primary colorectal cancer (PCRC), and colorectal cancer liver metastasis (HM) samples (proteins that meet the criteria of a fold change greater than 3.0 (up or down) and a p-value less than 0.05 are considered significantly differentially expressed proteins). The data quality was demonstrated by the consistent protein identification rates among the three groups, ranging from 9,716 proteins in the benign samples to 10,262 proteins in PCRC samples, as well as 10,308 proteins in the HM samples. This comprehensive dataset provides a robust foundation for the subsequent differential abundance and bioinformatics analyses. The lengths of the peptide segments range from 10 to 15. The distribution of isoelectric points is primarily below 1,000 kDa, and the protein density distribution of each group of samples is relatively concentrated. Other relevant information includes the sequence coverage obtained, the reproducibility of proteomic measurements, the analytical depth of proteins detected across the entire cohort, and the data recorded for other biological, technical, and analytical repeat measurements, which have also demonstrated good quality control for the different enriched proteins among the three groups (Fig. S1 and S2).

The raw data have been deposited to the Proteome X change Consortium (https://proteomecentral.proteomexchange.org) via the iProX partner repository with the dataset identifier PXD067521. Principal component analysis (PCA) separated Control, HM (liver metastasis), and PCRC (primary CRC) samples with minimal overlap (Fig. 1A), indicating distinct proteomic signatures. HM and PCRC showed partial overlap, suggesting similar tumor states. CONT samples clustered tightly in the lower left quadrant, indicating uniformity among normal tissues. HM samples were displaced along PC1 (19.14%) but remained relatively cohesive, while PC2 (14.49%) further distinguished the inter-group separation. PCRC samples spread across both axes, reflecting the highest inter-sample heterogeneity. These findings demonstrate that metastatic lesions not only shift their overall proteomic signature but also exhibit increased variability, likely due to divergent adaptation to the hepatic microenvironment.

Biomarker protein screening

To identify potential biomarkers for colorectal cancer (CRC) metastasis, particularly hepatic metastasis (HM), our study integrated data-independent acquisition (DIA) proteomics and parallel reaction monitoring (PRM)-targeted proteomics. From the differential protein results, we first selected the top 100 proteins with the highest fold changes and statistical significance for PRM analysis. Concomitantly, we screened differential proteins using k-means clustering to analyze abundance trends, focusing on those consistent with CRC progression toward HM—specifically, proteins showing a sustained increase or decrease in abundance across the three groups. Notably, proteins with discordant abundance between primary CRC (PCRC) and HM tissues were prioritized as primary screening targets. After removing overlapping proteins from these two screening strategies, a total of 65 candidate proteins were selected for PRM validation. Subsequent exclusion of proteins lacking statistical significance, those inconsistent with initial DIA results, and those with minimal fold changes yielded three top candidates: zinc finger protein 34 (ZNF34), olfactomedin-4 (OLFM4), and haloacid dehalogenase-like hydrolase domain-containing protein 3 (HDHD3), which were identified as potential biomarkers associated with CRC hepatic metastasis.

Among these, HDHD3 showed promising diagnostic value: ROC curve analysis revealed an AUC of 0.830 (Fig. 5A), indicating its potential as a robust diagnostic biomarker for CRC. Additionally, box plot analysis (Fig. 5B) demonstrated that HDHD3 abundance was significantly elevated in both the HM and PCRC groups compared to the control group, with statistical significance confirmed in pairwise comparisons (all P < 0.05). Notably, HDHD3 abundance was markedly higher in the HM group, with a significant difference between PCRC and CONT (P = 1.91e−02) (Fig. 5D), suggesting its involvement in CRC metastatic progression, particularly liver metastasis. OLFM4 levels were significantly increased in the PCRC group relative to both the CONT and HM groups (Fig. 5C), with an ANOVA test revealing a p-value of 1.52e−02 for the HM vs PCRC comparison. ROC analysis showed an AUC of 0.760 for OLFM4 (Fig. 5E), supporting its role in the aggressive phenotype of advanced CRC and potential utility as a biomarker for liver metastasis.

ZNF34 was significantly overexpressed in PCRC, with an AUC of 0.860 in ROC analysis (Fig. 5F), highlighting its diagnostic potential in advanced CRC stages. Although no significant difference in ZNF34 levels was observed between the HM and CONT groups, its higher abundance in PCRC suggests that ZNF34 may be a key mediator of CRC progression. Further investigation is warranted to elucidate its role in tumor invasion and dissemination.

Collectively, these findings indicate that HDHD3, OLFM4, and ZNF34 hold promise as biomarkers for CRC diagnosis and prognosis, especially in relation to liver metastasis. Future studies are needed to clarify the precise molecular mechanisms by which these proteins contribute to CRC invasiveness and metastasis, with a focus on their roles in signaling pathways governing tumor progression.

Pathological study of the specimen

The following are the histopathological findings from the three specimen groups: first, normal colorectal mucosa (Figs. 6A–6C) displays intact crypt architecture with uniformly aligned epithelial cells and no evidence of hyperplasia or dysplasia; next, non-metastatic CRC (Fig. 6D–6F) shows moderate crypt irregularity, hyperplastic glands, occasional glandular distortion, and mild cellular atypia confined to the primary lesion without stromal invasion; finally, hepatic metastases (Fig. 6G–6I) are characterized by disrupted tissue architecture, glandular clusters enveloped by fibrous stroma, extensive mitotic activity, necrotic foci, and clear vascular invasion, all hallmark features of advanced metastatic disease.

Discussion

Metastasis is a major contributor to cancer-related mortality, and liver metastasis specifically accounts for the majority of deaths in colorectal cancer (CRC) patients. Colorectal predominantly metastasize to the liver, with peritoneal involvement being rare; the liver thus represents the primary target for hematogenous dissemination of CRC, making colorectal cancer liver metastasis (CCLM) a key clinical challenge in CRC management (Biondo, Frago & Kreisler, 2024; Allgayer, Leupold & Patil, 2020; Liu et al., 2024). Clinically, about 15%–25% of CRC patients present with liver metastases at diagnosis, and an additional 15%–25% develop liver metastases following radical resection of the primary tumor. Notably, 80%–90% of these liver metastases are initially unresectable, and patients with CCLM exhibit significantly poorer overall survival compared to those with nonmetastatic CRC (Canellas-Socias, Sancho & Batlle, 2024; Sendi et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2023).

Liver metastasis remains the leading cause of death in CRC patients, underscoring the urgent need to unravel the molecular mechanisms driving CRC progression to hepatic metastasis. Our findings provide critical insights into the proteomic landscape associated with CCLM, identifying key biomarkers and dysregulated signaling pathways that hold promise as novel therapeutic targets (Ladabaum et al., 2024; Wang & Fang, 2023; Zhang et al., 2023).

Our proteomic analysis, encompassing primary CRC, metastatic CRC, and normal tissues, revealed distinct protein abundance profiles across these groups. These results highlight the involvement of multiple pathways in CRC progression, particularly those governing cell migration, adhesion, extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, and immune evasion (Li et al., 2023b; Lee et al., 2024; Giguelay et al., 2022; Muniz Pedrogo, Sears & Melia, 2024; Li et al., 2023a; Chu et al., 2024). Notably, ZNF34, OLFM4, and HDHD3 emerged as promising biomarkers candidates for diagnosing and monitoring CRC, especially in the context of liver metastasis.

ZNF34, OLFM4, and HDHD3 were significantly abundant in advanced CRC, particularly in primary CRC (PCRC) samples, implicating their pivotal roles in tumor progression and metastatic potential. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis validated their diagnostic utility, with AUC values of 0.860 (ZNF34), 0.760 (OLFM4), and 0.830 (HDHD3), reinforcing their potential as preliminary biomarkers for PCRC and the related liver metastasis.

The molecular pathways identified herein suggest that these proteins contribute to the aggressive phenotype of CRC, particularly in the context of liver metastasis. ZNF34 modulates ECM interactions, cell adhesion, and signaling cascades, while OLFM4 is linked to cellular motility and invasion—processes critical for metastasis. HDHD3, conversely, regulates cellular stress responses, potentially supporting cancer cell survival within the metastatic microenvironment.

The KEGG and GO pathway analyses point to significant enrichment of pathways such as “Cell adhesion”, “Cell migration” and “Regulation of cell signaling” across metastatic CRC samples, particularly those from the HM. These pathways are essential for tumor cell dissemination and metastatic colonization, reinforcing the importance of proteins like ZNF34, OLFM4, and HDHD3 in these processes. Moreover, pathways related to immune modulation, such as “Regulation of immune response” and “Negative regulation of blood coagulation”, were significantly enriched, suggesting that CRC liver metastasis may also involve immune evasion mechanisms that allow metastatic cells to thrive in the liver (Pelka et al., 2021; An et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2022; Fan et al., 2021; Al Zein et al., 2023).

Furthermore, the enrichment of ECM remodeling pathways in metastatic samples underscores the critical role of the tumor microenvironment in facilitating liver metastasis. Targeting tumor-ECM interactions may thus offer therapeutic opportunities for preventing or treating CCLM. Similarly, inhibiting immune escape mechanisms—such as modulating immune checkpoints—could enhance the efficacy of immunotherapies in patients with CCLM (Liu et al., 2022; Bao et al., 2023; Yan et al., 2024; Li et al., 2023c).

Limitations of the Study

While this proteomic analysis offers valuable insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying CRC progression to liver metastasis, several limitations should be noted. First, the study was based on a relatively small cohort (38 samples), which may restrict the generalizability of the findings. Although the sample size is adequate for an exploratory study, future validation in larger, more diverse cohorts is needed to confirm the robustness of the identified biomarkers and proteomic profiles. Moreover, we used benign adenomatous polyps rather than normal colonic tissues as controls due to ethical constraints in obtaining healthy tissue samples. While adenomas represent pre-malignant lesions in the adenoma-carcinoma sequence and are clinically relevant controls, they harbor early molecular changes distinct from normal mucosa. Future studies should incorporate truly normal tissues for more rigorous comparisons.

Second, the cross-sectional design limits our ability to capture temporal dynamics of proteomic changes during metastasis progression. Without longitudinal data tracking CRC from primary tumor to liver metastasis, we cannot assess how protein abundance evolves with disease advancement or the impact of early intervention.

Third, while HDHD3, OLFM4, and ZNF34 are proposed as potential biomarkers, their mechanistic roles in CRC liver metastasis (e.g., in regulating ECM remodeling, immune evasion, or metabolic adaptation) remain unvalidated. In addition, our PRM validation was conducted in a small cohort (n = 4 per group), which limits the statistical power and generalizability of these findings. Consequently, the proteins identified (ZNF34, HDHD3, and OLFM4) should be considered preliminary candidate biomarkers rather than validated biomarkers. Validation in larger, independent cohorts using both targeted proteomics and orthogonal methods (e.g., immunohistochemistry, ELISA) is essential before clinical application can be considered. Functional studies (e.g., in vitro cell models or in vivo metastasis assays) and clinical trials are required to confirm their causal involvement and therapeutic potential. Additionally, the use of formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissues may introduce biases, as formalin-induced protein degradation or modification could affect quantitative accuracy; fresh-frozen samples in future studies may provide more reliable proteomic profiles.

Lastly, the study focused primarily on tumor cell-intrinsic proteomic changes, with limited exploration of the metastatic microenvironment. The interplay between tumor cells and stromal components (e.g., immune cells, fibroblasts) and its association with ECM remodeling or angiogenesis was not detailed. Integrating multi-omics data (transcriptomics, metabolomics) in future research could provide a more comprehensive understanding of CRC metastasis.

Conclusion

In this study, we provide crucial insights into the proteomic alterations associated with colorectal cancer liver metastasis. By analyzing tissue samples from benign adenomas, primary CRC, and liver metastases, we identified distinct protein signatures that drive CRC progression and metastatic dissemination to the liver. Our findings show that liver metastasis is marked by selective proteomic changes, with fewer alterations observed compared to primary tumors, suggesting that metastatic colonization relies on targeted molecular reprogramming rather than widespread alterations.

Among the proteins identified, HDHD3, OLFM4, and ZNF34 show promise as potential biomarkers for both liver metastasis and high-risk primary CRC. The pathways enriched in metastatic CRC, including extracellular matrix remodeling, immune evasion, and coagulation, further emphasize the key roles of the tumor microenvironment in metastasis.

These findings not only enhance our understanding of the molecular mechanisms driving liver metastasis in CRC but also offer new avenues for developing diagnostic tools and targeted therapies. However, further validation in larger cohorts and functional studies will be essential to fully translate these insights into clinical practice.
