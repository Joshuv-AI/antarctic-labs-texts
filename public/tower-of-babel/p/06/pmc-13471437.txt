Cerebral venous sinus thrombosis secondary to relapsed minimal change disease: a case managed with endovascular thrombolysis
Authors: Li Tian, Hailong Du, Jiaomei Zhu, Lingling Xing, Shaomei Li, Mengsi Zhou, Liying Wen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCerebral venous sinus thrombosis (CVST) is a rare type of stroke characterized by the formation of blood clots within the dural venous sinuses which has a high mortality rate. Despite its potential severity, cerebral venous sinus thrombosis secondary to nephrotic syndrome remains rarely reported and under-recognized in clinical practice.Case presentationWe described a 20-year-old male who presented with a relapse of minimal change disease, complicated by extensive CVST. On the basis of anticoagulant therapy, his neurological condition deteriorated rapidly, manifesting as altered mental consciousness, progressive limb weakness, and new-onset seizures. Given the failure of conventional medical management, endovascular therapy of catheter-directed thrombolysis was performed. The patient’s neurological status improved dramatically within days.ConclusionsThis case demonstrates that cerebral venous sinus thrombosis should be considered in nephrotic syndrome patients presenting with even subtle or non-specific neurological symptoms. Patients with focal neurological deficits, altered mental status, new-onset seizures, or unusual headache should undergo prompt neurological evaluation and appropriate brain imaging, including computed tomography venography (CTV) or magnetic resonance venography (MRV) when CVST is suspected. In the specific setting of nephrotic syndrome, clinicians should maintain a heightened index of suspicion for CVST, even when initial neurological findings are subtle or non-specific. As illustrated by this case, early recognition and timely escalation to endovascular therapy can be life-saving when conventional anticoagulation fails to prevent neurological deterioration. Our patient achieved complete neurological recovery and sustained remission of nephrotic syndrome, underscoring the value of multidisciplinary collaboration and individualized treatment strategies.

Background

Cerebral venous sinus thrombosis (CVST) is a rare type of stroke, which accounts for 0.5%-1% in all stroke patients, with an estimated incidence of > 10 per million per year [1, 2] and predominantly affects younger individuals [3]. CVST has a multifactorial etiology, encompassing both heritable thrombophilic conditions and acquired prothrombotic triggers like infammatory reaction, autoimmune diseases, use of oral contraceptive, hormonal changes, puerperium or pregnancy states, and systemic diseases. Common clinical symptoms include headache, seizures, altered consciousness, blurred vision, papilledema, exophthalmos, nystagmus, and focal neurological damage caused by cranial nerve palsy [4]. The mainstay of management is anticoagulation therapy when the diagnosis is confirmed [5–8]. However, clinical or radiographic deterioration occurs in approximately 20% of patients even with adequate anticoagulation [9]. In this subgroup, endovascular therapies offer a valuable strategy for reducing thrombus burden, promoting recanalization, and alleviating venous congestion.

Minimal change disease (MCD) affects approximately 10–15% of adult patients with idiopathic nephrotic syndrome and has a high recurrence rate. Therefore, patients often present with hypercoagulable state during disease onset and relapse, making thromboembolic disease a major complication of nephrotic syndrome. Commonly observed sites of thrombosis include the renal veins and the deep veins of the lower extremities. Cerebral venous sinus thrombosis rarely reported. We present a case of a patient with relapsed refractory nephrotic syndrome who was concurrently diagnosed with cerebral venous sinus thrombosi.

Case description

A 20-year-old young male presented to the nephrology department with a self-reported relapse of kidney disease. Six years ago, the patient developed bilateral lower limb edema, massive proteinuria, and hypoalbuminemia. A renal biopsy performed at our hospital revealed minimal change disease. He was treated with a full dose of prednisone acetate 50 mg once daily (1 mg/kg), and his urine protein turned negative after two weeks. Since then, the patient experienced relapses whenever the prednisone acetate dose was tapered to 10–15 mg/day. According to the precipitating factors of each relapse and the patient’s response to subsequent therapy, the clinical diagnosis was refractory nephrotic syndrome, namely steroid-dependent nephrotic syndrome. Consequently, a repeat renal biopsy was not indicated. Given the frequent relapses, other immunosuppressive agents were introduced in combination with steroids. Over the past three years, leflunomide and cyclosporine were sequentially added to the steroids. In the last six months, the patient has been treated with a combination of steroids and cyclosporine, maintaining complete remission. One day ago, he noticed bilateral lower limb edema, reduced urine output, accompanied by headache, nausea, and vomiting, suggesting a relapse of nephrotic syndrome, and thus came to the hospital for evaluation. There was no prior history of thromboembolic complications.

After admission, a preliminary physical examination revealed that the patient’s heart rate was 109 beats per minute, blood pressure of 115/91 mmHg, a respiratory rate of 22 breaths per minute, a temperature of 36.7 °C, and oxygen saturation of 99%. Mild edema is present in both lower limbs. Neurologic examination revealed no significant abnormalities. The patient was alert, oriented to person, place, and time, and exhibited normal muscle strength in all four limbs. Pupillary reflex and eye movements were normal. Laboratory results showed proteinuria 10,760 g/24 h (Normal range: < 150 mg/24 h), hypoalbuminemia 1.63 g/dl (Normal range: 4.0 to 5.5 g/dL), total cholesterol 13.54 mmol/L (Normal range: < 5.2 mmol/ L), Creatinine 76 µmol/L (Normal range :67 to 97 µmol/L). Complete blood count showed white blood cells at 14.72*109/L (Normal range: 3.50 to 9.50*10⁹/L), hemoglobin at 200 g/L (Normal range: 135 to 175 g/L), and platelets at 247*109/L (Normal range :125 to 350*10⁹/L). Coagulation panel indicated activated partial thromboplastin time is 49.4 s (Normal range: 25.4 to 38.4 s), fibrinogen is 7.83 g/L (Normal range: 2.38 to 4.98 g/L), and D-dimer is 3.86 µg/mL (Normal range: 0–0.243 µg/mL). In terms of autoimmunity, autoantibodies were negative, and the IgG level was 3.27 g/L (normal range: 7.5–15 g/L). Regarding liver function, alanine aminotransferase was 9.7 U/L (Normal range: 9–50 U/L), and aspartate aminotransferase was 29.6 U/L (Normal range: 15–40 U/L). Thrombophilia screening revealed no abnormalities. Laboratory investigations confirmed a relapse of nephrotic syndrome. There was also concurrent hemoconcentration. The patient was promptly started on methylprednisolone sodium succinate (40 mg daily) and therapeutic low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH,4100IU daily). These managements were provided alongside aggressive fluid resuscitation. While awaiting a scheduled cranial computed tomography (CT) scan, his neurological status acutely deteriorated, manifesting as sudden-onset muscle weakness. Neurological examination revealed muscle strength of Grade 4/5 in the right lower limb and Grade 3+/5 in the left lower limb according to the Medical Research Council (MRC) scale, with preserved (5/5) strength in the upper limbs. An urgent non-contrast cranial CT was obtained, which revealed linear and patchy hyperdensities are noted within the quadrigeminal cistern, along the midline structures, in the posterior horns of both lateral ventricles, and along the course of the bilateral transverse sinuses (Fig. 1A, B). These findings were suggestive of dural venous sinus thrombosis. Further investigation was recommended for definitive diagnosis. Subsequently, magnetic resonance imaging examination was performed, including plain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), magnetic resonance venography (MRV) (Fig. 2A, B), diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) (Fig. 2C), and susceptibility-weighted imaging (SWI) (Fig. 2D). The examination demonstrated the following findings: (1) Extensive cerebral venous sinus thrombosis. (2) Signal abnormalities in the right parietal lobe and left frontal lobe, compatible with acute-to-subacute venous infarction. (3) Multiple tortuous venous collateral vessels, consistent with prominent venous reflux, observed in the cerebellar, occipital, and bilateral frontoparietal regions, most notably within the cerebellar and occipital areas. In addition to anticoagulation, dehydration therapy for intracranial pressure reduction and antiepileptic prophylaxis were administered. Nevertheless, the patient experienced worsening neurological symptoms, including impaired consciousness and seizures. An emergent consultation was obtained with the neurosurgery department. Given the critical and progressive nature of the thrombosis, they deemed the condition a neurosurgical emergency and proceeded to urgent endovascular intervention. A puncture site was identified approximately 2 cm above the anterior border of the right inguinal ligament. Following successful puncture of the right femoral vein, a 6 F sheath was inserted. Venography was performed via a 6 F guiding catheter over a guidewire, revealing occlusion of the superior sagittal sinus and bilateral transverse sinuses (Fig. 3A, B). A guide catheter combined with a distal access catheter was advanced over a guidewire to the level of the transverse sinus. Subsequently, a microcatheter was navigated over a guidewire and positioned in the anterior portion of the superior sagittal sinus. Urokinase (300,000 U) was slowly administered through the microcatheter, which was then left in place. The right femoral venous puncture site was secured with a compression device. Postoperatively, anticoagulation and sodium valproate for antiepileptic therapy were continued, and urokinase was administered via continuous infusion at a rate of 20,000 U per hour. Three days postoperatively, the patient regained consciousness, experienced no seizures, but remained anuric and was transferred back to the nephrology department. Continue administering corticosteroids therapy for the primary disease, anticoagulation therapy, antiepileptic therapy, and thrombolytic therapy to the patient. Five days after operation, the patient’s responsiveness had largely returned to normal, and cerebral angiography was performed again. A repeated manual contrast injection via the intermediate catheter demonstrated patency of the partial superior sagittal sinus and left transverse sinus (Fig. 4A, B). The intermediate catheter and arterial sheath were subsequently withdrawn, and compression was applied to the right femoral venous puncture site. Following the angiography procedure, anticoagulation was transitioned to a regimen of oral warfarin combined with low-molecular-weight heparin, and subsequently maintained with warfarin monotherapy (adjusted according to the patient’s international normalized ratio). On the 10th day after cerebral venous sinus thrombolysis, the patient’s muscle strength had returned to normal. At discharge (20 days post-thrombolysis), the patient was in satisfactory general condition, free from headache, dizziness, limb weakness, and bilateral lower extremity edema. Urine output had normalized after progressing through phases of anuria, oliguria, and polyuria. The patient continued glucocorticoid therapy for nephropathy, along with oral warfarin for anticoagulation and sodium valproate for seizure prophylaxis. During the 2.5-year outpatient follow-up in the nephrology department, glucocorticoids were recently discontinued, and the patient declined biologic agents to reduce the risk of relapse due to financial constraints. Oral anticoagulation was discontinued approximately six months after the remission of nephrotic syndrome.

Discussion

CVST is a rare but life-threatening subtype of stroke, associated with high mortality and significant therapeutic challenges. Patients with nephrotic syndrome (NS) are hypercoagulable due to an imbalance between pro-coagulant and anti-coagulant factors, predisposing them to thromboembolic events [10]. However, CVST remains a rarely reported complication of NS [11, 12]. Here we describe a young male with steroid-dependent NS who developed CVST during a relapse, presenting with non-specific symptoms that rapidly progressed to limb weakness, altered consciousness, and new-onset seizures. This case highlights the importance of early recognition and aggressive intervention in NS-associated CVST.

The patient in our study was a young male with steroid-dependent nephrotic syndrome who was hospitalized due to a relapse. He initially presented with lower extremity edema, anuria, headache, mild nausea, and vomiting, which rapidly progressed to limb weakness, altered mental status, and new-onset seizures. The diagnosis of CVST can pose a clinical challenge due to its presentation with non-specific headache and variable focal neurological deficits. Initially, CVST was not considered in this patient; it was not until he developed limb weakness that an emergent head CT scan was performed. In fact, previous case reports have described CVST as an initial or recurrent manifestation of nephrotic syndrome [11–13]. Patients with serum albumin below 2.8 g/dL have a 2.5-fold elevated risk of thromboembolic events [14], though the intrinsic etiology of CVST in nephrotic syndrome remains unclear.

Non-contrast cranial CT is often the first imaging modality in the diagnostic workup. In the acute stage of CVST, increased density signals may be observed within the vessel [15]. However, initial non-contrast CT findings can be normal in up to one-third of cases [16]. Computed tomography venography (CTV) and magnetic resonance venography (MRV), preferably with contrast, are the imaging modalities of choice for diagnosing CVST and should be employed in all suspected cases. If CTV and MRV are inconclusive, formal catheter-based cerebral angiography may be performed [15]. In our patient, emergent non-contrast head CT revealed linear and patchy hyperdensities, leading to further confirmation. Prior to the diagnosis of CVST, the patient had already received prophylactic anticoagulation with 4100 IU of LMWH once daily due to severe hypoalbuminemia. After diagnosis confirmation, the anticoagulation regimen was intensified to 4100 IU twice daily subcutaneously. Anticoagulation serves as the cornerstone of clinical management for CVST, as it prevents thrombus propagation and facilitates recanalization [3, 5–7]. The German consensus-based (S2k) guideline on cerebral venous and dural sinus thrombosis recommends that LMWH be preferred over unfractionated heparin in the acute phase. Lee et al. reported a case of CVST complicating relapsed minimal change disease, in which the patient improved following anticoagulation and increased corticosteroids [13].

In our patient, despite therapeutic anticoagulation, neurological manifestations worsened, prompting urgent endovascular intervention (continuous local urokinase thrombolysis via an indwelling microcatheter). Given that the patient’s clinical symptoms improved after local thrombolysis and repeat angiography demonstrated partial recanalization of the cerebral venous sinus, no additional thrombectomy was pursued. Endovascular interventional therapy enables the direct mechanical removal or pharmacological dissolution of the thrombus. This, in turn, restores venous outflow, rapidly lowers intracranial pressure, and interrupts the deleterious cycle of cerebral ischemia and edema, ultimately rescuing viable but threatened brain cells. Endovascular therapy may be considered in patients who deteriorate despite anticoagulation, or who present with severe neurological deficits or coma [6, 17]. Endovascular treatment options include pharmacological contact thrombolysis (intraoperative or continuous infusion via microcatheter) and mechanical thrombectomy. Prompt recanalization of the venous sinus is independently associated with favorable functional outcomes. However, some studies have linked endovascular interventions to increased mortality, suggesting they should be reserved for rescue therapy or when standard treatments are contraindicated. The TO-ACT randomized controlled trial found that endovascular treatment (mechanical thrombectomy, local thrombolysis, or both) was not superior to anticoagulation alone [18]. Moreover, a recent propensity score-matched study across 27 centers in four countries found no significant benefit of endovascular mechanical thrombectomy on 90-day functional outcomes [19]. Despite these controversies, our patient’s neurological symptoms improved rapidly after combined anticoagulation and endovascular treatment. Urokinase (300,000 U) was slowly administered through the microcatheter, followed by a continuous postoperative infusion at 20,000 U/hour for 5 days, after which the catheter was removed based on symptom improvement. Currently, insufficient evidence exists to determine the optimal endovascular technique or thrombectomy device. Regarding the choice between urokinase and alteplase for local thrombolysis in CVST, no high-level comparative evidence is available; both are considered reasonable options in clinical practice, with selection depending on patient-specific factors and operator experience. Jia et al. [20] reported a 32-year-old woman with CVST caused by hyperthyroidism who received endovascular intervention after conventional anticoagulation failed, initially with local thrombolysis followed by balloon dilation and thrombus aspiration when the effect was unsatisfactory, ultimately leading to neurological improvement. Thus, safe and effective treatment must be individualized based on the patient’s specific condition and clinical course.

After transition from the acute to the stable phase, warfarin (a vitamin K antagonist) was discontinued approximately six months after remission of nephrotic syndrome. To date, at 2.5 years post-thrombolysis, the patient has maintained complete remission of nephrotic syndrome with no recurrence of CVST. For maintenance therapy in CVST, oral warfarin, rivaroxaban, or dabigatran is recommended, with a minimum anticoagulation duration of 3 to 12 months. Direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) are generally preferred over vitamin K antagonists (VKAs) for oral anticoagulation in CVT patients, with the exception of those with triple-positive antiphospholipid syndrome, for whom VKA therapy should be preferred [6]. The Chinese Expert Consensus on the Diagnosis and Management of Cerebral Venous Outflow Disorders recommends warfarin as the first choice during the stable phase, with the INR maintained between 2 and 3. For CVT patients unsuitable for warfarin, oral dabigatran or rivaroxaban may be considered; the use of other novel oral anticoagulants requires further research evidence [7].

In the long-term follow-up of this patient, he was regularly seen in the nephrology outpatient clinic every 2–3 months for evaluation of his renal status. Given that his cerebral venous sinus thrombosis was considered to be associated with the underlying renal disease, no routine scheduled follow-up with the neurosurgery department was conducted. In fact, for patients with CVST, the decision regarding the need for secondary prevention requires a comprehensive risk assessment that weighs the risk of recurrent thrombosis after stopping anticoagulation against the risk of bleeding in case of continued anticoagulation. For both this patient and patients with nephrotic syndrome, early anticoagulation therapy is important to prevent the occurrence and progression of thrombotic events. According to Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) 2021 Clinical Practice Guideline for the Management of Glomerular Diseases [21], when the patient has a serum albumin level of < 20–25 g/L accompanied by any of the following criteria—including proteinuria > 10 g/d, body mass index > 35 kg/m², a genetic predisposition to thromboembolism, New York Heart Association functional class III or IV heart failure, recent orthopedic or abdominal surgery, or prolonged immobilization—prophylactic anticoagulation is recommended. For patients without a bleeding tendency, warfarin or low-molecular-weight heparin is recommended for anticoagulation. We speculate that early anticoagulation therapy in this patient also contributed to his remarkably rapid clinical recovery.

During our treatment process, the medical team promptly conducted a risk assessment, provided specialized treatment recommendations, and immediately performed cerebral angiography and thrombolysis. The rapid and coordinated efforts in delivering timely treatment ultimately contributed to the patient’s favorable recovery, with no neurological sequelae and complete remission of nephrotic syndrome. This case highlights the critical importance of multidisciplinary collaboration and the importance of timely adjustment of the treatment strategy.

Conclusion

This case demonstrates that cerebral venous sinus thrombosis should be considered in nephrotic syndrome patients presenting with even subtle or non-specific neurological symptoms. Patients with focal neurological deficits, altered mental status, new-onset seizures, or unusual headache should undergo prompt neurological evaluation and appropriate brain imaging, including CTV or MRV when CVST is suspected. In the specific setting of nephrotic syndrome, clinicians should maintain a heightened index of suspicion for CVST, even when initial neurological findings are subtle or non-specific. As illustrated by this case, early recognition and timely escalation to endovascular therapy can be life-saving when conventional anticoagulation fails to prevent neurological deterioration. Our patient achieved complete neurological recovery and sustained remission of nephrotic syndrome, underscoring the value of multidisciplinary collaboration and individualized treatment strategies.
