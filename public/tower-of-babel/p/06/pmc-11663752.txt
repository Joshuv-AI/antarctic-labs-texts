A quantitative proteomic approach to evaluate the efficacy of carnosine in a murine model of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)
Authors: Alfonsina D’Amato, Alessandra Altomare, Ettore Gilardoni, Giovanna Baron, Marina Carini, Elsa Melloni, Gloria Padoani, Silvia Vailati, Giovanni Caponetti, Giancarlo Aldini
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The aim of the work was to study a dose-dependent effect of inhaled carnosine (10, 50 or 100 mg/kg/day) in mice exposed to cigarette smoke as a model of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). A dose-dependent loading of the dipeptide in lung tissue and bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) was firstly demonstrated by LC-ESI-MS analysis. Cigarette smoke exposure induced a significant lung inflammation and oxidative stress in mice which was dose-dependently reduced by carnosine. Inflammation was firstly evaluated by measuring the cytokines content in the BAL. All the measured cytokines were found significantly higher in the smoke group in respect to control, although the data are affected by a significant variability. Carnosine was found effective only at the highest dose tested and significantly only for keratinocyte-derived cytokine (KC). Due to the high variability of cytokines, a quantitative proteomic approach to better understand the functional effect of carnosine and its molecular mechanisms was used. Proteomic data clearly indicate that smoke exposure had a great impact on lung tissue with 692 proteins differentially expressed above a threshold of 1.5-fold. Protein network analysis identified the activation of some pathways characteristic of COPD, including inflammatory response, fibrosis, induction of immune system by infiltration and migration of leukocyte pathways, altered pathway of calcium metabolism and oxidative stress. Carnosine at the tested dose of 100 mg/kg was found effective in reverting all the pathways evoked by smoke. Only a partial reverse of the dysregulated proteins was evident at low- and mid-tested doses, although, for some specific proteins, indicating an overall dose-dependent effect. Regarding the molecular mechanisms involved, we found that carnosine upregulated some key enzymes related to Nrf2 activation and in particular glutathione peroxidase, reductase, transferase, SOD, thioredoxins, and carbonyl reductase. Such mechanism would explain the antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects of the dipeptide.

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a respiratory disease characterized by ongoing respiratory symptoms and airflow limitations that result from airway or alveolar abnormalities induced by the inhalation of toxic particles or gases [1]. Cigarette smoke (CS) has been identified as the most common risk factor for COPD in several studies, and increasing evidence suggests that air pollution, host genetics, and environmental factors also contribute to the development of COPD [2,3].

In addition to being responsible of significant social and economic burdens, COPD is one of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality worldwide. Unfortunately, no treatment is currently available to halt COPD progression. Therefore, it is crucial to investigate drug targets for COPD and potential compounds that could lead to the development of effective therapies [4].

Various biological mechanisms have been found to be involved in the development of COPD and among these, inflammation and oxidative stress are two conditions strictly related each other and both highly involved in the onset and development of COPD [5]. In general, when oxidative stress appears as a primary disorder, inflammation develops as a secondary disorder and further adds to oxidative stress. On the other hand, inflammation as a primary disorder can induce oxidative stress as a secondary disorder, which can further heighten inflammation [6]. In the context of COPD, oxidative stress impacts the release of inflammatory mediators, affects cell growth, and indirectly triggers cell apoptosis [[7], [8], [9]]. Additionally, oxidative stress damages the phagocytic function and clearance of macrophages, contributing to persistent inflammation in the airway [10]. In patients with COPD, oxidative stress promotes inflammation and remodeling of the airway’s smooth muscles through mitochondrial dysfunction [11,12]. Notably, oxidative stress also plays a role in autophagy regulation by generally amplifying its induction [13]. Currently, many studies have reported increased levels of oxidative stress biomarkers, such as lipid peroxidation products, protein oxidation, and total oxidant status, in patients with COPD. Some researchers have suggested that these markers could be utilized to predict the progression and development of COPD [[14], [15], [16]]. In mouse models of COPD, increased oxidative stress has been observed, suggesting that it is a major mechanism involved in the pathogenesis and worsening of the disease.

Together with oxidative stress, inflammation is also considered a critical event in the development of COPD [17]. The accumulation of inflammatory cells and the release of inflammatory cytokines, including NF-κB, IL-8, IL-6, and TNF-α, have been proposed as a mechanism for airway structure damage, airway remodeling, and small airway obstruction that contribute to COPD development [18]. The expression of these proinflammatory markers has been correlated with poor clinical outcomes in COPD patients, suggesting that inflammation is a critical driver in the pathogenesis and development of COPD [8,19].

Taken together, oxidative stress and inflammation are two key processes involved in the onset and progression of COPD, to be considered as target for a pharmacological approach.

Carnosine is an endogenous dipeptide with a well-established anti-inflammatory and antioxidant activity as found in several in vitro and ex vivo studies and characterized by an excellent safety profile which is requested for a chronic treatment as for COPD [20]. Carnosine has been found to prevent several oxidative/inflammatory based diseases in preclinical models, including type 2 diabetes and its micro and macrovascular complications [21,22], cardiovascular disorders [23,24], neurodegenerative [25,26] and kidney disease [27,28]. Moreover, many studies demonstrate carnosine as a bioactive compound effective in alleviating inflammation and oxidative stress in the lung. In a seminal study, Cuzzocrea et al. [29] showed a significant reduction of tissue damage and cellular apoptosis in the lungs of bleomycin treated mice after an oral administration of carnosine. Carnosine significantly reduced lung inflammation resulting in reduced fibrosis and the preservation of the alveolar architecture.

The anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effect of carnosine in the lung was then reported in other preclinical studies using different inducers of oxidative stress and inflammation, including LPS sepsis [30], swine influenza virus [31] as reviewed by Ken-Ichiro Tanaka et al. [32]. More recently, Jaehyun Park et al. demonstrated the anti-fibrotic and anti-inflammatory effects of carnosine via reactive oxygen species (ROS) regulation in bleomycin (BLM)-induced Idiopathic Pulmonary fibrosis mice [33]. Carnosine significantly alleviated cholestasis-induced lung injury by alleviating oxidative stress, inflammatory response, and by blunting the development of tissue fibrosis [34].

By considering the involvement of oxidative stress and inflammation in COPD and the effectiveness of carnosine in modulating these processes in the lung, as found in different pre-clinical studies, investigating the activity of carnosine in preventing/treating COPD deserves a pre-clinical investigation and represents the aim of the present study. So far, the effect of carnosine on COPD has not yet been examined using animal models but only in in vitro cell studies, where it was found that the inflammatory and oxidative cell response induced by cigarette smoke extract (CSE) was significantly inhibited by carnosine, acting by enhancing antioxidant genes and inhibiting CSE-induced nuclear translocation of NFkB [33].

Mode of administration is another important aspect to consider when carnosine is given for lung treatment. Endogenous carnosine and derivatives, namely anserine and homocarnosine, have an organ-specific distribution pattern, being mainly distributed in some organs such as skeletal muscles and brain but present in negligible amounts or absent in others, such as lung [35], where carnosinases and carnosine synthase are present in negligible amounts or absent as found by searching the human protein atlas (https://www.proteinatlas.org). In most of the preclinical studies aimed at evaluating the biological activity of carnosine in lung tissue, the dipeptide was given orally but the tissue content of carnosine was not reported. However, it is plausible to consider a distribution of carnosine from the circulation to the lung, being carnosine stable in rodent serum due to the lack of serum carnosinase (CN1). However, such a tissue distribution should not occur in humans due to the serum carnosinase which rapidly hydrolyzes the absorbed carnosine in the two constitutive amino acids which are back synthetized only in tissues where carnosine synthase is expressed, such as brain and skeletal muscle, but not in lung cells, such as alveolar type cells, where the expression of carnosine synthase does not occur. Hence, in view of a possible clinical application, in the present paper, carnosine was administered in the airway and to make this possible an innovative airway administration technology (Edry™) was applied. Edry™ is a formulation technology platform using spray drying which combines inhaled drugs with excipients engineer dry powder particles with optimal size and deagglomeration characteristics leading a consistent aerosolization performances. The formulation is designed to provide more efficient lung deposition, low systemic exposure and thus reduced possible systemic side effects [36].

Based on the above-mentioned background, the aim of the present paper is to evaluate the efficacy of carnosine in mitigating the harmful effects of cigarette smoke exposure, used as a model for chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). A quantitative LC-MS analysis was firstly used to determine the levels of carnosine in lung tissue and BAL following carnosine airway administration at three different doses. Quantitative label-free proteomics was then employed to assess the molecular pathways involved in the damaging effects of smoke and the protective effects of carnosine as well as its mechanism of action.

Material and methods

Animal studies

Animal studies were performed by Covance CRS Limited (now Labcorp Early Development Laboratories Ltd, Woolley Road, Alconbury Huntingdon Cambridge shire, PE28, 4HS UK). The in-life experimental procedures undertaken during the course of this study were subject to the provisions of the United Kingdom Animals (Scientific Procedures) Act 1986 Amendment Regulations 2012 (the Act).

Smoke exposure

The mice were exposed to 3R4F reference cigarettes with filters removed, for a period of approximately 60 min, twice daily 4 h apart, for 12 consecutive days. Target Total Particle Matter (TPM) concentration in the smoke exposure box was approximately 600 μg/L. Animals were placed in an acrylic box comprised of individual compartments which held 1–2 mice. The box was connected to a ventilator with a cigarette attached. Animals not exposed to smoke (group: air) were placed into an acrylic box and exposed to air for the equivalent period of time. All animal experiments were approved by the local ethics committee of CRO, where the experiments were performed: Covance CRS LLC (now Labcorp Early Drug Development), Huntingdon, Cambridgeshire, UK.

Treatment with carnosine

Female C57BL/6J mice (weight and age range on day of first dosing: 16.2–19.0 g and approximately 8–9 weeks) were randomly assigned to the following 5 experimental groups (n = 12 animals for each group): air, control smoke, smoke and carnosine treated with a dose of 10, 50 or 100 mg/kg/day.

On Days −3 to 12 Female C57BL/6J mice were dosed via nose-only inhalation once daily with either air control or carnosine at target inhaled doses of 10, 50 or 100 mg/kg/day.

On Days 1–12 (approximately 1 h following inhalation dosing) the animals were placed in Perspex boxes and exposed to either room air or cigarette smoke for approximately 60 min. Animals were then returned to their home cages. Approximately 4 h later animals were returned to the exposure boxes and exposed to air or cigarette smoke for a further 60 min.

After 18 h from last smoke exposure the animals were sacrificed and Bronchoalveolar Lavage (BAL), lung tissue and plasma collected as reported below and stored at −80 °C until the analyses for the measurement of carnosine content.

BAL collection and cytokine analysis

On Day 13, approximately 18 h after the end of the final smoke exposure, animals were terminated by an overdose of pentobarbitone administered by the intraperitoneal route.

For all animals, following confirmation of death, BAL was collected by using three lots of 0.3 mL of PBS to lavage the lungs. The BAL fluid was pooled from the three lavages into an eppendorf and placed on wet ice until centrifugation. The single eppendorf from each mouse was centrifuged at 1000×g for 10 min at ca 4 °C. The lavage fluid supernatant was kept on dry ice until being stored deep frozen (−70 to −90 °C) prior to cytokine analysis.

Following completion of the BAL procedure the thorax of all animals was opened and the lungs of each animal removed, weighed and stored at −80 °C until the proteomic analyses. Cytokines KC, IL-6, and MCP-1 were analyzed using a multiplex system in singlet. Subsequently cytokines IL-10, TNF-α and IL-1β were analyzed using a multiplex system.

Fluorescence AGEs measurement

Several types of Advanced Glycation End-products (AGEs) fluoresce, mainly those larger molecules corresponding to cross-linking products, and measurement of their inherent fluorescence is effective for determining the presence of AGEs.

AGE products were measured in both collected plasma and homogenized lung tissue samples for all experimental conditions. 50 μl aliquots of plasma and homogenate per sample were desalted and filtered by using Amicon Ultra-0.5 mL centrifugal filters (MWCO 1000 Da) against a saline solution (NaCl 0.9 %) to remove potential interferents. The protein content was measured on the filtrate using the Bradford assay. 40 μL of each filtered sample was diluted to 1 mL with saline, and the measurement of AGE-associated fluorescence was measured by using the Luminescence Spectrometer LS 50 B (PerkinElmer) and setting the excitation and emission wavelengths at 370 nm at 345 nm, respectively. Fluorescence Units (F.U.) values recorded were finally normalized to the protein content.

Determination of l-carnosine in BAL, lung tissue and plasma by LC-ESI-MS analyses

Sample preparation: Lung tissue was homogenized in PBS at the final concentration of 0.1 g/mL by using a Bead Bug homogenizer (Benchmark USA). The homogenate, BAL or plasma were spiked with deuterated D3 Carnosine (α-d1, imidazole-2,5-d2) as internal standard at a fixed concentration before sample deproteinization which was carried out at 4 °C for 10 min by adding 9 vol of acetonitrile. Samples were then centrifugated at 14,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C before being directly analyzed or diluted, where necessary. Calibration curves were prepared by using the matrices of the control mice.

LC-MS: Samples were directly injected into an EXION LC100 system by the autosampler as previously reported [37]. The chromatographic separation was carried out with a Hypersil GOLD HILIC column (150 × 2.1 mm p.size 3 μm; Thermo Scientific) equipped with Guard Cartridge (Hypersil GOLD HILIC 10 × 2.1 mm 3 μm; Thermo Scientific) and a 0.5 μm filter (OPTI-SOLV; Supelco). Column temperature was kept at 40 °C, mobile phase flow rate was 250 μL/min and analyte elution was achieved with the gradient program reported in SI (Table S1).

The LC system was directly connected to a mass spectrometry detector, API 4000 (AB Sciex), and the analytes were ionized by a Turbo V ESI source (AB Sciex) with the following parameters: curtain Gas, 25 a.u., Temperature 550 °C, Spray voltage 3500 V, Interface Heater On. The mass spectrometer worked in multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) following the following transitions for carnosine and IS, respectively: 227.2 m/z → 110.2 m/z and 230.2 m/z → 113.2 m/z. For each analysis the following parameters were set: scan time 100 ms; clustering potential 45; Entrance Potential 10; Cell Exit Potential 10 and 4 units of collision gas. Parameters were previously optimized in a semi-automatic mode by a direct infusion of the standard samples.

The Software Analyst 1.6.3 (AB Sciex) was used for instrument control and peak integration. Calibration curves were built by adding external quantities of carnosine at blank matrices from control mice. Due to the basal presence of carnosine in matrices, calibration curves based on addition method were carried out. The linear regression was obtained by calculating the ratio between the carnosine area and the I.S. area in the same sample. Curves were expressed as pmol/μL injected and the endogenous amount of carnosine in the matrices (expressed as pmol/μL injected) which was calculated by setting Y = 0.

Carnosine stability

In a set of experiments aimed to evaluate carnosine stability, human serum and BAL were spiked with carnosine at a final concentration of 50 nmol ml−1 and lung homogenate with 10 nmol mg−1 of proteins. The relative content of carnosine in the biological fluid and tissues was determined as above mentioned.

Proteomic studies

Sample preparation: Each sample was homogenized by glass bead beating (3 cycles, 60″, 350 rpm) in 200 μL of protein extraction buffer (8 M urea in 50 mM Tris-HCl, 30 mM NaCl (Bio-Rad) at 8.5 pH and 1 % of protease inhibitor cocktail (Sigma-Aldrich)). The supernatant was collected in fresh tubes and centrifuged at 14,000×g, 4 °C for 10 min. The amount of proteins was quantified by the Bradford Reagent (Sigma-Aldrich) following the manufacture’s procedure. 20 μg of proteins were reduced with 5 mM DL-dithiothreitol (DTT, Sigma-Aldrich) in 50 mM NH₄HCO₃ for 30 min at 52 °C shaking, then alkylated with 15 mM iodoacetamide (Sigma-Aldrich) for 20 min in the dark at room temperature. The protein digestion was performed in 1:100 lysC:protein ratio (w/w) for 3 h at 37 °C shaking at 650 rpm, followed by 1:20 trypsin: protein ratio (w/w) (Trypsin Sequencing Grade; Roche, Monza, Italy) overnight at 37 °C shaking at 650 rpm.

High-resolution mass spectrometry analysis (nLC-HRMS): To increase the quality of instrumental analysis, the digested samples were further purified and concentrated by 0.6 μL C-18 resin ZipTip (Millipore, Milan, Italy). Tryptic peptides were analyzed using a Dionex Ultimate 3000 nano-LC system (Sunnyvale CA, USA) connected to Orbitrap Fusion™ Tribrid™ Mass Spectrometer (Thermo Scientific, Bremen, Germany) equipped with a nano-electrospray ion source (nESI). Peptide mixtures were pre-concentrated onto an Acclaim PepMap 100 - 100 μm × 2 cm C25 and separated on EASY-Spray column, 50 cm × 75 μm ID packed with Thermo Scientific Acclaim PepMap RSLC C18, 2 μm, 100 Å. The temperature was set to 35 °C and the flow rate was 300 nL min−1. Mobile phases were the following: 0.1 % formic acid (FA) in water (solvent A), 0.1 % FA in water/acetonitrile with 2/8 ratio (solvent B). The elution gradient was from 96 % buffer A to 40 % buffer B for 110 min. MS spectra were collected over an m/z range of 375–1500 Da at 120,000 resolutions, operating in data dependent scan mode, cycle time 3 s between master scans. Higher-energy collision dissociation (HCD) was performed with collision energy set at 35 eV in positive polarity. Each sample was analyzed in three technical replicates.

Data analysis: The instrumental raw files were processed by MaxQuant software v1.6.6.0 set on Uniprot_Homosapiens database against the Andromeda search engine1. The quantification of peptides and related proteins for each control and treated sample in biological duplicate and technical triplicates was based on the LFQ intensities. Lys-C and trypsin as the digestive enzymes, variable modification of carbamidomethylation of cysteine (+57.021 Da), fixed modification of methionine oxidation (+15.995 Da), N-terminal acetylation (+42.011 Da) and LFQ minimum ratio count to 2 were set as further parameters. The interpretation and visualization of results obtained from MaxQuant software were performed by a two-sample t-test using Perseus (v1.6.1.3, Max Planck Institute of Biochemistry, Germany). Statistical parameters (p < 0.05; q < 0.05, q = FDR adjusted p-value) were set to identify the differentially expressed proteins between samples (log2 fold changes). The proteins were selected with a minimum of two peptides. Variability of biological replicates were measured using the scatter plot with Pearson correlation coefficient values of the LFQ intensities. The network protein analysis related to significantly altered proteins was carried out by Ingenuity Pathways Analysis (IPA) (last release; Qiagen) The statistical enrichment of involved pathways is performed by the right-tailed Fisher’s exact test, in correlation with QIAGEN Knowledge Base, assigning a p-value (https://digitalinsights.qiagen.com/products/features/analysis-match/). The significance indicates the probability of association of molecules from the experimental dataset with the pathway by random chance alone. The core analyses performed by IPA, using the differentially expressed proteins in the uploaded dataset, assess signaling pathways, molecular interaction network and biological functions that are likely to be perturbed. The overall activation/inhibition states of canonical pathways are predicted based on a z-score algorithm. This z-score is used to statistically compare the uploaded dataset with the pathway patterns. The pathways are colored to indicate their activation z-scores: orange predict a gain of function, while blue a loss of function. The pathway is activated when molecules' causal relationships with each other (i.e., activation edge and the inhibition edge between the molecules based on literature findings) generate an activity pattern for the molecules and also the end-point functions in the pathway.

Results

Carnosine stability in BAL and lung homogenate

As expected, carnosine was rapidly degraded in human serum with a hydrolytic rate of 2.91 nmol mL−1 min−1. By contrast, carnosine was found stable in murine BAL and lung homogenate, being the percentage of remained carnosine after 60 min of incubation of 99 % ± 3.2 and 94 % ± 5.1, respectively (SI, Figure S1).

Carnosine in bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL), lung tissue and plasma after carnosine airway administration

Once we had established that carnosine is stable in murine BAL and lung homogenate, we then evaluated the concentration of carnosine in the bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL), lung tissue and plasma of mice after 0.5 h receiving carnosine by airway administration and at three different doses.

A basal content of the peptide was found in plasma, lung and BAL, 4.75 ± 0.63 nmol/mL, 10.71 ± 1.56 nmol/mg wet tissue and 0.47 ± 0.03 nmol/mL, respectively. The presence of carnosine in plasma is due to the absence of carnosinase which makes dietary carnosine detectable in rodent plasma as already previously reported [35]. By considering the absence of the mRNA for carnosine synthase in the lung tissue (https://www.proteinatlas.org), the negligible lung and BAL content of carnosine probably derives from the circulation. The concentration of carnosine in BAL reported as nmoles/ml is reported in Fig. 1 (panel A) which evidences a dose-dependent response and a significant difference among the three groups receiving scaling doses. Fig. 1 panel B reports the carnosine concentration in plasma evidencing a substantial systemic absorption of carnosine when given by airway administration and reaching up to a 200 nmol/ml concentration at the highest tested dose. The variability within each group was found higher in respect to that found in BAL and no significant differences were found between the 50 and 100 mg/kg dose. Daily inhalation dosing with carnosine from Day −3 to Day 12 at an estimated achieved inhaled dose of 10.9 and 51.8 mg/kg/day did not result in any biologically significant difference in BAL total or differential cell counts or BAL cytokine concentrations when compared to the smoke control animals (Fig. 3). Daily inhalation dosing with carnosine from Day −3 to Day 12 at an estimated achieved inhaled dose of 102.0 mg/kg/day resulted in a significantly lower BAL concentration of KC and 57 % and 39 % lower BAL concentrations of IL-6 and MCP-1, respectively, when compared to the smoke control animals at Day 13 (Fig. 3). The significantly lower BAL pro-inflammatory cytokine recorded following carnosine administration at 102.0 mg/kg/day did not correlate with any significant reduction in BAL total and differential cell counts.

The lung tissue of these groups was, homogenized, lysated, digested and analyzed by nLC-MS/MS. Discussion

The aim of the work was to study a dose-dependent effect of airway administered carnosine in mice exposed to cigarette smoke as a model of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). The rationale of testing carnosine in a COPD model is supported by two main aspects, on one hand, the involvement of inflammation and oxidative stress in the onset and propagation of COPD, on the other, the ability of carnosine to alleviate inflammation and oxidative stress in lung as demonstrated in different in vivo models. The first aspect we faced was how to administer carnosine in order to distribute it in the lung tissue. Pre-clinical studies so far reported on the effect of carnosine administration on lung inflammation and oxidative stress were based on an oral administration of carnosine [29,30,32,33]. Although in most of these studies the tissue content of carnosine was not reported, we can easily consider a rapid diffusion of carnosine from the circulation, where it is stable due to the lack of serum carnosinase (CN1), to the lung, where exerts its pharmacological activity. However, in humans, the rapid hydrolysis of orally administered carnosine by serum carnosinase prevents its distribution in lung tissue; the two amino acids (histidine and beta-alanine) formed by carnosine hydrolysis can only be resynthesized in tissues where carnosine synthase is expressed, such as the brain and skeletal muscle, but not in lung cells, like alveolar type cells, where the gene for carnosine synthesis is not expressed, as documented by the absence of mRNA (https://www.proteinatlas.org). In view of a potential application of carnosine in humans, we first applied a novel airway administration technology (Edry™) to administer carnosine to the lung.

In the first part of the work, we documented, in in vitro studies, that carnosine is stable for more than 60 min in murine BAL and lung tissue homogenate, thus confirming the absence of carnosinases and in general of carnosine dipeptidases in the lung tissue. The formulation of carnosine and its airway administration in mice resulted in a dose-dependent loading of the dipeptide in lung tissue and BAL, as well as in a systemic absorption, as demonstrated by the plasma content which reached a concentration up to 200 nmol ml−1, to indicate the ability of the airway technology to make carnosine diffusible in the alveoli. In line of principle, this technology can be applied to humans to deliver carnosine intact into the lung where it should remain stable, taking into account the absence of carnosinases, as indicated by the absence of mRNA (https://www.proteinatlas.org). It should also be considered that the airway administration in humans is the only way to deliver carnosine to the lung, since, in line of principle, this is not feasible when administered by oral treatment because of the presence of plasma carnosinases and the lack of carnosine synthase in the lung.

In the next step, we found that twelve days of twice daily cigarette smoke exposure induced a significant lung inflammation and oxidative stress in mice. Oxidative stress was monitored by measuring protein fluorescence in lung tissue. When proteins undergo excessive carbonylation/oxidation, they undergo structural rearrangements that lead to the formation of fluorescent species characterized by a particular excitation and fluorescent emission wavelength [41]. It has also been shown that monocarbonyl species present in cigarette smoke, such as formaldehyde and acetaldehyde, react with the terminal ε-amino residue of lysine and with carbonyl species from lipid peroxidation, such as malondialdehyde, to form fluorescent condensation products [42]. Smoke induced an almost three-fold increase of fluorescent proteins in lung tissue which was dose-dependently and significantly reduced by carnosine treatment. Different mechanisms can explain the antioxidant effect of carnosine and in particular the dose-dependent inhibition of fluorescent protein adducts. One mechanism is the ability of carnosine to react and detoxify reactive carbonyl species (RCS) including 4-Hydroxynonenal (HNE) and malondialdehyde (MDA) which are lipid-peroxidation by-products and whose formation has been demonstrated to occur in lung upon exposure to cigarette smoke and in COPD patients [[43], [44], [45], [46]]. Carnosine could also detoxify RCS contained in the smoke, such as α,β-unsaturated aldehydes, including acrolein and crotonaldehyde [47] and which together with lipid-peroxidation derived RCS are responsible for the formation of fluorescent protein adducts. The RCS sequestering ability of carnosine, besides inhibiting the formation of fluorescent protein adducts, could also result in a tissue protecting effect, given that RCS and the protein (fluorescent) adducts are involved in tissue damaging and inflammation [48]. Another antioxidant mechanism of carnosine regards its ability to quench oxidant species which are involved in the onset and propagation of oxidative stress associated with lung inflammation such as H2O2, oxygen species [49] and HOCl [50], forming an oxidized metabolite which in turn is characterized by an efficient antioxidant capacity, as confirmed in ex vivo samples by identifying the oxidation product [51]. The ability of carnosine to directly quench oxidant species could result in reduced oxidative stress and hence in inhibiting oxidation by-products which are the precursor of fluorescent proteins. A third antioxidant mechanism of carnosine regards its capacity to indirectly counteract oxidative stress by enhancing the expression and activation of the Nrf2 pathway which regulates the transcription of more than two hundred genes that contain an antioxidant response element (ARE) in their promoter region as recently reviewed by us [52]. The ability of carnosine to activate Nrf2 is discussed below.

A significant increase of fluorescent proteins was also found in plasma indicating a systemic effect of smoke-induced oxidative stress or a systemic absorption of the oxidative by-products which are the precursors of the fluorescent proteins. Carnosine was found to be effective even at systemic level although with a lower potency in respect to lung tissue.

The second step was to measure the effect of smoke on lung inflammation and the dose-dependent anti-inflammatory effect of carnosine on inflammation.

Inflammation and oxidative stress, as a continuous distortion in the prooxidant/antioxidant balance favoring the former [53], are two related cellular processes. Among the numerous transcription factors that regulate inflammatory proteins, nuclear factor (NF)-κB stands out for its pivotal role in controlling the expression of various pro-inflammatory genes. This leads to the upregulation of cytokines, adhesion molecules, chemokines, growth factors, and enzymes. The expression of most of the genes for the pro-inflammatory mediators involved in inflammatory process in the lungs in COPD, including IL-1, IL-6, IL-8, MCP-1 and TNF-α, are all activated by NF-κB [54]. Oxidative stress activates NF-κB-mediated transcription of pro-inflammatory mediators either through activation of its activating inhibitor of κB-α kinase or the enhanced recruitment and activation of transcriptional co-activators [45,55].

The first parameter of inflammation we measured was the cytokine content in the BAL. All the measured cytokines were found significantly higher quantities in the smoke group in respect to control, although the data are affected by a significant variability, reaching a CV% even more than 120 %. Carnosine was found effective only at the highest dose tested and significantly only for KC, even if a clear inhibitory trend can be observed for all the measured cytokines. Based on the high variability of cytokines as biomarkers of inflammation we then explored the molecular mechanisms involved in the lung tissue exposed to cigarette smoke using a quantitative proteomic approach which should give a more robust and significant view of the carnosine’s effect.

Quantitative proteomics is a powerful approach used for both discovery and targeted proteomic analyses with the aim of understanding global proteomic dynamics in a cell, tissue or organism [56,57]. Quantitative proteomic analyses entail the isotopic labeling of proteins or peptides in the experimental groups (SILAC, ICAT, ICPL and isobaric tags) or label-free strategies, as used in the present study. Quantitative proteomics plays a crucial role in the comprehension of the molecular mechanisms behind biological processes and disease states, as it goes beyond simple protein identification and incorporates detailed information about protein modifications and abundance. Despite being more complex than targeted protein identification, these strategies are essential for a comprehensive understanding of the global expression of proteins and the modifications that occur thus underlying the molecular mechanisms that drive biological processes and disease states. Quantitative proteomics has many applications, among others: I) the identification of proteins that are differentially expressed in disease samples and which can be used as biomarkers for disease diagnosis and treatment, ii) identification of protein targets for drug development, iii) protein expression and modification patterns in response to treatment, iv) elucidation of the molecular mechanisms of drug action by identifying the proteins and pathways that are affected by drug treatment.

By considering that the aim of the present investigation is the understanding of the functional effect of carnosine in a COPD model and the elucidation of the molecular mechanisms involved, quantitative proteomics was identified as the most suitable analytical approach to address the above mentioned aims of the study.

Quantitative proteomics, also in association with other OMICs strategy, such as transcriptomics, has been found to be a powerful analytical tool to characterize the pulmonary proteome during the development and progression of cigarette smoke-induced COPD [[58], [59], [60], [61]]. The differential expression of COPD mice induced by cigarette smoke was enriched in pathways related to the mitochondrion, energy metabolism, inflammation, immune regulation, and skeletal muscle [[62], [63], [64], [65]]. These pathways were closely related to COPD and extrapulmonary injury. A time-resolved proteomic profiling of cigarette smoke-induced experimental chronic obstructive pulmonary disease has also been reported [66]. This is a seminal paper which on one hand identifies proteins associated with the pathogenesis and disease progression of COPD and on the other identifies the quantitative proteomic approach as a valuable and comprehensive approach to monitor at molecular level, the onset and progression in a murine model of smoke-induced COPD.

We first analyzed the biochemical pathways and functions significantly modified in lung tissue by the cigarette smoke in respect to control. In our experimental condition (mice exposed to cigarette smoke for 12 days), proteomic data clearly indicate that smoke exposure had a great impact on lung tissue with 692 proteins differentially expressed above a threshold of 1.5 fold change in the lung tissue of mice exposed to smoke in respect to controls. Protein network analysis identified the activation of some pathways characteristic of COPD and including inflammatory response (30 genes involved), fibrosis (46 differentially regulated product genes), induction of immune system by infiltration and “migration of leukocyte” pathways (68 genes involved). Moreover, as expected, the oxidative stress response to smoke was clearly evidenced. In particular, the following pathways: “generation of reactive oxygen species” and the “synthesis of reactive oxygen species” were induced together with “synthesis, metabolism and production of hydrogen peroxide”. An altered pathway of calcium metabolism was also detected, leading to a decrement of contraction and an accumulation of free calcium.

We then found that carnosine at the tested dose of 100 mg/kg, is effective in reverting all the pathways evoked by smoke, namely, inflammation, fibrosis, oxidative stress and immune response. The complete reverse of the dysregulated proteins was not observed for the two other tested doses, although, for some specific proteins, a partial effect is evident, indicating an overall dose-dependent effect. To get a clearer dose response, a quantitative analysis for each protein should be carried out by using a targeted analysis.

It is interesting to compare the protective effect of carnosine as evidenced by targeted approach (measuring the cytokines in the BAL fluid) in respect to the proteomic data of lung tissue. Targeted analysis only shows a trend of carnosine to reduce the inflammation. Of the five cytokines measured, only one was found to be reduced in a significant manner while the others, even though reduced even more than 50 %, were not found to be significantly regulated due to the high variability. By contrast, proteomic data clearly evidenced a significant protective effect of carnosine on the different molecular pathways involved in smoke tissue damage, the dysregulation of proteins being completely reversed by carnosine treatment. Moreover, proteomic data also evidenced a dose-dependent effect at the lower doses which were not appreciated by the targeted approach. Hence, the present study, clearly evidences the powerful of the quantitative proteomic approach in evaluating the efficacy of bioactive compounds in phenotypic animal models, as it is able to evaluate the modulation of the disease-related molecular pathways by the tested compound.

Some considerations should be drawn regarding the mechanism through which carnosine acts as a protective molecule in the COPD model. A first mechanism regards the direct antioxidant activity of carnosine as above discussed, through a direct scavenging of oxidant species as well as of carbonyls derived from both smoke exposure and lipid-peroxidation which are the precursor of protein adducts such as ALEs, well known pro-inflammatory mediators.

Quantitative proteomics is a tool used in phenotypic drug discovery to elucidate the molecular mechanisms of the active compounds. In the present study, proteomic data were mainly useful to characterize, at molecular level, the phenotypic animal model and to evaluate the protective effect of carnosine. In addition, proteomics was able to identify some characteristic pathways evoked only by carnosine and presumably related to its molecular mechanism. Carnosine was found to upregulate some key enzymes related to Nrf2 activation and in particular glutathione peroxidase, reductase, transferase, SOD, thioredoxins, and carbonyl reductase (SI, Table S4). Nrf2 activation by carnosine can explain some of the effects observed in the COPD model and in particular the antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities, mediated by the activation of antioxidant enzymes and through NFKB inhibition, respectively [[67], [68], [69]]. The ability of carnosine to act as antioxidant and anti-inflammatory agent thought Nrf2 activation has been found in cell as well as in animal models, as recently reviewed by us [52].

Given the central role of oxidative stress in COPD, boosting endogenous antioxidants is considered a promising strategy to address the disease’s underlying pathogenic mechanisms. Several different approaches to reducing oxidative stress in COPD have been explored in animal models of the disease, but few have been tested clinically. Most clinical trials in COPD have focused on antioxidants that promote glutathione production, such as N-acetylcysteine (NAC), which has been shown to reduce exacerbations in COPD patients [70]. Besides acting as building block of GSH, NAC also acts as antioxidant by regenerating mercaptoalbumin from the cysteinylated disulfide [[71], [72], [73]]. However, it remains unclear whether the effects on exacerbation come from the antioxidant properties, its mucolytic action or both [74]. Research is now focusing on potent antioxidants, including, dietary antioxidants, superoxide dismutase mimetics, NADPH oxidase inhibitors, mitochondria-targeted antioxidants, and Nrf2 activators [5,74,75]. Some of these antioxidants such as SOD mimetics [76], the GPx mimetic ebselen [77] and dietary or natural extracts including plant falvonoids [78], resveratrol [75] are effective in animal models of COPD and some of these have already been studied in other clinical diseases, so there is a good case for their study in COPD patients [74].

Among these molecular approaches, the Nrf2 activators represents a very promising strategy as also emerged by a recent paper reporting that in COPD, an inherent loss of metabolic plasticity leads to metabolic exhaustion and reduced redox capacity, which can be rescued by activation of the NRF2 pathway. The Authors conclude that targeting these defects, via NRF2 augmentation, may therefore present an attractive therapeutic strategy for the treatment of the aberrant airway inflammation described in COPD [79].

Nrf2 activation is a powerful mechanism for inhibiting oxidative and inflammatory damage, for cell redox regulation and it currently represents a promising drug target [80]. Electrophilic compounds are so far the most promising Nrf2 activators acting by covalent binding the thiols of KEAP1 which make the complex KEAP1-NRF2 dissociated, thus leading Nrf2 to translocate at nuclear level where it acts as nuclear translocation factor [81].

Pre-clinical studies support the protective effect of Nrf2 activators for COPD treatment as found for electrophilic molecules including isothiocyanates [82], cryptotanshinone [83], aucubin [84].

However no clinical studies have so far been reported on this class of compounds and this is also due to the side effects for the electrophilic Nrf2 activators as also found in clinical trials. In particular, due to their electrophilic chemical nature, these agents are very likely to perturb multiple targets, and the nonspecific effects of electrophilic Nrf2 activators may because of failure in clinical development [85]. Moreover, the covalent binding of proteins can induce idiosyncratic reactions [86]. As reviewed by Barnes [74] a synthetic triterpenoid bardoxolone methyl is effective in a cigarette smoke exposed mouse model of COPD [87], but a Phase 3 clinical trial in renal disease was terminated due to adverse effects and increased mortality [88]. Dimethyl fumarate (BG-12) is also an Nrf2 activator and has been approved for use in multiple sclerosis, although side effect such as flushing, nausea and diarrhea are reported [89].

Given carnosine’s nucleophilic and non-reactive nature, making it a highly safe peptide, its widespread use in clinical and intervention studies and its compatibility with a chronic and high-dose treatment [20], carnosine’s ability to activate Nrf2 is a very promising mechanism with potential applications in treating oxidative and inflammatory based-diseases such as COPD. Understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying carnosine’s Nrf2 activation which is still unknown [52] is now required to further stimulate research in this field and lead to the development of a novel class of bioactive molecules.
