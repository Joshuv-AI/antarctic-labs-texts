An OpenSEES graphical user interface for structural dynamics and earthquake engineering instruction
Authors: Gökhan Yazici, Bilal Ein Larouzi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractStructural dynamics and earthquake engineering are fundamental subjects for civil and structural engineering students at undergraduate and graduate levels, particularly the students preparing to work in seismically active regions. However, these topics often pose challenges, especially for undergraduates, due to the complexities involved in solving differential equations of motion numerically. This paper introduces an educational Graphical User Interface (GUI) for OpenSEES, developed using MATLAB App Designer, to address these challenges. OpenSEES is a widely-used open-source software framework for simulating structural and geotechnical behavior under static and dynamic loads. The GUI simplifies the modeling process by focusing on essential input parameters for creating structural and seismic isolation system models. It also provides visualization tools for interpreting analysis results. The GUI consists of two primary components: one for dynamic analysis of single-degree-of-freedom (SDOF) systems, solved using a MATLAB-based numerical solver, and another for linear time history analysis of multi-degree-of-freedom fixed-based and seismically isolated shear frame structures. By generating input files for OpenSEES, the GUI aids in understanding the software’s fundamental principles, enabling students to transition to modeling more complex structures. This tool is designed to be intuitive and accessible, making it a valuable resource for both graduate and undergraduate students studying structural dynamics and earthquake engineering.Keywords: Graphical user interface, OpenSEES, Modal analysis, Time history analysis, Seismic isolation, Structural dynamics, Earthquake engineering, Engineering education

Introduction

Earthquakes are one of the most serious natural hazards, leading to significant loss of life and economic disruption. Over the past two decades, the United States Geological Survey (USGS)1 has recorded more than 38,000 earthquakes worldwide with magnitudes greater than 5.0. As a result, understanding seismic design principles and regulations has become essential in civil engineering education, helping students learn how to reduce these negative impacts effectively.

One important way to minimize structural damage during earthquakes is through seismic isolation systems. These systems use flexible layers, like elastomeric bearings and lead rubber bearings, to increase the flexibility of structures. By enhancing their natural movement and ability to absorb energy, these systems significantly reduce the forces acting on buildings during an earthquake. To understand how these forces work, it is necessary to solve equations of motion, often expressed as ordinary differential equations. Techniques like the Newmark-β and Wilson-θ algorithms are commonly used to solve these equations in real-time. However, many undergraduate students find these numerical methods challenging. To help with this, various educational software tools have been created to make learning about structural dynamics and earthquake engineering easier and more accessible.

Various standalone educational software programs have been developed to facilitate instruction in the fields of structural dynamics and earthquake engineering. Among these software tools are NONLIN, BISPEC, and EQTOOLS, each serving specific educational purposes. NONLIN, for instance, is extensively utilized in graduate-level courses focusing on structural dynamics and earthquake engineering2. BISPEC, on the other hand, is designed to extract both linear and nonlinear spectra from earthquake ground motion records, while also offering analysis capabilities for single-degree-of-freedom systems under earthquake-induced motions3. EQTOOLS, another educational software, aids students in comprehending the principles underlying the selection and modification of earthquake ground motion records for linear and nonlinear time-history analysis of structures4. Recognizing the complementary functionalities of NONLIN and EQTOOLS, a merged tool called NONLIN-EQT was developed to combine their capabilities in addition to standalone software tools, the development of virtual laboratories powered by Java has been undertaken to enhance the conceptual understanding of earthquake engineering and structural control5. Gao et al. (2005) and Sim et al. (2009) have contributed to this area, creating virtual laboratories that provide interactive simulations and visualizations to aid in the comprehension of these subjects6 and7. Furthermore, (Panagiotopolous and Manolis 2016) have developed a web-based educational software package for structural dynamics8. This software encompasses multiple modules that enable dynamic response analysis of single-degree-of-freedom systems, modal and time-history analysis of single-story space frames, modal analysis of plane frames, generation of response spectra for earthquake acceleration records, and synthesis of ground motions for layered soil formations These software tools and virtual laboratories serve as valuable resources for educators and students in the field of structural dynamics and earthquake engineering, offering interactive learning experiences and practical applications of theoretical concepts.

The Open System for Earthquake Engineering Simulation (OpenSEES) represents an open-source software framework that serves as a computational tool for simulating the response of structural and geotechnical systems under static and dynamic loading. One notable feature of OpenSEES is its modular nature, which enables developers to extend its functionality. This modularity and flexibility afford researchers the opportunity to introduce new algorithms and integration schemes into the OpenSEES framework9. Diverging from the majority of commercially available Finite Element Analysis (FEA) software, OpenSEES does not provide a graphical user interface. Instead, users of OpenSEES are required to construct a text-based input file that comprehensively defines the model and specifies the necessary analysis parameters. The analysis results are typically stored in text files which need to be imported into spreadsheet applications for postprocessing.

A range of graphical user interfaces (GUIs) has been developed to address the challenges associated with pre-processing and post-processing operations in OpenSEES, enhancing its usability across various structural analysis applications. OpenSEES Navigator10 and STKO11 are general-purpose GUIs designed to simplify model creation, visualization, and analysis, offering intuitive environments for interacting with the OpenSEES framework. Build-X12 is an expert system specifically developed for the seismic analysis and performance evaluation of three-dimensional (3D) building structures. It features a user-centric visual interface that streamlines modeling workflows, supports efficient pushover analysis, and enables accurate prediction of seismic responses. MDOF13 serves as an educational platform that allows users to explore how different building parameters influence the time-varying dynamic response of structures under seismic and other dynamic loads. Lu et al. (2011) created a graphical user interface OpenSeesPL, for modelling pile-soil interaction problems14 Focelliniadapted OpenSeesPL to simulate the mechanical response of elastomeric bridge bearings15 MSBridge16,17, developed by Elgamal et al. (2017), is a PC-based GUI that supports nonlinear finite element analysis for multi-span bridge systems, including pushover, mode shape, 3D base input acceleration, equivalent static, and performance-based earthquake engineering (PBEE) analyses. DYANAS18 is a MATLAB-based GUI that leverages OpenSEES for nonlinear dynamic analysis of single-degree-of-freedom (SDOF) oscillators, featuring a user-friendly interface, efficient computation, and integrated post-processing tools. NextFEM Designer19 is a flexible finite element software that supports static, modal, dynamic, and nonlinear analyses, offering advanced pre- and post-processing capabilities tailored for seismic applications. GID + OpenSEES20 connects the OpenSEES solver with the GID environment, providing extended tools for defining model geometry, materials, elements, boundary conditions, and analysis settings, all within a graphical interface. PileGroupTool21 enables rapid, interactive simulation of the behavior of single piles or pile groups in layered soils, making it effective for foundation analysis. FeView22, a Python-based GUI, allows users to visualize finite element models and perform post-processing based on Tool Command Language (TCL) scripts defined for OpenSEES. Among these tools, OpenSEES Navigator, STKO, FeView, and NextFEM Designer stand out as general-purpose platforms, while others such as Build-X, MSBridge, and DYANAS serve specialized functions in seismic and geotechnical applications.

This paper introduces a novel Graphical User Interface (GUI) for OpenSEES, developed using MATLAB App Designer, specifically designed to support the teaching of structural dynamics and earthquake engineering. The GUI consists of two main components: one for the analysis of single-degree-of-freedom (SDOF) systems and another for linear time history analysis (LTHA) of multi-degree-of-freedom systems, including those equipped with seismic isolation. Unlike existing OpenSEES-based GUIs, which are often designed for professional or research applications and may involve steep learning curves or advanced modeling requirements, the proposed GUI focuses on educational usability. It simplifies model definition, automates input file generation, and provides immediate visual feedback through integrated plotting tools, making it accessible for undergraduate and graduate students alike. This instructional focus, combined with the GUI’s ability to bridge theoretical principles and practical simulation in a single environment, distinguishes it from existing tools and highlights its value as an innovative educational platform in structural engineering.

Structure and components of the GUI

Structure of the GUI

The graphical user interface (GUI) developed in this study comprises two primary components, each designed to address specific analytical needs. The first component, dedicated to Single Degree of Freedom (SDOF) systems, leverages MATLAB libraries to solve ordinary differential equations and graphically represent the system’s dynamic response. The second component, tailored for three-dimensional (3D) frame analysis, functions as both a pre-processing and post-processing tool for the OpenSEES platform as Fig. 1 shows the flowchart of the second component, which inherently lacks a graphical interface. During the pre-processing phase, the GUI facilitates the definition of simplified modeling parameters, processes these inputs, and automatically generates the corresponding modeling code for OpenSEES. In the post-processing phase, the GUI not only enables users to visualize the analysis results through graphical outputs, but also facilitates a deeper understanding of the structural behavior by providing time-history plots of displacements, velocities, accelerations, base shear, and bearing forces. These visualizations help users identify peak responses, assess the effectiveness of seismic isolation systems, and interpret the dynamic behavior of structures under different loading conditions. By transforming raw numerical data into interpretable graphs, the GUI bridges the gap between computational results and engineering insight, thus enhancing the user’s ability to critically evaluate the structural performance under investigation.

Single degree of freedom systems (SDOF)

Single-degree-of-freedom (SDOF) systems serve as prominent tools for elucidating fundamental principles in the realm of structural dynamics. These systems consist of three essential components: a lumped mass (m), a spring stiffness (k), and a damping coefficient (c). Notably, when the external loading F(t) is null, an SDOF system is considered to undergo free vibration, initiated by the initial displacement or velocity of the mass. Conversely, forced vibration describes the dynamic response of a system when it is subjected to an external loading F(t). SDOF systems with energy dissipation are categorized as damped systems, characterized by a positive damping coefficient, c.

This specific segment of the graphical user interface (GUI) has been devised to support students in comprehending the dynamic response of both damped and undamped SDOF systems subjected to forced and free vibrations. Single degree of freedom GUI interface.

Although SDOF systems serve as fundamental tools in structural dynamics education, it is important to recognize their limitations in representing real structures. In practice, an SDOF model approximates the dynamic response of a structure by idealizing it as a lumped-mass system with linear stiffness and damping, governed by a single equation of motion. This simplification is especially effective for low-rise buildings or structures where the first vibration mode dominates the overall response. It provides valuable insight into fundamental dynamic behavior such as free and forced vibration, resonance, damping effects, and the influence of loading characteristics.

However, SDOF models have inherent limitations when used to represent actual structures. They neglect higher-mode contributions, which are critical in the seismic response of mid- to high-rise buildings and irregular configurations. Torsional effects, variations in mass and stiffness distribution, and structural irregularities in plan or elevation cannot be captured by an SDOF approximation. Additionally, the model assumes a uniform response across the height of the structure and cannot simulate element-level damage, inelastic behavior, or interaction effects between different structural components. Therefore, while SDOF systems are valuable for introductory studies and for validating more complex models, they are not sufficient for detailed seismic assessment or performance-based design of real structures. More comprehensive models, such as multi-degree-of-freedom (MDOF) or finite element models, are necessary to accurately evaluate structural behavior under realistic loading scenarios.

Linear time history for frames

The Linear Time History Analysis (LTHA) component of the graphical user interface (GUI) in Fig. 3 has been specifically developed to facilitate modal analysis and linear time history analysis of three-dimensional shear frames. The users are able to define the masses of individual floors within the frame by inputting values for the distributed dead load and live load. Subsequently, the GUI employs these specified floor masses, along with the approximate fundamental vibration mode period of the structure (denoted as T1), to computationally derive the stiffnesses of each story. This calculation is conducted through the reverse application of the B method, as prescribed in ASCE 7–1624. Furthermore, the graphical user interface encompasses the capability to incorporate seismic isolation bearings within the structural model. In this context, the superstructure is treated as a linear elastic system, while the components associated with the seismic isolation system are assumed to exhibit nonlinear behavior.

Eigen analysis

Eigen analysis, also known as modal analysis, is a widely employed methodology in the assessment of vibrational characteristics in structural systems. The outcome of Eigen analysis serves the purpose of uncoupling the equations of motion of multi-degree-of-freedom systems, thereby enabling an analysis approach that treats the structural system as a collection of single-degree-of-freedom systems. This technique assumes a pivotal role as a fundamental stage in the dynamic analysis of structural systems, encompassing both temporal and frequency domains. The Eigen command within OpenSEES is used in performing Eigen analysis.

Linear time history analysis

Time history analysis is used to calculate the dynamic response of structures subjected to dynamic loading, including seismic excitations in the time domain. In the context of linear time history analysis, the structural system is presumed to exhibit a linear elastic response. Material nonlinearities are accommodated by employing equivalent linear properties. Conversely, nonlinear time history analysis takes into account the nonlinear characteristics of the structure, where response parameters such as displacements and internal forces are computed at each time increment utilizing numerical techniques such as the Newmark–Beta Method.

Seismic isolation bearings

Seismic isolation and energy dissipation systems protect the structures and their contents by shifting their natural vibration period beyond the predominant period of earthquakes to reduce the inertial forces transmitted to the superstructure as shown in Fig. 4. The period shift effect achieved by installing horizontally flexible bearings under the structure results in higher displacements. However, these displacements are essentially concentrated at the seismic isolation interface. Moreover, energy dissipation provided by the seismic isolation system reduces these displacements. Property modification factors are used in the design of seismically isolated structures to account for changes in isolator properties over time due to factors such as aging, temperature, wear, and contamination24. Examples of seismic isolation bearings widely used in practice include elastomeric isolation bearings25, Friction Pendulum bearings, and Triple Friction Pendulum bearings.

Single Friction pendulum bearings

Single Friction Pendulum bearings (SFP) reduce the lateral seismic loads imposed on the structures by using the characteristics of a pendulum for lengthening their vibration period. Single Friction Pendulum (SFP) bearings consist of an articular slider on a curved stainless-steel surface Fig. 5. The contact surfaces of the articulated slider are covered with a self-lubricating PTFE-based bearing material. The radius of curvature of the curved surface is used to adjust the target isolation period whereas, the friction coefficient on the sliding surfaces is controlled by the properties of the PTFE-based bearing material. Extensive information on the Friction Pendulum System can be obtained from28–31, and32.

Triple Friction pendulum bearings

The Triple Friction Pendulum (TFP) system, as depicted in Fig. 9, encompasses a configuration of three pendulums connected in series, specifically designed to dissipate seismic energy through frictional mechanisms. The sliding surfaces of the system are coated with a bearing material based on polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE). The selection of the radius of curvature and coefficients of friction for these sliding surfaces is crucial, as they ensure an adaptive response to varying levels of seismic ground motions36 and37.

Section for the TFP element (Adapted from37).

The Triple Friction Pendulum bearings are characterized by four concave surfaces, each assigned a distinct numerical designation from bottom to top. Each surface possesses unique parameters including its radius of curvature I, height (h), coefficient of friction (μ), and displacement capacity (d). Notably, the middle surfaces (2 and 3) typically share identical properties, denoted as R2 = R3, h2 = h3, and μ2 = μ337.

Graphical user interface structure and guideline

The graphical user interface (GUI) comprises five distinct tabs, wherein the initial tab pertains to the Single Degree of Freedom (SDOF) system, while the subsequent tabs are dedicated to 3-Dimensional frames.

The single degree of freedom systems

Within the GUI, the first tab is specifically designed to address SDOFs shown in Fig. 13. This tab contains editable fields that allow the user to define the system’s properties. The ordinary differential equation of motion is solved utilizing the pre-existing solvers within the MATLAB framework, subsequently enabling the visualization of displacement response over time. This components GUI is shown in Fig. 14.

SDOF system GUI interface.

The three-dimensional frames

The graphical user interface (GUI) for linear time history analysis encompasses a total of four tabs, with the first two tabs featuring editable fields. These editable fields serve as a means for the user to define crucial aspects such as the model geometry, loading conditions, and properties of seismic isolators in the context of seismically isolated frames. To provide a clear overview of the GUI’s operational process, the following chart Fig. 15 delineates the primary steps involved.

Flow chart for using the linear time history analysis component of the GUI.

Within the second tab of the graphical user interface (GUI) shown in Fig. 16, the user is required to specify the number of spans and stories for the structural system under consideration. Given the symmetrical nature of the frames addressed by the GUI, it suffices for the user to define the story height and span width solely for the initial story and frame. The subsequent stories and frames will be automatically duplicated based on this initial definition.

Model geometry tab.

Following this, the user is required to assign dead and live loads to the structure. The next step involves defining the first natural period. Using the B-Method, the graphical user interface (GUI) then calculates the corresponding stiffness of the structural system based on the specified period. The B-Method has been used for educational aim, helping students in estimating realistic structural properties—such as mass and stiffness—which are often difficult to define accurately. The distributed dead and live loads are subsequently converted into equivalent point loads and lumped masses applied at the story-level nodes.

The third tab of the graphical user interface (GUI) shown in Fig. 17 asks the user to provide essential inputs related to seismic analysis. Firstly, the user is expected to define the earthquake file, which serves as the input for the seismic excitation. Additionally, the user is required to specify the damping ratio, a critical parameter influencing the system’s response to seismic forces. Moreover, within this tab, the user is granted the flexibility to choose between two types of seismic bearing isolation options: single-friction pendulum bearings or triple-friction pendulum bearings. These options allow for the incorporation of advanced seismic isolation mechanisms (SFP and TFP), providing enhanced resilience and protection against seismic events.

Dynamic loading and seismic isolators tab.

The code tab of the graphical user interface (GUI) shown in Fig. 18 serves as a platform for displaying the analysis code generated by the system. Within this tab, the user is presented with three distinct options for analysis types, namely Modal analysis, Time history analysis, and bearing test analysis. The user retains the autonomy to select the desired analysis type based on the nature of the investigation. Subsequently, after configuring the analysis parameters and selecting the appropriate analysis type, the user is required to execute the generated code utilizing the OpenSEES platform. OpenSEES serves as the designated platform for running the analysis, enabling the comprehensive evaluation of the structural response as per the specified analysis type.

Code tab.

The final tab of the Graphical User Interface (GUI) shown in Fig. 19 deals with the post-processing phase, wherein four distinct subtabs are available, each dedicated to presenting specific types of results. Within this tab, the user is tasked with selecting the relevant result file corresponding to the desired analysis outcome. Upon selecting the appropriate result file, the GUI facilitates the graphical representation of the results. This graphical representation serves as a visual depiction of the analyzed data, enabling users to effectively interpret and comprehend the outcomes of their analysis.

Discussion

The developed GUI demonstrates its educational value through various case studies that illustrate core principles in structural dynamics and earthquake engineering. The SDOF component allows users to evaluate systems under free and forced vibration with different damping conditions, offering visual feedback to reinforce theoretical understanding. Case studies highlight the GUI’s ability to simulate realistic seismic behavior using the Newmark–Beta integration scheme and provide a comparison between damped and undamped responses, as well as harmonic excitation scenarios.

The linear time history module extends learning to multi-degree-of-freedom systems, including the impact of seismic isolation. Through modal and time history analyses of fixed-base and isolated frames, the tool helps users interpret structural responses such as story displacements, base shears, and hysteretic behavior of isolation devices. The GUI simplifies complex modeling procedures, enabling users with limited OpenSEES experience to conduct meaningful structural analysis. The effectiveness of seismic isolators like Triple Friction Pendulum (TFP) bearings is visually and quantitatively demonstrated, reinforcing concepts of equivalent damping and hysteresis energy dissipation.

By offering clear visualizations and automating code generation, the GUI bridges theoretical knowledge with practical simulation skills. It reduces the barrier for students to explore structural behavior under dynamic loads and provides flexibility for varying model configurations.

Conclusion

This study presents an educational Graphical User Interface (GUI) designed to enhance the teaching of structural dynamics and earthquake engineering using OpenSEES. The GUI includes two main components an SDOF analysis module using Ordinary Differential Equation (ODE45) solver and a linear time history analysis module for MDOF structures, including those with seismic isolation systems.

The tool simplifies model definition and visualization of dynamic responses, making it especially beneficial for undergraduate and graduate students with limited experience in numerical modeling or OpenSEES scripting. The GUI promotes active learning and deeper understanding of structural behavior under dynamic loading conditions.

Limitations include the current restriction to linear elastic superstructures and predefined seismic isolator models. Future work could expand the GUI to include nonlinear behavior of superstructures, additional element types, and a wider variety of ground motions. The educational and practical potential of this tool makes it a valuable addition to the resources available for structural dynamics instruction.
