Hypertension-attributable stroke burden in younger vs older people: analysis of GBD 2021 and NHANES databases
Authors: Fanqi Li, Jiasong Li, Siyuan Tan, Murong Xie, Haixiong Wang, Qiming Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHypertension is a leading global cause of stroke. Cardiovascular diseases are occurring at progressively younger ages. This study aims to compare the younger and older age groups burden of stroke attributable to hypertension.MethodsAnalyses were conducted using two databases: the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study and the U.S. National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES). For GBD study, stroke mortality attributable to hypertension was extracted. Age-period-cohort model was used to assess differences in annual mortality reduction rates across age groups. Estimated Annual Percentage Change (EAPC) was calculated to quantify the temporal trend in age-standardized mortality. Health inequalities were assessed using the concentration index. For NHANES, analyses were restricted to participants age≥25 years with hypertension. Among these participants, proportions of uncontrolled hypertension and hypertension unawareness were calculated, with sampling weights applied to ensure nationally representative estimates.ResultsHypertension was the dominant risk factor for stroke-related deaths, contributing to 50.5 % of global mortality. Age-period-cohort model revealed a key age-specific pattern: annual mortality rate reductions were slower in adults aged 25–54 years than in those aged≥55. EAPC values further confirmed this trend, with slower annual death rate declines in adults aged 25–54 years (-1.37 %) versus those aged ≥55 years (-1.74 %). NHANES data showed worse hypertension control and awareness in younger hypertensive populations: uncontrolled hypertension rose from 79.6 % (2013–2014) to 88.9 % (2021–2023) in 25–54-year-olds, while remaining relative stable (71.2–71.8 %) in older groups. Hypertension unawareness increased from 35 % to 48 % in younger adults over the same period, consistently higher than in older hypertensive populations. Concentration index indicated widening health inequalities, becoming increasingly concentrated in lower socioeconomic status countries. More importantly, this concentration trend was more pronounced among younger adults (25–54 years) than in older groups across all study years.ConclusionsHypertension drives most stroke deaths. Younger adults experience slower mortality improvements, worse hypertension control and awareness, and more severe health inequalities compared to older adults. Therefore, age-specific interventions are urgently needed.Keywords: Hypertension, Stroke, Young, Disease burden

Introduction

Stroke is one of the leading causes of death and long-term disability worldwide, contributing substantially to global disease burden and healthcare expenditures [1]. Hypertension, often termed the "silent killer," remains the most important modifiable risk factor, accounting for more than half of stroke-related mortality [2]. Additionally, hypertension increases the individual risk of stroke, with a 13 % reduction in risk for every 5-mmHg decrease in systolic blood pressure [3,4]. However, the current state of hypertension management remains concerning. It affects around 1.3 billion people worldwide according to a recent World Health Organization report [5].

To effectively reduce the stroke burden attributed to hypertension, it is crucial to identify the most vulnerable aspects of hypertension management, as targeted interventions are required to address these gaps. Disparities in hypertension management persist across many aspects. Geographic studies have shown significant variations in hypertension awareness and control between high-income and low- to middle-income countries [6]. Similarly, age-specific disparities are evident, with younger adults facing greater challenges in hypertension awareness and control compared to older populations [7]. In addition, disparities also exist in gender, diet, and life style [[8], [9], [10]]. These differences highlight the urgent need for tailored intervention strategies to address hypertension-related risks in diverse demographic groups.

Our previous research examined age disparities in cardiovascular diseases attributable to hypertension, revealing that younger patients experience the least effective management of their condition [11]. Given that stroke is a subtype of cardiovascular disease, it may exhibit similar age-related patterns in its association with hypertension; however, whether this is the case remains an important and largely unexplored question. While some studies have reported an increasing incidence of stroke in younger populations [12,13], direct comparisons between younger and older adults remain underexplored. These gaps are concerning, as stroke in younger adults leads to premature morbidity, loss of productivity, and significant socioeconomic consequences [14,15].

To address these issues, data from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study and the U.S. National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) were integrated. In this study, we aim to investigate the age-specific differences in the burden of hypertension-attributable stroke, to provide evidence for targeted intervention strategies.

Methods

Study design

This study is a secondary analysis of population-based databases, aiming to compare the rate of disease improvement in stroke mortality attributable to hypertension over the past few decades between younger and older populations, global health inequalities, and the proportions of hypertension awareness and control.

Study population

We utilized two primary data sources for our analysis: the GBD database and the NHANES database.

GBD database

The GBD database is a globally recognized, comprehensive resource that provides detailed estimates of mortality, morbidity, and risk factors for over 300 diseases and injuries, spanning across 204 countries and territories. Detailed information about the GBD database can be found at https://www.healthdata.org/gbd. The GBD database is unparalleled in its global scope, offering high-quality, comparable health data from countries at all stages of development. For this analysis, we extracted data on stroke mortality attributed to hypertension, stratified by age, sex, and geographic region. Additionally, we included the socio-demographic index (SDI) data to evaluate health inequalities associated with hypertension-attributable stroke. These data were used to assess the burden of stroke due to hypertension.

NHANES database

The NHANES database is one of the most robust and widely recognized health surveys in the United States. It collects comprehensive data on the health, nutritional status, and demographic characteristics of the U.S. population, with a large sample size of over 5000 participants annually. More information on NHANES can be found at https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/index.htm. For this study, we extracted data on hypertension prevalence, control, and awareness, categorized by age groups (25–54 years and ≥55 years). This information was used to analyze trends in the proportions of hypertension control and awareness, focusing on the differences between younger and older adults.

SAMPLING criteria

The GBD study relies on national-level data obtained from sources such as death registries, health surveys, and public health reports. Sampling is based on the availability of country-level data, with estimates for countries with limited or no data being modeled based on similar regions. NHANES employs a multistage probability sampling approach to select a representative sample from the U.S. civilian, non-institutionalized population. Participants are selected from geographic regions across the United States, ensuring broad representation of the population.

Participant selection in the NHANES database

For the NHANES analysis, participants aged≥25 years who completed the physical examination and had available blood pressure measurements were eligible for inclusion. The cutoff age of 25 years was selected to align with the GBD database, which reports disease burden data starting from age 25. We excluded individuals who were pregnant at the time of the survey, those with all blood pressure measurements missing, and those without information on current use of blood pressure lowering medication. Participants with normal blood pressure were also excluded. Data from the following survey cycles were included: 2011–2012, 2013–2014, 2015–2016, 2017–2020, and 2021–2023.

As an example, during the NHANES 2011–2012 survey cycle, a total of 4795 participants aged ≥25 years underwent examination. After excluding participants with normal blood pressure (n = 2250), missing blood pressure data (n = 57), or pregnancy (n = 7), 2481 individuals were included in the final analysis. All enrollment processes are detailed in the Supplementary Table S1.

Disease definitions

In the GBD database, stroke was defined as a rapidly developing clinical condition marked by focal neurological disturbances lasting longer than 24 h or resulting in death. High SBP is defined as systolic blood pressure above the theoretical minimum risk exposure level [11].

In the NHANES database, systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) values were calculated as the average of up to three consecutive measurements. Hypertension was defined according to the 2025 ACC/AHA guidelines, where SBP ≥ 130 mm Hg or DBP ≥80 mm Hg, or if they were currently using blood pressure-lowering medication, regardless of their BP readings [16]. Uncontrolled hypertension was defined as having an average SBP ≥130 mm Hg or DBP ≥80 mm Hg, irrespective of whether the participant was taking antihypertensive medication [17]. Hypertension unawareness was determined by asking participants, "Has a doctor or health professional ever informed you that you have high blood pressure, also known as hypertension?" Those who answered "no" or "don't know" were considered unaware of their hypertensive status.

Statistical analysis

Ranking of Risk Factors for Stroke Mortality

To identify and compare the contributions of secondary risk factors to stroke mortality, we used the comparative risk assessment framework of the GBD study. Stroke deaths attributable to each risk factor were obtained by multiplying the cause-specific stroke mortality by the corresponding population attributable fraction. All secondary risk factors were ranked according to the absolute number of stroke deaths attributable to each factor and their proportional contribution to total stroke mortality. These rankings were subsequently used to highlight the relative importance of major modifiable risk factors in stroke burden.

Age–period–cohort analysis

We applied the age–period–cohort model to assess the effects of age, period, and birth cohort on stroke mortality attributable to high SBP. This mode primarily utilizes the NIH APC Web tool (https://analysistools.cancer.gov/apc/). Local drift and net drift values were calculated to estimate the annual percentage change in mortality within each age group and across the entire population, respectively. A negative value indicated a decline in mortality, and the absolute value represented the magnitude of change.

EAPC

EAPC was used to describe the temporal trend in age-standardized stroke mortality attributable to high SBP. EAPC was calculated by comparing the disease burden at the start (1990) and end (2021) of the study period, using the ratio of these values adjusted for the length of the period to estimate the average annual change in the burden of disease. A negative EAPC value indicates a decreasing trend over time, while a positive value reflects an increasing trend. The absolute value of the EAPC represents the speed of change, with larger values showing more rapid increases or decreases in mortality rates. In addition, EAPC was calculated separately for individuals aged 25–54 years and those aged ≥55 years to compare temporal improvements between younger and older populations.

NHANES analysis

In the NHANES dataset, descriptive statistics were used to calculate the prevalence of uncontrolled hypertension and hypertension unawareness across age groups (25–54 years and ≥55 years). Sampling weights provided by NHANES were applied to account for the complex survey design and ensure nationally representative estimates. Proportions were compared across survey cycles, and temporal trends were assessed to capture changes over time.

Health inequalities

To measure health inequality in stroke mortality attributable to high SBP, we calculated the concentration index using the SDI as the ranking variable. A negative CI indicated that the burden was concentrated in lower-SDI populations, while the absolute value reflected the degree of inequality. Comparisons were made between younger (25–54 years) and older (≥55 years) adults to evaluate age-specific disparities.

Statistic

All statistical analyses were performed using R software. A two-sided P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Attribution of stroke death to hypertension

In our previous study, we reported the age-related characteristic in the effectiveness of controlling cardiovascular diseases attributable to high SBP [11]. However, this characteristic is not well understood among cardiovascular disease subtypes. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S1, stroke ranks second among cardiovascular disease subtypes attributable to hypertension, with 4212,697 deaths, accounting for 40.6 %. Furthermore, of all secondary risk factors for stroke, hypertension ranks first, contributing up to 50.5 % (Fig. 1). This leading role of hypertension has remained consistent over the past three decades, making it the most persistent risk factor for stroke-related mortality (Supplementary Fig. S2). Given the significant impact of hypertension on stroke, it is crucial to refine strategies aimed at more effective hypertension management, particularly targeting those at higher risk.

Death attributable to risk factors for stroke. Percentage of stroke-related deaths attributable to all level 2 risk factors, with high systolic blood pressure being the leading contributor.

Age–period–cohort results

We examined the effects of age, period, and cohort on stroke mortality using the age–period–cohort model. The analysis revealed a marked increase in stroke mortality with age (Supplementary Fig. S3A). Additionally, period effects indicated a consistent decline in stroke mortality rates from 1995 to 2020, suggesting overall improvements in disease control, especially after 2005 (Supplementary Fig. S3B). Cohort effects showed that earlier birth cohorts experienced higher mortality rates than later cohorts, with a gradual reduction over time (Supplementary Fig. S3C).

Local drift indicates the 5-year age group–specific annual changes in mortality rates from 1992 to 2021, whereas net drift reflects the overall mortality trend across all age groups during this period. Negative values of local drift or net drift indicate a decrease in mortality, and the absolute value reflects the change rate over this period, with larger values indicating a faster decline. As shown in Fig. 2, the local drift curve intersects with the net drift dashed line around the age of 55. For individuals under 55, the local drift is consistently above the net drift, with the absolute value of local drift smaller than that of net drift, indicating a slower decline in mortality rates among younger populations. In contrast, for those aged 55 and older, the local drift falls below the net drift, with the absolute value of local drift greater than that of net drift, reflecting a faster decline in mortality rates in older populations. These results confirm that among stroke patients attributable to high SBP, younger individuals experience poorer disease control compared to older adults, emphasizing the need for age-specific prevention and intervention strategies.

The black solid line shows the local drift, representing 5-year age-specific annual percent changes in rates, while the blue dashed line shows the net drift, the overall average annual percent change across all ages. Negative drift values indicate declining trends, with larger absolute values reflecting faster decreases.

Age-specific EAPC

To further evaluate the impact of age on stroke burden, we calculated the age-specific EAPC. EAPC is a measure of the change rate in mortality during a specified period, where negative values indicate a decrease in mortality, and positive values represent an increase. A larger absolute value of EAPC reflects a faster rate of change. The results revealed that for male and female both groups, the EAPC for individuals under 55 was −1.37 and over 55 was −1.74 (Fig. 3). The absolute value of EAPC for individuals under 55 less than those over 55, consistent of the results of age–period–cohort model, indicating a less rapid disease improvement rate. Besides, this inferiority persists in male group (under 55 was −0.96 and over 55 was −1.37) and female group (under 55 was −1.84 and over 55 was −2.05). Moreover, male patients exhibited a poorer disease improvement response compared to females, with the worst outcomes observed in younger males and the best in elderly females. Supplementary Fig. S4 presents the global distribution of stroke mortality attributable to hypertension by age group. These findings underscore the importance of age-specific strategies in addressing stroke burden, particularly for younger males, who show slower disease improvement.

EAPC in deaths from stroke attributable to high blood pressure, by age group and sex. EAPC quantifies the average annual percent change in mortality rates from 1990 to 2021, with larger absolute values representing faster declines. EAPC, Estimated annual percent change.

Evidence from NHANES database

To expand on the relationship between hypertension and age, we analyzed data from the NHANES database, using 55 years as the cutoff point, in line with the GBD analysis. We calculated the proportion of uncontrolled hypertension within the hypertensive population for both age groups (25–54 years and ≥55 years). The findings persistently indicated a higher proportion of uncontrolled hypertension in individuals under 55 than those over 55 across all five NHANES surveys conducted over the past decade. Among individuals aged 25–54 years, the proportion of uncontrolled hypertension decreased in the 2013–2014 survey, reaching the lowest level of 79.6 %. However, this proportion subsequently increased steadily, rising to 88.9 % in 2021–2023. In contrast, the proportion in those aged ≥55 years remained relatively stable throughout the survey cycles, 71.2 % in 2011–2012 and 71.8 % in 2021–2023 (Fig. 4A). In addition, we assessed the proportion of hypertensive patients who were unaware of their condition. The results demonstrated a pattern comparable to that observed for uncontrolled hypertension: individuals aged 25–54 consistently exhibited higher levels of unawareness than those aged ≥55. Furthermore, beginning from the 2013–2014 survey cycle, the prevalence of unawareness in the younger group showed a steady upward trajectory (Fig. 4B). Taken together, these findings underscore a growing burden of poor hypertension awareness and control among younger adults, highlighting the need for more targeted prevention and management strategies in this population.

Age-specific inequalities

To explore potential reasons underlying the age-specific patterns of stroke attributable to hypertension, we assessed health inequality using the concentration index in two specific age groups (25–54 years and ≥55 years). The concentration index measures the extent to which deaths are distributed across populations ranked by the SDI. A negative concentration index indicates a concentration of disease burden among populations at lower SDI, while a greater absolute value reflects more severe inequality. Our results showed that from 1990 to 2021, the absolute value of the concentration index increased in both age groups, and all values were negative (Fig. 5A and B). This suggests that health inequalities associated with hypertension-attributable stroke have intensified over time, disproportionately affecting lower-SDI populations. Moreover, comparison between age groups revealed that the absolute value of concentration index was consistently larger in adults aged 25–54 years than in those aged ≥55 years, both in 1990 and 2021, highlighting that younger adults face a greater degree of inequality (Fig. 5A and B). Taken together, these findings suggest that socioeconomic disparities may contribute importantly to the higher burden of hypertension-related stroke observed among younger adults.

The value of CI quantifies inequality, with more negative values indicating a greater concentration of deaths in populations with lower SDI. Discussion

By combining GBD 2021 and NHANES databases, we concluded several key findings. First, hypertension drives more than half of all stroke deaths globally. Second, for stroke attribute to hypertension, younger adults experience slower mortality improvements and more severe health inequalities compared to older adults. Third, hypertension control and awareness were also worser among younger adults. These converging findings underscore the need for age-specific strategies to reduce stroke burden and support the imperative for intensified hypertension management in younger populations.

Managing blood pressure is one of the most effective ways to reduce stroke. For the general population, the GBD 2021 stroke risk factor collaborators reported a rising global stroke burden from 1990 to 2021, with hypertension identified as the leading contributor [2]. At the individual level, blood pressure lowering has been shown to be effective for both primary and secondary prevention of stroke. A meta-analysis of 54 studies indicated a 27 % relative stroke risk reduction [4]. Serval clinical trials demonstrated that achieving SBP ≤130 mmHg significantly reduced stroke recurrence, with a pooled relative risk of 0.78 (95 % CI 0.64–0.94) [18,19]. In addition, mendelian randomization studies further support a causal relationship between elevated blood pressure and stroke, strengthening the evidence that hypertension is not merely correlated but a fundamental driver of stroke risk [20]. Given the strong association between hypertension and stroke, this study focuses on the burden of stroke attributable to hypertension.

Notably, we observed substantial declines in age-standardized stroke mortality across all ages from 1990 to 2021, but younger adults experienced only modest improvements compared with their older counterparts. The age–period–cohort model revealed that the annual decline in mortality was more gradual for individuals aged 25–54 years compared to those aged≥55. Moreover, the absolute values of the EAPC were lower in the younger age group (–1.37 %) compared to the older group (–1.74 %). These findings highlight pronounced age-specific disparities in the stroke burden attributable to hypertension, which represent a central observation of this study. Therefore, we mainly discuss the age-related heterogeneity of hypertension and stroke.

For hypertension, the situation among younger populations is concerning [21,22]. In the United States, a nationwide cross-sectional survey demonstrated that both uncontrolled and unawareness rates of hypertension are highest among the youngest adults, with uncontrolled hypertension affecting 93.4 % of those aged 18–44 years, compared with 74.5 % in adults aged 45–64 years and 69.7 % in those ≥65 years; corresponding unawareness rates were 68.4 %, 50.2 %, and 37.0 %, respectively [7]. Similarly, the more severe hypertension management issues in younger populations are also prevalent in China [23]. The reasons behind this situation may include several factors: current hypertension management models largely overlook younger adults, there is a lack of high-quality evidence showing that blood pressure reduction in early adulthood improves later cardiovascular outcomes, and adherence in this population tends to be poor [24,25]. Besides, genetic factors are also an important consideration for hypertension in young populations [26]. Our NHANES analysis used 55 years as the cutoff point, comparing the populations aged 25–54 years and those aged≥55, which aligns with the age groups used in the GBD study. Across five NHANES cycles spanning 2011–2023, uncontrolled hypertension remained substantially higher among adults aged 25–54 years than in those aged ≥55 years and worsened over time, rising from 79.6 % to 88.9 % in younger adults while remaining relatively stable in older adults. Moreover, the proportion of younger hypertensive individuals who were unaware of their condition increased from 35 % to 48 %. This worsening trend among younger populations may also be related to the COVID-19 pandemic [27]. These findings highlight the persistent and growing challenge of hypertension among younger adults.

For stroke, an increasing number of studies have begun to focus on younger populations [[28], [29], [30]]. A GBD study focusing on the ischemic stroke burden in young adults reveals a rebound in disease burden since 2015, highlighting the growing challenge in this age group [12]. At the same time, stroke risk factors in younger populations vary across different SDI regions, genders, and races [[31], [32], [33]]. These differences emphasize the need for targeted interventions. Furthermore, the direct comparisons between younger and older groups provide strong theoretical support for age-specific intervention policies. A meta-analysis revealed age-specific diverging trends in stroke incidence, with younger adults (<55 years) experiencing less favorable trends compared to older individuals [34]. In the United States, hospitalization rates for acute ischemic stroke have significantly decreased among those aged 65–84, while rising considerably for individuals aged 18–54 [35]. The similar trend has also been observed in Denmark [36]. There were similar to our findings; however, these studies included only populations from high-SDI regions, so their conclusions are only applicable to those areas. In contrast, our study used GBD 2021 data, which demonstrates that the improvement rate of stroke attributed to hypertension is slower in younger adults compared to older individuals globally.

Another notable finding is the marked socioeconomic gradient in hypertension-attributable stroke mortality and its differential impact by age. Using the concentration index to measure relative inequality, we observed that stroke deaths attributable to hypertension were disproportionately concentrated in countries with SDI. This inequality increased from 1990 to 2021 for both age groups. As for the comparison between the two age groups, the absolute value of the concentration index was consistently larger for adults aged 25–54 years than for those aged ≥55 years, indicating that younger adults bear a greater share of the excess burden in socioeconomically disadvantaged settings. These results are consistent with a recent analysis of the GBD 2021, which found that both absolute and relative inequalities in stroke burden widened from 1990 to 2021. Specifically, the concentration index for total stroke worsened from –0.0819 to –0.1119, indicating a greater burden in countries with lower SDI and a worsening of inequalities over time [37]. Dietary factors have also been implicated in contributing to this inequality [38]. Unlike previous studies that focused on populations of all ages, our research specifically targeted distinct age groups and compared the inequalities between these two cohorts. This is a key distinction of our study.

Our findings underscore the urgent need for age-specific strategies in addressing hypertension-related stroke, particularly among younger adults. Public health policies should prioritize early intervention and targeted education programs aimed at improving hypertension awareness and control among younger populations. Moreover, our study suggests that young adults face unique challenges in managing hypertension and stroke prevention, and these should be addressed through tailored interventions. Policies that focus on improving access to affordable treatment, increasing medication adherence, and enhancing public health education.

Limitations

Despite providing new insights into age-specific disparities, our study has limitations. First, our reliance on GBD and NHANES datasets means that the analyses are constrained by the quality and completeness of the underlying data. The GBD study estimates mortality using modelling approaches and may underestimate stroke deaths in countries with weak surveillance. Likewise, NHANES data pertain to the United States and may not be generalizable to low- and middle-income countries where hypertension awareness and control are poorer. Potential selection bias may have arisen due to missing blood pressure data. Second, we focused on hypertension as the primary risk factor and did not explore interactions with other modifiable risk factors, such as obesity, diabetes, or tobacco use. Third, the concentration index captures relative inequality but does not identify the specific mechanisms driving disparities, limiting our ability to propose targeted interventions. Fourth, this study retrospectively applies the 2017 AHA/ACC definition of hypertension (≥130/80 mmHg) to all NHANES cycles, including those conducted when the prevailing definition in the U.S. was ≥140/90 mmHg. This discrepancy may affect the temporal comparability of hypertension prevalence and control across different survey periods.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our findings emphasize the critical need for age-specific strategies to mitigate the growing burden of hypertension-related stroke, particularly among younger adults. Younger adults experience slower mortality improvements, worse hypertension control and awareness, and more severe health inequalities compared to older adults.
