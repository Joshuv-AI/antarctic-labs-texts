Incidental Discovery of an 8.2-cm Pulmonary Artery Aneurysm During Routine Ablation Evaluation
Authors: Shiv Brij Gakhar, Durga Amy Kumar, Jose Nicolas Codolosa, Kelsi Thompson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPulmonary artery aneurysms (PAAs) are most commonly identified in the setting of pulmonary hypertension or congenital heart disease. Clinical Course of an Incidentally Discovered Giant Pulmonary Artery Aneurysm

Intraprocedural transesophageal echocardiography during elective atrial tachycardia ablation revealed a markedly enlarged pulmonary artery. Subsequent hemodynamic evaluation demonstrated normal pulmonary pressures, and multidisciplinary assessment supported conservative management with surveillance. History of presentation

A 73-year-old woman presented for elective radiofrequency ablation of atrial tachycardia. Prior operative and imaging reports from the mitral valve repair did not describe pulmonary artery dilation. She denied chest pain, dyspnea, syncope, hemoptysis, or exercise intolerance and reported stable functional capacity after the procedure.

Past medical history

Her medical history included prior mitral valve repair, atrial septal defect closure, and atrial fibrillation treated with catheter ablation.

Differential diagnosis

Because the patient was asymptomatic, no specific diagnostic concern prompted evaluation after the procedure. The pulmonary artery abnormality was identified incidentally during routine intraprocedural imaging.

Investigations

Baseline physical examination and laboratory evaluation were unremarkable. During standard intraprocedural assessment, transesophageal echocardiography demonstrated severe dilation of the main pulmonary artery measuring 8.2 cm in maximal diameter (Figure 1, Figure 2, Figure 3). The right ventricle (RV) was mildly enlarged with preserved systolic function, and no intracardiac shunt was identified. The ablation procedure was completed without immediate complication, although postprocedure atrial fibrillation occurred and cardioversion was deferred given hemodynamic stability.

Given the unexpected finding, right- and left-heart catheterization was performed for hemodynamic assessment. This demonstrated normal pulmonary artery pressures and normal pulmonary vascular resistance, excluding pulmonary hypertension.

Therapeutic intervention

After hemodynamic assessment, the patient was referred for multidisciplinary evaluation involving cardiology and cardiothoracic surgery. Discussion considered aneurysm size, absence of pulmonary hypertension, preserved RV function, and lack of symptoms. Given the low estimated rupture risk relative to operative risk, conservative management with serial imaging surveillance was recommended.

Outcome and follow-up

The patient remained clinically stable after the procedure and was discharged with outpatient cardiology follow-up and planned longitudinal imaging surveillance.

Discussion

Pulmonary artery aneurysm (PAA) is an uncommon vascular abnormality with heterogeneous etiologies including congenital heart disease, pulmonary hypertension, connective tissue disorders, and inflammatory vasculitides. Most available data derive from small surgical series, in which aneurysms are frequently identified late in their course, often at diameters approaching 6 cm at the time of intervention.1 Consequently, the natural history of incidentally discovered large PAAs remains incompletely defined.

Historically, surgical repair has been considered when the aneurysm diameter exceeds approximately 5 to 6 cm, particularly in symptomatic patients or in the presence of pulmonary hypertension.2 However, these thresholds are largely extrapolated from heterogeneous cohorts, and their applicability to patients without elevated pulmonary pressures is uncertain. Several reports suggest that the clinical trajectory of the RV and overall hemodynamic profile may be more important determinants of risk than size alone.3

In the present case, chronic abnormal pulmonary flow related to prior atrial septal defect and subsequent mitral valve repair likely resulted in progressive pulmonary artery remodeling despite normal pulmonary artery pressures. This supports the concept that prolonged flow disturbance alone may contribute to aneurysm formation independent of pressure overload. Recognition of this mechanism is important because rupture risk and clinical behavior may differ from aneurysms associated with pulmonary hypertension.

Management therefore requires individualized risk stratification, incorporating symptoms and hemodynamic findings rather than aneurysm diameter alone.4 Factors favoring intervention include symptoms, pulmonary hypertension, RV dysfunction, thrombus formation, or evidence of progressive enlargement. In contrast, asymptomatic patients with preserved RV function and normal pulmonary pressures may be managed conservatively with surveillance after multidisciplinary evaluation involving cardiology and cardiothoracic surgery.

As advanced intraprocedural imaging becomes routine during electrophysiological procedures, incidental identification of structural vascular abnormalities may become more frequent. Incidental discovery of PAAs during cardiac imaging has been previously described.5 This case illustrates the importance of systematic evaluation and coordinated decision-making when a large PAA is discovered because management may not align with traditional size-based assumptions.

Conclusions

This report describes the incidental discovery of a giant PAA during elective ablation evaluation. In asymptomatic patients with normal pulmonary pressures, management should be guided by clinical status and RV function rather than aneurysm diameter alone. Multidisciplinary assessment is essential to determine surveillance vs operative management.
