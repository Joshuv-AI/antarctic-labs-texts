Local low‐frequency vibration accelerates healing of full‐thickness wounds in a hyperglycemic rat model
Authors: Daijiro Haba, Qi Qin, Chihiro Takizawa, Sanai Tomida, Takeo Minematsu, Hiromi Sanada, Gojiro Nakagami
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractAims/IntroductionLocal low‐frequency vibration (LLFV) promotes vasodilation and blood flow, enhancing wound healing in diabetic foot ulcers with angiopathy. However, vibration‐induced vasodilation does not occur, owing to chronic hyperglycemia and inflammation. We hypothesized that LLFV improves glycometabolism and inflammation, leading to vasodilation and angiogenesis in diabetic wounds. Therefore, this study investigated the effect of LLFV on wound healing in hyperglycemic rats, primarily focusing on glycometabolism, inflammation, vasodilation, and angiogenesis.Materials and MethodsStreptozotocin‐induced hyperglycemic Sprague–Dawley rats were used in this study. We applied LLFV to experimentally‐induced wounds at 50 Hz and 0, 600, 1,000 or 1,500 mVpp for 40 min/day from post‐wounding days (PWD) 1–14.ResultsThe relative wound areas in the 600 and 1,000 mVpp groups on PWD 5–7 were significantly smaller than those at 0 mVpp. The expression of Glo‐1 (1,500 mVpp) and Slc2A4 (1,000 and 1,500 mVpp) was upregulated on PWD 4 and 14, respectively. However, there was no difference in methylglyoxal expression levels in any group until PWD 14. At 1,000 mVpp, the expression of Tnfa on PWD 4, and that of Ptx3 and Ccl2 on PWD 14 was downregulated. Furthermore, the M1/M2 macrophage ratio was considerably decreased on both days. The expression of Nos3, Vegfa and vascular endothelial growth factor A was upregulated on PWD 4. In addition, vasodilation and angiogenesis were more obvious on PWD 14 with 1,000 mVpp.ConclusionsThe results suggest that LLFV promotes wound healing, improves glycometabolism and inflammation, and enhances vasodilation and angiogenesis in hyperglycemic wounds.Keywords: Hyperglycemia, Low‐frequency vibration, Wound healing

INTRODUCTION

Diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs) are a severe symptom of diabetes mellitus and stem from angiopathy caused by hyperglycemia in insulin‐resistant patients. The treatment of this symptom is inhibited by chronic inflammation related to diabetes mellitus and diminished peripheral blood flow at the wound site 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 . Local low‐frequency vibration (LLFV) reportedly promotes regional blood flow through vasodilation by producing nitric oxide (NO) and heals wounds in pressure injuries 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 . In DFUs, hyperglycemia inhibits NO production by blocking endothelial NO synthase (eNOS) activation and impairing arginine production through the polyol pathway. Therefore, LLFV was expected to be ineffective in vasodilation 2 , 9 . However, a clinical study showed that LLFV promotes peripheral blood flow and wound healing in DFUs 10 , although the underlying mechanism in vasodilation disorders remains to be clarified. We hypothesized that LLFV might promote blood flow and wound healing by improving local glycometabolism, thereby reducing inflammation and enhancing vasodilation and angiogenesis.

Our previous study showed that LLFV promotes adenosine monophosphate‐activated protein kinase (AMPK)‐mediated glucose transporter 4 (GLUT4) translocation and glucose uptake in 3T3‐L1 adipocytes 11 . This suggests that LLFV can alleviate hyperglycemia by increasing non‐insulin‐mediated glucose uptake. Whole‐body LFV promoted wound healing in diabetic model animals 12 , 13 , 14 , and alleviated blood glucose levels by enhancing AMPK activation in skeletal muscle 15 . However, there has been no in vivo study on full‐thickness wounds in diabetic or hyperglycemic animal models. Additionally, the optimal LLFV conditions for DFU healing remain unknown.

The present study aimed to determine the optimal LLFV intensity that promotes the healing of full‐thickness wounds, focusing on local glycometabolism, inflammation, vasodilation and angiogenesis in hyperglycemic rats.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Reagents

Streptozotocin was purchased from FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation (Osaka, Japan). Other essential reagents included: (i) primary antibodies against methylglyoxal (MG; mouse‐monoclonal; Japan Institute for the Control of Aging, Nikken SEIL CO., Ltd., Shizuoka, Japan), GLUT4 (rabbit‐polyclonal), CD68/SR‐D1 (ED1; mouse‐monoclonal), inducible NO synthase (iNOS; rabbit‐polyclonal) and lymphatic vessel endothelial 1 (LYVE‐1; rabbit‐polyclonal) from Novus Biologicals (Centennial, CO, USA), CD163 (EPR19518; rabbit‐monoclonal) from Abcam (Cambridge, UK), α‐smooth muscle actin (α‐SMA; rabbit‐polyclonal) and cytochrome c oxidase subunit 4 (COX4; rabbit‐polyclonal) from GeneTex (Irvine, CA, USA), and β‐actin (rabbit‐polyclonal; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA); (ii) secondary antibodies against horseradish peroxidase (HRP)‐conjugated anti‐mouse and anti‐rabbit immunoglobulin G (IgG; Peroxidase AffiniPure Donkey Anti‐Mouse IgG [H + L] and Anti‐Rabbit IgG [H + L]) from Jackson ImmunoResearch (West Grove, PA, USA); and (iii) fluorescent antibodies Alexa Fluor 488 (donkey, anti‐rabbit IgG) and Alexa Fluor 594 (donkey, anti‐mouse IgG) from Jackson ImmunoResearch.

Animals

Seven‐week‐old male Sprague–Dawley rats (SLC Japan, Shizuoka, Japan), weighing 250–270 g, were individually kept in a pathogen‐free room (temperature 23 ± 2°C, humidity 55 ± 10%). After acclimatization for 1 week, acute hyperglycemia was induced through a single intraperitoneal administration of streptozotocin (55 mg/kg) dissolved in 0.1 mol/L citrate buffer (pH 4.5); rats in the control group were injected with citrate buffer alone. Rats with consistent blood glucose levels >300 mg/dL 2 week after injection were used as hyperglycemic model animals. The humane endpoint was rapid weight loss (>20%) within 1 week.

Wounding procedure

Full‐thickness wounds were created 14 days after streptozotocin administration in all rats. One day before wounding, we removed the fur on the trunk using a shaver and depilatory cream under anesthesia (isoflurane inhalation, 2%, 0.2 L/min; Pfizer, Tokyo, Japan). We created a round full‐thickness excisional wound, 2 cm in diameter, centered at the caudal end of the rib cage 16 . We applied a dressing (FoamLite; Convatec Japan, Tokyo, Japan) to the wound and wrapped the rats with gauze. The dressing was changed daily until the end of the experimental period. Wound appearance was recorded using a digital camera (WG‐6; RICOH, Tokyo, Japan).

Vibration treatment

A previously developed non‐portable vibrator was used to apply vibrations 5 , 6 , 8 , 11 . Vibration treatment was applied to the wound from post‐wounding day (PWD) 1 to the end of the experimental period. After the daily dressing change, an oscillator was placed directly on the gauze over the center of wound. Then, vibration intensities of 600, 1,000, or 1,500 mVpp were set according to the previous study and applied to the wound at a fixed frequency of 50 Hz for 40 min/day over 14 days to determine the optimal vibration intensity for wound healing (Figure 1). Changes in vibration intensity were achieved by changing the voltage of the power supply. Control rats were subjected to the same procedure without vibration.

Full‐thickness wounds were created on the lateral abdomen of a hyperglycemic rat model. After the daily dressing change, anesthetized rats were laid on rubber foam mats. An oscillator was attached directly to the gauze over the wound. After daily dressing changes, anesthetized rats were laid on rubber foam mats, and local low‐frequency vibration was applied to the wound from post‐wounding days 1–14.

Experimental design

We determined the most effective vibration intensity for wound‐size reduction. We first euthanized five animals in each group on PWD 14 using carbon dioxide gas. We subsequently harvested tissue samples from the wound site for histological and immunohistological analyses.

Next, we euthanized and harvested samples from five animals in the control, most‐effective vibration and least‐effective vibration groups on PWD 4. This was done to investigate the mechanisms of wound healing during the early phase of LLFV for histological analysis, immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence, as well as gene and protein expression analyses.

Glycation analysis

We investigated the gene and protein expression levels of glycation‐related factors, including GLUT4, MG and glyoxalase 1 (Glo‐1).

Cells use the glycolytic system to generate energy. Their metabolism produces MG – a potent cytotoxic agent, a major precursor of advanced glycation end‐products (AGEs), which introduces direct toxicity, and an indicator of glycation 17 . Glo‐1 is an enzyme in the glyoxalase system, a metabolic pathway that detoxifies MG 18 . However, Glo‐1 expression is impaired in people with diabetes mellitus. Excessive glucose uptake and the promotion of GLUT4 translocation by LLFV might accelerate glycation. Thus, an appropriate balance between glucose uptake promotion and glycation due to glucose control reduces MG and AGEs formation 19 , 20 .

Histology, immunohistology, and immunofluorescence

All harvested tissues were fixed overnight in 10% neutral buffered formalin at 25°C. They were subsequently dehydrated in a series of ethanol and xylene substitutes (G‐Nox; Genostaff, Tokyo, Japan) and embedded in paraffin. The paraffin‐embedded tissues were sectioned (3‐μm thick) for hematoxylin–eosin staining, immunohistological and immunofluorescence analysis.

For immunohistochemistry, the sections were incubated in 3% hydrogen peroxide diluted with methanol for 30 min to quench endogenous peroxidase activity for MG staining. We carried out antigen retrieval by autoclaving the sections at 121°C for 15 min in 0.01 mol/L citrate buffer (pH 6.0). The sections were incubated with anti‐MG (1:100), GLUT4 (1:100), CD68/SR‐1 (1:100), iNOS (1:50), CD163 (1:500), α‐SMA (1:100) and LYVE‐1 (1:50) antibodies overnight. After removing the primary antibodies, the sections were each washed with phosphate‐buffered saline three times for 5 min. Sections that reacted with the anti‐MG antibody were incubated with HRP‐conjugated anti‐mouse immunoglobulin antibody (1:1,000) for 1 h. Then, immunoreactivity was visualized through a reaction with 0.2 mg/mL 3,3′‐diaminobenzidine (Wako Pure Chemical) in 0.05 mol/L Tris–HCl buffer (pH 7.4) supplemented with 2% hydrogen peroxide. We carried out counterstaining using hematoxylin. The remaining sections were incubated with the secondary antibodies Alexa Fluor 488 (1:1,000) and Alexa Fluor 594 (1:1,000) at room temperature for 1 h. After staining, the sections were dehydrated with ethanol, cleansed with G‐Nox, and mounted with 4′,6‐diamidino‐2‐phenylindole. The sections were observed under a light microscope (BX41; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) and photographed using an inverted microscope (BZ‐X800; Keyence, Osaka, Japan).

The number of α‐SMA‐positive blood vessels and LYVE‐1‐positive cells were counted in each group to assess new blood or lymphatic vessel formation. To determine the effects of LLFV on inflammation, we calculated the M1/M2 macrophage ratio based on the immunofluorescence of pro‐inflammatory M1 macrophages (CD68/iNOS) and anti‐inflammatory M2 macrophages (CD163) 21 . Finally, the number of CD68/iNOS‐ and CD163‐positive cells was counted by several blinded researchers for each group.

Real‐time reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction

Expression levels of the candidate messenger ribonucleic acid were individually measured using real‐time reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT–PCR). Complementary deoxyribonucleic acid (cDNA) was synthesized using a high‐capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Ma, USA). The target cDNA was amplified using an Mx3000P QPCR System (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) and a TaqMan Gene Expression Assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific). We analyzed the expression of genes associated with glycometabolism (Glo‐1, which encodes an MG‐degrading enzyme of the same name; Slc2A4, a gene encoding GLUT4), inflammation (Tnfa, an inflammation marker; Ptx3, a vascular inflammatory marker in diabetes; Ccl2, a monocyte chemoattractant protein 1 marker), vasodilation (Nos3), angiogenesis (Vegfa, vascular endothelial growth factor A) and lymphangiogenesis (Vegfc). The program for real‐time RT–PCR was as follows: 50°C for 2 min, 95°C for 10 min, followed by 50 cycles at 95°C for 15 s and 60°C for 1 min. All samples were tested in duplicates or triplicates. Relative expression levels of the target genes were normalized to 18S ribosomal ribonucleic acid expression and compared across the groups using the ΔΔ cycle threshold (Ct) method. The ΔΔCt values, which show relative expression levels (log2 fold), were used for statistical analysis.

Enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Wound samples were homogenized in a radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer (Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan) containing protease inhibitors. Homogenates were centrifuged at 12,000 g for 15 min. According to the manufacturer's instructions, vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF)‐A, VEGF‐C and MG expression levels were quantitatively determined using Rat VEGF Quantikine (RRV00; R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MA, USA), VEGFC Rat (BMS626‐2, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and OxiSelect MG Competitive (Cosmo Bio, Tokyo, Japan) ELISA Kits, respectively. The protein content of the samples was determined to be 1 mg using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay (Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit; Thermo Fisher Scientific). The data were expressed as the quantity of target molecules (pg) per total protein (mg) for each sample. All samples were measured in duplicates and the average values were recorded.

Western blotting

COX is the terminal enzyme of the mitochondrial respiratory chain. Furthermore, COX4 expression is enhanced through mitochondrial activation 22 . The protein components of samples (10 μg of total protein for β‐actin and COX4) were subjected to 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate‐polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis for their separation. They were subsequently transferred onto a polyvinylidene difluoride membrane (iBlot Transfer Stack, polyvinylidene fluoride, regular; Thermo Fisher Scientific) using an iBlot gel transfer device (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Protein concentrations were determined using the BCA assay. In addition, immunostaining of the membrane was carried out using the SNAP i.d. 2.0 protein detection system (Merck Millipore Billerica, Burlington, MA, USA). Non‐specific binding was blocked using a blocking solution (Blocking One; Nacalai Tesque) for 10 min, and then the reaction was allowed with β‐actin (1:1,000) and COX4 antibodies (1:2,000) for 1 h. Antibody binding was visualized using the HRP‐conjugated anti‐rabbit immunoglobulin antibody (1:1,000) for 30 min and the Immobilon Forte Western HRP substrate (WBLUF0100, Merck Millipore Billerica). The image was captured using LumiCube (Liponics, Tokyo, Japan) and separated into RGB channels. The average intensity of the signal was measured in the blue channel image through ImageJ version 1.53i (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA), and the intensity was normalized to that of β‐actin immunoreactivity.

Immunofluorescence image analysis

Fluorescence intensity of the obtained fluorescence micrographs was measured by ImageJ software 11 . The captured image was separated into RGB channels, and the immunofluorescent staining intensity of GLUT4 was measured in the green channel. We measured the intensity of green fluorescence in the plasma membranes from whole cells. Finally, we measured the relative intensity of the GLUT4 immunofluorescence at the plasma membrane in each group.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation. We calculated the relative wound area with that at PWD 0 being 1. Differences across groups were analyzed through one‐way analysis of variance (anova), and Dunnett's post‐hoc test for wound area, gene expression and protein expression. All statistical analyses were carried out using JMP 15 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

RESULTS

Vasodilation and relative wound area reduction by LLFV

There was no significant difference in mean blood glucose levels across the groups on PWD 0 and 14 (445.2 ± 34–508.6 ± 34 mg/dL at 0 mVpp, 476.0 ± 49–490 ± 64 mg/dL at 600 mVpp, 457.4 ± 34–524.4 ± 67 mg/dL at 1,000 mVpp and 460.2 ± 21–479 ± 29 mg/dL at 1,500 mVpp). There was no abnormal behavior, such as stress‐induced self‐harm or hair loss, and no death caused by LLFV.

Relative wound areas in the 600 and 1,000 mVpp groups on PWD 5–7 were significantly smaller than those in the 0 mVpp group (P = 0.004, 0.007 on PWD 5; P = 0.009, 0.002 on PWD 6; and P = 0.007, 0.004 on PWD 7, respectively; Figure 2a,b). There was no significant difference between 0 and 1,500 mVpp until PWD 14. From PWD 8 to 14, epithelialization was obvious in the 0 and 1,000 mVpp groups, with no significant difference in the relative wound area. These results suggest that LLFV at 600–1,000 mVpp had healing effects during the early stages of wound healing. Contrastingly, LLFV had no beneficial healing effect at 1,500 mVpp, but delayed wound healing compared with that at 0 mVpp. Furthermore, LLFV at 1,000–1,500 mVpp promoted glucose uptake in adipocytes. However, the worst effect was observed at 1,500 mVpp. During histological analysis, more vasodilated blood vessels were observed at the wound site and edges with 1,000 and 1,500 mVpp than those with 0 mVpp on PWD 4 (Figure 2c) and 14 (Figure 2d), whereas hemorrhages were observed in the 1,500 mVpp group (Figure 2d).

The results are presented as the mean ± standard deviation. *P < 0.01, 0 mVpp versus 600 mVpp, † P < 0.01, 0 mVpp versus 1,000 mVpp (one‐way anova and Dunnett's test). The right‐hand insets are magnifications of the areas in black squares in the left images. Yellow arrowheads show vasodilated vessels and white arrows show hemorrhages. Scale bar, 500 μm (left insets) and 100 μm (right insets).

Based on these results, we further investigated the effects of LLFV at 1,000 and 1,500 mVpp compared with that at 0 mVpp. Additionally, we evaluated the samples on PWD 4 at 0, 1,000 and 1,500 mVpp, because a considerable reduction in relative wound area was observed on PWD 5.

Improvement of local glycometabolism by LLFV

On PWD 4, the adipose tissue layer was still absent at the wound site. Therefore, GLUT4 immunofluorescence was measured around the wound. Relative GLUT4 fluorescence intensity in adipose tissue at 1,000 mVpp compared with that at 0 mVpp (P = 0.028; Figure 3a). On PWD 14, adipose tissue was observed over the wound area; relative GLUT4 fluorescence intensity was significantly enhanced in the plasma membrane of this tissue at 1,500 mVpp compared with at 0 mVpp (P = 0.039; Figure 3a). This showed that LLFV promoted GLUT4 translocation to the plasma membrane. The promotion of GLUT4 translocation and glucose uptake by LLFV could trigger excessive AGEs production and glycation. Thus, we investigated MG production and enzyme degradation. Comparable amounts of MG‐positive cells were observed in each group on PWD 4 and 14 (Figure 3b). The results of real‐time RT–PCR showed that Glo‐1 expression was significantly upregulated in the 1,500 mVpp group on PWD 4 compared with the 0 mVpp group (P = 0.017). On PWD 14, there was no difference in Glo‐1 expression across the groups, although Slc2A4 expression in the 1,000 and 1,500 mVpp groups was significantly higher than in the 0 mVpp group (P = 0.015 and 0.042, respectively; Figure 3c).

Western blot results showed that COX4 expression in the 1,500 mVpp group on PWD 4 and in the 1,000 mVpp group on PWD 14 was significantly higher than in the 0 mVpp group (P = 0.049 and 0.034, respectively; Figure 3d).

Improvement of inflammation and alteration of macrophage phenotypes

M1 macrophages (CD68+/iNOS+) were stained in red and green (merge), whereas M2 macrophages (CD163+) were stained in green. M1 macrophages were expressed at 0 mVpp on PWD 4 (Figure 4a), with no quantitative difference across the groups (Figure 4b). In contrast, M2 macrophages were significantly upregulated at 1,000 mVpp (P = 0.046; Figure 4a,b). On PWD 14, the number of M1 macrophages was significantly decreased in the 1,000 mVpp group (P = 0.008), and the number of M2 macrophages were significantly increased (P = 0.021; Figure 4a,b). The ratio of M1/M2 macrophages at 1,000 mVpp was significantly lower than that at 0 mVpp on PWD 4 and 14 (P = 0.044 and 0.002, respectively; Figure 4b).

Real‐time RT–PCR analysis of inflammatory markers showed that Tnfa expression on PWD 4 in the 1,000 mVpp group and that of Ccl2 on PWD 14 in the 1,000 and 1,500 mVpp groups was downregulated (P = 0.017, 0.004, 0.022, respectively). Ptx3 expression on PWD 14 in the 1,000 mVpp group was significantly lower than that in the 0 mVpp group (P = 0.022; Figure 4c).

Vasodilation and angiogenesis by LLFV

On PWD 4, the number of α‐SMA‐positive blood vessels at the wound site was significantly higher in the 1,000 mVpp group than in the 0 mVpp group (P = 0.003; Figure 5a,b). However, there was no difference in the number of LYVE‐1‐positive lymphatic vessels at the wound site across the groups. On PWD 14, epithelization was apparent in each group, with no difference in relative wound area across the groups. However, more vasodilated blood vessels at the original wound site were observed in the 1,000 and 1,500 mVpp groups than in the 0 mVpp group. The number of α‐SMA‐positive blood vessels was higher in the vibration‐treated groups than in the 0 mVpp group on PWD 14 (P = 0.027; Figure 5a,b).

Real‐time RT–PCR analysis showed that Nos3 and Vegfa expression was upregulated in the 1,000 mVpp group on PWD 4 compared with the 0 mVpp group (P = 0.043 and 0.016, respectively; Figure 5c). On PWD 14, Nos3 expression at 1,000 mVpp was upregulated compared with the 0 mVpp group (P = 0.036; Figure 5c). The ELISA of angiogenesis and lymphangiogenesis protein markers on PWD 4 showed upregulated VEGF‐A expression levels at 1,000 mVpp (P = 0.029; Figure 5d). In contrast, there was no significant quantitative difference in Vegfc or VEGF‐C expression in the lymph vessels (Figure 5b–d).

DISCUSSION

In our hyperglycemic rat model, LLFV promoted the healing of full‐thickness wounds, improved glycometabolism, altered the macrophage phenotype (ameliorated inflammation), and enhanced vasodilation and angiogenesis.

In the animal experiments, wounds could not become hard‐to‐heal, and the differences in wound area between the non‐vibration and vibration group might eventually converge not only in the hyperglycemia rat model, but also in the diabetic rat model 23 , 24 . Among these situations, the significant differences in relative wound area on PWD 5–7 by LLFV showed that the effect of LLFV on wound healing promotion was notable, indicating LLFV can be expected to promote wound healing at the early stage of healing process in the clinical setting.

LLFV promotes glucose uptake through GLUT4 translocation, as evidenced by quantitative analysis of GLUT4 immunofluorescence intensity, and that excessive MG production is inhibited by Glo‐1 expression. This could further lead to reduced local glucose levels by promoting local glucose uptake. However, at 1,000 mVpp, Glo‐1 expression was not enhanced. In contrast, COX4 expression tended to increase on PWD 4, and was significantly enhanced on PWD 14. Chronic hyperglycemia impairs mitochondrial activity 25 , enhances only the anaerobic glycolysis pathway, and promotes the polyol pathway and MG production 26 . Furthermore, activation of mitochondrial function promotes normal glycometabolism from the anaerobic glycolysis pathway to the citric acid cycle 27 . Additionally, whole‐body LFV to diabetic db/db mice improved mitochondrial function in skeletal muscle 15 . Therefore, the enhanced COX4 expression in the 1,000 mVpp group might improve glycometabolism, thereby suppressing excessive MG production. It can be said that excessive MG production was inhibited, and the important result is that the MG level did not increase in conjunction with glucose uptake.

LLFV altered the macrophage phenotype and improved inflammation during the early phase of healing. A previous in vitro study reported the change in macrophage phenotype from M1 to M2 with low‐intensity LLFV 28 . Other in vivo studies showed that whole‐body LV promoted wound healing by alleviating inflammation, suppressing the infiltration of inflammatory cells, downregulating the expression of inflammation‐related genes and promoting angiogenesis 12 , 29 . Similar results were observed in the present study, indicating that LLFV can promote the transition from M1 to M2 macrophages. Furthermore, LLFV can accelerate the early wound healing phase by promoting the formation and function of blood vessels. The present results suggest that early improvement in inflammation promotes wound healing on PWD 5–7.

Finally, vasodilated and newly formed vessels were observed in the wound site by LLFV. Previous in vivo studies have shown that LLFV promotes regional blood flow via vasodilation through eNOS activation 5 , 6 . In our hyperglycemic rat model, LLFV enhanced Nos3 expression, which then activated eNOS and induced vasodilation to promote wound healing. LLFV‐induced fluid shear force in the vascular lumen stimulates vascular endothelial cells and promotes the formation of new capillary vessels 30 . Thus, LLFV at 1,000 mVpp could improve inflammation, and promote vasodilation and angiogenesis on the application of mechanical stress. Although LLFV at 1,500 mVpp enhanced vasodilation and COX4 expression, it did not alter the M1/M2 macrophage ratio, promote angiogenesis or promote wound healing. In contrast, bleeding and infiltration of neutrophils were observed at 1,500 mVpp. High‐intensity vibration induces vascular disturbances, such as Raynaud's syndrome 31 , and decreases the levels of angiogenic growth factors. LLFV at 1,500 mVpp could damage the blood vessels instead, thereby inducing hemorrhage and prolonged inflammation. These findings provided important insights into the beneficial and deleterious effects of LLFV intensity in a diabetes model, which could advance the application of this technology in clinical practice.

The present study had certain limitations. First, it was not possible to directly measure the actual local glucose levels, thus the changes in glycometabolism were assessed only indirectly. Additionally, the enzymatic activity of Glo‐1 was not measured, and the contribution of mitochondrial activation for improvement of glycation was not investigated. The investigation of glycometabolism in this study was limited to indirect methods. Further studies are needed to explore the potential of LLFV in improving glucose levels at the wound site.

In conclusion, the present study showed that LLFV at 600–1,000 mVpp promotes the healing of full‐thickness wounds in a hyperglycemic rat model, improves glycometabolism and inflammation, and enhances vasodilation and angiogenesis. As LLFV is non‐invasive, it is a promising strategy for the clinical treatment of DFUs.
