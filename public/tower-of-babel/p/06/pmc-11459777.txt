Machine learning-based prognostic prediction for hospitalized HIV/AIDS patients with cryptococcus infection in Guangxi, China
Authors: Baili Zhan, Wudi Wei, Zhiman Xie, Sirun Meng, Xiuli Bao, Xiaotao He, Xiaoting Xie, Meng Zhang, Li Ye, Junjun Jiang, Shixiong Yang, Hao Liang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo develop and validate a machine learning model for predicting mortality-associated prognostic factors in order to reduce in-hospital mortality rates among HIV/AIDS patients with Cryptococcus infection in Guangxi, China.MethodsThis retrospective prognostic study included HIV/AIDS patients with cryptococcosis in the Fourth People’s Hospital of Nanning from October 2011 to June 2019. Clinical features were extracted and used to train ten machine learning models, including Logistic Regression, KNN, DT, RF, Adaboost, Xgboost, LightGBM, Catboost, SVM, and NBM, to predict the outcome of HIV patients with cryptococcosis infection. The sensitivity, specificity, AUC, and F1 value were applied to assess model performance in both the testing and training sets. The optimal model was selected and interpreted.ResultsA total of 396 patients were included in the study. The average in-hospital mortality of HIV/AIDS patients with cryptococcosis was 12.9% from 2012 to 2019. After feature screening, 20 clinical features were selected for model construction, accounting for 93.8%, including ART, Electrolyte disorder, Anemia, and 17 laboratory tests. The RF model (AUC 0.9787, Sensitivity 0.9535, Specificity 0.8889, F1 0.7455) and the SVM model (AUC 0.9286, Sensitivity 0.7907, Specificity 0.9786, F1 0.8293) had excellent performance. The SHAP analysis showed that the primary risk factors for prognosis prediction were identified as BUN/CREA, Electrolyte disorder, NEUT%, Urea, and IBIL.ConclusionsRF and SVM machine learning models have shown promising predictive abilities for the prognosis of hospitalized HIV/AIDS patients with cryptococcosis, which can aid clinical assessment and treatment decisions for patient prognosis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-024-10013-y.Keywords: HIV, Cryptococcus, Machine learning, Prediction of prognosis

Introduction

Cryptococcus is a globally distributed invasive fungus that is often in decaying wood, bird droppings, and contaminated soil [1]. In particular, Cryptococcus neoformans is the top-ranked fungal pathogen in the World Health Organization Fungal Priority Pathogens List [2]. Inhalation of its spores is considered the primary route of infection for humans [3]. Cryptococcus is one of the leading causes of death in AIDS patients, responsible for 19% of AIDS-related mortality, and resulting in approximately 112 000 global deaths each year [4]. The Asia-Pacific region has the second highest number of cases globally [5]. Guangxi, located in a subtropical region with warm climate and humid environment, has become a high-incidence area for invasive fungal diseases in China. At the same time, Guangxi is one of the high-incidence areas for HIV/AIDS in China [6]. It is also a major region for pigeon farming, posing a high risk for the spread of cryptococcosis. In a retrospective study, cryptococcal infection accounted for 8.6% of fungemia in Guangxi [7]. Thus, it is evident that cryptococcosis is a significant public health issue in Guangxi.

Cryptococcus infections are characterized by their insidious onset, association with significant mortality, and propensity for relapse. The mortality rate for cryptococcal meningitis is 100% if infections remain untreated [8]. The mortality rate at 10 weeks post-treatment with amphotericin B reaches as high as 39.7%, whereas the combination therapy of fluconazole and flucytosine sustains a mortality rate of 35.1% at the same time point [9]. Ayomide Loye et al. also reported that a young patient with AIDS, admitted with a diagnosis of cryptococcal meningitis, was declared brain dead within a week [10]. Even after treatment, the relapse rate of cryptococcosis patients is still 10.5%, with 30.4% of those being HIV-related [11]. Furthermore, Cryptococcus has the ability to enter a dormant state to evade immune clearance, and can reactivate and proliferate when the patient’s immune function is compromised [12]. Nevertheless, the factors that impact the clinical progression of Cryptococcus infection patients remain unclear. Thus, there is an imperative need to discover prognostic markers and to devise predictive strategies for therapeutic efficacy.

Traditional statistical methods often assume a linear relationship between variables, whereas the clinical manifestations of cryptococcal disease may exhibit nonlinear characteristics. In the recent years, the application of Artificial Intelligence (AI), particularly Machine learning, has become a significant force propelling medical advancements [13, 14]. Machine learning, through the analysis and learning of vast datasets, captures their features. J. M. Nolde et al. employed neural networks to predict myocardial injury and mortality, achieving an area under the curve (AUC) exceeding 0.7, which indicates a robust predictive capability [15]. Concurrently, the machine learning models developed by You Won Lee et al. demonstrated exceptional accuracy in malaria prediction, with Random Forest (RF) and Support Vector Machine (SVM) models surpassing a precision rate of 0.9 [16]. Furthermore, a systematic review on hypertension prediction algorithms identified SVM, Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), and Random Forest (RF) as the optimal algorithms, all exhibiting AUC values above 0.7 [17]. Machine learning have been extensively applied and recognized within the domain of medical research.

As of now, there is no machine learning model for predicting the prognosis of HIV/AIDS patients with cryptococcosis. This study aimed to apply machine learning algorithms for analyzing the clinical characteristics of HIV/AIDS patients with cryptococcosis and to provide personalized risk assessments from a scientific and systematic perspective. This approach is expected to optimize disease management, reduce mortality rates, and provide new perspectives and data support for future research.

Materials and methods

Data source

This study was conducted in the Fourth People’s Hospital of Nanning, a major tertiary hospital in Guangxi that primarily treats patients with infectious diseases. The information of AIDS patients hospitalized from October 2011 to June 2019 was obtained through the hospital’s electronic medical record system. Collect patients’ basic information and terminal examination results before discharge or death, including comorbidities, antiretroviral therapy (ART), biochemical assay outcomes, and routine hematological test result. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) hospitalized cases, (2) HIV antibody test was positive, (3) Cryptococcus was detected in tissue or body fluid samples, and (4) cases with less than 50% missing laboratory test data; exclusion criteria were: (1) cases with more than 50% missing laboratory test data, and (2) duplicate cases. After screening, 396 patients were included in the study, among which there were 345 surviving cases and 51 cases of deceased patients. Based on discharge or death, patients were divided into the survival group (345 cases) and the death group (51 cases). The research process is depicted in Fig. 1.

The flow diagram of machine learning

Data processing and feature extraction

The dataset for this study comprises 75 variables, which include ART, 21 comorbidities (such as Talaromyces marneffei, pneumonia, pulmonary tuberculosis, oral fungal infection, herpes zoster, syphilis, cytomegalovirus, etc.) and 54 laboratory testing indicators (CD4+ T-cell count, aspartate aminotransferase, lactate dehydrogenase, etc.).

For co-infections, feature variables with P < 0.05 were selected using the Pearson chi-square test. Laboratory testing data underwent with P < 0.05 were selected using the Mann-Whitney U Test. Quantitative data included in the study were assessed with a correlation matrix analysis, where a correlation coefficient above 0.8 was considered to indicate a high degree of association. Additionally, the importance of feature data was evaluated using Random forests [18], and less critical features within highly correlated variables were removed. The top twenty variables were selected for model construction.

Constructing the model

In medical research, the collection of clinical data is often partially missing [19, 20]. In this study, missing data were filled using median imputation. The patients were randomly assigned to training and testing sets in accordance with a ratio of 7:3. Ten machine learning classifiers, namely Logistic Regression, K-Nearest Neighbor (KNN), Decision Tree (DT), RF, Adaptive Boosting (Adaboost), eXtreme Gradient Boosting (Xgboost), Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM), Gradient Boosting (Catboost), SVM, and Naive Bayes Model (NBM), were applied to the training set to construct machine learning models with appropriate parameters. Subsequently, internal validation was conducted using the testing set.

Model performance evaluation

Performance of the model was assessed based on sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, AUC, and the Precision-Recall Curve. The final model was selected based on performance metrics, with the best-performing model being chosen.

Feature importance analysis

Identifying features that significantly contribute to disease development is crucial for disease prognosis assessment [21]. In this study, the value of each variable was assessed and ranked based on entropy gain, where a higher value indicates greater influence on the model. Based on the analysis of feature importance in the optimal model, the primary predictive factors for the prognosis of hospitalized HIV/AIDS patients with cryptococcal infection can be determined.

Statistical analysis

This study employed the chi-square test for analyzing categorical variables and utilized the Mann-Whitney U test for non-parametric variables. A two-tailed p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics 23.0 and Anaconda 3 (Python v 5.4.1).

Results

General data of patients

A total of 396 patients were included in the study, defined based on outcomes as deceased (n = 51) or surviving (n = 345).

The general characteristics of the patients are presented in Table S1. Among the 396 patients, 73.7% were male, with the majority of individuals aged between 19 and 60 years (87.6%). The Han ethnic group accounted for 62.4%, and farmers constituted the primary infected population (52.0%), while 61.9% were married. The distribution of marital statuses between the survival and death groups shows a statistically significant difference (P < 0.05), with the smallest number of individuals in the “Other” category found in the survival group and the fewest individuals in the divorced category within the death group.

Mortality of hospitalized HIV/AIDS patients with cryptococcosis

Among the 396 hospitalized patients, the average mortality rate was 12.9% (51/396). The annual mortality rates were as follows: 7.7% (4/52) in 2012, 9.4% (8/85) in 2013, 21.2% (11/52) in 2014, 14.8% (8/54) in 2015, 14.6% (7/48) in 2016, 8.6% (3/35) in 2017, 10.5% (6/57) in 2018, and 30.8% (4/13) in the first half of 2019. The inpatient mortality rate showed an increasing trend from 2012 to 2014, followed by a gradual decrease from 2014 to 2017. However, from 2018 to 2019, the mortality displayed a rising trend again (Fig. 2).

The clinical co-infections, and ART treatment of the patients are shown in Table S2-3. Incorporating patients into the study, the proportion of ART treatment was 28.9% (114/396). The most common complications were tuberculosis (45.2%, 179/396) followed by pneumonia (39.4%, 156/396). Septic shock is the most impactful comorbidity on prognosis, with a case fatality rate as high as 90% (9/10), which is significantly higher than the 11% (42/386) observed in the non-infectious shock group (Fig. 3A, P < 0.05). Additionally, hypoalbuminemia (35%, 6/17) and electrolyte disturbances (31%,23/75) were also noteworthy factors. Screening features

By analyzing the features with significant differences between the two groups (Fig. 3C), 7 pairs of features were highly correlated (R > 0 0.8). After excluding six low-contributing features labeled as CD8, Na, STY, DBIL, EOS, and LYMPH%, the construction of the model involved selecting the top 20 features based on their contribution values (Fig. 3D), which collectively accounted for approximately 93.8% of the total importance weight. Among these, MONO%, CD4, electrolyte imbalance, and BUN/CREA were identified as several of the most critical features in model construction.

Performance of machine learning models

The cases included in the study were divided into training and testing sets in a 7:3 ratio. Based on 20 included features, ten machine learning models were constructed, including Logistic, KNN, DT, RF, Adaboost, XGBoost, LightGBM, Catboost, SVM, and NBM.

At a threshold of 0.24, the AUC, Sensitivity, and Specificity for the testing set are 0.9212, 0.8750, and 0.8108, respectively, and for the training set are 0.9787, 0.9535, and 0.8889(Table 1). Although the area under the ROC curve of the RF model training set (area = 0.9787) is slightly lower than that of Xgboost (area = 0.9951) and Catboost (area = 0.9930), its testing set (area = 0.9212) indicates the most optimal diagnostic value among the ten models (Fig. 4A-B). Overall, the RF model had the best predictive ability. Additionally, the KNN model shows clear overfitting as indicated by the training set’s AUC, Sensitivity, and Specificity, all being 1.0000.

Explanatory evaluation of the model

In order to intuitively analyze and assess the practical predictive significance of clinical features within the prognostic models, we utilized SHAP values to depict the influence of features on the outcomes, reflecting both the importance of each feature and whether the feature has a positive or negative effect on the samples (Fig. 5). Clearly, it is evident that BUN/CREA is the most important feature, followed by electrolyte disorder, and subsequently NEUT%, Urea, and IBIL, all positively associated with mortality. Furthermore, Anemia and increased levels of LDH, NEUT, and DBIL are associated with an increased risk of mortality. On the other hand, CD4/CD8, CD4, MONO, MONO%, EOS%, UA, CL, CD3, RDW-SD, and BASO% indicate that as the levels of these features decrease, the risk of mortality increases.

The capabilities of the models and the impact of various indicators were verified through the analysis of misclassified cases. Figure 4E-F and G-H depict the confusion matrices for both the RF and SVM models, respectively. In the RF model, out of 277 patients in the training set, 208 were correctly predicted as survivors, and 41patients were correctly predicted to die, with a total of 28 false negatives and false positives (ACC = 89.89%); among the 119 patients in the testing set, 97 were correctly classified, while 22 were misclassified (ACC = 81.51%). For the SVM model, 263 cases were correctly classified in the training set, with 14 cases incorrectly predicted (ACC = 94.95%); in the testing set, 98 patients were correctly predicted, while 21 were misclassified (ACC = 82.35%) (Fig. 6A). Among the misclassified cases in both the training and testing sets for both models, the incidence of electrolyte disorders in patients who were actually alive but were misclassified as deceased is significantly higher than those correctly identified as survivors. Conversely, the actual mortality cases that were predicted to survive have a lower incidence of electrolyte disorders compared to those correctly identified as deceased (Fig. 6B, P < 0.05).

Discussions

Cryptococcosis is an invasive fungal infection that commonly occurs in immunocompromised individuals and is considered an opportunistic infection associated with HIV/AIDS. Research indicates that the incidence and mortality rates of cryptococcal disease have generally decreased. However, in regions such as Europe and North America, particularly among individuals with compromised immune functions, both the incidence and mortality rates are still increasing [5]. Cryptococcosis has an insidious onset, is prone to misdiagnosis and underdiagnosis, and can even present asymptomatically [22], potentially contributing to fluctuations in both the incidence and mortality rates. This study indicates that among hospitalized AIDS patients, there is an annual fluctuating trend in cryptococcal infections, with a downward trend observed in the last two years. However, from 2017 to the first half of 2019, the mortality rate increased to 30.8%. AIDS/HIV combined with cryptococcosis is a great threat to human health. In this study, we included data from 396 HIV/AIDS patients with Cryptococcus infection to develop and test a machine learning-based model to aid in patient treatment.

In recent years, artificial intelligence has been gradually applied to the field of medicine. Machine learning has been used to construct models for AIDS combined with other diseases [23] as well as models for cryptococcosis combined with other diseases [24], to predict disease prognosis. Prognostic studies for HIV/AIDS-associated cryptococcal infections are relatively scarce, with a historical reliance on traditional statistical methods for analysis [25, 26].In 1996, Darras-Joly conducted a study using the Cox regression model and found that age, cerebrospinal fluid glucose levels, previous admission to an intensive care unit, and mechanical ventilation affected the prognosis of patients with HIV-associated cryptococcal meningitis [27]. Previous research has found that a Th1 cytokine profile may potentially serve as one of the prognostic markers for cryptococcal meningitis [25]. However, to date, no studies have been identified regarding the construction of machine learning models to predict the prognosis of hospitalized HIV/AIDS patients with cryptococcosis. Traditional statistics and machine learning can both analyze the correlation between clinical characteristics and prognosis. Compared to traditional statistical methods, this study employs a variety of machine learning algorithms, incorporates a vast array of clinical features to construct a more comprehensive prognostic prediction model, and is capable of ranking clinical features based on their importance. Utilizing existing data, employing modern and mature machine learning models for prognostic analysis of patients is an economical, fast, and standardized medical approach.

Ten machine learning-based models were constructed, utilizing clinical data obtained from the Fourth People’s Hospital of Nanning, and the performance of each model was tested and compared to predict the death risk of HIV/AIDS patients with cryptococcus during hospitalization. In this study, a statistically significant difference in the distribution of marital status was observed between the survival and death groups, which presents a challenge to interpret. This statistical discrepancy may be attributed to incomplete records of marital status for patients categorized under “Other,” suggesting that there might not be a significant difference in the actual distribution of marital status between the two groups. Nonetheless, marital status was not included as a variable in the model construction, thus its impact on the research findings is considered negligible. RF and SVM showed the best ability to judge the death risk of patients among the ten models. Previous studies have demonstrated that the RF model and SVM model also show excellent judgment performance in the research of chronic inflammation, cancer and parasitic infection, and can screen the risk factors and diagnostic markers of diseases [28–31]. However, in this study, patients with concurrent electrolyte disorder are prone to misdiagnosis when utilizing the RF model for prediction. Furthermore, patients with concurrent electrolyte disorder or elevated BUN/CREA levels are susceptible to misdiagnosis when utilizing the SVM model for prediction. In the clinical application of machine learning models for predicting patient prognoses, conducting a detailed assessment of patients presenting with concurrent electrolyte disorders or elevated BUN/CREA levels is of paramount importance, as is the formulation of optimized therapeutic strategies. Future research endeavors should focus on further refining these models to more precisely reflect patient outcomes, thereby reducing the prevalence of false-positive results.

Amphotericin is commonly used in the treatment of HIV/AIDS patients with cryptococcosis. Amphotericin has obvious toxic effects on the liver and kidney of patients, with particular impact on kidney function [32, 33]. Additionally, patients with Cryptococcus often have hepatosplenomegaly. In an iterative manner, SHAP values for each feature in the model were computed considering to the clinical characteristics and outcomes of each patient in the dataset. The results showed that BUN/CREA value was significantly increased in the death patients, which was the most positive effect on the death of patients. In the prognostic model constructed in this study, the BUN/CREA ratio contributed most significantly to patient prognosis. Research indicates that an elevated BUN/CREA ratio typically signifies renal impairment and is associated with an increased mortality rate in patients with heart disease [34]. Concurrently, an increase in UREA levels in the death group compared to the survival group, along with a decrease in UA levels compared to the survival group, suggests the presence of renal impairment. These evidences support a positive correlation between renal impairment and adverse outcomes in prognosis. Furthermore, the levels of IBIL and DBIL are elevated in the death group compared to the survival group, which often suggests the presence of liver function impairment and positively influences the model. Previous studies have indicated that in patients with heart failure, those with increased IBIL and DBIL levels also have an elevated risk of mortality [35]. Additionally, LDH is primarily present in the liver and kidneys, and its release increases during tissue damage. Data analysis in this study also revealed that LDH levels were higher in the death group compared to the surviving group. Apparently, hepatic and renal impairment significantly impacts the prognosis of HIV/AIDS patients with Cryptococcus infection.

This study found that electrolyte disturbance was also an important predictor of prognosis in HIV/AIDS patients with cryptococcosis. Studies have shown that Cryptococcus has a strong predilection for the central nervous system. When the patient’s immunity is low, Cryptococcus can proliferate and disseminate to other organs via the bloodstream, leading to cryptococcal meningitis, characterized by increased intracranial pressure, vomiting, fever, which can easily result in electrolyte disturbances. Even HIV-negative patients with cryptococcosis can experience refractory electrolyte disturbances [36]. Additionally, the therapeutic drug amphotericin B affects potassium ion metabolism due to its nephrotoxicity, necessitating the use of dehydrating diuretics. The use of amphotericin B for treatment can easily lead to hypokalemia and may also result in hyponatremia and hypochloremia, constituting electrolyte disturbances [32]. Severe electrolyte disturbances can also be accompanied by acid-base imbalances, leading to metabolic acidosis or alkalosis, potentially resulting in fatality. The appropriately preemptive electrolyte supplementation can mitigate the impact of medications on patients and improve survival rates [37, 38]. Similarly, anemia is also one of the prognostic risk factors in this study. The HIV/AIDS patients with cryptococcal meningitis commonly experience anemia, which may lead to cerebral hypoxia and subsequent mortality [39].

We also note that the contribution of NEUT% in the model ranks third, and both elevated NEUT% and NEUT levels are identified as factors contributing to patient mortality. Fungal invasion of the body stimulates the human immune system, often leading to an increase in neutrophils. Depletion of neutrophils during Cryptococcus tracheal infection has been shown to increase mouse survival rates [40], possibly due to the damaging effects of neutrophil recruitment on tissues. In a study involving Candida (another type of fungus), neutrophil count was found to be a predictive factor for mortality [41].

Similarly, upon HIV virus invasion, it primarily targets CD4 cells, which are a crucial type of cell within the immune system. As the number of CD4 cells decreases, the CD4/CD8 ratio correspondingly diminishes, reflecting a weakening of immune system function. The CD4 and CD8 are the primary subset for killing Cryptococcus neoformans in peripheral blood. But in HIV patients, their activation becomes dysregulated, leading to impaired fungicidal activity [42]. A study in Anambra State, Nigeria, revealed that HIV-positive patients infected with Cryptococcus had CD4 counts lower than 200 cells/µL [43]. Healthy males show lower percentages of CD3, CD4, and CD8 when infected with Cryptococcus [44]. In this study, the death group exhibited lower levels of CD4, CD4/CD8, and CD3 compared to the survival group. Among these, CD4 showed the most significant decrease, with a median of 16.50 in the death group, significantly lower than the survival group (48.00). The decrease in immune cells such as CD3, CD4, and CD8 makes patients more susceptible to fungal, bacterial, and viral infections, leading to escalating damage.

Eosinophils, as immune cells, also participate in the development of HIV-associated cryptococcosis. Compared to the death group, the percentage of eosinophils is significantly higher in the survival group. Previous studies have shown that eosinophils, as antigen-presenting cells for fungal antigens, can initiate and modulate Cryptococcus-specific immune responses, thereby playing a protective role [45].

Notably, a decrease in the levels of MONO, EOS%, and RDW-SD is associated with an increased risk of patient mortality. However, no previous studies have reported such associations. Furthermore, the model constructed using data from this investigation indicates that the receipt of ART treatment does not significantly influence the clinical outcomes of hospitalized patients. Nevertheless, within the cohort of hospitalized individuals undergoing ART,87.5% of those who had fatal outcomes exhibited CD4 + T cell counts below 200 cells/µL.

The study has certain limitations in terms of sample size and data source diversity, which may lead to biased outcomes and restrict the external validation of the models. Sample bias in the construction of a machine learning model for predicting the prognosis of cryptococcosis may lead to the model being overly optimistic in its predictions for the survival group and inaccurate for the mortality group, thereby affecting the model’s generalizability and the accuracy of clinical decision-making. Consequently, future research endeavors should focus on expanding the sample size, diversifying the data sources, and implementing external validation procedures to enhance the generalizability and precision of the study’s findings. Furthermore, the study exhibits limitations in terms of model diversity, having examined only a limited number of models. Therefore, it is imperative for future studies to explore a broader spectrum of clinical features and employ a more diverse array of machine learning algorithms to construct more comprehensive and accurate models. This will contribute to improving the predictive accuracy of cryptococcal disease prognosis and provide a more robust scientific foundation for clinical decision-making.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the RF and SVM models contribute to the prognostic assessment of inpatient HIV/AIDS patients with cryptococcosis, holding potential value for application. These models aid in formulating individualized and more rational treatment plans, thereby effectively reducing mortality rates.
