Efficacy and safety of fast-track surgical management for acute orthopedic fractures: a retrospective cohort analysis
Authors: Lingyun Wang, Ludan Ding, Lishen Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcute orthopedic fractures pose significant treatment challenges, requiring efficient and effective management to improve patient outcomes. This study compares the efficiency and prognosis of fast-track surgical management versus conventional early definitive treatment in managing acute orthopedic fractures.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included 116 patients with acute orthopedic fractures treated between June 2024 and June 2025. Patients were assigned to either a fast-track surgical management group (fast-track group, n = 60) or a conventional early definitive treatment group (control group, n = 56). The primary outcome was in-hospital mortality. Secondary outcomes included operation time, intraoperative blood loss, transfusion volume, body temperature recovery time, time to first ambulation, length of hospital stay, hospitalization cost, postoperative pain assessed using the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) at 24, 48, and 72 h, fracture healing quality, postoperative complications, and 6-month health-related quality of life evaluated using the 36-Item Short Form Health Survey (SF-36). Logistic regression analyses were performed to identify factors associated with in-hospital mortality.ResultsIn-hospital mortality was significantly lower in the fast-track group compared with the control group (5.00% vs. 16.07%, P = 0.044). The fast-track group demonstrated significantly shorter operation time, reduced intraoperative blood loss and transfusion requirements, earlier postoperative ambulation, shorter hospitalization duration, and lower total medical costs (all P < 0.05). VAS scores at 24, 48, and 72 h were consistently lower in the fast-track group (all P < 0.05). The overall complication rate was significantly reduced (5.26% vs. 19.15%, P = 0.027). The good healing rate was higher in the fast-track group (P = 0.013). At 6 months, several SF-36 domains, including physical functioning, role physical, vitality, social functioning, and general health, were significantly higher in the fast-track group (all P < 0.05). Multivariable logistic regression analysis demonstrated that fast-track group remained significantly associated with lower in-hospital mortality (OR = 0.352, 95% CI: 0.118–0.947, P = 0.041), whereas higher Injury Severity Score was an independent risk factor (OR = 1.196, 95% CI: 1.031–1.387, P = 0.018).ConclusionFast-track surgical management was associated with lower in-hospital mortality, enhanced perioperative efficiency, reduced complications, and improved functional recovery compared with conventional early definitive treatment in patients with acute orthopedic fractures.

Background

Orthopedic fractures represent a substantial global health burden and remain a leading cause of trauma-related hospitalization and long-term disability [1]. The incidence of fractures has steadily increased over recent decades, particularly among older adults due to population aging, osteoporosis, and increased life expectancy [2]. Fracture-related morbidity is not limited to the skeletal injury itself but is also associated with systemic inflammatory responses, immobilization-related complications, prolonged hospitalization, and increased healthcare expenditure [3, 4].

Early surgical stabilization is widely recognized as a cornerstone of modern fracture management, as it restores mechanical stability and enables early mobilization, thereby reducing secondary complications [5]. Conventional early definitive treatment has been widely implemented in clinical practice, emphasizing comprehensive preoperative evaluation, correction of metabolic disturbances, optimization of cardiopulmonary function, and careful surgical planning prior to fixation [5]. Such preparation is particularly important in elderly patients or those with multiple comorbidities, where perioperative risk may be high [6, 7]. Although thorough optimization may improve intraoperative safety, this strategy often involves a delay between admission and surgery [8, 9]. During this waiting period, prolonged immobilization may increase the risk of deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary infection, pressure ulcers, and muscle deconditioning [10, 11]. Delayed fixation has also been linked to persistent pain, longer hospitalization, and slower functional recovery in some patients [12].

In response to these concerns, fast-track surgical management has gained increasing attention in recent years. Several randomized controlled trials and meta-analyses have suggested that fast-track surgical management may reduce immobilization-related complications, shorten hospitalization, and facilitate postoperative recovery in selected trauma populations [13–16]. This strategy advocates for prompt surgical management soon after hospital admission, aiming to reduce the duration of immobilization and mitigate secondary complications [17, 18]. Advances in perioperative anesthesia, rapid laboratory assessment, standardized trauma protocols, and multidisciplinary team collaboration have enhanced the feasibility and safety of fast-track surgical management [19]. In many trauma centers, structured multidisciplinary care pathways integrated with enhanced recovery programs now support the intervention for selected fracture patients [20–22]. The pathway may attenuate systemic inflammatory response, decrease soft tissue swelling, and facilitate earlier ambulation, which is particularly beneficial for elderly or high-risk individuals [21, 22].

Despite the theoretical advantages of both approaches, the optimal management pathway remains controversial [23]. While some clinical observations suggest that fast-track surgical management may shorten hospitalization and reduce complication rates, concerns persist regarding inadequate preoperative optimization and potential increases in perioperative risk [22]. Conversely, although conventional early definitive treatment allows for more thorough preparation, prolonged preoperative waiting may expose patients to immobilization-related morbidity [24]. Importantly, existing studies often focus on specific fracture types or limited patient subgroups, and direct comparative data between fast-track surgical management and conventional early definitive treatment in routine clinical practice remain insufficient. Variability in institutional protocols further contributes to the lack of consensus.

Given these uncertainties, the present retrospective cohort study aimed to compare perioperative outcomes, postoperative complications, fracture healing outcomes, health-related quality of life, and hospitalization parameters between fast-track surgical management and conventional early definitive treatment in patients with orthopedic fractures. By providing real-world evidence from a structured clinical cohort, this study seeks to inform surgical timing decisions and contribute to the optimization of fracture management strategies.

Methods

Study design and patients

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at the Department of Critical Care Medicine, Yinzhou District Second Hospital. Clinical data of patients admitted with traumatic orthopedic fractures between June 2024 and June 2025 were reviewed. A total of 116 eligible patients were retrospectively divided into two groups according to the perioperative management pathway implemented after admission: the fast-track surgical management group (fast-track group; n = 60) and the conventional early definitive treatment group (control group; n = 56). Treatment allocation was determined by multidisciplinary trauma team assessment according to the institutional perioperative management pathway at the time of admission. Factors considered during pathway selection included hemodynamic status, fracture characteristics, anesthetic evaluation, perioperative risk profile, and the anticipated need for preoperative systemic optimization. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Yinzhou District Second Hospital (Approval No. IIT2026SL0062). Due to the retrospective design and anonymized data analysis, the requirement for written informed consent was waived.

Inclusion criteria: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) radiologically confirmed traumatic orthopedic fracture; (3) Injury Severity Score (ISS) ≥ 16; (4) definitive surgical fixation performed during hospitalization; and (5) complete perioperative and follow-up data available.

Exclusion criteria: (1) pathological fractures secondary to malignancy; (2) severe traumatic brain injury requiring priority neurosurgical intervention; (3) advanced hepatic, renal, or cardiac failure contraindicating surgery; (4) staged damage-control procedures without definitive fixation during the index admission; and (5) incomplete medical records.

Treatment protocols

Patients in the fast-track group were managed under an expedited trauma pathway immediately after admission. Upon arrival, rapid triage and primary assessment were performed according to standardized trauma care principles, including evaluation of airway, breathing, and circulation. Hemodynamic stability was assessed through continuous monitoring of heart rate, blood pressure, respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, and urine output. Simultaneously, laboratory investigations—including complete blood count, coagulation profile, serum electrolytes, renal and liver function tests, and arterial blood gas analysis—were obtained under an accelerated protocol. Electrocardiography and definitive imaging examinations, such as radiography or computed tomography (CT), were prioritized to facilitate early surgical planning. Once hemodynamic stability was confirmed and no absolute contraindications to anesthesia or operative intervention were identified, definitive fracture fixation was scheduled as soon as feasible. The decision to proceed with surgery was made collaboratively by the orthopedic surgeon, anesthesiologist, and critical care team. Surgical procedures were selected based on fracture type, anatomical location, and soft tissue condition, and were performed according to established orthopedic operative principles, including internal fixation, plate fixation, or intramedullary nailing as appropriate. Intraoperative management included continuous electrocardiography, non-invasive or invasive blood pressure monitoring when indicated, pulse oximetry, capnography, and urine output measurement. Intraoperative blood loss was carefully recorded, and transfusion was administered based on institutional transfusion thresholds and patient hemodynamic status. Prophylactic intravenous antibiotics were administered within 30 min prior to skin incision in accordance with surgical infection prevention guidelines. Postoperative pharmacologic thromboprophylaxis was initiated unless contraindicated. A standardized multimodal analgesia protocol was implemented after surgery, and early supervised mobilization was encouraged once the patient’s clinical condition permitted.

Patients in the control group were managed according to current standard clinical practice guidelines for traumatic fracture care. Following admission, patients underwent comprehensive systemic evaluation and medical optimization prior to definitive surgical intervention. This included correction of electrolyte disturbances, stabilization of cardiovascular and respiratory function, management of anemia or coagulation abnormalities, and optimization of preexisting comorbid conditions such as hypertension, diabetes, or chronic pulmonary disease. Multidisciplinary consultation was obtained when clinically indicated. Surgical intervention was scheduled after completion of guideline-recommended preoperative optimization rather than through an expedited pathway. Importantly, this approach reflects routine standard-of-care practice and was not intended to delay treatment unnecessarily. Timing of surgery was determined based on patient stability, clinical prioritization, and operating room availability. To ensure comparability between groups, operative techniques, anesthesia management strategies, intraoperative monitoring standards, antibiotic prophylaxis protocols, thromboprophylaxis measures, and postoperative rehabilitation procedures were consistent across both treatment pathways.

Data collections

Baseline demographic characteristics, injury-related variables, and perioperative risk profiles were collected from the hospital electronic medical record system. The recorded variables included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), comorbidities, anticoagulant use, ISS, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) classification, fracture type, presence of multiple fractures, primary fracture location, shock status on admission, admission lactate level, severe soft-tissue injury, preoperative hemoglobin concentration, preoperative platelet count, mechanism of injury, and time from admission to surgery. BMI was calculated as body weight in kilograms divided by the square of height in meters (kg/m²). The severity of trauma was assessed using the ISS, which ranges from 1 to 75, with higher ISS indicating greater injury severity. All included patients had an ISS ≥ 16, consistent with moderate to severe trauma [25]. ASA classification was recorded according to standard preoperative anesthetic risk assessment criteria and categorized as grade I–II or III–IV [26]. Fracture type was classified as open or closed according to clinical examination and radiographic findings. Multiple fractures were defined as fractures involving two or more distinct anatomical regions identified on imaging studies. Primary fracture location was defined according to the principal fracture requiring definitive fixation during the index admission and categorized as long bone fractures, pelvic fractures, periarticular fractures, or other fractures. Fractures confined to the same anatomical region (e.g., tibia and fibula of the same limb) were classified as a single-region fracture. Shock on admission was defined as systolic blood pressure < 90 mmHg or the requirement for vasopressor support during initial emergency resuscitation. Admission lactate level was measured from serum biochemical testing performed after admission. Severe soft-tissue injury referred to clinically significant soft-tissue damage requiring additional wound management, debridement, or staged soft-tissue treatment. Preoperative hemoglobin concentration was measured in grams per liter (g/L) prior to surgery and categorized as < 110 g/L or ≥ 110 g/L to reflect the presence or absence of anemia according to commonly accepted clinical thresholds. Preoperative platelet count was measured in ×10⁹/L and categorized as < 100 × 10⁹/L or ≥ 100 × 10⁹/L, with values below 100 × 10⁹/L considered indicative of thrombocytopenia. Mechanism of injury was classified into four categories based on patient history and emergency records: traffic accident, fall from height, violence-related injury, and other causes. Time from admission to surgery was defined as the interval in hours between hospital admission and initiation of definitive surgical fixation. It was determined according to multidisciplinary clinical assessment, perioperative readiness, hemodynamic stability, and institutional operative coordination capacity. No predefined mandatory time cutoff was used for pathway allocation.

Outcomes measurements

The primary outcome of this study was in-hospital mortality, defined as death from any cause occurring after surgical intervention and before hospital discharge during the index admission.

Secondary outcomes included postoperative complications, perioperative indicators, treatment efficiency measures, fracture healing status, and 6-month health-related quality of life assessed using the 36-Item Short Form Health Survey (SF-36).

Postoperative complications were defined as clinically significant adverse events occurring after surgery and prior to discharge. Major complications included acute respiratory distress syndrome, multiple organ failure, deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, surgical site infection, and reoperation due to bleeding. Perioperative recovery indicators included operative duration, intraoperative blood loss, intraoperative blood transfusion volume, time to recovery of normal body temperature, time to first postoperative ambulation, and postoperative pain intensity. Operative duration was defined as the time from skin incision to wound closure and recorded in hours. Blood loss and transfusion volumes were documented in milliliters according to standardized operating room protocols. Time to recovery of normal body temperature was defined as the interval in hours from surgery completion to the first core temperature between 36.0 °C and 37.3 °C maintained for at least 12 consecutive hours without antipyretic use. Time to first ambulation was defined as the number of days from surgery to the first supervised out-of-bed mobilization. Postoperative pain was assessed using the Visual Analog Scale (VAS; range 0–10), a validated tool in clinical pain research [27], with scores recorded at 24, 48, and 72 h after surgery.

Treatment efficiency parameters included operative duration and length of hospital stay. Total hospitalization cost, expressed in Chinese Yuan (CNY), was calculated as the sum of all direct medical expenses incurred during the index admission, including surgical fees, anesthesia, medications, laboratory testing, imaging examinations, and inpatient care. Fracture healing was evaluated at follow-up using both radiographic and clinical criteria. Radiographic union was defined as bridging callus formation across at least three cortices on orthogonal radiographs. Clinical union was defined as absence of pain at the fracture site during weight-bearing and absence of local tenderness on examination. Healing outcomes were categorized as excellent, good, acceptable, or poor according to predefined radiographic and clinical assessment criteria based on fracture union status, pain during weight-bearing, local tenderness, and functional recovery [28, 29]. Excellent healing was defined as complete radiographic union with restoration of normal function and absence of pain or tenderness. Good healing indicated satisfactory fracture union with only mild residual symptoms or minimal functional limitation. Acceptable healing referred to delayed radiographic remodeling or incomplete functional recovery without evidence of nonunion or fixation failure. Poor healing was defined as nonunion, fixation failure, persistent pain, or substantial functional impairment requiring additional intervention. The good healing rate was defined as the proportion of patients categorized as excellent or good. Radiographic assessments were independently performed by two experienced orthopedic surgeons according to predefined evaluation criteria. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion and adjudication by a third investigator.

All patients who survived to hospital discharge were followed for 6 months postoperatively. The SF-36 questionnaire, a validated and widely used instrument, was used to assess health-related quality of life across eight domains [30]: physical functioning, bodily pain, role physical, role emotional, social functioning, mental health, vitality, and general health. Each domain score ranges from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating better perceived health status. SF-36 assessments were completed by trained nursing staff according to standardized evaluation procedures.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were first assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using the independent-samples t-test. Non-normally distributed variables were expressed as median (interquartile range) and analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages and compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. SF-36 analyses were performed among patients who survived to hospital discharge, and missing 6-month SF-36 follow-up data were handled using multiple imputation to reduce potential bias associated with incomplete follow-up. Variables with < 10–20% missing values were handled using multiple imputation, whereas cases with > 20% missing data were excluded from the analysis. Univariate logistic regression analysis was performed to evaluate the association between baseline variables and in-hospital mortality. Variables with P < 0.05 in univariate analysis were considered candidate variables for multivariable logistic regression analysis to identify independent predictors of in-hospital mortality. To reduce the risk of model overfitting, the number of variables included in the multivariable model was intentionally restricted according to the events-per-variable (EPV) principle, which recommends approximately 10 outcome events per predictor variable [31]. Because only 12 in-hospital mortality events occurred in the study cohort, the number of covariates included in the final multivariable model was deliberately limited. Candidate predictors were further evaluated for potential multicollinearity using the variance inflation factor (VIF), with VIF > 5 considered indicative of substantial collinearity. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for logistic regression analyses. Risk ratios (RRs) with 95% CIs were additionally reported for the crude comparison of in-hospital mortality between groups. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Perioperative clinical outcomes

Perioperative recovery outcomes were analyzed among patients who survived to hospital discharge, including 57 patients in the fast-track group and 47 patients in the control group. Significant differences were observed in multiple perioperative parameters (Table 3). Operation time was significantly shorter in the fast-track group (P < 0.001). Intraoperative blood transfusion volume and blood loss were also significantly lower in the fast-track group (P < 0.001 for both). Additionally, body temperature recovery time and time to first postoperative ambulation were markedly reduced in the fast-track group (P < 0.001 for both). Postoperative pain scores assessed by VAS were significantly lower in the fast-track group at 24 h (P = 0.014), 48 h (P < 0.001), and 72 h (P < 0.001). Furthermore, the fast-track group demonstrated shorter length of hospital stay (P < 0.001) and lower hospitalization cost (P < 0.001).

Fracture healing outcomes

As presented in Table 4, no statistically significant differences were observed in the distribution of individual fracture healing grades (excellent, good, acceptable, poor) between the two groups (all P > 0.05). However, when healing outcomes were dichotomized, the good healing rate (excellent or good) was significantly higher in the fast-track group compared with the control group (89.47% vs. 70.21%, χ² = 6.152, P = 0.013). Inter-rater reliability of radiographic fracture healing assessment was high (Cohen’s κ = 0.82), indicating excellent agreement between the two independent evaluators.

Health-related quality of life at 6 months

At 6-month follow-up, significant improvements were observed in several SF-36 domains in the fast-track group (Table 5). Specifically, scores for physical functioning (P = 0.002), role physical (P < 0.001), social functioning (P < 0.001), vitality (P < 0.001), and general health (P < 0.001) were significantly higher compared with the control group. No significant differences were observed in bodily pain (P = 0.656), role emotional (P = 0.199), or mental health (P = 0.175).

Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses for in-hospital mortality

Univariate logistic regression analysis was performed to identify potential factors associated with in-hospital mortality (Table 7). Cardiovascular disease (OR = 3.833, 95% CI: 1.029–14.279, P = 0.041), higher ISS (OR = 1.248, 95% CI: 1.083–1.438, P = 0.002), ASA classification III–IV (OR = 8.067, 95% CI: 1.001–64.997, P = 0.026), shock on admission (OR = 3.425, 95% CI: 1.001–11.722, P = 0.041), elevated admission lactate level (OR = 2.287, 95% CI: 1.404–3.726, P < 0.001), and longer time from admission to surgery (OR = 1.312, 95% CI: 1.129–1.525, P < 0.001) were significantly associated with increased risk of in-hospital mortality. In contrast, fast-track surgical management was associated with a lower risk of in-hospital mortality (OR = 0.318, 95% CI: 0.102–0.992, P = 0.048). Variables with P < 0.05 in univariate analysis were considered candidate variables for multivariable logistic regression analysis. Because only 12 in-hospital mortality events occurred in the study cohort, the number of variables included in the final multivariable model was intentionally restricted according to the EPV principle to reduce the risk of model overfitting. Considering the potential clinical collinearity among ISS, ASA classification, shock status, admission lactate level, and time from admission to surgery, ISS was selected as the representative indicator of overall injury severity for multivariable adjustment. As shown in Table 8, multivariable logistic regression analysis demonstrated that fast-track surgical management remained independently associated with reduced in-hospital mortality after adjustment for ISS (OR = 0.352, 95% CI: 0.118–0.947, P = 0.041). In addition, higher ISS remained an independent risk factor for in-hospital mortality (OR = 1.196, 95% CI: 1.031–1.387, P = 0.018).

Discussion

Acute orthopedic fractures in patients with moderate-to-severe trauma represent a critical challenge in trauma care, where the timing of definitive surgical fixation may substantially influence early survival and long-term recovery [5, 6]. Delayed stabilization can prolong systemic inflammatory activation and physiological instability, whereas fast-track surgical management may interrupt the progression toward organ dysfunction [14, 32]. In the present study, fast-track surgical management was associated with lower in-hospital mortality, lower overall complication rates, improved perioperative efficiency, better fracture healing quality, and improved 6-month functional outcomes. Multivariable logistic regression analysis further demonstrated that fast-track surgical management remained significantly associated with lower in-hospital mortality after adjustment for ISS. In contrast, higher ISS remained independently associated with increased in-hospital mortality.

The reduction in in-hospital mortality observed in the fast-track group may be associated with the significantly shorter time from admission to surgery achieved under the fast-track perioperative management pathway. Such earlier definitive stabilization may reduce the duration of fracture instability, persistent tissue injury, sympathetic stress activation, and inflammatory amplification during the acute post-trauma phase [16, 32]. Severe fractures and soft tissue injury trigger a systemic inflammatory response syndrome, characterized by cytokine release, endothelial activation, coagulation imbalance, and microcirculatory dysfunction [33, 34]. Prolonged mechanical instability of major fractures may act as a persistent inflammatory stimulus, sustaining immune dysregulation and increasing the risk of multi-organ dysfunction syndrome [35]. Early fracture stabilization may reduce ongoing tissue damage, limit secondary bleeding, and decrease the release of damage-associated molecular patterns [36, 37]. By restoring mechanical integrity and reducing nociceptive and inflammatory signaling, fast-track surgical intervention may blunt the “second hit” phenomenon described in trauma physiology, in which additional stressors exacerbate systemic immune imbalance [38]. Previous trauma research suggests that early definitive fixation, when performed under hemodynamically optimized conditions, may reduce pulmonary complications and organ failure [24, 29, 39]. Our findings are consistent with previous reports and support a potential association between fast-track surgical management and improved short-term survival [14–16].

Furthermore, fast-track surgical management may improve microcirculatory perfusion and reduce prolonged hypoperfusion-related metabolic acidosis [40]. Stabilization of long bone fractures has been shown to reduce intramedullary embolic load and inflammatory mediator release, potentially lowering the risk of respiratory deterioration and systemic complications [41]. The lower complication rate in the fast-track group is consistent with these proposed physiological interpretations. The significantly shorter operative time, reduced blood loss, and lower transfusion requirements may reflect reduced soft tissue edema with earlier intervention. Delayed surgery allows for progressive soft tissue swelling, inflammatory exudation, and hematoma organization, which can complicate surgical exposure and prolong operative manipulation [42]. From a pathophysiological perspective, prolonged fracture instability increases microvascular permeability and leukocyte infiltration, contributing to interstitial edema. Fast-track surgical management may help limit this cascade, leading to a technically less complex procedure [16, 42]. Reduced transfusion requirements are clinically meaningful, as blood transfusion itself has been associated with immunomodulatory effects and increased infection risk in trauma patients [43]. Shortened body temperature recovery time in the fast-track group may also reflect more efficient intraoperative management and reduced metabolic stress. Hypothermia, coagulopathy, and acidosis form a well-known triad in trauma physiology [44, 45]. Fast-track surgical management may be associated with limitation of the progression of these interrelated disturbances.

Although individual healing grades were not statistically different, the significantly higher good healing rate in the fast-track group may reflect more favorable conditions for bone repair. Fracture healing is governed by mechanical stability, preserved vascular supply, and controlled inflammatory signaling [46]. Persistent instability can prolong the inflammatory phase of healing, disrupt angiogenesis, and impair callus formation [47]. Early definitive fixation may help restore biomechanical alignment and reduce repeated microtrauma at the fracture site [48]. Experimental studies have shown that stable fixation decreases excessive inflammatory cytokine expression and promotes transition from inflammatory to reparative phases of healing [49]. Clinical studies of early fixation in long bone fractures have similarly reported trends toward improved union rates and reduced delayed union [50]. The findings of the present study align with this biological framework.

The improvement in physical functioning, role physical, vitality, and social functioning at 6 months suggests potential benefits of fast-track surgical management in rehabilitation outcomes. Early ambulation may reduce muscle atrophy, preserve cardiopulmonary capacity, and enhance neuromuscular coordination [29]. Immobilization-related complications, including venous thromboembolism and pulmonary dysfunction, may significantly delay rehabilitation. Prior longitudinal trauma studies have demonstrated that early mobilization is strongly associated with improved long-term independence and quality of life [51, 52]. The present findings support the hypothesis that perioperative efficiency translates into sustained functional gains. The lack of difference in mental health, role emotional, and bodily pain domains suggests that psychological adaptation, emotional recovery, and chronic pain perception may be multifactorial, involving psychosocial support, pre-injury health status, and rehabilitation quality beyond surgical timing alone [38, 53].

Furthermore, multivariable logistic regression analysis demonstrated that the association between fast-track surgical management and lower in-hospital mortality remained significant after adjustment for ISS. In contrast, higher ISS independently increased the risk of in-hospital mortality, which is consistent with previous trauma studies showing that greater systemic injury burden is strongly associated with physiological deterioration, organ dysfunction, and poor survival outcomes [54]. These findings suggest that both trauma severity and perioperative management strategy may influence early prognosis in patients with acute orthopedic fractures [14, 54].

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design may have introduced selection bias and confounding by indication, as treatment allocation was based on clinical judgment and institutional treatment pathways rather than random assignment. Although baseline characteristics were generally comparable between groups, only 12 mortality events occurred in the study cohort. Therefore, to reduce the risk of overfitting according to the EPV principle, the number of variables included in the final multivariable model was intentionally restricted. As a result, some clinically relevant factors could not be simultaneously adjusted for. In addition, the relatively small number of mortality events resulted in relatively wide CI for some effect estimates, and the observed associations should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Second, the single-center setting limits generalizability. Trauma system organization, perioperative coordination efficiency, surgical prioritization strategies, resource availability, and postoperative rehabilitation protocols may differ substantially across institutions and healthcare settings. Therefore, the findings may not be directly applicable to centers with different trauma management infrastructures or perioperative care pathways. Third, the fracture cohort included a heterogeneous spectrum of traumatic orthopedic injuries. Because detailed stratified analyses according to fracture subtype and injury severity were limited by subgroup sample size, residual heterogeneity within the study population cannot be completely excluded. Fourth, follow-up was limited to 6 months. Longer-term orthopedic and functional outcomes, including nonunion, post-traumatic arthritis, chronic functional decline, and implant-related complications, were not evaluated. Fifth, patients who died during hospitalization could not complete postoperative recovery evaluation. Therefore, potential survivor selection bias in perioperative recovery comparisons cannot be completely excluded. Sixth, because of the retrospective study design, formal blinding of outcome assessors was not performed. Although multiple imputation was used for incomplete follow-up data, follow-up and assessment bias may still have influenced the SF-36 results. Finally, inflammatory and physiological biomarkers were not systematically analyzed, limiting mechanistic interpretation. Future prospective multicenter studies or randomized trials with larger sample sizes are needed to confirm these findings. Incorporating inflammatory markers, coagulation parameters, frailty status, more detailed perioperative risk stratification, and physiological monitoring may help clarify the underlying mechanisms associated with different perioperative management pathways. In addition, fracture type- and anatomical location-specific subgroup analyses, longer-term follow-up, and cost-effectiveness evaluations would further improve the robustness, clinical interpretability, and translational relevance of these findings.

Conclusion

Fast-track surgical management for acute orthopedic fractures in patients with moderate-to-severe trauma was associated with lower in-hospital mortality, improved perioperative outcomes, and enhanced mid-term functional recovery. The shorter time from admission to surgery observed under the fast-track surgical management pathway may be associated with improved early clinical outcomes and rehabilitation trajectories. These findings suggest that structured fast-track surgical pathways may provide potential clinical benefits in appropriately selected trauma patients. Prospective multicenter studies are warranted to confirm these benefits and to further define optimal patient selection criteria and long-term outcomes.
