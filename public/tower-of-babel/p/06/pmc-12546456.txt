The genomic design of fruit metabolomes
Authors: Hui Xue, Zhongjie Liu, Wenwen Liu, Guizhou Huang, Xu Wang, Mengrui Du, Chaochao Li, Xiaodong Xu, Jiacui Li, Xuanwen Yang, Hua Xiao, Yanling Peng, Yongfeng Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs a major source of vitamins, minerals, and dietary fiber, fruits are critical to global nutritional security. Domestication and crop improvement have increased fruit yield and disease resistance but reduced flavor and aroma. With the rapid development of multi-omics, including genomics and metabolomics, and gene-editing technologies such as CRISPR–Cas9, it has become feasible to improve fruit quality through genome design. However, the sensory traits of fruits, including sweetness, sourness, aroma, and color, are complex and regulated by multiple genes, presenting challenges for the study of their genetic basis. Metabolomics, when combined with reverse genetics, reflects gene expression and protein–protein interactions underlying fruit sensory traits more intuitively than other omics approaches. Establishing correlations between metabolites and phenotypic changes and integrating metabolomics with other omics data are crucial aspects of metabolomics research on fruit quality and genomics-assisted breeding. This paper provides a comprehensive review of research on the metabolomics of fruit sensory traits, alongside future prospects for integrating genomic design with metabolomics. It aims to deepen the understanding of multi-omics technologies, promote the application and advancement of metabolomics in fruit research, and establish a robust theoretical foundation for improving fruit sensory quality.Key words: metabolomics, genomic breeding, color, flavor, aroma, horticulture

Introduction

Metabolomics, which emerged in the late 1990s following genomics and proteomics, serves as a critical bridge between genotype and phenotype (Fiehn, 2002; Nicholson and Lindon, 2008; Shen et al., 2023). It has been widely applied in medicine, particularly in disease diagnosis, prediction of disease progression, and evaluation of drug metabolism efficacy (DeBerardinis and Keshari, 2022). It has also gradually begun to play a vital role in crop and tree breeding, livestock disease prevention, and food chemistry and processing. However, in contrast to widely used model systems such as Arabidopsis (Wu et al., 2018b; Huang et al., 2019a), tobacco (Goetz et al., 2017), and tomato (Klee and Tieman, 2013; Zhu et al., 2018; Gao et al., 2019), the development of metabolomics technologies in fruit research has been constrained by the complexity and environmental sensitivity of metabolites during fruit development, challenges in sample handling, and high costs.

Over the past century, yield and disease resistance have emerged as the primary objectives of fruit crop improvement (Zhou et al., 2019). However, the phenomena of “peach lacking peach flavor” (Cao et al., 2022) and “tomato lacking tomato flavor” (Zhu et al., 2018; Gao et al., 2019) have become pressing issues that demand urgent solutions. Moreover, color reflects flavor-related metabolism and significantly influences consumers’ assessment of fruit quality. Understanding the complex interplay among fruit sensory traits—sweetness, sourness, aroma, and color—requires comprehensive and in-depth research. Given the complex interactions within metabolic networks, single-trait breeding approaches face significant limitations. Therefore, it is essential to systematically characterize fruit metabolic pathways and their regulatory networks. For instance, some intermediates and derivatives of the carotenoid metabolic pathway also contribute to the formation of volatile organic compounds (VOCs) (Nisar et al., 2015). Similarly, high sugar content promotes the synthesis and accumulation of anthocyanins, and differences in anthocyanin levels are associated with traits such as fruit astringency and antioxidant capacity (Jaakola, 2013). The intricate network linking taste, color, and aroma demands extensive and detailed investigation.

Breeding 4.0 (Wallace et al., 2018) refers to intelligent precision breeding that is supported by modern technologies such as genomics (Crossa et al., 2017), big data, and artificial intelligence (Huang et al., 2022), enabling significant acceleration of the breeding process. Compared with traditional breeding methods, it emphasizes the in-depth mining and use of genetic information. However, given the remarkably complex genetic background and high level of heterozygosity characteristic of fruit trees, together with the fact that agronomic traits are often influenced by both genes and the cultivation environment, research on fruit sensory traits is relatively limited. This review primarily discusses the latest research progress in metabolomics related to fruit sensory traits. It summarizes the similarities and differences in fruit metabolomics across species, the application of genomic design to fruit sensory traits, and the developmental trends and future directions of this research. These insights provide theoretical support for targeted fruit quality improvement and offer a roadmap for accelerating the breeding of high-quality fruit varieties.

The fruit sensory traits lost during domestication

Fruits play a pivotal and multidimensional role in enhancing global food security. Domestication, achieved through long-term artificial selection and the refinement of genetic resources, has produced fruits that better suit growers’ preferences and requirements, but it is accompanied by the reduction or loss of fruit flavor (Zhao et al., 2019; Zhang and Hao, 2020).

Following the historic breakthrough of the “telomere-to-telomere” human genome assembly (Miga et al., 2020), related research has also been reported in fruits (Figure 1B). Grape was the first fruit to have its genome sequenced (Figure 1) (Jaillon et al., 2007), and genome information for diverse species is continuously updated (Velasco et al., 2007; Girollet et al., 2019; Shi et al., 2023). Genome sequences have also been reported for papaya (Ming et al., 2008), apple (Velasco et al., 2010; Daccord et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2019), strawberry (Shulaev et al., 2011; Edger et al., 2019; Jin et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2023), banana (D’Hont et al., 2012; Huang et al., 2023), melon (Garcia-Mas et al., 2012; Ruggieri et al., 2018; Li et al., 2023), sweet orange (Xu et al., 2013; Wu et al., 2018a), peach (International Peach Genome Initiative et al., 2013; Cao et al., 2021), and others, with continuous updates (Guo et al., 2013; Huang et al., 2013, 2016; Wu et al., 2013, 2014; Chagné et al., 2014; Liu et al., 2014; Ming et al., 2015; Lin et al., 2017; Qin et al., 2017; Teh et al., 2017; Xiao et al., 2017; Colle et al., 2019; Rendón-Anaya et al., 2019; Sahu et al., 2019; Zhu et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2020b, 2022b; Jiang et al., 2020; Luo et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2021a; Wang, 2021; Deng et al., 2022; Bao et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2023b; Han et al., 2023; Sun et al., 2023). This genomic information provides a solid theoretical foundation for precision breeding, enabling the precise mapping of genes related to fruit flavor, the elucidation of their genetic mechanisms, and the exploration of high-quality wild germplasm resources for targeted flavor improvement.

Metabolomic analyses have identified numerous differentially accumulated metabolites between wild and cultivated fruits (Figure 1C) (Ren et al., 2021; Renner et al., 2021; Huang et al., 2022; Jing et al., 2023; Xiao et al., 2023). For instance, wild apples contain significantly higher levels of compounds associated with astringency, sourness, and bitterness—such as tannins and organic acids—than cultivated varieties, which indicates that the accumulation of these compounds decreased markedly during apple domestication (Liao et al., 2021). A decrease in acidity and an increase in sugar content also occurred during the domestication of sour jujube (Huang et al., 2016). Notably, in tomato, sugar content exhibits a negative correlation with fruit size. Interestingly, elevated levels of VOCs enhance perceived sweetness, which challenges the conventional view that fruit size correlates weakly, or not at all, with flavor-related traits (Goulet et al., 2012; Tieman et al., 2017; Klee and Tieman, 2018). In conclusion, the integration of multi-omics data to build comprehensive regulatory networks, combined with precise edits to key genes and the application of machine learning, can enable the rapid, accurate, and targeted breeding of new fruit varieties, thereby providing technical support for both fruit domestication and quality improvement.

The genetic basis and biosynthetic pathways underlying fruit sensory traits

Currently, over 300 000 plant metabolites have been identified and classified as either primary or secondary metabolites based on their origins and functions. Carbohydrates, lipids, and nucleotides are essential for plant life and growth, whereas secondary metabolites, including alkaloids, phenols, and flavonoids, primarily contribute to stress responses and functions such as protection and competition. Fruit sugars, organic acids, VOCs, and pigments are biosynthesized through interconnected metabolic processes that rely on carbon sources derived from photosynthesis (Figures 2 and 3). These pathways diverge at a central metabolic junction, namely phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP). Sugar metabolism provides essential carbon skeletons not only for the tricarboxylic acid cycle, which leads to organic acid accumulation, but also for the biosynthesis of VOCs through lipid, amino acid, and terpenoid pathways, and for the production of pigments, including carotenoids via the methylerythritol phosphate (MEP) pathway and anthocyanins via the phenylpropanoid pathway. Light signaling is critical to the coordinated regulation of these metabolic branches, particularly through the transcription factor (TF) HY5. Furthermore, organic acid levels can exert feedback inhibition on sugar metabolism, contributing to a dynamic and complex metabolic network that maintains the balance of carbon flux among the processes that shape fruit quality–related traits.

Sugar metabolism: Dominant sucrose accumulation and regulation

The sweetness of fruits is primarily attributed to soluble sugars such as fructose, glucose, and sucrose, along with other compounds such as starch, sorbitol, inositol, galactose, mannitol, pyruvate, and gluconic acid (Yamaki and Ishikawa, 1986). The predominant sugar varies considerably across fruit species and varieties; for example, hexoses (fructose and glucose) are the major soluble sugars in grapes and apples (Zhu et al., 2023). Based on the proportions of fruit sugar components and clustering analysis, loquat primarily accumulates sucrose (Sortino et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2024a) and watermelon primarily accumulates hexoses (Umer et al., 2020; Ren et al., 2021) (Supplemental Table 1).

Sucrose accumulation has been the predominant form of soluble sugar accumulation during evolution (Ren et al., 2021). This stems from sucrose’s role as the primary sugar for long-distance transport in plants, as well as its central functions in energy storage and signaling (Chen et al., 2021b). Compared to monosaccharides like fructose, sucrose offers significant evolutionary advantages due to its greater chemical stability and lower effect on osmotic pressure; this facilitates efficient transport and bulk storage, which are crucial for coping with environmental stress and ensuring effective energy allocation. In-depth evolutionary analyses (e.g., comparative genomic and phylogenetic analyses) have identified members of multiple key gene families that control sucrose content. These include genes involved in sucrose biosynthesis (e.g., sucrose phosphate synthase [SPS] genes) (Vimolmangkang et al., 2016), transport (e.g., sucrose transporter [SUT/SUC] genes and sugars will eventually be exported transporter [SWEET] genes) (Stein and Granot, 2019; Xue et al., 2022), and catabolism (e.g., sucrose synthase [SuSy] and invertase [INV] genes) (Wei et al., 2021). Importantly, the functions of these key genes, identified through evolutionary analyses, have been experimentally validated using stable genetic transformation techniques (e.g., transgenic overexpression and gene knockout/knockdown) in various model plants and crops (Deng et al., 2019; Ren et al., 2021). Experimental evidence confirms that modulating their expression significantly alters sucrose accumulation, thereby influencing plant growth, development, yield, and stress tolerance.

Sugar metabolism is further orchestrated by TFs (e.g., bHLH, MYB, and WRKY) (Cao et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2023), which dynamically balance synthesis, transport, and storage in response to energy and stress signals, epigenetic reprogramming (e.g., DNA methylation and histone modification) (Huang et al., 2019b; Li et al., 2020; Ma et al., 2021), and environmental cues (e.g., hormones, stress, and microbes), thereby shaping carbon partitioning. Beyond sucrose, soluble sugars play multifaceted roles in fruit quality: sorbitol is the primary carbon transport sugar in Rosaceae and combines low osmolarity with antioxidant capacity (Li et al., 2018), fructose confers high sweetness and supports osmoregulation, glucose is an energy substrate and a precursor for pigment glycosylation, and trehalose and raffinose family members enhance stress tolerance by stabilizing biomacromolecules. Through coordinated carbon partitioning (e.g., sorbitol-to-fructose conversion optimizes the sugar/acid ratio), signaling (e.g., through the glucose-HXK pathway), and cellular homeostasis (e.g., through trehalose-mediated membrane protection), these sugars collectively shape fruit flavor, color, and postharvest resilience.

Acid metabolism: Dynamic balance of organic acids

The organic acids present in fruits include malic acid (MA), citric acid (CA), tartaric acid (TA), quinic acid, succinic acid, oxalic acid, acetic acid, and ascorbic acid (AsA, vitamin C) (Guo et al., 2019). Fruits can be classified as MA types (e.g., apples), CA types (e.g., lemons), and TA types (e.g., grapes) according to their dominant organic acid. In addition, amino acids are key contributors to fruit flavor (Kader, 2008; Klee, 2010); for example, glutamic acid is responsible for “umami” or savory flavors, alanine and lysine are strongly associated with sweetness, and phenylalanine and tyrosine contribute to bitterness (Song et al., 2019).

The accumulation of organic acids in fruit is primarily governed by the balance of biosynthesis, interconversion, transport, and degradation, which are controlled by a network of interconnected metabolic pathways (Etienne et al., 2013; Hu et al., 2016). Among these, the tricarboxylic acid cycle serves as the central route for organic acid biosynthesis. CA and MA are the predominant components, synthesized by key enzymes such as citrate synthase, PEP carboxylase (PEPC) (Zhi et al., 2021), and malate dehydrogenase (MDH), and degraded by malic enzyme, aconitase (Liu et al., 2024b), and isocitrate dehydrogenase (Zhang et al., 2021). The γ-aminobutyric acid shunt, acetyl-coenzyme A-associated pathways, and glycolytic branches also contribute to the complexity of organic acid metabolism (Sheng et al., 2017). Organic acid transporters (e.g., aluminum-activated malate transporters, located at the plasma membrane) also regulate the dynamic balance of organic acids in the cytoplasm, ensuring metabolic continuity and stability (Bai et al., 2012).

Organic acid accumulation is not the result of a single pathway but rather is determined by the coordinated regulation of multiple metabolic processes across different developmental stages and organelles. During early development, glycolysis, PEPC, and malate dehydrogenase synergistically promote organic acid synthesis. During the middle stage of cell expansion, vacuolar storage capacity increases, and organic acids are sequestered into the vacuoles. During ripening, the expression of degradative enzymes such as malic enzyme and isocitrate dehydrogenase rises, leading to decreased acidity. These processes are finely regulated by TFs (e.g., bHLH, MYB, and WRKY), enzymatic activity, and environmental cues, ultimately giving rise to the diverse organic acid profiles characteristic of different fruit species. Environmental factors such as insufficient light, elevated temperature, and calcium deficiency can also inhibit organic acid accumulation. Notably, during fruit development, the dynamic changes in organic acids are more pronounced and sensitive than those of sugars—sugars often accumulate steadily from early development to maturity, whereas organic acid content undergoes dramatic shifts (e.g., rapid synthesis in early stages and gradual degradation during ripening), making them the key variable in adjusting flavor balance.

Color metabolism: Pigment synthesis and coloration

Anthocyanins

Anthocyanins are water-soluble pigments widely distributed in plants. They belong to the flavonoid family and share a basic C6 (A ring)–C3 (C ring)–C6 (B ring) carbon skeleton (Winkel-Shirley, 2001; Liang et al., 2008; Zhang et al., 2020). Different types of anthocyanins, such as pelargonidin, cyanidin, peonidin, delphinidin, malvidin, and petunidin (Figure 4), are produced via methylation and hydroxylation substitution reactions at the R1 and R2 positions of the B ring. The predominant type of anthocyanin varies across different fruits; for example, apple is dominated by cyanidin 3-galactoside, whereas grapes primarily contain cyanidin-3-O-glucoside and malvidin-3-O-glucoside (Supplemental Table 1).

The biosynthesis and metabolism of anthocyanins are complex and finely regulated processes that have been relatively well characterized (Figure 2). The anthocyanin biosynthetic pathway involves three stages: (1) precursor synthesis via the phenylpropanoid pathway, which starts from the aromatic amino acid phenylalanine and produces flavanone-type precursors; (2) flavonoid backbone biosynthesis, which is a key step in the formation of the flavonoid skeleton and provides a branch point leading to different classes of compounds such as flavanones, flavanols, and anthocyanins; and (3) modification and sequestration, in which anthocyanins are stabilized through glycosylation, acylation, and methylation, often followed by storage in vacuoles.

Anthocyanin metabolism is regulated at multiple levels, including transcriptional control (Amato et al., 2019), post-transcriptional modifications, hormone signaling, and environmental stimuli (LaFountain and Yuan, 2021). The MYB–bHLH–WD40 (MBW) complex (Hu et al., 2016) is the most important transcriptional regulatory module. Plant hormones such as ethylene (Ni et al., 2023), abscisic acid (ABA) (Lama et al., 2020), auxins (Wang et al., 2020a), and jasmonic acid (An et al., 2021), along with environmental signals such as temperature (Ryu et al., 2020) and light (Alabd et al., 2022), are also key regulators of anthocyanin biosynthesis.

Carotenoids

Carotenoids are lipid-soluble pigments that are mostly localized to plastids. With over 1200 natural carotenoids identified, they are the second most abundant natural pigments on Earth (http://carotenoiddb.jp/) (Nisar et al., 2015; Sun et al., 2022). They are classified into two types, based on whether their chemical structure contains oxygen: carotenes (e.g., α-carotene, β-carotene, and lycopene) and xanthophylls (e.g., lutein and zeaxanthin). Their color and photoprotective abilities depend on the number of conjugated double bonds they contain. Carotenoids are highly accumulated in fruits such as citrus and mango. For example, lycopene is known for its antioxidant, cardioprotective, and anticancer properties, and is ubiquitously present in tomato, watermelon, guava, papaya, and grapefruit (Story et al., 2010). They also act as precursors of plant hormones such as ABA and strigolactone, and participate in their synthesis and metabolism (Alder et al., 2012).

Carotenoid metabolism is precisely regulated in a dynamic balance between synthesis and degradation. In different fruits, the final carotenoid composition is determined by the metabolic bias toward either synthetic accumulation or degradative transformation. Biosynthesis is initiated via either the mevalonic acid pathway in the cytoplasm or the MEP pathway in plastids, with the core steps occurring in plastids (Figure 3). This process involves three major stages: (1) precursor synthesis, mainly via the MEP pathway, catalyzed by enzymes such as 1-deoxy-D-xylulose-5-phosphate synthase (DXS), 1-deoxy-D-xylulose 5-phosphate reductoisomerase (DXR), and geranylgeranyl pyrophosphate synthase (GGPS); (2) carotenoid backbone biosynthesis, catalyzed by phytoene synthase (PSY), phytoene desaturase (PDS), ζ-carotene desaturase (ZDS), and carotenoid isomerase (CRTISO); (3) cyclization and subsequent modifications, where β-cyclization is mediated by lycopene β-cyclase (LCYB) to produce β-carotene, and α-cyclization requires the combined action of LCYB and lycopene ε-cyclase (LCYE) to generate α-carotene (Figure 2). Carotenoid degradation primarily occurs through oxidative cleavage, which can be either specific or non-specific. Specific cleavage is catalyzed by carotenoid cleavage dioxygenases (CCDs) and 9-cis-epoxycarotenoid dioxygenases to generate physiologically active products, including aroma compounds (such as β-ionone) and xanthoxin (a precursor for ABA synthesis), respectively (Figure 3). Carotenoids can also be degraded by non-specific enzymes such as lipoxygenase (LOX), a process that occurs during fruit senescence and under stress conditions.

Carotenoid biosynthesis is regulated at multiple levels. TFs such as MYB (Wu et al., 2020), bHLH, and AP2/ERF regulate the expression of key biosynthetic genes by binding to their promoter regions. Plant hormones, particularly ethylene and ABA, regulate carotenoid-related gene expression during fruit ripening through signal transduction pathways. In addition, environmental factors such as light and temperature affect carotenoid accumulation by modulating regulatory signaling networks.

Betalains

Unlike carotenoids and anthocyanins, the biosynthesis of betalains has not been fully elucidated (Gandía-Herrero and García-Carmona, 2013; Polturak et al., 2018; Chen et al., 2021a). Betalains accumulate in vacuoles and are classified into two types: betacyanins (red–purple) and betaxanthins (yellow–orange), which are both abundant in pitaya (Hatlestad et al., 2012, Hatlestad et al., 2015). Recent studies have identified TFs such as WRKY, MYB, and ERF as key regulators of betalain biosynthesis (Cheng et al., 2017, Hatlestad et al., 2015). Notably, betalains and anthocyanins are mutually exclusive across plant species, with no known example of their co-occurrence in the same organism (Figure 3) (Maeda and Dudareva, 2012). This exclusive distribution is hypothesized to result from evolutionary divergence or potential metabolic redundancy, whereby the simultaneous presence of both pigment pathways may be unnecessary or even detrimental to the plant’s physiological efficiency.

Aroma metabolism: Volatile compound (VOC) synthesis and regulation

Currently, over 2000 aromatic compounds have been detected in fruits (Hui et al., 2010; El Hadi et al., 2013; Liu et al., 2018; Sánchez-Rodríguez et al., 2019). Comprising esters, alcohols, aldehydes, ketones, terpenoids, and other compounds, VOCs function as a plant defense mechanism against diseases, pests, and herbivores, and also contribute to their unique aroma characteristics (Supplemental Table 1; Figure 1) (Mayuoni-Kirshinbaum and Porat, 2014; García-Gómez et al., 2020; Xia et al., 2020; Li et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2024c). For example, apples primarily contain esters and alcohols, such as ethyl butyrate and hexanol, producing a fresh, sweet, fruity aroma (Espino-Díaz et al., 2016; Yang et al., 2021). Strawberries contain esters and aromatic compounds, such as methyl acetate and phenylethanol, which produce a rich, sweet fragrance, whereas grapes feature terpenes and alcohols, such as geraniol and linalool, resulting in floral and fruity notes (Schwab et al., 2008). Mangoes contain aldehydes and terpenes, such as hexanal and myrcene, which provide a tropical fruity scent (Li et al., 2017). Citrus fruits are characterized by monoterpenes, such as limonene and terpinene, releasing a typical citrus aroma (Nguyen et al., 2009; Zhang et al., 2017). These differences determine the unique flavor profiles of fruits and provide a basis for aroma fingerprinting to support breeding strategies (Xiao et al., 2021).

The synthesis of fruit VOCs involves multiple metabolic pathways and enzyme-catalyzed reactions. Straight-chain aldehydes, alcohols, and esters (especially in apples and bananas) are primarily synthesized via the LOX pathway, whereas branched-chain aldehydes are synthesized via the amino acid and β-oxidation pathways, with key enzymes including alcohol acyltransferases (AATs) and alcohol dehydrogenases (Obando-Ulloa et al., 2008; Eduardo et al., 2010; Besada et al., 2017; Chen et al., 2018; Abbas et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024b). Terpenoids are synthesized through the MEP and mevalonic acid pathways, whereas polyphenols and phenylpropanoids are derived from the shikimic acid and phenylpropanoid pathways (Lin et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2022). Terpene synthase enzyme families a, b, d, g, and h are primarily responsible for terpenoid biosynthesis in plants (Jia et al., 2022). In addition, carotenoids can be converted into β-ionone, pseudoionone, and geranylacetone by CCDs (Figure 3). Phenylethanol and benzaldehyde are derived from phenylalanine via the phenylpropanoid pathway, in which phenylalanine ammonia-lyase is a crucial enzyme. During early fruit development, C6 aldehydes and alcohols predominantly derived from the LOX pathway act as defensive VOCs. During the expansion stage, enhanced activation of the MEP pathway drives the accumulation of photoprotective monoterpenes, whereas maturation triggers the explosive synthesis of esters and lactones via AAT gene activation, thereby attracting seed dispersers (George et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023).

The accumulation of VOCs in fruits is highly spatiotemporally specific. It is not only constrained by the availability of precursors and the expression of metabolic enzymes but also fine-tuned by complex regulatory factors, including TF activity, hormonal signals (e.g., ethylene) (Tobaruela et al., 2021), and environmental cues (e.g., temperature and light) (Yan et al., 2018). Among the TFs, the AP2/ERF family is critical for the biosynthesis of fatty acid derivatives and terpenoids (Feng et al., 2020), the MYB family is broadly involved in the regulation of phenylpropanoids and other aromatic compounds, and the bHLH and WRKY families often act as coregulators with MYB or ERF TFs (Lu et al., 2021).

Metabolic crosstalk and coordinated regulation of fruit sensory traits

The four primary fruit sensory traits—sweetness, sourness, aroma, and color—are interconnected through a coordinated metabolic network (Zhang et al., 2023). The regulation of this network is reflected not only in the allocation of resources for biosynthesis but also in precise coordination through signal transduction, substrate competition, and transcriptional regulation, thereby providing a systematically targeted breeding approach for fruit quality improvement.

Sugars act as the central hub, directing carbon flux and signaling. As the end products of photosynthesis, sugars not only serve as the basis for energy supply and biosynthesis in fruits but also function as core signaling molecules that regulate other sensory traits. For example, sucrose accumulation in apple fruits inhibits the activity of PEPC (Wei et al., 2021). Sucrose and sorbitol function as molecular triggers that activate anthocyanin biosynthesis in peach flesh, whereas fructose appears to attenuate pigmentation intensity by inducing transcriptional repressors (Wang et al., 2022a).

Organic acids (e.g., CA and MA) bridge sugars, pigments, and aroma by regulating intracellular microenvironments (e.g., vacuolar pH) and substrate availability. For example, anthocyanin stability is highly pH-dependent: they remain stable as red cyanidin under acidic conditions (pH < 5), but if pH rises (e.g., if vacuolar pH exceeds 6 due to MA degradation), they readily isomerize into colorless or brown forms. In citrus, CA maintains vacuolar acidity (pH 3.5–4.5) to stabilize carotenoids (e.g., β-cryptoxanthin); reduced CA due to metabolic imbalance causes carotenoid oxidation and faded color. In addition, acidic conditions activate ester-synthesizing enzymes (e.g., AAT), promoting alcohol-to-ester conversion (e.g., hexanol to ethyl hexanoate), whereas neutral or alkaline conditions inhibit this process, shifting the aroma profile from fruity to “grass-like.”

The synthesis of VOCs and pigments is often coordinated through shared transcriptional regulators and signaling pathways, with the MBW complex and ethylene signaling acting as core regulatory nodes. Multi-target regulation by the MBW complex is key to this coordination. In strawberry fruit, the complex formed by FaMYB10 and FabHLH3 not only activates anthocyanin biosynthesis genes (e.g., FaANS) but also upregulates ester synthesis genes (e.g., FaAAT1), enabling the simultaneous accumulation of anthocyanins (red) and methyl acetate (sweet aroma) during ripening. As a ripening signal, ethylene coordinates aroma and color metabolism through the reactive oxygen species signaling network. In apple fruit, ethylene treatment upregulates LOX and DFR (a key enzyme in anthocyanin biosynthesis), thereby promoting the accumulation of ethyl hexanoate and cyanidin, a red pigment. This ethylene–reactive oxygen species-mediated coordination explains why fully ripe fruits often exhibit both rich aroma and vivid color.

The metabolic crosstalk among fruit sensory traits provides a “multi-target synergy” strategy for precision breeding, avoiding quality imbalances caused by single-trait improvement. In the future, combining multi-omics data to construct a “sugar–acid–aroma–color” interaction network model, supplemented with machine learning or artificial intelligence to predict key regulatory nodes, will enable the customized design of fruit sensory traits. For example, apple varieties with “high sugar–low acid–strong fruity aroma–bright red color” or citrus varieties with “low sugar–high acid–fresh floral aroma–orange color” could be cultivated through precision breeding to meet diverse consumer demands.

The genomic design of fruit sensory traits

Plant precision breeding refers to a breeding strategy that uses modern biotechnological tools to precisely identify, manipulate, and combine the genetic basis of target traits, thereby achieving the targeted improvement of crops (Figure 4) (Mascher et al., 2024). The rapid development of precision breeding is driven by three major societal needs: food security, climate change adaptation, and consumer demand for higher-quality food. Core areas of progress in precision breeding include the innovation and optimization of gene-editing technologies (including CRISPR–Cas, epigenetic editing, and delivery systems) (Mou et al., 2024), high-throughput genomics and molecular design (including pan-genome research and artificial intelligence-assisted design), and synthetic biology applications (including artificial chromosomes and metabolic pathway reconstruction) (Li et al., 2024). These technologies have gradually been applied to crops such as rice (Wang et al., 2017) and wheat (Lin et al., 2020), but research focused on fruit quality improvement remains relatively limited (Ma et al., 2023).

As key regulatory sequences in the 5′ untranslated regions of mRNA, upstream open reading Frames (uORFs) can precisely regulate the expression of downstream genes by fine-tuning translation efficiency through methods such as base editing. For example, the conserved uORF of the strawberry FvebZIPs1.1 gene was edited using the plant-efficient cytosine base editor A3A-PBE, producing 7 new uORF mutations in the T0 generation. Homozygous mutants exhibited significantly increased fruit sugar content without affecting plant growth, which demonstrates that precise uORF editing can finely regulate quantitative traits in fruit trees and that new genotypes and traits can be stably transmitted through asexual reproduction (Xing et al., 2020). This technology provides a new path for quality improvement in strawberries and other asexually propagated fruit trees and is expected to enable the cultivation of varieties with customized sweetness levels to meet diverse market demands.

Targeted knockout of SlCDPK27 and SlCDPK26 in tomato enhances fruit sweetness while maintaining optimal weight and yield (Zhang et al., 2024a). This breakthrough has provided critical insights into the genetic regulation and molecular pathways underlying sugar accumulation in tomato fruits. Starting from a red-fruited tomato background, a multiplex gene-editing strategy has been used to rapidly and precisely generate seven distinct tomato varieties with different fruit colors (Yang et al., 2023a). This approach had no significant effect on key agronomic traits, including single-fruit weight, yield per plant, soluble solid content, and vitamin C content (Yang et al., 2023a). Similarly, the VvDXS1 gene in grapes has been edited using CRISPR–Cas9 technology to significantly increase monoterpenoid content, thereby creating a muscat flavor in non-muscat grape varieties (Yang et al., 2024).

A fruit quality improvement strategy that extends precise genetic modification to upstream elements has begun to take shape. Although current research remains relatively limited, it has already provided new insights and approaches for fruit quality improvement. As research continues to advance and new technologies are innovated, more breakthroughs are expected, which will inject new momentum into the development of the fruit industry.

Challenges and prospects

Although precision breeding technologies are increasingly used to improve crop stress resistance and quality, they still face numerous challenges. Fruit sensory traits are jointly regulated by multiple genes and environmental factors, making it difficult to decipher their complex genetic networks. Strengthening interdisciplinary research is necessary in order to deeply explore key genes and regulatory elements. In addition, despite continuous improvements in gene-editing technologies, off-target effects remain difficult to completely eliminate. The development of more precise and efficient tools for gene editing and off-target detection is essential to ensure the safety of precision breeding.

With the continued development of technologies such as metabolomic detection and genome sequencing, metabolite coverage and identification accuracy will continue to improve, providing data to support the joint analysis of traits and genes. Furthermore, the continuous development of novel gene-editing tools, together with the deepening integration of multi-omics, artificial intelligence, and precision breeding, will enable the precise design and regulation of fruit sensory traits and other complex plant traits.
