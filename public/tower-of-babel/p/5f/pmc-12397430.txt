Podoplanin expression is associated with local inflammation and survival in glioma
Authors: Pegah Mir Seyed Nazari, Maximilian J Mair, Julia Riedl, Benedikt Schöllkopf, Florian Moik, Gerda Ricken, Georg Widhalm, Karin Dieckmann, Cihan Ay, Ingrid Pabinger, Matthias Preusser, Anna Sophie Berghoff
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlioma subtypes differ in prognosis and treatment strategies. We aimed to determine the correlation of podoplanin (PDPN) expression in glioma subtypes with survival as well as systemic and local inflammation. In a prospective cohort study including 192 patients with glioma, tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs), programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression and podoplanin (PDPN) expression were analyzed by immunohistochemistry. PDPN and PD-L1 expression were more frequently observed in IDH1 wildtype (wt) compared to IDH1 mutant (mut) glioma (p = 0.015). In IDHwt glioma, overall survival was significantly longer in glioma patients without PDPN expression compared to those with PDPN expression (median 26.3 vs. 12.5 months, log-rank test, p < 0.001). Levels of tumor-infiltrating CD8 + and CD68 + cells were highest in IDH1wt glioma with both PDPN and PD-L1 expression (p < 0.001). IDH1wt, PDPN and non-PD-L1 expressing glioma presented with the highest neutrophil-to-lymphocyte (NLR) ratio in the blood compared to other subtypes (p = 0.023). Gliomas differ in the composition of the inflammatory microenvironment according to the presence of IDH1 mutation. Introduction

Gliomas are the most common malignant primary brain tumors and are categorized and graded according to the World Health Organization Classification of Central Nervous System Tumours1,2. In the past decade, updated versions of this classification framework acknowledged the molecular heterogeneity of gliomas. Overall, gliomas comprise therefore a very heterogenous group with survival ranging from only few months in isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH) 1 wildtype (IDH1wt) glioblastoma to several years in IDH1 mutant (IDH1mut) glioma3. In consequence, a precise diagnosis identifying a prognostically and biologically homogenous cohort in terms of survival is essential for clinical decision-making and clinical trial planning in glioma.

In glioblastoma, immune checkpoint inhibitors have shown limited clinical efficacy in unselected glioblastoma cohorts, as progression-free and overall survival were not prolonged4–6. However, promising results were observed in small studies on neoadjuvant treatment arguing that potentially the timepoint of administration as well as patient selection could be relevant determinants of treatment response towards immune modulatory agents7. Indeed, the inflammatory microenvironment may differ according to molecular glioma subtypes, as IDH1 mutations were shown to have immunosuppressive properties, resulting in only minor infiltration with lymphocytes in astrocytomas and oligodendrogliomas8. Programmed cell death ligand-1 (PD-L1) was previously described as an immunosuppressive molecule potentially impacting the immunosuppressive properties of the glioma microenvironment, however no correlation with response to checkpoint inhibitors targeting the PD-1 axis was observed4,9,10.

Podoplanin (PDPN) expression, rather involved in activation of the innate immune system, might also impact the composition of the inflammatory microenvironment11. Indeed, PDPN was previously shown to be expressed in glioma and associated with tumor cell-induced platelet activation and venous thrombosis12,13. Therefore, the aim of the current study was to analyze the correlation of podoplanin expression with the local immune microenvironment in the context of molecular glioma subtypes.

Methods

Patient cohort

The patient cohort consisted of 192 patients with glioma who were recruited in the framework of the Vienna Cancer and Thrombosis Study (CATS), an observational single-center cohort study at the Medical University of Vienna performed between 2003 and 2021. The detailed study design has been described in prior publications13,14. In brief, adult patients (≥ 18 years) with newly diagnosed glioma or recurrence after previous brain tumor resection have been included after providing written informed consent. CATS has been approved by the local ethics committee (approval number 126/2003) and has been performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. In the present analysis, all glioma patients recruited from 2003 until 2014 with available tumor tissue samples in the Neuro-Biobank of the Division of Neuropathology and Neurochemistry, Department of Neurology of the Medical University of Vienna were included. Glioma cases were classified according to the WHO Classification of Central Nervous System Tumours 2007. The date of death was obtained via the official Austrian death registry. At time of recruitment and prior to chemotherapy, a single blood draw was performed via sterile venipuncture in vacuum tubes (Vacuette, Greiner BioOne, Kremsmünster, Austria). Blood cell counts were analyzed within 2 h from sampling in an accredited process using a Sysmex XE-5000 haematology analyser. Fibrinogen plasma levels were determined according to Clauss (STA Fibrinogen; Diagnostica Stago, Asnieres, France; normal range: 180–390 mg/dL). CRP serum levels were measured with an immunonephelometric assay (Olympus Diagnostics, Southall, UK; normal range: 0-0.5 mg/dL) on an AU 2700 chemistry analyser.

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemical staining was performed using a Ventana Benchmark Ultra immunostaining system. Formalin-fixed and paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue blocks of glioma were cut into serial 3–5 μm slices. All sections of IDH1mut R132H, PD-L1 and PDPN stainings were reviewed on a multi-headed microscope by two independent experienced investigators, which were blinded to corresponding clinical data. IDH1mut was assessed with the monoclonal anti-IDH1 R132H antibody (Dianova, Germany) on a qualitative level. PD-L1 expression was assessed via the monoclonal mouse anti-human PD-L1 antibody (1:50, Clone 22C3, Dako)15,16. The PD-L1 signal was semi-quantitatively scored based on the percentage of tumor cells presented with a specific membranous signal as previously described. Samples with 1% distinct membranous anti-PD-L1 labeling of all tumor cells were classified as PD-L1 positive16. PDPN was investigated with the monoclonal mouse anti-PDPN antibody D2-40 (Cell Marque, Rocklin, CA, United States)13. The intensity of PDPN was semi-quantitatively classified as positive if at least 50% of tumor cells expressed PDPN and/or when tumor cells showed mild staining intensity. Tumor-infiltrating CD8+ and CD68+ cells were investigated with the monoclonal mouse anti-human CD8 antibody (DakoCytomation, Glostrup, Denmark) and monoclonal mouse anti-human CD68 antibody (DakoCytomation, Glostrup, Denmark), respectively. The density of tumor-infiltrating CD8+ and CD68+ cells was measured and analyzed using Definiens Tissue Studio 4.4.3 (Definiens AG, Munich, Germany). Prior to measurement, necrotic areas were excluded.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 15 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Count data and continuous variables were reported as absolute frequencies (percentages) and medians (with 25th -75th percentile), respectively. A Kaplan-Meier estimator and univariable cox regression models were used to analyse overall survival. Survival differences between groups were compared with log-rank tests. Two-sided p-values lower than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Due to the exploratory and hypothesis-generating design of the present study, no adjustment for multiple testing was conducted17.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the entire study population

The patient cohort comprised 192 glioma patients. Median age was 55 years and 71/192 (37.0%) patients were female. In total, 146/192 (76.0%) patients were diagnosed with glioblastoma (WHO grade IV), 37/192 (19.3%) with a WHO grade III glioma, and 9/192 (4.7%) with a WHO grade II glioma. IDH1 mutation was present in 40/192 (20.8%) gliomas (Table 1).

Inflammatory subgroups according to IDH status in glioma

PDPN expression was found in 126/152 (82.9%) of IDH1wt gliomas (Fig. 1). In contrast, all IDH1mut gliomas had either no PDPN expression (33/40, 82.5%) or showed only low PDPN expression (7/40, 17.5%). Survival times differed significantly among these glioma subgroups. Glioma patients with an IDH1 mutation had longer overall survival compared to patients with IDH1wt (median 49.5 vs. 13 months, log-rank test, p < 0.001; glioblastoma: median 24.7 vs. 12.8 months, log-rank test, p < 0.001). Of the overall IDH1wt glioma cohort, overall survival was significantly better in patients without PDPN expression compared to those with PDPN expression (median 26.3 vs. 12.5 months, log-rank test, p < 0.001). These results prevailed in subgroup analyses for glioblastoma (median 15.0 vs. 12.8 months, p = 0.024) (Fig. 1, Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 1).

Inflammatory glioma subgroups. Distinct inflammatory glioma subgroups can be further categorized according to IDH1 mutation, podoplanin and PD-L1 status. PD-L1 is predominantly present in IDH1 wildtype glioma with podoplanin upregulation. IDH1 wildtype glioma with podoplanin expression are also linked to poor prognosis. (IDH1 isocitrate dehydrogenase 1, PDPN podoplanin, PD-L1 programmed cell death ligand-1, wt wildtype, mut mutant)

In univariable cox regression analysis of IDH1wt glioma, the hazard ratio (HR) of death in patients with PDPN expression as compared to patients with no PDPN expression was 2.636, 95% confidence interval (95%CI): 1.585 to 4.383 (p < 0.001). This significant result prevailed in multivariable cox regression analyses after adjustment for potential confounders such as tumor grade (adjusted HR: 2.123, 95%CI 1.235 to 3.652, p = 0.006) and tumor grade, age and sex (adjusted HR: 2.008, 95%CI 1.150 to 3.506, p = 0.014).

Membranous PD-L1 expression on tumor cells was found in 20/192 (10.4%) glioma specimens. The PD-L1 status had no significant impact on survival in our patient cohort (PD-L1 + vs. PD-L1-, median survival in glioma: 12.8 vs. 15.6 months, log-rank test, p = 0.440; median survival in glioblastoma: 11.0 vs. 14.7 months, p = 0.217) (Supplementary Table 1). In gliomas with IDH1 mutation, only one patient showed PD-L1 expression on tumor cells. In patients with IDH1wt and PDPN expression, 19/126 (15.1%) glioma specimens presented with PD-L1 expression while none (0/26) of the patients with IDH1wt without PDPN expression presented with PD-L1 expression (Chi-Square-Test, p = 0.045; Fig. 1).

The distribution of tumor-infiltrating CD8 + and CD68 + cells significantly differed in glioma subgroups (Kruskal-Wallis test, p < 0.001) (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 2). Levels of tumor-infiltrating CD8 + and CD68 + cells were highest in IDH1wt glioma with both PDPN and PD-L1 expression (median [Q1-Q3]: 49.02 CD8 + cells/mm2 [19.83–81.49] and 475.93 CD68 + cells/mm2 [268.42-735.71]) and second highest in patients with IDH1wt glioma with PDPN expression but absence of PD-L1 (13.62 CD8 + cells/mm2 [5.75–32.07] and 244.14 CD68 + cells/mm2 [133.91-361.75]). There was no significant difference in the distribution of CD8 + tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) or CD68 + cells between IDH1wt glioma without PDPN expression and those with IDH1 mutation (p > 0.05). Similarly, there was no difference between IDH1mut glioma with and without PDPN expression (p > 0.05).

Correlation of systemic and local inflammation in the cohort

Systemic inflammation and blood-based markers of systemic inflammation significantly differed in glioma subgroups (Fig. 3, Supplementary Tables 2 and 3). IDH1wt glioma was significantly associated with higher NLR (median 5.1 vs. 3.3, Mann-Whitney-U test, p = 0.016), higher neutrophils (G/L: 6.6 vs. 4.7, p = 0.021) lower platelets (G/L: 232 vs. 303, p < 0.001) and lower albumin (g/L: 40.2 vs. 45.3, p < 0.001) compared to IDH1mut glioma. In further subgroup analysis of IDH1wt glioma, patients with both PDPN and PD-L1 expression, showed significantly higher monocyte (G/L: 0.65 vs. 0.45 vs. 0.40; p = 0.024 and p = 0.050), fibrinogen (mg/dL: 382 vs. 303 vs. 293; p < 0.001 and p = 0.003) and CRP levels (mg/dL: 0.48 vs. 0.10 vs. 0.08; p = 0.006 and p = 0.004) and CRP/albumin ratio (0.0139 vs. 0.0020 vs. 0.0016; p = 0.013 and p = 0.005) than those with IDH1wt, PDPN expression and no PD-L1 expression and those with IDH1wt without PDPN expression. No significant differences of systemic inflammation parameters were observed in subgroup-analysis for IDH1mut glioma in regard of PDPN or PD-L1 expression.

Discussion

In this prospective cohort study, inflammatory characteristics presented with a heterogenous distribution in gliomas and were associated with molecular subtypes and survival prognosis. PDPN, as a marker of the innate immune system, correlated with PD-L1 expression and density of TILs, arguing for PDPN as a potential target of interest for immune modulating therapies in glioma.

In this study, PDPN was differentially expressed between glioma subgroups and associated with impaired survival in IDH1wt glioma. Accordingly, previous publications postulated that PDPN expression on tumor cells is associated with tumor progression and worse outcome in different cancer types including glioblastoma, squamous cell carcinoma of the skin, esophageal cancer and head and neck cancer18,19. Interestingly, parameters of the local inflammatory microenvironment including PD-L1 and tumor-infiltrating CD8 + T cells were strongly associated with PDPN expression, indicating that the inflammatory microenvironment and, as a consequence, potentially the response to immune modulating therapies differs between glioma subgroups. The presence of PDPN was also associated with a distinct systemic immune response as NLR was highest in IDH1wt, PDPN and non-PD-L1 expressing glioma, altogether leading to the assumption that PDPN might play a role in modulating the immune response in glioma on local and systemic levels. Supportive to that, previous studies have implicated a mechanistic role of PDPN in inflammatory processes in cancer11,12,20. PDPN was identified as a co-inhibitory receptor on T cells regulating T cell function and thereby potentially facilitating immune escape in glioma21. In line, PDPN-targeting monoclonal antibodies as well as PDPN-targeting CAR-T cells were shown to inhibit the growth of PDPN-expressing brain tumor xenografts in vivo, suggesting that PDPN could be an evolving therapeutic target in glioma20,22.

So far, immune modulating therapies failed to create a clinically meaningful effect in glioma4–6,23, although early results of locoregional CAR-T cell administration as well as vaccination approaches showed early signals of tumor regression and immune activation in small cohorts of uncontrolled clinical trials24–28. In general, activation of antitumoral immune responses depends on many factors including the inflammatory microenvironment. The results of this study corroborate previous findings on differing cancer cell – immune system interactions according to molecular glioma subgroups8,29,30. Indeed, IDH mutation is associated with decreased inflammation via production of the oncometabolite (R)-2-hydroxyglutarate (R-2-HG), which is taken up by T cells causing suppression of T cell activity31. The subgroup of IDH1wt glioma with expression of PDPN and PD-L1 expression, presented with increased infiltration of CD8 + lymphocytes as well as the highest CRP levels in the present study. Based on our data, in addition to IDH mutation, further local inflammatory parameters such as PDPN expression are associated with the local and systemic immune response and could therefore also impact immune modulating therapeutic approaches.

Mechanistically, PDPN is particularly able to interact with the C-type lectin-like receptor 2 (CLEC-2) on platelets and thereby induce platelet activation32. Accordingly, tumor-expressed PDPN was linked to platelet aggregates within brain tumors13. In line, in an orthotopic murine glioma model, PDPN deletion in glioma cells resulted in decreased platelet aggregates within the tumor33. It is well known that the activation of platelets leads to platelet degranulation and subsequent release of several signaling molecules, cytokines and growth factors, which are able to modulate the immune response and promote tumor progression34–36. These released mediators might not only affect systemic inflammation but also induce the recruitment of different immune cells and thereby likely modulate the local immune tumor microenvironment36,37. Additionally, the expression of adhesion molecules on (activated) platelets enables the interaction with leukocytes and might also attract immune cells into the tumor microenvironment38,39.

Apart from platelets, the PDPN-specific receptor CLEC-2 was also found on murine immune cells (e.g. myeloid cells, NK cells, B cells) and particularly upregulated upon inflammatory conditions40. It was postulated that CLEC-2 signaling in murine myeloid cells might be able to regulate distinct cytokine levels (e.g. interleukin-10 [IL-10]) and thereby impact the immune system40. Another animal study reported that the interaction of PDPN on fibroblastic reticular cells with CLEC-2 on dendritic cells resulted in dendritic cell migration implying a potential involvement of the PDPN/CLEC-2 axis in recruiting immune cells41. However, CLEC-2 was only found in murine immune cells but was not detected on human leukocytes42,43. In humans, CLEC-2 was mainly described on platelets so far43. Whether CLEC-2 can also be upregulated on normal human white blood cells and whether potential interactions of CLEC-2 with tumor-expressed PDPN might directly impact systemic and local inflammation in glioma needs to be elucidated in dedicated (experimental) studies.

We also propose future studies focusing on flow cytometric analysis of immune cell subpopulations and specific lymphocyte subsets (e.g. Th1/Treg ratios) as well as on investigating distinct cytokine profiles (e.g. IL-6, TGF-β) in order to gain further insights into the underlying mechanisms of our clinical observations and to explore the complex network of different inflammatory players in molecular glioma subgroups.

Some limitations of the current study need to be addressed. First, the rather small sample size and the unequal patient numbers in glioma subgroups might have caused a lack in statistical power regarding our survival analyses. However, an imbalance of IDH mutations is expected in normal glioma populations and in line with previous reports3,44,45. Intriguingly, He et al. recently reported that PDPN overexpression in the subgroup of IDHmut astrocytoma was associated with poor prognosis as well46. In the current study, we were not able to confirm the prognostic effect of PDPN in IDHmut glioma. Due to our limited patient number, we propose future independent investigations with larger glioma subgroups in order to validate findings of our exploratory study. Second, patients included in this study were recruited from 2003 until 2014. Therefore, brain tumors were classified before the updated versions of the WHO classification system of CNS tumors1,2. Consequently, the entire molecular work-up according to the WHO2021 classification was not available in the current patient cohort. Thus, we did not provide information regarding the O6-methylguanine-DNA methyltransferase (MGMT) promoter methylation status or other potentially relevant molecular markers. However, in a previous study we found that the MGMT promoter methylation status did not correlate with local inflammation parameters, arguing that this is unlikely to impact our findings10. Also, it was reported that the systemic NLR did not differ according to the MGMT status47,48. Still, the unknown MGMT promoter methylation status also represents a potential confounder regarding our survival analyses, particularly in patients with IDH1wt glioma49.

Furthermore, we used immunohistochemical staining only to identify the IDH1 R132H mutation, which embodies by far the most frequent IDH mutation50,51. Therefore, other relevant and less common variants such as IDH1 R132C or IDH2 R172K mutations have not been detected and were excluded from our analysis50,51. Another methodological limitation is that we did not perform IDH sequencing, which might have led to misclassifications in our patient cohort. IDH sequencing is particularly recommended by the 2021 WHO classification system in patients under the age of 55 years2.

In addition, we did not obtain data regarding treatment modalities or the extent of tumor resection. Thus, we did not include these factors as potential confounders to evaluate whether PDPN is an independent prognostic factor in our glioma subgroups. However, previous studies reported that PDPN expression in glioma was associated with worse survival and these results prevailed in multivariable cox regression analyses upon adjustment for potential confounders such as tumor grade, radiochemotherapy or extent of surgical resection (subtotal tumor resection/biopsy vs. gross total resection)46,52. Moreover, we did not record the application of steroids in our glioma patient cohort, which might have potentially impacted peripheral blood levels of neutrophils and lymphocytes53. Blood parameters were analyzed via standard laboratory techniques. Thus, we are not able to provide data on subsets of lymphocytes, monocytes or neutrophils, which would give further insights into the underlying inflammatory mechanisms. We propose future prospective studies to investigate peripheral immune cell subsets with the local tumor inflammation in order to create a more specific ‘cancer immunogram’ for distinct glioma subgroups.

In conclusion, distinct inflammatory glioma subgroups showed differences in local and systemic inflammation patterns as well as overall survival. These novel insights might provide an important basis to define patient subgroups based on their inflammatory ‘fingerprint’ and thereby enable the conduction of personalized immunotherapy trials in selected patients with predicted clinical benefit based on rational biomarkers.
