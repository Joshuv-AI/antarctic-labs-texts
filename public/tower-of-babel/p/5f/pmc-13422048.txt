Should we still treat asymptomatic Neisseria gonorrhoeae and Chlamydia trachomatis contacts in the era of NAATs? Reviewing data from the GetaKit study to inform practice
Authors: Patrick O’Byrne, Lauren Orser
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesWe sought to determine positivity rates for participants in the GetaKit study who identified as asymptomatic contacts of Neisseria gonorrhoeae (NG) and/or Chlamydia trachomatis (CT). Our goal was to shed light on current discrepancies between international sexually transmitted infection (STI) guidelines, some of which recommend blanket empiric treatment for all NG and CT contacts (eg, Canadian and United States guidelines) versus others which recommend waiting for positive results (BASHH, Australian, and European).MethodsWe extracted data for the period of 1 June 2023 to 30 June 2025 from GetaKit, which is a research website through which persons in Ontario, Canada can complete an online risk assessment and obtain access to laboratory-based testing for STIs, including NG, CT, HIV, syphilis and hepatitis C. From these data, we calculated positivity rates for NG and CT and the numbers needed to treat. We also evaluated for correlations of risk factors for testing positive.ResultsDuring our 25-month study period, participants ordered NG/CT testing 12 775 times. In a total of 6.7% of these orders, participants identified as an NG and/or CT contact. Positivity rates, for all completed testing, were 1.2% (n=115 diagnoses) for NG and 4.3% (n=395 diagnoses) for CT. Positivity among asymptomatic contacts ranged from 0% for NG in heterosexual males to 30.6% for CT in females who were ≤24 years old, with most positivity rates being <20% for both infections.ConclusionsOur results suggest that blanket recommendations to treat or defer treatment seems inappropriate. More nuance is required. Empiric treatment of all NG and CT contacts will likely result in mass overuse of antibiotics. In contrast, deferring treatment seems inappropriate for asymptomatic females who are ≤24 years old and for anyone who cannot immediately obtain testing and treatment.

HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY

Blanket recommendations to treat or not treat all sexual contacts of NG or CT may not be ideal. Further research is needed to determine the replicability of the groups we identified with higher and lower positivity rates. Introduction

The clinical management of Neisseria gonorrhoeae (NG) and Chlamydia trachomatis (CT) has historically focused on detection and treatment. Detection involves testing patients with risk factors or symptoms. Treatment includes antibiotic administration to (1) symptomatic patients before test results are available, (2) patients who test positive for NG or CT and (3) the sexual partners (henceforth ‘contacts’) of anyone diagnosed with NG or CT. The Public Health Agency of Canada’s Sexually Transmitted and Blood-Borne Infections: Guides for Health Professionals (Public Health Agency of Canada, p5)1 2 states that for NG/CT, ‘empiric treatment is indicated regardless of clinical findings and without waiting for results’. The United States Centres for Disease Control (CDC) has matching recommendations in its 2021 guidelines3 for immediate empiric treatment.

In contrast, the updated 2025 British (BASHH) NG guideline (Fifer et al, p10)4 states that, ‘treatment is not needed for all sexual contacts and ideally treatment should only be given to those who test positive’. The 2025 European CT guideline (White et al, p7)5 similarly states that, ‘treating all sexual contacts empirically risks overuse of antibiotics’, as only 12%–64% of contacts test positive. Both BASHH4 and European guidelines5 do, however, recommend empiric treatment for contacts if patients are pregnant and/or there are concerns about follow-up, including geographic or psychosocial barriers to care. The Australian guidelines6 from 2024 similarly only recommend a ‘consider(ation of) presumptive treatment if there has been sexual contact within the past 2 weeks’ or if there are concerns about follow-up. More recent guidelines (from the UK,4 Europe5 and Australia6) thus recommend a test-and-wait approach, instead of immediate empiric treatment (as found in the older Canadian1 2 and US3 guidelines).

These recent changes to international guidelines regarding treatment for contacts relate to global concerns about antibiotic-resistant strains of NG4 7 and antibiotic overuse,1 5 when contacts receive unnecessary or inadequate treatment based on exposure type, particularly when they are asymptomatic. These changes also acknowledge that nucleic acid amplification testing (NAAT) has near-perfect sensitivity for NG and CT and fast turnaround times8 for results.

Considering these modifications to international recommendations,4–6 we sought to use data from a study in Ontario, Canada to discuss if Canadian guidelines should still blanket recommend empiric treatment for all NG and CT contacts, or if there are scenarios when treatment for asymptomatic contacts could be deferred until test results return from the laboratory. The main question we had was, Should we still treat all asymptomatic NG and CT contacts in the era of NAATs? To inform our analysis, we extracted data from GetaKit.ca—a study through which persons in Ontario can obtain testing for NG, CT, HIV, syphilis and hepatitis C by completing an online risk assessment and then visiting a laboratory for specimen collection. Using data from this platform, we analysed the number of participants who (1) indicated they were an NG and/or CT contact, (2) reported symptoms, (3) completed testing and (4) tested positive. While we have reported on GetaKit previously,9 this is our first publication focusing on data about NG and CT contacts, diagnosis and treatment.

Methods

GetaKit is a prospective observational open cohort study through which persons in the Canadian province of Ontario who are ≥17 years old and sexually active can complete an online risk assessment and, when eligible, obtain STI testing at no cost. This testing includes urine samples and pharyngeal and rectal swabs for NG and CT, serology for HIV, syphilis and hepatitis C, and self-tests for HIV. Testing through GetaKit follows Ontario guidelines,10 such that, for NG and CT, genital testing is offered to all sexually active persons and extragenital (ie, pharyngeal and rectal) testing is only offered to sexually active persons who engage in sex work or identify as men who have sex with men (MSM) or transwomen. Clinician-developed computer algorithms11 process participants’ risk information in real-time to immediately offer individualised test lists to eligible participants. Recruitment occurred through posters, social media and public health unit websites.

Before participants can complete testing through GetaKit, a nurse reviews each request. When participants decline recommended testing but self-report being a contact or having symptoms, nurses contact the participant to review and add relevant tests if consent is obtained. Once nurses approve the order, participants receive requisitions that are mailed with swabs, available for download in the participant’s online account, or faxed to a laboratory. Participants then complete self-swabs and/or provide a urine sample at the laboratory, where serology is also drawn when clinically indicated and consented to. Requisitions are valid for 8 weeks from the order date and laboratories are open 6 days per week on a walk-in basis with fees covered under all health plans. Persons without insurance can visit an STI clinic in Ottawa for free specimen collection and testing. Within <24 hours to 2 days, laboratories report results to GetaKit, and participants are sent a message to view their results in their GetaKit accounts. Participants with positive results receive free treatment from their public health units within two business days, with >90% of treatment being administered within 24 hours of receipt of a positive test result.

Pharyngeal and rectal specimens are tested for NG/CT using the Roche Cobas assay,8 with sensitivities of 100% for pharyngeal NG/CT, 95.1% for rectal CT and 99.0% for rectal NG. Specificities are 98.9% for pharyngeal NG and 99.8% for pharyngeal CT. For rectal infections, specificities are 99.3% for NG and 99.2% for CT. Urine samples are analysed using the Hologic Aptima NAAT,12 with sensitivities of 100% for NG and 99.7% for CT. Both the Roche Cobas and Hologic Aptima NAAT concurrently analyse for NG and CT. To reduce the risk of false positive results, all positive results are reflexively retested immediately. Results that are concordantly positive on both analyses are reported by the laboratory as positive. Those that are positive on a first test and negative on the second test are reported as inconclusive, with the recommendation for submission of a new sample for retesting.

Data collection occurred via the GetaKit website, with registration, risk factor and diagnostic outcomes being logged online. See table 1 for full details. For this analysis, we extracted data from 1 June 2023 (date of launching NG/CT testing via GetaKit) to 30 June 2025. Data analysis involved descriptive analysis of variables (counts, frequencies and means) and χ2 calculations using a p value of 0.05 to determine significance. We calculated a number-needed-to-treat (NNT) for contacts by dividing the total number of contacts in a group by the number of diagnoses within that group; we rounded these numbers up to the closest whole number because we cannot treat partial patients.

We defined ‘contacts’ as any participant who self-identified as an NG and/or CT contact in the online risk assessment. See table 2 for question wording. We classified ethnicity according to Statistics Canada13 and classified sex based on reported genitals (external genitals as ‘male’ and internal genitals as ‘female’). Following local public health surveillance practices, we counted a participant as an NG or CT case if they tested positive on any test; the same infection at multiple sites in the same testing episode counted as a single case.

Results

Overall Uptake

From 1 June 2023 to 30 June 2025, participants ordered NG/CT testing 12 775 times (including those who reported being a contact or not, and who reported being symptomatic or asymptomatic). In 6.5% (n=836/12 775) of these orders, participants reported being an NG and/or CT contact: 2.3% (n=292/12 775) were an NG contact, 5.4% (n=692/12 775) were a CT contact and 1.2% were an NG and CT contact (n=148/12 775).

Test completion rates

Of all orders placed, 72.2% (n=9219/12 775) resulted in completed testing. Testing completion was 73.8% (n=617/836) for participants who were contacts (n=215 for NG, n=504 for CT). There were no differences for testing completion among those who reported being an NG contact (71.0%) or CT contact (71.1%), compared to those who did not report being an NG contact (71.4%) or CT contact (71.4%). Notably, all orders involving participants who reported being an asymptomatic NG contact (n=190) or CT contact (n=436) were completed. All missed testing among reported NG and CT contacts occurred in participants who had symptoms—suggesting they sought care for their symptoms elsewhere.

Participant demographics

Orders from participants who reported being an NG and/or CT contact were an average of 30 years old, primarily white (56.3%), cis male (61%) and heterosexual (64.5%). See table 3 for full demographic information.

Positivity Rates

Positivity rates for all orders were 1.2% (n=115/9219) for NG and 4.3% (n=395/9219) for CT. For orders where participants reported being an NG contact, the positivity rate was 8.4% (n=18/215), while the positivity rate was 19.4% (n=98/504) for orders from those who identified as CT contacts. Of the 35 participants who were an NG and/or CT contact and who reported sex work, all who tested positive for NG or CT identified as MSM.

Focusing on our question, should we treat asymptomatic NG/CT contacts, we calculated the positivity rates for orders involving participants who reported being contacts and denied symptoms: the positivity rate for NG contacts was 7.9% (n=15/190), compared with 1.0% (n=81/8151) for non-contacts; the positivity rate for CT contacts was 18.1% (n=79/436), compared with 3.3% (n=262/7905) for non-contacts. Comparison of positivity rates

χ2 analyses identified that the difference in positivity rates for all reported NG contacts (8.4%) compared with all non-contacts (1.1%) was significant (χ2=90.71, p<0.001). Significance also emerged for positivity rates for all CT contacts (19.4%), compared with all non-contacts (3.4%) (χ2=298.77, p<0.001). This finding held true for orders involving participants who were asymptomatic, where 7.9% of asymptomatic NG contacts tested positive compared with 1.0% of asymptomatic non-contacts, and 18.1% of asymptomatic CT contacts compared with 3.3% of asymptomatic non-contacts (NG: χ2=77.72, p<0.001; CT: χ2=230.98, p<0.001).

We also assessed for possible demographic differences in diagnoses among contacts, looking at age (comparing ≤24 year old to >24 year old) and sex (comparing males to females). For age, the positivity rate among NG contacts was 11.3% for ≤24 years old, compared with 7.4% for >24 years old—which was not significant (χ2=0.797, p=0.37). In contrast, age was significant for CT. For CT contacts, participants ≤24 years old had a positivity rate of 27.5%, compared with 14.1% in >24 years old (χ2=13.74, p<0.001). Sex did not show significance between males and females. For NG contacts, 8.9% of males compared with 6.5% of females tested positive (χ2=0.26, p=0.61). For CT contacts, 16.8% of males compared with 23.4% of females tested positive (χ2=3.3, p=0.069).

Knowing that age correlated for CT contacts, but sex did not, we did within-sex analyses stratified by age and symptom status. Asymptomatic females ≤24 years old who were CT contacts had a positivity rate of 30.6%, compared with 5.4% in non-contacts (χ2=74.97, p<0.001). Similarly, asymptomatic male CT contacts ≤24 years old had a positivity rate of 22.8%, compared with 5.1% for non-contacts (χ2=35.34, p<0.001). For asymptomatic female CT contacts, the positivity rate was 30.6% in participants ≤24 years old, compared with 14.8% in >24 years olds (χ2=5.84, p=0.015). For asymptomatic male CT contacts, the positivity rate was 23.4% in those ≤24 years old, compared with 12.0% in >24 years olds (χ2=5.44, p=0.02).

Lastly, we reviewed sexual orientation in male participants to see if there were significant findings. For asymptomatic male NG contacts, the positivity rate was 11.5% (n=13/113) in MSM, compared with 0% (n=0/39) in heterosexual males (χ2=4.91, p<0.027). No male NG contact, irrespective of sexual orientation, who was >46 years old (n=24) tested positive for NG. The 13 infections in MSM were extragenital only (ie, identified in the pharynx and/or rectum, but not in urine). For asymptomatic CT contacts, we found a higher positivity rate in heterosexual males (18.8%), compared with MSM (7.9%) (χ2=5.52, p=0.019). Of note, for all asymptomatic MSM (NG/CT contacts or not), 88.5% (n=69/78) of NG infections were extragenital only, and 68.1% (n=47/69) of CT infections were extragenital only.

Discussion

In this paper, we reviewed data from the GetaKit study from 1 June 2023 to 30 June 2025. For this period, we reported data on NG/CT testing, including risk factors, if participants were self-identified NG or CT contacts or had NG/CT-like symptoms, and test completion rates and results. Our goal was to determine if being an asymptomatic NG or CT contact should warrant empiric treatment, as recommended in Canadian1 2 and US3 guidelines, or if it makes more sense to follow the newer BASHH,4 European5 and Australian6 guidelines, which recommend waiting for test results before administering treatment (barring pregnancy or barriers to follow-up).

In our study, we had 12 775 orders for NG/CT testing, with ~73% of these orders for testing being completed overall. Notably, all orders for testing were completed by participants who identified as asymptomatic NG and CT contacts (n=190 and n=436, respectively). Positivity rates were 1.2% for NG and 4.3% for CT overall, increasing to 8.4% for NG contacts and 19.4% for CT contacts. Sex did not correlate with positive results, but age did for CT contacts who were ≤24 years old. Our highest positivity rates were in CT contacts (20%–30%) who were ≤24 years old and lowest (0%) in asymptomatic heterosexual male NG contacts and males >46 years old. As well, most infections in MSM were extragenital. These results raise a few points for discussion.

First, our data suggest that a blanket approach to treating all asymptomatic NG and CT contacts may not be best-case. While Canadian1 2 and US3 guidelines recommend empiric treatment of all contacts, our results identified that this would result in non-judicious antibiotic administration. In our study, this applied more strongly to NG contacts, with the most notable situation being asymptomatic heterosexual males and males >46 years old, of whom none was diagnosed with NG. Empirically treating both groups would have resulted in the administration of antibiotics to 63 persons for NG, without having treated a single infection. Asymptomatic female NG contacts also had low rates of diagnosis (at 5.3%), which meant that, to have treated one NG case, we would have had to administer antibiotics to 19 persons.

Our results align with international literature. One study14 from the Netherlands identified a positivity rate of 34.2% for CT contacts in their STI clinics. The same findings emerged in a study from Seattle,15 which found that the cumulative NG/CT positivity rate in their STI clinics was 34% between 2014 and 2018. Another study16 from Australia, involving 100 male patients who were NG and/or CT contacts in an STI clinic, found that ‘overall, 80% of patients were incorrectly treated (5% received the wrong treatment and 75% received unnecessary treatment)’.

In the context of globally worsening antimicrobial resistance for many organisms,7 our results make us question the appropriateness of immediately treating all NG contacts, when almost all positivity rates were <10%. Our findings of NG contacts instead support the BASHH NG guideline,4 which recommends awaiting positive lab results, especially considering that genital NG infections are almost always symptomatic for males and often symptomatic for females.2

Our findings did, however, identify an important nuance that is part of the recommendation to wait for test results: clinicians must ensure patients complete all clinically indicated testing. Current BASHH,4 European5 and Canadian1 2 guidelines indicate that MSM should collect samples at pharyngeal and rectal sites, in addition to via urine, especially when they are NG or CT contacts. In asymptomatic MSM who were NG contacts in our study, all NG infections were extragenital, as were most (n=5/7) CT infections. Aligning with our previous work,17 from all testing in our MSM participants (contacts and non-contacts), 88.5% of NG and 68.1% of CT infections were exclusively extragenital, meaning that urine-only testing would have missed most diagnoses. To consider deferring treatment for NG contacts, especially for MSM, clinicians must ensure comprehensive testing is completed. Otherwise, empiric treatment should be considered.

Our data similarly make us question if nuance is needed regarding the European CT guideline,5 which has adopted a blanket approach to withhold treatment for asymptomatic CT contacts unless there are reasons suggesting patient follow-up may be compromised. Our findings from ≤24 years old asymptomatic female CT contacts, who had a positivity rate of 30.6%, suggest that this no-treatment strategy might not always be ideal. For these participants, the NNT was 4, compared with 19 for asymptomatic female NG contacts, yielding a more judicious use of antibiotics. Nevertheless, an NNT of 4 still means giving antibiotics to four patients to treat one infection. When clinicians are confident that a patient will quickly complete testing and promptly return for treatment, deferring antibiotic administration can be appropriate. However, if rapid access to testing and treatment cannot be guaranteed, more consideration should be given to empirically treat ≤24 years old asymptomatic female CT contacts, especially considering the lower NNT and risks of non-treatment, including pelvic inflammatory disease.18 Similar results were observed in the UK, where ‘in those aged under 25 years, over half of contacts had chlamydia infection’.19 The main point we infer from our results is that a nuanced approach is needed, not blanket recommendations to treat all contacts or to not treat any contacts.

Second, our results highlight that the web-based STI testing strategy we implemented and evaluated in Ontario, Canada was successful at offering access to testing. Over a 25-month period, 12 775 orders were placed for NG/CT testing, resulting in the diagnosis and treatment of 115 persons with gonorrhoea and 395 persons with chlamydia. As noted, on average, laboratory turnaround times for NG/CT results were <24 hours to 2 days, and access to treatment occurred almost entirely within 1 day. Considering increasing NG and CT rates worldwide20 and general restrictions in access to healthcare and sexual healthcare, novel assessment, testing and treatment strategies like GetaKit are warranted. Importantly, the rapid access to care that GetaKit provided also suggests that interventions like it are an important component of the new BASHH4 and European5 NG/CT guidelines. Deferring treatment until results are available is feasible, if (and likely only if) persons can rapidly obtain accurate testing and treatment. We are less certain our results would support deferred treatment if less readily available and less sensitive testing was used or if timelines for accessing testing and treatment were longer. In such instances, clinical decision-making may differ from what we found. Further research is needed in different contexts.

Limitations

Our results must be interpreted considering certain limitations. First, our study only occurred in Ontario, Canada—with a single payer public healthcare system that provides free STI testing and treatment. Results may have differed if participants had to pay for care. Second, all our participants self-identified as an NG and/or CT contact. While we attempted clarity in the question table 2, it is possible that some may have misinterpreted what constituted a ‘contact’ or misrepresented being a contact to obtain testing. Third, we did not provide extragenital testing to all participants but followed local guidelines.10 Although most research shows little yield for extragenital testing in other populations,20 21 additional cases may have been identified. Whether these infections would have been among contacts or non-contacts or both requires further research. Fourth, we did not collect information on the timing of exposure to testing for NG and CT contacts. It is possible that some people tested too soon after an exposure, which may have artificially decreased the positivity rates we observed. Fifth, there is a possibility of false positive results for pharyngeal NG using the Roche Cobas system, which has a specificity of 98.9%. However, we feel this would have been minimal, at only a rate of 1 per 100 and and even less due to our laboratory reprocessing all positive test results twice. Sixth, we did not have data on whether our participants had used doxycycline postexposure prophylaxis (doxyPEP), which may have muted the positivity rates and NNTs we calculated for CT22 and supported a test-and-wait approach, rather than empiric treatment of contacts. Again, further research is required.

Conclusions

In this paper, we presented data from GetaKit, which included 12 775 orders for NG/CT testing and identified 115 NG infections and 395 CT infections over a 25-month period. Unsurprisingly, NG and CT contacts were more likely to test positive, compared with non-contacts. Our results suggest it is appropriate to withhold treatment for asymptomatic contacts, but only because we were able to ensure rapid access to three free items: (1) highly sensitive NAATs, as clinically indicated, for all-sites testing (pharyngeal, rectal, urine), (2) laboratory-based testing with walk-in access 6 days per week and (3) treatment within 24 hours of a positive test result. We feel that future implementations and expansions of systems like GetaKit must maintain this level of access to achieve the results we observed. If such implementation is possible, then perhaps we can strike a balance between identifying and treating many infections and being good stewards of antibiotics. In the absence of the rapid access we created, we are concerned that the latest guidelines may leave people with undiagnosed and untreated infections. Now is the time to test novel ways to safely and effectively implement the newer BASHH, European and Australian guidelines. It also appears to be time to update Canadian and US guidelines to remove recommendations for blanket treatment of NG and CT contacts. In short, our answer is, in the era of NAATs, we should not empirically treat all NG and CT contacts.
