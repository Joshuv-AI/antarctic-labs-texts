Dosimetric Comparison Between Brass Aperture-Enhanced Proton Pencil Beam Scanning and Photon Stereotactic Radiosurgery for Cerebral Arteriovenous Malformations
Authors: Will Sperduto, Mattison J Flakus, Ed Clouser, Sujay A Vora, Jonathan B Ashman, Fan Yang, Pedro R Lara, Bernard R Bendok, Chandan Krishna, Abhijith R Bathini, Martin Bues
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe current study aimed to quantify potential dosimetric advantages of a novel, brass aperture-enhanced, proton-based stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) technique compared to conventional photon linac-based SRS in patients with cerebral arteriovenous malformations (AVMs).Materials and MethodsA retrospective review was performed of patients treated with proton SRS for cerebral AVMs at our institution between 2023 and 2025. Volumetric modulated arc therapy linac-based SRS plans were generated for dosimetric comparison and normalized to match the clinical target volume (CTV) prescription coverage of the delivered proton plan. SRS plan quality was evaluated by Radiation Therapy Oncology Group conformity and homogeneity indices (CI, HI). The V12 Gy representing the normal brain volume receiving ≥ 12 Gy including the CTV (total brain) was measured.ResultsFourteen patients were included, and most had no prior hemorrhage (64%), embolization (79%), or AVM-directed radiotherapy (86%). Three patients received staged radiation, totaling 17 treatment plans for review. Mean AVM size was 8.8 cc (0.4-30.6). SRS dose ranged from 17 to 20 Gy with 3 larger AVMs treated using volumetric staging. For the 4 AVMs ≤2.0 cc in size, the average CI was 1.89 and 1.81 for photons and protons, respectively. The average CI for AVMs >2 cc was 1.15 and 1.17 for photons and protons, respectively. A statistically significant difference was observed with HI (P = .0034) and V12 Gy (P = .0017). Fourteen of 17 (82.4%) HI were lower with protons. There was on average a 5.86 cc improvement in V12 Gy with protons.ConclusionBrass aperture-enhanced proton PBS SRS improved V12 Gy in 15 of 17 plans (88.2%), including small volume AVMs and AVMs close to the brainstem. These findings are hypothesis-generating and must be interpreted cautiously. Maturation of our clinical outcome data (AVM obliteration and symptomatic radionecrosis rates) will yield further insights.Keywords: Proton PBS SRS, Cerebral AVMs, Benign

Introduction

Intracranial arteriovenous malformations (AVMs) are tangled clusters of abnormal connections between arteries and veins and can be found in approximately 1 in 100,000 individuals.1 AVMs pose a hemorrhage risk of 2% to 3% per year, an even higher recurrent hemorrhage risk, and a mortality risk with initial rupture as high as 10%.2, 3 Proper management of AVMs requires collaboration between neurosurgery and radiation oncology. To guide treatment decision making and predict surgical risk of morbidity and mortality, Spetzler and Martin designed a grading system based upon AVM size, depth of venous drainage pattern, and eloquence of adjacent brain regions.4 A stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS)-based grading system—using patient age, AVM volume, and location—was also designed and strongly correlated with obliteration rate after single-session SRS.5 Microsurgery and embolization are other treatment options for accessible AVMs.6 For inoperable cases, photon SRS is a well-established alternative that achieves obliteration rates of 40.9%-93% and has demonstrated significantly reduced hemorrhage rates by 54% during the typically 2-to-3-year latency period and by 88% after obliteration.7, 8

SRS induces smooth muscle proliferation and extracellular collagen accumulation, which causes intimal thickening, thrombosis, and ultimately vascular lumen occlusion.9 Months to years following SRS, patients unfortunately may develop radionecrosis—a local inflammatory reaction due to tissue death commonly associated with edema. Predictive models have found higher likelihood of post-SRS symptomatic radionecrosis to be associated with larger AVM volume, higher marginal dose, larger V12 Gy (volume of uninvolved brain tissue including AVM target receiving ≥ 12 Gy), AVM location, higher Spetzler-Martin grade, and higher SRS-based grade.10, 11

Historically, photon SRS was the predominant radiation modality for treating cerebral AVMs; however, as proton therapy was developed, passive scatter proton SRS became a well-established treatment for inoperable cases.12, 13, 14, 15 Due to the physical properties of protons, proton SRS may potentially reduce V12 Gy and therefore the risk of symptomatic radionecrosis. The aim of this study was to quantify potential dosimetric advantages of a novel, brass aperture-enhanced, proton-based SRS, which combines the advantages of pencil beam scanning proton beam therapy with the additional beam shaping made possible by collimating apertures, compared to conventional photon linac-based SRS in patients with cerebral AVMs.

Materials and methods

We performed an IRB-approved retrospective review of 14 patients treated from 2023 to 2025 with proton SRS for cerebral AVMs at our institution. Each patient had 4 3- or 4-mm titanium screws placed into the skull as fiducial markers. All patients were simulated in a supine, arms down position with rigid thermoplastic mask immobilization. The CT simulation scan was registered to the most recent brain magnetic resonance angiogram (MRA) without intravenous contrast and gadolinium Stealth MRI. MRA and MRI slice thicknesses were 0.5 and 1.0 mm, respectively. 2D orthogonal x-ray angiograms were also used in delineating the target. The registration of the 2D x-ray angiograms with other 3D images was carried out using the Elements software package (Brainlab, Munich, Germany). Target volumes were delineated in-person by the treating radiation oncologist and collaborating neurosurgeon.

Proton SRS was delivered via the Hitachi ProBeat system with brass aperture-enhanced, spot scanning. A uniform relative biological effectiveness of 1.1 was employed. A 4 cm water-equivalent thickness range shifter, integrated into the SRS assembly upstream of the aperture, was employed only as needed for superficially located AVMs. Treatment plans were generated using single- and multi-field optimization (SFO and MFO). For each patient, custom field-specific brass apertures were designed during planning to conform to the target while blocking OARs, as shown in Figure 1. Apertures were manufactured by DotDecimal (Sanford, Florida, United States). RayStation software (RaySearch Laboratories, Stockholm, Sweden) was used for proton treatment planning. For all cases, SFO or MFO, robust optimization accounted for 1 mm of patient setup uncertainty (using titanium fiducials) and ±3% of range uncertainty (based on CT HU to stopping power estimates). A dedicated applicator for proton SRS was attached to the proton nozzle, which held the brass aperture with its downstream face 15 cm from the isocenter. Our institutional single-fraction brainstem constraint was Dmax <15 Gy.16 Linear energy transfer (LET) was carefully evaluated in all cases. LET was reviewed after treatment planning in our earliest cases and optimized during treatment planning in later cases due to LET optimization software becoming available. All final proton plans satisfied the LET < 6 keV/um criterion within the 50% isodose volume. Dose-averaged LET was measured by RayStation software and double-checked by our in-house treatment planning software, Shiva.17, 18 Large AVMs (>10 cc) were planned with volumetric staging, with sections treated at 3-month intervals. Proton treatment plans were completed and underwent quality assurance.19

Beam’s eye view of proton SRS treatment with brass aperture open on the target and closed over OARs. Stage 1 CTV (blue), Stage 2 CTV (green), brainstem (red), left optic nerve (yellow), and right optic nerve (pink). Position: Table-180°, Gantry-0°.

Volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT) linac-based SRS plans were retrospectively generated in Varian Eclipse v15.6 by a medical physicist for dosimetric comparison. A standard arc arrangement was used for all VMAT-based plans utilizing the Varian Eclipse HyperArc templates, which included 1 full arc at table 0°, and 3 non-coplanar 180-° arcs at table angles 45°, 315°, and 270°. The dose calculation anisotropic analytical algorithm version 15.6.06 was used with a calculation grid size of 0.125 cm. The photon plan was normalized to match the clinical target volume (CTV) prescription coverage to that of the delivered proton plan. Prescription isodose lines were determined by target proximity to adjacent organs at risk and ranged from 83% to 100%. The contours of the stages of the AVM were reduced by 1 mm in the direction they would have touched another stage, which partially reduced the total dose in the overlap region. These overlap regions were allowed to extend to doses in the 140–150% range on photon plans. These values were derived from the hotspots of techniques such as GammaKnife, which could reach 200%.

To evaluate the quality of SRS plans, the Radiation Therapy Oncology Group (RTOG) conformity and homogeneity indices (CI and HI) were calculated.20 CI was defined as VRI / TV, where VRI was the entire volume receiving prescription dose and TV was the target volume. HI was defined as Imax / RI where Imax was the maximum dose to the target, and RI was the prescription dose. The optimal value for CI and HI was 1.0. Dose conformity to the target was the highest priority when OARs were at least 5 mm from a target. When an OAR was closer than 5 mm, the management of the dose to that structure was at the discretion of the physician and could vary from patient to patient based on other factors specific to them. Although CI and HI were used to assess plans within a specific patient to determine which plan to use, a global value was not used in a pass/fail type criteria because of the geometrically irregular nature of AVM targets. To evaluate normal tissue sparing, the total brain V12 Gy was reported. Total brain V12 Gy represented the total brain volume receiving ≥ 12 Gy, including the target volume (CTV). V12 Gy absolute difference values were calculated as photon-proton. Our dataset included 17 treatment plans from 14 patients (as 3 patients had 2-staged treatments). Using the treatment plan as the unit of analysis, paired, 2 tailed t tests were performed to determine statistically significant differences between groups. Statistical significance was defined as a P value < .05.

Results

Fourteen patients with cerebral AVMs were treated with proton SRS and included in our analysis. Patient ages ranged from 8 to 69. Most patients had no prior hemorrhage (64%), embolization (79%), or AVM-directed radiotherapy (86%). All AVMs were intracranial and assigned Spetzler-Martin grades—grade 1 (4), grade 2 (3), grade 3 (2), grade 4 (3), and grade 5 (2). Average AVM volume was 8.8 cc. SRS prescription dose ranged from 17 to 20 Gy, with 3 larger AVMs treated using volumetric staging. Patient, AVM, and treatment characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Conformity indices (CI) ranged from 0.68 to 2.83 and 0.68 to 2.38 for photon and proton plans, respectively. Table 2 compares various dosimetric parameters between photons and protons. For the 4 AVMs ≤ 2 cc in size, the average CI was 1.89 and 1.81 for photons and protons, respectively. The average CI for AVMs > 2 cc in size was 1.15 and 1.17 for photons and protons, respectively. The 2 cc cutoff was selected based on generally observing considerably higher CIs with our smallest AVMs (<2 cc). For the 3 patients who received 2-stage SRS for large AVMs (>19 cc), the average CI was 1.11 and 1.07 for photons and protons, respectively. No statistically significant difference in CI was identified (Table 2). Fourteen of 17 (82.4%) homogeneity indices were lower with protons. V12 Gy was reduced with protons in 15 of 17 (88.2%) treatment plans. The 2 cases where photons achieved lower V12 Gy were not AVMs near the brainstem. These 2 cases had large central axis air gaps of 8.29 and 8.72 cm. The V12 Gy absolute difference was decreased, on average, by 5.86 cc with protons. Four of the 5 proton plans for AVMs within 3.5 mm of the brainstem met the institutional single-fraction SRS constraint of Dmax < 15 Gy; the 1 outlier achieved a Dmax of 15.22 Gy. Results of the paired, 2 tailed t tests comparing photon versus proton CI, HI, and V12 Gy values are found in Table 2. A statistically significant difference was observed with HI (P = .0034) and V12 Gy (P = .0017), favoring protons.

Discussion

Intracranial AVMs are a benign entity, but without proper treatment, considerable morbidity and mortality can occur. For inoperable cases, SRS is a reasonable, noninvasive alternative with favorable outcomes in terms of high obliteration rates and low risk of long-term toxicities.6 Flickinger et al. analyzed 307 patients with AVMs who were treated with Gamma Knife SRS at the University of Pittsburgh from 1987 to 1993.21 Multivariate logistic regression generated risk prediction curves, revealing that a V12 Gy of 10 cc, 20 cc, and 30 cc correlated approximately with symptomatic radionecrosis rates of 9%, 18%, and 35%, respectively.21 An International Gamma Knife Research Foundation multicenter retrospective analysis of 2236 patients with cerebral AVMs treated with GK-SRS between 1988 and 2013 reported a symptomatic radionecrosis (RN) rate of 9.7%.22 Post-SRS symptomatic RN rates appear similar between children (ages 2-15) and adults (ages 17-70) and are associated with AVM size: <10 cc (0%-20%) and >10 cc (15%-40%).23, 24

A growing body of evidence is emerging that high LET within the high dose region can also contribute to brain necrosis.25, 26, 27, 28 While Levegrun et al. suggests there is minimal difference in the predictive power for symptomatic radionecrosis between V12 Gy and marginal V12 Gy values, Flickinger et al found that V12 Gy was a better predictor of RN than marginal V12 Gy.21, 29 Continued surveillance of our cohort with magnetic resonance imaging, clinical exams, and angiography will determine if the proton SRS average reduction in V12 Gy (5.86 cc) carries clinical significance. The patient from Figure 3A and B with challenging anatomy may represent a clinically relevant scenario where photon VMAT SRS cannot readily achieve an acceptable plan, but brass aperture-enhanced proton PBS SRS may provide a feasible treatment option. This interpretation should be interpreted with caution, as photon plan quality depends on available technology, planning approach, and institutional expertise. Target size and nidus shape contribute to how much dose spills from the target into the normal brain. Inclusion of such a challenging photon case could influence the magnitude of the average V12 Gy benefit seen with protons. Notably, the 2 instances where photons achieved lower V12 Gy than protons were the 2 cases that required the 4 cm water-equivalent thickness range shifter due to AVMs being superficially located near the skull. The range shifter inherently increased the spot size and lateral penumbra. The dosimetric differences in these 2 early cases, where photons achieved lower V12 Gy, were most likely attributed to large (8.29 and 8.72 cm) central axis air gaps. As our proton SRS practice evolved, planners began to routinely add additional isocenters to reduce air gaps. Treating superficial AVMs, with a range shifter and our novel brass aperture-enhanced technique, may require other planning considerations: trialing SFO vs MFO, adding additional fields, or introducing another isocenter to reduce the air gap between the aperture and the patient to help mitigate lateral penumbra.

Publications investigating brass aperture-enhanced proton SRS for treating cerebral AVMs are limited and results vary. A small University of Florida series comparing VMAT-based SRS to passive scatter proton SRS with brass collimation, including 3 cerebral AVMs (5.02-10.04 cc), reported VMAT achieved lower HIs and V12 Gy (2.05 cc lower on average).30 Whether these results truly contradict our findings is challenging to determine given that Boczkowski et al had a small sample size, used passive scatter (not PBS) proton therapy, and AVM characteristics (size, shape, location) differed. A Taiwanese cohort comparing PBS proton SRS with and without customized brass apertures, including 6 cerebral AVMs (0.55-21.53 cc), found brass apertures significantly enhanced homogeneity and conformity indices, sharpened dose fall-off, and improved normal tissue sparing.31 Our experience suggests brass aperture-enhanced PBS may improve HI and V12 Gy.

Protons provide a distinct dosimetric advantage of having no exit dose and achieve 2 to 3 times lower integral dose (total body energy deposition) compared to 3D-conformal and intensity-modulated radiation therapy.32 Especially for patients with a benign condition (like AVMs) who generally have long life expectancies, lower integral dose may be important to reduce radiation-related late effects. With the advent of pencil beam scanning (PBS), further advantages have been realized over passive scatter techniques. PBS delivers dose as a series of small spots. This approach enables simultaneous intensity and range modulation and offers both distal and proximal dose conformity, whereas passive scatter approaches sacrifice proximal dose conformity. Furthermore, PBS allows for the optimization of LET. The inclusion of patient-specific brass apertures allows sharper penumbra. In the case of large volume AVMs, the reduced penumbra plays a role in reducing the brain V12 Gy, and therefore the risk of symptomatic radionecrosis. Follow-up studies of patients treated with proton SRS are needed to quantify any realized toxicity risk.

Limitations

Planning comparison studies between proton and photon techniques have several limitations. Treatment planning is highly dependent upon planner expertise and available technology. One limitation is that our analysis was conducted at the treatment plan level and not the patient level, with 3 patients receiving staged treatments and contributing 2 plans. Due to the small sample size, plans for these 3 patients were not clustered, which may underestimate the variance and thus increase the chance of Type I error. Future studies with larger sample sizes should account for this clustering by using more advanced statistical models like mixed-effects models or generalized estimating equations. Analysis of our small sample size is susceptible to selection bias. The novelty of our approach may not be generalizable to other proton SRS programs. Another limitation is properly accounting for the prescription volume. Proton therapy typically prescribes to the CTV with robustness criteria for setup and range uncertainty achieved by using an optimization target volume. As proton SRS evolved at our institution, the practice shifted from prescribing to the CTV to the optimization target volume. This is different than the conventional margin expansion from CTV to the planning target volume used in photon planning. Normalization enables adequate comparison between photon and proton plans and is crucial for plan evaluation but challenging when doses are prescribed to different volumes depending on the delivery technique. For the current study, CTV coverage was matched, but these nuances make it difficult to reliably interpret CI and HI.

Conclusion

Proton SRS aided by the inclusion of custom brass apertures yielded an improved plan in terms of V12 Gy in 15 of 17 cases (88.2%), including small volume AVMs and ones adjacent to the brainstem. There was on average a 5.86 cc improvement in V12 Gy as compared to photon-based SRS. These findings are hypothesis-generating and must be interpreted cautiously. For superficial AVMs, other planning techniques (SFO vs MFO, adding additional fields, introducing another isocenter) should be investigated. Maturation of our clinical outcome data, such as AVM obliteration and symptomatic radionecrosis, will yield further insights.
