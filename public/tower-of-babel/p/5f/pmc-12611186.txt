Intramural Blood Vessels as a Primary Site of Vascular LUTS
Authors: Hikaru Hashitani, Retsu Mitsui
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIschaemia has been considered a primary cause of lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS). The existing concept is that ischaemic LUTS develops subsequent to structural narrowing of feeder arteries of the LUT due to atherosclerosis. However, the distribution of blood flow within each LUT organ that is regulated by intramural microvasculature should also be considered. Thus, the blood supply of the mucosal and detrusor smooth muscle (DSM) in the bladder and the blood flow of the mucosal, smooth muscle and striated muscle in the urethra need to be adjusted to meet their differing energy consumption. Sympathetic overdrive that is commonly seen in aged populations and patients with metabolic syndrome enhances arteriolar constrictions resulting in a disturbed intramural flow distribution so that cell populations with a higher energy demand are more readily affected. In addition to endothelial nitric oxide (NO) that plays a pivotal role in regulating vasocontractility, NO released from perivascular parasympathetic nerves appears to counteract sympathetic activity (sympatholysis) in the LUT. Thus, any diminished neuronally released NO would cause sympathetic overactivity. Capillary rarefaction, the reduced density/function of capillaries, the site of blood‐tissue exchange, would also be critically involved in the pathogenesis of LUTS. In the bladder, capillary pericytes appear to function as pacemaker cells driving arteriolar vasomotion facilitating capillary perfusion and may also play a role in maintaining suburothelial homeostasis. Considering the fundamental roles of the intramural microvasculature in maintaining LUT functions, enhanced NO‐mediated sympatholysis and/or capillary revascularization could have therapeutic and preventive potential for the ischaemic LUT.

Introduction

The prevalence of lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) increases with age [1] and closely correlates with obesity [2, 3], metabolic syndrome [4, 5] or cardiovascular diseases [6], and therefore LUTS has been considered as a manifestation of systemic vascular dysfunction [7, 8, 9]. The existing concept of ischaemic LUTS is that the bladder or urethral dysfunction results from structural narrowing of their feeder arteries (Figure 1A), for example, internal iliac, pudendal arteries or vaginal arteries and their branches due to atherosclerosis primarily arising from endothelial dysfunction associated with the impaired release of endogenous vasoprotective factors such as nitric oxide (NO) [10]. Consequently, ischaemic overactive bladder (OAB) animal models were created by inducing endothelial injury in iliac arteries with or without high‐fat diets [11, 12]. However, in a rat model of chronic bladder ischaemia, application of tadalafil, a phosphodiesterase type 5 (PDE5) inhibitor (PDE5I) [12], silodosin, an α1A‐adrenoceptor antagonist [13] or mirabegron, a β3‐adrenoceptor agonist [14] improves functional and morphological changes in the bladder without preventing or reversing luminal occlusion of the iliac arteries. This suggests that the common site of action of these drugs is the intramural microvasculature within the bladder wall. Thus, it was envisaged that intramural microvasculature, including arterioles, capillaries and venules, could be a primarily site as well as therapeutic target of vascular LUTS (Figure 1A).

Intramural microvasculature in the LUT as a site of vascular LUTS. Feeder arteries regulate the blood flow to the bladder and urethra, while the blood distribution to different layers in each LUT organ is directed by intramural microvasculature. The balance between protective and risk factors determines the vasocontractility, in turn the blood supply to and within the LUT organs. Risk factors are associated with sympathetic overdrive and reduced NO production resulting in excessive vasoconstrictions. Protective factors reduces sympathetic activity and enhances NO production to maintain vasodilatory state.

Intramural arterioles regulate the distribution of blood supply within the LUT. In the bladder, the blood distribution in the mucosa is maximal during urine storage phages corresponding to a high energy demand of continuously‐active mucosal components [15, 16]. In contrast, the blood flow in detrusor smooth muscle (DSM) must be increased during the voiding phase. In the urethra, blood supply to the mucosal, smooth muscle and striated muscle layers should also meet the energy consumption of each layer. High‐resolution imaging of the urethral vasculature has demonstrated that the mucosa of the human female urethra possesses a richer blood flow than its muscle layer [17]. Thus, besides structural narrowing of the LUT feeder arteries, a functional narrowing of intramural arterioles should also be considered as a cause of ischaemic LUTS.

Arteriolar contractility is predominantly regulated by perivascular neural activity, particularly sympathetic vasoconstrictor nerves. Therefore, enhanced sympathetic nerve activity that is commonly associated with aging [18], obesity [19] or cardiovascular diseases (CVD) [20, 21] could be a primary cause of functional arteriolar narrowing in the LUT [22] in which ‘vasoconstrictor’ sympathetic activity is dominant over ‘vasodilating’ NO signaling (Figure 1B). On the other hand, physical activity [23] and healthy diet [24] that reduce sympathetic nerve activity would be beneficial for preventing vascular LUTS (Figure 1B). It is likely that the bladder mucosa would be more vulnerable to ischaemia than the DSM [25] and thus be the primary site of afferent overactivity causing urinary urgency in ischaemic OAB [26]. Similarly, the striated muscle layer in the urethra, known as the external urethral sphincter (EUS) would be more readily affected by hypoperfusion than the smooth muscle layer and could be the site of ischaemic stress urinary incontinence (SUI) [27].

In addition to endothelial NO that serves as a potent vasodilator, neuronal NO released from perivascular parasympathetic nerves appears to play an important role in regulating arteriolar contractility in the LUT [28, 29]. Despite the fact that the musculature of the urethra continuously develops muscle tone and thus has a high energy consumption, ischaemic dysfunction of the urethra is less prevalent than that of the bladder. This paradox could be at least partly explained by the actions of neurally‐released NO in the urethra [29]. Neurally‐released NO diminishes sympathetic transmitter release by activating soluble guanylyl cyclase (sGC) expressed in sympathetic nerve fibers, and thus is capable of inducing pre‐synaptic sympatholysis, the intrinsic reduction of sympathetic activity [29]. Therefore, a reduction in both endothelial and neural NO would enhance vasoconstrictor sympathetic activity resulting in hypoperfusion of the LUT (Figure 1B).

Research interest in the ischaemic and/or vasculogenic LUTS has primarily focused on the arteries/arterioles that regulate blood supply to the LUT as well as their intramural distribution within each organ. As a result, less attention has been paid to the capillaries and venules in the LUT, despite their critical importance in exchanging molecules between the blood and the cells and drainage of tissue metabolites, respectively. In the suburothelial microvascular network of the rodent bladder, capillary pericytes, the mural cells covering the walls of the microvascular segments [30], appears to drive spontaneous phasic contractions (SPCs) known as vasomotion in the upstream pre‐capillary arterioles, while pericytes in post‐capillary venules may drive the downstream venular vasomotion [31]. Spontaneous arteriolar vasomotion is considered to reduce the blood flow resistance [32] facilitating capillary‐tissue exchange [33], while venular vasomotion is considered as having a pumping action facilitating venular drainage [34]. Therefore, reduced capillary exchange or venular drainage due to dysregulation of spontaneous activity in these microvascular segments should also be considered as a pathogenesis of vasculogenic LUTS.

In this review, morphological and functional properties of the intramural microvasculature of the bladder and urethra will be discussed based on our findings mostly in rodent LUT in the last 15 years, focusing on their roles in regulating blood flow distribution within both organs. Possible alternations of intramural microvasculature in LUTS and its potential as a therapeutic target will also be discussed in relating with current knowledge of vascular LUTS.

Structure of Intramural Vasculature in the LUT

Morphological Properties of LUT Intramural Vasculature

Intramural blood flow of the bladder is mucosa dominant during the storage phase [15, 16] due to the higher metabolic activity in the mucosal components including the urothelium or muscularis mucosae (only in human, pig and guinea‐pig, [35]) than detrusor smooth muscle (DSM) [25, 36]. Such a mucosa dominant distribution of blood supply is maintained by an extensive capillary network in the bladder mucosa, while the capillary density in DSM is much sparser [37, 38, 39] (Figure 2A).

Microvascular density in different layers of the LUT. In the bladder of NG2‐DsRed mice, NG2+ mural cells (arteriolar SMCs and pericytes) are densely distributed in the mucosa, while NG2+ mural cells are sparsely distributed in the DSM layer. Staining with CD31, an endothelial marker, visularized a well‐developed microvascular network, predominantly consisting of capillaries in the mucosa, whereas much sparser microvasculature is distributed in the DSM layer. In the urethra of NG2‐DsRed mice, NG2+ mural cells are densely distributed in the mucosa and the striated muscle layer, while arteriolar SMCs but not capillary pericytes are evident in the smooth muscle layer. CD31 staining visualized longitudinally‐running capillaries in the mucosa, circumferentially‐running capillaries in the striated muscle layer, whereas larger vessels, arterioles and presumably venules, but not capillaries are evident in the smooth muscle layer.

The blood flow to pig bladders is well maintained during the course of bladder filling with no or little rise in the intravesical pressure, whereas it decreases in non‐compliant bladder associated with intravesical pressure rises [40]. In pigs with bladder outlet obstruction, blood flow is maintained during bladder filling but largely decreases during prolonged, high‐pressure voiding [41], indicating that the bladder blood flow is critically and inversely reliant on the intravesical pressure. In accordance with this notion, in isolated pig bladder, the perfusate flow decreases during bladder filling only after the intravesical pressure starts to rise above approximately 300 mL of bladder volume [42]. In male human bladder, bladder filling to or above 300 mL results in a blood flow reduction as the intravesical pressure rises [43, 44]. Bladder perfusion decreases with bladder filling in patients with interstitial cystitis but increases in those without interstitial cystitis [45]. Thus, the bladder blood flow during the filling phases appears to be maintained by intrinsic mechanisms to minimize rises in intravesical pressure.

In addition to the mechanisms for maintaining a low intravesical pressure during bladder filling, the maintenance of blood supply to and within the bladder is ensured by structural and functional properties of intramural microvasculature counteracting rises in perfusion resistance. Structural adaptations, including a tortuously‐arranged intramural microvasculature, abundant venous valves and extensive arteriolar‐venular anastomosis [37, 38]. The increased blood flow with bladder filling until the threshold volume [44] is considered to be due to topographical rearrangements of tortuously‐arranged blood vessels in the bladder wall [38, 46]. Bladder blood flow only transiently decreases during voiding phases [40] in which the blood distribution must be switched to be DSM dominant to meet an increased energy demands of vigorously contracting DSM generating voiding pressure.

In the human urethra of both sexes, the submucosal vascular plexus is most well developed with the microvascularisation being denser in the striated muscle layer when compared to the smooth muscle layer [47, 48], corresponding to the high energy demands of the mucosal components and striated muscle fibers [17, 27] (Figure 2B). Both the smooth and striated muscles generate sustained muscle tone for most of the day. However, energy demand in smooth muscles is considered to be lower than that in straited muscles due to their latch bridge formation during contraction that is associated with a significant reduction in ATP consumption [49].

Different Segments of Intramural Microvasculature in the LUT

In rodent bladder mucosa (suburothelium), arterioles are surrounded by circumferentially‐arranged contractile smooth muscle cells (SMCs) expressing α‐smooth muscle actin (α‐SMA) [50]. Venules are wrapped by α‐SMA+ stellate‐shaped pericytes forming a network via their processes, while flat ribbon‐like SMCs that circumferentially orientated around the venular wall become apparent in larger venules [50, 51] (Figure 3A). Arteriolar SMCs strongly express NG2 chondroitin sulphate proteoglycan (NG2) immunoreactivity, while venular pericytes or SMCs do not exhibit NG2 immunoreactivity. Capillaries and pre‐ and post‐capillary microvessels are covered by pericytes displaying a typical bump‐on‐a‐log morphology [31]. NG2+ ensheathing pericytes in precapillary‐arterioles (PCAs) and NG2− stellate‐shaped pericytes in venules express α‐SMA indicating their contractile phenotype, whereas NG2+ thin‐strand pericyte pericytes in capillary lacking α‐SMA immunoreactivity appear to be non‐contractile (Figure 3A).

Periarteriolar nNOS+ nerve fibers co‐express VAChT, indicating that they are parasympathetic origin. In the urethra of female rodents, bilateral feeder arteries enter the urethra wall and form an anastomotic ring [29] that appears to be a structural feature to ensure the blood supply to the urethra. Intramural arterioles dividing from the anastomotic ring ramify rostrally, caudally, and predominantly run along the urethral long axis. The trunk of intramural arterioles branching into smaller arterioles is located in the connective tissue layer adjacent to the smooth muscle layer where relatively sparse capillaries are also distributed. A dense network of pericyte‐covered capillaries runs along the urethral long axis in the mucosal layer, while in the striated muscle layer, these capillaries predominantly run perpendicularly to the urethral long axis alongside the striated muscle fibers (see Figure 2B).

Innervation of Intramural Vasculature in the LUT

In the rodent bladder, SMC‐wrapped arterioles receive a dense perivascular innervation comprising tyrosine hydroxylase (TH)‐positive sympathetic nerve fibers, choline acetyltransferase (ChAT)‐positive parasympathetic nerve fibers and sensory nerve fibers containing substance P and CGRP, neuropeptides that can be released from sensory nerves [31, 50, 52] (Figure 3A). Vesicular acetylcholine transporter (VAChT)‐positive periarteriolar nerve fibers that are assumed to be identical to ChAT‐positive fibers co‐express neuronal nitric oxide synthase (nNOS), suggesting that they are parasympathetic, nitrergic nerve fibers (Figure 3C). Periarteriolar nitrergic nerves are closely intertwined with TH‐positive sympathetic nerves, indicating the possibility of presynaptic interaction between sympathetic and parasympathetic nerves as shown in another vascular bed [53]. TH‐positive sympathetic nerve fibers around venules are less dense compared with their arteriolar projections. ChAT‐positive nerve fibers and sensory nerve fibers containing substance P and CGRP are also in close apposition with venules. Unlike SMC‐wrapped arterioles, pericyte‐covered PCAs receive nNOS‐positive but not TH‐positive perivascular nerve fibers, whereas capillaries do not appear to receive any innervation [31]. Thus, different microvascular segments display heterogeneity in the patterns of innervation (Figure 3A). In the pig bladder mucosa, TH‐positive sympathetic nerve fibers, nNOS–immunoreactive nitrergic fibers and calcitonin gene–related peptide (CGRP)–immunoreactive afferent nerves project to both suburothelial arterioles and venules [54]. Perivascular nitrergic nerves are intermingled with TH–positive sympathetic or CGRP–positive afferent nerves [54].

In the rodent urethra, intramural arterioles receive both TH‐positive sympathetic nerve fibers and VAChT/nNOS‐positive parasympathetic, nitrergic nerve fibers in close proximity with each other as well as CGRP‐containing sensory nerve fibers [29]. In the mucosa of the pig urethra, nitrergic nerves are also in close apposition with sympathetic or afferent nerves around suburothelial arterioles and venules [54], suggesting pre‐synaptic interaction among the three nerve populations.

Neural Regulation of Arteriolar Contractility in the LUT

Sympathetic Overdrive as a Primary Cause of LUTS

Feeder arterioles of rodent bladder do not develop intravascular pressure‐induced myogenic constrictions because of the hyperpolarized membranes of the arteriolar SMCs due to the predominant opening of inward‐rectifier K+ channels [55]. The lack of autoregulation in feeder arterioles may be beneficial for maintaining the vascular segment in a dilated state during urine storage phases. Nevertheless, it is envisaged that bladder blood supply is actively regulated by neurohumoral influences on arteriolar contractility and/or autoregulation of intramural microvasculature.

In rodent bladders, electrical filed stimulation (EFS)‐induced suburothelial arteriolar constrictions are abolished by guanethidine and diminished by phentolamine or prazosin, indicating the involvement of noradrenaline activating α‐adrenoceptors, predominantly α1A‐adrenoceptors, in sympathetic arteriolar SMCs vasoconstrictions [28, 56]. In human superior vesical artery, α‐adrenoceptors‐induced vasoconstrictions are predominantly mediated by α1B‐adrenoceptors [57], suggesting species and/or vascular segment differences in α‐adrenoceptor subtypes mediating sympathetic arterial/arteriolar constrictions. In porcine superior vesical arteries, EFS‐induced sympathetic vasoconstrictions are attenuated by the desensitization of P2X purinoceptors with α, β‐methylene ATP, suggesting that the major co‐transmitter is ATP [58]. In contrast to bladder arterioles, sympathetic constrictions of intramural arterioles of rodent urethra appears to be predominantly mediated by ATP [29]. Sympathetic noradrenaline‐induced, α1‐adrenoceptor mediated vasoconstrictions become evident only after the blockade of NO synthesis, suggesting that NO preferentially counteract noradrenaline released from sympathetic nerves.

In suburothelial venules of the bladder in which spontaneous phasic contractions (SPCs) are periodically generated, noradrenaline released from perivascular sympathetic nerves accelerate SPC frequency via the activation of α‐adrenoceptors in venular pericytes with a reduction in basal venular diameter [52]. After the blockade of α‐adrenoceptors with phentolamine, sympathetic noradrenaline reduces the frequency of SPCs with a venular dilatation via the activation of β‐adrenoceptors that appears to be linked with NO release from the endothelium. Thus, unlike sympathetic transmission in arterioles that exclusively exert vasoconstrictor actions, sympathetic transmission appears to simultaneously activate vasoconstrictor and vasodilator pathways to limit excessive vasoconstrictions.

Sympathetic overdrive is commonly seen in aged and/or patients with metabolic syndrome who have a high prevalence of LUTS [1, 4, 5]. Current dogma is that noradrenaline released from sympathetic nerves is capable of directly relaxing detrusor smooth muscle (DSM) to reduce its energy expenditure [59, 60]. However, it has long been revealed by histological examinations that DSM of most species including human with the exception of cat possesses an exceedingly sparse supply of sympathetic nerves [61, 62]. In accordance with this lack of sympathetic innervation to the DSM, sympathetic, β‐adrenergic DSM relaxations are little evident, whereas bath‐applied β‐adrenergic agonists are capable of relaxing DSM by stimulating β‐adrenoceptors expressed in DSM cells [35, 63]. Thus, increased activity of sympathetic nerves is harmful for the bladder as it reduces bladder blood flow by constricting arteries/arterioles without reducing DSM contractility or in turn its energy consumption (Figures 1B and 3E). Continuously‐active mucosal components with a high energy demand [25, 36] would be more vulnerable to hypoperfusion than DSM with a relatively low energy consumption, and thus the mucosa could be a primary site of ischaemic bladder dysfunction.

Parasympathetic Nitrergic Sympatholysis as a Counteracting System

Bladder blood flow, particularly in the mucosa, appears to be largely regulated by NO‐mediated modulation of bladder microcirculation resistance [46, 64]. Is has been considered that endothelium‐derived NO plays a predominant role in NO‐mediated arterial/arteriolar dilatation, reducing perfusion resistance to maintain adequate blood supply to/within the bladder. Urothelium‐derived NO [65] may also contribute to the maintenance of blood supply to the suburothelial circulation, particularly the capillaries that are in very close apposition with the urothelial cells [66, 67].

Besides endothelial and urothelial NO, neurally‐released NO that play a predominant role in smooth muscle relaxation in the urethra may also regulate the contractility of arteries/arterioles in the LUT [68]. In rodent suburothelial arterioles and vesical arteries, tadalafil attenuates nerve‐evoked sympathetic vasoconstrictions or Ca2+ transients that are prevented by Nω‐propyl‐L‐arginine hydrochloride (L‐NPA), an nNOS inhibitor or N ω‐nitro‐L‐arginine (L‐NA), a broad‐spectrum NOS inhibitor [28]. Both vascular segments are densely innervated with nNOS‐positive nerves in close apposition to TH immunoreactive sympathetic nerves expressing sGC (Figure 3C), suggesting that neurally‐released NO inhibits sympathetic transmitter release. L‐NPA alone doesn't enhance sympathetic vasoconstrictions presumably due to a powerful PDE5 degrading NO in sympathetic varicosities. However, this NO‐mediated pre‐synaptic sympatholysis functions only upon the inhibition of sympathetic PDE5. The nNOS‐positive nerve fibers are found to co‐express VAChT as previously shown in the rat major pelvic ganglion [69], indicating that they are parasympathetic in origin (Figure 3B). During the urine storage phases, sympathetic nerves innervating the bladder are dominantly active, while parasympathetic nerve can also be activated upon bladder distension or as intravesical pressure rises [70]. Thus, NO‐mediated, parasympathetic inhibition of sympathetic vasoconstrictor transmission is likely to have a physiological significance.

In PCAs, perivascular nitrergic nerves release NO to induce transient Ca2+ reductions during sustained Ca2+ rises or interruption of spontaneous Ca2+ transients in ensheathing pericytes, whereas sympathetic Ca2+ transients are hardly induced [28]. Thus, unlike the upstream SMC‐wrapped arterioles that receive both sympathetic, excitatory and parasympathetic, inhibitory nerves, the contractility of PCAs appears to be excessively modulated by inhibitory nitrergic innervation.

Ischaemia is considered as a major pathogenesis of LUTS [7, 8, 9]. However, ischaemic dysfunction of the urethra appears to be less prevalent than that in the bladder. This seems to be paradoxical as urethral musculature comprising of both smooth and striated muscles that tonically contract during the bladder storage phases and thus would have a higher energy consumption than the bladder DSM that develops only phasic, asynchronous contractions. Moreover, human EUS is predominantly composed of slow, type I straited muscle fibers with a high oxygen demand [27]. Thus, the urethral vasculature must have intrinsic properties ensuring an adequate blood supply to meet energy demands of the urethral musculature.

In intramural arterioles of female rodent urethra, nerve‐evoked sympathetic vasoconstrictions are predominantly mediated by neurally‐released ATP acting on P2X purinoceptors [29]. However, after the blockade of NOS with L‐NA, α‐adrenergic vasoconstrictions become evident, suggesting that there is a mechanism to inhibit the release of noradrenaline but not ATP from sympathetic nerves. As in the case of suburothelial arterioles of the bladder, NO released from parasympathetic nerves co‐expressing nNOS and VAChT appears to attenuate sympathetic transmitter release by activating sGC in sympathetic nerves [29] (Figure 3D). Unlike bladder arterioles, L‐NPA alone enhances nerve‐evoked sympathetic vasoconstrictions, suggesting that parasympathetic NO continuously inhibit sympathetic transmission presumably because PDE5 in sympathetic nerves is less powerful compared to that in the bladder. In mouse urethral arterioles, neurally‐released NO also induces vasodilatations and Ca2+ reductions in arteriolar SMCs by activating sGCs, and thus parasympathetic NO counteracts sympathetic vasomotions both pre‐ and post‐synaptically.

In addition to the NO‐mediated presynaptic inhibition of sympathetic nerves, perivascular parasympathetic nerves in the urethra release ACh that activate endothelial muscarinic receptors to increase endothelial Ca2+ level resulting in arteriolar dilatation [29] presumably via endothelium‐derived hyperpolarizations [71]. Thus, parasympathetic nerves in the urethra exert dual inhibitions on sympathetic vasoconstrictions ensuring sufficient blood supply to the urethra even under continuous sympathetic neural input that is required to maintain smooth muscle tone.

Sensory Innervation to Microvasculature

In addition to a parasympathetic, nitrergic inhibitory innervation, neurally‐released CGRP induces an NO‐independent reduction in pericytes Ca2+ contributing to a prolonged reduction of Ca2+ in mouse PCAs [28]. Considering that CGPR could be released from perivascular afferent nerves [52], CGRP‐mediated neurotransmission may have a more prominent role in inflammatory hyperaemia. In intramural arterioles of female mouse urethra, neurally‐released CGRP also induces vasodilatations or Ca2+ reductions in arteriolar SMCs by an NO‐independent mechanisms [29].

Roles of Capillary Pericytes

Bladder Suburothelial Pericytes as Microvascular Pacemaker Cells

The extensive capillary network in the bladder suburothelial layer [66, 67] is considered to have a fundamental role in maintaining the high metabolic demands of the urothelium–sensory nerve complex where bladder sensations are sent to the central nervous system [72]. In species that have vigorously contracting muscularis mucosae, including humans, the capillary network also provides an adequate blood supply to meet the high energy demand of muscularis mucosae [36].

Unlike the ensheathing pericytes in PCAs or arteriolar SMCs in the upstream microvascular segments, thin‐stranded capillary pericytes in the bladder suburothelium do not receive either an excitatory or inhibitory innervation [28, 31], indicating that their activity relies on intrinsic properties and humoral influence of neighboring cells. Moreover, capillary pericytes that do not express α‐SMA are likely to be non‐contractile, and thus not able to directly alter capillary caliber. This raises the question as to how thin‐stranded capillary pericytes regulate capillary perfusion to meet energy demand of their neighboring cells, for example, urothelial cells?

Capillary pericytes in the mouse bladder suburothelium express ANO1 Ca2+‐activated chloride channels (CaCCs) [73] (Figure 4A) that serve as pacemaker channels in several smooth muscle organs [74], including the urethra [75] and renal pelvis [76], and thus are capable of converting spontaneous Ca2+ transients into ANO1 CaCC‐dependent spontaneous transient depolarization's (STDs). Spontaneous Ca2+ transients in capillary pericytes propagate among themselves (supplementary movie 1) and also travel into PCA triggering spontaneous Ca2+ transients in contractile pericytes (Figure 4B). Propagating Ca2+ transients have been demonstrated in other microvascular beds and are considered to be mediated by spreading depolarization's [77]. In the retinal microvasculature forming an electrotonic architecture in which the electrical transmission is highly efficient [78], localized capillary pericyte constrictions induced by depolarizing current injection propagate to constrict distant pericytes [79]. Similarly, capillary pericytes in the bladder appear to electrically drive spontaneous Ca2+ transients and associated SPCs in contractile PCA pericytes by means of propagating ANO1‐dependent STDs to facilitate arteriolar‐capillary perfusion [31]. The synchrony of Ca2+ transients in capillary pericytes appears to rely exclusively on ANO1 CaCC activation without involving the opening of L‐type voltage‐dependent Ca2+ channels (LVDCCs), whereas subsequent LVDCC activation is required for the synchrony of Ca2+ transients in PCA pericytes.

Propagating depolarizations between PCA pericytes requires LVDCC activation (purple lightning mark). Capillary pericytes could also be affected by urothelial/epithelial substances in both the bladder and urethra. Capillary pericytes may regulate water and ion absorption via the urothelium and be capable of differentiating into fibroblasts.

In venules of the bladder suburothelium, α‐SMA+ venular pericytes develop spontaneous Ca2+ transients and corresponding STDs resulting in SPCs [31, 51]. Pericytes in post‐capillary venules appear to generate ANO1 CaCC‐dependent STDs to electrically drive spontaneous activity in the downstream venules. Unlike STDs in capillary pericytes, propagation of STDs among venular pericytes requires the activation of LVDCCs as ANO1 CaCC‐dependent STDs in venular pericytes are not sufficiently large to trigger regenerative excitation [31].

Roles of Capillary Pericytes in Urothelial Reabsorption and Fibrosis

Besides the role in regulating microvascular contractility, capillary pericyte likely has other functions in the LUT. In the rat bladder, suburothelial capillaries are situated within grooves of the basal urothelial surface where they are surrounded by urothelial cells on three sides with a separation of 0.3 μm [66]. Such a close anatomical relationship between the dense network of suburothelial capillaries and the urothelium implies specific functional interactions between the capillaries and the urothelium. Thus, in addition to the fundamental role of suburothelial capillaries in maintaining urothelial metabolism and barrier function, the capillaries may have a role in urothelial transport function.

The urothelium had been considered as a barrier preventing the unrestricted diffusion of solutes and water across the epithelium. However, it is now known to allow trans‐urothelial transport of water or urine solutes via aquaporin or other ion channels/transporters expressed in the plasma membrane of urothelial cells [80, 81]. Since the driving force for trans‐urothelial influx of water or solutes is their concentration and/or osmotic gradient across the urothelium, reabsorbed substances must be removed from the suburothelial space by capillary blood flow. Suburothelial capillaries of the bladder possess a fenestrated endothelium, especially on their aspect adjacent to the urothelium, that seems to be ideal to facilitate the movement of nutrients from the capillaries to the urothelium [66, 82]. The fenestrated endothelium would also allow rapid removal of reabsorbed substances. Changes in coverage or morphology of pericytes would alter capillary permeability and in turn substance exchange between the suburothelium and capillary (Figure 4B). Decreased pericyte coverage in response to chronic cerebral hypoperfusion [83] is associated with increases in blood–brain barrier permeability, whereas in the aged microvascular network, pericyte coverage is increased and associated with decreased proangiogenic function [84].

In both mucosal and detrusor layers of the bladder, interstitial cells expressing PDGFRα are abundantly distributed [85]. Considering the critical role of PDGFRα signaling in fibrosis [86], PDGFRα+ cells in the bladder are assumed to be fibroblasts that can differentiate into myofibroblasts. Pericytes have been suggested to serve as precursor cells of myofibroblasts that play a pivotal role in fibrosis [30]. Indeed, the roles of pericytes in fibrosis and/or inflammation in which they interact with immune cells such as macrophages have been demonstrated in several organs, including the kidney [87], brain [88] and lung [89]. Thus, capillary pericytes in the bladder and urethra may also serve as a source of inflammatory and fibrotic responses in pathological conditions such as ischaemia or aging (Figure 4B).

Age‐Related Loss of Capillaries and Pericytes

It has been widely accepted that bladder ischaemia is a major cause of bladder dysfunction associated with aging or comorbidities, such as metabolic syndrome [1, 4, 5]. Considering the critical role of the dense mucosal capillary network in the bladder [37, 38, 39] in maintaining the function of the urothelium‐afferent nerve complex responsible for bladder sensations [72], a reduced density or function of bladder capillaries can be a primary cause of ischaemic OAB. However, no information on capillary function and/or density in OAB or underactive bladder is available to date. On the other hand, a reduced vascular density in the urethra with aging has been reported [90], although not specifically focusing on the different vascular segments and/or layers of the urethra.

Since the prevalence of stress urinary incontinence (SUI) increases with age [91, 92, 93], age‐related loss of external urethral sphincter (EUS) volume and/or function can be considered a manifestation of sarcopenia, the age‐related loss of skeletal muscle volume and/or strength. However, sarcopenia is characterized by a deterioration of fast (type II) muscle fibers due to the preferential loss of fast α‐motor neurons [94, 95]. Capillary rarefaction [95] is another characteristic of sarcopenia and may precede muscle atrophy [96]. Considering that human EUS is predominately composed of slow (type I) striated muscle fibers with a relatively high oxygen demand [27], age‐related capillary rarefaction may play a critical role in aged EUS atrophy [90, 97, 98] contributing to the pathogenesis of age‐related SUI.

In EUS of aged female mice, the density of capillaries and capillary pericytes is reduced, along with the reduced number of muscle fibers and increased PDGFRα+ fibroblasts, namely fibrosis [99]. Electrical field stimulation (EFS) triggers an EUS contraction and a NO‐mediated reduction in Ca2+ of EUS capillary pericytes. Capillaries do not receive nNOS+ perivascular nitrergic innervation, while the sarcolemma of EUS muscle fibers expresses nNOS immunoreactivity as shown in other species including humans [100, 101], suggesting that contracting EUS fibers release NO to reduce [Ca2+]i in capillary pericytes (Figure 4B). Since capillary pericytes in the EUS express ANO1 CaCCs, the EFS‐induced, NO‐mediated reduction in Ca2+ would cause hyperpolarization as ANO1 CaCCs close, while not directly altering capillary caliber as α‐SMA− EUS pericytes are likely to be non‐contractile. The resultant hyperpolarizations may propagate to the upstream arterioles resulting in arteriolar dilatation to increase the blood supply to contracting EUS fibers. In the brain capillaries, ‘non‐contractile’ thin‐stranded pericytes generate hyperpolarization by the opening of ATP‐sensitive potassium channels in response to increased neuronal activity. The resultant hyperpolarizations propagate to the upstream microvascular segments causing arteriolar dilatation to increase capillary perfusion [102]. Thus, EUS pericytes may well sense increased energy demand of EUS muscle fibers upon their contractions and increase blood flow to contracting muscle fibers by means of propagating hyperpolarization.

Clinical Perspectives

Induced Sympatholysis

Since the vascular resistance in the LUT is primarily determined by intramural arterioles and pre‐capillary arterioles where arteriolar constrictions are under sympathetic control, pharmacological sympatholysis counteracting sympathetic excitatory transmission would be the first strategy for the treatment of vascular LUTS (Figure 3E). The current strategy for the treatment of vascular LUT, particularly ischæmic OAB, is drug‐induced vasodilatation to improve LUT blood flow [103]. Alpha‐blockers, which attenuate sympathetic vasoconstrictor action by antagonizing α‐adrenoceptors in vascular SMCs, improve bladder blood flow and bladder overactivity in chronic bladder ischaemia [13], spontaneous hypertension [104] or bladder outlet obstruction (BOO) rat model [105]. PDE5‐Is, which enhance the vasodilatory action of endogenous NO, primarily considered to be endothelial NO, by inhibiting the degradation of cGMP, ameliorate chronic bladder ischaemic bladder overactivity [12] and also improve bladder blood flow in rat models of abdominal aorta clamping or bladder overdistension [106]. Beta3 agonists, which exert vasodilatory actions by stimulating adenylyl cyclase to increase cAMP in vascular SMCs, ameliorate ischaemic bladder overactivity [14] and also improve bladder dysfunction and blood flow in the BOO rat model [107]. Collectively, these clinically used drugs would effectively counteract sympathetic overdrive in the LUT by their post‐synaptic actions.

Besides, post‐synaptic sympatholysis, pre‐synaptic sympatholysis, namely the reduction in sympathetic transmitter release, could be a novel therapeutic target for vascular LUT. As described above, in rodent bladder and urethra, perivascular parasympathetic nerves release NO that stimulates sGC in sympathetic nerve fibers to preferentially inhibit the release of noradrenaline [28, 29], although this mechanism has not been explored in human LUT. Thus, PDE5‐Is could exert both pre‐ and post‐synaptic inhibitory actions on vasoconstrictor sympathetic innervation (Figure 3D). Importantly, parasympathetic NO‐mediated, pre‐synaptic sympatholysis in bladder arterioles becomes apparent only upon the inhibition of cGMP degradation with PDE5‐Is, highlighting the therapeutic and preventive potential of PDE5‐Is for ischaemic OAB.

Prevention of Capillary Rarefaction

Since capillary rarefaction is evident in the EUS of aged‐female mice associated with EUS atrophy and fibrosis [99], reduced density of capillaries or their dysfunction could be more widely involved in the pathogenesis of LUTS. Low‐intensity extracorporeal shock wave therapy (Li‐SWT) has been shown to exert its therapeutic effects in soft tissue or ischæmic heart by increasing vascular density [108, 109], and thus is a promising option as a non‐invasive regenerative medicine procedure. Li‐SWT restores leak‐point pressure (LPP) associated with EUS regeneration and increased vascularity in a vaginal balloon dilation induced rat model [110]. A randomized‐controlled clinical study demonstrated that Li‐SWT not only relieves SUI symptoms but also ameliorates urinary urgency [111]. Moreover, in diabetic [112] or ischaemic [113] rat models, Li‐SWT prolongs voiding intervals and increases leak point pressure (LPP) associated with an improvement of urethral vascularization or bladder blood flow. Interestingly, Li‐SWT not only increases the expression of sGC, vascular endothelial growth factor or CD31, an endothelial marker, that is tightly linked with vasodilatation/vascular growth, but also restores nNOS expression and attenuates TH expression in urethral smooth muscle. These later actions would result in inducing ‘sympatholysis’ to increase the blood supply to the LUT.

Imaging Assessments of Vascular LUTS

The diagnosis and follow‐up of vascular LUTS requires minimally invasive, high‐resolution imaging devices to visualize mucosal blood flow and/or intramural microvascular environments of the LUT, preferably with low costs.

Bladder mucosal blood flow can be quantitatively assessed using a cystoscopic laser Doppler flowmeter [43]. A study demonstrated that holmium laser enucleation of the prostate (HoLEP) increases blood flow in the bladder mucosa leading to the improvement of the Overactive Bladder Symptom Score (OABSS) [114], indicating the correlation between mucosal blood flow and bladder storage function.

Some optical imaging techniques for bladder cancer detection and diagnosis can be applied for assessing intramural microvasculature in the LUT [115]. Magnifying endoscopy with narrow band imaging (NBI) is capable of enhancing microvessels on mucosal surfaces, including the subepithelial capillary network, and is widely used for the diagnosis of various gastrointestinal diseases [116]. NBI has also been incorporated into cystoscopy to improve the detection of bladder cancer over standard white light imaging (WLI) cystoscopy allowing clearer visualization of suburothelial capillaries and venules compared to WLI [117]. Optical coherence angiography (OCA) is most widely used in ophthalmology to visualize three‐dimensional microvascular changes in the retina and choroid at micron‐scale resolution [118]. Since OCT (optical coherence tomography)–assisted cystoscopy has been adopted as an adjunct to cystoscopy for bladder cancer staging [119], cystoscopic OCA that enables depth microvascular visualization would be useful for the evaluation of microvascular LUTS. Artificial intelligence (AI)–based analysis of NBI and OCTA images can significantly improve diagnostic accuracy, while saving time and effort for the examiners to accumulate their experiences.

Conclusion

Current concept of ischaemic or vascular LUTS should be extended to microvascular LUTS focusing on structural and functional changes in intramural blood vessels of the bladder and urethra. Thus, in addition to reductions in LUT blood supply due to arteriosclerotic narrowing of feeder arteries, disturbed intramural blood flow distribution, in which arterioles, capillaries and venules play a fundamental role, could be a primary cause of LUTS. Induced sympatholysis and pericyte conservation/restoration in microvascular segments have considerable therapeutic potential for the novel treatment of microvascular LUTS.
