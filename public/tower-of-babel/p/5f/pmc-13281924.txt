Configuring large language models to deliver patient-facing explanations of pathology reports
Authors: Will D Jeong, Sherman X J Lin, Morgan Black, Kyla Trinh, Haechan J Yang, Alexandra Brown, Matthew J Cecchini, Jason K Wasserman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTimely access to pathology reports has increased the need for clear patient-facing explanations. We evaluated whether large language model (LLM)–generated responses to pathology report questions from patients are comparable in quality to explanations written by pathologists and assessed how LLM configuration influences performance.MethodsSixty-five anonymized real-world patient questions from an online pathology education platform were answered using 5 LLM configurations varying by model architecture, prompting strategy, and retrieval-augmented generation. Responses were evaluated using a structured rubric that assessed accuracy, relevance, clarity, empathy, and safety; they were compared using pairwise arena testing with Bradley-Terry modeling to rank performance.ResultsAcross rubric domains, LLM responses demonstrated performance comparable to pathologist explanations, with 1 configuration meeting noninferiority criteria. Pairwise arena comparisons indicated that the configuration parameters strongly influenced performance, with both model size and retrieval augmentation associated with improved response preference. The highest-performing configuration combined a larger model with retrieval from a curated pathology knowledge base and was strongly preferred over pathologist-written responses.ConclusionsCarefully configured LLM systems can generate patient-facing explanations of pathology reports comparable in quality to pathologist-written explanations. Prompting strategy, model size, and retrieval integration were associated with performance differences, underscoring the importance of system configuration in developing LLM-based tools to expand access to understandable pathology information.

INTRODUCTION

Pathology reports are highly technical medical documents written primarily for communication between physicians. They contain specialized terminology, complex diagnostic classifications, and detailed narrative descriptions that guide clinical decision-making. Advances in diagnostic and molecular pathology have further increased the complexity of these reports as new biomarkers, molecular alterations, and increasingly granular prognostic assessments have been incorporated into routine reporting. As a result, pathology reports now often contain dense medical language and complex diagnostic information that can be difficult for patients to interpret.1,2

At the same time, patients now have direct electronic access to their medical records through online portals. In the United States, the 21st Century Cures Act Final Rule requires timely patient access to electronic health information, including pathology reports, enabling near-real-time release of results, often before a patient’s follow-up appointment with the treating clinician. Comparable patient-access policies in other countries have similarly accelerated medical report disclosure.3,4 Although this increased transparency has important benefits, patients are frequently confronted with highly technical pathology information without adequate support to understand its meaning.

The clinical consequences of this interpretive gap are increasingly apparent. When patients receive abnormal or highly technical results without timely explanation, they commonly report distress, uncertainty, and increased worry.5‐7 Immediate release of results has been associated with a marked increase in patients viewing their reports before they are reviewed by the treating physician as well as a rise in patient-initiated electronic messages shortly after results become available, contributing to additional downstream clinical workload.4 In contrast, patient-centered pathology reports have been shown to reduce worry compared with standard pathology reports, supporting the value of clearer patient-facing pathology communication.5

To address the gap between rapidly released pathology reports and patients’ need for timely, understandable explanations, MyPathologyReport.com was developed to provide free, plain-language explanations of pathology diagnoses and terminology. The publicly accessible site receives approximately 750 000 visitors per month and includes a library of curated articles and a glossary written and reviewed by board-certified pathologists.1 Available user feedback suggests that many patients, pathologists, and learners find the content helpful, with 1397 of 1641 (85%) who completed an online article feedback survey reporting that the article they read was helpful. MyPathologyReport.com also offers an “Ask a Pathologist” service, allowing patients to submit their reports and receive individualized written explanations from volunteer pathologists. Although this approach delivers high-quality, individually tailored responses, it is constrained by limited pathologist time and cannot scale to meet the growing number of patients seeking help interpreting their pathology reports.

Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) offer immense promise in extending this work. Conversational models can interpret complex clinical text and produce generally accurate, readable explanations for patients.8,9 These systems, however, are prone to omitting important context, introducing factually incorrect statements, or adopting tone that is inappropriately reassuring or unduly alarming.10 Pathology reporting adds further complexity due to subtle, discipline-specific terminology distinctions that often carry major clinical implications. These considerations underscore the need for rigorous, domain-specific evaluation before LLMs are used to communicate pathology results directly to patients.

In this study, we evaluated whether LLMs can generate patient-facing explanations of pathology reports that are comparable in quality to those written by pathologists. We compared several LLM configurations that differed in model size, prompting strategy, and the use of retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) to evaluate how these factors affect response quality. We then assessed response quality by benchmarking model outputs against explanations written by a board-certified pathologist. Our primary goal was to identify an LLM configuration capable of delivering clear, accurate, and safe explanations in a cost-effective manner, thereby expanding patient access to understandable information about their pathology reports.

METHODS

Study design

We conducted a retrospective comparative evaluation of 5 LLM configurations generating responses to 65 patient-submitted pathology questions from the MyPathologyReport database. These 5 configurations varied in their model architecture, prompting strategy, and use of RAG. Specifically, 3 configurations used OpenAI GPT-5 (2025-08-07): the base model (GPT-5), with a custom prompt (GPT-5_p), and with a custom prompt plus RAG (GPT-5_p_rag). Two configurations used GPT-5-nano (2025-08-07) with a custom prompt (GPT-5-n_p) and with a custom prompt plus RAG (GPT-5-n_p_rag). A sixth reference configuration consisted of pathologist-written responses (J.W.). Pathologist-written explanations were selected as the reference standard to ensure accuracy and provide an in-domain benchmark for diagnostic interpretation, completeness, and pathology-specific communication. This choice aligned with the study context because MyPathologyReport.com is built around pathologist-written, patient-facing content and individualized responses to patient-submitted questions, and it is consistent with emerging patient-pathologist consultation models.11,12 Examples of responses generated by each configuration are provided in Table S1.

Questions submitted between April 20, 2022, and June 14, 2023, were screened for eligibility and excluded if they were not in English, unrelated to pathology or diagnostic interpretation, requested explicit medical advice, or lacked sufficient detail for meaningful response generation. From the remaining eligible questions, a random sample of 65 questions was selected to reflect the range of patient submissions, including benign and malignant diagnoses and varying clinical complexity. The final dataset was fully anonymized before analysis and contained no patient identifiers or protected health information.

Prompt development and RAG

A custom system prompt was developed using 20 queries representative of typical platform use. The prompt structure and content were developed based on expert judgment; LLMs were used only for minor language editing and not to generate the core prompt. The prompt defined each model’s role as an educational assistant providing explanations of pathology terminology, report components, and background disease information while avoiding patient-specific interpretation, medical advice, treatment recommendations, and any discussion of prognosis.

For RAG-assisted configurations, articles from MyPathologyReport were segmented, embedded using the text-embedding-ada-002 model, and stored in a ChromaDB vector database. For each query, semantically relevant article segments were retrieved and appended to the prompt context before response generation.

Rubric evaluation framework

Response quality was evaluated using an investigator-created rubric covering 5 areas: accuracy, relevance, clarity, empathy, and safety. The selection of accuracy, relevance, clarity, and safety was influenced by previous research on LLM assessments and their importance in patient-oriented pathology explanations.13 Empathy was added to capture tactful and sensitive communication when explaining potentially serious diagnoses. Each domain was scored on a 4-point ordinal scale (1-4), with higher scores indicating superior performance (Supplementary Methods S1). Domain definitions were established through expert review of representative sample questions and corresponding pathologist responses.

All question-response pairs (n = 65) were evaluated against this rubric by an LLM-based grader (OpenAI GPT-4o [2024-11-20]). Total scores for the configurations were compared with their corresponding human-written responses using paired t tests. Domain-level differences were assessed by applying repeated-measures analysis of ­variance (ANOVA) to the combined configuration, followed by Bonferroni-adjusted post hoc paired comparisons.

A randomly selected subset of question-response pairs (n = 8 questions) from the original 65-question dataset was independently graded by 2 pathologists (J.W., M.C.). For overlapping responses, total rubric scores were averaged across pathologists before analysis. Differences in total scores across response sources (5 LLM configurations and pathologist-written responses) and across rubric domains were assessed using a repeated-measures ANOVA, with question ID as the repeated factor.

To evaluate grading differences between LLM and pathologist ­evaluators, paired t tests were performed to compare total scores assigned by LLMs with the average pathologist scores separately for pathologist-written and LLM-generated responses. To assess whether grading differences varied by response source, the evaluator score difference (LLM score minus pathologist score) was calculated for each response and compared between human-written and LLM-generated responses using an independent 2-sample t test.

Noninferiority analyses were performed using pathologist-assigned total rubric scores. The noninferiority margin (Δ) was defined as the median absolute inter-rater difference in total rubric scores between pathologists. For each model configuration, paired differences between model-generated and corresponding pathologist-written responses were calculated. Mean differences and corresponding 95% CIs were calculated using the Student t test distribution. A configuration was considered noninferior if the lower bound of the CI exceeded − Δ.

Pairwise arena evaluation

Pairwise arena testing was conducted across all 6 response configurations using 2 blinded pathologist evaluators. Testing included 57 questions from the original 65-question dataset, excluding the 8 graded by pathologists under the previously described rubric. Of these 57 questions, 7 were evaluated by both pathologists, while the remaining 50 were divided evenly. For each question, all 6 response configurations were compared in a complete round-robin design, yielding 15 pairwise comparisons per question. Each pathologist completed 480 comparisons in total, comprising 105 overlapping and 375 unique evaluations.

Pairwise outcomes were aggregated across evaluators and analyzed using a Bradley-Terry model to estimate relative configuration performance. As the Bradley-Terry framework requires binary win-loss outcomes, discordant preferences for questions evaluated by both pathologists were treated as ties and excluded from modeling. Ability differences were transformed into win probabilities for interpretability. Quasivariance methods were used to derive comparison intervals, and Wald tests were used to assess statistical significance. Results are reported as log-odds differences with corresponding 95% CIs.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using R, version 4.5.0, statistical software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). The BradleyTerry2, version 1.1.3, package was used for pairwise arena modeling, and DescTools, version 0.99.60, software was used for inferential testing. All statistical tests were 2-sided, with statistical significance defined as P < .05. Bonferroni adjustment was applied for multiple comparisons where appropriate.

Ethics statement

This study involved retrospective analysis of fully anonymized data and did not involve interaction with individuals or access to identifiable personal health information. In accordance with the Tri-Council Policy Statement, the study did not require oversight by a research ethics board.

RESULTS

Models can produce coherent, high-quality pathology explanations

When graded by an LLM evaluator, total rubric scores differed substantially across response sources and LLM configurations (1-way repeated-measures ANOVA, F5,320 = 99.5, P < .001) (Figure 1). Post hoc paired comparisons showed that each LLM configuration received statistically significantly higher total scores than their corresponding pathologist-written response (all adjusted P < .001). Direct comparison of evaluator scores, however, revealed differences in grading patterns. For pathologist-written responses, scores assigned by the LLM evaluator did not differ from those assigned by pathologist evaluators (paired mean difference, −0.25; P = .67). Model size and RAG can improve response quality

To evaluate relative configuration performance, a pairwise arena design was used in which blinded raters selected the preferred response between each model pair (Figure 4). Results were analyzed using a Bradley-Terry model to estimate relative preference strength on the log-odds scale.

Points represent estimated model ability on the log-odds scale, with horizontal bars representing quasivariance 95% CIs. BT indicates Bradley-Terry; GPT-5_p, GPT-5 with a custom prompt; GPT-5_p_rag, GPT-5 with a custom prompt plus retrieval-augmented generation; GPT-5-n_p, GPT-5-nano with a custom prompt; GPT-5-n_p_rag, GPT-5-nano with a custom prompt plus retrieval-augmented generation.

Under this framework, GPT-5_p outperformed GPT-5-n_p, suggesting that increased model size improves response quality under a standard prompt (difference, 0.55; P < .001; 63% win rate). In contrast, prompting alone did not improve performance: The base GPT-5 model was modestly preferred over GPT-5_p, although this difference did not reach statistical significance (difference, 0.28; P = .08; 57% win rate). Incorporation of RAG appeared to improve performance in both architectures. Although the difference between GPT-5_p_rag and GPT-5_p was not statistically significant, GPT-5-n_p_rag scored 0.40 points higher than GPT-5-n_p (P = .02; 60% win rate).

Among all configurations, GPT-5_p_rag achieved the highest ability estimate and was the only model statistically significantly preferred over pathologist-written responses (difference, 0.33; P = .04; 58% win rate). Conversely, GPT-5-n_p was the only model that performed statistically significantly worse than pathologist responses (difference, −0.58; P < .001; 36% win rate).

DISCUSSION

Our study demonstrates that carefully configured LLMs can generate patient-facing explanations of pathology reports for real, deidentified patient questions that are comparable in quality to those written by pathologists. Using a rubric designed around key dimensions of high-quality pathology explanations, model-generated responses received consistently strong scores that were comparable to pathologist-written answers. This pattern was observed under both automated scoring and the subset of pathologist grading, where GPT-5_p_rag met noninferiority criteria relative to pathologist responses on total score. Importantly, accuracy was consistently high across configurations, supporting the use of these systems for straightforward questions that benefit from clear, flexible explanations. In contrast, empathy was among the lowest-scoring domains, though these scores were similarly low across pathologist-written responses. This finding suggests that pathology explanations may systematically prioritize factual clarity over emotional framing, highlighting empathy as a concrete target for improvement, particularly for more sensitive inquiries.

Response quality was consistently strong for both pathologist-written and LLM-generated outputs, limiting the ability to distinguish differences under a rubric-based approach. To improve sensitivity, we supplemented rubric scoring with a blinded pairwise arena evaluation and Bradley-Terry model. Consistent with the previous noninferiority findings, GPT-5_p_rag was the most favored configuration and the only one statistically significantly preferred over human responses. This finding is likely in part due to differences in model size: When other system components were held constant, the larger GPT-5 configurations were preferred over their corresponding GPT-5-nano variants. Retrieval augmentation was also associated with rater preference, suggesting that maintaining reliable, expert-verified knowledge bases like MyPathologyReport may be key for optimizing response quality rather than relying solely on a model’s base knowledge.

In contrast, the more elaborate prompting strategy did not appear to improve performance and was instead associated with a clear decline in preference relative to the base GPT-5 configuration. This finding suggests that prompt design is not uniformly beneficial and that overly prescriptive instructions may constrain an otherwise capable model. Because we did not include a configuration that isolated retrieval with minimal prompting, the independent contribution of RAG alone may also have been underestimated.

These findings have direct implications for patients seeking help interpreting pathology reports. Strong accuracy and safety performance, together with noninferiority to pathologist-written explanations, support the use of LLMs to provide timely, plain-language interpretation for common questions that may otherwise receive delayed or no response because of limited specialist availability. In practice, LLMs could provide immediate, standardized explanations at the time of report release, allowing pathologists and treating clinicians more time for clinical decision-making and discussions that require nuanced judgment or delivery of serious news. In addition, large-scale deployment could generate a valuable corpus of patient questions that can be analyzed to identify recurring misconceptions, inform the development of new educational content, and guide ­modifications to report templates to better align with patient understanding.

Our findings contribute to a growing body of literature demonstrating that LLMs can improve patient accessibility to and comprehension of medical reports. Prior studies have shown that models such as GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 can generate accurate and readable explanations across a range of pathology domains, including colorectal cancer, prostate biopsy and surgical pathology reports, breast cancer, various malignant tumors, and even radiology reports.9,14‐17 Across these studies, LLMs consistently improved readability and often achieved accuracy comparable to human explanations, although some authors noted that simplification could introduce occasional inaccuracies. Unlike prior evaluations that relied on general-purpose LLMs trained on broad datasets, however, we grounded responses in a single, verified, authoritative information source. We directly benchmarked LLM outputs against pathologist-written patient explanations and incorporated a noninferiority framework, providing a more rigorous standard of comparison. In addition, some prior work acknowledged that artificial intelligence–generated explanations may lack emotional nuance, and none formally measured empathy as a core domain.18 By explicitly evaluating empathy and safety alongside accuracy and clarity, our work offers a more comprehensive assessment of communication quality.

These findings should be interpreted in the context of several design considerations. Human grading was performed on a subset of responses by a limited number of pathologists, although the consistency across analytic methods supports the stability of the conclusions. Questions were drawn from a single English-language platform, and performance may differ in other clinical or linguistic settings. Although initial user survey data suggest that patients find MyPathologyReport.com articles helpful, the platform has not yet formally evaluated whether individualized responses reduce distress, uncertainty, or worry; prospective outcome measurement among consenting users is a planned next step. Empathy was measured to capture tactful and sensitive phrasing rather than emotional resonance; these outputs are intended to supplement, not replace, clinician engagement, and the appropriate boundaries of artificial intelligence–mediated empathy remain an open ethical question.18 Finally, although pathologists provided an appropriate in-domain reference standard for pathology-specific accuracy and completeness, future studies could incorporate patients and treating clinicians to better assess translation to real clinics.

In summary, contemporary LLMs, when carefully configured, can generate patient-facing explanations of pathology reports that are comparable in quality to those written by pathologists. The optimized configuration identified here performed best across evaluation methods and has been implemented as a live patient-facing tool within MyPathologyReport to support report interpretation internationally. Future work will test performance across more diverse question sets, incorporate direct measures of patient comprehension and satisfaction, and refine prompt and retrieval strategies through empirical optimization. As digital pathology continues to expand access to results, integrating trustworthy LLM-based explanations offers a path toward reports that are not only accurate but also accessible to the patients they ultimately serve.
