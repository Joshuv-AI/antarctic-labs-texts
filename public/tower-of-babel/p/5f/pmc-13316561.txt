Development and Clinical Validation of a Protocol-Agnostic Machine Learning Platform for Automated Treatment Planning in External Beam Radiation Therapy
Authors: Julie K. Shade, Pranav Lakshminarayanan, Peter Hoban, Michael Bowers, Kevin Gorman, Saida Muktar Reshid, Thomas Lilieholm, Christian Velten, Siddharth Kapoor, Tyler Smith, David Murray, Wolfgang A. Tomé, Harry Quon, Daniel Y. Song, Todd McNutt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo develop and validate a protocol-agnostic machine learning platform (“Predictive Planning”) for knowledge-based planning (KBP) in external beam radiation therapy.Methods and MaterialsFive thousand three hundred thirty-four retrospective photon beam treatment plans (1145 Head and Neck [H&N], 1623 Thoracic, 781 Abdominal, 1785 Pelvis) from 2 institutions were used to develop and internally test general-purpose models for organ at risk (OAR) dose-volume histogram prediction. Models were deployed in a commercial system (Plan AI, Sun Nuclear Corporation), and 72 retrospective clinical plans (18 H&N, 20 Thoracic, 17 Abdominal, 17 Pelvis) were replanned using treatment planning system optimization objectives predicted by the models (“Predictive Plans”; PPs), without objective value changes. Dose metrics were compared for 25, 11, 8, and 7 OARs for H&N, Thoracic, Abdominal, and Pelvis plans, respectively, and for planning target volumes.ResultsIn the plan comparison study, there were no OAR dose metrics for which PPs were statistically significantly greater than clinical plans. For H&N, PP mean dose (Gy) was significantly lower for Brain (5.8 vs 7.3, P = .0003), Brainstem (9.6 vs 13.8, P < .0001), Glottis (26.9 vs 31.9, P = .0017), OpticChiasm (7.3 vs 12.2, P < .0001), Parotid_L/R (19.0/20.8 vs 22.3/25.7, P < .0001/<.0001) and SpinalCord (10.1 vs 15.7, P < .0001). For Abdominal, PP mean dose was significantly lower for Heart (2.2 vs 3.4, P = .0039), Kidney_R (6.2 vs 8.6, P = .002), SpinalCanal (4.8 vs 6.7, P = .0004), and Stomach (9.1 vs 10.9, P = .002). For Pelvis, PP mean dose was significantly lower for Bladder (30.7 vs 33.7, P < .0001), Femur_Head_L/R (13.8/13.9 vs 17.0/16.5, P = .0026/.0046), PenileBulb (15.8 vs 21.9, P = .0039), and Rectum (28.1 vs 34.2, P = .0002). Mean planning target volume coverage was significantly higher for H&N and Thoracic, and equivalent for Abdominal and Pelvis.ConclusionsPredictive planning represents a shift in KBP from protocol-specific models trained with small, uniform data sets to protocol-agnostic, disease-site-specific models trained with large, heterogeneous data sets. Plans generated using optimization objectives predicted by the models had equivalent or superior dosimetric outcomes.

Introduction

The central objective in radiation therapy treatment planning is ensuring that a prescribed dose covers the targeted volume(s) (TV) while maximally sparing organs at risk (OARs). Achieving the ideal dose distribution requires balancing competing priorities: delivering the prescribed dose to the entire TV while minimizing the dose delivered to nearby OARs. There are further trade-offs between organs due to the geometries of external beam delivery, such that preferentially sparing one OAR generally increases the dose to others. Although modern intensity modulated treatment techniques enable overall reduction in OAR doses, the challenge remains to ensure OAR doses are as low as achievable, given complex geometric factors and clinical trade-offs.

Conventional treatment planning involves an inefficient strategy of setting optimization objectives for an average patient without considering individual patient geometry. The planner then manually adjusts these after each optimization cycle until the optimizer produces a satisfactory result. This is a complex process and, as a result, there can be large variations in plan quality due to planner skill level.1 A far more effective strategy is to consider the impact of the patient's unique anatomic geometry at the outset, such that initial OAR objectives reflect true lowest achievable dose levels, which mitigates the dependence of plan quality on planner experience.2

Knowledge-based planning (KBP) approaches aim to increase planning efficiency by first predicting either the OAR dose-volume histograms (DVHs) or the full 3-dimensional (3D) dose distributions that are most likely to be associated with a specific patient's anatomy.3 Predicted DVHs can be used to define optimization objectives4 and predicted 3D dose distributions can be used as input to the inference of beam characteristics that will produce the distribution.5, 6, 7 This study pertains to the former of these approaches. In a world where deep learning architectures are increasingly prevalent, an advantage of a DVH-based KBP tool is that it can act as a radiation therapy quality assurance (QA) tool in the everyday clinic as well as in clinical trials,8 because predicted DVHs can be used to evaluate clinical dose/volume goals.

Existing DVH-based KBP approaches have been shown to improve plan quality while decreasing planning time,3,9 but clinical implementation and practical utilization have been hampered by the need for highly specific models trained with small, homogenous data sets. For example, van Gysen et al10 implemented KBP in their clinic by developing 24 separate models. They reported that this required 4 months of effort to create the first model trained with 70 plans, and “one week or less” for the other 23 models, which were trained with between 20 and 68 plans. Importantly, even with 24 models, 11% (26/243) of nonpalliative/nonbreast patients at their clinic still did not have an appropriate model because they were from a subsite without enough plans for model development. Although the authors found that KBP ultimately improved planning efficacy, their study highlights the massive challenge in clinically implementing KBP.10

The considerable time and resources required for clinical implementation of existing KBP systems are largely due to: (1) the need for careful selection of training data in terms of plan quality,11, 12, 13 and (2) the usual practice of building models that address specific geometries (which has been found to be advantageous).10,14,15 It is common practice to employ an iterative procedure of model training, creation of high-quality plans using model outputs, and using the resulting plans to retrain models such that outputs produce yet higher quality plans.16,17 This cycle involves, and ultimate model effectiveness depends on, the skill and experience of the model builder and their ability to judge plan quality.12,18 There is a possibility for the biases of the model builder to be introduced, obscuring the clinical trade-offs that would be inherent in real-world data or compromising the deliverability of the plans because the replans are not subject to QA. Finally, this form of KBP places an additional mental load on the user, who must choose an appropriate model for a new plan or recognize when one does not exist.

Much of this requirement for models corresponding to specific geometries, and whose effectiveness is highly dependent on the quality of training data, is due to inherent limitations of the underlying algorithm, principal component analysis (PCA).3,19 PCA-based models are sensitive to dosimetric outliers, where sparing is poor relative to that expected given target-OAR proximity and other factors, because these outliers disproportionately inflate the variance and distort the principal components, leading to misleading transformations of the data that do not accurately capture the space of possible DVHs. PCA assumes that the variance-maximizing directions best represent the data. However, when geometric or dosimetric outliers are present, the directions of largest variance shift toward the outliers, causing important features to be lost.20 The result is that the models’ effectiveness in predicting treatment planning system (TPS) optimization objectives depends on the quality and uniformity of all the cases included. Finally, because PCA-based models are typically trained with small numbers of very similar plans, only a few features can be included before overfitting occurs.

Here, we propose a comprehensive, protocol-agnostic machine learning (ML)-based DVH prediction platform for KBP in external beam radiation therapy, the Predictive Planning platform (Plan AI, Sun Nuclear Corporation, A Mirion Medical Company) (Fig. 1). In contrast to other KBP systems, the platform uses a single model per OAR/anatomic region to predict DVHs for plans with any protocol, target geometry (eg, prostate-only vs prostate-plus-nodes, nasopharynx vs neck, lung vs esophagus), and treatment technique. Two separate predictions are carried out: (1) an “expected” DVH, where achieved dose values are approximately equally likely to be lower or higher than the predicted value, and (2) a “lowest achievable” DVH, where achieved dose values have a small likelihood of being lower than the predicted value. These predictions have distinct utility. Predicted expected DVHs can be used for plan quality evaluation (eg, to flag high plan dose values), and predicted lowest achievable DVHs can be used as objectives to initialize treatment plan optimization (whereby dose values represent suitably aggressive yet realistic goals for a plan optimizer).Figure 1Overview of the Plan AI Predictive Planning platform. In this study, which is the largest study of KBP to our knowledge, we develop and validate 4 models for the Head and Neck (H&N), Thoracic, Abdominal, and Pelvic regions with over 5300 approved retrospective clinical plans (CPs) from 2 institutions. We then replan 72 CPs with end-to-end use of the Predictive Planning system interfaced with a TPS, driving plan optimization with OAR dose objectives derived from predicted lowest achievable DVHs, and demonstrate superior or equivalent OAR and planning target volume (PTV) dosimetric outcomes across a variety of treatment types. The results show that the Predictive Planning platform has the potential to transform KBP from a promising yet practically challenging concept into a clinically viable and easily adoptable tool that improves plan quality and reduces planning time without adding to the burden on health care providers.

Methods and Materials

Source of data and participants

Development and internal testing data

For model development and internal testing, retrospective H&N, Thoracic, Abdominal, and Pelvis RT plans collected during routine care at Johns Hopkins Hospital (JHU; all anatomic regions) and Montefiore Einstein Comprehensive Cancer Center (Abdominal: Liver) were reviewed and deidentified. JHU plans were created between 2008 and 2019, and Montefiore Einstein Comprehensive Cancer Center plans were created between 2009 and 2020. Plans were included if they had been approved for clinical delivery and if they exhibited sufficient target coverage (90% for H&N, 92% for Thoracic, 85% for Abdominal, and 94% for Pelvis); thresholds were determined in collaboration with clinicians to represent clinically acceptable plans. Importantly, there were no protocol, PTV/OAR geometry, or prescription-based inclusion or exclusion criteria—all available external photon beam treatment plans were included. This included the following delivery techniques and treatment types: volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT), fixed-gantry intensity modulated radiation therapy (IMRT), helical tomotherapy, conventional and hypo-fractionated, and stereotactic body radiation therapy (SBRT). Data curation consisted of OAR name standardization and identification of PTVs and their associated prescriptions.

Clinical validation data

A set of commonly used treatment protocols to be assessed during clinical validation was established for each anatomic site. These included:•For H&N, plans with 1 to 4 target dose levels, 10 to 48 fractions, in a variety of anatomic locations: lip, larynx, base of tongue, parotid, nasopharynx, scalp, neck, etc.•For Thoracic, targets in multiple locations within the lungs and esophagus, including single-target, simultaneous integrated boost (SIB), and multiphase courses, treated with conventional- and hypo-fractionation, and SBRT.•For Abdominal, targets in the liver and pancreas, single-target and SIB courses, treated with conventional- and hypo-fractionation and SBRT.•For Pelvis, gynecologic (GYN) and prostate TVs, with and without nodes, including single-target, SIB, and multiphase courses, preprostatectomy and postprostatectomy for prostate.

Outcomes

Models were trained to predict the relative dose (normalized to the highest prescription dose) to each relative percent volume of each OAR. Two types of DVH are predicted:1.Expected DVH: the prediction is intended to match the clinically achieved dose; the model is optimized with a custom loss function to minimize the area between the predicted and achieved DVH, such that it simultaneously considers the error in both dose and volume (Fig. E1).2.Lowest achievable DVH: the model is optimized with a quantile loss function to predict the conditional fifth percentile, explained further in the Introduction.

Predictors

Table E1 lists all features that were available for selection during model training, along with methods for calculating each feature. Features were characterized into the following categories: distance, overlap volume histogram (OVH), target and OAR geometry (complexity, volume, etc.), and prescription. The OVH is a characterization of the 3D PTV-OAR geometric relationship, described in previously published work.21 Briefly, an OVH is the relative volume versus distance plot (note that it is closely related to a DVH, which gives relative volume vs dose) where relative volume is the proportion of an OAR that is within a given distance from the surface of a given target. The projection OVH is an extension of the OVH concept that considers only the volume of the OAR in the axial plane of the PTV. Feature calculation is discussed further in Appendix E1.

Analytical methods

Model architecture

The model used to predict the normalized dose-at-volume at a series of volume points is a custom ensemble made up of 2 histogram-based gradient boosting regression tree submodels: one to predict dose to close-distance points, and one to predict dose to far distance points. The use of submodels effectively constrains the first branch of the tree to divide relative OAR volumes according to whether they are in the overlap/falloff regions, or farther from the PTV, because achievable doses in these 2 regions have different relationships with the inputs. Points that overlap the PTV may have their dose determined mainly by their distance to or within the PTV, whereas far distance volume points may additionally have their dose determined by the proportion of OAR that is in-plane of the PTV (characterized by the projection OVHs). It is critical that predictions within and near the overlap region are accurate, as this represents the highest doses delivered to the OAR and is commonly where dosimetric goals are concentrated. Dose is first predicted at each relative volume point independently by the appropriate submodel, then predictions are concatenated and smoothed with a generalized additive model to enforce a monotonic decreasing constraint in the final predicted DVH. Further details about model architecture, training, optimization, and internal testing are provided in Appendix E1.

Platform implementation

The Predictive Planning platform includes a cloud-based web application for configuring treatment protocols and performing plan evaluation, a .NET application (called the Edge Agent) that runs as a service on a server within the customer's clinic, and scripts that run on the customer's TPS to provide a graphical user interface to Plan AI. Scripting interfaces have been validated for RayStation (RaySearch Laboratories), Eclipse (Varian Medical Systems), and Pinnacle (Philips). In the case of RayStation, the script is written in Python. The TPS script provides connectivity to the Edge Agent via DICOM and HTTPS. All PHI sent to the Edge Agent is deidentified or encrypted before being sent to Plan AI cloud-based resources. After the Predictive Planning system produces the desired sparing objectives, the user at the TPS requests, via the script, the planning objectives (and all the planning setup, such as prescriptions and beams). The Predictive Planning system responds with a JSON stream of plan setup instructions, including objectives, and the script processes the JSON and sets up the planning process, so that the plan is ready to be optimized. The process generally lasts no more than 15 to 20 seconds.

Clinical evaluation

The models were deployed within a research version of the Predictive Planning web application. Treatment planning was performed with RayStation version 2023b. A script in RayStation was used to implement the instruction set based on the selected protocol: create derived structures such as rings or planning organs at risk volumes (PRVs), create the plan, create beam sets for each phase of the plan, create the objectives for IMRT, and create the clinical goals for plan evaluation. Structure sets were uploaded to the web application for each clinical validation plan, and the resulting lowest achievable DVH predictions were converted to objectives and transferred to the RayStation TPS for automated plan optimization by an experienced clinical treatment planner.

Planning for the evaluation cases was performed with OAR objectives derived from the lowest achievable DVH predictions. Predicted dose values (objectives) were not modified during the process. Objective weights were allowed to be changed for subsequent optimization runs. Nearly all weight changes were for target objectives to improve the target coverage and uniformity. In a few cases, weights were increased on the higher dose portions of critical structures to adjust the dose in the falloff region around the target. No more than 5 optimizations were run to complete the planning. Statistical analysis of predictive plans (PPs) versus CPs was performed after all PPs were deemed clinically acceptable by the user. To control for the influence of beam geometry/technique and TPS, each PP was generated with the same VMAT arc strategy and TPS as the corresponding CP (see Clinical validation data). Sample size calculation was performed prior to the study as discussed in Appendix E1.

Model performance metrics

Expected-DVH model accuracy was assessed by calculating the nearest distance error between the predicted expected dose and the clinically achieved dose. The lowest-achievable-DVH model accuracy was assessed by comparing the actual proportion of low-dose predictions for which the clinically achieved dose was even lower than the intended proportion (5%).

The primary outcome of interest for the CP comparison study was the mean dose for clinically relevant OARs, and the intent of the trial was to demonstrate noninferiority of OAR doses for PP compared to CP. A lower mean dose is a simple and suitable indicator of superior OAR sparing. Doses were compared for 25 OARs for H&N (Brainstem, SpinalCord, Brain, Cochlea_L/R, Eye_L/R, Lens_L/R, OpticNrv_L/R, OpticChiasm, Bone_Mandible, Parotid_L/R, Glnd_Submand_L/R, Cavity_Oral, Glnd_Sublings, Palate_Soft, Glottis, Glnd_Thyroid, Musc_Constrict, Cricopharyngeus, and Esophagus), 11 OARs for Thoracic (Esophagus, Heart, Kidney_L/R, Liver, Lung_L/R, Lungs, SpinalCanal, Stomach, Trachea), 8 OARs for Abdominal (Bowel, Duodenum, Heart, Kidney_L/R, Liver, SpinalCanal, Stomach), and 7 OARs for Pelvis (Bladder, Rectum, Femur_Head_L/R, Colon_Sigmoid, PenileBulb, Bag_Bowel). The Bonferroni correction was used to adjust the alpha level required for significance according to the number of OARs being compared in an anatomic region (alpha = 0.05/num_OARs), and nonparametric Wilcoxon signed-rank tests between PP and CP were used to assess significance.

Consistent with the recommendations by Baroudi et al,22 in addition to OAR dose metrics, comparison of PTV coverages and conformity indices23 was also performed. Maintaining PTV coverage and conformity is an indicator that superior OAR sparing has not been achieved via a clinically unacceptable tradeoff. Secondary comparison of additional dose metrics and whole-DVH differences was also performed.

Model performance

Table E3 contains quantitative performance metrics for all models referenced in this study. Overall, the average error in normalized dose and relative volume (nearest distance error) was less than 10% for all models. The models’ performance was weakly correlated with the number of training plans for each OAR (r = –0.235 for nearest distance error and r = –0.130 for percent below achievable), but this was not statistically significant (P = .192 and .406, respectively), and in fact some of the best-performing models were trained with lower numbers of plans (Fig. E2). The percentage of the lowest achievable dose predictions above the clinically achieved dose converged to near 5% when N was large and was below 25% for all models. This indicates imperfect model calibration; however, note that 5% is an arbitrary choice of a “small proportion” of plans with actual dose less than the predicted value, such that this value represents a suitably (but not overly) aggressive optimization objective. Thus, the effectiveness of the achievable dose model can better be assessed in terms of whether the predicted objectives result in equivalent or lower OAR doses (see Clinical planning study results). Future work could optimize this percentile in terms of its effectiveness in plan optimization.

Discussion

In this study, we presented the Predictive Planning platform, which represents a shift in KBP from protocol-specific models trained with small uniform data sets to protocol-agnostic, disease-site-specific models trained with large heterogeneous data sets. The key innovations in our approach are: (1) the models are trained using diverse data sets (in terms of geometry, prescription, etc.) and in turn can be applied to diverse subject plans, (2) training plans are not required to be of uniformly high quality, and (3) the above translates to practical implementation benefits. Although the platform is not the first to use ML for KBP,24 to our knowledge, it is the first DVH prediction model that uses a large set of shape relationship features and shape complexity features while including multiple integrated boost targets in a single model, enabling protocol-agnostic application of the model in practice.

The internal testing results show that the same model can accurately predict expected OAR DVHs for disparate plan types that would need to be divided into separate models in existing KBP systems,10 and the clinical validation results show that PP OAR dose metrics were superior or equivalent to CP for all 51 OARs in all 4 anatomic regions, while PTV coverage metrics were maintained or exceeded. These findings demonstrate that the expected-DVH model provides a robust framework for plan evaluation, while the TPS objectives predicted by the achievable DVH model can effectively drive plan optimization without the need for manual objective dose value editing, greatly simplifying the traditional iterative, skill-dependent process.

We have developed a robust and adaptive approach for DVH prediction, with a feature set, model architecture, and platform implementation that overcomes each of the following limitations of existing KBP systems3:1.Strong association of model effectiveness with the quality of all plans in the training data set: The core of our model is a tree-based gradient boosting regression ensemble.25 For prediction of the lowest achievable DVH, the model is optimized with a quantile loss function to predict the conditional fifth percentile. Dosimetric outliers with relatively poor OAR sparing mostly fall into separate leaves of the trees in the ensemble than high-quality plans with relatively good OAR sparing; hence, they minimally influence predictions in the mid-to-lower tail of the distribution. Importantly, this approach does not assume that the data are normally distributed, unlike a traditional standard deviation–based confidence interval, which would be widened by dosimetric outliers (in either tail of the distribution) that increase variance.Unlike in other systems, plans with suboptimal OAR sparing were not excluded when training our models. Only plans with unacceptable target coverage were excluded. The results show that our models are effective at predicting expected DVHs for plan evaluation and driving plan optimization despite the inclusion of plans with suboptimal OAR sparing in the training data, as can be seen in Fig. 3.2.Need for outlier removal and small, uniform, extensively curated data sets: As discussed in #1, the model architecture is minimally sensitive to outliers and is thus suited to large, heterogeneous training data sets. Although training plans are required to satisfy target coverage requirements, they do not need to be restricted to only those perceived as highest “quality” in terms of OAR sparing. Because the model builder does not make subjective judgments of plan quality, the models reflect clinical trade-offs inherent in real-world data.The models in this study were developed and validated with data from thousands of plans representing a range of OAR-PTV geometries, OAR dosimetric characteristics, target types and locations, treatment techniques, protocols, and prescriptions (Figure 2, Figure 3)—outliers were not identified or removed. The results show that a single model per OAR/anatomic region can predict DVHs with consistent accuracy across a range of geometric and dosimetric situations (Fig. 4) that are enveloped by the training data. For example, models for Pelvis OARs have similar accuracy for plans with large targets that include pelvic lymph nodes, as for targets that include only the prostate or prostate bed.The results also show that the effectiveness of the model is not solely a consequence of being trained with large data sets (relative to those typically used to train KBP models in existing systems). Individual OAR models within each anatomic region were trained with 41 to 4079 OAR/RT dose examples. There was a weak positive correlation of model accuracy with increasing numbers of training plans. However, there was no association between the number of training plans and the observed decrease in mean or near-maximum OAR dose when comparing PP with CP—models trained with relatively few samples were equally effective in driving improved OAR sparing. This suggests that the model's effectiveness is due to the model design and feature set itself, which represent the anatomic and dosimetric constraints that govern achievable OAR sparing, rather than the size of the data set. However, a larger and more heterogeneous data set expands the applicability of the model by allowing the trees to learn different relationships for different ranges of data.3.Requirement for models corresponding to specific treatment geometries and protocols: Tree-based models naturally split data into distinct regions corresponding to learned behavior for different ranges of features (such as target size and fractionation).26 For example, if there are different behaviors between conventionally fractionated, large target pelvic node irradiation versus small target prostate SBRT, different regions of the trees will cater to these plan types. Thus, a single ensemble model can be trained using a wide range of plans.The models account for many factors that influence OAR dose, such as proximity to targets (via submodels for close and far distance points and OVH features), in-plane versus out-of-plane and overlap volumes (through initial tree branching according to the number of feature sets and multiple OVH variations), and shape characteristics. By preserving spatial features and enhancing them with characteristics of the OVH, such as slope of the curve and in-plane and out-of-plane volume ratios, the models can capture subtle influences on the OAR DVH. We experimented with including treatment technique as an input, but found that it had zero importance compared to the spatial features, and it increased the complexity of retrospective data curation; hence, it was not included. We hypothesize that the impact of the technique was minimized due to the planner's skill in achieving technique-independent dosimetric goals during the creation of the training plans.1 Characteristics of the treatment protocol and intent, such as the fractionation scheme, were used as an input for the model rather than as data set filters. This knowledge of the protocol associated with each training plan and with the plan being optimized further reinforces the protocol-agnostic nature of the models.The plans used in this study for evaluating the effectiveness of predicted optimization objectives represent many protocols and target geometries per model tested (Table E2), in contrast to prior KBP plan comparison studies which typically include a single treatment protocol or target geometry per model.9 Importantly, it is not required that a specific protocol be represented in the development data set for the model to be applied to that protocol—it can be seen in Fig. 2 that several protocols in the clinical validation data set were not present in the development and internal testing data set. This represents a significant advancement in the practical clinical application of KBP—given a broad set of training data enveloping the types of plans the models should be applied to, users of the PP system do not need to create new models as the protocols used for a given anatomic region evolve, as long as the treatment characteristics are reasonably similar. Note that this implies the validity of interpolation within the training set feature space, but we did not evaluate extrapolation outside of the space.4.Burden and biases associated with KBP model development10: Data preparation for model development consisted of reviewing the intended targets and prescription doses for each plan in a large retrospective data set and aligning the structure names with standardized terminology in bulk. Plans were not replanned if OAR sparing was suboptimal, and every plan used to train the model was approved for clinical delivery through the institution's QA process, meaning that the models reflect the trade-offs inherent in real clinical data and not idealized, replanned data sets that are subject to the biases of the model builder. Consequently, the appropriate model can be automatically selected in the Predictive Planning platform using only the disease location. Importantly for clinical implementation, the same ML algorithm and model development process can be used across all anatomic regions/OAR combinations, and this process does not involve any manual selection of training plans.

Dosimetric outliers with relatively poor OAR sparing mostly fall into separate leaves of the trees in the ensemble than high-quality plans with relatively good OAR sparing; hence, they minimally influence predictions in the mid-to-lower tail of the distribution. Importantly, this approach does not assume that the data are normally distributed, unlike a traditional standard deviation–based confidence interval, which would be widened by dosimetric outliers (in either tail of the distribution) that increase variance.

Unlike in other systems, plans with suboptimal OAR sparing were not excluded when training our models. Only plans with unacceptable target coverage were excluded. Limitations

One limitation of our study is that single-institution training data were used for H&N, Thoracic, and Pelvis. Thus, although achievable OAR sparing is largely governed by radiation physics, there will be OAR sparing trade-offs inherent in the data that reflect one institution's sparing preferences. The models may, therefore, not be optimal for use at all institutions: although the models are protocol-agnostic, they are not necessarily data-agnostic. External validation of the models with different-institution data is outside the scope of this work, but the models have been externally clinically validated for prostate cancer (IMRT/VMAT) as reported by Velten et al.27 It should be noted that deployment of the models in any clinical environment should include performance evaluation in terms of benefit in arriving at an acceptable plan. We hypothesize that models trained with multi-institution data would converge on a general set of best practices, and we hope to explore this in future work.

Another limitation is that the study protocol did not include the collection of the number of optimization iterations and objective weight changes for each PP, nor was this information available retrospectively for CP; hence, we are unable to quantify the human-in-the-loop contribution. We note that the study protocol included a limitation on the maximum number of optimization iterations (5); no predicted objective dose values were manually changed for any plan, and the planner reported that nearly all objective weight changes were for targets to ensure adequate target coverage. Future work will include multi-institution prospective validation of the platform across a diverse range of treatment indications, and may assess whether PPs are more likely to pass QA tests than CPs. These studies will ensure that the human-in-the-loop contribution is quantified via the standardized collection of the metrics described.

Finally, plan comparison was performed using DVH-based metrics and target conformity. As suggested by Baroudi et al,22 experienced physicians often prefer plans based on “look,” and thus, a qualitative blinded plan comparison study could further explore the differences between PP and CP. PPs did not undergo physician review. Presumably, because the CPs were delivered plans, and the OAR and PTV dose metrics were statistically equivalent or superior for the PPs, the PPs are also clinically acceptable.

Conclusions

Use of Predictive Planning allows the creation of a high-quality plan without the need to adjust OAR objective dose values initially provided to the optimizer, thus lessening reliance on planner expertise and shortening the time required to arrive at a final plan. Although this statement could be made to conclude any study of the performance of a KBP platform that uses suitably trained models,28 we have shown that the Predictive Planning platform overcomes limitations of existing KBP systems related to the flexibility of each model in terms of treatment protocol parameters, and the need for resource-intensive, hands-on model training. Our protocol-agnostic approach to predicting expected and lowest achievable OAR DVHs yields accurate results across a wide range of plan parameters within 4 anatomic regions. Use of the predicted lowest achievable dose values as TPS optimization objectives leads to high-quality plans across this broad scope. We hope that this work will contribute to the adoption of KBP as a means of increasing efficiency and ensuring consistent plan quality.
