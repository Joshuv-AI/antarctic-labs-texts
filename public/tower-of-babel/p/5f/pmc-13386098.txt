Refined classification and phenotype-driven analysis of PIEZO1 variants in hereditary red blood cell and iron disorders∗

ABSTRACT
Key Points•An integrative framework reclassified PIEZO1 variants, revealing domain-specific pathogenic clustering.•Patient analyses identified 3 phenotypic clusters, linking PIEZO1 variant domains to disease severity and presentation.

Introduction

The PIEZO1 gene (HUGO Gene Nomenclature Committee: 28993; Gene ID: 9780) is located on chromosome 16q24.3 and spans ∼69 883 base pairs in the human reference genome. It comprises 51 coding exons encoding a mechanosensitive, nonselective cation channel composed of three identical subunits, each contributing to a propeller-shaped structure within the plasma membrane and are activated by several mechanical stimuli.1,2 PIEZO1 is primarily expressed in nonsensory tissues exposed to fluid pressure and shear stress and is involved in a broad range of mechanotransduction processes, including lymphatic development, angiogenesis, and red blood cell (RBC) deformability.3,4 Because of its widespread expression, variants in PIEZO1 gene exert pleiotropic effects. In humans, loss-of-function pathogenic variants cause generalized lymphatic dysplasia with nonimmune fetal hydrops.5 In contrast, gain-of-function (GoF) mutations disrupt normal channel inactivation, resulting in excessive cation efflux, dehydration, and hemolysis of RBCs. Germ line GoF variants in PIEZO1 are associated with dehydrated hereditary stomatocytosis (DHS, or hereditary xerocytosis), an autosomal dominant pleiotropic syndrome characterized by well-compensated hemolytic anemia, reticulocytosis, a tendency to macrocytosis, and jaundice.6,7 The worst complication is the iron overload, which is partially dependent on the degree of anemia, as PIEZO1 itself affects hepcidin regulation.8, 9, 10 Beyond anemic phenotype, affected individuals may present with polycythemia,11 particularly with PIEZO1 variants leading to an increased hemoglobin (Hb) oxygen affinity,12 hyperferritinemia,13 bone marrow failure, and myelodysplastic syndromes.14 In addition, predisposition to colorectal adenomatous polyposis syndrome has been associated with PIEZO1 variants.15 Notably, some patients may remain clinically silent, with variants discovered incidentally during family screening. Widespread PIEZO1 GoF mutations are also linked to malaria resistance and worsening of anemia in sickle cell disease.16,17 This wide phenotypic variability poses a substantial challenge for variant interpretation. The increasing accessibility to next-generation sequencing has led to the identification of numerous PIEZO1 variants. Applying the classification framework provided by the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) and the Association for Molecular Pathology (AMP) is essential for determining the pathogenicity of variants.18 However, this framework has several limitations when applied to PIEZO1, mainly because of limited functional data, challenges in phenotype correlation, insufficient predictive power of in silico tools, and, above all, high allelic heterogeneity. As a result, most of the variants in this gene are classified as variants of uncertain significance (VUS), complicating genetic counseling and clinical decision-making.19,20

In this study, we systematically curated and reclassified 2565 PIEZO1 variants using updated ACMG/AMP guidelines, applied quantitative and structural metrics to prioritize pathogenic changes, and explored the phenotypic consequences in a cohort of 176 patients with suspected hereditary RBC disorders (H-RBCDs) enrolled in our Medical Genetics unit from 2020 to 2024. Our aim was to provide a systematically curated and clinically annotated list of PIEZO1 variants to improve genotype-phenotype correlation.

Materials and methods

Variant curation

PIEZO1 nonnull variants (missense, in-frame deletions, and in-frame insertions) were curated from gnomAD (v2.1.1, GRCh37)21 and ClinVar22 databases. Variant annotation and filtering were performed using variant effect prediction.23 Both rare and common variants were included. Intronic, nonsense, synonymous, and frameshift variants were excluded. Variants were denoted by nucleotide change on the negative strand of hg19/GRch37 and cDNA position based on transcript GenBank NM_001142864.4. The protein level nomenclature was also reported. In addition, private variants found in the internal database were also included for reclassification.

ACMG-based variant classification

Criteria provided by the ACMG/AMP guidelines were applied to the curated variants. Scores were computed using a Bayesian classifier with a prior probability of 0.1 and translated for interpretations according to Table 1.24 Evidence criteria PM1, PM2, PM4, PM5, PS1, PS3, PS4, PP3, and PP5 were applied for pathogenicity. Evidence codes BA1, BS1, BP4, BP6, and BP7 were used to support benign classification (supplemental Tables 1 and 2, available on the Blood website). The application of PP3/BP4 evidence was based on a composite predictive score (CPS), which was developed by integrating multiple computational tools, as detailed in the supplemental Methods (supplemental Table 3). For the phenotype-based reclassification, criteria PP1, PP4, PM7, BS2, and BP8 were applied (supplemental Table 4). According to the ClinGen Sequence Variant Interpretation Work Group recommendations, the weight of listed criteria was adjusted using available information and professional judgment. Particularly, impact was modified as supporting (_sp), moderate (_m), strong (_s), and very strong (_vs), as detailed in the supplemental Methods.

Pathogenicity interpretation based on var_score

Variants reclassifications were compared with ACMG-based automated tools for clinical interpretation of genetic variants, particularly InterVar25 and Franklin (https://franklin.genoox.com) for variants left unclassified by InterVar.

Collection of patients, phenotypic categorization, and genotype-phenotype correlation

A total of 176 patients from 141 unrelated families carrying nonnull PIEZO1 rare variants, enrolled between 2020 and 2024, were retrospectively included in the study. For phenotype-based analyses, patients carrying additional causative genotypes involving other H-RBCD-related genes were excluded. To allow for standardized comparison of hematological parameters across patients, we implemented a z score normalization strategy based on gender- and age-specific reference ranges for each individual and laboratory variable. A genotype_score (gen_score), based on the variant_scores (var_scores) described previously, was established. For patients carrying a single variant, gen_score was equal to var_score; for those with multiple PIEZO1 variants, the gen_score corresponds to the highest var_score. Patients with gen_score ≥0.699 were considered genetically diagnosed.

Statistics

Statistical analyses were performed in R (version 4.4.1). Quantitative data were compared using Mann-Whitney U test. Multiple comparisons were performed using the Kruskal-Wallis test, with post hoc correction using Dunn’s multiple comparison test. Fisher exact test was used for enrichment analyses and considered statistically significant for P < .05 and odds ratio (OR) >2 or <0.5. Normalized mutational burden for each protein domain was computed and then adjusted for domain length. A 2-proportion z test was used to calculate differences in var_scores between the flat and stratified groups. A global χ2 test of independence was performed to assess differences in the overall distribution of variant scores. Hierarchical clustering was performed using Ward’s linkage.26 The first 2 principal components were used for unsupervised clustering using k-means algorithm. The number of clusters was set to 3, based on silhouette optimization, clinical interpretability, and empirically estimated by the elbow method.

The local university ethical committee approved the collection of patient data (Dipartimento di Medicina di Laboratorio, Federico II University of Naples; No. 252/18, October 2018). DNA samples were obtained from patients after they had signed their informed consent, and in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Updated interpretation of PIEZO1 variants using ACMG guidelines

Variant curation was carried out on 2565 gnomAD and ClinVar-annotated variants (regardless of the population frequency), as well as on private variants observed in our internal cohort of patients suspected of H-RBCDs. Specifically, 1447 out of 2565 variants were found exclusively in gnomAD, 355 only in ClinVar, and 755 in both databases. In addition, 8 variants identified in our internal cohort and absent from both databases were also included (Figure 1A). Among the coding nonnull curated variants, most were missense (2489; 97%), whereas a small fraction consisted of in-frame deletions (48; 1.9%) or insertions (28; 1.1%) (Figure 1B). After applying our reclassification pipeline, we observed a consensus for 101 pathogenic (P)/likely pathogenic (LP) or benign (B)/likely benign (LB) variants that were automatically classified according to ACMG guidelines by InterVar. No P/LP variants were downgraded to B/LB; however, 1 variant previously classified as B/LB was upgraded to LP. Among VUS, 1005 were reclassified, including 487 P/LP and 518 B/LB variants. Furthermore, 1345 variants remained as VUS, of which 40 were VUS-leaning pathogenic, with var_scores between 0.699 and 0.90 (possibly pathogenic), whereas 583 were VUS-leaning benign, with scores between 0.099 and 0.399 (possibly benign). In addition, 81 in-frame insertions/deletions, unclassified by InterVar were manually classified using weighted ACMG criteria. Most of these were classified as VUS (48 VUS, 1 VUS-LP, 21 VUS-LB), whereas 3 variants were reclassified as P/LP and 8 as B/LB (Figure 1C). The application of stratified ACMG criteria significantly affected overall reclassification. We compared flat with stratified classification scores, observing significant differences in var_score distribution (overall χ2 = 1399.3, P < 2.2 × 10−16), particularly affecting the reclassification of VUS (Figure 1D). Based on these scores, variants with var_score ≥0.699 were considered to be of diagnostic utility and designated as diagnostic pathogenic variants (DPVs), whereas those with var_score ≤0.399 were considered diagnostic-B/LB variants (DBVs).

In silico prediction in the context of PIEZO1 variant classification

One of the primary challenges in classifying PIEZO1 variants is the intrinsic gene tolerance to missense variations. This complicates the interpretation of potential pathogenicity, limiting the applicability of in silico predictive tools used to assess ACMG criteria PP3 (supporting pathogenicity) and BP4 (supporting benignity). To address this, we developed a CPS aiming to enhance the discriminatory power of in silico prediction tools in the PIEZO1 context (see the supplemental Methods). Based on CPS thresholds, PP3 and BP4 criteria were assigned to 1306 and 1366 variants, respectively. Application of stratified PP3/BP4 criteria succeeded in the resolution of 956 variants, otherwise classified as uncertain, with significant differences in var_score distribution (overall χ2 = 933.88; P < .001) (Figure 1E).

Pathogenic PIEZO1 variants are selectively distributed across PIEZO1 protein and are associated with distinct functional properties

To explore the spatial distribution of pathogenic variants along the PIEZO1 protein, an enrichment analysis was performed on all the curated variants. Variants were first mapped to the 22 defined domains of the PIEZO1 channel. For residues not falling within established domain boundaries, an extended annotation strategy was applied by assigning them to the closest neighboring domain (eg, clasp/transmembrane helix units [THU] 8, latch/clasp, THU2/THU3), allowing comprehensive coverage of the entire protein sequence. The analysis revealed a nonrandom distribution of pathogenic variants across the protein (Figure 2A). Specifically, we found that 4 domains were significantly enriched in DPVs. Particularly, the anchor domain has the strongest enrichment (OR > 15; P < .001). Besides, the inner helix (IH) presented the highest normalized mutational burden (ie, high number of DPVs for the domain length) with an OR >9 and P < .001. The C-terminal domain (CTD) was also found to be significantly enriched in DPVs. On the other hand, 7 domains were significantly depleted (OR < 0.5). These included THU5, THU1, THU2, and the central plug, as well as undefined linker regions like clasp/THU8, THU2/THU3, and latch/clasp, potentially subjected to less evolutionary constraint (Figure 2B). Then, we investigated whether DPVs and DBVs clustered within domains linked to specific mechanistic features, given the functional overlap and diversity of protein domains. Interestingly, DPVs were significantly associated with gating, ion permeation, rapid-response sensitivity, and protein interactions. Conversely, DBVs were predominantly located in uncharacterized or structurally peripheral domains, often predicted to have limited functional involvement (Figure 2C).

Phenotype-based variant classification in a cohort of patients carrying GoF PIEZO1 variants

The correct assessment of variant pathogenicity critically depends on robust phenotypic characterization of affected individuals. To this end, we selected a subset of patients referred to our diagnostic unit of Medical Genetics from 2020 to 2024, carrying nonsynonymous and nonnull PIEZO1 variants and presenting with clinical features suggestive of hereditary erythrocyte and iron metabolism disorders. Patients were classified into 6 major diagnostic categories based on their initial suspicion such as (1) membrane defects, including patients initially suspected for DHS, hereditary spherocytosis, hereditary elliptocytosis, and hereditary pyropoikilocytosis; (2) iron metabolism disorders, including patients with an initial suspicion of iron deficiency anemia, iron-refractory iron deficiency anemia, primary hyperferritinemia, and hereditary hemochromatosis; (3) erythrocytosis, encompassing patients presenting with polycythemia, or more generically referred with increased Hb or increased hematocrit (Hct); (4) other hematological conditions, including patients with the suspicion of erythrocyte enzyme defects, myelodysplastic syndrome, thalassemia, and congenital dyserythropoietic anemias; (5) incidental findings, referring to asymptomatic individuals, usually relatives of index cases investigated for other disorders, in whom a PIEZO1 variant was discovered through familial screening; and (6) H-RBCDs, encompassing all patients that were generally referred as affected by erythrocyte defects (Figure 3A). To increase phenotypic specificity, patients with multilocus inheritance were excluded from the analysis. Thus, the phenotype-based variant reclassification was performed for a total of 106 variants from 176 individuals. Segregation data were available for 30 patients from 13 unrelated families. In 1 case, 2 variants were observed in healthy family members, leading to the application of the ACMG criterion BS2. For the remaining pedigrees, PP1 was applied as supporting evidence. Another key piece of evidence for variant classification was the application of PM7_m and BP8_m (additional evidence), relying on ektacytometry results performed on 59 patients (supplemental Figure 1A). This integrative approach led to the reclassification of 39 variants from 56 patients (supplemental Table 5). Among them, 13 VUS were classified as DPVs (10 P/LP and 3 possibly pathogenic) (Figure 3B). All patients harboring reclassified variants had been referred for evaluation of suspected H-RBCDs, 3 of whom were initially assessed for membrane defects. Notably, 12 of the 13 individuals with newly classified DPVs underwent ektacytometry, which showed abnormal deformability curves consistent with a DHS phenotype. To classify the PIEZO1 variant in each patient, a genotype score (gen_score) was calculated. The gen_score was subsequently used to filter the patient cohort and guide downstream genotype-phenotype correlation analyses. High pathogenicity scores clustered in specific domains enriched for DPVs, particularly in the CTD and IH domains. Notably, variants annotated as LP or P in ClinVar frequently aligned with high pathogenicity scores and occurred within functionally constrained regions. Conversely, B or LB variants were mostly observed in peripheral or unstructured domains, consistent with the enrichment of DBVs (Figure 3C; supplemental Figure 1B-C). Most variants were observed in a single patient, highlighting the high prevalence of private variants. Notably, highly recurrent variants were generally benign, except for those located in mutational hotspots, such as the most common DHS1-causative variant, p.Arg2456His.

Genotype-phenotype correlation reveals distinct PIEZO1-associated clinical subtypes

To investigate the functional impact of PIEZO1 variants within a clinical framework, we analyzed patients carrying DPVs by integrating hematochemical and iron balance data. Z scores were computed to quantify the deviation of selected parameters from the reference ranges for each patient (supplemental Table 6).

A summary of the relative direction of change for each parameter is reported in Table 2. Hierarchical clustering based on z scores revealed a clear separation into 3 clusters (Figure 4A; supplemental Figure 2A-B), each characterized by distinct clinical features.

Cluster 1 comprised patients with features consistent with a typical DHS phenotype, characterized by macrocytic hemolytic anemia and severe iron overload. Hb levels were moderately reduced (11.65 ± 2.16 g/dL), with low RBC count ([3580 ± 510] × 10³/μL), and macrocytosis (93.4 ± 11.8 fL) with elevated mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration (MCHC) levels (34.9 ± 2.1 g/dL). Reticulocyte percentage was increased (6.22% ± 4.78%) as well as absolute reticulocyte count ([167.9 ± 61.2] × 10³/μL), whereas bone marrow responsiveness index values varied widely (159 ± 229; range, 6.7-802), indicating ineffective erythropoiesis in several cases. Hemolysis indices confirmed active hemolysis, with increased lactate dehydrogenase (234.4 ± 51.1 U/L) and total bilirubin (4.58 ± 6.35 mg/dL). Iron metabolism revealed marked overload, with increased ferritin levels (885.7 ± 715 ng/mL) and transferrin saturation (78.1% ± 61.9%) (supplemental Table 6B).

Cluster 2 comprised patients with a more complex and severe phenotype, divergent from the canonical DHS profile. Hb was lower (9.78 ± 1.31 g/dL), but unlike cluster 1, mean corpuscular volume (MCV; 66.9 ± 10.1 fL) and mean corpuscular Hb (MCH; 20.8 ± 3.3 pg) were significantly reduced, consistent with microcytic anemia. Erythrocyte count was moderately preserved ([4760 ± 660] × 10³/μL), and reticulocyte percentage was nearly normal (1.36% ± 0.61%). Iron status revealed moderately elevated ferritin levels (mean, 115 ± 96 ng/mL), with normal transferrin saturation (19.0% ± 16.9%) (supplemental Table 6C). To further characterize the genetic background underlying this peculiar phenotype, patients in cluster 2 underwent molecular screening for hemoglobinopathies. Of these, 5 of 9 patients carried globin gene variants. Specifically, 4 patients harbored a β0-trait, whereas 1 carried a β0-trait in association with α-trait (supplemental Table 7). The remaining 4 patients showed no detectable globin gene alterations.

Cluster 3 included patients with mild hematological abnormalities. Hb levels were near normal (14.56 ± 1.5 g/dL), with a tendency toward macrocytosis (MCV, 86.76 ± 8.59 fL). Reticulocytes were mildly increased (1.98% ± 1.1%), and hemolysis markers showed a slight increase (lactate dehydrogenase, 207.3 ± 42.77 U/L; bilirubin, 1.03 ± 0.97 mg/dL), supporting a compensated or low-grade chronic hemolysis. Ferritin levels were elevated in ∼30% of patients, although with a variable degree (155.4 ± 205.3 ng/mL), transferrin saturation remained normal (21.5% ± 11.7%) (supplemental Table 6D). Overall, cluster 3 reflects a mild DHS-like phenotype with subclinical hemolysis and limited iron dysregulation (Figure 4B-C).

Finally, the 3 clusters also differed in median age at diagnosis, with patients in cluster 1 being significantly older (42 ± 6.5 years), compared with those in cluster 2 (16 ± 7.7 years) and cluster 3 (32 ± 3.3 years), suggesting heterogeneous disease onset and progression trajectories (supplemental Table 6A,D). Variant- and domain-level associations reveal distinct PIEZO1-related clinical subtypes

To better understand the molecular basis of distinct clinical manifestations observed among phenotypic clusters, we investigated whether specific variants were enriched in particular phenotypic contexts. Individual variants were correlated with mean z scores. Despite substantial allelic heterogeneity, recurrent variants displayed consistent phenotypic clustering. Notably, the variant p.Arg1036His, found in 2 patients, showed significant deviation from reference values in erythrocyte count, Hct, total bilirubin, and ferritin, suggesting a potential impact on cell volume and iron metabolism. In contrast, the variant p.Arg141Cys, although observed in a single individual, presents markedly negative values for the same parameters, indicating potentially divergent phenotypic effects. Variants found in more patients, such as p.Pro2510Leu (n = 9) and p.Phe1000Leu (n = 4), exhibited consistent z score patterns across multiple parameters, including moderate reductions in Hb, MCV, and MCH (Figure 5A).

Nevertheless, the interpretation of variant-level association is limited by allelic heterogeneity. To overcome this, we examined mean z scores of key parameters grouped by PIEZO1 domains. This domain-level approach revealed distinct profiles. Variants within the CTD (n = 8) were associated with low RBC indices, mild hemolytic anemia, reticulocytosis, and a tendency toward iron overload. The IH domain was marked by a strong hyperferritinemia with only slight increases in transferrin saturation. Hb levels were near normal, but the reduced erythrocyte count and strongly elevated reticulocytes suggested enhanced but ineffective erythropoiesis.

Patients harboring variants in the THU domains (THU1-THU9) displayed heterogeneous hematological and biochemical profiles, though several shared features emerged. Most of these domains (eg, THU2-THU5) were associated with markedly reduced RBC count, and decreased Hb and Hct levels. Macrocytosis was frequently observed, particularly in THU1, THU3, THU4, and THU5. Reticulocyte counts and ferritin were increased in most domains (especially THU3, THU4, THU5, THU6). This profile is compatible with chronic iron loading, likely exacerbated by disrupted erythropoiesis (Figure 5B).

Discussion

Interpreting the clinical relevance of genetic variants in genes with complex functional roles remains a major challenge in the field of genetics. PIEZO1, the causative gene of DHS, encodes a mechanosensitive ion channel regulating erythrocyte volume, membrane tension, and deformability.1,2 PIEZO1 variants are frequently identified in other RBCDs or incidentally detected in large-scale sequencing, complicating pathogenicity assessment in the absence of a well-defined phenotype. This factor, along with the limited recurrence data and high gene tolerance to missense variation, restricts the utility of ACMG criteria and the predictive reliability of computational tools. This underlines the importance of continuous variant reinterpretation, particularly in disorders with complex and overlapping phenotypes, where diagnostic uncertainty may directly affect patient management and follow-up strategies.27,28

In this study, we developed a comprehensive framework for the interpretation of PIEZO1 variants, based on ACMG guidelines, quantitative in silico predictions, and detailed phenotypic data. We curated 2565 unique nonsynonymous, nonnull PIEZO1 variants from gnomAD and ClinVar, and private variants from our cohort of patients, focusing on exonic changes likely resulting in a GoF. Variant classification was performed using a Bayesian scoring system integrating weighted ACMG criteria, functional annotations, population data, and quantitative in silico predictors. A new predictive score, the CPS, was introduced to stratify damaging vs benign effects using 13 distinct predictors normalized and aggregated for each variant. CPS allowed a more confident application of PP3/BP4 criteria and resolved nearly 1000 VUS that would otherwise remain uninterpretable. Domain-based enrichment analysis revealed a nonrandom clustering of DPVs, revealing key insights into the structural and functional constraints of the channel. The most pronounced enrichments were observed in the anchor, IH, and CTD domains, implicated in gating and conformational transitions of the channel.1,29 Conversely, DBVs were significantly enriched in a distinct set of domains and within undefined interdomain regions, probably because of their lower structural constraint.1,30 Mechanistic analysis indicated that DPVs mainly affect channel gating and ion permeation, producing altered kinetics and increased cation influx typical of PIEZO1 GoF variants.7,31 These findings suggest that PIEZO1 is not uniformly sensitive to variation, but specific domains may serve as hotspots for pathogenicity. Another key aspect of variant interpretation is the integration of phenotypic data. Integration of phenotypic data further strengthened variant interpretation.

Phenotype-informed classification was applied to 106 variants observed in a cohort of 176 patients with suspected H-RBCDs. The weighted application of PM7, BP8, PP1, PP4, and BS2 criteria resulted in the further reclassification of 39 variants from 56 patients, expanding the list of high-confidence DPVs.

Notably, most patients carrying reclassified variants showed abnormal ektacytometry profiles consistent with DHS, underscoring the biological plausibility of the refined variant interpretation. These findings highlight the importance of integrating functional red cell membrane analysis with genetic data to improve diagnostic accuracy and support clinical decision-making for PIEZO1-related disorders. Unsupervised clustering, based on the integration of hematochemical and iron balance data of patients carrying DPVs, revealed 3 distinct phenotypic clusters, highlighting meaningful biological differences. Cluster 1 reflected the classic DHS1 features, including moderate macrocytic hemolytic anemia, reticulocytosis, and severe iron overload. Conversely, patients belonging to cluster 2 displayed microcytosis and anemia in the absence of overt hemolysis, diverging from the classical DHS1 presentation. Although the exact mechanism remains elusive, these findings may reflect a subtle alteration in PIEZO1 channel kinetics, leading to impaired erythroid maturation rather than RBC dehydration. Notably, most of these patients carried heterozygous α- or β-thalassemia variants, suggesting that coinheritance with PIEZO1 variants contributes to the atypical phenotype, with PIEZO1 acting as a genetic modifier. This is consistent with recent evidence demonstrating that the coexistence of PIEZO1 variants and other RBC defects can account for symptomatic presentations in mild hemoglobinopathy carriers.32

Cluster 3 was characterized by a milder, often subclinical phenotype, characterized by a compensated hemolytic profile, with signs of ineffective erythropoiesis and low-grade iron dysregulation. The incomplete penetrance of this phenotype may be attributed to the later median age at diagnosis, supporting the evidence that several DHS1-related features are age dependent.9,19 The identification of distinct clusters raises the clinically relevant question of whether these phenotypic differences are already present early in life or instead evolve with disease duration. Although our study design does not allow assessment of temporal trajectories, the presence of pediatric patients across clusters suggests that phenotypic divergence may begin early. Longitudinal studies will be necessary to clarify whether early cluster assignment translates into differential long-term risk of iron overload.

To explore the molecular basis of the phenotypic heterogeneity, we performed genotype-phenotype correlation analyses at both the variant and domain levels. Although single-variant associations were limited by the high allelic heterogeneity, some recurrent variants demonstrated consistent patterns of hematological abnormalities. Interestingly, when aggregated by domains, clear phenotypic trends emerged, highlighting domain-level clustering of functional phenotypes. Variants within the CTD domain are associated with macrocytic anemia, increased reticulocytes, and iron overload. The IH domain showed a profile of hyperferritinemia and nearly normal Hb levels, with a reduced RBC count and high reticulocytes, suggesting enhanced but ineffective erythropoiesis. Finally, despite some heterogeneity observed across the THU domains, most variants led to macrocytic or normocytic anemia and frequent iron overload, reinforcing the functional relevance of THU regions in red cell homeostasis.30,33 Notably, a unique variant (p.Gly1394del) located in the central plug domain, although identified in only 1 patient, was associated with the most severe clinical presentation. This patient exhibited severe microcytic anemia, marked hemolysis, and significant iron overload. Remarkably, this domain is generally enriched in benign variants, suggesting that specific residues may exert critical functional effects when altered.

Our integrative analysis shows that PIEZO1 variants produce domain-specific and phenotypically discrete clusters rather than a uniform disease spectrum. This stratification enhances diagnostic precision and supports genotype-guided patient management, including iron overload surveillance and cautious evaluation of splenectomy, and reinforces the need for domain-level and phenotype-aware variant classification. This study provides a framework through which the genetic result itself may inform the expected hematological phenotype and guide individualized follow-up, particularly regarding hemolysis severity and iron overload risk.
