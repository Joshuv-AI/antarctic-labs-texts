Is self-report brain fog after mild COVID-19 truly induced by COVID-19 infection? A longitudinal fNIRS study
Authors: Qian Ding, Cheng Wu, Zi-tong Ou, Shan-tong Yao, Lei Zhang, Yue Lan, Guang-qing Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionBrain fog (i.e., subjective cognitive deficits) is one of the most common long-lasting symptoms post-COVID-19. Cognitive deficits and psychological factors (such as depression) are possible causes of brain fog. This study is to determine whether brain fog post-COVID-19 results from cognitive deficits or depression induced by COVID-19 infection.MethodsWe implemented psychological (depression scale) and behavioural measures (dual task: subtraction while walking) with simultaneous functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) recording in 25 healthy young adults pre-, 3, 7 and 14 weeks post-COVID-19. Depression level, dual task performance and fNIRS results were compared among sessions. The relationships between brain fog severity, depression and dual task performance were assessed.ResultsDepression level and dual task performance remained unchanged post-COVID-19. Brain fog severity was positively correlated depression at all sessions (p’s < 0.05). Brain activation was significantly reduced at all sessions post-COVID-19 (p’s < 0.001), while global efficiency was significantly reduced at 7 weeks post-COVID-19 (p = 0.003). Changes in left somatosensory and motor cortices activation were inversely correlated with changes in walking cost (p’s = 0.005 and 0.007, respectively), while changes in global efficiency were associated with changes in walking cost (p = 0.006) from pre- to 7 weeks post-COVID-19.ConclusionsMild COVID-19 infection does not lead to apparent cognitive deficits or depression, although brain activation and global efficiency are reduced post-COVID-19. Brain fog is likely to result from depression rather than cognitive deficits induced by COVID-19. People should pay attention to their psychological states during COVID-19 pandemic.Keywords: Brain fog, COVID-19, depression, dual task, fNIRS

Introduction

Coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) represents a worldwide burden not only due to the severe respiratory symptoms during the acute phase, but also for the long-lasting symptoms (Yong 2021; Furlanis et al. 2023). Among the most common symptoms post-COVID-19, brain fog refers to subjective cognitive complaints (not objectively measured cognitive deficits), which is manifested as the inability to concentrate and thought slowness (Ocon 2013). Brain fog has been reported to persist for more than 3 months in over 20% patients post-COVID-19 including mild infection (Asadi-Pooya et al. 2022; Furlanis et al. 2023). Reports about brain fog post-COVID-19 frequently appear in social media and TV news, making people worry about cognitive symptoms even when the infection was mild. However, it remains unclear what causes the high prevalence of brain fog post-COVID-19.

As self-report symptoms, brain fog has been recognised as a multifactorial construct influenced by cognitive, psychological, physiological, and psychosocial factors (Azcue et al. 2022; Schild et al. 2023). Previous studies reported cognitive deficits in patients with severe COVID-19 infection compared with healthy adults (Giardini et al. 2022; Morelli et al. 2022), but whether mild infection of COVID-19 causes cognitive deficits remains unclear. As to depression, a previous study reported that about 30% of patients after COVID-19 infection show persistent depression (Premraj et al. 2022). As lacking pre-COVID data, depression is not necessarily induced by COVID-19 infection itself, which is also possibly due to chronic stress resulting from social and economic challenges of COVID-19 pandemic (Ceban et al. 2022). Therefore, whether brain fog symptoms post-COVID-19 are induced by COVID-19 infection needs further investigation.

Dual-task (DT) is a sensitive measure for higher cortical functions and can be used to objectively assess cognitive deficits post-COVID-19 (Leone et al. 2017). Monitoring brain activities during DT would help understand neural mechanisms underlying cognitive function changes, which can be measured by functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS). fNIRS is a well-established non-invasive tool that monitors regional tissue oxygenation (Wagshul et al. 2019; Chen et al. 2020), and can be used to assess brain activation and functional connectivity during tasks (Ding et al. 2021). To our knowledge, no study has measured fNIRS during DT post-COVID-19. Brain hypometabolism has been reported post-COVID-19 (Hosp et al. 2021; Hugon et al. 2022; Kiatkittikul et al. 2022). As fNIRS reflects metabolic patterns of brain regions (Caliandro et al. 2013), hypometabolism could cause reduction in brain activation during high-demanding tasks such as DT. Widespread white matter disruption has also been reported post-COVID-19 (Yang et al. 2021; Rau et al. 2022; Paolini et al. 2023). As white matter integrity is the basis of normal transmission efficiency in human brain (Sbardella et al. 2015; Liu et al. 2022), white matter disruption could cause reduction in functional connectivity during DT. But the above speculations have not been previously tested.

Importantly, most existing COVID-19 studies lack pre-COVID-19 data. Comparisons between patients post-COVID-19 and healthy controls are unable to determine whether abnormalities are induced by COVID-19. Therefore, longitudinal studies with both pre- and post-COVID-19 data are needed to investigate alterations caused by COVID-19. We previously implemented depression scale and measured DT performance with simultaneous fNIRS recording in 30 healthy adults from September 15 to November 10, 2022. By that time, none of the subjects has ever been infected with COVID-19. After the opening policy acted in early December, most people in China were infected with COVID-19 in a short period of time. There were 25 subjects who participated in the previous study infected with COVID-19 from December 12 to 27, 2022. Infection was mild in all subjects (no pneumonia or hospitalization). Taking this unique opportunity, we repeated all previous measures as well as brain fog questionnaire at ∼3, 7 and 14 weeks after the onset of COVID-19. To understand the possible causes of brain fog post-COVID-19, this study aims to examine associations rather than causal pathways between brain fog, depression and DT performance, particularly testing (1) whether brain fog severity is related to depression level or DT performance, and (2) whether depression level, DT performance and neural processes during DT (e.g., brain activation and global efficiency) change post- compared with pre-COVID-19.

Material and methods

Participants

Due to the unique timing of the COVID-19 outbreak, our sample size was opportunistic. A total of 30 healthy adults were screened from September 15 to November 10, 2022, and 25 of them was infected with COVID-19 in December. These 25 subjects [9 males; mean age: 24.1 (SD = 4.4) years] were therefore invited to complete the following sessions. The study was carried out in conformity with standards set by the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained prior to enrolment. All procedures were approved by the Guangdong Provincial People’s Hospital Human Research Ethics Committee (KY-Z-2022-221-02).

Participants were included if (1) completed all measures pre-COVID-19; (2) absence of neurological or musculoskeletal disease; (3) had a confirmed COVID-19 diagnosis by a positive result on polymerase chain reaction testing or antigen detection of oropharyngeal samples, accompanied by typical COVID-19 symptoms. Demographic characteristics are described in Supplementary Table S1.

Experimental procedure

This is a prospective observational study, which employed a longitudinal pre-post design. All participants were assessed both before and after COVID-19 infection, allowing each participant to serve as their own control. Session 1 was conducted 2–3 months before COVID-19 infection (25 subjects). Session 2 was ∼3 weeks [mean (SD): 19.2 (3.0) days] after onset of COVID-19 (10 subjects), and 15 subjects missed this session because of quarantine policy. Session 3 was ∼7 weeks [mean (SD): 52.2 (3.3) days] after the onset of COVID-19 (25 subjects), and Session 4 was ∼14 weeks [mean (SD): 97.5 (5.7) days] after the onset of COVID-19 (24 subjects), and one subject missed this session because of moving to another city.

Questionnaires

Questionnaires included self-report COVID-19 questionnaire, brain fog questionnaire, the centre for epidemiological studies-depression (CES-D) and the Pittsburgh sleep quality index (PSQI), with the first two implemented only post-COVID-19 and the last two implemented in all sessions. COVID-19 questionnaire included symptoms and severity of COVID-19, such as date of COVID onset, diagnostic method, vaccine, and symptoms. Brain fog questionnaire included questions regarding severity of brain fog symptoms (such as difficulty in concentration, thought slowness, forgetfulness, mental fatigue, etc.). Of note, brain fog questionnaire was not included pre-COVID-19 infection. CES-D is a widely applied measure for depression, with a score of 16 or higher suggesting clinical depression (Lewinsohn et al. 1997). The PSQI provides a global measure of sleep quality over the past month and is widely used in both clinical and research settings (Buysse et al. 1989).

Behavioural tasks

Behavioural tasks included cognitive tasks while standing, walking alone and DT. Each task included 3 trials; each trial was 30s, with a 25s rest break between two adjacent trials. The order of all tasks was completely randomised.

Cognitive tasks

The cognitive tasks included serial 3 and 7 subtractions (i.e., Subtraction3 and Subtraction7, respectively). During serial subtraction tasks, subjects recited serial subtractions of 3 or 7 out loud starting from a random number from 140 to 150. The number of correct responses for each trial were counted and averaged for further analysis.

Walking task

Participants were instructed to walk up and down a 10 m-long, 3 m-wide hallway at their self-selected walking speed (i.e., Walking). Walking speed was recorded and averaged for further analysis.

Dual tasks

Participants were instructed to walk while subtraction, including serial subtraction of 3′s and 7’s (i.e., DT3 and DT7 condition, respectively). Participants were instructed to pay equal attention to cognitive and walking tasks. The correct responses and walking speed for each trial were recorded and averaged for further analysis. The DT walking paradigm used here is well validated for probing attentional resource allocation and aspects of executive control such as divided attention and task coordination (Yogev-Seligmann et al. 2008), while it may be less sensitive to subtle executive dysfunction (e.g., working memory manipulation, complex planning) that could manifest in more demanding neuropsychological tasks (Miyake et al. 2000).

fNIRS

fNIRS acquisition

Measures were conducted with a 49-channel portable fNIRS device (Danyang Huichuang Medical Equipment Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) at rest (3 min, eye closes, standing) and during behavioural tasks described above. The wavelengths were 730 and 850 nm, and sampling frequency was set at 11 Hz. The light sources and detectors were placed on bilateral prefrontal and sensorimotor cortices (Supplementary Figure S1).

fNIRS analysis

Selected regions of interest (ROIs) included prefrontal cortex (channels 26, 29–31, 34–36, 44, 45 for left and 9, 10, 15, 19–24 for right), motor cortex (channels 32, 33, 41, 43, 49 for left and 2, 6, 8, 13, 14 for right) and somatosensory cortex (channels 27, 40, 42, 48 for left and 1, 5, 7, 16 for right). All channels with >50% overlap within a region were averaged together based on MRIcro registration (Rorden and Brett 2000; Wan et al. 2018).

Using the modified Beer-Lambert law under the constant-scattering assumption, fluctuations in delta oxygenated haemoglobin (HbO2) concentration were derived from variations in detected light intensity (Sakatani et al. 2006). After obtaining delta HbO2 signals, preprocessing began with a 3s moving average filter to smooth the data (Ding et al. 2021). Segments whose sliding-window standard deviation exceeded a preset threshold were flagged as artefacts and corrected via cubic-spline interpolation (Scholkmann et al. 2010). The signals were then filtered with a band pass filter (0.01–0.2 Hz) to eliminate physiological noise and low-frequency drift (Arun et al. 2020; Ding et al. 2024). Subsequently, each trial underwent baseline correction. For each task, the hemodynamic response function (HRF) was obtained by averaging the time series across all channels within each ROI (Xu et al. 2025). Finally, the mean amplitude of each HRF during the whole 30s task block was computed for statistical analysis (Ou et al. 2024).

Graph theory analysis was performed by GRaph thEoretical Network Analysis (GRETNA) toolbox (Wang et al. 2015). In this framework, a network is defined by nodes linked via edges, which together model the brain’s functional architecture. In the current study, weighted and undirected networks were built based on coherence (Vecchio et al. 2019), which was calculated by the Welch’s averaged, modified periodogram method for each pair of channels (Welch 1967). In the absence of a single optimal threshold, we evaluated connectivity measures across the 0.1–0.4 range (stepped by 0.05) and integrated these values into an area under the curve (AUC) metric to comprehensively describe the brain network topology (Wang et al. 2015; Yan et al. 2017). Global efficiency, a key index of functional integration, is the average of interregional efficiency between every pair of brain region over the entire brain, indexing the brain’s overall information-transfer capacity (G) (Park et al. 2014). Such integration reflects how specialised processing in distributed regions is coordinated into unified network dynamics (Rubinov and Sporns 2010). Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed in Graphpad Prism (version 8.3.0). Linear mixed effects (LME) models were performed to test differential changes in questionnaires, DT performance and fNIRS measures among sessions. For depression, sleep quality and brain fog severity, Session was included as a fixed effect and was set as repeated covariance structure. For DT performance and global efficiency, Session and Condition were included as fixed effects and were both set as repeated covariance structures. For each condition of HbO2, Session and ROI were included as fixed effects and were both set as repeated covariance structures. For all LME models, subject was included as a random effect. Normality of the residuals was visually assessed for each model with conditional residual quantile-quantile plots, and all were found to reasonably conform to the assumption of normality. Post-hoc comparisons were performed when F-tests were significant. Effect sizes (Cohen’s d for two group comparisons, where effect sizes = 0.2 are considered small, 0.5 medium, and ≥0.8 large; or Cohen’s f for comparisons between more than two groups, where 0.1 is considered small, 0.25 medium and ≥0.4 large) (Rosenthal 1994) were also computed for all LME models and multiple comparisons.

All data was found to meet the normality assumption tested by the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Pearson correlations were performed to investigate the relationship between questionnaires, behavioural and fNIRS measures at baseline as well as the changes over sessions.

Of note, there were 15 missing data points in Session 2 and one missing value in Session 4. LME models allow missing values, but the LME model results are valid only if those values are missing for completely random reasons; otherwise, the results are misleading. Therefore, it is needed to make sure the missing follow-up data were due to completely random reasons. We created a new variable named ‘missing pattern’. The value for subjects who completed all 4 sessions (9 subjects) was set as 0, and the value for subjects who missed one session (16 subjects) was set as 1. Pearson correlations were performed between the variable ‘missing pattern’ and other variables (such as depression symptom, brain fog severity, COVID-19 infection severity, DT performance, etc.) to investigate whether the missing pattern was completely random.

Bonferroni corrections were applied for multiple comparisons in current study, and all p values presented are those after correction. For all analyses, the statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 (two sides).

Results

Questionnaires

For depression, the LME model revealed significant main effect of Session (F(1.8,34.0) = 3.38, p = 0.05, f = 0.43). Post hoc revealed that depression level was significantly reduced in Session 4 compared with Session 3 (p = 0.022, d = 0.65) (Figure 1A), suggesting a relief of depression symptoms at 14 weeks post-COVID 19 infection.

Questionnaires and task performance among sessions. (A) Depression level was significantly reduced at 14 weeks compared with 7 weeks post-COIVD-19. (B) Brain fog severity was not significant changed among sessions. (C) Sleep quality was not significantly changed among sessions. (D) Walking speed was not significant changed among sessions. Walking speed was significantly slower during Walking than DT3 and DT7 regardless of session. (E) Correct responses were significantly greater in Subtraction 3 than Subtraction 7, DT7 and DT3, and correct responses were significantly less in Subtraction 7 and DT7 than DT3 regardless of session. (F) Cognitive costs were significantly less than walking costs for both DT3 and DT7 regardless of session.

For brain fog severity, the LME model did not reveal a significant main effect of Session (F(1.0,15.2) = 0.08, p = 0.77, f = 0.07) (Figure 1B), suggesting brain fog severity was not significantly changed over time post-COVID 19 infection.

For sleep quality, the LME model did not reveal a significant main effect of Session (F(1.0,19.1) = 1.57, p = 0.23, f = 0.29) (Figure 1C), suggesting sleep quality remained stable and was unlikely to confound other findings.

Behavioural tasks

For walking speed, the LME model revealed significant main effect of Condition (F(1.7,40.5) = 130.80, p < 0.0001, f = 2.33). Post hoc revealed that walking speed was significantly slower during Walking than DT3 and DT7 (p’s < 0.0001, d = 1.62 and 1.53, respectively) (Figure 1D).

For subtraction performance, the LME model revealed significant main effect of Condition (F(1.6,37.9) = 125.5, p < 0.0001, f = 2.28). Post hoc revealed that correct responses were significantly greater during Subtraction 3 than Subtraction 7, DT7 and DT3 (p’s < 0.001, d = 2.27, 2.06 and 0.46, respectively), and significantly greater during DT3 compared with Subtraction 7 and DT7 (p’s < 0.0001, d = 1.96 and 2.06, respectively) (Figure 1E).

For cognitive and walking costs, the LME model revealed significant main effect of Condition [F(1.3,31.9) = 32.63, p < 0.0001, f = 1.17]. Post hoc revealed that cognitive cost was significantly less than walking cost during both DT3 and DT7 (p’s < 0.0001, d = 0.86 and 0.98, respectively) (Figure 1F).

Brain activation

During Subtraction 7 and DT7, the LME model revealed significant main effects of Session (F(2.6,62.1) = 3.43, p = 0.028, f = 0.38; F(1.9,46.0) = 4.74, p = 0.015, f = 0.45). Post hoc revealed that HbO2 was significantly greater in Session 1 than Sessions 2 to 4 in both Subtraction 7 (p’s < 0.001, d = 0.77, 0.35 and 0.42, respectively) and DT7 conditions (p’s < 0.0001, d = 0.74, 0.45 and 0.45, respectively) (Figure 2A,B), indicating reduced brain activation during these tasks after COVID-19 infection.

Graph theory

The LME model revealed significant main effect of Condition (F(4.1,99.2) = 67.68, p < 0.0001, f = 1.68) and Session (F(1.7,41.8) = 4.01, p = 0.030, f = 0.41). Post hoc revealed that global efficiency was significantly less at rest than all other conditions (including Walking, Subtraction 3, Subtraction 7, DT3 and DT7) (p’s < 0.0001, d = 1.25, 1.70, 1.85, 1.41 and 1.41, respectively). Global efficiency was significantly less during Walking than Subtraction 3 and 7 (p’s < 0.0001, d = 0.55 and 0.64, respectively) and DT3 and DT7 conditions (p’s < 0.001, d = 0.52 and 0.46, respectively). Those results suggest that global efficiency tends to be greater in higher-demanding tasks (Figure 2C). Global efficiency was significantly greater in Session 1 than Session 3 (p = 0.0028, d = 0.29), indicating reduced brain network efficiency after COVID-19 infection (Figure 2D).

Correlations

Brain fog severity in Sessions 2 to 4 was positively correlated with depression score in the respective session (r’s = 0.70, 0.45 and 0.53, p = 0.025, 0.025 and 0.008, respectively). Brain fog severity in Session 2 was also positively correlated with depression score in Session 1 (r = 0.75, p = 0.012) (Supplementary Figure S2). Those results suggest the strong relationship between depression level and brain fog severity regardless of COVID-19 infection.

Changes in HbO2 in left somatosensory and motor cortices from Session 1–3 was negatively correlated with changes in walking cost during DT7 (r’s = 0.64 and 0.53, p’s = 0.005 and 0.007, respectively) (Supplementary Figure S3A,B), suggesting that individuals with more reduction in sensorimotor activation tend to have worse walking performance during DT post-COVID-19.

At baseline, global efficiency during DT3 was positively correlated with walking cost (r = 0.41, p = 0.042). Changes in global efficiency was positively correlated with changes in walking cost during DT7 from Session 1 to Sessions 3 and 4 (r’s = 0.53 and 0.50, p’s = 0.006 and 0.012, respectively) (Supplementary Figure S3C–E), suggesting that individuals with more reduction in brain network efficiency tend to have better walking performance during DT post-COVID-19.

There was no significant correlation between missing pattern and other variables (including depression, brain fog severity, COVID-19 infection severity, DT performance, etc.) (p’s > 0.05), suggesting that the missing follow-up data in the current study are likely to result from completely random reasons, and the results of LME models should be valid.

Discussion

Our study investigated whether brain fog post-COVID-19 results from cognitive deficits or psychological factors such as depression induced by COVID-19 infection. Our main findings are (1) brain fog severity was positively correlated depression level; (2) depression level and DT performance remained unchanged post-COVID-19; (3) brain activation and global efficiency during DT were reduced post-COVID-19.

Consistent with previous literature (Ceban et al. 2022; Schild et al. 2023) suggesting brain fog is one of the most prevalent symptoms post-COVID-19, around 80% (20 out of 25) subjects in current study reported more than one brain fog symptoms post-COVID-19. Therefore, most people may experience subjective cognitive deficits post-COVID-19 to some degree, even the infection was mild.

In the current study, the prevalence of depression was 28% pre- and 30%, 28% and 21% at 3-, 7- and 14-weeks post-COVID-19, respectively. Due to comparable prevalence pre- and post-COVID-19, depression possibly existed pre-COVID-19 and was unlikely induced by COVID-19 infection. Although the prevalence of depression appears to be high, this prevalence is consistent with population‑level data during the COVID‑19 pandemic (25%–33%) (Ettman et al. 2020; Salari et al. 2020; Bueno-Notivol et al. 2021), suggesting the high prevalence of depression is within the range reported globally during the same period and likely result from widespread pandemic‑related stress (such as social isolation, academic disruption, economic uncertainty due to shut-down policy) (Ceban et al. 2022). Additionally, the high prevalence of depression may be also influenced by the high sensitivity of the depression scale used in the current study (CES-D). CES-D is a screening tool designed to detect even mild depressive symptoms in community samples with sensitivity estimates around 0.9 and specificity around 0.7 (Lewinsohn et al. 1997; Vilagut et al. 2016). Compared with clinical diagnostic interviews or stricter clinical scales (e.g., Hamilton Depression Rating Scale), CES‑D identifies mild or transient symptoms and prioritises sensitivity over specificity (Vilagut et al. 2016), which may overestimate prevalence of depression in the current study. By analysing depression severity, we found that the majority of participants scoring above the cut-off of 16 fell within the mild-moderate range (i.e., 16–26) of depression, and only 4%, 10% and 8% of participants fell within the severe range (i.e., ≥27) pre‑, at 3- and 7-weeks post‑COVID‑19 infection, respectively. This pattern indicates that although the prevalence of depression was high, the severity of depression symptoms was generally mild, consistent with population-level findings during the COVID-19 pandemic (Ettman et al. 2020; Salari et al. 2020; Bueno-Notivol et al. 2021). Interestingly, we observed a relief in depression at 14 weeks post-COVID-19, which may relate to the recovered social and economic activities and reduced fear of COVID-19 in the society.

In line with previous study reporting higher depression level in people complaining about cognitive impairment post-COVID-19 (Paolini et al. 2023), we observed that brain fog severity was positively correlated with depression level at all sessions (including pre-COVID-19), suggesting that brain fog symptoms strongly relate to a person’s depression level and are unlikely induced by COVID-19. Importantly, this association does not imply directionality. Depressive symptoms may heighten subjective cognitive complaints (Castaneda et al. 2008), while subjective cognitive difficulties may worsen mood, or both may arise from shared underlying factors such as stress, inflammation, or autonomic dysregulation (Becker et al. 2021; Graham et al. 2021). Future longitudinal work is required to disentangle these possibilities.

We did not observe a change in DT performance post-COVID-19. Some studies reported impaired DT performance in people after severe COVID-19 infection (Giardini et al. 2022; Morelli et al. 2022). The DT task used in this study was relatively simple and may not detect subtle executive dysfunction. The absence of behavioural changes in the current study does not rule out subtle or domain-specific cognitive alterations, particularly those not captured by the chosen task (i.e., DT). More demanding or specific neuropsychological tasks may be required to detect such changes. Therefore, our findings are not strong enough to draw the conclusion that mild COVID-19 infection does not influence higher cortical functions, and further studies with more complex tasks are still needed.

Brain activation was reduced during both Subtraction 7 and DT7 at all sessions post-COVID-19. Our results are similar to a pilot study reporting reduced HbO2 in frontal and temporal regions in people post-COVID-19 than healthy controls (Ho et al. 2021). The mechanisms are possibly related to hypometabolism caused by COVID-19 infection of the central nervous system (Hosp et al. 2021; Kiatkittikul et al. 2022). Positron emission tomography (PET) imaging, particularly [18F] FDG-PET, has been widely used to investigate brain functions by quantifying regional glucose metabolism (Horowitz et al. 2024). Some recent PET studies have reported hypometabolism in frontal, temporal, and limbic regions in post-COVID-19 patients compared with healthy controls (Guedj et al. 2021; Debs et al. 2023; Horowitz et al. 2024). Although fNIRS and PET assesses different physiological signals (hemodynamic responses vs. glucose metabolism), both modalities can reflect altered neurovascular coupling and brain functions (Caliandro et al. 2013; Horowitz et al. 2024). Therefore, reduced brain activation post-COVID-19 observed in the current study may reflect underlying metabolic alterations similar to those detected by PET studies.

Furthermore, we observed that individuals with more reduction in sensorimotor activation tend to have worse walking performance during DT post-COVID-19. The activation of sensorimotor cortex has been associated with gait control under a high-demanding circumstance, such as DT (Suzuki et al. 2004; Harada et al. 2009), which is needed to maintain walking speed and perform cognitive task simultaneously (Lu et al. 2015; Stuart et al. 2019). As a result, the capability of maintaining walking speed during DT may be insufficient in those with large reduction in sensorimotor activation.

Global efficiency was reduced at 7 weeks post-COVID-19, which is similar to a DTI study (Yang et al. 2021) reporting reduced global efficiency in the white matter network in COVID-19 patients compared with healthy controls. As global efficiency reflects information transmission efficiency, these findings suggest the impaired capacity of information transfer in the brain post-COVID-19 from perspectives of both functional (ours) and structural (Yang et al. 2021) connectivity. The mechanisms are possibly related to the disruption of white matter connectivity post-COVID-19 due to neuroinflammation (Hosp et al. 2021; Yang et al. 2021; Douaud et al. 2022; de Paula et al. 2023). As white matter integrity is the basis of normal functional connectivity in human brain (Sbardella et al. 2015; Liu et al. 2022), disruption of white matter possibly causes reduction in global efficiency. As with brain activation, it remains unclear whether these changes are transient, reversible or longer-lasting. Our follow-up period of up to 14 weeks post-COVID-19 infection provides only a limited window on recovery. Longitudinal studies with extended follow-up and repeated imaging are essential to determine the time course and potential reversibility of these neural alterations.

We observed a negative correlation between the change in global efficiency and walking performance during DT post-COVID-19. This pattern could reflect compensatory mechanisms. As DT is a high-demanding task, participants with less cognitive reserve may need to allocate more neural resources to maintain walking speed during DT, but these compensatory processes appear to be insufficient to maintain walking speed in DT (Droby et al. 2022). Together with the dissociation between unchanged DT performance and altered neural measures post-COVID-19 infection, these results might suggest that participants maintain behavioural performance by compensatory mechanisms such as recruiting additional or alternative neural resources (Cabeza et al. 2018). Apart from compensatory mechanisms, alternative explanations (such as limited sensitivity of the DT paradigm or methodological constraints of fNIRS) are also possible. These findings indicate the value of combining behavioural and neuroimaging measures when assessing post‑COVID-19 effects.

To our knowledge, ours is the first study to perform psychological and behavioural measures with simultaneous fNIRS recording before and after COVID-19. The longitudinal prospective design is our major strength. Compared with previous studies only with post-COVID-19 data, our study has larger statistical power and more reliable results by comparing individuals’ pre- and post-COVID-19 data directly.

Our findings also have clinical implications. As persistent neurological symptoms post-COVID-19 have been widely reported in social media and TV news, many people worry about those symptoms, even when the infection was mild. Although our findings are unable to lead to a definite conclusion whether COVID-19 induces cognitive deficits, they at least suggest that mild COVID-19 infection does not induce apparent cognitive deficits. The strong and consistent relationship between brain fog severity and depression level suggest that people should pay more attention on their psychological states, and there is no need to fear brain fog induced by COVID-19. Of note, these findings apply specifically to young adults with mild COVID-19.

The current study has some limitations. The lack of baseline brain fog severity scores is a major limitation of the current study. Baseline assessments (September 15-November 10, 2022) took place under China’s stringent lockdown. At that time, the concept of ‘brain fog’ was not yet widely recognised among lay audiences in China, and all the subjects were healthy young adults without any history of neurological diseases. As a result, we did not include brain fog questionnaire in our initial protocol. When we collected the baseline data, we could not anticipate the abrupt lifting of lockdown measures in early December 2022, nor the rapid, widespread COVID-19 infection wave shortly after the reopening policy acted. Although the lack of pre-COVID-19 brain fog severity scores might weaken our conclusions, our study still benefits from the rare opportunity to compare behavioural, neural, and mood measures within the same subjects before and after COVID-19 infection.

Another limitation is small sample size (N = 25), with missing data at follow-up sessions. We acknowledge that the missing data may mislead the results. To minimise the influence of missing data on the results, we used LME models that allow missing values, and performed correlation analyses between missing pattern and other variables to make sure the missing pattern was completely random. The results suggest that the missing values are likely to result from completely random reasons and unlikely to distort our results. Furthermore, all the cases in current study were young adults with mild COVID-19 infection, so our results should not be extrapolated to older populations, patients with moderate-to-severe COVID-19 infection, or clinically referred samples. Future studies are needed to replicate our study in a larger sample with different severity of COVID-19 infection.

In addition, the behavioural task used in the current study is simple, which may not be sensitive enough to detect subtle cognitive deficits. Future studies are needed to use more complex cognitive tasks to test subtle cognitive deficits before and after COVID-19 infection. Additionally, repeated testing across sessions may introduce familiarity or learning effects that could mask subtle performance changes. Although the task was simple and unlikely to produce large practice effects, we acknowledge that repeated testing remains a potential confound in longitudinal designs.

In conclusion, mild COVID-19 infection in young adults does not lead to apparent cognitive deficits or depression, although brain activation and global efficiency are reduced post-COVID-19. Brain fog is likely to result from depression rather than cognitive deficits induced by COVID-19. Our findings suggest that people should pay more attention to their psychological states rather than worrying too much about brain fog induced by COVID-19 infection during COVID-19 pandemic. Future studies combining more challenging cognitive tasks with longitudinal neuroimaging are needed to clarify the mechanisms underlying brain fog after COVID-19 infection.
