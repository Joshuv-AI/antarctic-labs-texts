Cancer Therapy–Related Cardiac Dysfunction in Patients With Prostate Cancer Undergoing Androgen Deprivation Therapy
Authors: Dong‐Yi Chen, Cheng‐Hung Lee, Ming‐Lung Tsai, Ming‐Jer Hsieh, Cheng‐Keng Chuang, See‐Tong Pang, Shao‐Wei Chen, Chi‐Nan Tseng, Shang‐Hung Chang, Pao‐Hsien Chu, I‐Chang Hsieh, Victor Chien‐Chia Wu, Wen‐Kuan Huang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe risk of cardiac dysfunction for patients with prostate cancer undergoing androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) in the real‐world setting remains unclear.Methods and ResultsA total of 1120 patients with prostate cancer and a baseline echocardiography scan were identified from Chang Gung Research Database between January 1, 2001 and December 31, 2019. Patients were treated with gonadotropin‐releasing hormone agonist therapy, gonadotropin‐releasing hormone antagonist therapy, or bilateral orchiectomy. Changes in left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) were further assessed in 421 patients using repeated measurements of LVEF before and during ADT treatment. The incidence of cancer therapy–related cardiac dysfunction (CT‐RCD) was evaluated and defined as a ≥10% absolute decline in LVEF from baseline to a value of <53%. Among 421 patients undergoing ADT, LVEF declined from 66.3±11.3% to 62.5±13.6% (95% CI of mean difference: −5.0% to −2.7%) after a mean follow‐up period of 1.6±0.8 years. CT‐RCD occurred in 58 patients (13.7%) with a nadir LVEF of 40.3±9.1% after ADT. Lower baseline LVEF was significantly associated with CT‐RCD (odds ratio, 1.07 [95% CI, 1.04–1.10]). The area under the curve of baseline LVEF for discriminating CT‐RCD was 75.6%, with the corresponding optimal cutoff value of 64.5% (sensitivity, 79.3%; specificity, 67.2%).ConclusionsADT with gonadotropin‐releasing hormone agonist therapy, gonadotropin‐releasing hormone antagonist therapy, and bilateral orchiectomy were associated with an increased risk of CT‐RCD in patients with prostate cancer. In addition, lower baseline LVEF was a significant predictor of CT‐RCD in patients with prostate cancer undergoing treatment with ADT.

In recent years, androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) has become the treatment of choice in patients with advanced and metastatic prostate cancer (PCa). ADT, accomplished by gonadotropin‐releasing hormone agonist (GnRHa) therapy, gonadotropin‐releasing hormone (GnRH) antagonist therapy, or bilateral orchiectomy, induces hypogonadism to achieve castration levels of testosterone. Although it improves survival in patients with PCa, ADT has been suggested to be linked to diabetes, metabolic syndrome, and cardiovascular disease, including myocardial infarction and ischemic stroke. 1 , 2 , 3 Although data are currently limited, 1 early study suggested that GnRHa was associated with an increased risk of incident heart failure (HF) and hospitalization attributable to HF. 4 Another study using speckle‐tracking echocardiography reported a significant decrease in left ventricular (LV) longitudinal, circumferential, and radial strain measures after 6 months of ADT treatment in patients with PCa. However, the researchers did not detect any significant differences using conventional echocardiographic measurements. 5

Because cardiovascular complications, such as cardiac function deterioration, have a significant impact on quality of life and survival, there is an urgent need for a detailed analysis of cancer therapy–related cardiac dysfunction (CT‐RCD) in patients with PCa receiving ADT. 6 Therefore, we conducted a retrospective cohort study to estimate the incidence of CT‐RCD and identify high‐risk groups among patients with PCa treated with ADT (namely, GnRHa, GnRH antagonist, and bilateral orchiectomy) in a real‐world setting.

Methods

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request.

Data Source

This is a retrospective, multicenter, cohort study using deidentified patient data from the Chang Gung Research Database (CGRD) of the Chang Gung Memorial Hospital health system, the largest health care provider in Taiwan. 7 , 8 The CGRD includes electronic medical records, including all emergency services, and inpatient and outpatient visits from 7 branches of the Chang Gung Memorial Hospital system (4 tertiary academic medical centers and 3 teaching hospitals). Diseases were registered using the International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD‐9‐CM), before 2016 and the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD‐10‐CM), from 2016 onward (Table S1). The ethics review board of Chang Gung Memorial Hospital approved the study (No. 202000017B0). All patient data were anonymous and deidentified to protect personal privacy, and therefore, informed consent was waived.

Study Cohort Identification

Patients with newly diagnosed PCa (ICD‐9‐CM code 185 and ICD‐10‐CM code C61) were retrieved from the Cancer Registry subdatabase of CGRD between January 1, 2001, and December 31, 2019. We included patients with PCa who received therapy with a GnRHa, a GnRH antagonist, or bilateral orchiectomy and excluded patients without baseline echocardiography scans before ADT treatment. In addition, to investigate the true effects of single GnRH therapy, patients who had been shifted to another GnRH therapy were excluded. The use of GnRHa and GnRH antagonists was identified through prescription records in CGRD. GnRHa drugs included leuprorelin (Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical Classification System [ATC] code L02AE02), goserelin (ATC code L02AE03), buserelin (ATC code L02AE01), and triptorelin (ATC code L02AE04). The GnRH antagonist examined in the present study was degarelix (ATC code L02BX02). Bilateral orchiectomy was identified according to surgical records in CGRD. Within this cohort, we further identified patients who underwent repeated echocardiography before and after ADT treatment (Figure 1). The index date (cohort entry date) was defined as the date of initiating ADT treatment.

Flowchart for the inclusion of the study patients.

Cancer therapy–related cardiac dysfunction (CT‐RCD), defined by a decrease in left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of >10% to <53%, was observed in 58 patients. The remaining 363 patients did not experience CT‐RCD. ADT indicates androgen deprivation therapy.

Covariates

The covariates at baseline were extracted from CGRD, which were demographic characteristics (age, smoking status, and body mass index, and body surface area), vital signs (heart rate and systolic and diastolic blood pressure), ADT type, disease extent, comorbidities, historical events, baseline echocardiography, laboratory data, and concomitant medications. Information on body mass index and vital signs was extracted from the medical records in the previous 3 months. Comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, and 8 others) were defined by the presence of 2 outpatient diagnoses or 1 inpatient diagnosis before the index admission. 9 History of cardiovascular events (previous myocardial infarction, previous coronary intervention, HF hospitalization, and cerebrovascular disease) was defined by 1 inpatient diagnosis before the index admission. Data on the use of medications in the previous 3 months were extracted, including angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors/angiotensin receptor blockers/angiotensin receptor–neprilysin inhibitors, β‐blockers, and 8 others (Table 1). We also extracted antiandrogen medication, including flutamide, bicalutamide, and cyproterone. Laboratory data in the previous 3 months were captured, including serum creatinine level, hemoglobin level, and 18 others.

Cardiovascular Outcomes

Major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs) were defined as a composite outcome of acute myocardial infarction, ischemic stroke, HF hospitalization, and all‐cause mortality. The outcome of acute myocardial infarction or ischemic stroke was extracted from CGRD, defined as an admission with a primary diagnosis of acute myocardial infarction or ischemic stroke. The accuracy of the ICD diagnostic code for acute myocardial infarction or ischemic stroke (Table S1) has been validated in previous Taiwanese studies. 10 , 11 The date, place, and causes of death were detected by linking to the Taiwan Death Registry. The MACEs and individual components were calculated from the index date (the lowest LV ejection fraction [LVEF] date) until the day of developing an outcome, the day of death, or the end of follow‐up (December 31, 2019), whichever occurred first.

Echocardiography Evaluation of Cardiac Dysfunction

Two‐dimensional echocardiographic data were extracted from CGRD in 421 patients who underwent repeated echocardiography before and after ADT initiation. LV end‐diastolic volume, LV end‐systolic volume, and LVEF were determined using the modified Simpson method, which uses the Teicholz formula, and were based on the M‐mode images and measurements. Baseline echocardiography data were recorded within the previous 3 months before ADT initiation. The lowest LVEF between the period of ADT initiation and death or the end of follow‐up (December 31, 2019) was noted. Patients were divided into 2 groups according to the degree of change in LVEF: CT‐RCD and non–CT‐RCD. CT‐RCD was defined as a >10% decline in LVEF from baseline to a value of <53%, according to the American Society of Echocardiography/European Association of Cardiovascular Imaging Guidelines. 12 To study the effect of ADT on LV function change, we did not exclude patients with baseline LVEF of <53%.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics of patients with and without CT‐RCD were compared using the Fisher exact test for categorical variables, independent‐sample t test for continuous variables, and the Mann‐Whitney U test for the apparently skewed continuous variables (ie, prostate‐specific antigen and testosterone). Within‐ and between‐group comparisons of changes in cardiac parameters were made using a generalized estimating equation (GEE) with a normal distribution and identity link function for continuous variables or with a binomial distribution and logit link function for binary variables (ie, moderate‐to‐severe mitral regurgitation). The amount of LVEF reduction from baseline to follow‐up between the CT‐RCD and non–CT‐RCD groups was compared using GEE by adding an interaction term of time and study group. The LVEF change from baseline to follow‐up was also analyzed and stratified by different types of ADT using the GEE model. To investigate the potential risk factors of CT‐RCD, those baseline characteristics that were significantly associated with CT‐RCD in the univariate analysis (Table 1) were further introduced into a multivariable logistic regression model. Body weight was not included in the multivariable model because of its collinearity with body surface area. The ability of baseline LVEF to discriminate subsequent CT‐RCD was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic curve analysis, and the optimal cutoff was determined using the Youden index. The relationship between baseline LVEF and the risk of subsequent CT‐RCD was exported using the restricted cubic spline logistic model with 4 knots. Finally, the risk of subsequent clinical cardiovascular outcomes was compared between the CT‐RCD and non–CT‐RCD groups using the univariate Cox proportional hazards model.

To further confirm the effect of ADT on cardiac dysfunction, we included a control group of patients with PCa who did not receive ADT treatment during follow‐up and had repeated echocardiography between January 1, 2001 and December 31, 2019. The index date of the non‐ADT cohort was defined as the date of baseline echocardiography examination. Covariates (the known cardiac risk factors) at baseline were extracted, including smoking, diabetes, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, coronary artery disease, chronic kidney disease, HF hospitalization, and baseline LVEF. We further compared the risk of cardiac dysfunction between the ADT and non‐ADT groups using a logistic regression model with multivariable adjustments for age, covariates at baseline, and year of index date. The LVEF change from baseline to follow‐up between the ADT and non‐ADT groups was compared using the GEE model as well.

To assess the impact of changes in cardiovascular therapy on risk factor management at different time periods, we divided the ADT population into 2 cohorts: one from the decade spanning 2001 to 2010 and the other from 2011 to 2019, based on the index date. We compared the incidence of CT‐RCD between the 2 cohorts using the χ2 analysis. The restricted cubic spline modeling was conducted using R, version 4.0.2 (R Project for Statistical Computing), and the package rms, version 5.1 to 3.1. Other statistical analyses were performed using SAS, version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC). All statistical tests were 2‐sided, and P<0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Cohort Characteristics

We identified 1120 patients diagnosed with PCa who had initiated ADT treatment with GnRHa, GnRH antagonist, or bilateral orchiectomy and completed baseline echocardiography between January 1, 2001 and December 31, 2019 (Figure 1). Ultimately, 421 patients with repeated 2‐dimensional examinations before and after ADT initiation were analyzed in the study. The mean age of the patients at baseline was 76.6 years (SD, 7.5 years). Of those patients, 342 (81.2%) received GnRHa therapy, 29 (6.9%) received GnRH antagonists, and 50 (11.9%) underwent bilateral orchiectomy. The patient characteristics are presented in Table 1. Of the 421 patients, 58 (13.8%) developed CT‐RCD and 363 (86.2%) met the non–CT‐RCD criteria. Patients who developed CT‐RCD, compared with non–CT‐RCD, were more likely to have lower body weight (64.2 versus 68.4 kg; P=0.03), lower body surface area (1.70 versus 1.75 m2; P=0.021), more prior HF hospitalizations (12 patients [20.7%] versus 36 patients [9.9%]; P=0.017), and lower baseline LVEF (57.8% versus 67.7%; P<0.001), or were currently receiving angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitor/angiotensin receptor blocker/angiotensin receptor–neprilysin inhibitor therapy (34 patients [58.6%] versus 152 patients [41.9%]; P=0.017). The pattern of ADT was not significantly different between the CT‐RCD and non–CT‐RCD groups (Table 1).

Cardiac Function Change During ADT Treatment

The mean duration from ADT initiation to the follow‐up echocardiography scan was 1.6 years (SD, 0.8 years), with an interquartile range of 0.9 to 2.3 years, and the overall follow‐up period was 3.8 years (SD, 2.6 years; maximum, 15.3 years) (data not shown). Overall, LVEF significantly decreased from 66.3±11.3% at baseline to 62.5±13.6% (P<0.001) after ADT treatment (Table S2 and Figure S1). Although the LVEF significantly decreased in both study groups (P<0.001 in both groups), there was a significantly greater reduction in the LVEF in the CT‐RCD group (57.9% to 37.3%) than in the non–CT‐RCD group (67.7% to 66.5%; P for interaction in GEE model=0.001) (Figure 2). Patients in the CT‐RCD group had a significant increase in LV end‐diastolic diameter (52.4 to 56.0 mm; P<0.001), LV end‐systolic diameter (35.7 to 44.3 mm; P<0.001), LV end‐diastolic volume (135.3 to 157.9 mL; P<0.001), and LV end‐systolic volume (57.6 to 94.8 mL; P<0.001) (Table 2). For the 363 patients in the non–CT‐RCD group, there was no significant change in LV chamber size (LV end‐diastolic diameter, LV end‐systolic diameter, LV end‐diastolic volume, or LV end‐systolic volume). There were no significant changes in interventricular septum thickness or posterior wall thickness during ADT treatment in either the ADT‐RCD or the non–ADT‐RCD group (Table 2).

Risk Factors for CT‐RCD

Demographics and common comorbidities relative to ADT‐induced LVEF reduction were evaluated as predictors of CT‐RCD in patients with PCa receiving ADT (n=421), including body surface area, previous HF hospitalization, baseline LVEF, and use of angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitor/angiotensin receptor blocker/angiotensin receptor–neprilysin inhibitor at baseline. Multivariable logistic regression analysis revealed that baseline LVEF (per 1% decrease) was independently associated with an elevated risk of CT‐RCD among patients with PCa receiving ADT (odds ratio [OR], 1.07 [95% CI, 1.04–1.10]) (Table 3). The ability of baseline LVEF to discriminate CT‐RCD was modest, with the area under the curve of 75.6% (95% CI, 68.6%–82.7%), with an optimal cutoff point of LVEF of 64.5%, according to the Youden index (Figure 3A). The sensitivity and specificity were 79.3% (95% CI, 66.6%–88.8%) and 67.2% (95% CI, 62.1%–72.0%), respectively. There was an increased risk of cardiac dysfunction with a baseline LVEF below the threshold of 64.5% (Figure 3B).

LVEF Changes During Different Types of ADT

The LVEF change during different types of ADT is presented in Table S3. Patients receiving GnRHa experienced significantly decreased LVEF, from 66.5±11.2% to 62.2±13.4% (P<0.001). However, patients receiving GnRH antagonist or bilateral orchiectomy did not experience a significant LVEF change during ADT treatment. The patients who developed CT‐RCD had a higher incidence of MACEs than patients who did not have CT‐RCD (38.2 versus 15.6 per 100 person‐years; P<0.001). In addition, there was a higher incidence of HF hospitalization (17.8 versus 1.9 per 100 person‐years; P<0.001) and all‐cause mortality (18.9 versus 12.8 per 100 person‐years; P=0.022) in the CT‐RCD group, compared with the non–CT‐RCT group (Table S4).

Sensitivity Analysis of Non‐ADT Cohort

We identified 668 patients diagnosed with PCa who did not receive ADT treatment and underwent repeated echocardiography during the study period. The baseline characteristics of age and important cardiac risk factors are presented in Table S5. The mean duration from baseline to follow‐up echocardiography was 5.2 years (SD, 3.6 years) in the non‐ADT cohort. Overall, cardiac dysfunction occurred significantly higher in the ADT group (n=58/421 [13.8%]) compared with the control group of non‐ADT patients (n=41/668 [6.1%]) (adjusted OR, 2.41 [95% CI, 1.52–3.82]) (Table S6). In the subsequent analysis of cardiac function change, there was a significant decrease in the LVEF observed in both the ADT and non‐ADT groups. However, the reduction in LVEF was significantly greater in the ADT group than in the non‐ADT group (−3.8% versus −1.6%; P for interaction=0.003) (Table S7 and Figure S2).

Additional Analysis

Of the 421 patients, 96 started ADT during the decade from 2001 to 2010, and 325 started ADT in the time period of 2011 to 2019. There was a numerically lower incidence of CT‐RCD in the time period of 2011 to 2019 (n=42/325 [12.92%]) compared with time period of 2001 to 2010 (n=16/96 [16.67%]), although this was not statistically significant (Figure S3).

Discussion

This multicenter, population‐based cohort study is the first to describe the clinical characteristics of ADT‐induced LVEF reduction in a real‐world setting. Overall, ADT in patients with PCa tended to cause CT‐RCD at a frequency of ≈13.8% within a mean of 1.56 years of treatment. We observed a reduction of LVEF from 57.9% to 37.3% in patients who developed CT‐RCD. We identified LVEF to be strongly associated with CT‐RCD in patients with PCa receiving ADT. When baseline LVEF was <64.5%, there was an increased risk of developing CT‐RCD. Furthermore, compared with those without CT‐RCD, once patients developed ADT‐related cardiac dysfunction, there was an increased risk of MACEs in the following period. Our results demonstrated that reduction in LVEF is a noteworthy cardiotoxicity event associated with ADT in patients with PCa.

Evidence suggests that ADT in PCa is positively associated with cardiovascular events, including ischemic stroke, myocardial infarction, coronary artery disease, and cardiovascular death. 13 , 14 , 15 However, reports of adverse outcomes of cardiac dysfunction associated with ADT in patients with PCa are scarce. A nested case‐control study using data from the UK General Practice Research Database suggested that combination therapy with GnRHa and antiandrogens was associated with a significant increase in the risk of incident HF (OR, 3.19 [95% CI, 1.10–9.27]) and hospitalized HF (OR, 3.39 [95% CI, 1.07–10.70]) compared with not using these drugs. 4 Another study using data from the National Prostate Cancer Register of Sweden included 76 600 patients with PCa and reported that GnRHa use was associated with a higher risk of HF (adjusted standardized incidence ratio, 1.46 [95% CI, 1.28–1.67]) compared with matched men without cancer. 16 Kanar et al used speckle‐tracking echocardiography to study the change of LV function in a small population of 49 patients with PCa receiving ADT within 6 months. 5 Although they did not detect any significant differences in conventional echocardiographic measures, they reported significant decreases in LV longitudinal, circumferential, and radial strain measures after the sixth month of ADT treatment. Our analysis provides more detailed changes of echocardiographic parameters, including LVEF, during ADT treatment compared with the previous study. Our study had a longer follow‐up time, with a mean follow‐up time of 3.8±2.6 years and a maximal follow‐up time of 15.3 years. We demonstrated a decline in LVEF from 57.9% to 37.3% in the CT‐RCD group within a mean follow‐up time of 1.56±0.84 years. Furthermore, a significantly greater reduction in the LVEF was observed in the ADT group compared with the non‐ADT group (−3.8% versus −1.6%; P for interaction=0.003). Therefore, our study demonstrated that men with PCa who received ADT have an increased risk of developing cardiac dysfunction.

Although the underlying mechanisms of the effects of ADT on cardiac dysfunction remain unclear, some evidence relating to ADT's effect on myocardial structure and function has been proposed. First, early animal studies document a reduction in contractility of isolated cardiac myocytes and contractile performance, including cardiac output and ejection fraction, after gonadectomy of male rats. 17 , 18 Another study reported that 10 weeks of testosterone deprivation, with gonadectomy, in adult mice attenuates contractile function, with a decrease in ejection fraction, as demonstrated by M‐mode echocardiography. 19 Second, low testosterone levels are common in men with chronic HF, and a deficiency of testosterone has been suggested as an independent marker for increased mortality and HF rehospitalization. 20 , 21 On the other hand, in 1 study, testosterone supplementation therapy improved exercise capacity in patients with congestive HF. 22 Testosterone influences cardiac cells by playing an essential role in intracellular Ca2+ homeostasis. 23 Through the androgen receptor, testosterone activates L‐type calcium channels, which lead to an increase in the intracellular levels of calcium and, thus, regulate myocardial contractility. 24 Suppression of testosterone to castration levels with ADT may have an adverse effect on the cardioprotective effect of testosterone. Finally, ADT is associated with cardiovascular events, including myocardial infarction, ischemic heart disease, and arterial stiffness, which may predispose patients to a risk of HF. 25 Taken together, the above evidence suggests that ADT might induce cardiac dysfunction.

Our study included 3 types of ADT, including a GnRHa, a GnRH antagonist, or bilateral orchiectomy. On further analysis, we did detect a significant reduction in LVEF of 4.3% (from 66.5±11.2% to 62.2±13.4%; P<0.001) in patients receiving GnRHa after a mean follow‐up period of 1.4±0.8 years. On the other hand, we did not detect significant LV reductions in the GnRH antagonist (LVEF reduction, 1.02%; P=0.58) or bilateral orchiectomy (LVEF reduction, 1.92%; P=0.22) groups. There is still much debate about the differences in cardiovascular risk between the use of GnRHa and GnRH antagonists. In 1 population‐based cohort study including 1998 patients with PCa, we demonstrated that GnRHa therapy was associated with significantly higher cardiovascular death (hazard ratio, 4.76 [95% CI, 1.42–16.2]) compared with GnRH antagonist therapy after a mean follow‐up period of only 1.21 years. Another cardiovascular proteomics study using serum samples from a phase 2 randomized clinical trial among men with PCa identified 2 proteins related to reducing oxidative stress: hydroxy acid oxidase 1 and superoxide dismutase 2. These 2 proteins were significantly lower in subjects receiving GnRHa compared with those receiving GnRH antagonists. Growing evidence supports the concept that increased oxidative stress may partly result in myocardial systolic dysfunction. 26 Furthermore, GnRHa may affect cardiac contractility via direct action on cardiomyocytes. 27 GnRH receptor expression has been reported in the heart. 28 GnRHa can regulate protein kinase A signaling in cardiac cells via GnRH receptors and alter the intracellular calcium. This could lead to mitochondrial influx and could induce cardiomyocyte apoptosis, eventually impairing cardiac contractility. 29 On the other hand, it remains unclear whether the GnRH antagonists have a similar mechanism of GnRHa‐induced cardiac dysfunction. Therefore, the differing presentations of LVEF changes with the use of a GnRHa, a GnRH antagonist, or orchiectomy in our study could be hypothesis generating. Further prospective studies are warranted to confirm these results and elucidate the mechanisms.

Study Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, although this is a multicenter study, there was no standardized protocol for echocardiographic monitoring and follow‐up in patients with PCa under ADT, potentially resulting in additional detection bias. Second, speckle‐tracking echocardiography was not routinely performed in the Chang Gung Memorial Hospital system; therefore, we did not have information about LV strain measures. Third, although we included 3 types of ADTs, including GnRHa, GnRH antagonist, and bilateral orchiectomy, most patients (81%) received a GnRH antagonist. A small proportion of the patients receiving a GnRH antagonist or bilateral orchiectomy may limit the power to detect statistically significant reductions in LVEF. Finally, the timing of the follow‐up echocardiogram varied because of the lack of an established, standardized protocol for cardiovascular monitoring and follow‐up in patients with PCa undergoing ADT.

Conclusions

This multicenter cohort study showed that patients with PCa treatment with ADT exhibit a risk for a reduction in LVEF. CT‐RCD (namely, ADT‐induced LVEF reduction) is more likely to be a significant risk in patients with LVEF of <64.5%. In addition, patients who developed CT‐RCD will experience a higher risk of MACEs, which in this study included myocardial infarction, ischemic stroke, HF hospitalization, and all‐cause mortality. These findings may guide clinicians and support the evaluation of LV function when considering ADT for patients with PCa in a real‐world clinical practice setting.
