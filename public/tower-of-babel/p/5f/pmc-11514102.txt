Superior locking plate with braided PDS coracoclavicular fixation for the unstable distal clavicle fracture: a technical trick and case series
Authors: Rachel L. Honig, Ankur Khanna, Sherrea Jones, Katherine E. Mallett, Jonathan D. Barlow
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The ideal method of operative fixation for unstable distal clavicle fractures remains controversial, with particular emphasis on the need to restore the normal coracoclavicular distance. Our preferred method of fixation includes open reduction and internal fixation with a distal clavicle locking plate in combination with a polydioxanone suture wrapped around the plate and coracoid to restore the coracoclavicular distance and offload the bony repair. The purpose of this study is to report clinical, radiographic, and functional outcomes in patients with unstable distal clavicle fractures treated with this method of fixation.

Distal clavicle fractures account for approximately 30% of all clavicle fractures with subtle variations in injury patterns carrying clinical significance. Unstable distal clavicle fractures are particularly challenging, due to a high rate of delayed union or nonunion.1,13,16, 17, 18, 19 Nonoperative treatment was traditionally preferred, but studies have shown nonunion rates as high as 33% when these fractures are treated nonoperatively.17 Additionally, mounting data have supported improved outcomes with surgically treated unstable distal clavicle fractures. However, the ideal surgical repair construct remains controversial in the literature.1 Thus, further investigation about the preferred surgical construct is warranted.

Thorough anatomic understanding of the distal clavicle is needed to create a biomechanically sound reconstruction. The 2 opposing forces on the distal clavicle are the proximal muscular attachments and the weight of the upper extremity. The acromioclavicular, coracoclavicular (CC) and coracoacromial ligaments provide static stability to the lateral clavicle. Specifically, the CC ligaments are critical to suspend the shoulder from the clavicle. Unstable distal clavicle fractures, as classified by Neer, are defined as distal third fractures located medial to the CC ligaments (type II fracture) or comminuted fractures with a small inferior fragment attached to the CC ligaments (type V fractures).14,15 Furthermore, the significance of the CC ligaments is highlighted by the Arbeitsgemeinschaft für Osteosynthesefragen classification which centers on whether these ligaments are functional. Bishop et al found that fracture stability (functional integrity of the CC ligament complex) had the highest interobserver agreement, and was the key determinant of treatment, rather than Neer classification.2 Thus, surgical management has focused on techniques to promote clavicle union, while maintaining reduction of the CC interval.

Treating these fractures using various fixation approaches including transosseous sutures, tension band constructs, hook plates, superior plates, and CC joint reconstruction has been widely described and debated.6, 7, 8, 9,11,20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25, 26

Here, we describe our technique and outcomes for open reduction and fixation (ORIF) of unstable distal clavicle fractures using a surgical approach that utilizes a superior distal clavicle locking plate with adjuvant braided polydioxanone sutures (PDS). Though variations of this technique are performed worldwide, the authors have not, to date, identified an English-language publication with an associated case series detailing this specific technique. Therefore, the purpose of this series is to describe clinical outcomes, specifically complications and reoperations, treating 14 patients with this technique. Secondary outcome measures include radiographic analysis and functional outcomes.

Methods

A retrospective review was conducted of patients with unstable distal third clavicle fractures who underwent surgical treatment with a distal clavicle locking plate and an adjuvant braided PDS suture around the coracoid between 2018 and 2022. Patients were required to be greater than 18 years old. Revision surgeries and concomitant upper extremity injuries were excluded. There was a minimum follow-up time of 3 months for radiographs, range-of-motion (ROM) testing, and patient-reported outcome scores.

Tobacco use, diabetes, and American Society of Anesthesiologists scores were used to capture preoperative comorbidities (Table I). Patient records were reviewed for details related to their injury. This included mechanism of injury, laterality, chronicity, and operative indication (Table II). Operative notes were reviewed for surgical time and intraoperative complications. Radiographs were individually reviewed by the authors. Injuries were classified as either Neer type II or type V fractures.14,15 The CC distance was defined as the distance from the inferior cortex of the clavicle to the superior cortex of the coracoid and measured for each patient on injury radiographs, first postoperative radiographs, and final radiographs. Contralateral CC distance was measured when available for comparison purposes. Maintained reduction was defined as ≤3 mm change in the CC distance from postoperative radiographs to final radiographs. Union was defined by the disappearance of the cortical interruption and/or the presence of bridging bone at the fracture site due to callus formation.

Additionally, records were reviewed for postoperative complications, reoperations, ROM/strength assessment, and patient reported outcome measures. Forward elevation, external rotation, internal rotation, and strength testing were recorded. We chose the American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons (ASES) Standardized Shoulder Assessment Form for patients reported outcomes measures due to its verified validity, reliability, and responsiveness.12

Descriptive statistics were assessed using R (version 4.3.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Technique description

This technique is used for unstable fractures of the distal clavicle, in which fixation of the distal segment are tenuous. Typically, this involves fractures within 3 cm of the CC ligaments or those involving the CC ligaments (through bone or ligaments).

This operation is performed in the beach chair position with the entire operative extremity prepped and draped. An anterior incision is then made from the mid clavicle medially to the posterior aspect of the acromioclavicular joint laterally. Fascial flaps are created to expose the underlying fracture. While respecting the soft tissues, the fracture is prepared without excess stripping of fracture fragments. A portion of the anterior deltoid is taken down to identify the coracoid. A small window of soft tissue is dissected off the coracoid both medially and laterally to allow the braided PDS suture to be passed around the coracoid. A small pediatric vascular clamp can be used to facilitate this passage. Typically, a high tensile suture is passed as a shuttling suture before fracture reduction, and this is exchanged after plate fixation.

The fracture is then reduced using a combination of standard reduction tools and Kirschner wires to maintain the reduction once it is obtained. A superior distal clavicle locking plate is placed over the fracture. Two bicortical locking screws are first placed in the distal fragment followed by a bicortical nonlocking screw medially. Fluoroscopic imaging is then used to verify the reduction and additional locking and nonlocking screws are added as needed. Six strands of No. 2 PDS suture are braided on the back table. After the plate is in place, this braided PDS suture is then passed around the coracoid, the clavicle, and the plate. The CC interval is reduced (typically with a ball-spiked pusher on the dorsal surface of the clavicle plate and upward force on the elbow) and the braided PDS is tied (Fig. 1). The knot can be large, and should be placed anteriorly, between the coracoid and the clavicle. Final fluoroscopic images are then obtained. Postoperative protocol

All patients are non-weightbearing in a shoulder immobilizer for 6 weeks. Supine passive external rotation and forward elevation are added at 2 weeks. At 6 weeks, ROM is allowed as tolerated, with a 1-pound limit in the surgical arm until 3 months. At 3 months, a progressive return to normal activities is allowed.

Results

Clinical outcomes

From 2018-2022, 14 patients met the inclusion criteria with an average age of 41 ± 14 years, an overall male predominance (n=13; 93%), and an average body mass index of 26 ± 4 kg/m2. All injuries were closed with no skin compromise. The indication for operative management was acute fracture in 12 patients (86%) and pending nonunion in the remaining 2 patients (14%). Both patients with nonunion had initially been treated nonoperatively. The average time from injury to surgery was 24 ± 38 days. There were no intraoperative complications.

Postoperative complications and reoperations

Patient complications and reoperations are detailed in Table III. One patient had a mountain bike crash, with bending and refracture of his plate (Fig. 2). This was treated nonoperatively, with union and an acceptable outcome (ASES 97). One patient developed a possible nonunion without hardware failure (Fig. 3). However, their function has remained acceptable (ASES 83), and they have not pursued revision surgery. Two patients reported hardware prominence. One patient elected to continue monitoring, and their symptoms resolved. The other patient opted for elective hardware removal 1 year after ORIF. One patient had a superficial wound infection with drainage. This was treated with open irrigation and débridement, and his fracture went on to uneventful union. These were the only reoperations in the cohort (14%). The mean change in CC distance from injury films to immediate postoperative films was 11.3 ± 7.1 mm, the mean change in CC distance from injury films to final films was 9.7 ± 7.6 mm, and the mean change in CC distance from postoperative films to final films was 1.6 ± 0.8 mm. There was a 100% rate of maintenance of reduction (defined as ≤3-mm change in CC distance from immediate postoperative films to final follow-up). Furthermore, the difference between the CC distance on the contralateral side and CC distance on final follow films on the injury side was −0.1 ± 5.5 mm.

Functional outcomes

Functional outcomes were assessed by both ROM and ASES scores. ROM data were available for 12 out of the 14 included patients at an average of 242 ± 260 days after surgery. The average forward elevation was 167 ± 25º and the average external rotation was 69 ± 21º. All patients could achieve internal rotation of L5 or greater. All patients had full strength in all planes. ASES scores were collected at a minimum 1 year after surgery and were available for 10 out of the 14 included patients (71%). The average ASES score was 94 ± 6 points.

Discussion

While relatively rare injuries, distal clavicle fractures remain challenging with a high rate of nonunion without surgical fixation.1,13,16, 17, 18, 19 There are numerous described methods of fixation but no consensus. Fixation is challenging due to the lack of bone availability in the lateral fragment, the paucity of soft tissue overlying the distal clavicle, and the need to not only reduce the fracture but to additionally restore the native ligamentous relationships.1 The CC ligaments provide vertical stability to the distal clavicle, and our preferred method of fixation uses a braided PDS suture around the coracoid and distal clavicle locking plate to reduce the CC distance. Advantages of this technique include the strong reinforcement of the bony clavicle repair (by using the coracoid), low cost, and the temporary nature of fixation, as the PDS sutures absorb over time. To our knowledge, this is the only description of this method of fixation and the purpose of this paper is to report our clinical, radiographic, and functional outcomes.

Clinical outcomes were excellent with 2 cases of hardware prominence and 1 ultimately undergoing hardware removal. Levy et al and Soliman et al described using sutures alone to fix distal clavicle fractures to minimize the need for hardware removal.10,22 However, they only included type II fractures, and this is a difficult technique to employ for comminuted fractures. Recently, Brereton et al reported excellent outcomes in a series of distal clavicle fractures treated with a promising transosseous suture technique that included several comminuted fractures.3 However, they did not perform any CC fixation. Our preference is to restore the normal ligamentous relationship of the disrupted CC ligaments and achieve absolute stability with a locking plate, knowing that hardware removal will potentially be needed in the future.

One patient developed fibrous union but did not undergo revision ORIF. All other patients had radiographic union, and all patients had maintained reduction of the CC distance. All patients had <2 mm (average 1.4 mm) of change from immediate postoperative radiographs to final radiographs indicating that the PDS suture is somewhat dynamic but effectively maintains the relationship long enough for ligamentous healing. There were no cases of coracoid fracture. This technique is advantageous compared to previously described screw fixation which requires removal of the CC screw.4,7 A similar method of fixation described by Wang et al uses a distal clavicle locking plate with a titanium cable around the coracoid rather than an absorbable suture and they had an 82% rate of elective hardware removal compared to our 7%.25

Functional outcomes were reassuring with excellent strength and ROM at the final follow-up. ASES scores were comparable to other studies and improved over time. Final functional outcomes were excellent for patients treated with delayed surgery with this technique.

The importance of the CC ligaments is emphasized in this study, but it is difficult to decipher the contribution of the restoration of this ligamentous relationship on clinical outcomes compared to ORIF alone. Prior studies have disputed the need for CC augmentation in addition to plate fixation alone and argue that even if the CC distance remains high, clinical outcomes are not affected.21 Furuhata et al reported a series of 60 patients treated with plate fixation alone.5 They reported that CC distance improved with plate fixation alone, but a certain degree of separation remained even with bony union. However, functional outcomes were not assessed, and all patients underwent elective hardware removal so clinical significance remains difficult to ascertain.

There are several limitations of this study. Due to the rarity of the injury pattern, the cohort is small. Final ROM and ASES scores were not available for all patients, and preoperative functional outcomes were not available for comparison. This study is limited to a single surgeon’s experience, and there is no control group for comparison purposes. Furthermore, follow-up is limited and further work in the form of randomized trails should be conducted to compare operative vs. nonoperative management and to compare specific methods of fixation. However, this is a technique that has not been previously described and holds promise based on our results.

Conclusion

Fixation of unstable distal clavicle fractures with a superior lateral locking plate and PDS suture augmentation of the CC ligaments is a safe and effective treatment of these injuries. The locking plate provides absolute stability and the PDS around the coracoid provides vertical stability to the distal clavicle normally provided by the CC ligaments. The PDS is preferred because it effectively restores the normal ligamentous vector, remains dynamic, and absorbs overtime which reduces the need for hardware removal.
