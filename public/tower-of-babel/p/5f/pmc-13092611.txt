Direct integration of deep learning–based GTV auto-segmentation into a clinical radiotherapy planning system
Authors: Bao Ngoc Huynh, Cecilia Marie Futsaether, Oliver Tomic, Kristian Hovde Liland, Manish Kakar, Taran Paulsen Hellebust, Eirik Malinen, Einar Dale, Karsten Rydén-Eilertsen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstract

Introduction

Accurate delineation of the Gross Tumor Volume (GTV) is crucial for radiotherapy planning [1], [2]. However, manual contouring is time-consuming and subject to both inter- and intra-observer variability [3], [4]. These variations can lead to inconsistencies in treatment planning and delivery.

Artificial intelligence (AI)-based automatic segmentation models, including those implemented using Deep Learning (DL), have been demonstrated to generate faster and more consistent GTV contours [5], [6], [7]. Although many models have shown strong performance in research settings, few have been successfully integrated into clinical workflows, where usability, reliability, and long-term maintainability are essential for safe, efficient, and sustainable clinical practice [8], [9]. This gap is evident in current commercial Treatment Planning Systems (TPS), which already offer DL‑based auto‑segmentation for organs at risk (OAR) but still lack such tools for GTV delineation.

Most state-of-the-art TPS offer a flexible scripting environment and user interface (UI) that support customization and integration of external scripting libraries [10]. This capability provides an opportunity to integrate custom AI models directly into clinical workflows, enabling real-time interaction and adaptation within the treatment planning process.

In this study, we aimed to integrate in-house developed DL models for GTV segmentation into a commercial TPS, using its scripting environment. Because an existing GTV auto‑segmentation DL model is a prerequisite for this integration, our in-house DL model for Head‑and‑Neck Cancer (HNC) was used as an example. A key success criterion was that the integration should allow physicians to interact with DL-generated contours directly within the treatment planning UI, with the ability to modify DL-based contours and employ them in treatment planning. Another criterion was that the integration should support models requiring either single‑ or multi‑modality inputs, allowing for flexible use across different imaging protocols such as Computed Tomography (CT) and Positron Emission Tomography (PET). The final criterion was that model deployment within the TPS should preserve the model’s expected performance, meaning performance differences should fall within the typical range when testing the model on new clinical data. This integration represents a step toward more efficient, consistent, and accessible radiotherapy planning, transforming AI research into a practical clinical solution.

Materials and methods

Model and data preparation

The integration required (1) a TPS with a scripting environment, (2) a trained DL model, and (3) clinical data to verify that the model behaved as expected within the radiotherapy workflow. The latter included a set of manually created GTV contours that were used as reference. The integration workflow was generalizable to any DL‑based segmentation model, assuming the required libraries and input modalities were available in the scripting environment of the TPS.

All scripting, automatic segmentation, and contour evaluation were performed in RayStation 2024A SP11 (RaySearch Laboratories, Stockholm, Sweden). As a demonstration, we used an in‑house developed DL model for Head‑and‑Neck Cancer (HNC) GTV auto‑segmentation.

The DL model was based on the U-Net architecture [11], and generated the primary tumor (GTVp) and affected lymph node (GTVn) segmentations from co-registered 18F-Fluorodeoxyglucose PET (18F-FDG-PET) and CT images, fulfilling the multimodality requirement of the integration. It was trained and fine-tuned using data from HNC patients from Oslo University Hospital (OUS: n = 177, treated in 2007–2013) and Maastricht University Medical Center (MAASTRO: n = 100, treated in 2008–2014); and was validated on an independent set (OUS: n = 19, MAASTRO: n = 13). Details on image preprocessing, model architecture, training procedures, uncertainty estimation, post-processing, and software are provided in Supplementary Material A. All patients had been included in previous studies [12], [13], [14], [15].

The integrated GTV segmentation model was tested directly within the RayStation TPS using ten patients enrolled in the RADPAINT-2 clinical trial, treated in 2021–2022 (NCT04910308) [16].

The studies involving human participants were reviewed and approved by the Regional Ethics Committee (REK) for Medical and the Health Research Ethics and Institutional Review Board (IRB). For the study involving patients from the OUS dataset, exemption from study-specific informed consent was granted by REK. For the RADPAINT-2 study, written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to inclusion.

Integration workflow

The RayStation TPS provided a Python-based scripting environment that supported external packages and programmatic access to RayStation’s internal functions, including multimodal image series retrieval, co-registration, and resampling, as well as contour creation and modification.

A custom script was developed and configured to load the DL model (stored either locally or as a Python resource) and included a simple UI allowing physicians to select input images and set contour parameters (e.g., color, name). Using these configurations, the script executed the full auto‑segmentation pipeline: image preprocessing, head and neck region localization, model prediction, and post‑processing. GTVp/GTVn contours were created directly within RayStation, readily available for the physicians to review and adjust. To support clinical decision-making and contour verification, test‑time augmentation (TTA) [17] was used to identify high‑uncertainty regions (with uncertainty value above 20% of the maximum uncertainty value based on the predictive entropy [18] calculated after TTA, see Supplementary Material A). The entire workflow ran inside RayStation TPS without any external software.

Evaluation of the DL model integration

To evaluate the performance of the integrated model, DL-generated segmentation predictions were compared to the manual contours using the following metrics:i.Dice Similarity Coefficient (DSC) [19], [20], [21]: Measures the spatial overlap between predicted and manual contours.ii.Surface DSC (3 mm tolerance) [21]: Measures boundary agreement as the proportion of surface points within 3 mm between predicted and manual contours. The 3 mm tolerance corresponds roughly to the PET slice thickness (∼3 mm) in our dataset.iii.95th Percentile Hausdorff Distance (HD95) [21]: Evaluates boundary deviation using the 95th‑percentile distance between manual and predicted contour surfaces.

Technical details

Model training was performed using the Orion High-Performance Computing Cluster at the Norwegian University of Life Sciences using an NVIDIA RTX Quadro 8000 (48 GB) GPU. The RayStation system ran on Windows machines, with NVIDIA L40S (48 GB) GPUs. The DL model in this study was implemented in TensorFlow.2

Results

When executing the custom script for automatic segmentation of HNC GTV (Fig. 1), a dialog box first appeared and prompted the physician to select the appropriate CT and PET images, as well as to configure the output contour settings (e.g., contour name and line color). Once the required information was confirmed, the dialog box closed, and the RayStation progress bar was displayed to indicate the ongoing auto-segmentation process. Upon completion, the DL segmentations for GTVp and GTVn were automatically added to the patient database, together with the high-uncertainty regions. These outputs were immediately available for review and manual adjustment within RayStation (Fig. 1, last step). A demonstration of this workflow is available in the Supplementary video. On average, the entire auto-segmentation process, including manual configuration time, took approximately three minutes before the DL-generated contours and the uncertainty areas were available for review within RayStation.

The integrated DL model showed similar segmentation performance on the RADPAINT‑2 test data as on the validation data (Table 1), confirming that TPS deployment preserved the model’s expected behavior. For GTVp segmentation, high DSCs and Surface DSCs and low HD95 values indicated strong spatial overlap and boundary accuracy on both datasets. For GTVn segmentation, performance on RADPAINT-2 patients was more varied, similar to validation performance. Lower GTVn segmentation performance occurred when the model missed one or more lymph node structures (false negative, FN) or predicted additional structures not present in the manual contours (false positive, FP), resulting in high HD95 values despite the moderate DSC and Surface DSC. Discussion

This study demonstrated a successful integration of an in‑house multimodal DL‑based GTV auto‑segmentation model into a commercial TPS. The integrated workflow ran seamlessly within the TPS and allowed direct review and editing of DL‑generated contours, with a total runtime of approximately three minutes. Model performance in the TPS was comparable with that observed during validation. Together, these findings show that the DL model integration met the study’s objectives and was feasible for clinical workflows.

In the TPS testing environments, performance remained consistent with validation results. GTVp auto-segmentation performance was high and stable on both validation and RADPAINT-2 sets. GTVn performance showed expected variation across patients, reflecting the known challenges in segmenting small or ambiguous nodal structures, including missed nodes and false positives [22]. Interestingly, in one patient (#04), lymph nodes initially contoured manually but later confirmed negative by cytology were also segmented by the model, indicating concordance between DL output and physician assessment.

Manual review of auto-segmented contours remains an essential part of the radiotherapy planning process [23]. The AI integration included uncertainty visualization as supplementary confidence information, highlighting regions where the model may be less confident. These high-uncertainty regions mostly corresponded to segmentation errors, such as FPs and FNs (see Supplementary Material C). Thus, this feature may help guide oncologists to areas that may require closer inspection, potentially enhancing the DL model’s interpretability and trustworthiness.

Integrating in-house AI models for GTV auto-segmentation into existing radiotherapy workflows remains relatively rare. Some approaches, such as [8] and [24], used containerized environments to automate parts of the workflow. While such solutions offer scalability and modularity [25], [26], they often require administrative permissions and software development expertise, which can be challenging in clinical environments. Another category of solutions involves standalone external applications. For example, Wei et al. [27] developed an interactive DL-based tool for GTVp/GTVn segmentation in which users guide tumor localization before auto‑segmentation. Unlike these approaches, our approach uniquely embedded the DL model within the TPS, enabling auto-segmentation and contour editing within the planning system itself, without external software. In addition, the framework was flexible and could accommodate additional trained models with minimal configuration, making it adaptable to other cancer types and institutions.

This study is limited by its focus on demonstrating DL integration feasibility rather than evaluating model performance or generalizability. The TPS implementation was demonstrated using one in‑house model and a small test cohort, and the work was not designed to evaluate model generalization or comparative performance. In addition, although uncertainty estimations were visually available within the TPS and showed potential for identifying auto-segmentation errors, their practical usefulness for oncologists during contour review has not been assessed. Future work should include evaluating the clinical utility of uncertainty visualization and exploring different approaches for uncertainty estimation at both the patient level and the contour level. Additional future improvements could include extending the workflow to automatically derive clinical target volumes (CTVs) from the auto‑segmented GTVs, as well as enabling automated activation of dose optimization when the auto-segmentation output meets a predefined confidence threshold. These enhancements will improve the clinical applicability of the DL models and support a more efficient radiotherapy workflow.

In conclusion, the integration of a deep learning-based auto-segmentation model into a commercial treatment planning system demonstrated promising clinical feasibility, with performance in the TPS consistent with validation results. The demonstrated runtime of the segmentation pipeline further indicates potential improvements in workflow efficiency. The solution offers potential clinical safety benefits through manual review and uncertainty visualization, and its flexible design supports adaptation to other cancer types and institutions. Moreover, our scripting-based approach avoids any potential administrative and infrastructure hurdles, enabling seamless deployment within radiotherapy workflows.
