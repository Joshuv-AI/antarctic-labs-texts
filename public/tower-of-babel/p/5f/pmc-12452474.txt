Validation and psychometric evaluation of the Arabic Adult Vaccine Hesitancy Scale
Authors: Radwa Nour, Leigh Powell, Wafa K Alnakhi, Heba Mamdouh, Randa Sleibi, Hamid Y Hussain, Hanan Al Suwaidi, Nabil Zary
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTVaccine hesitancy is one of the top ten global health threats, with a culturally adapted, validated tool being essential for accurately measuring it to guide public health interventions. Although translations of the Adult Vaccine Hesitancy Scale (aVHS) to local languages and their validation have occurred in several contexts, no validated Arabic version exists for the Middle East population. This, however, impedes the process of generating locally and regionally reflective data for targeted interventions and evaluation of vaccine outreach efforts. To translate and validate the Adult Vaccine Hesitancy Scale (aVHS) for Arabic-speaking adults. This cross-sectional study followed a five-phase process: translation, expert review, preliminary testing, pilot testing, and validation with psychometric analysis. Translation and back translation were conducted then reviewed by an expert committee. The approved version of the Arabic aVHS (Ar-aVHS) was tested against the English version (E-aVHS) on 50 participants and demonstrated excellent internal consistency (Ar-aVHS Cronbach’s α = 0.8699). The overall correlation between Arabic and English scale scores was strong (Spearman’s rho = 0.8418, p < .001). During pilot phase on 1000 participants, the Ar-aVHS demonstrated adequate internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.636 overall). Exploratory factor analysis supported a two-factor structure, confidence in vaccines and risk perception, both explaining 40.5% of total variance. Using the established cutoff score, 66.2% of participants were classified as vaccine hesitant. The results of the study suggest that the Ar-aVHS is a valid, culturally relevant, and psychometrically sound instrument for assessing vaccine hesitancy among Arabic-speaking adults.KEYWORDS: Patient acceptance of health care, vaccination, surveys and questionnaires, psychometrics, language, eastern mediterranean, public health, Cross-cultural comparison

Introduction

Vaccine hesitancy, defined by the World Health Organization (WHO) as “a delay in acceptance, or refusal, of vaccines despite the availability of vaccine services and supporting evidence,” represents a significant global health challenge.1 The WHO’s designation of vaccine hesitancy as one of the top 10 threats to global health underscores its critical importance,2 this threat has become increasingly apparent as global vaccination rates for preventable diseases such as tuberculosis, diphtheria, measles, and polio have shown concerning declines since 2012.3,4

The causes of vaccine hesitancy are complex and multifaceted, encompassing safety concerns, lack of trust in health authorities, cultural factors, and the influence of misinformation.5 Additionally, vaccine availability plays a critical role in shaping vaccine hesitancy, as emphasized by both the WHO’s 3Cs model (Confidence, Complacency, Convenience)6 and the 5As framework proposed by Thomson et al (Access, Affordability, Awareness, Acceptance, Activation).1 In the 3Cs model, convenience captures the importance of access, similarly, the 5As model includes availability and accessibility as key dimensions, underscoring that vaccines must be physically present and easy to obtain for uptake to occur.

The COVID-19 pandemic brought unprecedented attention to vaccine hesitancy. As the scientific community worked to develop vaccines, the parallel spread of misinformation through social media platforms created new challenges to vaccine acceptance.7–9 This phenomenon highlighted the urgent need for reliable tools to measure and understand vaccine hesitancy across different populations and cultural contexts. During the COVID-19 pandemic, numerous researchers developed, modified, or translated instruments to measure vaccine hesitancy.10–13 Many of these instruments, however, were implemented without proper validation or cultural adaptation for their target populations. This oversight potentially compromises the reliability and validity of research findings, as instruments validated in one population may not perform equivalently in different cultural contexts.14–16

The Strategic Advisory Group of Experts on Immunization (SAGE) working group developed the Vaccine Hesitancy Scale (VHS) to position individuals along a continuum from complete vaccine acceptance to total refusal.17 This scale was initially designed to measure parental hesitancy regarding childhood vaccinations and was later validated through psychometric analysis by Shapiro et al.18 In 2021, Akel et al.19 modified the VHS to assess vaccine hesitancy in adults, creating the Adult Vaccine Hesitancy Scale (aVHS). The (aVHS) has been tested and validated in several countries, including the U.S., China, Greece and Poland, showing strong reliability and consistency across different populations and languages.19–21 The aVHS is widely used in research and public health studies, particularly in surveys on vaccine hesitancy for influenza, COVID-19, and other adult vaccines.22,23 Its strong psychometric performance has made it a trusted tool for exploring factors that influence vaccine acceptance, and its translated versions are being made publicly available to support wider use among researchers and healthcare providers. Unlike earlier tools that focused on parental attitudes toward childhood vaccination, the aVHS is one of the few validated instruments specifically designed for adult vaccine hesitancy. Its standardized design has led to its widespread use in related research. While the aVHS has been translated to various languages and validated in many contexts, no validated Arabic version currently exists for use in the Middle East population, creating a gap in regional research and public health practice. Without reliable instruments tailored to local languages and contexts, it is difficult to generate accurate data, design targeted interventions, or evaluate vaccine outreach efforts effectively.

This study aims to address this gap by translating and validating the aVHS into Arabic. A validated Arabic version of the aVHS will provide researchers and public health practitioners with a reliable tool for assessing vaccine hesitancy in Arabic-speaking populations, enabling more effective intervention strategies and public health planning.

Methods

Study design and overview

This cross-sectional validation study followed a five-phase process based on the methodology proposed by Tsang et al. and the AMEE guide for questionnaire development.14,16 The study protocol was published in JMIR Research Protocols.24 The study commenced in February 2022 and the final version of the instrument was piloted and data was collected in March 2023.

In April 2022, forward translation of the aVHS to Arabic was performed by two bilingual experts with experience in health-related questionnaire translations. The two translations were reconciled into a single version, which was then back-translated into English by a third independent translator. A four-member expert committee, comprising bilingual (Arabic-English) public health specialists, evaluated the translation quality. Committee members reviewed all versions of the scale (forward translations, reconciled version, and back-translation) and rated the final reconciled version against the original English version using a three-point scale (accept, reject, modify). For items requiring modification, experts provided specific recommendations. A separate translator incorporated these modifications, and the revised version was sent back to the committee for final approval. In September 2022, the approved Arabic version (Ar-aVHS) was tested against the English version using a convenience sample of 50 participants. The Ar-aVHS was uploaded to Microsoft Forms,20 followed by the English version with randomized item order. A link to the survey was sent to participants, all of whom were adult Arab nationals, bilingual in English and Arabic, and known to the authors. Reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, and correlation between language versions was evaluated using Spearman’s correlation coefficient.

After establishing preliminary reliability, the Ar-aVHS was pilot tested on a sample of 1000 participants using Pollfish, a market research platform enabling real-time data collection with targeted sampling criteria. The Pollfish platform uses Random Device Engagement (RDE), a form of probability sampling, to recruit survey participants. RDE involves delivering surveys within apps and websites where users are already present.

The survey was launched in March 2023 and included demographic questions and the Ar-aVHS.

Demographic questions included age, gender, and marital status, and country of residence. Socioeconomic status was assessed through questions about income, education, and occupation, with occupational categories based on the International Standard Classification of Occupations (Arabic version)25 (e.g., managers, professionals, technicians, students, housewives/men, unemployed, etc.). Gender was categorized as female, male, or other. Age was collected as a categorical variable of five categories: 18–24, 25–34, 35–44, 45–54, and >54 years. Marital status included single, married, separated, divorced, and widowed. Education level was classified into seven categories: primary or less, middle school, secondary school, vocational training, university degree, master’s degree, and PhD and annual income was reported in five brackets: <30,000; 30000–59,999; 60000–119,999; 120,000–300,000; and >300,000 USD. Current vaccination status was also recorded as a categorical variable of 5 categories (yes, 1 dose only, yes, 2 doses, no, but intend to in the future, no, and don’t intend to). We specified 2 doses since vaccines in the region at the time of the study were mostly available in the form of 2 doses.

Ar-aVHS

The survey also included the Ar-aVHS, consisting of 10 items using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from “Strongly disagree” to “Strongly agree.” Total scores ranged from 10 to 50, with higher scores indicating greater hesitancy. A cutoff score of 25 was used for dichotomizing responses into “vaccine hesitant” and “not vaccine hesitant” categories, based on the original aVHS validation.19

Study sample and eligibility criteria

A sample of 1000 participants was recruited and screened for eligibility based on the following criteria: Adults aged 18 years or older who self-reported as proficient in Arabic and are nationals of a country defined within the WHO Eastern Mediterranean Regional Office (EMRO).26 We restricted the population to individuals from the EMRO region to ensure linguistic and cultural relevance during the Arabic translation and adaptation of the tool. Participants were presented with an introductory text explaining the purpose of the study and the nature of participation and ensuring the anonymity of participation as well as their ability to withdraw without penalty.

Only authors had access to the data and the data is stored internally for 5 years as per institutional policy. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 25) and Stata version 14. Sample populations were described through demographic and socioeconomic status (SES) categories, presented as numbers and percentages. Ar-aVHS scores were calculated as continuous variables and presented as mean with standard deviation (SD). The scores were then categorized using a cutoff score of 25, where participants scoring ≥ 25 were classified as vaccine hesitant (VH), and those scoring ≤ 24.9 were classified as non-hesitant (NH).

The scale was tested for reliability using (Cronbach’s alpha) and exploratory factor analysis (EFA) to determine that the translation retained intended constructs via identification of factor loadings compared to the psychometric properties of the original VHS. The EFA was conducted using Principal Axis Factoring with orthogonal rotation (varimax). Pearson correlations were conducted between the aVHS scores with related attitudes, specifically vaccination status. Chi square tests were used to detect significant associations between socio-demographic characteristics and aVHS scores.

Ethical considerations

The study received approval from the institutional review board of Mohammed Bin Rashid University of Medicine and Health Sciences (MBRU IRB-2021–87).

Results

Translation and expert committee review

An expert committee of four members reviewed the original aVHS, the reconciled Arabic translation, and the back translation. Each translated item was evaluated individually, and items were accepted if at least three of the four reviewers approved them. During the first round, the committee recommended changes to items 3 and 4 (as noted by three reviewers) and items 6 and 7 (as noted by two reviewers). These changes were incorporated by a different translator and, in the second review round, all modified items received unanimous approval. The finalized Arabic version is provided in Appendix 1 - Ar-aVHS.

Preliminary testing

The preliminary testing of 50 participants demonstrated excellent internal consistency for the Arabic version (Ar-aVHS, Cronbach’s α = 0.8699) and English version (E-aVHS, Cronbach’s α = 0.8737). The overall correlation between Arabic and English scale scores was strong (Spearman’s rho = 0.8418, p < .001). Table 1 presents the item-by-item correlations between the Arabic and English versions. All items showed significant positive correlations ranging from 0.656 to 0.805 (p < .001), indicating strong consistency between the two language versions.

Validation and psychometric evaluation

Sample characteristics

The Ar-aVHS was tested on a sample included 1000 participants with diverse demographic and socioeconomic backgrounds (see Table 2). Male participants made up 56.8% of the sample, and female participants 43.0%, with two individuals not reporting their gender. Nearly half of the participants (51%) were aged 25–44 years. The sample comprised nationals from 22 countries in the region, with the highest numbers from Egypt (18.5%), Morocco (11.8%), and Algeria (7.8%), Saudi Arabia (6.8%), Tunisia (4.6%), together making up 49.5% of the sample (see Table 2). In terms of vaccination status, 45.4% of participants had received both doses of the COVID-19 vaccine, while 14.4% indicated they did not intend to get vaccinated.

Scale performance

The mean score on the Ar-aVHS was 26.8 (SD = 6.3). Using the established cutoff score of 25, 662 participants (66.2%) were categorized as vaccine hesitant, while 338 (33.8%) were categorized as non-hesitant. Psychometric properties

The Ar-aVHS demonstrated adequate internal consistency in the pilot phase (Scale reliability coefficient, Cronbach’s α = 0.6359). The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin test indicated adequate sampling (KMO = 0.827), and Bartlett’s test of sphericity confirmed sufficiently large item correlations (χ2 = 1152.207, df = 45, p < .001). Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) identified two factors with eigenvalues greater than 1, together accounting for 40.5% of the total variance in the 10-item scale (LRT chi-square p < .001). Factor 1, representing confidence in vaccines, explained 27.6% of the variance, while Factor 2, representing risk perception of vaccines, accounted for 12.9%.

Factor 1 showed strong loadings (0.580–0.664) for seven items related to confidence in vaccines (items 1–4, 6–8). Factor 2 demonstrated strong loadings (0.558–0.698) for three items related to risk perception (items 5, 9, 10) (see Table 4). When tested separately, items loading on Factor 1 had higher internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.7394), while items on Factor 2 showed lower internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.3).

Associations with demographic characteristics

In this study sample, vaccine hesitancy demonstrated statistically significant associations with multiple sociodemographic variables. Vaccination status emerged as the strongest predictor of hesitancy (Pearson χ2 = 142.5, p < .001). Hesitancy was lowest among those who had received both vaccine doses (46.9%; 213 out of 454). In contrast, hesitancy was substantially higher among participants who had received only one dose (75.5%), were not vaccinated but intended to in the future (86.0%), had no intention to vaccinate (83.3%), or preferred not to disclose their vaccination status (84.9%), Age also showed significant association with vaccine hesitancy (Pearson χ2 = 24.6, p < .001). The highest rates of hesitancy were observed in the youngest (18–24, 71.2%) and oldest ( > over 54 years, 80.0%), while participants aged 25–34, 35–44, and 45–54 reported moderate hesitancy levels of 57.3%, 62.5%, and 69.3%, respectively.

Gender was not significantly associated with hesitancy (Pearson χ2 = 3.4, p = .181). Among females, 69.3% (298 out of 430) were hesitant, compared to 63.9% of males (363 out of 568). The “other” gender category included only two participants, evenly split between hesitant and non-hesitant. In contrast, annual income was significantly associated with vaccine hesitancy (Pearson χ2 = 30.2, p < .001). Hesitancy was reported by 61.4% of participants earning less than $30,000 per year, 56% for those earning between $30,000–60,000, 69.4% of those earning $60,000–119,999, and reached its highest level (81.6%) among participants with an annual income exceeding $300,000.

Educational level was also significantly associated with hesitancy (Pearson χ2 = 114.4, p < .001). Hesitancy was highest among those with only primary (81.1%) or middle school education (91.1%). Among those with secondary education, 72.2% were hesitant. Surprisingly, hesitancy remained high even among those with advanced degrees: 85% among PhD holders and 69.5% among master’s degree holders. University graduates showed a comparatively lower hesitancy rate of 46.8%.

Marital status showed a strong association with vaccine hesitancy (Pearson χ2 = 150.7, p < .001). The highest hesitancy rates were observed among separated (94.9%), divorced (86.6%), and widowed participants (89.5%), followed by single participants (65.6%), while married participants had the lowest hesitancy at 46.6%. Occupation was also significantly associated with hesitancy (Pearson χ2 = 57.4, p < .001). Hesitancy was lowest among managers (46.5%) and professionals (57.9%), and highest among elementary workers (81.4%), agricultural workers (83.0%), students (66.7%), housewives/househusbands (73.0%), and the unemployed (72.3%).

Discussion

This study aimed to translate and validate the adult vaccine hesitancy scale (aVHS) from English to Arabic. To our knowledge, this is the first study to translate and validate the widely used aVHS to the Arabic-language. Using the methodology outlined by Tsang et al.14 and the recommendations from the AMEE guide for developing questionnaires,16 we developed a reliable Arabic version of the scale-the Ar-aVHS-to assess vaccine hesitancy among Arabic-speaking adults. The aVHS has already been translated and validated in numerous languages and settings,19–21 enabling meaningful comparisons of vaccine hesitancy across different cultures and regions.

The psychometric evaluation of the Ar-aVHS demonstrated strong properties during preliminary testing, with excellent reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.87) and significant correlation with the original English version (Spearman’s rho = 0.8418, p < .001). These results indicate successful retention of the scale’s original meaning through the translation process, supporting its utility in measuring vaccine hesitancy within Arabic-speaking populations.

The scale’s internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.87) during preliminary testing is aligned with other international validations of the aVHS. For instance, Duplaga et al.21 and Gialama et al.20 reported high overall internal consistency in their Polish and Greek versions (α = 0.935 and 0.884, respectively), while Akel et al.19 reported a Cronbach’s α of 0.89 in their development of the aVHS. Although the Ar-aVHS overall Cronbach’s alpha in the pilot phase (α = 0.6359) showed a decrease from preliminary testing (α = 0.87), the value remains adequate and the reduction can be attributed to increased sample heterogeneity. The expansion from 50 participants in preliminary testing to 1000 participants in the pilot phase introduced greater demographic diversity, a known factor affecting internal consistency measures. This pattern is consistent with psychometric literature suggesting that larger, more diverse samples often yield lower internal consistency coefficients compared to smaller, more homogeneous samples.27

The factor analysis revealed a two-factor structure explaining 40.5% of total variance, consistent with previous validations of vaccine hesitancy scales.18,20,21 Factor 1, representing confidence in vaccines (items 1–4, 6–8) explained 27.6% of variance and showed strong internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.7394) across seven items and Factor 2 capturing risk perception (items 5, 9, 10) explained 12.9% of variance and demonstrated lower internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.3) across three items. This pattern of factor loadings aligns with findings from Shapiro et al.18 and Domek et al.28 and is further supported by the Greek validation by Gialama et al.20 which also identified a similar structure with robust reliability. Likewise, Duplaga’s Polish version confirmed a two-factor model and emphasized the need to refine risk-related items due to variability in item performance. Sampling adequacy (KMO = 0.827) and significant Bartlett’s test results further supported the appropriateness of the factor analysis, underscoring the structural validity of the Ar-aVHS.

Our study identified several sociodemographic factors significantly associated with vaccine hesitancy in line with findings from other global and regional research. Vaccination status was the strongest predictor of hesitancy in our sample, with those fully vaccinated significantly less likely to report hesitancy. This is consistent with literature indicating that previous vaccine acceptance is a strong predictor of future vaccine behavior, reflecting patterns of trust in health systems and perceived risk.29 Age was another important factor, with the highest levels of hesitancy observed among the youngest (18–24 years) and oldest ( > 54 years) participants. This U-shaped association has been observed in other studies as well, where young adults often report skepticism, misinformation exposure, or a perceived lack of risk,30,31 while older adults may harbor distrust or concerns about side effects.

Contrary to some prior studies, gender was not significantly associated with vaccine hesitancy in this sample. While other research often reports higher hesitancy among women, due to concerns about fertility, side effects, or risk perception,32 the lack of gender disparity in our findings may reflect local contextual factors or a balanced distribution of vaccine messaging across genders.

Income and education levels in our study were significantly associated with hesitancy, though not always in expected directions. While previous research often shows lower hesitancy with higher socioeconomic status,33 our results showed that hesitancy remained high or even increased in higher income brackets, including 81.6% among those earning over 300,000 USD annually. Similarly, while hesitancy decreased among those with a university degree (46.8%), it rose again among participants with a master’s or PhD (around 70–72%). These findings deviate from global trends and may indicate a more complex relationship between education, income, and vaccine attitudes, possibly reflecting distrust in authorities, political views, or overconfidence in one’s own knowledge among highly educated or affluent groups.34,35

Marital status emerged as a strong determinant, with separated, divorced, and widowed individuals exhibiting significantly higher hesitancy. This may reflect social isolation, reduced exposure to health-promoting messages, or higher psychological stress, all factors previously associated with lower vaccine uptake.36 Married individuals, who often benefit from greater health support networks, showed significantly lower hesitancy (46.6%).

Occupation also played a role, with white-collar professionals and managers showing lower levels of hesitancy compared to those in manual, service-oriented, or unemployed roles. This aligns with other studies where job security, access to information, and workplace vaccine requirements shape vaccine attitudes.37

While the internal consistency of the full scale was slightly lower than reported in other contexts, this may be attributed to cultural or linguistic differences, particularly in how risk-related items are interpreted. Akel et al. noted that risk perception items are often more variable across populations, which may explain the lower reliability of Factor 2 (Cronbach’s α = 0.3) in our sample. Overall, the Ar-aVHS appears to reflect the underlying constructs of vaccine hesitancy reliably. These results support its application in measuring Arabic speakers’ vaccine hesitancy. While our convenience sample is not representative of the entire EMRO region, we believe it is still valuable to present our findings alongside research from this region to aid interpretation and contextualization. Our sample exhibited substantial vaccine hesitancy levels, with 66.2% categorized as vaccine hesitant. This level of hesitancy aligns with reports from various studies conducted in the region, which have documented similarly high rates of vaccine hesitancy. For example, during the Covid-19 pandemic, one large multinational study reported extremely high hesitancy rates among Arabs both inside (83%) and outside (81%) the Arab region.38 Other country-specific research found that in Jordan, 55.3% of participants were hesitant about receiving an annual COVID-19 vaccine, and 24.7% outright declined yearly vaccination, despite strong uptake of the initial vaccine series.39 In Egypt, although 68.3% indicated willingness to receive the annual vaccine, only 58.6% had received the seasonal influenza vaccine.39 While our findings are not generalizable to the broader EMRO region, they are consistent with patterns documented in other studies from EMRO countries and offer valuable context for understanding vaccine hesitancy within Arabic-speaking populations. To strengthen the evidence base and support more representative conclusions in the future, we recommend that subsequent research employ more rigorous sampling strategies-such as random sampling methods-to ensure broader generalizability and deeper insight into the determinants of vaccine hesitancy across diverse communities in the region.

Overall, the findings reinforce the importance of targeted public health communication for Arabic speakers, especially for groups identified as more hesitant, such as young adults, older individuals, those with low or high education levels, and the socially isolated. Understanding that hesitancy may arise for different reasons across demographics is essential for tailoring interventions.

Study limitations

While every effort was made to follow a rigorous and methodologically sound approach, certain limitations are inevitable in research of this nature. First, participant recruitment via Pollfish, a market research platform, may have introduced selection bias and limits the generalizability of our findings beyond the study sample. As such, our results should not be interpreted as representative of the entire EMRO population. Nonetheless, our sample included participants from all EMRO countries, providing a diverse convenience sample of Arabic-speaking adults and reflecting a range of demographic backgrounds.

The psychometric properties remained consistent across study phases and the two-factor structure aligns closely with findings from previous validation studies in other languages and populations, supporting the scale’s stable performance. Furthermore, a particular strength of our study lies in the robust translation process, which incorporated multiple expert reviews and iterative refinements to ensure linguistic accuracy, enhancing its relevance across Arabic-speaking populations.

Although COVID-19 vaccines were generally available during the study period, equitable access across the countries included in our sample cannot be fully assumed. This may have influenced some participants’ vaccination status. To address this, our survey included response options capturing vaccination intention (e.g., “No, but I intend to in the future”), which aligns with contemporary definitions of vaccine hesitancy that encompass both action and intent.

It is worth noting that our scores clustered around the neutral range. This could be an indicator of true neutrality in our sample or unwillingness to express a socially undesirable opinion, a phenomenon that is well-documented in the Arab world. The scores could also be influenced by our sampling method and we recommend future studies use probability-based sampling approaches to validate and expand upon these findings. Nevertheless, the neutral mean scores in our study were comparable to the results from the Greek version by Gialama et al and the Polish versions of Duplaga et al. whose versions also exhibited similar structural qualities to the Ar-aVHS, supporting our aim of producing a reliable and valid instrument.

Future research

We encourage researchers to utilize this validated Arabic version of the aVHS in future studies with Arabic speakers. Future research should examine the temporal stability of vaccine hesitancy measurements using the Ar-aVHS through longitudinal studies. Studies should also assess the scale’s sensitivity to change following public health interventions and investigate the relationship between measured vaccine hesitancy and actual vaccination behaviors. Given the demographic patterns identified in our study, research exploring the different manifestations of vaccine hesitancy across various population groups, including samples within specific countries, would be particularly valuable. The validated Ar-aVHS provides a foundation for evidence-based assessment of vaccine hesitancy in Arabic-speaking populations, supporting the development and evaluation of targeted interventions to address this critical public health challenge.

Conclusion

The Ar-aVHS demonstrates strong psychometric properties and serves as a valid and reliable tool for assessing vaccine hesitancy among Arabic-speaking adults. The two-factor structure aligns with previous validations of the vaccine hesitancy scale, supporting its construct validity. Internal consistency metrics and significant correlations with vaccination status provide evidence for the scale’s reliability and convergent validity. The successful validation of this Arabic version addresses a critical gap in vaccine hesitancy measurement tools for Arabic speaking populations.
