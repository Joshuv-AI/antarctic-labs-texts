Topography of entheseal lesions on ultrasound: a comparative study between axial spondyloarthritis, psoriatic arthritis and healthy controls
Authors: Gehad G Elsehrawy, Delphine S Courvoisier, Erik Deman, Diana Dan, Raphael Micheroli, Pascal Zufferey, Laure Brulhart, Michael J Nissen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo determine which anatomical sites and which ultrasonographic entheseal lesions are best able to discriminate between spondyloarthritis (SpA) patients and healthy controls (HC).MethodsWe included patients with psoriatic arthritis (PsA) and axial SpA (axSpA), from six Swiss hospital outpatient clinics, as well as HC. Participants completed quality of life and physical activity questionnaires and underwent a clinical examination of both joints and entheses, followed by a detailed musculoskeletal ultrasound examination including nine entheseal sites bilaterally. Entheses were scored according to the Outcome Measures in Rheumatology criteria, with an additional evaluation of bursae and power Doppler (PD) in the 2–5 mm zone.ResultsOverall, 121 participants were included, including 41 with PsA (mean age in years (SD), percentage male: 54.5±11.0, 63.4%), 39 with axSpA (45.1±10.0, 51.3%) and 41 HC (43.9±10.9, 56.1%), with a total of 2178 entheses evaluated. The PsA and axSpA groups showed no significant differences regarding inflammatory markers or disease activity scores.In the univariable analysis, all ultrasonographic lesions at the enthesis showed a significant association with SpA vs HC. Only B-mode inflammatory lesions (OR=1.38, p=0.034) and active enthesitis (OR=4.45, p=0.030) retained this association in multivariable analyses. While 4/9 entheses were associated with SpA in univariable analyses, only the distal patellar ligament insertion remained significantly associated with SpA (OR=1.74, p=0.039) in multivariable analyses.ConclusionTo distinguish SpA patients from controls, the sonographic scoring system used should account not only for the presence of specific entheseal lesions (structural and inflammatory) but also for the individual site affected.Keywords: Arthritis, Psoriatic; Axial Spondyloarthritis; Spondylitis, Ankylosing; Ultrasonography; Autoimmune Diseases

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

There is a high prevalence of abnormal sonographic findings at the enthesis that may be misinterpreted as inflammatory ‘enthesitis’ in the general population that are otherwise healthy, particularly in males, those with higher body mass index and older individuals, underscoring the need for further improvements on existing ultrasound scoring systems to more reliably distinguish enthesitis from non-inflammatory enthesopathy.

Introduction

Current knowledge supports the concept that enthesitis is a key feature in the pathogenesis of spondyloarthritis (SpA), including both psoriatic arthritis (PsA)1 and axial SpA (axSpA).2 Therefore, enthesitis has been included in the Classification Criteria for PsA (CASPAR)3 as well as for the Assessment of Spondyloarthritis International Society (ASAS) classification criteria for both peripheral SpA and axSpA.2 However, enthesopathy and enthesitis can occur in otherwise healthy individuals and are correlated with mechanical overload, male sex, increasing age and raised body mass index (BMI).4

Despite being relatively nonspecific and lacking sensitivity, physical examination is the standard technique for evaluation of the entheses in routine clinical practice. Ultrasound (US) has emerged as a valuable tool for detecting enthesitis and offering real-time assessment of entheseal pathological abnormalities.5

The Outcome Measures in Rheumatology (OMERACT) US group categorises the sonographic elementary lesions defining SpA-related enthesitis into ‘inflammatory’ lesions (ie, entheseal thickening, hypoechoic areas and power Doppler (PD) signal) and ‘structural damage’ lesions (ie, bone erosions, calcifications and enthesophytes).6 Subsequently, OMERACT developed a definition of US enthesitis: a hypoechoic and/or thickened insertion of the tendon within 2 mm of the bony cortex that exhibits a Doppler signal if active, with possible erosions and enthesophytes/calcifications as signs of structural damage.7 However, the Group for Research and Assessment of Psoriasis and Psoriatic Arthritis (GRAPPA) US working group maintains that the enthesis boundary could also extend beyond this 2 mm cut-off, as well as involving the adjacent bursa, in agreement with the concept of the ‘synovio-entheseal complex’.8 9

Several studies have demonstrated that some of these US entheseal lesions (ie, entheseal thickening, hypoechoic areas and enthesophytes) can also be detected in patients with non-inflammatory conditions (such as fibromyalgia (FM) and metabolic syndrome), as well as in healthy subjects,10 11 thus questioning the diagnostic value of enthesitis on US in patients with SpA, as well as the specificity of the OMERACT definition.12 Notably, there are few studies evaluating the ability of specific US lesions at individual entheseal sites to differentiate between SpA patients and healthy controls (HC), and little data comparing these lesions between patients with axSpA versus PsA.

Despite the fact that the US demonstrates good sensitivity for detecting inflammatory changes at the enthesis,13 its specificity is more variable between studies, depending on the specific sites and the lesions evaluated.8 14 15 This highlights a fundamental need to better define the diagnostic and prognostic value of assessing each entheseal site and the various US-detected lesions of enthesitis, in the context of SpA.16 While feasible in a research setting, the evaluation of a large number of entheses and multiple lesions is too time-consuming in routine clinical practice. Given these unmet needs, we designed this current study to assess the performance of six different sonographic entheseal lesions at 18 entheseal sites in patients with PsA and axSpA, as well as in a population of HC, in order to identify a potential set of key entheses and US lesions that can reliably differentiate between patients with and without SpA.

Materials and methods

Study population

This observational, cross-sectional, multicentric study across Switzerland was conducted in five university hospital rheumatology clinics and one regional hospital. We enrolled patients included in the nationwide Swiss Clinical Quality Management (SCQM) registry for inflammatory rheumatic diseases. Patients with a clinical diagnosis of SpA in the SCQM registry were included in the current study if they fulfilled either the ASAS or the Modified New York criteria for axSpA or they fulfilled the CASPAR. Exclusion criteria included participants aged less than 18 years, SpA patients with another rheumatic disease, HC with any inflammatory rheumatic disease, patients with prior joint replacements or prosthetic implants, pregnant or breastfeeding women, or participants unable to understand and complete the questionnaires independently.

Data collection

For all participants, data were collected on age, sex, BMI, socioeconomic status, smoking status, alcohol intake, medication intake in the past 3 months (including antibiotics, lipid-lowering therapy, aromatase inhibitors, retinoids and corticosteroids) and comorbidities (including endocrinological conditions), as many of these may influence the enthesis. Additional information was collected in SpA patients, including disease duration, current/past presence of enthesitis, psoriasis, uveitis, psoriatic nail disease, dactylitis and inflammatory bowel disease.

The clinical evaluation was performed by board-certified rheumatologists, including the 68 tender joint count (TJC) and the 66 swollen joint count (SJC), as well as for enthesitis using both the Maastricht Ankylosing Spondylitis Enthesitis Score17 and the Spondyloarthritis Research Consortium of Canada (SPARCC) score.18

All participants completed the Global Physical Activity Questionnaire (GPAQ),19 the short form-12 health survey (SF-12)20 and the FM rapid screening tool (FIRST).21 The GPAQ evaluates physical activity in multiple aspects (work, moving between places, leisure activities and sedentary behaviour).

The following disease activity indices were also recorded: the Disease Activity in Psoriatic Arthritis (DAPSA) score in PsA patients and the Ankylosing Spondylitis Disease Activity Score (ASDAS), the Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Disease Activity Index (BASDAI), the Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Functional Index (BASFI) and the Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Metrology Index (BASMI) in axSpA patients, as well as the Health Assessment Questionnaire (HAQ),22,26 health-related quality of life measurement (EuroQoL)27 and ASAS Health Index (ASAS-HI)28 in all SpA patients.

Additional data from the SCQM registry, including current and past medication history, biological markers of inflammation (erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) and C-reactive protein (CRP)), and HLA-B27 status, were collected.

Ultrasound protocol

Entheseal sites

All participants underwent a detailed US examination of 18 entheses (nine sites bilaterally), including the four OMERACT sites and five additional sites, included to match the SPARCC score sites from the clinical examination performed on the same day. The US examination was performed by one of six board-certified rheumatologists (ED, DD, RM, PZ, LB or MJN), all with at least 10 years of experience in musculoskeletal US, and all members of the ‘SONAR’ group (https://www.irheuma.com/de/rheumatology-a-z/s/sonar). Sonographers performed the US evaluation following their clinical examination.

Therefore, we assessed the following nine entheses: (1) the supraspinatus (SS) tendon, (2) the common extensor tendon at the lateral humeral epicondyle (LE), (3) the common flexor tendon at the medial humeral epicondyle (ME), (4) the gluteus medius tendon at the greater trochanter (GT), (5) the quadriceps tendon (QT) at the base (proximal pole) of the patella, (6) the proximal insertion of the patellar ligament into the distal pole of the patella (DP), (7) the distal insertion of the patellar ligament to the tibial tuberosity (TT), (8) the Achilles tendon (AT) and (9) the plantar fascia (PF).

Elementary lesions of enthesitis

The following elementary lesions of enthesitis were evaluated in both longitudinal and transverse scans, according to the OMERACT definitions:6 entheseal thickening, hypoechoic areas, and PD signal, as ‘inflammatory lesions’, and enthesophytes, calcifications and bone erosions as ‘structural damage’ lesions. Enthesophytes and calcifications were merged into a single lesion according to OMERACT guidelines.7 The OMERACT definition states that PD should be evaluated in the zone within 2 mm of the bony cortex. In accordance with the synovio-entheseal complex model, the local bursa is considered to be an integral part of the enthesis, and therefore, PD in the neighbouring bursa may also represent inflammatory enthesitis.29 Therefore, in the current study, we extended the areas evaluated for the presence of PD and defined three anatomical zones: (1) within 2 mm of the cortical bone (OMERACT zone); (2) within 2–5 mm of the cortical bone (GRAPPA zone); and (3) within the adjacent bursa (online supplemental figure 1).8 We decided à priori to merge entheseal thickening and hypoechoic changes into a single parameter, in order to decrease the time required to complete the US examination. ‘Active enthesitis’ was defined as a positive PD signal at the enthesis within 2 mm of the bony cortex (≥ grade 1) in association with entheseal thickening and/or hypoechoic areas, according to OMERACT.7 All ultrasonographic lesions were scored as binary outcomes (absent/present).

Sonographic evaluation

For grey-scale (B-mode), a high-frequency linear probe was used (10–18 MHz) (online supplemental table 1). Standardised machine presets were established prior to the study, with a PD frequency of 10.1 MHz and a pulse repetition frequency of 500 Hz.7 Moreover, the study ultrasonographers attended a kick-off meeting prior to enrolment to standardise the US technique, including machine settings and the positioning of each anatomical site for both B-mode and PD evaluations.

Statistical analysis

Categorical data were expressed as percentages and compared using Fisher’s exact test or the χ² test. Continuous data were presented as mean±SD or as median values with an IQR, as appropriate. Continuous data were compared using the student’s t-test or the Wilcoxon’s test in the case of two groups, while the analysis of variance or the Kruskal-Wallis test was chosen for the comparisons between three groups, as appropriate.

To identify the optimal combination of information on entheseal sites and lesions that best discriminates between SpA patients and controls, we used univariable logistic regression to analyse the relationship between the US ‘enthesitis lesions’ and a diagnosis of SpA. We then selected the best subset of variables using the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) method, which uses L1 regularisation to shrink the coefficients of less important variables or even sets some of them to zero, thereby selecting out less predictive variables. Multivariable analyses were then done using the variables selected, as well as age and BMI, to account for confounding. The same procedure was used to analyse the relationship between ‘entheseal sites’ and a diagnosis of SpA.

Exploratory analyses employed univariable and multivariable regression to investigate the relationships between US findings and clinical characteristics of patients with SpA. Logistic regression models assessed dichotomous variables, while linear regression models were used for continuous variables (eg, CRP, HAQ, ASDAS).

Inter-observer reliability was performed in a blinded fashion by three of the co-authors (MJN, DD, and LB) in 23 randomly selected study participants, including nine HC, seven PsA patients and seven axSpA patients, and was estimated by calculating Light’s kappa. A kappa value < 0.20 was considered slight, 0.21–0.40 fair, 0.41–0.60 moderate, 0.61–0.80 good and 0.81–1.00 excellent according to Landis and Koch. Values of p < 0.05 were considered significant. All data were processed and analysed with the statistical analysis software R 4.4.2.

For sample size calculation, we expected a prevalence of PD at the enthesis in 20% of patients with SpA and in 5% of HC, according to previous studies.30 We considered an allocation ratio of 2:1 for SpA cases to controls. With a desired power of β=0.85 and a significance level of α=0.5, and accounting for continuity correction, the sample for a two-sample test with a two-sided hypothesis was estimated to be 64 cases and 43 controls. We therefore concluded that with approximately 80 patients and 40 controls, our study would be sufficiently powered to address the outlined objectives.

Ethics approval

This study was conducted according to local regulations and the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Cantonal ethics committee in Geneva, Switzerland (CCER, project ID: 2021–02271). Informed written consent was obtained from all participants prior to inclusion in the study.

Results

A total of 121 participants were enrolled, 80 with SpA (39 axSpA/41 PsA) and 41 HC. Overall, 57.0% of the participants were male, without significant gender differences between groups. AxSpA patients and HC were younger (mean age±SD (years)) compared with PsA patients (axSpA 45.1±10.0 and HC 43.8±10.2 vs PsA 54.5±10.9, p<0.001). BMI (kg/m2) was higher in patients with PsA (27.3±3.8) compared with patients with axSpA (25.3±5.3) and HC (23.1±3.0), p<0.001. The median (IQR) of disease duration was significantly higher in axSpA compared with PsA (15.11 (9.05, 26.27) vs 7.16 (4.81, 12.88) years, respectively, p<0.001). Regarding axSpA patients, 63.6% were HLA-B27 positive, while 83.3% were classified as radiographic axSpA. Axial involvement was present in 28.6% of PsA patients (table 1). The mean (±SD) clinical and US examination time was 61.35±17.5 min.

Conversely, no differences were found between axSpA and PsA regarding most comorbidities and other measures, including ESR, CRP, ASDAS-CRP, BASDAI, BASFI, HAQ and EuroQoL. Nevertheless, SpA patients had significantly higher prevalences of hypertension and dyslipidaemia compared with HC, as well as poorer SF-12 scores and higher rates of FM (for more details, see online supplemental tables 2 and 3). There were no significant differences regarding physical activity scores across the three groups.

Overall, 84.8% of SpA patients were receiving a biological disease-modifying anti-rheumatic drug (bDMARD) and 12.1% were on a targeted synthetic (ts) DMARD (online supplemental table 4). A higher percentage of axSpA patients (67.9%) were on a tumour necrosis factor inhibitor compared with PsA patients (39.5%). There were no significant differences in the use of statins, antibiotics or glucocorticoids between the three groups.

Interobserver reliability

PD signal at both the 0–2 mm zone and the 2–5 mm zone showed the highest overall inter-observer reliability (kappa: 0.74 (95% CI 0.66 to 0.80) and 0.75 (95% CI 0.68 to 0.81), respectively), followed by enthesophytes/calcifications (0.65, 95% CI 0.58 to 0.71). Interobserver reliability was moderate for erosions (0.61, 95% CI 0.53 to 0.67) and fair for B-mode inflammatory lesions (0.38, 95% CI 0.29 to 0.48).

Prevalence and distribution of US lesions

A total of 2178 entheses were evaluated by US: 702 in axSpA patients, 738 in PsA patients and 738 in HC. The total prevalence of each elementary lesion was significantly higher in both forms of SpA compared with HC (table 3 and online supplemental table 5). The most frequent lesion across all groups at the patient level was enthesophyte/calcification (in 90.1%). While inflammatory B-mode lesions were observed in 24.4% of the HC, enthesophytes/calcifications were observed in the majority of HC (78%). There were no differences in the prevalence of each elementary lesion between patients with PsA versus axSpA, except for erosions at the entheseal level, with a significantly higher percentage in axSpA patients (11.3% vs 3.7% in PsA), and a numerically higher percentage of erosions in axSpA versus PsA at the patient level (53.8% vs 31.7% respectively). However, the differences between the SpA and HC groups were dependent on the specific entheseal site and lesion (figures1 2). Interestingly, pronounced differences in inflammatory B-mode lesions (thickening and hypoechogenicity) between SpA patients and HC were only observed at certain sites (ie, LE, QT, AT and PF). In addition to being the most prevalent lesion across all groups, enthesophyte/calcification was significantly higher in SpA patients compared with HC at 4/9 locations (SS, LE, GT and TT insertions). The prevalence of PD signal at the enthesis in both GRAPPA (6.3%) and OMERACT (5.4%) zones was similar and was significantly higher in both axSpA and PsA patients compared with HC, particularly at the AT and TT insertions. Active enthesitis also showed significant differences at LE and AT (figure 3). Combining PD positivity at the entheseal level in both GRAPPA and OMERACT zones did not provide any additional information over that of the individual zones.

Identification of key elementary lesions and sites distinguishing SpA from controls

All US lesions of enthesitis (ie, inflammatory B-mode lesions, PD within both 2 mm and 2–5 mm from the bony cortex, enthesophyte/calcification, erosions and bursitis) in addition to active enthesitis (as defined by OMERACT) were significantly associated with SpA in the univariable regression analysis. However, LASSO model selection retained only three elementary lesions (inflammatory B-mode, PD within 2 mm and bursitis), while only inflammatory B-mode was significantly associated with a diagnosis of SpA in the multivariable regression, adjusting for age and BMI (table 4).

Association of clinical measures with ultrasound lesions

Erosions and PD at the 2–5 mm zone showed a significant association with CRP. In addition, inflammatory B-mode lesions were associated with SPARCC, DAPSA and SF-12 (mental component) scores. PD at the 2–5 mm zone showed a significant association with BASFI and a borderline association with ASDAS-CRP (p=0.052). Bursitis was significantly associated with BASDAI, BASFI and ASAS-HI (online supplemental tables 10 and 11). None of the elementary entheseal lesions were associated with either the HAQ or the physical component of the SF-12.

Discussion

This study provides new insights into entheseal involvement in patients with axSpA and PsA, contrasting the ultrasonographic findings between these two groups, and in comparison to HCs, with detailed data on the prevalence, topography and discriminative value of the elementary entheseal lesions. Our results demonstrate the clinical relevance of the PD assessment in the 2 mm, 2–5 mm zones and bursae, as well as the impact of evaluating these zones separately vs in combination.

Previous studies have shown a wide variability in the prevalence and distribution of US findings of enthesitis in SpA.1031,33 In the current study, enthesophytes/calcifications were the most frequent lesions in all study participants (visualised in at least one site in 96% of SpA patients and 78% of HC), and bursitis was the least frequent lesion (in 25% of SpA patients and 4.9% of HC). Similar to a study by Di Matteo et al,15 we reported that B-mode inflammatory lesions had the highest prevalence among the inflammatory lesions. Regarding the structural damage lesions, enthesophytes/calcifications were more prevalent than erosions, in line with Bakirci et al results.10

At the patient level, PD at the 0–2 mm and the 2–5 mm zones showed comparable prevalences, with significantly higher rates of involvement in SpA (49%–54%) compared with HC (25%). These prevalences are similar to those reported by Marchesoni and colleagues34 but lower than those described by previous studies. For example, D’Agostino et al33 reported that the frequency of at least one vascularised enthesis was 64% for 51 SpA patients, compared with 19% in non-SpA patients. Molina Collada et al31 also reported higher prevalences of 81.3% and 75% using the Madrid Sonographic Enthesis Index (MASEI) and OMERACT PD definitions, respectively. MASEI PD refers to a PD signal in the cortical bone profile, intratendon, paratenon or bursa, aligning more closely with the GRAPPA PD definition.35 Wervers et al36 also found a very high prevalence (96%) of MASEI PD enthesitis in PsA patients. The relatively moderate frequency of PD observed in our study may reflect the moderate disease activity scores of our patients and the fact that the vast majority of patients were currently receiving treatment with an advanced therapy, thus supporting the association between inflammatory activity at the enthesis and high disease activity, as previously proposed by Mease et al37 and highlighting the importance of the PD assessment of the entheses in SpA.

Each US lesion of enthesitis was more prevalent in patients with SpA compared with HC. However, results varied among individual entheses, in line with previous studies.38 39 Furthermore, we found that each evaluated lesion was significantly associated with a diagnosis of SpA in the univariable analyses. Surprisingly, B-mode inflammatory changes had the highest discriminative value between SpA patients and HC. A study by Olivas-Vergara et al.40 also found that the presence of B-mode enthesitis (combined variable of entheseal thickening and hypoechoic changes) at the large entheses (LE, AT, DP and TT) was predictive of a diagnosis of PsA. In addition, a systematic review reported that while increased Achilles enthesis thickness and erosions were more common in the inflammatory tendinopathy group, compared to non-inflammatory tendinopathy (NIT) and healthy groups, increased vascularity was commonly reported in NIT.41

However, this finding is not consistent with other studies, where erosions and PD had the most significant association with a diagnosis of SpA.15 42 In a study including 23 HC with a mean age of 46.3 years, examining five entheseal sites, B-mode inflammatory changes were observed more often than in our population of HC across nine sites and who were somewhat younger.43 Overall, such differences may be related to (1) the different control groups included in other studies; (2) to the relative proportion of patients with axSpA and PsA in the SpA group, particularly with regard to erosions, as we found significantly higher percentages of erosions in axSpA patients compared with PsA patients; or (3) to the particular entheseal sites that were chosen for each study. While we included HCs, other studies enrolled patients with FM and/or osteoarthritis (OA) as a control group. Inclusion of non-HC groups (psoriasis, OA or FM) in other studies may result in the lower prevalence and specificity of the B-mode inflammatory lesions. Additionally, other studies included patients earlier in the disease course, while our cohort of SpA patients had longer disease duration and very high rates of current treatment with an advanced therapy, which could decrease the prevalence and sensitivity of PD.44 These findings suggest that the presence of PD signal may be more sensitive in early active disease and less so in established SpA patients on long-standing therapy with low to moderate disease activity status.

Given that inflammatory B-mode changes were the only significant lesion in multivariable analyses, we hypothesise that combining both entheseal thickening and hypo-echogenicity lesions together into a single variable may increase its specificity and discriminative ability. In addition, this combined B-mode lesion was the only US lesion that was associated with the clinical evaluation assessed by SPARCC score (p=0.001) and DAPSA score (p=0.008) in the multiple regression model, indicating a good correlation between US-detected and clinically measured inflammation. This further supports the concept that the identification of B-mode lesions is a valuable tool for capturing enthesitis. The prevalence of active enthesitis was significantly higher in SpA patients (33.8%) in comparison to HC (7.3%), in both the univariable and multivariable analyses (p=0.003 and p=0.03, respectively), and demonstrated very high odds ratios (6.45 and 4.54, respectively). This confirms its capacity to discriminate between diseased and healthy entheses and suggests that the presence of positive PD in combination with B-mode inflammatory lesions is more indicative of a diagnosis of SpA than either finding alone.

Regarding the individual entheseal sites, most previous studies focused on the lower limb entheses in patients with PsA, which might be more susceptible to biomechanical confounding factors such as obesity, repetitive physical activity and mechanical overloading.10 Eder et al45 demonstrated that they could not distinguish between HC and psoriasis or PsA patients when the BMI was >30. Therefore, we included three entheseal sites from the upper extremities to attenuate the potential effects of these confounding factors. From these three sites, the ME did not demonstrate any association with any particular entheseal lesion. Conversely, the SS showed several important differences, with significantly more erosions in axSpA patients and significantly more bursitis in PsA patients, while both enthesophytes and erosions were significantly more common at this site compared with HC. The lateral epicondyle demonstrated significant differences between SpA patients and HC for most of the lesions, although there were no significant differences at this site between axSpA and PsA patients. Therefore, we suggest that the inclusion of an evaluation of the upper extremity entheses, such as the lateral epicondyle and the SS, could minimise the impact of mechanical factors on any proposed US index.

Our study provides important preliminary information about the performance of the various sonographic entheseal sites in both axSpA and PsA. Employing different analytical models, we found that four entheseal sites (ie, lateral epicondyle, distal patellar ligament insertion, AT and PF) could effectively distinguish between SpA patients (both axSpA and PsA) and controls. These four sites have also been identified as being significant in other studies.8 14 15

Erosions demonstrated a significant association with a diagnosis of SpA, but only when their presence was evaluated in the four significant sites (LE, TT, AT and PF), which was not the case when this was evaluated across all nine examined sites. This emphasises that both the presence of these elementary lesions and their specific anatomical locations play a crucial role in the evaluation of the US examination of the entheses. One of our key objectives was to evaluate the importance of both the OMERACT and GRAPPA definitions of PD enthesitis. While both are significantly associated with a diagnosis of SpA in our univariable analyses, only the 0–2 mm PD zone appears as an important predictor for a diagnosis of SpA in the LASSO model. However, both PD definitions showed no significance in the multivariable models. We found that 2–5 mm PD was significantly associated with CRP and BASFI in both univariable and multivariable regression, suggesting that it could potentially be associated with systemic inflammation and a more severe SpA disease profile. The consistent association of bursitis with functional indices suggests its contribution to physical impairment. These findings support the concept that evaluation of the enthesis should also extend beyond this 2 mm cut-off from the cortical bone, which is in accordance with the concept of the ‘enthesis organ’ or the ‘synovio-entheseal complex’.29 46 Other studies demonstrated that active PsA patients showed not only PD at the enthesis junction (≤2 mm), but also in nearby bursae and the zone immediately beyond 2 mm from the cortex, similar to our findings.47 48 Additionally, MRI findings support this view, since SpA patients who present clinically with heel enthesitis frequently demonstrate local tendonitis and bursitis.49 Given these findings, we suggest that both concepts are useful in detecting enthesitis, and while the 2 mm cut-off is probably more specific for the diagnosis of SpA, the presence of PD beyond this cut-off suggests higher disease activity and functional impairment.

Our findings suggest that patients with axSpA have more erosions than do patients with PsA. Although this could be partially explained by the higher disease duration in axSpA, we found that, in the multivariable regression, male gender and HLA-B27 were significantly associated with erosions. Since both groups were comparable with regards to gender but not for HLA-B27, this would suggest that HLA-B27 is a contributing factor to the occurrence of erosions. Compared with PsA patients, axSpA patients presented a significantly higher probability of presenting erosions (OR 3.08, 1.18–8.01) even after accounting for disease duration in the regression analysis.

There are some study limitations that need to be mentioned. Our cross-sectional study only examined participants at a single time-point, with most patients currently treated with an advanced therapy. Ideally, future studies examining patients longitudinally, both pre- and post-therapy, as well as comparing patients with early and longstanding disease, would be informative. Lack of investigator blinding is another limitation. Unfortunately, it was not practically feasible to mobilise two experienced physicians for each participant visit, and we aimed to mitigate this aspect with protocolised scanning, device presets and investigator training prior to the study. In addition, the clinical examination findings and the US findings were recorded on different case report forms. Nevertheless, it would be preferable in future studies that the US assessor is completely blinded to the clinical findings. There were some difficulties in matching between the three groups, particularly regarding age and BMI, due to the different epidemiological and demographic characteristics of the evaluated conditions. To compensate for this, we adjusted all multivariable analyses for age and BMI. Combining entheseal thickening and hypoechogenicity into a single variable limits our ability to provide the specific diagnostic value of each feature individually, although previous studies also adopted the same approach.40 Further studies are needed to evaluate this combined B-mode inflammatory lesion and to compare it with the individual lesions.

Strengths of our study include the participation of multiple rheumatology centres across Switzerland, enhancing the generalisability of our findings to real-world practice. In order to avoid the omission of potentially relevant entheseal sites, we decided to scan a wide range of sites, in both upper and lower extremities. Investigators participated in a reliability exercise on the US lesions of enthesitis with the aim of standardising US assessments. The inter-observer variability in our study was consistent with previous studies.50

Conclusion

Our findings emphasise the value of ultrasonographic assessment of the entheses in SpA, particularly in identifying the type of lesion and its topographic distribution, as well as comparisons between patients with axSpA and PsA. We highlight the importance of the distal patellar ligament insertion, which was the only site significantly linked to a diagnosis of SpA in multivariable analyses. B-mode inflammatory lesions and erosions had the highest discriminative value between SpA patients and controls. We provide further data emphasising the key role of an evaluation of ‘active enthesitis’ and found that this was strongly indicative of SpA at both the AT and the common extensor tendon insertions. Overall, this study contributes to better defining a specific and time-efficient US scoring system for routine clinical use in SpA.
