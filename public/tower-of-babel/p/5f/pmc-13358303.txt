Prescribing in the dark: a qualitative study exploring perceptions and practices behind potentially inappropriate vitamin D use in Pakistan
Authors: Irzah Farooq, Javeria Saleem, Fatima Ali, Afia Zafar, Abida Tehreem, Zainab Naeem, Hafiz Waqas Ahmed, Muhammad Arshed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionVitamin D plays a crucial role in maintaining bone health, regulating the immune system and enhancing metabolic function. Although supplementation is recommended for individuals with confirmed deficiency, the increasing trend of empirical prescribing and self-medication has raised concerns about inappropriate use, particularly in low- and middle-income countries like Pakistan. This study aimed to explore perceptions, attitudes and practices regarding the prescription of vitamin D supplementation among healthcare professionals and self-medicating patients in Pakistan, with a specific focus on identifying factors contributing to non-evidence-based prescribing and unregulated consumption.MethodsA qualitative, exploratory design was adopted to investigate the study objectives. In-depth, semistructured interviews were conducted with 20 doctors from five medical specialities and 17 patients who reported using vitamin D supplements. Participants were recruited from healthcare settings in Lahore using a purposive sampling approach. Thematic analysis was used to identify key patterns in prescribing and self-use behaviour.ResultsFour themes emerged from the doctor interviews: empirical prescribing without diagnostic testing, the influence of pharmaceutical marketing, the perceived harmlessness of vitamin D and the absence of standardised guidelines. From patients, themes included self-medication and over-the-counter access, belief in vitamin D as a general wellness booster, long-term unmonitored use and lack of awareness about dosing and risks. Neither group was aware of the potential toxicity and follow-up testing was done rarely.ConclusionsThe study’s findings highlight a widespread perception of availability, potentially inappropriate and unregulated use of vitamin D, underscoring the need for nationwide action through education, regulation and public awareness. Coordinated efforts by health organisations, clinicians and pharmacists can promote safer supplementation practices and help prevent long-term adverse health effects.

Background

Vitamin D, a fat-soluble secosteroid hormone, plays a vital role in metabolic regulation, including calcium-phosphorus homeostasis, bone health, immune regulation and neuromuscular function.1 It is primarily synthesised endogenously through exposure of the skin to ultraviolet B rays and, to a lesser extent, obtained from dietary sources. Globally, vitamin D deficiency is a widespread concern, particularly in South Asia and the Middle East. The cultural practices, urbanisation and limited sun exposure contribute to suboptimal vitamin D status across populations.2

In response to high deficiency rates, vitamin D supplementation has become increasingly common, with doctors prescribing high-dose treatments and individuals reporting self-medication.3 While supplementation is medically necessary for individuals with a deficiency, the unregulated use of vitamin D without diagnostic confirmation has raised concerns about overprescription and overconsumption.4 Studies show that excessive intake leads to adverse health outcomes, including hypercalcaemia, vascular calcification, hardening of soft tissues and kidney dysfunction.5 In many low- and middle-income countries, including Pakistan, this issue is worsened by the unregulated availability of high-dose supplements over the counter, strong pharmaceutical marketing, and a lack of national clinical guidelines.1 6

Despite the prevalence of vitamin D use in Pakistan, there is a lack of qualitative research exploring the underlying factors that drive prescribing and consumption behaviours. Understanding how both healthcare professionals and patients perceive and use vitamin D is crucial to developing safe and evidence-based supplementation policies and educational strategies. This study aims to explore the perceptions, motivations and reported practices of doctors and patients regarding vitamin D supplementation in Pakistan, with a specific focus on the factors contributing to its perceived overuse.

Methods

Study design

This qualitative exploratory study examined perceptions, attitudes and practices regarding the perceived overuse of vitamin D supplements among healthcare practitioners and patients in Lahore, Pakistan. In view of the limited literature on the irrational use of supplements in a local setting and the issues surrounding when and how prescriptions were made and taken, a qualitative methodology was selected to provide in-depth, context-rich data.4 This structure facilitated an exploration of how knowledge, culture and social network factors influence decisions about dietary supplements. This study focused on understanding participants’ experiences and opinions through an interpretive paradigm.

Study area and population

This research was carried out in urban healthcare facilities (public and private hospitals and clinics) in Lahore, a large metropolitan city with a diverse socioeconomic mix and a greater number of healthcare providers. The setting was convenient due to its high patient volume, a wide range of medical specialities and the availability of vitamin D to both prescribers and users.

Twenty (20) physicians, from five important specialties (nephrology, gynaecology, orthopaedics, paediatrics and psychiatry), were selected according to their usual contribution in prescribing of vitamin D. Seventeen (17) patients who had used a vitamin D supplement within the past 12 months were also recruited through outpatient clinics/clinician referrals identified based on symptoms that are known to be associated with supplementation (eg, bone pain, fatigue, anaemia or neuropathy).

Sampling technique

A purposive sampling technique, based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, was used to select participants with direct experience with vitamin D prescribing or use. This method is beneficial in qualitative health research, where information-rich cases can be included in accordance with the study’s aims.7 Inclusion criteria for healthcare professionals were current practice in a relevant specialty and routine prescribing of vitamin D. For patients, the only requirement was documented or self-reported vitamin D supplementation in the past 12 months, with or without adverse events. Sampling continued until theoretical saturation. Data saturation was reached when no new ideas or themes were coming from the interviews. Redundancy in the data was observed at the 16th interview, after which additional interviews confirmed that no new information emerged and that saturation had been achieved.

Data collection methods

The first author, an experienced public health researcher and qualitative researcher, collected the data. All participants underwent face-to-face, semistructured interviews in private consulting rooms to enhance their comfort, privacy and confidentiality. Participants were interviewed in either Urdu or English based on their preference, and interviews ranged from 25 to 40 min. Consent was obtained for the audio recording of all sessions. There was no previous relationship between the researcher and participants. None of the participants refused to participate in the study.

Interview guides for healthcare providers and patients were designed separately and included the following topics:

Interview guides were developed following a review of the literature and expert feedback from public health and clinical professionals. Two pilot interviews (not included in the final analysis) were undertaken to enhance question clarity and sequencing. The COREQ (Consolidated criteria for REporting Qualitative research) checklist was used to ensure detailed methodology.8

Participant checks were not conducted due to time constraints. The interviewer had training in qualitative research and a background in public health.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of our research. Interview guide questions, however, were formed based on previous literature.12 4 79 Their responses were then summarised into themes and subthemes.

Data analysis

The audio-recorded interviews were transcribed, and the interviews taken in Urdu were translated into English. This study employed a reflexive thematic analysis approach as outlined by Braun and Clarke.10 This approach emphasises the active role of the researcher in interpreting meaning from the data rather than aiming for objectivity or inter-coder reliability. Coding was conducted iteratively, with ongoing discussion among the research team to refine interpretations and theme development. Additionally, none of the software was used in the coding process. Initially, open coding was conducted by all research teams to generate initial codes. Then, all formed codes were cross-checked by the whole research team several times, followed by the development of a coding framework. Any discrepancies in the codes were resolved through an internal meeting and lengthy discussions. Codes were then grouped into themes and subthemes. Then, data, codes, themes and subthemes were handed to a third-party researcher, unrelated to the current study, for validation and cross-checking of the whole coding process. Data were handled and explored in NVivo software, using tools for systematic coding, extracting text segments, structuring themes and visualising relationships across the dataset. To maintain credibility, peer debriefing, the code-recode technique and triangulation of views between patient and provider perspectives were used. Relevant quotes were also used to support the interpretation of themes, enhancing the transparency and credibility of the analysis.

The reflexivity

To maintain reflexivity, the first author kept a reflective journal throughout the study to record personal assumptions, emotions and methodological decisions. This was expected to reduce the bias in the data collection and analysis. There were no pre-existing relationships with any participants, and care was taken to create a non-judgemental, neutral interview environment. Consistent with a reflexive thematic approach, the researchers acknowledged their active role in interpreting the data and engaged in continuous reflection throughout the analysis process.

To minimise interview bias, the researcher practised reflexivity by keeping a field diary after each interview to record reflections, participant behaviour and any environmental or contextual factors that might influence responses.11 The researcher’s interest in the topic, driven by professional observations of irrational supplement use, was acknowledged and bracketed during data collection and analysis to ensure objectivity. Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of our research.

Ethical considerations

Participants were informed about the study’s purpose, confidentiality measures and their right to withdraw at any time without penalty, as part of the informed consent process. Written or verbal informed consent was obtained before the interviews. All interviews were conducted under fictitious names and coded, and recordings were stored securely to protect participant confidentiality. Also, it was approved by the Institutional Research Committee (IRC) of Green International University (GIU).

Results

Demographic profile of doctors

The study involved 20 health professionals from Lahore, Pakistan, representing a diverse cross-section of specialities, levels of experience and healthcare settings. Eleven were male and nine were female, reflecting a fairly even gender split. Participants were from various specialities, including orthopaedics (5), psychiatry (4), gynaecology (3), paediatrics (3), nephrology (3) and one each from physiotherapy and urology, providing multi-speciality perspectives. Most (13) are in the private sector, with 7 working in public healthcare settings. Their work experience ranged from 4 to 18 years, encompassing early-career to senior practitioners. Empirical prescribing without diagnostic confirmation

Empirical prescription of vitamin D without pre-diagnostic confirmation, mostly based on non-specific signs, was reported by participants. Many doctors agreed that they prescribe vitamin D regularly based on general symptoms such as fatigue, bone pain or lethargy rather than confirmed laboratory deficiencies. Physicians from orthopaedics and gynaecology reported the doses they commonly prescribe, specifically high-dose vitamin D (usually 200 000 IU injections or 50 000 IU capsules weekly), often under the name Indrop-D, for routine treatment of musculoskeletal complaints. According to one orthopaedic doctor,

Most of my patients present with joint pain or weakness, so I just go ahead and prescribe vitamin D injections … we don’t wait for lab results unless the patient insists. (D02)

Gynaecologists also reported that they prescribed high doses of vitamin D in cases of menstrual irregularities or after menopause, without ordering serum 25(OH)D testing for the patient. This theme highlights the widespread reliance on symptom-based judgement over evidence-based protocols.

In our setup, lab tests take time and cost money, so we usually treat based on symptoms. (D01)

These prescription patterns indicate that physicians rely on experiential diagnosis rather than scientific protocols, potentially contributing to inappropriate prescribing.

Influence of marketing by pharmaceutical companies

Almost all the doctors who participated in this study highlighted the perceived influence of marketing of different pharmaceutical companies on vitamin D prescribing practices. This often comes in the form of representatives from various companies.

The companies keep sending me different samples and pamphlets about immunity benefits, so we prescribe it often. (D15)

This theme was common in almost every specialty of doctors, like

They send samples almost every week, so of course I end up prescribing them more. (D11)

Even nephrologists, who had no direct connection to bone health or strength, also acknowledged that they were prescribing it under the influence of marketing strategies. “The pharma reps always emphasise immunity, energy, and bone strength; they make it sound like everyone needs vitamin D” (D05).

However, doctors were also aware that even if they were not easily impressed by marketing techniques and did not prescribe vitamin D, patients still obtained it because marketing efforts extended beyond pharmaceutical representatives to various social media platforms. “Sometimes patients even ask for a specific brand they’ve heard about. I think the marketing is working on both ends” (D03).

This indicates that commercial publicity is a crucial factor in increased, potentially inappropriate use. This further underscores the need for robust regulatory legislation to ensure ethical marketing practices.

Perception of vitamins as a harmless nutrient

Vitamin D was regarded as a generally safe nutrient, often considered safe to include in general prescriptions without strict protocols. Most doctor respondents did not express any concerns about vitamin D toxicity, even when it was used at high doses.

It’s a vitamin, not a drug; even if it’s extra, the body will manage it. (D13)

Psychiatrists commonly use vitamin D as a mood regulator for everyone here.

It’s not like a drug; it’s just a vitamin. It won't harm them even if they don’t need it. (D20)

Doctors were also unbothered by the harm caused by perceived overuse of vitamin D, as they do not even feel the need to reassess or monitor biochemical levels after initiating supplements. There is a dose adjustment of vitamin D, even after the patients have systemic relief.

We don’t worry about toxicity unless someone is on it for years. (D02)

This safer nutrient perception increases the potential for inappropriate vitamin D use, underscoring the need for practitioners to promote safer prescribing patterns.

Lack of uniform guidelines

Participants reported a perceived lack of standardised guidelines for vitamins at both the institutional and national levels, leading to differences in prescribing and use patterns. Prescribing practices also vary significantly across specialities, and only a few practitioners reported keeping themselves updated on safe dosing values through self-study.

There’s no single protocol for all; each doctor does what they feel is right. (D10)

Additionally, the institute did not provide proper protocols; instead, doctors often seek advice from colleagues or adopt their practices without thorough consideration.

In our hospital, everyone has their way. There’s no consistent policy on how often to give it. (D04)

There is variability in clinical practices, which becomes apparent when comparing approaches across different specialities. For example, while orthopaedists and nephrologists referred to international guidelines or relied on their past clinical experience, other doctors, such as psychiatrists and gynaecologists, reported that they often relied on recommendations from peers.

There’s no one telling us the exact dosing, so we just go with what we’ve seen others doing. (D08)

Sometimes we give high doses weekly, sometimes monthly; it depends on who’s treating the patient. (D16)

These perceived gaps in standard protocols may lead to irregular clinical prescriptions, requiring the implementation and regulation of evidence-based clinical protocols.

Demographic profile of patients

Seventeen patients (10 women, 7 men), aged 20–50 years, were interviewed who had been supplemented with vitamin D in various forms, primarily through injections and tablets. Nine self-medicated, and eight took a prescription. The duration of supplementation ranged from 2 months to 1 year, with most individuals using it for conditions such as fatigue, joint pain, weakness and neuropathy, for both medical and individual health reasons. Self-medication and peer advice

Patients frequently reported using vitamin D supplements without consulting a doctor, often at the advice of family, friends and relatives. According to them, consumption was often based on recommendations from friends or family, online social media content or their pharmacist’s advice.

My cousin had back pain and took vitamin D. I also started using it because my body aches. (P12)

In general, working individuals preferred an easy solution that did not require a visit to the doctor for mild symptoms. Many patients reported that vitamin D was a safe, everyday solution for their issues, such as fatigue, backache or low energy. These conditions were often self-diagnosed. Therefore, individuals adjust their doses independently based on their perception of the benefits rather than seeking medical advice.

I heard on YouTube that vitamin D is good for mood and bones, so I take it every week. (P09)

Some patients reported receiving vitamin D injections directly from pharmacists. Pharmacists typically do not inquire about a patient’s medical history or existing health conditions. This unrestricted access to vitamin D highlights a regulatory gap in Pakistan, posing a significant public health risk.

My friend was taking vitamin D injections and felt better, so I started the same. (P03)

These behaviours indicate reliance on peer groups, potentially leading to inappropriate use, highlighting the need for improved patient awareness and access to evidence-based health information.

Over-the-counter availability

One of the most important causes of perceived vitamin overuse reported by patients was the easy and unrestricted availability of vitamin D supplements over the counter. Patients also described how easy it is to be able to walk into pharmacies and purchase high-dose tablets or even injection-based formulations without prescriptions or any medical inquiry.

You can buy vitamin D injections from almost any pharmacy; they never ask if a doctor has prescribed them to you or not. (P11)

This open access, combined with widespread marketing and casual advice from peers or even pharmacists, leads many people to view these supplements as a safe alternative for everyday wellness, rather than medicines that require proper clinical supervision.

I went in to buy something for back pain, and the pharmacist suggested I try vitamin D capsules weekly. (P02)

Several respondents shared that they were never asked for a prescription or warned about dosage at the pharmacy counter. The easy availability of these nutrients, particularly in areas lacking public education or regulation, is a concern. As one of them mentioned;

Injections and soft gels, both available at shops, no questions asked. It feels like anyone can use them. (P05)

This easy access to vitamins without standard protocols at pharmacies was perceived as the crucial cause of inappropriate use, requiring the importance of regulatory mechanisms.

Limited awareness about risks and dosage

Patients demonstrated limited knowledge of recommended dosages, side effects or the necessity of testing before initiating any supplementation. Vitamin D was widely perceived as a general health booster with no harmful effects; however, its popularity increased significantly after the COVID-19 pandemic.

Everyone says it’s good for bones and energy, I take it every week for strength after Covid based on relative advice. (P07)

Although vitamin D supplements are widely used, many patients lack a clear understanding of the potential risks associated with overconsumption. Also, they do not know the importance of standard dosage protocols. This knowledge gap is evident among individuals who self-prescribe without consulting healthcare professionals.

I didn’t know you could overdose on vitamins. I thought the more, the better. (P12)

While vitamin D is often recommended for its benefits to bone health and immune function, patients generally do not distinguish between the necessary dosage for their specific needs and the amounts shown on supplement labels.

It’s just vitamin D, not a medicine. I didn’t think it needed a doctor. (P15)

Moreover, healthcare providers did not discuss the importance of regular blood tests to monitor vitamin levels, which could help prevent toxicity. “No one told me about testing or follow-up, I just keep using it if it helps” (P10). Therefore, targeted education and explicit policy guidance for proper dosage and risk are required.

Symptom-driven consumption

Patients reported vitamin D use based on symptoms such as fatigue, joint stiffness or low mood. They believed these problems were due to vitamin D deficiency and began supplementation without medical evaluation. Some interpreted any weakness or tiredness as a sign of low vitamin D, often influenced by online health content or social media influencers.

I felt tired all the time and read online that vitamin D helps, so I started injections. (P04)

A significant number of patients in this study initiated vitamin D supplementation based on perceived symptoms rather than through medical advice or diagnostic testing. This symptom-driven consumption reflects a larger trend where individuals turn to supplements as a quick fix for vague health complaints.

My body felt weak all the time, so I started taking vitamin D. It helped a bit, so I kept using it. (P09)

While some patients believe their fatigue, joint stiffness or low mood directly result from a deficiency in vitamin D, there is a lack of recognition that these symptoms could also be associated with other underlying conditions.

I didn’t need a test… I just knew from my body that I was low on vitamins. (P12)

The rise in online health information, much of which is unverified, plays a substantial role in influencing this behaviour.

I had joint pain and fatigue. A friend told me vitamin D worked for her, so I started taking it. (P15)

Vitamins were often seen as the first line of self-treatment for these vague complaints, which they believed indicated a deficiency.

I get tired after work, and someone said Vitamin D helps, so I tried it. It made sense to continue. (P05).

To protect the public from the perceived overuse, diagnostic assessment and evidence-based guidance before supplementation is needed.

Trust in injectable over oral supplements

Several patients strongly believed in the superiority of injectable vitamin D, describing it as more effective or faster than oral formulations. Many patients had received injections at clinics without testing, often on repeated occasions.

Tablets don’t work for me… injections give me strength quickly. The doctor also gave them without tests. (P13)

Injection forms of vitamin D were often viewed by patients as more potent and effective than oral supplements, perhaps because of their faster onset of action. This perception was supported by some recommendations from friends or local clinics, where injection-based vitamin D was commonly administered.

I don’t trust tablets. Injections work better and faster. The clinic near my house gives them without any test. (P02)

Many patients trust injections because of their visible or immediate effects, such as improved energy or reduced pain, which they believe result from their high absorption rate.

Tablets feel too slow; I just go to the pharmacy and get the injection. (P10)

Although this belief sometimes leads patients to receive multiple injections without any medical guidance, which increases the risk of exceeding the recommended dosage.

Injections give immediate relief. My doctor also says they work better. (P08).

The attraction of quick results without the need for ongoing testing or monitoring further reinforces the preference for injectable forms, despite the lack of scientific backing for their superiority over oral supplements in the long term.

Whenever I feel down, I get an injection; it helps me within a day. (P14)

Lack of follow-up or monitoring

Patients reported limited follow-up and monitoring to assess vitamin D levels after initiating supplementation. Some patients continued taking supplements for even years as they were unaware of the need for dosage adjustment or monitoring.

I never did any test, I just kept taking it because I felt better. No one told me to stop. (P03)

Once the patients began supplementation, whether oral or injectable, no particular approach was adopted to screen for changes in their vitamin levels through follow-up testing.

I never thought about testing again. I assumed it keeps working if I’m still tired. (P15)

This absence of monitoring results in patients unknowingly consuming excessive amounts of vitamin D over time, which could easily lead to toxicity.

I was never told to stop or repeat the tests. So, I kept taking them monthly. (P17)

A general lack of education about the potential risks of prolonged supplementation contributed to failure to monitor vitamin D status, necessitating structured follow-up procedures.

Long-term use without dosage adjustment

Patients reported using vitamin D supplements for extended periods, sometimes for months or even years. Also, they used it without any medical supervision or follow-up. This is often due to a common perception among people in our country that it offers many benefits and that they do not feel any immediate adverse effects.

I’ve been taking Vitamin D injections every month for almost a year now, I never involved any doctor. (P13)

The long-term self-medication or management also highlights the lack of connection between patients’ behaviour and the doctor’s expertise in the safe use of supplements.

My joint pain reduced, so I just got stuck to it. No one told me to stop. (P01)

Doctors in our country mostly rely on their judgement and past experiences while prescribing supplements. Promotions from different pharmaceutical companies also influence their thoughts.

I’ve been taking Vitamin D every month for over a year based on my doctor’s prescription. I don’t go to the doctor anymore because it makes me feel better. (P04)

Patients also reported self-medicating with vitamin D for various reasons, including social influence, perceived benefits and easy access to (Over-the-Counter) OTC products.

I’m using it regularly like any other multivitamins, and I don’t think about checking levels. (P08)

I bought a big pack of vitamin D and kept taking it. I didn’t think I needed to ask a doctor every time. (P16)

Findings indicate that vitamin D use, as perceived, is supported by non-specific symptoms, empirical prescribing and ready over-the-counter access across both clinicians and patients. Additionally, pharmaceutical companies’ marketing practices, a preference for injectables and limited knowledge of dosing considerations risk overuse. Further influencing these behaviours were variable practices and a perceived lack of evidence, leading to recommendations being interpreted as vague and with poor follow-up. Collectively, they indicate a need for better awareness, uniformed recommendations and greater clinical supervision.

Discussion

This study explored the perceptions, behaviours and practices associated with vitamin D supplementation among doctors and patients in Pakistan through exploratory qualitative interviews. The findings of this study indicate that physicians perceived vitamin D prescribing to be based on symptoms, patients’ self-medication practices and long-term use, suggesting both clinical-level and public-level misconceptions. The aspects align with global trends described below while also highlighting the challenges faced by this region, which are attributed to limited regulation and low health literacy.

The most prominent finding reported by doctors was the prescription of high-dose vitamin D based on symptoms without confirming deficiency through laboratory tests. Doctors mentioned a routine reliance on general symptoms, such as fatigue or musculoskeletal pain, to initiate treatment.3 Similar prescribing patterns have reportedly been observed in other low- and middle-income countries, where common issues include a lack of appropriate diagnostic services, such as biochemical screening for vitamin levels, leading doctors to use symptom-based management practices.12 However, such practices were against international guidelines that recommend supplementation only for confirmed deficiencies.8 This disconnect between evidence-based medicine and the perceived prescribing behaviour of our doctors stemmed from both a lack of national protocols and a common belief that vitamin D is naturally harmless.

Non-evidence-based prescribing driven by pharmaceutical companies’ marketing efforts is another crucial issue. Doctors’ frequent interactions with pharmaceutical representatives were found to promote prescribing vitamin D as a general wellness enhancer. Studies from both India and Pakistan have yielded similar findings on how promotional activities can influence doctors’ clinical decisions regarding supplements.6 13 The easy availability of free vitamin D samples and injections has further normalised prescribing, leading to more vitamin D supplementation without diagnosis.

The practice of self-medication was another prevailing issue found in patients. Vitamin D use based on peer advice or pharmacist suggestions without involving the physician was a common practice. Over the counter access to high-dose formulations, especially in the form of injections, additionally has facilitated long-term and unsupervised use. This finding in the study was supported by existing research showing that more than half of the Pakistani population self-medicate with vitamins, primarily due to a misconception that supplements are safe and beneficial for everyone.14 People from South Asia mostly prefer injectable forms of any medicine over tablets based on their cultural belief that injections give faster and better relief.15 This perception aligns with existing literature from Pakistan and other low- and middle-income countries, which shows that regulations are not commonly followed.9

Both doctors and patients who participated in the study shared a common belief that vitamin D has minimal or no side effects. While vitamin D is generally safe when taken within the recommended doses, excessive intake can lead to hypercalcaemia, kidney damage, and vascular calcification.4 5 The lack of awareness about risks and limited follow-ups can cause significant harm, particularly when high doses are taken for long periods.

This study reveals a crucial systemic gap for clinical guidelines and public health policies governing the use of supplements in Pakistan. Doctors reported a lack of standard dosing or follow-up protocols; on the other hand, patients had poor knowledge of standard dosages or potential side effects of these supplements. All these factors contribute to an extensive and inappropriate use of vitamin D supplements in Pakistan. However, this qualitative research has some restrictions. The study was based on a small purposive sample drawn from Lahore, which limits generalisability to larger groups. Since data were self-reported, recall and social desirability bias are risks, particularly for overuse and self-medication. It is important to note that this study does not provide clinical or biochemical confirmation of vitamin D deficiency or inappropriate use, as laboratory evidence of vitamin D levels was not included. The findings are based on self-reported practices and perceptions, and therefore reflect behavioural patterns rather than objectively verified overuse. Furthermore, the health and cultural context of Lahore may have influenced participants’ opinions, and the researcher’s presence throughout the interviews could have introduced bias.

National health authorities need to implement a multifaceted initiative encompassing education, policy and clinical oversight to curb unregulated use. The Ministry of Health and medical boards need to develop national guidelines for screening, dosing and follow-up, and to incorporate current, evidence-based information on supplementation and its hazards into curricula for medicine and pharmacy studies. There should be ongoing professional education to encourage diagnostic-prescribing, and laws should compel the dispensing of high-dose preparations, particularly injections, only on a valid prescription. Public education and counselling by medical professionals should be encouraged to promote safe use.

Conclusion

The study’s findings highlight a strong perception of high-dose vitamin D availability and potentially inappropriate use, underscoring the need for nationwide action through education, regulation and public awareness. Common actions by health organisations, clinicians and pharmacists can ensure safe supplementation and prevent long-term adverse health effects.
