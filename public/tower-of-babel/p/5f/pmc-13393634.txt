A xenogeneic developmental matrix hydrogel with MnO2-gallic acid enables spatiotemporal immunometabolic reprogramming and endochondral bone repair in steroid-associated osteonecrosis
Authors: Yi-Wei Chen, Jia-Li Lin, Nai-Sheng Zhai, Shi-Hao Zhou, Jia-Peng Li, Jian-Cuo A, Yu-Wei Ge, Hui Sun, Lin-Yuan Shu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSteroid-associated osteonecrosis (SON) is characterized by a refractory bone defect microenvironment involving oxidative stress, persistent inflammation, and impaired vascularization, which limits the efficacy of conventional graft materials. Here, we developed a temporally programmable composite hydrogel, GelMA/dECM@MnO2-GA, by integrating human induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived decellularized extracellular matrix (iPSC-dECM) with an MnO2-gallic acid (GA) reactive oxygen species (ROS)-responsive module. Following implantation, elevated ROS may induce the release of MnO2 and GA, which was associated with ROS scavenging, alleviation of local hypoxia, and attenuation of inflammatory signaling. This early redox modulation was accompanied by a shift in macrophage polarization from M1 toward the M2 phenotype, together with increasing regulatory T cell (Treg) enrichment. Our findings further suggest that CCL22-mediated Treg recruitment via the CCR4-PI3K/AKT axis may contribute to Treg survival and migration, reinforce M2 polarization, and help sustain a pro-regenerative osteoimmune microenvironment. At later stages, the retained iPSC-dECM served as a developmental matrix template to guide host progenitor cells through an endochondral ossification-like process, while enhanced H-type vessel formation was associated with coupled angiogenesis and osteogenesis. In a rat steroid-induced femoral defect model, GelMA/dECM@MnO2-GA significantly promoted bone regeneration and reduced the local expression of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α. Transcriptomic and functional analyses supported an association between GelMA/dECM@MnO2-GA treatment, coordinated M2 macrophage polarization, Treg enrichment, and improved bone repair. Collectively, these findings suggest that this spatiotemporally orchestrated hydrogel may provide a promising strategy for overcoming the hostile microenvironment of steroid-associated bone defects, with potential relevance to other refractory osteonecrotic conditions.Keywords: Induced pluripotent stem cells, Decellularized extracellular matrix, Bone regeneration, Endochondral ossification

Introduction

Steroid-associated osteonecrosis (SON) is a frequent and clinically intractable complication of prolonged or high-dose corticosteroid therapy, representing a major cause of non-traumatic osteonecrosis [[1], [2], [3]]. Among its manifestations, steroid-associated osteonecrosis of the femoral head (SONFH) is the most prevalent. To avert femoral head collapse and preserve hip function, various hip-preserving interventions have been developed, including core decompression, osteotomy, and bone grafting strategies such as non-vascularized or vascularized free fibular grafting [[4], [5], [6]]. These procedures typically involve the implantation of cancellous bone or bone substitutes around the grafted fibula to enhance osseointegration and obliterate residual voids. Although such techniques may improve mechanical stability and local perfusion, they inevitably require necrotic tissue debridement, decompression drilling, or tunnel creation. Their long-term efficacy therefore depends on sustained osseointegration within a biologically compromised microenvironment [7]. Autologous bone grafting remains the benchmark, yet its application is limited by donor-site morbidity, and current substitutes do not recapitulate its regenerative capacity [[8], [9], [10]]. Accordingly, effective repair likely requires a dynamic and temporally orchestrated strategy capable of reshaping the local immune and metabolic milieu [11,12].

Such a strategy should enable early scavenging of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and promote macrophage polarization from a pro-inflammatory (M1) phenotype toward a pro-reparative (M2) phenotype, with potential involvement of regulatory T cells (Treg) in reinforcing this transition [[13], [14], [15]]. In SONFH, ROS are not merely a general marker of oxidative stress but appear to contribute to tissue injury through multiple interconnected pathways. Excessive ROS may suppress osteogenic differentiation of mesenchymal progenitors by inhibiting Wnt/β-catenin and PI3K/AKT signaling, while also promoting apoptosis of osteoblasts and endothelial cells. In the vascular compartment, ROS may impair endothelial barrier function, reduce nitric oxide bioavailability, and disrupt angiogenesis-osteogenesis coupling. In addition, ROS can activate NF-κB signaling in macrophages and osteoprogenitor cells, thereby favoring a pro-inflammatory immune state and further aggravating bone loss and vascular injury [16,17]. Together, these observations suggest that strategies capable of directly scavenging ROS and reprogramming immune-metabolic crosstalk may be critical for reversing the hostile microenvironment in SONFH. Resolution of inflammation is likely a prerequisite for subsequent osteogenesis and angiogenesis, thereby supporting functional bone restoration [18]. Recent studies have further underscored the potential of multifunctional and hierarchically designed biomaterials to orchestrate the spatiotemporal microenvironment required for complex tissue repair [[19], [20], [21], [22], [23], [24]].

Human induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) represent a versatile cell source because of their unlimited self-renewal capacity and multilineage differentiation potential [25,26]. In bone regeneration, developmental engineering seeks to recapitulate the native endochondral ossification (ECO) cascade to mimic physiological repair [27]. Our previous studies showed that human iPSCs can be directed toward mesenchymal progenitor cells (MPCs) and, through sequential chondrogenic and hypertrophic maturation, generate engineered hypertrophic cartilage tissues that repair rat femoral defects, thereby establishing a cross-species regenerative paradigm [28]. These findings suggest that human cell-derived developmental matrices may preserve evolutionarily conserved osteoimmune cues that can be interpreted by host tissue across species barriers. Building on this concept, we generated an iPSC-dECM from such constructs. As a result, it provides spatial and biochemical cues that resemble a transitional ECO template and may direct host progenitor cells toward bone formation through a cartilage intermediate. Importantly, decellularization reduces immunogenicity [29,30] while preserving the instructive potential of the human-derived ECM, thereby enabling host immune and progenitor cells to interpret its developmental cues. This process may contribute to a repair-associated inflammatory milieu characterized by M2 macrophage predominance and Treg involvement, thereby supporting a pro-remodeling immune environment.

To address the acidic and ROS-rich microenvironment characteristic of early SON lesions [31,32], we engineered a ROS-responsive hydrogel for sequential delivery of therapeutic components. Gallic acid (GA) was first encapsulated with MnO2 nanoparticles to form an antioxidant MnO2-GA module, which was subsequently integrated into a gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA)-based hydrogel together with iPSC-dECM. This stepwise assembly yielded a tripartite architecture in which the MnO2-GA module was embedded within the hydrogel matrix and iPSC-dECM was distributed throughout the scaffold, collectively creating a bioactive microenvironment that recapitulates key features of the developmental niche. During the initial inflammatory phase, elevated local H2O2 may trigger hydrogel destabilization and accelerate release of MnO2 and GA. MnO2 can catalytically decompose H2O2 into molecular oxygen and water, thereby reducing oxidative stress and hypoxia; its degradation also releases Mn2+ ions, which have been reported to promote osteoblast function and suppress osteoclast activity [33]. GA, a polyphenolic compound, may further quench ROS, and it has been reported to exert anti-inflammatory and pro-osteogenic effects, potentially involving regulation of the Nrf2/NF-κB signaling axis [34,35]. Collectively, these early events may alleviate oxidative stress, favor M1-to-M2 macrophage repolarization, and facilitate Treg recruitment, thereby establishing a pro-regenerative niche primed for repair. In the subsequent phase, the retained iPSC-dECM may provide structural and biomechanical cues that guide host cell behavior through an endochondral ossification-like program, ultimately supporting spatially organized bone formation.

Here, we present a multifunctional composite in which a MnO2-GA antioxidant nanomodule and human iPSC-derived dECM are hierarchically integrated within a ROS-responsive GelMA hydrogel to support temporally coordinated repair of SON defects (Scheme 1). In the early phase, ROS-triggered release of MnO2 and GA may reduce reactive oxygen species, improve local oxygenation, and suppress inflammatory signaling.

Schematic illustration of the multifunctional hydrogel GelMA/dECM@MnO2-GA, constructed by integrating iPSC-dECM into a ROS-responsive GelMA network containing MnO2 nanoparticles and GA. Following implantation into a rat SON bone defect, the hydrogel may reduce ROS levels and support bone repair with features of an endochondral ossification-like process, while modulating the local immune microenvironment. Hydrogel treatment was accompanied by macrophage polarization toward the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype. Elevated CCL22 expression and increased Treg enrichment were observed, suggesting that CCL22/CCR4 signaling and the PI3K/AKT pathway may be involved in Treg recruitment, survival, and migration. In parallel, the hydrogel was associated with enhanced formation of pro-osteogenic H-type vessels. Collectively, these findings suggest the establishment of a pro-regenerative niche associated with improved bone reconstruction and neovascularization.

Subsequently, the iPSC-dECM may serve as a xenogeneic but biologically instructive developmental template, guiding rat host cells to regenerate bone through a cartilage intermediate. By integrating a human stem-cell derived matrix with a rodent host system, this approach establishes a cross-species regenerative framework that bridges developmental biology and translational tissue engineering. By embedding temporal logic into its architecture, this platform couples early immunometabolic intervention with subsequent developmentally inspired bone reconstruction and may offer a multidimensional strategy for steroid-associated refractory bone defects.

Results

SONFH is characterized by systemic inflammation, impaired osteogenesis, and reduced formation of H-type vessels

To elucidate the pathophysiological hallmarks of SONFH, we initially interrogated the NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus (GSE123568), comparing peripheral serum gene expression profiles between SONFH patients and healthy controls. Differential expression analysis, visualized as a volcano plot and heatmap, revealed a distinct set of significantly dysregulated genes (Fig. 1A and B). Notably, hierarchical clustering demonstrated pronounced upregulation of inflammation-related transcripts, including IL1B, IL6R, TLR2, and TLR4 (Fig. 1B). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis corroborated their significant overrepresentation in inflammatory cascades, pointing to a systemic pro-inflammatory signature inherent to SONFH (Fig. 1C). To directly juxtapose this molecular phenotype with that of a major non-inflammatory etiology, we next analyzed femoral head specimens obtained from total hip arthroplasty, 30 cases each of SONFH and traumatic osteonecrosis of the femoral head (TONFH) (Fig. 1D). Gross inspection and micro-CT-based three-dimensional reconstruction of the reactive interface uncovered distinct morphological features, with quantitative micro-CT parameters indicating compromised trabecular microarchitecture in SONFH (Fig. 1E and F). Histomorphometric assessment using Goldner's trichrome staining and subsequent quantification revealed markedly attenuated osteogenic activity in SONFH relative to TONFH (Fig. 1G and H). Immunofluorescence staining further showed substantially elevated iNOS protein expression at the reparative front in SONFH (Fig. 1I and J). To evaluate the angiogenic status accompanying osteogenesis, we performed immunostaining for H-type vessels, a specialized capillary subtype defined by CD31 and EMCN co-expression that plays pivotal roles in coupling angiogenesis with osteogenesis during bone repair [36,37]. SONFH exhibited a significant reduction in H-type vessel formation relative to TONFH (Fig. 1K and L). Consistent with this pro-inflammatory tissue milieu, multiplex cytokine profiling of matched serum and synovial fluid samples disclosed significantly higher concentrations of IL-6 and IL-1β in SONFH patients than in their TONFH counterparts (Fig. 1M and N). Taken together, these findings demonstrate that SONFH is associated with a systemic inflammatory state and, compared with TONFH, is characterized by more severe inhibition of osteogenesis, greater impairment of osteogenesis-coupled angiogenesis, and a more pronounced local inflammatory microenvironment.

Characterization and property of GelMA/dECM@MnO2-GA

The morphologies of GelMA/dECM@MnO2-GA composite and scaffold were examined. Scanning electron microscope (SEM) imaging revealed that the GelMA scaffold exhibited a typical layered porous architecture, characterized by continuous and smooth pore walls and interconnected, sheet-like stacked channels, with a regular overall structure and uniform pore distribution (Fig. 2A). Following incorporation of iPSC-dECM, the layered structure was maintained; however, the pore walls became wrinkled and rough, and the channels more tortuous, thereby increasing structural complexity while retaining porous interconnectivity. Quantitative SEM analysis demonstrated a progressive reduction in pore size and porosity from the GelMA scaffold to the GelMA/dECM, GelMA/dECM@MnO2, and GelMA/dECM@MnO2-GA groups; whereas the overall layered porous architecture remained well preserved (Fig. S1). SEM and transmission electron microscope (TEM) images further confirmed that the MnO2 nanoparticles possessed a flower-like morphology, good crystallinity, uniform size, and a diameter of approximately 100 nm (Fig. 2B). The nitrogen adsorption-desorption isotherm of MnO2 nanoparticles exhibited a typical type IV isotherm with an H3 hysteresis loop, indicative of a mesoporous structure (2-50 nm) (Fig. 2C). The pore size distribution curve showed that the pore sizes were mainly concentrated in the 2-10 nm range, along with a few macropores, indicating a rich mesoporous architecture. Nanoparticles were observed adhering to the pore wall surfaces, and MnO2 was uniformly dispersed on the scaffold skeleton without significant disruption of the original layered pore architecture (Fig. 2D). Furthermore, the high adsorption capacity (close to 60 mmol/g) suggested a large specific surface area and pore volume, providing a structural basis for subsequent gallic acid (GA) loading. Elemental mapping was then performed for the dECM group and the GelMA/dECM@MnO2-GA group (Fig. 2E and F). In the EDS spectra of the dECM group, only characteristic peaks of C, N, O, and P were observed, with no Mn signal, confirming that this group was not loaded with MnO2 and served as a pure dECM component control. In contrast, the EDS spectra of the GelMA/dECM@MnO2-GA group revealed a distinct Mn characteristic peak (at approximately 6 keV) alongside retained C, N, O, and P peaks, further confirming successful MnO2 incorporation. Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy exhibited characteristic peaks corresponding to amide I, II, and III at 1630 cm−1, 1546 cm−1, and 1236 cm−1, respectively [38] (Fig. 2G). Notably, the characteristic peaks associated with MnO2 and GA were not distinctly observable in the spectra of GelMA/dECM@MnO2 and GelMA/dECM@MnO2-GA, likely due to spectral overlap with the GelMA/dECM background. To further confirm the successful incorporation of MnO2 and GA, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis was conducted. The Mn 2p, C 1s, and O 1s spectra provided further evidence for the successful incorporation of MnO2 and GA and indicated their interactions with the GelMA/dECM matrix (Fig. S2). As shown in Fig. 2H, distinct diffraction peaks at 2θ = 28.78° and 56.44° were indexed to the (310) and (600) crystal planes, respectively, in good agreement with the standard card (JCPDS No. 44-0141) [39]. Drug release studies demonstrated that approximately 60% of GA was released within the first 24 h, followed by a sustained release phase thereafter (Fig. 2I). Furthermore, ICP-MS analysis revealed that Mn2+ release from GelMA/dECM@MnO2-GA increased in an H2O2 concentration-dependent manner over 144 h under 0.01, 0.1, and 1 mM H2O2, further confirming the ROS-responsive and temporally programmable behavior of the MnO2-GA module (Fig. S3). In addition, the phenotypic characterization and multilineage differentiation potential of iPSC-derived MPCs are presented in Fig. S4 and S5.

GelMA/dECM@MnO2-GA confers cytoprotection and enhances angiogenic function under oxidative stress

To assess whether the composite hydrogel shields cells from oxidative insult, we co-cultured rat bone marrow mesenchymal stromal cells (rBMSCs) and human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) with four hydrogel formulations, GelMA, GelMA/dECM, GelMA/dECM@MnO2, and GelMA/dECM@MnO2-GA, and subsequently exposed them to H2O2 to recapitulate a pathological oxidative microenvironment (Fig. 3A). Biocompatibility, antioxidant capacity, and functional protection were systematically evaluated. Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assays showed comparable viability across all hydrogel groups and the control (Fig. 3B). Further hemocompatibility evaluation demonstrated that the hemolysis rates of the four hydrogels were 0.54%, 0.49%, 0.41%, and 0.50%, respectively, all of which were below the accepted 5% threshold and comparable to that of the saline control. Moreover, analyses of prothrombin time (PT) and activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT) showed no significant differences among the four groups, indicating that neither the hydrogel nor their composites elicited detectable adverse effects on coagulation function (Fig. S6). Live/dead staining and quantification confirmed the non-cytotoxicity of all formulations (Fig. 3C and D). After 24 h of H2O2 challenge, intracellular ROS detection in rBMSCs revealed that both GelMA/dECM@MnO2 and GelMA/dECM@MnO2-GA most efficiently scavenged excess ROS (Fig. 3E and F). Functional assays demonstrated that under oxidative stress, the GelMA/dECM@MnO2-GA group sustained the highest HUVEC migratory capacity, as determined by wound healing (Fig. 3G and H) and Transwell assays (Fig. 3I and J). Moreover, in vitro tube formation assays quantified a marked increase in capillary-like structures in the GelMA/dECM@MnO2-GA group (Fig. 3K and L). Collectively, these in vitro data establish that the GelMA/dECM@MnO2-GA composite possesses excellent biocompatibility, potent ROS-scavenging activity, and the capacity to preserve endothelial migration and angiogenic function under oxidative stress.

GelMA/dECM@MnO2-GA promotes in vitro endochondral ossification of rBMSCs

To assess the therapeutic potential of composite hydrogels for steroid-induced bone defects, we evaluated their capacity to induce osteogenic differentiation in rBMSCs. rBMSCs were assigned to five groups: a control group cultured in osteogenic induction medium alone, a GelMA group treated with GelMA hydrogel, a GelMA/dECM group, a GelMA/dECM@MnO2 group, and a GelMA/dECM@MnO2-GA group treated with corresponding hydrogels. Osteogenic activity was assessed by alkaline phosphatase (ALP) staining as an indicator of early osteogenic differentiation, Alizarin Red S (ARS) staining for matrix mineralization and calcium deposition, as well as immunofluorescence staining and gene expression analyses.

After 7 days of osteogenic induction, ALP staining and quantification showed markedly higher ALP activity in the GelMA/dECM@MnO2-GA group compared with control and GelMA groups (Fig. 4A and B). At day 14, ARS staining and quantification revealed that GelMA/dECM@MnO2-GA treatment resulted in the most extensive calcium deposition, indicative of enhanced matrix mineralization (Fig. 4C and D). Immunofluorescence staining for the osteogenic markers Osterix (OSX; nuclear) and osteopontin (OPN; cytoplasmic), together with fluorescence intensity measurements, confirmed protein expression across all groups, with the strongest signals observed in the GelMA/dECM@MnO2-GA group (Fig. 4E and F). Consistently, qRT-PCR analysis demonstrated that GelMA/dECM@MnO2-GA treatment significantly upregulated genes associated with chondrocyte hypertrophy, including Col10a1 and Ibsp [40,41], as well as key osteogenic genes such as Col1a1, Opn, Osx, and Runx2 in rBMSCs (Fig. 4G and H). Supporting these findings, comparative RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) of chondrocyte sheets cultured with or without hypertrophy priming revealed that hypertrophic conditions markedly induced the expression of genes linked to a chondro-osteoblast-like phenotype (e.g., Col10a1, Ibsp, and Mmp13) and osteogenic differentiation (e.g., Osx, Col1a1, and Col1a2) [42,43]. GO analysis further showed significant enrichment of differentially expressed genes in biological processes related to endochondral ossification, bone development, bone mineralization, and associated cellular functions (Fig. S7). Collectively, these results indicate that the GelMA/dECM@MnO2-GA composite hydrogel potently stimulates osteogenic differentiation in rBMSCs, likely by activating an endochondral ossification-like program. To further validate the specific contribution of the human iPSC-derived dECM, we introduced a non-developmental rat tail type I collagen control within the same GelMA@MnO2-GA matrix. Compared with GelMA/Collagen@MnO2-GA, the GelMA/dECM@MnO2-GA group showed significantly higher expression of Ibsp, Col10a1, Osx, and Opn, indicating that the developmental dECM more effectively promoted endochondral ossification-related differentiation (Fig. S8).

GelMA/dECM@MnO2-GA promotes bone regeneration and neovascularization in vivo

Building on the robust pro-osteogenic effects observed in vitro, we next evaluated the capacity of GelMA/dECM@MnO2-GA to support bone regeneration in vivo. Using a steroid-induced rat femoral defect model, we performed longitudinal assessments via micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) at 2 and 4 weeks post-implantation (Fig. 5A). Reconstructed 2D and 3D images revealed substantially greater bone ingrowth and mineral density at defect sites treated with the composite hydrogel compared to GelMA alone (Fig. 5B and C). Quantitative micro-CT analysis confirmed these observations, showing significantly increased bone volume fraction (BV/TV), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), and trabecular number (Tb.N), along with reduced trabecular separation (Tb.Sp), in the GelMA/dECM@MnO2-GA group at both time points. Cortical bone parameters, including cortical thickness (Ct.Th) and cortical area fraction (Ct.Ar/Tt.Ar), were also significantly increased in the GelMA/dECM@MnO2-GA group, indicating improved cortical bone formation and structural restoration (Fig. 5D). Collectively, these parameters point to the formation of a denser, more structurally mature trabecular network accompanied by enhanced cortical regeneration. Angiography further demonstrated that the composite hydrogel markedly enhanced neovascularization within the defect region at 4 weeks (Fig. 5E and F). Histological evaluation using Goldner's trichrome staining revealed disorganized fibrous tissue in the GelMA group, whereas the GelMA/dECM@MnO2-GA group exhibited a higher proportion of mature lamellar bone (Fig. 6A–D). Immunohistochemical staining for pro-inflammatory cytokines, IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, showed significantly reduced expression at the injury site in the composite group (Fig. 6E and F). In parallel, H&E staining of major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) revealed no histopathological abnormalities, confirming the systemic biocompatibility of the material (Fig. S9). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that GelMA/dECM@MnO2-GA synergistically promotes osteogenesis and angiogenesis in vivo, likely through modulation of the local inflammatory microenvironment, thereby enhancing bone defect repair. Furthermore, we directly compared the regenerative efficacy of the GelMA/dECM@MnO2-GA with that of the collagen-control hydrogel in the same rat defect model. At 4 weeks post-implantation, histological evaluation demonstrated limited bone regeneration in the collagen-control group, with fibrous tissue occupying most of the defect area. In contrast, the GelMA/dECM@MnO2-GA group exhibited abundant mature lamellar bone formation and well-organized osteoid deposition (Fig. S10). Quantitative analysis further demonstrated that the new bone area was significantly greater in the GelMA/dECM@MnO2-GA group than in the collagen-control group, suggesting that the developmentally derived ECM template contributes substantially to robust endochondral repair.

Transcriptomic profiling unveils immunomodulatory and osteogenic mechanisms of GelMA/dECM@MnO2-GA

To investigate the mechanistic basis underlying GelMA/dECM@MnO2-GA-enhanced endochondral ossification, we performed comparative RNA-seq analysis on bone tissue harvested from rat femoral defects. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed close clustering of biological replicates within each group and an overall trend toward separation between the Control and GelMA/dECM@MnO2-GA groups in transcriptomic space (Fig. 7A). Subsequent differential expression analysis identified 416 significantly upregulated genes and 361 significantly downregulated genes in the GelMA/dECM@MnO2-GA group relative to the Control group (Fig. 7B). Hierarchical clustering further revealed distinct transcriptional patterns between the two groups, including elevated expression of osteogenesis-related genes such as Runx2, Bmp4, Col1a1, and Col1a2 in the GelMA/dECM@MnO2-GA group (Fig. 7C). GO enrichment analysis showed significant enrichment of biological processes associated with macrophage activation involved in immune response, regulatory T cell differentiation, and bone mineralization (Fig. 7D). Consistently, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis identified significant enrichment of pathways related to immunoregulation and cell survival, including cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction and the PI3K–AKT signaling pathway (Fig. 7E). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) further supported the immunomodulatory effects of the composite hydrogel, showing suppression of gene sets associated with macrophage activation and the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β and IL-6, together with enrichment of gene sets related to regulatory T cell differentiation (Fig. 7F–H). To directly assess whether the composite hydrogel influences macrophage polarization, we established a co-culture system in which macrophages were cultured with each hydrogel formulation (Fig. 7I). ELISA of conditioned media showed that GelMA/dECM@MnO2-GA significantly reduced secretion of the inflammatory cytokines IL-1β and IL-6, while markedly increasing levels of cytokines associated with M2 macrophage differentiation, including IL-4 and IL-10 (Fig. 7J). Flow cytometric analysis confirmed that GelMA/dECM@MnO2-GA potently promoted macrophage polarization toward the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype and suppressed the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype (Fig. 7K and L). Consistently, qRT-PCR analysis further supported macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype in the GelMA/dECM@MnO2-GA group, as evidenced by decreased expression of the M1-associated genes Nos2 and Il1b and increased expression of the M2-associated genes Mrc1 and Arg1 (Fig. S11). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that GelMA/dECM@MnO2-GA establishes a pro-osteogenic immune niche by attenuating inflammation and promoting M2 macrophage polarization, thereby orchestrating a regenerative microenvironment conducive to endochondral ossification.

GelMA/dECM@MnO2-GA promotes Treg accumulation and differentiation involving the CCL22-CCR4 axis

Guided by transcriptomic evidence implicating Treg involvement in the regenerative response, we next sought to delineate the immunomodulatory mechanisms through which GelMA/dECM@MnO2-GA promotes Treg differentiation in vitro. Rat peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were exposed to conditioned medium (CM) derived from hydrogel co-culture (Fig. 8A). ELISA of the CM revealed significantly elevated levels of the Treg-polarizing cytokines TGF-β1 and IL-2 [44,45] in the GelMA/dECM@MnO2-GA group (Fig. 8B). Given that M2 macrophages secrete CCL22 and regulate Treg behavior through CCR4-dependent signaling pathway [46,47], we measured this chemokine and observed a marked increase in the composite hydrogel group (Fig. 8C). Flow cytometric analysis confirmed a substantial rise in the frequency of CD4+Foxp3+CD25+ Treg following hydrogel co-culture (Fig. 8D–F). As CCL22 specifically binds to the CCR4 receptor on Treg [[48], [49], [50]], protein-protein docking using the HDOCK server was performed, predicting a stable, high-affinity CCL22-CCR4 interaction (Fig. 8G–I). Functional validation via siRNA-mediated CCR4 silencing abolished the hydrogel-induced upregulation of key Treg-associated genes, including Foxp3, Ctla4, Gitr, Il-10, and Tgf-β1 [51,52] (Fig. 8J). Prompted by in vivo transcriptomic data suggesting activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway, a signaling cascade closely linked to Treg proliferation and differentiation, we examined the expression of pathway components by Western blot (Fig. 8K). This analysis demonstrated that co-culture with GelMA/dECM@MnO2-GA activated PI3K/AKT signaling, concomitant with increased protein levels of the downstream effectors FOXP3 and IL-10 (Fig. 8L). Collectively, these findings, including increased Treg accumulation by flow cytometry, elevated levels of CCL22 and Treg-associated cytokines in the conditioned medium, the effect of CCR4 silencing, and activation of PI3K/AKT signaling along with upregulated FOXP3 and IL-10 protein expression, suggest that GelMA/dECM@MnO2-GA hydrogel promotes Treg enrichment via a macrophage-CCL22-CCR4 axis coupled with PI3K/AKT pathway activation. We propose a model in which the hydrogel promotes M2 macrophage polarization, leading to CCL22 secretion and subsequent recruitment of peripheral Tregs. These locally enriched Treg, in turn, secrete factors such as IL-10 that further reinforce M2 polarization, establishing a positive feedback loop between M2 macrophages and Treg that collaboratively sustains an immune microenvironment conducive to osteogenesis [53,54]. Notably, the decline in Treg abundance at later stages does not necessarily indicate a loss of immunoregulatory function [55]. Rather, early Treg recruitment likely serves as a critical initiating event that shifts the local microenvironment from a pro-inflammatory state toward a pro-resolving and reparative state [56]. Once this transition is established, sustained M2 predominance may be maintained by multiple downstream mechanisms, including the self-reinforcing anti-inflammatory activity of M2 macrophages, continued alleviation of oxidative stress by the MnO2-GA module, and progressive improvement of the local regenerative niche supported by the retained iPSC-dECM matrix.

GelMA/dECM@MnO2-GA modulates early immune response and promotes angiogenesis to support endochondral ossification

To validate the immunomodulatory and pro-angiogenic effects of GelMA/dECM@MnO2-GA in vivo, we performed immunofluorescence staining on femoral defect tissues at 2 and 4 weeks post-implantation. At 2 weeks, the composite hydrogel group exhibited a marked reduction in pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages and a significant increase in anti-inflammatory M2 macrophages compared to controls (Fig. 9A and B). This M2-dominant phenotype persisted at the defect site through week 4 (Fig. 9C and D). Concurrent analysis of regulatory T cells revealed that at 2 weeks, the proportion of CD4+FOXP3+ Treg was significantly elevated in the GelMA/dECM@MnO2-GA group, indicating early recruitment and enrichment at the defect site (Fig. 9E and F). By week 4, however, Treg frequencies no longer differed from controls, suggesting their primary functional involvement may occur predominantly during the early inflammatory phase (Fig. 9G and H). To assess the formation of osteogenesis-coupled vasculature, we performed immunostaining for H-type vessels. The GelMA/dECM@MnO2-GA group displayed significantly enhanced CD31+EMCN+ vessel formation relative to controls (Fig. 9I and J). Collectively, these in vivo findings suggest that GelMA/dECM@MnO2-GA promotes early M2 macrophage polarization, Treg accumulation, and enhanced H-type vessel formation, thereby establishing a local microenvironment favorable for endochondral ossification.

Discussion

SON is increasingly recognized as a biologically compromised defect in which aberrant inflammation, oxidative stress, and impaired vascularization collectively disrupt bone regeneration, rendering purely structural grafting approaches insufficient for durable repair [31]. In this study, we engineered a temporally responsive composite hydrogel, GelMA/dECM@MnO2-GA, by integrating an iPSC-derived decellularized extracellular matrix component with a MnO2- and GA-loaded GelMA platform. This design was intended to address the hostile early microenvironment while supporting subsequent endochondral ossification-like reconstruction. Across in vitro and in vivo evaluations, the hydrogel showed a consistent regenerative advantage, manifested by enhanced cytoprotection under oxidative challenge, coordinated immune modulation, improved angiogenic behavior, and accelerated bone repair.

GelMA provides the basic structural framework of the scaffold, forming a layered porous architecture that supports cell adhesion, proliferation, and nutrient transport [57]. GelMA was selected as the in vivo control to specifically assess the incremental regenerative effects conferred by iPSC-dECM and MnO2-GA functionalization. After incorporation of dECM, the scaffold may better mimic the native extracellular matrix microenvironment and thereby improve biocompatibility and cell affinity [55,56]. In addition, the mesoporous structure (2-10 nm) of the flower-like MnO2 nanoparticles, together with the macroscopic layered porous network of the scaffold, creates a hierarchical pore system. This architecture not only supports cell infiltration and mass transport at the macroscopic level but also provides high specific surface area and functional interfaces at the microscopic level, which may facilitate gas exchange and drug loading/release [58,59]. Thus, the hierarchical porous architecture of GelMA/dECM@MnO2-GA is likely an important contributor to its multifunctionality. The macroporous GelMA network permits tissue ingrowth and nutrient diffusion, whereas the mesoporous MnO2 phase provides a reservoir for GA loading and ROS-responsive release [60]. MnO2 can catalytically decompose H2O2 into O2 and H2O, thereby helping to alleviate hypoxia, while GA may further contribute to ROS scavenging. In this way, the design may reconcile the need for open porosity for tissue integration with the need for confined microdomains for controlled release [61,62].

A central finding of this study is that GelMA/dECM@MnO2-GA buffered oxidative stress and preserved the function of both osteoprogenitor and endothelial compartments, two cell populations that are particularly vulnerable in steroid-associated injury. Under H2O2-induced oxidative challenge, the composite hydrogel reduced intracellular ROS in rBMSCs while maintaining HUVEC migration and tube formation capacity, suggesting that early redox correction may help preserve angiogenic competence. This pro-angiogenic effect is likely associated with the combined action of MnO2-GA-mediated ROS attenuation and the bioactive support provided by iPSC-dECM. Specifically, MnO2-GA may improve endothelial performance by alleviating oxidative stress, whereas iPSC-dECM may provide a permissive microenvironment for endothelial migration and vascular organization. Consistent with these in vitro observations, the femoral defect model showed superior structural regeneration in the composite group compared with GelMA controls, including increased BV/TV, Tb.Th, Tb.N, Ct.Th, and Ct.Ar/Tt.Ar, together with reduced Tb.Sp and enhanced vascularization on micro-CT angiography. Rather than indicating a single linear mechanism, these benefits are more likely associated with a coordinated program involving immune modulation, vascular support, and osteogenic activation [53,63,64].

Transcriptomic profiling of defect tissues further supported this interpretation. RNA-seq analysis revealed enrichment of osteogenesis-related signatures, including RUNX-associated programs, together with immune processes related to macrophage responses and regulatory T cell differentiation, while pro-inflammatory cytokine programs involving IL-1β and IL-6 were suppressed. These transcriptional trends were consistent with tissue-level validation showing reduced IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α expression in the composite hydrogel group. In addition, our in vitro qRT-PCR findings were generally consistent with the RNA-seq results, providing complementary support for the involvement of osteogenic and immunomodulatory programs in the regenerative effects of GelMA/dECM@MnO2-GA. Collectively, these findings support an association between composite hydrogel treatment and immune reprogramming toward a reparative state characterized by early Treg enrichment and sustained M2 macrophage predominance, in line with the recognized importance of inflammatory resolution during bone healing.

The observation that GelMA/dECM@MnO2-GA was associated with enhanced Treg enrichment and M2 polarization provides a plausible link between early microenvironment correction and subsequent tissue rebuilding. In our in vitro studies, the composite hydrogel promoted macrophage polarization toward an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype. These M2-like macrophages exhibited increased CCL22 expression, suggesting a potential mechanism for Treg recruitment through CCR4 signaling [46,65]. Our findings further suggest that this process may involve the PI3K/AKT pathway, which has been implicated in Treg survival, activation, and migration [66,67]. In this context, increased Treg accumulation at the defect site may contribute to a more anti-inflammatory local milieu, potentially through cytokines such as IL-10 [68,69]. Such changes could in turn reinforce macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype, thereby forming a reciprocal immunoregulatory circuit favorable for tissue repair. Importantly, although pathway analysis indicated involvement of the PI3K/AKT axis in PBMCs exposed to GelMA/dECM@MnO2-GA, the present data do not establish a definitive causal sequence in vivo. Therefore, we interpret the CCL22/CCR4/PI3K/AKT-associated axis as a supported but not fully resolved mechanism underlying Treg enrichment and immune remodeling.

Immunofluorescence analysis also showed enhanced formation of H-type vessels in the composite hydrogel group, consistent with improved angiogenesis-osteogenesis coupling. At the same time, the local immune landscape was characterized by early Treg enrichment at week 2 and a sustained shift toward M2 macrophage predominance through week 4. Together, these findings suggest that immune resolution and vascular specialization are closely associated with the observed regenerative response and may jointly support an endochondral ossification-like mode of bone repair.

The finding that GelMA/dECM@MnO2-GA promoted osteogenic differentiation of rBMSCs and supported cartilage-related transitional features is particularly relevant in the steroid-impaired niche. ECO is a developmental cascade in which mesenchymal progenitors first form cartilage, subsequently undergo hypertrophy, and are ultimately replaced by bone [70]. This process is increasingly being leveraged in bone regeneration because it naturally couples angiogenesis with osteogenesis [71]. In our study, the composite hydrogel did not simply function as an antioxidant depot; rather, the retained iPSC-dECM appeared to provide a developmental matrix context that may guide host progenitors through an ECO-like route. This interpretation is supported by the known composition of the matrix, which retains a three-dimensional architecture enriched in collagens, glycosaminoglycans, and partially mineralized deposits, and may sequester angiogenic factors with high affinity, thereby reproducing key aspects of the transitional microenvironment of ECO [72,73]. In the setting of steroid-induced injury, such a template may be especially advantageous because it provides developmental guidance at a stage when the local niche is otherwise unfavorable for direct bone regeneration.

From a materials and developmental engineering perspective, the composite strategy offers complementary and temporally coordinated functions. MnO2 and GA primarily target early oxidative and inflammatory stress, which represent major barriers in steroid-associated lesions, whereas iPSC-dECM may provide an instructive template for the subsequent cartilage-to-bone transition. Decellularization is expected to reduce immunogenicity while preserving matrix-bound developmental cues [29,74]. Our in vivo observations are consistent with the possibility that this matrix contributes to a repair-associated immune environment characterized by M2 macrophage predominance and Treg involvement, although the relative contribution of matrix composition versus redox correction remains to be further dissected. Thus, the superior performance of the composite hydrogel over simpler formulations across vascular and osteogenic endpoints is likely attributable to the synergy of these modules, underscoring that successful repair in this setting depends not only on scaffold architecture but also on spatiotemporally coordinated microenvironment reprogramming.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although the rat femoral defect model captures important aspects of steroid-associated bone injury, it does not fully recapitulate the biomechanical complexity of human osteonecrosis [75,76]. Moreover, although the high-dose steroid protocol used in this study does not fully mimic the chronic and heterogeneous clinical exposure pattern observed in patients with SONFH, it remains a well-established model for reproducing key early pathological features of the hostile osteonecrotic microenvironment, including high oxidative stress, inflammatory activation, endothelial dysfunction, immune dysregulation and defective bone regeneration. Future studies using orthotopic models that better reflect femoral head loading and collapse risk will be important. Second, although our data support the involvement of the CCL22-CCR4 axis in Treg accumulation, the present study does not distinguish whether these Tregs are predominantly recruited from the periphery or expanded locally in situ. This question will require dedicated in vivo cell-tracing approaches. Third, the current in vivo evaluation was limited to 2- and 4-week time points. While these time points are suitable for assessing early immune modulation, angiogenesis, and initial bone regeneration, they do not fully capture later processes such as complete endochondral ossification, cartilage-to-bone transition, or long-term scaffold degradation. Future studies with extended follow-up periods (e.g., 8-12 weeks) will be needed to more comprehensively evaluate the long-term fate of the scaffold and the completion of the endochondral bone repair process, including cartilage remodeling and mature bone formation. Fourth, while our results support an association between composite hydrogel treatment and ECO-like repair, the exact cellular lineage progression and the relative contribution of host versus matrix-derived cues remain to be further resolved. Finally, translational potential will depend on scalable and reproducible iPSC-dECM manufacturing, strict batch consistency, controlled degradation kinetics, comprehensive long-term biosafety evaluation, and extended in vivo follow-up [[77], [78], [79]].

Conclusion

In summary, this work presents a regenerative strategy for steroid-associated bone defects based on a temporally programmable composite hydrogel that combines early redox and inflammatory modulation with a developmentally inspired matrix template. Our findings show that GelMA/dECM@MnO2-GA was associated with reduced oxidative stress, attenuation of inflammatory responses, early Treg enrichment, sustained M2 macrophage predominance, enhanced angiogenesis including H-type vessel formation, and improved endochondral ossification-like bone repair. By integrating microenvironmental regulation with developmental engineering principles in a single platform, GelMA/dECM@MnO2-GA may represent a promising approach for improving healing in refractory steroid-associated bone defects.

Materials and methods

Culture of Human iPSCs

Human induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs; Cellapy, CA4025106) were generated from PBMCs via Sendai virus-mediated reprogramming. The iPSCs were maintained in mTeSR1 medium (Stemcell Technologies) on vitronectin-coated 6-well plates, with daily medium replacement.

Differentiation and characterization of MPCs

Human iPSCs were dissociated using TrypLE and seeded onto culture plates pre-coated with 1% vitronectin. To direct differentiation toward MPC fate, a staged chondrogenic induction protocol was applied. From days 1 to 2, cells were cultured in DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with B27, 25 ng/mL WNT3A, 50 ng/mL activin-A, and 20 ng/mL FGF2. During days 3-4, the medium was replaced with DMEM/F12 containing B27, 2 μM SB431542, 25 ng/mL WNT3A, 20 ng/mL FGF2, and 20 ng/mL noggin. From days 5 to 6, cells were maintained in DMEM/F12 with B27, 2 μM SB431542, 20 ng/mL noggin, and 1 μM Wnt-C59. Finally, on days 7-8, differentiation was completed in DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with B27, 20 ng/mL SHH, and 1 μM WNT-C59. The resulting MPCs were subsequently expanded for at least five passages in a dedicated mesenchymal stem cell culture medium to ensure phenotypic stability prior to functional assays.

For immunophenotypic validation by flow cytometry, 1 × 106 cells were incubated with fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-conjugated monoclonal antibodies against a panel of surface markers: CD44, CD73, CD90, and CD105 (positive markers), as well as CD11b, CD19, CD34, CD45, and HLA-DR (negative markers). All antibodies were used at 1:100 dilution. Parallel samples stained with corresponding isotype-matched antibodies served as controls for nonspecific binding.

Generation of iPSC-Derived chondrocyte sheets

Chondrocyte sheets were generated by seeding MPCs in 10-cm culture plates at a density of 3 × 106 cells/mL and maintaining them in chondrogenic medium for 20–30 days. Two experimental groups were established: the iCDCH group was cultured in chondrogenic medium supplemented with 5% (v/v) human platelet lysate (HPL; UltraGRO-Advanced, Helios, HPCFDCRL50), while the iCDC group received the same base medium without HPL. The base chondrogenic medium consisted of α-MEM supplemented with TGF-β3, dexamethasone, ascorbate-2-phosphate, L-proline, and ITS + premix. The HPL used in the iCDCH group contained the following growth-factor concentrations: TGF-β1, 128.5 ng/mL; PDGF-AB/BB, 67.8 ng/mL; VEGF, 0.85 ng/mL; and IGF-1, 42.3 ng/mL.

Decellularization of iPSC-derived chondrocyte sheets to generate extracellular matrix scaffolds

After chondrogenic induction, the resulting chondrocyte sheets were harvested and decellularized using a tailored protocol to generate an immunogen-reduced extracellular matrix (ECM) scaffold. A gentle, multi-step procedure was employed to maximize cellular removal while preserving the delicate architecture and bioactivity of the iPSC-derived ECM. First, the sheets underwent osmotic lysis in ultrapure water, followed by three freeze-thaw cycles (−80 °C to 37 °C). Cellular membranes and debris were then solubilized by incubation in a solution of 1% (v/v) Triton X-100 and 0.25% (w/v) sodium deoxycholate under gentle agitation at 4 °C for 12 h. To eliminate residual genetic material, a critical consideration for iPSC-derived tissues, the constructs were treated with 100 U/mL DNase I and 25 U/mL RNase A at 37 °C for 8 h. After thorough washing, residual telopeptides were optionally digested with 0.1% (w/v) pepsin at 4 °C for 2 h to further reduce immunogenicity. Finally, the acellular ECM scaffolds were lyophilized and sterilized by gamma irradiation, yielding a stable, bioactive matrix ready for subsequent applications.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent (Life Technologies) and quantified with a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from equal amounts of RNA using the PrimeScript RT reagent kit (Takara, Shiga, Japan) according to the manufacturer's instructions. qRT-PCR was performed on an ABI 7900 system with SYBR Green chemistry. Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) was used as the endogenous control for normalization. Gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences are listed in Table S1.

Co-culture of rBMSCs with hydrogels and conditioned medium collection

rBMSCs were seeded at 2.0 × 105 cells/well in 6-well plates or 2.0 × 104 cells/well in 48-well plates. Upon reaching 70-80% confluency, cells were co-cultured with composite hydrogels in osteogenic medium to evaluate the osteogenic differentiation capacity of BMSCs in the presence or absence of the composite hydrogels. The osteogenic medium consisted of high-glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% penicillin-streptomycin, 0.1 μM dexamethasone, 10 mM β-glycerophosphate, and 50 μM L-ascorbic acid.

CCK-8 assay

rBMSCs were seeded in 96-well plates at 1.0 × 104 cells/well in α-MEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. To evaluate long-term cytotoxicity, rBMSCs were co-cultured with hydrogels for 7 days. Cell viability was assessed on days 1, 3, 5 and 7 using the CCK-8 according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Live/dead and Cytoskeleton staining

The HUVECs were co-cultured with the hydrogels. After culturing for 7 days on the hydrogels, live/dead staining was performed. Additionally, after 7 days of co-culture, cells were incubated with calcein acetoxymethyl ester (calcein-AM, green fluorescence) and propidium iodide (PI, red fluorescence). Images were captured using an Olympus IX81 microscope with a UC90 camera.

Intracellular ROS scavenging

rBMSCs were seeded in the lower chamber of a 24-well Transwell system at 4 × 104 cells/well. After 12 h, cells were stimulated with 100 μM H2O2 to induce oxidative stress. A single H2O2 concentration was selected to establish a reproducible in vitro oxidative stress condition that mimics a ROS-rich pathological microenvironment while avoiding excessive acute cytotoxicity [80]. Test scaffolds were placed in the upper chamber; a group with an empty upper chamber served as the oxidative stress control. After 24 h, intracellular ROS levels were measured using the fluorescent probe DCFH-DA, and cell viability was assessed by live/dead staining. For human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs), a scratch wound assay was performed following the same H2O2 and hydrogel treatment scheme.

Transwell assay

HUVECs were seeded into the upper chamber of a 24-well Transwell insert (5 × 104 cells/mL in serum-free medium). The lower chamber was filled with medium containing the test treatments or controls. After 24 h, non-migrated cells on the upper surface were removed with a cotton swab. Cells that had migrated to the lower surface were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with 0.5% crystal violet, and counted under a light microscope (Leica DM6).

In vitro tube formation

HUVECs were seeded onto Matrigel-coated 96-well plates after the respective treatments. Following 6 h of incubation, cells were fixed and stained with rhodamine-phalloidin to visualize actin filaments. Tube networks were imaged by confocal microscopy (Leica), and the number of complete capillary-like structures and branching nodes per well was quantified using ImageJ software.

Isolation and culture of rat BMDMs

Bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) were isolated from male Sprague-Dawley rats in accordance with protocols approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Shanghai Sixth Hospital. Rats were euthanized by intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital (150 mg/kg). Under sterile conditions, bilateral femurs and tibiae were dissected, and the bone marrow was flushed from the medullary cavities using ice-cold PBS containing 2% FBS through a 25-gauge needle. The cell suspension was filtered through a 200-mesh cell strainer and centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 10 min at room temperature. The pellet was resuspended in complete medium supplemented with 20 ng/mL macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF; MedChemExpress, HY-P7386) and seeded into culture dishes. After 24 h, non-adherent cells were removed by washing with PBS. Adherent cells were cultured for an additional 5 days in M-CSF-containing medium, with medium refreshed every 2 days. Differentiated macrophages exhibiting typical morphological features were used for subsequent experiments.

Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) and Alizarin Red S (ARS) staining assays

After 7 days of osteogenic differentiation, rBMSCs were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity was first visualized using the BCIP/NBT Alkaline Phosphatase Colorimetric Kit (Beyotime, C3206), and then quantified with the Alkaline Phosphatase Assay Kit (Beyotime, P0321S) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

For mineralization assessment, cells were cultured under osteogenic conditions for 14 days, fixed, and stained with 2% Alizarin Red S (pH 8.3) for 10 min. After washing with ultrapure water, stained nodules were imaged. Quantitative analysis was performed by dissolving the calcium deposits in 10% hexadecylpyridinium chloride in 0.01 M sodium phosphate, and measuring the absorbance at 570 nm.

Western blot analysis

Proteins were extracted from rat peripheral blood mononuclear cells using RIPA buffer. Equal amounts of protein (50 μg per lane) were separated on 4-20% gradient Tris-glycine gels and transferred onto PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5% non-fat milk in TBST for 1 h at room temperature, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies (all from Affinity): PI3K (AF6241; 1:1000), phospho-PI3K (AF3242; 1:1000), AKT (AF0836; 1:1000), phospho-AKT (AF0016; 1:1000), IL-10 (DF6894; 1:1000), and Foxp3 (AF6544; 1:1000). Membranes were then incubated with HRP-conjugated anti-rabbit or anti-mouse secondary antibodies (1:10,000; Millipore) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence and imaged with a Bio-Rad Image Lab system. Band intensities were quantified in ImageJ, with GAPDH serving as the loading control.

Cytokine quantification by ELISA

Cytokine levels in culture supernatants were measured using commercial ELISA kits according to the manufacturers’ protocols. In LPS-stimulated BMDM-hydrogel cocultures, concentrations of IL-1β, IL-6, IL-4, and IL-10 were determined using eBioscience assays. For supernatants from regulatory cell cultures, IL-2 and TGF-β1 levels were quantified with corresponding eBioscience kits. All assays were performed in technical duplicates, with standard curves included for quantification.

Flow cytometric identification of Treg

Peripheral blood was collected from rats and processed to obtain single-cell suspensions. Cells were stained with fluorescently labeled antibodies against CD4, CD25, and Foxp3 (all from BioLegend). Samples were analyzed on a BD FACSAria III flow cytometer. Tregs were defined as CD4+CD25+Foxp3+ cells, and their frequency was calculated within the CD4+ T-cell population. Data analysis was performed using FlowJo software.

siRNA-mediated CCR4 silencing

Rat PBMCs were seeded in 6-well plates and transfected with Ccr4-specific siRNA or scrambled control siRNA using a lipid-based transfection reagent according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, siRNA-transfection reagent complexes were prepared in Opti-MEM and added to the cells to achieve a final siRNA concentration of 50 nM. After 6 h, the medium was replaced with complete culture medium, and the cells were incubated for an additional 18 h. The transfected PBMCs were then treated with conditioned medium collected from hydrogel-macrophage co-cultures for 24 h. Total RNA was extracted using Trizol reagent, and the expression levels of Ccr4, Foxp3, Ctla4, Gitr, Il-10, and Tgfb1 were analyzed by qRT-PCR as described above. The knockdown efficiency of Ccr4 was verified by qRT-PCR.

Surgical models of femur defect and hydrogels implantation

Healthy adult male Sprague-Dawley rats (8 weeks old, 200-300 g) were used in this study. All animal procedures were approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Shanghai Jiao Tong University Affiliated Sixth People's Hospital. To establish a steroid-associated osteonecrosis model, rats first received two intraperitoneal injections of lipopolysaccharide (LPS; 10 mg/kg) spaced 24 h apart, followed by three consecutive intramuscular injections of methylprednisolone (MPS; Pfizer) administered every 24 h. After modeling, rats were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital (40 mg/kg). Both femurs were surgically exposed, the periosteum was removed, and standardized cortical bone defects (3.0 mm wide × 6.0 mm long) were created using a dental handpiece with a circular bur. The defects were thoroughly irrigated before hydrogel implantation. The periosteum and skin were then closed in separate layers with sutures. Rats were euthanized at 2 or 4 weeks post-surgery. Femoral specimens containing the defect site, along with heart, liver, lung, kidney, spleen, and brain samples, were collected and fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for subsequent analysis.

Histological and immunohistochemistry analysis

For histological evaluation, samples were dehydrated, embedded in a methyl methacrylate-based hard resin, and sectioned to assess new bone formation within the femoral defects. Sections were stained with H&E, Masson's trichrome, and Goldner's trichrome according to standard protocols. For immunohistochemistry, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against IL-1β (Affinity, AF4006; 1:100), IL-6 (Affinity, DF6087; 1:100), or TNF-α (Affinity, AF7014; 1:100). After washing, slides were incubated with appropriate HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Santa Cruz Biotechnology; 1:100), and antibody binding was visualized using a diaminobenzidine (DAB) substrate kit (Sigma). All stained sections were examined under a light microscope.

Immunofluorescence staining

Tissue sections were permeabilized with 0.3% (v/v) Triton X-100 in PBS for 15 min at room temperature and blocked with 5% (v/v) goat serum in PBS for 30 min. Sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against the following targets: iNOS (Affinity, AF0199; 1:100), OPN (Affinity, AF0227; 1:200), OSX (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-393060; 1:200), ARG1 (Affinity, DF6657; 1:200), CD4 (Affinity, DF16080; 1:200), FOXP3 (Affinity, AF6654; 1:200), CD31 (Affinity, AF6191; 1:200), and Endomucin (Affinity, DF13357; 1:200). After three washes with PBS, sections were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with species-matched secondary antibodies: Alexa Fluor 647-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG (Invitrogen, A21244; 1:1000) and Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-mouse IgG (Invitrogen, A11029; 1:1000). Nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; 1:200) for 1 min. Finally, sections were mounted with antifade medium and imaged using a KF-PRO-120/FL whole-slide scanner (Olympus).

H-type vessels were quantified based on double immunofluorescence staining of CD31 and EMCN in bone defect sections. Following deparaffinization, rehydration, antigen retrieval, and blocking, the sections were incubated with anti-CD31 and anti-EMCN primary antibodies, followed by appropriate fluorescent secondary antibodies. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and stained sections were imaged using a confocal microscope with identical imaging settings for all samples.

In this study, H-type vessels were operationally defined as CD31hi EMCNhi vessel-like structures displaying visible tubular and/or luminal morphology, rather than individual double-positive cells. During image analysis, isolated non-luminal double-positive signals and discontinuous staining without evident vascular architecture were excluded. Only intact or structurally recognizable vascular profiles within the defect area were included in the final quantification.

For each sample, several randomly selected non-overlapping fields were analyzed in a blinded fashion using ImageJ software to measure fluorescence intensity or count positively stained cells.

Neovascularization analysis by microfil perfusion and micro-CT

At 4 weeks post-implantation, rats were anesthetized and placed in a supine position. The abdominal aorta and inferior vena cava were carefully dissected, and catheters were inserted into each vessel. After systemic heparinization with 1 mL of heparinized saline (50 U/mL) via the aortic catheter, 10 mL of Microfil contrast agent (Flow Tech) was slowly infused through the same catheter. Both catheters were then clamped to allow complete filling of the hindlimb vasculature. Rats were euthanized and stored at 4 °C overnight. Bilateral femurs were harvested, fixed in 10% formalin for 72 h, and decalcified in 10% EDTA (pH 7.4). Angiographic imaging was performed using a high-resolution micro-CT system (Skyscan 1176, Bruker). Three-dimensional reconstructions were generated with NRecon and CT-Analyzer software, applying a grayscale threshold of 90-255 HU to segment vascular structures.

Micro-CT-based bone morphometry

Femoral specimens were scanned at an isotropic resolution of 18 μm using a micro-CT scanner (Skyscan, Kontich). Three-dimensional reconstructions were analyzed with CT-An software to quantify the following histomorphometric parameters: bone volume fraction (BV/TV), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), trabecular number (Tb.N), trabecular separation (Tb.Sp), cortical thickness (Ct.Th), and cortical area fraction (Ct.Ar/Tt.Ar).

RNA sequencing and bioinformatic analysis

Total RNA was extracted from rat femoral bone using TRIzol reagent (Takara Bio). RNA quality was assessed by NanoDrop 2000 (OD260/280 ≥ 1.8) and agarose gel electrophoresis. Libraries were constructed with the NEBNext Ultra II RNA Library Prep Kit and sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq 4000 platform (2 × 150 bp). Raw reads were trimmed with Trim Galore (v0.6.7) and aligned to the Rattus norvegicus genome (mRatBN7.2) using HISAT2 (v2.2.1). Transcript quantification and differential expression analysis (|log2FC| ≥ 1, adjusted p < 0.05) were performed with StringTie (v2.2.1) and DESeq2 (v1.38.3). Functional enrichment was assessed via GO, KEGG pathway analysis, and GSEA using MSigDB (v7.5.1). Analyses were conducted on the OmicStudio and Bioinformatics online platforms.

Protein-protein docking

The three-dimensional structure of CCL22 was obtained from the Protein Data Bank, while CCR4 was modeled based on UniProt/AlphaFold sequences. Docking was performed using HDOCKlite (v1.1) with default settings. The highest-ranked cluster according to the HDOCK scoring function was selected as the representative complex. Interactions were subsequently analyzed and visualized using PyMOL (v3.1) and Discovery Studio 2019.

Preparation of nano-MnO2 and composite scaffolds

Flower-like MnO2 nanostructures were synthesized via a well-established redox reaction between potassium permanganate (KMnO4) and oleic acid (OA) at an emulsion interface [81]. Briefly, 100 mg of KMnO4 was dissolved in 50 mL of aqueous solution, followed by the addition of 300 μL of chloroform and 700 μL of oleic acid. The mixture was stirred at 45 °C for 24 h. The resulting brownish-black MnO2 precipitate was collected by centrifugation and purified through three consecutive washing cycles with deionized water and ethanol [81]. The as-prepared MnO2 exhibited a negative surface potential, enabling electrostatic adsorption [81]. To prepare MnO2-GA nanoparticles, GA (CAS: 149-91-7, 100 μM) was mixed with the synthesized MnO2 (250 μM) and stirred for 24 h, followed by freeze-drying. The protocol of producing iPSC-ECM has been described previously [82]. For the fabrication of GelMA/dECM scaffolds, decellularized extracellular matrix (dECM, 0.5 g) was added to GelMA (10 g) containing LAP (lithium phenyl-2,4,6-trimethylbenzoylphosphinate), yielding a final dECM concentration of 0.5% (w/v) in the pre-gel solution. The mixture was stirred for 2 h, then rapidly transferred to −40 °C for 2 h, and subsequently to −80 °C for another 2 h. Finally, the sample was freeze-dried. To obtain GelMA/dECM@MnO2-GA composite scaffolds, 1 mL of the GelMA/dECM mixture was combined with 70 μg of MnO2-GA, followed by freeze-drying.

Preparation of rat tail type I collagen control hydrogel

Rat tail type I collagen solution (Corning®, 354236; 3-4 mg/mL in 0.02 N acetic acid) was employed as a non-developmental ECM control. Prior to use, the collagen solution was dialyzed against 0.1% acetic acid for 2 h at 4 °C to replace the original solvent. The collagen concentration was adjusted to 5 mg/mL (0.5%, w/v) with sterile water after dialysis, matching the total protein concentration of the iPSC-dECM hydrogel as determined by BCA assay. Unless otherwise specified, all in vitro and in vivo experiments were performed using the same fabrication and handling procedures for both the iPSC-dECM and collagen-control hydrogels.

Characterization of scaffolds

The morphology and chemical composition of GelMA, GelMA/dECM, GelMA/dECM@MnO2, and GelMA/dECM@MnO2-GA scaffolds were examined using scanning electron microscopy (SEM; JSM-6380LV, JEOL, Japan), transmission electron microscopy (TEM; JEOL2100, Japan), and Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR; Thermo Scientific Nicolet iS20, USA). Phase compositions were determined by X-ray diffraction (XRD; D/max-III C, Japan). The mesoporous structure of the flower-like MnO2 was characterized at 77 K using an AUTOSORB-1-C automatic specific surface area and porosity analyzer (Quantachrome, USA), with pore size distribution analyzed via the Barrett-Joyner-Halenda (BJH) method.

Drug release study

The drug loading and release characteristics of the composite scaffold were evaluated using GelMA/dECM@MnO2-GA constructs (dimensions: 15 mm × 2 mm). Each scaffold was immersed in 5 mL of deionized water as the release medium. At predetermined time points, 1.00 mL of the medium was withdrawn, and an equal volume of fresh deionized water was added to maintain a constant volume. The concentration of released GA was quantified using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC; Agilent 1100, USA). All release experiments were performed in triplicate under identical conditions. The loading efficiency of Mn2+ was quantified by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS; iCAP-TQ, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS)

XPS analysis was conducted using a Thermo Scientific K-Alpha spectrometer with a monochromatic Al Kα source (1486.6 eV). Freeze-dried samples were measured under vacuum, and survey and high-resolution spectra for C 1s, O 1s, and Mn 2p were acquired. The spectra were calibrated to the C 1s peak at 284.8 eV and analyzed by background subtraction and peak fitting.

Hemolysis assay

The concentration of free hemoglobin was quantified using the cyanmethemoglobin method with Drabkin's reagent, which rapidly converts free hemoglobin into a stable cyanmethemoglobin derivative. The absorbance of the resulting solution was measured at 540 nm [83].

Coagulation cascade

Prothrombin Time (PT) and activated Partial Thromboplastin Time (aPTT) were measured to assess the extrinsic and intrinsic coagulation pathways, respectively. Citrated platelet-rich plasma (PRP) was prepared from anticoagulated rat whole blood and incubated with the test materials at 37 °C for 30 min. For PT measurement, 100 μL of PRP was mixed with 100 μL of PT reagent in a cuvette, and the clotting time was determined using a coagulation analyzer. For aPTT measurement, 50 μL of PTT reagent (cephalin) was added to 50 μL of PRP and incubated for 3 min, followed by addition of 50 μL of 0.025 M CaCl2 to initiate clot formation. The clotting time was then recorded as the aPTT value. Reduced PT or aPTT indicated activation of the coagulation cascade by the tested material [84,85].

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). GraphPad Prism 8 was used for statistical testing. Comparisons between two groups were performed with two-tailed Student's t-tests; comparisons among multiple groups were analyzed by one- or two-way ANOVA as appropriate. A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.
