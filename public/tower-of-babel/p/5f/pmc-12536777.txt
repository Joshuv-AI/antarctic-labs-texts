Correlation between LC-MS/MS and ELISA methods for quantitative analysis of desmosine-containing solutions
Authors: Arisa Araki, Mahiro Takano, Christian Nanga Chick, Takumi Yamazaki, Jun Nojima, Toyonobu Usuki
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundDesmosine and isodesmosine are crosslinking amino acids found in extracellular matrix protein elastin, which imparts elasticity to tissues such as those of the lungs and arteries. These compounds are promising biomarkers for diseases involving elastin degradation, such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.Research design and methodThis study examined the correlation between isotope-dilution LC-MS/MS and a newly established ELISA for in vitro diagnosis using a variety of samples.ResultsResults of ELISA and LC-MS/MS analyses exhibited a high correlation coefficient (0.9941). However, whereas the LC-MS/MS measurements deviated approximately 2-fold from the theoretical values, the ELISA measurements ranged from 0.83 to 1.06 (avg 0.94) times the theoretical values. Precise measurement of the absorbance of synthetic desmosine revealed a molar extinction coefficient of 2403, which differed markedly from the previously reported value of 4900 in 1963. Using this value to recalculate the amount of added desmosine, the LC-MS/MS measurements were 0.68 to 0.99 (avg 0.87) times the theoretical values.ConclusionThus, the developed ELISA enables highly accurate determination of desmosine concentrations, comparable to LC-MS/MS, suggesting that ELISA is a potentially useful in vitro diagnostic tool.

Introduction

Elastin is an extracellular matrix protein that imparts elasticity to tissues of the arteries, ligaments, lungs, and other organs. Desmosine and isodesmosine (Figure 1), crosslinking amino acids found in elastin, contain pyridine rings with four substitutions. Desmosine is thought to play an important role in the elasticity of elastin [1]. Partridge and coworkers published the first reports of desmosine and isodesmosine in 1963 [2,3]. The total synthesis of desmosine was achieved in our laboratory [4] via the Sonogashira and Negishi cross-coupling reaction [5–7] or Chichibabin pyridinium synthesis [8,9]. Desmosines are considered potentially useful biomarkers of diseases that involve elastin degradation, such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). COPD is an inflammatory lung disease caused primarily by long-term inhalation of harmful substances such as tobacco smoke [10]. In 2019, COPD was responsible for 3.23 million deaths, making it the third leading cause of death worldwide [11]. COPD is a chronic and progressive disease with no cure, but early detection is important, as the disease symptoms can be managed by smoking cessation and avoidance of air pollution, in conjunction with appropriate treatment to slow progression and alleviate symptoms [12]. A hallmark of COPD progression is the destruction of the alveoli resulting from the breakdown of elastin. Elastin breakdown in turn leads to an increase in the systemic concentrations of elastin degradation products, which are subsequently metabolized and excreted via the blood and sputum.

Desmosine and isodesmosine are specific metabolites of elastin, and thus, research has focused on the determination of their concentrations throughout the body [13–15]. For example, Lin and coworkers developed a method to detect desmosine and isodesmosine in the plasma and urine of COPD patients. Detection of desmosine and isodesmosine was demonstrated through acid hydrolysis of plasma and urine samples from COPD patients, followed by liquid chromatography – mass spectrometry (LC-MS) analysis [16]. Using this method, analysis of plasma and urine samples from healthy individuals and patients with COPD revealed that levels of desmosine and isodesmosine were significantly elevated in patients with these conditions compared with healthy individuals [17].

Liquid chromatography – tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) is useful for the analysis of trace components such as desmosines in biological samples. LC-MS/MS combines high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) with mass spectrometry to isolate and identify target molecules. LC-MS/MS instruments contain a collision cell in which fragmentation of molecules occurs between two mass spectrometers that precisely determine the characteristic mass of each fragment, thus enabling highly selective and precise compound identification. Especially, isotope-dilution method is a powerful tool for precise quantitation using isotopically labeled internal standards [18–20]. However, LC-MS/MS analyses are time-consuming and require expensive, large-scale equipment and instruments.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISAs) are quantitative methods in which target antigens or antibodies in sample solutions are captured using specific antibodies or antigens and detected via enzymatic reactions. ELISA methods offer the advantages of simplicity and the ability to analyze a large number of samples simultaneously. However, traditional ELISA methods provide relatively low selectivity and precision, but these drawbacks have been overcome to some degree due to recent developments in high-selectivity antibody reagents.

The objective of the present study was to determine the correlation between quantitation results obtained using an isotope-dilution LC-MS/MS method established in our laboratory [18–20] and a newly developed antibody reagent – based ELISA by examining multiple samples. The isotope-dilution LC-MS/MS method was evaluated using isodesmosine-13C3,15N1 (Figure 1) [21] synthesized via the Chichibabin pyridinium synthesis. The ELISA was evaluated using a competitive method suitable for quantifying low-molecular-weight antigens. This is the first report to compare the desmosine quantitation directly with LC-MS/MS and ELISA.

Materials and methods

In this study, we analyzed samples prepared by dissolving bovine-derived desmosine, purchased from Elastin Products Company Co., Inc. (EPC) in injectable H2O and three commercially available human serum samples (human serum No. 1, 2, and 3) (Table S1). The concentration of bovine-derived desmosine dissolved in injectable H2O was set by measuring the absorbance using 4900 of molar extinction coefficient value [3]. The solution was then appropriately serially diluted to prepare samples at concentrations of 625, 1250, 2500, and 5000 ng/mL. Bovine-derived desmosine dissolved in injectable H2O was also added to three commercially available human serum samples confirmed as negative for human immunodeficiency virus, hepatitis B virus, and hepatitis C virus to achieve final concentrations of 500 and 5000 ng/mL. The samples prepared as described above as well as control samples were aliquoted into 1 mL volumes in Eppendorf tubes and stored frozen prior to analysis.

For LC-MS/MS analysis, 10 µL of 100 ppm isodesmosine-13C3,15N1 was added as an internal standard to 0.2 mL samples of H2O for injection. To prepare human serum samples, the same amount of internal standard was added, followed by hydrolysis. After hydrolysis, impurities were removed using a cellulose column. After the removal of the solvent, the samples were dissolved in 2 mL of 1-butanol/acetic acid/H2O (4:1:1) and charged onto a cellulose cartridge. The cartridge was washed three times with 3 mL of 1-butanol/acetic acid/H2O (4:1:1), and desmosine was eluted using 3 mL of H2O. The samples were then dried, and the resulting residue was dissolved in 210 µL of distilled H2O and analyzed using isotope-dilution LC-MS/MS under the conditions listed in Table S2. The analyses were performed using a Shimadzu LCMS-8030plus instrument. Each sample was analyzed three times (Figure 2). Figure 2.Hydrolysis and purification of human serum samples.

Hydrolysis and purification of human serum samples.

For the ELISA method, the antibody was newly prepared by an antigen which was synthesized by organic synthesis in our previous work [22]. The antigen was fixed on the plate, and then a competitive assay was performed with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-labeled desmosine and desmosine in sample solution. For the ELISA analysis, samples were diluted appropriately for measurement. Injectable H2O samples were diluted 10-fold prior to analysis. Human serum samples containing added desmosine at 5000 ng/mL were diluted 20-fold and 40-fold, whereas samples containing 0 ng/mL or 500 ng/mL desmosine were diluted 2-fold and 4-fold. Calibration curves were prepared for each microplate, and each sample was analyzed three times.

Results and discussion

In LC-MS/MS analyses, the productions m/z 232.10 and 397.25 (Figure S1) for desmosine, m/z 401.25 (Figure S2) for isodesmosine-13C3,15N1 were selected, respectively. LC chromatographs were shown in Figure S3. Analysis of the calibration curve for desmosine revealed that the R2 value for m/z 397.25 was close to 1.0 (Figure S4). Therefore, m/z 397.25 was selected as the fragment ion for desmosine in this study. The deviation from the theoretical values in the LC-MS/MS analyses of desmosine ranged from 1.69 to 2.43 times (Table S3). By contrast, the coefficient for the correlation between the amount of desmosine added and the measured values was 0.9941, indicating an appropriate correlation.

The calibration curve for the ELISA method exhibited good linearity over the range 5 ng/mL to 500 ng/mL (Tables S4 & S5; Figures S5 & S6). In analyses of human serum, values for samples diluted 4-fold or 40-fold were generally closer to the theoretical values compared with values for samples diluted 2-fold or 20-fold (Table S6); thus, the latter values were adopted. The measurement results were 0.83 to 1.06 (average: 0.94) times the theoretical values (Table S7). Additionally, the coefficient for the correlation between the amount of desmosine added and the measured values was 0.9999, indicating a very strong correlation. The coefficient for the correlation between the ELISA and LC-MS/MS results was 0.9889, demonstrating a high level of agreement between methods.

The deviation from the theoretical values observed in LC-MS/MS analyses was considered to be due to differences in the methods used to calculate the concentration of the standards; these differences were therefore investigated further. The desmosine added to injectable H2O and human serum was purchased from EPC and derived from bovine sources. It is difficult to accurately measure the mass of this desmosine product due to its low crystallinity and high deliquescence. Therefore, the concentration was calculated based on absorbance measurements after dissolution in distilled H2O, according to Lambert-Beer’s law. In the ELISA method, the bovine-derived desmosine solution prepared using this method was diluted and used as a calibration curve sample. By contrast, desmosine synthesized in our laboratory was used for the calibration curve in the LC-MS/MS analyses, and the concentration was set based on mass.

To address this discrepancy, we calculated the molar extinction coefficient using desmosine synthesized and purified via HPLC in our laboratory. At first, the effect of counter anion for the calculation of molar extinction coefficient has been investigated. A comparison of absorbances with three different compounds containing individual counter anions was carried out. 1-Butylpyridinium chloride (6.98 mg), 1-butylpyridinium bromide (2.60 mg), and 1-butylpyridinium hexafluorophosphate (2.35 mg) were used as model compounds for this study. When the calculation was done by molecular weight of only cation, molar extinction coefficients were estimated to be 945–1623, which was not a good agreement (Table S8). In contrast, when the calculation was conducted using molecular weight including counter anion, the molar extinction coefficient showed 1950–2046, which were acceptable values. Therefore, it was concluded that the molar extinction coefficient values should be led by molecular weight of both cation and anion.

Next, we verified the counter anion of synthetic desmosine. During desmosine synthesis, the final deprotection step is carried out using trifluoroacetic acid (TFA), so the synthesized desmosine should be in the form of trifluoroacetate as the counter ion. However, because 0.1% formic acid – H2O and 0.1% formic acid – acetonitrile were used as solvents for HPLC purification, formate could also have been present. To ensure that desmosine was in the trifluoroacetate form, 0.5 mL of TFA and 0.1 mL of H2O were added to the synthesized desmosine, and this process was repeated five times. When 13C-NMR of synthetic desmosine was then measured, no peak derived from FA could be confirmed, and only peaks for TFA were observed at 163.6 and 117.0 ppm (Table S9). The absorbance of a solution of 9.3 mg of desmosine as TFA salt was then measured. The resulting calculated molar extinction coefficient was 2403 with an R2 value of 0.9993, which was different from the value of 4900 (Table S9; Figures S8 & S9) previously reported by Partridge and coworkers in 1963 [3]. Using this molar extinction coefficient (2403), the amount of added desmosine in the samples was recalculated not by desmosine-TFA salt, but by desmosine only (Table S10). Consequently, the LC-MS/MS measurements were 0.68 to 0.99 (average 0.87) times the theoretical values (Table S11; Figure 3). Figure 3.Comparison of ELISA and LC-MS/MS results (DES: desmosine).

Conclusion

The amount of desmosine in liquid samples was measured using isotope-dilution LC-MS/MS and a newly developed competitive ELISA. The results obtained using the LC-MS/MS and ELISA methods exhibited a high degree of correlation. However, although the ELISA results exhibited good agreement with the theoretical values, the LC-MS/MS results were approximately twice the theoretical values. Therefore, the absorbance of synthesized desmosine was measured, and the recalculated molar extinction coefficient of desmosine was revised to 2403. This coefficient value was then used to recalculate the sample concentrations, which showed that the LC-MS/MS results were 0.68 to 0.99 (average 0.87) times the theoretical values, thus demonstrating consistency. The newly developed antibody reagent – based ELISA allows measurement of desmosine concentrations with high accuracy similar to that of LC-MS/MS. These data suggest this ELISA could be useful as an in vitro diagnostic tool for diseases such as COPD. Further studies for LC-MS/MS and ELISA will be conducted using plasma samples of COPD patients in our laboratories.
