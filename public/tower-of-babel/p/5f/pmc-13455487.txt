Comparative evaluation of Hepassocin, DCP, and AFP-L3 for non-invasive assessment of liver fibrosis in Hepatitis B infection
Authors: Dakshina. M. Nair, Leela Kakithakara Vajravelu, Vishnupriya Paneerselvam, Rahul Harikumar Lathakumari, Poornima Baskar Vimala, Jayaprakash Thulukanam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHepatitis B virus (HBV) infection often leads to progressive liver fibrosis, which may remain undetected until irreversible hepatic damage occurs. This study evaluated Hepassocin (HPS), des-gamma-carboxy Prothrombin (DCP) and Lens culinaris agglutinin-reactive fraction of alpha-fetoprotein (AFP-L3) as serological indicators associated with APRI-defined fibrosis risk in HBV infection.MethodsThis observational study included 150 HBsAg-positive patients (acute and chronic HBV) and 50 healthy controls. Serum concentrations of HPS, DCP and AFP-L3 were quantified using ELISA alongside routine liver function tests. Fibrosis risk was evaluated with the AST-to-Platelet Ratio Index (APRI) and classified based on recognized clinical cut-offs. Group comparisons using non-parametric testing, correlations were assessed by Spearman correlation and binary logistic regression was applied to identify independent predictors of severe fibrosis. Diagnostic accuracy was assessed using ROC analysis.ResultsHBV patients exhibited significantly altered liver biochemistry, including elevated liver enzymes, reduced platelet counts, higher APRI scores, and increased serum levels of all three biomarkers compared with controls. HPS and DCP levels increased progressively across APRI-defined fibrosis categories, whereas AFP-L3 showed limited discriminatory ability. Multivariate analysis identified HPS and DCP as biomarkers independently associated with higher APRI-defined fibrosis risk, while AFP-L3 lacked independent predictive value. ROC analysis showed that HPS and DCP demonstrated good discrimination for higher APRI-defined fibrosis risk categories within the study population, and their combined use further improved diagnostic performance. The integrated HPS + DCP model demonstrated the highest discriminatory performance within APRI-based fibrosis risk stratification (AUC = 0.935).ConclusionHPS and DCP are potential non-invasive biomarkers associated with APRI-defined fibrosis risk in HBV infection and showed superior predictive performance compared with AFP-L3 and routine biochemical markers within APRI-based fibrosis stratification. Their combined application may enhance APRI-based fibrosis risk stratification; however, these findings should be considered preliminary and require validation in independent cohorts using accepted fibrosis reference standards before clinical adoption can be considered.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13718-4.

Introduction

Hepatitis B virus (HBV) still affects hundreds of millions of people worldwide and is a major cause of chronic liver disease, fibrosis, cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [1]. Liver fibrosis is a sequence of clinical and physiological processes associated with liver cell necrosis and degeneration after chronic liver damage, ultimately resulting in extracellular matrix and collagen deposition [2]. Early identification of individuals at risk of significant fibrosis is crucial because fibrosis often progresses silently until irreversible liver damage occurs. Conventional liver function tests (LFTs) have limited utility for fibrosis assessment because they mainly reflect ongoing inflammation rather than long-term structural damage.

Liver biopsy is the conventional gold standard [3] for evaluating liver fibrosis nevertheless, its clinical applicability is limited by invasiveness, elevated costs, sample variability and inadequate practicality for repeated assessments [4]. Imaging modalities including ultrasound, CT and MRI provide non-invasive evaluation, but they are limited by operator reliance, restricted specificity and the need for sophisticated equipment and experience [5, 6]. Non-invasive indices such as the Aspartate Aminotransferase-to-Platelet Ratio Index (APRI) have been increasingly used due to their simplicity and accessibility [7]. However, using APRI cut-offs may help to identify clinically significant changes in fibrosis risk.

To improve the accuracy of fibrosis risk assessment, incorporation of molecular biomarkers has been proposed. Hepassocin (HPS)/Fibrinogen-like protein 1 (FGL1) is a hepatocyte-derived protein involved in liver regeneration, metabolic regulation and hepatic stress responses [8, 9]. Evidence suggests that HPS expression increases during liver injury and may exert protective effects through regulation of endoplasmic reticulum stress and hepatocyte survival [10, 11], supporting its potential role as a biomarker of hepatic damage. Des-gamma-carboxy prothrombin (DCP), commonly referred to as Protein Induced by Vitamin K Absence or Antagonist-II (PIVKA-II), is widely used as a biomarker for HCC and reflects altered hepatic synthetic function [12]. DCP’s efficacy in HCC detection is widely recognized, its potential as a biomarker for fibrosis progression remains under explored. AFP-L3, an AFP isoform, has primarily been investigated for early detection of HCC due to its higher specificity for malignant liver conditions [13]. However, its relevance in non-malignant HBV-associated fibrosis remains unclear.

Despite advances in non-invasive fibrosis assessment, comparative evaluation of HPS, DCP and AFP-L3 in HBV-associated fibrosis remains limited. Given these gaps, this study aimed to compare the diagnostic performance of HPS, DCP and AFP-L3 for assessing APRI-defined fibrosis risk categories in HBV infection using APRI-based stratification and multivariate analysis.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional study conducted for participants attending SRM Medical College Hospital and Research Centre from September 2024 to September 2025 after obtaining approval from the Institutional Ethics Committee (ECR/8882/Inst/TN/2013/RR-19). A written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrolment. The sample size was determined based on the reported prevalence of HBV infection (9.23%) from previous literature [14], using a significance level (α) of 0.05, a 95% confidence level, and a 5% margin of error. Additionally, healthy individuals were included as controls to provide a reference population for comparative evaluation of biomarker levels and diagnostic performance.

Eligibility criteria

A total of 150 patients with Hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) positivity and 50 healthy individuals were included in the study. A total of 150 patients (both male and female) with HBsAg positivity and clinical features suggestive of HBV infection were included in the study. Acute HBV infection was defined by HBsAg positivity with detectable anti-HBc IgM above the manufacturer’s recommended cut-off, together with clinical and biochemical evidence suggestive of recent infection. Chronic HBV infection was defined by HBsAg positivity with anti-HBc IgG positivity and persistence of infection history exceeding six months, where available. HBV DNA status was recorded but was not used as the primary criterion for classification. Patients with other co-infections (HCV, HDV, HIV), treatment with vitamin K antagonists, other hepatic diseases (ALD, NAFLD, autoimmune hepatitis), pregnant-women and those on antiviral treatment at the time of sample collection were excluded. NAFLD and ALD were excluded based on clinical history, and alcohol consumption assessment. The control group comprised age-matched healthy individuals with no history of liver disease, chronic medical conditions, recent acute illness, any long-term medications, tested negative for HBsAg with normal biochemical test findings were included in the study. Laboratory analysis

Sample collection and processing

5 ml of venous blood were collected from each participant under aseptic conditions. Serum was separated by centrifugation at 5000 rpm for 5 min and stored at − 80 °C until analysis.

HBsAg testing

A two-tiered testing protocol is used for the identification of HBsAg. Initially, all patient samples undergo screening by a rapid immunochromatographic assay, a single-step lateral flow test [14] designed to qualitatively detect HBsAg in serum sample. The analytical sensitivity indicated by the manufacturer for the rapid card was 0.5 ng/ml. Samples that provide reactive results on the rapid card undergo additional validation using the Abbott Architect i1000SR system, an automated Chemiluminescent Microparticle Immunoassay (CMIA) platform. This confirmatory technique utilizes microparticles coated with anti-HBsAg antibodies to selectively bind to HBsAg present in the patient’s blood sample [15].

Measurement of antibodies

Anti-HBc IgM and anti-HBc IgG were quantified using a commercial Enzyme Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA) kit (Bioneovan Co. Ltd., India) in accordance with the manufacturer’s guidelines. Results were interpreted using the sample-to-cutoff ratio (S/CO), where anti-HBc IgM values ≥ 1.1 were considered positive and < 0.9 negative, while anti-HBc IgG values ≥ 1.0 were considered positive and < 1.0 negative. Acute HBV infection was classified based on anti-HBc IgM positivity together with clinical findings, whereas chronic HBV infection was identified based on anti-HBc IgG positivity in conjunction with patient history suggestive of chronic infection. HBV DNA levels were not used as primary criteria for acute or chronic HBV classification.

Measurement of biomarkers

Serum concentrations of HPS, DCP and AFP-L3 were measured using commercially available sandwich ELISA kits (Origin Diagnostics & Research, India). All tests were conducted in line with the manufacturer’s standard operating procedures and performed under consistent experimental conditions. Serum samples were stored at − 80 °C until analysis and tested under standardized laboratory conditions. The analytical ranges of the assays were 1.56–100 ng/mL for HPS, 0.32–20 ng/mL for DCP and 0.63–40 ng/mL for AFP-L3. Standard curves and internal controls provided by manufacturers were used for quality assurance, and concentrations were calculated according to kit protocols.

Measurement of liver parameters

The Liver Function Test (LFT) parameters, comprising total bilirubin, direct bilirubin, indirect bilirubin, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT), total protein, albumin, globulin and the albumin-globulin ratio, were evaluated utilizing standard automated techniques on a fully automated clinical chemistry analyzer (Beckman Coulter DXC 700).

Measurement of risk score

The Aspartate aminotransferase to Platelet Ratio Index (APRI) risk score was calculated for all participants using the formula [7, 16].

APRI = {AST(IU/L) / ULN of AST*100} / platelet count (109/L).

The upper limit of normal (ULN) for AST was considered as 40 IU/L according to institutional laboratory reference ranges. APRI was used as a non-invasive surrogate marker for fibrosis risk stratification in HBV-associated liver disease, and the resulting categories were interpreted as indicators of fibrosis risk rather than definitive fibrosis staging.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS 27.0). The normality of the quantitative variables was assessed using Shapiro-Wilk test. Depending on the data distribution, either Student’s t-test / Mann-Whitney U test was applied to compare differences between groups. Correlation between variables was assessed using the Spearman’s correlation analysis. Binary logistic regression analysis was performed to identify independent predictors of significant fibrosis. Variables included in regression models were selected based on biological relevance and significant univariate associations. Model 1 included biomarkers only; Model 2 was adjusted for age and sex; and Model 3 was additionally adjusted for liver biochemical parameters (ALT, AST, ALP and GGT). Potential confounding factors including age, sex, BMI, diabetes status and HBV DNA positivity were considered during analysis, and adjustments were performed where applicable to minimize confounding effects on fibrosis prediction. Receiver Operating Characteristic curve (ROC) analysis was used to assess the diagnostic performance of biomarkers. Optimal cut-off values were determined using the Youden Index. Area under the ROC curve (AUC), sensitivity, and specificity were calculated to evaluate predictive performance. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 150 HBsAg-positive individuals were enrolled as the case cohort, along with 50 age- and gender-matched healthy individuals recruited as controls. Among the 150 cases, 108 were identified as acute hepatitis B and 42 were classified as chronic hepatitis B, based on serological differentiation using IgM-anti-HBc and IgG-anti-HBc antibody status respectively.

Baseline characteristics of the participants

The study included 150 hepatitis B patients and 50 healthy controls, with a male predominance among cases (62.6% males). Most participants in both groups had a normal BMI (70.7% cases; 76% controls), while overweight status was observed in 29.3% of cases and 24% of controls. The median HBsAg level in cases was 2937.95 IU/mL (IQR: 1547.12–4418.88) and 75.3% had detectable HBV DNA. Type 2 diabetes mellitus was present in 21.3% of cases and 12% of controls. Among cases, 18.6% reported a family history of liver disease, while histories of tattooing (11.3%), blood transfusion (14.7%), and prior surgery (27.3%) were noted [Table 1]. Overall, the cohort represents a clinically relevant HBV population for evaluating fibrosis-related biomarkers.

Baseline biochemical characteristics

A comparative analysis demonstrated that hepatitis B positive patients exhibited significant alterations in conventional liver function parameters when compared with healthy individuals. These biochemical disruptions formed the background physiological variations against which novel biomarkers were evaluated. To determine whether the levels of liver injury biomarkers differed between disease and non-disease states, Mann–Whitney U tests were employed to compare liver function parameters, APRI scores and serum biomarker concentrations. HBV patients showed significantly elevated liver injury markers, including ALT, AST, ALP and GGT, along with reduced platelet counts and higher APRI scores, indicating increased hepatocellular injury and higher APRI-defined fibrosis risk. Serum concentrations of HPS, DCP and AFP-L3 were also significantly increased in HBV cases compared with controls (p < 0.05), although HPS and DCP demonstrated stronger associations with fibrosis-related changes [Table 2]. These findings suggest that the assessed biomarkers reflect both active viral-mediated liver injury and altered hepatic function.

Stratification of Hepatitis B cases by disease severity and associated biomarker variations

To further evaluate biomarker variation across clinical status and APRI-defined fibrosis risk categories, cases were sub-grouped according to clinical and APRI-based categories [Supplementary Table 1]. Comparative analysis showed progressive increases in HPS and DCP across advancing disease categories, whereas AFP-L3 demonstrated comparatively minimal changes between acute and chronic HBV groups [Fig. 2A, B, C]. These findings suggest that HPS and DCP are more closely associated with increasing APRI-defined fibrosis risk, while AFP-L3 shows limited variation across disease severity groups.

Distribution of APRI and Corresponding biomarker variation

Participants were stratified based on APRI risk categories demonstrated significant differences in biochemical parameters, platelet counts and serum biomarker levels [Table 3]. Advancing APRI scores were associated with a significant increase in ALT, AST, ALP, GGT and direct bilirubin levels (all p < 0.001), while total bilirubin and indirect bilirubin did not differ significantly across groups. Platelet counts showed a progressive decline with increasing APRI categories. Albumin levels were significantly lower and globulin levels significantly elevated in higher APRI groups, resulting in a reduced A/G ratio, whereas total protein levels remained comparable. Notably, serum HPS, DCP and AFP-L3 concentrations increased significantly in a stepwise manner across APRI categories.

Biomarker associations and predictive diagnostics for APRI-defined fibrosis risk in Hepatitis B

Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was performed to examine the associations between serum biomarker levels and liver function parameters in hepatitis B patients. For HPS, a strong positive correlation was observed with total protein, albumin, ALT, AST, ALP, GGT and APRI. Platelet count showed an inverse trend, although not statistically significant. For DCP, meaningful positive correlations emerged with ALT, AST, ALP, GGT and APRI, whereas bilirubin fractions and plasma proteins showed non-significant trends. AFP-L3 correlated positively with total protein, AST and APRI, while ALP, GGT and bilirubin parameters remained unlinked [Fig. 3A, B, C]. Binary logistic regression was performed to identify factors independently associated with higher APRI-defined fibrosis risk (APRI > 1) [Table 4]. In the univariate model, higher HPS and DCP levels were significantly associated with increased odds of having APRI > 1, with odds ratios of 1.539 (95% CI: 1.317–1.799; p < 0.001) and 1.859 (95% CI: 1.509–2.292; p < 0.001), respectively. AFP-L3 did not show a significant association in the unadjusted model. After adjusting for age and sex (Model 2), both HPS and DCP remained significantly associated with APRI-based fibrosis risk. HPS retained its positive predictive ability with an OR of 3.091 (95% CI: 1.825–5.234; p < 0.001), indicating an even stronger influence after considering demographic factors. Similarly, DCP remained significant with an OR of 2.412 (95% CI: 1.480–3.931; p < 0.001). AFP-L3 continued to show no statistical significance in Model 2. In the fully adjusted multivariate model (Model 3), which incorporated liver function parameters in addition to age and sex, both HPS and DCP continued to remain significantly associated with higher APRI-defined fibrosis risk. HPS demonstrated the strongest association with higher APRI-defined fibrosis risk, with an OR of 3.515 (95% CI: 1.758–7.030; p < 0.001), while DCP exhibited an OR of 2.396 (95% CI: 1.356–4.232; p = 0.002). In contrast, AFP-L3 still did not show significance after full adjustment. Conventional liver enzymes (ALT, AST, ALP, GGT) did not retain predictive value in the fully adjusted model. Overall, these findings indicate that HPS and DCP independently associated with higher APRI-defined fibrosis risk within this study population, outperforming both AFP-L3 and traditional liver function indices. Their sustained significance across progressively adjusted models suggests that these biomarkers could serve as robust indicators of higher APRI-defined fibrosis risk in hepatitis B–infected patients.

Diagnostic accuracy of biomarkers

ROC analysis was performed to evaluate the discriminative ability of HPS, DCP, AFP-L3, and the combined HPS + DCP panel in identifying individuals with significant fibrosis across APRI-defined categories [Fig. 4, Supplementary Table 2]. Among the individual biomarkers, HPS and DCP showed good discriminative performance with relatively narrow confidence intervals, indicating stable predictive ability. HPS demonstrated an AUC of 0.872 (95% CI: 0.813–0.932), while DCP showed an AUC of 0.869 (95% CI: 0.807–0.930). DeLong’s test revealed no statistically significant difference between the diagnostic performances of HPS and DCP (Z = 0.085, p = 0.932), indicating comparable discriminatory ability for identifying significant fibrosis. At their respective optimal cut-offs, HPS yielded 85.7% sensitivity and 75% specificity, whereas DCP showed 92.9% sensitivity and 74.1% specificity, indicating strong predictive performance for significant fibrosis. In contrast, AFP-L3 demonstrated poor discriminative capacity, with an AUC of 0.580 (95% CI: 0.483–0.677) and a low Youden’s index of 0.216. Its cut-off value of 7.14 ng/mL provided only 59.5% sensitivity and 62% specificity, and did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.129), suggesting limited utility in fibrosis risk detection within non-malignant HBV cohorts. The combined HPS + DCP model demonstrated improved discriminatory performance, yielding the highest AUC of 0.935 (95% CI: 0.895–0.975). The corresponding confidence interval indicated stable discriminative performance, suggesting enhanced discrimination for APRI-defined fibrosis risk categories when both biomarkers were assessed together. At the optimal cut-off value of 4.79, the combined model achieved 90.5% sensitivity and 86.1% specificity, with a Youden Index of 0.766, indicating an improved balance between sensitivity and specificity. These findings suggest that simultaneous assessment of HPS and DCP may improve fibrosis prediction, potentially reflecting their complementary roles in HBV-related liver injury and fibrogenesis. Furthermore, Decision Curve Analysis (DCA) demonstrated that the combined HPS + DCP model provided a greater net clinical benefit across a range of threshold probabilities compared with the “treat-all” and “treat-none” strategies [Fig. 4E], suggesting improved performance for APRI-based fibrosis risk stratification within this study population.

Discussion

Our study evaluated the potential of three serum biomarkers HPS, DCP and AFP-L3 to predict fibrosis severity in HBV infection by APRI-based stratification and multivariate modelling. Our findings revealed clear biochemical and biomarker differences between HBV-infected individuals and healthy controls, as well as with significant associations between HPS and DCP and the probability of higher APRI-defined fibrosis risk. These findings contribute new insights to the expanding domain of non-invasive fibrosis assessment, especially in resource-limited settings where HBV prevalence remains high.

HBV patients exhibited substantial alterations in liver function markers relative to healthy individuals. Elevated levels of AST, ALT, ALP and GGT, together with reduced platelet counts, reflect increased hepatocellular injury and fibrosis burden associated with HBV infection [17, 18]. Decreased platelet counts observed in cases correspond to portal hypertension and splenic sequestration, a typical consequence of advancing fibrosis [19]. Altered bilirubin fractions and reduced A/G ratios observed in HBV patients may indicate impaired hepatic synthetic and excretory function alongside persistent immune activation, findings commonly reported in chronic viral hepatitis [20, 21]. In our cohort, the reduced A/G ratio further supports previous observations that HBV infection is associated with altered protein synthesis and inflammatory responses, even in the absence of advanced fibrosis. These findings provide biological support for the observed changes in fibrosis-associated biomarkers and strengthen interpretation of their potential role in HBV-related liver injury.

The APRI score was used in this investigation as a validated, non-invasive surrogate measure for hepatic fibrosis. Shin et al. indicated that among the simple serum indicators originally created for chronic hepatitis C, APRI is one of the most precise and practical indices for predicting severe fibrosis in chronic hepatitis B [22]. In line with this finding, our investigation revealed a gradual elevation in transaminases, cholestatic enzymes, direct bilirubin and biomarkers (HPS, DCP, AFP-L3), along with a progressive reduction in platelet count and A/G ratio across APRI risk groups. Furthermore, elevated APRI values were observed in acute hepatitis B patients within our cohort, likely reflecting necroinflammatory activity in addition to fibrosis-related changes, suggesting that APRI may capture broader liver injury processes in the acute setting. These findings support the biological relevance of APRI-based classification; however, APRI-defined categories should be interpreted as indicators of fibrosis risk rather than definitive fibrosis severity, particularly in acute HBV where AST elevations may be influenced by active inflammation. The concurrent increase of HPS and DCP with APRI supports their potential role as biomarkers associated with higher APRI-defined fibrosis risk, rather than indicators of mere biochemical variation.

The markedly increased HPS levels observed in our HBV group align with its established function as a stress-responsive hepatokine. Evidence from NAFLD and NASH models indicate that HPS is increased at the first stages of hepatic damage and provides protective benefits by mitigating oxidative stress and hepatocyte apoptosis [10]. In mice models, HPS deletion exacerbated steatosis, inflammation, fibrosis, and insulin resistance, whereas HPS supplementation reversed these pathological alterations. These results underscore the biological validity of our data, indicating that elevated HPS in HBV infection may represent a compensatory hepatoprotective response triggered by viral-induced hepatocellular damage. DCP was also markedly raised in HBV patients, consistent with research indicating that impaired carboxylation of prothrombin rises in chronic liver illness prior to HCC onset [23, 24]. This underscores the importance of DCP beyond oncological monitoring. Conversely, AFP-L3 did not exhibit significant rise across fibrosis categories, aligning with findings that suggest its specificity is mostly for malignant transformation rather than benign fibrosis [25].

The correlation analysis of HPS in our work was corroborated by experimental models of viral hepatitis and steatohepatitis, in which HPS expression corresponds with the degree of damage [6, 7]. The persistent negative correlation between platelet count and both biomarkers highlights their significance in fibrosis development, since thrombocytopenia is a recognized indirect indicator of portal hypertension [17]. AFP-L3 demonstrated negligible correlations, underscoring its limited value outside HCC screening.

Multivariate logistic regression showed that HPS and DCP remained independent predictors of higher APRI-defined fibrosis risk even after adjusting for demographic and biochemical variables in our study. This finding is significant, since it demonstrates that these biomarkers provide predictive information beyond conventional LFT-based indicators. However, as APRI incorporates AST levels and platelet counts, part of the observed predictive performance in regression and ROC analyses may reflect associations with components of the APRI score and liver injury-related processes, rather than independent prediction of histologically confirmed fibrosis. Therefore, the associations observed in this study should be interpreted within the context of APRI-based fibrosis risk stratification rather than definitive fibrosis assessment. Nevertheless, the persistent association of HPS and DCP after multivariable adjustment supports their potential biological relevance as markers associated with elevated fibrosis risk in HBV infection. Our results concerning AFP-L3 contrast with those documented in other studies, including the research of Hau-Tsai Cheng et al. [26], which indicated that AFP-L3 has considerable predictive value for HCC development in patients with consistently increased AFP levels. In that group, AFP-L3 proficiently identified individuals at elevated risk of tumor development, exhibiting sensitivity and specificity of around 71% and increased positive in larger tumors. Conversely, AFP-L3 exhibited no significant correlation with fibrosis severity in our HBV population and did not retain its prognostic efficacy after multivariate adjustment. This gap is likely due to intrinsic disparities in study populations and disease states. The prior investigation primarily assessed patients with significantly high AFP levels and latent tumorigenic risk, whereas our work concentrated on non-malignant HBV infection without imaging evidence of HCC, where AFP-L3 is recognized to stay low and lack discriminative capability. Thus, while AFP-L3 may be essential in detecting early malignant transformation, its effectiveness as a marker for fibrosis or non-malignant liver damage seems constrained, aligning with our findings.

The ROC analysis underscored the diagnostic efficacy of HPS (AUC 0.872) and DCP (AUC 0.869), exhibiting exceptional discriminative ability with elevated sensitivity and specificity at optimal thresholds. Similar AUC values have been shown for DCP in chronic liver disease populations, however its use in HBV fibrosis has been less extensively investigated [27]. Zhang et al. [28] indicated that the highest AUC of AFP-L3 for diagnosing AFP-negative HCC was 0.609 (95% CI: 0.599–0.799), which is already modest. In our study, AFP-L3 exhibited even lower performance, with an AUC of 0.58 for fibrosis prediction, further highlighting its restricted diagnostic utility beyond malignant contexts. The integrated HPS + DCP model showed the highest AUC, suggesting potential improvement in predictive performance within APRI-based fibrosis stratification. This synergistic enhancement is physiologically feasible, since HPS indicates hepatocyte stress and regenerative activity, while DCP signifies synthetic dysfunction, two independent but complimentary mechanisms in the evolution of fibrosis. Research assessing integrated biomarker panels has shown enhanced discriminatory performance relative to individual markers [29], supporting our findings. However, the present results should be considered preliminary, as validation in independent cohorts and comparison with accepted fibrosis reference standards are required before clinical implementation or routine use can be recommended.

First, this was a single-centre cross-sectional study, limiting generalizability to broader HBV populations. Second, fibrosis risk was assessed using APRI-based stratification rather than histological examination or transient elastography. As APRI incorporates AST levels and platelet counts, elevated APRI values, particularly in acute HBV infection may reflect necroinflammatory activity rather than true fibrotic remodeling, potentially affecting fibrosis classification accuracy. In addition, the observed associations between biomarkers and APRI-defined fibrosis risk may partly reflect shared relationships with APRI components and liver injury-related processes, introducing potential incorporation bias in regression and ROC analyses. Therefore, APRI-defined categories should be interpreted as surrogate indicators of fibrosis risk rather than definitive fibrosis staging. Third, the cross-sectional design prevents assessment of temporal relationships or causal inference between biomarker levels and fibrosis progression. In addition, external validation in independent cohorts was not performed, and longitudinal follow-up was unavailable.

In conclusion, our findings suggest that HPS and DCP may serve as potential non-invasive biomarkers of higher APRI-based fibrosis risk in hepatitis B, whereas AFP-L3 showed limited diagnostic utility within APRI-based fibrosis stratification. Combined assessment of HPS and DCP may improve APRI-based fibrosis risk evaluation and support routine HBV monitoring through minimally invasive blood-based testing. Given the widespread availability of APRI and standard biochemical assays, integration of additional serological biomarkers could potentially enhance APRI-based risk stratification without reliance on invasive procedures. However, the cost-effectiveness, assay accessibility and feasibility of implementing combined biomarker panels in low-resource settings require further investigation before clinical adoption.
