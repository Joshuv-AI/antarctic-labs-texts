Discrimination, Social Support, and Identity Development in Relation to Career Agency Among LGB Emerging Adults: A Latent Profile Analysis
Authors: Jeongeun Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionEmerging adulthood is a critical developmental period for identity consolidation and career preparation, yet lesbian, gay, and bisexual (LGB) emerging adults often face intersecting challenges during this stage. Using a person‐centered approach, this study explored heterogeneity in developmental experiences among LGB emerging adults and examined how these experiences relate to career agency, conceptualized as work volition.MethodsData from 270 Korean LGB college students (202 women, 68 men) collected in 2023, were analyzed to identify distinct profiles based on perceived discrimination, social support, and sexual identity development status. Profile differences in career agency and demographic predictors of profile membership were also examined.ResultsFive distinct profiles emerged: Identity‐Affirmed but Socially Isolated, Marginalized and Unsupported, Socially Supported‐Emerging Identity, Adaptive, and Empowered. Profiles characterized by higher social support and more advanced identity development reported greater work volition. Gender predicted profile membership, with men overrepresented in profiles reflecting lower relational and identity resources.ConclusionsFindings reveal diverse developmental pathways among LGB emerging adults, underscoring the protective roles of social support and identity integration in career agency. These results highlight the importance of promoting affirming relationships and identity development to support positive transitions into adulthood and career development.Keywords: career agency, discrimination, identity development, LGB emerging adults, social support

Introduction

Emerging adulthood often involves simultaneously managing identity formation and career preparation, making this period especially demanding (Arnett 2000). During this period, young people are expected to form intimate relationships, consolidate their sense of self, and prepare for participation in the world of work (Super 1990). Successfully managing these overlapping developmental tasks has lasting implications for their well‐being and future career trajectories. During college years, these challenges are intensified, as students must adapt to new academic and social environments while making career‐related decisions that influence their transition to adulthood (Schmidt et al. 2011). For sexual minority students, these normative challenges are compounded by their exploration, disclosure, and integration of a sexual identity (Mobley and Slaney 1996; Schmidt and Nilsson 2006). College years frequently represent the first opportunity to explore and express a sexual identity within a larger community (Schmidt et al. 2011) while making career decisions. This process often occurs within heteronormative contexts where disclosure entails social and psychological risk (Cass 1979; D'Augelli 1994). The result is a dual developmental demand in which identity and career formation occur concurrently under conditions that can heighten psychological strain.

The bottleneck hypothesis (Hetherington 1991) provides a useful framework for understanding this tension. When multiple developmental processes demand substantial psychological resources, progress in one domain temporarily limits progress in another. For instance, when psychological resources are invested in sexual identity formation, fewer resources remain available for career‐related tasks. However, revisiting the bottleneck hypothesis, Morris and Lent (2023) found no differences in career outcomes between sexual minority and heterosexual college students. Instead, sexual minority college students differed in their career decision‐making outcomes depending on the stage of their sexual identity development. Similarly, Jang et al. (2020b) found that identity affirmation and centrality were associated with greater career decision‐making self‐efficacy among lesbian, gay, and bisexual (LGB) college students, indicating that advanced identity development can function as a vocational resource rather than a constraint.

While these findings highlight the importance of advanced identity development for career development among LGB emerging adults, developmental pathways and indicators of more developed identity vary across sociocultural contexts (Bishop et al. 2020; Morgan 2013; Savin‐Williams 2001). For example, developmental markers considered normative in Western contexts where LGB is more visible and same‐sex marriage is legalized hold different significance in non‐Western contexts, such as South Korea (hereafter Korea), where high stigma around sexual minorities persists. Research from Korea provides empirical support for this cultural variability. A qualitative study of sexual identity development among Korean sexual minority adolescents (Cheong 2021) challenged traditional frameworks that position disclosure as a marker of developmental progress, suggesting that developmental growth is better understood through individuals' capacity to determine when and how to disclose, rather than by disclosure behavior per se. Additionally, although traditional frameworks consider coming out as the beginning of an optimistic developmental journey (Corrigan et al. 2013) and concealment as a stressor (Meyer 2003), a recent study found that disclosure to family members was associated with increased depressive symptoms among Korean sexual minorities (Lee and Jeong 2025).

Cultural adaptation studies of LGB identity measures have supported these observations. The Korean validation study of the Lesbian, Gay, Bisexual Identity Scale (LGBIS; Lee and Kim 2022) found that only the identity affirmation subscale correlated significantly with well‐being outcomes, while the concealment motivation subscale showed poor reliability (α = 0.57) and no associations with psychological functioning. In contrast, the Korean validation of the LGB Positive Identity Measure (LGB‐PIM; Jang and Ahn 2018) demonstrated that all five subscales showed significant criterion validity with well‐being outcomes and consistently high reliability. These patterns reflect different conceptual frameworks. The LGBIS includes multiple subscales assessing negative or challenging aspects of sexual identity (e.g., internalized homonegativity, concealment motivation) under the assumption that the absence of such difficulties indicates developmental progress (Riggle et al. 2014). However, in high‐stigma contexts, such as Korea, where concealment can be adaptive and disclosure carries substantial risk, measures that assess developmental progress through the absence of concealment or internalized negativity do not fully capture advanced identity development.

This cultural specificity has important developmental implications in Korea, as sexual identity exploration and career preparation tend to converge more tightly during college years. As a highly collectivist and stratified society, the transition from education to employment is characterized by intense competition and persistent job insecurity, with bachelor's degree holders facing lower‐than‐expected employment stability despite near‐universal college enrollment (Ministry of Education 2024). This structural uncertainty fosters a pragmatic and often anxious orientation toward work, emphasizing survival and economic necessity over self‐expression (Kim and Lee 2022). For LGB emerging adults, these conditions are compounded by persistent stigma in a context where social acceptance of sexual minorities remains among the lowest in Organization for Economic Co‐operation and Development member countries (OECD 2019). Moreover, Korea's competitive pre‐college educational environment further constrains opportunities for identity exploration during adolescence (Cheong 2021). As a result, many LGB students begin to explore or integrate their sexual identity only after entering college in Korea, which is often their first relatively safe space for exploration (Kim et al. 2022). In this study, we examine how identity development and career preparation unfold among Korean LGB emerging adults in a high‐stigma, collectivist context. Because sexual identity development and career preparation occur at the same time during college for Korean LGB emerging adults, this period is especially important for examining how identity‐related processes and vocational planning interact.

Contextual Influences on LGB Career Development

Over the past 2 decades, vocational psychology has come to recognize that career development takes place within sociopolitical and cultural contexts. For sexual minority individuals, this recognition has been particularly consequential: marginalized individuals construct their vocational paths not only through personal exploration but also through negotiation with structural barriers and social meanings surrounding identity (Morrow et al. 1996; Schmidt and Nilsson 2006). Research on LGB career development has drawn on multiple theoretical frameworks, including Social Cognitive Career Theory (Lent et al. 1994), Career Construction Theory (Savickas 2005), and the Psychology of Working Theory (Duffy et al. 2016a), that span cognitive, constructivist, and structural perspectives but share a fundamental premise: environmental contexts influence self‐efficacy, narrative construction, and perceived opportunity in ways that are inseparable from career development (Allan et al. 2019; Parnell et al. 2012).

Across these frameworks, three contextual influences consistently appear in studies of LGB emerging adults' career development: experiences of discrimination, availability of social support, and the developmental status of sexual identity. Each operates in relation to the others, together determining the resources and constraints emerging adults face when making career decisions.

Discrimination

Experiences of discrimination based on sexual orientation represent a persistent contextual constraint on career development for LGB emerging adults. In the career development literature, discrimination has been conceptualized to include both overt experiences of unequal treatment and anticipated or perceived barriers rooted in heterosexist social norms (Parnell et al. 2012; Schmidt et al. 2011). Empirical studies have operationalized these experiences along a spectrum from direct victimization to anticipatory concerns. Direct experiences include physical assault, verbal harassment such as heterosexist slurs, and exclusion from professional or academic networks (Schneider and Dimito 2010; Winderman et al. 2018), while anticipatory forms encompass concerns about biased evaluation, fears of discrimination in hiring or promotion, and anxiety about workplace harassment if one's sexual orientation becomes known (Parnell et al. 2012; Schmidt et al. 2011). Research has documented multiple pathways through which these discrimination experiences undermine vocational functioning, including reduced career decision‐making self‐efficacy (Parnell et al. 2012), impeded vocational identity formation (Tomlinson and Fassinger 2003), and narrower perceived career options (Schneider and Dimito 2010).

Within educational contexts, perceived heterosexist campus climates have been shown to interfere with vocational development by increasing uncertainty around identity disclosure and constraining students' ability to see themselves pursuing particular career paths. Schmidt et al. (2011) assessed campus‐specific discrimination with an 8‐item scale measuring experiences such as feeling unaccepted by others and perceiving that one's sexual identity is underrepresented in class materials. They found that campus climate was a stronger predictor of vocational identity and career decision‐making self‐ efficacy than sexual identity status itself, suggesting that discriminatory environments shape career development indirectly by constraining exploration and self‐definition.

Qualitative studies with Korean sexual minority college students describe widespread expectations to conceal one's sexual identity in academic and employment settings, alongside concerns about social stigma and potential disadvantages in hiring or workplace relationships (Choi and Oh 2021; Kim et al. 2022). Participants described experiences of what they termed “sociocultural violence,” including educators propagating homophobic ideas in classrooms and being told that same‐sex relationships are “disgusting” (Choi and Oh 2021). Although overt institutional discrimination was not always explicitly reported, participants frequently described approaching career entry with the expectation of bias and little structural protection, suggesting that discrimination operates as an everyday contextual constraint rather than a single discrete event. However, the effect of discrimination is not uniform. Schmidt et al. (2011) found that LGB students reporting high discrimination, but also strong social support, exhibited lower career indecision than might be expected, suggesting that contextual resources buffer or reconfigure how discrimination affects development. Similarly, Winderman et al. (2018) reported that minority stress indicators, such as prejudice events, concealment, and internalized homonegativity, were not independently predictive of career outcomes among LGB college students, a pattern they attributed to protective factors, such as community support or timing‐related resilience.

Social Support

Social support has long been recognized as a key facilitator of career development, but it has developmental significance for LGB emerging adults who must simultaneously navigate identity consolidation and vocational exploration. Support from family, peers, and mentors enables these parallel developmental tasks by offering practical guidance alongside affirmation of identity, thereby reducing the psychological burden of addressing stigma while planning for work (Blustein 1997; Schmidt and Nilsson 2006). Empirical evidence supports this dual function. Winderman et al. (2018) found that higher levels of social support were associated with greater career readiness and lower decision‐making conflict, especially for those experiencing minority stress. Building on this, Jang et al. (2020b) reported that social support mediated the relationship between positive identity and career decision‐making self‐efficacy, indicating that supportive relationships help translate identity integration into vocational confidence. Qualitative research with Korean LGB college students extends these findings by showing that peer and community networks serve not merely as sources of emotional comfort but as contexts that enable active career engagement and empowerment (Choi and Oh 2021). These studies position social support as a developmental resource that supports the integration of identity and career during emerging adulthood.

Sexual Identity Development Status

Sexual identity development has been recognized as an important factor in career decision‐making among LGB emerging adults, with early research documenting both constraints and facilitative effects. Prior work has linked sexual identity development to career decision‐making among LGB emerging adults, often showing that identity‐related conflict can interfere with vocational planning. Schmidt and Nilsson (2006) found that sexual identity conflict predicted vocational indecision among LGB adolescents. Building on this work, Schmidt et al. (2011) found that competing developmental processes can constrain career exploration during emerging adulthood. Parnell et al. (2012) reported that sexual identity conflict was associated with lower career decision‐making self‐efficacy, and Chen and Zhou (2021) demonstrated similar patterns among high school students, suggesting that identity‐related uncertainty can interfere with career planning. Person‐centered research has moved beyond simple associations between variables to explore how patterns of sexual identity development and career outcomes co‐occur. Lyons et al. (2010) identified several distinct patterns in a longitudinal sample of LGB emerging adults. One cluster, characterized by high sexual identity conflict, reported that exploration of their sexual identity interrupted their engagement in career development. However, the largest group in their study was a low‐conflict cluster, where both identity conflict and career interference were minimal. These individuals pursued identity and career tasks concurrently without substantial interference. This evidence demonstrates that identity development status, rather than sexual minority status itself, differentiates career outcomes among LGB emerging adults. Such heterogeneity indicates that identity development operates through qualitatively different configurations, which are not well captured by variable‐centered approaches.

We selected an outcome that captures perceived choice under constraint and fits the developmental timing of college students. Many commonly used indicators in career research (e.g., career decision‐making self‐efficacy) primarily reflect confidence in performing career tasks, whereas career agency captures whether individuals perceive themselves as having occupational choice in the face of constraints. Accordingly, the present study assessed career agency using work volition, which indexes perceived capacity for occupational choice under structural limitations. Among college students, work volition has been associated with adaptive vocational functioning, including career adaptability, career decision‐making self‐efficacy, and academic satisfaction (Autin et al. 2017; Duffy et al. 2016b). Longitudinal evidence further suggests that work volition precedes subsequent growth in career adaptability, indicating that career agency is not merely a concurrent perception but a developmental capacity that facilitates later career progress (Autin et al. 2017). In this developmental sequence, career agency can serve as a prerequisite for sustained exploration and for converting emerging preferences into decisions when opportunities become actionable. For emerging adults, this sense of agency reflects career readiness rather than attainment, consistent with the exploratory developmental tasks that are characteristic of this life stage (Super 1990).

The Present Study

The present study aimed to identify distinct developmental profiles among Korean LGB college students based on experiences of discrimination, perceived social support, and sexual identity development status and to examine the relationship between these profiles and career agency. We employed latent profile analysis (LPA) to identify subgroups characterized by distinct configurations of these three factors. We focused specifically on lesbian, gay, and bisexual identities. Although sexual and gender minorities are often discussed together under the LGBTQ+ umbrella, sexual orientation and gender identity represent distinct constructs (American Psychological Association (APA) 2015). Research demonstrates that LGB and transgender individuals face distinct patterns of minority stress and career discrimination (Schneider and Dimito 2010; Testa et al. 2015). Based on documented variability in these developmental pathways, we expected multiple profiles with qualitatively different patterns of discrimination, social support, and identity development. Consistent with prior research (Morris and Lent 2023; Winderman et al. 2018), profiles characterized by stronger social support and more advanced identity development were expected to report higher career agency even when discrimination is present, whereas profiles marked by high discrimination combined with limited support and identity development were expected to show reduced career agency.

Method

Participants and Data Collection

Participants were 270 LGB college students (202 women, 74.8%; 68 men, 25.2%) enrolled in Korean universities. The sample included 1st‐year (19.3%), sophomore (19.3%), junior (27.0%), senior (27.0%), and students enrolled beyond 4 years (7.4%). Sexual orientation included 39.3% bisexual, 31.5% lesbian, 21.5% gay, and 7.8% identifying with other sexual minority identities (e.g., pansexual). Most participants (68.9%) lived in the Seoul metropolitan area at the time of data collection, with others residing in major cities (17.4%) or non‐metropolitan regions (13.7%).

Participants were recruited as part of a larger project examining career development among sexual minority individuals in Korea and data were collected between March and May 2023. Eligibility required current college enrollment and self‐identification as sexual minorities, including lesbian, gay, and bisexual. After institutional review board approval, participants were recruited through LGBTQ+ organizations, university counseling centers, student clubs, and social media. Participation was voluntary and anonymous. All participants provided informed consent before completing an online questionnaire and received a gift card worth 4000 Korean won (approximately US$3.50) as compensation.

Measures

Discrimination

Perceived discrimination based on sexual orientation was assessed using the Korean version of the Stigmatization Scale (ByungChul Kang 2011), adapted from Harvey (2001) original scale. This 12‐item unidimensional measure assesses perceived social rejection and discrimination toward sexual minority individuals. Sample items include “Society discriminates against me because of my sexual orientation” and “I feel comfortable in society” (reverse‐coded). Items were rated on a 5‐point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree), with higher scores indicating greater perceived marginalization. Internal consistency was α = 0.92 for the original version and α = 0.83 in the current sample.

Social Support

Perceived social support was measured using the Korean version of the Medical Outcomes Study Social Support Survey (Lim et al. 2003), originally developed by Sherbourne and Stewart (1991). This 19‐item instrument assesses four functional dimensions: tangible support (e.g., “Do you have someone who can help you if you are confined to bed?”), affectionate support (e.g., “Do you have someone who shows you love and affection?”), positive social interaction (e.g., “Do you have someone to have a good time with?”), and emotional/informational support (e.g., “Do you have someone you can count on to listen to you when you need to talk?”). Items were rated on a 5‐point Likert scale (1 = none of the time, 5 = all of the time). Reliability in the original version was α = 0.98; internal consistencies in this study were α = 0.92–0.95 across subscales and α = 0.96 overall. Additionally, structural social support was assessed using a single item from Lim et al. (2003), which asked participants to report the number of people they could rely on for support.

Sexual Identity Development Status

Sexual identity development status was operationalized through the Korean version of the LGB‐PIM (Riggle et al. 2014), translated and validated by Jang and Ahn (2018). The LGB‐PIM was selected over the LGBIS due to its focus on positive identity dimensions and demonstrated psychometric soundness in Korean contexts (Jang and Ahn 2018). Consistent with Riggle et al. (2014), the scale conceptualizes positive identity as the developmental outcome of identity formation—a synthesis phase in which individuals integrate their sexual identity as a valued and coherent aspect of the self rather than the absence of confusion or distress. Unlike measures that primarily assess identity development through difficulties such as concealment and internalized homonegativity, the LGB‐PIM operationalizes identity development through positive psychological processes such as self‐awareness and authenticity—an approach particularly suited to contexts where concealment can be adaptive and disclosure carries substantial risk. The 24‐item LGB‐PIM includes five empirically supported dimensions that reflect multifaceted growth across identity domains: self‐awareness (e.g., “I can better understand myself through my lesbian/gay/bisexual identity”), authenticity (e.g., “I am honest with myself about my lesbian/gay/bisexual identity”), community (e.g., “I feel a sense of belonging in the lesbian/gay/bisexual community”), intimacy (e.g., “My lesbian/gay/bisexual identity helps me become closer to my partner”), and social justice (e.g., “My experience as a lesbian/gay/bisexual person helps me advocate for the rights of others”). Responses were rated on a 7‐point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 7 = strongly agree). Internal consistency in the Korean validation study was α = 0.83, and reliabilities in the present sample ranged from acceptable to excellent across subscales—self‐awareness (α = 0.74), authenticity (α = 0.65), community (α = 0.79), intimacy (α = 0.75), and social justice (α = 0.78)—with an overall α = 0.94.

Career Agency

Career agency, commonly defined as individuals' perceived capacity to direct and influence their own career paths (Lemke 2020), has been conceptualized across major career theories as a marker of self‐determination in vocational behavior (Savickas 2013; Lent et al. 1994). Within the Psychology of Working Theory, this agentic capacity is operationalized through work volition, or the perceived ability to make career choices despite external constraints (Duffy et al. 2016a; Taylor et al. 2023). In this study, career agency was assessed using the Korean version of the Work Volition Scale–Student Version (WVS‐SV; Kim and Lee 2014), adapted from Duffy et al. (2012). The 15‐item measure comprises two subscales—volition and constraints—and is rated on a 7‐point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 7 = strongly agree). Sample items include “I will be able to change jobs if I want to” and “I worry that my life circumstances will prevent me from achieving my long‐term career goals.” Higher scores indicate greater perceived volition. Internal consistency was α = 0.92 for the original scale and α = 0.83 for the present sample.

Covariates

Gender, sexual orientation, socioeconomic status (SES), and grade were included as covariates in subsequent analyses, given their documented links to minority stress and career development among LGB populations (Jang et al. 2020a; McGarrity 2014; Parnell et al. 2012; Schneider and Dimito 2010). SES was assessed using two measures of subjective social status: a single‐item 5‐point Likert scale (1 = lower class, 5 = upper class) and the MacArthur Scale of Subjective Social Status (Adler et al. 2000), which uses a 10‐rung ladder representing societal standing (1 = bottom, 10 = top). We used subjective rather than objective SES indicators because subjective social status more reliably captures individuals' perceived position within their social context (Adler et al. 2000). Both measures were standardized and averaged to create a composite SES score.

Analytic Plan

Analyses were conducted in four steps. First, preliminary analyses were performed in SPSS 22 to examine descriptive statistics, univariate and multivariate normality, and bivariate correlations among all study variables. Second, latent profile analysis (LPA) was conducted in Mplus 8.7 to identify distinct patterns of discrimination, social support, and sexual identity development status among Korean LGB college students. Following best‐practice recommendations (Spurk et al. 2020; Tein et al. 2013), models with two to six profiles were estimated using robust maximum likelihood (MLR) with 10,000 random start values and 2000 final optimizations to minimize the risk of local maxima. Model selection was informed by multiple statistical criteria, including the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and sample‐size‐adjusted BIC (SaBIC), with lower values indicating better fit (Nylund et al. 2007). The Lo–Mendell–Rubin adjusted likelihood ratio test (LMR; Lo 2001) and the Bootstrap Likelihood Ratio Test (BLRT) were used to evaluate whether a k‐profile model provided a significantly better fit than a k − 1 solution. Classification accuracy was assessed through entropy values, with values of 0.80 or higher indicating good classification accuracy (Clark and Muthén 2009). To avoid over‐extracting profiles, each profile was required to include at least 5% of the sample (Spurk et al. 2020). Third, differences in career agency (operationalized as work volition) across the identified profiles were examined using the BCH (Bolck–Croon–Hagenaars) approach (Bolck et al. 2004), which adjusts for classification error when estimating distal outcomes. Wald χ² tests were used to determine whether profiles differed significantly in participants' perceived ability to make career choices. Lastly, demographic characteristics associated with profile membership were examined using multinomial logistic regression with the R3STEP procedure in Mplus (Vermunt 2010), which accounts for classification uncertainty and reduces bias compared to one‐step estimation (Asparouhov and Muthén 2014). Gender, sexual orientation, SES, and grade were included as predictors.

Results

Preliminary Analysis

Prior to latent profile analysis, data quality and distributional properties were examined. Response validity checks (completion time, straight‐lining) revealed no problematic cases. No missing values were present in the dataset. Outlier screening identified two univariate and seven multivariate outliers, which were removed to ensure model stability (Tabachnick and Fidell 2013). The final sample consisted of 261 participants. Skewness and kurtosis values indicated acceptable normality across all indicator variables (|skewness| < 2, |kurtosis| < 7; Curran et al. 1996). Despite limited social support, the profile showed relatively strong identity development. The Marginalized and Unsupported group (n = 32, 12.3%) reported elevated discrimination together with the lowest scores across both identity development and social support, with community belonging and affectionate support particularly low. The Socially Supported‐Emerging Identity and Adaptive groups experienced moderate to low discrimination and differed mainly in the balance between social support and identity development. Differences in Career Agency Across Profiles

We then examined whether LGB college students with different profiles differed in their level of career agency, operationalized as work volition, using the BCH approach. The overall test was significant, χ²(4) = 56.98, p < 0.001, indicating that work volition differed across profiles. Pairwise comparisons showed that the Empowered and Socially Supported‐Emerging Identity profiles reported higher work volition than the Adaptive, Identity‐Affirmed but Socially Isolated, and Marginalized and Unsupported Profiles (ps ≤ 0.015). No significant difference was observed between the Empowered (M = 5.094) and Socially Supported‐Emerging Identity profiles (M = 5.091, p = 0.991). The Adaptive (M = 4.574) Profile demonstrated a moderate level of work volition, scoring higher than the Marginalized and Unsupported group (M = 4.077, p < 0.001) whereas no significant difference was observed between the Adaptive (M = 4.574) and the Identity‐Affirmed but Socially Isolated (M = 4.365, p = 0.418). Lastly, no significant difference was observed between the Identity‐Affirmed but Socially Isolated (M = 4.365) and Marginalized and Unsupported Profiles (M = 4.077, p= 0.281). Discussion

This study identified developmental pathways among LGB emerging adults by examining how discrimination, social support, and identity development jointly operate within individuals using a person‐centered approach. It makes two contributions to the literature. First, it examines discrimination, social support, and sexual identity development together, an integration absent in previous research. Second, it reveals how these contextual factors interact within individuals to form distinct developmental subgroups, each representing different patterns of development.

Five profiles emerged, each reflecting unique patterns of discrimination, social support, and identity development status. The Identity‐Affirmed but Socially Isolated profile and Marginalized and Unsupported profile shared high levels of perceived discrimination and relatively low social support, yet they differed in identity development status. Students in the Identity‐Affirmed but Socially Isolated profile scored high on identity development, particularly self‐awareness and social justice orientation. In contrast, those in the Marginalized and Unsupported profile displayed the lowest identity development status. This difference illustrates that exposure to similar contextual constraints can lead to qualitatively different developmental pathways. In a previous study examining the relationship between discrimination, social support, and career development among LGB college students, Schmidt et al. (2011) found that students reporting both high perceived discrimination and high social support unexpectedly showed lower career indecision than those with low discrimination. To explain this pattern, Schmidt et al. (2011) used the concept of crisis competence (Ritter and Terndrup 2002), suggesting that repeated encounters with discrimination foster adaptive coping. Additionally, high social support itself reflects individuals' capacity to build and maintain supportive relationships in response to adversity. Drawing on this framework, the present findings suggest that, whereas Schmidt et al. (2011) interpreted building social support as evidence of crisis competence, crisis competence may operate through identity development in the Identity‐Affirmed but Socially Isolated profile. This profile showed the highest social justice orientation across all five profiles, which reflects how these individuals interpret and respond to discrimination. Rather than internalizing discrimination as personal failure, they attribute their distress to structural inequality, fostering critical reflection on societal injustice (Russell et al. 2012).

The patterns described above represent profiles at the extremes of discrimination and support. However, the Adaptive profile, which represented the second‐largest proportion of the sample (35.6%), closely following the largest profile at 36.4%, warrants additional consideration as it reflects a normative developmental pathway for many LGB emerging adults. Students in this profile demonstrated near‐average levels of discrimination, moderate social support, and modest identity development, suggesting neither pronounced constraint nor particularly favorable developmental conditions. Their work volition fell between the profiles characterized by high social support and low discrimination (Empowered and Socially Supported–Emerging Identity) and those marked by elevated discrimination and limited support (Identity‐Affirmed but Socially Isolated and Marginalized and Unsupported). This pattern indicates that when discrimination is relatively manageable, moderate social support and identity development are enough to maintain adequate career agency; however, they may not be sufficient to support the highest levels of career agency observed in more advantaged profiles. From a developmental perspective, this profile represents students who are actively engaged in identity exploration and career preparation but have not yet consolidated either strong identity integration or robust support networks. The prevalence of this pattern implies that interventions should focus on helping students establish a sufficient foundation of support and self‐understanding that enables continued developmental progress rather than on achieving exceptional outcomes.

The contrast between the Empowered and Socially Supported‐Emerging Identity profiles offers additional insight. Both groups experienced relatively low discrimination and high social support, yet the Empowered profile exhibited more advanced identity development than the Socially Supported‐Emerging Identity profile. One possible interpretation is that supportive environments or low exposure to discrimination do not automatically foster identity growth. Members of the Socially Supported‐Emerging Identity profile had lower identity scores, despite reporting the lowest levels of discrimination, compared with the Adaptive and Empowered profiles. This pattern aligns with emerging adulthood theories emphasizing that identity development requires active exploration rather than simply the absence of adversity (Arnett 2000; Riggle et al. 2014). Another possible explanation is that these students might anticipate, rather than directly experience, discrimination in career contexts and therefore manage their identity more cautiously. As Croteau (1996) reported, many LGB individuals expected sexual orientation‐based discrimination if their sexual orientation became known, and some LGB emerging adults opt not to disclose their identity as a protective strategy to avoid potential stigma. Although strong relational networks buffer against anticipated distress, sexual identity may remain peripheral to self‐concept and career development without salient identity exploration.

Beyond describing profile characteristics, these patterns have important implications for career agency. Students in the Identity‐Affirmed but Socially Isolated profile demonstrated higher work volition than those in the Marginalized and Unsupported profile, suggesting that advanced identity development may support career agency even under high discrimination and limited social support. For instance, Schneider and Dimito (2010) described a case in which coming out led a gay engineering student to question whether his major reflected social expectations rather than personal values, which eventually led him to pursue a career path more closely aligned with his authentic self. Such narratives demonstrate that when sexual identity is well integrated, it can strengthen individuals' capacity to make self‐determined and meaningful career choices despite external constraints.

However, the relationship between profile characteristics and career agency is more complex than this example suggests. Initially, social support appears to play a more prominent role than positive identity in determining work volition. For example, the Socially Supported‐Emerging Identity profile showed higher career agency than the Identity‐Affirmed but Socially Isolated profile despite substantially lower identity development scores (M = 3.91 vs. M = 5.69). However, closer examination reveals that neither discrimination, social support, nor identity development alone consistently predicts work volition differences. The non‐significant difference between the Identity‐Affirmed but Socially Isolated and Adaptive profiles (p = 0.418) is particularly revealing given their similar levels of identity development but substantial differences in social support. This indicates that when identity development is sufficiently advanced, additional social support does not translate into significant gains in career agency, suggesting that identity development functions as a protective factor that maintains career agency even under conditions of limited social support.

Conversely, advanced identity development does not guarantee greater career agency when other conditions remain unfavorable. The non‐significant difference between the Identity‐Affirmed but Socially Isolated and Marginalized and Unsupported profiles (p = 0.281) illustrates this limitation. Despite substantially higher identity development in the former group (M = 5.69 vs. M = 3.69), both profiles demonstrated comparable work volition levels when social support remained consistently low. Similarly, the near‐identical levels of work volition observed in the Socially Supported–Emerging Identity and Empowered profiles (Ms = 5.09 and 5.08, p = 0.991) suggest that once adequate social support and low discrimination are established, further identity development may offer limited additional benefits for career agency. These patterns indicate that positive identity functions most effectively when complemented by favorable environmental conditions rather than as a standalone protective factor.

Among demographic variables, only gender emerged as a statistically significant antecedent. Students identifying as men were significantly more likely to belong to the Identity‐Affirmed but Socially Isolated and Marginalized and Unsupported profiles compared to the Empowered profile. This pattern indicates that men's overrepresentation in profiles characterized by greater constraints contributed to gender differences. In the Korean context, non‐sexual minority individuals tend to hold more negative attitudes toward male sexual minorities than toward female sexual minorities (Ko and Park 2023), suggesting gay and bisexual men may face stronger social exclusion and higher levels of perceived discrimination. Additionally, the timing and context of compulsory military service during early college years may intensify constraints on identity development for sexual minority men (Jeong and Lee 2018). Although this study did not assess actual military experience, students who returned to college after completing military service are likely to have experienced social exclusion and discrimination more acutely, and even the anticipation of entering military service in the near future could heighten perceptions of discrimination. Nevertheless, given that the sample comprised predominantly students identifying as women, these differences should be interpreted with caution. Future research should include more balanced and inclusive gender representation to clarify how gendered experiences and norms influence LGB emerging adults' navigation of discrimination, support, and identity development in their career trajectories.

Finally, it is worth noting that the dimensions of positive identity development were not uniformly associated with other variables in the present study. For example, only authenticity and social justice orientation showed significant correlations with perceived discrimination, whereas social support was significantly associated with all identity subscales. In contrast, only self‐awareness and authenticity were significantly related to work volition. Although the precise reasons for these selective associations cannot be determined within the present design, these patterns suggest that different dimensions of identity development may serve distinct developmental functions rather than operating as a single, unified construct.

Limitations and Future Directions

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the present findings. First, while this study used a Korean sample, extending the literature beyond Western contexts and providing insights from a cultural setting with limited legal and social protections for sexual minorities, the findings are not representative of all non‐Western settings. Second, although the present study employed established measures of discrimination and social support, these instruments have not been culturally validated specifically for Korean LGB college students; thus, they may not have fully captured context‐specific forms of stigma or support relevant to this population. Third, the present study reflects specific conceptual and measurement choices regarding both sexual identity development and career development. Sexual identity development was assessed using the LGB Positive Identity Measure (LGB‐PIM), which emphasizes strengths‐based aspects of identity integration but does not encompass all social or behavioral dimensions of identity processes, such as outness, concealment, or identity expression. Readers should note that the findings reflect associations between career agency and selected dimensions of positive identity integration rather than the full spectrum of sexual identity development. Fourth, the modest sample size, while understandable given the challenges of recruiting Korean LGB populations, may have limited the detection of smaller effects. Finally, information regarding participants' academic disciplines and institutional contexts was not collected, although acceptance of sexual minorities and access to identity‐affirming resources are likely to vary across fields of study and universities in Korea.

Future research should build on these findings by examining how different dimensions of sexual identity development relate to specific developmental outcomes. Greater attention is needed to clarify which aspects of identity development are most relevant for career‐related processes, as distinct from outcomes such as psychological well‐being or social adjustment. Methodologically, future studies would benefit from the development or adaptation of measures that are both culturally grounded and psychometrically robust for Korean sexual minority populations while also incorporating social and behavioral dimensions of identity that may function differently across sociocultural contexts. Longitudinal designs are particularly important for tracing how identity development, discrimination, and social support co‐occur and unfold over time in relation to career agency during emerging adulthood. Finally, future research should extend beyond LGB samples to include gender minority populations, whose career development trajectories may be shaped by distinct forms of stigma and structural constraint, a wider range of institutional contexts, and a more balanced gender representation.

Implications

The findings point to the importance of addressing LGB emerging adults' career development within broader social and institutional contexts, rather than focusing solely on individual‐level change (Masdonati et al. 2021). Across profiles, higher career agency was associated with lower discrimination and stronger social support, suggesting that structural and relational conditions shape career challenges (Kim et al. 2022; McFadden 2015; Velez et al. 2021). From this perspective, career‐related difficulties among LGB emerging adults are better understood as responses to constrained environments rather than indicators of individual deficits. Effective career support, therefore, requires coordinated efforts that reduce contextual barriers while also supporting individual developmental processes so that responsibility for navigating discriminatory contexts does not rest solely on sexual minority students themselves (APA 2021).

At the institutional level, colleges and universities play a central role in shaping the conditions under which LGB students engage in career development (Choi and Oh 2021; Tomlinson and Fassinger 2003). Prior research has demonstrated that campus climate is a robust predictor of vocational purpose and psychological career development, explaining variance beyond individual sexual identity development (Tomlinson and Fassinger 2003). Institutional support thus extends beyond formal non‐discrimination policies to include the availability of identity‐affirming resources such as LGB student organizations, dedicated resource centers, and counseling services with demonstrated competence in working with sexual minority students (Kim et al. 2022; Winderman et al. 2018). Career services can further influence students' sense of belonging by signaling inclusivity through visible messaging, intake procedures, and staff practices that explicitly acknowledge diverse sexual orientations (McFadden 2015; Velez et al. 2021). Faculty advisors and supportive staff members also serve important mentoring and advocacy functions, while campus‐wide educational initiatives may contribute to longer‐term shifts in institutional climate and norms (Choi and Oh 2021; APA 2021).

Beyond providing emotional support, campus‐based LGB student organizations function as critical sites for identity consolidation and agency mobilization. Korean qualitative research has shown that participation in such organizations enables sexual minority students to normalize their experiences, access peer‐based information, and develop collective strategies for coping with heteronormative environments (Choi and Oh 2021; Kim et al. 2022). In several cases, involvement in these communities has also shaped career aspirations toward advocacy, education, counseling, or human rights–related fields, suggesting that institutional spaces can actively expand perceived career possibilities rather than merely buffering stress.

While institutional conditions play a critical role in shaping career development, individual‐ and group‐level interventions also offer meaningful opportunities to support LGB emerging adults. Within more affirming institutional environments, individual‐level interventions are better positioned to facilitate developmental processes related to both identity integration and career preparation (Masdonati et al. 2021). Career counseling offered to LGB students can address how sexual identity development intersects with career planning, helping students contextualize experiences of discrimination as products of broader social structures rather than internal shortcomings (APA 2021; Winderman et al. 2018). Prior work has suggested that the psychological demands of sexual identity integration may temporarily constrain career exploration, creating a developmental bottleneck in which career‐related tasks are delayed rather than abandoned (Hetherington 1991; McFadden 2015). In this context, both individual and group‐based counseling approaches that foster critical awareness of structural inequality and heterosexist privilege may support more adaptive coping and promote self‐determined career decision‐making, particularly where concealment and role conflict are common (Choi and Oh 2021; J. Kim et al. 2025).

Taken together, the findings underscore the necessity of multi‐level career interventions that address structural, institutional, relational, and individual dimensions of LGB emerging adults' development (Masdonati et al. 2021). Consistent with reviews of the sexual minority vocational literature, progress in this area requires shifting analytic and intervention priorities away from individual adjustment alone and toward the transformation of educational and social contexts that systematically constrain opportunity (McFadden 2015; Velez et al. 2021). Advocacy efforts aimed at reducing institutional exclusion, increasing visibility, and expanding legal and policy protections should therefore be understood as integral to career development support, particularly in cultural contexts where formal safeguards remain limited.

Conclusion

This study identified five developmental profiles among LGB emerging adults, revealing distinct patterns in how discrimination, social support, and sexual identity development combine within individuals to shape career agency. By adopting a person‐centered approach, the findings extend prior variable‐centered research and demonstrate that career development among LGB students unfolds through qualitatively different configurations rather than along a single continuum of risk or resilience.

The prevalence of profiles demonstrating adequate to high career agency (72% combined) suggests that many LGB emerging adults successfully navigate career development despite contextual challenges. However, the varied pathways to career agency, from optimal conditions (Empowered) to resilience under adversity (Adaptive), reveal that supportive environments and identity development can each serve as resources, though neither alone guarantees positive outcomes. These findings indicate that career agency among LGB emerging adults depends on the interaction of developmental conditions, not on isolated protective factors.
