Multi-dimensional surveillance of hepatitis E: a 20-year observational analysis in Shanghai, China
Authors: Kaiyun Chen, Wei Wang, Xin Shen, Di Xu, Yanting Lee, Zheng Teng, Jiming Zhang, Hong Ren, Yihan Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHepatitis E virus (HEV) remains a major public health concern in China, with a significant increase in notified incidence over the last two decades. We aimed to examine trends and potential contributing factors associated with hepatitis E incidence and infection in Shanghai, a highly developed metropolis.MethodsHepatitis E notified incidence data were retrieved from the National Notifiable Infectious Disease Reporting System (2004–2024). Six cross-sectional community-based serological surveys were conducted between 2001 and 2024, involving a total of 6,305 participants. Pathogen surveillance was annually conducted in confirmed cases, swine bile, and shellfish between 2018 and 2024. HEV was genotyped via amplification of a 150-nt fragment within ORF2 and phylogenetically analyzed.ResultsA total of 14,071 hepatitis E cases were notified in Shanghai during 2004–2024, with a Joinpoint detected in 2012 and an increasing trend thereafter (annual percentage change 5.03%, p = 0.044). In serological surveys, the age-standardized anti-HEV IgG positivity rate ranged between 18.56% and 34.43% among 6,305 participants. The exploratory ratio of notified clinical cases to estimated asymptomatic infections (clinical-to-asymptomatic ratio, C/A ratio) increased most markedly in the 1961–1970 birth cohort between 2010 and 2020. Moreover, 548 of 1,367 cases and 126 of 3,900 swine bile samples tested positive for HEV RNA; none of 724 shellfish samples were positive. HEV genotype 4 accounted for 97.35% of cases, and eight genotype 3 sequences were isolated.ConclusionsHEV infection remained common in both humans and swine in Shanghai. A comprehensive control strategy may be essential for achieving hepatitis E elimination, especially by identifying high-risk populations to reduce the burden of clinically apparent hepatitis E, in addition to routine HEV surveillance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12879-026-14060-5.

Introduction

Hepatitis E, which is caused by the hepatitis E virus (HEV), is a zoonotic disease primarily transmitted through the fecal-oral route [1]. The majority of HEV infections are asymptomatic or cause only mild illness without jaundice. However, HEV infection can lead to severe illness and even death in patients with chronic liver disease, the elderly, and pregnant women [2]. It is also associated with a range of extrahepatic manifestations [3]. Moreover, HEV infection can become chronic in immunocompromised individuals, potentially leading to liver fibrosis [4]. The Global Burden of Disease study reported an increase in global HEV incidence from 15.7 million to 19.4 million between 1990 and 2021 [5]. During the same time, the notified incidence rate of hepatitis E cases in China increased more than tenfold [6].

To eliminate viral hepatitis as a public health threat, the WHO has targeted a 90% reduction in new hepatitis infections and a 65% reduction in mortality by 2030, compared with the 2015 baseline [7]. In 2010, a hepatitis E vaccine was successfully developed in China [8]. Since 2012, the hepatitis E vaccine has been licensed in China and introduced to multiple countries [9]. However, in countries and regions traditionally endemic for HEV, hepatitis E cases and outbreaks remained increasingly documented [10]. In multiple European countries, such as France, Germany, Italy, and Spain, HEV infection has become increasingly endemic [11, 12]. Furthermore, both the types and numbers of potential animal reservoirs increased in the past two decades [13]. The continually upward trend in hepatitis E incidence has become a crucial public health concern that demands urgent attention in China and globally.

HEV can be classified into four genera, including Paslahepevirus and Rocahepevirus (the latter containing rat-origin HEV), reflecting high diversity and variability [14]. Among these genera, the genus Paslahepevirus comprises eight genotypes (1–8), to which humans and multiple mammals are susceptible [15]. HEV genotypes 1 and 2 infect humans exclusively and have frequently caused large waterborne outbreaks in regions with poor sanitation, such as Africa and South Asia. Genotypes 3 and 4 are primarily responsible for sporadic cases via foodborne transmission in regions including Europe, North America, and East Asia [16]. Genotypes 5 to 8 mainly infect mammals. In 1986, the world’s largest documented HEV outbreak, caused by genotype 1, occurred in northwestern China [17]. With the advancement of urbanization and improvement in sanitation and hygiene, the dominant HEV genotype in China has gradually shifted from genotype 1 (waterborne transmission) to genotype 4 (foodborne transmission) [18, 19]. Additionally, in 2010 and 2018, the first human cases of infection with HEV genotype 3 and Rocahepevirus HEV were reported in Eastern China and Hong Kong, respectively [20, 21]. It further suggested the complex scenario of HEV prevalence in China. Mutations in the dominant HEV genotypes and the emergence of zoonotic transmission pathways may lead to changes in transmission capacity and manifestations. However, the observed increase in notified hepatitis E incidence is likely multifactorial, also reflecting enhanced surveillance sensitivity, expanded diagnostic testing, demographic aging, and improved reporting completeness over time.

Shanghai Municipal Center for Disease Control and Prevention (SCDC) has initiated the establishment of a comprehensive hepatitis E surveillance system that covers viruses, patients, infections, animals such as swine, and potential viral contamination in aquatic products since 2004 [22]. It may promptly monitor the epidemiological patterns, viral mutations, and zoonotic dynamics, while also prevent viral spillover from animals into human environment. Relying on the surveillance system, we have facilitated further understanding of zoonotic transmission, risk factors for human infection, and vaccine immunization strategies [23–26]. In this study, we aim to analyze the hepatitis E notified incidence trends over the past two decades in Shanghai, along with six serological surveys. Furthermore, two periods with high-quality HEV surveillance data (2004–2008 and 2018–2024) were included to analyze the predominant HEV genotypes and subtypes. This study provides insights into the potential drivers of the increase in hepatitis E incidence in Shanghai.

Materials and methods

Surveillance system of hepatitis E

We have implemented a surveillance program for hepatitis E since 2000 in Shanghai, a highly developed metropolis on the eastern coast of China, in SCDC. The surveillance program has been integrated into the Shanghai Infectious Disease Surveillance Program under the authority of the Shanghai Municipal Health Commission. The surveillance system consisted of four parts, including hepatitis E incidence and mortality, HEV genotypes and viral variations, and HEV infections among confirmed hepatitis E cases, community residents, and potential animal reservoirs. Study data were retrieved from the above surveillance program.

Data collection of notified hepatitis E cases

Hepatitis E is classified as one of the national notifiable infectious diseases in China. Diagnosis for confirmed hepatitis E cases refer to Health Industry Standards of People’s Republic of China - Diagnostic criteria for hepatitis E. Hospital physicians register each confirmed patient’s information in the China Information System for Disease Control and Prevention within 24 h. Community physicians then conduct an epidemiological investigation, health education, and a three-month follow-up of each patient and their family members. Hepatitis E incidence data, including demographic information (such as sex, age, date of onset, ZIP code of residential address, etc.) from 2004 to 2024, were retrieved from the National Notifiable Infectious Diseases Reporting System. Annual census data from 2004 to 2024 were obtained from the Shanghai Public Security Bureau. Additionally, recurrent hepatitis E cases were defined as having more than two confirmed episodes with an interval of over six months between onsets, according to the National Notifiable Infectious Diseases Reporting System.

Serological survey and anti-HEV IgG antibody testing

Serological surveys among community residents were conducted in 2001, 2004, 2012, 2014, 2018, and 2024, respectively, which were conducted in four consistent districts, including two national disease surveillance points for viral hepatitis(one urban and one rural district) [27], and two additional districts selected based on similar sociodemographic characteristics (population density, age structure, and income level) to enhance representativeness of the Shanghai population. Each survey employed a two-stage probability proportional to size sampling (PPS) method, which involved randomly selecting two sub-districts within each selected district, and finally one neighborhood community within each selected sub-district, resulting in a total of eight neighborhood communities as survey sites. At each site, we first compiled a complete list of all permanent residents from the local household registration system. Separate sampling frames were then constructed for each of the three age groups (1–4, 5–29, and 30–69 years), and eligible participants were selected from each frame using simple random sampling. All selected individuals were invited to participate after providing written informed consent. Those who initially declined were revisited once; persistent refusals were classified as non‑respondents and excluded. We continued the simple random sampling process from the corresponding age‑specific frame until the quota for each age group was fulfilled. A total of 6,305 participants were enrolled in six surveys. From each participant, a 2 mL venous blood sample was collected. These samples were tested for anti-HEV IgG using an ELISA kit (Genelabs Diagnostics Pte. Ltd., Singapore, 2001–2004; Beijing Wantai Biological Pharmacy Enterprise Co., Ltd., China, 2007 to present). Both kits hold appropriate regulatory certifications for use in China, and all testing procedures were performed according to the manufacturers’ instructions with results interpreted after passing the specified quality controls. The limits of detection for the two kits have been reported as 0.25 WHO units/mL for the Wantai assay and 2.5 WHO units/mL for the Genelabs assay [28].

The ratio of clinical cases to asymptomatic infections (C/A ratio) was calculated as the number of notified hepatitis E cases divided by the estimated number of anti-HEV IgG-positive individuals in the same population and time period, assuming that the vast majority of anti-HEV IgG-positive individuals represent past asymptomatic or subclinical infections. This ratio was used as an exploratory indicator to compare the relative frequency of clinically overt disease across different birth cohorts and time periods, but its interpretation is limited by potential changes in healthcare-seeking behavior, diagnostic intensity, and reporting completeness over time.

Sample collection of humans and animals

The above hepatitis E surveillance program annually collected samples from notified confirmed cases, community residents, and potential animal reservoirs. The sampling was divided into two phases. In phase I (January 1, 2004 – December 31, 2008), the samples primarily consisted of confirmed hepatitis E cases, with their information sourced from notifiable case records and previous studies by the research team [22]. In phase II (January 1, 2018 – December 31, 2024), the samples primarily consisted of confirmed hepatitis E cases, swine bile, and shellfish. No pathogen surveillance was conducted between 2009 and 2017, owing to a temporary reallocation of public health resources toward emerging infectious disease responses. This 9-year gap creates an important discontinuity in the pathogen surveillance data, which should be taken into account when interpreting temporal changes in genotype/subtype distributions between the two surveillance periods (2004–2008 and 2018–2024). Specific details were presented as follows:

A total of 24 hospitals capable of providing hospitalization services for hepatitis E therapy were selected as surveillance sentinels in Shanghai. For confirmed hepatitis E cases, physicians retained the serum samples (at least 0.2 mL) that were collected on the day of admission. Furthermore, local center for disease control and prevention staff collected the cases’ clinical information and laboratory tests, including alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and total bilirubin (TBIL) levels from illness onset, symptoms (e.g., fever, gastrointestinal symptoms), and signs (e.g., dark urine, scleral icterus, liver area discomfort).

A swine slaughterhouse was also selected for surveillance. Gallbladders were collected from five-month-old pigs slaughtered on the sampling day. Swine bile samples were aspirated using disposable sterile syringes and then stored at -20 °C. Approximately 100 to 150 swine bile samples, along with sampling information including sampling dates and sources of the swine, were collected quarterly. Additionally, a total of 120 shellfish samples (e.g., oysters, clams) of various types were collected quarterly from farmers’ markets, supermarkets, and restaurants. The shellfish sample preparation was the same as swine bile sample.

Anti-HEV antibody testing and HEV RNA examination

Serum samples from confirmed HEV cases were tested for anti-HEV IgM and anti-HEV IgG using an ELISA kit (Beijing Wantai Biological Pharmacy Enterprise Co., Ltd., China).

Viral RNA was extracted from serum, swine bile, and shellfish samples using the SSNP-9600 A automated nucleic acid extraction system (Jiangsu Shuoshi Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China). Detection of HEV RNA was performed with a real-time quantitative fluorescent PCR (qPCR) kit (Beijing Applied Biological Technologies Co., Ltd, China) on a LightCycler 480 real-time PCR cycler (Roche Diagnostics, USA). Subsequently, a 150nucleotide fragment within the ORF2 region of HEV (corresponding to positions 6317–6466 nt of the Burmese strain, GenBank accession no. M73218) was amplified by nested RT-PCR following the methodology described previously [29] and subjected to Sanger sequencing, which was performed by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) Co., Ltd.,.

Phylogenetic analysis

For genotyping reference, 29 complete HEV genome sequences covering HEV genotypes 1 through 4 and their subtypes were retrieved from the GenBank database [15]. Sequence alignment and phylogenetic tree reconstruction were performed using MEGA 7.0 software (https://www.megasoftware.net/). Multiple sequence alignment was carried out using the Clustal W method. Genetic distance and nucleotide identity among sequences were calculated using the Kimura 2-parameter model. Phylogenetic trees were reconstructed using the Neighbor-Joining method. The reliability of the inferred phylogenies was assessed using the bootstrap method with 1 000 replications; phylogenetic groupings with bootstrap values greater than 70% were considered sufficiently supported. All the HEV sequences isolated in this study were deposited under the GenBank accession number PX621505-PX621995.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics, analysis of variance (ANOVA), and chi-square test were performed using SPSS 29.0 software (IBM SPSS, Chicago, Illinois, USA). Temporal trend in hepatitis E incidence was analyzed using the Joinpoint Regression Program with Joinpoint 5.4.0 software (https://surveillance.cancer.gov/joinpoint/). A p-value of 0.05 was considered significant for two-sided tests. The geographic layer was conducted by ArcMap 10.1(ESRI, Redlands, California, USA).

Results

Temporal trend of notified hepatitis E cases

A total of 14 071 hepatitis E cases were notified from 2004 to 2024 in Shanghai, comprising 8 580 males (60.98%) with the mean age of 55.21 ± 15.35 years, and 5 491 females (39.02%) with the mean age of 55.21 ± 15.95 years. All the notified cases were sporadic. The average annual notified incidence was calculated to be 3.92 per 100,000 populations. A seasonal peak was observed annually from January to April, accounting for 44.67% (6,285/14,071) of total cases. The incidence of male (3.57/100,000) and urban resident (3.65/100,000) were significantly higher than that of female (2.44/100,000, p < 0.001) and suburban resident (2.71/100,000, p < 0.001), respectively.

From 2004 to 2024, the notified incidence of hepatitis E in Shanghai showed a fluctuating trend (average annual percentage change 1.09%, 95%CI: -1.05% to 3.80%; p = 0.329). One Joinpoint was detected in 2012, with an increase from 2012 to 2024 (annual percentage change, APC = 5.03%, 95%CI: 0.81% to 26.51%; p = 0.044). Further analysis by age group detected one Joinpoint in the incidence trends for the 0–29, 30–59, and over 60 years, respectively. The notified incidence in 0–29 age group demonstrated a significant decreasing trend from 2017 to 2024 (APC=-10.88%, 95%CI: -45.25% to -2.38%; p = 0.009). In contrast, the 30–59 age group experienced a significant increase in the incidence from 2012 to 2024 (APC = 3.94%, 95%CI: 0.45% to 7.54%; p = 0.029). Similarly, a significant increasing trend was observed in the over 60 age group from 2007 to 2024 (APC = 4.10%, 95%CI: 1.78% to 6.47%; p = 0.002). Detailed information from the Joinpoint regression was shown in Fig. 1A.

HEV, hepatitis E virus

From 2004 to 2024, a cohort of 69 hepatitis E cases was identified as recurrent cases with an interval of over six months between onsets. Of them, 65 cases experienced two incidences, while 4 cases had three incidences. The cohort consisted of 40 males and 29 females, with the mean age at first onset of 52.58 ± 12.98 years. The mean interval between episodes was 2.82 ± 2.30 years, with a median interval of 2.11 years (interquartile range, IQR: 1.21, 3.95).

Prevalence of anti-HEV IgG in the serological surveys

A total of six community-based serological surveys were conducted in Shanghai from 2001 to 2024, involving 6,305 community residents. The anti-HEV IgG positivity rate ranged from 14.96% to 26.58% (age-standardized: 18.56% to 34.43%), with peaks observed in 2012, 2014, and 2018. The seropositivity of each survey demonstrated an increasing trend with age, as shown in Table 1. Using the year of 2012 as a cutoff, the anti-HEV IgG positivity rate for the period 2001–2012 was 20.08%, which was significantly lower than the rate of 22.53% for the period 2014–2024 (χ²=5.589, p < 0.05).

Moreover, to further explore the increasing trend in notified incidence among residents aged over 30 years, a birth cohort analysis was conducted using the ratio of clinical cases to asymptomatic infections (C/A ratio). Four birth cohorts were included in the analysis. The annual anti-HEV IgG positivity rates were used to estimate the number of individuals with a history of HEV infection, which primarily comprised asymptomatic infections in the respective years, as illustrated in Fig. 1B.

Over the 20-year observation period, the C/A ratios exhibited a “peak–valley–peak” pattern, with all four birth cohorts showing a similar trend. Among them, the 1961–1970 birth cohort experienced the most pronounced fluctuations. Between 2000 and 2010, the 1971–1980 birth cohort showed a significant decline in the C/A ratio, from 34.01 per 100,000 to 10.94 per 100,000, followed by the 1961–1970 birth cohort. During 2010–2020, the 1961–1970 birth cohort displayed a significant increase in the C/A ratio, rising from 7.52 per 100,000 to 24.41 per 100,000. These fluctuations may reflect changes in healthcare-seeking behavior, diagnostic intensity, or host susceptibility, and should be interpreted with caution.

HEV RNA prevalence in the pathogen surveillance

A total of 1,367 serum samples were collected from confirmed hepatitis E cases. Of the cases, 860 (62.91%) were male, with a mean age of 56.12 ± 14.24 years, and 507 (37.09%) were female, with a mean age of 57.18 ± 15.51 years. Temporally, 142 cases were included in 2004–2008, and 1 225 were included in 2018–2024. Geographically, 603 cases were from central urban regions in Shanghai, while 764 were from suburban regions. In these serum samples, the overall positivity rates for anti-HEV IgM, anti-HEV IgG, and HEV RNA were 78.93% (1,079/1,367), 85.74% (1,172/1,367), and 40.09% (548/1,367), respectively.

From 2018 to 2024, a total of 3,900 swine bile samples were collected, among which 126 tested positive for HEV RNA, yielding a positivity rate of 3.23%. The quarterly distribution of HEV RNA positivity rates was as follows: 7.00% (63/900) in the first quarter, 0.38% (4/1,050) in the second quarter, 1.89% (17/900) in the third quarter, and 4.00% (42/1,050) in the fourth quarter. These rates differed significantly (χ²=75.345, p < 0.001). During the same period, 724 shellfish samples, including oysters and clams, were all tested negative for HEV RNA, as shown in Table 2.

Among the 548 HEV RNA-positive serum samples, 354 HEV 150-nt sequences were obtained by nested PCR (64.60%). In addition, we included 23 HEV sequences from local hepatitis E cases in our previous study in 2004–2008 and 29 reference sequences for analysis. A phylogenetic tree was reconstructed using Neighbor-Joining method. Moreover, based on nucleotide identity with reference sequences, the above 377 HEV sequences were classified into 15 distinct genotypes/subtypes. In the genotype 4 branch, nine subtypes were identified, as shown in Table 3. Subtypes 4a, 4d, and 4e collectively represented 77.93% of all genotype 4 sequences. Among genotype 4 sequences, subtypes 4a, 4d, and 4e together accounted for 77.93%. Subtypes 4a and 4b were most frequently identified in 2004–2008, averaging 45.21% of genotype 4 cases, with subtype 4d rising from 0% to 22.22% over this period. In 2018–2024, subtypes 4d and 4e were more commonly observed, representing 45.58% and 18.03%, respectively. However, because pathogen surveillance was interrupted between 2009 and 2017, this apparent shift in subtype predominance should be interpreted with caution, as it may reflect genuine viral evolution, temporal sampling variation, changes in sequencing success rates, or a combination of these factors. Among the genotype 3 sequences, four belonged to subtype 3a, two belonged to subtype 3j, with one sequence each classified as 3b, 3f, 3 g, and 3 h. Additionally, various HEV subtypes were extensively distributed across districts in Shanghai, with no obvious spatial pattern (Fig. 3). Among the 126 HEV RNA-positive swine bile samples, 114 HEV sequences were successfully obtained by nested RT-PCR, with yearly distributions as follows: 4 in 2020, 69 in 2021, 17 in 2022, 13 in 2023, and 11 in 2024. Of them, 93 swine sequences were classified as genotype 3, and 21 sequences as genotype 4. Nucleotide identity was calculated between these sequences and 304 human HEV sequences collected in 2018–2024. Notably, 24 swine HEV sequences showed 100% nucleotide identity with human HEV sequences over the 150-nt fragment analyzed. These matched pairs were isolated across multiple years (2019–2024) and originated from three suburban districts of Shanghai, suggesting close genetic relatedness between human and swine strains. However, due to the short fragment length, this finding does not confirm direct zoonotic spillover and could also reflect common environmental sources or limited phylogenetic resolution.

Genotype-specific clinical manifestations

A total of 301 genotype 4 cases had available liver function testing results (ALT and/or TBIL). It indicated that 93.36% (281/301) exhibited abnormal ALT levels. The mean value of ALT in the cases collected in 2004–2008 was 1456.88 ± 1435.33 U/L, which was significantly higher than that in the cases in 2018–2024 (972.07 ± 1292.78 U/L; F = 4.96, p < 0.05). However, it did not significantly differ in the mean ALT and TBIL levels, or in the symptoms and signs, among hepatitis E cases infected with subtypes 4a, 4d, or 4e. Nevertheless, this comparison is limited by incomplete clinical data retrieval and should be considered exploratory.

Among the eight genotype 3 cases identified, the majority presented with mild symptoms. Most (7/8) showed abnormal liver function, with mean ALT and TBIL values of 731.50 ± 1056.31 U/L and 41.18 ± 23.51µmol/L, respectively. However, due to the very small number of genotype 3 cases (n = 8) and incomplete laboratory data for some cases, no robust comparison of genotype-specific pathogenicity can be made. The clinical presentation of genotype 3 cases in this study appears generally mild, but this observation should be interpreted with caution. Six cases reported gastrointestinal symptoms such as fatigue and loss of appetite, as well as jaundice. Only two cases presented with fever, and one remained asymptomatic throughout the course of illness. Moreover, epidemiological investigations did not identify links among these genotype 3 cases. All cases denied contact with poultry or livestock. Most cases reported regularly consuming food such as cold dishes, marinated drunken delicacies, raw or lightly marinated seafood, and hot pot. Additionally, one case reported a history of travel prior to the onset of illness.

Discussion

In our study, the notified incidence of hepatitis E in Shanghai demonstrated an increasing trend over the past two decades, consistent with the epidemiological patterns observed both nationally in China and globally [30]. Serological surveys indicated that HEV infection among the general population exhibited an upward trend over time and with advancing age. These findings suggest that sustained HEV infection and transmission may be extensively occurring in Shanghai. Furthermore, they implied that asymptomatic or mild HEV infections might progress to acute symptomatic infections due to multiple contributing factors, including enhanced surveillance and diagnostic capacity, as well as the aging urban population. A methodological caveat warrants mention: the serological surveys used two different ELISA kits (Genelabs for 2001–2004 and Wantai from 2007 onward), and the Wantai assay has a substantially lower limit of detection compared with the Genelabs assay [28, 31]. This suggests that seroprevalence from 2001 to 2004 is likely underestimated, and the apparent increase between 2004 and 2012 may be partly attributable to assay differences. However, the age-specific gradient and the post-2012 trend remain unaffected. This pattern is exemplified by the 1961–1970 birth cohort, which displayed a significantly increasing trend, particularly between 2010 and 2020, and this trend may be partly attributable to early-life exposures, such as the Great Chinese Famine (1959–1961), as previous studies have linked famine exposure to long-term health vulnerabilities [32]. However, this hypothesis requires further investigation with individual-level exposure data and the observed trend could also be influenced by cohort-specific differences in healthcare access and diagnostic testing. Additionally, the peaks and valleys of C/A ratio observed between 2000 and 2020 may reflect a recurring pattern in hepatitis E transmission, potentially driven by population immunity dynamics, changes in viral characteristics, and variations in host susceptibility. However, because anti-HEV IgG reflects cumulative exposure rather than incident infection, these patterns should be interpreted primarily as surveillance signals that warrant further investigation.

Over the past two decades, HEV genotype 4 has remained the predominant strain in Shanghai, with sporadic genotype 3 cases identified since 2018. The mean ALT level among genotype 4 cases was significantly higher during 2004–2008 than in 2018–2024. This shift may reflect expanded diagnostic testing that identifies milder cases, rather than a genuine reduction in viral pathogenicity. Alternatively, changes in host immunity or viral subtype could also contribute, though current data do not allow disentangling these factors. Genotype 3, predominantly reported in Europe and Japan [31, 32], has been sporadically identified in China since 2010, mostly in Jiangsu Province bordering Shanghai [20]. In this study, genotype 3 cases generally presented with mild clinical symptoms, consistent with findings from other settings where genotype 3 has been associated with milder acute illness but with a risk of chronic infection in immunocompromised individuals [31, 32]. This chronic, often subclinical nature raises public health concerns, as silent transmission could further increase hepatitis E incidence. However, because only eight genotype 3 cases were identified in our cohort, our data are insufficient to draw robust conclusions about genotype-specific pathogenicity in the Shanghai population. Although subtyping based on a 150-nt fragment has limited resolution, an apparent shift in circulating genotype 4 subtypes was observed between the two surveillance periods, from predominance of 4a and 4b (2004–2008) to co-circulation of 4d and 4e (2018–2024). However, given the 9-year surveillance gap (2009–2017), this observed shift must be interpreted cautiously. It could reflect genuine viral evolution, but also differences in sampling coverage, case mix, or sequencing success rates across the two periods. Continuous, uninterrupted surveillance will be essential to confirm whether this represents a stable trend in subtype replacement. For genotype 3, observed pathogenicity of its subtypes aligns with findings from Italy [34]. Collectively, these results underscore the need for full-length or longer HEV genomic fragments to improve subtyping resolution and systematically investigate epidemiological characteristics and clinical outcomes associated with distinct HEV genotypes and subtypes. Such efforts are critical for identifying subtypes potentially linked to enhanced virulence or transmissibility.

A notable pattern emerged when comparing genotype distributions between species: genotype 3 predominated in swine samples (81.6% of swine sequences), whereas genotype 4 overwhelmingly predominated in human cases (97.35%). This disparity is consistent with published data from Shanghai. Genotype 3 HEV was first identified in swine in a Shanghai suburb in 2006, and subsequent surveys confirmed its widespread circulation—and even predominance—in local pig herds, with genotype 3 accounting for 68.75% of sequenced swine HEV isolates [33, 34]. In contrast, human genotype 3 infections in Shanghai have only been detected recently, as shown in the present study. Limited consumption of undercooked or raw pork among locals, together with differential host adaptation and transmission efficiency between genotypes, may have delayed efficient zoonotic transmission of genotype 3 HEV from swine to humans until recent years. Nevertheless, HEV RNA was detected in 3.23% of swine bile samples collected in Shanghai, with the highest positivity rate observed in winter and spring, consistent with the seasonal distribution of human hepatitis E cases in the city. Both genotype 3 and genotype 4 were identified in swine bile samples. Notably, 21.05% of the swine-derived HEV sequences exhibited 100% nucleotide identity with human strains over the 150-nt fragment. While this close genetic relatedness is compatible with possible zoonotic transmission or shared exposure sources, the short sequence length precludes definitive conclusions. Future studies employing full-length genomic sequencing and detailed epidemiological tracing are needed to clarify the direction and frequency of cross-species transmission. Although Shanghai is an international port city where the introduction of imported genotype 3 strains remains a possibility, neighboring Jiangsu province has also reported the presence of genotype 3 [20, 35, 36]. In this study, no HEV RNA was detected in commercially available shellfish products in Shanghai. This contrasts with previous studies conducted in Bohai Bay, China, and along the Atlantic coast of Portugal, where HEV RNA was detected in shellfish and showed high nucleotide identity with local human and swine HEV sequences [37, 38]. Possible reasons include smaller sample size, differences in shellfish species, depuration practices, or lower environmental contamination levels in Shanghai’s supply chain. In conclusion, these findings underscore the need for continued long-term surveillance to monitor the shifts in the HEV pathogen spectrum, assess the risk of zoonotic transmission, and evaluate potential implications for local hepatitis E incidence.

An important limitation of the pathogen surveillance component is the interruption between 2009 and 2017. This gap precludes a continuous assessment of genotype and subtype dynamics over the full 20-year period and limits our ability to determine whether the observed differences between 2004 and 2008 and 2018–2024 represent a gradual evolutionary process or abrupt changes related to sampling or methodological factors. Ongoing, uninterrupted surveillance is therefore critical for robustly tracking HEV genetic evolution and zoonotic risk.

In addition to this limitation, there are four other limitations in this study. First, given that hepatitis E is an acute infectious disease, external risk factors vary annually, along with the use of different ELISA kits across survey years, contributing to heterogeneity in seroprevalence estimates. Second, symptom onset dates were retrospectively collected, introducing recall bias. Third, HEV subtyping and human–swine sequence comparison was based on a 150-nt ORF2 fragment. Although this region is widely used for genotyping, its limited length restricts the resolution for robustly inferring zoonotic transmission events. Longer genomic fragments or full-length sequences, combined with epidemiological trace-back investigations, would be required to confirm spillover. In addition, the clinical severity analysis comparing HEV genotypes and subtypes was based on incomplete laboratory data (ALT and TBIL were not available for all cases) and a very small number of genotype 3 cases (n = 8). Therefore, our observations regarding genotype-specific clinical manifestations should be interpreted as preliminary and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Despite these limitations, leveraging an established multi-dimensional surveillance system and a 20-year observation period, this study robustly characterizes hepatitis E incidence and HEV infection dynamics in human and swine populations in Shanghai.

Conclusion

The notified incidence of hepatitis E in Shanghai has exhibited a sustained upward trend since 2012, paralleled by serological evidence of ongoing HEV infection in the general population. While this trend may partly reflect a combination of true epidemiological changes and enhanced surveillance sensitivity, including expanded diagnostic testing, increased clinical awareness, improved reporting systems, and demographic aging. Furthermore, the persistent predominance of HEV genotype 4 in humans, coupled with the high genetic relatedness between local human and swine HEV sequences, suggests potential zoonotic transmission and may have implications for viral infectivity and pathogenicity. Collectively, our findings underscore the importance of implementing comprehensive, evidence-based control strategies tailored to the local epidemiological context. In particular, targeted surveillance and proactive intervention among high-risk populations are essential to mitigate the clinical burden of hepatitis E and to advance progress toward the goal of hepatitis E elimination in Shanghai.
