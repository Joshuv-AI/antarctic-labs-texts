Characterization of Drug–Drug Interactions for Remdesivir, an Intravenous Antiviral for SARS‐CoV‐2, in Healthy Participants
Authors: Jack Chang, Aryun Kim, Rita Humeniuk, Naveed A. Shaik, Helen Winter, Chi‐Chi Peng, Deqing Xiao, Mazin Abdelghany, Joe Llewellyn, Santosh Davies, Kandace Golden, Shuguang Chen, Robert H. Hyland, Luzelena Caro
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRemdesivir is an intravenous antiviral approved worldwide for the treatment of COVID‐19 in adults and children. Remdesivir, as a prodrug inhibitor of RNA polymerase, undergoes intracellular activation to form the active metabolite along with 2 inactive circulating metabolites, GS‐704277 and GS‐441524. Based on drug–drug interaction (DDI) risks identified in vitro, three Phase 1 crossover DDI studies were conducted to evaluate remdesivir as a potential (i) object, (ii) inhibitor, or (iii) inducer of enzymes and transporters. As an object of organic anion transporting polypeptide (OATP) 1B1/1B3 and P‐glycoprotein inhibition (cyclosporine A), remdesivir and GS‐704277 exposures increased 2‐fold and 3‐fold, respectively, while GS‐441524 was unchanged. Increased exposures were not clinically relevant as remdesivir and GS‐704277 exposures remained transient (approximately 1‐h half‐life) and were unlikely to affect the formation of the active metabolite. As an object of strong cytochrome P450 (CYP) 3A induction (carbamazepine), exposure changes of remdesivir and its metabolites were not clinically significant. As an inhibitor, single‐dose remdesivir did not impact pitavastatin (OATP1B1/1B3 substrate) exposures but increased midazolam (CYP3A substrate) C max (29%) to a greater extent than AUCinf (20%). Given that remdesivir exposures are transient, CYP3A inhibitory effects were similarly transient and were not clinically relevant. As an inducer with midazolam (CYP3A substrate), multiple‐dose remdesivir did not decrease exposures but rather transiently increased midazolam exposures to a similar degree as observed in the inhibition study. Remdesivir may be coadministered with other medications without restrictions.

Introduction

SARS‐CoV‐2 was first identified in November 2019 and subsequently spread globally, resulting in the COVID‐19 pandemic [1, 2]. The World Health Organization declared a Public Health Emergency of International Concern due to this novel coronavirus on January 30, 2020 [2]. From 2020 to 2023, COVID‐19 caused significant morbidity and mortality worldwide, with 765 million confirmed cases, 6.9 million reported deaths, and continued evolution of SARS‐CoV‐2 [3].

Remdesivir (Veklury) is an intravenous, once‐daily, adenosine analog nucleotide prodrug inhibitor of SARS‐CoV‐2 viral RNA polymerase. The clinical dosing regimen for remdesivir is a single loading dose of 200 mg on Day 1, followed by maintenance doses of 100 mg once daily from Day 2 onward, for a total duration of up to 10 days. Currently, remdesivir is approved in > 50 countries worldwide for the treatment of mild, moderate, and severe COVID‐19 in adults and pediatric patients [4, 5]. Remdesivir first received emergency use authorization by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in May 2020, for use as an antiviral treatment against COVID‐19. Subsequently, FDA approval was granted in October 2020 for the treatment of hospitalized patients, and in early 2022, remdesivir was approved for the treatment of nonhospitalized patients. As part of the approvals in the United States and the European Union, postmarketing requirements mandated the conduct of clinical studies to evaluate the impact of drug–drug interactions (DDIs) with remdesivir.

Remdesivir is extensively metabolized to the active metabolite GS‐443902 through an intracellular metabolic pathway, resulting in the sequential appearance of 2 inactive metabolites, GS‐704277 and GS‐441524, in plasma [6, 7, 8]. In a sequential manner, (i) remdesivir leads to the formation of the intermediate metabolite GS‐704277 in the bloodstream and intracellularly, (ii) which leads to the intracellular formation of a nucleoside analog monophosphate, (iii) which is further phosphorylated to the active triphosphate GS‐443902, which can be efficiently converted back to the monophosphate, and (iv) dephosphorylation of the monophosphate results in the nucleoside analog GS‐441524, which is not as efficiently rephosphorylated back to the monophosphate. GS‐443902 is only detectable intracellularly by using peripheral blood mononuclear cells, while remdesivir, GS‐704277, and GS‐441524 are detectable in plasma [9].

Remdesivir and GS‐704277 are primarily eliminated by metabolism, and GS‐441524 is eliminated renally [4]. Following intravenous administration, remdesivir is approximately 80% metabolized by carboxylesterase 1 (CES‐1) with the enzymes cathepsin A (CatA) and cytochrome P450 (CYP) 3A, each contributing approximately 10%; GS‐704277 is metabolized by histidine triad nucleotide‐binding protein 1 (HINT1) [6]. Remdesivir and GS‐704277 are quickly detected in plasma, with peak concentrations occurring at 0.67 and 0.75 h, respectively, following a 0.5‐h intravenous infusion. Plasma exposures of both remdesivir and GS‐704277 are transient as elimination half‐lives are 1 and 1.3 h, respectively, showing no accumulation with once‐daily dosing. For GS‐441524, peak plasma concentrations occur later, at 1.5–2.0 h, and its elimination half‐life is significantly longer, at 27 h.

In vitro, remdesivir is a substrate for CYP3A as well as the organic anion transport polypeptide 1B1 (OATP1B1) and P‐glycoprotein (P‐gp) transporters. Its metabolite, GS‐704277, is also a substrate for OATP1B1 and OATP1B3, while GS‐441524 is not [4, 10, 11]. In vitro studies have shown that remdesivir has an inhibitory effect on CYP3A, UDP glucuronosyltransferase 1 family polypeptide A1 (UGT1A1), OATP1B1, OATP1B3, and multidrug and toxin extrusion protein 1 (MATE1) [4]. Remdesivir was not shown to be an in vitro inducer of CYP450 enzymes; however, a clinical assessment for both the potential inhibition as well as the potential induction of CYP3A was specifically requested by certain regulators. All the DDI liabilities identified in vitro were primarily associated with the remdesivir prodrug; no other risks, including inhibitory interactions for GS‐704277 or GS‐441524, were identified [4].

Three separate Phase 1 studies were conducted in healthy participants to evaluate (i) remdesivir as a potential object of OATP1B1/1B3 and P‐gp inhibition and CYP3A induction, (ii) remdesivir as a potential inhibitor of CYP3A and OATP1B1/1B3, and (iii) remdesivir as a potential CYP3A inducer. These clinical DDI studies aimed to elucidate the clinical relevance of the enzyme‐ and transporter‐mediated DDIs that were identified to be the highest risks for remdesivir in vitro.

Methods

Study Design, Oversight, and Participants

All study protocols, consent forms, administrative amendments, and advertisements were approved by an institutional review board before the studies were initiated (Study 1: Midlands Independent Review Board [Lenexa, KS, USA], approved February 7, 2022; Study 2: Advarra Institutional Review Board [Columbia, MD, USA], approved January 10, 2024; Study 3: Advarra Institutional Review Board [Columbia, MD, USA], approved April 5, 2024). The studies were conducted at multiple centers located in the United States under a US investigational new drug application in accordance with the International Council for Harmonisation Guidelines for Good Clinical Practice and the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent. Eligible participants were healthy male or nonpregnant, nonlactating female participants aged 18–45 years with a body mass index (BMI) between 19 and 30 kg/m2. Inclusion criteria were based on an electrocardiogram (ECG) with no clinically relevant abnormalities as assessed by the investigator, normal kidney function (estimated creatinine clearance ≥ 90 mL/min using the Cockroft‐Gault method), normal liver function (as determined by alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, alkaline phosphatase, and total bilirubin within normal limits), nonsmoking (≥ 90 days), and determined by the investigator to be in good health based on their medical history and physical examination. Full inclusion and exclusion criteria can be found in Methods S1.

Remdesivir as an Object of OATP1B1/1B3 and P‐gp Inhibition and Strong CYP3A Induction (Study 1)

Study 1 was a Phase 1, open‐label, fixed‐sequence, crossover study conducted to evaluate the DDI between remdesivir and cyclosporine A or carbamazepine. Cyclosporine A (Novartis Pharmaceuticals Corporation, East Hanover, NJ, USA) was used as an index precipitant to evaluate the effect of OATP1B1/1B3 and P‐gp inhibition, while carbamazepine (Torrent Pharma Inc., Basking Ridge, NJ, USA) was used as an index precipitant to evaluate the effect of strong CYP3A induction on the pharmacokinetics (PK) of remdesivir (Jubilant HollisterStier LLC, Spokane, WA, USA). Participants were administered single‐dose remdesivir 100 mg (the maintenance dose level) as a 30‐min infusion, either alone or coadministered with either oral cyclosporine A or carbamazepine (Table S1). In the study treatment sequence, remdesivir was first administered alone with a subsequent 3‐day washout period, followed by remdesivir administered 1 h after a single dose of cyclosporine A 400 mg and a subsequent 3‐day washout period. Carbamazepine was then administered with dose titration up to 300 mg twice daily under fed conditions for 25 days. On the 26th day of carbamazepine dosing, remdesivir was first administered under fasted conditions in the morning, then followed by carbamazepine administration with food in the morning and evening. Under fed conditions, carbamazepine was then tapered down in dose over 4 days.

Remdesivir as a Precipitant of CYP3A and OATP1B1/1B3 Inhibition (Study 2)

Study 2 was a Phase 1, open‐label, multiple‐cohort, fixed‐sequence, crossover study in which 2 cohorts evaluated the DDI between remdesivir (Jubilant HollisterStier LLC, Spokane, WA, USA) and midazolam (Padagis US LLC, Allegan, MI, USA) or pitavastatin (Kowa Pharmaceutical Co. Ltd., Montgomery, AL, USA). Midazolam and pitavastatin were used as probe CYP3A and OATP1B1/1B3 substrates, respectively, to evaluate the effect of remdesivir as a potential inhibitor. Participants were administered single doses of oral midazolam and pitavastatin, either alone or coadministered with a single dose of remdesivir 200 mg (the loading dose level) as a 30‐min infusion (Table S1). In 1 cohort, midazolam 2.5 mg was first administered alone, followed by remdesivir coadministered (at the end of infusion) with midazolam 2.5 mg; a washout period of 1 day occurred between midazolam doses. In another cohort, pitavastatin 2 mg was first administered alone, followed by remdesivir coadministered (at the end of infusion) with pitavastatin 2 mg; a washout period of 2 days occurred between pitavastatin doses. All doses of remdesivir, midazolam, and pitavastatin were administered under fasted conditions.

Remdesivir as a Precipitant of CYP3A Induction (Study 3)

Study 3 was a Phase 1, open‐label, fixed‐sequence, crossover study that evaluated the DDI between multiple‐dose remdesivir (Jubilant HollisterStier LLC, Spokane, WA, USA) and single‐dose midazolam (Padagis US LLC, Allegan, MI, USA). Midazolam was used as a probe CYP3A substrate to evaluate the effect of remdesivir as a potential inducer. Participants were administered single doses of oral midazolam, either alone or coadministered with remdesivir 100 mg (the maintenance dose level) as a 30‐min intravenous infusion following multiple‐dose administration of the clinical remdesivir regimen (Table S1). In the study treatment sequence, midazolam 2.5 mg was first administered alone on Day 1, followed by the clinical remdesivir regimen for 9 days (200 mg loading dose on 1 day, followed by 8 days of 100 mg once daily), followed by remdesivir 100 mg coadministered (at the end of infusion) with midazolam 2.5 mg on Day 11. There was a period of 10 days between midazolam doses to allow for multiple‐dose administration of remdesivir to its maximal inductive state.

Pharmacokinetic Assessments and Analysis

In Study 1, intensive PK sampling occurred predose (≤ 5 min before dose) and 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1, 1.5, 2, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24, 48, 72, and 96 h postdose following each remdesivir administration. In Study 2, intensive PK sampling occurred predose (≤ 5 min before dose) and postdose following each administration of oral midazolam (0.5, 1, 2, 4, 6, 10, 12, 16, and 24 h postdose) and pitavastatin (0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 6, 12, 24, 36, and 48 h postdose). In Study 3, intensive PK sampling occurred predose (≤ 5 min before dose) and 0.25, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, and 24 h postdose, following each administration of oral midazolam.

Blood samples in all 3 studies were collected and processed to plasma for PK analysis. Briefly, blood was collected into tubes containing K2EDTA, and plasma samples were obtained by centrifugation. To avoid carboxylesterase‐induced degradation of remdesivir, select plasma samples were treated with a 20% aqueous formic acid solution (100:8). Samples were stored at −70°C until analysis. Plasma concentrations of remdesivir, GS‐704277, and GS‐441524 were determined in all 3 studies using a high‐performance liquid chromatography with tandem mass spectrometry method, previously validated at QPS LLC (Newark, DE, USA) [12]. Plasma concentrations of the probe substrates and metabolites (midazolam, 1′‐OH‐midazolam, and pitavastatin) in Studies 2 and 3 were also quantified using validated and commercially available methods. A description of bioanalytical methods is provided in Methods S1.

PK parameters were estimated using noncompartmental methods using a linear‐up/log‐down method for area under the concentration‐time curve (AUC) estimation (Phoenix WinNonlin 8.2; Certara Inc., USA). PK parameters assessed in all studies included maximum observed plasma concentration (C max), time of C max occurrence (T max), AUC from time zero to the last quantifiable concentration (AUClast), AUC extrapolated to infinity (AUCinf), and terminal elimination half‐life (t 1/2), as applicable.

Safety

Safety assessments included treatment‐emergent adverse events (TEAEs) and serious adverse events (SAEs) coded using the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities, version 26.0, review of clinical laboratory analyses, physical examinations, vital signs, ECGs, and concomitant medications.

Statistical Analysis

All studies were designed to have 80% (Study 2) or 90% (Studies 1 and 3) power to conclude a lack of change in PK parameters (C max, AUClast, and AUCinf) by means of equivalence testing (2‐sided 90% confidence interval [CI] for the geometric least squares mean [GLSM] ratio of test vs. reference) [13, 14]. The required number of evaluable participants was n = 7 in Study 1, n = 16 (midazolam cohort) or n = 19 (pitavastatin cohort) in Study 2, and n = 12 in Study 3. The prespecified no‐effect bounds were chosen based on the ability to detect meaningful changes in PK parameters. The prespecified no‐effect bounds were 0.70–1.43 in Study 1 (remdesivir as an object), 0.80–1.25 in Study 2 (remdesivir as an inhibitor), and 0.70–1.43 in Study 3 (remdesivir as an inducer).

For all studies, the PK analysis set included all participants who received ≥ 1 dose of the study drug and had ≥ 1 nonmissing postdose concentration. The safety population included all participants who received ≥ 1 dose of the study drug. Participant demographic and baseline characteristics, plasma PK, and safety data (i.e., incidence of TEAEs and laboratory abnormalities) were summarized by treatment using descriptive statistics. Statistical comparisons of the PK parameters (C max, AUClast, and AUCinf) were performed for each analyte of interest using a mixed‐effects analysis of variance model to obtain point estimates of the GLSM ratio and corresponding 2‐sided 90% CIs. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS software (version 9.4).

Results

Participant Demographic and Baseline Characteristics

In all 3 studies, most participants were White, with a similar age and BMI range across cohorts. In Study 1, 9 participants were randomized and 8 completed the study (1 participant prematurely exited during the carbamazepine dosing period due to scheduling conflicts). In Study 2, 19 participants were randomized to the remdesivir with midazolam cohort and all participants completed the study, while 23 participants were randomized to the remdesivir with pitavastatin cohort and 20 completed the study (2 participants withdrew due to adverse events (AEs) that were not study drug related; 1 participant withdrew consent). In Study 3, 14 participants were randomized and all participants completed the study (Table 1).

Safety

All study participants were included in the safety analysis. Remdesivir was well tolerated in all 3 studies. No SAEs, Grade 4 AEs, or deaths were reported in any study (Tables S6–S8).

Remdesivir as an Object of OATP1B1/1B3 and P‐gp Inhibition and Strong CYP3A Induction (Study 1)

The majority of AEs were Grade 1 or 2 in severity. One Grade 3 AE was reported (hepatic enzyme increased) following treatment with multiple‐dose carbamazepine alone; this AE was considered related to carbamazepine and resolved 5 days post discharge. AEs were reported in 2 out of 9 participants (22.2%) with remdesivir alone, 7 out of 9 participants (77.8%) with remdesivir plus cyclosporine A, 9 out of 9 participants (100%) with carbamazepine alone during dose up‐titration, 1 out of 8 participants (12.5%) with remdesivir plus carbamazepine, and 4 out of 8 participants (50.0%) with carbamazepine alone during dose tapering. AEs were most frequently reported after treatment with carbamazepine alone and remdesivir plus cyclosporine A. Dizziness was the most frequently reported AE across all treatments and occurred in 5 participants treated with carbamazepine alone. No individual AE (by preferred term) was reported in ≥ 2 participants following remdesivir alone or remdesivir with carbamazepine.

Remdesivir as a Precipitant of CYP3A and OATP1B1/1B3 Inhibition (Study 2)

All AEs were Grade 1 in severity. Four participants (10.3%) who received remdesivir experienced an AE, and of these, 2 (5.1%) were considered related to the study drug. Among remdesivir‐treated participants, insomnia was the only AE reported in ≥ 2 participants.

Remdesivir as a Precipitant of CYP3A Induction (Study 3)

The majority of AEs were Grade 1 in severity. One Grade 2 AE was reported (hepatic enzyme increased) that was considered related to the study drug. AEs were reported for 7 participants (50.0%), with hepatic enzyme increased being the most reported TEAE, occurring in 5 participants (35.7%).

Discussion

Clinical DDI studies were conducted to assess remdesivir and its circulating metabolites, GS‐704277 and GS‐441524, as potential objects or precipitants of enzyme or transporter‐mediated DDIs. Based on in vitro data, remdesivir was identified as a substrate of CYP3A, OATP1B1, and P‐gp, while GS‐704277 was an OATP1B1/1B3 substrate. As a potential precipitant, only the remdesivir analyte was identified as an inhibitor of CYP3A, UGT1A1, OATP1B1/1B3, and MATE1 in vitro [5]. GS‐704277 and GS‐441524 did not have any in vitro DDI liabilities as a precipitant.

Study 1 evaluated remdesivir and its metabolites as an object of OATP1B1/1B3 and P‐gp inhibition and strong CYP3A induction. Consistent with in vitro data, inhibition of OATP1B1/1B3 and P‐gp by cyclosporine A increased plasma PK exposures of remdesivir and GS‐704277 but not GS‐441524. Changes in plasma exposures of remdesivir and GS‐704277 are unlikely to affect the formation of the intracellular active metabolite GS‐443902, as evidenced by the unchanged plasma exposures of GS‐441524 (GS‐441524 is formed as one of the final steps in the remdesivir metabolism pathway and is formed from the same nucleoside analog monophosphate that leads to the formation of the active metabolite). Despite 2‐fold and 3‐fold increases, respectively, in remdesivir and GS‐704277 exposures, these exposures remain transient given the short plasma half‐life of approximately 1 h for both analytes regardless of cyclosporine A coadministration. Clinical relevance of the DDI study results was based on the totality of remdesivir clinical data, as well as regulatory guidance related to the interpretation of DDI study results [15]. In terms of clinical data, the exposure increases reported herein were within the broad range (3‐fold) of exposures observed in Phase 3 studies, which established the efficacy and safety profile of remdesivir, such as the REDPINE study in hospitalized COVID‐19 patients with severe kidney impairment (remdesivir: n = 163) [4, 16]. Moreover, the degree of increase was well below exposures of remdesivir and metabolites observed in a Phase 1 thorough QT study, where single supratherapeutic doses of 600 mg (6‐fold higher) were well tolerated without serious adverse effects [17]. Thus, increased remdesivir and GS‐704277 exposures are not considered clinically relevant and no remdesivir dose adjustment is recommended with inhibitors of OATP1B1/1B3 and P‐gp.

Strong induction of CYP3A by carbamazepine did not impact the PK exposures of remdesivir, GS‐704277, and GS‐441524 in a clinically significant manner, since none of these analytes were decreased by more than 20%. Plasma half‐lives for remdesivir and its metabolites were also unchanged following multiple days of carbamazepine dosing, consistent with the minimal role CYP3A (~10%) plays in the metabolism of remdesivir and the lack of CYP3A involvement for its metabolites. As a result, remdesivir can be coadministered with strong CYP3A inducers without concern for loss of efficacy. The lack of clinically relevant changes in PK exposures of remdesivir and its metabolites following carbamazepine coadministration may also indicate that CES enzymes are not inducible by carbamazepine in a clinically significant manner. Carbamazepine has been shown to induce the expression of drug‐metabolizing enzymes, transporters, and nuclear receptors in human hepatocyte studies, including multiple CYP450 genes and pregnane X receptor (PXR) [18, 19]. PXR in turn regulates drug‐metabolizing enzymes in the liver and intestine, including CES1 and CES2 [20]. Since the majority of remdesivir metabolism (80%) is via CES1, the absence of a clinically relevant change in PK exposures of remdesivir and its metabolites following carbamazepine coadministration indicates that CES1 activity is not altered in a clinically significant manner, even at steady‐state exposures of carbamazepine.

Study 2 evaluated remdesivir as a potential inhibitor of CYP3A and OATP1B1/1B3 using the highest clinical dose of remdesivir at 200 mg, the loading dose level. Following coadministration of single‐dose remdesivir 200 mg with midazolam (sensitive CYP3A substrate), midazolam exposures were increased with a greater impact on C max (29% increase, outside no‐effect bounds) compared with AUCinf (20% increase, within no‐effect bounds). Differing effects on midazolam C max versus AUCinf are consistent with the transient nature of remdesivir given its short 1‐h half‐life; thus, the CYP3A inhibitory effects decline with decreasing plasma exposures of remdesivir. This is further supported by the lack of change in the parent‐to‐metabolite (midazolam‐to‐1′‐OH‐midazolam) ratio of plasma molar concentrations when administered with or without remdesivir. Following coadministration of single‐dose remdesivir 200 mg with pitavastatin (sensitive OATP1B1/1B3 substrate), no clinically relevant changes in pitavastatin exposures were observed. Consequently, remdesivir is not a clinically relevant inhibitor of CYP3A or OATP1B1/1B3 and may be safely coadministered with CYP3A and OATP1B1/1B3 substrates.

Study 3 evaluated remdesivir as a potential inducer of CYP3A using the full 10‐day regimen of a 200 mg loading dose followed by daily maintenance doses of 100 mg. Following multiple‐dose remdesivir 100 mg, coadministration with midazolam (sensitive CYP3A substrate) did not result in reduced exposures of midazolam, thus demonstrating that remdesivir is not a CYP3A inducer. Rather, exposures of midazolam were increased by a similar degree to what was observed in Study 2, which was designed to definitively assess remdesivir as a potential CYP3A inhibitor under the worst‐case scenario by using the highest clinical dose level of 200 mg. Similar to Study 2, midazolam exposures were increased in Study 3 with a greater impact on C max (45% increase, outside no‐effect bounds) compared with AUCinf (30% increase, within no‐effect bounds), displaying the transient nature of remdesivir exposures and the associated weak inhibition of CYP3A. Notably, while the magnitude of increase by the GLSM ratio point estimate appears larger with multiple‐dose remdesivir 100 mg (Study 3) than with single‐dose remdesivir 200 mg (Study 2), these midazolam results cannot be compared head to head as the studies were statistically powered using different no‐effect bounds. As the definitive inhibition study, Study 2 was powered with a larger sample size, using the more conservative 0.80–1.25 bounds, and evaluated the higher remdesivir dose level of 200 mg. Study 3, the induction study, was powered with a smaller sample size, using broader 0.70–1.43 bounds, and evaluated the lower remdesivir dose level of 100 mg. Moreover, there is no mechanism by which a greater magnitude of CYP3A inhibition would be shown with multiple‐dose remdesivir as (i) remdesivir exposures do not accumulate with daily dosing [6] and (ii) in vitro experiments have concluded that remdesivir and its metabolites are not time‐dependent inhibitors of any CYP enzymes (data on file). Like in Study 2, an exploratory analysis of midazolam‐to‐1′‐OH‐midazolam ratios of plasma molar concentrations did not result in notable differences between midazolam alone versus midazolam with remdesivir, further illustrating that there are no clinically relevant differences in the CYP3A metabolism of midazolam with multiple‐dose remdesivir.

Remdesivir was also identified as an in vitro inhibitor of UGT1A1 and MATE1, and although clinical studies were not conducted, a reasonable estimation for the likelihood of clinically relevant DDIs can be made by comparing the in vitro DDI data and extrapolating the clinical DDI results for enzymes and transporters that hold a similar degree of in vitro risk. In vitro evaluation of remdesivir as an inhibitor showed a similar DDI risk between UGT1A1 and CYP3A enzymes and between MATE1 and OATP1B1/1B3 transporters, as evidenced by similar half‐maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values and guidance threshold calculations [15]. Because Study 2 concluded that remdesivir is not a clinically relevant inhibitor of CYP3A or OATP1B1/1B3, clinically relevant inhibition of UGT1A1 or MATE1 is not anticipated. The lack of clinically relevant UGT1A1 interactions is further supported by no observation of increased bilirubin, an endogenous substrate of UGT1A1, in clinical studies with remdesivir [4]. Additionally, remdesivir concentrations at the site of MATE1 transporters in the kidney are anticipated to be lower compared with OATP1B1/1B3 in the liver (for which no clinical DDI was observed) given that remdesivir undergoes extensive metabolism primarily in the liver.

The DDI profiles of remdesivir and its metabolites GS‐704277 and GS‐441524 were comprehensively evaluated as part of a rolling clinical development program following initial approval during the COVID‐19 pandemic. Overall, the safety profiles of remdesivir and probe drugs were unchanged with coadministration and they were well tolerated. Remdesivir and its metabolites were not identified as either objects or precipitants of clinically relevant DDIs. Remdesivir can be used concomitantly with inhibitors of OATP1B1/1B3 and P‐gp, inducers of CYP3A, and substrates of CYP3A, UGT1A1, OATP1B1/1B3, or MATE1 without dose adjustment. Remdesivir may be coadministered with other medications without restrictions.
