Study on the evolutionary mechanism and synergistic control technology of floor heave in deep high-stress roadways
Authors: Caiyun Yin, Long Yin, Jan Han, Yajun Zhang, Leiyu Gu, Jinchuan Chen, Zhiyu Gao, Yunfeng Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo address floor heave control in deep high-stress roadways, this study established a slip-line field model of the roadway floor based on plastic mechanics. The quantitative relationships between floor failure depth, internal friction angle, and coal seam parameters were derived, and the linear positive correlation between floor heave magnitude and both the rib-side limit equilibrium zone width and floor failure depth was clarified. An engineering case from the tailgate of Panel 1802 in Xinzhuang Coal Mine revealed four-stage failure characteristics of high-stress roadway floor heave. Field measurements showed that the influence range of advance abutment stress extended to approximately 90 m, and a 109° deflection in the azimuth of principal stress σ1 intensified shear slip in the floor. The vertical stress peak of 15.2 MPa at the floor corners triggered initial failure, while the continuous increase in deep horizontal stress to 7.2 MPa was the main cause of floor heave. The proposed “shallow pressure relief-deep anchoring” collaborative control system reduced vertical stress peaks by 33%–38% through pressure-relief grooves and decreased the horizontal stress concentration peak by 60% to 4.7 MPa through bottom-corner cable anchoring, reducing floor heave from 510 mm to 150 mm. Numerical simulations and field monitoring confirmed that this strategy transformed the plastic failure pattern from inverted triangular composite failure to localized shear failure at groove shoulders by inducing bimodal stress redistribution and reinforcing the surrounding rock structure. The roof-to-floor convergence stabilized at 37.4 cm, providing both theoretical foundation and engineering paradigm for floor heave control in deep roadways.Keywords: High-stress roadway, Floor heave, Slip line, Synergistic controlSubject terms: Geology, Petrology

Introduction

With the continuous increase in the depth of mineral resource extraction, the stability of roadways in high-stress environments has become increasingly prominent1. Among these issues, floor heave, as a typical manifestation of roadway surrounding rock instability, poses a significant threat to the safe production and efficient mining of mines. Floor heave refers to the upward bulging or plastic deformation of the roadway floor caused by factors such as surrounding rock stress, mining disturbances, and geological conditions2,3. Statistics show that over 60% of roadways in deep coal mines in China experience varying degrees of floor heave, leading to the failure of support structures, reduction of roadway cross-sections, and even triggering secondary disasters such as roof collapse and rib spalling4,5. Therefore, an in-depth exploration of the mechanical mechanisms, morphological characteristics, and control technologies of floor heave holds significant theoretical and engineering importance for ensuring the safe extraction of deep resources.

The formation of floor heave is the result of the coupling of multiple factors, and its mechanism research primarily revolves around stress field evolution, rock mass mechanical behavior, and environmental factors. Firstly, the stress-driven mechanism is investigated. In high-stress environments, roadway excavation disrupts the original stress equilibrium of the surrounding rock, causing the floor strata to endure the superposition of vertical stress concentration and horizontal tectonic stress6,7. When the stress exceeds the strength of the rock mass, shear failure or plastic flow occurs in the floor, leading to floor heave8,9. Secondly, the mechanical response of the rock mass is studied. The physical and mechanical properties of the floor strata directly influence the mode of floor heave10,11. Weak strata are prone to plastic extrusion12,13, while hard strata are dominated by fracturing and interlayer dislocation14,15. For example, the softening of mudstone upon water contact reduces its shear strength, accelerating the development of floor heave16,17, whereas sandstone may experience brittle failure due to crack propagation18. Lastly, the dynamic disturbance effects are examined. Dynamic loads from blasting vibrations and mechanical excavation can induce fatigue damage in the rock mass, reducing the bearing capacity of the floor19,20. Additionally, the impact waves generated by the collapse of the goaf roof further exacerbate the dynamic response of floor heave21,22.

The floor heave in high-stress roadways is, in terms of mechanical essence, a composite instability phenomenon involving stress-dominated plastic flow and shear slip23,24. As deep rock masses are situated in environments with high tectonic stress or mining-induced stress, the deviatoric stress in the surrounding rock increases sharply after roadway excavation25,26. The floor strata (particularly layered soft rock) undergo significant dilation and creep upon exceeding their yield strength. Meanwhile, under high-stress compression, the two sidewalls move inward toward the roadway, creating a strong squeezing and wedging effect at the floor corners, forcing the floor strata to experience shear slip along bedding planes or overall bending buckling27,28. This results in characteristics such as large deformation, rapid displacement, asymmetry, and pronounced rheological persistence.

The control of floor heave in high-stress roadways should follow the synergistic principle of rib control, corner stabilization, pressure relief, and surface sealing. The core is to reinforce the roadway ribs with high-strength bolts or cables to restrain rib convergence and cut off the lateral driving force of floor heave29. At the same time, grouting reinforcement or large-diameter corner cables should be installed at key floor-corner positions to improve the shear strength of the floor strata and constrain plastic flow. For severe floor heave30,31, pressure-relief grooves or deep-hole blasting pressure relief can be combined to transfer high-stress concentration zones, thereby controlling floor deformation within a safe range and achieving long-term stability of the roof-rib-floor surrounding rock system32,33.

Based on the above analysis, the primary limitations of conventional research on the mechanisms and control methods of floor heave under high stress are twofold. First, there is an insufficient understanding of the stress evolution pattern, with a lack of effective investigation into the mechanisms of stress concentration and redistribution under high-stress conditions. Second, floor heave control predominantly relies on single-form passive support, overlooking the active regulation capacity of the surrounding rock.

On this basis, this paper establishes a “three-zone linkage” slip-line field model for the floor of a high-stress mining roadway. By combining logarithmic spiral and straight slip lines, the analytical expressions for floor failure depth, maximum floor heave, and the floor-heave influence zone length are systematically derived, revealing the intrinsic mechanism of the linear positive correlation between floor heave and the width of the rib-side limit equilibrium zone. Unlike conventional methods that emphasize single floor reinforcement, this study theoretically demonstrates that expansion of the rib plastic zone is the fundamental driving force of floor heave, and that passive support alone is insufficient to interrupt the plastic flow path of the slip-line field. Accordingly, a synergistic control technique combining pressure-relief grooves and floor anchor cable compensatory support is proposed.

By actively cutting the continuous force-transmission path of the slip-line field through pressure-relief grooves, the extrusion channel of the passive-zone rock mass toward the roadway interior is blocked. Meanwhile, floor anchor cable compensatory support applies high-prestress constraints to the floor strata, forming a synergistic control mechanism of “active cutting and strong constraint,” which transforms the conventional reinforcement approach from passive resistance to active stress-path regulation.

Zone I represents the active limit zone, Zone II serves as the transition zone, and Zone III constitutes the passive limit zone. The slip lines in Zones I and III consist of two sets of straight lines, while those in Zone II are composed of one set of straight lines and a family of logarithmic spirals. The rock mass in the roadway floor is assumed to exhibit elastoplastic characteristics, wherein the elastic medium transforms into plastic medium under the action of supporting pressure. Engineering case study

Project overview

The Xinzhuang Coal Mine is located in the southeastern part of the Longdong Loess Plateau, characterized by landforms dominated by loess tablelands, loess broad ridges, and valley terraces. The No. 8 coal seam, situated in the first member of the Yan’an Formation, exhibits stable stratigraphic positioning. The burial depth of the seam ranges from 653.97 m to 1248.78 m, with an average depth of 951.34 m. The coal seam thickness varies between 0 m and 27.42 m, averaging 8.50 m. The No. 8 coal seam’s 1802 working face is arranged in the western wing of the first panel, with a dip width of 240 m and a strike length of 4100 m. The stopping line is located 150 m east of the central main roadway. The mining method employed is the longwall mining with full-seam height mechanized extraction.

The tailgate of panel 1802 is a coal roadway driven along the floor of the coal seam, with a total designed length of 3023 m. The airway has a rectangular cross-Sect. (5200 mm in width × 3500 mm in height) and adopts a combined support system comprising rock bolts, wire mesh, and cable bolts.

Roof bolts are high-strength left-hand threaded steel bolts with a diameter of Φ22 mm and a length of 2400 mm. Rib cable bolts are steel strand cable bolts with a diameter of Φ21.8 mm and a length of 4200 mm, arranged in a grid pattern of 1500 mm × 1600 mm (row × line spacing). Roof cable bolts are steel strand cable bolts with a diameter of Φ21.8 mm and a length of 8300 mm, installed in a grid of 1000 mm × 1600 mm (row × line spacing). The detailed support configuration of the tailgate of panel 1802 is illustrated in Fig. 3.

Tailgate of panel 1802 support section diagram.

The immediate roof and floor strata of the No. 8 coal seam belong to the Jurassic Yan’an Formation, primarily composed of interbedded mudstone, sandy mudstone, siltstone, and fine-grained sandstone. These strata are generally medium-thick layered, weakly cemented, and exhibit sedimentary structures such as low-angle wavy bedding, horizontal bedding, and graded bedding. The rock dip angle ranges from 6° to 7°. The uniaxial compressive strength (UCS) of the rock mass ranges from 11 MPa to 34.3 MPa, and the Rock Mass Quality Index (M) ranges from 0.06 to 0.15, indicating poor rock mass quality classified as weak to moderately competent. The immediate floor is mainly composed of mudstone, which has a low uniaxial compressive strength (11–18 MPa), is prone to softening when exposed to water, and exhibits well-developed bedding, making it the main bearing layer and weak link for floor heave deformation. The surrounding rock structure is categorized as a thin-layered interbedded soft-hard rock formation with good interlayer bonding, corresponding to Class III–IV surrounding rock with moderate to poor stability. The stratigraphic column of the panel is illustrated in Fig. 4.

Coal and rock column diagram.

According to the Impact Propensity Evaluation Report for the No. 8 Coal Seam in the Xinzhuang Coal Mine, the No. 8 coal seam and its floor exhibit weak impact propensity. During roadway excavation near fold axes, localized coal bursts and floor heave phenomena were observed. Combined with an analysis of the onsite geological conditions, the primary causes of floor heave include: first, the “extrusion-wedging” effect on the floor corners induced by rib convergence resulting from coal seam mining; and second, the strength degradation of the immediate floor mudstone under high stress, which exacerbates plastic flow and dilatancy deformation.

Field investigation and measurement

Failure characteristics of surrounding rock in roadways

The failure characteristics of the tailgate of panel 1802 are illustrated in Fig. 5. As shown, the floor failure exhibits distinct structural-mechanical characteristics. The maximum floor heave reached 55 cm. After excavation, the originally intact layered rock mass fractured into blocks of varying sizes. The floor rock mass near the roadway ribs underwent spatially heterogeneous fragmentation and presented a loose, disintegrated state, whereas the central floor region retained relatively intact block structures. The primary failure mechanisms included stress-driven block dislocation, shear dilation and crack propagation, activation and propagation of weak planes, and compaction-induced flow.

Failure characteristics of the tailgate of panel 1802.

Stress-driven block dislocation occurs when in-situ stress exceeds the peak strength of the rock mass, causing fractured blocks to undergo continuous rotation, misalignment, or sliding along pre-existing vertical joints and horizontal bedding planes. Shear dilation effects emerge during block sliding, generating diverse fracture surfaces whose morphology depends on the coupling between rock mass structures such as bedding planes and joint density, as well as stress states including magnitude and direction. Failure paths preferentially propagate along bedding planes, forming fracture networks that result in a discrete block structure in the floor. Additionally, the cumulative displacement of fractured blocks under sustained stress leads to compaction flow deformation, manifesting as floor heave dominated by compressive flow and shear flow.

Stress distribution characteristics of roadway floor

(1) Monitoring scheme design.

For the tailgate of the panel 1802, hollow inclusion triaxial stress cells were employed to dynamically monitor in-situ stress distribution. Based on the fundamental principles of stress measurement point layout and the underground production conditions of Xinzhuang Coal Mine, one measurement point was selected in an undisturbed area of the panel 1802 for floor stress monitoring. The measurement point was installed in the tailgate 170 m ahead of the panel 1802. The point was positioned in the floor region of the tailgate, featuring a 12 m deep borehole with an azimuth of 180° and a dip angle of 50°. The layout scheme of floor stress monitoring points is shown in Fig. 6.

Layout scheme of floor stress measurement points.

The KX-81 hollow inclusion triaxial stress cell was utilized, comprising 12 resistive strain gauges (No. 1–No. 12) embedded within a sealed epoxy resin cylinder. These gauges were evenly distributed at 120° intervals along the circumference of the cell, encapsulated by an outer epoxy resin layer to ensure long-term stability under high-stress conditions. The monitoring period spanned 27 days, covering both stable and mining-affected phases.

(2) Continuous strain monitoring results and distribution characteristics.

The strain characteristics recorded by resistance strain gauges of the stress cell are illustrated in Fig. 7. Based on the strain-rate characteristics, the entire monitoring period can be divided into four distinct stages.

Strain characteristics of the resistance strain gauges of the stress cell.

Stage 1 corresponds to the strain recovery period (170–130 m), during which stress within the borehole gradually recovered to the original rock stress level under surrounding rock stress after grout solidification, with no mining-induced disturbances observed at the measurement point.

Stage 2 represents the slow strain growth period (130–90 m). As the panel advanced, the measurement point began experiencing mining-induced stress disturbances, accompanied by moderate strain accumulation.

Stage 3 is identified as the rapid strain growth period (90–30 m), where continuous face advancement positioned the measurement point within the peak zone of advance abutment stress. This triggered a significant increase in mining-induced stress and accelerated strain growth.

Stage 4 marks the failure period (30–0 m), characterized by abrupt strain gauge value surges exceeding measurement limits. This resulted from excessive deformation of the hollow inclusion cell under advance abutment pressure, indicating the transition of the coal mass to a plastic failure state.

(3) Principal stress evolution law.

By further calculating the strain components, the principal stress at the measuring point can be obtained. Combined with the advancing distance of the working face, the evolution law of the principal stress was derived. The variation characteristics of the principal stress are shown in Fig. 8.

Characteristics of principal stress variations.

Phase 1 (170–90 m ahead): σ₁ and σ₃ remained nearly constant, while σ₂ showed minor increments, indicating negligible mining-induced stress influence.

Phase 2 (90–0 m ahead): σ₁ and σ₂ increased rapidly, whereas σ₃ initially decreased before rising. Except for three datasets recorded before cell failure, σ₃ values remained below the initial stress level. This indicates that the floor strata underwent unloading in the vertical direction, while σ₁ and σ₂ in the horizontal direction continued to increase. This stress-state transition agrees with the fundamental assumption of the slip-line field theory model that horizontal stress governs floor failure. The results suggest significant mining-induced stress redistribution, with an advance influence range of approximately 90 m.

The variation characteristics of the principal stress azimuth are shown in Fig. 9. Key observations include: σ₁ azimuth shifted northward from 103° to 259° at 90 m ahead, then gradually stabilized at 212° (south-southwest). σ₂ azimuth rotated from 7° to 347° (north-northwest) and eventually settled at 337°. σ₃ azimuth exhibited nonlinear variations, predominantly within 261°–264°, before aligning to 90° (due east) near the panel.

Variation characteristics of the principal stress azimuth.

This phenomenon indicates that under the action of mining-induced stress, the direction of the maximum principal stress undergoes a significant deflection, gradually tending toward the horizontal direction, while the direction of the minimum principal stress remains essentially vertical. This dynamic deflection of principal stress orientation subjects the floor strata to greater compressive action in the horizontal direction, thereby inducing shear slip along the bedding planes. This is consistent with the mechanism of slip surface formation in Zone III (the passive zone) within the slip-line field model.

Evolution of floor deformation characteristics

(1) Stage I (0–20 MPa): Deformation characteristics under initial stress loading.

When the stress of the surrounding rock in the roadway is loaded from 0 MPa to 20 MPa, the deformation and failure characteristics of the floor are illustrated in Fig. 11. As shown in the figure, the overall deformation of the surrounding rock is relatively minor, indicating that the surrounding rock is in the elastic deformation stage. Due to the low stress magnitude, deformation primarily manifests as minor morphological adjustments, and the roadway profile remains largely intact.

Floor deformation characteristics (step 4).

No significant fractures are observed on the surrounding rock surface, with the exception of microcracks resulting from inherent structural weaknesses or initial stress release. During this stage, the roadway floor exhibits no noticeable failure, manifesting only localized minor cracks induced by stress redistribution, which can be primarily attributed to self-weight and stress relaxation.

Longitudinal cracks of low density (spacing: 0.5–1 m) and shallow depths develop in the roadway ribs. Transverse cracks sporadically appear in the roof, characterized by short, discontinuous segments and wider spacing. Crack widths typically range from 1 to 2 mm.

(2) Stage II (20–30 MPa): Initial plastic deformation stage.

When the stress of the surrounding rock in the roadway increases from 20 MPa to 30 MPa, the deformation and failure characteristics of the floor are illustrated in Fig. 12. As shown in the figure, the surrounding rock enters the initial stage of plastic deformation during this phase, exhibiting certain convergence deformation, particularly in the shallow regions of the two sides and the roof of the roadway, which manifest as noticeable bulging and subsidence.

Floor deformation characteristics (step 6).

Floor heave initiates in the central roadway area due to lateral stress transfer from the compressed surrounding rock, resulting in upward arching. The maximum heave reaches 30 cm, with cracks predominantly concentrated in the central floor zone, aligned parallel to the roadway axis. Crack widths increase, though depths remain limited to 4.7 m.

At this stage, the driving effect of the high-stress environment on floor heave begins to manifest. As the surrounding rock stress increases, the deviatoric stress level intensifies significantly. Meanwhile, under high-stress compression, the two sidewalls converge toward the roadway interior, exerting a squeezing and wedging effect on the floor corners, which forces the floor strata to undergo initial shear slip. The floor is subjected to horizontal compressive stress from the surrounding rock on both sides, in combination with the shear action induced by the redistribution of surrounding rock stress, collectively promoting the initial floor heave and the formation of cracks.

(3) Stage III (30–40 MPa): Significant plastic deformation and localized failure stage.

When the stress of the surrounding rock in the roadway increases from 30 MPa to 40 MPa, the deformation and failure characteristics of the floor are illustrated in Fig. 13. As depicted in the figure, the deformation intensifies markedly, with substantial bulging observed in the roof and ribs, accompanied by localized plastic failure zones. The roadway geometry undergoes significant distortion, reflecting a pronounced decline in load-bearing capacity.

Floor deformation characteristics (step 8).

Floor heave amplifies, forming distinct localized protrusions in the central area, while shear failure or spalling becomes evident in the ribs. Cracks propagate radially and extend deeper into the surrounding rock, with floor fractures widening and penetrating further into the substrate.

During this stage, the driving effect of high stress on floor heave is significantly enhanced, and its mechanical mechanism is primarily manifested in the following two aspects. First, the stress concentration in the deep floor exceeds its strength limit. As the surrounding rock stress increases, the shear stress in the deep floor intensifies markedly, and the high stress induces a composite instability characterized by sustained plastic flow and shear slip within the floor strata. Second, the continuous convergence of the two sidewalls under high-stress compression exerts a pronounced extrusion and wedging effect on the floor corners, further aggravating the floor heave.

(4) Stage IV (40–50 MPa): Large deformation and instability stage.

When the stress of the surrounding rock in the roadway is loaded from 40 MPa to 50 MPa, the deformation and failure characteristics of the floor are illustrated in Fig. 14. As depicted in the figure, the roadway experiences severe deformation characterized by roof subsidence, cross-sectional shrinkage, and pervasive shear failure. The system transitions into an irreversible instability phase, marked by interconnected fractures and localized collapses.

Floor deformation characteristics (step 10).

Floor heave reaches its peak, often manifesting as a dome-shaped structure with potential delamination or shear-induced bed separation. Fractures interconnect to form shear bands, while floor cracks coalesce into blocky patterns, indicative of shear slippage or stratified failure.

This stage is characterized by the dominance of high stress in governing the floor instability and failure process. Under the persistent action of high stress, large-scale floor heave accompanied by dislocation occurs, fully manifesting the composite failure characteristics of plastic flow and shear slip. The severe convergence of the sidewalls under high compressive stress maximizes the squeezing and wedging effect at the floor corners. Meanwhile, the instability and failure of the roof and sidewalls further aggravate the mechanical conditions of the floor. Consequently, local failure of the surrounding rock induces stress redistribution toward the floor, rendering it the primary stress concentration zone. Driven by sustained high stress, floor failure progressively intensifies, and deformation transitions into an irreversible phase.

(3) Stress analysis of roadway floor.

Under excavation and mining-induced stresses, the floor exhibited significant spatial heterogeneity in stress distribution. In shallow regions (e.g., No. 4 and No. 3 transducers), the vertical stress initially increased to 12.3 MPa owing to unloading effects, and then gradually decreased and stabilized as loading progressed. In contrast, deep regions (e.g., No. 11 and No. 12 transducers) maintained elevated vertical stresses due to stress transfer, with values increasing continuously. Overall, the vertical stress distribution showed a “central low, peripheral high” pattern, with minimum stress along the central axis and increasing stress toward both sides.

Horizontal stress in shallow zones (e.g., No. 5 transducer) remained low due to unloading, whereas deep zones (e.g., No. 13 transducer) exhibited rising stresses, peaking at 7.2 MPa. This mechanical response governed the floor failure mode. To mitigate heave, traditional localized floor reinforcement must be replaced by stress transfer path optimization, such as deep pressure relief (e.g., borehole destressing) to reduce stress concentration and enhance floor stability.

Study on floor heave control in roadways

Design of floor heave control scheme

Given the substantial thickness of mudstone in the floor of Xinzhuang Coal Mine (up to 7.0 m at maximum), a combined strategy of roadway pressure-relief groove and compensatory support with floor cables was implemented to mitigate stress concentration and enhance floor stability. The design considered safety factors, required reinforcement support resistance, and targeted reinforcement depth.

The pressure-relief groove was designed with a width of 0.6 m and a depth of 1.5 m. The prestressed cables had a diameter of φ18.9 mm and a length of 7300 mm. Two cables were installed in each row, with a horizontal spacing of 4600 mm, and a row spacing of 1600 mm. The diameter and length of the anchor cables were determined based on the range of the floor loosening zone and the depth of the potential sliding surface obtained from physical simulations, ensuring that the anchorage sections were located in stable strata. The anchor cable pre-tightening force was designed as 100 kN, grounded in the required compensatory support resistance of the floor surrounding rock and the collaborative control principle of “rib control and corner stabilization.” Numerical simulation to effectively limit the expansion of the plastic zone at the corner and suppress shear sliding of the rock strata. The anchorage length of 1800 mm employs two CK2360 resin cartridges, designed in accordance with the bond strength at the anchorage–rock interface and the pullout resistance requirements, thereby ensuring that the anchor cable provides stable and reliable anchorage force throughout its service life. Numerical simulation analysis of floor heave control

3.2.1 Design of numerical simulation schemes

Based on the geological conditions of the tailgate of panel 1802 in Xinzhuang Coal Mine, this study investigated the control effects of two support schemes on roadway floor deformation: the original support scheme (Scheme 1) and the roadway pressure-relief groove combined with bottom-corner cable support (Scheme 2). In Scheme 2, the vertical stress contour map of the surrounding rock in the roadway is illustrated in Fig. 21(b). As can be observed from the figure, after pressure-relief groove treatment, the vertical stress distribution transitions into a double-peak pattern, forming a “release-transfer-bearing” tripartite mechanism. The vertical stress direction undergoes differentiation, with the stress component along the groove axis significantly reduced, while the transverse component increases by 15%. As an artificial weak plane, the pressure-relief groove transferred the original vertical stress to both sides, generating a local low-stress zone (< 5.3 MPa) at the groove bottom and high-stress concentration zones at the groove shoulders. The pressure-relief groove induced stress rebalancing by weakening the continuity of the floor, thereby forcing stress to redistribute along the edges of the groove, thereby reducing the vertical stress at the centerline of the floor by 5%–10%.

Analysis of floor heave control effectiveness

To validate the feasibility of the pressure-relief groove and anchor cable reinforcement support scheme, a 500 m long section of the tailgate of panel 1802 was selected for field testing. Pressure-relief grooves were constructed along the strike direction in the central floor region using pneumatic picks, with smooth groove edges to avoid secondary damage to the surrounding rock. A removable steel cover plate was installed above the pressure-relief groove to ensure the safety of vehicle and personnel passage. Simultaneously, anchor cables were installed at the floor corners of both ribs, as illustrated in Fig. 17. The pre-tightening force (100 kN) and anchorage force parameters of the bottom-corner anchor cables were comprehensively determined based on the mechanical properties of the floor strata and the results of numerical simulation inversion. Through simulations conducted with FLAC3D to analyze the plastic zone and displacement response of the floor, 100 kN was ultimately selected as the optimal pre-tightening force. The anchorage force was verified through field pullout tests, with the measured average anchorage force reaching 150 kN, satisfying the design safety factor requirements and confirming the rationality of the selected anchorage parameters.

To monitor the support effectiveness, cross-grid monitoring points were deployed on the ribs, roof, and floor, with one monitoring cross-section every 100 m (totaling 5 sections). A laser rangefinder was employed to measure convergence daily, and the average values were recorded. The deformation curve of the surrounding rock in the roadway is shown in Fig. 25.

Deformation curves of surrounding rock.

During the monitoring period, the maximum rib-to-rib convergence reached 22.4 cm and gradually stabilized over time, indicating that the pressure-relief grooves and anchor cables effectively controlled surrounding rock deformation. The pressure-relief grooves released horizontal stress, while the anchor cables improved the overall stability of the rock mass, which are the primary factors contributing to deformation control.

The maximum roof-to-floor convergence was 37.4 cm, with significant initial deformation followed by gradual reduction. This demonstrates that the pressure-relief grooves effectively alleviated floor stress concentration. By redirecting stress to deeper strata, the grooves reduced deformation pressure on the roof and floor, serving as the key mechanism for convergence mitigation.

It is recommended that a hierarchical control strategy be adopted in practical engineering applications. When geological conditions are relatively simple, for example, when the floor strata are competent, no obvious weak interlayers or water inflow are present, and mining-induced stress disturbance is limited, pressure-relief grooves alone may be prioritized to reduce construction difficulty and engineering cost. Under such conditions, the baseline groove depth, width, and spacing can be adopted without additional reinforcement. When floor deformation is significant and high-stress characteristics are prominent, the collaborative control scheme of pressure-relief grooves and floor anchor cables should be employed. Under challenging geological conditions, the parameters and support configuration should be further adjusted. In water-rich soft floor strata, drainage or waterproof grouting should be conducted before groove construction, and the groove spacing may be reduced or combined with full-section grouting reinforcement to prevent water-induced softening. In strongly layered floors with weak interlayers, anchor cables should penetrate the key weak interfaces and be lengthened or densified where necessary to ensure reliable deep anchoring. Under extremely high horizontal tectonic stress, the groove depth or width may be increased, a double-row groove layout may be adopted, and yielding or energy-absorbing support components may be added. In addition, long-term deformation monitoring should be maintained so that groove and anchoring parameters can be recalibrated during service.

Conclusions

(1) Based on the theory of plastic mechanics, a slip-line field model for the floor strata was established, revealing the quantitative relationship between floor failure depth, internal friction angle, and coal seam parameters. The results indicate that floor heave exhibits a linear positive correlation with the rib-side limit equilibrium zone width and failure depth. The derived formula for the influence zone length of floor heave further verifies that floor control should integrate the coupled mechanism of vertical stress transmission and horizontal compression effects.

(2) Through engineering case analysis of the tailgate of Panel 1802 in Xinzhuang Coal Mine, this study elucidates the evolutionary mechanism and control strategies for floor heave in high-stress roadways. Physical simulation tests reveal four-stage characteristics of floor failure: elastic deformation, plastic deformation, localized failure, and instability failure. Field measurements indicated that the influence range of advance abutment stress extended approximately 90 m, while the azimuth angle of principal stress σ1 deflected from initial 103° to 212°, intensifying shear slip in the floor strata. Stress distribution characteristics showed that the vertical stress peaks of 15.2 MPa at the floor corners triggered initial failure, whereas sustained growth of deep horizontal stress to 7.2 MPa was the primary cause of floor heave.

(3) The proposed “shallow pressure relief-deep anchoring” coordinated control strategy demonstrates remarkable effectiveness through numerical simulations. Pressure-relief grooves reduced vertical stress peaks by 33%–38% in the floor strata while inducing bimodal stress distribution through stress differentiation, resulting in floor heave reduction from 510 mm to 150 mm. Floor corner cable anchors suppress horizontal stress concentration, decreasing peak stress by 60% to 4.7 MPa. This approach transforms plastic failure modes from inverted triangular composite failure to localized shear failure at slot shoulders. Field monitoring confirms stabilized roof-to-floor convergence at 37.4 cm, verifying that the strategy achieves synergistic floor stability control through optimized stress pathways and enhanced surrounding rock structural integrity.
