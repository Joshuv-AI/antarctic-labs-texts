Lineages of Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex associated with pulmonary drug-resistant tuberculosis in Africa: a systematic review
Authors: Cornelius Gweba, Adegboyega Omoniyi Oyefabi, Abimbola Awopeju, Regina Doro Jabaka, Leonard Ighodalo Uzairue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe emergence of multidrug-resistant tuberculosis has eroded the gains made in the fight against tuberculosis. This review aims to provide current perspectives on the lineages of the Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex (MTBC) associated with drug-resistant tuberculosis (DR-TB) in Africa.MethodPrimary studies were retrieved from PubMed, AJOL, Scopus and Africa Index Medicus databases, including worldwide science and Bielefeld Academic Search Engine websites. Retrieved articles were imported into Rayyan.ai for the selection and screening process. The quality of included studies was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal checklist for analytical and cross-sectional studies. Articles published between 2013–2023, studies conducted among humans with pulmonary DR-TB that reported MTBC lineages or species were included. On the other hand, studies that reported lineages or species in animals and studies conducted outside Africa were excluded. An Excel sheet was developed and used for data extraction by two independent reviewers, and the extracted data were compared and discrepancies resolved through discussion. Two independent reviewers assessed the risk of bias by applying the eight items on the JBI checklist to each of the included studies.ResultA total of 491 articles were retrieved; however, 73 eligible articles were included in the final analysis after the selection and screening process. The characteristics of the selected studies show that the studies were from 21 countries, and varieties of molecular methods (IS6110-RFLP, MIRU-VNTR-16, Spoiligotyping and whole genome sequencing) were used in the included studies. Of the included studies, 75% were assessed to have low risk of bias using the JBI checklist, indicating that the overall quality of evidence is moderate to high. Across the continent, L4 was the dominant lineage 51% (8244/16 172) associated with DR-TB infection, which was followed by L2 41% (6649/16 172), L3 4.6% (741/16 172).ConclusionM. tuberculosis genotypes associated with drug-resistant pulmonary tuberculosis in Africa are L4, along with L2 and L3. The review included only studies published in the English language, and authors received no external funding for the review, authorship or publication.PROSPERO registration numberCRD42024512834.

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) is an achingly infectious disease caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex (MTBC), which is reportedly the leading cause of death by a single infectious agent globally, particularly in low- and middle-income countries.1 In 2020, and for the first time in almost a decade, there was a significant setback on global TB targets, where the annual decrease in TB incidence was halted, followed by an increase in TB deaths, and the TB case notification rate declined by 18%, when compared with the previous year. While these setbacks can be attributed to the COVID-19 pandemic, Chakaya et al observed that some of the targets were already off track, a situation that the COVID-19 pandemic aggravated.2 The increase in TB mortality occurred among the 30 high TB-burdened nations, the majority of which are African countries.3 Of the 30 high TB-burdened countries, six are in East Africa, four in Southern Africa, three in Central Africa and three in West African countries.1

Though there has been a modest decline in the TB epidemic in the past decade, the burden in low and middle-income countries remains disproportionately high. In 2018, the TB incidence rate in the African region was 275 per 100 000 populations as against 10.7 per 100 000 in the European Union.1 Also, the development of resistance to anti-TB drugs by MTBC strains, which is responsible for what is known as drug-resistant TB (DR-TB), now constitutes a major setback in the fight against TB. Multidrug-resistant TB (MDR-TB) is defined by WHO as a form of TB that is resistant to rifampicin and isoniazid,4 which has extremely limited treatment options and reduced chances of successful treatment outcome.5 Exacerbating the situation is the emergence of pre-extensively drug-resistant TB (Pre-XDR) and extensively drug-resistant TB (XDR-TB). Pre-XDR-TB refers to MDR-TB strains that are resistant to any fluoroquinolones, while XDR-TB refers to Pre-XDR-TB that exhibits additional resistance to either bedaquiline or linezolid or both.4 Globally, it is estimated that only 1 in 10 MDR-TB patients get successfully treated with the highest burden of DR-TB concentrated in WHO regions of Africa, Asia and Europe.6 The African region is characterised by weak healthcare systems and low economy which may be associated with poverty, malnutrition and poor living conditions7 that are known to aggravate and facilitate the spread of DR-TB.

Human MTBC genotypes known to cause TB disease in humans have been classified into seven phylogenetic lineages (L). Whereas L1, L2, L3, L4 and L7 are classified under Mycobacterium tuberculosis sensu stricto, L5 and L6 are under Mycobacterium africanum.8 Though MTBC species are globally ubiquitous, the African continent has three additional species to deal with within its region, L7 (restricted to the Horn of Africa) and L5 and L6 (restricted to West Africa). These lineages (L5, L6 and L7) that are restricted to the African region constitute an additional burden for the continent to deal with in the fight against TB, making TB epidemiology in Africa unique.8

Rationale

In 2021, the WHO African region scored 44 out of 100 on the service coverage index, which is an indicator used to measure Sustainable Development Goal 3.8, which seeks to achieve universal health coverage. This was the lowest score among all WHO regions, indicating poor access to TB diagnostics and treatment services in the region. In 2023, 24% of people who developed TB were from the WHO African region, the second after Southeast Asia which contributed 45% of the total global incidence. Also, the WHO African and Southeast Asia regions accounted for 81% of the total TB-related mortality in 2023.9 Given the abysmal performance of the WHO African region in respect to its achievements toward TB global targets, coupled with the burden of DR-TB and how it is threatening TB elimination in the region, it is therefore imperative for the region to be proactive in its fight against TB. Hence, knowledge of the MTBC lineages associated with DR-TB in Africa would inform on the best epidemiological intervention targeted at interrupting the chain of infections. A literature search identified some systematic review studies on MTBC lineages associated with DR-TB in Africa; however, these studies have some limitations. Whereas two of the systematic review studies were focused either on East or West Africa,10 11 one whose scope covered the whole of the African continent12 extracted articles published on the PubMed database alone. The previous review reported lineages within the MTBC in individual African countries.12 Additionally, it was revealed from each of the included studies that L4 was the most reported lineage associated with DR-TB in the individual countries. The previously reported evidence was determined through a single PubMed database search, which may have missed eligible studies in other databases. Hence, this study reported the pooled prevalence of MTBC lineages associated with pulmonary DR-TB in Africa and expanded the search for eligible studies using five databases. Given the limitations of previous systematic review studies, this current study seeks to provide a more holistic and comprehensive perspective on the subject matter.

Research question

Which MTBC lineages are associated with pulmonary DR-TB in Africa?

Objectives

The objectives of this systematic review are:

To identify the MTBC lineage(s) associated with DR-TB in Africa.

To identify the MTBC lineage(s) associated with DR-TB in Africa.

To describe the distribution of MTBC lineages associated with DR-TB in Africa.

To describe the distribution of MTBC lineages associated with DR-TB in Africa.

Methods

Review protocol and registration

This systematic review has been registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews. The review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-analysis guidelines.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The following studies were included in the review: primary studies, articles published in English language, articles published between January 2013 and December 2023, studies conducted among human patients with pulmonary DR-TB, participants of any age or gender diagnosed with pulmonary DR-TB and studies that reported MTBC lineages with DR-TB. On the other hand, the following studies were excluded from the study: studies that failed to report MTBC lineages with DR-TB, studies that reported DR-TB lineages or species in animals were excluded, systematic reviews were excluded, studies involving subjects with extra-pulmonary TB were excluded and studies conducted outside the African continent were also excluded. This review included primary studies that used analytical cross-sectional and cohort study designs. These study designs are appropriate for determining the prevalence of DR-TB and answering the review question. However, interventional, case series and reviews were excluded due to limited relevance and methodological differences.

Information sources and search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted between 18 May 2024 and 25 May 2024 on PubMed (18 May 2024), African Journal Online (AJOL, 18 May 2024), WHO African Index Medicus (18 May 2024) and Scopus (18 May 2024) electronic databases including worldwide science (21 May 2024) and Bielefeld Academic Search Engine (25 May 2025) websites. Research articles published in English language between January 2013 and December 2023 were retrieved using a combination of key words in line with Medical Subject Heading. The search was conducted by reviewer CG, using the following search terms: ((“multidrug resistant tuberculosis” OR “drug resistant tuberculosis” OR “extensively drug resistant tuberculosis”)) AND ((“molecular epidemiology” OR “spoligotyping*” OR “MIRU” OR “IS6110” OR “VNTR” OR “DNA fingerprint*” OR “RFLP” OR “WGS”)) AND ((“lineage*” OR “genotype*” OR “specie*”)) AND ((Characterization* OR Diversity* OR Profiling*)) AND the individual country names of 54 African countries.

Selection process

The records retrieved from databases and websites were imported into rayyan.ai.13 Duplicates detected by rayyan.ai13 were manually resolved and the remaining articles were subjected to two levels of screening. First, title and abstract screening was followed by full text screening. Briefly, following the exclusion of duplicates, 365 articles were remaining. Two reviewers (CG and RDJ) independently screened the title and abstract of the remaining 365 records. Conflicts were resolved by a third reviewer (LIU), and 161 articles were remaining following conflict resolution. However, the full text of 153 potentially eligible studies was retrieved and uploaded on rayyan.ai.13 Two independent reviewers (CG and RDJ) screened the full text of the 153 potentially eligible articles, of which 73 were included in the final analysis after conflicts were resolved by the reviewers through discussion (figure 1).

Data collection process and data items

Excel sheet was developed and used for data extraction from included studies by two independent reviewers (CG and RDJ). No corresponding author was contacted for additional information; however, supplementary information provided online by the authors was consulted. Extracted data from the two independent reviewers were compared, and discrepancies were resolved through discussion. Data items extracted include the first author’s name, year of study, country, sample size, genotyping method, resistant pattern and MTBC lineages. Using a data extraction form, relevant information was collected from each included study. Drug resistance patterns were categorised based on their association with MTBC lineages, grouped by country and then pooled to estimate the overall prevalence of each MTBC lineage across Africa

Quality assessment

The quality of each included study was assessed by two independent reviewers (CG and RDJ) using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal checklist for analytical and cross-sectional studies. The eight items on the JBI critical appraisal checklist for an analytical cross-sectional study were used in assessing the risk of bias by scoring Yes=1, No=0, Unclear=0 and Not applicable=0. The scores from the two reviewers were compared, and discrepancies were resolved through concession. Total scores obtained were converted to percentages with studies categorised according to the level of risk of bias. Studies with a score range of 20%–49%, 50%–79% and 80%–100% were categorised as having high, moderate and low risk of bias respectively.14 15

Data analysis and presentation

The data were managed using Excel spreadsheet. The pooled proportion was calculated by dividing the number of DR-TB isolates by the total number of MTBC isolates identified across all studies. Additionally, the proportion of MTBC lineages associated with DR-TB was determined by dividing the number of each lineage that was DR-TB by the total number of DR-TB lineages. The lineage data from individual countries were then pooled to estimate the overall prevalence of each MTBC lineage across Africa. Results were presented on tables, maps and figures.

Result

A total of 491 articles were retrieved from PubMed, AJOL, WHO African Index Medicus and Scopus databases, including worldwide science and Bielefeld Academic Search Engine websites, using a combination of search terms (online supplemental material appendix table IV: search terms used for different sources). All identified articles (n=491) were imported into rayyan.ai,13 duplicates were detected and resolved manually. Following the removal of duplicates, 365 articles were available for title and abstract screening. Of the 365 articles, 206 were excluded after title and abstract screening, leaving 159 potentially eligible articles for full-text screening. Full text of 153 articles was retrieved, of which 73 were included in the review, while 80 were excluded (see figure 1: flow diagram, and online supplemental material appendix table III: excluded studies with reasons, appendix I: reference list of included studies and appendix II: reference list of excluded studies). Primary studies included in the review were from 21 countries. South Africa accounted for the highest number with 19 studies, followed by Ethiopia, Uganda and Nigeria, with 10, 7 and 6 studies, respectively. Ghana, Tanzania and Zambia had four studies each, Eswatini and Mozambique had three studies each, while Sudan had two studies. Others, including Algeria, Angola, Cameroon, Chad, Kenya, Mali, Morocco, Namibia, Niger, Rwanda and Tunisia, had one study each.

Whole genome sequencing (WGS) was the genotyping method used in the majority of the studies followed by mycobacterial interspersed repetitive units-variable number tandem repeats (MIRU-VNTR) and spoligotyping (online supplemental material appendix table I: characteristics of included studies). A total of 17 406 DR-TB isolates were obtained from 73 eligible studies, of which 70.2% (12 217/17 406) were MDR-TB, 19.8% (3445/17 406) were Mono/Poly resistant, 3.2% (550/17 406) were Pre-XDR-TB and 6.9% (1194/17 406) were XDR-TB (figure 2: distribution of resistant patterns). Of the 17 406 MTBC lineages in the study, 93% (16 572) of lineages (L1–L7) were associated with DR-TB infection. Across the continent, L4 was the dominant lineage 51% (8244/16 172) associated with DR-TB infection retrieved from published studies, followed by L2 41% (6649/16 172), L3 4.6% (741/16 172), L1 1% (154/16 172), L7 0.54% (87/16 172), L5 0.2% (34/16 172), L6 0.04% (7/16 172). However, Mycobacterium bovis and orphan strains were found in 0.04% (6/16 172) and 1.5% (250/16 172) of the samples, respectively (table 1: MTBC lineages associated with pulmonary DR-TB in Africa). The distribution of the MTBC lineages by countries in the African continent is shown in figure 3. The result shows that most countries have L4 as the most common lineage associated with DR-TB. While Sudan and Ethiopia have diversity in the occurrence of lineages, South Africa has nearly equal occurrences of L2 and L4 in the DR-TB cases identified in the retrieved studies from the country (figure 3: distribution of MTBC lineages in Africa). In assessing the risk of bias of included studies using the JBI critical appraisal checklist for analytical cross-sectional study, 75% (55/73), 21% (15/73) and 4% (3/73) of included studies scored 100%, 88% and 75%, respectively (online supplemental material appendix table II: risk of bias assessment for included studies).

Discussion

The risk of bias assessment conducted using the JBI critical appraisal checklist for analytical and cross-sectional studies suggests a low risk of bias in the included studies. Of the included studies, three-quarters scored 100% indicating low risk of bias and a rigorous methodological approach. The studies clearly defined in detail selection criteria, study settings, used valid and reliable methods of measuring exposure, identified confounders and reported adequate control measures. On the other hand, one quarter of included studies scored less than 100% suggesting a moderate risk of bias due to partial fulfilment of checklist criteria. The common methodological limitation observed in these studies was the lack of clear definition of inclusion and exclusion criteria. Additionally, a few studies failed to clearly describe the study population and participants. Though the majority of the included studies had low risk of bias, which supports the robustness and reliability of findings of the review. Nevertheless, caution should be exercised when interpreting the findings, given the moderate methodological gaps reported in some included studies. In addition, this review included only studies published in English language, which constitutes a selection bias that necessitates cautious interpretation of findings.

The seven major MTBC lineages (L1, L2, L3, L4, L5, L6 and L7) were identified to be associated with DR-TB across Africa at different proportions, with some lineages being predominant in some regions of the continent. The MTBC L4 is the dominant lineage in all the countries except for South Africa and Sudan. The dominance of L4 has been reported by previous systematic reviews conducted in Africa1 12 and the genotype (L4) is widely recognised among other MTBC lineages as one that has global presence.16 This global presence of L4 coupled with its association with DR-TB constitutes a significant challenge to TB control efforts globally. The resistant pattern from the review shows that the majority of the DR-TB in Africa are MDR-TB (70.2%). This shows that Africa has a huge MDR-TB burden and an uphill task in the fight against TB. This burden of MDR-TB might be grossly underestimated due to the few African countries (38.8%) that had eligible studies that were included in the review. Also, more than half (52.4%) of the countries with eligible studies in the review contributed only one study. Although the included studies used advanced molecular genotyping techniques such as WGS, spoligotyping and MIRU-VNTR, their application was significantly limited in scope. Therefore, Africa needs to increase its utilisation of molecular genotyping techniques in both routine diagnosis and research to facilitate the generation of the needed body of knowledge on the MTBC lineages circulating in the continent. In addition, studies included in the review analysed archived isolates, in which identified genotypes were not triangulated with patients’ demographic data in most of the studies included in the review. This is a significant gap as some occupations such as herding predispose one to bovine TB caused by Mycobacterium bovis. Therefore, future studies should endeavour to investigate the association between genotypes and patients’ important demographics including occupation, address and travel history. This will guide in identifying hotspots of transmission and attribution of source of infection. Again, this review did not report outbreaks caused by genotypes or person-to-person transmission by a single strain.

Whereas L4 was the predominant genotype in most of the countries (90.5%) with included studies, the predominant genotypes in South Africa and Sudan were L2 (accounting for 48% of the isolates from the country) and L3 (accounting for 55% of the isolates from the country) respectively. The dominance of these genotypes (L2 and L3) of Asian descent17 in South Africa and Sudan clearly depicts Africa as the continent bearing the brunt of DR-TB. In addition, the L2 genotype, which was the second predominant genotype (41%), has been known for its increased transmissibility and strong association with DR-TB, particularly MDR-TB and XDR-TB.17 18 Bhembe et al reported that the L2 genotype, particularly the Beijing family, is responsible for ongoing DR-TB transmission in South Africa.19 Unlike other MTBC lineages, the L2 (Beijing family) genotype is characterised by elevated virulence and fast progression of disease.17 The significant presence of the L2 genotype in the African continent explains the debilitating condition of TB patients in Africa for which high morbidity and mortality are reported in the continent. Therefore, the utilisation of molecular diagnostic tools in the surveillance of DR-TB in Africa is key in identifying transmission hotspots for targeted intervention that would disrupt the chain of transmission. Moreover, a significant proportion of the genotype L3, which was the third predominant lineage (4.6%), was found in Eastern African countries including Tanzania, Sudan, Ethiopia, Uganda and two Southern African countries, Zambia and Mozambique. The presence of the L3 genotype in Eastern and Southern African regions may be attributed to the geographical proximity of East Africa to the Red Sea which historically facilitated trade and migration from Asia where the L3 genotype originates and constitutes a major driver of TB transmission.20 Bakuła et al. had described how cross-border migration facilitated the spread of MTBC lineages from one region to another and across continents.21 Hence, human activities through the Red Sea, Gulf of Aden and Sinai Peninsula have facilitated the spread of L3 (specifically Central Asia Strain 1-Kili) from Asia to East Africa and Africa in general.

The L1, L5, L6 and L7 genotypes are genotypes with geographical restrictions. The L1 genotype (1%) is often referred to as Indo-Oceanic lineage due to its restriction along maritime and coastal trade routes. The genotype was reported by African countries with maritime trade routes such as South Africa, Ethiopia, Eswatini and Tanzania.2224 The L7 (0.54%) genotype, which was known to be geographically restricted to the horn of Africa,25 was reported by one country from the horn of Africa, Ethiopia.26 27 Lineage 7 has no documented evidence of human-to-human transmission with an exceptional genetic diversity that suggests the presence of a yet-to-be-identified environmental reservoir.28 Though incidences of TB due to L7 are rare and limited to East Africa, mostly Djibouti,28 identification of the ecological reservoir would inform effective prevention and control policies in the region where L7 is endemic. Hence, there is a need for additional studies to identify the ecological reservoir of L7 genotype in the horn of Africa where L7 is endemic. The genotypes L5 (0.2%) and L6 (0.04%), otherwise known as West African 1 and West African 2 respectively, were mostly reported by West African countries, Nigeria, Ghana and Cameroon.2931 The notable low presence of these genotypes in Africa can be attributed partly to the adaptability of foreign genotypes in Africa which have spread to overshadow indigenous genotypes and under-utilisation of molecular genotyping techniques by the majority of clinical laboratories in Africa.25 The under-utilisation of molecular genotyping methods by clinical laboratories in Africa might be responsible for the under-reporting of African indigenous lineages. This accentuates the need for increased utilisation of molecular genotyping tools for surveillance of DR-TB by clinical laboratories in Africa. The animal-adapted lineage reported was Mycobacterium bovis (0.04%), though its prevalence is low, yet its presence raised the concern about zoonotic transmission of TB in Africa, as reported in a previous study.25 Orphan (1.5%) genotypes reported in this review could be from studies that used molecular genotyping techniques with low discriminatory power. In conclusion, the MTBC genotypes associated with DR-TB in Africa are L4, L3 and L2. Among these, L4 is the predominant genotype associated with DR-TB in Africa. Molecular genotyping of MTBC strains is an important tool in the surveillance and control of DR-TB in Africa. Although it is sparingly used in Africa, its increased utilisation is crucial in providing patient-centred care and deepening the understanding of DR-TB epidemiology in the continent. Understanding the prevalence of L4 and L2 lineages linked to pulmonary DR-TB will have profound implications for the direction of research and practice. This will lead researchers and policymakers on the African continent to focus on understanding the specific characteristics of these lineages that may be enhancing pulmonary DR-TB infection and its transmission dynamics.

Limitations of the study

This study has some limitations. First, due to the heterogeneous nature of the data generated in this study, which is attributed to the diversity in the study designs employed by the included studies, statistical analysis (meta-analysis) was not feasible in this study. Second, although this study primarily included only studies that reported lineages in drug-resistant pulmonary TB infection in Africa, for appropriate research to be able to determine the exact lineages that are associated with drug-resistant TB infection, there is a need to design a study that employs a more rigorous design that uses robust statistical randomisation. Lastly, the inclusion of only studies published in English is also a limitation, as it may introduce selection bias that would have omitted studies published in languages other than English.
