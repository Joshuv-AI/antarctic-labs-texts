Transcriptional signatures of cortical morphometric variability in temporal lobe epilepsy
Authors: Yaping Dong, Shuai Wang, Jingjie Luo, Juan Meng, Yuanmei Liu, Song Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveWidespread cognitive deficits in temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE) are associated with macroscale brain structural changes. However, the correlation between the changes in the cortical morphological similarity of the TLE and gene expression remains unclear.MethodsStructural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was collected from 31 TLE patients and 35 healthy controls to construct an individual‐level Morphometric INverse Divergence (MIND) network. Changes in cortical morphological similarity attributable to TLE and their cognitive correlates were assessed. Using a public transcriptional dataset, we linked TLE‐related structural changes to specific gene expression and biological pathways.ResultsTLE patients exhibited macroscopic morphological changes in the posterior cingulate cortex and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, which were associated with cognitive impairments such as processing speed and working memory. Notably, TLE‐related MIND changes were spatially correlated with the expression of epilepsy‐associated genes, which were predominantly enriched in biological processes including cellular regulation and synaptic transmission.SignificanceMIND reveals macroscopic morphometric abnormalities of TLE. The microscopic transcriptional patterns have facilitated the understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying cortical susceptibility.Plain Language Summary TLE patients often have memory and thinking problems. This study used brain scans to show that TLE patients have structural changes in two brain regions, the posterior cingulate cortex and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex. These changes are linked to slower processing speed and poorer working memory. Importantly, the brain changes were spatially related to the activity of TLE susceptibility genes involved in cell regulation and signal transmission between neurons. This helps explain how microscopic gene patterns may contribute to large‐scale brain changes and cognitive difficulties in TLE.

INTRODUCTION

Temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE), the most common form of focal epilepsy, is frequently characterized by long‐term memory deficits. 1 Accumulating neuroimaging evidence reveals widespread brain alterations in patients with TLE, ranging from neocortical thinning and subcortical atrophy to white matter abnormalities. 2 , 3 , 4 Such extensive structural alterations are associated with deficits across multiple cognitive domains, including memory. 5 Motivated by the fact that epilepsy is fundamentally characterized by synchronous neuronal discharges in specific brain regions, increasing studies have supported the distributed epileptic network hypothesis. 6 , 7

From a network perspective, recent magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) studies have revealed widespread disruptions of structural connectivity in TLE, including thalamocortical connectivity, default mode network, and reward/emotion networks. 8 , 9 Investigations of brain structural connectomes have primarily focused on white‐matter networks derived from diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) tractography, and structural covariance networks based on inter‐subject correlations of structural measures. However, DWI tractography remains challenging, particularly in estimating the long‐range connectivity strength, 10 while structural covariance analysis requires large sample sizes to ensure reliability and is typically not applicable to single‐subject studies. 11

Morphometric INverse Divergence (MIND) analysis integrates cortical network organization by integrating multiple morphological features, enabling the capture of interregional similarity at the single‐subject level. 12 Biologically, greater morphometric similarity between cortical regions is indicative of stronger axonal connectivity, greater gene co‐expression, and shared cytoarchitectonic classes. MIND has been applied to characterize macroscopic structural abnormalities in both neurological and psychiatric disorders, including schizophrenia. 13 , 14 Alzheimer's disease, 15 and migraine. 16 Although MIND has been established as a reliable and robust method for characterizing cortical morphology, its potential to reveal disease‐specific structural vulnerability in TLE remains unexplored.

Genetic factors play important roles in shaping brain connectomes. 17 , 18 In this context, recent construction of brain‐wide gene expression atlases bridges the gap between neuroimaging and transcriptomes. 19 , 20 The Allen Human Brain Atlas (AHBA) microarray dataset quantifies the transcriptional activity of thousands of genes across multiple anatomical locations, enabling the linkage between spatial variations in gene expression and distributed properties of brain connectomes in both health and disease. 21 , 22 For example, specific gene expression patterns contribute to individual variability in both intrinsic functional connectivity and cortical morphology. 23 , 24 Accumulating studies have associated cortical structural variability in neurological disorders with distinct transcriptional signatures, such as major depressive disorder, 25 autism spectrum disorder, 26 and schizophrenia. 27

In this study, we link TLE‐related MIND alterations and gene expression data to reveal the underlying molecular mechanisms of cortical structural changes in epilepsy. First, we construct individual MIND networks and describe the regional morphological alterations in TLE. Second, we evaluate the cognitive association related to the cortical morphological similarity across multiple cognitive domains. Finally, we investigate the relationship between TLE‐related regional MIND alterations and anatomically patterned gene expressions using the AHBA dataset. To gain deeper insights, we conduct functional enrichment analysis to elucidate the ontological pathways of TLE‐related genes. These findings provide insights into the molecular and transcriptional signatures underlying cortical structural vulnerability in TLE.

METHODS

Participants

This study included 31 individuals with TLE and 35 age‐ and sex‐matched healthy controls (HCs) from the OpenNeuro (data set ds004469, https://openneuro.org/datasets/ds004469/versions/1.1.4) (Table 1). Patients were enrolled and diagnosed at the outpatient epilepsy clinics of Hospital General de Mexico, following the ILAE criteria based on clinical information, conventional neuroimaging, and surface EEG. All HCs had no history of any neurological or psychiatric disorders. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Institute of Neurobiology, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. This complied with the Council for International Organizations of Medical Sciences International Ethical Guidelines for Health‐Related Research Involving Humans.

MRI images acquisition and pre‐processing

Three‐dimensional T1w images were acquired using a 3D magnetization‐prepared gradient echo sequence in a 3 T Philips Achieva TX scanner (Best, The Netherlands). Image acquisition parameters were as follows: repetition time (TR) = 8.1 ms, echo time (TE) = 3.7 ms, matrix size = 256 × 256, flip angle = 8°, and voxel size = 1 × 1 × 1 mm3. Structural MRI images were preprocessed in surface‐based space using FreeSurfer v6.0. This process reconstructed each participant's cortical surface from 3D images via skull stripping, motion correction, hemispheric and subcortical segmentation, and generation of the gray/white interface and pial surface. 28

Cognitive assessment

Cognitive assessments were conducted with the Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale (WAIS‐IV) and the Wechsler Memory Scale (WMS‐IV). Nine indices were derived to assess performance across specific cognitive domains, including verbal comprehension (VCI), perceptual reasoning (PR), processing speed (PSI), working memory (WMI), visual memory (VMI), visual working memory (VWMI), immediate memory (IMI), delayed memory (DMI), and auditory memory (AMI). Test scores were transformed into percentiles adjusted for age and education, using normative data from the Mexican population.

MIND network

Five surface‐based structural MRI features, cortical thickness (CT), surface area (SA), gray matter volume (Vol), mean curvature (MC), and sulcal depth (SD), were extracted and standardized across all vertices to construct the MIND network. For each cortical region defined by the HCP_MMP atlas, these features were aggregated across all vertices to form a multivariate distribution. The symmetric KL divergence between each pair of regions was calculated using the K‐nearest neighbor density algorithm and subsequently transformed into a MIND similarity score, where higher scores denote greater similarity between regions. 12

Case–control analysis

Regional MIND was calculated as the sum of weighted correlation coefficients between a region and all other regions. To estimate the spatial pattern, regional MIND values were averaged across all TLE patients and HCs separately. According to previous studies, 29 , 30 a generalized additive model was applied to examine the case–control differences of regional MIND, with age, gender, and TIV (total intra cranial volume) included as covariates. Given the controversial role of TIV in volumetric analyses, we compared TIV between TLE patients and HCs, and subsequently examined its effect on results. TLE patients exhibited reduced but not significant whole‐brain volume, and the spatial patterns of regional MIND differences were highly robust regardless of TIV correction (Figure S1). The model was computed using the mgcv package (https://cran.rproject.org/web/packages/mgcv/index.html). Cohen's d values were used to quantify effect sizes for group comparisons. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05, with false discovery rate (FDR) correction applied across 360 regions to control for type I error. For network‐level analyses, the 360 cortical regions were assigned to seven canonical functional networks to assess MIND differences. 31 This complementary approach facilitated the identification of systems‐level cortical morphological alterations in TLE.

Power estimation

To assess the statistical power, we estimated the minimal effect size for case–control differences between 31 individuals with TLE and 35 HCs using G*Power (v 3.1.9.7). At a two‐tailed significance level of α = 0.05 with a desired power of 0.8, we detected a Cohen's d greater than 0.70. Such large effect sizes are generally regarded as robust in neuroimaging studies, indicating a sufficient signal‐to‐noise ratio to uncover true biological distinctions.

Cognitive association analysis

Behavior partial least squares (PLS) analysis was employed to examine the association between MIND and cognitive function. The explained variance for each latent variable (LV) was calculated as the proportion of the total cross‐covariance accounted for by that LV. To assess the statistical significance of each LV, we performed permutation testing with 5000 iterations. Furthermore, bootstrap resampling (5000 iterations) was conducted to evaluate the stability and reliability of the brain and behavioral weights. Brain–behavior correlation was calculated as the Pearson correlation between the brain scores and phenotypic scores across all participants, with 95% confidence intervals estimated from the bootstrap resamples. All analyses were implemented using the pyls toolbox (https://pyls.readthedocs.io/en/stable/index.html).

Transcriptomic analysis

The AHBA dataset (http://human.brain‐map.org) bridges the gap between neuroimaging and transcriptomes. 19 Whole‐brain microarray gene expression data were obtained from six adult human donors (42.50 ± 13.38 years; 1 female), comprising 3702 spatially distinct tissue samples. 22 Here, gene expression data were pre‐processed using the abagen toolbox with default parameters (more details see Supplement). 32 Since only two donors had right hemisphere data, we extracted expression values exclusively from the left hemisphere, resulting in a 180 × 15 633 region‐by‐gene matrix for further analysis.

A partial least squares (PLS) regression model (https://github.com/SarahMorgan/Morphometric_Similarity_SZ) was used to relate regional MIND changes to gene transcriptional activity (predictor variables). Permutation testing with spherical rotations accounted for spatial autocorrelation and tested the significance of variance explained by PLS components. To evaluate the robustness of individual gene contributions, we employed bootstrap resampling with 1000 iterations. We computed Z‐scores by dividing each gene's PLS weight by its bootstrap standard error, ranked genes accordingly, and assessed their statistical significance using one‐sample Z‐tests. Genes were ranked by their contribution to PLS components and split into PLS+ and PLS−groups (P FDR <0.05).

According to a previous study, 32 TLE susceptibility genes were selected to assess the contribution of epilepsy‐related genes to MIND alterations. 33 To preserve the full spectrum of cortical gene expression, we identified overlapping genes with the AHBA background genes and examined the relationship between their expression levels and TLE‐related MIND alterations.

Functional enrichment analysis for Gene Ontology (GO) gene sets was performed using Metascape. 34 Pairwise similarity between enriched terms was assessed with a Kappa‐test score, followed by hierarchical clustering of the similarity matrix. The resulting tree was trimmed into separate clusters, with significance set at FDR‐corrected P < 0.05.

RESULTS

TLE‐related alterations in MIND

The spatial distribution of regional MIND in TLE patients is similar to HCs, with notable extremes observed in the primary sensorimotor moves to the default mode cortex (Figure 1A). Compared to HCs, TLE patients exhibited significantly increased regional MIND in the orbital and polar frontal cortex, dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) and posterior cingulate cortex, whereas decreased MIND was observed in part of the inferior frontal cortex (Figure 1B, Table S1). Elevated regional MIND values, indicative of greater morphometric similarity (i.e., reduced morphometric differentiation) with other regions, suggest a disruption of inter‐cortical functional differentiation in TLE patients, whereas decreased MIND values imply abnormal functional integration between regions.

Cognitive correlates of MIND in TLE

We further assessed the relationship between case–control differences in MIND and cognition using behavior PLS analysis. The model identified a significant LV that accounted for 90.8% of the cross‐covariance between regional MIND values and the cognitive indices (P = 0.008, 5000 permutations). The brain‐behavior correlation for this LV was R = 0.69 (95% CI: [0.10, 0.65], P perm = 0.009), indicating a robust association between cortical morphometric similarity and cognitive function in TLE patients (Figure 2A). The brain scores of the first latent dimension exhibited significant correlations with all cognitive indexes (VCI: R = 0.50, P perm = 0.005; PR: R = 0.51, P perm = 0.003; PS: R = 0.45, P perm = 0.007; WMI: R = 0.53, P perm = 1.0e‐3; VMI: R = 0.72, P perm <0.001; VWMI: R = 0.55, P perm = 0.002; IMI: R = 0.70, P perm <0.001; DMI: R = 0.74, P perm <0.001; AMI: R = 0.67, P perm <0.001; Figure S2).

Cognitive correlates of MIND. (A) Correlation between latent dimensions of the brain and phenotype. (B) Phenotypic loadings of the first latent dimension. AMI, auditory memory; DMI, delayed memory; IMI, immediate memory; PR, perceptual reasoning; PSI, processing speed; VCI, verbal comprehension; VMI, visual memory; VWMI, visual working memory; WMI, working memory.

Regions with positive weight (i.e., higher MIND values associated with the latent cognitive pattern) were predominantly located in the posterior cingulate cortex and orbital frontal cortex (Table S2). The behavioral weights of all cognitive indexes for the significant LV showed higher loadings (Figure 2B). These findings suggest that TLE‐related MIND alterations in higher‐order cortices are strongly linked to cognitive impairments in processing speed, memory, and executive functions.

Transcriptional correlates of TLE‐related alterations in MIND

Using the AHBA dataset, we evaluated the gene expression patterns consistent with the different anatomical distributions of the case–control differences in MIND. The PLS1 identified the linear combination of gene expression values that exhibited the strongest correlation with regional MIND alterations. We found that PLS1 accounted for 11.5% of the variance in MIND differences among TLE patients, a proportion significantly greater than expected by chance (P spin = 0.02). Furthermore, the weighted gene expression displayed a significant positive spatial correlation with the case–control differences (R = 0.34, P spin <0.01; Figure 3A), suggesting that PLS1‐weighted genes were concurrently overexpressed in regions with increased MIND in TLE patients. After ranking and correcting the normalized weights of each gene, we found 507 PLS1+ genes and 400 PLS1‐ genes were over‐expressed/under‐expressed as regional changes in MIND, respectively (Figure 3B, Figure S3).

We further examined the association between the expression of 32 TLE susceptibility genes and regional MIND alterations. Of these, 21 genes were present in the AHBA background dataset. GRM4 and SCN2A showed positive (R = 0.16, P spin = 0.026) and negative (R = −0.21, P spin = 0.004) spatial correlations with TLE‐related MIND alterations, respectively (Figure 3C).

Functional enrichments for MIND‐related genes

We performed the functional enrichment analysis to investigate the biological significance of PLS1+/− gene sets. The PLS1+ genes were significantly enriched in biological processes related to cellular regulation, including regulation of cell projection organization, regulation of cell differentiation, regulation of mitotic cell cycle, mitotic cell cycle process, mesenchymal cell migration, glial cell differentiation, and cell adhesion molecule binding (Figure 4A). Significant enrichment was observed for PLS1‐ genes in biological processes related to synaptic transmission, such as glutamatergic synapse, trans‐synaptic signaling, dicarboxylic acid metabolic process, regulation of axonogenesis, and dendrite (Figure 4B).

DISCUSSION

Leveraging multiple cortical morphological features to characterize cortical macroscopic structural architecture, we identified weakened cortical differentiation ability in TLE patients, especially in higher‐order cognitive regions such as the DLPFC and posterior cingulate cortex. Furthermore, these regional MIND abnormalities were associated with specific cognitive impairment in TLE. Transcriptomic analysis revealed that these MIND disturbances were associated with the expression patterns of epilepsy‐related genes. Subsequent functional annotation further demonstrated that MIND‐related genes were significantly enriched in biological pathways related to cellular regulation and synaptic transmission. These findings link macroscopic morphological abnormalities in TLE and microscopic transcriptional patterns, advancing our understanding of the underlying molecular mechanisms for cortical structural alterations in TLE.

MIND characterizes the macroscopic cortical architecture by quantifying the similarity of multidimensional distributions of multiple structural features across cortical regions. 12 TLE‐related MIND alterations appear to concentrate in specific higher‐order regions, including the orbitofrontal cortex, DLPFC, and posterior cingulate cortex. Atrophy and white matter microstructural abnormalities in these core default mode network regions have been demonstrated in TLE. 4 , 35 Functional MRI studies have also revealed widespread abnormal activity and connectivity in the default‐mode regions of TLE patients. 2 , 36 Moreover, cortical vulnerability is associated with the extensive cognitive impairments observed in TLE patients. 37 Consistent with these findings, we discovered that alterations in cortical macroscale morphology are associated with various memory impairments in TLE. Overall, these findings suggest that MIND may serve as an effective biomarker for both the diagnosis and prognosis of TLE.

Multiple factors, such as genetic, molecular, and neuronal alterations, may contribute to TLE‐related cortical structural alterations. Here, spatial correlations between MIND changes and the expression of epilepsy‐related genes (e.g., GRM4, and SCN2A) indicate distinct molecular mechanisms. GRM4 encodes the metabotropic glutamate receptor, and its sequence variants may contribute a modest risk to epilepsy etiology. 38 A case–control study showed that a SNP of the GRM4 gene locus was associated with epilepsy susceptibility. 39 SCN2A loss‐of‐function variants can account for the neuronal hyperexcitability underlying epileptic seizures, 40 , 41 and targeted reduction of their expression significantly improves clinical outcomes. 42 These findings suggest that aberrant expression of epilepsy risk genes may serve as the molecular basis underlying abnormal cortical morphology in TLE.

Transcriptional analyses revealed that MIND‐related genes were spatially enriched in biological processes related to cellular regulation and synaptic transmission, such as regulation of cell projection organization and cell differentiation, and trans‐synaptic signaling. The mechanism of epileptogenesis is linked to cellular regulatory pathways and disruptions in protein expression at multiple levels. These alterations primarily manifest as neuroinflammation, abnormal lipid metabolism, and oxidative stress during the disease course. 43 Moreover, MTOR mutations may obstruct the autonomous migration of cells at the radial glial foot, a disruption that could explain the observed cortical fragility. 44 Recent research has suggested that reduced synaptic connectivity may serve as a biomarker for cognitive deficits in TLE. 45 Overactivated microglia in epilepsy exhibit a preferential engulfment of inhibitory synapses, disrupting synaptic excitation‐inhibition homeostasis and consequently driving neuronal hyperexcitability. 46 The potential mechanisms underlying the antiepileptic and neuroprotective effects of tropisetron in TLE may involve inhibition of synaptic remodeling. 47

Limitations

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, the cross‐sectional design and relatively modest sample size preclude causal inferences. Future longitudinal studies with larger samples are required to elucidate the molecular characterization of MIND. Second, the gene expression data used in this study were derived from healthy individuals, which may not confirm the actual molecular status of our cohort. Future studies should utilize subject‐specific transcriptional data or incorporate molecular imaging techniques to validate and extend our findings. Finally, gene expression analysis was restricted to the left cortical regions, raising concerns about lateralization bias and incomplete assessment of subcortical roles. Future investigations should examine gene‐structure relationships in the whole brain to obtain a more comprehensive understanding.

CONCLUSIONS

This study used MIND phenotypes to reveal TLE‐related macroscopic structural abnormalities. Morphological dedifferentiation of the prefrontal cortex and posterior cingulate cortex was associated with extensive cognitive impairment in TLE. Additionally, the macroscale structural changes captured by MIND were linked to alterations in the expression of epilepsy‐related genes. Transcriptional analysis implicated cellular regulation and synaptic transmission in TLE pathophysiology and pharmacology. Together, these findings provide novel insights into the potential molecular basis of macroscopic cortical morphological alterations in TLE. While these promising findings indicate potential biomarkers for TLE, large‐scale, multi‐center validation studies are still required to translate these results into clinical practice.
