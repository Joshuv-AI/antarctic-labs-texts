Polyurethane Depolymerization With Alkyl, Aryl, and Mixed Carbonates
Authors: Ege Hosgor, Ricardo P Martinho, Willem Verboom, Jean‐Paul Lange, Jurriaan Huskens
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChemical recycling of polyurethane foams (PUFs) remains challenging due to their crosslinked structure. Depolymerization with organic carbonates offers a phosgene‐free route to recover both polyols and aromatic monomers through carbonate‐mediated carbonyl exchange. Here, we evaluate the reactivity of a series of carbonates, including dimethyl carbonate (DMC), diphenyl carbonate (DPC), methyl phenyl carbonate (MPC), and mixed DMC/DPC, in the depolymerization of TDI‐based flexible PUF (190°C, 4 h, with Zn(OAc)2 as a catalyst). DPC promotes equilibration between urea and carbamate species but generates side products resulting in lower selectivity. MPC behaves similarly to the DMC/DPC mixture, resulting in the formation of mixed carbamate species, thus confirming carbonyl exchange as the underlying mechanism. In contrast, DMC yields cleaner products but suffers from limited dissolution of the aromatic hard blocks at lower temperatures. Using anisole as a cosolvent resolves this limitation, enhancing the accessibility of the hard domains, resulting in the recovery of 85% of methyl‐functionalized toluene dicarbamate (Me‐TDC) and of 95% of the polyol. Additionally, DMC also allows easy product separation. Overall, this study clarifies how carbonate structure affects depolymerization pathways, side‐product formation, and hard‐domain accessibility providing selective and phosgene‐free chemical recycling strategies for PUFs.Keywords: chemical recycling, flexible polyurethane foam, organic carbonates, sustainable chemistry and exchange interactions

Introduction

Thermoset polyurethanes (PUs) are essential materials in modern manufacturing, used globally in rigid and flexible foams, elastomers, coatings, adhesives and fibers owing to their outstanding mechanical properties and chemical resistance [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. The global market is expected to grow from 24 million tonnes in 2020–31 million tonnes by 2030 driven by the high demand of these sectors [6]. Industrial flexible polyurethane foams (PUFs) are produced by a polyaddition reaction between aliphatic polyols, that is, polyester or polyether polyols, and aromatic diisocyanates, such as 4,4′‐methylene diphenyl diisocyanate (4,4′‐MDI) and toluene diisocyanate (TDI) [7, 8, 9]. This reaction forms crosslinked polymer networks containing both carbamate and urea linkages [10]. The choice of diisocyanate and polyol varies depending on the targeted application, as their structure and reactivity are tailored to meet specific PUF product properties such as flexibility, rigidity, or thermal stability [11, 12].

Recycling PUF materials—a high priority for European PU producers [13]—poses a significant challenge due to their thermoset nature. They cannot be mechanically recycled through remelting or reprocessing, leading to the accumulation of large volumes of waste in landfills or incinerators [13, 14, 15]. Only a minor fraction is recycled as trim foam, by cutting clean production waste (no postconsumer waste) to small pieces and used as such or to produce bonded foam [16]. Various chemical recycling methods have been developed to depolymerize PUFs into their constituent monomers, for example, alcoholysis, hydrolysis, acidolysis, and aminolysis [16, 17]. They generally allow the recovery of recycled polyols that is presently used at low concentration in PUF formulation. However, few companies can recover the diisocyanate component [16], and they proceed by substance of very high concern (SVHC), according to REACH legislation, namely by recovering diamines and using phosgene to make diisocyanate, a third SVHC. Moreover, these chemical recycling processes have limitations that hinder their scalability for industrial application [18, 19, 20, 21, 22]. As a result, achieving closed‐loop recycling of PUFs remains a major challenge.

In our previous work [23], we introduced a selective depolymerization method using diethyl carbonate (DEC), which enabled the recovery of both the polyols and the aromatic counterparts. This strategy avoids the intermediacy of two SVHC by avoiding the formation of free aromatic amines and enables the separation of polyols and aromatics, offering a promising route toward closed‐loop recycling without relying on phosgene chemistry. Alkyl carbonates, including DEC and dimethyl carbonate (DMC) are versatile, can be fully renewable based on CO2 and biobased ethanol and are environmentally friendly reagents that serve as key compounds for sustainable polymer synthesis [24]. Their production from alcohols or epoxides and captured CO2 offers a renewable and low‐toxicity route, consistent with the principles of green chemistry and supportive of more sustainable polyurethane recycling approaches [25]. However, the equilibration chemistry of the urea‐to‐carbamate formation using carbonates remained insufficiently understood [26]. In particular, the role of different carbonate reagents, notably alkyl versus aryl ones, and their influence on product selectivity, separation behavior and completeness of degradation is yet to be explored.

Here, we present a study of the carbonate‐induced depolymerization of PUFs and related model compounds using carbonates with both alkyl and aryl substituents. As a proof of concept, we employ DMC, methyl phenyl carbonate (MPC), and diphenyl carbonate (DPC) as depolymerization reagents and solvents. Our main objective is to evaluate the product distribution upon each PUF depolymerization case, to study potential differences in depolymerization rate and side product formation, and to assess whether a similar equilibrium reaction is established. As a hypothesis, we considered that an aromatic side group could improve solubilization of the aromatic hard domains of the PUF, thus promoting depolymerization. The performance of each carbonate was studied based on multiple parameters: (i) polyol recovery yield, (ii) distribution and selectivity of aromatic products, and (iii) completeness of degradation (solid residue formation and weight percentages). As a model system to provide insight into possible side products, we focused on N,N′‐bis(4‐methylphenyl) urea decomposition with various carbonates and used 19F NMR‐based quantification of isocyanate intermediates. By integrating these model studies with PUF depolymerization experiments, we provide new insights into the chemistry controlling the recovery of, in particular, the aromatic component, enabling further development of phosgene‐free circular strategies for thermoset recycling.

Results and Discussion

Depolymerization of PUF with Alkyl and Aryl Carbonates

To explore how the carbonate structure affects the zinc acetate‐catalyzed depolymerization of PUF, we varied the carbonate side groups by employing four different reagent cases: DMC, DPC, MPC, and an equimolar DPC/DMC mixture. DMC and DPC are used to compare alkyl versus aryl reactivity. The mixed alkyl/aryl carbonate MPC is used to probe whether there is a preference for placing alkyl or aryl groups at the end of the monomers upon depolymerization. Lastly, the (equimolar) DPC/DMC mixture is used to probe whether equilibration of all carbonyl groups leads to a similar product distribution as obtained for MPC. Each carbonate was used at the same time both as reagent and as solvent. The carbonate was added to a PUF sample together with the zinc acetate catalyst, and the mixtures were reacted under identical conditions to assess the influence of the carbonate leaving group character. We used a commercially available flexible PUF, which contains typical additives and reflects real PUF formulations (see Tables S1 and S2 for composition). The foam is based on a toluene diisocyanate (TDI)‐polyether system with a TDI 2,4:2,6 isomer ratio of 80:20 and exhibits characteristic urethane and urea linkages.

Figure 1 shows the depolymerization process to recover both the polyol and aromatic monomers. In a typical experiment, PUF and Zn(OAc)2 are suspended in the carbonate medium and heated at 190°C for 4 h, facilitating the carbonate exchange reaction between the carbonate reagent and the urea/carbamate linkages in the polymer network. In our previous work [23], we showed that this process occurs through a chemical equilibration between all carbonyl compounds. After the depolymerization, we quantify the amounts of recovery in the liquid phase and the residual solid. The liquid phase predominantly contains carbonated polyols and aromatic carbamates, while residual solids may contain catalyst, incompletely degraded hard‐domain fragments, and insoluble additives. Comparing the liquid product outcomes across the four carbonate systems allows us to correlate carbonate side group structure with solvation of the aromatic urea hard domains of PUF, differences in product distribution and monomer yields.

Conceptual representation of the depolymerization of PUF for four different cases using alkyl and aryl carbonates: dimethyl carbonate (DMC), diphenyl carbonate (DPC), and methyl phenyl carbonate (MPC).

The depolymerization of PUF using DMC at 190°C efficiently yields both the polyol and aromatic monomers. Reaction with DMC was conducted in an autoclave under autogenous pressure of 10 bar, using a carbonate loading corresponding to 0.45 mol of DMC per g of PUF (carbonate‐to‐(urethane+urea) carbonyl molar ratio of 170:1). We quantified the monomer yields of the depolymerization in the liquid phase using qNMR spectroscopy, integrating the signals of the characteristic protons from both monomers and comparing them to those of the commercially available dicarbamates (2,4 and 2,6‐toluene dicarbamates, 2,4/2,6‐TDC, Table S3) and the corresponding functionalized polyol (for detailed quantification procedures, see the Materials and Methods section). The 1H NMR analysis of individual additives used in commercial PUF show no signal overlap with those of the aromatic monomers of the polyols. Figure S1a shows the chemical structures of methyl dicarbamates (2,4/2,6‐Me‐TDC) and methyl carbonate‐functionalized polyol. The 1H NMR spectrum of the depolymerization crude (Figure S1b) shows that both polyol and aromatic monomers were present in the liquid phase. As we showed in our recent work using DEC [23], no mono‐carbamates or diamines remained after the depolymerization. Furthermore, the characteristic signal at 1.04 ppm of the methyl protons in the propylene oxide (PO) units confirms the presence of the polyol in the liquid phase. The methyl peak at 3.66 ppm shows that the polyol has reacted with DMC and has become completely functionalized by methyl carbonate end groups (Figure S1b).

At the selected reaction temperature of 190°C and 4 h reaction time, the depolymerization of PUF in DMC yielded 75% of the polyol and 80% of the aromatic monomers, of which about half is 2,4/2,6‐Me‐TDC, confirming that dimethyl toluene dicarbamate (Me‐TDC) is the dominant product under these conditions. Figure 2a,b presents the results of temperature variation experiments conducted at 180–210°C. As shown in Figure 2a, the residual solid weight fraction decreases with increasing temperature, indicating more breakdown of the PUF at elevated temperatures. In contrast, Figure 2b shows a consistently lower yield of Me‐TDC compared to polyol across the temperature range, suggesting that the cleavage of urea bonds amid the aromatic hard domains proceeds less readily than the cleavage of carbamate bonds to the soft polyol fraction. This difference points to the limited solubility and accessibility of the rigid, aromatic hard domains in DMC, which likely limits their reactivity despite increasing temperature. These results followed a similar trend to the reaction with DEC [23], indicating that alkyl carbonates promote the carbonyl exchange reaction, enabling the recovery of (carbonated) polyols and aromatic dicarbamates.

To further explore the influence of carbonate structure, depolymerization experiments were performed with all carbonates under identical conditions. All depolymerization reactions were performed at a constant carbonate loading, defined as the number of moles of carbonate per gram of PUF (0.45 mol carbonate per g PUF, carbonate‐to‐(urethane+urea) carbonyl molar ratio of 170:1). This ratio was kept the same across all carbonate systems to ensure comparability. As a result, the volumes differed slightly but were all around 4–6 mL. All depolymerization experiments with DEC, DMC, DPC, MPC, DMC/DPC, and DMC/anisole are summarized in Table S4.

As shown in Figure 2c, depolymerization in DPC resulted in an approximate recovery of 60% polyol, which is lower than that obtained with DMC under identical conditions. This reduced yield is consistent with the lower nucleophilicity of phenoxide relative to methoxide, showing its better leaving group ability. In addition, phenol appeared as a major side product, with an estimated yield of ∼5% relative to the initial amount of DPC, as of the total integrated aromatic 1H NMR signals of phenol (Figure 2d). The main difficulty appeared to be the incomplete removal of excess DPC and phenol, as both are solids at room temperature. With a large excess of it dominating the crude sample, the product distribution was difficult to quantify. Therefore, we measured the sample by high‐temperature 1H NMR at 80°C (Figure S2) and the data confirmed the presence of both diphenyl toluene dicarbamate (Ph‐TDC) and phenol. To further analyze potential side products, we took the 2D HMBC spectrum of the crude sample and detected a signal for phenyl acetate overlapping with the methyl peak of Ph‐TDC (Figure S3a). To determine the origin of this signal, a control reaction containing only DPC and zinc acetate was carried out at 190°C. The resulting mixture also produced phenyl acetate (Figure S3d), demonstrating that zinc acetate can react with DPC under depolymerization conditions. As a result, the exact quantification of the Ph‐TDC in the crude samples could not be reported.

The appearance of phenol in the aromatic region was initially assigned to phenyl carbamate dissociation. However, the experimentally observed phenol amounts are far too large to be explained by phenyl carbamate decomposition alone. The 0.36 mmol of phenol that was detected corresponds to ∼8x the maximum amount of phenyl carbamate that could be formed, at a carbonate/carbonyl ratio of 170:1 applied here. A more plausible explanation is hydrolytic cleavage of DPC promoted by traces of water present in the system. The possible role of moisture from PUF was further evaluated by a simple mass balance estimate based on the number of N‐H groups and the likelihood that these basic N‐H groups will bind at least 1 or 2 molecules of water from the air, as discussed in detail in the Supporting Information. Accordingly, the PUF could contain at least 1.38–2.76 mmol water (no more than 6.2–12.4 wt.% of the PUF), which could theoretically generate 2.76–5.50 mmol phenol through hydrolysis, or 7.5–15x the amount measured in this experiment. Importantly, this estimate does not imply such moisture is impossible to remove but highlights the importance of moisture control in aryl carbonate‐based depolymerization routes.

In comparison to the DPC system, the alkyl‐aryl carbonate MPC yielded a higher (88%) polyol recovery (Figure 2c) and a similar aromatic side product formation (Figure S4). The similar presence of both methylated and phenylated dicarbamates indicates that MPC behaves like a combination of alkyl and aryl carbonates, supporting a mixed carbonyl‐exchange pathway consistent with the reactivity pattern observed for DPC. To test whether the same result can be obtained from the, more easily available, symmetric DMC and DPC, we depolymerized PUF in an equimolar DMC/DPC system. The mixed carbonate experiment reached a similar polyol recovery as the MPC (Figure 2c) and generated both Me‐TDC and Ph‐TDC in nearly equal proportions, along with phenolic side products such as phenyl acetate (Figures S3b, S3c, and S5). These results demonstrate that MPC behaves similarly to an equimolar mixture of DMC and DPC, qualitatively confirming that equilibration occurs between alkyl and aryl carbamates under the reaction conditions.

Next, we investigated whether a chemically inert and aromatic solvent could provide the solubilization benefits of an aryl carbonate on the urea‐rich hard domains without influencing the TDC yield. Anisole was selected due to its aromatic nature and its excellent thermal stability under the reaction conditions. To evaluate its effect on the product distribution, anisole was introduced as a cosolvent in the alkyl carbonate (DMC) system. The reaction proceeded with higher yields of both aromatics and polyol than for DMC alone (Figure 2c). Moreover, a Me‐TDC yield of 70% was obtained, which is also better than obtained for the MPC/DPC case. This demonstrates that anisole enhances the accessibility of hard domains, thereby facilitating more efficient carbonyl exchange, without compromising on the aromatic selectivity.

When compared to the pure MPC case, the presence of anisole provided comparable yields of polyol, aromatics and TDC while operating at a lower carbonate‐to‐(urethane+urea) carbonyl molar ratio of 85:1 (with only half of the amount of carbonate). The partial 1H NMR spectra presented in Figure 2d,e (full spectra in Figure S6) support the improved alkyl dicarbamate formation without phenol side products when using anisole instead of DPC. The corresponding region in Figure 2e for the DMC/anisole system shows formation of Me‐TDC with fewer side products. These results indicate that anisole acts as an inert aromat,ic medium that enhances alkyl dicarbamate formation, probably by improving the accessibility of the aromatic hard block domains in the PU foam through swelling.

Notably, the MPC and mixed DMC/DPC carbonate systems yielded higher recoveries of both polyol and aromatic products than expected from the average yields of the single carbonate systems (DMC or DPC), indicating a synergistic effect rather than a simpler reaction. The origin of the synergy is not yet understood and requires further mechanistic analysis.

The results from the comparison of PUF depolymerization by different carbonates revealed three key insights: (i) aryl carbonates undergo equilibration and produce phenol, complicating product distribution and purity; (ii) alkyl carbonates are preferred for selective carbamate formation; and (iii) the presence of an aromatic, nonreactive solvent together with an alkyl carbonate enhances the swelling of the urea‐rich hard domains and increases aromatic monomer yield.

Model Reactions to Study Carbonyl Exchange

The carbonyl exchange between carbamate/urea aryl/alkyl carbonates was confirmed by performing reactions with simplified model compounds. We chose the model reactions between N,N′‐methylene diphenyl urea (MDPU) and the same carbonates (DMC, DPC, MPC, and DMC/DPC) as described above (see Figure 3a). In a typical reaction, the crude product contained carbamate and intermediates (phenol and isocyanate) besides unconverted MDPU and carbonate. The decomposition products were analyzed and quantified by NMR, showing the formation of carbamate derivatives along with side products. The chemical shifts of all compounds were confirmed by comparison with the reference spectra of commercially available methyl phenyl carbamate (MePC), and phenyl phenyl carbamate (PPC). To further understand the influence of carbonate structure on the equilibria with urea and carbamate, we compared the reactivity of MDPU with alkyl and aryl carbonate reagents. The large excess (30:1 molar) of alkyl carbonate (DMC) drives the reaction with MDPU to the right to make the carbamate, similar to what was shown for DEC previously. Reactions of MDPU with aryl carbonate reagents in the same molar excess led to the formation of PPC, accompanied by the formation of phenol as a side product. We observed a phenol yield of ∼5% relative to the amount of initial DPC. We attribute the phenol formation to the hydrolytic cleavage of DPC, promoted by trace amounts of water present in the system (Figure S7). Based on the Karl‐Fischer analysis of DPC and accounting for the water introduced with the zinc acetate catalyst (∼0.08 mmol), the phenol yield that can be generated by hydrolysis is estimated to be ∼4% under these experimental conditions (see SI, calculations of residual water content in the Materials and Methods), which is sufficient to fully account for the observed phenol yield.

Figure 3b represents the schematic of a method to trap PI, which enables stabilization and quantification of reactive isocyanate intermediates through derivatization with a nucleophilic agent. The PI trapped with 2,2,2‐trifluoroethylamine was analyzed and quantified using 19F NMR spectroscopy [27], as shown in Figure 3c. We used an internal standard for accurate quantification of PI formation. This analytical approach confirmed the presence of small, yet significant, amounts of isocyanate species in all systems containing aryl carbonates, reinforcing the hypothesis of carbonate dissociation and isocyanate release under the applied conditions.

The comparative results of MDPU decomposition with different carbonates are shown in Figure 3d, which highlights the relative yields of MePC, PPC, and their decomposition product PI. The data reveal clear differences in product selectivity depending on the carbonate structure. Decomposition of MDPU in DMC produced nearly 95% MePC, indicating selective carbonyl exchange with no side reactions (Figure S8). The decomposition in DPC yielded 75% PPC, with 15% PI and trace <3% phenol formation based on initial DPC.

For decomposed MDPU in MPC, the product distribution was more equilibrated: 45%–55% of both MePC and PPC as is shown in Figure S9. The presence of phenol in these samples suggested the MPC hydrolysis in the presence of traces of water from the reagents. Additionally, the equimolar DMC/DPC mixture yielded 35% MePC and 45% PPC following with a complex aromatic fraction containing additional aromatic signals (Figure S10). Interestingly, the mixed carbonate system yielded a slightly higher proportion of PPC relative to MePC despite the higher stability expected for MePC. From a leaving group perspective, phenoxide (PhO‐) is regarded as a better leaving group than methoxide and therefore a poorer nucleophile, which would suggest more MePC formation rather than PPC. Since this is contrasting the observations, we attribute the slightly higher PPC fraction to the product distribution being governed by reversible carbonyl exchange and scrambling processes. Therefore, the final MePC/PPC ratio likely reflects a combination of kinetic and equilibrium effects rather than a single reactivity trend. A detailed kinetic analysis would be required to solve these points and is not covered here.

Mass balance analysis further revealed that 10%–20% of the aromatic content could not be accounted for as phenolic species. This missing aromatic fraction increased with higher PI yield, indicating that these species might be present as oligomeric‐PI species that are not detected as small aromatics. The formation of oligomeric species provides a possible explanation for the loss of the aromatics in mixed carbonate systems with PI participation.

These findings demonstrate that the equilibrium composition and side product formation are highly dependent on the carbonate structure and its leaving‐group ability. Together, these results also show that the presence of both alkyl and aryl carbonates leads to approximately equal end capping of the carbamate with methyl and phenyl groups.

Overall, the MDPU model experiments were consistent with the bulk PUF depolymerization studies. The data collectively indicate that alkyl and aryl carbonates both favor carbonyl exchange reactions yielding stable carbamates. The reactivity and selectivity of PUF depolymerization is intrinsically correlated with the equilibrium between carbamate species, which is set by the electronic nature of the carbonate reagent. These findings are essential for a better understanding of carbonate‐assisted PUF depolymerization and to maximize monomer recovery bypassing unwanted side products.

Separation of Carbonated Polyol and Me‐TDC in DMC‐Assisted Depolymerization

A key difference between aliphatic and aromatic carbonates was observed in the postdepolymerization separation behavior. Depolymerization with aromatic carbonates did not allow effective separation of polyol and aromatic products with mixed aromatic fractions. In contrast, the use of an aliphatic carbonate was essential to enable product separation. In particular, methyl functionalization of both the polyol and Me‐TDC appeared to be essential for achieving an efficient separation of polyols from aromatic products. This is particularly valuable because DMC produces high yields of Me‐TDC with distinct solubility behavior that can be used for purification. During DMC depolymerization, we observed that the carbonated polyol fraction contained suspended Me‐TDC species that gradually undergo spontaneous crystallization upon cooling. This observation demonstrated that the Me‐TDC has an intrinsically low solubility in the methyl carbonate‐functionalized polyol, providing a first indication that the two monomeric products resulting from the depolymerization of PUF could be separated. It should be noted that the use of DEC [23] did not provide such a separation, likely due to increased conformational freedom of the ethyl group. Nevertheless, ethyl carbonate capping of the polyol reduced its polarity sufficiently to enable selective extraction from the aromatic fraction. These observations highlight that aliphatic carbonate group plays a crucial role in downstream separation either by promoting crystallization (methyl) or by facilitating polarity‐driven phase separation (ethyl).

To identify a suitable solvent for the separation of Me‐TDC and the methyl carbonate‐functionalized polyol, we screened several polar and nonpolar solvents (chloroform, toluene, DMSO, DMF, water, n‐heptane, and cyclohexane). All attempts showed that cyclohexane was the only effective solvent for obtaining a pure polyol fraction free from aromatics after PUF depolymerization. The addition of cyclohexane selectively dissolved the carbonated polyol while leaving the Me‐TDC undissolved, allowing the polyol monomer to be washed away. To prove the separation of the polyol from the Me‐TDC, the 1H NMR spectrum of the separated polyol is shown in Figure S11. This step yielded an aromatic solid fraction that can be isolated by simple filtration, as shown in Figure 4 steps III–V. The remaining filtrate contained pure methyl‐carbonated polyol, free from aromatic monomers. The isolated Me‐TDC was obtained in 58% yield, while the recovered carbonated polyol was obtained in ∼85% yield, consistent with the corrected theoretical mass by additional methyl carbonate groups. This approach demonstrates that DMC not only ensures efficient urea‐to‐carbamate conversion but also provides a useful separation method that allows recovery of both monomers under mild, phosgene‐free conditions, which is an essential requirement for our proposed closed‐loop recycling.

Conclusion

In this study we evaluated how the structure of alkyl and aryl carbonates affects the depolymerization of PUF by carbonyl‐exchange chemistry. Direct comparison of DMC, DPC, MPC, and mixed carbonate systems revealed that the nature of carbonate plays a decisive role in product selectivity, side‐product formation and overall monomer quantification. Aryl carbonates such as DPC improved the solubility of the urea hard domains, however, they also undergo partial hydrolysis generating phenol that originates primarily from the reagent rather than from PUF cleavage. The behavior of MPC closely resembled that of an equimolar DMC/DPC mixture confirming that mixed alkyl–aryl carbonates undergo exchange and support equilibration as the underlying mechanism. In contrast, alkyl carbonates, specifically DMC, provided cleaner carbamate formation and more controlled product distribution. Using anisole as an inert aromatic cosolvent improved the solubility of urea hard domains without participating in carbonyl exchange. Quantitative NMR analysis confirmed high efficiency and selectivity of the depolymerization in anisole addition, with liquid products containing 85% of the aromatics as Me‐TDC monomers, and 95% of the polyol functionalized with methyl carbonate end groups. Additionally, high yields of Me‐TDC monomers spontaneously solidified in methyl carbonate‐functionalized polyol and allowed a separation of the precursors of PU building blocks in cyclohexane.

Overall, our findings demonstrate that alkyl carbonates are the preferred reagents for selective PUF depolymerization, while anisole addition improves accessibility of urea hard domains and enables monomer separation. The polyol can be conveniently separated from TDC by separation, and this allows both of the compounds to be used for other applications. Importantly, DMC and DEC can be produced from CO2 and renewable alcohols, such as biobased ethanol or bio/CO2‐based methanol, further strengthening the renewable character of this approach. Together with the avoidance of SVHC reagents such as phosgene, these features support the green chemistry profile of carbonate‐assisted PUF depolymerization. These results suggest a promising control on the carbonate‐assisted PUF depolymerization advancing phosgene‐free routes for thermoset recycling. Future work should address practical process variables such as contaminant tolerance, buildup of trace impurities over multiple recycling cycles, heat‐transfer control, purification of separated products, and operational safety, all of which demand dedicated process design and engineering development.
