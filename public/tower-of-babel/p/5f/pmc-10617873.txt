Neurocognitive impairment in obesity and liver disease—An unsolved mystery
Authors: Lyes Kheloufi, Nicolas Weiss, Dominique Thabut, Raluca Pais
Published: 2023

To the editor,

We read with great interest the very interesting article by Wernberg et al published in Hepatology Communications in May 2023.1 The authors investigated the prevalence and the relationship between cognitive impairment and the severity spectrum of metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) and metabolic dysfunction–associated steatohepatitis (MASH) in a cohort of 180 obese patients undergoing bariatric surgery. Although there was a tendency toward poorer cognitive test performances in patients with advanced fibrosis, no association was found with the histological lesions of MASH. Results are interesting as they question the real role of brain ageing linked to obesity. However, candidates for bariatric surgery have several particularities that differentiate them from the “classical” MASLD patients.

First, although the prevalence of steatosis is high, the prevalence of MASH and significant fibrosis is low; therefore, bariatric surgery patients are not representative of the whole spectrum of MASLD. Only 5% of patients (9/180) in this cohort had a NAS >5, therefore, it is not surprising that no association was found between MASH and cognitive impairment.

Second, body composition and fat distribution are major determinants of adipose tissue insulin resistance and chronic systemic inflammation and might confound the relationship between obesity, liver damage, and brain dysfunction. Previous bariatric surgery studies have shown that trunk/limb adipose tissue ratio and adipocyte size progressively increase from normal liver to steatosis and MASH.2 Expansion of visceral adipose tissue and adipocyte hypertrophy are associated with loss of insulin sensitivity, increased cytokine production, progression of liver damage, and cognitive impairment even after adjustment for confounders.3 It has been shown that younger adults have a better capacity to store free fatty acids in subcutaneous adipose tissue rather than in the liver. This explains why younger adults being candidates for bariatric surgery are more prone to be otherwise “metabolically healthy” and have less severe liver damage.2 In the study of Wernberg et al, bariatric surgery patients were young (mean age 45±12 y) and had a low prevalence (<25%) of metabolic comorbidities or significant liver damage (23% MASH, 25% ≥F2, 6% F3F4).

Third, the role of the gut-liver-brain axis in neuroinflammation and cognitive impairment is well established. Gut microbiota is significantly altered in morbidly obese patients and is characterized by reduced microbial gene richness and diversity.4 Some of these signatures are common in obesity and MASLD, while others are liver-specific and correlated to histological severity. Therefore, specific bacterial signatures related to obesity rather than MASH or advanced fibrosis (whose prevalence was low in this cohort) might account for the lack of correlations between cognitive impairment and MASH.

Fourth, the authors attribute cognitive impairment to potential HE. Nevertheless, even if the tests used have been validated in HE, their abnormal results cannot, on their own, validate the diagnosis of HE. Other neurological diseases can mimic such abnormalities.5 In the present study, the differences in ammonia levels between groups were not statistically significant (p = 0.05 and p = 0.848), and more importantly, ammonia levels were almost always within the normal range and low (median 29 micromole/l), rending the diagnosis of HE very unlikely. Cognitive tests validated for HE (PSE, CRT, Stroop) are based on the evaluation of attention, psychomotor speed, and executive functions that can also be impacted by other neurological or psychological conditions. Additionally, short-term and long-term memories appear to be independent of each other and rely on different neuronal pathways. While long-term memory is not supposed to be affected by HE, short-term memory is strongly correlated to attention and thus can be affected in patients with HE but also in other metabolic, neurologic, or psychological conditions.

Finally, the context of cognitive assessment can lead to several biases in the results reported here. Information is lacking whether the cognitive tests were performed in fasting conditions and whether the RBANS was proposed directly after the 3 cognitive tests. This kind of information is crucial as it can lead to significant biases, as previously reported, and might potentially explain the divergence between tests according to their order of presentation.

We believe that more standardized conditions are required to perform the cognitive tests to avoid fatigue, stress, and anxiety. Further studies in patients with histologically proven MASLD outside bariatric surgery cohort to avoid potential confounders and longitudinal follow-up would be welcome to unravel this very important area when the incidence of metabolic cirrhosis is growing year after year worldwide.
