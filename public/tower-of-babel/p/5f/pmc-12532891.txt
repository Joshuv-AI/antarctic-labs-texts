A novel prognostic nomogram for elderly patients with severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome
Authors: Zhongwei Zhang, Xue Hu, Qunqun Jiang, Qian Du, Qianhui Chen, Xiaoping Chen, Mingqi Luo, Miao Tan, Liping Deng, Yong Xiong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSevere fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome (SFTS) is a life-threatening infectious disease triggered by a novel bunyavirus (SFTSV). The elderly population is vulnerable to infectious diseases. This study aimed to explore the clinical features and outcomes of elderly patients with SFTS and establish an effective prognostic nomogram for them.MethodsData on demographics, comorbidities, clinical manifestations, laboratory parameters, and outcomes of patients with SFTS were collected. Independent predictors for in-hospital mortality identified by multivariate logistic regression were used to construct the predictive model.Results253 patients with SFTS were retrospectively enrolled, including 142 (56.1%) elderly patients and 111 (43.9%) non-elderly patients. Compared with non-elderly patients, elderly patients had higher serum levels of laboratory parameters indicating liver, kidney, pancreas, heart, coagulation system injury, and higher viral load. The in-hospital mortality rate of elderly patients was significantly higher than that of non-elderly patients. The univariate and multivariate binary logistic regression analyses demonstrated that neurological manifestations, creatinine, CKMB, and prothrombin time were proven to be independent predictors for in-hospital mortality of elderly patients with SFTS, which were adopted as parameters of the nomogram. The nomogram showed a good calibration and discrimination, with an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.849 (95% CI 0.774–0.923). Decision curve analysis confirmed the clinical utility of the predictive model.ConclusionsElderly patients are more susceptible to adverse outcomes, and the nomogram can help clinicians predict in-hospital mortality of elderly patients with SFTS, which may facilitate optimal therapeutic strategies to improve their prognosis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-025-06423-5.

Introduction

Severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome (SFTS) is a rapidly progressive emerging zoonosis caused by a novel bunyavirus (SFTSV). It was initially reported in the rural areas of China in 2009 [1], and appeared in Korea [2], Japan [3], Thailand [4], and Vietnam [5]. As SFTSV can be transmitted from person to person [6], the number of SFTS cases has continued to increase notably in recent years, making it a newly life-threatening emerging infectious disease around the world. Patients with SFTS usually present with fever and thrombocytopenia, accompanied by some nonspecific symptoms such as dizziness, headache, nausea, vomiting, and myalgia. Most critically ill patients with SFTS develop multiple-organ failure, including encephalitis, respiratory failure, severe pancreatitis, acute kidney injury, and disseminated intravascular coagulation, and the case fatality rate ranges from 11 to 30% in various epidemic regions [1, 7–10].

Previous studies have reported that the prevalence of SFTS significantly increases with age, and SFTS onset and death primarily occur in elderly patients. It is proven that age is an independent risk factor for the prognosis of patients with SFTS and can be used to construct the predictive model for identifying high-risk patients [11–13]. However, these studies have partly shown the relationship between elderly patients and adverse outcomes in SFTS. To our knowledge, the clinical characteristics and outcomes of elderly patients with SFTS have not been elucidated in detail before, and there is no clinical predictive model for the prognosis of elderly patients with SFTS. Therefore, an accurate assessment of disease severity, early screening, and timely treatment remain important.

In the present study, we aimed to illuminate the clinical characteristics and outcomes of elderly patients with SFTS. Then we identified the independent predictors for in-hospital mortality of elderly patients with SFTS, which were adopted as parameters of a nomogram to predict the prognosis of elderly patients with SFTS.

Methods

Patients

A total of 253 patients with SFTS admitted to the Department of Infectious Diseases, Zhongnan Hospital of Wuhan University between August 2016 and October 2024 were included in a retrospective cohort (Supplementary Fig. 1). Patients were assigned into elderly (≥ 65 years) and non-elderly (< 65 years) groups. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Zhongnan Hospital of Wuhan University (2024178 K).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients with fever (temperatures ≥ 37.3 ˚C); (2) patients with decreased platelet count; (3) patients with positive results of serum SFTSV RNA.

Patients were excluded if they met one or more of the following reasons: (1) the presence of preterminal comorbidities (heart disease New York Heart Association III–IV, severe chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, chronic renal failure), (2) any other types of immunodeficiency, (3) incomplete clinical data or missed follow-up. (4) the existence of other viral infections such as coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19).

Data collection

The medical records of patients with SFTS were reviewed, demographics, comorbidities, clinical manifestations and laboratory test results including white blood cell (WBC) count and percentage, neutrophils count and percentage, lymphocyte count and percentage, platelet count, hemoglobin, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), total bilirubin (TBIL), albumin, alkaline phosphatase (ALP), gamma glutamyl transpeptidase (GGT), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), amylase, lipase, total cholesterol (TC), triglyceride (TG), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine, cystatin-C, sodium, potassium, myoglobin, creatinine kinase (CK), creatinine kinase myocardial b fraction (CKMB), troponin I, brain natriuretic peptide (BNP), prothrombin time (PT), international normalized ratio (INR), prothrombin activity (PTA), activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), thrombin time (TT), fibrinogen, D-dimer, SFTSV viral load, occult blood test (OBT), C-reactive protein (CRP), procalcitonin, erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), serum amyloid A (SAA), ferritin, interleukin-2 (IL-2), interleukin-4 (IL-4), interleukin-6 (IL-6), interleukin-10 (IL-10), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interferon-γ (IFN-γ), CD3 + cells count, CD3 + CD4 + cells count, CD3 + CD8 + cells count, CD19 + cells count, CD16 + CD56 + cells count on admission were collected. The primary outcome was in-hospital mortality, and the survival time of non-survivors was calculated from the day of admission to death.

Neurological manifestations in this study were defined as a set of clinical symptoms indicating dysfunction of the central nervous system. These manifestations included coma, lethargy, confusion, and dysphoria. Coma is defined as a state of unarousable unresponsiveness, where the patient does not respond to any external stimuli, including painful stimuli. Coma was assessed based on the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), and a score of 8 or less indicated coma. Lethargy is characterized by drowsiness, sluggishness, or a lack of energy. Patients with lethargy were difficult to arouse and required repeated stimuli to maintain wakefulness. Lethargy was assessed through clinical observation and patient interaction. Confusion is defined as a state of disorientation, impaired memory, or difficulty in thinking clearly. Confusion was assessed using the Confusion Assessment Method (CAM) or through clinical judgment by the attending physician, considering factors such as disorientation to time, place, or person, and impaired attention or memory. Dysphoria is described as a state of unease, dissatisfaction, or restlessness. Patients with dysphoria often exhibited irritability, anxiety, or a general sense of malaise. Dysphoria was assessed through patient self-report (when possible) and clinical observation by the healthcare team.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were shown as numbers (percentages) and were compared by the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Continuous variables were shown as medians with interquartile ranges (P25-P75) for data with a non-normal distribution, which were compared by the Mann–Whitney U test. The independent predictors of in-hospital mortality of elderly patients with SFTS were identified by univariate and multivariate binary logistic regression. To identify independent predictors of the in-hospital mortality of elderly patients while adjusting for confounding effects of other variables, variables that were significant in the univariate analysis (P < 0.05) were included in the initial multivariate model. A stepwise backward elimination approach was applied. The variable with the highest non-significant P value (P > 0.05) was removed from the model. The model was refitted, and the process repeated until all remaining variables were statistically significant (P < 0.05). The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) was evaluated for the discrimination of the nomogram. The goodness of fit of the nomogram was assessed by using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test combined with the calibration curve. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was used to evaluate the clinical utility of the nomogram. The rms package (version 6.8-2) was used to develop and visualize the nomogram. Calibration curves were generated using the rms package (version 6.8-2), ggplot2 package (version 3.5.1), and extrafont package (version 0.19). ROC curves and AUC metrics were computed using the pROC package (version 1.18.5). The rmda package (version 1.6) was employed to perform DCA and assess the clinical utility of the model. All data were analyzed with SPSS statistical analysis software version 26.0 (IBM), R statistical analysis software (version 4.4.1, Vienna, Austria), and P < 0.05 (two-sided) was considered statistically significant.

Results

Comparison of demographics, comorbidities, and clinical manifestations between non-elderly and elderly patients

Among the 253 patients, 111 (43.9%) were aged < 65 years, whereas 142 (56.1%) were aged ≥ 65 years. The median age was 58 years (interquartile range [IQR], 53–62 years) for the non-elderly group and 71 years (IQR, 69–75 years) for the elderly group. Compared with non-elderly patients, elderly patients had a higher proportion of men and a higher prevalence of neurological manifestations, including coma and lethargy. The incidence of chest distress, dyspnea, petechia and ecchymosis, gingival bleeding and myasthenia in elderly patients was significantly higher than in non-elderly patients. However, there were no significant differences in the proportion of diabetes, hypertension, confusion, dysphoria, cough, sputum, anorexia, nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, diarrhea, haematemesis, melena, epistaxis, fever > 38 ℃, chills, headache, dizziness, and myalgia between the two groups (Table 1).

Comparison of laboratory test results between non-elderly and elderly patients

The serum levels of acute-phase proteins, cytokines, and immune cells counts were also compared between the two groups. Elderly patients had significantly higher serum levels of CRP, procalcitonin, SAA, ferritin, IL-2, IL-6, IL-10, and TNF-α than non-elderly patients. Additionally, the CD3 + cells and CD3 + CD4 + cells counts in elderly patients were significantly lower than those in non-elderly patients (Fig. 1).

Comparison of duration of platelet count normalization, duration of SFTSV RNA shedding, and in-hospital mortality rates between non-elderly and elderly patients

The median duration of platelet count normalization of non-elderly and elderly patients was 16 days (IQR, 12–21 days) and 19 days (IQR, 16–24 days), respectively. The findings indicated that the duration of platelet count normalization of elderly patients was significantly longer than that of non-elderly patients after hospitalization (Fig. 2A). The median duration of SFTSV RNA shedding of non-elderly and elderly patients was 12 days (IQR, 8–15 days) and 16 days (IQR, 12–19 days), respectively. The results showed that the duration of SFTSV RNA shedding of elderly patients was significantly longer than that of non-elderly patients after hospitalization (Fig. 2B). As shown in Fig. 2C, the in-hospital mortality of elderly patients was almost three times that of non-elderly patients (26.1% vs. 9.0%, P < 0.001).

Comparison of demographics, comorbidities, and clinical manifestations between survivor and non-survivor groups in elderly patients

Compared with elderly patients in the survivor group, elderly patients in the non-survivor group had a higher incidence of coma and lethargy, and a higher prevalence of cough, chest distress, dyspnea, abdominal pain, myalgia, and myasthenia. Univariate and multivariate risk analysis for in-hospital mortality of elderly patients with SFTS

Univariate and multivariate risk analysis for in-hospital mortality of elderly patients with SFTS

By univariate analysis, the presence of neurological manifestations, dyspnea, abdominal pain, myalgia, myasthenia, serum levels of AST, LDH, BUN, creatinine, cystatin-C, potassium, lipase, CKMB, myoglobin, BNP, PT, INR, APTT, TT, D-dimer, viral load, and low serum levels of hemoglobin, albumin, fibrinogen, and PTA were identified as risk factors for in-hospital mortality of elderly patients. Among these parameters, neurological manifestations (aOR, 3.960; 95% CI, 1.317–11.911; P = 0.014), serum levels of creatinine (aOR, 1.010; 95% CI, 1.002–1.018; P = 0.012), CKMB (aOR, 1.016; 95% CI, 1.004–1.029; P = 0.010), and PT (aOR, 1.848; 95% CI, 1.257–2.717; P = 0.002) were proven to be independent predictors for in-hospital mortality on multivariate analysis (Table 4).

Predictive model for in-hospital mortality of elderly patients with SFTS

Based on the univariate and multivariate logistic regression analysis, a nomogram incorporating four predictors was constructed to predict the probability of in-hospital mortality of elderly patients with SFTS. In the nomogram, creatinine, CKMB, and PT were used as continuous linear predictors and had continuous scales. Each predictor was given a corresponding score by projecting its status to the upper point scale (0–100) with a straight line. A total score was then calculated by summing up the whole scores of the four predictors. The predicted probability of in-hospital mortality could be obtained by projecting the total score line straight to the probability scale line at the bottom (Fig. 3).

The discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility of the nomogram

The AUC of the nomogram was 0.849 (95% CI 0.774–0.923), showing a perfect discriminative performance (Fig. 4A). The goodness-of-fit χ2 of the nomogram was 9.227 (P = 0.323), which indicated no evidence of poor fit. Additionally, the calibration plot of the nomogram also showed a good agreement between the predicted and actual probabilities (Fig. 4B). DCA is an appropriate method for assessing predictive models by the net benefit and the range of threshold probabilities. In the present study, DCA also evaluated the clinical utility of the nomogram. The plots revealed that the nomogram showed a greater net benefit with a wider range of threshold probabilities for predicting in-hospital mortality of elderly patients with SFTS (Fig. 4C).

Discussion

With an increasingly ageing society, clinicians should encounter more elderly patients in clinical practice. Accumulating evidence has shown that the incidence of SFTS significantly increases with age, and SFTS onset and death primarily occur in elderly patients [14, 15]. However, little is known about the clinical characteristics and risk factors of this infectious disease in elderly patients. Thus, we explored the clinical characteristics and outcomes of elderly patients with SFTS and established a simplified nomogram model to accurately predict the in-hospital mortality of these patients.

Our study elaborated, for the first time, the clinical characteristics of elderly patients with SFTS. We revealed that elderly patients had significantly more presence of neurological manifestations, chest distress, dyspnea, petechia and ecchymosis, gingival bleeding, and myasthenia, which generally reflect multiple-organ dysfunction. We also unveiled that the elderly patients had higher serum levels of laboratory parameters referring to liver, pancreas, kidney, cardiac, coagulation system injury, and higher viral load than non-elderly patients. The extremely abnormal levels of these laboratory indicators usually reflect multiple-organ failure, even high mortality [16]. It is generally recognized that neurological manifestations and high viral load are the independent predictors for poor prognosis of patients with SFTS [17].

In the present study, it was demonstrated that elderly patients had higher serum levels of CRP, procalcitonin, SAA, ferritin, IL-2, IL-6, IL-10, and TNF-α than non-elderly patients. Some studies have proven that CRP, procalcitonin, and ferritin are independent predictors for the adverse outcomes of patients with SFTS [18, 19]. Chen et al. showed that serum concentrations of SAA were substantially higher in fatal patients with SFTS [20]. Previous studies have reported that serum levels of IL-2, IL-6, IL-10, and TNF-α were significantly elevated in the death group, and could be independent risk factors for the adverse outcomes of patients with SFTS [21–23]. Immune function is generally weakened with age, and impaired immune function can promote the progression of viral infectious diseases. Similarly, we found that the CD3 + and CD3 + CD4 + cells counts were significantly decreased in elderly patients. Some studies have reported that T cell subsets, including CD3 + and CD3 + CD4 + cells counts were correlated with the disease progression and severity of patients with SFTS [24, 25]. These results provided an underlying mechanism to explain why elderly patients tended to have adverse outcomes than non-elderly patients in SFTS.

In our cohort, the durations of platelet count recovery and serum SFTSV RNA shedding in elderly patients were significantly longer than in non-elderly patients. The prolonged platelet counts recovery and viral shedding have been proven to be associated with the high mortality of patients with SFTS [26, 27]. In addition, we revealed that some life-threatening complications, including respiratory failure, acute kidney injury, shock, SIRS, bacterial or fungal infections, and disseminated intravascular coagulation were more frequent in elderly patients. Previous studies have reported that these complications are independent predictors of poor prognosis in patients with SFTS [7–10, 28]. We showed that elderly patients received a higher frequency of noninvasive and invasive mechanical ventilation, continuous renal replacement therapy, use of vasoactive drugs, and platelet transfusion (Supplementary Table 2). However, we revealed that the in-hospital mortality rate of elderly patients was significantly higher than that of non-elderly patients. We also confirmed that age ≥ 65 years was an independent risk factor for the prognosis of patients with SFTS (Supplementary Table 3).

In the present study, neurological manifestations, serum levels of creatinine, CKMB, and PT were proven to be independent predictors for in-hospital mortality of elderly patients with SFTS. As mentioned above, neurological manifestations are confirmed to be a life-threatening complication of patients with SFTS and are generally associated with fatal outcomes [29]. The rapid rise in serum levels of creatinine in a short time generally represent acute kidney injury. It has been reported that acute kidney injury is associated with a high case fatality rate in SFTS [7, 30]. Previous studies have also shown that serum levels of creatinine can be an independent risk factor for mortality in patients with SFTS [31, 32]. CKMB is one of the specific biomarkers of myocardial injury, and it is correlated with adverse outcomes of patients with SFTS [31, 33]. PT is one of the blood tests routinely used in clinical practice to evaluate the coagulation status of patients. Prolonged PT has been proven to be an independent risk factor for the prognosis of patients, which can be used to construct a clinical risk scoring model to predict fatal risk in SFTS hospitalized patients [15, 34].

Neurological manifestations can be assessed through basic clinical examination, which does not require advanced equipment. In low-resource settings, clinicians can be trained to recognize key neurological signs using standardized checklists (e.g., Glasgow Coma Scale). Serum creatinine measurement is a routine laboratory test that can be performed using basic biochemical analyzers. These analyzers are increasingly available even in resource-limited settings, particularly in remote areas. While CKMB assays may be less accessible than creatinine tests, rapid point-of-care tests for cardiac enzymes (including CKMB) are being developed in low-resource settings for managing acute cardiovascular disease. point-of-care coagulometers (including PT) are now used in many low-resource settings for managing bleeding disorders (e.g., trauma, maternal health). Therefore, serum creatinine, CKMB, and PT are now widely available in resource-limited settings where SFTS is common. For elderly patients identified as high-risk based on the nomogram, more frequent monitoring of these parameters can be instituted. This allows for early detection of deterioration and timely initiation of appropriate interventions, potentially improving elderly patient survival and reducing morbidity.

There were certain limitations in this study. First, the sample size of this study was relatively small, and subsequent validation of the nomogram was not conducted, which limits the generalization of our findings. Second, because this was a single-center retrospective study, the observations made here could not be extrapolated to other centers. Therefore, further multicenter, prospective studies are required to verify the discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility of the nomogram.

Conclusion

In summary, adverse outcomes, including multi-organ injury or even failure, and death are more common in elderly patients with SFTS. Neurological manifestations, serum levels of creatinine, CKMB, and PT are independent predictors for in-hospital mortality of elderly patients with SFTS. We established a practical clinical scoring system, through which the risk of in-hospital mortality in elderly patients with SFTS can be estimated. We can detect high-risk elderly patients and provide intensive medical interventions for them as soon as possible, potentially reducing the mortality rate in SFTS.
