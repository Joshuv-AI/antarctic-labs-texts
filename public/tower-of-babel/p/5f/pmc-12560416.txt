Effect of sub-anesthetic dose of esketamine on oculocardiac reflex of pediatric strabismus surgery in total intravenous anesthesia: study protocol for a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Jiaqi Liu, Guiyu Lei, Guyan Wang, Hao Cheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe oculocardiac reflex (OCR) is the most common complication in pediatric strabismus surgery, and its main clinical manifestation is bradycardia. Esketamine can activate the sympathetic nervous system, leading to an indirect cardiovascular stimulation effect, which results in increased blood pressure and a faster heart rate. The aim of this study is to explore whether a sub-anesthetic dose of esketamine can effectively inhibit the oculocardiac reflex during strabismus surgery.MethodThis prospective, single-center, randomized, double-blinded trial will enroll 204 pediatric patients undergoing strabismus surgery. Participants will be randomly assigned to esketamine (group S) and control groups (group C) in a 1:1 ratio. Esketamine (0.2 mg/kg) or an equal volume of normal saline will be administered after anesthesia induction. The OCR is defined as a sudden decrease in heart rate (HR) of 20% from baseline, and severe OCR will be defined as profound bradycardia (HR < 50 beats per minute). The primary outcome is the incidence of overall OCR during surgery. Secondary outcomes include severe OCR during all stages, the incidence of OCR at various stages of surgery, postoperative agitation, postoperative nausea and vomiting, procedure suspension frequency, and atropine administration requirements.DiscussionThe purpose of this study is to explore whether the administration of a sub-anesthetic dose of esketamine in pediatric strabismus surgery can reduce the incidence of OCR during surgery. The findings of this study may offer a new perspective and direct clinical evidence for the prevention and treatment of OCR during the perioperative period.Trial registration.Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2400094053). Introduction

Background and rationale {6a}

The strabismus surgery is one of the most common types of ophthalmic surgeries in the pediatric population, aimed at fine-tuning the extraocular muscles (EMOs) responsible for eye movement to enhance ocular coordination [1]. During the surgical process, traction on the extraocular muscles is often required, which can lead to a common clinical complication known as the OCR. The OCR is a vagal nerve reflex that can cause bradycardia or arrhythmias when the eyeball or extraocular muscles are stimulated [2]. In strabismus surgery, the occurrence of OCR is directly related to surgical manipulation, especially during the traction of extraocular muscles or the application of pressure to the eyeball. Therefore, the association between strabismus surgery and the risk of OCR is particularly significant among all types of surgeries [3].

The OCR is known as the trigemino-vagal reflex. The afferent limb of the OCR comprises the long and short ciliary nerves of the trigeminal nerve, and the efferent limb includes the cardiac branch of the vagus nerve and the intracardiac ganglia [4], which diminishes sinoatrial node impulses, leading to bradycardia [5]. The OCR has been defined as a sudden decrease in heart rate greater than 10% to 20% of baseline with traction of the extraocular muscles [6]. The incidence of the OCR in strabismus surgery has been reported to reach 96.6% [1]. The most common manifestations are bradycardia and dysrhythmia [1]. Usually, these situations are mild and transient and can be resolved by releasing the extraocular muscles. But in severe cases, it can lead to cardiac arrest and respiratory cessation [7].

The risk of developing OCR is significantly higher in children than in adults [6]. This elevated risk is primarily attributed to the dominance of vagal nerve activity in pediatric populations[8]. As the central nervous system matures, the incidence of the OCR decreases with age. Additionally, the decrease in heart rate after general anesthesia in children is more dangerous than in adults. Children’s cardiac output is dependent on heart rate; therefore, the only way to increase cardiac output is by raising the heart rate [9]. Many methods have been studied to prevent the occurrence of the OCR, such as premedication with anticholinergics [6]. However, no single method has been conclusively proven to completely prevent the occurrence of the OCR.

Ketamine is an N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor antagonist, clinically used as an anesthetic, sedative, and analgesic in pediatric strabismus surgery [10]. Previous studies have indicated that ketamine can not only reduce the incidence of the OCR in children undergoing strabismus surgery [11, 12] but also decrease the occurrence of postoperative agitation and postoperative pain [10, 13].

Esketamine is the S-enantiomer of ketamine and shares similar pharmacological mechanisms with ketamine. It has twice the potency of ketamine but with fewer side effects [14]. Compared to ketamine, esketamine demonstrates several significant advantages in clinical applications. It features a shorter recovery period, milder postoperative pain, faster cognitive recovery, and a lower incidence of psychotropic side effects [15, 16]. Additionally, esketamine has a faster clearance rate and stronger controllability [17]. Moreover, esketamine has a minimal impact on respiratory and circulatory systems, a characteristic that is particularly important in pediatric anesthesia [18]. Given that children’s respiratory systems are more sensitive to anesthetic agents, esketamine may be more suitable for pediatric anesthesia than ketamine. Esketamine can cause the release of catecholamines, inhibit the reuptake of norepinephrine, activate the sympathetic nervous system, thereby producing an indirect cardiovascular stimulation effect, leading to increased blood pressure and accelerated heart rate [19, 20]. Even in small doses, it can stimulate the cardiovascular system. Furthermore, recent studies have shown that a sub-anesthetic dose of esketamine could maintain the stability of hemodynamics during anesthesia induction and reduce postoperative pain in the PACU in pediatrics, without affecting postoperative nausea and vomiting, and postoperative agitation [16, 19]. However, there is currently rare research on the impact of a sub-anesthetic dose of esketamine for the OCR in pediatric strabismus surgery.

Based on the sympathomimetic activity of esketamine, which can increase heart rate, we hypothesize that its use would be associated with a relatively low OCR. The purpose of this study is to investigate the impact of a sub-anesthetic dose of esketamine on the occurrence of OCR during pediatric strabismus surgery.

Objectives {7}

The aim of this study is to investigate whether the sub-anesthetic doses of esketamine will reduce the occurrence of the OCR during pediatric strabismus surgery.

This trial is a prospective, single-center, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial with two parallel arms, with a 1:1 allocation ratio. The framework for the trial is superiority. Interventions

Anesthesia induction

Patients are routinely required to fast for 6–8 h and to refrain from drinking for 2 h; none of the pediatric patients have been premedicated. Upon admission to the room, they are routinely monitored for electrocardiogram (ECG), arterial oxygen saturation, and non-invasive blood pressure. After the pediatric patients are admitted, intravenous access is established, and a 24G intravenous catheter is placed into a dorsal hand, arm, or foot vein. All patients receive 250 ml of an invert sugar and electrolytes solution.

Both groups will be induced intravenously. Before induction, atropine 0.01 mg/kg will be administered intravenously to inhibit gland secretion, followed by propofol 2–3 mg/kg, sufentanil 0.1 μg/kg, and mivacurium chloride 0.15 mg/kg. After the completion of anesthesia induction, when the child is motionless, an appropriately sized LMA (laryngeal mask airway) was inserted.

Intervention description {11a}

After the insertion of the LMA, the group S will be given esketamine 0.2 mg/kg, and group C will be given an equal volume of normal saline.

Explanation for the choice of comparators {6b}

The participants in the control group will be given an equal volume of normal saline.

Intraoperative anesthetic management and postoperative

Both groups will maintain anesthesia intravenously with remifentanil at 0.1–0.2 μg/kg/min and propofol at 6–10 mg/kg/h continuously infused. All patients will be ventilated with volume control, at a frequency of 14–18 breaths per minute, a tidal volume of 6–8 ml/kg, maintaining end-tidal carbon dioxide partial pressure at 35–45 mmHg. In the PACU and within 24 h postoperatively, a visit will be paid to the participants. The follow-up will be conducted by researchers who are unaware of the group allocation.

Before the surgery begins, the ophthalmologist performs a subconjunctival block with 2% lidocaine. After the completion of anesthesia induction and preparation of the surgical field, once the vital signs such as HR are stable, the HR is recorded every 5 s, and the average of three measurements is taken as the baseline HR. Changes in HR are recorded during surgical manipulation, especially during the conjunctival incision (Phase I), extraocular muscle dissection (Phase II), and continuous traction of the extraocular muscles (Phase III). If the HR drops more than 20% from the baseline during the traction of the extraocular muscles, an OCR occurrence is recorded. If HR is less than 50 beats per minute, a severe OCR occurrence is recorded. When the OCR occurs, spontaneous recovery is first assessed. If spontaneous recovery does not occur, the surgeon is immediately instructed to halt the procedure, release the extraocular muscles, and wait for the HR to stabilize before resuming surgical manipulation. If the HR remains unrecovered, atropine (0.01 mg/kg) is administered intravenously. If the HR still does not recover after atropine administration, immediate chest compressions are initiated.

Criteria for discontinuing or modifying allocated interventions {11b}

The participants who meet one of the following criteria will withdraw from this study: (1) participant’s demand; (2) unable to cooperate in establishing intravenous access.

The criteria for modifying the allocated interventions include any suspected unexpected serious adverse reactions or serious adverse events (SAEs) such as serious allergies. In such circumstances, the study may be prematurely terminated, or the research protocol may require immediate modification.

After a participant withdraws from the study, the data collected prior to withdrawal will remain in the study database. Researchers are required to meticulously document the reasons for withdrawal, the exact time of withdrawal, and whether the withdrawal is initiated by the participant or the investigator.

Strategies to improve adherence to interventions {11c}

Establish a positive interactive relationship with the subjects and their families, clearly explain the purpose, steps, and value of their participation in the study. Conduct meticulous follow-ups with the subjects before surgery and within 24 h postoperatively, ensuring that their questions and concerns are promptly and effectively addressed and resolved.

Relevant concomitant care permitted or prohibited during the trial {11d}

Children undergoing strabismus surgery are at an increased risk for postoperative nausea and vomiting. To preempt these complications, dexamethasone is administered intravenously during anesthesia induction for all pediatric patients. In cases where children continue to experience nausea or vomiting after being admitted to the PACU or the ward, ondansetron is administered intravenously as a treatment.

Provisions for post‑trial care {30}

In this study, all medications used have a proven safety profile and a broad history of use in pediatric patients. The anesthesiologists performing the anesthesia are highly skilled in their techniques; therefore, compared to conventional anesthesia, this study does not pose additional risks. Postoperatively, patients will receive the same standard clinical care as usual.

Sample size {14}

Using PASS software, with the incidence rate of OCR during the patient's surgery as the primary outcome measure. According to the pre-experiment from this study, the incidence rate of OCR is expected to be approximately 70% in the control group and around 50% in the esketamine group. With a statistically significant level of bilateral α = 0.05, β = 0.2, and power of 0.8, it is calculated that the sample size for each group is 91. Considering a dropout rate of 10%, we are going to recruit 204 patients.

Recruitment {15}

When the anesthesiologist visits the patients to discuss the anesthesia plan, patients scheduled for elective strabismus surgery will be screened based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Furthermore, once patients are found to be eligible for enrollment, they will be provided with the informed consent form.

Assignment of interventions: allocation

Sequence generation {16a}

Randomization will be conducted by a computer-generated blocked randomization sequence. Participants will be randomly assigned to either the esketamine group (group S) or the control group (group C), and the allocation ratio will be 1:1.

Concealment mechanism {16b}

The allocation sequence will be concealed in opaque sealed envelopes.

Implementation {16c}

On the day of surgery, the pharmacist will unblind according to the group allocation and prepare the medication. The medication will be prepared on the day of enrollment (group S: 0.2 mg/kg esketamine; group C: an equal volume of normal saline), labeled uniformly.

Blinding {17a and 17b}

To ensure the safety of pediatric patients, the attending anesthesiologist will not be blinded, but they will not be involved in subsequent procedures. Pediatric patients, postoperative assessors, statisticians, and other study investigators involved in data analysis will remain blinded to group assignments. Unblinding will only be allowed when serious adverse effects (SAEs) occur.

Data collection and management

Plans for assessment and collection of outcomes {18a}

The demographic characteristics, including the age, height, weight, ASA, duration of surgery, anesthesia duration, occurrence of OCR during surgery, number of times atropine will be used intraoperatively, postoperative nausea and vomiting, and postoperative agitation, will be recorded. We will extract the medical history, diagnosis, and treatment process of participants from the hospital's electronic medical record system and use standardized Case Report Form (CRF) to record various data of participants during the trial, including baseline data, interventions, and follow-up results. All data will be collected by researchers who are blinded to the group assignments to ensure objectivity. Laboratory tests will be conducted by the central diagnostic laboratory, and radiological examinations will be performed in the hospital's radiology department. To protect the privacy of participants, personal information will be anonymized. The collected data will be securely stored on a protected server and regularly backed up to prevent data loss. Moreover, access to the data will be strictly restricted to members of the study team.

Plans to promote participant retention and complete follow‑up {18b}

The study process and requirements will be communicated to patients in detail. The follow-up period of this study is 24 h, which is the duration of hospitalization for.

most patients. If the participant is absent from the hospital for any reason, a telephone follow-up can also be completed at 24 h after surgery.

Data management {19}

All data and results collected in this study will be recorded in the paper version of the CRF in a timely manner by a designated member of the research group. These records will then be checked by another member of the research group who is not involved in the study for electronic input to ensure the authenticity and accuracy of the research data. Data entry and management will be performed by the research group staff. The paper version of the CRF will be stored by the staff, and the original data CRF and related records will be retained for at least 5 years and then destroyed according to the hospital's standard procedures.

Confidentiality {27}

During the data processing, researchers will remove all directly identifiable information, such as names and addresses, to protect privacy. Sensitive data will be encrypted for storage and transmission to prevent unauthorized access. Research data will be kept confidential until the end of the study, and personal identifying information will not be disclosed when results are published.

Biological specimens {33}

Statistical methods

Statistical methods for primary and secondary outcomes {20a}

The primary analysis will be conducted according to the principles of intention-to-treat (ITT), and all patients randomly enrolled in the study (regardless of protocol deviations) will be included in the final analysis. Normality will be tested using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and quantitative indicators will be described using the mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range, IQR). Qualitative indicators will be described using frequency and percentage. Comparisons between groups for quantitative indicators will use the independent samples t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test, and comparisons for qualitative data will use the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test.

All statistical tests will use a significance level of 5%, and the statistical analysis software will be SPSS version 21.0.

Interim analyses {21b}

The trial does not have an interim analysis plan.

Methods for additional analyses (e.g., subgroup analyses) {20b}

There are no subgroup analyses planned.

Methods in analysis to handle protocol non‑adherence and any statistical methods to handle missing data {20c}

Missing data due to patient withdrawal will not be included in the final data analysis. Intention-to-treat will be used to handle protocol non-adherence except for participant withdrawal, such as losing the contact of the participant. The missing data will be minimized by using the methods mentioned above. When the primary outcome is missing, multiple imputation will be used to address the missing values. When fewer than 5% of secondary outcomes are missing, we will directly delete the missing values. When more than 5% of secondary indicators are missing, we will still use multiple imputation to handle the missing values.

Adverse event reporting and harms {22}

A serious adverse event (SAE) is defined as any untoward medical occurrence that meets one or more of the following criteria: (1) results in death; (2) is life-threatening; (3) requires inpatient hospitalization or prolongation of existing hospitalization; (4) results in persistent or significant disability or incapacity; (5) results in a congenital anomaly or birth defect. In our study, any SAE will be immediately reported to the principal investigator. The principal investigator will subsequently report all SAEs or any unanticipated problems involving risks to participants to the research ethics board and the principal investigator at Beijing Tongren Hospital. If these problems are thought to be associated with participating in the trial, then they will also be reported to the research ethics.

The most likely adverse events in this study are oculocardiac reflex, postoperative nausea and vomiting, and postoperative agitation. All AEs reported by participants or observed by investigators will be recorded. We will use the standardized language MedDRA to classify and describe the adverse events.

Frequency and plans for auditing trial conduct {23}

Protocol amendments {25}

During the trial process, any revision to the protocol must first be submitted to the ethics committee for review and approval. The ethics committee will assess the risks and benefits of the revised protocol and provide its opinion. To avoid urgent harm to the subjects, researchers may make modifications beforehand and promptly submit a written report to the ethics committee thereafter.

Discussion

Ketamine is widely used in pediatric anesthesia due to its mild hemodynamic stimulation and lack of significant respiratory depression [21]. As the S-enantiomer of ketamine, esketamine demonstrates higher efficacy, approximately double that of ketamine [22]. Therefore, to achieve the same effect, a significantly lower dose of esketamine is required compared to ketamine, and it also takes effect more rapidly [20]. Concurrently, esketamine has an advantage in reducing adverse reactions, particularly in lowering the incidence of psychotropic side effects [19]. Esketamine has been used in pediatric patients for a considerable length of time and can be safely and effectively utilized as a preoperative medication, as well as an adjunctive medication for various diagnostic and therapeutic procedures and for anesthetic and analgesic purposes during surgeries [20, 23]. The advantages of using esketamine in pediatric anesthesia are mainly reflected in the following aspects: First, esketamine can effectively alleviate preoperative separation anxiety and postoperative agitation in children, which is of great significance for reducing psychological stress responses in children during the perioperative period [16, 24]. Second, intravenous administration of a sub-anesthetic dose of esketamine can provide good analgesic effects without significant respiratory depression [19]. Additionally, with the reduction of the drug dose, the side effects of esketamine are also significantly decreased [25].

Given that the side effects of eketamine are dose-dependent [25, 26], a lower dose of esketamine has the potential to reduce the risk of adverse reactions during anesthesia, which is the reason we have chosen to use a sub-anesthetic dose of esketamine in our trial. The sub-anesthetic dose refers to a dosage that is lower than that required for anesthesia, and it can play a role in enhancing the effects of anesthesia [19]. According to the literature, the sub-anesthetic dose of ketamine generally ranges from 0.3 to 0.5 mg/kg [27]. Since the potency of esketamine is twice that of ketamine, it is commonly believed that the sub-anesthetic dose of esketamine is between 0.15 and 0.25 mg/kg [20, 28]. In recent years, the use of sub-anesthetic dose of esketamine as an adjunctive medication in pediatric surgery has been proven to be safe and feasible [23, 29]. This method not only effectively reduces the intraoperative need for propofol and opioid drugs [19] but also helps alleviate preoperative anxiety and postoperative agitation in children [16], promoting their rapid recovery. In the majority of clinical studies, a sub-anesthetic dose of 0.2 mg/kg of esketamine has been widely applied in various surgical scenarios, demonstrating favorable safety and efficacy [16, 28]. A randomized controlled trial [30] revealed that intravenous administration of 0.2 mg/kg of esketamine for pediatric painless gastroscopy not only reduces the dosage of propofol but also maintains hemodynamic stability, decreases the incidence of adverse reactions, and provides effective sedation and analgesia. Based on these findings, we have opted for a 0.2 mg/kg dose of esketamine as the intervention in our study, with the aim of ensuring safety while reducing the incidence of oculocardiac reflex.

In conclusion, in this prospective randomized controlled trial, we want to verify whether using a sub-anesthesia dose of esketamine in pediatric strabismus surgery can reduce the incidence of OCR during surgery and to further explore the potential impact of this dosage of esketamine on postoperative complications. This may provide a simple and feasible new method for the prevention of OCR during surgery and offer direct clinical evidence for anesthesiologists.
