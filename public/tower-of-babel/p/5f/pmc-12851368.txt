Heatwave resilience in Buenos Aires: Translational research and co-created strategies for public policy benefiting older adults across diverse urban settings
Authors: Ana Laura Torlaschi, Gabriela Carneglia, Natalia Pereiro
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Heatwaves negatively impact the well-being of older adults and their caregivers.•Community solidarity, both among neighbors and within families, is crucial for coping with critical situations such as power outages.•Every intervention should be customized to address diverse heterogeneities.Keywords: Heat waves, Older adults, Climate change, Public health service

Introduction

Over the past six decades, the City of Buenos Aires (CABA) has experienced an increase in the frequency, duration, and intensity of extreme climatic events, including heatwaves, floods, and storms, likely influenced by climate change. Heatwave frequency has risen sharply since the 1990s, more than doubling in the period between 2010 and 2018 compared to the 1990s [1]. The National Weather Service (SMN), defines that a heatwave in Buenos Aires is recorded when the minimum temperature exceeds 22 °C (71.6 °F) and the maximum temperature exceeds 32.3 °C (90.14 °F) for at least three consecutive days [2]. In March 2023, a new record was set for both minimum and maximum temperatures for the month of March reaching 32.3 °C (90.14 °F) and 38.6 °C (101.48 °F), respectively since official records began in 1906, and this record occurred during a heatwave that lasted 15 consecutive days [3].

Heatwaves have a significant impact on public health, increasing morbidity and mortality, particularly from heatstroke, hyperthermia, and exacerbated cardiovascular and respiratory illnesses [4]. Older adults (OA) are one of the most affected population groups due to a combination of physiological factors and the presence of chronic diseases [5,6]. The 2023 Latin America report of the Lancet Countdown highlighted the impact of climate change on the health of the population in this region and described that heat-related mortality among individuals aged 65 and older in Latin America has reached alarming levels [7]. Chesini et al. analyzed the health impact of the 2013 heatwave in the CABA and observed an increase in mortality rates across the entire population, with a more pronounced effect on OA [8].

The City of Buenos Aires is characterized by an aging demographic, with individuals aged 60 years and over constituting one-fourth of its resident population [9]. This cohort exhibits socioeconomic heterogeneity and varying degrees of vulnerability across different urban areas, consequently leading to differential health outcomes [[10], [11], [12]]. Furthermore, the city is marked by heterogeneity in its urban infrastructure. The southern sector presents the most unfavorable indicators, as this area encompasses the majority of the city's vulnerable neighborhoods, informal settlements, and temporary housing (including hotels and tenements) [[13], [14], [15]]. Within these neighborhoods, it is not uncommon to observe individuals of middle age exhibiting physical manifestations often associated with aging. However, these individuals are not classified as older adults based on socially defined chronological age criteria (Social clock). From a life-course perspective, the aging process can be understood within its specific context, shaped by prevailing socio-historical and cultural determinants [5,16,17].

The CABA has the Climate Action Plan 2050 (PAC 2050) [1] which establishes actions, tools and strategies for adaptation and mitigation to climate change, aiming to reduce human and natural system vulnerabilities. One of its key components is the Adaptation Program for Extreme Climatic Events. The city also has the Emergency Master Plan [18] whose function is to respond to community threats, both natural and human-induced, that jeopardize safety, lives, and the environment. The CABA has also, since 2023, been involved in global initiatives for the establishment of climate shelters in urban areas. These locations are designed to provide the population with a temperature-controlled and comfortable environment during extreme temperature events [19]. The first climate shelter in Buenos Aires was formalized at the Botanical Garden, where temperatures are recorded to be almost 5 °C lower than in surrounding areas. Currently the initiative has 62 geolocated shelters and water fountains [20].

In terms of healthcare provision, the older adult population of the city exhibits a significant dependence on the Comprehensive Medical Care Plan (PAMI) offered by the National Institute of Social Services for Retirees and Pensioners for their healthcare needs [21]. The entire public health network under the jurisdiction of the city's Ministry of Health serves as a provider of services for PAMI. The public health network is structured across various levels of care, providing services that include health promotion, prevention, treatment, and surveillance to guarantee healthcare access for all city residents [22]. Furthermore, in 2024, the city's Ministry of Health joined the pilot project for the “Implementation of surveillance of health effects from extreme temperatures”, spearheaded by the National Ministry of Health. This project was implemented in three hospitals through the establishment of sentinel units to monitor health effects associated with extreme temperatures [23].

This translational study aimed to generate actionable information for management, consequently enhancing the adaptive and response capacity of older adults in the CABA to climate change. Our ability to design intersectoral response strategies for older adults facing these events stemmed from understanding their coping mechanisms, needs, and vulnerabilities during heatwaves (considering their heterogeneous aging processes), along with identifying relevant public policies and key response-capable actors.

Materials and methods

Study setting

The research team, affiliated with the public administration, employed a transdisciplinary framework [24] that integrated non-academic participants to generate knowledge pertinent to public policy formulation. Two older adults were invited to join the team, alongside various governmental actors, under a common epistemological framework. Data collection was conducted from January to June 2024. The results chain model was utilized to monitor the project's progress and facilitate necessary course corrections (Fig. 1).

Result Chain Model. This tool was used to monitor the project's progress and make necessary course corrections, (Author's own elaboration).

Study design

This study employed translational research conducted through focus groups, in-person survey, questionnaires and strategic management working groups to design tailored strategies.

Ethical approval

Ethical approval was obtained from the Research Ethics Committee of the General Acute Care Hospital Jose Maria Ramos Mejia in Buenos Aires (Approval no: 12,293). Confidentiality and pseudonymity were maintained throughout data collection and analysis.

Project stages

The research was divided into two stages.

Stage 1: problem identification and data collection

The design of the qualitative- quantitative methodology was developed with the technical guidance of two specialists.

Survey

A survey was designed [25] based on pre-validated questionnaires [26] and vulnerability reports [5,27] produced by various governmental and non-governmental organizations.

The survey's validation was conducted with subject matter experts and a specialist in quantitative research. A pilot test was subsequently carried out across diverse sociocultural groups and urban sectors to account for existing heterogeneity.

The 2022 Census indicates 715,910 older adults reside in Buenos Aires (CABA), defined as individuals aged ≥65 years, or ≥60 in vulnerable neighborhoods (VN) to account for diverse aging processes. A minimally proportional and representative sample was developed, with minimum quotas established for region, gender, and age to ensure statistical representation.

Two sectors represented the population: Vulnerable Neighborhoods (VN), encompassing informal settlements and housing complexes, and the Formal City (FC), comprising all other city areas.

Geographic universe: City of Buenos Aires

Selected universe: individuals aged 60 years and over residing in VN and individuals aged 65 years and over residing in FC.

Sample Size: 634 people

In-person surveys were conducted near randomized sampling points of the geographic universe, segmented by age group and sex to reflect the diversity of older adults across the city.

To ensure greater sample representativeness, preliminary data underwent cleaning and weighting using the 2022 Census as a parameter. This process involved aligning the sample with the sociodemographic distribution of the population, specifically considering commune, age range, and gender. This weighting factor corrected potential selection biases and accurately reflected the survey's target population.

The statistical analysis of the results was conducted using R software version 4.3.3. Absolute frequencies and weighted percentages were calculated for each of the variables, applying a weighting factor designed to adjust the sample and ensure representativeness of the results.

For group comparisons (Vulnerable Neighborhoods and Formal City), statistical tests appropriate to the nature of the data were applied. A statistical significance threshold p < 0.05 was established to determine significant differences between groups. Qualitative variables were analyzed using chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests, as appropriate, to evaluate whether significant differences existed between the two groups.

For conditional survey questions (those answered only by a subgroup that responded affirmatively to a preceding question), comparisons between neighborhoods were also conducted to observe differences in these specific subgroups.

Focus groups

Between March and April 2024, four focus groups were held using a customized guideline based on the WHO/PAHO Vancouver protocol model, plus specific questions related to the research topic. Participants for the focus groups were selected using the snowball sampling technique through various organizations that work with older adults in the city.

Three of the four focus groups included older adults. Two of these groups consisted of participants from the formal city, segmented by age (65–74 and 75+), while another group was specifically for residents of vulnerable neighborhoods, aged 60 and over.

The fourth group consisted of caregivers of older adults, recognizing their critical role in decision-making during their professional activities.

Each focus group with older adults consisted of 6 to 12 participants. The group of caregivers included 18 participants.

Policy and actor mapping

To determine the state of the art regarding local and national public policies, an official information search was conducted. This included reviewing resolutions, decrees, and national, provincial and CABA-specific guidelines on climate change and health, accessed via the internet.

The identification of governmental agencies with the capacity to respond to heatwaves was conducted based on a request form the Ministry of Health and Executive Office of the CABA. Additionally, community health promoters (General Directorate of Community Health [DGSCOM] of the Ministry of Health of CABA) with territorial outreach in the city identified non-governmental organizations operating in vulnerable neighborhoods that could provide assistance during critical moments (social organizations, community kitchens, churches and others)

Questionnaire

A questionnaire with open-ended questions was designed to be completed by governmental actors with the capacity to respond to heatwaves, aiming to gather information on actions related to extreme events and potential areas for improvement. It was self-administered and distributed through digital channels.

Participants were selected using the snowball sampling technique. Consultations were conducted with authorities from the Ministry of Health and the Executive Office of the CABA. A total of five questionnaires were distributed and four responses were received and subsequently analyzed.

Mapping of climate shelters in vulnerable neighborhoods

A field survey of potential spaces that could serve as climate shelters was conducted through health promoters working in the Health and Community Action Centers (CeSACs) of vulnerable neighborhoods. These spaces were geolocated and marked on a map using ArcGIS and QGIS software.

Geographical information

Maps were developed using population data, urban settlement types, green spaces, surface and ambient temperatures, and power outages. Key data sources included Landsat 8 imagery (from the U.S. Geological Survey, this series of U.S. satellites is developed for high-resolution Earth surface observation), the National Meteorological Service, the National Electricity Regulatory Entity (ENRE), the National Institute of Statistics and Census (2022 Redatam Census), the Secretary of Urban Development, the Ministry of Environment and Public Space, and the Environmental Protection Agency. Green space information integrated various pre-2023 sources, such as photogrammetry, cadastral data from Buenos Aires Data, and technical documentation of projects inaugurated between 2019 and 2023. These maps were designed and developed in collaboration with other technical government teams, with data processed using ArcGIS and QGIS software.

Stage 2: participatory planning of solutions. management forums for strategy construction

Through a transdisciplinary approach, and in collaboration with older adults and identified stakeholders, an integrated analysis of qualitative and quantitative components was conducted. This was achieved via a problem prioritization methodology across three meetings of the intersectoral management board, driven by this research, enabling the creation of strategies for the entire city and those specific to each urban cluster type.

Surveys and focus groups

Knowledge and experiences regarding heatwaves and their effects

Most FG participants and over 95 % of survey respondents (from both VN and FC) were aware of extreme heat symptoms. However, VN residents reported significantly more negative heat-related effects and a greater need for assistance with water or food (p < 0.001).

Remarkably, 23 % of VN respondents and 13 % of FC respondents had to leave their homes on extreme heat days, primarily for work or medical appointments. While healthcare service proximity in Buenos Aires was generally well-regarded, public health system reliance differed significantly: 79.8 % in VN versus 47.4 % in FC (p < 0.001). During heatwaves, 26.6 % of unwell individuals in VN and 12.3 % in FC sought health service consultation (p = 0.012).

Social networks and communication channels

Family support and neighbor networks were crucial for coping with extreme heat, as highlighted by all focus groups, underscoring strong community solidarity. While 21 % of respondents consistently required assistance, the source of this support varied significantly: cohabiting family members were the primary providers in VN (p = 0.0041), whereas domestic workers or contracted caregivers provided assistance in FC (p < 0.001). A small percentage (5.7 %) reported receiving no assistance (p = 0.50).

Social participation was lower in VN than in FC (30.7 % vs 44.1 %, p = 0.0054). FC residents most frequently engaged in clubs, recreational/educational institutions, and retiree centers, with clubs showing a significant difference (p < 0.001) compared to VN. Conversely, VN participants favored retiree centers, recreational/educational institutions, and temples/churches, with the latter significantly differing from FC (p = 0.01).

Television was the main source for preventive information across the study population. Radio use was significantly higher in FC (p = 0.0001), while health personnel were a more significant information source in VN (p = 0.0002). Over half of survey respondents and most FG participants desired more information, with 94 % preferring Facebook (p = 0.99).

Housing and neighborhood characteristics

Air conditioning ownership was significantly lower in VN than in FC (34 % vs 67 %, p < 0.001). Fan ownership was comparable (82.2 %, p = 0.32), but cross-ventilation was statistically more prevalent in FC (p < 0.001).

Around 40 % in both sectors bought bottled water due to chlorine taste or distrust in tap water (p = 0.0758); in VN, this was linked to precarious home water connections. Heatwaves brought more water scarcity (p < 0.001) and power outages (p = 0.0005) to VN, leading VN residents to believe the State should provide water (p = 0.0002). Caregiver focus groups noted increased workload during outages due to inadequate equipment.

Over 80 % of respondents (p = 0.079) found local institutions or spaces useful/very useful as climate shelters. Both FG and surveys highlighted a general lack of green spaces in CABA.

Coping measures during extreme temperature days

While over 89 % of respondents reported hydrating during extreme temperatures, only 50 % (p = 0.28) consumed the recommended daily water intake (over 2 liters). Furthermore, <10 % prioritized avoiding alcoholic, high-sugar, or caffeinated beverages, or heavy meals. VN residents more frequently used traditional protective methods like wetting/bathing (p = 0.014) or using hats/umbrellas (p < 0.001) than those in FC. However, VN individuals also experienced more sun exposure during unrecommended hours (p = 0.0017). Notably, VN residents faced more difficulties implementing preventive measures compared to FC (10.2 % vs 5.6 %, p = 0.028).

Discussion

Transdisciplinary research teams, integrated within public administration and involving the target population, can enhance the development of solutions with both immediate and medium-term impacts [28]. Translational mixed-methods research effectively explores complex, context-specific dynamics and nuanced human experiences, revealing intricate relationships within multifaceted questions [29]. Despite these strengths, this study had limitations, including potential interviewer bias duringin-person surveys, low online questionnaire response rates, and an underrepresentation of less publicly active older adults.

Our findings informed the creation of city-wide strategies and tailored interventions for each urban cluster type. City-wide health system preparedness strategies for heatwaves include: strengthening alerts for health system preparedness and priority care for heatstroke (especially for the public system), supporting telemedicine for routine check-ups during extreme heat, and strengthening information systems for recording heatwave-related health events through Sentinel Units. Some of these strategies align with international protocols and official government websites [[30], [31]].

Building community and neighborhood networks was a key strategy, reinforcing local communication on optimal individual, family, and community heatwave coping measures. For VN, disseminating prevention measures through territorial workers from various agencies (e.g., Ministry of Health, Housing Institute) was proposed, consistent with PAHO experiences [32]. Based on VN household composition, implementing territorial training for family members in prevention also aligned with successful published experiences [33]. Conversely, in FC, contacting building managers and domestic workers/senior caregivers to disseminate recommendations and actively monitor older adults (OA) living alone was suggested. Empowering trained OA as environmental promoters to disseminate best practices was also defined for the entire city, mirroring initiatives in other countries [34].

A notable alignment was observed between OA's perceived and implemented preventive measures. Hydration, avoiding sun exposure during specific hours, and staying in cool, well-ventilated places were most cited, consistent with previous city-wide campaigns [35]. However, many measures were inadequately performed, similar to findings in other countries [36]. Strategies to optimize heatwave prevention focus on reinforcing common recommendations through simple, accessible measures that have fallen into disuse in FC, such as promoting traditional cooling (e.g., bathing/wetting with cool water and using hats/umbrellas). For the entire city, proposals included: disseminating the importance of using light clothing and sunscreen; encouraging reduced consumption of alcoholic, high-sugar, or caffeinated beverages and heavy meals; managing hydration points with designed maps and distributing reusable bottles with refill indicators; promoting the existence and utility of Climate Shelters; and encouraging the use of the city's various green spaces, as practiced globally [30,37]. Another strategy involves designing mass campaigns for TV, radio, and community media, and disseminating information via Facebook during extreme temperature days.

Regarding infrastructure and housing, findings underscore water access issues in VN, emphasizing the importance of increasing hydration by reinforcing the frequency and quantity of safe water tanker trucks, especially for those experiencing power outages. Adding climate shelters and promoting green spaces, while addressing security needs in VN, are adaptation measures studied worldwide [38]. Despite FC having verified quality tap water [39], bottled water consumption is frequent. Promoting tap water use and reducing bottled water consumption would decrease the environmental impact of this industry on climate change [40].

Analysis of local and national public policies aided understanding of the political-administrative context, barriers, and facilitators for strategy implementation, and identified how findings could translate into viable policy recommendations.

The produced maps, overlaying dimensions like higher OA density, more power outages, higher temperatures, and VN locations, enable pinpointing priority intervention areas. Observed temperature differences between heatwaves and summer periods highlight the importance of preventive activities during extreme temperature days. A limitation of the maps is their inability to reflect the severity of power outages in VN; despite their high frequency (per surveys and focus groups), informal electrical connections prevent accurate official recording and georeferencing of service disruptions. Geo-referencing potential climate shelters in VN facilitated coordination for their inclusion in the city's Network [25].

Our findings align with Yuni's observations (17), confirming that diverse aging processes lead to varied impacts from extreme climatic events, such as heatwaves, depending on the city sector and living conditions.

Conclusions

This translational research, conducted within the public administration, successfully elucidated the heterogeneous needs and vulnerabilities of older adults in Buenos Aires when responding to heatwaves. Its integrated framework, which robustly incorporated non-academic participants such as older adults themselves and identified the fragmented response capacities existing across various public management sectors, generated knowledge to influence the public policy formulation process.

The diverse ways of aging further revealed distinct adaptive strategies across different segments of older adults. These insights allowed for the design of tailored strategies aimed at boosting older adults' response capacities during extreme temperatures, demonstrating the imperative of context-specific solutions. These customized interventions specifically account for the varying urban contexts and unique challenges prevalent in both vulnerable and formal urban areas of the city.

This study provides decision-makers with evidence-based strategies for developing response plans that genuinely enhance the resilience of older adults to heatwaves across the city's diverse sectors. The active sharing of this knowledge within governmental bodies is already underway and is expected to enable its seamless integration into existing and new policy programs. This direct engagement fosters a more adaptive governance framework, facilitating the establishment of clear priority intervention lines and targeted geographical sectors where interventions are most critically needed.

Looking forward, the methodology employed in this study, particularly the transdisciplinary approach and integrated knowledge co-creation, offers a valuable model for future climate adaptation research within urban public administrations. Continued monitoring and evaluation of the implemented strategies will be essential to assess their long-term effectiveness and to adapt them to evolving climatic conditions and demographic shifts. Furthermore, exploring the generalizability of these findings to other Latin American mega-cities with similar socioeconomic and climatic pressures could foster regional best practices in climate resilience for aging populations.
