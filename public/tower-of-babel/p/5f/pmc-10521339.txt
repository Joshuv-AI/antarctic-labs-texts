Selective adhesion inhibition and hyaluronan envelope reduction of dermal tumor cells by cold plasma-activated medium
Authors: Anna-Christin Golz, Claudia Bergemann, Finja Hildebrandt, Steffen Emmert, Barbara Nebe, Henrike Rebl
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe sensitivity to cold plasma is specific to tumor cells while leaving normal tissue cells unaffected. This is the desired challenge in cancer therapy. Therefore, the focus of this work was a comparative study concerning the plasma sensitivity of dermal tumor cells (A-431) versus non-tumorigenic dermal cells (HaCaT) regarding their adhesion capacity. We found a selective inhibiting effect of plasma-activated medium on the adhesion of tumor cells while hardly affecting normal cells. We attributed this to a lower basal gene expression for the adhesion-relevant components CD44, hyaluronan synthase 2 (HAS2), HAS3, and the hyaluronidases in A431. Noteworthy, after plasma exposure, we revealed a significantly higher expression and synthesis of the hyaluronan envelope, the HAS3 gene, and the transmembrane adhesion receptors in non-tumorigenic HaCaTs.

Introduction

Cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma is the second most frequent nonmelanoma skin cancer and is responsible for 75% of all deaths caused by cutaneous cancer (except melanoma). Due to the population demographic shift and the administration of skin cancer screening, the incidence of cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma is steadily increasing [1,2]. Surgical removal is the current standard therapy for cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma. Patients with high-risk tumors subsequently receive treatment with chemotherapy or radiotherapy. Albeit, surgical removal, chemo- and radiotherapy of the affected tissue result in lesions of varying size and depth that may be difficult to cure [3,4]. For this reason, the need to find new therapeutic approaches that can overcome these limitations is of clinical interest; i.a., the focus is on cold atmospheric pressure plasma (referred to as plasma).

Plasma medicine

Plasma medicine is a promising field of interdisciplinarity between biology, chemistry, physics, and medicine [5–7]. Physical plasma is generated when energy is supplied to a neutral gas by an applied voltage or heat, resulting in ionization [8]. In addition to ions, electrons, radicals, and ultraviolet photons, physical plasma also contains uncharged atoms and molecules [9]. Cold atmospheric pressure plasma, has gained interest in clinical applications, such as wound healing, skin barrier disorders, treatment of atopic eczema, disinfection, itching, pain, and scars [10–12]. The primary aim of anti-tumor therapy is to selectively kill tumor tissue with minimal or no effect on healthy tissue. Treating four different pancreatic cancer cell lines directly with plasma (kINPen) resulted in a significant reduction in their metabolic activity [13]. In addition to direct plasma treatment of cells, plasma-treated physiological liquids [14], buffers, such as PBS [15] or cell culture medium [16–20], can induce effects similar to those discovered by direct plasma treatment of cells [12]. Tanaka et al. demonstrated a significantly reduced viability of human glioblastoma cells U251SP after incubation with plasma-treated Ringer’s lactate solution [21], and various research groups confirmed the antitumorigenic effect of plasma-treated Ringer’s solution in vitro and ex vivo on epithelial ovarian cancer, osteosarcoma, and pancreatic cancer [22–24]. Also, the plasma-activated cell culture medium was antitumorigenic [25–28] and, e.g., significantly inhibited the viability of dermal tumor cells (A-431, A-375) [9] and opened the tight junctions in mHepR1 epithelial cells [18].

During the treatment of liquids with plasma, reactive oxygen and nitrogen species, such as hydrogen peroxide, nitrates, and nitrites (relatively long-lived species), are generated in the liquid phase by plasma-liquid interactions [29–31]. Reactive species dissolved in the liquid must permeate through cell membranes to induce an intracellular effect in cancer cells [32].

There are several studies that have investigated possible mechanisms behind plasma-treated medium-mediated cell effects [33,34]. Neither the formation of ozone [33], lipid oxidation [17], nor changes in pH, temperature, or hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) concentration [18,34] were responsible for the evoked cellular effects. Bergemann et al. [7] showed that both the pericellular hyaluronan coat and the adhesion of HaCaT cells were perturbed by plasma-activated medium but not as strong as by H2O2.

Adhesion receptors

Adhesion of cells to surrounding cells and/or to the ambient extracellular matrix is the basis for developing and maintaining a functioning cellular network in poly-cellular organisms [35–38]. Integrins provide a link between the extracellular matrix molecules and the intracellular actin cytoskeleton via adaptor proteins [39]. Integrins regulate numerous physiological processes, such as adhesion, proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, homeostasis and migration, motility, gene expression, and polarity [39–41]. The α2β1, α3β1, α5β1, α6β4, as well as αV integrins combined with β3, β5, and β6 subunits, are the major integrins expressed in the skin [42,43].

CD44 is a transmembrane glycoprotein involved in regulating cell-cell contacts and cell-matrix interaction [44–46]. CD44 binds specifically to the extracellular ligand hyaluronan (HA) [44,45].

Syndecans (Syn) are transmembrane proteoglycans and interact with ligands like growth factors, proteinases, chemokines, and matrix proteins such as collagens I, III, V, fibronectin, thrombospondin, and tenascin to provide structural support for cell adhesion [46–50]. Syn1 and Syn4 are abundantly expressed in the human epidermis [51–53]. Syn1 predominantly locates in the stratum granulosum and spinosum. In basal keratinocytes, Syn1 is only slightly expressed, while it is not expressed at all in the stratum corneum of healthy skin [53–56]. Syn1 is highly frequent at cell-cell contacts [53,57] and is essential for intracellular signaling.

Calcium ion channels

There are numerous studies postulating that the observed anti-cancer effect due to plasma is related to reactive oxygen (ROS) and nitrogen species (RNS) [15,58,59]. Furthermore, calcium (Ca2+) signaling is crucial for the production of ROS. Conversely, ROS might regulate cellular Ca2+ signaling [60,61]. Intracellular Ca2+ ions control several critical cellular processes, like proliferation, motility, cell cycle, transcription, exocytosis, hormone release, cell motility, and cell death [62–64], and disturbances in intracellular Ca2+ regulation are frequently associated with cancer. Both cancer formation and progression can be related to almost all of the different calcium channels [63–65]. T-type Ca2+ channel expression can be associated with proliferation in many cancers, therefore, numerous research groups have hypothesized that T-type Ca2+ channels have an important role in the growth and progression of cancer [64,66–69]. Su et al. postulated that the mRNA expression of the T-type Ca2+ channel isoform hCav3.2/α1 H was significantly higher in cancers compared to the surrounding normal tissue [70]. Studies also revealed the broad expression of voltage-sensitive L-type Ca2+ channels in cancer cells and clinical samples by gene expression analysis, although the general assumption was that L-type Ca2+ channel expression and activity is restricted to excitable cells, such as muscle and neuronal cells [71,72].

Hyaluronan

The glycosaminoglycan hyaluronan (HA) is a major component of the extracellular matrix, e.g., in connective tissues. It is a long-chain, linear polysaccharide that plays an important role in cell attachment, cell proliferation, and migration. In cell adhesion, the HA pericellular coat plays an important role, especially in the initial adhesion processes [73,74]. In cell development and maturation, HA has a divergent role [75]. Thus, HA expression is high in cells with low differentiation or in tissues with high metabolic turnover, e.g., in wound healing. However, in various tumor tissues, cells have been found to overexpress HA and show high proliferation but low differentiation rates. Experimentally increased HA production was shown to stimulate tumor growth and metastasis in xenograft models, induce drug resistance, and elevate tumor invasiveness [76–78]. Novel tumor therapy strategies, therefore, include approaches for inhibiting HA production and promoting HA degradation.

In previous studies, we demonstrated that the pericellular hyaluronic acid coat is affected by plasma-activated medium in non-tumorigenic HaCaT cells after only 3 hours [7].

Aquaporins

Direct cold plasma and also plasma-activated medium both act selectively on tumor cells [17,79,80]. In search of an explanation for this selective effect, Yan et al. suggested a possible mechanism via the aquaporins, which are differentially expressed in tumor and normal cells [81]. Aquaporins (AQP) are proteins that form channels in the cell membrane to facilitate the passage of water and some other molecules with very low molecule mass. AQPs are the confirmed membrane channels of H2O2, which was identified as an important ROS generated by plasma in liquid media. From the fact that aquaporins are more highly expressed in tumor cells than in normal cells, they derived the model of a selective effect of plasma on tumor cells via an aquaporin-mediated mechanism. This allows more H2O2 to enter the tumor cells, which already have an overloaded antioxidant system that is no longer able to protect the cells adequately [81]. They demonstrated that the anti-glioblastoma capacity of plasma-activated medium could be inhibited by silencing the expression of AQP8 in glioblastoma cells (U87MG) or by using silver atoms as aquaporin blockers. Yusupov et al. confirmed the model by molecular dynamics simulations for the permeation of H2O2 for AQP1 [82]. Cells of the skin can express six different AQPs (AQP1, 3, 5, 7, 9, and 10) [83]. However, only altered expression of AQP3 seems to be associated with tumor development in the skin. While AQP3 is downregulated in basal cell carcinomas, it is also partially upregulated in squamous cell carcinoma [83]. Therefore, it is interesting to determine the basal expression of AQP3 in non-tumorigenic HaCaT and tumorigenic A-431 cells.

The focus of this work was to find a selective influence of cold atmospheric pressure plasma-activated medium on the adhesion capacity of dermal tumor (A-431) versus non-tumorigenic cells (HaCaT). The methodological approaches impedance, flow cytometry, and real-time RT-PCR were used to detect the time-dependent cell adhesion, the adhesion receptors, the calcium ion channel expression, the synthesis of the glycosaminoglycan hyaluronan as well as the gene expression of hyaluronan synthases.

Materials and methods

Argon plasma source

The cold atmospheric pressure plasma jet kINPen®09 (Neoplas Tools GmbH, Greifswald, Germany) [84] consists of a quartz capillary (inner diameter of 1.6 mm). In the middle of this quartz capillary, there is a needle electrode (diameter of 1 mm) located to which a high frequency (HF) voltage of 1.1 MHz/2–6 kV is applied. The needle electrode is embedded in a hand-held unit which is connected to a DC power supply (8 W at 220 V, 50–60 Hz). For the experiments, the settings 60.1 V and 54 mA was used, and argon gas (purity 99.996%) with a gas flow of 1.9 slm. Through the excitation of argon by a gas discharge ignition at the tip of the high-voltage needle, low-temperature plasma is generated: The generated plasma jet outside the nozzle has a length of 12–14 mm and is approximately 1 mm wide [34]. The temperature at the visible tip of the plasma jet does not exceed 50°C [7,34].

Argon plasma activation of cell culture media

Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; high glucose, GlutaMAX, 1 mM pyruvate, 10569; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) without fetal calf serum (w/o FCS) was treated with argon plasma for 30, 60, or 120 s (plasma-activated medium – PAM; hereinafter referred to as PAM30, PAM60, or PAM120). 100 µl DMEM w/o FCS was pipetted in each well of a 96-well plate (Greiner Bio-One). For the plasma treatment, the kINPen®09 was positioned vertically, and the quartz capillary was located at the top edge of the well using an automated XY-positioning table (OWIS, Staufen, Germany). The argon plasma had immediate contact with the cell culture media [9,34] because the distance between the tip of the quartz capillary and the 96-well plate was not higher than 1 mm. After plasma treatment of the medium, the resulting PAM was pooled in a 2 ml tube (Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany). The following controls were used: argon gas treated medium for 60 s (AAM) without igniting the plasma, as well as untreated DMEM w/o FCS (DMEM). PAM, AAM, and DMEM were stored for 24 h at 37°C and 5% CO2 to be sure that the observed cell effects by PAM were neither caused due to remaining hydrogen peroxide nor by changed oxygen concentrations [9,34]. These were shown to be balanced 24 h after plasma treatment [34].

Cell culture

The cutaneous squamous cell line A-431 and the non-tumorigenic human keratinocyte cell line HaCaT (Human adult low Calcium high-temperature keratinocytes) were used as representative model cell lines. A-431 is a human skin epidermoid carcinoma cell line [85], while HaCaT represents spontaneously transformed keratinocytes [86]. Both cell lines were obtained from CLS Cell Line Service (Eppelheim, Germany) and cultured in DMEM (Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium; high glucose, GlutaMAX, 1 mM pyruvate) supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum (FCS) (FCS Superior; Biochrom GmbH, Berlin, Germany) and 1% P/S solution (100 units/mL Penicillin, 100 µg Streptomycin/mL, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, Missouri, USA) in Cellstar® filter top cell culture flasks (25 cm2, Greiner Bio-One, Frickenhausen, Germany) at 37°C and 5% CO2. The cells were cultured until they reached an approximate confluency of 85%. Then, cells were detached from the culture flask using trypsin/ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) (0.25% trypsin, 0.38% EDTA, Invitrogen, Gibco, Paisley, UK) for 8–12 min (A-431 8 min; HaCaTs 12 min). The reaction was stopped by DMEM with 10% FCS and the cell number was measured in a 200 µl aliquot using the NucleoCounter NC-3000 (ChemoMetec, Steen Søndergaard, Denmark) cell count and viability assay. After a centrifugation step, cells were resuspended in 10% FCS-containing or serum-free DMEM, depending on the experiment, and the desired cell number was seeded.

Immunofluorescence staining of different cellular components

Actin cytoskeleton: Cells were seeded in 2ml DMEM containing 10% FCS on a 20×20mm coverslip (ThermoFisher Scientific) and placed in a 6-well plate (1.15×106 HaCaT cells, 5×105 A-431 cells). Cells were incubated for 24h at 37°C and 5% CO2. After 24h, the medium was removed, and 2ml PAM60 was added. DMEM w/o FCS was used as a control. Cells were then incubated for another 24h at 37°C and 5% CO2. After a total incubation time of 48h, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, Missouri, USA) for 10min at room temperature (RT) and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton-X 100 (Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany) for 10min at RT. Cells were incubated with phalloidin-TRITC (in PBS, HaCaT 33µg/ml, A-431 20µg/ml) (Sigma-Aldrich) in the dark for 40min at RT. Finally, the cells were embedded in a mounting medium containing DAPI for cell nuclei staining (Sigma-Aldrich) on a cover slip and observed by an inverted confocal laser scanning microscope (LSM 780, Carl Zeiss, Jena, Germany) using the ZEISS 63× oil immersion objective (C-Apochromat) and for image analysis the ZEN 2011 (black version) software (Carl Zeiss).

Aquaporins: Cells were seeded into 150µl DMEM with 10% FCS in a cell culture µ-Dish (35mm, ibidi GmbH) (3×105 HaCaT, 1×105 A-431) and cultivated at 37°C and 5% CO2. After 24h, cells were fixed with 100% ice-cold methanol (VWR, Darmstadt, Germany), permeabilized with 0.1% Triton-X 100 (Merck KGaA) in PBS for 10min at RT, and blocked with 2% FCS in PBS. Cells were incubated with rabbit anti-AQP3 antibody (1:100, 200µl, Invitrogen) in 0.001% TWEEN (VWR) in PBS for 30min at RT. Finally, cells were incubated with the secondary antibody goat-anti rabbit (Alexa Fluor 488, 1:100, 200µl, Invitrogen) for 30min at RT in the dark. The nucleus of the cells was stained with Hoechst 33,342 (10mg/mL, 1:1000 in PBS, Invitrogen), and cell images were taken with the LSM 780, 63× oil objective (see LSM details above).

Hyaluronan pericellular coat: Cells were seeded into 150 µl DMEM w/o FCS, PAM30, PAM60, or AAM in a cell culture µ-dish (35 mm, ibidi GmbH) (3×105 HaCaT and 1 × 105 A-431 cells) and cultivated at 37°C and 5% CO2. After 24 h, cells were fixed with 4% PFA (Sigma-Aldrich), stained with 2 mg biotinylated HA binding protein (Calbiochem, San Diego, CA, USA) for 120 min, followed by 0.5 mg streptavidin-FITC (Becton Dickinson, San Diego, CA, USA) for additional 30 min at RT in the dark. The nucleus was stained with Hoechst 33,342 (10 mg/mL, Invitrogen), and cell images were taken with the LSM 780, 63× oil objective.

Monitoring cell adhesion in real time

Cell adhesion: The living cell adhesion in a time frame of up to 48 h was investigated with the impedance-based xCELLigence RTCA S16 instrument (ACEA Biosciences, San Diego, USA). The measuring principle is based on gold electrodes in an impedance plate to which a voltage is applied. Resistance is caused by adhering, spreading, or proliferating cells. This resistance is recorded by the system as a signal and outputted as a so-called cell index (CI). This enables the monitoring of living cell adhesion in real-time. For the experiments, we used the E-plate VIEW 16 (ACEA Biosciences, San Diego, USA) [7]. The PAM, AAM, and DMEM, w/o FCS, were equilibrated to room temperature in order to enable a constant sinking of the cells in the plate. Then 100 µl of PAM, AAM, or DMEM was added to the particular well of the E-plate, and the background measurement was conducted. Afterward, the cells (9.6 × 104 HaCaT, 6 × 104 A-431) were resuspended in 10 µl DMEM w/o FCS and 90 µl of PAM, AAM, or DMEM was added to the cell suspension, and then this mixture was pipetted to the 100 µl in the E-plate. The total volume of each well was finally 200 µl. The plate was then inserted into the xCelligence station, and data were collected for 48 h at 37°C and 5% CO2. The data were analyzed using the RTCA Data Analysis Software 1.0 (ACEA Biosciences) (n = 4 independent experiments).

Live-dead staining: After the impedance-based 48 h experiment, the live-dead staining of adherent and non-adherent cells from the E-Plates was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Molecular Probes, Eugene, USA, ThermoFisher Scientific) with 2 µM calcein-acetoxymethyl ester (Calcein-AM), 4 µM ethidium homodimer-1 (ethD-1) and 10 µg/ml Hoechst (AppliChem GmbH, Darmstadt, Germany) in PBS. The non-adherent cells were collected from the supernatant in a 1.5 ml tube (Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany) by centrifugation at 1200 rpm and resuspension in 100 µl of the freshly prepared staining solution and added to the adherent cells into the E-plate. After incubation for 20 min at 37°C and 5% CO2, the cells were analyzed by confocal microscopy (LSM 780, 10× water objective) with the ZEN 2011 (black version) software (Carl Zeiss AG) for image analysis (n = 4 independent experiments).

Cell viability

The CellTiter 96®AQueous non‐radioactive cell proliferation assay (Promega, Madison, USA) was used to determine the cell viability. Cells (9.6×104 HaCaT and 6 × 104 A-431 cells/well) were seeded in triplicates directly into PAM30, PAM60, and PAM120, DMEM, and AAM in a 96‐well plate (Greiner Bio-One). The total volume per well was 200 µl. For measuring the background absorption, 200 µl of the respective PAM or control medium without cells was used. After an incubation of 48 h, 20 µl MTS-reagent was added. During the following incubation at 37°C and 5% CO2, the 96‐well plate was shaken carefully every 10 min until the color change (A-431 30 min, HaCaT 55 min). Then, 70 µl from each well was transferred into a new 96‐well plate. The absorption was measured with the Rosys-Anthos-Microplate-Reader 2010 (Anthos Mikrosysteme GmbH, Friesoythe). The values were normalized by subtraction of the blank and calibrated to the value of the cells in the DMEM control (n = 4–5 independent experiments).

Flow cytometry of cellular components expression

Adhesion receptors: To study the influence of PAM on the adhesion-receptor expression, the cells were resuspended in 35 µl DMEM (w/o FCS, 1% P/S) (per well: 57.6 × 104 HaCaT, 36 × 104 A-431) and were added to 665 µl PAM30 or control media in a 24-well plate (Greiner Bio-One GmbH) and cultivated for 24 h. Cells were washed twice with PBS, trypsinated, collected, and centrifuged for 5 min at 1200 rpm. Then, cells were incubated for 30 min at RT with primary monoclonal antibodies: CD44 (1:100, mouse), α2 (1:33, mouse), α3 (1:40, mouse), α5 (1:10, mouse), αV (1:160, goat), α6 (1:20, mouse, FITC), β1 (1:25, mouse), β3 (1:20, mouse, FITC) (all from ThermoFisher Scientific, diluted in PBS), and syndecan-1, syndecan-2, syndecan-4 (1:20, Santa Cruz, diluted in PBS). Cells were washed with PBS, centrifuged for 5 min (1800 rpm), and resuspended in 100 µl secondary antibody anti-mouse IgG (whole molecule F(ab’) fragment-FITC (1:15) for CD44, α2, α3, α5, β1 (Sigma-Aldrich). For αV the secondary antibody donkey anti-goat Alexa Fluor 488 (1:100, ThermoFisher Scientific) and for the syndecans goat-anti mouse Alexa Fluor 488 (1:200, ThermoFisher Scientific) was used. To eliminate the nonspecific background signal, we stained cells exclusively with the secondary antibody. (n = 3–6 independent experiments)

Flow cytometry: Fluorescence and light scatter characteristics of 10,000 cells were measured in the flow cytometer FACSCalibur (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) equipped with an argon ion laser (488 nm) with the software CellQuest Pro 4.0.1 (BD Biosciences) for acquisition and FlowJoTM Version 7.6.5 for Windows (FlowJo LLC, Tree Star, Inc., Ashland, OR, USA) for cell analyses.

Aquaporin 3: Cells were seeded into 150 µl DMEM with 10% FCS in a cell culture µ-Dish (35 mm, ibidi GmbH) (3×105 HaCaT, 1 × 105 A-431) and cultivated at 37°C and 5% CO2. After cultivation for 24 h, cells from the supernatant were collected by centrifugation (1200 rpm, 5 min) and cells from the surface of the dish were trypsinated. Cells from the supernatant and trypsinated cells were pooled, and AQP3 expression was quantified by flow cytometry. For this purpose, cells were fixed with 100% ice-cold methanol (VWR, Darmstadt, Germany), permeabilized with 0.1% Triton-X 100 (Merck KGaA) in PBS for 10 min at RT, and blocked with 2% FCS in PBS. Cells were stained as described for confocal laser microscopy. An extra DMEM sample was incubated with the secondary antibody goat-anti rabbit only and served as a blank control. (FACS see above, n = 4 independent experiments).

Hyaluronan (HA) pericellular coat: Cells were seeded into 150 µl DMEM w/o FCS, PAM30, PAM60, or AAM in a cell culture µ-dish (35 mm, ibidi GmbH) (3×105 HaCaT, 1 × 105 A-431, two dishes each) and cultivated at 37°C and 5% CO2. After cultivation for 3, 6, and 24 h, cells from the supernatant were collected by centrifugation (1200 rpm, 5 min), and cells from the surface of the dish were trypsinated. Cells from the supernatant and trypsinated cells were pooled, fixed with 4% PFA (Sigma-Aldrich), and stained with 2 mg biotinylated HA binding protein (Calbiochem, San Diego, CA, USA) for 120 min, followed by incubation with 0.5 mg streptavidin-FITC (Becton Dickinson, San Diego, CA, USA) for additional 30 min at RT in the dark. An extra DMEM sample was incubated with 0.5 mg streptavidin-FITC only and served as a blank control. (FACS see above, n = 3 independent experiments).

Voltage-gated calcium channels: HaCaT (3×105) and A431 cells (3.6×105) were resuspended in 35 µl DMEM (w/o FCS, 1% P/S) and added to 665 µl PAM30 or control DMEM (w/o FCS, 1% P/S) in a 24-well plate (Greiner Bio-One GmbH) and incubated for 24 h. Then, cells were fixed with 4% PFA (Sigma-Aldrich) for 10 min at RT, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton-X 100 (Merck KGaA) for 10 min at RT, washed with PBS, and incubated for 30 min with the primary antibodies rabbit anti-hCav1.2, -hCav1.3, -hCav3.1, -hCav3.2, and -hCav3.3 (all 1:100, diluted in PBS, Alomone Labs, Jerusalem, Israel). Cells were washed with PBS and incubated with the secondary antibody goat anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 488 (1:200, diluted in PBS, Life Technology, Carlsbad, USA) for 60 min in the dark. To eliminate the nonspecific background signal, we stained cells exclusively with the secondary antibody. (FACS see above, n = 3–4 independent experiments).

Gene expression analysis

HaCaT (2×105) and A-431 cells (1×105) were seeded into 6-well plates (Greiner Bio-One) in PAM30, PAM60, and control DMEM and cultured for 24 h. Total RNA was isolated using the NucleoSpin RNA kit (Macherey-Nagel, Dueren, Germany). First-strand cDNA was synthesized from at least 400 ng total RNA by reverse transcription with SuperScript II (Invitrogen) using 2.5 µM random hexamers (Invitrogen). Quantitative real-time PCR assays were performed and monitored using the ABI PRISM 7500 sequence detection system (Applied Biosystems, Darmstadt, Germany). The PCR reactions contained 8 µl diluted cDNA (5-fold) in a reaction volume of 20 µl, 1× TaqMan Universal PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems), and 1 µl assays-on-demand gene expression assay mix (Applied Biosystems) for the detection of hyaluronan synthase 2 (HAS2, Hs00193435_m1), hyaluronan synthase 3 (HAS3, Hs00193436_m1), hyaluronidase 1 (HYAL1, Hs00201046_m1), hyaluronidase 2 (HYAL2, Hs00186841_m1), hyaluronan receptor (CD44, Hs99999195_m1) and for the endogenous controls glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH, Hs99999905_m1), ATP synthase H+ transporting mitochondrial Fo complex subunit F6 (ATP5J, Hs01081389_g1) and ribosomal protein lateral stalk subunit P0 (RPLP0, Hs00420895_gH). Relative gene expression values were calculated based on the comparative ΔΔCT method, normalized to the geometric mean of GAPDH, ATP5J, and RPL0 as endogenous controls (housekeeping genes), and calibrated to HaCaT in DMEM control medium. In general, for gene expression analysis, PCR amplification was performed in triplicate and repeated in n = 2–3 independent experiments.

Statistics

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad PRISM Version 7.02 for Windows (GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, USA) with paired t-test (adhesion receptors, AQP3, HA, basal gene expression, under PAM: cell viability, HA coat, and HAS3 gene expression), Wilcoxon t-test (under PAM: adhesion via cell index, adhesion receptors, syndecans, and Ca2+ channels). To check the normal distribution, the Shapiro-Wilk normality test (GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, USA) was used. All data were presented as mean ± the standard error of the mean (sem). Results of p < .05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Dermal tumor A-431 cells differ in their expression patterns of adhesion receptors, aquaporin, and hyaluronan-associated genes versus non-tumorigenic HaCaT cells

The human dermal A-431 tumor cells and the non-tumorigenic HaCaT cells were characterized in their morphology (Figure 1) after 24 h cell growth. Both cell types grow insularly and in association with closely spaced cells. The organization of the actin cytoskeleton did not differ substantially (Figure 1a). The submembranous-formed actin is found in the cell-cell contacts, although this is more pronounced in A-431 cells. Furthermore, long diagonal actin filaments are expressed in both cell types. Concerning adhesion receptor expression, the integrins α2, α5, α6, and β1, the syndecans 1, 2, and 4 as well as the HA receptor CD44 were enhanced in HaCaT cells compared with A-431 tumor cells (Figure 1b). In A-431, only the integrin subunit α3 was strongly expressed. In both cell lines, we could not detect β3 integrins (Figure S1). In confocal images, it is obvious that the dermal A-431 tumor cells expressed significantly less aquaporin (AQP3) in the membrane compared with HaCaT cells, which was also quantified by FACS (Figure 1b). While AQP1 and AQP10 were not expressed in these two cell lines, we were able to demonstrate a trend toward higher expression of AQP5, AQP7 and AQP9 in HaCaT cells.Concerning hyaluronan (HA) we found nearly similar amounts in A-431 and HaCaT cells (Figures 1a,b).

We investigated the basal expression of genes that are relevant for the formation and maintenance of the HA pericellular coat, the HA synthases (HAS), and the hyaluronidases (HYAL). We selected marker genes expressed in normal skin cells and in skin tumors as follows: HAS2, HAS3, HYAL1, HYAL2, and the HA receptor CD44. The basal gene expression for all genes examined is significantly lower in A-431 cells than in HaCaT cells (Figure 1b). HAS2 is not even expressed at all in A-431 cells.

In summary, the A-431 dermal tumor cells differed from the non-tumorigenic HaCaT cells mainly in their expression patterns of the adhesion receptors (not α3, αv), the AQP3, the HA pericellular coat, and the genes involved in hyaluronan synthesis and were found to be downregulated.

Plasma-activated medium selectively hampered A-431 cell adhesion and viability

Plasma-activated medium influenced adhesion-related cellular components

Cell adhesion receptors

To clarify why the adhesion capacity in A-431 tumor cells is selectively perturbed already by PAM30 compared with non-tumorigenic HaCaTs (see Figure 2) the expression of different cell adhesion receptors was quantified by flow cytometry. Similarly to the adhesion experiments in real-time, cells were also seeded directly into PAM30 and incubated for 24 h. The expression of syndecans (Figure 4b) was not significantly altered, although a trend toward upregulation following plasma is apparent in A431 cells. Hyaluronan pericellular coat

We found that plasma-activated medium (PAM30) resulted in selective inhibition of the adhesion process of dermal tumor A-431 cells. But after PAM, the adhesion receptor level stayed unchanged in these cells. To find out further cellular mechanisms responsible for the hampered adhesion compared to HaCaTs, the hyaluronan coat under PAM was investigated. We knew from our own experiments that the hyaluronan coat was the first mediator for cell adhesion [87], and osteoblasts without the HA coat – due to cleavage by hyaluronidase – remained in a rounded shape without any spreading efforts. In an earlier publication [7] we have observed the significant influence of PAM60 on the HA coat of non-adherent HaCaT cells. Therefore, we quantified the cell’s hyaluronan coat by FACS after 3, 6, and 24 h of incubation in PAM vs. DMEM. HA of both cell lines was significantly affected by PAM60 up to 6 h incubation (Figure 5a). After 3 h, the HA coat of HaCaTs revealed only 56% of the value of cells in DMEM (not significant, p < .066), and the HA coat of A-431 cells was 78% of the control. However, whereas after 24 h, the initial HA level of HaCaT cells was reached again, we could not detect HA in treated A-431 cells. In general, we observed lower amounts of HA in A-431 cells. Hyaluronan synthases and hyaluronidases

We investigated the influence of PAM on the expression of genes that may be relevant for the formation and maintenance of the HA pericellular coat of the cells, the HA synthases (HAS), the hyaluronidases (HYAL), and the HA receptor CD44. We selected marker genes expressed in normal skin cells and in skin tumors as follows: HAS2, HAS3, HYAL1, HYAL2, and CD44. We incubated HaCaT and A-431 cells with PAM30, PAM60, and control DMEM for 24 h and determined the relative gene expression by real-time RT-PCR using the ΔΔCt method and calibration to the gene expression of HaCaT cells in DMEM control (Figure 5b).

In A-431 cells, the basal gene expression for CD44, HAS2, HAS3, and HYAL1, HYAL2 is generally lower than in HaCaT cells (see Figure 1). Since the HA coat in HaCaT and A-431 cells was affected by PAM incubation (see Figure 5a), it was interesting to investigate how the gene expression of the cells is changed by incubation with PAM. Interestingly, HaCaT cells respond to the incubation in PAM by a strong upregulation of HAS3. While HaCaT cell HAS3 gene expression was already stimulated by PAM30, A-431 did not react at all (Figure 5b). In both cell types, the gene expression of HAS2, HYAL1, HYAL2, and CD44 was not significantly affected (Figure S3).

Actin cytoskeleton

A typical feature of the actin cytoskeleton in epithelial cells is the adhesion girdle in the apical zone of the cells related to the cell-cell junctions. In both cell types, we observed the actin fibers in the lateral domain of cell-cell contact sides. Treatment of A-431 cells with PAM60 resulted in a markedly loosened cell association with a reduction in submembranous expressed actin (Figure 6). The effect of PAM60 on the organization of the actin cytoskeleton in HaCaT cells is marginal. Here, actin is well organized in cell-cell contact sides. Discussion

The use of cold atmospheric pressure plasma in cancer research as a new and innovative therapeutic approach has attracted interest in recent years due to the potential selectivity of plasma toward cancer cells compared to non-tumorigenic cells [12]. A constantly growing number of in vivo animal studies support the potential use of cold gas plasma in cancer treatment [88]. Furthermore, two clinical case series revealed an impressive efficacy of cold gas plasma (partial or total remission of > 70% of treated lesions) for the treatment of actinic keratosis, a precancerous lesion of the skin that may evolve into squamous cell carcinoma [89,90]. In our experiments, the cells were cultured in the same medium, and stimulation with plasma-activated medium (PAM) was performed under the same conditions to establish comparability between tumor cells and non-tumorigenic cells, as suggested by several authors [91,92].

Cellular adhesion, hyaluronan and adhesion receptors

Our experiments on the cell adhesion capacity and viability revealed a selective PAM effect on A-431 tumor cells, which were more strongly affected than HaCaT normal cells. Even though we formerly reported the anti-adhesive influence of PAM on HaCaT cells up to 1.5 h [7] we now show that this effect is much more prominent in tumorigenic cells.

Direct seeding of A-431 and HaCaT cells into AAM also resulted in a slightly significant reduction in cell adhesion. But argon gas flow alone did not influence adhesion as strongly as argon plasma did. This may be explained by the increase in osmolality in the treated medium. Argon gas flow induced evaporation processes during the treatment of the medium, which in turn led to an increase in osmotic strength in the medium [19].

Based on these results, we wanted to further investigate the underlying mechanisms. Since the ROS indurated by plasma first encounter the outer membrane of the cells, we examined the AQP protein level, hyaluronan pericellular coat, transmembrane adhesion receptors, and the voltage-gated calcium ion channels in more detail to illustrate the differences between the two cell types.

Present knowledge assumes that the reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (RONS) generated by plasma cause oxidative damage in the cell, which subsequently leads to cell death [91,93,94]. Dysbalance in the oxidative system appears more readily in tumor cells compared to their healthy counterparts. Therefore, it is suggested that the perturbation of the oxidative balance in cancer cells might be responsible for the selectivity toward plasma [91,95,96]. Since AQPs are responsible for the membrane transport of H2O2 we investigated their basal expression level in our model cells. Surprisingly, we found significantly lower expression of AQP3, 5, 7 and 9 in A-431 squamous carcinoma cells than in normal HaCaT cells. This was contrary to our expectations, since higher sensitivity toward ROS (and thus plasma treatment) was correlated with higher AQP protein levels [81]. However, also Bekeschus et al. could not find a correlation between AQP expression with plasma sensitivity in a broad cell study [97]. Qin et al. showed that decreased AQP3 expression correlates with increased keratinocyte proliferation and tumor formation [98]. Thus, we conclude that the selective effect of PAM on A-431 tumor cells is apparently not mediated by AQPs in these cells.

Concerning hyaluronan, we found that the HA pericellular coat did not differ substantially between these cell types, albeit the HA expression is slightly reduced in the tumorigenic A-431 cells. This is in contrast to the findings that overexpression of HA is a negative prognostic factor in tumor development in different tissues and that HA expression is high in cells with low differentiation or in tissues with high metabolic turnover, e.g., in tumors or in wound healing [75]. However, skin tumors represent an exception to this rule because, interestingly, in epidermal carcinomas, the lower HA content correlates with tumor progression [99]. Also, for oral squamous cell carcinoma, other groups found that reduced expression of HA is a marker for poor survival [100,101]. Especially studies of Pinhal et al. confirm our finding that A-431 have a lower HA basal level than HaCaT cells [100].

We hypothesize that the reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (RONS) generated during plasma treatment first encounter the pericellular hyaluronan coat of the cells before they can interact with various proteins within the cell membrane. Bergemann et al. demonstrated that the HA pericellular coat of HaCaT cells was impaired after exposure to PAM [7]. These findings were in line with the results of other groups showing that HA is degraded by free radicals [102,103]. Experiments on the HA pericellular coat and the interaction with CD44 after oxidative stress revealed a degradation of HA and a decreased recognition by its receptor CD44 in different cancer cells [104].

Here, we have extended our earlier study to skin tumor cells A-431 and compared the HA expression to that of normal skin HaCaT cells. While the HA coat is slightly more prominent in HaCaTs than in A-431 cells, it is significantly impaired by PAM60 after 6 h by 30% and 15%, respectively. Interestingly, in HaCaTs, this effect was compensated after 24 h, possibly by higher HA production, but not in A-431 cells.

To look at this in more detail, we performed mRNA expression studies for genes that are important for the HA cell coat formation, maintenance, and regulation. We were able to show that the mRNA expression for the investigated genes HAS2, HAS3, HYAL1, and HYAL2 in unstimulated cells was lower in the tumorigenic A-431 cells than in the HaCaT cells. This could then also explain the slightly lower basal HA pericellular coat expression found in A-431 cells, especially concerning the data for HAS2 and HAS3. Interestingly, we found a highly significant upregulation of the HAS3 expression in HaCaT cells after incubation in PAM60 for 24 h. Thus, in HaCaT cells a new synthesis of HA molecule seems to occur after PAM treatment, which may be controlled by the increased expression of HAS3 at the mRNA level. Another possibility to explain the re-synthesis of HA in cells after exposure to PAM is that HA synthases, which are localized at the inner cell membrane and extrude HA to the outside [105], remain functional despite exposure to RONS from PAM.

We further analyzed other membrane components like cell adhesion receptors, such as the transmembrane integrins, which not only have a crucial role in the regulation of cell motility in physiological processes but also in cancer spreading [106,107]. There are numerous studies showing that various cancers can be associated with changed integrin expression profiles [107,108]. Therefore, we decided to first investigate the basal expression of the integrin subunits α2, α3, α5, αV, α6, β1 as well as the hyaluronan receptor CD44 and the syndecans 1, 2, and 4 in HaCaT and A-431 cells. Former analyses showed that integrin expression is impaired in squamous cell carcinomas [44,109,110]. Altogether, our studies proved a lower proportion of integrin receptors in A-431 cells than in HaCaT, confirming this general assumption.

Nevertheless, after plasma treatment of A-431 cells, there was no decrease in integrin expression that could have explained the reduced adhesion capacity. Therefore, we argue that the involvement of integrins in the observed adhesion-reducing effect is not relevant.

In contrast, PAM30 tends to have a stimulatory effect on the expression of the hyaluronan receptor CD44 and the integrins in HaCaT. It might be that these cells upregulate the integrins in order to ensure further adhesion of the cells even under the influence of PAM. Haertel et al. determined the expression level of α2 and β1 subunits in HaCaT after 30 s direct plasma treatment. They demonstrated significantly increased β1 expression levels after plasma treatment, whereas α2 integrin subunit expression remained unchanged [111].

Although we could not detect altered expression of integrin receptors upon PAM treatment In A-431 cells, one could speculate that the functionality of the integrin receptors is perturbed by the RONS in PAM. Radicals might interfere with the adhesion signaling pathway by altering the structure of the extracellular integrin part and leading to oxidative damage within the integrin molecule, thus omitting ligand binding. In such a case, the thicker pericellular HA coat of HaCaT cells might prevent the reactive oxygen species from attacking the underlying integrin molecules, thus protecting them from degradation, while in carcinoma cells, they are more accessible to the peroxidation processes. Additionally, the regulation of integrin function in cells is not limited to the level of expression of integrins within the cell membrane. There are other regulatory levels involved in the regulatory cascade. Integrins underlie the continuous endocytosis and are thereafter transported back to the cell membrane. The kinetics of endocytosis and the transport processes to the cell membrane are often altered in cancer cells. This shifts the physiological ratio of receptors on the cell surface and in the endosomal pool [107,112,113]. Moreover, the physiological activity of integrins is subject to strict regulation. It is therefore conceivable that variations in integrin activity in cells lead to altered adhesion dynamics and increased integrin signaling, which in turn confers oncogenic properties on these cells [107,114].

Calcium channels

Ca2+ is a highly versatile intracellular signal that can regulate many different cellular functions. The Ca2+- influx is mediated by the tiny and transient (T)-type Ca2+ channels: Cav3.1, Cav3.2, Cav3.3, and the large and long-lasting (L)-type Ca2+ channels: Cav1.1, Cav1.2, Cav1.3, and Cav1.4 [115]. Ca2+ channels are associated with the actin cytoskeleton [116], interestingly depending on the stage of actin polymerization [117], and require integrin-actin interaction [118]. We have been able to identify the T-type Ca2+ channels Cav3.1, Cav3.2, Cav3.3, and L-type channels Cav1.2 and Cav1.3 on the protein level in both cell types studied. After the influence of PAM, we detected an increase in Ca2+ channel expression (except Cav1.2) in treated HaCaT cells. In cellular signaling, second messengers such as reactive oxygen species (ROS) and calcium ions (Ca2+) play an important role in activating downstream signaling. A fundamental aspect of the interaction between intracellular Ca2+ and ROS is their tightly interconnected regulation since Ca2+ signaling is crucial for the production of ROS, and vice versa, intracellular Ca2+ signaling can be controlled by RONS [61,119]. Cells produce a well-regulated amount of RONS due to normal metabolism, and it is thus tolerated by the cells. However, lethal effects such as DNA damage or lipid peroxidation can trigger apoptosis that may occur if a maximum tolerable threshold is exceeded. Since tumor cells per se are characterized by altered metabolism and associated impaired redox homeostasis [120], they are thought to be more susceptible to damage from additional oxidative stress attacks than cells of normal tissue.

All kinds of ROS, such as O2-, HO., and H2O2, can influence the intracellular Ca2+ balance. Mild oxidative stress transiently increases the Ca2+ concentration via release from intracellular stores. In contrast, severe oxidative stress causes an influx of Ca2+ from the extracellular space. Studies on melanoma cells showed a marked increase in intracellular Ca2+ after plasma [121]. This increase was due to Ca2+ release from intracellular stores (mitochondria and endoplasmic reticulum). Based on our study, we assume that the non-tumorigenic HaCaT cells respond to the RONS by regulating the calcium channels to restore cellular homeostasis, while the tumor cells cannot regulate the channel expression accordingly. This may further contribute to the understanding of the selective effect of plasma on tumor cells.

Conclusion

The sensitivity of tumor cells to cold plasma opens up new potentials in cancer therapy. Comparative studies on normal HaCaT cells and tumorigenic A-431 cells under exactly the same culture conditions revealed a strong reduction in the adhesion of tumor cells, while normal cells were almost unrestricted in their adhesion capacity. We suggest that differences in the adhesion-relevant membrane components CD44, HAS2, HAS3, and the hyaluronidases are responsible for these effects. In addition, we detected increased amount of hyaluronan, which may protect the membrane of normal cells from peroxidative attack by oxygen radicals. These ROS are closely related to calcium homeostasis and intracellular calcium-dependent signaling pathways. Our initial studies showed that normal cells respond to plasma stimulation with a slightly increased expression of calcium channels. These interactions need to be investigated in more detail in future experiments.
