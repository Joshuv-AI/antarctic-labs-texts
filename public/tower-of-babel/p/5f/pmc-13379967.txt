“What is data?” Learnings from paediatric patient and sibling involvement in co-producing a research communication tool for children and young people
Authors: Laura J Smith, Adam Cavill, Angus McKinney, Poppy Smith, Tsz-Yan Milly Lo, the Intensive-Share Group
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEmpowering children and young people (CYP) to actively participate in research development is essential to ensure impactful outcomes. Meaningful involvement helps researchers to pose relevant questions, design acceptable methodologies, and disseminate findings effectively. However, the inclusion of CYP in research, particularly in paediatric intensive care (PIC), is rarely reported. This is partly due to the challenging PIC environment, and most patient and public involvement and engagement activities (PPIE) focus only on the parents’ experience and perspective. The Intensive-Share group was established in Scotland in 2022 to facilitate PPIE activity in PIC research. The group includes family members with a range of lived experiences with the youngest member aged 7 years. They meet regularly to contribute to various aspects of research including research design, study materials and procedures, and public engagement. This article describes the co-production approach adopted in the ‘What is data?’ Project, which was co-created with researchers based on an idea from the Intensive-Share group. The project aimed to co-develop a short-animated video to explain healthcare data research to CYP in an engaging and accessible format. CYP meaningfully participated in all stages of the project and were integral to its success. Initial evaluations indicated the animation was well-received by families and they self-reported improved understanding of and willingness to participate in research. Co-production with CYP can be resource-intensive and challenging, but this project demonstrated it was feasible and incredibly valuable. Meaningful and authentic involvement challenged the research teams assumptions on inclusive language and the nature and level of involvement CYP preferred. Adopting a broader approach to PPIE in PIC research to include paediatric patients and siblings, perhaps on a national level, could facilitate similar initiatives in research communication and co-production. The open-source animated video is available as a resource to the wider research community to aid communication about paediatric healthcare data research.Keywords: Patient involvement, Siblings, Children and young people, Co-production, Communication, Healthcare data, Paediatric research.

Background

Empowering children and young people (CYP) to be meaningfully involved in research co-creation is key to delivering impactful research [1, 2]. CYP can work with researchers to ensure they are asking the right questions, carrying out their research in ways that are appropriate and acceptable, using accessible and inclusive language, and disseminating results in an impactful way. Despite a growing awareness of the importance and value of patient and public involvement and engagement (PPIE) in paediatric research [3], reporting of CYP involvement is suboptimal [4].

It could be argued that PPIE is even more crucial for paediatric intensive care (PIC) research. PIC is a very stressful and overwhelming environment and experience for patients and their families. They suddenly enter a time-pressured environment with a wealth of new technology and language, and heightened emotions and anxiety associated with their child’s critical illness. Approaching a family about research in the acute stage and asking them to comprehend information about research in such situations can prove difficult.

There are only a small number of published examples of PPIE in PIC, and the majority focus on the parental perspective [5, 6] rather than CYP. This is understandable given the challenges of involving CYP who may be seriously unwell and/or unable to communicate at the time of admission. PPIE activity with parents, carers and CYP post-PIC experience has been utilised to inform research design and communication [7–10].

Siblings have been shown to have a good understanding of the PIC environment and patient experience [11] but there is also a paucity of published PPIE activity including this group. Manning et al. [6] demonstrated the value of involving ‘survivors’ and siblings, using novel creative approaches to identify post-Paediatric Intensive Care Unit (PICU) care priorities.

Intensive-share PPIE group

Involving families in PPIE work in PIC is difficult partly due to the heterogeneous health conditions and experiences, lack of unifying charity or patient organisation, and ongoing care demands which the families may be experiencing.

Intensive-Share was set up as a PPIE group for PIC research in 2022 for families with lived experience of PIC in Scotland, United Kingdom. Multiple ‘recruitment’ channels were utilised to try and reach this under-represented population, including online fliers distributed on social media by third-sector organisations (e.g., condition-specific charities such as brain injury) and physical fliers in community groups and hospital non-clinical settings (e.g., hospital charity hubs and parental accommodation). The group has an intentionally broad eligibility including parents/carers, siblings and patients with a range of health conditions and PIC experiences.

7 families initially joined the group in 2022, including 13 adults and 17 CYP (‘patients’ and siblings). The lived experiences of the group are detailed in Table 1. Since the group inception, 1 family no longer participates and 2 families have joined.

Edinburgh currently leads an international PIC data-informatics research programme to improve patient care and outcomes and the Intensive-Share group were consulted about routinely collected healthcare data secondary research. The resulting discussions with group members sparked the idea for the project which is the focus of this commentary.

Feature box: Intensive-Share PPIE group - Who are we?

We are a group of children and young people aged 7 to 17 who have all had different hospital experiences in Scotland. Some of us have been patients in paediatric intensive care, and some of us are brothers and sisters of patients. Some of us have had short stays in the hospital and others have had very long stays. We have had to go to hospital for breathing problems, spinal surgery, neuromuscular and heart conditions, cancer and genetic disorders. One of our group members lost their sister after their stay in hospital. We joined Intensive-Share because we wanted to learn more about research and to help researchers. It is important that they understand what we have been through and what we think. We meet online or in-person every few months.

It was at one of our meetings that researchers talked to us about ‘data’. Our first question was, ‘what is data?’. This seemed to be a word used a lot, but many of us did not understand what they meant. We wanted to work with researchers to help them communicate better with people like us.

Co-production

Co-production is an approach where researchers, practitioners and members of the public with lived experience work together on a project in equal partnership from start to finish; within a culture of value, respect, ownership and shared decision-making [12]. Co-production with CYP, however, can be particularly challenging. Power dynamics in the team must be considered to ensure CYP feel valued, respected and genuinely engaged. Having CYP with a range of perspectives and experiences on the team is incredibly valuable, but it is important that all CYP feel able and are given opportunities to participate equally. Co-production can be very time and resource intensive when working with CYP. This is because many CYP may need to balance their roles with school and other activities, and may require additional support with traveling and healthcare needs for example. There is also a delicate balance to achieve true partnership working but at the same time being mindful that CYP may feel burdened, overwhelmed or disinterested.

Aim

This paper reports on the co-production approach utilised to create an informative and engaging communication tool to improve public understanding of healthcare data research in paediatric intensive care.

‘What is data?’ Project

A co-production approach was adopted at all stages of the project, from applying for funding through to dissemination and evaluation (see Fig. 1).

Co-production approach to develop the animation

Our project team (7 CYP from Intensive-Share aged 7–17, a clinician researcher, a PPIE lead, and a graphic designer (GD)) jointly applied for a seed fund for PPIE activity at the University of Edinburgh and were awarded a small grant to co-develop the communication tool. The seed fund included costs for the GD time, an audio recorder, travel and expenses for team members, and voucher recognition for the contributions of the Intensive-Share CYP.

The Intensive-Share PPIE group set the design brief of a short, easy to process information resource as opposed to lengthy text. A video animation was the preferred format for all members as it would allow researchers to convey important information quickly and in an engaging and memorable way. Using a ‘wish-list’ approach ensured that all team members were valued and respected, and the jointly agreed ‘project plan’ aimed to help manage expectations. CYP felt it was important that they could opt-in and opt-out at different stages of the project, and were encouraged to be open and honest in expressing their views and their desired level of participation.

Workshops explored what data meant to different stakeholders – clinicians, researchers and families – and used a variety of creative interactive activities to address communication and language barriers and collate views. These included word association games and art activities alongside more traditional discussion prompts. There was variable awareness of ‘healthcare data’ among CYP, parents and carers. CYP were concerned about sharing of personal data and mental health data, but also did not want to feel they were “reduced to numbers”. Parents and carers had more understanding of data in a healthcare context but not how and what was routinely collected, and concerns were more around data security and data leaving the NHS or University. CYP, parents and carers were all positive and supportive of helping other families through research.

The brief and workshop themes were shared with the GD to draft a storyboard for the animation. Workshops were then held with CYP to jointly agree the storyboard, co-create the script, design the visuals and record the voiceover. Having a staged approach and an opt-in/opt-out agreement built in flexibility for the CYP to participate whilst maintaining a feeling of ownership of the final product. Some CYP were involved in all stages, and others preferred to focus on one or two activities. The variable ages and experiences of the group led to rich creative discussions and ideas. The only workshop where younger and older CYP participated differently, was in the co-creation of the script. Older CYP co-wrote an initial draft, with younger CYP “marking their work” (quote from CYP aged 10) and colouring in words that they didn’t understand. Younger members then worked with the PPIE lead to suggest alternatives. Siblings, some of whom had had experience visiting PICU and some who had not due to COVID restrictions, were very motivated to attend sessions and had sustained participation throughout. CYP were also involved in organising the in-person workshops; helping to choose the date and time, venue and catering. For CYP who were unable to travel or unwell, the team arranged for them to contribute remotely.

As part of the funding for the project, travel and expenses were paid to families and CYP were recognised and rewarded for their contribution with e-vouchers in line with NIHR payment guidance [13].

The co-produced animation was evaluated in three stages. First, 2 families from Intensive-Share (4 adults and 3 CYP aged 12–15) who were not involved in the project were invited to watch it and provide informal feedback. This led to refinement of the animation in a number of ways: an error in the captions was corrected, the way the captions were presented was modified, and background music was added.

Secondly, the refined version was shared with CYP who weren’t involved in the project through a third sector organisation (8 members of Edinburgh Children’s Hospital Charity Youth Forum aged 12–17). The animation was presented by the PPIE lead and 3 CYP who had been involved in the project, and the Youth Forum were asked for their thoughts in an open discussion and in a brief survey. Responses were very positive. CYP stated that the animation was “cool” and “easy to understand” and that they would “remember the video”. One young person shared that they would “much rather watch a video than read something”.

Lastly, parents and carers (n = 8) not involved with the project also completed a brief feedback survey and all respondents reported that their knowledge of healthcare data improved “a little” or “a lot” after watching the animation, and all stated that that they felt “a lot” more willing to consider taking part in research in the future.

The animation “Understanding Healthcare Data & Research for Young People” is open-source and can be viewed online [14].

Lessons learned

The significant input of CYP on the language and script demonstrated that the video would not have been fit for purpose without their partnership. Researchers need to challenge their assumptions of what they consider to be accessible and inclusive language in paediatric research.

The input of siblings alongside former patients was particularly striking. The siblings in the project shared their own unique insights, and some siblings shared how important they felt it was for them to be able to speak for their non-verbal brothers or sisters.

We anticipated that the varying ages and developmental stages of the CYP could be a barrier to co-production, but giving thought to how to meaningfully involve and work with different age groups mitigated this [15]. For example, the script workshop activity was adapted for younger CYP but they had a valuable and equal contribution and identified many words in the script draft that they could not understand, e.g., ‘paediatric’. Guidance advises involving CYP aged over 12 [16], but it is worth considering how we can engage and support younger children in PPIE, particularly as the average age of the PIC population tends to be much younger [17].

We were concerned about the burden of the project for the CYP. In contrast, we found that group members requested more roles and responsibilities during the project than we had anticipated. This sustained interest and motivation may have been due in part to the project idea being generated by the Intensive-Share group themselves, and the fact that relationships had already been established in the PPIE group. We were limited by the number of e-vouchers we had costed in the original grant, and in future would cost for more involvement to allow additional activity to be undertaken.

At our end of project celebration all team members were invited to share their feedback and reflections on the project. CYP reported enjoying the experience, feeling like a team, and being involved in decision-making: all principles of effective co-production. One example of what CYP had enjoyed most was “choosing the food for the meetings!”; illustrating the importance of choice, control and shared decision making.

We are mindful that although the PPIE Lead kept a reflective notebook and we held an informal debrief with CYP at the end of the project, we did not undertake formal evaluation of the PPIE process and would endeavour to do so in the future. It would be important to involve CYP and other stakeholders in agreeing an evaluation strategy and adapting tools or techniques so that they are age-appropriate, e.g., visual tools, narrative approaches and storytelling [18].

We did not provide formal training for the CYP in the project, but the PPIE Lead acted as a source of support and contact for both the CYP and their parents. As each stage of the project was co-developed with shared decision-making, CYP said they felt very comfortable with their roles and participation. The PPIE Lead had already built a relationship with the CYP having been involved with the Intensive-Share group for over 12-months, but support needs and how these could be met should be carefully considered from the outset.

The project was not without its challenges, particularly the cost involved and the time commitment. It would not have been possible without the project funding and local support, and ongoing resources for activities such as this should be prioritised if we truly want to meaningfully empower CYP to be involved and engaged in research. Moving towards national (UK-wide) PPIE groups for PIC research could help pool resources, share expertise, and share PPIE opportunities with more families.

Conclusion

The successful co-production of the animation and co-delivery of the ‘What is data?’ project demonstrated the feasibility and value of meaningfully involving CYP in PIC research. The project challenged our assumptions on the nature and level of involvement CYP preferred, and we would encourage researchers to broaden their approach to PPIE to include CYP and siblings, not just parents and carers. This paper highlights how practical facilitators can ensure the core attributes of co-production can be embedded in a project, contributing to the success of the approach. CYP were eager to contribute when given flexibility, control, input in decision-making, and recognition.

The process also challenged our assumptions on what we considered to be inclusive language, highlighting the importance of listening to families when developing research communications. Working in partnership enabled us to produce an engaging and informative communication tool that families found acceptable, accessible and useful. We hope the open-source video can be utilised by other researchers in PIC and paediatric research in a wider context.
