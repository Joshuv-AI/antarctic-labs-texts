Continuous suture ligation of segmental hilar structures is associated with reduced prolonged air leak after pulmonary segmentectomy
Authors: Ziyao Fang, Jiajun Song, Jinfeng Ge, Ziqing Shen, Yanxia Zhu, Jiaxi Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSegmentectomy has gained increasing acceptance as a surgical option for early-stage non-small cell lung cancer. However, segmentectomy is considered to be associated with a higher incidence of prolonged air leak (PAL) compared with lobectomy. Although PAL is rarely life-threatening, it is consistently associated with prolonged hospital length of stay and higher healthcare costs. Several methods have been developed to reduce the incidence of PAL, including the application of surgical sealants and modified surgical techniques such as pleural tenting. This study aims to evaluate the efficacy and safety of a segmentectomy technique involving continuous ligation of the segmental hilar structures (SHS) to prevent PAL.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective comparative cohort study including consecutive patients who underwent anatomical segmentectomy between December 1, 2023 and December 31, 2025 in The First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University. Patients were divided into two groups based on the intraoperative use of continuous suture ligation for the SHS during anatomical segmentectomy. SHS were defined as the anatomical region comprising the segmental artery, segmental bronchus and adjacent pulmonary parenchyma. PAL was defined as a continuous air leakage for more than 5 days.ResultsA total of 240 patients were included in this study: 101 in the suture group and 139 in the control group. After propensity score matching (PSM), 78 patients were included in each group. The median duration of chest tube placement was shorter in the suture group than in the control group {3 [interquartile range (IQR), 2–4] vs. 4 (IQR, 3–5) days, P=0.001}. Patients in the suture group had a shorter postoperative air leakage duration than that in the control group [1 (IQR, 0–2) vs. 1 (IQR, 0–3) days, P=0.001]. The 24 h chest tube drainage after surgery was significantly lower in the suture group than in the control group [50 (IQR, 30–100) vs. 90 (IQR, 50–120) mL, P=0.001]. The absolute risk reduction of PAL was 11.5 percentage points (P=0.01), and both the exact matched analysis (P=0.01) and conditional logistic regression (P=0.03) supported lower odds of PAL after suture ligation.ConclusionsIn this retrospective study, continuous suture ligation of the SHS demonstrated potential efficacy in preventing PAL and reducing postoperative thoracic drainage volume following segmentectomy. Application of this technique was associated with a lower incidence of PAL, as well as shorter chest tube duration and reduced length of hospital stay.Keywords: Anatomical segmentectomy, prolonged air leak (PAL), uniportal video-assisted thoracic surgery (UVATS), Hilum of pulmonary segment

Highlight box

Key findings

• Continuous suture ligation of the segmental hilar structures (SHS) demonstrated safety and potential efficacy in preventing prolonged air leak (PAL) and reducing postoperative thoracic drainage after segmentectomy.

What is known and what is new?

• Segmentectomy is considered to be associated with a higher incidence of PAL compared with lobectomy. Although PAL is rarely life-threatening, it is consistently associated with prolonged hospital length of stay and increased healthcare costs. Several methods have been developed to reduce PAL incidence.

• In the described surgical technique, intersegmental plane was divided using endostaplers, while SHS was dissected by energy devices followed by continuous suture ligation, thereby integrating the advantages of both approaches. Continuous suture ligation of SHS manifests a protective effect to the pulmonary parenchyma divided by energy devices by reducing air leaks and fluid exudation from the bronchiolo-alveolar structures and thus results in an earlier chest tube removal.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Continuous suture ligation begins at the proximal margin of the staple line and proceeds toward the stumps of the segmental artery and bronchus. We recommend that continuous suture ligation encompasses as much pulmonary parenchyma as feasible at the SHS, while complete encirclement of the segmental artery or bronchus is not required. The described technique is compatible with other measures for preventing PAL, including PGA meshes and surgical sealants.

Introduction

Increased use of high-resolution computed tomography (CT) has led to more frequent detection of solitary pulmonary nodules (SPNs). The majority of detected SPNs are ultimately diagnosed as early-stage pulmonary adenocarcinoma or precancerous lesions (1,2). Thoracoscopic pulmonary segmentectomy has been widely adopted for the treatment of early-stage lung cancer as a minimally invasive surgical technique that offers overall survival and postoperative outcomes comparable to those of lobectomy (3-5). However, controversy persists in the incidence of prolonged air leak (PAL) following pulmonary segmentectomy. While several studies report a higher risk of PAL after segmentectomy compared with lobectomy, others report a lower or comparable risk (6-8).

PAL is one of the most common complications after pulmonary surgeries (9). Although PAL is rarely life-threatening, it is consistently associated with prolonged hospital length of stay and increased healthcare costs (9-12). Moreover, PAL is associated with prolonged chest tube drainage duration, thereby increase the risk of other complications like wound infection and empyema (13). The definition of PAL varies across published studies, with the minimum air leakage duration ranging from postoperative day (POD) 5 to POD10 (9). In this study, PAL is defined as prolonged air leakage lasting more than 5 days, consistent with recent studies based on minimally invasive pulmonary surgery (7,14,15).

Several methods have been developed to reduce PAL incidence. Among intraoperative strategies, the most studied approach is the application of surgical sealants to the resected lung surface and the staple line (14,16,17). Other studies focused on refining surgical technique to decrease the risk of PAL in lobectomy (18,19). In the present study, we introduce a novel intraoperative technique to reduce the incidence of PAL: continuous suture ligation of the segmental hilar structures (SHS) after division of the intersegmental plane (ISP) by endostaplers during pulmonary segmentectomy. We define the SHS as the anatomical region encompassing the origin of the segmental artery and bronchus, along with the adjacent pulmonary parenchyma. This retrospective cohort study was designed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of the novel segmentectomy technique. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-1303/rc).

Methods

Patient data and perioperative interventions

Consecutive patients who underwent pulmonary segmentectomy in The First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University between December 1, 2023 and December 31, 2025 were included. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (I) preoperative chest CT confirmed a solitary pulmonary nodule measuring ≤2 cm in diameter; (II) segmentectomy, subsegmentectomy, or multiple segmentectomy was performed based on tumor location and the requirement for an adequate oncologic margin; (III) age ≥20 years, irrespective of sex. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (I) prior ipsilateral thoracic surgery involving lung resection; (II) intraoperative conversion to lobectomy prompted by intraoperative frozen-section pathology indicating inadequate tumor margin or unexpected invasive histology; (III) severe comorbidities or concurrent active malignancy; (IV) loss to follow-up or incomplete clinical data.

All patients underwent routine preoperative examinations, including pulmonary function tests. The surgeries were performed by a dedicated team of four surgeons—all experienced in uniportal video-assisted thoracic surgery (UVATS)—comprising one professor, one associate professor, and two attending surgeons. The suture technique was introduced to our center in 2021, and our team commenced performing the procedure concurrently. By the start of the cohort in 2023, the SHS suturing technique was already well established. Patients in both the suture and control groups were evenly distributed across the aforementioned time period to minimize potential confounding caused by increased surgical experience or advancements in perioperative care. All patients underwent bedside chest radiography on POD1 to evaluate pleural effusion and pulmonary expansion. A routine blood test was performed on POD2 to assess for inflammatory response suggestive of postoperative pneumonia. Postoperative systemic analgesia was initiated with intravenous flurbiprofen axetil (10 mg twice daily); tramadol was administered as a rescue analgesic for persistent pain. Complex segmentectomy is defined as a segmentectomy requiring division of two or more ISP. The definitions of postoperative pneumonia, wound infection, and arrhythmia are based on the European Society of Thoracic Surgeons (ESTS) and The Society of Thoracic Surgeons (STS) databases (20). Postoperative bleeding was defined as progressive pleural effusion with grossly bloody appearance, requiring hemostatic intervention or blood transfusion. PAL was defined as a continuous air leakage for more than 5 days. All patients underwent pulmonary mobilization and respiratory exercises on POD1. Standard chest tubes connected to a water-seal drainage system were used in all patients; neither digital nor analog electronic chest drainage systems were employed. Surgeons performed twice-daily clinical assessments to evaluate the presence of air leak. Patients were initially assessed at rest; if no air leak was detected, they were instructed to perform coughing and forced expiration. Postoperative air leak duration was defined as the time interval from surgery to the final documented time of air leak, including all intermittent episodes. Chest tube was removed when all of the following criteria were met: complete pulmonary expansion on chest radiography, absence of air leak for at least 24 consecutive hours, and a 24-hour drainage of less than 5 mL/kg. Pulmonary function tests were performed 3 months postoperatively during outpatient follow-up. A study flowchart is demostrated in Figure 1.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the ethics committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University (No. 2026481) and individual consent for this retrospective analysis was waived.

Surgical technique

Both patient groups underwent general anesthesia with double-lumen endotracheal intubation. All surgical procedures were performed using the UVATS approach. The patient was placed in the lateral decubitus position. A 4-cm incision was made in the fourth or fifth intercostal space at the anterior or mid-axillary line, selected according to tumor location. A wound protector was applied and a 30-degree thoracoscope was inserted. The pulmonary fissure and hilar structures were dissected using an electrocautery hook or an ultrasonic scalpel. The pulmonary parenchyma was dissected to expose the segmental vessels and bronchi. The segmental arteries and bronchi were transected using endoscopic staplers. Transection of the segmental vein was determined by tumor location and the planned segmental plane resection. For subsegmentectomies or combined subsegmentectomies, transections of the vessels or bronchus are based on the exact surgical procedures. The ISP was confirmed using indocyanine green (ICG) fluorescence imaging and divided with endoscopic staplers. Regional lymphatic nodes were dissected during the procedure. Following Handa’s 2019 study, simple segmentectomies are defined as procedures requiring division of only one ISP. The following segmentectomies were defined as simple: S6, left S1+2+3, left S4+5 and the basilar segmentectomies (S7–10). All other segmentectomies were considered as complex segmentectomies (21). In the suture group, continuous ligation of the SHS was performed using 4-0 Prolene sutures. The SHS comprises of the proximal stumps of the segmental bronchus and pulmonary artery, along with the surrounding pulmonary parenchyma. The continuous suture was initiated at the peripheral margin of the segment and advanced toward the segmental hilum to envelop the uncovered pulmonary parenchyma which was exposed during prior dissection with visceral pleura. The proximal stumps of the segmental bronchus and artery were not routinely sutured and covered with visceral pleura due to increased tension at this site. Figure 2 illustrates the surgical procedure for a representative case of right S2b+3a segmentectomy and labels the location of SHS. An underwater ventilation test was performed, and sealants or polyglycolic acid (PGA) mesh were applied if significant air leakage was detected. In the control group, only underwater ventilation test and sealants were used to detect and prevent air leakage. A single 24-F chest tube was placed at the apex of the thoracic cavity via the surgical incision at the end of the surgery.

Statistical analysis

This retrospective study utilized de-identified clinical data including operative records, anesthesia records, and intraoperative surgical videos obtained from The First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation for normally distributed data and as median with interquartile range (IQR) for non-normally distributed data. Categorical variables are presented as size and percentage. The propensity scores were analyzed by logistic regression models, and the matched factors included gender, age, body mass index, smoking history, alcohol consumption history, comorbidites, tumor diameter, complex segmentectomy, intraoperative air leakage, intraoperative PGA or sealant application and intraoperative pleural adhesion. Nearest neighbour matching was performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) software (version 26, IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) and R version 3.5.0, and a calliper width of 0.2 with a 1:1 match without replacement. Match balance between the two groups was assessed using the absolute standardized mean difference (ASMD), an ASMD <0.1 was considered as a relatively small imbalance. Before propensity score matching (PSM), Welch t-test or the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to compare continuous variables, and the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test was used to compare categorical variables. After PSM, Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used for paired continuous variables, and McNemar’s test or McNemar-Bowker test was used for paired categorical variables. A two-sided P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant, and confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated at the 95% level. Multivariable logistic regression was performed on the unmatched (pre-PSM) cohort to ensure adequate statistical power, given the higher number of outcome events in this sample. Odds ratios (OR) and corresponding 95% CI were estimated. Paired absolute risk difference (ARD), exact matched OR, and conditional logistic regression were conducted in the PSM cohort to further assess the association between intervention and PAL. The paired ARD was calculated as the suture group risk minus the control group risk. The exact matched OR was derived from matched pairs, and the P value was based on the exact McNemar test. Conditional logistic regression was stratified by matched pair. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P value < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 26, IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) and R version 3.5.0.

Results

Patients’ baseline characteristics

A total of 240 patients were included in this study: 101 underwent continuous suture ligation of the SHS following ISP division with an endostapler, whereas 139 underwent standard segmentectomy without manipulation of the SHS. No significant differences were observed between the groups in age, sex, alcohol consumption status, comorbidities, tumor size, forced vital capacity (FVC), or forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1). The proportions of current and former smokers were significantly higher in the suture group than in the control group, and the proportion of never smokers was significantly lower (15.8% vs. 14.4%, 35.6% vs. 22.2%, and 48.5% vs. 73.4%, respectively; P<0.001). After propensity score matching (PSM), 78 patients were selected from each group A total of 61 unmatched patients were excluded from the control group, and 23 unmatched patients were excluded from the suture group. Several baseline variables were unbalanced between the two groups, such as gender, body mass index (BMI), smoking status, alcohol consumption, comorbidities, tumor size, preoperative FVC, intraoperative air leakage and PGA or sealant application (ASMD >0.10). After PSM, baseline variables were well-balanced between the two groups (ASMD <0.10) (Tables 1,2).

Surgical outcomes

All patients enrolled in the study underwent uniportal video-assisted thoracoscopic segmentectomy, with no conversions to open thoracotomy. Surgical procedures are summarized in Table 3. Left S1+2 segmentectomy was the most frequently performed procedure (14.6%), followed by right S1 (13.3%) and right S3 (12.5%) segmentectomies. In the suture group, right S3 segmentectomy was the most frequently performed procedure (15.8%), followed by right S1 (11.9%) and left S1+2 (9.9%) segmentectomies. In the control group, left S1+2 segmentectomy was the most frequently performed procedure (18.0%), followed by right S1 (14.4%) and right S6 (11.5%) segmentectomies. Postoperative outcomes

The postoperative outcomes of all patients are presented in Table 5. The median duration of chest tube placement was shorter in the suture group than in the control group [3 (IQR, 2–4) vs. 4 (IQR, 3–5) days, P=0.001]. Patients in the suture group have a shorter postoperative air leakage duration than that in the control group [1 (IQR, 0–2) vs. 1 (IQR, 0–3) days, P=0.001]. The incidence of PAL was significantly lower in the suture group than in the control group (1.3% vs. 12.8%; P=0.01). The maximum duration of postoperative air leak of the entire cohort was 8 days. No patient required pleurodesis, chest tube reinsertion, or prolonged outpatient pleural drainage. No patient was readmitted for PAL. 24h chest tube drainage after surgery was significantly lower in the suture group than in the control group [50 (IQR, 30–100) vs. 90 (IQR, 50–120) mL, P=0.001]. The incidence of postoperative complications did not differ significantly between the two groups (P=0.31). Pulmonary infection was the most common postoperative complication both before and after PSM (7.5% and 6.4%). No statistical significance in FVC, FEV1 and FEV1% of predicted 3 months after surgery between the two groups. Table 6 presents the results of the multivariable logistic regression analysis conducted on the unmatched (pre-PSM) cohort. After logistic regression, suture ligation of SHS was significantly associated with a reduced risk of PAL (OR 0.22, P=0.01), while pleural adhesion was significantly associated with an increased risk of PAL (OR 2.91, P=0.045). The results of the matched analysis of PAL following PSM are presented in Table 7. The absolute risk reduction of PAL was 11.5 percentage points (P=0.01), and both the exact matched analysis (P=0.01) and conditional logistic regression (P=0.03) supported lower odds of PAL after suture ligation. Table 8 represents the safety analysis of suture ligation after PSM. The overall postoperative complication rate was not increased in the suture group compared with the control group (P=0.19).

Discussion

In recent years, anatomical segmentectomy has demonstrated comparable outcomes to those of lobectomy in patients with early-stage lung cancer (3,5). The primary advantage of anatomical segmentectomy over lobectomy is the preservation of pulmonary parenchyma, which results in a better preservation of pulmonary function (22). However, segmentectomy is technically more demanding than lobectomy, requiring precise identification and dissection of segmental bronchi and vessels, as well as division of ISP. The incidence of postoperative air leakage following segmentectomy remains a debate compared with lobectomy. Several studies reported that segmentectomy has comparable postoperative air leakage incidence between VATS segmentectomy and lobectomy (6). However, more evidences suggest that segmentectomy is associated with a longer duration of postoperative air leakage compared with lobectomy attributable to additional procedures to divide ISP and consequent injury to the pulmonary parenchyma (8,23,24).

Postoperative air leakage is the most common complication following pulmonary resection, occurring in up to 50% of patients on POD1 (25). However, prolonged postoperative air leakage adversely affects postoperative recovery. Prolonged postoperative air leakage is associated with extended hospitalization, higher healthcare costs, and an elevated risk of other complications like empyema (10,12,13,26). The definition of PAL varies across published studies, with the thresholds ranging from more than 5 to 10 PODs. Based on recent studies, PAL is defined as an air leak persisting beyond POD5, which is consistent with the STS recommendation for hospital length of stay (20,27). The risk factors associated with PAL remain incompletely characterized and are under investigation. Divisi et al. confirmed that longer duration of surgery, male sex, pleural adhesion, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) were risk factors for the predictionof PAL, while middle lobe resections and ground glass opacity were protective factors from PAL (28). However, the study did not focus on UVATS segmentectomies. Gioutsos et al. conducted a study in 2025 to investigate the risk factors of PAL in patients underwent UVATS segmentectomy and concluded that upper lobe surgery, lower BMI, an additional wedge resection in another segment, and hypertension were associated with an increased risk of PAL (29). Several intraoperative methods have been employed to reduce the risk of PAL. The application of PGA mesh sheets is widely investigated to prevent PAL. PGA meshes were applied either directly over the ISP divided using energy devices or along the staple line. The application of PGA meshes has been shown to reduce intraoperative air leaks and shorten the duration of postoperative air leaks, although it does not consistently reduce the duration of postoperative chest tube drainage (16,30). Several sealants, including polyethylene glycol (PEG)-based absorbable sealants and fibrin-based sealants, have been investigated for reducing intraoperative and postoperative air leaks. Similar to PGA meshes, sealants have been shown to reduce the duration of postoperative air leaks, though they do not consistently shorten chest tube drainage duration (31,32). Compared with the direct application of PGA meshes or sealants to the ISP, combined application of both materials along the staple line has demonstrated efficacy in reducing PAL (16). At our institution, PGA meshes or sealants are used exclusively when intraoperative air leaks are detected by underwater ventilation test. Other investigators have explored surgical procedural modifications to reduce the risk of PAL. Pleural tenting involves dissecting the parietal pleura from the lateral or superior thoracic wall and suturing it to the intercostal space adjacent to the resection site, thereby enclosing the remaining pulmonary parenchyma. Allama et al. demonstrated the efficacy of pleural tenting in reducing PAL in a prospective, randomized trial (19). Zhao et al. proposed a modified technique for dividing the ISP by inserting the endostapler directly into the pulmonary parenchyma after precise dissection and preservation of the segmental vein (33). These surgical techniques have been shown to be effective in reducing PAL, although they require advanced technical expertise. Pleural tenting necessitates precise dissection of the parietal pleura to minimize injury to the thoracic wall, and direct endostapler insertion into the pulmonary parenchyma demands advanced familiarity with segmental anatomy to avoid inadvertent injury to segmental vessels.

In this study, we describe a method for managing the SHS after ISP division using endostaplers, which demonstrated advantages over conventional techniques in preventing PAL. In conventional segmentectomy procedures, the ISP is identified and divided using endostaplers. However, the pulmonary parenchyma adjacent to the segmental bronchus and artery remains ‘unstapled’ and thus directly exposed to the thoracic cavity, potentially leading to air leaks from the bronchiolo-alveolar structures (27). We routinely covered the SHS with PGA meshes or sealants when air leaks were detected during underwater ventilation testing. However, minor postoperative air leaks persisted in some patients during coughing. We hypothesize that PGA meshes or surgical sealants may displace from their intended intraoperative position postoperatively, owing to pulmonary ventilation and patient mobility. In this retrospective study, continuous suture ligation of the SHS demonstrated considerable efficacy in preventing PAL compared with conventional segmentectomy techniques. The incidence of PAL in the control group was 14.4%, which was in consistent with recent studies (29,34). Only 4 cases of PAL were observed among the 101 patients enrolled in the suture group. After PSM, the suture group exhibited significantly lower incidences of PAL, reduced 24-hour chest tube drainage volume, and shorter chest tube duration compared with the control group. Notably, no statistically significant difference in operating time was observed between the two groups. Suturing time varied across cases depending on the anatomical location and suturing area of the SHS. Compared with the variability in total operative duration across different segmentectomies, the differences in continuous suturing time did not exert a statistically significant effect on overall operative duration. After multivariable logistic regression analysis, continuous suture ligation of the SHS was significantly associated with a reduced risk of PAL. By contrast, pleural adhesions requiring intraoperative adhesiolysis were identified as a significant risk factor for PAL, likely due to the associated risk of pulmonary parenchymal injury during adhesiolysis. In the present study, complex segmentectomy was not significantly associated with an increased risk of PAL, consistent with findings reported by Galvez et al. Galvez concluded that, by definition, simple segmentectomy typically involves greater resection of pulmonary parenchyma, potentially compromising postoperative pulmonary expansion and thereby increasing the risk of PAL (35). In the present study, gender, BMI and smoking status were not identified as risk factors for PAL, which was not consistent with prior reports (28,29). This might be caused by potential confounding resulting from the relatively small sample size of this single-center study. Continuous suture ligation of SHS manifested a protective effect to the pulmonary parenchyma divided by energy devices by reducing air leaks and fluid exudation from the bronchiolo-alveolar structures and thus result in an earlier chest tube removal. Furthermore, additional suture ligations did not impede postoperative re-expansion of the residual lung parenchyma, and 3-month pulmonary function tests revealed no significant differences between the two groups in this study.

Essentially, the surgical technique described in this study integrates endostapler with suture ligation during segmentectomies. The efficacy of endostaplers in preventing PAL during division of the ISP has been demonstrated in multiple studies (36). However, endostaplers lack the flexibility required to transect pulmonary parenchyma adjacent to the segmental hilum. Although energy devices enable precise division of the ISP, thereby preserving greater pulmonary parenchymal volume, the thermal injury and eschar formation may impair postoperative recovery of the residual lung parenchyma (37). In the described surgical technique, ISP was divided using endostaplers, while SHS was dissected by energy devices followed by continuous suture ligation, thereby integrating the advantages of both approaches. When performing this technique, iatrogenic injury to adjacent structures including the arterial stump and intersegmental veins should be avoided. Unintentional injury to the arterial stump during suturing may cause intraoperative bleeding, and unintentional narrowing of the intersegmental veins due to suturing may lead to postoperative segmental congestion. Compared with other surgical approaches to prevent PAL, continuous suture ligation of the SHS is technically less demanding and results in less injury to adjacent thoracic structures provided that the aforementioned risks are properly treated. To minimize potential confounding related to the increased surgical experience, cohort enrollment initiated two years after the SHS suturing technique was introduced at our center, thereby precluding a conventional learning curve analysis. However, in our experience, surgeons already proficient in thoracoscopic suturing demonstrated rapid technical acquisition of this procedure. Compared with the suturing procedure itself, identification of the SHS and planning of the suture area represent the primary challenges for novice surgeons. In our experience, continuous suture ligation begins at the proximal margin of the staple line and proceeds toward the stumps of the segmental artery and bronchus. We recommend that continuous suture ligation encompass as much pulmonary parenchyma as feasible at the SHS, while complete encirclement of the segmental artery or bronchus should be performed based on intraoperative anatomical considerations. If the segmental artery or bronchus stump was short and could be covered by adjacent pulmonary parenchyma, incorporation of the stump was recommended to minimize tissue injury associated with SHS dissection. Incorporation of a relatively long segmental artery or bronchus stump may increase suture tension and induce potential injury to the stumps. It should be emphasized that continuous suture ligation of the SHS is not universally applicable across all segmentectomies, as it necessitates adequate pulmonary parenchymal tissue surrounding the segmental artery and bronchus to ensure safe needle passage. Normally, at least 1cm adjacent pulmonary parenchyma with visceral plaura should be incorporated. Optimal incorporation of pulmonary parenchyma is critical to prevent visceral pleural injury during postoperative pulmonary re-expansion. An important consideration when performing suture ligation is the presence of underlying COPD or emphysema. Our clinical experience indicates that pulmonary parenchyma in patients with COPD or emphysema is more fragile and is more susceptible to iatrogenic injury during surgical interventions including stapling, dissection, and suture placement. The low prevalence of COPD in our cohort precluded a robust subgroup analysis evaluating the safety and efficacy of this technique in patients with COPD. Although patients with COPD or emphysema are at increased risk of PAL and may theoretically benefit from continuous suture ligation of the SHS, the technique should be applied cautiously in this population due to the absence of robust clinical evidence. Moreover, the described technique is compatible with other measures for preventing PAL, including PGA meshes and surgical sealants.

This study has several limitations. First, the retrospective study design may introduce selection bias in patient enrollment. Secondly, the single-center design restricts the sample size and limits the generalizability of the findings. The limited sample size precluded robust subgroup analyses in patient subgroups at elevated risk of PAL, including older adults and patients with COPD or emphysema. Finally, this study focused exclusively on the UVATS approach. Future investigations should be conducted to evaluate the efficacy and safety of the described method in patients with an elevated risk of PAL, including older adults and patients with COPD or emphysema.

Conclusions

In this retrospective study, continuous suture ligation of the SHS demonstrated potential efficacy in preventing PAL and reducing postoperative thoracic drainage volume following segmentectomy. This technique may contribute to improved overall postoperative recovery after segmentectomy, supporting the goals of enhanced recovery after surgery.
