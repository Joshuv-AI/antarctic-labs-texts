Chloroplast and mitochondrial genomes of Sphaerosorus coelastroides Pascher (Xanthophyceae) from Central Appalachia, Clinch River, Virginia, United States
Authors: Gavin S Collins, Robin A Matthews, A Bruce Cahoon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis report describes the isolation and culture of Sphaerosorus coelastroides from the plankton of a freshwater river in the central Appalachian mountain chain. This alga was first observed in a dry freshwater riverbed in Central Europe in 1908 and, since then, has been included in Xanthophyceae surveys from nearly all continents with temperate climates. Differential interference microscopic analysis of our isolate revealed cell sizes ranging from 10.2 to 19 μm in diameter occurring in groups of two to 32 cells with a mucilage layer that changed thickness depending upon the phase of the life cycle. Chloroplasts were light green, and confocal fluorescence microscopy revealed three or more per cell. Genetic markers were sequenced, annotated, and deposited into GenBank, and include the 18S nuclear rRNA gene (PX102079) and completed chloroplast (PV826133) and mitochondrial (PV826132) genomes. Phylogenetic analyses using 18S and rbcL gene sequences revealed that this isolate was closely related to S. composita, the only other known species from this genus. This isolate was deposited into the UTEX Culture Collection of Algae, making it the only strain of this alga available for phycological study at this time.Keywords: algal organelle genome, freshwater algae, river plankton, stramenopile

INTRODUCTION

The algal genus Sphaerosorus was established by Pascher (1938) with the description of S. coelastroides. This species was cultured from a soil sample collected from the dry bed of a freshwater stream in Central Europe. It was described as having uniformly sized cells with one or “few” chloroplasts growing in spherical clusters of two to 32 cells. Pascher noted that the species occurred infrequently in bodies of freshwater across Central Europe. Since its initial description, S. coelastroides has been reported in surveys from Central Europe, South and West Asia, and South America (Ettl, 1978; Ettl & Gärtner, 1995; Gupta, 2012; Hindák & Hindáková, 1998; Kant & Gupta, 1998; Mikheyeva, 1999; Tell, 1985). A single North American record exists from the Red Lakes Wetlands of Minnesota, United States (Ott et al., 2015; Tarapchak, 1972). A second species, S. composita, was described from marine environments off the coast of Australia by Moewus (1951).

Genetic markers are now commonly produced for algal species from culture collections or environmental samples, serving as valuable tools for taxonomic studies and species identification (e.g., barcoding). Commonly used markers include the nuclear 18S ribosomal small subunit (SSU) gene, chloroplast genes such as 23S, rbcL, psaA, and the mitochondrial gene coxI, among others (Fawley & Fawley, 2020; Kezlya et al., 2023; Leliaert et al., 2014). Beyond individual markers and whole organellar genomes, that is, mitochondrial (mitogenomes) and chloroplast (plastomes), are increasingly produced from type specimens, culture collections, and environmental samples as high‐throughput sequencing technologies have become more affordable and widely available. These methods provide opportunities for phycologists to apply multi‐marker approaches to distinguish cryptic species and explore genome evolution (Chang et al., 2025; Díaz‐Tapia et al., 2017; Turmel et al., 2015; Verbruggen et al., 2025). For the genus Sphaerosorus, publicly available genetic markers only exist from the marine species, S. composita (Maistro et al., 2007; Negrisolo et al., 2004; Rybalka et al., 2009).

In this study, we describe the isolation and culture of Sphaerosorus coelastroides from the Clinch River in Virginia, United States. Originating in Tazewell County, Virginia, the Clinch River flows through southwestern Virginia and northeastern Tennessee before joining the Tennessee River. It is recognized as a highly biodiverse freshwater ecosystem in North America, harboring one of the largest known assemblages of freshwater mussels, with 46 documented species (Jones et al., 2014; Zipper et al., 2014). The cultured strain reported here represents the second record of S. coelastroides from North America. It provided material for sequencing the nuclear ribosomal SSU gene marker, as well as the complete chloroplast and mitochondrial genomes of this alga.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

In July 2023, microalgal samples were collected from the Clinch River near Saint Paul (Russell County), Virginia, using a 20‐μm plankton net. The samples were transported to a laboratory at the University of Virginia's (UVA's) College at Wise, where they were centrifuged at 3000 × g for 10 min in 250‐mL bottles. The pelleted cells were resuspended in a small volume of the remaining river water. Aliquots of 25, 50, or 100 μL were spread onto agarose plates containing 1/4× Bolds Modified Basal Freshwater Nutrient Solution (Sigma‐Aldrich, Saint Louis, Missouri, United States), Hutner's trace elements (Harris, 2009), sodium silicate (275 mg · L−1, 2.25 mM), and Tris HCl (0.2 g · L−1), dissolved in Clinch River water passed through a 0.4‐μm filter and adjusted to pH 7. Plates were incubated under LED growth lights (AC Infinity ION Frame EVO3, City of Industry, California, United States), a daylight cycle of 16:8 h light:dark, 170 μmol photons · m−2 · s−1 at room temperature. Colony appearance and/or growth was monitored daily. Visible colonies were picked with wooden toothpicks, patch‐plated, and incubated under the same conditions as described above. Microalgae that successfully grew on patch plates were preliminarily identified using nuclear 18s rRNA gene barcode primers (Stoeck et al., 2010). Whole cells were introduced directly into the polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification mix and cycled using a BIO‐RAD C1000 Thermo Cycler (Hercules, California, United States), using the following program: 95°C for 5 min (95°C for 30 s, 55°C for 10 s, 72°C for 30 s) 40×, 72°C for 10 min. Amplicon production was confirmed using gel electrophoresis. Amplicons were prepared for sequencing using Exonuclease 1 and Shrimp Alkaline Phosphatase (ExoSAP) treatment to remove unused primers and nucleotides. Amplicons were sequenced using Sanger sequencing by the commercial sequencing facility Azenta (South Plainfield, New Jersey, United States) with the same primers used for PCR amplification. The 18S rRNA gene sequence for the culture that is the focus of this study indicated the alga was likely a member of the genus Sphaerosorus, so the patch‐plated sample was transferred to a new plate and liquid medium for long‐term growth and maintenance as well as to obtain enough cells for whole genome sequencing.

Live and stained wet mounts of Sphaerosorus cells were examined by placing droplets of the cultured material on glass slides and compressing them slightly using #1 coverslips. One drop of 2% Lugol's iodine solution was added to a fresh wet mount to test for the presence of starch inside the chloroplasts; one drop of 1% methylene blue stain was added to separate fresh mounts to highlight the mucilage surrounding groups of progeny cells. The cells were examined using a Nikon 80i microscope equipped with differential interference contrast objectives and polarizing filters. The digital images were captured as high‐resolution uncompressed tif files using an Accu‐Scope Excelis 4K UHD digital camera. Scale bars were stamped on the images using custom scripts created for the GNU Image Manipulation Program (GIMP 2.10.30). The widths of 20 mature parent cells and 20 clustered progeny cells were measured with GIMP using a pixel‐to‐micron conversion script. Cell biovolume estimates were calculated based on a spherical geometric shape. Confocal microscopic analysis was completed at the East Tennessee State University Microscopy Core Facility using a Leica TCS SP8.

DNA extractions for whole genome sequencing were performed using the Quick DNA miniprep kit (Zymo Research, Irvine, California, United States). Cells grown in liquid medium were pelleted by centrifugation at 3000 × g for 5 min. The pellet was suspended in the DNA extraction buffer provided in the kit, and cell lysis was carried out for 10 min using ZR BashingBead Lysis Tubes (Zymo Research) and a Vortex‐Genie 2 equipped with a horizontal microtube holder attachment (Scientific Industries, Bohemia, New York, United States). After vortexing, the tubes were centrifuged briefly to remove bubbles. Following cell lysis, DNA extraction was carried out according to the protocol provided with the kit. Whole genome sequencing was performed by Azenta using their non‐human next‐generation sequencing Illumina paired‐read sequencing service.

Sequences received from Azenta in FASTQ format (11,193,357 paired‐end reads) were imported into Geneious Prime v.2025.0 (Dotmatics, Boston, Massachusetts, United States). Paired reads were merged using BBMerge v38.84 and de novo assembled into 117,881 consensus sequences using the Geneious assembler. A full‐length 18S rRNA gene sequence was identified from the assembled consensus sequences using Geneious's map to reference function with the previously obtained 18S rRNA gene barcode fragment used as bait. Chloroplast and mitochondrial genome candidates were identified using a similar strategy. For the chloroplast genome, an rbcL gene sequence from Vaucheria litorea (NC_011600) was used as bait; for the mitochondrial genome, the nad4L gene from Koliella longiseta (PP746562) was used. Protein‐coding genes in the organellar genomes were identified and annotated using the Geneious Prime Find ORF function and the U.S. National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI, www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST), specifically protein BLAST and BLASTx. Each gene was confirmed manually, and areas of the genome where no open reading frames (ORFs) were identified by automated methods were manually screened to ensure genes were not overlooked. Transfer RNAs (tRNAs) were identified using tRNA scan (Chan et al., 2021). Any rRNAs were identified by comparing the organelle genome candidates to other organelle genomes and locating homologous sequences. Completed genetic markers were deposited into Genbank: plastome (PV826133), mitogenome (PV826132), and 18S rRNA gene (PX102079). Raw genome data were deposited into GenBank's SRA database (SRR36487171).

For phylogenetic analysis, the chloroplast rbcL, nuclear small rRNA 18S, and mitochondrial coxI gene sequences from our isolate were aligned to other rbcL, 18S, or coxI gene sequences from species in the class Xanthophyceae that were available in NCBI/Genbank (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) as of October 2025. Sequences were aligned using MUSCLE with default parameters. Maximum likelihood trees were produced using IQTREE v.3.0.1 (Trifinopoulos et al., 2016) using the HKY model and ultrafast bootstrapping for both (Hoang et al., 2018).

This strain is maintained at UVA Wise, Wise, Virginia, United States, as Clinch2023‐4 and deposited in the UTEX Culture Collection of Algae at the University of Texas, Austin (UTEX B 3240).

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Culture and microscopic descriptions

On semi‐solid medium, the isolate appeared as round, green, smooth‐edged agglomerations. Growth was attempted on media with various pH levels, and this alga grew most rapidly between 6.5 and 7.0.

Microscopically, this isolate of Sphaerosorus coelastroides existed as spherical to globose solitary cells enclosed within a thin mucilage layer or as colonies of two to 32 progeny cells enclosed within a much larger spherical mucilage layer (Figure 1a,b). This is consistent with previous descriptions, with some small differences. The genus and species are described as occurring in spherical colonial groups of two to 30 uniformly shaped cells (Pascher, 1938); eight, 16, or 32 cells (Tarapchak, 1972); and four to 32 cells (Ettl, 1978). We observed this colonial behavior, but also noted many single cells; the presence of solitary cells was either briefly mentioned in earlier publications or not mentioned at all.

Sphaerosorus coelastroides cells visualized using phase contrast microscopy and fluorescent confocal microscopy. Individual cells (a) and a group of eight progeny cells encased in a polysaccharide sheath (b). Confocal microscopy image showing chloroplast autofluorescence (c). A mix of single and progeny cells stained with Lugol's solution (d).

We hypothesized that the mucilage layers surrounding groups of progeny cells originate from gelatinized parent cell walls. This interpretation was supported by observations of many large parent cells exhibiting progressive thickening of the cell wall. The thickness of the mucilage enclosing progeny cells ranged from 2 to 14 μm, with increased radial stratification appearing in the wider mucilage layers (Figure 1d). Similarly, the thickness of the parent cell mucilage varied considerably with cell size and, presumably, cell age, suggesting that mucilage development occurs prior to cell division. This hypothesis is consistent with the observations of Tarapchak (1972, p. 14) who described “a hyaline envelope” around younger colonies and hypothesized it to be a “partially gelatinized mother‐cell wall.”

Sphaerosorus coelastroides did not stain positive for starch (Figure 1d), which is consistent with Ott et al. (2015), who noted the absence of starch when S. coelastroides was stained with potassium iodide.

Mature solitary cells were 10.2–19.0 μm in diameter (median = 14.7 μm), with an estimated biovolume of 556–3591 μm3 (median = 1663 μm3). Progeny cells were 5.4–11.0 μm in diameter (median = 7.1 μm), with an estimated biovolume of 82–697 μm3 (median = 187 μm3). These were smaller than the 8–12 μm and 7–12 μm measurements previously reported (Ettl, 1978; Tarapchak, 1972). This discrepancy could be explained by our strain having been in culture for about 14 months when measurements were taken. Cultured algal cells are often smaller than their environmentally derived counterparts, possibly due to rapid cell divisions on optimal medium.

The chloroplasts had a yellow‐green color and, using fluorescence confocal microscopy, z‐stack images revealed three or more chloroplasts per cell. They appeared compressed along the outer plasma membrane, and their shapes could be described as lobed or undulating (Figure 1c). Previously, chloroplasts had been described as “few in number,” pale, and mural (or parietal) discoid “chromatophores” (Ettl, 1978; Pascher, 1938; Tarapchak, 1972). This is consistent with the Xanthophyceae, which were described as having disc‐shaped yellow‐green chloroplasts (Van den Hoeck et al., 1995) or more generally as having several chloroplasts (Friedl, 2024). Although the color of the chloroplasts in our isolate was consistent with that of other Xanthophytes, the chloroplasts were not disc‐shaped. We interpret this difference in chloroplast morphology as a limitation of using light microscopy in the early and middle 20th century versus fluorescence confocal with z‐stacking that was used in this study.

rbcL , 18S SSU, and coxI gene phylogenies

No archived cultures or reference sequences of Sphaerosorus coelastroides were available for direct comparison at the time of this study. Using publicly available 18S rRNA SSU and rbcL gene sequences, phylogenetic analyses placed our isolate in a well‐supported clade with the marine‐dwelling S. composita, the only other species in this genus.

Maximum likelihood analysis was conducted using 18S rRNA, rbcL, and cox1 genes from the Sphaerosorus isolate alongside publicly available sequences from other Xanthophyceae. Our isolate formed a shared branch with S. composita with both the 18S rRNA and rbcL gene markers and high SH‐aLRT%/bootstrap branch support, 98.5/100 for SSU (Figure 2a) and 100/100 for rbcL (Figure 2 and Figure S1). The strong branch support across two independently evolving markers provides multi‐locus support and confirmation of the morphological identification of the isolate.

Phylogenetic analyses of rbcL and 18S rRNA gene markers. (a) 18S rRNA gene phylogeny. (b) rbcL gene phylogeny. Both were produced as maximum likelihood analyses using iqTree. A complete rbcL gene phylogeny showing all individual species is included as Figure S1.

Sequence divergence between the two markers was detectable. Sphaerosorus coelastroides and S. composita 18S rRNA gene sequences differed from one another by three nucleotides across an alignment of 1764 bp, reflecting the relatively slow evolutionary rate of essential ribosomal RNA genes (Smit et al., 2007). The rbcL gene sequences differed from one another by 17 nucleotides across an alignment of 1383 bp; this gene evolves more rapidly and is commonly used as a genetic barcode marker for species‐level identification (Rimet et al., 2019; Zou et al., 2016).

A third phylogenetic analysis was conducted using the limited number, nine, of publicly available Xanthophyceae mitochondrial cox1 gene sequences (Figure S2). The information derived from this analysis is limited due to the small number of sequences, but it is broadly consistent with patterns seen in the rbcL and 18S rRNA gene analyses. As more mitochondrial genomes and coxI gene markers are produced from this class, phylogenetic resolution using a third independently evolving marker should improve.

Chloroplast genome

The chloroplast genome (Figure S3) was assembled from 247,709 Illumina reads, resulting in a 141,669‐bp chromosome with an average sequencing coverage of 262. It contained 65 identifiable protein‐coding genes (PCGs), 18 undefinable ORFs, eight hypothetical reading frames (YCFs), 24 tRNAs, and three rRNAs (Table S1). It had the common quadrapartite structure with inverted repeat regions (IRA and IRB) 8031 bp in size, containing the three rRNA genes and two tRNA genes. The small single copy region (SSC) was 53,354 bp, and the long single copy region (LSC) was 72,253 bp. The overall GC content was 32.36% (45,854 bp).

Sphaerosorus spp. belongs to the class Xanthophyceae, a lineage within the stramenopiles (heterokont) wherein its photosynthetic members acquired plastids through a secondary endosymbiotic event with a red alga (Friedl, 2024). Despite there being over 4000 recognized species that can be common constituents of freshwater habitats, at the time of writing, only one other Xanthophyte plastome was publicly available, Vaucheria litorea (Rumpho et al., 2008), restricting comparisons to these two genera. The V. litorea plastome is 115,341 bp in size with 116 PCGs, whereas the S. coelastroides plastome is considerably larger at 141,669 bp but encodes only 65 protein‐coding genes. This is a notably small number of genes for a photosynthetic alga; for example, a meta‐analysis of 58 Trebouxiophyceae plastomes revealed an average of 79 PCGs (Song et al., 2025), pointing out the potential of extensive gene reduction in this lineage. Plastome gene relocation to the nucleus is a well‐documented phenomenon (Wicke et al., 2011), and it is possible that gene migration has occurred at a high rate in the Sphaerosorus lineage. It is also hypothetically possible that there are additional PCGs in the S. coelastroides plastome. Our analyses located 18 ORFs located in intergenic regions, 1207, 1935, 2639, 3002, and 8162 pb in length. Manual screening of these ORFs determined none of the translations coded for recognizable proteins in GenBank, but it is possible these are alternative versions of common chloroplast encoded genes.

Among the recognizable PCGs, 60 were conserved between Sphaerosorus coelastroides and Vaucheria litorea (Table S2). These conserved genes are consistent with those found across 120 diatom plastomes, including genes encoding ATP synthase, the cytochrome b/f complex, and photosystems I and II; the four light‐independent chlorophyll biosynthesis genes (chlB, chlI, chlL, and chlN); the rbcL and rbcS genes; the four plastid‐encoded RNA polymerase subunits; and genes associated with large and small ribosomal subunits.

A meta‐analysis of diatom plastomes showed that gene gain, loss, and pseudogenization are common within stramenopile lineages and that evolutionary rates vary substantially among genera (Chang et al., 2025). Therefore, it is possible that Sphaerosorus coelastroides is an example of extreme photosynthetic plastome plasticity. Given the distant relationship between Vaucheria and Sphaerosorus within the Xanthophyceae, pronounced differences in plastome size and gene content between the two taxa are plausible.

Mitochondrial genome

The mitogenome (Figure S3) was assembled from 37,676 Illumina reads, resulting in a chromosome 55,007 bp with an average sequencing coverage of 102. It contains 24 identifiable PCGs, one undefined ORF, 22 tRNAs and two rRNAs (Table S1). The overall GC content was 32.97% (18,137 bp). This represents a fully sequenced mitogenome from the class Xanthophyceae.

Due to the absence of other complete mitogenomes within this class, comparative analyses were conducted at a broader taxonomic scale. To compare the size of the Sphaerosorus coelastroides mitogenome, we surveyed publicly available mitogenomes from photosynthetic stramenopiles. We analyzed 243 entries—one entry per species (redundant species records and ones with no specific epithet were removed) with the taxonomic makeup: Phaeophyceae, 129 mitogenomes; Bacillariophyta, 93; Eustigmatophyceae, 11; Pelagophyceae, three; Opalazoa, two; Chrysophyceae, one; Schizocladiophyceae, one; Xanthophyceae, one; Raphidophyceae, one; and Synurophyceae, one. Mitogenome sizes in this dataset ranged from 30,995 to 103,605 bp, with a mean of 40,561 bp and a median of 37,814 bp. The S. coelastroides mitogenome was larger than the average stramenopile (exceeding one standard deviation above the mean) but still fell within the observed size range. The overall size, in bp, of mitogenomes results from expansions and contractions of intergenic regions. This is a common phenomenon in algal organellar genomes (Guo et al., 2015; Pogoda et al., 2019), and the unique nature of intergenic regions makes them useful as species‐specific molecular markers (Zhang et al., 2021).

A total of 24 identifiable PCGs were identified in the Sphaerosorus coelastroides mitogenome, which is within a normal range among algae. Recent meta‐analyses of diatom mitogenomes demonstrated an average of 36.5 PCGs in diatoms, whereas most species in the green algal class Chlorophyceae have fewer than 10 (Cahoon et al., 2025; Chang et al., 2025). Within those lineages, 13 PCGs were found in nearly all species: atp6, atp9, cob, cox1, cox2, cox3, nd1, nd2, nd3, nd4, nd4L, nd5, and nd6 (Cahoon et al., 2025; Chang et al., 2025). Of these genes, S. coelastroides carried nine, lacking atp9, cob, nd3, and nd4L.

Whether the size and gene content of the Sphaerosorus coelastroides reflect species‐specific phenomena or are representative of a more expansive clade can only be determined with the sequencing of more mitogenomes from this class.

Occurrence of Sphaerosorus coelastroides

Sphaerosorus coelastroides was initially described by Paschal as “infrequently occurring” across Central Europe. Since then, it has been recorded in Europe, North America, South America, and South Asia, suggesting it may have a cosmopolitan distribution (Ettl & Gärtner, 1995; Gupta, 2012; Hindák & Hindáková, 1998; Kant & Gupta, 1998; Mikheyeva, 1999; Ott et al., 2015; Tell, 1985). For nearly 10 years, our group has been sampling and culturing from the Clinch River and other bodies of water in Southwestern Virginia using microscopic and metabarcoding analyses (Cahoon et al., 2017; Fawley et al., 2021; and A. B. Cahoon, unpublished data). We have only observed S. coelastroides on the occasion that led to the culture described in this study. We screened metabarcoding results from other projects completed in our locality and found no evidence of it in those sequences (data not shown). It is possible that the primers used for those metabarcoding studies were incompatible with S. coelastroides or that this species truly occurs “infrequently” as it was described nearly 100 years ago.

CONCLUSIONS

Using a combination of morphological features and phylogenetic analyses, we conclude that we established a culture of Sphaerosorus coelastroides from the Clinch River. This is significant as, prior to our study, no type culture for this species existed in a culture collection, and no genetic markers were available. This isolate was deposited into the UTEX algal collection, and its genetic markers were sequenced and deposited into GenBank. The chloroplast genome is only the second archived xanthophyte sequence, and the mitogenome is, to our knowledge, the first. This culture and these genetic markers provide new resources for the phycological community.
