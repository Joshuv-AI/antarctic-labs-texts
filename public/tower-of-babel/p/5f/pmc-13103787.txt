Transparent Wood with Polymer-Dispersed Liquid Crystals for Next-Generation Smart Windows: Prospective for Energy Efficiency, Diurnal Light, and CO2 Emission Mitigation
Authors: Reenu Rajan, Saboor Shaik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Window components play a crucial role in managing heat transfer in buildings, but traditional passive windows often fail to adapt to varying environmental conditions, which impact occupant comfort. To overcome this limitation, this study presents the novel concept of PDLC integrated between two layers of transparent wood (PITW) glazings, showcasing their dual capability for dynamic light modulation and thermal insulation across multiple wood species and establishing their significant energy-saving and daylighting potential for adaptive building envelopes in diverse climatic zones. Also, this integration concept of PITW glazing enhances energy efficiency, thermal insulation, and the ability to regulate heat gain and loss. To validate these benefits, this study experimentally analyzes the solar and material characteristics of four different PITW glazings utilizing balsa, poplar, pine, and bamboo (designated as PITW1, PITW2, PITW3, and PITW4) when in both their operational and non-operational states. In addition to this, a thermal and cost–benefit assessment was conducted by using a theoretical model to evaluate two climatic zones in India, including the hot arid and hot humid regions. Notably, in both the off and on states, PITW3 glazing showed significant air-conditioning cost savings of $9,011 and $7,190 in Vellore and New Delhi, respectively, with favorable payback periods and adequate daylight access compared with all other PITW glazings studied.

Introduction

The building sector is a major contributor to global energy consumption and emissions, being responsible for about 40% of energy use and 33% of greenhouse gas emissions, mainly through the increased need for cooling, heating, and lighting. As an important element in the building, the type of glazing significantly influences thermal loads and energy usage with its optical and thermal properties. Traditional glazing materials, including glass, not only enhance the aesthetic value of a building but also establish a visual connection with the outside world, but they come with significant drawbacks, including glare, fragility, excessive solar heat gain, diminished thermal comfort, and poor energy efficiency. Therefore, it is essential to investigate alternative glazing solutions that can overcome these problems and contribute to a more sustainable building environment.

Over the past several decades, extensive research and development efforts have been undertaken to enhance the thermal efficiencies of architectural glazings. These studies have growing emphasis on the incorporation of heat-insulating materials along with glass components and demonstrated the performance of these measures for improving thermal or energy efficiency while reducing carbon dioxide emissions. Among the various innovative glazing systems, the utilization of electronic film coating, double glazings or insulation glazing, multipane, aerogel, hydrogel, switchable glazing, , laminated glazing, vacuum glazing and reflective glasses has also been researched extensively to promote the performance of thermal insulation. These glazing systems have significant potential for cutting down energy consumption associated with heating and cooling by efficiently managing both solar and thermal properties.

In these innovative materials, notably aerogel glazing has shown a reduction of about 21% in energy usage compared to traditional double glazing, with a payback period of around 4.4 years in colder climates, such as Oslo. Moreover, maintaining a desirable daylight amount through these glazing systems has the potential to decrease the need for artificial lighting, enhancing visual comfort. Researchers have examined the application of novel materials, including slimes and hydrogels, alongside phase change materials (PCMs) and aerogels, for potential applications in glazings. The latest studies indicate that these materials can considerably improve thermal efficiency, resulting in a 21.3% reduction for double-glazing units filled with slime and a 31.67% decrease in summer solar thermal gain for double-glazing units filled with hydrogels when evaluated against conventional double-glazing systems. Nonetheless, these glazing options are more expensive and have limited durability, increased complexity, and issues linked to their sustainability. This highlights a current need in the industry for materials that not only perform well but also comply with eco-friendly standards.

The challenge of overcoming the limitations associated with existing materials necessitates a consideration of three important factors, such as strength, aesthetic appeal, and environmental sustainability. In this context, transparent wood composites have emerged as a promising substitute, which can be used for a variety of purposes such as glazing, roofing systems, and decorative elements. In comparison to traditional glass, wood is naturally a thermal insulator with high mechanical strength, making it a suitable material for construction for thousands of years. Its vertically oriented cell lumens enable the transport of water and nutrients for photosynthesis and are made of cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin, which contribute to its opacity due to light absorption and microsized scattering. , The fabrication of transparent wood involves delignification and the infusion of a polymer matrix such as epoxy resin, polyester resin, poly methyl methacrylate (PMMA), and poly­(vinyl alcohol) (PVA), which aligns with the wood refractive index, increasing transparency and functionality in modern architecture. Transparent wood has a thermal conductivity 4 to 5 times lower than that of glass and exhibits superior resilience, as it does not shatter upon impact. It also optimizes daylight penetration in buildings, improving indoor environments and being a considerable alternative to standard glazing and synthetic polymers in creating energy-efficient architectural designs.

To achieve optimal thermal comfort and energy efficiency, windows should have a suitable solar heat gain coefficient (SHGC) and U values. This aspect suggests switchable glazing to provide a dynamic solution in which the light transmittance can be controlled by the ability to switch between transparent and translucent states when necessary. Such an outstanding feature not only improves daylighting but also is known to save a significant amount of heating and cooling loads on a building, eliminating the use of conventional shading devices. Notably, among the emerging “smart” solutions are electrochromic, polymer-dispersed liquid crystal, and suspended particle devices (SPDs), which enable the transmission of light to be controlled in detail and provide a continuous change between opaque and transparent states. The retrofitting of existing building curtain walls with PDLC resulted in a 22.4% reduction in energy consumption while improving daylight access and distribution within the interiors. Together, these technological achievements result in improving the level of comfort of the user and sustainable building design and in the necessity to introduce innovative glazing solutions in contemporary building design.

The study presents a new system of glazing that combines transparent wood with polymer dispersed liquid crystal technology. A PDLC layer is positioned between two thin layers of transparent wood with strategic positioning to maximize the efficiency of energy and minimize the effects on visible light transmittance. The integration between the layers can be achieved by optically clear adhesives. Figure presents a schematic diagram illustrating the arrangement of integrated polymer-dispersed liquid with transparent wood. In the absence of applied electrical voltage, the PDLC layer effectively blocks thermal radiation and serves as an efficient thermal barrier. Although traditional glass is commonly used in buildings, its high thermal conductivity and heat transmittance make it difficult to achieve energy efficiency. On the other hand, transparent wood has great potential, such as better thermal insulation, being a renewable and thus sustainable material, and efficient transmission of natural light. However, that does not allow dynamic adaptation to changing environmental conditions or user preferences. Therefore, transparent wood is not capable of providing on-demand privacy or actively regulating glare and solar heat gain, features that are becoming more significant in modern residential and commercial building systems. Moreover, this study evaluates the efficacy of the developed glazing system in daylighting, color rendering, and reduction in carbon emission with respect to a traditional 3 mm clear glass window. A comprehensive thermoeconomic evaluation was conducted based on a tested MATLAB model as well as simulations performed with EnergyPlus.

Stages in the Fabrication of Transparent Wood, Chemicals Used, and Layout of Office Building

Materials Used in This Study

Transparent wood samples were fabricated using veneer sections sized 50 mm × 50 mm × 1.2 mm from four distinct types. The lignin extraction process utilized various chemicals such as caustic soda (NaOH) pellets, acetic acid glacial, and sodium chlorite. Solvents such as deionized water (DI), acetone, and ethanol were employed during the experimental process. For resin infusion, a low-viscosity bisphenol A-type epoxy resin mixed in a 3:1 ratio with the hardener was used. Commercial PDLC films were acquired from the Indian market and utilized in this study without any modifications. Chemicals used in the fabrication of transparent wood and their chemical composition are given in Table . The PDLC films were incorporated between two layers of transparent wood for this study. PDLC glazing typically operates at an AC voltage of 20–100 V and requires less than 2 W/m2 (approximately 48 V AC) to switch from opaque to transparent and to maintain transparency. The response time is nearly instantaneous (∼0.05 s), and continuous power is required to sustain the transparent state. For the analysis, a power demand of 2 W/m2 was assumed, resulting in an estimated annual electricity cost of $1.4/m2,, which was included in the cost–benefit analysis.

Delignification and Fabrication of Transparent Wood

Four wood veneer samples were oven-dried for 24 h and then treated with 1 wt % sodium hydroxide at 70 °C for 10 h to remove extractives. Following this treatment, the specimens were rinsed with deionized water (DI) and subsequently bleached using sodium chlorite and acetic acid glacial at a pH of 4.6 until the wood color transitioned from brown to white. The veneers were then dehydrated with solvents, specifically ethanol and acetone, followed by pure acetone. Once the lignin had been removed, the veneers were vacuum-treated using a low-viscosity resin (3 to 1 proportion) for 30 min. The process of fabrication of transparent wood is shown in Figure . Solar Traits of Conventional and PITW Glazings

To determine the thermal performance of glazing, there is a need to analyze the solar characteristics. This study used a PerkinElmer Lambda 950 spectrophotometer to measure the distribution of the solar radiation that are transmitted through various glazing materials at different wavelengths, 0.3 to 2.5 μm, at a 2 nm interval. An integrating sphere was used to evaluate both the spectral transmission and reflection as a thorough evaluation of the performance characteristics of the PITW glazings.

This spectral information is essential for assessing the thermal and optical properties of glazings in accordance with European standards, with solar-reflective characteristics derived from eq . TSR=∑λ=300λ=2500Pλτ(λ)Δλ∑λ=300λ=2500PλΔλ,RSR=∑λ=300λ=2500Pλρ(λ)Δλ∑λ=300λ=2500PλΔλ 1

The symbols ρ­(λ), τ­(λ), and Pλ denote distributions of spectral profiles for light reflection, light transmission, and solar radiation in different wavelengths (λ) within the 0.3 to 2.5 μm solar spectrum. The quantitative interrelationship among these properties is given in eq , relating solar absorptance (A SR), solar transmission (T SR), and solar reflection (R SR). ASR=1−(TSR+RSR) 2

To measure the haze, the procedure involves four distinct steps. First, the light intensity is measured with no specimen present and with the white light standard in place, representing the reference transmittance (T 1). Second, the light intensity is measured with the sample positioned at the input port of the integrating sphere, while the white standard remains in place to determine the transmitted light through the sample (T 2). Third, the light intensity is measured without the sample and with a light trap replacing the white standard, which accounts for instrument-scattered light (T 3). Finally, the light intensity is measured with both the sample present and the light trap in place, capturing the light scattered by both the instrument and the specimen (T 4) (eq ). haze=TdTt=[T4T2−T3T1]×100% 3 where T t denotes the total transmittance and T d represents the transmittance of diffused light. Notably, the ratio of T 3/T 1 accounts for the error introduced by the experimental setup.

Results and Discussions

X-ray Diffraction Analysis

The X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was used to examine the crystal structure of cellulose in three different stages of the wood processing. The XRD patterns for natural wood (NW), bleached wood (BW), and transparent wood (TW) from four wood species are shown in Figure . The natural wood showed strong peaks of diffraction to 2θ = 16.5°, 22.4°, and 33.6°, which represent the (101), (002), and (040) planes of the cellulose crystal structure, respectively. Following the bleaching process, a pronounced increase in intensity was observed at 2θ = 22.4°, confirming the dominance of the main crystalline peak associated with cellulose I. The improvement in the crystallinity of cellulose is explained by the fact that amorphous cellulose is converted to the crystalline form of cellulose as a result of removing hemicellulose and lignin. Upon epoxy impregnation, it was found that a peak was present at 2θ = 18° (100) in the TW samples, which is due to the crystalline nature of the epoxy resin where the wood-resin interface occurs. All four wood species demonstrated comparable trends in their XRD patterns. However, the aforementioned findings suggest that both the molecular structure of cellulose and its crystalline structure remained intact throughout the fabrication process. This preservation of structural integrity indicates that the processing methods employed do not adversely affect cellulose at either the molecular or crystalline level.

Comparing the performance of various systems like GL–ON, PITW1, PITW2, PITW3, and PITW4 in their on and off states, these have significant reductions in the solar transmittance. Particularly in the off state, solar transmittance reduces by 39.77%, 53.47%, 63.64%, 68.18%, and 73.86%, respectively, when compared to the normal 3 mm clear glass, which is in the off condition. Similar trends are also observed in the on state, where it reduces by 34.09%, 45.45%, 54.55%, 62.5%, and 69.32%. This consistent decrease points out the possibility of PITW glazings in the successful reduction of the solar heat gain, which is a significant aspect in the energy-efficient design of buildings.

Besides the solar transmittance, the visible transmittance is also affected by the glazing designs. The off-state variants yielded visible transmittance of 57.9%, 41.3%, 30.68%, 26%, and 18.18%, and the on-state configurations exhibited 64%, 51%, 38.8%, 30%, and 22.72%. The implications of these reductions are significant, and they suggest that PITW glazings may limit natural light entry. Besides this, it is also observed that the density of wood is significantly correlated with the anisotropic character of the wood microstructure and the reduction of visible light transmittance, indicating that as wood density increases, light penetration diminishes.

Further, spectral transmittance analysis, as shown in Figure , shows a significant reduction of transmittance of PITW glazing across the visible spectrum, with even greater decreases at the infrared wavelengths as compared to conventional glazing materials. The fact that the solar transmittance of PDLC glazing decreases substantially, irrespective of the state, implies that there is a promising way of limiting the transmission of solar radiation, which is quite useful in hot climates.

In such environments, the solar heat gain coefficient (SHGC) is of greater importance compared to the thermal transmittance value of the glazing materials. The decreased g value reflects a lowered capability of solar heat penetration through the window, which is an important factor for reducing the cooling needs of buildings. PITW glazings have been found to have considerably lower g values than traditional materials, which therefore gives them advantage in reducing solar heat gains and thus saving energy.

PITW4–ON has the highest haze of 92.9% in the visible range, while PITW1–ON shows a considerably lower haze of 84.1% at 550 nm. In contrast, traditional clear materials like regular glass are highly transparent (over 90%) but have very low optical haze (less than 20%). The pronounced haze (translucency) of transparent wood can be attributed to its capacity for scattering and trapping incident light. Specifically, PITW1–ON displays the lowest haze, increasing from 78% at 400 nm to 91% at 800 nm. PITW2–ON exhibits a moderate increase, rising from 82% to 93% with increasing wavelength. Both PITW3–ON and PITW4–ON maintain consistently high haze levels, exceeding 90% all over the spectrum, with PITW4–ON reaching the highest values (94–96%). The observed increase in haze with the wavelength suggests greater scattering efficiency at longer wavelengths. Overall, the high haze levels illustrate the great potential for these glazing systems to be used in optoelectronics, glazing systems, and light-management applications where the transmission of diffused light is beneficial.

Assessment of the Thermal Properties for Existing and Proposed Glazing Options

The volumetric specific heat capacity (C p) and thermal conductivity (k) of different samples of transparent wood were measured. The thermal conductivities recorded were 0.21 W/(m K) for TW1 (balsa), 0.27 W/(m K) for TW2 (poplar), 0.30 W/(m K) for TW3 (pine), and 0.32 W/(m K) for TW4 (bamboo). It is also clear that TW1 and TW2 demonstrate lower thermal conductivities in the range of 0.21 to 0.28 W/(m K), and due to this, the conduction of heat is naturally restricted primarily because of lower density. In contrast, TW3 and TW4 , characterized by medium-density structures, exhibit improved heat transfer capabilities; however, their thermal conductivities remain significantly lower than that of glass, which is approximately 1 W/(m K). The presence of anisotropic channels can primarily explain this disparity within the transparent wood matrix, which impedes thermal conduction relative to denser materials like glass. Also, specific heat capacities for materials TW1, TW2, TW3, and TW4 have been quantified as 1550, 1610, 1695, and 1715 J/(kg K), respectively. The thermal conductivity, density, and specific heat capacity of PDLC film are 0.15 W/(m K), 1457 kg/m3, and 1000 J/(kg K), respectively.

Furthermore, the steady-state thermal transmittance (U value) measures the heat or thermal transmission rate as a result of a temperature gradient. Although this is an important parameter for evaluating the effectiveness of various glazing materials in providing insulation, the recorded U values for the PITW glazings PITW1, PITW2, PITW3, and PITW4 were found to be 5.37, 5.46, 5.49, and 5.51 W/(m2 K), respectively. Although the U values of PITW glazings (∼5.3–5.5 W/(m2 K)) may appear relatively high, they are, in fact, slightly lower than those of standard single clear glass glazing systems, which typically exhibit U values around 5.66 W/(m2 K) in similar conditions. This indicates that PITW samples offer a marginal improvement in thermal insulation compared to conventional glazing. It is important to note that the U value is only one of several parameters considered in evaluating thermal performance additional material properties, such as specific heat capacity and density, also play significant roles, as they affect the thermal mass and energy storage capabilities of the glazing system.

The findings showed that PITW glazing systems significantly lower the solar heat gain regardless of their operational states. Interestingly, the PITW4–OFF system recorded the highest decrease of 74.51% and 46.48% in Vellore and New Delhi, respectively. The ranked order of solar reductions between the various off-states PITW4–OFF, PITW3–OFF, PITW2–OFF, and PITW1–OFF indicates the efficiency of these systems to reduce heat gains.

Interestingly, the off-state performance of the PITW glazing was better than that of the on-state (transparent) configuration, which can be explained critically by the large increase in solar transmittance when the glazing is set to the on state. The data indicate that the large low values of solar transmittance in PITW glazing are effective in limiting the entry of solar radiation. As a result, in the off-state, the predominant inward influx of absorbed sunlight or solar radiation overshadows direct transmission, attributed to the high absorptance of PITW glazing. Conversely, in the on-state, an equivalent measure of solar irradiance is admitted into the office building interior due to direct transmittance.

Also, the analysis revealed that south- and north-oriented glazing had insignificant heat gains relative to other orientations, which have substantial implications for cooling-dominated climates such as Vellore and New Delhi. It is important to have large glazing areas at the south and north facade to reduce thermal gains and save on air-conditioning expenses. Alternatively, the west and east orientations had relatively equal solar heat fluxes, indicating an imperfect strategy in the building design where more glazing on these sides should be avoided. This suggestion is supported by the order of the glazing arrangements about heat gain losses on both the climates considered: South < North < Southeast < Southwest < Northeast < Northwest < East < West. This ranking presents not only the possibility of better thermal performance in terms of orientation but also the necessity of architects and constructors to take an approach in designing glazing systems.

Carbon Emission Mitigation of Glazings

Global greenhouse gas emissions (GHGEs) of buildings and construction continue increasing, and most nations are promoting the use of retrofitting and energy-efficiency initiatives as a remedy to global warming and carbon dioxide emissions. The construction industry accounts for about one-third of energy-associated carbon emissions and plays a major role in climate change and global warming. To counter this, many countries have adopted policies and programs that intend to ensure that energy usage and GHGE emissions in the building industry are minimized to foster the use of sustainable development practices. In India, the estimates of CO2 savings have been derived by using the emission factor associated with the consumption of electrical energy.

An annual average of 0.98 kg CO2 per kilowatt-hour factor of carbon dioxide emissions at the usage phase (including commercial and transmission losses) was used in estimating the potential reduction of carbon emissions. The mitigation of carbon dioxide emission was calculated by calculating the reduction in cooling loads, divided by the coefficient of performance (COP), and by the emission factor. The CO2 emission reductions (Figure ) of the PITW glazing system in the on and off states of the PITW glazing systems indicate that out of all glazing alternatives, the PITW4–OFF state glazing recorded the highest CO2 saving of 8.6 tCO2/year in Vellore and 5.32 tCO2/year in New Delhi. These significant energy savings can be explained by the fact that PITW4–OFF has an increased ability to help substantially lower building cooling loads.

Daylight Accessibility inside the Building

When developing glazing systems, it is important to consider both energy efficiency and visual performance. An important measure in this evaluation is the average daylight factor (ADTF), which measures the amount of daylight illuminance within a building. The contribution of different glazing setups, especially that of the PITW glazing type, was assessed by daylight simulations that were carried out in DesignBuilder. The simulations have been analyzed for clear sky conditions with a height of 0.75 m above the finished ground level.

The NBC (National Building Code of India) states that an office building must ensure that a minimum of 7 h of daylight is available during the service hours, which is normally between 8 a.m. and 5 p.m. To meet this requirement, the hottest day of the summer (April 21st) and the coldest day of the winter (December 21st) were simulated with ADTF simulations between 6 a.m. to 6 p.m. and 7 a.m. to 5 p.m., respectively.

Conclusions

This study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness, economic, and performance benefits of PITW glazing as an energy-saving alternative to traditional glazings. The analysis was conducted in a comprehensive manner in terms of solar–optical aspects, thermal efficiency, color rendering measures, cost savings, carbon dioxide emission, and repayment periods. The results highlight the benefits of PITW glazing over conventional systems, especially in the climates of Vellore and New Delhi. A thermal analysis was performed to assess the thermal properties and their influence on thermal gain with respect to PITW glazing on buildings. Daylight performance tests (average daylight factor) with the EnergyPlus simulation tool revealed that office buildings with this glazing had received adequate natural daylight (except PITW4) and reduced reliance on artificial lighting.

The crystalline phase and structural transformations of the transparent wood samples were characterized by X-ray diffraction.

The cooling cost savings of the off-state performance of PITW4 glazing (PITW4–OFF) were $10,568 with a payback period of 1.09 years in the case of Vellore and $8,433 with a payback period of 1.36 years in the case of New Delhi. The hierarchy of PITW glazing by the order of cost reductions in the off state is PITW4 > PITW3 > PITW2 > PITW1.

The cost savings per square meter linked to PITW glazings when in a nonoperational state were significantly higher compared to those identified for PITW glazing during operational conditions. This difference can be explained by the different thermal and solar properties that arise in the case of phase transitions.

PITW configurations have demonstrated substantial reductions in carbon dioxide emissions. Specifically, the PITW4 setup in the nonoperational state achieved the highest reported CO2 savings, with an annual reduction of 8.6 tons of carbon dioxide for Vellore and 5.32 tons of carbon dioxide for New Delhi, while other PITW glazing variants (both operational and nonoperational state) also contributed to meaningful CO2 emission reductions.

The repayment periods (RPPs) for PITW glazing systems in both operational and nonoperational states ranged between 1.09 and 2.04 years, remaining below 10 years, and are considerably shorter than the longevity of PDLC glazing, which is approximately 25 years.

Simulations of daylight revealed that the average daylight factor (ADTF) for the interior zones of buildings that use PITW glazing configurations consistently exceeded the minimum recommended levels for office buildings, except for PITW4 glazing, as per the Indian standards.

The significantly lower g values and varied solar characteristics of PITW glazing in both operational and nonoperational states resulted in notable reductions in solar heat gain and reductions in cost for the buildings.

The visible light transmittance of PITW glazings is less compared to that of conventional glazing systems. Also, the U values of PITW glazings did not show much difference, which show the limitations of glazing systems.

PITW4 has shown better performance in all aspects, but it did not maintain adequate daylight factor inside the building in both states. PITW3 glazing showed significant air-conditioning cost savings of $9,011 and $7,190 in Vellore and New Delhi, respectively, with favorable payback periods and adequate daylight access compared with all other PITW glazings studied.

The ranking of PITW glazings based on total operating costs in ascending order is as follows: PITW4–OFF > PITW4–ON > PITW3–OFF > PITW2–OFF > PITW3–ON > PITW2–ON > PITW1–OFF> PITW1–ON. However, when evaluated according to daylight performance characteristics, the ranking changes (highest to lowest) to PITW1–ON > PITW1–OFF > PITW2–ON > PITW2–OFF > PITW3–ON > PITW3–OFF > PITW4–ON > PITW4–OFF.

Further research should investigate advanced glazing architectures such as multilayer transparent wood structures incorporating functional interlayers to enhance thermal, optical, and mechanical performance. A systematic assessment of mechanical properties, long-term durability, large-area fabrication methods, and performance across diverse global climatic conditions is necessary to ensure the reliability and scalability for practical building applications. Moreover, future work should integrate climate-responsive and time-dependent control strategies for PDLC operation.
