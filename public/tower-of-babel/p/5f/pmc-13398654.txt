A Delphi Consensus on Clinical Features, Diagnosis, and Treatment of Chronic Rhinosinusitis With Nasal Polyps in the ASEAN Region
Authors: Baharudin Abdullah, Revadi Govindaraju, Salina Husain, Jin Keat Siow, Pongsakorn Tantilipikorn, Neville Wei Yang Teo, De Yun Wang, Deasi Anggraini, Tze Choong Charn, Chao‐Wu David Chin, Dichapong Kanjanawasee, Maung Maung Khaing, Jern‐Lin Leong, Luan Viet Tran, Harvinder Singh, Ing Ping Tang, Kim Thye Mark Thong, Gil M Vicente, Yoke Yeow Yap, Soma Subramaniam

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundChronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps (CRSwNP) impacts patients' concentration, ability to work, and overall quality of life. Current treatment options include topical and oral medications, and surgery. Recent evidence supports the efficacy of biologics in improving symptoms and quality of life. This study addresses the lack of consensus in the ASEAN region, providing guidance for otolaryngologists on appropriate CRSwNP management.MethodsA modified Delphi panel was conducted to gather expert opinion and establish consensus on the use of biologics and management of CRSwNP among otolaryngologists in the ASEAN region. Statements were developed based on a literature review and inputs from a Steering Committee of eight experts from Malaysia, Singapore, and Thailand. A three‐stage process was utilized for consensus generation: two online surveys for voting by at least 12 panelists from seven ASEAN countries, followed by moderated online Consensus Meetings with the Steering Committee.ResultsConsensus was achieved on 61/69 statements (88%). In Rounds 1 and 2, 37/67 statements (55%) and 13/28 statements (46%) achieved consensus, respectively. During the moderated Consensus Meetings, consensus was achieved on 12/16 statements (75%). Key aspects addressed include symptoms, comorbidities, diagnosis, and treatment pathways, with a focus on biologics. Eligibility criteria and considerations for biologic use were also defined.ConclusionsInsights from this Delphi consensus provide guidance on biologic use for managing CRSwNP in the ASEAN region. Future efforts, such as developing a regional guideline, continued medical education, and further research are essential for appropriate biologic use.Keywords: ASEAN, biologics, chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps, Type 2 inflammation

Introduction

Chronic rhinosinusitis (CRS) refers to the inflammation of the mucosa of the nose and paranasal sinuses. It is broadly differentiated into two phenotypes: CRS with nasal polyps (CRSwNP) and CRS without nasal polyps (CRSsNP) [1]. This phenotypic classification is based on endoscopic examination of the nasal cavity [2]. Different underlying pathophysiologic mechanisms and etiologic factors result in different forms of inflammation, presenting as two phenotypes of CRSwNP and CRSsNP [3]. Endotyping classifies CRS based on the underlying pathogenesis and type of inflammation. In CRSwNP, Type 2 inflammation is the most prominent, characterized by elevated levels of eosinophils and Type 2 cytokines (IL‐4, IL‐5, IL‐13) [4]. This classification offers the potential for targeted, pathophysiology‐driven therapies for CRSwNP [5]. As such, there has been a growing consensus among clinicians in Asia to shift from the current classification system of phenotypes toward endotyping CRS into eosinophilic and non‐eosinophilic CRS [6].

Globally, CRSwNP has a prevalence between 1% and 4% and accounts for 30% of CRS patients [7]. However, within the Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN) region, there is insufficient evidence to determine regional prevalence [8]. CRSwNP is diagnosed by the presence of subjective and objective evidence of chronic sinonasal inflammation [7]. Symptoms typically include nasal obstruction, anterior or posterior rhinorrhea, hyposmia/anosmia, and facial pressure/pain that lasts for more than 12 weeks [7]. CRSwNP presents with more severe symptoms compared to CRSsNP, significantly impacting patients' overall quality of life [9].

The clinical management of CRSwNP can be challenging as there is no defined best treatment for each patient [10]. The current standard of care includes intranasal corticosteroids (INCS), oral corticosteroids (OCS), and endoscopic sinus surgery (ESS). Despite receiving maximal medical therapy, patients may continue to experience persistent symptoms or polyp recurrence. Until recently, repeated ESS was the only reasonable option for management to control symptoms and optimize the delivery of topical medications. However, this approach carried an increasing risk of perioperative complications and a shorter duration of symptom control between surgeries [11].

Recent advancements in biological therapies targeting Type 2 immune effectors (e.g., IL‐4, IL‐13, IL‐5, and IgE) offer new therapeutic options for CRSwNP patients who fail to achieve adequate symptom control even with conventional therapies [12, 13]. Biologics are found to be well tolerated and effective, significantly reducing the disease burden, including nasal symptoms and quality of life [14]. The availability of biologics varies within the ASEAN region: two biologics—omalizumab and dupilumab—are approved in countries such as Malaysia and Thailand [15, 16] while all three biologics—omalizumab, dupilumab, and mepolizumab—are approved in Singapore [17, 18].

While there are international clinical guidelines from the European Position Paper on Rhinosinusitis and Nasal Polyps (EPOS) and the European Forum for Research and Education in Allergy and Airway Diseases (EUFOREA) on biologics use for CRSwNP [12, 19], there is a lack of clear consensus on the appropriate use of biologics in the ASEAN region. A modified Delphi panel study was therefore conducted to elicit expert opinion among otolaryngologists in the ASEAN region, with the aim to guide the development of a regional CRSwNP management guideline, focusing on biologic treatments.

Materials and Methods

This study was designed based on a modified Delphi method, which included a systematic and structured approach for identifying the consensus views of a group of experts in areas where the available evidence was limited or conflicting [20]. An iterative process was followed, involving two rounds of surveys completed independently by a panel of experts. After each round, a group of otolaryngologists reviewed the collated anonymized responses and revised survey statements, if needed, for re‐voting until consensus was reached based on pre‐defined criteria. Following the surveys, expert otolaryngologists attended the moderated Consensus Meetings to facilitate discussions on the survey results.

The modified Delphi panel study was conducted to generate consensus on research questions related to the management of CRSwNP and the use of biologics among otolaryngologists practicing in the ASEAN region. Phase 1: Identification and Recruitment of Experts (Steering Committee and Delphi Panelists)

The Steering Committee consisted of eight experienced otolaryngologists practicing in Singapore, Malaysia, and Thailand with at least 12 years of experience diagnosing, treating, and managing CRSwNP patients. Beyond clinical practice, the experts have extensive experience in clinical research and publications in major journals. Steering Committee members provided oversight and input on questionnaire development, Delphi panelists' recruitment, manuscript development, and attended the Consensus Meetings to discuss and share further insights.

The Delphi panel consisted of 16 otolaryngologists currently practicing across the ASEAN region, including Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, Philippines, Vietnam, Indonesia, Brunei, Cambodia, and Myanmar. Each panelist had at least 7 years of experience in managing CRSwNP patients, along with extensive clinical research and publication experience in CRSwNP treatment.

Phase 2: Development of Survey Statements

To understand the CRSwNP disease landscape, current treatment guidelines, use of biologics, and best practices within the ASEAN region for CRSwNP management, a targeted literature review (TLR) was conducted using literature search strategy (Supporting Information S1: Table 1). Additionally, to gain insight into the current clinical practice of CRSwNP management, 1‐to‐1 interviews with the Steering Committee were conducted. Following the outputs from the TLR (Supporting Information S1: Table 2) and Steering Committee interviews, any variances in clinical practices and remaining evidence gaps were used to guide the development of the Delphi panel statements for the Round 1 survey.

Survey statements addressed key themes, including symptoms, comorbidities, diagnosis, and the treatment landscape of CRSwNP. Panelists were asked to rate each statement according to a 9‐point Likert scale, which quantifies how strongly the participant agrees/disagrees with the statement (1–3: Disagree; 4–6: Neither agree nor disagree; 7–9: Agree). Panelists were encouraged to provide free‐text responses to improve clarity, modify statements, and provide a rationale for their responses. For multiple‐option questions, panelists selected any of the options they considered applicable. However, panelists selecting “None of the above (please provide rationale)” and “Others (please specify)” were required to provide additional input in free‐text form. For single‐choice statements, only one option could be selected.

Phase 3: Collection and Analysis of Survey Data

After providing consent to participate in the study, each Delphi panelist received a personalized email link to a password‐protected electronic survey platform. Panelists completed the first round of surveys in February 2024 and the second round of surveys in June 2024. The surveys were completed through a blinded process where the identities of panelists were blinded to other panelists and the Steering Committee. Responses for each statement were analyzed using a widely adopted method based on the “% of panelists who selected the option” [21]. Statements that achieved consensus were included in the final list of consensus statements, while those with moderate to nearing consensus were modified for subsequent rounds. All statements that did not achieve consensus were removed from subsequent rounds, except three statements that were modified in Round 2 according to panelists' suggestions to facilitate consensus. While all 16 panelists participated in both rounds of the survey, 4 members from Brunei, Cambodia, Thailand, and Malaysia withdrew before Round 2, resulting in 12 members participating in both rounds of the modified Delphi panel.

Phase 4: Moderated Consensus Meetings

After the survey rounds, two moderated virtual meetings were held to facilitate discussions among the Steering Committee members on the survey results. The consolidated results and analyses from Round 1 and 2 surveys were presented to the Steering Committee, enabling the members to review the outcomes, finalize statements, and vote on statements that did not achieve consensus in Round 2. Additionally, these discussions provided insights into areas in the current clinical practice within the ASEAN region that require further research. Re‐voting of the statements was conducted using real‐time, anonymous electronic polling functionalities.

Results and Discussion

A total of 69 statements were presented to the panelists and Steering Committee members during the survey rounds and Consensus Meetings. Overall, consensus was achieved on 61 statements (88%). Survey statements are summarized into the following four thematic areas: CRSwNP symptoms and comorbidities, diagnosis of CRSwNP, treatment landscape and clinical definitions of disease control, and future work to promote the appropriate use of biologics (Supporting Information S1: Tables 3–7, statements S1–S69).

CRSwNP Symptoms and Comorbidities

All statements in this thematic area achieved consensus and are summarized in Supporting Information S1: Table 3. These statements highlight that CRSwNP symptoms and comorbidities observed in the ASEAN region were consistent with those observed globally [22, 23, 24]. During the Consensus Meeting, it was agreed that CRSwNP should be characterized by patients having at least two of the following symptoms lasting ≥ 12 weeks: nasal obstruction, rhinorrhea, post‐nasal drip, hyposmia/anosmia, and facial pressure [S1–S2; Supporting Information]. Facial pain, though a possible symptom, is rarely observed and was therefore removed as a characteristic symptom of CRSwNP. According to the survey results, most panelists identified asthma and allergic rhinitis as comorbidities of CRSwNP [S6]. They also concurred that while symptoms and comorbidities are largely similar globally, select comorbidities such as cystic fibrosis and non‐erosive reflux disease (NERD) can vary geographically and by ethnicity [S4, S5, and S7] [22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27]. Lastly, in the Consensus Meeting, it was agreed that while eosinophilic CRSwNP is increasingly recognized in the ASEAN region, further research is needed to deepen our understanding of this condition [S8].

Diagnosis of CRSwNP

All but one statement achieved consensus in this thematic area and is summarized in Supporting Information S1: Table 4. All panelists agreed that diagnosis of CRSwNP requires clinical symptom assessment and nasal endoscopy. However, in settings with limited access to endoscopes, anterior rhinoscopy can be used for the diagnosis of Grade 3 and 4 polyps [S9, S10]. Panelists emphasized that this should be performed by specialists with experience in identifying nasal polyps. Conversely, panelists noted that computed tomography (CT) scan is not sufficient as a standalone diagnostic tool. However, its use was recommended before surgery to provide a surgical roadmap for symptomatic patients [S11–S14] or when secondary causes are suspected. Panelists also recommended endotyping through tissue or blood eosinophils to determine Type 2 inflammation for treatment guidance [S15]. However, there is a lack of consensus on the need to carry out serum total IgE and allergy tests [S17], partly due to their limited test availability among otolaryngologists in various healthcare settings.

Treatment Landscape and Clinical Definitions of Disease Control

The statements that achieved consensus in this thematic area are summarized in Supporting Information S1: Tables 5 and 6. Treatment options for CRSwNP include intranasal and oral medications, ESS, and more recently, biologics.

Intranasal and Oral Medications

Consensus was achieved on all statements regarding the use of intranasal and oral medications for CRSwNP treatment [S23–S27]. In the primary care settings, panelists agreed that the first‐line treatment for suspected CRSwNP includes INCS sprays, nasal saline irrigation, antihistamines, and occasionally, short‐term oral medications such as antibiotics [S23]. If symptoms persist, panelists concurred that patients should be referred to an otolaryngologist for prompt confirmatory diagnosis via nasal endoscopy and second‐line treatment [S24] [28]. The panelists also agreed that treatment may include INCS, OCS, and/or antibiotics, with ESS considered based on treatment evaluation results after 4–6 weeks [S25–S27].

Surgery

All panelists agreed that the removal of inflammatory load during ESS alleviates nasal obstruction and optimizes the delivery of topical medications, including INCS and topical steroid irrigation [S28] [29]. However, studies have indicated that up to 40% of patients may continue to experience persistent symptoms and require oral medications despite having ESS [30]. In cases where polyps recur following surgery, revision surgery or biologics may be recommended upon consideration. Revision surgery may be recommended if the previous surgery was inadequate in opening the affected sinuses, or to address other surgical sequelae [S29] [31].

Indication for Biologics Use

Due to limited literature and consensus in the clinical practice for biologics use, majority of the statements around the indications of biologics use did not achieve consensus among the panelists during survey rounds and were subsequently addressed during Consensus Meetings with the Steering Committee members. Additionally, extensive discussion focused on the terminology used to define the eligibility of biologics among patients with CRSwNP, as multiple terms with overlapping definitions such as “Difficult‐to‐Treat,” “Uncontrolled,” and “Severe” currently exist across various guidelines with no clear definition. The most common definitions include “Difficult‐to‐Treat” and “Uncontrolled” [4, 19, 31, 32, 33]. During the Consensus Meeting, it was agreed that biologics should be indicated for patients with “Difficult‐to‐Treat” Type 2 CRSwNP. This designation is defined as CRSwNP that fails to reach an acceptable level of control despite “adequate sinus surgery” and “appropriate medical therapy” [S19 and S34]. This differs from the EPOS and EUFOREA guidelines, given the varying advancements in biologics adoption and clinical management of CRSwNP between the ASEAN region and the global counterparts. The Steering Committee recommended using biologics after surgery for cost‐effective care and recommended considering biologics based on the most appropriate treatment option for each patient, rather than as a last resort after all other therapies have been exhausted.

Another terminology, “Severe” CRSwNP, is noted in international guidelines and consensus statements [4, 19, 31, 32]; however, this terminology was removed as an indicator for biologics use during the Consensus Meeting, given the significant overlap between the previously defined “Uncontrolled” and “Difficult‐to‐Treat.”

In the Consensus Meeting, it was agreed that the use of biologics can be discussed preoperatively as part of the long‐term management options of CRSwNP and offered only if appropriate medical therapy and adequate sinus surgery failed to control the disease in patients with Type 2 inflammation [S32].

These statements are in line with the EPOS/EUFOREA 2023 guidelines [S36 and S37] [12]. To avoid overprescription of biologics, patients refusing surgery were excluded as a predefined patient group eligible for biologics. For these patients, biologics would only be used off‐label and require self‐funding. Monitoring Treatment Effectiveness of Biologics

Given the high cost of biologics, all panelists agreed that treatment effectiveness should be monitored through both patient‐reported and physician‐reported outcomes [S47]. While the updated EPOS/EUFOREA 2023 guideline recommends an assessment after 6 months to avoid premature discontinuation in late responders, the panelists suggested an initial evaluation at 4 months [S48] [12]. This is a distinction between guidance for the ASEAN region, in comparison to the EUFOREA guidelines which suggested evaluation after 6 months based on Phase 3 studies with dupilumab and omalizumab where patients reported reductions in symptoms and NPS within 24 weeks [19]. The panelists agreed that effectiveness can be evaluated through nasal congestion scores, quality of life, smell tests, nasal polyp size, OCS use, and/or the impact of comorbidities. Monitoring Side Effects of Biologics

In addition to monitoring the treatment effectiveness of biologics, assessing side effects is crucial. While biologics are generally considered to be safe, common side effects associated with biologics use include headache, asthma exacerbation, nasopharyngitis, epistaxis, worsening nasal polyps, injection‐site reaction, and common cold [34].

High blood eosinophils and transient hypereosinophilia have been noted with the use of anti‐IL4/IL13 (dupilumab). Hence, experts recommended conducting frequent monitoring among patients with high blood eosinophils [S56–S58] [12]. For patients with > 500 cells/µL, it is recommended to measure baseline eosinophil count at 1 and 3 months following biologic initiation [12], and for patients with > 1500 cells/µL, every 2–4 weeks. When blood eosinophil levels exceed 3000 cells/µL for three consecutive months or exceed 5000 cells/µL, and no organ damage is identified, a short course of systemic corticosteroids or temporization of dose (to every 4 weeks) can be considered. If blood eosinophil levels exceed 5000 cells/µL for three consecutive months or if organ damage is identified, dupilumab should be discontinued. The recommendation is based on the current literature available [12, 35, 36] and further evidence is required to strengthen the recommendation.

Types of Biologics

Given the high cost of biologics, experts emphasized the importance of leveraging existing literature for optimal outcomes. The experts agreed that omalizumab (anti‐IgE) may be considered the first option for patients with high serum IgE, mepolizumab (anti‐IL‐5) for patients with eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis (EGPA) or significantly elevated blood eosinophils, and dupilumab (anti‐IL‐4, anti‐IL‐13) for all other cases due to its superior effects on olfactory function [S33]. These recommendations are consistent with current clinical consensus, literature and severe asthma guidelines for biologic use [27, 37, 38, 39].

Strengths and Limitations of the Study

The modified Delphi process is an established approach, and the current study achieved a high level of consensus in establishing regional guidelines for CRSwNP management, with a focus on biologics use. A TLR was conducted to inform statement generation, providing robustness to the study outputs. Another strength of this study is the representation of panelists across the ASEAN region, capturing diverse CRSwNP management approaches.

Conversely, one limitation of the study is that 25% of panelists lacked experience in prescribing biologics; hence, insights on biologics use are derived from their knowledge rather than direct clinical experience. Moreover, while the panel size of 12 is within the recommended range of 10–50 members, a larger group can provide a more comprehensive and holistic view of regional health systems [42, 43].

Conclusion

Effective management of CRSwNP, encompassing an appropriate diagnosis and treatment plan, is essential to achieve optimal clinical outcomes and provide cost‐effective care. While there is an established understanding of CRSwNP diagnosis, this study offers additional insights into areas with limited literature to guide appropriate biologics use, including indications, patient eligibility criteria and treatment monitoring. Although long‐term research on biologics is still needed, the modified Delphi consensus findings serve as a reference for otolaryngologists, facilitating the development of region‐specific guidelines for the management of CRSwNP.
