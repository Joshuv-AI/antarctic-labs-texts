Transcriptomics-proteomics analysis reveals the role of SiNRX1 in regulating drought stress in foxtail millet (Setaria italica L.)
Authors: Xiling Chang, Shuangxing Zhang, Jianfei Zhou, Jiandong Ren, Xiaoxing Wang, Tianqi Song, Yaning Bu, Xinpeng Lei, Yuxin Wang, Changyu Cao, Jishan Xiang, Xiaoke Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractNucleoredoxin (NRX), a member of the thioredoxin (TRX) superfamily, plays a crucial role in regulating plant growth and development, as well as in responses to both abiotic and biotic stresses. However, its contribution to drought resistance in foxtail millet remains unclear. In this study, the SiNRX1 of foxtail millet was knocked out using CRISPR/Cas9 system, and the drought resistance of sinrx1 mutants was identified at both the germination stage and the seedling stage. Moreover, through transcriptome sequencing and data-independent acquisition (DIA) quantitative proteomics determination of sinrx1 mutants and wild types (WT) at the seedling stage under drought and control conditions, the molecular mechanism of SiNRX1 regulating drought resistance was preliminarily analyzed. The results indicated that after 7 days of simulated drought treatment during the germination period, the germination rate, root length and bud length of the sinrx1 mutant decreased significantly in comparison with the WT. During the seedling stage under drought stress, the survival rate, chlorophyll content, proline content, peroxidase (POD) activity and catalase (CAT) activity of the sinrx1 mutant decreased markedly compared to those of the WT plants. However, the malondialdehyde (MDA) content of the sinrx1 mutant was significantly higher than that of the WT. This indicates that, during the germination and seedling stages, the drought resistance of the sinrx1 mutant decreased significantly compared to that of the WT. The transcriptome findings suggested that the 2253 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) (1179 up-regulated DEGs and 1074 down-regulated DEGs) are drought-responsive genes specifically influenced by SiNRX1. The 2253 DEGs were significantly enriched in pathways including phenylalanine, tyrosine and tryptophan biosynthesis, plant hormone signal transduction, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, and plant-pathogen interaction. The proteomic results suggests that the 155 differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) (127 up-regulated DEPs and 28 down-regulated DEPs) are drought-responsive proteins specifically influenced by SiNRX1. The 155 DEPs were significantly enriched in pathways including biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, brassinosteroid biosynthesis, metabolic pathways, and phenylpropanoid biosynthesis. Moreover, 2253 DEGs and 155 DEPs were significantly and jointly enriched in the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway. This study provides theoretical guidance for analyzing the drought resistance mechanisms of foxtail millet plants and for drought-resistance breeding.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12123-6.Keywords: Foxtail millet, Drought, SiNRX1, RNA-seq, Proteomics

Introduction

Global warming has intensified droughts in different regions worldwide to varying degrees, but drought, an abiotic stress, limits agricultural production and poses a threat to food security. Hence, it is particularly crucial to identify drought resistance genes, analyze the drought resistance mechanism of drought-tolerant crops and undertake drought resistance breeding [1–4]. When plants are exposed to drought stress, reactive oxygen species (ROS) can accumulate. This accumulation, in turn, damages biological macromolecules within plants and hinders their growth [5–7]. To prevent ROS imbalance in the body, plants have evolved an efficient antioxidant defense system to eliminate ROS. This allows plants to demonstrate a certain degree of resistance to abiotic stress [8–10]. The analysis of the function and pathway of the proteins involved in the antioxidant defense system has certain guiding significance for drought resistance breeding.

Thioredoxin (TRX), featuring a thiol-disulfide active site, participates in the oxidoreductase system and regulates ROS [11–15]. Based on the active sites, TRX is classified into typical TRX (types f, h, m, o, x, y, and z) and atypical TRX (such as NRX, CDSP32, NTRC, etc.) [3, 16–19]. The NADPH-dependent TRX reductase A (NTRA) and B (NTRB), as well as mitochondrial thioredoxin o1 (TRX o1) in Arabidopsis thaliana, are implicated in regulating the drought resistance via metabolic pathways [20]. In watermelon (Citrullus lanatus), ClTRX h2 interacts with ClMPKK5 to inhibit the phosphorylation of ClMPK3, thereby regulating the CBF-COR signaling pathway under low temperature stress and establishing a connection between TRX and the MAPK cascade signaling [21]. TRX h2 responds to aluminum stress and regulates leaf senescence through the ABA-mediated ROS pathway in Arabidopsis thaliana [22]. TRX h5 promotes the expression of immune genes induced by salicylic acid (SA) by regulating the conformational state of the SA response coactivator NPR1 [23, 24]. TRX regulates the levels of ROS or affects plant pathways by modulating the activity of related proteins, thereby regulating plant growth and responding to biological or abiotic stresses.

Nucleoredoxin (NRX), a member of the TRX superfamily, possesses multiple TRX folding and active sites, which might enable NRX to interact with various substrates and NRX has the redox capacity characteristic of TRX [24–26]. The independent TRX-like domain in ZmNRX possesses redox activity and plays a significant role in cell growth and differentiation [27]. Two types of NRX (AtNRX1 and AtNRX2) were identified in Arabidopsis, and the mutant of atnrx1 exhibited reduced pollen fertility. Moreover, AtNRX1 regulates catalase activity, mediates the antioxidant enzyme system, and safeguards plant cells against oxidative stress [25, 26]. AtNRX2 functions in the formation of Arabidopsis trichomes by mediating the jasminate (JA) hormone pathway [28]. In plants, NRX can interact with phosphofructokinase 1 (PFK1) to regulate the activity of PFK1, thereby maintaining a balance between glycolysis and the pentose phosphate pathway [29]. Overexpression of TaNRX1 in wheat improved the drought resistance of transgenic wheat [30]. SlNRX1 regulates heat tolerance by enhancing antioxidant capacity and inducing heat shock proteins (HSPs). Additionally, it promotes Pseudomonas syringae pv. Maculicola (Psm) pathogen infection by interfering with the salicylic acid (SA) hormone signaling pathway of the plant and increasing the accumulation of H2O2 content in tomato [31, 32]. Liu et al. (2025) identified ten NRX genes across three rice genomes and discovered that the expression of OsNRX genes is influenced by drought stress, exhibiting variability among rice varieties with differing levels of drought tolerance. This finding suggests that OsNRX may play a significant role in regulating drought tolerance in rice [33]. SiNRX1, SiNRX2 and SiNRX3 were identified in foxtail millet and the overexpression of SiNRX1 in Arabidopsis enhanced the drought and salt tolerance of transgenic Arabidopsis [3]. NRX mediates the redox system, the hormone transduction pathway and other pathways in vivo to regulate plant growth and development and respond to abiotic and biotic stress.

Foxtail millet (Setaria italica L.) serves as a C4 model crop, boasting a high-quality reference genome. This high-quality reference genome provides a dependable genomic resource for researching the stress resistance of foxtail millet. In addition, it can be utilized as an alternative genome for the research and improvement of switchgrass (Panicum virgatum) and related biofuel crops [34]. Moreover, foxtail millet serves as a major source of food and feed in certain parts of Asia and Africa. Nevertheless, due to environmental changes, drought has emerged as one of the main factors restricting the production of foxtail millet [35–37]. Studying the drought resistance regulation mechanism of foxtail millet is of great significance for analyzing the drought resistance mechanism of C4 crops and for drought resistance breeding [4, 38–40]. NRX is a member of the TRX superfamily, which possesses TRX enzyme activity and might maintain the redox balance in plants. The overexpression of SiNRX1 in Arabidopsis enhanced the tolerance of transgenic Arabidopsis plants to drought stress; however, the regulatory pattern of SiNRX1 in response to drought stress requires further investigation [3]. In this study, the CRISPR/Cas9 system was utilized to knockout the SiNRX1 gene in foxtail millet, and to determine the drought resistance of sinrx1 mutant lines at the germination and seedling stages. The regulation of SiNRX1 in response to drought stress was analyzed through RNA-seq and data-independent acquisition (DIA) quantitative proteomics of samples under normal treatment and drought stress. The objective of this research is to gain a profound understanding of the drought-resistance mechanisms in foxtail millet plants and offer theoretical guidance for drought-resistance breeding endeavors.

Materials and methods

Acquisition of mutants of sinrx1 from Foxtail millet

To verify the drought resistance of SiNRX1 in foxtail millet, the CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene editing system was employed to knockout the SiNRX1 gene in foxtail millet [41]. The sinrx1 mutant was generated through CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genetic transformation. The 20 bp sequence of single guide RNA (sgRNA) with the protospacer adjacent motif (PAM) on exon 2 of SiNRX1 was used as the target. The receptor of the sinrx1 mutant is Jingu 21 (Jin21; WT). Jingu 21 is a high-quality foxtail millet variety developed by the Economic Crops Research Institute of Shanxi Academy of Agricultural Sciences through continuous single-plant selection after irradiating the strain Jin Fen 52 with cobalt-60 gamma rays. This variety was approved by the Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Affairs of the People’s Republic of China in 1991 and has been widely planted as a main multigrain crop due to its wide adaptability and good stability in the arid and semi-arid regions of northwest China (including Shanxi, Shaanxi and other provinces) [42–45]. In addition, the DNA containing the target sequence was amplified from the leaves of the transgenic plants. After gel recovery and purification, the PCR products were transferred into the pEASY-T1 cloning vector (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) and transformed into DH5α competent cells (Tsingke, Beijing, China) through the heat shock method. Subsequently, the samples were sent to Beijing Tsingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd (https://www.tsingke.com.cn/) for sequencing and were compared with the SiNRX1 sequence to identify the editing type. The specific primers are shown in Table S1.

The growth conditions of the wild type (WT) and sinrx1 mutants of foxtail millet

Seeds of WT and the T4 generation of sinrx1 mutant were sterilized with 1% NaClO and placed in nutrient soil (soil: vermiculite = 3:1, v/v) and incubators (temperature 28 ℃/22 ℃, light 15 h/dark 9 h, humidity 60–70%, light intensity 17000 lx). The WT and sinrx1 mutants were planted in the same pot to control environmental effects. The control (CK) and drought treatment (DS) groups were established. Each group consists of 24 pots as one repetition, and there are three replicates for each experiment. When the foxtail millet reached the three-leaf stage, the control group (CK) was watered normally, while the drought treatment group (DS) ceased watering until the foxtail millet phenotype emerged, in order to identify the drought resistance of the WT and sinrx1 mutant seedlings at this stage. When the plants in the drought treatment group were exposed to drought stress for 8 days and the leaves of the plants presented a wilting state, the leaf materials of foxtail millet were collected and stored at −80 ℃ for RNA-seq analysis, proteome analysis and physiological parameter estimation.

Estimation of biochemical parameters

To estimate various biochemical parameters under drought stress, it was categorized into the germination stage and the seedling stage. According to the inspection of grain and oils-germination test of seeds (GB/T 5520 − 2011), the identification of simulated drought in foxtail millet at the germination stage was carried out. 100 seeds WT and 100 seeds of sinrx1 mutants were disinfected with 1% NaClO, followed by 20% PEG 6000 osmotic simulated drought treatment, with distilled water treatment being used as the control. Biological repetition was conducted three times. The germination rate, root length, and bud length were measured after 7 days of dark treatment at a temperature of 25 ℃/20 ℃. The biochemical parameters of the seedling stage were evaluated by culturing the WT and sinrx1 mutants under the drought treatment of three-leaf stage. Subsequently, the drought tolerance indexes of CK and DS were measured, including chlorophyll content, proline content, malonaldehyde (MDA) content, peroxidase (POD) activity and catalase (CAT) activity. The chlorophyll content of foxtail millet leaves in the same part was determined using the SPAD-502 chlorophyll analyzer, with 15 biological replicates and three technical replicates carried out. The contents of proline (G0111W), MDA (G0109W), POD (G0107W) and CAT (G0105W) were determined by using the microplate assay kit of Suzhou Gris Biological Company (http://www.geruisi-bio.com/). Three biological replicates and three technical replicates were performed.

RNA-seq and data analyses

The leaves of sinrx1 mutants (M-3) and Jingu 21 (WT; Jin21) plants were collected following normal watering and drought treatment, and their transcriptomes were analyzed through RNA-seq analysis. The samples from every three plants were combined into one independent sample, and the three independent samples were aggregated for analysis. The RNA library preparation and sequencing were accomplished by Novogene Co., Ltd (Beijing, China). Employ the Agilent 2100 bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA) to precisely determine the integrity and total quantity of RNA. Once the library test is passed, different libraries are combined in accordance with the requirements of effective concentration and target data volume, followed by Illumina sequencing, generating 150 bp paired end reads. The fundamental principle of sequencing is sequencing by synthesis. Clean reads were obtained by processing raw reads with fastp software and eliminating reads containing adapter, reads containing N and low-quality reads. Clean reads were then mapped to the foxtail millet reference genome (https://phytozome-next.jgi.doe.gov/info/Sitalica_v2_2) using HISAT2 v2.0.5 software [34, 46].

The new gene was predicted by StringTie (1.3.3b) and FPKM was calculated with featureCounts (1.5.0-p3) [47, 48]. The differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the two comparative combinations was analyzed using DESeq2 software (1.20.0) [49]. DESeq2 utilizes a negative binomial distribution model to analyze digital gene expression data for the identification of DEGs. The Benjamini-Hochberg method is applied to adjust the p-values (padj), thereby controlling the false discovery rate. |log2(FoldChange)| >1 & padj < 0.05 were used as the screening criteria. GO enrichment and KEGG enrichment analysis of DEGs were conducted by clusterProfiler (3.8.1) software [50, 51]. All normalized cluster analysis and correlation analysis were carried out using R (Version 3.5.0) software.

Reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) validation

To verify the reliability of the transcriptome, RT-qPCR was employed to verify the expression levels of 8 genes. Using the total RNA extraction kit (article number: ZP402) from Beijing Zoman Biotechnology Co., Ltd (http://zomanbio.com/), RNA was extracted from the leaves. The first strand cDNA was synthesized by reverse transcription using the EX RT kit (gDNA remover) (article number: ZR-108) from Beijing Zoman Biotechnology Co., Ltd. According to the manufacturer’s instructions, RT-qPCR was performed on the QuantStudio 3 real-time PCR system using 2×HQ SYBR qPCR Mix (article number: ZF502) of Beijing Zoman Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The expression levels of the genes were subsequently calculated based on −∆∆ct. All samples were processed with at least three biological replicates and three technical replicates, and SiActin-7 (ID: Seita.5G393000) was employed as an internal reference primer. The specific primers utilized above are presented in Table S1.

Proteome analysis

Leaves of sinrx1 mutants (M-3) and Jingu 21 (WT; Jin21) plants, which were normally watered and drought-treated were collected. The sampling method was based on transcriptome sample sampling, including three biological replicates, and proteomic analysis was conducted. Sample extraction, protein identification, and quantification were conducted by Novogene Co., Ltd (Beijing, China). The Vanquish™ Neo UHPLC -Astral liquid DIA method was employed to analyze the sample extract. Based on the foxtail millet protein database (https://phytozome-next.jgi.doe.gov/info/Sitalica_v2_2) [34], the raw files were searched and analyzed using the DIA-NN software. The quantitative protein results were statistically analyzed through t-test. To minimize false positives, differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) were identified using stringent thresholds: When the Fold Change >2.0 & p < 0.05, up-regulated proteins were screened out; when the Fold Change < 0.50 & p < 0.05, down-regulated proteins were screened out. All the DEPs were mapped to the Gene Ontology (GO) database (http://www.geneontology.org/) [51], and for each term, the number of proteins was computed. Then, a hypergeometric test was employed, comparing with the background of all proteins to identify the GO terms significantly enriched with DEPs. KEGG enrichment analysis was carried out with the KEGG pathway as the unit, and the hypergeometric test was employed to identify the pathways that were significantly enriched in the differential proteins in comparison with all the identified protein backgrounds. The Interproscan (IPR) software was used to obtain the domain annotation information of the DEPs based on the Pfam database, and the Cell-mPLOC 2.0 website (http://www.csbio.sjtu.edu.cn/bioinf/Cell-PLoc-2/) was employed for the annotation of subcellular localization information of DEPs [52].

Data statistics and analysis

Data analysis and charting were performed using Microsoft Excel 2019 software, and statistical analysis was conducted with IBM SPSS Statistics V27.0 (SPSS). The normality of the data was verified using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and the homogeneity of variance was confirmed by the Levene’s test. After meeting the assumptions of parametric tests, Duncan’s method of One-Way ANOVA was used to test for significant differences (p < 0.05 was indicated by letter marking method).

Result

sinrx1 mutant plants were generated using CRISPR/Cas9 system

To verify the drought resistance of SiNRX1 in foxtail millet, the CRISPR/Cas9 system was employed to knockout the SiNRX1. A total of 24 independent plants were obtained in the T0 generation. Insertion/deletion mutations were identified in four individual plants, resulting in an editing efficiency of 16.7%. Among these plants, four types of edits were detected in the sgRNA target region: an eight-base deletion, a one-base insertion, a ten-base deletion, and a one-base deletion (Fig. 1A). Previous research by Cheng et al. (2021) demonstrated a 40% editing efficiency when targeting the SiMTL gene in the foxtail millet variety Ci846 using the CRISPR/Cas9 system [53]. In contrast, our study observed a lower editing efficiency of 16.7% for the SiNRX1 gene. This discrepancy may be attributed to differences in the characteristics of the target genes or the genetic backgrounds of the recipient varieties.

In the study, the positive strains were propagated up to the T4 generation, resulting in four pure lines with distinct editing types, which were obtained in a greenhouse in Chifeng, China. Deletions of eight bases, insertion of one base, deletion of ten bases, and deletion of one base were respectively named mutant−1 (M-1), mutant−2 (M-2), mutant−3 (M-3) and mutant−4 (M-4). The expression of the SiNRX1 was detected by RT-qPCR and the results indicated that M-1, M-2, M-3 and M-4 were 0.32, 0.22, 0.25 and 0.34 times that of wild type (WT) plants, respectively (Fig. 1B). The knockout of SiNRX1 using the CRISPR/Cas9 system led to a reduction in the transcriptional expression of SiNRX1. Furthermore, M-1, M-2, and M-3 were selected for further study.

Response of the sinrx1 mutant and the WT to drought stress at the germination and seedling stages

To assess the drought resistance of sinrx1 mutant lines, the experiments were performed at germination and seedling stages. In the control group, the germination rates of the sinrx1 mutant lines M-2 (81.3%) and M-3 (78.7%) were markedly lower than that of WT (95.3%), while no significant difference in the germination rate was observed between M-1 (95.3%) and WT (95.3%). After 7 days of treatment with 20% PEG 6000, the germination rate of sinrx1 mutant lines M-1 (68.7%), M-2 (45.3%) and M-3 (60.7%) was significantly lower than that of WT (92.7%) plants (Fig. 1C). In the control group, the root lengths of M-2 (3.99 cm) and M-3 (4.08 cm) were significantly shorter than that of WT (5.01 cm), but no significant difference in root length was found between M-1 (4.71 cm) and WT (5.01 cm). After 7 days of treatment with 20% PEG 6000, the lengths of roots of M-1 (2.65 cm), M-2 (1.97 cm) and M-3 (1.99 cm) were significantly decreased compared with the WT (3.54 cm) plants (Fig. 1D). In the control group, the shoot lengths of M-2 (6.01 cm) and M-3 (5.96 cm) were significantly shorter than that of WT (7.96 cm), while no significant difference in shoot length was detected between M-1 (7.26 cm) and WT (7.96 cm). After 7 days of treatment with 20% PEG 6000, the lengths of shoots of M-1 (5.70 cm), M-2 (4.62 cm) and M-3 (5.20 cm) were also significantly decreased compared with the WT (6.95 cm) plants (Fig. 1E). In the control group, the root/shoot values of the WT and the three edited strains (M-1, M-2, and M-3) were 0.63, 0.65, 0.66, and 0.68, respectively (Fig. 1F). After 7 days of treatment with 20% PEG 6000, the root/shoot values of the WT and the three edited strains (M-1, M-2, and M-3) were 0.51, 0.47, 0.43, and 0.38, respectively (Fig. 1F). These values represent decreases of 19%, 28%, 35%, and 44% compared to those observed under normal conditions. After treatment with 20% PEG 6000 to simulate drought conditions, the germination rate, root length, and shoot length of the sinrx1 mutant lines M-1, M-2 and M-3 were significantly lower than those of WT (Fig. 1C and E). This suggests that the drought resistance of the sinrx1 mutants during the germination period is inferior to that of the WT.

The sinrx1 mutant wilted to a greater extent than the WT after 12 days of drought stress treatment, and the WT recovered more effectively than the sinrx1 mutant after 3 days of rewatering (Fig. 2A). Under drought stress, the survival rates of the sinrx1 mutant lines M-1 (88.9%), M-2 (73.6%) and M-3 (86.1%) were significantly lower than that of WT (95.8%) plants (Fig. 2B). Under normal treatment, the soil and plant analyzer development (SPAD) values of M-1 (15.24), M-2 (14.88), and M-3 (15.16) were significantly lower than that of WT (17.3) plants (Fig. 2C). Under drought stress, The SPAD values of M-1 (14.98), M-2 (14.27) and M-3 (14.81) were significantly lower than that of WT (16.50). This indicates that SiNRX1 might mediate the chlorophyll-related pathways in plants, resulting in a significantly lower chlorophyll content in sinrx1 mutant plants under normal conditions compared to WT plants. Under normal conditions, there were no significant differences in proline content, peroxidase (POD) activity, catalase (CAT) activity and malondialdehyde (MDA) content among the sinrx1 mutant lines. Under drought stress, the proline contents of M-1 (166.53 µg/g), M-2 (179.44 µg/g), and M-3 (162.66 µg/g) were significantly lower than that of the WT (210.39 µg/g); the POD activities of M-1 (204.31 U/g), M-2 (183.75 U/g) and M-3 (218.53 U/g) were significantly lower than that of the WT (298.53 U/g); the CAT activities of M-1 (565.25 U/g), M-2 (417.12 U/g), and M-3 (548.29 U/g) were significantly lower than that of the WT (635.31 U/g); the MDA contents of M-1 (40.36 nmol/g), M-2 (37.20 nmol/g), and M-3 (45.61 nmol/g) were significantly higher than that of the WT (27.88 nmol/g) (Fig. 2D-G). These results suggest that the drought resistance of sinrx1 mutant plants at the seedling stage is lower than that of the WT.

Analysis of the regulation of drought resistance mediated by SiNRX1 through RNA-seq

To analyze the responses of sinrx1 mutants and WT (Jin21) to drought, we carried out transcriptome sequencing on the leaves of WT and sinrx1 mutants (M-3) under control treatment (CK) and drought stress treatment (DS), namely Jin21_CK, M_CK, Jin21_DS and M_DS respectively, and performed three biological replicates, with a total of 12 samples. A total of 39,221,040 to 50,232,842 original reads were generated through RNA sequencing, and 38,401,500 to 48,978,330 clean reads were obtained by filtering the original data (Table S2). The mapping rate of each sample exceeded 90% when compared with the foxtail millet genome database, and this was utilized for the subsequent analysis (Table S2). A total of 1134 novel genes were assembled by StringTie software, and these novel genes were annotated through Pfam, SUPERFAMILY, GO, KEGG and other databases. The annotation revealed that some of the novel genes belonged to the WAKY, GARS, ERF and C3H families. Some of the novel genes were mainly enriched in the metabolic process (GO:0008152) during BP and in metabolic pathways (sita01100) in KEGG (Table S3).

Quantitative analysis of gene expression levels was carried out for all samples, and correlation coefficients of intra-group and inter-group samples were computed based on the FPKM values of all genes in each sample. The intra-group R2 was higher than 0.95, suggesting that the intra-group expression patterns were close (Fig. 3A). In principal component analysis (PCA), samples between groups were dispersed, while samples within groups were clustered together, indicating that the biological replication within groups was satisfactory (Fig. 3B). By adopting |log2 (FoldChange)| ≥ 1 & padj ≤ 0.05 as the screening criteria, 3910 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified in Jin21_DSvsJin21_CK and 5264 DEGs in M_DSvsM_CK. Additionally, 596 DEGs were found in M_CKvsJin21_CK (Fig. 3C). The sum of all the numbers within the circle in Venn’s diagram represents the total quantity of differential genes in the comparison combination, and the overlapping area indicates the number of common differential genes between the combinations. It is revealed that the three comparison groups have 85 common differential genes, including 8 ERF transcription factors, 5 C2H2 members, and 4 heat shock transcription factors (HSF) (Fig. 3D; Table S4). The distribution and difference of DEGs are presented through the volcano map (Fig. S1; Table S4).

The Venn diagram indicates that 2253 DEGs (1179 up-regulated DEGs and 1074 down-regulated DEGs) are exclusive to the M_DSvsM_CK group (Fig. 3D; Table S4). This suggests that the 2253 DEGs are drought-responsive genes specifically influenced by SiNRX1. Additionally, gene ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were performed on these 2253 DEGs. The GO enrichment analysis of 2253 DEGs reveals that, within the biological process (BP) category, these genes are primarily enriched in regulation of hormone levels (GO:0010817), hormone metabolic process (GO:0042445), amine metabolic process (GO:0009308), cellular carbohydrate biosynthetic process (GO:0034637), carbohydrate biosynthetic process (GO:0016051), aromatic amino acid family metabolic process (GO:0009072), cytokinin metabolic process (GO:0009690), cellular hormone metabolic process (GO:0034754) and disaccharide biosynthetic process (GO:0046351) (p < 0.01) (Fig. 4; Table S5). In the cell components (CC) category, 2253 DEGs are predominantly enriched in protein phosphatase type 2 A complex (GO:0000159), protein serine/threonine phosphatase complex (GO:0008287) and phosphatase complex (GO:1903293) (p < 0.05) (Fig. 4; Table S5). In the molecular function (MF) category, 2253 DEGs are predominantly enriched in DNA-binding transcription factor activity (GO:0003700), cytokinin dehydrogenase activity (GO:0019139), cellulose synthase activity (GO:0016759; GO:0016760), protein phosphatase regulator activity (GO:0019888; GO:0019208), carbohydrate binding (GO:0030246), oxidoreductase activity (GO:0016645), transferase activity (GO:0008194; GO:0016747; GO:0015020; GO:0035251; GO:0015018; GO:0008374) and hydrolase activity (GO:0016811) (p < 0.05) (Fig. 4; Table S5).

Discussion

NRX, a member of the TRX subfamily, has the electron transfer function and participates in plant growth and development, as well as responses to biotic and abiotic stresses [25, 27, 28, 30–32]. SiNRX1, SiNRX2 and SiNRX3 were identified in the genome of foxtail millet, among which SiNRX1 possesses a typical TRX domain (with WCPPC and WCGPC redox active sites). The overexpression of SiNRX1 in Arabidopsis enhanced the drought resistance of transgenic Arabidopsis thaliana [3]. However, it remains unclear whether SiNRX1 is implicated in regulating the drought resistance of foxtail millet. In this study, the CRISPR/Cas9 system was utilized to knock out SiNRX1 to generate sinrx1 mutant. The sinrx1 mutant exhibited reduced drought resistance during both the germination and seedling stages compared with the WT. Furthermore, under normal growth conditions during the seedling stage, there were no significant differences in superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity, peroxidase (POD) activity, proline content, and malondialdehyde (MDA) content between the sinrx1 mutant and the WT. However, under drought stress, the mutant exhibited significantly lower SOD activity, POD activity, and proline content compared to the WT, while its MDA content was significantly higher. These findings suggest that the sinrx1 mutant may experience oxidative damage due to the secondary accumulation of ROS under drought stress. SiNRX1 might regulate drought resistance by participating in the antioxidant system to eliminate ROS. Zhang et al. (2021) discovered that the wheat TaNRX1 gene plays a positive role in regulating drought tolerance in wheat. RNA-seq analysis revealed that the DEGs were predominantly enriched in pathways such as plant-pathogen interaction, plant hormone signal transduction, and phenylpropanoid biosynthesis [30]. In this study, knockout of the SiNRX1 gene in foxtail millet resulted in mutants exhibiting significantly decreased drought tolerance during the germination and seedling stages. Further analysis identified 2253 specific-mutant DEGs that were significantly enriched in phenylalanine, tyrosine and tryptophan biosynthesis, plant hormone signal transduction, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, plant-pathogen interaction. These findings are consistent with those reported by Zhang et al. (2021), suggesting that the NRX1 gene may regulate drought responses through conserved metabolic pathways across different species.

Seed development and germination are accompanied by extensive alterations in the redox state of seed storage proteins [54–58]. Studies have demonstrated that the NTR/TRX system and the mitochondrial TRX system exert significant functions in the germination of certain plant seeds, and the redox alterations mediated by TRX are implicated in regulating the germination and emergence of seeds [57–60]. In this study, the germination rates, root lengths, and shoot lengths of the sinrx1 mutant M-2 and M-3 lines in the control group were significantly lower than those of WT (Fig. 1). Therefore, SiNRX1 might play a certain role in regulating the seed germination process. However, the performance variations among different sinrx1 mutant strains might be ascribed to the different editing types of SiNRX1. SiNRX1 might regulate seed germination by adjusting the redox state of seed reserve proteins, but the specific mechanism still requires further exploration.

TRX is a redox enzyme that participates in key processes related to photosynthetic activity, redox homeostasis, and antioxidant mechanisms within chloroplasts of plants [14, 61]. NRX is a member of the TRX superfamily with redox sites, and NRX may have a similar function to TRX [15, 25]. In this study, the chlorophyll content of the sinrx1 mutant at the seedling stage under normal treatment and drought treatment was significantly lower than that of WT plants. Proteomic analysis indicated that, based on GO enrichment analysis, 155 DEPs were significantly enriched in the chlorophyll catabolic process. Among these, Seita.2G134400, a gene related to chlorophyll catabolic process, was identified as one of the 10 upregulated gene-protein pairs (DEGs and DEPs). This suggests that the upregulation of chlorophyll-degrading enzymes may result in a decline in chlorophyll content and a reduction in drought resistance in the sinrx1 mutant under drought treatment. Nevertheless, additional research is still required to elucidate the specific mechanism underlying the reduction in chlorophyll content in the sinrx1 mutant leaves under normal or drought treatment.

Phenylpropanoids are a group of chemicals originated from phenylalanine, constituting a structurally diverse set of secondary metabolites (such as flavonoids and lignin) which play a crucial role in responding to drought stresses [62–65]. The initial steps of the phenylpropanoid pathway are jointly referred to as the general phenylpropanoid pathway (GPP) [63, 66, 67]. The first enzymatic step involves the deamination of phenylalanine by phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL), yielding cinnamic acid. Subsequently, various secondary metabolite products are generated through other reactions [68–72]. Secondary metabolites primarily eliminate ROS to protect plants from lipid peroxidation. They also synthesize and transport metabolites and enzymes, stabilize cellular components, transmit signals, regulate related gene expression, and perform other defense functions [73, 74]. In this study, DEGs and DEPs were significantly and jointly enriched in the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway. Significantly, both the Seita.7G168800 and Seita.J003100 genes/proteins were detected in the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway that was jointly enriched by DEGs and DEPs. In addition, Seita.7G168800, exhibits a protein sequence similarity of 77% with that of Arabidopsis AT3G53260 (AtPAL2). Studies have indicated that AtPAL2 possesses the catalytic ability for L-phenylalanine deamination [75, 76], and Seita.7G168800 may have similar functions to AtPAL2. The Seita.J003100 gene, which encodes a secondary metabolite (peroxidase), shows downregulation at both the transcriptional and protein levels. Significantly, peroxidase (POD) activity, which is crucial for alleviating oxidative stress, was markedly reduced in the sinrx1 mutant compared with WT plants under drought conditions. This is in line with the alterations in the expression of peroxidase genes/proteins before and after drought stress, as indicated by the RNA-seq/proteomic results. Previous research has demonstrated that DTT-reduced TRXy1 can significantly enhance PAL enzyme activity [77]. The activation of PAL requires redox proteins [77]. In addition, TRXy specifically reduces a chloroplastic glutathione peroxidase [77, 78]. In this study, based on the relationship graphs depicting the expression levels of these 14 gene/protein pairs across the four sample groups, the results indicate that, at the protein level, the genes with the higher proportional weights are Seita.7G168800 and Seita.J003100. These findings indicate that SiNRX1 may activate its expression by specifically modulating the redox states of Seita.7G168800 and Seita.J003100, thereby regulating the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway and contributing to the regulation of drought resistance in foxtail millet.

TRX regulates the structure and biological functions of related proteins by catalyzing the formation and reduction of disulfide bonds in specific target proteins in different cellular compartments [79–81]. In recent years, with the rapid development of proteomics technology and the continuous improvement of protein labeling techniques and affinity chromatography, certain progress has been made in the identification of TRX target proteins [82]. Yoshida et al. (2013) detected 101 potential TRX target proteins in the mitochondria of Arabidopsis, spinach, and potato by using affinity chromatography in combination with proteomics techniques. These proteins are implicated in the respiratory chain, TCA cycle, photorespiration, amino acid metabolism, redox regulation system, antioxidant defense, etc. They include dehydrogenases, transferases, oxidoreductases (such as TRX), protein kinases, etc [83]. As a member of the TRX superfamily, NRX features a conserved CXXC redox-active site. This structural trait suggests that it may recognize similar target proteins in a way analogous to TRX. In this study, the results of transcriptome and proteome analyses indicate that 14 pairs of DEGs and DEPs had transcriptional levels corresponding to protein level expression. These 14 proteins included protein phosphatases, dehydrogenases, transferases, etc. It is highly probable that these 14 proteins contain the target proteins of SiNRX1. Among these, Seita.5G195800 (protein phosphatase 2 C; PP2C) and Seita.J003100 (peroxidase) exhibited downregulated expression of DEGs and DEPs. PP2C plays a central regulatory role in the abscisic acid (ABA) signaling pathway by modulating interactions among various proteins, such as SnRK2 kinase and ABA receptors [84]. The ABA signaling pathway is a crucial regulatory mechanism that enables plants to respond to drought stress. In this study, Seita.5G195800 (PP2C) exhibited downregulated expression of DEGs and DEPs. SiNRX1 may mediate the ABA signaling pathway by regulating the phosphorylation state of Seita.5G195800 (PP2C), thus influencing the drought response mechanism in foxtail millet. Moreover, previous research has indicated that the peroxiredoxin TPx and vacuolar peroxidase 23 may be potential target proteins of TRXy [77]. In this study, Seita.J003100 (peroxidase) exhibited downregulated expression of DEGs and DEPs. Seita.J003100 (peroxidase) could potentially be the target protein of SiNRX1. Moreover, Seita.5G346500 (SiNRX2) is upregulated at both the transcriptional and protein levels. Research has demonstrated that the subcellular localization information of SiNRX2 is consistent with that of SiNRX1 [3]. In addition, SiNRX2 shows certain responses under simulated drought stress, suggesting that SiNRX2 and SiNRX1 might act synergistically in the drought response of foxtail millet. Seita.7G168800 (PAL): The activation of PAL requires redox proteins [77]. Besides, Seita.6G105300 (UDP-glucose 6-dehydrogenase), a constituent of the oxidative pentose phosphate pathway, is upregulated at both the transcriptional and protein levels. Research has indicated that TRX is related to the activity of glucose 6-phosphate dehydrogenase [82, 85]. The findings suggest that Seita.5G195800 (PP2C), Seita.J003100 (peroxidase), Seita.5G346500 (SiNRX2), Seita.7G168800 (PAL) and Seita.6G105300 (UDP-glucose 6-dehydrogenase) could be a potential target protein of NRX. However, additional experiments such as co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) are required for verification. This study elucidates the molecular mechanism by which SiNRX1 participates in the drought response of foxtail millet through the regulation of the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway. It provides a novel theoretical basis for understanding the drought resistance mechanisms in C4 crops.

Conclusion

Knocking out SiNRX1 using CRISPR/Cas9 system decreased the drought resistance of foxtail millet during the germination and seedling stages. The findings from transcriptomics and proteomics suggest that the expression of related genes/proteins under drought stress is specifically influenced by SiNRX1. Moreover, these 2253 mutant-specific DEGs and 155 mutant-specific DEPs are significantly and collectively enriched in the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway. In addition, the Seita.7G168800 and Seita.J003100 genes/proteins were jointly enriched in the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway. The study is beneficial for comprehending the pathways by which NRX takes part in regulating the drought resistance of foxtail millet, and offers theoretical guidance for the breeding of drought-resistant foxtail millet varieties.
