Multidrug-Resistant Gram-Negative Bacteremia among Pediatric Oncology Patients with Neutropenic Fever: Associated Risk Factors and Mortality Outcomes
Authors: Zulfikar Ihyauddin, Bambang Ardianto, Ida Safitri Laksanawati
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChildren with cancers remain at a high risk of mortality due to infection, compounded by rising rates of multidrug-resistant Gram-negative bacteria (MDR-GNB), particularly in developing countries. This study aimed to determine the occurrence of bacteremia caused by MDR-GNB and identify the associated risk factors and the clinical outcomes.Materials and MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted using medical records from children with cancers aged 0-18 years at Dr. Sardjito Hospital, a tertiary care center in Indonesia, between 2020 and 2024. Patients with neutropenic fever and bacteremia were enrolled in the analysis. Multivariate logistic regression was used to analyze the associated factors of bacteremia caused by MDR-GNB, while Cox regression analysis was utilized to assess the outcomes of 30-days mortality rate.ResultsA total of 63 cases were included. MDR-GNB was identified in 28.6% (18/63) of patients, representing 52.9% (18/34) of all Gram-negative bacteremia. Most patients had hematologic malignancies (87.2%), with a median absolute neutrophil count (ANC) of 60 cells/mm3 (interquartile range: 0–320). The predominant pathogens were Escherichia coli (66.7%) and Klebsiella pneumoniae (16.7%). Multivariate analysis revealed that ANC <100 cells/mm3 (adjusted odds ratio [aOR], 5.8; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.1–30.1; P=0.038) and cytostatic administration within the preceding week (aOR, 6.0; 95% CI, 1.04–35.1; P=0.045) were significant risk factors for MDR-GNB bacteremia. Furthermore, MDR-GNB infection was associated with a significantly increased risk of septic shock (hazard ratio [HR], 4.4; 95% CI, 1.2–15.4; P=0.021) and 30-day mortality (HR, 9.5; 95% CI, 2.4–37.6; P=0.001).ConclusionChildren with cancers and neutropenic fever should be carefully evaluated for MDR-GNB infection, particularly if the ANC was low and cytostatics were administered within the past week. Comprehensive strategies are essential to mitigate the risks of mortality among pediatric patients with MDR-GNB.Keywords: Childhood cancers, Neutropenic fever, Gram-negative bacteria, Multidrug resistance, Mortality

Introduction

Neutropenic fever is a common complication encountered in childhood cancers. This condition arises as an adverse effect of cytostatics or as part of bone marrow suppression [1]. Neutropenic fever may serve as the first and sole symptom of infection in children with cancers [2]. Currently, it is estimated that one in every three patients receiving cytostatics or undergoing hematopoietic stem cell transplantation experiences neutropenic fever [3]. This condition poses a significant threat, with mortality rates reaching 21% in cases without adequate management [1]. Prior to the 1970s, neutropenic fever was among the leading causes of mortality in cancers. Despite the establishment of supportive care protocols, high mortality rates persist in developing countries, whereas declining rates toward 4% are reported in high-income countries [4].

Although an infectious agent can be identified in only approximately one-fifth of cases, Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria remain the most commonly identified pathogens in neutropenic fever, accounting for 76% of microbiologically documented infections. [5]. Differences in the microbiological aspect of neutropenic fever between developed and developing countries likely contribute to the disparities in infection-related mortality. In developed countries, Gram-positive bacteria are detected in the majority of cases, whereas Gram-negative bacteria are more prevalent in developing countries [6]. In Indonesia, Gram-negative bacteria remain the most commonly identified pathogens in children with neutropenic fever. Studies conducted in several major cities reported Gram-negative bacterial growth in 57-75% of cases [7–9]. Gram-negative bacteria infection is associated with a worse prognosis due to the higher probability of antibiotic resistance [1,6].

A new concern has emerged with the increasing incidence of infection caused by multidrug-resistant Gram-negative bacteria (MDR-GNB) which are defined as resistance to at least one antibiotic from three different classes [10–12]. The prevalence of MDR-GNB infection varies, with 33% reported in France and 44% in India [13,14]. MDR-GNB infection directly impacts patient management, particularly in the selection of empirical antibiotics, delayed administration of which beyond 48 hours may result in mortality rates as high as 50% [6]. Furthermore, inadequate early management of neutropenic fever increases the risk of intensive care unit admission and mortality, as observed in 83% of cases with MDR Pseudomonas aeruginosa infection [15].

Local data on microbiological profiles, along with the identification of the risk factors of MDR-GNB infection, are crucial to guide clinicians in selecting the broad-spectrum empirical antibiotics. Several studies reported the risk factors of MDR-GNB infection which include younger children with leukemia, prolonged hospitalization, hospital-acquired infection, prior MDR-GNB infection, and recent administration of antibiotics [16,17,18]. However, most of these studies were conducted in developed countries which limit their generalizability to the settings of inadequate resources. Indonesia also faces challenges with the high rates of irrational antibiotic prescription which in turn significantly accelerate the spread of antibiotic resistance [19]. As an initial step to improve the survival of children with neutropenic fever in Indonesia and other developing countries, we conducted a study that aimed to determine the occurrence of bacteremia caused by MDR-GNB among patients with microbiologically documented infection and identify the associated risk factors as well as the clinical outcomes of 30-days mortality rate.

Materials and Methods

1. Study design

This was an analytical observational study with a retrospective design conducted on patients aged 1 month to 18 years with cancers. These patients were admitted to Dr. Sardjito General Hospital, a tertiary care center affiliated to Universitas Gadjah Mada, with neutropenic fever between 2020 and 2024. In accordance with the hospital’s clinical practice guidelines, the definition of neutropenic fever in this study were adapted from the Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) guidelines and a study of Tanaka et al. [20,21]. Several modifications were implemented, including the use of a higher absolute neutrophil count (ANC) threshold to define neutropenia and the measurement of body temperature via the axillary route. Neutropenia was defined as ANC of less than 1.5 x 103 cells/mm3 or normal ANC predicted to decline toward less than 1.5 x 103 cells/mm3 within 48 hours following the administration of cytostatics and several non-cytostatics, such as rituximab, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, vancomycin, angiotensin converting enzyme inhibitors, and H2-receptor antagonists. A higher ANC threshold was adopted to reduce the risk of false-negative classification of invasive bacteremia and to minimize infection-related mortality in our setting. Additionally, body temperature was consistently measured from the axilla due to the limited feasibility of measuring core temperature in our clinical context. The axillary temperature of more than 37.7°C in a single measurement or a persistent temperature of 37.4–37.6°C for at least an hour was considered as fever.

Blood samples for culture were withdrawn using a sterile technique from a peripheral vein or each lumen of a central venous catheter. Each 1–3 mL of the blood samples was immediately inoculated into a BACT/ALERT® PF Plus culture bottle (bioMérieux, Inc., Durham, NC, USA) are used with and a BACT/ALERT® automated microbial detection system (bioMérieux, Inc., USA) was used. Bacterial identification and antibiotic susceptibility tests were performed using an VITEK®2 automated system (bioMérieux, Inc., USA). Patients with confirmed bacteremia were eligible for further analysis, whereas those with incomplete medical records related to the predictor and outcome variables were excluded. This study only included one febrile neutropenia episode per patient with microbiologically documented infection.

2. Ethics statement

The protocol of this study was reviewed and approved by the Medical and Health Research Ethics Committee of Universitas Gadjah Mada (Approval No. KE/FK/0355/EC/2024). The need for patient consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study.

3. Operational definition

Bacteremia caused by MDR-GNB was defined as presence of pathogenic Gram-negative bacterial growth, which was resistant to three or more antibiotic classes (penicillins, aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones, cephalosporins, monobactams, and carbapenems), in the blood [10]. The predictor variables in our study were type of cancers, previous episodes of neutropenic fever, cytostatic administration within a week, source of infection, low ANC (less than 100 cells/mm3), healthcare-associated infection, antibiotic administration within 30 days, high-intensity cytostatic administration, and previous episodes of MDR-GNB infection. Source of infection was determined based on clinician diagnosis, while healthcare-associated infection was defined as presence of fever with the onset of more than 48 hours after admission [17]. High-intensity cytostatics consisted of alkylating agents, anthracyclines, antimetabolites, hydroxyurea, and vinblastine [17,22].

Recent antibiotic administration might include prophylactic antibiotics or empirical antibiotics at 30 days preceding the onset of fever. According to our institutional clinical protocol for neutropenia, prophylactic antibiotics, even in the absence of signs of infection and/or neutropenia, are selectively administered to specific patient groups. These include patients with acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) in the maintenance phase (receiving three times a week of trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole) and patients with acute myeloid leukemia (AML) during the course of treatment (receiving daily ciprofloxacin). Patients presenting with febrile neutropenia who meet the established criteria are treated with empirical antibiotics based on their risk stratification. Patients at low risk for invasive bacterial infection who are not suitable for outpatient management receive intravenous ceftriaxone. In contrast, patients with a high-risk score are treated with intravenous ciprofloxacin or ceftazidime. Patients were followed from the time of neutropenic fever diagnosis and monitored for 30 days to assess the all-cause mortality rate within the follow-up period.

4. Statistical analysis

Collected data were analyzed using a STATA software (version 16.0, StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA). Bivariate analysis to assess for MDR-GNB associated risk factors was conducted using stepwise backward multiple logistic regression. Variables with P of less than 0.25 in univariate analysis as well as variables deemed as clinically important were enrolled in the multivariate analysis. The fitness of the model was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test, in which P of more than 0.05 indicated that the model was appropriate for use. The 30-days mortality outcome was assessed using Cox regression analysis.

Results

1. Patient characteristics

A total of 63 medical records of patients with neutropenic fever and bacteremia were reviewed, yielding 29 Gram-positive isolates (46.1%) and 34 Gram-negative isolates (53.9%). Among the Gram-negative isolates, 18 (52.9%) were identified as MDR-GNB. No patient with recurrent microbiologiclly documented infection was identified in our study. The characteristics of these patients are summarized in Table 1. Most of these patients were diagnosed with acute leukemia (87.2%) and presented with the respiratory complaints upon admission (38.1%), with the median age of 4 years (interquartile range [IQR], 2-9) and ANC of 60 cells/mm3 (IQR, 0-320). There was no significant difference on the characteristics between MDR-GNB and non-MDR-GNB groups, except for the median ANC (0 vs. 90 cells/mm3; P=0.002), source of infection (P=0.043), previous episodes of neutropenic fever (72% vs. 40%; P=0.021), and cytostatic administration within a week (89% vs. 58%; P=0.018). Mortality in the MDR-GNB group occurred in eight of 18 patients (44.4%) by day 30, while six patients died in the non-MDR-GNB groups (13.3%). Survival among participants with MDR-GNB showed a decline to 75% at 10 days of follow-up and further decreased to 55% at 30 days (Fig. 1). This differed with the non-MDR-GNB group where the survival function remained above 85% at 30 days of follow up period (P for the log rank test, 0.005).

2. Profile of MDR-GNB and pattern of antibiotics resistance

The number of cases with Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteremia were comparable (29 [46.1%] and 34 [53.9%] of 63 cases, respectively). Among Gram-positive isolates, the leading identified pathogens were Staphylococcus hominis, Corynebacterium sp, and S. aureus (data not shown), whereas the most commonly detected Gram-negative bacteria were Escherichia coli (38.2%), P. aeruginosa (17.6%), and Klebsiella pneumoniae (11.7%). Eighteen (52.9%) of 34 Gram-negative isolates, which included 66.7% of E. coli, 16.7% of K. pneumoniae, and 11.1% of P. aeruginosa isolates, were considered as MDR-GNB (Table 2). There was a case with Sphingomonas paucimobilis bacteremia. This isolate demonstrated resistance to the trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, cephalosporin, and monobactam classes of antibiotics and therefore, was classified as a MDR pathogen. Each of 12 E. coli isolates was resistant to ampicillin and ciprofloxacin, whereas each of three MDR K. pneumoniae isolates was resistant to the antibiotics from penicillin, fluoroquinolone, and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole classes (Supplementary Table 1).

3. Risk factors of bacteremia caused by MDR-GNB

Univariate analysis involving each of predictor variables revealed ANC less than 100 cells/mm3 (odds ratio [OR], 7.0; 95% confidence interval [95% CI], 1.43-34.0; P=0.016), history of experiencing neutropenic fever (OR, 3.90; 95% CI, 1.18-12.8; P=0.025), and cytostatic administration within a week (OR, 5.85; 95% CI, 1.19-28.5; P=0.029), as the potential risk factors. These variables were then included in the multivariate logistic regression analysis with the adjustment for sex and health-care associated infection. The final model showed ANC less than 100 cells/mm3 (adjusted OR [aOR], 5.76; 95% CI, 1.1-30.1; P=0.038) and cytostatic administration within a week (aOR, 6.04; 95% CI, 1.04-35.1; P=0.045; Table 3) as the risk factors for MDR-GNB bacteremia.

4. Outcomes associated with MDR-GNB

Associations were found between MDR-GNB and 30-days mortality rate and septic shock (Table 4). In univariate analysis, a significant association was found between MDR-GNB and mortality within 30 days with an HR of 4.0 (95% CI 1.40-11.7; P=0.011). This result was consistent with multivariate analysis with adjustment for variables considered to be confounders with an HR of 9.5 (95% CI 2.4-37.6; P=0.001). In addition, MDR-GNB infection was associated with an increased risk of septic shock (HR 4.2; 95% CI: 1.55–11.3; P=0.013). This association was consistent in the multivariate analysis between MDR-GNB and septic shock with an HR of 4.4 (95% CI: 1.2–15.4; P=0.021).

Discussion

Our study highlights the occurrence of bacteremia caused by MDR-GNB in children with neutropenic fever in a tertiary care center in Indonesia. Gram-negative bacteria still posed as the leading pathogens and 52% of which were considered as MDR-GNB. Significant pathogens causing Gram-negative bacteremia were E. coli, P. aeruginosa, and K. pneumoniae. The patterns of bacterial growth in our study were similar to those reported in Thailand, in which Gram-negative bacteria were responsible for 49% of bacteremia among children with neutropenic fever [23]. However, the prevalence of MDR-GNB in our study was higher than those described in other developing countries. Another study conducted in Brazil, focusing on children with neutropenic fever, disclosed that MDR-GNB infection accounted for 47% of all Gram-negative bacterial infection episodes. The most common MDR pathogens identified in their study were Acinetobacter baumanii, Stenotrophomonas maltophilia, Enterobacter sp, and K. pneumoniae [17].

It is known that there has been a shift in the bacterial growth patterns in children with neutropenic fever in developed countries, where Gram-positive bacteria have become the leading causes of infection. However, most developing countries continue to report Gram-negative bacteria as the predominant causes of neutropenic fever in children [1,3]. Gram-negative bacteria thrive rapidly in a humid environment and survive on dry surfaces for several months [24]. These are largely attributed to the higher rates of antibiotic resistance, particularly to the fluoroquinolone class [25]. Apart from these physical characteristics, the MDR bacteria acquire several enzymes capable of modifying or degrading antibiotics, such as aminoglycoside-modifying enzymes, extended-spectrum beta-lactamase, and carbapenemase. In addition, these bacteria also acquire several mutations that result in the synthesis of defective proteins and the altered bacterial targets of antibiotic action, such as topoisomerase, penicillin-binding proteins, and outer membrane porins [26]. These mechanisms may be associated with the irrational use of antibiotics, which is prevalent in Indonesia [19].

A previous episode of febrile neutropenia was associated with MDR-GNB in the univariate analysis but not in the multivariate analysis in our study. In contrast, recent antibiotic exposure, generally reported as a potential risk factor in previous studies, was not associated with MDR-GNB in our study [27]. We postulate that patients with a history of febrile neutropenia may be more likely to develop MDR-GNB due to prior documented clinical and/or microbiological infections, which could alter bacterial colonization patterns, leading to selective antibiotic pressure, and subsequently increase the risk of antimicrobial resistance [28]. Meanwhile, patients categorized as having recent antibiotic exposure in our study predominantly received antibiotics as prophylactic therapy rather than as empirical or definitive treatment for active infections. Therefore, in our cohort, a history of febrile neutropenia appears to be more strongly associated with MDR-GNB than recent antibiotic exposure.

Our study observed a significant association between cytostatic administration within the past week and the occurrence of bacteremia caused by MDR-GNB. Similar association has been demonstrated in previous studies in Egypt and India which reported the odds ratios of 1.5 and 2.4, respectively [12,29]. However, the study in Egypt focused on adults with neutropenic fever, whereas the study in India analyzed a limited number of cases with MDR-GNB infection. It has been widely accepted that cytostatic administration is associated with neutropenia which may persist for weeks [30]. In addition, cytostatics also induce mucosal damage which facilitates bacterial translocation to the bloodstream, making gut microbiota as a commonly isolated pathogen in Gram-negative bacteremia [31,32].

Our study also highlighted low level of ANC, defined by less than 100 cells/mm3, as one of the important risk factors for bacteremia caused by MDR-GNB. This finding is aligned with a study from Israel which reported that severe neutropenia (ANC <100 cell/mm3) was associated with MDR-GNB, particularly for P. aeruginosa and A. baumanii [33]. In patients with malignancies, febrile neutropenia generally poses as a form of acquired neutropenia, mediated by drug-induced neutropenia or as a direct consequence of the malignancy itself, such as in metastatic involvement of the bone marrow [1]. This finding also supported a hypothesis proposed by Papanicolas et al., that drug-induced neutropenia induce antibiotic resistance through the increasing bacterial mutagenesis [32].

The survival function of 30-days mortality in pediatric malignancy patients with bacteremia due to MDR-GNB was 55% at 30 days from the onset of fever. A study conducted in Thailand among pediatric patients with malignancies experiencing bacteremia reported a mortality rate of 20% in the group with antibiotic-resistant bacteremia within two weeks of follow-up [23]. However, this finding differs from the results reported in a study conducted in a developed country, such as Australia, with a mortality rate of 7.1% in the group of pediatric malignancy patients with antibiotic-resistant Gram-negative bacteremia within 30 days [22]. The discrepancy in mortality rates between developing and developed countries can be explained by several factors, including delays in the initiation of empirical antibiotic treatment, the high prevalence of malnutrition in developing countries, and the use of inappropriate risk stratification for febrile neutropenia [4].

This study demonstrated a significant association between MDR-GNB and mortality within 30 days of fever onset. In multivariate analysis adjusted for potentially confounding variables, the association between MDR-GNB bacteremia and mortality was significant, with mortality rates reaching nine times higher in the MDR-GNB group compared to non-MDR-GNB. Previous studies, in line with this finding, reported a higher proportion of mortality in pediatric oncology patients with and without antibiotic resistance. A study conducted in South India reported a 28-day mortality of 43.8% versus 4.2% for the carbapenem-resistant group compared to the carbapenem-sensitive group, while another study also conducted in India reported a difference in proportion of 25% vs. 7% for the antibiotic-resistant group in first-line therapy at the hospital (meropenem and amikacin) compared to the non-resistant group [34,35]. However, antibiotic resistance in both studies was limited to the antibiotic groups used at the study site and did not specifically refer to the MDR group.

This study reported that septic shock was associated with MDR-GNB. This suggests that septic shock may act as a mediator in the relationship between MDR-GNB and mortality. In general, the incidence of sepsis and mortality in patients with febrile neutropenia is higher in Gram-negative infections compared to Gram-positive infections [36]. A study conducted in Spain in general pediatric patients hospitalized in intensive care and comparing the incidence of MDR in both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, reported that the prevalence of MDR increased significantly according to the severity of the inflammatory response with reported proportions reaching 25.3% for non-inflammatory responses, 35.7% for sepsis, and 48.9% for septic shock, respectively (P<0.001).

Our findings provide insights for shaping future recommendation in the management of children with neutropenic fever, particularly those affected by MDR-GNB infection. Firstly, it is evident that Gram-negative bacteria play a significant role as pathogenic agents in neutropenic fever among children with cancers. This underscores the necessity for establishing more stringent protocols for infection control in cancer patients. A strict adherence to infection control policies is essential and should include several measures, such as rigorous hand hygiene, environmental cleaning and decontamination practices, and the isolation of patients known to be colonized or infected with MDR-GNB [37]. Secondly, identifying the source of infection is a critical priority. As such, obtaining blood cultures should be performed whenever clinically feasible to guide the targeted treatment [37,38]. This should be complemented by regular updates to the local data on antibiotic resistance patterns which enable the selection of appropriate empirical antibiotics [38]. Lastly, given the statistically significant association of cytostatic administration within seven days and low ANC level for the occurrence of MDR-GNB infection, recommendation should focus on developing safe discharge planning protocols. This could involve formulating a discharge checklist based on further studies to ensure that patients at risk of MDR-GNB infection are either not discharged prematurely or are closely monitored through a post-discharge program. Previous studies have suggested that achieving specific ANC may be a prerequisite for safely discharging patients with neutropenic fever [39].

Being conducted in a tertiary care center, our study population represent the Indonesian children with cancers. However, our findings should be interpreted with caution due to several limitations. Firstly, the sample size in this study was designed to estimate the incidence of MDR-GNB infection and was not specifically powered to evaluate the potential risk factors. Consequently, our study might lack a sufficient power to thoroughly investigate the risk factors of bacteremia. Secondly, healthcare-associated infection was defined solely according to the onset of fever, whereas several guidelines outline it further using other parameters, such as central line-associated bloodstream infection, catheter-associated urinary tract infection, surgical site infection, hospital-acquired pneumonia, ventilator-associated pneumonia, and Clostridium difficile infection. Thirdly, the differentiation between blood culture contamination and true infection could not be formally established, as blood cultures were obtained from only a single site. Nevertheless, a thorough assessment of each patient’s clinical condition, immunocompromised status, and supporting investigations, including blood culture result, was continuously performed to strengthen data interpretation and inference.

In conclusion, Gram-negative bacteria remain the predominant pathogens causing bacteremia in pediatric oncology patients with neutropenic fever, 52% of which were attributed to MDR-GNB infection and associated with fatal outcomes. Patients with cytostatic administration within the preceding week and low ANC level are at an increased risk of bacteremia caused by MDR-GNB. Therefore, comprehensive strategies are essential to mitigate the risks of mortality among pediatric patients with MDR-GNB.
