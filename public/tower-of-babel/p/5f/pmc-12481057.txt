Iatrogenic migration from a multiple filler injections for tear trough: A case report

Published: 2025

Introduction

The tear trough deformity, anatomically characterized as a depression extending from the medial canthus to the mid-pupillary line, worsens with age due to a multifactorial etiology. Key contributing factors include congenital maxillary hypoplasia, volumetric depletion and gravitational descent of the malar fat pad, a pronounced transition at the orbitomalar junction, and the mechanical restraint imposed by the orbicularis retaining ligament [[1], [2], [3]]. Current therapeutic approaches aim to restore periorbital volume and enhance aesthetic contouring through either surgical or minimally invasive techniques. Among these, hyaluronic acid (HA) filler injection has become a widely adopted modality in clinical practice due to its favorable safety profile, procedural simplicity, reversible nature, and minimal recovery time [4]. However, filler augmentation in this region remains technically challenging.

Although filler-related complications typically include swelling, bruising, localized pain, and inflammation, more severe issues such as nodule formation, migration, and even tissue necrosis have been reported [5,6]. Nevertheless, instances of iatrogenic material migration resulting from multiple filler injections in the same area are rarely documented. This case report has been reported in line with the SCARE checklist [7].

Case report

A 48-year-old female patient received bilateral tear trough augmentation in May 2023 using a hyaluronic acid-based dermal filler (Hearty [8]). The procedure was conducted with a 23-gauge, 50-mm blunt cannula. Preoperative evaluation showed symmetrical lower eyelids, with no evidence of preexisting infraorbital fat prolapse or dermatochalasis. Immediate post-procedure assessment indicated successful correction of the tear trough depressions. The patient requested this second HA treatment because the decade-old filler had migrated inferiorly, causing the tear-trough hollow to re-emerge despite no true loss of volume.

Several days following the procedure, the patient developed a soft, non-tender subcutaneous mass (20 × 10 mm) in the right lower eyelid area, resulting in noticeable asymmetry compared to the contralateral side. Initial clinical evaluation by the injector suggested possible filler misplacement, leading to attempts at enzymatic degradation using 60 units of hyaluronidase (150 IU/mL). Although complete resolution of the injected Hearty filler was confirmed bilaterally, the right-sided mass remained unresponsive to hyaluronidase treatment (Fig. 1).

A progressively developing prominent mass beneath the right lower eyelid following a “Hearty” injection. The patient's condition showed no improvement post-hyaluronidase treatment.

Two months later, the patient presented to our clinic with a persistent mass in the right lower eyelid. Despite multiple rounds of enzymatic therapy, the mass showed no signs of clinical improvement. Notably, physical examination revealed that the left infraorbital region exhibited greater subcutaneous mobility compared to the right side, with a more fluctuant texture. We advised the patient to undergo local MRI or ultrasound imaging for further evaluation.

The MRI showed an encapsulated fluid pocket on the left side (10 × 10 × 8 mm) between the periosteum and orbicularis oculi muscle, with homogeneous T2 hyperintensity and clear borders. In contrast, the encapsulation on the right side was markedly ruptured, resulting in a significantly larger lesion on the right compared to the smaller left-sided lesion (Fig. 2).

MRI imaging revealed a left-sided mass situated between the periosteal surface of the anterior wall of the maxillary sinus and the orbicularis oculi muscle. The mass contained relatively homogeneous fluid, was encapsulated, and had well-defined margins (indicated by the red arrow). The right-sided mass, located in a similar scan position, was notably smaller in size (marked by the yellow arrow). (For interpretation of the references to colour in this figure legend, the reader is referred to the web version of this article.)

During a detailed consultation, a critical piece of medical history emerged: the patient had undergone bilateral tear trough augmentation with polyacrylamide hydrogel (Amazingel) ten years prior. This information, combined with the MRI findings and the resistance to hyaluronidase treatment, raised suspicions of iatrogenic material dispersion. We hypothesized that aggressive cannula manipulation during a recent hyaluronic acid (HA) injection procedure had disrupted the encapsulated Amazingel deposits, leading to the iatrogenic migration of polyacrylamide hydrogel.

A diagnostic puncture aspiration of the left infraorbital region yielded 0.3 mL of homogeneous, yellow, non-adhesive material (Video 1). Based on these findings, the patient was advised to undergo transcutaneous lower blepharoplasty to achieve a definitive resolution of the condition.

During the initial dissection of the right lower eyelid, extensive polyacrylamide hydrogel deposits were identified within the preseptal space, diffused on the surface of the orbital septum (Fig. 3a). Further dissection along the infraorbital rim revealed residual hydrogel encapsulated within a thin fibrous capsule at the arcus marginalis junction (Fig. 3b).

Intraoperative findings. a: A substantial quantity of filler material was observed between the submuscular layer and the surface of the orbital septum in the right lower eyelid. b: Following the complete removal of the filler from between the submuscular layer and the orbital septum, further dissection toward the infraorbital tear trough uncovered the initial filler deposit, which was encased within a thin capsule.

Contrary to preoperative imaging findings, the submuscular plane between the orbicularis oculi muscle and the orbital septum in the left lower eyelid showed no evidence of filler material. However, upon blunt dissection directed toward the infraorbital groove, an intact capsule was discovered, containing more than 1.0 mL of viscous yellow material. Capsulotomy performed under direct visualization allowed for spontaneous extrusion of the filler material with gentle pressure (Video 2). At the six-month postoperative follow-up, the patient expressed high satisfaction with the surgical outcome (Fig. 4). No supplementary HA injection, fat grafting, or skin-resurfacing procedure was necessary, as en-bloc hydrogel removal alone produced a stable, symmetrical contour. Written informed consent was obtained from the patient and all procedures involving human participants were approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee, and in accordance with institutional and National Research Council Ethical Standards and the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki.

At the six-month postoperative follow-up, the therapeutic outcome was highly satisfactory.

Discussion

In this case, the patient underwent bilateral tear trough augmentation using Hearty. While the left side achieved satisfactory aesthetic results, the right side developed a rare delayed-onset complication involving filler migration. Initial treatment with hyaluronidase failed to resolve the issue in the right lower eyelid, suggesting the presence of a non-HA filler material. Similar “recalcitrant” eyelid swellings after permanent fillers have been reported by Lee et al. and Lin et al., who emphasise early imaging for rapid diagnosis [9,10]. This underscores the critical importance of early diagnostic imaging, such as MRI or high-resolution ultrasound, to minimize diagnostic delays and guide appropriate management.

Notably, the left infraorbital region exhibited greater tissue fluctuation compared to the right side, which raised suspicion of mechanical disruption of previously injected material. We hypothesize that the injection procedure, despite using a cannula needle, may have disrupted a “capsule” formed by filler injected approximately ten years prior. Finally, Careful inquiry and the puncture biopsy confirmed our suspicions. Surgical intervention was subsequently performed. Finite-element and mechanical models of needle–tissue interaction indicate that insertion can generate shear, friction, and cutting forces that may cause membrane rupture in soft tissues [11].

Polyacrylamide hydrogel (Amazingel), a non-biodegradable filler once widely used for breast and facial augmentation, was banned in China in 2006 due to its association with late-onset complications such as migration, chronic inflammation, and granuloma formation [12,13]. In this case, the patient had undergone bilateral tear trough augmentation with polyacrylamide hydrogel a decade prior. The patient recalled that the injection effect of the tear trough at that time was satisfactory, but as time went by, the tear trough gradually reappeared so she injected again. Thus, since Amazingel is a non-absorbable filler, the reappearance of the tear trough was only caused by the downward movement of filler, and there was no volume reduction, which was confirmed by MRI, puncture biopsy and surgery. Xiong et al. documented a comparable downward drift of orbital PAAG over 8–12 years [14].

We propose that cannula-induced trauma to preexisting filler deposits, combined with post-injection massage, generated bidirectional pressure gradients. These gradients facilitated both the immediate dispersion of the filler into and out of disrupted tissue compartments and the delayed mobilization of hydrogel through microfistulae, ultimately resulting in iatrogenic filler migration. Although cannula needles are generally regarded as safer than sharp needles for filler injections due to their lower risk of vascular injury, this case illustrates that aggressive manipulation—even with a cannula—can lead to severe complications [15]. Reviews indicate that a significant proportion of reported PAAG migrations were preceded by secondary aesthetic procedures, underscoring the relevance of our hypothesis [16].

Conclusion

Persistent eyelid swelling that fails to respond to hyaluronidase should raise suspicion for residual permanent filler. In our patient, early MRI confirmed encapsulated polyacrylamide hydrogel and guided en-bloc excision through a trans-cutaneous blepharoplasty. Complete removal alone restored symmetric contours—no secondary fillers or fat grafting were required. Recognising filler material properties, taking a meticulous history, and using gentle cannula technique are essential for preventing and definitively treating similar complications.

Video 1

A puncture examination was performed. The procedure extracted a filler substance resembling fat—uniformly yellow and without granular texture underneath the skin. Additionally, a sensation akin to puncturing a capsule was noted upon needle insertion into the filler. When changing the position of needle and massaging the skin surface gently, more yellow filler was out.

Video 2

During surgery on the left eye, no filler was found between the orbicularis oculi muscle and orbital septum. Upon reaching the infraorbital tear trough, a thin capsular similar to that on the right side was identified. When this capsular was opened, a significant amount of yellow filler drained out with gentle pressure.
