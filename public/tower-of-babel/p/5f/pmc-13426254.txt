Deactivating Emission in Azulene via Solvent-Induced (Anti)Aromaticity
Authors: Kiser Z. Colley, Shilpa Debnath, Ulrike Salzner, Satish Patil, Andrew J. Musser
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Anti-Kasha emission is a coveted feature in optoelectronics, with promise in areas such as imaging, sensing, and the production of white-light LEDs via dual-photon emission. Despite being a rare feature in organic systems, anti-Kasha emission is readily observed in the deceptively simple molecule azulene, which possesses two bright singlet states in the UV–visible spectrum and readily emits from the S2 state. With dominant anti-Kasha emission and decades of synthetic study, azulene is a perfect candidate for novel material fabrication; however, large gaps persist in understanding the photophysics of even the simple parent compound. These range from competing, experimentally unverified models of azulene reactivity and aromaticity to the unexplained deactivation of anti-Kasha emission in a host of azulene derivatives. Herein, we use fluorescence and transient absorption spectroscopies to explore the detailed solvent dependence of azulene photophysics. We discover a tunable reduction in the S2 lifetime via weak complexation with aromatic solvents. Interestingly, our results are independent of polarity, highlighting the primacy of peripherally delocalized 10-π Hückel aromaticity over zwitterionic character. When the dipolar character is enhanced through chemical functionalization, we observe even greater sensitivity to solvent aromaticity and more rapid quenching, revealing the role of conical intersections in azulenes with zwitterionic excited states. Overall, this work provides essential mechanistic insight into the photophysics of azulene and reveals a simple new approach to control the excited-state aromaticity and anti-Kasha emission in this class of materials.

Introduction

Typically, when molecules are excited to higher energy states, Sn, they nonradiatively decay to the lowest-lying state of similar multiplicity, S1. Known as Kasha’s rule, this result arises from overlapping potential energy surfaces, enabling rapid internal conversion. , The majority of emitting molecular systems follow this trend, however there are notable exceptions. Anti-Kasha emission occurs when a molecular system emits from an excited-state manifold above the lowest-lying state, and it is a highly coveted photophysical feature in optoelectronics, with applications ranging from sensing to creating multiphoton emitters. − Over the past century, many materials have been identified as possessing anti-Kasha emission, but the best-known of these remains azulene. Azulene is a deceptively small and simple hydrocarbon with only 18 atoms, yet despite its close similarity to naphthalene, it is distinct for its vibrant blue color and rich photophysics. Appreciable emission in azulene is only observed from the S2 state, , with numerous reports finding methods to tune the emission energy and intensity. , Decades of spectroscopic and theoretical investigations, along with a library of synthetic innovations, should make azulene a prime candidate for novel materials that exploit anti-Kasha emission. However, this prospect is held back by critical gaps in understanding the fundamental photophysics of the azulene building block.

Approaches to synthetic design of azulene-containing materials most often invoke a zwitterionic description of azulene, − as shown in Figure a. Given the prevalence of this model in organic chemistry textbooks, the use of the zwitterionic picture is not surprising. Yet despite its widespread usage, the validity of this model has been scrutinized by recent theoretical work. An alternative explanation for azulene photophysics – most importantly, the slow relaxation from S2 to S1 and rapid nonradiative decay from S1 – arises from examining the aromaticity of individual states. Azulene in its singlet ground state is aromatic according to Hückel’s [4n+2] π electron requirement; however, electron delocalization is confined to the periphery of the molecule. Notably, aromatic azulene shows a complete lack of delocalization across the transannular bond (10-π Hückel model in Figure a), in direct opposition to the character predicted by the zwitterionic model. However, as with many polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, this behavior can change quite substantially in the excited states. , In the second excited singlet state, S2, the near-equivalence of the HOMO–1 to LUMO and HOMO to LUMO+1 character allows this state to remain aromatic and thus maintain perimeter delocalization, as verified by theoretical comparisons to the first quintet state. The first singlet excited state, S1, of azulene is antiaromatic as predicted by Baird’s rule, which states ground-state aromatic systems are antiaromatic in their excited states. Although traditionally meant for triplets, this rule extends to singlets as well, and the antiaromaticity of S1 has been verified through theoretical comparisons with the first triplet state. Structurally, this change in aromaticity results in significant distortions, as azulene spatially separates its unpaired electrons between the 5-membered and 7-membered rings, leading to substantial electron delocalization across the transannular bond and greater freedom of nuclear motion. Such freedom of motion can typically enable ready access to conical intersections, − and it is thus implicated in ultrafast decay of S1. ,, These theoretical findings highlight a discrepancy with the common model of the nature of electronic states in azulene, but experimental work to distinguish these pictures is lacking.

Furthering the need for greater mechanistic insight are recent spectroscopic investigations of azulene behavior in coupled systems. Many studies have shown that the photophysical dynamics of azulene can be readily altered, typically by decreasing the excited-state lifetime and the efficiency of anti-Kasha emission, when it is coupled to other chromophores or additional azulenes. At high concentrations, azulene is highly susceptible to self-quenching of its anomalous anti-Kasha emission despite the absence of aggregates or excimers. Still stronger self-interaction, with attendant quenching of the S2 emission and reduction of its lifetime from ∼1 ns to picosecond scales, was observed in azulene tetramers. The idea that coupling between azulenes significantly perturbs the excited-state lifetime of S2 was further substantiated in a study of phenyl-substituted and dimerized azulenes. These structures exhibited prominent distortions of the S0 → S1 and S0 → S2 absorption bands, pointing to significant modification of the electronic structure, and these effects were accompanied by drastic reductions in the S2 lifetime. Nor are these studies the first to show that synthetic modifications to azulene can readily enable rapid decay of S2. Early investigations of the anti-Kasha emission of azulene explored derivatives with varying energy gaps between S1 and S2, revealing that most derivatives exhibit weak or no anti-Kasha emission. ,, The evidence suggests that it is much easier to diminish than to enhance this property. Many of these investigations provide similar explanations for the rapid decay in S2, attributing it to an enhancement of internal conversion between S1 and S2. , However, the reasons for this enhancement and the mechanism by which azulene interacts with neighboring chromophores remain largely unexplored.

Herein, we investigate the excited-state dynamics of azulene and cyanoazulene in a range of chemical environments using steady-state and time-resolved spectroscopy. We provide experimental evidence for the validity of the 10-π Hückel aromatic model while showing that the zwitterionic model is inconsistent with polarity-dependent studies. Notably, we identify a simple, novel approach to tune the excited-state aromaticity and thereby the excited-state evolution of azulene using aromatic solvents, leading to deactivation of anti-Kasha emission via enhanced internal conversion, as summarized by Figure c. We identify complexation as the only viable mechanism for azulene:solvent interactions, although the resulting complexes are found to be weak and readily broken at room temperature. Lastly, we investigate cyanoazulene under identical conditions to those of azulene to explore how its zwitterionic character would impact the underlying internal conversion dynamics. Although theoretical analysis indicates that cyanoazulene exhibits a marginally increased zwitterionic character in the ground state, we find that it readily responds to both solvent polarity and aromaticity. This behavior indicates stronger excited-state zwitterionic characteristics than those of unfunctionalized azulene. Notably, we find that with enhanced excited-state zwitterionic character, the S2 lifetime is significantly reduced, eliminating the emission that makes azulene promising for many material applications.

Results and Discussion

Solvent-Independent Spectral Shapes

We dissolved azulene in aromatic and nonaromatic solvents of varying polarity to a concentration of 100 μg/mL and characterized these solutions by steady-state UV–vis and fluorescence spectroscopy, as shown in Figure . The absorbance shows no significant energetic shifts in either the S0 → S1 (500–700 nm) or the S0 → S2 (320–360 nm) transitions over a wide range of solvents. Moreover, the spectral shapes in both bands are consistent across the solvent series, with minimally evident vibronic structure such as the peaks at 355, 630, and 710 nm. Only in cyclohexane can we resolve finer structure, though the intensity progression of the vibronic structure appears to remain the same as in other solvents. We note that we obtain similar spectra in cyclohexane solutions up to 10 mg/mL (Figure S1), indicating that the difference in broadening is not simply due to aggregation. Excitation of the S0 → S2 transition at 355 nm yielded fluorescence with the characteristic anti-Kasha emission at 370–475 nm. , Comparison of the fluorescence from cyclohexane, tetrahydrofuran, and acetonitrile solutions reveals no polarity-induced shift in the emission energy or spectral shape. This behavior is unexpected for a molecule with a significant static dipole; by comparison, the fluorescence of Thiele’s hydrocarbon, which has a similar zwitterionic resonance structure to azulene when photoexcited, can be tuned over a 0.6 eV range through solvent polarity. − Comparing with other well-known zwitterions, such as hexaphenylcalicen or Reichardt’s dye, which have dipole moments that range from 5 to 15 D, − azulene is also unique in having a relatively small dipole moment around 1 D. , The lack of polarity dependence observed above thus raises questions about the applicability of the zwitterionic model. Yet, the fluorescence exhibits a noteworthy dependence on the aromatic character of the solvent. Whereas cyclohexane, tetrahydrofuran, and acetonitrile solutions all yield intense, similarly strong anti-Kasha emission, it is already distinctly suppressed in toluene. It becomes almost undetectable under these conditions in mesitylene and anisole.

S2 Signatures Depend on Solvent Aromaticity

The solvent-dependent quenching of fluorescence points to the opening of new photophysical pathways. To investigate this effect, we employed transient absorption spectroscopy. In this pump–probe technique, we photoexcite the S0 → S2 transition with 345 nm pulses (200 fs, 125 nJ) and track changes in the transmission of a visible/near-infrared white-light probe (475–900 nm) as a function of time after excitation. To enhance signal levels, we perform these measurements on more concentrated solutions (1 mg/mL). We verify in Figures S2–S3 that, under these conditions, the population dynamics are independent of azulene concentration and laser power. Equivalent measurements exciting the S0 → S1 transition at 700 nm are presented in Figures S4–S5. Plotted as the normalized differential change in transmission ΔT/T, any resulting positive spectral features can be assigned to bleaching of the ground-state absorption or to stimulated emission from fluorescent excited states. Negative features arise from photoinduced absorption of excited states (regardless of their ability to fluoresce), providing fingerprints of the species that are populated during excited-state relaxation. Spectral slices following S2 excitation are plotted in Figure for our full solvent series. The key spectral features can be most cleanly identified in cyclohexane. The spectrum is dominated by a broad excited-state absorption, with a narrow peak at 565 nm and a broad featureless envelope extending from ∼650 nm to beyond the edge of our probe range at 915 nm. Anisotropy measurements discussed below (Figure ) demonstrate that these two features have unique transition dipole moments and thus correspond to unique electronic transitions. The uniform decay of the transient spectrum confirms that both bands arise from the same electronic state, and, in the absence of any distinct spectral features before 500 fs, we infer that this spectrum is the signature of excited S2. That is, we assign the transient absorption spectrum as a combination of short-wavelength S2 → Sn and long-wavelength S2 → Sm≠n excited-state absorption. We note that these bands share characteristics analogous to the ground-state absorption: a sharp, intense transition like S0 → S2, versus a markedly weaker, broad transition like S0 → S1. We return to this point below. The last notable feature in the spectra in Figure a is the series of sharp, positive peaks superimposed on the absorptive envelope and marked by asterisks. These features closely match the vibronic peaks of the S0 → S1 absorption and signify ground-state bleaching. As anticipated from our steady-state measurements in Figure , we observe no significant differences in the transient absorption spectral shapes or decay behavior of azulene among nonaromatic solvents of any polarity. The same features can be identified in tetrahydrofuran and acetonitrile, with only slight changes in peak broadening. That is, there is no evident impact of solvent polarity.

Turning to aromatic solvents, we observe a pronounced change in the spectral fingerprint, yet the same underlying structure remains. On the short-wavelength edge of our detection range, we find a new excited-state absorption or broadening of the S2 → Sn transition that decays on ultrafast time scales. This feature is most clearly seen in the earliest spectral cuts in mesitylene and anisole, where it is marked by blue stars in Figure e,f. Still, its contribution can additionally be identified in toluene. Moreover, over the entire decay range, we find that the long-wavelength S2 → Sm≠n band is markedly more intense relative to the peak at 565 nm, compared to the nonaromatic solvents in Figure a–c. In contrast to the nonaromatic series, there are evident differences in the behavior of azulene in the aromatic solvents, but these still do not track with solvent polarity. For instance, both toluene and mesitylene are minimally polar, but the blue-edge feature is markedly more prominent in mesitylene. We find that measurements in benzonitrile (Figure S7) yield spectral shapes and dynamics very similar to those in anisole, despite their distinct polarities. Taken together, the spectra in Figure demonstrate that the relaxation dynamics of azulene are governed by S2 regardless of the solvent environment. While the behavior of this state appears fully independent of solvent polarity, its nature evidently changes subtly in aromatic solvents, leading to a rebalancing of the S2 → Sn and S2 → Sm≠n spectral weights.

Relaxation Dynamics Depend on Solvent Aromaticity

The impact of the solvent environment is especially pronounced in the decay kinetics. In Figure , we collect kinetics at representative wavelengths across the S2 → Sn (500 nm, 560 nm) and S2 → Sm≠n (650 nm, 750 nm) bands. In nonaromatic solvents the kinetics are identical, across the spectrum and between solvents. To highlight this similarity, we globally fitted the nonaromatic dynamics with a biexponential function, yielding a primary population decay of 970 ps and a minor ∼5 ps decay component (kinetic fitting results are tabulated in Table S1). These lifetimes are consistent with prior work, in which the fast process has been attributed to vibrational relaxation within excited S2. The usual features of hot-state relaxation, such as dynamically shifting excited-state absorption bands, are absent. − However, this fast process does correlate with a subtle decrease in the relative intensity of the S2 → Sm≠n band from 600 to 900 nm (Figure S6), which may be consistent with the hot-state model.

The contrast between these invariant dynamics and the behavior in aromatic solvents is stark. Namely, the change in spectral shape discussed above is accompanied by a solvent-dependent reduction in the primary population decay lifetime. In toluene, this effect is weak, reducing the lifetime by only a third. However, in mesitylene and anisole, it is more pronounced, with the lifetime reduced roughly 10-fold relative to the nonaromatic systems in mesitylene and 40-fold in anisole. This reduction in lifetime explains the quenching of anti-Kasha emission in aromatic solvents reported in Figure b. We note that excitation of the S0 → S1 transition leads to ultrafast relaxation on the order of a few ps in all solvents, with no evident dependence on polarity or aromaticity (Figures S4–S5). The behavior of S2 points to significantly accelerated nonradiative decay (either S2 → S0 or S2 → S1) in aromatic solvents, which we return to below. In addition to an altered S2 lifetime, the kinetics clearly reveal the presence of new ultrafast spectral components. These include the previously discussed excited-state absorbance at the blue edge of the S2 → Sn band, as well as an ultrafast intensity buildup in the S2 → Sm≠n band. Best seen in anisole (Figure f), this effect manifests as a sharp decay on the order of a few hundred fs at 500 nm and a sharp rise at 750 nm. A global fit reveals that these two processes share a 290 fs lifetime, indicating a conversion between the two signals. The same analysis holds for mesitylene, toluene, and benzonitrile (Figure S7), although the intensity of this effect and the time-constant vary across solvents. The observed spectral behavior and relaxation dynamics are challenging to reconcile with the zwitterionic model of azulene, which would predict a pronounced dependence on solvent polarity rather than aromaticity. , These results suggest there is a more appropriate way to describe the aromaticity in this system.

Anisotropy Distinguishes Excited-State Absorption Bands

To tease out the nature of the S2 → Sn and S2 → Sm≠n excited-state absorbances, we turned to anisotropic transient absorption. The measurements presented to this point were acquired at magic-angle (54.7°) polarization between the pump and probe, allowing extraction of population dynamics unaffected by changes in the transition dipole moment orientation. Here, we perform the same measurements in cyclohexane solution with the pump and probe polarizations parallel or perpendicular. Measurement in these conditions enables us to compare the transition dipole moment orientation of the S2 → Sn and S2 → Sm≠n bands to that of the S0 → S2 band that we photoexcite. The transient absorption anisotropy can then be calculated as Anisotropy=(ΔT/T)∥−(ΔT/T)⊥(ΔT/T)∥+2(ΔT/T)⊥

With parallel polarization (Figure a), we observe a gradual buildup of the S2 → Sn peak at 575 nm (blue star), while the red-edge S2 → Sm≠n absorbance centered around 825 nm (orange star) strengthens into a notable peak compared to magic-angle measurements. Under perpendicular polarization, the absorbance peak at 825 nm is initially suppressed but then gradually increases, whereas the signal at 575 nm reaches its maximum intensity immediately upon photoexcitation. The evolution of these peaks could be attributed to the progression of the electronic state or the physical rotation of azulene, leading to certain excited-state absorbances aligning better or worse with the probe. Crucially, the difference in behavior between the S2 → Sn and S2 → Sm≠n bands provides evidence that the two have markedly different transition dipole moment orientations. Indeed, the calculated anisotropy signal in Figure c reveals that the two regions have opposite signs, pointing to nearly orthogonal transition dipoles for S2 → Sn and S2 → Sm≠n, which confirms that these transitions involve two different upper electronic states.

10-π Hückel Aromaticity Explains Solvent Dependence

Interestingly, TD-DFT calculations of the ground-state absorption transitions, performed in Orca at the B3P86-30%/TVP-f level of theory, − reveal that the transition dipole moments are likewise orthogonal for the S0 → S2 and S0 → S1 transitions (Figure d), aligning well with previous experimental determinations. , This trend of orthogonal transition dipole moments continues for transitions from S0 to still higher singlet states; therefore, we infer that transitions between excited states should likewise be short-axis or long-axis polarized, depending on the nature of the upper state. In transient absorption, this effect would result in excited-state absorption transitions that are oppositely polarized depending on whether the upper state is similar to S1 or S2. Considering our observed polarization dependence and the relative strengths and line widths of the transitions discussed above, we observe that there is a compelling analogy between the pairs of transitions originating from S0 (S0 → S2, S1) and S2 (S2 → Sn, Sm), in terms of the characteristics summarized in Figure e. We can thus infer that S2 and Sn share a similar nature, as do S1 and Sm≠n. This behavior, and indeed our full set of solvent-dependent observations, can be readily understood in terms of the 10-π Hückel aromaticity model of azulene. In that model, both S0 and S2 are aromatic, while S1 is antiaromatic. Transitions between aromatic states such as S0 → S2 are strong and well-defined, whereas transitions between states of differing extents of (anti)­aromaticity such as S0 → S1 are suppressed and broadened. Given S2 is aromatic, we can identify S2 → Sn as an aromatic–aromatic transition and S2 → Sm≠n as an aromatic-antiaromatic transition. The balance between the intensities of these two transitions evidently depends on solvent aromaticity (Figure ). Accordingly, we hypothesize that aromatic solvents alter the nature of S2 by tuning its aromaticity, with consequences for both spectral shapes and relaxation dynamics.

To further understand the nature of this interaction, we explore excited-state quenching in different solvent mixtures. In Figure a–c, we consider the impact of varying ratios of cyclohexane:anisole. The transient absorption spectral slices collected at 500 fs capture a progressive increase in the S2 → Sm≠n band and in the short-wavelength shoulder of the S2 → Sn band with increasing aromaticity of the environment. The appearance of this effect on ultrafast time scales, significantly faster than typical molecular motion time scales, suggests that this change arises from a static mechanism. Indeed, the enhanced short-wavelength excited-state absorption can be distinguished within the instrument-response function, ruling out molecular collisions as an explanation. The most likely mechanism to explain a spectral shape change on a sub-ps time scale that is specific to aromatic solvents is complexation between azulene and the solvent molecules, presumably through π-π stacking. On the other hand, the extracted kinetics at 560 nm (Figure b) reveal that a progressive reduction in the decay lifetime accompanies this spectral shape change. Converting these decays to a Stern–Volmer plot yields a linear fit, which is most often observed in diffusional quenching processes. , This result could suggest a mechanism in which azulene deactivates upon collision with anisole. Yet, because of the instantaneous spectral response, a purely diffusive mechanism cannot apply. We rationalize the linear Stern–Volmer dependence as evidence for dynamic, weak azulene:anisole complexation. In this picture, the percentage of aromatic solvent in the environment increases the probability of azulene being complexed at any given time, and the azulene:anisole interaction is sufficiently weak that the complex can be broken thermally at room temperature.

We observe similar behavior even in mixtures of two aromatic solvents: toluene and anisole (Figure d–f). As we add anisole to toluene, there is a gradual increase in the strength of the blue-edge excited-state absorbance and a decrease in the intensity of the S2 → Sm≠n band in the ultrafast response (<500 fs, Figure d). This observation aligns with our previous measurements (Figure ), which showed that anisole solutions exhibit a more prominent ultrafast component than toluene. However, the spectra in Figure e show that, on time scales beyond the ultrafast component (>2 ps), the aromatic effect is uniform regardless of the solvent balance. Once the fast relaxation is complete, there is no spectral difference between azulene in toluene or anisole. Nonetheless, the decay kinetics on longer time scales are highly sensitive to the anisole fraction, and we observe a gradual reduction in the lifetime from toluene to anisole (Figure f). A Stern–Volmer plot yields a similarly linear dependence (Figure S8), consistent with a statistical balance between azulene complexes with toluene and anisole. We thus find that, while aromatic solvents complex with azulene through π-π stacking, the interaction is weak enough that there is no preference between solvents detectable in the present conditions – again, consistent with an interaction strength below thermal energy at room temperature.

To connect this model of aromatic solvent complexation with the full set of solvent-dependent observations above, we recall further salient details of the 10-π Hückel aromaticity model of azulene. In the aromatic S0 and S2 states, the π-electron density is fully delocalized around the perimeter of the molecule, with no density along the transannular bond. The antiaromaticity of S1, on the other hand, is connected with significant electron density in the transannular bond. As a consequence, the aromatic states have relatively rigid geometries, whereas the antiaromatic S1 exhibits significant conformational flexibility. These differences can help explain the very slow S2 → S1 relaxation and the rapid S1 → S0 decay through a conical intersection. Any π-stacked azulene:solvent complex should induce an increase in transannular bond electron density, resulting in a decrease in the aromatic and an increase in the antiaromatic character of S2. Such alteration of excited state aromaticity through π-stacking has been theoretically predicted in the triplet state of naphthalene, but, to our knowledge, this is the first experimental evidence of this tunability. For azulene, this change in character manifests as an increase in the relative intensity of the aromatic to antiaromatic S2 → Sm≠n excited-state absorption. We can then interpret the ultrafast spectral evolution reported above – a decrease in the S2 → Sn band and increase in the S2 → Sm≠n band – as a further increase in the antiaromatic character of the S2 state in the azulene:solvent complex. This process naturally maps onto geometric rearrangement and likely reduction in the π-π distance, analogous to exciplex formation. Such an increase in the antiaromatic character of the S2 state would induce greater conformational flexibility and increase the coupling between S2 and S1. Thus, we expect that the rapid quenching of S2 and the loss of anti-Kasha emission are due to solvent-induced activation of S2 → S1 internal conversion, affording a rapid decay channel to the ground state. In this picture, differences in quenching kinetics between aromatic solvents would reflect differences in the degree to which the complex alters the transannular bond electron density, which should depend on both the solvent’s electronic and steric properties.

Stabilized Dipole in Cyanoazulene

Our solvent-dependent optical studies indicate that the photophysics of azulene are dominated by its aromatic/antiaromatic character rather than its zwitterionic character. To gain further insight into the interplay between these concepts, we sought to probe whether we could synthetically modulate the zwitterionic character using established procedures. , Cyanoazulene, as shown in Figure , places a cyanide group onto the 5-membered ring. In the zwitterionic model, this ring carries a negative charge to achieve aromaticity, so removing electron density with a withdrawing group should further stabilize this resonance structure and encourage charge separation. With this derivative, we thus expect to enhance the zwitterionic character. However, theoretical calculations of charge separation and population analysis, performed using Gaussian 16 and the NBO program at the B3P86-30%/6311G* − level of theory, reveal a surprising lack of zwitterionic character in the ground state (Figure S11). This result serves as yet another reminder that the zwitterionic model, although chemically intuitive, may not accurately describe azulenes. We measured the steady-state absorbance and fluorescence of cyanoazulene solutions in the same solvent series used above. Overall, the absorption is similar to that of unfunctionalized azulene, with a broad, low-intensity S0 → S1 transition from 500 to 750 nm and a brighter S0 → S2 transition from 320 to 360 nm. Although similar in shape and energy, there are subtle differences between cyanoazulene and azulene in both of these regions. First, the vibronic bands at 345 and 355 nm have different weights, with 355 nm dominating over 345 nm in cyanoazulene. The S0 → S1 transition is distinctively weaker relative to S0 → S2 in cyanoazulene than in azulene. Furthermore, the intensity of this band varies with solvent, becoming more intense with increasing polarity or aromaticity, in parallel with a reduction in the excited-state lifetime (see below). Notably, this effect extends to polar nonaromatic solvents. Fluorescence experiments on the S0 → S2 transition likewise highlight an important distinction: the anti-Kasha emission in cyanoazulene is directly polarity-dependent, diminishing in intensity as solvent polarity increases. Interestingly, the emission intensity drastically decreases when cyanoazulene is exposed to aromatic solvents, just as with azulene, suggesting that, although not a significant factor in the ground state, zwitterionic dipole-stabilization and aromatic complexation effects are both at play in the excited-state photophysics.

Accelerated S2 Relaxation in Cyanoazulene

Examining cyanoazulene with transient absorption reveals why the anti-Kasha emission is so heavily quenched. In cyclohexane (Figure a), we observe several features similar to those of azulene, namely a strong excited-state absorption near 600 nm (S2 → Sn), a broad excited-state absorption band at longer wavelengths (S2 → Sm≠n), and a superimposed ground-state bleach of S0 → S1, as marked by the asterisks. Though the same basic features can be identified, the overall spectral envelope has changed. Most notable is the blue-edge excited-state absorption, which is much broader and contains numerous peaks, compared to a single band at 575 nm in azulene, and the relatively stronger S2 → Sm≠n band seen in cyanoazulene. In nonaromatic polar solvents (Figure b,c), cyanoazulene exhibits a change in spectral shape similar to what we observed for azulene exposed to aromatic solvents in Figure . These effects include a stronger red-edge S2 → Sm≠n band relative to the peak at 600 nm and a new excited-state absorption feature at the short-wavelength edge of our probe range, as marked by the blue star. Just as in the aromatic versus nonaromatic solvent effects on azulene, this polarity-induced change in spectral shape in cyanoazulene behaves as a binary. Comparison of the tetrahydrofuran and acetonitrile data shows that the line shape does not change with increased polarity.

When we instead dissolve cyanoazulene in aromatic solvents, we observe qualitatively similar spectra to the polar nonaromatic solvents, with one crucial difference: the S0 → S1 ground-state bleach signal is distinctly stronger, to the extent that it becomes a ΔT/T > 0 signal on longer time scales in mesitylene and anisole (orange stars in Figure e,f). This enhanced bleach is consistent with the relatively stronger S0 → S1 absorption seen in aromatic solvents in Figure . The unique, positive bleaching signal we observe on the longest time scales is consistent with the formation of a vibrationally hot ground state, which is itself a consequence of the extremely rapid population decay in aromatic solvents discussed below. , Similar hot ground-state features are observed following direct excitation of S0 → S1 in azulene (Figure S4) and cyanoazulene (Figure S9). Overall, although cyanoazulene demonstrates a similar aromatic solvent dependence as unfunctionalized azulene, it differs notably in its strong response to polarity. Having been ruled out in the ground state, this new sensitivity points to a change in the excited state. As a functional group, cyanide can stabilize excess negative charge when the charge can be delocalized into the cyanide LUMO, which may only be possible in the excited state of azulene. From this reasoning and the evidence of faster decay and polarity dependence, we infer that we have induced stronger zwitterionic character in S2.

In Figure , we present transient absorption decay kinetics extracted at 490, 610, and 835 nm to capture how the evolution of the spectral features described above changes in polar and aromatic solvents. Similarly to azulene (Figure a), the cyanoazulene dynamics in cyclohexane (Figure a) were best described with a biexponential function, capturing a minor fast solvation or vibrational cooling process (∼2.4 ps) and a primary population decay lifetime of 96 ps (time-constants are tabulated in Table S2). That is, in nonpolar, nonaromatic solvents, cyanoazulene already exhibits a 10-fold shorter S2 lifetime than azulene. This observation highlights an interesting impact of the enhanced excited-state zwitterionic character on azulene photophysics: rapid decay. This impact is still more pronounced in polar solvents, with the S2 decay lifetime reduced to 44 ps in tetrahydrofuran and 34 ps in acetonitrile (Figure b,c). Notably, the dynamics in these polar solvents are well described by a monoexponential decay function. The effect of accelerated S2 decay may result from increased molecular flexibility, as azulene in its zwitterionic state can fold around its transannular bond. Previous theoretical work has implicated this folding motion in the ultrafast decay of the S1 state via a conical intersection, , and enabling this motion in S2 in cyanoazulene may result in a similar effect. In aromatic solvents, we observe still further reduction in the S2 lifetime of cyanoazulene, but the impact is substantially stronger than for azulene. For instance, the S2 lifetime of azulene in toluene is 1.3-fold shorter than in cyclohexane (Figure ). The same comparison for cyanoazulene reveals a 12-fold reduction, down to 8 ps. Still further reduction of the lifetime is observed in mesitylene and anisole, with the latter exhibiting 50-fold faster decay than in cyclohexane (Figure f). Indeed, at a little over 2 ps, the S2 state of cyanoazulene in anisole has a lifetime similar to that of the S1 state (Figure S10), suggesting a conical intersection as the most likely source of rapid deactivation. This rapid nonradiative decay from such a high-energy state deposits a large amount of energy into ground-state nuclear motion, resulting in prominent hot ground-state features that persist for multiple ps (most easily discerned at 610 nm in anisole). ,

By comparing the solvent-dependent behavior of cyanoazulene with that of azulene, we identify three salient effects of stabilizing the zwitterionic character in the excited states. In all conditions, the S2 lifetime is markedly reduced, which can be attributed to a similar mechanism of conformational flexibility as invoked for azulene’s interactions with aromatic solvents. Zwitterionic character imparts a direct sensitivity to solvent polarity in the intensity and lifetime of the anti-Kasha emission from S2, an effect absent in our data on azulene. Most intriguingly, the presence of stabilized zwitterionic character additionally appears to enhance cyanoazulene’s sensitivity to solvent aromaticity, leading to a much greater quenching effect in toluene, mesitylene, and anisole than in azulene. This observation highlights that both models described above can apply and, in fact, reinforce each other. For instance, we anticipate that the electron-deficient seven-membered ring in the zwitterionic structure will engage more readily in π-stacking with aromatic solvents, leading to stronger complexation and greater stabilization of antiaromatic character. In this regard, it is notable that even when the excited-state zwitterionic character is deliberately stabilized in cyanoazulene, the impact of solvent aromaticity outweighs that of solvent polarity. We thus infer that even in this case, the more important descriptor of the electronic states follows the 10-π Hückel aromaticity model.

Conclusions

Taken together, our results indicate that the photophysics of azulene are inconsistent with a zwitterionic model, but are consistent with the 10-π Hückel aromaticity model. The full collection of observations – solvent polarity independence, anisotropy, and sensitivity of the excited-state spectral shapes and relaxation dynamics to solvent aromaticity – is readily rationalized by considering the electronic structure of azulene in terms of alternating aromatic and antiaromatic states. Weak complexation with aromatic solvents through π-π interactions is sufficient to perturb the aromatic S2 state and induce greatly accelerated relaxation to S1 and, from there, to the ground electronic state. Stabilizing the excited-state zwitterionic character in a polar derivative, cyanoazulene, provides a clear point of contrast. Cyanoazulene demonstrates distinct solvent polarity dependence and, importantly, reveals that just enhancing zwitterionic character in its excited states enables a universally faster decay pathway in azulenes that suppresses the coveted anti-Kasha emission. We hypothesize this effect extends to other substitutions that enhance zwitterionic character, such as adding electron-withdrawing groups at the 1 or 3 position and donating groups to the seven-membered ring. Indeed, previous studies have shown that these substitutions lead to dramatically lower fluorescence quantum yields, which we attribute to enhanced zwitterionic character inducing antiaromaticity in S2. Thus, where efficient anti-Kasha emission is desired, such structural motifs should be avoided. Moreover, in both azulene and cyanoazulene we have uncovered a new mechanism to tune the photophysics nonsynthetically. Weak complexation with aromatic solvents directly modifies the aromaticity of the excited states, leading to sometimes dramatic changes in the excited-state dynamics and emission properties. This effect offers a facile new route to control, through precise solvent mixtures, the excited-state properties of materials that incorporate azulene as a structural motif. We expect this property is universal for azulene-containing materials unless appropriate steps are taken to prevent complexation. Thus, we hypothesize adding significant steric bulk to shield the azulene core from close solvent interactions can preserve anti-Kasha emission when in aromatic solvents. Yet our results further highlight pitfalls for incorporating azulene into more complex materials. Namely, the most photophysically interesting property of azulene, its anti-Kasha emission, relies on the 10-π Hückel aromaticity of S2, but that aromaticity is particularly fragile. The weak solvent complexation we infer here is insufficient to shift the ground- or excited-state absorption bands detectably, yet it can rapidly destabilize S2 by introducing antiaromatic character. Similarly, we can rationalize the much shorter S2 lifetime in cyanoazulene through the introduction of electron density in the transannular bond, which reduces aromaticity and thus induces conformational softening of S2. These results suggest that structural modifications of azulene can quite readily compromise this key property of the S2 state. Indeed, prior work has observed strong fluorescence quenching in functionalized azulenes and rapid, unexplained S2 decay in azulene dimers and tetramers. , Our results, by providing crucial insight into the nature of azulene’s electronic states and environmental interactions, offer a simple framework to rationalize these effects, and they provide a new framework for understanding the photophysics of this fundamental chromophore.
