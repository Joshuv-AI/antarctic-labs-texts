Discrepancies in reporting lifestyle and dietary habits by children and parents and their association with anthropometric parameters in 10–12-year-old Polish students: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Jadwiga Hamulka, Dawid Madej, Ewa Niedźwiedzka, Ewa Czarniecka-Skubina, Krystyna Gutkowska
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Although parents seek the best for their children, discrepancies often arise between what children and parents report about their lifestyles, including diet quality. To address this gap, this study aimed to assess the relationships between selected lifestyle determinants, diet quality, and nutritional knowledge, and anthropometric parameters in Polish children aged 10–12, taking into account discrepancies between children’s self-assessment and their parents’ reports. A cross-sectional, nationwide study of 4,407 Polish schoolchildren aged 10–12, based on children and their parents’ reports, was conducted between 2022 and 2023. Data on diet quality, nutritional knowledge, lifestyle determinants (screen time, physical activity, sleep duration), and sociodemographic data were collected using a paper questionnaire. Anthropometric measurements included body weight, height, and waist circumference, from which the Body Mass Index (BMI) and Waist-to-Height Ratio (WHtR) indices were calculated to assess weight status and central obesity, respectively. A logistic regression model was performed to assess how these lifestyle and dietary factors predict BMI and WHtR. Predictive models indicated that excessive screen time (> 4 h/day), based on children’s self-reports, significantly decreased the odds of having a normal BMI and a WHtR < 0.5. Conversely, high physical activity reported by both groups strongly increased the odds of maintaining a WHtR < 0.5. Furthermore, higher child-reported nutritional knowledge increased the odds of a normal BMI, while adherence to the ‘healthy products’ dietary pattern favoured a normal WHtR. Selected lifestyle determinants among school-age children were found to be associated with BMI and WHtR indicators of nutritional status. At the same time, the significant discrepancies observed between children’s self-assessments and parental reports indicate the need to use multi-source data in the assessment of children’s lifestyles and to exercise caution when interpreting research results based solely on parental reports.

Background

Excess body weight and metabolic disorders among children and adolescents constitute key challenges in contemporary public health. They result from a complex interaction of environmental and behavioral determinants, such as inappropriate dietary patterns, poor nutritional knowledge and physical activity, excessive screen time, and reduced sleep duration1–3. The coexistence of these factors increases the risk of later cardiometabolic morbidity (diabetes, hypertension, ischemic heart disease, and stroke) in adulthood4–6.

In recent decades, the epidemiology of childhood overweight and obesity has become a critical public health concern globally, and Poland is no exception to this trend. Data from recent years indicate a concerning, continuous upward trend in the prevalence of excessive body weight among Polish children and adolescents. According to nationwide and regional epidemiological studies, including data aligned with the WHO European Childhood Obesity Surveillance Initiative (COSI), approximately 25–30% of Polish school-aged children are currently affected by excessive body weight7–9. Specifically, within the 10–12 years age group—a critical developmental period marked by the onset of puberty and increasing autonomy in lifestyle choices—the prevalence of excess body weight is estimated at around 20–25%, with clinical obesity affecting approximately 5–10% of these children, depending on the diagnostic criteria applied10,11. This persistently growing trend underscores the urgent need to thoroughly understand the underlying behavioral and lifestyle factors to develop effective, targeted preventive strategies.

A review of the literature reveals that, while several studies have assessed sleep duration, screen time, physical activity levels and dietary patterns in the same age group, few have done so from the perspectives of both children and their parents12,13. For example, Chaputet et al.12 demonstrated that moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) was associated with a lower risk of obesity in children aged 9–11 years, regardless of screen time and sleep duration. Gale et al.13 found that reduced sleep and prolonged screen time were associated with a higher fat mass index in children aged 11–14 years. In contrast, our previous study was limited to determine the relationship between screen time (ST) duration, body weight status (BWS), and selected lifestyle behaviors in children, without comparing these data with parental reporting14. Furthermore, our recent nationwide study within the same project framework demonstrated that lifestyle behaviors, such as limited screen time and adequate sleep duration, are significantly associated with regular breakfast consumption, which in turn correlates with lower odds of being overweight or obese in this population15.

The ambiguity of these results highlights the importance of analyzing healthy eating habits and lifestyles of children and adolescents during the key developmental period of 10–12 years of age. This is a time when changes in children’s lifestyles occur: a decrease in physical activity levels, longer screen time, and shorter sleep, as well as an increase in the consumption of high-energy and ultra-processed foods with low nutritional value16–18. At this age, children also become more independent in making decisions regarding dietary choices, which often differ from parents’ expectations and recommendations19,20. Increased independence and time spent outside the home (school, extracurricular activities) weaken the effectiveness of parental supervision over children’s lifestyles. At the same time, there is an increasing discrepancy between the actual health behaviors of children and their assessment by parents, which may lead to systematic measurement error in studies based on parental declarations21,22.

Research shows that parents overestimate their children’s health-promoting behaviors. They are more likely to report longer sleep, higher levels of physical activity, and shorter screen time than children self-report21,22. Meanwhile, evening use of electronic devices and delayed sleep onset23, as well as low-intensity physical activity (MVPA) and excessive screen time, have been shown to contribute to accelerated puberty, poorer diet quality, and an increased risk of overweight and obesity13,18.

Therefore, identifying the coexistence and relationships between key lifestyle behaviors (sleep duration, screen time, physical activity level), dietary patterns, and nutritional knowledge can provide a basis for designing effective and targeted prevention programs. Implemented in early adolescence, these programs have the potential to reduce the risk of overweight, obesity, and metabolic disorders in school-age children.

Taking the above into account, the study aimed to assess the relationships between selected lifestyle factors, diet quality, and nutritional knowledge, and anthropometric parameters in children aged 10−12, taking into account discrepancies between children’s self-assessment and their parents’ reports. While previous studies have explored specific lifestyle aspects, they have often focused on isolated behaviors—such as only physical activity or screen time—or relied on smaller, regional samples. This study expands the existing literature by simultaneously analyzing a comprehensive set of lifestyle factors, dietary quality, dietary patterns, and nutritional knowledge within a large, nationally representative cohort of Polish children and their parents, which significantly strengthens the generalizability of the findings. Furthermore, previous discrepancy analyses in pediatric populations have predominantly focused on Body Mass Index (BMI) as the sole indicator of nutritional status. A key novelty of our research is the simultaneous evaluation of the WHtR, allowing us to investigate how child-parent reporting discrepancies relate specifically to central obesity, a critical predictor of future cardiometabolic risk. Ultimately, by quantifying these discrepancies, this study improves the interpretation of proxy-reported behavioral exposures in epidemiological studies. They highlight the systematic biases inherent in parental reports—such as the overestimation of health-promoting behaviors—and underscore the critical need for multi-source data when assessing the health risks of children transitioning into adolescence.

Methods

Study structure, participants, and recruitment

This cross-sectional study, part of the National Junior-Edu-Żywienie (JEŻ) Project, was conducted in primary schools across Poland between 2022 and 2023. Participants were selected using a two-stage cluster sampling method: first, 140 schools were selected from 2218 eligible primary schools across Poland, and then specific classes were selected from the selected institutions (Fig. 1).

Fig. 1Flowchart of sample selection in the study population.

Flowchart of sample selection in the study population.

During the initial recruitment for the overarching project, 2218 out of 14,698 invited schools nationwide (15.1%) registered to participate. For this specific cross-sectional analysis, 140 schools were randomly selected. At the participant level, out of 13,106 eligible children aged 10–12 years attending these selected schools, 4,407 were included in the final analysis. This corresponds to a participant inclusion rate of 33.6%, reflecting the strict criteria requiring complete self-reports, parental reports, and full anthropometric measurements. Further extensive details regarding the general sampling framework and methodology are provided in the previously published study protocol24.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The Ethics Committee associated with the Institute of Human Nutrition at the Warsaw University of Life Sciences reviewed and approved the research protocol (Resolution No. 18/2022). Parents gave written informed consent, allowing their children to participate in the study.

Data collection

Diet and nutritional knowledge

Data on children’s lifestyle determinants, including sleep duration, screen time, and physical activity, as well as their eating habits and nutritional knowledge (together with their parents’ nutritional knowledge), were collected using self-administered structured questionnaires. Responses were provided separately by the children (C-Res) and their parents (P-Res).

The validated SF-FFQ4Polish-Children® tool25, designed for Polish adolescents, served as the basis for these questionnaires. Children completed the paper-and-pencil questionnaires in a classroom setting under the supervision of research staff and a teacher. Parents completed their questionnaires at home and returned them via the teacher. Further details of the study protocol and methodology can be found in a previous publication24.

A food frequency questionnaire was used to assess children’s eating habits over the previous 12 months. Children and their parents indicated the frequency of consumption of various foods using categories ranging from “never or almost never” to “several times a day” (including “less than once a week,” “once a week”, “2–4 times/week”, “5–6 times/week” and “every day”). Based on these frequency data (using an a priori approach), three diet quality indicators were derived: pro-Healthy Diet Index (pHDI) - assessing consumption of 6 potentially positive impact food groups (dairy, whole grains, fish, legumes, vegetables, fruits); non-Healthy Diet Index (nHDI) - measuring intake of 5 potentially negative impact food groups (fast foods, salty snacks, sweets, sugar-sweetened beverages, energy drinks); and Diet Quality Index (DQI) - combining all 11 food groups (6 healthy, 5 unhealthy). Both the pHDI and the nHDI were calculated by summing the daily frequency of consumption (frequency/day) of each food group, as described in the study protocol24.

Using data from the food frequency questionnaires of the children (C-Res) and their parents (P-Res) and factor analysis based on the consumption of individual food groups, 3 dietary patterns were identified. Factor loadings were calculated to measure the strength of association between each food group and the identified dietary patterns. Based on the strongest associations of food groups, 3 main dietary patterns were identified: the “healthy products” pattern, characterised by high consumption of fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and dairy products; the “unhealthy products” pattern, defined by frequent consumption of salty snacks, sweetened drinks, fast food, and sweets; and “protein products” pattern, associated with increased consumption of protein-rich foods, especially fish and legumes.

The nutritional knowledge of both children and parents was assessed using a 20-item closed-ended test. Five questions were taken from the validated SF-FFQ4PolishChildren® questionnaire25, while the remaining 15 questions were developed based on current Polish dietary recommendations for children and adolescents26. Correct answers were worth 1 point; incorrect, ‘don’t know’ or missing answers were worth 0 points, resulting in a total possible score of 0–20. Participants’ knowledge was categorised as low (0–7 points), medium (8–14 points), or high (15–20 points) based on score tertiles.

Assessment of sleep duration, screen time, and physical activity levels

The children’s daily sleep duration was self-reported by the children (C-Res) and parents (P-Res) with options of < 6 h, 6–8 h, or > 8 h. The sleep duration categories were established during the study’s design phase based on the National Sleep Foundation (NSF) guidelines27. Although the optimal recommended range for this age group is 9–11 h, the NSF also indicates that 7–8 h may be appropriate for some children. Consequently, the ≥ 8 h category was used in this analysis as a marker for sufficient sleep, while durations of less than 8 h were defined as inadequate.

To assess children’s screen time, children (C-Res) and their parents (P-Res) reported the amount of time they spent using computers and electronic devices each day. Participants chose from intervals: <2 h/day, 2 to < 4 h/day, 4 to < 6 h/day, 6 to < 8 h/day, 8 to < 10 h/day, or ≥ 10 h/day. In the subsequent analysis, responses were grouped into < 2 h/day, 2–4 h/day, and > 4 h/day. The higher intervals (4 to < 6 h/day, 6 to < 8 h/day, 8 to < 10 h/day, and ≥ 10 h/day) were combined into a single > 4 h/day group. This aggregation was performed to ensure adequate sample sizes for the multivariable logistic regression models, particularly for the parental reports where frequencies in the highest categories were very low. Furthermore, this three-tier categorization establishes clinically meaningful thresholds, allowing comparison with the American Academy of Pediatrics recommendation of no more than 2 h per day28 against moderate use (2–4 h/day) and excessive use representing at least double the recommended limit (> 4 h/day).

Children’s leisure-time physical activity (after school and at weekends) was measured using a self-assessment tool (child responses - C-Res and parent responses - R-Res), which categorised activity as low, moderate, or high. Descriptions accompanied each option: low activity referred to mainly sedentary behaviour or short walks (≤ 2 h/week); moderate activity included activities such as walking or cycling for 2–3 h/week; high activity included running or other sports for more than 3 h/week24.

Anthropometric assessment

Children’s weight, height, and waist circumference were measured directly at schools by highly trained researchers, using standard methods and equipment according to the International Standards for Anthropometric Assessment29. Detailed information on the procedures of anthropometric measurements were described previously24. BMI and WHtR were calculated from these measurements.

Participants were classified based on BMI as underweight, normal weight, overweight, or obese according to established national (Polish) criteria adjusted for age and sex in children and adolescents30. Abdominal obesity was assessed using two criteria: the commonly used indicator of WHtR ≥ 0.531, and a specific age- and sex-adjusted cut-off based on Polish reference data for children and adolescents32. The cut-off was obtained by relating children’s measurements to established adult cardiometabolic risk thresholds, which indicate a higher risk of future health complications.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SAS software, version 9.4 (Statistical Analysis Software, SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Descriptive data were presented as n (%) for categorical variables for child (C-Res) and parent (P-Res) responses. Spearman rank correlation analysis was used to assess the relationship between child and parent responses. Agreement between child (C-Res) and parent (P-Res) responses was determined using the chi-squared test. For all tests, statistical significance was set at p ≤ 0.05.

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) with orthogonal Varimax rotation was conducted to identify dietary patterns based on the consumption frequency of each food group. Prior to extraction, the suitability of the data for factor analysis was verified using the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy and Bartlett’s test of sphericity. Dietary patterns were retained based on the Kaiser criterion (eigenvalues > 1.0) and the interpretability of the factors. Finally, tertiles and factor loadings were used to determine the strength of association between each food group and the identified dietary patterns.

Multiple logistic regression analyses were performed to examine the associations between different factors (including selected lifestyle factors, diet quality indicators, identified dietary patterns, and nutritional knowledge) and anthropometric characteristics: BMI (model 1; where ‘normal BMI’ was defined strictly as normal weight, and ‘abnormal BMI’ encompassed the underweight, overweight, and obese categories), and WHtR category (model 2) as dependent variables.

Potential socio-demographic confounders, including parental education, socioeconomic status, and place of residence, were initially selected for evaluation based on established theoretical associations with childhood obesity. However, during the model-building phase, they were excluded from the final multivariable models to maintain parsimony, as they neither reached statistical significance nor acted as meaningful confounders (i.e., their inclusion did not substantially alter the estimates of the primary associations). Furthermore, age and sex were not included as independent covariates, because the outcome variables (BMI and WHtR categories) were already inherently adjusted for these factors through the use of age- and sex-specific reference standards.

Adjusted odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated. The statistical significance of ORs was assessed using the Wald statistic, and the associations between dependent and independent variables were considered significant if the p-value was ≤ 0.05.

Results

Children’s sleep and screen time, physical activity and nutritional knowledge of children and parents

A comparative analysis of children’s sleep duration, screen time, and physical activity, as reported by both children (C-Res) and their parents (P-Res), were presented in Fig. 2. Statistically significant positive correlations were observed between parent and child reports for all variables; these correlations were weak in strength. Parents reported healthier behaviours for their children than children reported for themselves, particularly in relation to sleep and screen time.

According to parents’ reports, 60.4% of children slept at least 8 h per day, whereas only 41% of children self-reported this duration. Conversely, 9.6% of children reported sleeping less than 6 h per day, compared to 0.6% in parents’ reports. Regarding screen time, 28.8% of children reported spending ≥ 4 h per day on screens, while only 11.2% of parents reported this level for their children. Parents were more likely to estimate their child’s screen time in the range of 2–4 h (50.4%) compared to children’s self-reports (35.8%). Physical activity levels were similar between groups, with the majority reporting moderate activity (44.9% in P-Res vs. 44.2% in C-Res). Reports of high activity levels were more frequent among parents (45.0%) than among children (44.2%).

The nutritional knowledge of children and their parents was also compared (Fig. 2). Most children had a moderate level of nutritional knowledge (65.5%), while only a small percentage had high nutritional knowledge (7.7%). In turn, a significant proportion of parents (68.4%) had high nutritional knowledge, while 28.3% had moderate knowledge. A statistically significant moderate positive correlation was observed between the nutritional knowledge of children and parents.

Dietary patterns and quality of children’s diets

The data showed good suitability for factor analysis for both children and parents, with a Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) statistic of 0.70 for both models, and a significant Bartlett’s test of sphericity (p < 0.001). Three main dietary patterns were identified with eigenvalues > 1.0. For the children’s model, these patterns cumulatively explained 52.9% of the total variance, while for the parents’ model, they explained 54.3% of the variance. Figure 3 presented the factor loadings for individual food groups within the three dietary patterns—‘healthy products,’ ‘unhealthy products’, and ‘protein products’ - based on children’s (C-Res) and parents’ (P-Res) reports. Statistically significant positive correlations were found between children’s and parents’ responses to all dietary patterns, although these correlations were weak.

The children’s diet quality indicators pHDI, nHDI, and DQI based on child (C-Res) and parent (P-Res) reports are shown in Table 1. Statistically significant positive correlations were observed between children’s and parents’ responses for all indicators, although the strength of these correlations was weak. These results suggest that although parents and children rated diet quality similarly, parents reported healthier eating behaviours for their children than children reported for themselves.

Anthropometric characteristics of children

Most participants (73%) had a BMI indicating a normal body weight, while 15.2% were classified as having underweight and 11.8% as having overweight (Fig. 4). Based on the WHtR classification, 17.2% of the children had a WHtR ≥ 0.5, indicating an increased risk of central obesity. According to the WHtR cut-off classification, 14.2% of the children exceeded the threshold for abdominal obesity.

Predictive model for anthropometric characteristic (Model 1)

As shown in Table 2 (model 1), odds ratios (ORs) indicate the odds of having a normal BMI in relation to different lifestyle, diet, and knowledge determinants reported by children (C-Res) and parents (P-Res).

Children’s reported screen time showed that more than 4 h per day was associated with 28% lower odds of having a normal BMI compared to ≤ 2 h (reference group) (OR: 0.72; 95% CI 0.60–0.86), whereas 2–4 h showed no significant effect.

Based on children’s reports, moderate activity was associated with 26% lower odds of having a normal BMI compared to low activity (reference group) (OR: 0.74; 95% CI 0.59–0.93), while high activity showed no statistically significant association. In contrast, parental reports of high physical activity in the child were associated with a 68% increased odds of the child having a normal BMI compared with low activity (OR: 1.68; 95% CI 1.34–2.10).

Children’s self-reported nutrition knowledge was associated with higher odds of having a normal BMI; compared to the low knowledge group, those with moderate knowledge had 20% higher odds (OR: 1.20; 95% CI 1.03–1.39), and those with high knowledge had 50% higher odds (OR: 1.50; 95% CI 1.12–2.02). Conversely, high parental knowledge of nutrition was associated with 18% lower odds of the child having a normal BMI compared to low parental knowledge (OR: 0.82; 95% CI 0.71–0.95).

Similar relationships were also observed with regard to dietary patterns. Based on children’s reports, the terciles T2 and T3 of the ‘healthy products’ pattern were marginally associated with 18% higher odds of having a normal BMI compared to the lowest tercile (T1) (OR: 1.18; 95% CI 0.99–1.40). Also, from children’s reports, the highest tercile (T3) of the ‘protein products’ pattern was associated with 20% lower odds of having a normal BMI (OR: 0.80; 95% CI 0.67–0.94). According to parental reports, moderate (T2) and high (T3) adherence to the ‘unhealthy products’ pattern were unexpectedly associated with 17% (OR: 1.17; 95% CI 1.01–1.38) and 23% (OR: 1.23; 95% CI 1.04–1.45) higher odds of having a normal BMI, respectively, compared to the lowest tertile (T1). In addition, based on C-Res, a moderate nHDI score was marginally associated with 29% lower odds of having a normal BMI compared to a low score (OR: 0.71; 95% CI 0.51–1.01), while a high score was not significant.

Predictive model for anthropometric characteristic (Model 2)

As shown in Table 3 (model 2), odds ratios (ORs) were examined to understand the odds of having a WHtR < 0.5 (no abdominal obesity) in relation to various lifestyle and dietary determinants reported by children (C-Res) and parents (P-Res).

Sleep duration reported by children showed a negative association, with those sleeping 6–8 h per day having 35% lower odds of having an abdominal obesity than those sleeping less than 6 h (OR: 0.65; 95% CI 0.50–0.84).

Based on C-Res, screen time also showed a negative association with having no abdominal obesity; compared with ≤ 2 h per day (reference group), 2–4 h was associated with 27% lower odds (OR: 0.73; 95% CI 0.60–0.89) and more than 4 h with 44% lower odds (OR: 0.56; 95% CI 0.46–0.80) of having a WHtR < 0.5. Based on P-Res, 2–4 h of screen time showed a small negative association (15% lower odds, OR: 0.85; 95% CI 0.71–0.99), while screen time over 4 h was associated with 30% lower odds (OR: 0.70; 95% CI 0.53–0.91) of having a correct WHtR.

Agreement between child and parent reports

Agreement between children’s (C-Res) and parents’ (P-Res) reports on children’s lifestyle behaviours and nutritional knowledge (Table 4) and children’s diet quality indices and dietary patterns (Table 5) was assessed in 4407 child-parent pairs. Statistically significant associations (chi-squared, p < 0.001) were found between C-Res and P-Res in all domains assessed: physical activity, screen time, sleep duration, nutrition knowledge, diet quality indices, and dietary patterns.

However, the level of agreement varied considerably between these different domains. The agreement was highest for the diet quality indices, especially the non-Healthy Diet Index (n-HDI) (95.3%), with good agreement also observed for the Diet Quality Index (DQI) (72.8%) and the pro-Healthy Diet Index (p-HDI) (71.1%). Moderate agreement was found for lifestyle behaviours: physical activity level (58.1% concordance), sleep duration (50.5%), and screen time (47.6%). The agreement was lower for adherence (tertiles) to the derived dietary patterns, with concordance rates of 44.2% for the ‘unhealthy products’ pattern, 41.9% for the ‘protein products’ pattern, and 39.7% for the ‘healthy products’ pattern. By far, the lowest concordance was found for the level of nutritional knowledge (25.6%).

Discussion

Selected lifestyle determinants among school-age children, such as physical activity level, screen time, dietary patterns, and nutritional knowledge, were found to be associated with BMI and WHtR indicators of nutritional status. At the same time, significant discrepancies were found between children’s self-assessments and parental reports, particularly with regard to nutritional knowledge and dietary patterns. In a comparative analysis, parents consistently attributed more favorable health behaviors to their children (longer sleep duration, higher levels of physical activity, lower screen time) than the children themselves reported. This may reflect cognitive biases, a tendency to provide socially desirable responses, or limited access to actual data about children’s daily habits. These results emphasize the need to use multi-source data in assessing children’s lifestyles and caution in interpreting research results based solely on parental reports.

The highest level of agreement between children’s and parents’ declarations was observed for dietary quality indicators such as the healthy diet index (pHDI), the unhealthy diet index (nHDI), and the overall diet quality index (DQI). This agreement may be due to the fact that diet quality, unlike detailed dietary patterns, is easier to observe in the home environment. Parents, as the main decision-makers in food purchasing, meal planning, and preparation, have a better understanding of their child’s daily intake33–36. Furthermore, they demonstrate a relatively high awareness of general dietary recommendations, which facilitates the assessment of the child’s dietary compliance with the guidelines33. In children aged 9–12, dietary habits remain strongly linked to the parents’ diet, which results from limited nutritional autonomy during this period. Most meals are eaten at home, and food comes almost exclusively from parents34,36. Numerous studies confirm that children’s food preferences and frequency of consumption of many products reflect the consumption patterns observed in their parents37–39. With the transition from childhood to adolescence, the role of parents in shaping eating behaviors decreases, making this stage particularly important for early health interventions33,39.

It should be emphasized, however, that the dietary quality indicators used, based on national dietary guidelines, vary significantly depending on geographic and cultural context. Consequently, the observed associations demonstrated using specific country-specific dietary indices may not be directly generalizable to other populations36. The lowest level of agreement was observed with regard to dietary patterns and children’s nutritional knowledge. Children reported higher levels of nutritional knowledge than their parents reported and were more likely to report higher consumption of protein foods from the “protein foods” model. The discrepancy between parental and child reports may result from limited family dialogue about healthy eating and differences in information sources. While children increasingly use social media, mobile apps, and school education, parents rely on more traditional educational channels, such as doctors, brochures, and social campaigns40,41. Such a discrepancy may lead to the lack of a common language of communication and a different understanding of the principles of a healthy lifestyle, which hinders mutual understanding and effective health education at home42,43.

Moderate agreement was observed between children’s and parents’ reports regarding physical activity levels, sleep duration, and screen time. Similar results were also obtained in studies conducted among 10–12-year-old children in Germany33. Parents tended to attribute more favorable health habits to their children, declaring higher levels of physical activity, longer sleep, and shorter screen time than the children reported. These discrepancies may result from limited supervision of children outside the home, parents’ lack of knowledge about their children’s daily behaviors, and the social desirability bias, i.e., the tendency to provide responses consistent with social norms44–48. Research also suggests that children are more accurate in assessing their own sleep and screen time behaviors, especially when using personal devices without direct supervision e.g. just before bed33,49.

The majority of children and parents reported moderate levels of physical activity, but parents were much more likely to rate their children’s physical activity level as high in the analyzed study. Similarly, Kesten et al.48 demonstrated low agreement between parental assessments and children’s actual activity levels. A European study of children aged 6–12 years demonstrated moderate agreement between parent-reported and objectively (accelerometrically) measured MVPA levels46. Parents overestimated their children’s activity levels by several minutes per day, particularly in girls.

Parents also reported shorter screen time for their children. This may be due to parents underestimating their children’s actual screen time, as they lack full control over their children’s behavior, especially in older school-age children. Similarly, in Loucaides’s study50, children aged 11–12 reported significantly more time spent watching television and playing video games than their parents reported. However, the results of an American project involving adolescents aged 10–14 showed the opposite trend: parents reported longer screen time (on average 4.46 h/day) than children (3.87 h/day)49. In Gentile et al.’s study51, children more accurately predicted their own screen time than their parents. These results suggest that children’s self-reports may be a more reliable source of data in analyses of media habits.

With regard to sleep, moderately high agreement was found between children’s and parents’ reports of sleep duration exceeding 8 h. Significant discrepancies were found for sleep durations of less than 6 h; in this category, parents were significantly less likely than children to report such short sleep duration, suggesting a tendency to overestimate. Similar results were obtained in the study by Duarte et al.22, showing that parents of 735 children overreported sleep duration by an average of 0.9 h on weekdays and 1.15 h on weekends.

Taking into account the observed differences in self-reports and the importance of individual lifestyle determinants for children’s health, a detailed analysis of their associations with nutritional status parameters was conducted. Significant correlations between selected lifestyle determinants and the risk of overweight and obesity were demonstrated. A key risk factor is a sedentary lifestyle and low physical activity52. Moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA), which is correlated with children’s anthropometric parameters, plays a particular role53,54. In this study, children with high levels of physical activity, both according to their own and parents’ self-reports, were significantly more likely to maintain healthy BMI and WHtR values. Similar results were observed in an international study of children aged 9–11 from 12 countries. MVPA was shown to be significantly associated with a lower risk of obesity, independent of other lifestyle determinants such as sleep duration and television viewing54. This is also confirmed by studies among Estonian children aged 11–12 years, in which higher MVPA levels were associated with lower body fat percentage and a more favorable WHtR index53.

The importance of physical activity for maintaining normal anthropometric parameters should be considered in the context of the increasing role of screen time in the daily functioning of children and adolescents. The decline in physical activity and the increasing share of passive forms of spending time among children and adolescents pose a significant public health challenge52. Cheng et al.’s55 study showed that children and adolescents aged 10–12 spent an average of 8 h per day in a sedentary position, of which over 2 h were spent using electronic devices. Screen time encompasses various forms of passive digital activity, such as streaming, gaming, and socializing and using social media55,56. Current recommendations indicate that this time should not exceed 2 h per day28,52.

However, the results of studies on the relationship between screen time and the occurrence of overweight and obesity in children and adolescents are partially inconsistent57. These differences may result from the variety of methods used to measure screen time, different definitions and indicators of nutritional status, as well as the diversity of the studied populations in terms of gender, age, and socioeconomic status57,58. A meta-analysis by Haghjoo et al.4 confirmed that prolonged screen time was significantly more common among overweight and children with obesity, although some studies did not confirm such an association. Other studies indicated that exceeding 2 h of daily screen time was associated with a higher risk of obesity58,59. A similar relationship was observed in the present study, where both children and parents reported that longer screen time (2–4 h and more than 4 h) was associated with a lower probability of maintaining healthy WHtR and BMI values, with this relationship being stronger for WHtR. These observations are consistent with our other recent findings among Polish schoolchildren, where prolonged screen time (> 4 h/day) was identified as a key factor reducing the likelihood of healthy dietary habits (e.g., regular breakfast intake) and increasing the risk of excessive body weight15. This may indicate greater accuracy of children’s self-assessment or greater variability in health behaviors.

Literature data indicate that excessive screen time, particularly in the evening, can lead to circadian rhythm disruption, sleep deprivation, and delayed sleep onset. However, insufficient sleep time is a significant risk factor for the development of abdominal obesity60,61. Studies have shown that children sleeping 6–8 h per day were significantly less likely to have abdominal obesity compared to children sleeping less. Similar conclusions were presented in meta-analyses by Felső et al.62 and Li et al.63, which confirmed that shortened sleep time is a risk factor for overweight and obesity in children.

Another important lifestyle factor is the level of nutritional knowledge, which may influence not only food choices but also behaviors related to physical activity, sleep, and screen time64. In this study, we observed that a higher level of nutritional knowledge declared by children correlated moderately, but positively, with maintaining normal BMI and WHtR values. This result is confirmed by prospective and interventional studies, which demonstrated that improved nutritional knowledge is associated with more health-promoting habits and a lower risk of overweight and obesity65,66. However, in the case of parental declarations, an inverse relationship was observed, namely, a higher level of nutritional knowledge correlated with a lower probability of a normal child’s BMI. This may reflect the so-called a compensatory effect, which occurs when parents of children with overweight or obese intensify their search for nutritional knowledge in response to an existing problem. In cross-sectional studies, this can lead to an inverse-seeming relationship. Similar conclusions are presented by Cait et al.65, who found no significant correlations between the level of parents’ nutritional knowledge and indicators of their children’s nutritional status. This may suggest that the influence of parental knowledge on the nutritional behaviors of adolescents aged 10–17 is limited, especially in the period of increasing nutritional autonomy, more frequent eating out, and a natural tendency to question parental recommendations65,67.

Given that nutritional knowledge is not always reflected in daily dietary choices, this analysis also assessed the relationship between adherence to specific dietary patterns and anthropometric indicators in children. The analysis revealed significant correlations; in particular, adherence to the “healthy products” pattern was associated with a higher likelihood of maintaining a healthy WHtR, especially in the children’s self-reported assessment. These results are consistent with the research by Hourani et al.68 confirming the role of a diet rich in vegetables, fruits, whole grains, and dairy products in the prevention of overweight and obesity in children aged 10–12. Similarly, European studies have shown that adherence to the “healthy products” or “healthypattern” dietary pattern was associated with lower body fat levels and more favorable WHtR and BMI values69,70.

Regarding the “protein products” formula, it was shown that higher self-reported protein intake by children correlated with a lower probability of maintaining a healthy BMI. Similar results were reported by Qu et al.71, who found that high protein intake was positively associated with the occurrence of overweight and obesity among school-age children. However, epidemiological data suggest that the effect is dependent on the protein source. Protein from plant and dairy products may have a protective effect against the development of obesity, while high animal protein intake, especially from red meat, is associated with an increased risk of weight gain72,73.

In the case of the “unhealthy products” model, based on parental self-report, consumption of products belonging to the group was positively correlated. A positive correlation was found between its consumption and the absence of central obesity in the child (indicated by normal WHtR values). This result may reflect reverse causality, whereby children without central obesity may occasionally consume non-recommended products without negatively impacting anthropometric parameters. Alternatively, it may be due to methodological limitations, such as underestimation of parental intake or self-reporting errors.

Additionally, a moderate level of the unhealthy diet index (nHDI) showed a marginally significant negative association with the probability of maintaining a healthy BMI. This supports the hypothesis that frequent consumption of ultra-processed food (UPF) has an adverse effect on children’s body weight, especially in the long term10,65,71,74.

While many of our findings align with expected physiological relationships, several regression results appear counterintuitive and require careful interpretation. Specifically, child-reported moderate physical activity was associated with lower odds of a normal BMI; parent-reported adherence to the ‘unhealthy products’ dietary pattern was associated with higher odds of a normal BMI; and associations between sleep duration categories and abdominal obesity risk were inconsistent1,62. These unexpected outcomes likely reflect several well-known epidemiological phenomena inherent to cross-sectional designs. First, reverse causality is highly probable. For example, parents of children with a normal body weight may adopt less restrictive feeding practices, allowing for more frequent consumption of ‘unhealthy’ snacks, whereas parents of children with overweight might actively restrict these foods. Similarly, children with excess body weight might be explicitly encouraged by parents or doctors to engage in moderate physical activity as part of a weight management strategy. Second, reporting bias and social desirability likely play a role; children with abnormal weight status may overestimate their physical activity or underestimate their screen time to align with perceived health norms47,48. Third, misclassification effects are a known risk when using subjective, questionnaire-based assessments for complex variables like sleep duration and diet, which can distort the observed associations44,45. Finally, these results may be influenced by residual confounding from unmeasured factors, such as the children’s specific pubertal stage, total daily energy intake, or the exact timing of screen use before bed. Consequently, these specific counterintuitive findings should be interpreted with caution, as they underscore the complexity of relying on proxy-reported and self-reported behavioral exposures.

Strengths and limitations

The literature review indicates that this is one of the few studies conducted simultaneously in children and parents, assessing multiple health and diet quality factors simultaneously, and likely the only one in which this group of children simultaneously used multiple objective health assessment methods and analyzed dietary patterns and diet quality. This approach allowed for a comprehensive description of the study group and enabled strong conclusions. As part of the study, information was collected from children and their parents regarding their usual food consumption frequency over the past 12 months. Three diet quality indicators were derived from the presented data: the pro-Healthy Diet Index (pHDI), the non-Healthy Diet Index (nHDI), the Diet Quality Index (DQI), and three dietary patterns. Weight, height, and waist circumference on anthropometric measurements conducted by well-trained academic researchers not self-reported data, which are subject to bias. Data on children’s lifestyle determinants, including sleep duration, screen time, physical activity, and nutritional knowledge, was collected using structured questionnaires administered in a classroom setting under the supervision of research staff and a teacher. Any doubts or shortcomings were clarified with the children on an ongoing basis. This holistic approach enabled a multifaceted analysis of the correlation between the quality of the diet, lifestyle and nutritional knowledge of children and anthropometric indicators such as WHtR and BMI, as well as the correlation between children and parent reports.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes establishing causal relationships. Additionally, our statistical approach utilized standard multiple logistic regression models without applying sampling weights or accounting for the clustered nature of the data (i.e., students nested within classes and schools). As a consequence, ignoring this clustering effect may have impacted the accurate estimation of standard errors and the validity of the resulting confidence intervals. Second, the reliance on self-reported and parent-reported data carries the inherent risk of recall and social desirability biases, potentially distorting the observed associations. Similarly, the 12-month dietary questionnaire is susceptible to recall errors and cannot account for seasonal variations. Future research would benefit from incorporating objective measurements, such as accelerometry or digital tracking, to validate these behavioral assessments. Third, our dietary quality indices were based on national recommendations, which may limit the direct transferability of the findings to other cultural contexts. Fourth, as previously noted, the strict requirement for complete datasets reduced our final sample size. This introduces potential selection bias and may impact strict national representativeness, as health-conscious parents were likely more prone to participate. Finally, our models could not account for unmeasured confounders, such as detailed socioeconomic status, school environments, and the pubertal stage of the participants—a particularly relevant variable given its significant influence on rapid changes in body composition during early adolescence.

In summary, the results of this study provide a good basis for further, more detailed analyses examining the relationships between lifestyle, dietary patterns, physical activity, nutritional knowledge, and selected nutritional status indicators. The comprehensive approach used in this study, encompassing the assessment of multiple domains of lifestyle, diet quality, and two nutritional status indicators, combined with the perspectives of both children and parents, provides a valuable starting point for developing precise interventions and further, in-depth scientific analyses.

Conclusions

The study demonstrated that selected lifestyle determinants, including sleep, screen time, physical activity, dietary patterns, and nutritional knowledge, are associated with BMI and WHtR indicators of nutritional status in school-age children. Discrepancies between children’s self-reports and parental assessments indicate that parental reports tend to present a more favorable picture of children’s behaviors. Such differences may result from cognitive bias, social desirability, or lack of direct parental insight into daily habits their children.

These findings underline the need for multi-source data in assessing children’s lifestyles and suggest that interventions should explicitly account for discrepancies between parents’ and children’s perceptions, as these differences may influence both recognition of problems and intervention effectiveness.
