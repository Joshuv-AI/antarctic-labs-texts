Pulmonary embolism after treatment for massive hemoptysis: a therapeutic dilemma
Authors: Toshiyuki Yonezawa, Akiko Narita, Kojiro Suzuki, Naoki Ozeki, Takayuki Fukui, Satoru Ito
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Massive hemoptysis is a life-threatening condition requiring prompt hemostatic intervention. After hemostatic treatment, thromboembolic complications may pose a dilemma when anticoagulation is necessary but carries a risk of rebleeding. A 72-year-old man with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease was admitted for recurrent hemoptysis. Bronchial angiography revealed a markedly dilated right bronchial artery with intrapulmonary shunting. Because of massive hemoptysis and respiratory failure, emergency bronchial artery embolization was performed, followed by right middle lobectomy with corresponding bronchial artery ligation to reduce rebleeding risk. After surgery, persistent bleeding from segmental bronchi was controlled by endobronchial Watanabe spigot. Pulmonary embolism was subsequently detected by contrast-enhanced computed tomography. A second bronchial angiography confirmed complete hemostasis before anticoagulation. Heparin infusion was initiated, followed by a switch to edoxaban. He recovered without recurrent hemoptysis. This case highlights the challenge of balancing hemostasis and anticoagulation when pulmonary embolism develops after stepwise multimodal hemostatic therapy.

Introduction

Hemoptysis is one of the most serious respiratory emergencies, and massive hemoptysis can lead to fatal outcomes due to asphyxiation or hemorrhagic shock [1]. Therefore, prompt identification of the bleeding source and appropriate hemostatic intervention are crucial. In contrast, pulmonary embolism (PE) has an opposite pathophysiological and therapeutic profile to hemoptysis, as anticoagulation is the standard treatment for PE. When massive hemoptysis is complicated by PE, a critical therapeutic dilemma arises: hemostasis should be prioritized, whereas delaying anticoagulation therapy may result in fatal consequences. We report a case of PE that developed after massive hemoptysis.

Case presentation

A 72-year-old man with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease presented with recurrent hemoptysis. Two years earlier, contrast-enhanced chest computed tomography (CT) revealed dilatation of the right bronchial artery, and conservative hemostatic therapy with carbazochrome sodium sulfonate hydrate and tranexamic acid improved the symptoms.

He later developed recurrent hemoptysis, and bronchial angiography (BAG) demonstrated a markedly dilated and tortuous right bronchial artery with intrapulmonary shunting and parenchymal staining. Although bronchial artery embolization (BAE) using gelatin sponge (GS) initially controlled the bleeding, massive hemoptysis recurred three weeks later, leading to acute respiratory failure caused by airway flooding.

On admission, his oxygen saturation was 94% with 2L per minute of oxygen via nasal cannula, and laboratory data (Table 1) showed no anemia (Hb; 13.6 g/dL) but mild elevations of fibrin/fibrinogen degradation products (FDP, 6.60 μg/mL), D-dimer (2.71 μg/mL), and C-reactive protein (CRP, 0.78 mg/dL). Chest CT showed mucoid impaction, bronchial wall thickening, centrilobular nodules and consolidation in the right middle and lower lobes (Fig. 1A). Conservative hemostatic therapy and intravenous ceftriaxone (CTRX) was initiated; however, approximately 5 h after admission, his respiratory status rapidly deteriorated, with SpO2 falling below 90% despite 15 L/min of oxygen via a reservoir mask. Emergent BAG demonstrated findings similar to those seen three weeks earlier (Fig. 2A) and BAE using GS was performed. Although BAE temporarily controlled the bleeding, the extensive bronchial artery dilation and the severity of the hemorrhage indicated a high risk of rebleeding. Therefore, surgical intervention was performed for definitive hemostasis, consisting of a right middle lobectomy with corresponding bronchial artery ligation. Postoperatively, laboratory findings revealed mild anemia (hemoglobin, 11.9 g/dL) and an elevated FDP (14.30 μg/mL), along with increased white blood cell (10,200/μL) and CRP (8.43 mg/dL). CTRX was switched to sulbactam/ampicillin. Atrial fibrillation (AF) developed on postoperative day 2, but anticoagulation was deferred due to rebleeding risk. Multiple attempts at electrical cardioversion were performed for AF; however, sinus rhythm was not restored, and landiolol hydrochloride was initiated. On postoperative day 4, bronchoscopy showed no bleeding from the surgical site (Fig. 1C). However, persistent minor bleeding was observed from the right basal bronchi (B7and B9) and the left lingular bronchus (B4), for which endobronchial occlusion using endobronchial Watanabe spigots (EWS) was performed (Fig. 1D). Despite resolution of hemoptysis, FDP (220 μg/mL) and D-dimer (96.3 μg/mL) markedly increased, and the patient developed multiple segmental PE in the left lower pulmonary artery (Fig. 1E) and deep vein thrombosis. Although the proximal portion of the right bronchial artery was ligated and interrupted, its distal branches were faintly visualized, possibly through collateral circulation (Fig. 1F). After a second BAG confirmed complete hemostasis (Fig. 2B), heparin infusion was safely initiated.

The patient improved without recurrent hemoptysis and was transferred to the general ward on day 11. Anticoagulation was switched from heparin to edoxaban, and contrast-enhanced CT on day 18 showed improvement of the PE (Fig. 1G). He was discharged in stable condition on day 27.

Discussion

The simultaneous management of massive hemoptysis and PE remains highly challenging, and no established therapeutic strategy exists. In this case, stepwise multimodal hemostatic therapy achieved secure hemostasis for massive hemoptysis, allowing the safe initiation of anticoagulation. This case provides clinically relevant insights into therapeutic decision-making in patients with concurrent massive hemoptysis and PE.

BAE was selected as the initial treatment for massive hemoptysis. BAE is an effective and minimally invasive therapy, with a reported hemostatic success rate of 97.5%. GS was used because of its safety and low complication rate [2]. However, its biodegradability carries a risk of recanalization. In our patient, recanalization occurred approximately three weeks after the first BAE using GS.

Subsequently, right middle lobectomy with corresponding bronchial artery ligation was performed to reduce the risk of recurrent hemoptysis. Although BAE has reduced the need for surgery, surgical intervention remains important in selected high-risk cases, and preoperative BAE may improve surgical safety [3].

In the present case, complete hemostasis was not achieved even after surgery, and new bleeding from another segmental bronchus was observed. Because repeat surgery was not feasible, endobronchial occlusion using EWS was selected. Combination therapy with endobronchial tamponade and BAE has been reported to reduce recurrence [4].

The patient developed AF on postoperative day 2. In general, postoperative AF (PoAF) occurring after non-cardiac surgery, particularly thoracic surgery, is a relatively common early postoperative complication. The incidence of PoAF after pulmonary resection varies according to the surgical procedure and has been reported to be approximately 10-15% after lobectomy, and higher postoperative mortality has been observed in patients who develop PoAF [5]. Older age is a risk factor for PoAF, and the condition is more frequently observed in elderly patients undergoing thoracic surgery [6]. This may be attributable to the fact that PoAF is prone to be complicated by thromboembolic events such as PE, which can lead to hemodynamic collapse and a potentially fatal clinical course. In this case, the combination of older age and severe perioperative stress may have predisposed the patient to PoAF on postoperative day 2, followed by PE.

Herodotou et al. [7] also reported a case of massive hemoptysis complicated by PE in which anticoagulation was initiated after secure hemostasis had been achieved. Similarly, in our case, PE developed during the course of hemostatic treatment, and anticoagulation was deferred until secure hemostasis was achieved. Contrast-enhanced CT after EWS showed residual enhancement at the distal bronchial artery, suggesting possible vascular anastomosis and a risk of rebleeding during anticoagulation. However, a second BAG ensured complete hemostasis, allowing the safe initiation of anticoagulation. These findings indicate that secure hemostasis is essential before the safe initiation of anticoagulation.

Massive hemoptysis is a potentially fatal condition, and airway protection and secure hemostasis should be prioritized in management. When surgical intervention is required during the hemostatic course, the risk of cardiovascular complications, including PoAF and thromboembolic events, should be carefully assessed. This case suggests that, in complex situations involving massive hemoptysis complicated by PE, a stepwise and multimodal hemostatic strategy may allow the safe initiation of subsequent treatments, including anticoagulation.
