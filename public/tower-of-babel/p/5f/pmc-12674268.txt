Comparative analysis of ruminal and fecal microbiomes in Holstein, Jersey, and Jeju Black cattle using multiple taxonomic classifiers
Authors: Eun Tae Kim, Seung-Eun Lee, Sang-Min Shin, Dong-Hyun Lim, Tai-Young Hur, Sang-Bum Kim, Myeong-Gwan Oh, Wanho Jo, Woohyung Lee, Tansol Park
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe gastrointestinal microbiome is pivotal to nutrient utilization and productivity in dairy cattle. This study compared the ruminal and fecal microbiomes of Holstein, Jersey, and Jeju Black cattle using 16S rRNA gene sequencing and three taxonomic classification databases to identify microbial differences across breeds. A total of 25 samples were analysed to evaluate microbial composition, diversity, functional phenotypes, and interactions. Jeju Black cattle exhibited significantly higher ruminal diversity (p < 0.001), whereas Holstein and Jersey cows had more diverse fecal microbiomes. Prevotella, Ruminococcus and Succinivibrio dominated the rumen of commercial breeds, while Methanobrevibacter and Desulfovibrio were enriched in Jeju Black cattle. Amino acid prototrophs were abundant in Holstein and Jersey feces, while sulfur-metabolizing microbial phenotypes predominated in the Jeju Black rumen, with implications for nitrogen metabolism and methane production. Microbial network analysis revealed Mogibacterium as a high-centrality genus in Holstein and Jersey cows, and Anaerovibrio in Jersey cows, potentially linked to propionate production. Jeju Black cattle displayed distinct microbial interactions involving sulphur-reducing and methanogenic bacteria. These results might be showing breed-related microbiome variability, potentially influencing feed efficiency, fermentation, and emissions. However, future work should standardize diets, feeding conditions, sampling methods, and include performance metrics to clarify breed-specificity of microbiome-function relationships.Keywords: Ruminal, fecal, microbiome, breed, taxonomic classification

Introduction

The ruminal and fecal microbiomes of dairy cattle play key roles in the maintenance of animal health and productivity, particularly in breeds such as Holstein, Jersey, and Jeju Black. Understanding the similarities and differences in the microbiomes of these breeds can provide valuable insights into their metabolic efficiency, disease resistance, and overall performance.1,2

The rumen, a specialized foregut compartment in ruminants, harbours a diverse community of microorganisms that facilitate the fermentation of fibrous plant materials, a process that yields volatile fatty acids that serve as vital sources of energy for host animals.3 The composition of the ruminal microbiome is influenced by a diverse range of factors, including diet, genetics, and environmental conditions, leading to significant variations among the microbial populations in different cattle breeds.4,5 Recent research has highlighted the pivotal role played by the ruminal microbiome in influencing the feed efficiency and metabolic health of beef and dairy cows.6,7 Specific rumen microbial populations have been associated with enhanced fermentative activity and nutrient utilization, which can have a direct influence on milk yield and quality.7,8

In contrast, the fecal microbiome reflects the microbial composition of the lower gut and differs significantly among cattle breeds. Studies have indicated that genetic factors play a particularly important role in determining the composition of the fecal microbiome, with distinct microbial profiles being observed in Holstein and Jersey cows, even under identical dietary and management conditions.9 These findings accordingly indicate that breed-specific traits influence the gut microbiota, potentially determining the overall health and performance of animals.10,11 Additionally, the fecal microbiome serves as a non-invasive indicator of gut health, providing valuable information on the metabolic status and potential health-related issues in dairy cattle.12

Among dairy breeds, Holsteins are renowned for their high milk production, whereas Jerseys are recognized for their superior milk quality, particularly fat content.13 Jeju Black cattle, a breed native to South Korea, are gaining attention with respect to their unique traits and adaptability to local conditions.14 It is thus reasonable to assume that comparative studies of the ruminal and fecal microbiomes in these breeds could provide important insights into the associations between microbial diversity and composition and the respective production traits and health profiles of these cattle. Additionally, elucidating the functional roles and interactions of microbial populations within the gastrointestinal ecosystem can potentially contribute to the development of breed-specific nutritional and management strategies for optimizing performance. In this study, we employed three taxonomic classification methods (BLAST, Greengenes2, and Silva), on the basis of which, we were able to reduce mis-classification and capture relevant microbiome differences, thereby enabling us to identify breed- and gastrointestinal tract-associated microbial biomarkers.

Materials and methods

Animals, diets, and sampling of rumen and fecal contents

Animal care and experimental procedures were performed in accordance with the guidelines of the National Institute of Animal Science (NIAS; Rural Development Administration, Cheonan 31000 and Jeju 63242, Republic of Korea) and Animal Care and Use Committee (approval number: NIAS-2024-070).

Eight Holstein, eight Jersey cows were raised at the NIAS research farm in Cheonan (latitude: 36.931745, longitude: 127.102376), while nine Jeju Black cattle were raised at the NIAS research farm in Jeju (latitude: 33.427674, longitude: 126.548268). Those animals were selected for sampling gastrointestinal contents. Rumen fluid samples were collected via stomach tubing from all Holstein and Jersey cows after morning feeding. To minimize salivary contamination, the initial 150 mL of collected ruminal fluid was discarded and, subsequently, 45 mL of rumen fluid was collected and placed in 50-mL conical tubes (Thermo Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA). In the case of Jeju Black cattle, rumen fluid samples were collected at the time of slaughter. All samples were stored in a freezer at −80 °C until used for DNA extraction and microbial community analysis.

Fecal samples were collected using a sterile swab kit (fecal swab collection and preservation system; Norgen Biotek, Ontario, Canada). These samples were collected from sites at approximately 5 cm within the rectum, immediately placed in an enclosed tube containing a preservative, and thereafter stored at −80 °C until used for DNA extraction.

The diets of cows were formulated to meet or exceed their energy requirements and were provided ad libitum as a total mixed ration to avoid the preferential selection of different dietary components (Table 1). A total of 20 kg of feed was made available to each cow daily at 09:00 and 16:00. No orts were produced by any of the assessed cows. The Jeju Black cattle were fed concentrates formulated based on the Hanwoo feeding standard and ‘Jeju native’ barnyard millet was provided ad libitum during the experimental period. All cows were housed in separate pens according to their breed and were provided with free access to clean drinking water.

DNA extraction and sequencing

Metagenomic DNA was extracted from the 25 samples of rumen fluid and feces using repeated bead-beating and column purification methods following the protocol described by Yu and Morrison.15 The V3-V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified from each of the extracted DNA samples using the primer pair 341 F (5′-CCTACGGGNGGCWGCAG-3′) and 805 R (5′-GACTACHVGGGTATCTAATCC-3′). Sequencing was performed commercially by Macrogen, Inc. (Seoul, Republic of Korea) using the Illumina MiSeq platform.

Amplicon sequence processing and taxonomic classification

Sample sequences were processed using QIIME2 (version 2023.07).16 Initially, primer sequences were removed using Cutadapt (version 4.6),17 followed by quality filtering (quality score ≥ 25), denoising, and the elimination of chimeric sequences using the DADA2 denoise-paired plugin.18 Taxonomic classification of representative sequences was performed using three reference databases, namely, NCBI, Greengenes2 (version 2022.10), and Silva (version 138).19,20 The NCBI taxonomic database was manually created using 27,294 16S RefSeq sequences (1,135 archaeal and 26,159 bacterial sequences) accessed on November 19th, 2024. These sequences were linked to their respective taxonomic lineages using a publicly available script on GitHub (https://github.com/dib-lab/2018-ncbi-lineages). To ensure consistency among the different taxonomic classifications, amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) assigned as ‘unassigned,’ ‘chloroplast,’ or ‘mitochondria’ were removed from all three datasets, resulting in a uniform ASV count table for downstream diversity analysis. Additionally, certain taxonomic subgroups within the Greengenes2 dataset, including Bacillota (i.e., Firmicutes A, B, C and D), Methanobrevibacter (i.e., A, B and D-1148), and Ruminococcus (i.e., C-58660, C-59129 and D), were manually consolidated to identify the core taxa that were consistently detected within at least two of the three taxonomy classification datasets.

Alpha- and beta-diversity and prediction of microbial phenotypes

A rarefied ASV count table was generated by averaging 1,000 iterations of rarefaction at a depth of 25,036 ASVs using the q2-repeat-rarefy plugin.21 Alpha-diversity indices, including the observed ASVs and Chao1 estimates, Shannon’s diversity, and the inverse Simpson index, were calculated from the average rarefied ASV count table. Microbial phenotypes, represented by high-level microbial metabolic activities linked to IMG genomes22 were inferred from 16S ASVs using the Phylogenetic Investigation of Communities by Reconstruction of Unobserved States 2 (PICRUSt2) tool.23 We inferred microbial traits using the phenotype prediction module of PICRUSt2 with the—pheno option. This module predicts whether each ASV is likely to possess specific phenotypic traits (e.g., fermentation, aerobic respiration, nitrate reduction) by applying ancestral state reconstruction on a reference phylogeny. Phenotypes curated from reference genomes are then extended to uncharacterized lineages using maximum-likelihood hidden-state prediction. Finally, the predicted traits are integrated with ASV abundance data to generate a community-level phenotype profile. The structure of the microbial community and predicted functional profiles were analysed based on Bray-Curtis dissimilarities.

Microbial interaction networks

Correlations between microbial genera were computed using the SparCC algorithm (https://github.com/scwatts/fastspar)24 to account for compositional data. Microbial co-occurrence and mutual-exclusion microbial interactions were assessed using co-expression differential network analysis (CoDiNA)25 with significant interactions (p ≤ 0.05) as the input. Only those interactions identified in at least two taxonomic datasets were visualized using Gephi.26 Genera with high degree centrality within the shared and exclusive microbial networks were determined using network centrality metrics, including weighted degree, closeness centrality, betweenness centrality, and authority scores.

Statistical analysis

Alpha-diversity indices of the ruminal and fecal microbiota were analysed using the GLIMMIX procedure in SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA), with cattle breed as a fixed effect. For comparisons between rumen and fecal samples, the section of the gastrointestinal tract was used as the fixed effect. Normality and homogeneity of variance were assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk and Levene tests, respectively, with all data meeting the requisite assumptions. Pairwise breed comparisons were performed using Scheffé adjustments for multiple tests. Differential abundance analysis of microbial phyla, genera, and phenotypes was performed using the ANCOM-BC2 method,27 with significance determined at an adjusted p-value of ≤ 0.05. Differences were examined among breeds for each of the assessed sections of the gastrointestinal tract, as well as for the different sections within each breed. The Holm–Bonferroni method was applied for multiple test corrections, and permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA), implemented in Past 4.0328 was used to statistically analyse microbial community structure and predict functional profiles. For each significant PERMANOVA result, pairwise PERMDISP analyses were performed using vegan R package to evaluate differences in dispersion variability.

For both microbial taxa and phenotypic data, only core features, defined as those present in at least one breed or gastrointestinal compartment, were considered major findings in this study. In addition, differentially abundant microbial taxa were retained only if these were identified in at least two taxonomic datasets and were not associated with uncultured, unidentified, or candidate taxonomic groups.

Results

Amplicon sequence and core microbiome data

After denoising and quality filtration, an average of 78,318 (min. 38,554 and max. 119,268) and 49,368 (min. 24,684 and max. 81,500) sequences were obtained for the ruminal and fecal microbiota, respectively. In terms of diversity analysis, for all samples, we obtained a minimum Good’s coverage of 99.3%.

On the basis of the criterion designated in this study and omnipresence, we identified two archaeal phyla (Methanobacteriota and Thermoplasmatota) and 16 bacterial phyla were defined as the core taxa within the gastrointestinal tracts of the three cattle breeds (Figure 1(A,B)). Among these phyla, Bacillota and Bacteroidota accounted for at least 82% of the relative abundance in both the rumen and fecal samples. Among the core genera, Prevotella, Anaeroplasma, Clostridium, Ruminococcus, Saccharofermentans, Succiniclasticum, Selenomonas, and Treponema were detected in all rumen samples obtained from all cattle breeds, whereas 21 genera were defined as the core taxa in the fecal microbiomes of the three cattle breeds (Table 2). Thirteen amino acid prototrophs (able to synthesize glycine, l-alanine, l-asparagine, l-aspartate, l-cysteine, l-glutamate, l-glutamine, l-lysine, l-proline, l-selenocysteine, l-serine, l-threonine, and l-valine) and two phenotypes associated with l-methionine synthesis, sucrose utilization, and sulfur reduction were identified as core microbial phenotypes in both the ruminal and fecal microbiomes (Supplementary Table 1).

Rumen and feces dominant taxa and microbial phenotypes

In comparisons of the abundance of ruminal and fecal microbial taxa in the gastrointestinal tracts of each cattle breed, we identified seven core phyla and 18 core genera that were differently habituated (Supplementary Table 2), among which, Patescibacteria, Prevotella, and Saccharofermentans were identified as rumen-dominant among all three cattle breeds, whereas Bacillota, Clostridium, Bacteroides, and Alloprevotella were feces-dominant. Notably, Ruminococcus showed opposite habituation patterns of dominance between the two breeds, being more abundant in the rumen of Holstein and more dominant in the feces of Jeju Black cattle. Among the core microbial phenotypes, we identified 17 that were differentially abundant between the ruminal and fecal microbiomes of at least one cattle breed. Two l-methionine synthesis phenotypes and l-threonine prototrophs were rumen-dominant, whereas l-selenocysteine synthesis, sulphur reduction, and xylose utilization were dominant in the feces (Supplementary Table 3). Moreover, whereas dissimilatory sulphate reduction was identified as being feces-dominant in Holstein cows, it was found to be rumen-dominant in Jeju Black cattle.

Although not significantly different, nor confirmed in the NCBI dataset, the genera Succiniclasticum and Frisingicoccus were detected exclusively in the rumen and feces, respectively (Supplementary Table 4).

Diversity analysis

A further notable finding was the opposite patterns of alpha-diversity between the fecal and ruminal microbiomes of Jeju Black cattle and the two commercial dairy breeds, with a significant difference in all analysed indices (Table 3). In Jeju Black cattle, microbial diversity in the rumen was established to be more pronounced than that in the feces, whereas in comparisons of the alpha-diversities of the rumen and feces of each cattle breed, there was higher diversity in the feces of Holstein and Jersey cows, and in the rumen of Jeju Black cattle (Supplementary Table 5). We detected significant differences for all pairwise gastrointestinal microbiome comparisons, with the exception of the similar functional communities in Holstein and Jersey cows based on microbial phenotypes (Table 4).

Differentially abundant microbial taxa among cattle breeds

Comparisons of the ruminal and fecal microbiomes of Holstein and Jersey cows revealed no significant differentially abundant bacterial and archaeal phyla or genera or their corresponding phenotypes, whereas these microbes and their phenotypes were only determined as dominant taxa either in commercial dairy breeds (Holstein and Jersey) or in Jeju Black cattle (Figure 2(A,B)). With respect to the rumen microbiome, Bacillota, Bacteroidota, Cyanobacteriota, Patescibacteria, and Pseudomonadota were established to be predominant phyla in the commercial dairy breeds, whereas Chloroflexota and Planctomycetota were identified as dominant phyla in Jeju Black cattle. In addition, Methanobacteriota, which was dominant in the fecal microbiome of Jeju Black, was the only phylum that showed a distinct distribution. Among the core genera, Prevotella, Ruminococcus, and Succinivibrio were dominant in the rumen, and eight bacterial genera (Bifidobacterium, Alloprevotella, Bacteroides, Phocaeicola, Alistipes, Parabacteroides, Pygmaiobacter, and Phascolarctobacterium) predominated in the feces of the commercial dairy breeds (Figure 3(A,B)). In addition Methanobrevibacter and Mogibacterium were identified as predominating in both the ruminal and fecal microbiomes of Jeju Black cattle, whereas other dominant bacterial genera in this cattle breed, including Campylobacter, Flexilinea, Desulfovibrio, Streptococcus, Butyrivibrio, and Pyramidobacter in the rumen, and Clostridium, Blautia, and Turicibacter in the feces, showed at least one log-fold difference compared with those in the commercial dairy breeds with respect to their bias-corrected log abundances.

Differentially abundant microbial phenotypes among cattle breeds

In the case of the ruminal microbiome, among the three assessed breeds, differentially abundant microbial phenotypes were detected only in Jeju Black cattle, represented by the microbial phenotypes associated with coenzyme A, l-leucine, and l-selenocysteine synthesis, sugar utilization (lactose and sucrose), and dissimilatory sulphate and sulphur reduction (Figure 4(A)). Comparatively, within the fecal microbiome of commercial dairy breeds, 14 amino acids prototrophs or synthesis-related phenotypes and ‘aerobe’ phenotype were identified as being differentially abundant (Figure 4(B)), whereas only two sugar utilization microbial phenotypes were differentially abundant in the fecal microbiome of Jeju Black cattle compared with those in the two commercial dairy breeds.

Network analysis revealed shared and exclusive microbial interactions among cattle breeds

A comparison of rumen microbial interactions among the different cattle breeds revealed shared and exclusive networks among the core microbial genera (Figure 5(A,B)). For the two commercial breeds, we identified four exclusive nodes linked to 20 edges within the shared network. In both of the two exclusive networks associated with the ruminal microbiota of Jersey cows, Ruminococcus and Schwartzia were identified as exclusive nodes. Furthermore, based on the network centrality measurements obtained for each shared or exclusive network, Mogibacterium in the Holstein and Jersey shared network and Anaerovibrio and Ruminococcus in the exclusive Jersey network compared with those of Holstein and Jeju Black cattle, respectively, were designated as genera with high degree centrality. Comparatively, we failed to identify any shared or exclusive microbial networks with regards to the fecal microbial interactions.

Discussion

To date, studies examining the differences in gastrointestinal microbiomes among cattle breeds have tended to focus primarily on identifying breed-specific microbial signatures that can serve as biomarkers for desirable traits, such as feed efficiency, methane reduction, and milk production.29,30 For comparative purposes, in this study, in addition to two commercial dairy breeds, we assessed Jeju Black cattle, a native Korean breed with low genetic heterozygosity, which may contribute to the unique physiological and genetic characteristics of these cattle. These distinct features have enabled us to identify different ruminal and fecal microbial biomarkers that could potentially be used to modulate the gastrointestinal microbiome and thereby enhance productivity, whilst minimizing potentially detrimental environmental effects. Moreover, integrating data from three different taxonomic classification databases and focusing on consistently identified core taxa enabled us to identify more robust breed-related biomarkers, whilst reducing the risk of mis-classification.

Potential dietary effects on the ruminal and fecal microbiota

In dairy cows, diet plays a prominent role in shaping the gut microbiota. In this context, the Holstein and Jersey cows assessed in this study were fed a total mixed ration containing similar feed ingredients, whereas Jeju Black cattle received a local forage separately with concentrates, including a higher proportion of fibrous material. We accordingly found that these dietary differences contributed to a greater variation in the ruminal microbiome than in the fecal microbiome, which we assume to be attributable to the fact that the rumen is the primary site in which ingested feed undergoes microbial digestion.31,32 This assumption is further supported by the significantly higher alpha-diversity in the rumen of Jeju Black cattle than in commercial dairy breeds, which would tend to reflect the need for a more complex microbial community to efficiently digest fibre-rich dietary constituents.33,34 In line with expectations, we detected significant inter-breed differences in the overall microbial communities in both the rumen and feces, although this did not appear to correspond to any substantial differences in the functional profiles of Holstein and Jersey gut microbiotas. The findings of previous studies in this regard have revealed that even when subjected to identical feeding conditions, the rumen microbiota of Holstein and Jersey cows can differ significantly, thereby providing evidence to indicate inherent breed-specific effects on the composition of the microbiota.5,35 However, as a consequence of functional redundancy, wherein different microbial species may perform similar metabolic activities, differences in microbial composition may not necessarily be reflected in functional differences.36 Accordingly, this may indicate that whereas there may be clear differences in the gut microbial communities among different breeds, their core metabolic functions may remain essentially conserved.

Differentially abundant microbial taxa among cattle breeds

Among the key finding of this study is our identification of the genus Prevotella as the predominant microbial taxon in the rumen of all three assessed breeds of cattle, the abundance of which was particularly evident in Holstein and Jersey cows. This bacterial genus is frequently implicated in determining the feed efficiency in dairy cattle,37 notably with respect to their prominent role in amino acid and carbohydrate metabolism, thereby contributing to efficient nutrient digestion and utilization.38 Moreover, certain Prevotella isolates have also been shown to be associated with low methane and high propionate production, which may contribute to enhancing energy utilization by dairy cows.39

Compared with the other assessed breeds, we found that the fecal microbiome of Holstein cows was characterized by a higher abundance of bacteria in the genus Bifidobacterium, which have previously been linked to dietary shifts from high-forage to high-concentrate feeds.40 The predominance of this bacterial genus in commercial dairy breeds is associated with elevated levels of volatile fatty acid production, which provides an additional source of energy for host animals.41

Interestingly, we found that a major methanogenic archaeal genus Methanobrevibacter, which, given their abundance and activity,42 were enriched in both the ruminal and fecal microbiomes of Jeju Black cattle. In this regard, although it has been suggested that native cattle breeds may have a more pronounced capacity for methane production,43,44 this activity could conceivably be influenced by their dietary input, which typically consists of lower-quality forage that can result in higher methane yields or intensities.45

Additionally, whereas we failed to detect any breed-associated patterns for certain taxa, Succiniclasticum was found exclusively in the rumen, whereas Frisingicoccus was detected only in the feces. Succiniclasticum is among the most prevalent rumen bacteria globally,46 whereas strains of Frisingicoccus have been identified primarily in healthy dairy cows,47 providing further evidence to indicate the healthy status of the animals assessed in this present study.

Differentially abundant microbial phenotypes among cattle breeds

Compared with Jeju Black cattle, commercial dairy breeds exhibited significantly higher abundances of 12 amino acid prototrophs and two methionine synthesis-associated microbial phenotypes, which we speculate could be associated with the higher nutritional demands of milk-producing cows, leading to an enhanced passage of nitrogenous compounds to the lower gastrointestinal tract.48 Given the greater availability of nitrogen within the hindgut, the conditions herein would be particularly conducive to amino acid prototrophs. However, as there have been no direct comparisons of nitrogen excretion under identical feeding conditions for these cow breeds, it is conceivable that higher levels of dietary crude protein, which are typically provided for high-producing dairy cows,49 contribute to an enrichment of these microbial phenotypes in the feces of Holstein and Jersey cows.

In contrast, we detected notably higher levels of sulphur metabolism in the rumen of Jeju Black cattle, as indicated by a higher abundance of Desulfovibrio, which we speculate could be associated with a prolonged ruminal retention time necessitated by the ingestion of a more fibrous feed. Longer retention times are generally conducive to a more comprehensive microbial degradation of proteins and amino acids, potentially reducing the availability of nutrient within the small intestine.50 Whereas sulphate-reducing bacteria such as Desulfovibrio can theoretically compete with methanogens for hydrogen, methane production might not be reduced in Jeju Black cattle, given the concurrent enrichment of Methanobrevibacter, and it is thus plausible that the balance between these microbial groups may be influenced by the availability of sulfate in the rumen and the hydrogen produced from fibre-rich diets.51,52

Within the rumens of Jeju Black cattle, we also detected higher levels of coenzyme A synthesis associated with sugar fermentation, along with a higher abundance of Streptococcus, thereby providing evidence of higher levels of propionate production. This is consistent with the findings of a previous in vitro fermentation study using a rumen fluid inoculum from Jeju Black cattle,53 particularly during the early post-feeding period. However, further validation based on direct measurements of fermentation profiles under controlled feeding conditions is necessary in this regard.

Shared and exclusive microbial interactions identified using network analysis

Compared with that in Jeju Black cattle, network analysis revealed that Mogibacterium was a genus with high degree centrality in the exclusive microbial network of commercial dairy breeds. This genus produces phenylacetate as a fermentation end-product,54 which serves as a growth factor for Ruminococcus.55 Similar patterns of association between Mogibacterium and Ruminococcus and Succinivibrio have been detected in RNA-seq data obtained for beef cattle,56 thus tending to indicate that these microbial interactions may be widespread in the rumen of commercial dairy breeds.

Although a majority of the microbial biomarkers identified in the commercial breeds were detected in both Holstein and Jersey cows, these two breeds were also characterized by unique microbial interactions. For example, Anaerovibrio, identified as a genus with high degree centrality in the ruminal microbiome of Jersey cows, can utilize lactate and produce acetate, succinate, and propionate,57,58 and positive association between Anaerovibrio and Schwartzia (a succinate utilizer)59 and Succinivibrio (a lactate producer)60 indicate potential mechanisms for enhancing propionate production in Jersey cows, which may contribute to more efficient energy utilization for milk production than in Holsteins.61

Furthermore, we identified positive associations between Ruminococcus and Flexilinea and Saccharofermentans, both genera of which play key roles in carbohydrate degradation within the rumen.62,63 This interaction may be enhanced via syntrophic cooperation with Methanobrevibacter, thereby facilitating the utilization of hydrogen and formate62 and also highlights the roles of Ruminococcus species as central players in the ruminal microbial network.

Limitations of this study and cautious interpretation of breed differences

Although this study identified distinct microbial biomarkers across cattle breeds, several limitations should be considered when interpreting the results. First, unequal feeding conditions such as geographical location and dietary regimes as well as differences in rumen fluid sampling methods may have influenced the outcomes. While no direct comparison has yet been made between the sampling methods used here (stomach tubing vs. postmortem collection at slaughter), previous work comparing stomach tubing and rumen cannulation has shown that sampling strategy can affect microbiome composition.64 In this study, we relied on squeezing for postmortem samples, which likely enriched the liquid-associated fraction of the rumen microbiota and is therefore comparable to stomach tubing. Nevertheless, future research should directly compare sampling methods across multiple microbiome perspectives to validate such assumptions.

Second, although fecal microbiota is generally less influenced by diet, our findings on predicted microbial phenotypes in fecal samples from commercial dairy breeds should be interpreted with caution. The predictions were based on ASVs, which have lower functional resolution compared to shotgun metagenomics.65 Advances such as manual genome database updates in PICRUSt2-SC66 may improve accuracy in future analyses.

Finally, our network analysis was based on rumen fluid sampled at a single time point. Without longitudinal data, it is difficult to assess network stability over time.67 Therefore, biomarkers associated with cattle breeds identified here should be considered as exploratory and warrant future validation with sufficient number of animals per breed.

Conclusion

In this study, we compared the ruminal and fecal microbiomes of three cattle breeds under different feeding and environmental conditions based on an integration of data obtained from three taxonomic classification datasets to identify robust microbial biomarkers associated with feed utilization and animal productivity. We identified certain differences among the assessed cattle breeds with respect to the compositions of the microbiota colonizing the gastrointestinal tract, with distinct core taxa in both the rumen and feces differentiating commercial dairy breeds from the indigenous Jeju Black cattle. Interestingly, commercial dairy breeds were found to be characterized by an enrichment of amino acid prototrophs in feces, whereas the rumen of Jeju Black cattle was established to harbour a higher abundance of sulphur-metabolizing microbial phenotypes, which may have potential implications for nitrogen utilization and methane emissions in ruminants. However, due to the limitation of using different diets, sampling ages, feeding conditions, and rumen fluid sampling methods in addition to the lack of comprehensive animal performance measurements, such as those of body weight, residual feed intake, milk yield, and fermentation characteristics need to be incorporated to declare those differences are actually from breed-specific differences. An integrative approach can contribute to enhancing the identification of associations between microbial biomarkers and production traits and provide more comprehensive insights into the functional roles played by the gut microbiota in ruminant nutrition and sustainability.
