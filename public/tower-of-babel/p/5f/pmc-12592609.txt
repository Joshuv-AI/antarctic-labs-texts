Clinicians’ perspectives of managing common mental disorders among pregnant women in primary care settings: a qualitative exploratory study
Authors: Sandra Fremah Asare, Samuel Adjorlolo, Petra Brysiewicz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrimary antenatal care providers are seen as instrumental in shaping healthy pregnancy outcomes. However, there are organizational and system-level barriers that hinder the needed support for mental health care among pregnant women and there remains a dearth of research exploring the specific challenges and opportunities encountered by clinicians in providing mental health support to pregnant women with common mental disorders (CMDs) in the prenatal period.AimThis study explored clinicians’ (obstetric providers) perspectives in managing CMDs among women during the prenatal period.MethodsThis study employed a qualitative exploratory design. Twelve interviews were conducted with clinicians (doctors and midwives) working in primary care settings, guided by a semi-structured interview guide. Data were analyzed using qualitative content analysis.ResultsManaging CMDs in pregnancy was described as not a priority as illustrated in five categories: many warning signs, relying on own experiences, no time, overwhelmed in a role, and working with families.ConclusionsThis study's findings suggest that primary healthcare obstetric practice does not have the required training in mental health among clinicians to screen and treat for CMDs in pregnant women in Ghana. Therefore, healthcare facilities must prioritize the integration of mental health services within routine obstetric care, ensuring that pregnant women have access to comprehensive mental health interventions alongside their routine antenatal care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s44192-025-00164-y.

Background

Common mental disorders (CMDs) in pregnancy are a group of frightening conditions including depression, anxiety, and unexplained somatic symptoms normally encountered in primary care settings [1]. The World Health Organization (WHO, 2022) also defines common mental health disorders (CMDs) as a spectrum that includes temporary, moderate-to-serious mental health conditions. That impact an individual’s including a woman's functioning abilities and disruptions in mental well-being for less than two weeks or more [2, 3]. For Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 3—health and well-being to be achieved, there has been a global call to action for improved maternal mental health care [4–6] and better neonatal outcomes.

Pregnancy is typically perceived as a joyous moment in the life of women as it marks a phase where a new individual is added to the family. Despite the happiness that accompanies pregnancy, it may also be characterized by some mental health disorders [7]. According to the WHO (2022), one in ten women in high-income countries will experience mental health conditions during pregnancy or within the first year post-pregnancy. In contrast, one in five women in Low-and-Middle-Income Countries (LMICs), will experience nonpsychotic mental health disorders during pregnancy or within one year after pregnancy [3, 8]. This makes, mental health conditions a serious public concern in LMICs and Ghana, a sub-Saharan African country.

The extant literature indicates these CMDs have adverse health effects [3, 9]. Specifically, CMDs in the prenatal period have been implicated in immediate and direct impacts such as preterm births, low birth weight, frequent hospitalizations, and infant mortality. Women who experience CMDs have compromised antenatal care (ANC) attendance and an increased risk of suicidal ideation is also found among pregnant women [2, 10, 11]. Available evidence suggests that less education, low socioeconomic status, post-traumatic stressors, low social support, domestic violence, poor physical health before pregnancy, and unintended pregnancies constitute factors that exacerbate the risk of CMDs in the prenatal period [12–14]. Thus, making it imperative to understand the nuances that characterize CMD in the prenatal period.

According to the International Council of Nurses (2024), nurses and midwives are specially positioned in healthcare settings to combat stigmatization and discrimination and to ensure equal access to care while respecting the dignity and autonomy of pregnant women with mental health conditions [15]. Recent research shows however that some clinicians providing obstetric care have little knowledge of mental disorders in pregnancy and there is a lack of screening initiatives leading to underdiagnosis, undertreatment, and inadequate support for affected pregnant women [16]. During the COVID-19 pandemic, depressive and anxiety symptoms were reportedly more prevalent in pregnancy than they were in pre-COVID-19 and research showed a correlation between increased depression symptoms and loneliness [17]. Evidence also suggests that pregnant women in Sub-Saharan Africa have limited access to specialized perinatal mental health services and therefore rely on support systems and self-care for their mental health and well-being [18].

Clinicians who provide obstetric care are seen as the primary carers of women in the perinatal period and are well-placed to support their physical and mental health [18]. Obstetric providers who serve primary antenatal care are seen as instrumental in shaping healthy pregnancy outcomes. However, there are organizational and system-level barriers that hinder the needed support for prenatal mental health [19]. A recent literature highlighted the inability of obstetric providers to recognize and address their mental health challenges and this served as a barrier to mental health service provision in the antenatal care settings [20]. A key determinant of proper mental healthcare is the improvement of mental health literacy for pregnant women and their families as well as woman-centered care [46, 55]. Yet not much is done to facilitate the implementation of such policies especially in Ghana.

Within the context of Ghana, a recent study conducted in two major tertiary hospitals among obstetric providers revealed that psychiatrist consult was perceived to be for people with severe mental illness [21] thereby hindering the provision and acceptance of psychiatric support for pregnant women. Studies have investigated aspects of CMDs in the prenatal period, with one study that was conducted in three public health facilities in Ghana revealing that the prevalence of prenatal depression ranged between 8.6 percent and 41.1 percent [22]. Another study conducted among pregnant women found 32.7%, 27.4% and 9% suffering from mild, moderate, and severe depression, respectively [23, 24] Based on the preceding, it is evident that CMDs in the prenatal period are a public health concern in Ghana.

Earlier studies conducted in Ghana have also focused primarily on estimating the prevalence and examining the associated factors of CMD [25]. Those that have adopted qualitative methodologies have predominantly focused on the experiences of women navigating their CMDs throughout the prenatal and postnatal period [12, 26], and quantitatively the challenges that childbearing women face in accessing mental health care services [27]. To the best of our knowledge, after an extensive literature search, none of the emerging studies on perinatal CMDs in Ghana have explored the opportunities and challenges that characterize the management of CMDs from the perspective of clinicians. Primary antenatal care providers are seen as instrumental in shaping healthy pregnancy outcomes. However, there are organizational and system-level barriers that hinder the needed support for prenatal mental health care and there remains a dearth of research exploring the specific challenges and opportunities encountered by clinicians in providing mental health support to pregnant women with common mental disorders (CMDs) in the prenatal period. Thus, there is a lack of understanding of the nuances of managing CMDs. This presents a significant knowledge gap for empirical scholarly attention. To help narrow the knowledge gap, this study aimed to explore clinicians’ perspectives of managing CMDs among women in the prenatal period. Specifically, the study sets out to respond to the following questions:

How do clinicians manage CMDs among women in the prenatal period?

How do clinicians manage CMDs among women in the prenatal period?

Methods

Study design

A qualitative exploratory design was chosen to explore relatively uncharted territory within the context of perinatal mental health care in Ghana. Qualitative exploratory research allows for an in-depth exploration of participants' views, attitudes, and experiences, providing rich and nuanced insights into the phenomenon under investigation [28]. An open-ended questionnaire was developed to serve as an interview guide and helped clinicians providing obstetric care to pregnant women who suffer from CMD to explain. This method highlighted clinicians’ perspectives and described the knowledge gaps [29]. This study design was useful as little is known about clinicians' perspectives in providing maternal mental healthcare in antenatal care settings [30]. COREQ checklist for reporting qualitative study guided this study (supplementary file 1)[31].

Study setting

For this study, three hospitals, designated as A B, and C were strategically chosen due to their reputation as state-managed health facilities offering comprehensive primary-level antenatal and obstetric care as indicated in Fig. 1.

Map of Ghana showing study sites.

Source—Principal investigator (2024)

Site A runs the largest antenatal care services at the primary care level. The hospital is under the Ghana Health Service and is located within the Bantama constituency of the Ashanti Region. The maternity clinic, which is highly patronized provides services to an average of 169 pregnant women every month and 1053 pregnant women for antenatal care annually (Suntreso Gov’t Hosp, 2024).

Site B is affiliated with the Christian Health Association of Ghana (CHAG), and serves a population of 154,526 (GSS, 2021). It offers general, surgical, and maternal care services for both out-patients and in-patients, attracting pregnant women from Kwadaso Municipal and surrounding areas and patients from the Ahafo and Bono East regions. According to the hospital’s records the maternity clinic attends to an average of 70 pregnant women monthly and 918 annually.

Site C is a peri-urban state-owned hospital within Kumasi under the Atwima Nwabiagya North Municipal, which serves a population of 155,025. It provides general, obstetric/gynecological, and surgical services. Per the maternity’s statistics of registrants, the clinic also provides care to at least 50 pregnant women monthly and 540 annually.

Research participants

A purposive sampling technique was adopted to recruit and select the sample. The rationale behind the adoption of a purposive sampling technique was to ensure that we included only persons who were knowledgeable about and participated in the management of CMDs, thus ensuring we gathered rich and insightful perspectives that would offer comprehensive insights into the aims of the study [32, 33]. Participants were approached if they met the following inclusion criteria: (a) be an obstetric provider (i.e., a Registered Midwife or Obstetrician/Gynecologist); (b) working at the antenatal clinics of the selected healthcare facilities for at least 6 months; (c) be available at the healthcare facility at the time of the data collection. Out of the 17 eligible clinicians identified for participation in the study, we conducted interviews with twelve individuals. This decision was taken by the research team as by the 9th interview, no new analytical insights were emerging from the interviews. Following Francis et al.'s (2010) recommended criterion, three additional interviews were included to ensure data saturation had been reached.

Interview and data collection

Informed by previous literature [12, 34], a semi-structured interview guide comprising open-ended questions that explored the perspectives of clinicians was designed to aid in the data collection and answer the research questions: how do clinicians manage CMDs among women in the prenatal period, and the challenges during management. The interview guide was structured into four sections [30]. The first section solicited the participants’ demographic profiles. The second section explored the clinicians’ perspectives of the symptoms of CMDs that are usually presented whereas the third focused on how cases of CMDs are managed. The final section explored the clinicians’ perspectives on the opportunities and barriers to screening and managing the CMDs. Questions were redirected or probed further to ensure interviewees responded according to the study’s objective and enhanced clarity [35]. The interview guide was pilot-tested with three (3) midwives at the lying-in ward of a private maternity clinic in Kumasi-Ghana to ensure that the questions were appropriate. All face-to-face interviews were conducted by the first author (SFA) who has clinical and academic (teaching and research) experience in maternal mental healthcare. Data collection was carried out from March to April 2024. Before each interview, the first author provided information regarding the study, reassured the participants of confidentiality, anonymity, and the right to refuse to answer questions, and then obtained consent. Each interview was audio recorded with permission of the participants who also decided the date, time, and venue for the interview. Participants selected the premises of their health facility as the location for the interview. The doctors chose their offices whilst the midwives selected an empty consulting rooms after the close of work. The interviews lasted between 30 and 60 min and were conducted in English. The interviewer does not work in any of the study settings and hence had no direct influence on the study setting and participants but could identify with them because she is a registered mental nurse which strengthened the validity of the study.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed concurrently with data collection [36] and adopted a manifest content analysis [37]. Each audio recording was transcribed verbatim and analysis was done through a four-stage condensation process [38] where the visible, obvious components of the data were described to interpret the data at a minimal level of abstraction while focusing on answering the research question [37]. Within the transcripts, meaning units (significant phrases, statements, and expressions of their management experience) were identified, condensed and coded, with the codes then combined to form categories [36]. See Table 1. All authors engaged in discussions throughout this analysis process to identify and refine the emerging categories, aiming for clear and comprehensive descriptions that accurately conveyed participants' perspectives. Subsequently, QSR NVivo-14 (QSR International, Doncaster, Australia) a qualitative software was utilized for efficient data management and coding. The lead author (SFA) initially analyzed the data and the research supervisors (SA) and (PB) confirmed the findings to ensure that the realities of the participants were accurately presented.

Rigor and trustworthiness

Qualitative research relies on the capacity of researchers to demonstrate rigor and trustworthiness [39]. The following criteria were used to ensure the trustworthiness of this study; credibility, confirmability, dependability, and transferability [40]. The analysis was grounded in the verbatim narratives provided by the clinicians, a method chosen to bolster the credibility of our study. This approach ensured that the insights gleaned directly reflected the perspectives of the participants. In line with Shenton's (2004) recommendations, we ensured that all participants were allowed to accept or refuse to participate in the study. As such, only persons who showed interest voluntarily participated in the study [41]. The detailed description of the study setting, design, methodology, and the participants’ background provided the potential for replication and applicability of the findings in similar settings to ensure transferability [40]. To achieve confirmability, feedback from the interview was given back to participants to explore if the data accurately captured their perspectives of CMD management and if they agreed to the interpretation. Where necessary, some changes were made to ensure appropriate wording to reflect their perspectives accurately. Detailed field notes taken at each interview and discussion of findings among all authors ensured the auditability of the study [42].

Findings

Five categories emerged from the manifest content analysis: many warning signs, relying on own experiences, no time, being overwhelmed in a role, and working with families (Table 1).

Categories

Many warning signs

The participants reported that pregnant women on their initial visit usually complained of experiencing mild headaches, loss of appetite, lower abdominal pains, waist pain, frequent urination, and insomnia. These symptoms appear to intensify and diversify in the course of pregnancy which often leads to first consultations with the ANC.

An obstetrician/gynecologist said:

“Usually in the early pregnancy period, it is vomiting, lower abdominal pain. As pregnancy goes to the second trimester and the third trimester then we have the bleeding from the vagina” (FYNN).

Clinicians also added that as pregnancy progresses, poor socio-economic background and unplanned pregnancies could predispose women to aggravating physical symptoms amongst emotional, psychological and mental health challenges underscoring the intricate link between somatic symptoms and underlying CMD.

A participant stated:

‘’Also if you look at our clients, their socio-economic status and the fact that the pregnancies are also unplanned, when they don’t get partner support, when they don’t get family and social support, it predisposes them to mental health risk’’ (NURU).

With regard to mental symptoms, clinicians explained that the anticipation of delivery, coupled with the fear and uncertainty about the process, contributes substantially to their psychological distress. This result is highlighted in the quotes below:

A gynecologist shared this perspective:

“What most people complain about is depression and anxiety, fear especially when it is approaching their delivery. Especially for those who have never been through the process before.” (NANA).

According to the clinicians, pregnant women exhibiting mental symptoms often present with marked moodiness and silence, which can initially obscure the recognition of a mental health challenge.

Another midwife stated:

“I see them to be very reserved, not willing to talk, they are absent-minded, you can see that their focus is not on you and the more severe form of mental illness” (MARY).

Relying on own experiences

The lack of structured guidance/procedures leaves obstetric clinicians reliant on their training and experience, which can result in inconsistent and potentially suboptimal care. The clinicians expressed that as a result of the absence of guidelines and protocols, they had to draw upon knowledge and practices acquired during their training rotations.

“We don’t have laid down procedures to follow when you get a case of mental illness in pregnancy so, you the nurse have to use your learnt ideas to treat the patient” (KEZIA).

NURU asserted that they typically rely on their clinical judgment and the informal sharing of best practices, as there are no printed or widely distributed protocols tailored to the mental health challenges commonly encountered:

“No, I haven’t seen one. Not just in pregnancy maybe the psychiatric ward. Even at the emergency, so basically what we do is that, we rely on our experiences; we have not printed anything. If we do that, it will be based on the common conditions that we see. Unfortunately, we don’t have anything like that.”

The participants emphasized the need for clear, structured protocols for taking patient histories, especially concerning mental health. These protocols would provide a standardized approach to identifying and responding to mental health issues, ensuring that healthcare professionals ask the right questions and follow up appropriately based on patient responses. This is epitomized in NURU’S narration.

“If a guideline is out there, it makes it easier for non-psychiatric background health professionals to be able to administer because, it will be easy. If it is a guideline, you will just follow” (NURU).

Overwhelmed in a role

Another important challenge we identified was the unavailability of a multidisciplinary team to manage CMDs. In the view of the clinicians, their facilities did not have specialists like resident psychiatrists and psychologists. Moreover, there were times when the gynecologists had to manage CMD cases because the mental nurses might have closed and gone home. These assertions are evident in the quotes below:

“It is mental health nurses only. We do not have a psychiatric doctor” (RITA).

This statement was corroborated by GRACE:

“It is the gynecologist who manages the patients and gives them medication. When they want to call in the psychiatric nurses to help, in case it happens in the evening or midnight, they do what they can to control the client. In the morning, we call the psychiatric nurses to come and intervene as well.”

No time

Another challenge we identified from the content analysis was the seemingly neglect of mental health in the facilities. In their views, the high patient volumes, the emergency gynecological and obstetric cases and time constraints limit their ability to conduct thorough mental health assessments. In such environments, the focus tends to be on immediate physical health concerns, with mental health inquiries reduced to cursory questions about past mental health history.

“Unfortunately, here, we don’t even have that time. You see how busy this place is, when you come here, we screen you and just ask if you have a history of mental health” (NURU).

A specialist also asserted:

“It is basically a neglected area. As the saying goes, if you don’t look for something, you don’t find it and because people don’t pay much attention to mental health in the country and invariably clinicians have also become victims in that area” (NANA).

Our analysis revealed that the management of common mental disorders (CMD) within maternal healthcare often involves a systematic referral process to ensure that patients receive specialized care. Upon the initial detection of a mental health issue, the patient is promptly assigned to a psychiatric nurse for an in-depth assessment of her condition.

“I think the first visit or immediately we detect that she is having that problem (mental health challenges), we have to assign that client to a psychiatric nurse” (AKUA).

Working with families

Collaboration between clinicians and the family of a woman living with CMDs was another way of managing cases. In the perspective of the clinicians, this collaborative approach leverages the family's intimate knowledge of the patient and their ability to provide ongoing support, which is crucial for the patient's mental health and overall well-being. Family members are used to better understand the patient's condition and to utilize familiar and calming influences.

“If she comes with a relative, most of the time, we liaise with the relative in the course of the care. The relatives are more involved since she has a mental challenge, most of the time, we entreat that we involve a third party to assist” (ADWOA).

Discussion

This article presents the interpretative description of antenatal care providers’ current practices and perspectives regarding the management of CMDs among pregnant women in primary care settings. Our findings show that though pregnant women exhibited both physical changes and mental health symptoms, clinicians focus usually centered on the woman and baby’s physical health during routine antenatal care and less attention on mental health. This is consistent with other studies which found that midwives always focus on the physical health of the mother and baby thereby reducing the opportunity to initiate conversations about mental health issues [18, 43]. Hormonal and external factors such as unplanned pregnancies, poverty, and lack of partner support may precipitate a mental disorder in pregnancy. It is described as a disorder since it is mostly linked with psychological distress, emotional instability, and altered physical functioning abilities. This is consistent with a recent study [44]. Depression, anxiety, withdrawal, and non-participatory behavior were reported to be among the presented symptoms pregnant women with CMDs exhibited. This aligns with the WHO’s (2022) report that identifies depression and the feeling of having no pleasure in activities as common symptoms of CMD among women in the prenatal period [45]. The result is further corroborated by an earlier study conducted in Ghana [34] that found depression and anxiety to be among the symptoms that healthcare professionals look out for when assessing women in the prenatal period. However, clinicians may normalize symptoms presented by pregnant women or dismiss them which may also be a factor for underutilization of antenatal care services. Our findings corroborate other studies [46, 47]. Although CMDs in pregnancy are as common as obstetric emergency cases and may supersede their prevalence, much energy is channeled to obstetric cases as clinicians view them as being the primary cause of maternal mortality. This implies an absence of optimal mental health legislation and policies that promote equitable health systems including maternal mental health care. This is in line with previous studies [43, 48]. The participants found a key facilitator- thus if mental health assessment and treatment were part of standard care in antenatal settings, it would be more acceptable and less stigmatizing. This is in line with a previous study [49]. Evidence from this study also shows that overburdened and busy antenatal clinics limit the opportunities for detection, diagnosis, and treatment as clinicians confirmed there was no time for mental health screening as much concentration was on obstetric emergencies.

This study also highlights the significance of referral in managing CMDs. Our finding is consistent with [50] whose study emphasizes the relevance of timely referrals to the overall management of CMDs. Thus, referrals enhance the likelihood of early intervention and comprehensive treatment by facilitating timely connections between primary care providers and mental health specialists. Our study indicates that referral of CMDs contributes to continuity of care, ensuring that individuals receive ongoing support and monitoring throughout their mental health journey. Previous literature [51] has shown that the risk of CMDs including depression is high in situations where women find themselves in dysfunctional families. However, the present study revealed that clinicians also value the role of the family in managing CMDs among women during the prenatal period. As such, they worked together with the patients’ families to enhance the care provision, communication, and medication adherence.

While clinicians execute their tasks by referring, working with the patients’ families, and approaching patients in a person-centered manner, some factors serve as impediments to the effective management of CMDs. The major challenge as revealed in this study is the absence of guidelines and protocols. Similar findings have been reported by Falek et al. (2022) who identified the lack of consensus across guidelines as a challenge to managing depression in the prenatal period [52]. In the same vein, Dadi et al. (2021) corroborate our findings that the lack of guidelines, manuals, and treatment protocols is the major roadblock to the effective management of CMDs in pregnancy [53]. Without standardized procedures in place, clinicians may lack clear directives on how to systematically identify, assess, and respond to mental health issues in this population. This lack of guidance can result in inconsistent approaches to CMD management, leading to potential gaps or inefficiencies in care delivery as clinicians rely on their past experiences to provide care. Moreover, the absence of protocols may contribute to delays in diagnosis and treatment initiation, exacerbating the burden of CMDs on pregnant women and their families. Another key challenge that emerged from this study was the absence of a multidisciplinary team, especially resident psychiatrists to handle CMD cases which made midwives use their discretions and most of the time rely on their experience to manage pregnant women with CMDs. Previous studies [54–56] have highlighted that the absence of multidisciplinary teams to handle CMD cases is often due to the lack of training or education of healthcare professionals about CMDs. Without a multidisciplinary approach, there may be missed opportunities for holistic care coordination and collaboration, as different aspects of pregnant women's mental health and well-being may go unaddressed.

At the organizational level, the findings show that one of the key barriers to detecting mental symptoms was a lack of adequate training in mental health to prepare obstetric providers to address maternal mental health issues. This is consistent with a recent review [4]. In the face of the challenges, the clinicians identified some opportunities that can be leveraged to enhance the screening and management of CMDs. Among these opportunities is the call for integrating CMDs into the pre-service training curriculum of healthcare professionals. The result aligns with Santana Parrilla (2020) who also calls for an incorporation of management of CMD in the training curriculum of healthcare professionals. Similarly, Noonan et al. [54] call for continuous professional development to ensure that all general practitioners have adequate knowledge to manage CMDs. This incorporation is of utmost importance because, in a resource-constrained setting like Ghana, it is uncommon to have all healthcare facilities have the full complement of a multidisciplinary team. However, when all healthcare professionals are equipped with the knowledge to manage CMDs, they can function effectively without a specialist thus promoting task-shifting. The study also indicates that the integration should go beyond the training curriculum and infuse it into the routine ANC sessions. By incorporating mental health screening and education into routine ANC sessions, clinicians can proactively identify and address mental health concerns at an early stage, thereby facilitating timely intervention and support for pregnant women experiencing CMDs.

Our study also underscores a need for clear guidelines, manuals, and treatment protocols for CMDs in the prenatal and postnatal period—a finding that mirrors that of McCauley et al. [12]. Having clear guidelines can help streamline the management process, providing clinicians with a structured framework for identifying risk factors, conducting screenings, and implementing appropriate interventions. Additionally, the availability of comprehensive manuals and treatment protocols can serve as valuable resources for clinicians, offering evidence-based guidance on best practices and interventions for addressing CMDs in pregnant women.

Strengths and limitations

The use of a qualitative exploratory design constitutes a strength of this study as it generated rich, nuanced insights into a relatively underexplored area of perinatal mental healthcare in the country to possibly uncover unforeseen factors that may influence CMD management in this context. Moreover, qualitative methods are well-suited for capturing the complexity and contextual nuances inherent in healthcare practices, allowing for a more holistic understanding of the challenges and opportunities clinicians encounter in providing care to pregnant women with CMDs.

However, some limitations should be acknowledged. Firstly, the study's reliance on self-reporting through in-depth interviews which is one data source may have introduced social desirability bias, as participants may have been inclined to provide responses they deemed socially acceptable. This is because the nurse with expertise in the area under research conducted the interviews although was not a worker over there. Furthermore, the study focuses only on clinicians' perspectives, future research should look into the viewpoints of other key stakeholders, such as pregnant women themselves and their families, whose experiences and perspectives may also be critical in understanding CMD management.

Conclusions

This study revealed some new findings indicating that the transitional period to motherhood which is the prenatal period also represents a vulnerability to mental health challenges and clinicians providing obstetric care need preparedness. Obstetric care providers must be proactive in promoting and safeguarding Sustainable Development Goals 3 (health and well-being) and 5 gender equity. There is also an urgent need for the development and implementation of standardized guidelines and protocols tailored to mental health assessments in maternal healthcare settings. This could be followed by capacity training and comprehensive supervision of clinicians especially midwives to provide maternal mental health care. In conclusion, there are challenges with the management of CMDs in expectant mothers in Ghana. Therefore, healthcare facilities must prioritize the integration of mental health services within routine obstetric care, ensuring that pregnant women have access to comprehensive mental health interventions alongside their routine antenatal care. Further research should examine the association between trust and self-disclosure during antenatal care by pregnant women. This research highlights the important role multidisciplinary team plays in all spheres of clinical care and it is recommended that obstetric providers and mental health specialists collaborate effectively in managing maternal mental disorders in the perinatal period which is a global burden.
