Structural Properties of Hydrogen Fluoride in Aqueous Solution Using Reactive Force Field
Authors: Shota Uchida, Kunio Fujiwara, Masahiko Shibahara
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The dynamics of hydrogen fluoride in aqueous solutions at the atomic scale cannot easily be analyzed experimentally and computationally because of the high reactivity of hydrogen fluoride (HF). To elucidate the behavior and growth process of HF aqueous solutions, this study investigated geometric and energetic properties using molecular dynamics simulations with a reactive force field. First, we examined that the previously proposed reactive force field operates properly in bulk HF aqueous solution systems in terms of bond lengths, vibrational frequencies, and dissociation states. Furthermore, the dimer and trimer structures of the HF molecules corresponded to a stable structure with HF parallel shape, reported using ab initio calculations in an isolated environment. Although large clusters grew in combinations based on the parallel geometry of (HF)2, their interaction with water molecules tended to limit their growth in certain directions relative to the HF molecule. The clusters had mainly two layers of F atoms that spread and grew in the planar direction. These findings would provide valuable insights into the design of nanoscale devices, paving the way for new semiconductor manufacturing processes.

Introduction

Hydrogen fluoride solutions are common aqueous media that enable several naturally occurring chemical reactions both in the liquid phase and at interfaces. These aqueous solutions are employed in various industrial fields such as semiconductors, biotechnology, and chemistry, and are frequently used to induce etching reactions, particularly in semiconductor manufacturing processes, owing to their ability to dissolve SiO2 in silicon wafers. − Several studies have explained various physical phenomena in wet environments during the manufacturing of semiconductors miniaturized to less than a few nanometers. − The behavior of hydrogen fluoride (HF) molecules and their relationship with water molecules at the atomic scale are important for clarifying the physical properties of HF aqueous solutions. In particular, the dynamics of HF molecules in aqueous solution at this scale are significant in the etching process of semiconductor devices that have recently been miniaturized to less than a few nanometers. However, only a few studies have investigated this phenomenon because of the toxicity and difficulty of handling HF molecules, considering their extreme dissociation in water.

Experimental and analytical studies have elucidated the characteristics and properties of HF molecules and their aqueous solutions. Experimental studies have investigated the relationship between aqueous dissociation and pH and the temperature dependence of HF solutions using NMR. , In addition, several studies have investigated the radial distribution function (RDF) of liquid HF, and the vibrational frequency of bond stretching between H and F atoms in HF gas has been investigated using FTIR. In particular, several studies have compared the ab initio calculations of isolated HF molecules with experimental results for the vibrational frequencies of HF gas. , For HF without water molecules, analytical studies have been conducted to investigate the aggregation behavior of HF clusters, , the stable structure of the clusters, − and the etching reaction with silica surfaces using kinetic Monte Carlo and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. Analytical studies have investigated the stability − and dissociation of HF with water molecules in isolated systems. The stability of water molecules around a HF molecule using quantum mechanics/molecular mechanics (QM/MM) systems, − ab initio MD, , and MD simulations. Although the stability of a few HF and water molecules has been investigated, to the best of our knowledge, no studies have investigated the properties of multiple HF molecules involving thousands of atoms in aqueous solution at the nanometer scale.

MD simulation is a computational method for handling numerous molecules, and molecular behavior has been investigated, e.g., when NaCl is used as a solute in water. , Such studies employed classical MD and do not consider reactions. Moreover, they do not apply to simulations of HF aqueous solutions, where several active reaction phenomena occur. ReaxFF can be used to observe reactions occurring during MD simulations. It involves an empirical force field parametrized from various DFT data, enabling the description of bond breaking and formation in a large system. , A force field has been proposed for HF aqueous solutions, designed to reproduce the dissociation of isolated HF and water molecule systems and the bond distances of HF molecules. The above study investigated the RDF and diffusion coefficient of 782 water molecules placed around a single HF molecule, but did not report clustering or other behaviors with multiple HF molecules in water molecules.

From the above experimental and computational perspectives, the dynamics of HF molecules in aqueous solutions remain unclear. The purpose of this study is to provide the molecular details of the formation processes of clusters of multiple HF molecules in aqueous solutions using the reactive MD simulations. Simulations were conducted for a system of water and HF molecules. We investigated the length and vibrational frequency of the bonds between H and F atoms, and the relationship between HF concentration and the dissociation to show that the reactive force field reproduces the state of the HF aqueous solution correctly. Furthermore, we examined the clustering process of HF molecules in terms of atomic configuration and energy.

Method

Simulation Model

To simulate a HF aqueous solution, a bulk system comprising 7820 water molecules and 100 HF molecules was prepared. The size of the calculation system was 6.82 × 6.82 × 6.82 nm3 with periodic boundary conditions in all directions, and the temperature of the calculation system was increased from 1 to 300 K using a Nose–Hoover thermostat for 200 ps. After increasing the temperature, the simulation was performed without temperature control for 200 ps for relaxation. Subsequently, we conducted a simulation for 8 ns to investigate the clustering process with a temperature control at 300 K, using a Nose–Hoover thermostat.

The HF molecules were randomly placed in water molecules such that, in the initial atomic configuration, the HF molecules were mutually isolated. The ReaxFF force field for the electrolyte–water system was adopted and the time step was set to 0.25 fs for all atoms. LAMMPS software package was used for the calculations.

HF Cluster Definition and Geometrical Characterization

The identification of HF molecules was based on the H–F interatomic distance, with the nearest H atom to each F atom defined as forming an HF molecule. Regarding HF clusters, F atoms within 2.5 Å of each other were defined belonging to the same cluster. This threshold, determined from the RDF and the growth process of the number of F atoms belonging to the cluster (n of (HF) n ), is discussed in the following section. Cluster geometry was characterized using two metrics: width (r width), calculated as the maximum F–F distance within a cluster, and thickness (r thickness), defined as the vertical displacement of atoms from the cluster plane. When width and thickness are approximately equal, the cluster exhibits a spherical shape, whereas a markedly larger width relative to thickness indicates a planar shape.

Result and Discussion

Clustering Processes of HF Molecules

First, by applying the reactive force field provided in a previous study to HF aqueous solutions, we attempted to validate the force field by investigating several properties of HF molecules. Subsequently, we investigated the HF clustering process in terms of atomic configuration and energy.

The atomic configurations shown in Figure a were used as the initial conditions. As shown in Figure a, HF molecules are initially isolated in an aqueous solution and then aggregated to form clusters. In a HF and water molecule mixture, an energy decrease due to the aggregation of HF molecules has been reported. This finding is consistent with the trend of the calculation results. In this study, we performed five calculations under equivalent conditions with different initial configurations of atoms and confirmed that HF molecules formed clusters in all cases.

The bilayer around 1 Å in the RDF of F–H indicates that the first peak was an intramolecular bond (F–H) and the second was an intermolecular bond formed as the hydrogen bond between the other HF molecules (HF···H). The position of the first peak was 1.06 Å in the results. Existing studies have reported the bond length between H and F atoms of a system comprising a HF molecule and several water molecules to be 1.02 Å in a cyclic structure and 1.04 Å in a cubic structure that is calculated by the ab initio calculations. The second peak of the RDF due to hydrogen bonds was located at 1.42 Å, which is consistent with the results of 1.44–1.78 Å reported in previous studies. , Focusing on the distance between F and O atoms, the position of the first peak at 2.5 Å corresponds to that observed in the previous studies with few HF in water molecules. , Regarding the distance between F atoms, the values in this study (1.9–2.1 Å) underestimate those reported by ab initio calculations and experimental results of HF in the gas phase (2.53 Å and 2.73 Å). This tendency was owing to the ReaxFF parameter set which was developed using the training data comprising a single HF molecule and multiple water molecules. While development of F–F interaction in systems containing multiple F atoms was left for future work, the ReaxFF force field in ref reasonably reproduces bond lengths.

The bilayers in the F–H and F–F RDF profiles disappear before and after clustering to form a single peak. For F–H binding, the disappearance of the bilayers indicates a similar trend, as previous studies have reported an increase in intramolecular and a reduction in intermolecular bond length due to clustering. , In contrast, a few studies have investigated bond length of F–F atoms in aqueous solution; however, the details are not well understood. These changes in bond length before and after the clustering are discussed subsequently.

To investigate the vibrational frequency of the H–F bond, we employed the distance of 1.25 Å from the F atom to separate the intramolecular and intermolecular distances for all H–F bonds and calculated the vibrational frequency of each bond, as shown in Figure b (0.025 ns) and Figure c (5.0 ns). A comparative summary of vibrational frequencies is provided in Table . At t = 0.025 ns (Figure b), the results indicate that F atoms were isolated in the aqueous solution. The vibrational frequencies of intramolecular bonds in HF molecules (F–H: blue line) have a broad distribution in the range of 1300–2000 cm–1, and a frequency of 1992 cm–1 for a similar bond in hydrated HF systems has been reported in a previous study based on ab initio calculations. Although existing studies have calculated ideal atomic configurations, the broad frequency distributions in this study were due to the various configurations of water molecules around the HF molecules. The vibrational frequencies of the intermolecular bonds (HF···H), which correspond to hydrogen bonds, indicated by red lines, were less than 500 cm–1. This result is consistent with those of previous studies, which reported that vibrational frequency decreases (125–475 cm–1) due to dimer formation. At t = 5.0 ns (Figure c), F atoms were clustered, and the vibrational frequencies of both intramolecular and intermolecular HF bonds showed similar distributions. Assuming that higher vibrational frequencies correspond to stronger bonding, the results suggest weakening of intramolecular HF bonds and strengthening of hydrogen bonds due to HF clustering, consistent with trends reported previously. The vibrational frequencies of O–H bonds also showed good agreement with experimental values. Comparisons among ReaxFF, experimental data, and ab initio calculations revealed discrepancies in F–F distances and dissociation behavior at high concentrations. These discrepancies stem from the ReaxFF parameter set, which was developed using training data that assumed only limited stable states of HF and water molecules. In addition, recent theoretical studies have emphasized the importance of nuclear quantum effects (NQEs) in accurately describing proton dynamics in aqueous systems such as HF and water. , Incorporating information on lager stable structures and NQEs into the ReaxFF database would allow future simulations to more accurately reproduce the behavior of highly concentrated HF aqueous solutions.

The results obtained from our simulations of dilute HF aqueous solutions were consistent with the experimental and ab initio level analytical results in terms of bond length, vibrations between atoms, and dissociation.

Energy and Configuration of HF Clusters

To investigate the clustering process of HF molecules shown in Figure , we obtained the size and energy of the HF clusters during their growth. Figure shows the time evolution of n ((HF) n ), which indicates the size of the HF clusters. The number of F atoms belonging to the cluster (n) was counted by tracing the number of other F atoms within 2.5 Å, as defined from the RDF in Figure a. As shown in Figure , the majority of HF molecules are isolated (n = 1) immediately after the start of the calculation, and the number of clusters with large n increases with calculation time. After t = 7 ns, the cluster size remains significantly unchanged, indicating that the clustering of HF molecules has reached a steady state. The maximum number of clusters is n = 19, and the maximum value of n was approximately 20 for other trajectories with different initial conditions. Figure indicates the presence of several clusters consisting of fewer than 20 HF molecules and several isolated HF molecules in the concentration of the calculated system used in this study.

Time evolution of the number of clusters. Each line indicates the number of HF molecules constituting the cluster n ((HF) n ).

The relationships between the number of HF molecules forming a cluster (n) and the potential, kinetic energy, and size (r width and r thickness) are shown in Figure . Energy is the average value of F atoms belonging to each cluster, and r is the size calculated at each cluster size. Each value is obtained from 5000 coordinates and energy data at each time, every 0.25 ns, in five calculations with different initial conditions, and the error bars represent the deviation of 25000 data at each time. As shown in Figure a, the potential energy decreases as the size of clusters increases from n = 1 to 10, indicating that HF molecules in an aqueous solution become stable by aggregation. The larger potential energies for n = 11 and 18 are because few clusters of these sizes are formed, and the averaging is insufficient. The observation is also supported by the large error bars. For clusters with n > 10, the potential energy changes slightly, indicating that the clusters exhibit stable configurations regardless of size. In contrast, the kinetic energy is constant and independent of cluster size. The relationship between the number of F atoms and cluster size shown in Figure c indicates that r width increases to approximately 8 Å as the cluster grows and r thickness saturates at approximately 2 Å. For equal cluster width and thickness, the clusters exhibit a spherical shape. As shown in Figure c, as the cluster size increases, the difference between thickness and width also increases. This implies that the HF clusters grew in a planar shape rather than a spherical shape.

Atomic Distribution around HF Molecules

To investigate the hydration and clustering of the HF molecules in detail, the angular radial distribution (ARD) was computed, enabling the resolution of the structural properties of the binding water and other HF molecules.

Figure shows the distribution of surrounding atoms when a F atom is at the origin and the nearest H atom is on the y-axis. Each contour plot averaged 5000 atomic configuration data obtained at 12.5 fs intervals, representing atomic information over a period of 62.5 ps. As information about the angles of atoms around the vector from the F0 atom (origin) to the H1 atom was symmetric about the y-axis in the ARD map, each contour map contains information about the O and H atoms and were defined the typical regions where the atoms are abundant. The nearest neighbor H atoms from the F0 are defined as H1, which represents the HF molecule.

The ARD shown in Figure a was obtained at t = 0.025 ns, indicating that HF molecules were isolated in the aqueous solutions. For (HF)2, the F1 and F4 regions are located at 10 and 30°, respectively, and the F2 and F3 are located behind the F0–H1 vector. These atom configurations of F atoms indicate that the two HF molecules are arranged in two geometries, linear and parallel, which consisting of atoms F0–H1···F4–H4 and F0–H1···F1–H2. The angles between each atom and the HF vector are consistent with the results of previous study using ab initio calculations for isolated HF molecules in vacuum (F, 8 < θ < 14°; H, 15 < θ < 30°). The H atoms that are broadly distributed in H3 region are bonded with water molecules. In terms of distance from F0, both F1, F2, and F4, F5 exist in regions of 1.9 and 2.1 Å, respectively, corresponding to the two peak positions of the F–F RDF in Figure a. The separation of these distances depends on the number of hydrogen bonds between the two HF molecules: 1.9 Å for a single hydrogen bond (linear) and 2.1 Å for two hydrogen bonds (parallel).

Similarly, an evaluation of the configuration of (HF)3 revealed that the three HF molecules have cyclic and two parallel geometries, commonly known as transition states, as shown in Figure f. Based on the distance from F0, the cyclic shape between the three HF molecules bonded by a single hydrogen bond consisted of F0, F4, and F5, whereas the transition state bonded by two hydrogen bonds consisted of F0, F1, and F2 atoms. The H atoms belonging to the trimers were inferred to as H1, H5, and H3 in the cyclic shape and H1, H2, and H3 in the transition state. The strong peak intensities of the F1 and H2 regions in the angular profiles indicated a transition state consisting of the F0, F1, F1′, H1, H2, and H2′ atoms, where F1′ and H2′ represent atoms in the y-axis symmetric positions. See Figure a,b for examples of the F1′ and H2′ configurations. The peaks at F0, F4, and F5 indicated the existence of cyclic shapes, and the distribution in the F3 region implied the existence of few trimers with a linear shape of F3–F0–F4.

Details of Energy in HF Cluster

To interpret the details of the clustering process of HF molecules, the energies of the atoms in each region around the F0 atom were investigated. The potentials and kinetic energies were calculated as the energies per atom in each region. Each plot and error bar in the graph represents the average and deviation calculated using a procedure similar to that shown in Figure .

For the potential energy shown in Figure a, the H1 region, which is an isolated HF molecule, has the lowest energy at n = 1. The H3 and O1 regions, which form hydrogen bonds with water molecules behind the vector from the F0 to H1 atoms, are low energy and stable regions. At n = 2, the energies of H1 and H2 are lower than those of H3, indicating that the HF molecular pair in the parallel configuration is more stable than that in the linear configuration. For F atoms, F1 has a lower potential energy than F4, indicating that the parallel shape is more stable than the linear shape, which is similar to the trend observed for H atoms. For the cluster with n = 3, the energy of F1 is considerably lower than that of F2 and corresponds to the strong peak intensity of the F atoms in the F1 region, as illustrated by the atomic distribution shown in Figure d. These results show that the (HF)3 cluster is composed of F0–F1–F1′ and H1–H2–H2′. In such clusters, the energy of H2 is lower than that of H1 because the former is related to F0 and F1, and the latter is related to F0, F1, and F1′. The H1 atom confined to the three F atoms is more constrained and unstable, with a high potential energy. In addition, the high potential energy in the F3, F4, F5, and F6 region implies that cyclic and linear shapes cannot easily be formed.

Per-atom energies for atoms comprising various size cluster. The regions of each atom are defined as the area of abundance of the corresponding atom.

For clusters larger than (HF)4, the potential energy of F2 decreases, whereas it changes only slightly for F3, F4, F5, and F6. These energy changes reveal the formation of F0–F1–F1′–F2–F2′ clusters with four parallel shapes, as shown in Figure b, and are related to a decrease in the potential energies of H4 and H5. As this trend continues through the expansion of the cluster, large clusters consist of a combination of F0 with parallel shapes. In contrast, the potential energies of F3, F6, H3, and H6 were higher than those of the O atoms (O2, O3). This indicates that the clusters have difficulty expanded in the region behind the vector from F0 to H1 because of the presence of water molecules with lower potential energy. Although previous studies , demonstrated the stability of linear and cyclic geometries in an isolated environment, stable geometry differs because of the effect of water molecules in aqueous solutions. Limitations on the configuration of F atoms also affect the three–dimensional structure of the clusters; consequently, more than two layers of F atoms could not easily be stacked. The distance between layers (e.g., F1–F1′ and F2–F0–F2′’) was approximately 2 Å, which corresponds to the thickness of the cluster (r thickness) shown in Figure c. In terms of energy, a F atom cannot easily hold other F atoms in all directions simultaneously in an aqueous solution; thus, clusters are expected to grow in a planar rather than spherical shape.

In this study, calculations were performed under different initial conditions, and the maximum cluster size formed in all calculations was (HF)19. The states of larger cluster sizes and the relationship between HF concentration and maximum cluster size remain unclear. The dynamics of HF molecules in aqueous solutions with high concentrations of HF molecules require further investigation.

Conclusion

This study conducted MD simulations of HF aqueous solutions using the ReaxFF force field to investigate the cluster growth process of HF molecules. Results showed that the behavior of HF molecules in aqueous solutions differs from that in the isolated state shown in previous studies using ab initio calculations in terms of bond lengths and vibrational frequencies. Further, we demonstrated that the dissociated state of HF molecules in an aqueous solution reproduced the experimental results in the calculation system used in this study. During cluster growth of HF molecules, the potential energy of the constituent F atoms decreased as the size increased, and the clusters expanded by bonding F atoms in the planar direction.

Investigation of the atomic configurations in HF clusters revealed that the molecules exhibited typical linear and parallel geometry for small clusters and formed hydrogen bonds with another HF and water molecules, confirming that these atomic configurations were consistent with the stable structure reported in previous studies using ab initio calculations. This minimum structure remained essentially unchanged as the cluster grew, indicating that the two HF molecules were specifically assembled in a parallel shape to form a cluster. From an energetic point of view, a F atom is stable when the four surrounding F atoms form four parallel shapes in the same plane, rather than a three–dimensional structure. The positions of the F atoms in the clusters are restricted by other F atoms, and the O atoms with low potential energy exist in regions where the F atoms cannot easily exist. From a geometric and energetic perspective, the clusters grow more planar than in a three-dimensional structure.

Our simulations have contributed to reveal the growth process and structural properties of clusters by observing the atomic configurations and energies of HF molecules. This study is the first step toward enhancing our understanding of the atomic properties of HF molecules in aqueous solutions. However, considering the dependence of the concentration, temperature, and wall surface effects on the etching reaction, caution must be exercised, and further verification is necessary for HF aqueous solution.
