Evaluations of nurse managers’ work environment: a descriptive study
Authors: Aynur Yazici Sorucuoğlu, Metin Dinçer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe aim of this study is to determine nurse managers’ perceptions of their work environment and the factors influencing these perceptions.MethodThis descriptive and cross-sectional study included 407 nurse managers working in Turkey. Data were collected using a demographic information form and the “Managerial Nurse Work Environment Scale (MNWES)”. The data were analyzed using the SPSS 22.0 and Jamovi 2.7.18 for Windows (SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA) software package. Descriptive statistical values were presented as frequency, percentage, mean, and standard deviation. The normality of the data distribution was assessed using Skewness and Kurtosis values, and the variables were found to be normally distributed. Parametric methods were used in the analysis of the data. Independent groups t-tests, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), and post hoc (Turkey, LSD) analyses were used to compare the mean scale and subscale scores according to the participants’ descriptive characteristics. The relationship between the subscales was examined using Pearson correlation analysis. Cronbach’s Alpha coefficient was calculated to assess scale reliability. The statistical significance level was set at p < 0.05. To assess the effect sizes of the variables the chi-square or partial chi-square (for ANOVA) tests, Cohen’s d (for t-tests), and A multiple linear regression analysis was were performed. Confirmatory factor analysis was conducted, and correlations were calculated for all sub-dimensions. The article was prepared in accordance with the STROBE guidelines. Ethical committee approval and informed consent from participants were obtained to conduct the study.FindingsThe mean MNWES score for nurse managers was 4.82 ± 0.63. Gender, education level/title, perceived income status, type and duration of administrative duties, administrative unit/hospital, receipt of management training, susceptibility to the work environment, and satisfaction level were found to affect the mean MNWES scores (p < 0.05). In particular, it was determined that the level of satisfaction with the role showed a highly significant and high difference in all sub-dimensions of the MNWES and that the satisfaction status significantly affected the scores (p < 0.001). The level of influence of the environment also showed a significant difference in all sub-dimensions of MNWES and was found to significantly affect the scores (p < 0.05). Significant differences were found between those who received management training and those who did not in subscales 1, 2, 3, 4, and 8 of the MNWES (p < 0.05). When examined in terms of the effect sizes of the variables, job satisfaction (η² ≈ 0.09–0.16), having received management training (d ≈ 0.21–0.26), holding the titles of specialist or doctor (d ≈ 0.46), job position (d ≈ 0.26–0.31) and susceptibility to the environment (η² ≈ 0.05) can be said to produce more significant differences in certain sub-dimensions compared to other variables.ConclusionIt was found that participants perceived their working environment positively, and that, among the factors influencing this perception, job satisfaction, the impact of the environment, and whether they had received management training showed more significant differences compared to other variables. Considering the factors affecting the work environment perceptions of nurse managers in achieving institutional goals in terms of patient care, it is recommended that supportive policies be developed.Keywords: Manager nurses' work environment, Manager nurse, Leadership, Burnout, Organizational support

Introduction

Nurses play a critical role in the effective and efficient functioning of healthcare systems. Nurse managers are responsible not only for coordinating clinical services but also for numerous administrative activities such as personnel management, budgeting, quality control, and patient safety [1–6]. As one of the most effective members of the healthcare team, nurse managers control vital functions such as overseeing valuable financial resources, monitoring human resources, and implementing complex healthcare policy changes [7–9]. Since the implementation of value-based purchasing in healthcare, nurse managers have been held responsible for certain complex aspects of patient reimbursements [10]. Such responsibilities require comprehensive manager training to prevent significant revenue losses for institutions. Since it takes years for nurse managers to become fully competent in their roles, the loss of experienced managers can lead to decreased organizational functionality and numerous costly capital losses [11, 12]. Work environments directly affect the psychosocial conditions of nursing managers, such as job satisfaction, burnout levels, and organizational commitment [12]. The nursing manager work environment has been defined as an organizational environment that affects nursing, patient, and organizational outcomes [13]. This environment is also defined as an organizational context that affects nurses’ skills and competencies [14]. The organizational context affects employees’ job outcomes/performance [15]. Since the nurse manager is also an employee, their relationships with senior managers and management support are extremely important. Moreover, this support is reflected in the job performance of nurse managers and their relationships with other healthcare team members and nurses [16]. Conceptual models provide roadmaps to guide practice. In this context, the theory of complexity leadership applied to nursing management practice offers leaders a guide for designing a context that supports the job performance of nursing managers, thereby enabling the work of frontline staff. Creating environments that support and encourage creativity and innovation is crucial for success during turbulent times [17]. Based on the principles of complexity leadership theory, organizational leaders must foster cultures that support the needs of adaptable frontline work teams [18]. A primary administrative function is to set strategic direction and allocate resources accordingly. Equally important is the need for administrative leaders to involve empowering and adaptable leaders who actually do the work in strategic planning processes to position the organization for success. Administrative functions include creating organizational structures that facilitate rather than hinder adaptable-level work. Ensuring that structures exist to promote two-way communication flows across all levels of the organization is equally important. Scorecards, policies and procedures, and information-sharing meetings are examples of tools that promote communication across the organization [16].

Adaptive leadership is critical for organizational growth [17]. In today’s healthcare systems, frontline staff must develop innovative ways to get the job done. They must demonstrate an unwavering commitment to quality patient outcomes. This requires leadership to increase awareness among frontline teams regarding the challenges they face in their work and to generate solutions. While challenging traditional practice models, they must disseminate new best practices. Dissemination methods may include performance improvement fairs, evidence-based practice guidelines, and joint management councils [16].

Leaders must define their roles within the adaptable unit and the larger organization [18]. Their primary commitment to the departments to which they are assigned is unquestionable. Supporting the interdisciplinary team by fostering positive relationships and practice environments is crucial for the team’s success and achieving high-quality patient outcomes. Nurse managers are always tied to this role function. However, nurse managers frequently express that the competing demands of their roles within the broader organization negatively impact their roles in clinical departments. They must remember that both roles are equally important and contribute to overall organizational success. Finding the right balance is critical for successful job performance and organizational outcomes. The science of complexity enables nursing leaders to consider multiple perspectives when evaluating nursing managers’ job performance. Organizational success depends on developing synergy among an organization’s administrative, adaptive, and supportive leadership functions [16].

The literature has identified some fundamental studies that describe the work environment of nurse managers [14, 15]. These studies highlight eight critical areas that influence the work environment of nurse managers: “empowering administrative leaders to create a culture of patient safety, constructive nurse manager-administrator relationships, a culture of productivity, adequately budgeted resources, a culture of meaning, collegial nurse manager-physician relationships, effective nurse manager-unit staff relationships, and a fair and manageable workload.” In a study conducted by Flynn et al. [19], nurse managers identified key characteristics that create a work environment supportive of management practices; these characteristics included “collaborative supervisory partnerships, delegated authority, adequate resources, information sharing, collegial collaboration, and organizational commitment to quality and safety.” Additionally, Huddleston and Gray [20] examined the perceptions of both staff nurses and nurse managers regarding a healthy work environment, emphasizing the importance of “appropriate staffing, authentic leadership, effective decision-making, meaningful recognition, skilled communication, genuine collaboration, true teamwork, and physical and psychological safety” [20].

These studies emphasize the importance of empowered leadership, adequate resources, effective communication, and a safe working environment in creating conditions conducive to successful leadership among nurse managers. Most previous studies have focused on staff nurses’ perceptions of their work environments [21–23] but a few studies have evaluated the work environment from the perspective of nurse managers and found significant relationships between the work environment and various outcomes [13, 14, 24]. These include turnover, job satisfaction [24], burnout [13], organizational silence [25], and job enjoyment [24], as well as patient outcomes such as missed care and quality of care [24]. Furthermore, the work environment of nurse managers has been identified as a critical driver of performance [24]. Nurse managers are a key determinant of positive professional work environments, which are associated with nursing, patient, and organizational outcomes [26]. In most healthcare organisations, there is a multi-tiered leadership structure ranging from frontline staff to middle and senior managers. Each tier presents an opportunity that either facilitates or hinders the fulfilment of the organisation’s mission [27]. A better understanding of the current practice environment for nurse managers is beneficial for improving their working environment; this, in turn, will enhance the quality of care provided and nursing management practices [13]. A good practice environment for nurse managers can boost job enthusiasm and encourage leadership; this will create an environment conducive to supporting nursing staff [26]. Furthermore, nurse managers are indispensable in influencing the professional practice environment of frontline nurses. Providing a supportive working environment for nurse managers is key to retaining them in their roles [28]. Studies have also shown that a positive practice environment is of critical importance for nurse managers in terms of job satisfaction, retention and work performance [17]. In this regard, it is important to determine how nurse managers perceive their work environments (positive/negative) and what factors influence these perceptions. However, a review of the literature reveals that there are only a limited number of studies on this subject [29]. Looking at the studies conducted, in 2025, Yeşilyurt [30] examined the effect of nursing managers’ work environment and job-related characteristics on their perceived individual job performance. In 2024, Chen et al. [13] conducted a study evaluating the current state of nursing managers’ practice environments and related factors in China [13]. In a study conducted by Grandfield et al. [24] in 2023, the importance of nursing managers’ practice environments in influencing outcomes for direct-care nurses and patients was thoroughly investigated [24]. In 2021, Tosun and Yıldırım [31] conducted a descriptive and cross-sectional study to determine nursing managers’ views on their practice environments. In 2021, Tosun and Yıldırım conducted a validity and reliability study of the ‘Nurse Manager Work Environment Scale’ in Turkish. In a study by Wiggins and Rayens in 2016, the effect of the practice environment on nurse managers’ job satisfaction and intention to leave was examined [6]. In a study by Warshawsky et al. [14] in 2013, a secondary analysis of qualitative data from two cross-sectional studies regarding organisational characteristics affecting nurse managers’ practices was presented. In 2013, Warshawsky et al. [15] developed and tested a scale designed to describe and evaluate the work environments of nurse managers. When reviewing the literature on the working environment of nurse managers across Turkey, the study conducted by Tosun and Yıldırım [31] was carried out exclusively with 211 nurse managers in the province of Ankara. The study by Aslan et al. [25] on the other hand, was conducted with 169 nurse managers working in private hospitals. The study by Yeşilyurt et al. [30] on the other hand, was conducted with 219 nurse managers working in private hospitals. For this reason, this study has been carried out to examine the working environment of all nurse managers across Turkey. This study aims to contribute to the literature by identifying nursing managers’ perceptions of their working environments and the factors influencing these perceptions. In this context, the study seeks to answer the following research questions: What are the levels of perception regarding the working environment among nurse managers in Turkey? What are the individual factors influencing these perceptions? How do organisational factors influence perceptions of the working environment? Do nurse managers’ perceptions of the working environment vary according to demographic characteristics?

Method

The population of this descriptive and cross-sectional study consists of nurse managers working in Turkey. The snowball sampling method, one of the non-random sampling methods, was used as the sampling method. One-to-one online interviews were conducted with manager nurses working in Turkey, and the link to the online survey was shared in WhatsApp groups comprising nurse managers from both public, univerty and private hospitals, with participants asked to complete the survey. As the survey was conducted online, verbal consent was obtained. The survey was administered online. In addition, these reference persons were asked to forward it to other nurse managers. The online survey was kept open between December 2024 and June 2025. An attempt was made to cover the entire population without conducting a sample calculation, and the study was completed with the participation of a total of 407 nurse managers. It is considered that the sample size of 407 used in this study is appropriate in terms of both sample adequacy and statistical power, and that it supports the reliability of the findings. The study included nurses who had at least one year of experience working as managers and who were currently working as managers. Permission was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Health Sciences at Ankara Yıldırım Beyazıt University in order to conduct the study. (E-84892257-300-327331). Data were collected using an online survey consisting of a demographic information form and the Manager Nurse Work Environment Scale (MNWES). Manager nurses completed the questions in the online survey in approximately 30 min via self-report.

The NWSES was developed by Warshawsky et al. [15] in 2013 and adapted into Turkish by Tosun and Yıldırım [32]. The scale, which has a six-point Likert-type scoring, consists of 44 items and 8 subscales: 1) Patient Safety (PS) “Empowering administrative leaders fosters a culture of patient safety. An empowering administrative leadership team makes decisions consistent with the organization’s mission, vision, and values. Culture supports patient safety, innovation, empowerment, risk-taking, and professional development.“ 2) Organizational Culture (OC) ”The nurse manager creates a meaningful organizational culture by effectively communicating the organization’s mission, vision, values, and strategic plan to the staff.” 3) Productivity “A culture of productivity is one in which nurse managers find time to develop the staff they work with.” 4) Financial Resources (FR) “When budgeted resources are adequate, allocations of human and material resources meet the unit’s needs and are effective in the process of acquiring additional resources.” 5) Workload “Workload encompasses the number of directly subordinate employees, scope of responsibilities, number of patient care areas, type of support services available, and a fair workload distribution among managers.” 6) Nurse Manager-Director Relationships (NMR) “Supportive nurse manager-director relationships are based on trust, respect, and open communication. Managers facilitate the development and growth of the nurse manager by providing constructive performance feedback”. 7) Nurse Manager-Physician Relationships (NMR) ”The foundations of nurse manager-physician relationships are mutual respect and shared responsibility. Nurse managers and physician partners work together to solve problems and achieve quality patient outcomes.“ 8) Nurse Manager-Unit Staff Relationships (NM-USR) ”Effective nurse managers collaborate with the staff they work with to achieve quality patient outcomes. Nurse managers value working with staff who are committed and motivated to provide quality patient care”. Scale items are scored between [1] ”Strongly Disagree“ and [6] ”Strongly Agree.” All items are phrased positively, with scores of 1–3 indicating disagreement with the item and scores of 4–6 indicating agreement. The scale and subscales are evaluated on an average score, with scores closer to six indicating more positive perceptions of the work environment. The Cronbach’s alpha value for the general scale was 0.97 in the original study [15] and 0.96 in the Turkish adaptation study [32]. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha value of the scale was determined to be 0.96.

The data were analyzed using the IBM SPSS Statistics, V. 22.0 and Jamovi 2.7.18 for Windows software package. Descriptive statistical values were presented as frequency, percentage, mean, and standard deviation. The normality of the data distribution was assessed using Skewness and Kurtosis values, and the variables were found to be normally distributed. Parametric methods were used in the analysis of the data. Independent groups t-tests, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), and post hoc (Turkey, LSD) analyses were used to compare the mean scale and subscale scores according to the participants’ descriptive characteristics. The relationship between the subscales was examined using Pearson correlation analysis. Cronbach’s Alpha coefficient was calculated to assess scale reliability. The statistical significance level was set at p < 0.05. To assess the effect sizes of the variables the chi-square or partial chi-square (for ANOVA) tests, Cohen’s d (for t-tests), and A multiple linear regression analysis was were performed. Confirmatory factor analysis was conducted, and correlations were calculated for all sub-dimensions. The article was prepared in accordance with the STROBE guidelines.

Findings

90.7% of participants were women, 62.6% were aged 41 or over, 83.5% were married, and 68.3% had completed secondary school or held a bachelor’s degree. 83.05% of participants have children; 93.61% hold the title of nurse, whilst 6.39% hold the titles of specialist or Doctor of Nursing. 53.56% of participants have 21 years or more of professional experience, whilst 40.54% have served in managerial roles for 5 years or less. 78.13% of participants are charge nurses and supervising nurses, whilst 21.87% are managers and deputy managers. 35.63% of participants work in teaching and research hospitals or university hospitals, and 77.6% reported their income status as poor. 80.59% of participants reported being satisfied with their jobs, while 62.16% reported not having received training in management. 55.04% of nurse managers reported being affected by their work environment (Table 1). In the assessment of managerial nurses’ perceptions of their work environment, the highest average score was in the Manager-Unit Staff Relations dimension (5.41 ± 0.58), and the lowest average score was in the Financial Resources dimension (4.02 ± 1.01). It was determined that the mean scores for the MNWES and its sub-dimensions were in the 3–6 point range, indicating that participants evaluated their work environment positively (Table 1).

Table 1 presents a comparison of the MNWES and subscale mean scores according to various demographic characteristics of the participants. It was determined that the participants’ age, marital status, parenthood status, length of professional experience, and daily working hours did not affect the MNWES and subscale mean scores (p > 0.05) (Table 1).

The study found that male nurse managers had statistically significant higher mean scores on the PS, FR, and MN-MR subscales than female managers (p < 0.05). In assessing the effect size of the gender variable, men’s scores were found to be slightly higher than women’s on most dimensions, with Cohen’s d ranging from 0.10 to 0.42 (small to moderate effect sizes), and the most pronounced difference was observed in the PS and FR sub-dimensions(Table 1). The average scores for the Workload subdimension of participants with high school and bachelor’s degrees were significantly higher than those of participants with postgraduate degrees (p < 0.05). When the level of influence of the title variable was assessed, it was found that Specialist and Dr. nurse managers had a higher (d ≈ 0,46) level of influence in the productivity dimension compared to other nurse managers (Table 1). In addition, the average scores for the Productivity subdimension of participants with postgraduate degrees were significantly higher than those of other participants (p < 0.05). The mean scores for the Workload and Productivity subscales were higher among participants serving as directors/assistant directors than among charge nurses; however, the mean scores for the Workload-Healthy subscale were higher among charge nurses than among participants serving as directors/assistant directors (p < 0.05). The effect size of the ‘type of role’ variable was found to be higher for nurse managers holding the positions of director and deputy director compared to other nurse managers in certain dimensions (d ≈ 0.26–0.31) (Table 1).

Participants with 5 years or less of administrative experience have higher MN-MR subscale mean scores than participants with more than 10 years of administrative experience (p < 0.05). Participants who received training in management have higher mean scores on the İC, Productivity, FR, MN-USR, and total scale than participants who did not receive training (p < 0.05). When assessing the effect size of the variable ‘management training’, it was found that head nurses who had received training scored higher than those who had not, and that the effect size was small to moderate (d ≈ 0.21–0.26). Participants working in high-risk units have higher mean scores on the MN-PR subscale than participants working in administrative units (p < 0.05). Participants working in city hospitals and ER-UNIVersity hospitals have higher MN-PR and YH-BPI dimension mean scores than participants working in smaller hospitals (p < 0.05). Participants who reported their income status as moderate had higher mean scores on the İC, Productivity, and FR subscales than participants who reported their income status as poor (p < 0.05). When assessing the effect size of the income variable, it was found that senior nurses with higher incomes scored higher than those with lower incomes, and that the effect size was small to moderate, particularly in the FR sub-dimension (d ≈ 0.28).

Participants who reported being satisfied with their work environment had significantly higher MNWES and subscale mean scores than other participants (those who were dissatisfied and undecided) (p < 0.05). When the effect size of the job satisfaction variable was assessed, it was found to be higher among satisfied nurse managers than among those who were dissatisfied (η² ≈ 0.09–0.16). Participants who reported not being affected by the work environment had higher mean scores on all subscales except MN-PR and on the total scale than other participants (p < 0.05). When assessing the effect size of the ‘susceptibility to environmental influences’ variable, it was found that head nurses who were not susceptible to environmental influences scored higher than those who were susceptible, and that the effect sizes approached a moderate level in some sub-dimensions (η² ≈ 0.05) (Table 1).

In the assessment of nurse managers’ perceptions of their work environment, the highest average score was in the Nurse Manager-Unit Staff Relations dimension (5.41 ± 0.58), while the lowest average score was in the Financial Resources dimension (4.02 ± 1.01). It was determined that the mean scores for the MNWES and its sub-dimensions were in the 3–6 point range, indicating that participants evaluated their work environment positively (Table 2). Cronbach’s alpha coefficients were calculated to assess the internal consistency of the scale. The results indicated that the scale and its subscales possess acceptable and high levels of reliability (Table 2).

Discussion

For healthcare services to be delivered effectively and desired goals to be achieved, it is important for nurse managers to have a positive perception of their work environment [15]. This study was conducted to determine how nurse managers working in Turkey evaluate their work environments and to identify the factors that influence their perceptions of the work environment.

In this study, the mean and standard deviation of the total scale scores for the work environment of nurse managers were found to be 4.82 ± 0.63. Manager nurses generally evaluated their work environments positively (Table 1). Research indicates that environments which are supportive and empowering lead to increased job satisfaction, better retention rates, and higher levels of patient safety [33]. Similarly, both international studies [6, 13] and studies conducted in Turkey [25, 30, 31] report that nurse managers evaluate their work environments positively. This study was conducted with nurse managers working in public hospitals at a rate of 92% (n = 407). In contrast, Tosun and Yıldırım [31] found that nurse managers working in private hospitals (30%, n = 211) evaluated their work environments more positively than those working in Ministry of Health and university hospitals in Turkey (70%, n = 211). This finding suggests that the work environment may vary across different healthcare sectors in the country at different times.

The highest score among the subscales of the managerial nurse work environment scale was observed in the YH-BPI subscale, while the lowest score was observed in the FR subscale. The YH-BPI subscale refers to meaningful working relationships between managerial nurses and staff that contribute to high-quality patient outcomes [14]. Overall, this finding is consistent with the results reported in previous studies [6, 25, 31, 32, 34, 35]. It also highlights the importance of YH-BPI and draws attention to cross-country similarities regarding challenges related to budget constraints. Studies have shown that the quality of relationships between managers and staff is associated with improved nursing and unit outcomes. Relational leadership styles play an important role in developing positive work environments, job satisfaction, and quality patient care by enhancing nursing managers’ critical thinking, conflict management, and team-building skills [29, 30, 36, 37] Research has shown that nurse managers can reduce the stress and burnout that are common in high-pressure healthcare environments by actively engaging with nursing staff and addressing their concerns. Furthermore, by encouraging teamwork and recognising individual contributions, they can increase job satisfaction and retention rates among nursing staff [37]. Moreover, there is strong evidence that nurse managers with a relational leadership style are key drivers of positive working environments and job satisfaction among nurses [16].

Participants scored lowest on the FR subscale in the study. In a study by Warshawsky et al. [14], nurse managers stated that they wanted administrative leaders to define organizational priorities and make budget decisions in line with the organization’s mission, vision, and values. The same study emphasized that the current economic conditions in healthcare require the efficient management of human and material resources, but budget constraints conflict with providing the best and safest patient care and lead to burnout. The financial resources available to nurse managers are critical to the sustainability of nursing services and the improvement of patient care quality [39]. Nursing managers reported that practice environments characterised by positive cultures, relationships and adequate resources supported their work performance [40]. The findings of this study show that nurse managers face difficulties in accessing the resources necessary to fulfill their roles. Although structural and political differences vary between countries, there is a need for global strategies designed to support nursing managers in accessing adequate financial resources for sustainable care [28, 49].

In this study, male nurse managers had higher mean scores on the PS, FR, and MN-MR subscales than female managers. In contrast, Chen et al. [13] found that female nurse managers had higher work environment scale scores. This suggests that gender characteristics may differ between countries. In Turkey, the nursing profession still maintains a high concentration of female nurses. Culturally, male managers in Turkey are still in a more advantageous position than female managers. Therefore, in this study, it was predicted that male managers would have a positive work environment in terms of the PS, FR, and MN-MR dimensions. Organisational commitment and proactive work behaviours are particularly influenced by positive relationships with colleagues, managers and medical partners [42].

The average scores for the workload sub-dimension among participants with high school and undergraduate education are significantly higher than those with graduate education. Considering that nurse managers with postgraduate education have additional workload planning for research activities, the result found in this study is expected. Managers with postgraduate education may report lower satisfaction with the workload dimension in the work environment. In the study, managers with postgraduate education reported higher positive perceptions in the Productivity sub-dimension than other nurses. This result is similar to the study by Tosun and Yıldırım [31]. In that study, nurse managers with master’s and doctoral degrees in nursing evaluated their work environments more positively than other nurse managers. A commitment to professional development is one of the defining characteristics of effective nursing managers. This characteristic encompasses personal development, continuous education and the promotion of learning within the team. Nursing managers should not only pursue further education and qualifications, but also keep a close eye on current research and developments in the field of nursing. This commitment to lifelong learning not only enhances their own competence but also serves as an example for their teams [38].

Participants who received management training had higher mean scores on the İC, Productivity, FR, YH-BPI, and total scale measures compared to participants who did not receive training (p < 0.05). These results are consistent with previous studies [13, 31]. The Productivity dimension focuses on the development of unit staff. Educated managers place greater importance on the development of unit staff compared to other nurse managers. This is an indicator of leadership for educated managers. Warshawsky et al., [16], reported in their study that requiring advanced education for nursing manager positions and providing institutional support for nursing managers to complete advanced education is an important first step in creating a leadership pool. In institutions that prioritize manager training to achieve their goals and have the necessary budget and policies in this regard, it is likely that nurse managers working in these institutions have a more positive perception of the work environment as a reflection of the existing institutional culture. In our study, the majority of nurse managers reported satisfaction with the total scale and sub-scales related to the work environment. When these two variables are evaluated together, it can be considered that the culture of the institutions where the participants work provides a suitable environment for receiving managerial training. Tosun and Yıldırım’s [31] study also found that participants who were satisfied with their work environment had higher perceptions of their work environment. First and foremost, staff satisfaction is one of the key elements of a positive working environment. Workplaces that foster a supportive atmosphere, effective communication and a culture of recognition enhance job satisfaction among healthcare workers. Numerous studies have shown that job satisfaction is closely linked to staff retention and workforce stability. In a sector where staff turnover can negatively impact the continuity and quality of care, maintaining employee satisfaction is of paramount importance. High levels of staff loyalty and retention foster stronger relationships between staff and patients, which in turn contributes to improved patient experiences [38]. Furthermore, a positive working environment reduces burnout, a widespread issue in the healthcare sector. Burnout can lead to a decline in compassion, an increase in medical errors, and a negative impact on patient outcomes. Research shows that healthcare workers are less likely to experience burnout when they are genuinely satisfied with their jobs. This helps to maintain motivation and engagement, and has a direct positive impact on patient interactions. Satisfied healthcare workers tend to demonstrate greater empathy and care in their interactions with patients. Consequently, reducing burnout not only supports the psychological well-being of staff but also enhances the quality of care provided to patients [43]. The benefits of a positive working environment extend beyond patient care and also enhance the organisation’s overall performance. Healthcare organisations that prioritise staff well-being typically achieve lower absenteeism rates, reduced staff turnover and better financial outcomes. When staff are satisfied and committed to their work, this strengthens the organisation’s reputation and attracts both patients and qualified professionals. This creates a positive cycle that benefits all stakeholders and encourages organisations to continuously improve working conditions [44].

In the study, participants working in high-risk units rated the work environment more positively than administrative unit managers in the MN-PR sub-dimension (p < 0.05). Tosun and Yıldırım’s [31] study also found that nurse managers working in internal units scored higher on the “nurse manager-unit staff relationships” subscale and on the total scale. These results can be associated with the need for nurse managers working in clinics, including high-risk units, to establish more intensive cooperation, especially with physicians. Furthermore, for similar reasons, it is an expected result that nurse managers working as charge nurses/supervisors in clinics have a more positive perception of the work environment in the CC, Productivity, and MN-PR subscales in the study.

The study found that participants working at the city hospital and ER-UNIVersity Hospital perceived the work environment more positively in terms of MN-PR and MN-USR dimensions than participants working at smaller hospitals. Similarly, in the study by Chen et al. [13], the work environment assessments of nurse managers working in tertiary hospitals were more positive. In our study, the work environment assessments of nurse managers working in private hospitals were more negative compared to other hospitals. Considering the facilitators and limitations provided by private hospitals to nurse managers, it can be said that this result may vary. Indeed, previous studies have yielded different results [13, 31].

Participants with 10 years or more of administrative experience had a more negative perception in the MN-MR sub-dimension compared to those with less than 5 years of experience. This result is not consistent with the findings of previous studies [27, 30]. Experience is critical for developing competence as a nurse manager [26] and can mitigate the negative effects of challenging workplace experiences [33, 46]. The fact that nurse managers report that it takes an average of 7 years to become competent in their positions further emphasizes the importance of experience [12, 44, 46]. Beyond developing competence, length of tenure as a nurse manager has also been associated with increased self-efficacy [45] and perceptions of job control [47]. Shirey et al.[40] found that more experienced nurse managers used more effective coping strategies, resulting in reduced negative health outcomes stemming from workplace stress. While experience is important for developing managerial competence, working in a high-workload and stressful job for a long time can lead to burnout and may reduce satisfaction with the environment.

The study found that nurse managers who were not affected by the work environment perceived the work environment more positively than those who were affected. This result supports the study by Tosun and Yıldırım [31]. Conflicts in the work environment negatively affect job performance and can lead to negative consequences such as job dissatisfaction/low performance, negative effects on patient care, and increased costs [48]. Therefore, the positive assessments of those who believe that the work environment does not affect their performance are an expected result.

In the study, high-income nurse managers generally have a more positive perception of the work environment. Significant differences were found compared to other nurse managers, particularly in terms of organizational culture, productivity, and financial resources. An increase in income satisfaction increases the likelihood of a person being happy and having a higher level of life satisfaction. An individual’s satisfaction with their income is related to their proximity to their desired goals and therefore has a positive effect on their happiness [49]. Competitive remuneration remains a key factor in nurse manager retention. To attract and retain qualified nurse managers, organisations must offer salaries and benefits in line with industry standards [38]. Therefore, high-income managers can be explained by their satisfaction with organizational culture and financial resources and their ability to be more productive in their organizations.

Results and recommendations

This study evaluated the perceptions of 407 nurse managers working in Turkey regarding their working environments and the factors influencing these perceptions. It was found that nurse managers generally had a positive perception of their working environment; furthermore, it was determined that variables such as age, marital status, whether they had children, length of professional experience, and daily working hours did not influence these perceptions. It was found that the nurses’ gender, educational level/title, perceived income status, managerial position and tenure, administrative unit/hospital, whether they had received management training, their susceptibility to the work environment, and their level of satisfaction influenced their perceptions of the work environment. In particular, it can be said that variables such as job satisfaction, receipt of management training, title and managerial position create more significant differences in certain sub-dimensions.

Improving the working environment for nurse managers not only enhances their well-being but also makes a positive contribution to the quality of patient care. Healthcare organisations should develop supportive policies that take into account the needs of nurse managers. Healthcare organisations should develop supportive policies to foster a positive working environment, taking into account the factors that influence nursing managers’ perceptions of their working environment. In particular, educational level and they have received management training are key variables that should be prioritised in the recruitment of nursing managers, as these factors influence staff productivity and the achievement of organisational goals. Experience gained in management roles is also highly valuable for nurse managers. In this context, preventing the loss of experienced nurse managers due to adverse workplace conditions is equally important in terms of achieving organisational objectives. Retaining experienced managers by improving their working environment may be more cost-effective than investing in the development of new nurse managers. Strategies designed to support nurse managers in accessing sufficient financial resources for sustainable care are required. Healthcare organisations and senior hospital managers play a key role in fostering a healthy professional practice environment for nursing managers. Hospital managers should prioritise the working environment of nursing managers and strive to support, empower and guide change in order to create a practice environment and, ultimately, further optimise nursing practice. To this end, improving and developing the work environment of nurse managers is recommended. It is recommended that nurse managers undertake management training to achieve more effective outcomes.

Limitations

A few limitations were identified in our study. First, the method of convenience sampling and cross-sectional design used in this study may limit the generalization of the results. Although the study covers different hospital tiers and the whole country, the representativeness of the sample will specifically influence the results. Secondly, only online self-report questionnaires were used for data collection, which may lead to reporting bias. The use of multiple data collection methods could enrich the findings.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Permission was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Health Sciences at Ankara Yıldırım Beyazıt University in order to conduct the study. (E-84892257-300-327331). Data were collected using an online survey consisting of a demographic information form and the Manager Nurse Work Environment Scale (MNWES). The NWSES was developed by Warshawskyet al. [15] in 2013 and adapted into Turkish by Tosun and Yıldırım [32]. The scale and subscales are evaluated on an average score, with scores closer to six indicating more positive perceptions of the work environment. The Cronbach’s alpha value for the general scale was 0.97 in the original study [15] and 0.96 in the Turkish adaptation study [32]. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha value of the scale was determined to be 0.96. The data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics, V. 22.0 and Jamovi 2.7.18 for Windows software packages. Descriptive statistical values were presented as frequency, percentage, mean, and standard deviation. The normality of the data distribution was assessed using Skewness and Kurtosis values, and the variables were found to be normally distributed. Parametric methods were used in the analysis of the data. Independent groups t-tests, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), and post hoc (Tukey, LSD) analyses were used to compare the mean scale and subscale scores according to the participants’ descriptive characteristics. The relationship between the subscales was examined using Pearson correlation analysis. Cronbach’s Alpha coefficient was calculated to assess scale reliability. The statistical significance level was set at p < 0.05. Before participation, all participants were presented with the study information online. Electronic informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their enrollment in the study. Participants were asked to complete the online questionnaires independently and anonymously. The questionnaire and scale used in this study were published in the study by Tosun and Yıldırım [32].
