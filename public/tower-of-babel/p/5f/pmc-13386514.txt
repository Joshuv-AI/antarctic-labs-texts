Integrated Kinetic and Transcriptomic Insights Into Nano‐Assisted Tandemol Biodegradation by UV‐Mutagenized Pseudomonas synaxantha rhizo‐25
Authors: Nyashadzashe Pascalia Masvingwe, Isaac A. Sanusi, Ajit Kumar, Gueguim E. B. Kana, Ademola O. Olaniran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis study assesses the kinetics of Tandemol lubricant degradation by Pseudomonas synaxantha rhizo‐25 after UV treatment and nanoparticle inclusion. The degradation efficiency was assessed in terms of the Total Petroleum Hydrocarbons (TPH) and aromatic compounds degraded in a liquid medium containing Tandemol lubricant. The medium composition was further supplemented with nanoparticles to evaluate their impact on the process. Metabolite profiling was conducted using Gas Chromatography‐Mass Spectrometry (GC‐MS), while transcriptomic analysis identified key enzymes involved in the degradation process. For TPH degradation, biodegradation rate (K) and half‐life (t 1/2) values were found to be −0.39 M−1day−1 and 10.22 days (wildtype), and 0.013 M day−1 and 9.33 days (UV‐treated), respectively. For the aromatic compounds, the estimated K and t 1/2 values were −63.30 M−1 day−1 and 7 h (wildtype), and −0.69 day−1 and 1.01 days (UV‐treated). The nanoparticle supplemented aromatic degradation yielded K and t 1/2 values of −21.01 M−1h−1 and 0.85 h (UV‐treated). Aromatic compound degradation was observed to follow the meta‐cleavage pathway with terminal oxidation as an activation mechanism. Oxidoreductases, namely Cytochrome P450 and Tau D/Tfd A dioxygenase, were responsible for the primary step in the degradation pathway. Overall, these results showed that the UV treatment and the inclusion of nanoparticles enhanced biodegradation efficiency towards optimum petroleum contaminant bioremediation.

Introduction

The presence of hydrocarbon waste such as lubricant oils in the environment represents a significant ecological threat due to their persistence and toxicity [1, 2]. Globally, more than 1.3 million tons of oil waste are discharged annually into soil and freshwater systems. In South Africa alone, approximately 120 million liters of lubricant waste are generated each year, of which nearly 24 million liters ultimately enter the environment despite recycling efforts. Waste lubricant oil such as Tandemol consists of a complex mixture of hydrocarbons, including straight‐ and branched‐chain alkanes, cycloalkanes, aromatic compounds, and oxygenated derivatives, together with nitrogen‐ and sulfur‐containing molecules and heterocyclic compounds. Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), a highly recalcitrant and carcinogenic subset of aromatic constituents, are increasingly targeted in recent bioremediation studies [2, 3, 4, 5]. Many of these constituents exhibit environmental persistence and low natural biodegradability [1].

Conventional disposal practices such as landfilling frequently result in hydrocarbon leaching and groundwater contamination, necessitating sustainable remediation strategies [6]. Consequently, bioremediation using microorganisms has emerged as an environmentally compatible approach for transforming hazardous hydrocarbons into less toxic products [2, 7]. Microbial degradation is widely applied for hydrocarbon remediation, with bacteria, fungi, and microalgae reported as effective degraders [2]. Among these, bacteria are particularly important due to their metabolic versatility and ability to enzymatically transform diverse hydrocarbon classes [1]. Species belonging to genera such as Acinetobacter, Bacillus, Rhodococcus, Sphingomonas, and Pseudomonas have demonstrated strong hydrocarbon‐degrading capabilities [1, 8, 9]. Pseudomonas species are especially prominent owing to their environmental ubiquity, adaptive metabolism, and biosurfactant production, which enhances hydrocarbon bioavailability by increasing oil–water interfacial interactions [10, 11, 12, 13, 14]. For instance, UV‐adapted Pseudomonas pseudoalcaligenes strain PAH‐7 achieved over 92% degradation of phenanthrene and pyrene in lubricant‐contaminated soil within 14 days under nano‐Fe3O4 stimulation, while a transcriptome‐guided Pseudomonas synaxantha variant demonstrated upregulated phnAc and nahG homologs during naphthalene and fluorene catabolism in diesel–lubricant co‐contaminated microcosms.

Improving microbial biodegradation efficiency often involves modifying microbial performance through adaptive or induced mutations [15]. While advanced genome‐editing approaches exist, random mutagenesis using physicochemical agents such as ultraviolet (UV) radiation provides a cost‐effective alternative for generating strains with enhanced metabolic capabilities [16]. Mutagenesis may alter growth behavior and metabolic activity, making it essential to evaluate not only degradation efficiency but also microbial growth dynamics and biochemical responses during pollutant removal. However, systematic studies integrating microbial growth kinetics with mechanistic degradation analysis remain limited, particularly for P. synaxantha strains.

Process kinetics provide quantitative insight into microbial growth and substrate transformation during biodegradation. Growth models such as logistic and Monod kinetics describe microbial proliferation under varying process conditions, while degradation kinetics (zero‐, first‐, and second‐order models) characterize contaminant removal rates and reaction mechanisms [17]. Process parameters usually evaluated include growth phases, degradation rate constants, and half‐life; these values enable prediction of microbial behavior and optimization of bioremediation systems. Despite extensive kinetic modeling in hydrocarbon biodegradation studies, the degradation kinetics of Pseudomonas synaxantha rhizo‐25 remain largely unexplored [16].

Although numerous studies demonstrate microbial degradation of waste lubricants, most focus primarily on total petroleum hydrocarbon (TPH) removal without integrating kinetic modeling, enzymatic characterization, and molecular‐level analysis. For example, Streptomyces ginkgonis KM‐1–2 achieved 76.4% degradation mainly through biosurfactant activity, while Pseudomonas oleovorans strain NMA reached 98%–99% degradation supported mainly by Monod modeling but lacking enzymatic or molecular profiling. Other investigations report substantial hydrocarbon reductions following soil amendment (bioaugmentation), yet mechanistic insights into microbial metabolism and genetic responses remain limited. Even though Wang et al. [18] reported real‐time Raman–kinetic modeling of PAH depletion coupled with metatranscriptomic tracking of dioxygenase operons in Pseudomonas‐bioaugmented sediments, integrated studies with molecular input are limited. These gaps highlight the need for integrated studies combining kinetic, enzymatic, and molecular analyses, particularly involving improved microbial strains and nano‐catalytic enhancement strategies [19, 20].

Nanomaterials have recently gained attention as biocatalytic enhancers in biodegradation systems due to their high surface area, enhanced reactivity, and interactions with biological molecules. Metal nanoparticles, including copper, cobalt, nickel, and zinc, can function as enzyme cofactors and improve catalytic efficiency during hydrocarbon transformation, offering additional potential to enhance microbial remediation performance.

Moreover, while several studies have reported hydrocarbon biodegradation by Pseudomonas spp., these investigations have largely focused on degradation efficiency or conventional kinetic modeling without integrating molecular‐level transcriptional responses with biodegradation kinetics [11, 13, 14]. For example, studies involving P. oleovorans and other hydrocarbon‐degrading Pseudomonas strains primarily quantified substrate removal and growth kinetics, but did not resolve the transcriptional basis underpinning enhanced degradation performance or catalytic adaptation during lubricant transformation. In contrast, the present study provides a direct kinetic–transcriptomic integration that links degradation rate constants, metabolite evolution, and enzyme‐expression profiles in P. synaxantha rhizo‐25 during Tandemol lubricant biodegradation. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report describing an integrated kinetic and transcriptomic evaluation of Tandemol lubricant biodegradation by P. synaxantha rhizo‐25, also, the first to demonstrate how UV mutagenesis coupled with nanoparticle supplementation mechanistically enhances this process.

This study therefore aimed to evaluate the process kinetics of UV‐treated and wildtype P. synaxantha rhizo‐25 during Tandemol lubricant biodegradation. Specifically, the objectives were to: (1) compare growth and degradation kinetics between UV‐treated and wildtype strains; (2) elucidate the molecular basis of the degradation in terms of degradative enzymes and mechanisms involved; and (3) assess the role of nanoparticles as biocatalysts in enhancing biodegradation efficiency.

Materials and Methods

Tandemol Lubricant Sample

Waste Tandemol lubricant was obtained from a mid‐stream aluminium processing facility in Pietermaritzburg, South Africa, where it is used in foil mill operations. Samples were collected in sterile 1L Schott bottles and stored at 25°C prior to analysis. Detailed physicochemical composition of the lubricant is provided in the Supporting Information.

Isolation and Identification of Hydrocarbon‐Degrading Bacterium

Hydrocarbon‐degrading bacteria were isolated from soil collected at a Tandemol‐contaminated landfill site using enrichment culture techniques previously described by Nyashadzashe et al. [16]. Briefly, soil samples were enriched in Bushnell–Haas medium supplemented with Tandemol as the sole carbon source, followed by serial enrichment and purification to obtain pure cultures.

Genomic DNA extraction, 16S rRNA gene amplification, sequencing, and phylogenetic analysis were performed as described previously [16]. Sequence comparison against the NCBI GenBank database identified the isolate as P. synaxantha rhizo‐25 (Supporting Information S1: Figure 1).

Exposure of Pure Colonies to UV Radiation

Pure colonies were spread on nutrient agar plates and placed in a UV‐C PCR chamber (PLAS LABS, Lansing, Michigan, USA) equipped with a 254 nm germicidal lamp. Additionally, secondary mutagenesis screening trials were carried out using a UV Stratalinker 1800 (Stratagene, USA). The nominal chamber irradiance, specified by the manufacturer, was 1.4 mW cm−2 at a working distance of 15 cm. Irradiation was applied to surface‐plated colonies, the effective cellular dose was attenuated by colony architecture, agar‐associated absorption, and spatial non‐uniformity within the chamber. Exposure durations of 2, 4, 6, 12, 24, and 48 h were evaluated as part of a survivor‐selection mutagenesis screening strategy. Following each exposure, plates were incubated for recovery, and surviving colonies were sub‐cultured prior to biodegradation screening. The 24 h exposure yielded the highest viable degradative performance and was therefore selected for subsequent analyses.

Biomass Concentration Determination

The biomass concentration was determined using a pre‐established standard calibration curve, which defines the relationship between optical density (OD) and biomass dry weight. The cell dry weight was then calculated by extrapolating the OD measurements from this calibration curve [21].

Transcriptomic Analysis and Enzyme Identification

Rna Isolation and High‐Throughput Sequencing Library Preparation

Bacterial cells (Wildtype and 24 h UV treated) from an overnight culture stored in Luria‐Bertani broth solution were centrifuged separately at 12,000g for 2 min and subsequently resuspended in 100 μL of 0.4 mg/mL lysozyme in TE buffer. They were incubated for 5 min at 25°C. After lysozyme treatment, 300 μL lysis buffer supplemented with 20 μL of 14.3 M β‐mercaptoethanol was added to each lysed cell solution, and further lysis was carried out by mechanical disruption of each sample four times for 30 s at maximum speed in a Mini‐ Bead beater (Scientific Industries Inc.). The lysed cell solutions were placed in an ice bath for 2 min between bead beating cycles. Total RNA was analyzed on a 1.5% agarose gel prepared in 1X TAE buffer, electrophoresed for 55 min at 110 V. RNA extraction success was confirmed by visualizing the gel on a SYNGENE G:Box, with images captured using GeneSnap software. The quality and concentration of the isolated RNA were systematically quantified using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

RNA Computation

The initial unprocessed RNA sequencing dataset from Inqaba Biotech Laboratory, was acquired, and subsequently imported into the Galaxy web platform (usegalaxy.org). The dataset was then structured and arranged on the platform. The dataset underwent initial preprocessing and alignment procedures. The trimmomatic tool in Omics box (version 3.1.1) was used for preprocessing, cleaning RNA sequences by trimming low‐quality bases and removing adapter sequences to ensure high‐quality reads for downstream analysis. High‐throughput sequencing libraries were prepared using the NEBNext Ultra II RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina, and computational read alignment to the reference genome was performed using Bowtie2 software. The quality of the processed RNA samples was assessed using FastQC version (0.12.1), which confirmed their validity for further analysis (Supporting Information S1: Figure 4). Additionally, BUSCO (Benchmarking Universal Single‐Copy Orthologs) analysis results for the RNA sequences revealed that the majority of the expected single‐copy orthologs were identified as complete, confirming validity for downstream analysis (Supporting Information S1: Figure 5). The resulting aligned reads were then subjected to processing to obtain assembled transcript files (assembled transcripts were subsequently imported into Blast2GO software). Gene expression levels were quantified via RSEM, and downstream statistical modeling of differential expression profiles was accomplished using the DESeq. 2 package in R. The resulting IDs of similar sequences obtained from the National Centre for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) were then aligned with the Gene Ontology (GO) annotation dataset during the mapping stage. Afterwards, the acquired information was utilized for annotation, assigning the most reliable GO terms to the input sequences. Comparison of the GO annotation datasets obtained from the wildtype and modified isolate revealed differences in the genes and enzymes expressed by the isolates after oil exposure.

Gas Chromatography–Mass Spectrometry (GC–MS) Analysis of Biodegradation Products

Metabolite formation during biodegradation was monitored using GC–MS. Oil was extracted periodically using dichloromethane and analysed using a Shimadzu GC‐MS‐QP2010 SE system equipped with an HP‐5MS capillary column, following previously reported analytical conditions [16, 22]. Extracts tracking early and late phase metabolites were systematically concentrated using a vacuum‐driven rotary evaporator instrument, followed by trimethylsilyl derivatization prior to injection into a Shimadzu GC‐MS‐QP2010 Plus system. Compound identification was achieved by comparison with NIST spectral libraries and retention indices. Full instrumental parameters are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

Biodegradation Assessment

The biodegradation achieved in this study was assessed in terms of the TPH degraded. This was calculated from the GC‐MS data using the formula: %TPHdegraded=Initial(Control)TPH−ObservedTPHInitialTPH×100

Nanoparticle Inclusion for Tandemol Degradation

The nanoparticles (NPs): iron (II) oxide (Fe2O3) and composite (Fe3O4CuZnCoNi) used in this study were kindly provided by the Discipline of Microbiology, University of KwaZulu‐Natal, Pietermaritzburg campus. The detailed synthesis and characterization have been reported in Sanusi et al. [23].

Statistical Analysis

All experimental procedures were conducted using three independent biological replicates (n = 3) for each treatment condition. For GC–MS quantification, biomass determination, degradation efficiency measurements, and nanoparticle supplementation experiments, replicate cultures were independently prepared, inoculated, and processed under identical experimental conditions. Data were analysed using one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) within a Generalized Linear Model (GLM) framework in IBM SPSS Statistics version 29. Where significant differences were detected, means were separated using the Least Significant Difference (LSD) post hoc test. Statistical significance was accepted at p < 0.05. All data are presented as mean ± standard error (SE). In all graphical representations, error bars indicate the SE of the mean derived from the three biological replicates. Statistical significance is indicated as follows: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001.

NB: Unless otherwise stated, all biodegradation, growth kinetics, and nanoparticle supplementation experiments were performed using three independent biological replicates. Technical measurements within each replicate (e.g., spectrophotometric and chromatographic readings) were averaged prior to statistical analysis.

Results

P. synaxantha rhizo‐25 Growth Kinetics

The wildtype and UV‐mutagenized (P. synaxantha rhizo‐25) variants exhibited typical lag, exponential (Days 3–9), and death phases (Figure 1). The UV‐treated isolate demonstrated a higher growth constant (Kg = 0.12) and specific growth rate (µ = 0.14 day−1) compared to the wildtype (Kg = 0.094; µ = 0.094 day−1) (Figure 2; Table 1). Moreover, the growth constant and the specific growth obtained for the UV‐mutagenized P. synaxantha rhizo‐25 were supported by the transcriptomic analysis that revealed genes associated with organic substance metabolic processes comprised 75.63% of biological process annotations in the UV‐treated isolate, compared to 56.17% in the wildtype. Catalytic activity accounted for 76.97% versus 71.87%, respectively (Supporting Information S1: Figures 6–7). The UV‐treated isolate also showed an increase in membrane‐associated gene content (97.05% vs. 73.12%). Detected transport systems included ATP‐binding cassette (ABC) transporters, amino acid, sugar, and polyamine transporters (Figure 3; Supporting Information S1: Table 1). Transcriptomic analysis also identified MBL‐fold hydrolases, cobaltochelatase, and iron/copper/zinc‐associated proteins (Figure 3; Supporting Information S1: Table 8). Additionally, membrane‐associated efflux systems, such as drug/metabolite transporter (DMT) permeases and components of ATP synthase and the electron transport chain, were identified. The UV‐mutagenized P. synaxantha rhizo‐25 showed features and an outcome for satisfactory Tandemol lubricant oil degradation.

TPH Degradation Kinetics

For the wildtype, TPH degradation half‐lives were 13.89, 12.10, and 10.22 days for zero‐, first‐, and second‐order models, respectively (Table 2). Reaction constants K 0, K 1, and K 2 were 0.009 M day−1, 0.0057 day−1, and −0.39 M−1 day−1. The UV‐treated isolate showed higher reaction constants (K 0 = 0.013 M day−1; K 1 = − 0.17 day−1; K 2 = − 3.88 M−1 day−1) and shorter half‐lives (9.33, 4.12, and 1.03 days). After 17 days, the UV‐treated isolate achieved 95.46% TPH removal compared to 62.51% for the wildtype (Table 3). In the wildtype, biomass decreased between Days 10–12 (2.83 to 2.29 g L−1), while degradation increased (44.38% to 50.76%) (Figure 4). In the UV‐treated isolate, biomass declined after Day 13 (2.78 to 2.58 g L−1, yet degradation increased (67.86% to 77.25%) (Figure 4).

Aromatic Degradation Kinetics

The UV‐treated isolate's second‐order half‐life for aromatic compounds was 0.0025 days, compared to 0.28 days for the wildtype. Transcriptomic data showed oxidoreductases were enriched in the UV‐treated isolate (19.28% vs. 15.61%), including monooxygenases and dioxygenases. Nine hydrocarbon‐degrading enzymes were identified in the UV transcriptome, eight of which were oxidoreductases (Supporting Information S1: Table 2).

Wildtype aromatic degradation followed second‐order kinetics (R 2 = 0.93), whereas the UV‐treated isolate followed first‐order kinetics (R 2 = 0.98). This shift in kinetic behaviour likely reflects transcriptomically supported enhancement of oxidative enzyme systems in the UV‐treated isolate, enabling more rapid substrate transformation with reduced dependence on biomass‐mediated interaction effects. The UV‐treated strain achieved 99.98% degradation compared to 95.46% for the wildtype (Table 3). Aromatic degradation plateaued within ~5 days for both, with maximal removal reached at Day 12 for the UV‐treated isolate and Day 17 for the wildtype.

Mass Balance

By Day 17, the UV‐treated isolate degraded 23.57 ± 1.23 g of oil (94.3% efficiency), leaving 1.43 ± 0.05 g residual. The wildtype degraded 15.56 ± 0.78 g (62.2% efficiency), leaving 9.44 ± 0.47 g (Supporting Information S1: Table 4). For aromatics, the UV‐treated isolate achieved 100% degradation by Day 15, while the wildtype achieved 97.5% by Day 17 (Supporting Information S1: Table 5).

Nanoparticle Inclusion in Lubricant Waste Degradation

Biomass in the control experiment was 4.03 ± 0.18 g L−1 while biomass concentrations of 2.99 ± 0.14 g L−1 and 2.87 ± 0.13 g L−1 were obtained in the presence of 0.01% Fe2O3 and 0.01% composite nanoparticles, respectively. However, 1% composite (Fe3O4CuZnCoNi) nanoparticles resulted in the highest biomass concentration of 5.45 ± 0.27 g L−1 (Figure 5). This increase was statistically significant relative to the control treatment (p < 0.01). Degradation efficiency was 96.7% for the control, 98.48% for 1% Fe2O3, and 99.08% for 1% composite nanoparticles. The enhanced degradation observed in the composite nanoparticle treatment was statistically significant compared with the untreated control (*p < 0.05). No apparent reduction in bacterial growth or biodegradation performance was observed at the applied nanoparticle concentration, suggesting that the selected dosage was physiologically tolerable to the isolate under the experimental conditions.

Metabolite Profiling via GC–MS

GC–MS analysis revealed dynamic temporal changes in metabolite composition during Tandemol biodegradation (Supporting Information S1: Table 9). At Day 0, six aromatic compounds were detected, including monoaromatic hydrocarbons such as benzene 1,3‐dimethyl and p‐xylene, together with more structurally complex aromatic constituents. These compounds represented the dominant hydrocarbon fraction prior to microbial degradation. By Day 3, the metabolite profile shifted markedly, with the appearance of substituted aromatic derivatives and early ring‐transformation products. Compounds such as benzene (2,4‐dimethylpentyl) and the ring‐modified intermediate 2(1H)‐benzocyclooctenone were detected, indicating active biotransformation processes. Concurrent emergence of oxygenated aromatic structures suggested initiation of oxidative modification reactions. Intermediate metabolite formation continued over time. On Day 6, substituted bicyclic compounds were detected, reflecting continued structural modification of aromatic substrates. By Day 9, phthalic acid derivatives became prominent components of the metabolite profile, indicating extensive transformation of aromatic rings into more oxidized intermediates. The terminal metabolite detected on Day 12 was 1,2‐bis(trimethylsilyl)−3‐methylbenzene, accompanied by a substantial reduction in earlier aromatic compounds, demonstrating progressive degradation and transformation of complex hydrocarbons.

Analysis of non‐aromatic metabolites (Supporting Information S1: Tables 10–11) further revealed sequential changes in ester, alcohol, and carboxylic acid composition. Long‐chain esters detected at Day 0 decreased over time and were replaced by simpler esters by Day 3. Subsequent detection of dicarboxylic intermediates and short‐chain oxidation products indicated continued breakdown of hydrocarbon chains into smaller metabolic intermediates.

Metabolite Profiling With Nanoparticle Inclusion

GC–MS analysis of nanoparticle‐supplemented systems (Supporting Information S1: Tables 12–13) demonstrated distinct metabolite patterns compared with untreated cultures. Early‐stage samples contained triphenylphosphine oxide alongside iron‐containing complexes, suggesting modified chemical environments during degradation. As degradation progressed, aromatic oxidation products became increasingly prominent. Phthalic acid derivatives and associated diesters were detected during intermediate stages, indicating continued transformation of aromatic compounds into oxidized intermediates. Late‐stage metabolite profiles included isophthalic acid derivatives and substituted benzenediols. These compounds coincided with a reduction in higher‐molecular‐weight aromatic species, indicating further progression of hydrocarbon transformation toward simpler oxidation products.

Discussion

The enhanced growth kinetics of the UV‐treated isolate are consistent with UV‐induced mutagenesis, which can promote metabolic efficiency through DNA alterations, such as gene amplification that enhances growth‐related pathways [24]. The transcriptomic shift toward catalytic activity and membrane‐associated genes suggests a greater functional specialization. Increased catalytic representation is linked to accelerated biochemical reactions, while expanded membrane protein diversity improves nutrient uptake and metabolic flexibility [25, 26]. Specifically, polyamine transporters may enable the utilization of compounds like putrescine as carbon sources, supporting the metabolism of Tandemol lubricant oil [27].

The kinetic modeling of TPH degradation suggests that reaction rates are concentration‐dependent, a trend characteristic of second‐order kinetics where efficiency improves as concentrations decline [28, 29]. The UV‐treated isolate's accelerated biodegradation is likely a result of metabolic enhancements and possibly increased cell surface roughness, which promotes biofilm formation and attachment [30]. The observation that hydrocarbon removal persisted despite declining biomass suggests a decoupling of growth and degradation, indicating that processes are driven by sustained enzymatic activity rather than active cell division [31, 32].

The high substrate specificity for aromatic compounds, particularly in the UV‐treated isolate, is supported by the enrichment of oxidoreductases, such as monooxygenases and dioxygenases, which are essential for breaking down aromatic amines and phenols [33]. The transition from second‐order to first‐order kinetic dominance following UV mutagenesis suggests a shift from biomass‐coupled degradation toward substrate‐driven catalytic turnover. This likely reflects increased enzymatic efficiency and reduced diffusional or catalytic constraints, consistent with the observed enrichment of oxidoreductase‐associated transcripts [18]. The identification of gentisate 1,2‐dioxygenase, cytochrome P450, and TauD/TfdA dioxygenases highlights a pathway where stable benzene structures are converted into bioavailable intermediates like acetyl‐CoA for the Krebs cycle [34, 35].

Nanoparticle effects were concentration‐dependent. The inhibitory effects of nanoparticles may stem from the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and disruption of DNA synthesis [36, 37]. Conversely, at 1%, nanoparticles likely provide essential metal cofactors (Fe, Cu, Co) that enhance the catalytic activity of monooxygenases [38, 39]. Nanoparticles may also increase bioavailability through emulsification [40]. The transcriptomic presence of cobaltochelatase and zinc‐dependent enzymes supports the idea that the isolate mitigates stress while utilizing these metals to regulate catalytic activity and metal homeostasis [41, 42].

However, the improved hydrocarbon degradation performance observed in nanoparticle‐supplemented systems should be interpreted with caution. While transcriptomic enrichment patterns indicate potential modulation of stress‐response pathways, hydrocarbon‐associated metabolism, and cellular adaptation processes, the present data do not directly demonstrate enzymatic activation, ROS‐mediated regulation, or specific nanoparticle–cell interactions. Thus, rather than confirming a defined mechanistic role, these findings suggest a plausible association between nanoparticle supplementation and altered cellular response states that may contribute to enhanced biodegradation efficiency. In addition, although the selected nanoparticle concentration (1% w/v) did not exhibit observable inhibitory effects on P. synxantha rhizo‐25—as evidenced by sustained biomass production, continued hydrocarbon metabolism, and enhanced degradation efficiency—dedicated cytotoxicity and dose‐optimization assays were not performed. Transcriptomic evidence of proteins involved in metal transport, ion homeostasis, and detoxification further supports a cellular adaptation to nanoparticle exposure (Supporting Information S1: Table 8), rather than a confirmed stimulatory mechanism. Future studies incorporating concentration‐dependent toxicity assessments, enzymatic activity measurements, intracellular ROS quantification, and nanoparticle localization analyses will be necessary to validate these interpretations and establish safe and effective operational parameters for nanoparticle‐assisted bioremediation systems.

Metabolite profiling supports a sequential biodegradation mechanism characterized by progressive oxidation and structural modification of aromatic hydrocarbons. The early disappearance of monoaromatic compounds and simultaneous appearance of substituted derivatives indicate rapid microbial activation of recalcitrant hydrocarbons, consistent with oxidative initiation mechanisms reported for aromatic biodegradation [43, 44]. The emergence of oxygenated intermediates and ring‐modified compounds suggests enzymatic activation through monooxygenase and dioxygenase reactions. Such enzymes introduce hydroxyl groups that destabilize aromatic ring structures, facilitating subsequent cleavage reactions [34, 45]. Detection of benzocyclooctenone derivatives therefore reflects partial oxidation and rearrangement of aromatic substrates during early degradation stages.

The accumulation of phthalic acid derivatives represents a key transition point in the degradation pathway. These metabolites are widely recognized intermediates formed following oxidative ring cleavage and subsequent lactone formation and hydrolysis processes [46, 47]. Their presence indicates conversion of stable aromatic structures into compounds more readily assimilated into central metabolic pathways. The progressive shift from long‐chain esters to simpler esters and dicarboxylic intermediates further supports a dominant terminal oxidation pathway. Sequential oxidation of terminal carbon atoms produces alcohols, aldehydes, and carboxylic acids that are subsequently incorporated into intermediary metabolism [48, 49]. Detection of dicarboxylic intermediates suggests metabolic funneling toward compounds compatible with the tricarboxylic acid cycle.

Overall, the proposed degradation pathway (Figure 6) involves stepwise oxidation and cleavage of complex aromatic structures into progressively simpler intermediates, including phthalic acid derivatives, which are ultimately converted into central metabolic intermediates such as salicylate, pyruvate, and acetyl‐CoA that feed into the Krebs cycle [50]. Enzymes such as 3‐hydroxyacyl‐CoA further facilitate conversion of intermediates into acetyl‐CoA [50].

Aromatic compound degradation pathway used by Pseudomonas synaxantha rhizo‐25 during Tandemol degradation. The chromatographic profile reveals the presence of several notable metabolites identified through gas chromatography‐mass spectrometry (GC‐MS) analysis. Detected compounds include aromatic hydrocarbons, esters, and heterocyclic molecules such as benzene, (2,4‐dimethylpentyl)‐, 2,3‐methanonaphthalene‐9‐carboxamide, 4‐O‐methyl‐2,3‐O‐benzal‐D‐mannosan, and phthalic acid, furfuryl heptyl ester. Additionally, intermediates related to salicylate, pyruvate, and acetyl‐CoA, as well as Kreb's cycle components, were detected, suggesting active metabolic processing in response to UV exposure.

Nanoparticle supplementation appeared to enhance oxidative transformation processes. The presence of triphenylphosphine oxide may reflect enhanced redox activity and electron transfer dynamics, potentially facilitating oxidative reactions during hydrocarbon degradation [51, 52]. Additionally, iron‐containing complexes indicate the involvement of metal cofactors that may promote catalytic oxidation reactions.

The detection of isophthalic acid derivatives in later stages provides evidence for degradation via a meta‐cleavage pathway, in which hydroxylated aromatic intermediates undergo oxidative ring opening at the meta position [53, 54]. Formation of substituted benzenediols further supports continued oxidation of aromatic intermediates prior to conversion into aliphatic metabolites.

Collectively, these metabolite transitions confirm a stepwise degradation pathway involving oxidative activation, aromatic ring cleavage, and subsequent conversion into central metabolic intermediates. The observed metabolite progression aligns with established microbial hydrocarbon degradation mechanisms and demonstrates coordinated biochemical transformation leading to effective mineralization of Tandemol lubricant oil hydrocarbon.

This study demonstrates that UV treatment and nanoparticle supplementation significantly enhance the biodegradation efficiency of P. synaxantha rhizo‐25 for Tandemol lubricant oil waste. The UV‐treated isolate exhibited higher growth rates and improved degradation kinetics, particularly for aromatic hydrocarbons, by effectively enhancing the expression of genes involved in metabolic pathways. Enzymatic analysis revealed that oxidoreductases, including Cytochrome P450 and Tau D/Tfd A dioxygenase, played key roles in this process. Furthermore, the 1% composite nanoparticle inclusion achieved a near‐complete efficiency of 99.08% by optimizing enzyme activity and hydrocarbon bioavailability. However, the mechanistic role of nanoparticle supplementation remains inferential and requires validation through targeted enzymatic, oxidative stress, and nanoparticle–cell interaction analyses. These findings highlight the potential of integrating mutagenesis and nanoparticle catalysis for petroleum‐based waste bioremediation.
