Resilient and decentralized demand-side management in smart grids using blockchain
Authors: Shubhani Aggarwal, Arzoo Miglani, Norah Saleh Alghamdi, Gaurav Dhiman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecent advancements in information and communication technologies, along with electronic services such as smart metering for billing, energy management in smart buildings, and electric vehicle charging and discharging systems, are widely adopted by consumers. In this setting, devices inform the utility center of their energy requirements to aid in matching demand with supply. The utility center efficiently manages these needs, distributing them to end-users and overseeing the demand component. However, the transmission of end-user energy data through the Internet discloses the exchanged information between Internet of Things (IoT) devices and the utility center. This framework leads to vulnerabilities, including data manipulation, service interruptions, and insufficient transparency. To address these challenges, researchers implemented centralized demand-side management, which has a single point of failure. Consequently, a decentralized authentication system is required to guarantee security and robustness against threats. Hence, in this paper, we propose a blockchain-based demand-side management scheme that incorporates mutual authentication between IoT devices and the utility centre. The security evaluation is verified using the automated validation of Internet security protocols and applications (AVISPA) tool of our proposed scheme. The performance analysis, comparing metrics such as computation time and communication cost, demonstrates the superiority of the proposed scheme over existing state-of-the-art approaches.Keywords: Demand-side management, Mutual authentication, IoT-enabled smart grid systems, Blockchain, Security and privacySubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

In recent years, information and communication technologies (ICT) have significantly influenced modern society by delivering telecommunication, and computer services to end-consumers. With the ICT revolution around the globe, the traditional grid has been transformed into a fully automated smart grid, offering seamless services including advanced metering infrastructure (AMI), smart meter billing, and smart energy management to end-consumers. With that, customers can easily access any service at any time in smart grid systems1,2. In particular, Internet-of-Things (IoT) devices, such as sensors and smart meters, are widely used to access these services in a fast and efficient manner. These devices collect data from different geographical locations and transfer it to the utility centre to provide reliable energy transmission. According to a report from the U.S. Department of Science, the energy demand has witnessed a 30% growth since 1988, while the energy transmission capacity has only increased by 15%3. Therefore, demand-side management between IoT devices and the utility centre becomes challenging to ensure a reliable energy supply in smart grid systems4,5.

Demand-side management involves adjusting energy usage in response to fluctuating prices, such as real-time pricing (RTP), time-of-use (ToU) pricing, and inclining block rates (IBR)6. The development of smart grid systems, along with advancements such as demand response management, energy trading, and dynamic pricing, has historically resulted in a decrease in peak energy requirements7,8. These developments create opportunities to reduce dependence on costly power plants that incur significant maintenance expenses and generate large carbon emissions. As a result, demand-side management in smart grid systems supported by IoT provides considerable economic, social, and environmental advantages9–11 (Table 1).

From the above-mentioned, we observed that most researchers have used game theory and differential models to satisfy demand-side management in smart grid systems. Also, conventional communication protocols such as ISO/IEC/IEEE 18880, TCP/IP, IEEE 18881, and IEEE 18883 have been used for communication in smart grid systems. But these protocols are security and privacy loopholes in smart grid systems12,13. For example, Odelu et al.14 proposed an authentication protocol for identity-based signatures in smart grid environments. This protocol is vulnerable to security attacks and can compromise the privacy of smart meters used in smart grid systems. Mahmood et al.15 proposed an anonymous key agreement protocol based on identity signatures for smart grid systems. This protocol is also vulnerable to secret leakage and impersonation attacks. To overcome these issues, Challa et al.16 proposed an authentication scheme to provide security in smart grid environments. However, the authors in17 presented certificate-based access control mechanism that provides a secure connection between smart devices in smart grid systems. They have also stated stated that elliptic-curve cryptography (ECC)-based authentication scheme does not provide better authentication to the utility centre present in the paper18. From the facts mentioned above, we observed that these solutions have used centralized architecture, which is vulnerable to a single point of failure in smart grid systems. In addition to authentication mechanisms, recent studies have also explored blockchain-based solutions for secure data management. A related study19 explores the use of Non-Fungible Tokens on both public and private blockchain networks to create secure, tamper-proof, and traceable digital certificates, demonstrating the feasibility of NFT-based certification systems. Similarly, authors in20 proposed a secure, tamper-proof, and low-latency system for managing judicial evidence using blockchain, NFTs, and fog computing. However, such approaches primarily focus on certificate integrity and do not address secure mutual authentication and privacy preservation in IoT-enabled smart grid environments21–25.

From the literature, we observed major key challenges such as-single points of failure and resilience to various fault-tolerance issues and lack of an authentication scheme for demand-side management compromises the security and privacy of entities in smart grid systems. Hence, there is a need for a distributed platform for a mutual authentication between IoT devices and the utility centre in smart grid systems. For example, the authors in26,27 used blockchain technology to provide transaction security and privacy between IoT devices and the utility centre for energy transformation in smart grid systems. Similarly, the authors presented a blockchain-driven demand-side management framework for P2P energy trading in microgrid systems28. They have used the high computational power consensus mechanism, which is a key limitation. The other research29, where the authors investigated the impact of renewable energy source variations on energy storage system optimization in the presence of demand-side management. Here, a key limitation is the variability in renewable generation, which introduces uncertainties in optimization outcomes. However, in existing solutions, the authors have less focused on mutual authentication and more on secure transactions between consumers, which is a major concern in smart grid systems as shown in Table 1.

Moreover, the increasing integration of renewable energy sources and the growing demand for electricity necessitate an efficient and secure demand-side management system30–36. Traditional centralized DSM approaches suffer from security vulnerabilities, inefficiencies, and single points of failure. Hence, this paper focuses on the authentication scheme based on blockchain, which is designed not only for security transactions but also to ensure identity preservation in smart grid systems37–40. Therefore, the transparency and integrity of transactions on a blockchain contribute to a positive user experience, encouraging active participation in smart grid systems. The proposed solution aims to mitigate cyber threats, optimize energy distribution, and ensure real-time, tamper-proof energy transactions41–45.

System modeling

This section describes the blockchain-based system model for demand-side management in smart grid systems. This model mainly consists of the smart grid and the blockchain network. The smart grid aggregates the power data from different sources and provides efficient power management services to the end-consumers. The utility centre monitors the collected data, which consists of load balancing, demand response, energy trading, power consumption, load forecasting, and real-time pricing. All the information and transaction details are stored on the public ledgers of the blockchain network. Hence, there is no need for a trusted third-party authenticator to authenticate and secure the transactions. Blockchain itself is capable of providing a secure and transparent platform to communicate among IoT devices and the utility centre in smart grid systems. After the mutual authentication between the communicating parties (IoT devices, utility centre), the transaction details of each user’s energy information are stored on a blockchain, where cryptographic HMAC functions such as SHA-2 (HMAC()) are used to encrypt the data. Integration with existing smart grid infrastructure

The proposed blockchain DSM framework has been developed with interoperability as a central consideration, and this has been achieved with conformity to existing smart grid communication standards like dedicated short-range communication (DSRC), cellular vehicle-to-everything (C-V2X), and IEC 61850.

DSRC and C-V2X compatibility: these standards are primarily used in Vehicle-to-Grid (V2G) and grid-edge communication. Our system utilizes a modular communication layer where message formats conform to IEEE 1609 and ETSI ITS-G5 stack (for DSRC) and 3GPP Release 16 (for C-V2X) for secure exchanging of control messages among IoT nodes and energy management centers.

Dataset transparency

The proposed blockchain-enabled DSM framework has been tested using a realistic smart grid simulation environment built over NS-3. The platform consists of heterogeneous IoT devices, fog computing layers, and blockchain-enabled decision points, thus emulating real-time load-balancing scenarios quite realistically. The formal security verification is also performed using the AVISPA tool. Despite depending on static or artificial public data sets, the proposed scheme is based on dynamically generated DSM traffic profiles in NS-3. These models mimic energy consumption, dynamic prices, and request rates according to Poisson and Gaussian distributions, which are in line with models in earlier research like14,64,67. It is better for transparency, reproducibility, and responsiveness in testing.

Adversarial testing

The proposed approach has been simulated against various attack patterns in NS-3, including packet injection (Sybil and Replay attacks), node loss, and latency-driven denial-of-service (DoS) attacks. Findings indicate a threat detection rate exceeding 92%, an end-to-end latency of less than 5 ms, and a computational overhead of less than 2% on resource-constrained IoT nodes. These results showcase the robustness, practicality in real-world scenarios, and viability of the proposed approach for future smart grid systems.

Security validation beyond formal analysis

The AVISPA is a robust formal verification tool that ensures protocol correctness and symbolic attack resistance. Our work involves a broader validation process by simulating real attack scenarios on an NS-3-based smart grid infrastructure. The AVISPA tool checks the authentication scheme’s resistance to protocol-level attacks such as replay attacks, man-in-the-middle (MitM), and message tampering. For realistic robustness, however, we conducted dynamic adversarial simulations with active attackers in the DSM system. These are Sybil attacks, where an attacker generates multiple identities to manipulate DSM policies; replay attacks, where legitimate messages captured earlier are replayed to gain unauthorised access; and DoS attacks, which are designed to flood the system with spurious requests, thereby preventing or delaying decision-making.

The NS-3 simulation demonstrates that our framework achieves a threat detection rate of over 92%, a response time of less than 5 ms even during an attack, and less than 2% computational overhead on edge devices. This hybrid validation method with formal tools and realistic simulations leads to a multi-layered defense that is both theoretically sound and empirically correct, demonstrating the feasibility of real-time deployment in modern smart grid infrastructures.

Computational and latency overhead on IoT devices

In resource-constrained environments such as IoT-based smart grids, ensuring security and efficiency without overloading edge devices is critical. To quantify this, we experimented with the computational and latency overhead our proposed blockchain-based DSM framework imposes on various IoT nodes (e.g., Raspberry Pi 4, ESP32).

We have tested lightweight cryptographic computations, i.e., symmetric key encryption and hash functions, for authentication and data transfer operations. Based on our experiments, the average extra computer work per transaction is less than 2% CPU usage and memory usage on edge devices. This is drastically less than that of traditional asymmetric blockchain systems. We achieve this with efficiency by offloading arduous validation work to fog nodes to enable IoT devices to concentrate on lightweight verification and signing operations.

For latency, the total time from start to completion (identity verification, block creation, and control response) was measured under normal and stressful network conditions through NS-3 simulations. The system reliably provided response times less than 5 ms under normal conditions and 8 ms even under Sybil and replay attack conditions. This low latency means that the framework is well-suited for real-time applications, especially in smart grid applications that are delay-critical, like dynamic pricing, load shedding, and grid reconfiguration, where waiting times must be very short. Therefore, the proposed design supports security as well as quick responses without placing too much burden on IoT nodes, which is in line with the practical needs of real-world applications.

Comparative analysis

The graph shown in Fig. 7a displays the average latency experienced as the user load rises. It is evident that the proposed method exhibits notably lower latency compared to the current schemes. With a user load of 200, the latency for the proposed scheme is approximately 4.8 ms, while Odelu, Zong, and Aggarwal exhibit latencies of 9.2 ms, 10.1 ms, and 7.5 ms, respectively. This improved performance results from the use of lightweight cryptographic primitives and effective session key establishment techniques in the suggested protocol. Unlike other systems that rely on pairing-based or ECC-heavy processes that require significant computational resources, the proposed model employs streamlined HMAC-based authentication alongside improved identity management, resulting in enhanced response times and better scalability for increasing user demands.

The proposed methodology consistently exhibits the lowest computational overhead, ranging from 1.2% to 1.9% as the number of users increases. The proposed approach demonstrates a strong and stable positive detection rate exceeding 91%, even as the user base expands. The detection rates of the Odelu and Zong protocols, however, exhibit a notable decline, reaching as low as 80% and 83%, respectively, in environments with a high number of users. This important advantage arises from the combination of behavior-oriented intrusion detection with AVISPA-validated secure communication protocols. Through adaptation to evolving threat patterns and the integration of session validation methods, the proposed system provides a secure and proactive defense, in contrast to the conventional static-signature models utilized in the baseline protocols.

Conclusion

This study introduced a blockchain-based mutual authentication framework for secure interaction between IoT devices and the utility center in demand-side management in smart grid systems. The proposed framework functions in three stages: (i) registering IoT devices, (ii) registering the utility center, and (iii) mutual authentication utilizing a blockchain. Blockchain technology provides transparency and immutability of transactions, while the authentication system ensures data integrity and maintains privacy for end-users. To confirm the security of the proposed scheme, we performed formal verification using the AVISPA tool, demonstrating its resilience against potential threats. Furthermore, an extensive performance assessment was conducted, including an analysis of computational and communication costs, in comparison to current state-of-the-art methods. The findings suggest that the proposed method surpasses current techniques in terms of efficiency and security, making it a suitable option for smart grid systems powered by IoT. Future efforts will concentrate on improving scalability and incorporating AI-enabled optimization methods for better demand-side management.
