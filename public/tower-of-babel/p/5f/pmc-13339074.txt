Characteristics and Risk Factors for Mortality of Vascular Graft Infections: a Cohort Study
Authors: Meriam Lamloumi, Isabelle Javerliat, Clara Duran, Nathalie Dournon, Karim Jaffal, Anne-Laure Roux, Faten El Sayed, Ségolène Perrineau, Thomas Tritz, Raphaël Coscas, Marc Coggia, Aurélien Dinh, Salam Abbara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVascular graft infections (VGIs) are rare but severe complications associated with high morbidity and mortality.MethodWe conducted a retrospective study in a French reference center for vascular grafting, including all patients who underwent surgery for VGI between 2020 and 2025. Data on patient and infection characteristics, surgical and medical management, and follow-up up to 1 year after surgery were collected. Infection-related mortality was defined as death occurring within 1 year after surgery and directly attributable to the infection or its complications.ResultsWe included 129 patients with a median age of 71 years (IQR 61–81); most were male (82%). Major comorbidities were smoking (82%), high blood pressure (64%), and diabetes (32%). VGIs were mostly extracavitary (85%) and occurred early (<4 months after surgery) in 57% of cases. Revision surgery is mostly considered a conservative approach, namely, debridement without graft replacement (71%). Polymicrobial infections were common (49%), and Staphylococcus aureus was the predominant pathogen, accounting for 16% of isolates. The overall mortality rate reached 43% at 1 year. In univariate analysis, age >75 years, intensive care unit (ICU) admission, and infection with multidrug-resistant bacteria were associated with increased mortality. In multivariate analysis, age >75 years (P = .03) and ICU admission (P = .045) emerged as independent predictors of infection-related mortality.ConclusionsVGIs carry a poor prognosis despite surgical management and targeted antimicrobial therapy. Advanced age and disease severity at presentation appear to be the main predictors of mortality. The respective impact of surgical strategy and microbiological profile requires further investigation in larger multicentric studies.Keywords: antibiotics, mortality, prognosis, risk factors, vascular graft infection

Vascular grafting has profoundly transformed the management of vascular diseases. Yet, vascular graft infection (VGI) remains one of the most serious and life-threatening complications of this procedure [1]. Although uncommon, with a reported prevalence ranging from 0.5% to 4% [2], VGI is associated with severe outcomes. Perioperative mortality rates range from 10% to 45%, amputation rates from 10% to 30%, and reinfection rates from 6% to 27% [1, 3]. In addition, VGI represents a substantial financial burden [3, 4].

The diagnosis of VGI is often challenging and relies on a combination of clinical, radiological, and microbiological findings. Clinical presentations are heterogeneous and may be acute or chronic. VGI should be suspected in the presence of purulent discharge, erythema, or unexplained fever.

Despite advances in surgical and medical management, VGI remains a major clinical challenge, in terms of treatment. Therapeutic decision-making depends on several factors, including the timing of infection onset (early vs late), graft location, feasibility of surgical intervention, and the patient's overall clinical condition. Management usually requires complex surgical procedures combined with prolonged antimicrobial therapy. Nevertheless, the optimal strategy integrating surgical and medical approaches has yet to be clearly defined [5].

Given the challenges faced by clinicians and the substantial burden associated with VGI, there is a clear need to better define optimal therapeutic strategies and to assess risk factors associated with poor outcomes.

In this context, we aimed to describe the clinical and microbiological characteristics of patients with VGI and to investigate risk factors associated with mortality.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study Design and Population

We conducted a retrospective observational monocentric study at a French reference center for vascular grafting (Ambroise Paré University Hospital). We included all hospitalized adult patients (≥18 years old) with a diagnosis of VGI between 1 January 2020 and 31 December 2025. Only the incident infection episode per patient during the study period was included. We excluded patients with an unconfirmed diagnosis of VGI, insufficient follow-up, those who died from a noninfectious cause, or those who did not undergo surgery. All patients’ medical records were available and reviewed by both an infectious disease specialist and a vascular surgeon.

Infection Definition

VGIs were defined according to the Management of Aortic Graft Infection Collaboration (MAGIC) criteria. A diagnosis of VGI was established when at least 1 major criterion was present in association with another major or minor criterion [6].

Major criteria included microbiological evidence of infection, defined by positive intraoperative cultures obtained from peri-graft tissue or prosthetic material (in the case of potential contaminants, such as coagulase-negative Staphylococcus, Cutibacterium acnes, or corynebacteria, at least 2 concordant positive samples were required), as well as overt peri-prosthetic infection, including the presence of pus around the graft at surgery, an open wound with exposed graft, graft-related fistula, or mycotic aneurysm, and radiological evidence of infection, specifically persistent peri-graft air >7 weeks after surgery or persistent graft-related fluid/discharge >3 months postoperatively.

Minor criteria comprised clinical signs of infection (general signs such as unexplained fever, chills, or septic shock, and localized peri-prosthetic signs including erythema, swelling, warmth, pain, purulent discharge, wound dehiscence, or peri-graft collection), radiological findings suggestive of infection (peri-prosthetic abscess, false aneurysm, focal thickening of the vascular wall, graft occlusion or suspicious metabolic activity on PET scan), and biological abnormalities, including positive blood cultures with no other identifiable source of infection and/or biological inflammatory syndrome characterized by elevated C-reactive protein and leukocytosis.

Outcome Evaluation

Infection-related mortality was defined as death occurring within 1 year after surgery and directly attributable to the vascular graft infection or its complications. Attribution was based on a comprehensive review of medical records, microbiological data, and the clinical course and was determined by an infectious disease specialist in collaboration with a vascular surgeon. The diagnosis was established according to clinical judgment, particularly in the presence of local signs of infection or sepsis without an alternative identifiable cause.

Microbiological Procedures

At our center, intraoperative microbiological sampling during surgery for VGI was consistent with current international recommendations [6]. At least 3 samples were obtained from distinct sites surrounding the infected graft, including peri-graft tissue, anastomotic junctions, abscess cavities, and areas of necrosis or purulent exudate. When feasible, a segment of the prosthetic graft was also retrieved for microbiological analysis. All samples were processed for aerobic and anaerobic culture, with prolonged incubation of up to 14 days to detect slow-growing pathogens. In cases of culture-negative infection, molecular diagnostic techniques, such as 16S rRNA gene sequencing, were used to identify fastidious or noncultivable bacteria. Blood cultures were systematically performed in febrile patients, and superficial specimens (swab samples) were excluded. Antibiotic susceptibility testing was performed according to the standards of the French Society for Microbiology (CA-SFM) [7].

Antimicrobial Management

Empirical antibiotic therapy was started immediately after intraoperative microbiological sampling and consisted of broad-spectrum regimens (cefepime, daptomycin, and metronidazole combination). Antibiotic therapy was subsequently adapted based on the susceptibility profiles of intraoperative isolates, following national and international guidelines. Antimicrobial selection favored agents with high oral bioavailability, optimal tissue penetration, and activity against biofilm-forming organisms [8]. The duration of therapy varied according to the surgical strategy and clinical response. Treatment lasted approximately 2 weeks after complete graft removal, 4–6 weeks after graft replacement, and around 3 months in cases of graft retention [8]. Treatment decisions were systematically made in collaboration between infectious disease specialists and surgeons. In the rare instances of disagreement, cases were discussed during dedicated multidisciplinary meetings to reach a consensus.

Surgical Management

Optimal surgical treatment consisted of complete debridement of infected and devitalized tissues, total graft removal, and in situ reconstruction with an autogenous vein, an arterial allo- or homograft, or a new prosthesis. Conservative surgical treatment, limited to debridement without graft removal, was performed when local or general conditions were unfavorable, particularly in patients with severe comorbidities. In selected cases, partial graft removal was the only feasible surgical option.

Data Collected

Data were collected within 1 year after surgery. Patient variables included sex, age, body mass index (BMI), and comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, smoking, immunodepression). Characteristics of the VGI included the timing of infection, presence or absence of a prosthetic graft, surgical site, and type of surgical revision (total graft removal, partial graft removal, or graft retention). Clinical features of infection at presentation (fever, abscess, wound dehiscence, fistula, or purulent discharge) and admission to the intensive care unit (ICU) were also recorded. Early-onset infection was defined as an infection occurring within the first 4 months after VGI, whereas late-onset infection was defined as any infection occurring thereafter [9].

Empiric and definitive antimicrobial regimens were collected. Intraoperative microbiological results, as well as concomitant blood culture results, were also recorded. Multidrug-resistant (MDR) bacteria were defined as methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), methicillin-resistant coagulase-negative staphylococci, extended-spectrum β-lactamase–producing Enterobacterales (ESBL-E), or nonfermenting Gram-negative bacilli resistant to at least 1 agent in 3 or more antimicrobial classes that are normally active against the corresponding species [10, 11].

Data Analysis

All included patients were described according to their outcome 1 year after surgery. Intraoperative microbiological findings were analyzed according to culture results (monomicrobial vs polymicrobial). Definitive antimicrobial regimens were described in detail according to treatment strategy (monotherapy vs combination therapy). Descriptive statistics were presented as median with interquartile range (IQR) for continuous variables, and proportion for categorical variables.

Univariable and multivariable stepwise logistic regression models were carried out to identify risk factors of infection-related mortality at 1 year. All variables with a P-value ≤.20 in univariate analyses were eligible for inclusion in the multivariable models. Statistical significance was set at a 2-tailed P-value ≤.05. P-values in the multivariable analyses were calculated using the likelihood ratio test. The variable “infection delay” was forced into the multivariable model, with early infection as the reference category, based on literature data suggesting its potential impact on the outcome of vascular graft infections [5]. A sensitivity analysis including all-cause mortality (infectious or noninfectious) was performed, considering the same covariates as in the final multivariable model. We used R 4.0.0 (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, https://cran.r-project.org) to perform the statistical analyses.

Ethics Statement

This study received approval from the French Infectious Disease Society Institutional Review Board (No. IRB00011642). Specific local permissions from the Ethics Review Committee (ERC) of the promoting center were obtained prior to commencing recruitment. The study was conducted in accordance with European and French data protection regulations, including the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) and the CNIL MR004 methodology (Declaration of conformity No. 2211110 v0). Patient data were pseudonymized, and all information was handled in compliance with applicable data protection and confidentiality requirements.

This study follows the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology reporting guidelines [12].

RESULTS

Description of the Population

Infection Characteristics

A total of 129 patients were included, of whom 43% (n = 56) died from VGI within 1 year (Figure 1, Table 1). The median age was 72 [62–81] years with nearly half of the patients aged ≥75 years. Most patients (85%, n = 110) were male. Hypertension and diabetes were found in 64% (n = 83) and 32% (n = 41) of patients, respectively. The majority of patients were current smokers (82%, n = 106), and 16% (n = 21) were immunocompromised. Most of the infections involved extracavitary grafts (85%, n = 110), exclusively iliofemoral. Intra-abdominal locations included aorto-iliac (6%, n = 7) and aorto-femoral grafts (9%, n = 12). Prosthetic implants were used in most cases (88%, n = 114). VGI was classified as early-onset in 57% (n = 74) of patients. At presentation, fever was observed in 58% of cases, abscesses in 36%, and wound complications—such as wound dehiscence, fistula, or purulent discharge—in 44%. Admission to the ICU occurred in 9% of patients. Each surgical strategy was used in approximately one-third of patients: complete graft removal (29%), graft preservation with debridement (37%), and partial graft removal (34%) (Table 1).

Microbiological Documentation

Intraoperative microbiological documentation was obtained for all patients. Almost half of the patients had a polymicrobial infection (49%, n = 63) and approximately one in 6 patients was infected with at least 1 MDR organism (15.5%, n = 20). A total of 216 bacteria were isolated from intraoperative samples (Table 2), of which 57% (n = 122) were Gram-positive pathogens. The most common bacteria were S. aureus (16%, n = 35, all of which were methicillin-susceptible), coagulase-negative staphylococci (15%, n = 33, including 10 methicillin-resistant isolates), and Escherichia coli (9%, n = 20). The distribution of the most frequently isolated pathogens differed between monomicrobial and polymicrobial vascular graft infections. Monomicrobial infections were predominantly caused by Gram-positive bacteria (82%), mainly staphylococci, whereas polymicrobial infections exhibited a more heterogeneous microbiological profile, with higher proportions of Gram-negative bacteria (28%), anaerobes (17%), and fungi (5%), and a lower prevalence of staphylococci (S. aureus 12%, coagulase-negative staphylococci 1%). Fungal pathogens were detected exclusively in polymicrobial infections (Table 2).

Antimicrobial Regimens

The median total duration of treatment was 42 [37–42] days, including an average of 19 days of intravenous administration. Most patients received combination therapy as definitive treatment (70%, n = 91), whereas 30% (n = 38) of patients received monotherapy (Table 3). Combination regimens most commonly included fluoroquinolones (33%, n = 43) and rifampicin (26%, n = 34), non–anti-Pseudomonas beta-lactams (16%, n = 21), and glyco/lipopeptides (13%, n = 17). Less frequently used agents in combination therapy included anti-Pseudomonas beta-lactams, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, clindamycin, metronidazole, oxazolidinones, carbapenems, and aminosides. Monotherapy was mainly with non–anti-Pseudomonas beta-lactams (18%, n = 23), while other agents such as fluoroquinolones, anti-Pseudomonas beta-lactams, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, carbapenems, and clindamycin were used infrequently (≤4%) (Table 3).

Risk Factors of Infection-Related Mortality at 1 Year

Univariable Analyses

Patients who died from infection-related causes within 1 year after surgery were older (P = .018), with a higher proportion of patients aged ≥75 years (59% vs 36%, Table 1). They were also more likely to have infections caused by MDR bacteria (24% vs 9.9%; P = .036). ICU admission tended to be more frequent among patients who died from VGI, with a marginally significant difference (14% vs 4%; P = .056). No significant differences were observed between groups regarding sex, BMI, comorbidities, clinical features at presentation, or the rate of positive blood cultures. Surgical characteristics, including the type of surgery, the surgical site (aortic vs nonaortic), and the presence of prosthesis, were also comparable between groups.

Multivariable Analyses

Variables included in the multivariable analysis were age, BMI, ICU admission, type of surgical procedure, and the presence of MDR bacteria. Infection delay was forced in the multivariable model. In the final model (Table 4), age over 75 and ICU admission emerged as independent risk factors for infection-related mortality at 1 year (P = .05; OR = 3.03 [1.23–7.9] and P = .04; OR = 4.94; [1.03–36.8], respectively). Neither the presence of MDR bacteria nor the type of surgical procedure was associated with 1-year infection-related mortality.

DISCUSSION

In this large cohort of patients with vascular graft infections (VGI), outcomes remained poor, despite management in a reference center for vascular grafting, and seemed associated with advanced age and disease severity at presentation. The majority of cases involved prosthetic and extracavitary grafts, and patients had numerous comorbidities. The infectious-related mortality rate in our cohort reached 43%, consistent with previous series predominantly composed of aortic VGI cases [13, 14]. Advanced age over 75 years was significantly associated with increased mortality, in line with prior studies [14–16]. In addition, the need for intensive care support emerged as an independent risk factor of mortality with an odds ratio close to 5. This finding is in line with previous studies suggesting that both preoperative sepsis and postoperative SOFA score were associated with in-hospital mortality in VGI [14, 16].

The timing of infection onset has been suggested to be a prognostic factor. In a previous study, patients with late-onset VGI were older and experienced thrice as many reinfections and twice as many deaths [5]. Older age and incomplete biofilm eradication in late-onset infections were suggested as possible explanations. Once established, biofilms are harder to eradicate with standard antimicrobials and can upregulate matrix production under high shear stress [17, 18]. In our cohort, early VGIs (<4 months) were slightly more common (57%) than late-onset infections (43%). In contrast to these previous findings, late-onset VGI was not associated with increased mortality, after adjustment for age and other confounding factors.

In our cohort, surgical treatment was almost evenly distributed among complete graft removal (29%), partial implant removal (34%), and conservative surgery (37%), with no significant association between conservative treatment and mortality. This contrasts with data from the literature, where complete implant removal was the most frequently reported surgical approach, accounting for up to 87% of cases [19]. This difference could be explained by the high burden of comorbidities and the overall frailty of our patients, which often limited the feasibility of extensive surgical procedures, as well as by the evolution of surgical strategies toward more individualized and selective management. Moreover, compared to other studies, our cohort mainly included peripheral infections (85%), which may partly explain both the surgical distribution and the higher proportion of Gram-negative pathogens (25%) and multidrug-resistant bacteria (15.5%) observed.

Data regarding the prognosis of conservative management remain heterogenous. Nonoperative management has been associated with poor outcomes, with a recent meta-analysis reporting a mean 30-day mortality rate of 63% [20], and another study describing a 50% 30-day mortality, with deaths occurring a mean of 13.5 days after presentation [21]. In contrast, in our cohort, conservative surgery—including graft-retaining or partial removal strategies—was not associated with increased mortality, consistent with findings from other studies [22–24].

This issue has been addressed by the recent European Delphi Consensus on the Treatment and Follow-up of Vascular Graft and Endograft Infection [25], which emphasizes that although complete graft removal remains the standard of care, conservative or graft-preserving strategies may be considered in carefully selected patients, particularly those who are frail or at high surgical risk, provided that close clinical follow-up is ensured. It should be noted that this consensus primarily addresses intracavitary VGI, while our cohort consisted mainly of peripheral infections. Overall, the available evidence regarding the optimal surgical strategy in VGI remains limited and largely retrospective. Further studies are needed to better define which patients may benefit from conservative management, particularly those with significant comorbidities or frailty. Optimizing patient selection is essential to improve outcomes in this vulnerable population.

Beyond surgical strategy, the Delphi Consensus recommends prolonged antimicrobial therapy when prosthetic material is retained (4–6 weeks of intravenous therapy followed by oral treatment for a total duration of 12 weeks) with a low level of evidence [25]. Similar regimens were applied in our series. However, given the heterogeneity in clinical presentation and antibiotic regimens inherent to this retrospective study, we did not specifically assess the impact of antimicrobial strategy on outcomes.

Nearly half of our patients had polymicrobial infections. Staphylococcus species were the most frequently identified pathogens (16%), although this proportion was lower than the approximately 50% reported in smaller peripheral VGI cohorts [5, 12]. MDR organisms were identified in one out of 6 patients, mainly ESBL-producing Enterobacterales or MDR Pseudomonas aeruginosa, whereas a higher prevalence of MDR organisms (23%) has been reported in the literature [24]. No methicillin-resistant S. aureus was isolated, consistent with the generally low prevalence of MRSA reported in France [26].

The impact of bacteria and antimicrobial resistance on outcomes in VGI remains insufficiently characterized. Previous studies have reported conflicting results regarding the prognostic impact of MRSA in VGI [9, 27]. One study found that multidrug-resistant organisms were not associated with an increased risk of all-cause mortality [24], whereas another reported poorer outcomes in infections caused by P. aeruginosa [28]. In our study, MDR infections were associated with increased mortality in univariate analysis (P = .03), but this association was no longer observed after adjustment for confounding factors (P = .20). Larger studies are required to better clarify the role of specific pathogens and resistances on outcome in VGI.

Although aortic graft infections have been reported as risk factors for mortality [15], aortic localization was not associated with increased mortality in our study, possibly reflecting the predominance of peripheral infections and thus limited statistical power.

Finally, several risk factors previously identified in the literature were not specifically assessed in our analysis, including renal failure [9, 14, 16], fungal infections [16], and the appropriateness of empiric antimicrobial therapy [24].

The main limitations of this study include its retrospective and monocentric design and the heterogeneity of the study population. Nonetheless, this heterogeneity was common in other reports studying this complex topic, reflecting the wide diversity of clinical presentations of VGIs, and the lack of a clear and well-codified consensus on their optimal management [8, 9, 23]. In addition, methicillin-resistant coagulase-negative Staphylococci were classified as multidrug-resistant bacteria, which may be open to debate. Although no universally accepted definition of multidrug-resistant Staphylococci exists, methicillin resistance is frequently associated with limited therapeutic options. Conversely, the distinctive clinical profile of our cohort represents one of the strengths of the study. Our population was characterized by a predominance of patients of advanced age with a significant burden of comorbidities, as well as a high proportion of prosthetic and extracavitary VGIs, reflecting a particularly complex and high-risk clinical setting. In addition, the proportion of patients managed with conservative strategies was substantial, providing valuable insight into real-world therapeutic decision-making in patients who are frequently deemed unsuitable for surgical intervention. We also used uniform definition criteria for VGIs, which helped ensure consistency in diagnosis and management.

CONCLUSION

VGIs remain associated with high mortality and morbidity rates, reflecting their poor overall prognosis. In our study, advanced age and admission to the ICU emerged as the main factors associated with infection-related mortality. The influence of the timing of infection, the type of surgical procedure, or the causative bacteria on patient outcomes remains uncertain. These results should, however, be interpreted with caution due to the heterogeneity of our population. Further multicenter studies involving larger cohorts are needed to better define prognostic factors and to guide the optimal management of these complex infections.
