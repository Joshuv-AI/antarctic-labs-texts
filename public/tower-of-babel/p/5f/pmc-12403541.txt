Exogenous melatonin promotes anthocyanin accumulation and coloration in Chinese plum peels
Authors: Jiawei Zhu, Ziyao Huang, Mengxiao Guo, Yunying Xiao, Yuxing Li, Xiaoli Zhang, Hui Xia, Dong Liang, Xiulan Lv, Jin Wang, Lijin Lin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Melatonin (MT) is a growth regulator that influences anthocyanin synthesis during plant growth. However, the regulation mechanism of MT on the coloration of plum peels remains unclear. Here, the effects of MT on the anthocyanin accumulation and coloration in Chinese plum peels were examined after MT (100 μmol/L) or water (control) treatment. The results showed that MT treatment accelerated the changes in plum peels from green to red by increasing the anthocyanin and phenolic contents. MT treatment also increased the antioxidant capacity, including the scavenging capacity of 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) and ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP); and promoted the enzyme activities of anthocyanin synthesis, such as chalcone synthase (CHS), flavanone 3-hydroxylase (F3H), chalcone isomerase (CHI), and UDP-glucose 3-O-glucosyltransferase (UFGT). Moreover, transcriptome analysis indicated that MT treatment up-regulated the expression of several key anthocyanin-related genes, including CHS, F3H, dihydroflavonol 4-reductase (DFR), anthocyanidin synthase (ANS), and UFGT, revealing the regulatory role of MT in anthocyanin metabolism. In conclusion, MT treatment can promote the anthocyanin accumulation and coloration in plum peels.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07236-9.

Introduction

Anthocyanins, which are found predominantly in fruits, vegetables, and flowers, are a type of flavonoid compound that contributes to plant coloration and offers potential antioxidant benefits for human health [5, 34]. Anthocyanin accumulation not only affects the appearance and quality of fruits but also directly impacts their nutritional value and market competitiveness [24]. In recent years, the biological mechanisms regulating anthocyanin synthesis and accumulation have become a key focus in fruit science research. Anthocyanin biosynthesis is a complex and highly regulated pathway derived from the phenylpropanoid pathway, which is responsible for the production of a wide range of secondary metabolites in plants [43]. A series of key enzymes, including phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL), chalcone synthase (CHS), dihydroflavonol 4-reductase (DFR), flavanone 3-hydroxylase (F3H), anthocyanidin synthase (ANS), and UDP-glucose 3-O-glucosyltransferase (UFGT), work together to convert phenylalanine into stable anthocyanins, which ultimately affect fruit colour and antioxidant properties [26, 27]. The specific colour, stability, and chemical characteristics of anthocyanins are largely influenced by postsynthetic modifications, including glycosylation, methylation, and acylation [13, 15, 16]. Despite significant progress in understanding the biological mechanisms regulating anthocyanin synthesis, developing effective methods to increase the anthocyanin content in fruits through exogenous regulation, particularly the use of plant hormones, remains a key scientific challenge.

Among various plant hormones, melatonin (MT), an indole-based hormone, has garnered increasing attention because of its diverse regulatory functions in plants [28]. Studies have found that MT not only enhances stress tolerance in plants, but also plays an important role in regulating plant growth and development and boosting antioxidant capacity [4, 8, 23]. MT can also promote anthocyanin accumulation and increase coloration in horticultural crop species. The application of MT increased the anthocyanins contents in cabbages while also stimulating the expression levels of anthocyanin-related genes [58]. MT boosted the levels of polyphenols, anthocyanins, and antioxidants in grape berries by influencing ethylene signalling [53]. The preharvest application of MT promoted the colour of the pear peel, increased the anthocyanin and flavonol contents, and up-regulated the expression patterns of anthocyanin-related genes [41].

Plum (Prunus salicina L.) is a nutrient-dense fruit known for its sweet-tart flavour, firm flesh, and richness in amino acids, vitamins, and antioxidants such as carotenoids and polyphenols [57]. Peel colours range from green to yellow, red, and purple, which indicate ripeness and flavour characteristics, enhancing consumer appeal [39]. However, differences in tree position and light exposure result in the formation of a sunny side and a shady side on the fruit, causing variations in colour and quality that affect marketability and consistency [62]. Exogenous MT has diverse functions in the regulation of plant physiology [28], however, its roles in plum fruit coloration and anthocyanin accumulation are not yet fully understood. In this study, we applied MT to Chinese plum plants and measured various physiological and biochemical parameters, including fruit coloration, photosynthetic pigment contents, phenolic contents, anthocyanin synthesis-related enzyme activity, and antioxidant activity of plum peels. Additionally, transcriptomic analysis investigated the effects of MT on anthocyanin synthesis and related gene expression in of plum peels, aiming to clarify how MT influences anthocyanin synthesis and provides insights into enhancing coloration of plum peels.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

Chinese plum variety of ‘Wuyuecui’ was used in this study. Plum trees planted in Hong’an town, Longquanyi District, Chengdu, Sichuan Province (104°08’ E, 30°27’ N). The orchard layout used 3 m × 4 m spacing, and the plants were uniformly managed.

Experimental design

In May 2024, foliar sprays were applied 45 d before commercial harvest. For MT treatment, the plants were sprayed 100 μmol/L MT (Solarbio, Beijing), and the control (CK) received only water. Based on our previous research, 100 μmol/L MT was chosen as it showed the best effect [51]. Treatments were applied two times at 45 and 35 days before harvest. Each treatment consisted of three biological replicates, with each replicate comprising three trees, from which 30 fruits were collected per tree. Samples were collected at the same day of the first treatment (before spraying MT) and every 7 d thereafter. After damaged fruits were removed, uniformly sized, shaped, and coloured fruits were selected for analysis. Following colour evaluation, fruit peels from the equatorial region were chopped, mixed, and stored at − 80 °C in a freezer.

Determination of fruit coloration, phenolic compounds, and antioxidant capacity

Fruit colour development was evaluated via the fruit coloration index. The coloration index was determined by measuring the colour parameters lightness (L*), red–green (a*), and yellow–blue (b*) from each fruit’s equatorial region with a handheld colorimeter. The C* (chroma), h° (hue angle), and colour index for red grapes (CIRG) was also evaluated using the formula [CIRG = (180° − h°) / (C* + L*)] [10, 32]. The chlorophyll and carotenoid contents were measured via solvent extraction and spectrophotometry [29].

Phenolics were extracted using 70% methanol with 2% formic acid. Plum peels (0.2 g) were ground with 2 mL of extraction solvent at 0 °C, transferred to a centrifuge tube, and rinsed with an additional 3 mL of solvent. Ultrasonic extraction was performed for 30 min, followed by shaking at 30 °C and 250 r/min for 2 h. The mixture was centrifuged at 8000 r/min for 10 min, and the supernatant was filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane and stored at 4 °C. Total phenolic content was determined using the Folin-Ciocalteu method [2]. After extraction for 5 min, 2 mL of 10% Na2CO3 was added, and the mixture was incubated in the dark at room temperature for 2 h. Absorbance was measured at 765 nm. Total flavonoid content was measured according to Wang et al. [47]. In a 10 mL centrifuge tube, 0.2 mL of the phenolic extract was mixed with 0.8 mL distilled water and 0.06 mL of 5% NaNO2, and incubated for 5 min. Following the reaction, 0.06 mL of 10% AlCl₃ was added, and the mixture stood for 10 min. Then, 0.4 mL of 10% Na₂CO3 and 0.9 mL distilled water were added, and absorbance was measured at 510 nm. Proanthocyanidin and anthocyanin content were determined based on Fang et al. [17]. The enzyme activities of CHS, F3H, chalcone isomerase (CHI), and UFGT were assayed via assay kits (Gilead Biotechnology, Wuhan). The scavenging capacity of 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH), ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP), and 2,2-azino-di-(3-ethylbenzothialozine-sulphonic acid) (ABTS) was measured according to Brand-Williams et al. [7] and Benzie and Strain [6].

Transcriptome sequencing and analysis

RNA was extracted from plum peels via the RNAprep Pure Plant Kit (Tiangen, Beijing, China). For transcriptome library construction, 1 μg of RNA was prepared with the Hieff NGS Ultima Dual-mode mRNA Library Prep Kit (Yeasen Biotechnology, China), involving poly-T bead mRNA capture and subsequent cDNA synthesis. Libraries were processed through end repair and adapter ligation with NEBNext Adapters (NEB, USA), and the quality verification of PCR amplification was on an Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq platform. The raw reads were processed on the BMK Cloud platform and obtained clean reads with quality metrics. Clean reads were aligned with the reference genome via HISAT2, and known and novel transcripts were identified via StringTie. Differential gene expression (DEGs) was analysed with the DESeq2 package in R. Gene function annotation was performed via multiple databases, including KEGG, Nr, Pfam, KOG/COG, GO, and Swiss-Prot. The reference genome used is Prunus_salicina.ps_v1.0.genome provided by BMK Technology. Each treatment included three biological replicates.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted with the independent samples test in SPSS 26.0, applying a significance level of p < 0.05. Figures were created via TBtools and GraphPad Prism.

Results

Colour of plum peels

MT treatment promoted the colouring of plum peels and advanced the colouring time (Fig. 1). The a* value showed a marked increase in red pigmentation in MT treatment starting from 21 days after treatment (DAT), with significantly higher values than CK at 21, 28, and 35 DAT (Fig. 2B). This indicates that MT treatment accelerated the colour transition from green to red. The L* and b* values decreased more rapidly under MT treatment from 14 DAT onwards, suggesting that CK fruits remained lighter and more yellow in comparison (Fig. 2A, C). Similarly, C* values in CK were consistently higher than those in MT treatment after 14 DAT, except at 35 DAT (Fig. 2D). The h° value declined more rapidly in the MT-treated fruits at 21 and 28 DAT, further indicating an accelerated shift from green to red hues (Fig. 2E). Effects of MT treatment on transcriptomic alterations in plum

To explore the response mechanism of plum peels after MT treatment, we collected plum peels from the equatorial region of CK and MT treatment at 14 and 28 DAT for transcriptome sequencing, which yielded between 43,195,902 and 57,099,760 raw reads (Table S1). Following the removal of adapters and low-quality sequences, a total of 21,597,951 to 28,549,880 high-quality reads were obtained, with a Q30 score ≥ 91.81% and a GC content ranging from 45.31% to 45.57%. Between 93.33% and 94.20% of these genes were aligned to the plum reference genome, resulting in approximately 30,263 genes available for further analysis. The transcriptome profiles of CK and MT treatment at 14 and 28 DAT were examined to identify genes potentially influenced by MT treatment. There were 558 DEGs identified at 14 DAT and 952 DEGs identified at 28 DAT. At 14 DAT, the expression levels of 105 DEGs were up-regulated in MT treatment compared with CK, whereas 453 DEGs were down-regulated (Figure S1A). The expression levels of 365 DEGs were higher in MT treatment at 28 DAT compared with CK, while 587 DEGs were lower (Figure S1B).

Functional GO annotation of the DEGs

GO analysis clarified the roles of the DEGs in biological processes, cellular components, and molecular functions at 14 and 28 DAT (Fig. 7). For biological processes (Tables S2, S3), at 14 DAT, the main enriched biological processes were related to primary metabolism and cellular structural regulation, providing precursor substances for secondary metabolism, including anthocyanin biosynthesis (Fig. 7A). By 28 DAT, metabolic activities shifted toward secondary metabolism, with the anthocyanin biosynthesis pathway significantly activated, and related genes highly upregulated (Fig. 7B). Regarding cellular components, at 14 DAT, GO enrichment analysis revealed that DEGs were primarily localized to the plasma membrane and intracellular structures, suggesting that MT may influence membrane homeostasis, signal transduction, and nutrient transport (Fig. 7A). By 28 DAT, enrichment results shifted toward the vacuole and cell wall, indicating that secondary metabolites such as anthocyanins may be predominantly stored in vacuoles to maintain cellular homeostasis (Fig. 7B). For molecular functions, at 14 DAT, the most enriched GO terms included catalytic activity and binding activity, indicating increased enzymatic activity and the accumulation of metabolic intermediates (Fig. 7A). By 28 DAT, enriched functions included UDP-glycosyltransferase and oxidoreductase activity. Key anthocyanin biosynthesis genes such as F3H (evm.model.LG08.458.gene), CHS (evm.model.LG01.3701.gene and evm.model.LG01.3699.gene), and ANS (evm.model.Contig3.4.gene) were significantly upregulated, contributing to enhanced anthocyanin stability and solubility, thereby promoting fruit coloration (Fig. 7B; Table S3).Fig. 7GO annotation analysis of MT-responsive DEGs at 14 (A) and 28 (B) DAT in plum peels

Identification of anthocyanin synthesis in plums

To investigate how MT affects anthocyanin accumulation in plum peels, the expression of several key genes involved in the anthocyanin biosynthesis pathway at 14 and 28 DAT was analyzed (Fig. 9). The expression of PAL (evm.model.LG02.1175.gene) did not significantly increase with MT treatment at either time point. However, C4H (evm.model.LG06.2356.gene) was significantly up-regulated at 14 DAT. Several other genes, including two 4CL genes (evm.model.Contig1.544.gene, evm.model.Contig1.545.gene), three CHS genes (evm.model.LG01.3699.gene, evm.model.LG01.3700.gene, evm.model.LG01.3701.gene), the CHI (evm.model.LG03.1234.gene), three DFR genes (evm.model.Contig28.18.gene, evm.model.LG01.1661.gene, evm.model.LG04.1889.gene), ANS (evm.model.LG07.5432.gene), and two UFGT genes (evm.model.Contig2.23.gene, evm.model.LG02.656.gene), were significantly up-regulated, particularly at 28 DAT. Discussion

MT is essential for enhancing plant tolerance to both biotic and abiotic stresses, including diseases, salt, cold, and drought [21, 25, 35, 55], but it also significantly influences seedling growth, flowering time, and root development [9, 12, 38]. Additionally, MT influences the phenylpropanoid pathway, a pathway that leads to the anthocyanin synthesis [18]. As dietary components, anthocyanins, which are widely present in plants, provide protection against stress and offer health benefits. In horticultural crop species such as kale and grape, MT has been reported to promote anthocyanin accumulation and increase coloration [53]. We conducted an experiment applying MT to plum peels to explore the possible link between MT and anthocyanin, and we also assessed the fruit coloration, chlorophyll and carotenoid contents, phenolic contents, anthocyanin synthesis-related enzymes, and antioxidant activity, and examined the transcriptomic data at 14 and 28 DAT.

In Malus crabapple, MT treatment induced a purple-red leaf phenotype in both ‘Royalty’ and ‘Radiant’, indicating increased anthocyanin accumulation [11]. In red pear (Pyrus ussuriensis Maxim.), MT-treated fruit presented a markedly deeper red colour [41]. This study yielded similar results. In terms of colour parameters, MT treatment accelerated the colour change of plum peels, resulting in a duller appearance while promoting the transition from green to red (Figs. 1, 2). Besides, although both a* and h° values are commonly used to estimate red coloration in fruits, they may not always show consistent trends due to differences in calculation. Specifically, h° is computed as the arctangent of b*/a*, and its value is sensitive to the relative changes of both components. At 35 DAT, despite a strong increase in a*, the simultaneous sharp decrease in b* may have influenced h° disproportionately, resulting in a higher value. Therefore, a* and anthocyanin content are considered more reliable indicators of red pigmentation at this stage.

Chlorophyll content gradually decreased over time, and the effect of MT treatment became less evident (Fig. 3). Moreover, MT treatment decreased carotenoid content in plum peels at 21 DAT. MT treatment decreased carotenoid content in plum peels at 21 DAT, suggesting that MT may influence specific pigment pathways, such as downregulating carotenoid biosynthesis or promoting its degradation. Meanwhile, the observed decline in chlorophyll content appeared to follow the natural ripening process and was not significantly altered by MT treatment. Meanwhile, the reduction in carotenoid content at 21 DAT indicates that MT may influence specific pigment pathways, potentially downregulating carotenoid synthesis or enhancing its degradation and the effect of MT was mainly restricted to the early stages of fruit ripening. Notably, despite the decrease in carotenoids, the plum peels became redder, suggesting that MT may have promoted anthocyanin accumulation. This trend highlights the complex role of MT in fruit pigmentation during ripening.

Anthocyanins, phenols, and flavonoids are essential for plant physiology and interactions with the environment [2]. MT has been demonstrated to enhance the production of anthocyanins, possibly by boosting the activity of phenylpropanoid-related enzymes [58]. MT-treated wine significantly increased total phenol, flavonoid, anthocyanin, and proanthocyanidin contents [56]. MT treatment positively impacts the antioxidant components of postharvest litchi during cold or ambient storage [31, 60]. Here, large positive changes in total flavonoids, phenols, anthocyanins and proanthocyanidin compounds were detected in MT-treated plum peels, especially at 28 and 35 DAT (Fig. 4).

MT is a powerful antioxidant that is recognized for its ability to reduce the free radical production, prevent membrane lipid degradation, and boost nonenzymatic antioxidant activity [45]. DPPH, ABTS and FRAP assays revealed that MT significantly increased the antioxidant capacity of plum peels (Fig. 5). Similarly, increased antioxidant capacity was also detected in ‘Moldova’ grape, litchi, and peach fruits after MT treatment [20, 31, 60]. Moreover, a significant correlation was reported between antioxidant properties and polyphenol accumulation in grapes [52]. Therefore, MT might promote polyphenol accumulation in fruits to increase their antioxidant capacity.

Anthocyanin accumulation results from the coordinated action of several key enzymes within the flavonoid pathway [43]. Among these genes, CHI, CHS, F3H, and UFGT have been identified as essential in the anthocyanin biosynthesis process in fruits [11, 42]. The CHI, CHS and F3H enzymes are critical early steps that influence anthocyanin accumulation and expression [36, 44]. UFGT activity is considered the most important factor influencing anthocyanin stability and colour intensity [49]. Here, we observed increased enzyme activities of CHI, CHS, and F3H during the middle treatment phase, indicating that such enzymes have prominent roles in the initial phases of anthocyanin accumulation. UFGT activity, on the other hand, increased during the late treatment phase, aligning with the stabilization and intensification of the anthocyanin content (Fig. 6). These findings indicated a close relationship between anthocyanin accumulation and CHI, CHS, and F3H activities, with UFGT activity displaying a temporal shift consistent with anthocyanin stabilization. Similarly, these patterns aligned with earlier studies in crabapple and red pear [11, 41].

The GO and KEGG analyses of DEGs in MT-treated plum peels at 14 and 28 DAT revealed significant dynamic changes, highlighting the time-dependent regulation of anthocyanin accumulation and related metabolic pathways by MT. According to the GO analysis of biological processes, MT likely influenced early stress responses at 14 DAT, promoting the formation of precursors for anthocyanins rather than directly triggering their biosynthesis (Fig. 7A). By 28 DAT, fruits shifted from primary to secondary metabolism, with significant upregulation of flavonoid-related genes, indicating the activation of key anthocyanin biosynthesis steps (Fig. 7B). Interestingly, cell component analysis revealed differences between 14 and 28 DAT, particularly in DEGs linked to the vacuole and cell wall, suggesting that MT regulates the storage of secondary metabolites, such as anthocyanins, by modulating these cellular structures [30]. At 14 DAT, GO enrichment analysis for molecular functions revealed the upregulation of genes associated with catalytic and binding activities, suggesting that MT primarily enhances enzyme functions involved in primary metabolism [1, 59]. This likely supports the production of precursor molecules and the energy necessary for subsequent anthocyanin accumulation. Notably, the UDP-glycosyltransferase gene was enriched, potentially facilitating early modifications of anthocyanins, but few genes directly linked to anthocyanin biosynthesis were activated at this stage [37]. However, by 28 DAT, plants shifted toward secondary metabolism, with significant upregulation of genes associated with key enzymes such as CHS, ANS, and F3H associated with anthocyanin biosynthesis [48, 54]. These observations imply that MT actively promotes the anthocyanin biosynthetic pathway at this later stage, contributing to increased anthocyanin accumulation, stabilization, and, ultimately, fruit coloration and metabolite storage.

In the KEGG enrichment analysis, similar results were observed. At 14 DAT, MT treatment primarily triggered the initial phases of anthocyanin biosynthesis and facilitated the accumulation of precursor compounds (Fig. 8A). By 28 DAT, key enzymes associated with anthocyanin biosynthesis pathway showed significant upregulation, indicating pronounced activation of anthocyanin biosynthesis and modification in the final stages of fruit maturation (Fig. 8B). In the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway, 18 genes were enriched at 28 DAT, including peroxidase genes (evm.model.LG06.2107.gene and evm.model.LG04.207.gene), which may contribute to increased antioxidant activity, protecting anthocyanins from degradation [33]. Additionally, several transferase genes, such as shikimate O-hydroxycinnamoyltransferase, are involved in precursor modification, further supporting anthocyanin accumulation [19]. In the anthocyanin biosynthesis pathway, 3-O-glucosyltransferase (evm.model.LG02.656.gene) is an enzyme that stabilizes and increases the solubility of anthocyanins [46]. The upregulation of this gene at 28 DAT further confirmed that MT in directly promoted anthocyanin accumulation, particularly by increasing anthocyanin stability and storage through glycosylation. Besides, multiple genes in the plant-pathogen interaction pathway are significantly enriched in MT treatment, suggesting a role for MT in plant defense. Secondary metabolites are essential for stress adaptation and defense [22]. Anthocyanins and other phenolics not only determine fruit coloration but also contribute to antioxidant defense [3]. MT has been shown to enhance stress resistance in various plants [14]. Thus, we propose that MT may promote anthocyanin accumulation by enhancing antioxidant capacity or regulating defense-related signaling pathways.

PAL is an essential upstream gene associated with anthocyanin synthesis [26, 27]; however, the expression of PAL was not significantly up-regulated at 14 and 28 DAT, indicating that PAL may not serve as a primary regulator in anthocyanin accumulation. C4H was significantly up-regulated at 14 DAT, indicating its accelerated effect on the phenolic propane metabolic pathway in the early stages of MT treatment. The expression of CHS, 4CL, F3H, ANS, DFR, and UFGT significantly increased after MT treatment, especially at 28 DAT (Fig. 9). CHS, CHI, and F3H are recognized as initial-phase genes in the anthocyanin biosynthetic pathway, with their encoded enzymes playing crucial roles in the initial phases of anthocyanin synthesis [40, 50]. In contrast, DFR and ANS are considered as late biosynthetic genes, participating in the final steps of the pathway [61]. Additionally, UFGT is involved in the final steps that result in stable, coloured anthocyanins [63]. Similar results have been reported in kiwifruit [28], crabapple leaves [11], and tea [26, 27]. These findings suggested that MT successfully promoted the synthesis and anthocyanin accumulation in plum peels by activating key genes downstream of the metabolic pathway.

Conclusion

This study elucidated the impacts of MT treatment on anthocyanin accumulation and colour in plum peels. We found that MT treatment accelerated the transition from green to red, particularly during the middle and late stages of fruit development. MT treatment increased pigment levels; increased the accumulation of total phenols, flavonoids, anthocyanins, and proanthocyanidins; increased antioxidant activity; and promoted the activity of key enzymes in the anthocyanin biosynthetic pathway. Additionally, MT significantly up-regulated the expression of the CHS, F3H, DFR, ANS, and UFGT genes, contributing to anthocyanin stabilization and accumulation.
