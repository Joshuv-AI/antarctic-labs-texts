Incidence of Cancers Among Children Living With HIV in Malawi After Widespread Availability of Antiretroviral Therapy: A Population-Based Study
Authors: Casey L McAtee, Fatsani Manase, William Kamiyango, Jimmy Villiera, Mark Zobeck, Joseph Mhango, Allison Silverstein, Samuel Maloya, Apatsa Matatiyo, Eluness Simwela, Honour Mhango, Desire Hellings, Brigid O'Brien, Claudia Wallrauch, Tom Heller, Matthew Painschab, Kate Westmoreland, Atupele Mpasa, Rizine Mzikamanda, Nmazuo Ozuah, Joseph Lubega, Nader Kim El-Mallawany, Carl E Allen, Michael E Scheurer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPURPOSEFew data describe the modern epidemiology of pediatric HIV-associated cancers in sub-Saharan Africa, home to over 80% of the world's children living with HIV. This study evaluated the incidence of cancers among these children over 15 years after widespread antiretroviral therapy (ART) adoption in Malawi.METHODSWe conducted a retrospective population-based cohort study leveraging multiple independent data sources comprehensive of all cancer diagnoses among children living with HIV in Northern and Central Malawi between 2010 and 2024. HIV-associated cancers were defined as those cancers with well-established epidemiologic associations with HIV. Cumulative incidence and average annual percent change were estimated using joinpoint regression. Next, we conducted a subcohort analysis within the country's largest pediatric HIV clinic to evaluate cancer risk factors using log-binomial regression.RESULTSAmong 1,686 pediatric cancer diagnoses, 184 (10.9%) occurred in children with HIV. Kaposi sarcoma (KS) accounted for 96% of cases. The cumulative incidence of HIV-associated malignancies fell from 4.7 cases per million children in 2010 to 1.2 per million in 2024, representing an 84% decline from its peak in 2013 and an average annual percent decline of 13% (P < .001). KS dropped from the third- to seventh-most common childhood cancer in the region. Notably, 58% of affected children were ART-naïve at diagnosis. Initiating ART in infancy reduced the risk of KS by 69% (P < .001). Other HIV-associated cancers were rare, comprising 0.4% of all cases diagnosed.CONCLUSIONNational ART expansion substantially reduced pediatric HIV-associated cancers in Malawi, achieving declines comparable with those seen in high-income countries. Sustained early diagnosis and ART linkage remain essential to sustain progress.

INTRODUCTION

The burden of HIV in children has decreased significantly in recent decades.1,2 Between 2010 and 2023, new pediatric HIV infections declined by 60% globally, from 300,000 to 120,000 cases annually.1 In 2023, an estimated 1.4 million children were living with HIV, down from over two million in the mid-2000s.2 Progress is largely due to public health efforts that expanded access to HIV care, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa.1,3

Malawi's experience demonstrates that large-scale ART expansion can nearly eliminate pediatric HIV-associated cancers, even in extremely resource-limited settings. Sustaining early HIV detection and linkage to ART remains essential to prevent residual cancer incidence among untreated children.

Malawi, a country of 22 million people in southeast Africa, exemplifies this progress. In the 1990s, reports described HIV seroprevalence among urban Malawians reaching 30%.4 By 2023, national seroprevalence among adults had fallen to 7%.1 Over the same period, Malawian children living with HIV declined from 120,000 to 50,000, with new infections decreasing from 15,000 to 4,000 annually.1

The epidemiology of pediatric cancer in eastern Africa was transformed by HIV.5-7 Kaposi sarcoma (KS), once rare in children, emerged as one of the most common childhood cancers.6,8,9 By the early 2000s, KS was the most frequently diagnosed pediatric cancer in reports from Uganda and Mozambique.8 In Malawi and neighboring Zambia, it was more common than ALL, the most common pediatric cancer worldwide.6,9

Widespread antiretroviral therapy (ART) coverage led to 80%-90% declines in pediatric HIV-associated malignancies in high-income countries.10,11 Most African studies describing these cancers in children were conducted before ART was widely available.7,12,13 Consequently, few contemporary reports describe their epidemiology in sub-Saharan Africa.11,14

In 2004, the Malawi Ministry of Health launched a national ART scale-up program with an initial implementation period from 2004 to 2006 and expansion through 2010.3,15 Option B+ followed in 2011, and Treat-All was adopted in 2016.3,16 By 2024, ART coverage exceeded 90%.3,17

In light of limited data related to cancer epidemiology among children living with HIV in the region, this study aimed to evaluate the incidence of these cancers in Malawi in the years following initiation of its ART scale-up program. We hypothesized that, despite severe resource constraints, reductions in pediatric HIV-associated cancers comparable with those observed in high-income countries were achieved with sustained public health investment. We also sought to identify modifiable risk factors associated with HIV-associated cancer risk.

METHODS

Study Design and Setting

We conducted a retrospective population-based cohort study with a nested subcohort analysis to estimate incidence trends of HIV-associated pediatric cancers and to identify risk factors for cancer in children living with HIV in Northern and Central Malawi from 2010 to 2024 (Fig 1).

The population-based analysis used comprehensive case identification across all known regional sources of pediatric cancer care to estimate annual cumulative incidence of HIV-associated malignancies. The nested subcohort analysis leveraged longitudinal data from the largest pediatric HIV clinic in the country, the Baylor Children's Foundation Malawi Center of Excellence (Baylor COE), located in Lilongwe.

Catchment Area and Population

Northern and Central Malawi account for 56% of the national population.18 In Malawi, children younger than 14 years are treated as pediatric patients. This age threshold was used throughout the study. Pediatric HIV care is decentralized nationally, but all children with cancer in this region are referred to one of two colocated facilities in Lilongwe at Kamuzu Central Hospital: the Malawi National Cancer Centre and the Baylor COE.

Cancer Case Ascertainment

We identified cancer cases using three complementary data sources comprehensive for all sites that diagnose pediatric cancer in the catchment area:

Baylor COE electronic medical record (EMR): We queried all clinical notes since 2010 using keyword-based natural language processing to flag potential cancer diagnoses (eg, “Kaposi,” “tumor,” “cancer”). All flagged records were reviewed to confirm cancer diagnosis. A list of search terms and R code is included in the Data Supplement (Table S1).

Malawi National Cancer Centre EMR: We queried all children diagnosed with cancer in the region (data available from 2013).

University of North Carolina Project-Malawi—Lymphoma Study: A prospective cohort enrolling all pathologically confirmed lymphoproliferative malignancies in children and adults in the catchment area since 2013. This database was queried to capture rare cases of cancer where a child younger than 14 years was treated on the adult service.

To avoid duplication, we cross-matched cases across databases using patient identifiers (names, birth dates, medical record numbers) with fuzzy-matching algorithms based on Levenshtein distance, followed by manual confirmation by investigators.19

HIV Status Ascertainment

HIV serostatus was abstracted from the National Cancer Centre and Baylor COE EMRs. In Malawi, HIV testing is standard at first contact with the health care system. When electronic records were incomplete, we reviewed physical charts for laboratory results documenting serostatus. Cases with HIV status recorded only by history (ie, without direct documentation of laboratory results) were labeled “HIV-negative/unconfirmed.” Cases without any documentation were labeled “unknown.”

Only children with confirmed HIV infection with a diagnosis of an HIV-associated cancer were included in HIV-associated cancer incidence estimates. Patients with unknown serostatus were not included in the subcohort risk analysis.

ART status was documented as “ART-naïve” in cases where no previous documented ART initiation was noted and patients/guardians endorsed no previous use of ART; otherwise, patients were classified as “on ART” as of the date on record or as endorsed by patients/guardians.

Classification of HIV-Associated Cancers

We classified cancers occurring in children with HIV into two categories.

First, HIV-associated cancers referred to malignancies with a well-established epidemiologic association with HIV infection occurring at elevated rates in people living with HIV (eg, KS).20,21 Second, we identified all other cancers without a known causal link to HIV (eg, Wilms tumor). Cases of KS in HIV-seronegative children were separately reported to compare trends with HIV-associated cases.

Population-Based Incidence Estimation

We calculated cumulative incidence of HIV-associated cancers per million children. Population denominators were obtained from the US Census Bureau and the United Nations Population Division.18,22 Annual HIV prevalence and ART coverage were abstracted directly from the online tool provided by Naomi, a UNAIDS model producing annual district-level and age- and sex-specific metrics.17 Naomi combines household survey data, antenatal clinic surveillance, and programmatic ART data within a Bayesian small area estimation framework to produce estimates of HIV indicators at the subnational level.23,24

Trends in incidence were analyzed using joinpoint regression with a segmented Poisson model that detects inflection points in temporal trends and estimates average annual percent change.25 Potential joinpoints were identified by fitting piecewise log-linear Poisson regressions, and the Davies26 test was used to assess for significant changes in slope. Age standardization was not performed because the pediatric age structure in Malawi remained stable during the study period, and pediatric HIV-associated KS is almost entirely unique to this region of the world. Year-to-year comparisons within the same underlying pediatric population are therefore not subject to significant age distribution bias.

Subcohort Risk Analysis: Baylor COE Cohort

To evaluate individual-level risk factors for incident KS, we performed a subanalysis of children enrolled at the Baylor COE who were living with HIV and on ART for ≥3 months. Children contributed person-time from the date of HIV diagnosis until the earliest of KS diagnosis, death, or last clinic visit.

We calculated incidence density rates per 100,000 person-years and used log-binomial regression to estimate relative risk (RR) of developing KS according to age at ART initiation and sex. CD4 counts and HIV viral loads were not included in the model as laboratory acquisition was biased toward patients with suspected cases of AIDS-defining conditions. Hypothesis tests were two-sided, with α = .05. No sample size calculation was conducted as all eligible patients were included.

Sensitivity analyses for both incidence and risk analyses were conducted excluding patients without histopathologic confirmation of their disease. For incidence trend estimations, we performed an analysis removing years 2020 and 2021 because of general decreases in cancer incidence related to decreased health care utilization in Malawi during the COVID-19 pandemic.27 Finally, we performed a sensitivity analysis of risk variables identified in the log-binomial KS risk analysis using Poisson regression with robust variance.28

Ethics and Reporting

This study adhered to STROBE guidelines for observational studies.29 Ethical approval was obtained from the Malawi National Health Sciences Research Committee and the Baylor College of Medicine Institutional Review Board. All analyses were conducted using R (v4.4.2), with joinpoint models implemented using the segmented package.25,30

RESULTS

HIV in Malawi

Between 2010 and 2024, the prevalence of HIV among children age 0-13 years in Northern and Central Malawi fell from 12,487 cases/million (95% CI, 12,369 to 12,605) in 2010 to 3,455 cases/million (95% CI, 3,400 to 3,512) in 2024, an average annual decline of 9.1% per year ([95% CI, –9.1 to –9.0]; P < .001). In 2024, the ART coverage in Malawi was 58% (95% CI, 56 to 61) among children and 92% (95% CI, 91 to 93) among adults.

HIV-Associated Cancers in Children

Between 2010 and 2024, 1,686 children were diagnosed with cancer, including 184 (10.9%) living with HIV (Table 1). Of these, 175 (95%) had HIV-associated malignancies, 96% of which were KS. Other HIV-related cancers included non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL), Hodgkin lymphoma, and multicentric Castleman disease unassociated with KS. Overall, 58% of HIV-seropositive patients were ART-naïve at cancer diagnosis.

Cumulative incidence of HIV-associated malignancies increased from 4.7 cases/million (95% CI, 2.7 to 7.4) in 2010 to 7.2 cases/million (95% CI, 4.7 to 10.3) in 2013 and then declined to 1.2 cases/million (95% CI, 0.4 to 2.6) in 2024, representing an 84% decline from its peak. The average annual percent change between 2010 and 2024 was –13% ([95% CI, –18 to –9]; P < .001). A joinpoint in 2013 when incidence shifted from rising to falling was not significant, likely because of limited power.

Kaposi Sarcoma

Between 2010 and 2024, 208 KS cases were diagnosed, among which 168 (81%) were HIV-associated. Seventy-four percent were biopsy-confirmed. The median age at diagnosis was 7 years (IQR, 4-11). Among ART-enrolled children, the median time to KS after ART initiation was 2 years (IQR, 1-5), ranging up to 11 years.

Population-wide cumulative incidence of HIV-associated KS declined from 4.7 cases/million (95% CI, 2.7 to 7.6) in 2010 to 1.2 cases/million (95% CI, 0.4 to 2.7) in 2024, with an average annual percent change of –14% ([95% CI, –18 to –9]; P < .001) and an 82% decrease from its 2013 peak incidence of 6.7 cases/million (Fig 2).

Incidence of endemic KS remained stable, ranging from 0.6 cases/million (95% CI, 0.1 to 2.0) in 2011 (zero cases in 2010) to 0.5 cases/million (95% CI, 0.1 to 1.7) in 2024. The ratio of HIV-associated to endemic KS fell from 9:1 in 2011 to 2:1 in 2024.

Among children with HIV, KS incidence ranged from 373 cases/million (95% CI, 213 to 605) in 2010 to 338 cases/million (95% CI, 110 to 790) in 2024, peaking at 650 cases/million in 2013 and reaching a low of 48 cases/million in 2021 (Fig 3). There was no significant trend in KS incidence among children with HIV. The proportion of ART-naïve children developing HIV-associated KS compared with those already on ART remained stable. Notably, KS declined as a proportion of all pediatric cancer diagnoses at the National Cancer Centre. From 2013 to 2018, it was the third-most common cancer (13% of cases). Between 2020 and 2024, it had fallen to seventh (6%).

Risk of KS

Among 4,894 children living with HIV under care at the Baylor COE (20,648 person-years), the KS incidence density ranged from 260 cases/100,000 person-years in 2010 (95% CI, 85 to 608) to zero in 2024, peaking at 520 cases/100,000 person-years in 2013.

One hundred fifty-eight patients with HIV-associated KS were diagnosed at the Baylor COE during the study period and were available for subcohort analysis. Male sex and older age at ART initiation were associated with higher KS risk (Table 2). Multivariable analysis including sex and age showed a RR of KS given male sex of 1.4 ([95% CI, 1.1 to 2.0]; P = .02). Each year of ART delay from birth increased KS risk by 10% (RR, 1.1 per year [95% CI, 1.0 to 1.1]; P < .001). ART initiation before age one was associated with a 69% reduction in KS risk (RR, 0.3 [95% CI, 0.15 to 0.57]; P < .001) compared with those started afterward. There was no effect modification between sex and time to ART initiation.

Other Cancers in Children Living With HIV

Seven non-KS HIV-associated cancers were identified, most commonly mature B-cell NHL, accounting for 0.4% of all pediatric cancers during the study period (Table 1). Nine additional cancers not associated with HIV were also identified, including ALL and Wilms tumor. All cases were pathologically confirmed.

Missing Data and Sensitivity Analyses

Data were complete with the following exceptions. Biopsy status was unconfirmed in 2 of 168 (<1%) children with KS. ART status at cancer diagnosis was unconfirmed in 6 of 184 patients (3%). HIV status was negative by history (laboratory unconfirmed) in 282 of 1,686 (17%) patients because of missing primary laboratory data. HIV status was unknown in 144 of 1,686 (9%) patients with cancer. HIV status was laboratory-confirmed in all cases of KS. Patients with unknown HIV status were not included in risk or incidence analyses.

Incidence trend and risk variable analyses were robust on sensitivity analyses (Data Supplement, Tables S2 and S3).

DISCUSSION

ART scale-up in Malawi substantially reduced cancer incidence among children living with HIV. The fact that zero children on ART at Malawi's largest pediatric HIV clinic developed HIV-associated cancer in 2024 is a triumph. Malawi's scale-up program shows that reductions seen in high-income countries can be achieved in the communities where most children with HIV live.

Most of the declining cancer incidence reflected the steep fall in KS, once a leading pediatric cancer in Malawi. By 2024, it was once again a rare malignancy, and the incidence of HIV-associated cases again approached the rate of rare endemic, HIV-negative KS unique to children in the region.31

A decline in pediatric KS was uncertain in the early years of the HIV epidemic when it remained common throughout Africa after ART introduction.8,11,32 In Malawi, KS incidence continued rising during this transitional period, raising concern that high incidence might persist.33 Twenty years later, public health–based ART scale-up programs have reduced pediatric cancers even in very resource-constrained settings, following earlier reports from Uganda which observed modest declines in HIV-associated KS among children after ART introduction in the early 2000s.34

Reduced HIV-associated cancer incidence reflects shifts in both perinatal and horizontal HIV acquisition in Malawi. The introduction of Option B+ in 2011 enrolling all seropositive pregnant and breastfeeding women on ART markedly reduced mother-to-child transmission, limiting the pool of perinatally infected children at risk of HIV-associated cancers.3 The Treat-All policy that followed in 2016 expanded immediate ART access and strengthened linkage to care, reducing progression to severe immunosuppression and increased cancer risk in older children and adolescents living with HIV.16

While reductions in perinatal transmissions and expanded access to ART at centers such as the Baylor COE decreased overall HIV-associated cancer rates among children (Fig 2), rates within the broader population of children living with HIV remained relatively stable (Fig 3). Perinatally infected children unlinked to care remain at highest risk of HIV-associated cancers, maintaining a steady proportional cancer incidence among children living with HIV even as absolute numbers decline. Most incident cancer in this study arose among children determined to be ART-naïve, highlighting this high-risk group driving residual incidence.

The question of whether ART naivety was due to unknown serostatus or because diagnosed children were unlinked to care is important, but unclear. Patients diagnosed with HIV at the Baylor COE are most often initiated on ART within a month, suggesting that ART-naïve children had unknown serostatus at the time of cancer diagnosis in most cases. However, undocumented or undisclosed previous diagnoses and/or ART initiation at locations outside of the purview of this study could not be excluded. Whether ART enrollment failed at the point of diagnosis, linkage to care, or maintenance on ART could not be confirmed, leaving an important question for future studies highly relevant to program implementation.

Beyond KS, rates of other cancers among children with HIV were low. NHL, the second-most common HIV-associated cancer globally and most commonly diagnosed cancer among children in Malawi, was rare.7,12 The relative rarity of NHL in this study is surprising given the well-established relationship between HIV and NHL risk in children and adults in both high- and low-resource settings.12,21 Possible explanations include decreased diagnostic capacity for NHL in the region leading to underascertainment, particularly in the early years of the study when pediatric cancer programs in Malawi were new. Death before diagnosis is another consideration as pediatric HIV-associated NHL in Africa commonly presents in advanced stages.35

Other cancers associated with HIV, such as cervical cancers and epithelial carcinomas, were undetected in this study.10,21,36 The relatively young age of this mostly preadolescent cohort alongside underascertainment of rare cases is a potential explanation. These and other HIV-associated cancers are more common among adolescents and adults transitioning into lifelong HIV care versus the general public, highlighting the need for vigilant clinical and epidemiologic surveillance of cancer in this population as it ages.20

This study is strengthened by data from independent cohorts including all major regional care sites comprehensive for pediatric cancer diagnoses, and it relies on direct ascertainment of cases rather than model estimates common in epidemiologic research from the region.

The study is limited by a number of potential biases. First, limited health care access in the region risked underascertainment of cancer cases. This limitation was mitigated by increasing diagnostic capacity and cancer awareness during the study period, with risk of underascertainment being highest early in the study when cancer incidence was highest.

Second, while the positive predictive value of physical examination to diagnose KS is relatively high in low-resource settings, misclassification of biopsy-unconfirmed cases diagnosed by physical examination was possible.37 Third, unknown HIV serostatus in a minority of patients might have also resulted in underascertainment of cancer cases among children with HIV. As KS comprised 96% of all HIV-associated cancers, among which serostatus was known in all cases, underestimation of HIV-related cancer incidence because of unknown serostatus was likely modest.

In conclusion, since 2010, the incidence of HIV-associated malignancies fell dramatically among children in Malawi. This decline is a testament to the capability of concerted, well-funded, and sustained public health interventions to combat the HIV epidemic in the most vulnerable populations in the world. Nonetheless, most children developing HIV-associated cancer in Malawi remain those not yet enrolled in care and initiated on ART. Pediatric HIV-associated cancers are clearly preventable. Sustained HIV prevention, detection, and treatment are essential to maintain progress.
