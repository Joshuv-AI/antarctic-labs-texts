Predicting OCD severity from religiosity and personality: A machine learning and neural network approach
Authors: Brian A. Zaboski, Alixandra Wilens, Joseph P.H. McNamara, Gregory N. Muller
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) affects a significant portion of the United States population. The present study investigated the complex relationships among OCD severity, personality traits, religiosity, and spirituality with a dataset of 229 participants. We applied advanced machine and deep learning techniques to identify key predictors of OCD severity, uncovering nuanced relationships and unexpected findings. Notably, item-level features were more influential than aggregate scores, challenging traditional analytical approaches. Moreover, a neural network model, despite not surpassing a linear regression in predictive accuracy, provided a more comprehensive understanding of OCD’s heterogeneity and of the nonlinear relationships between our variables. The inclusion of demographic factors provided further explanatory power for predicting OCD severity, emphasizing the multifaceted nature of the disorder. Our results show that machine learning models can nearly match traditional linear models in predictive power while retaining nonlinear relationships essential to understanding OCD. Our study advocates for the adoption of sophisticated predictive models in examining complex psychological phenomena, encouraging a reevaluation of conventional analytical approaches when prediction is central to research questions.

Introduction

Obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) is a serious psychiatric condition affecting 2–3 % of the United States population [1], [2]. OCD is characterized by impairment in multiple domains including decision-making, suicidal ideation, and quality of life [3], [4], [5]. Scrupulosity, a subset of OCD, involves a fear of transgressing one’s religious, moral, or ethical values; it impacts up to one-third of individuals living with OCD and is associated with higher OCD severity [6], [7], [8]. Moreover, individuals with scrupulosity struggle with significant pathological doubt and have significant fear about the consequences of their thoughts and behaviors (e.g., being condemned to Hell) [8], [9].

Scrupulosity-related concerns may interact with religiosity and spirituality. Whereas religiosity tends to be narrow and institutional, spirituality is more personal and subjective [10]. Furthermore, spirituality does not require adherence to doctrines or even belief in a higher power [10], [11], [12]. Therefore, many atheists report at least some scrupulosity, especially immorality, as well as sinning and fear of God, though the latter two are less pronounced in non-Christian samples [13].

Studies have shown that in addition to spirituality and religiosity, personality also influences OCD severity. Personality factors—neuroticism in particular—have been extensively studied as predictors of OCD severity [14], [15], [16]. Moreover, demographic factors (e.g., biological sex, race, income) frequently influence and interact with these predictors [17], [18], [19], [20]. For example, prior research demonstrates that women tend to exhibit higher ratings on personality dimensions associated with OCD presentations, including neuroticism [21], [22], [23]. These complex relationships contribute to OCD’s heterogeneity and speak to the complicated nature of understanding psychopathology [24].

Machine learning accounts for this complexity by considering nonlinear relationships among variables. There is a strong precedent for using machine learning models in OCD, religiosity, spirituality, and personality. For instance, in OCD, Grassi and colleagues used a machine learning algorithm to find predictors for OCD remission, including severity and course of symptoms, as well as psychotropic medications and global functioning [25]. Religiosity researchers used machine coding and text modeling to compare cognitive processing differences between two religious groups in Southwest China [26]. Relatedly, researchers examined spiritual pain and social distress in terminally ill patients with cancer by utilizing clinical records and machine learning algorithms [27]. Within personality research, textual analyses of Indonesian social media profiles using machine learning techniques predicted personality traits [28]. Despite the utility of machine learning in each of these fields, these variables have not been studied conjointly to generate a predictive model in OCD research, even though individual studies recognize them as important predictors [29], [30], [31], [32], [33], [34]. Thus, what is needed is a simultaneous examination of these variables to uncover relevant OCD predictors.

Machine learning follows a structured process aimed at uncovering meaningful patterns or relationships in data [35]. It often requires domain knowledge and is guided by hypotheses and context. Within this background, we algorithmically selected features from a dataset containing variables for OC severity, demographics, personality, and religiosity. Specifically, we hypothesized that variables related to the total scores of spirituality, personality, religious health, and demographics (age, sex, race, religion, income) would be identified by the feature selection procure. Next, we aimed to account for multivariate, nonlinear relationships between the selected features and OCD symptom severity. To do this, we ran classic machine learning models and a neural network. We hypothesized that nonlinear models would provide a better prediction than linear models, as nonlinear models would better represent the complexity in the data. Moreover, we expected that the neural network would best predict OCD severity due to its ability to continuously represent categorical variables (discussed more below). To avoid fishing, we included specified constraints that minimized overfitting and sought validation and reproducibility through the use of training and test sets. Following our analysis, we discussed our findings and examined directions for future research and implications for clinicians.

Methods

Participants

All procedures received Institutional Review Board approval, and participants were reimbursed for their participation. Participants included U.S. volunteers that enrolled in Spring 2020 as workers through Amazon’s Mechanical Turk online platform. Participants were (a) between the ages 18 to 45 and (b) had English-language fluency. As non-clinical samples can be greatly relevant to understanding OC symptoms [36], to measure a continuum of symptom severity, participants did not require a diagnosis of OCD to participate.

All workers required at least a 95 % approval rating on the MTurk platform. N = 1226 workers responded to the study. A combination of failing to start the survey and failing to meet inclusion criteria resulted in 406 workers. From the pool of 406 workers, 65 participants were removed for taking fewer than six minutes to complete the survey. Sixty participants were dropped for failing an embedded honesty check (e.g., “Your compensation will not be affected by your response to this item. Did you answer these questions honestly?”), and 51 participants were removed for failing an attention check (e.g., “Please select ‘None at all’” for a multiple-choice question with “None at all” as an option). The final dataset consisted of 230 participants.

Measures

Dimensional Obsessive-Compulsive Scale

The Dimensional Obsessive-Compulsive Scale (DOCS) includes 20 items that evaluate the severity of intrusive obsessions/compulsions across four domains (i.e., germs/contamination, harm, unacceptable thoughts and “just-rightness) [37]. The DOCS has robust psychometrics related to total score test-retest reliability (r = 0.66 to r = 0.81) [37], [38]; good internal consistency (α = 0.90) [37]; and good convergent validity between the Yale-Brown Obsessive-Compulsive Scale (YBOCS) [39] and Obsessive-Compulsive Inventory–Revised (OCI-R) [40], r = 0.51 and r = 0.69 respectively.

Centrality of Religiosity Scale (CRS-15)

The Centrality of Religiosity Scale (CRS) is a 15-item measure providing a non-denominational examination of religiosity across five domains: (a) intellectual, (b) ideology, (c) public practice, (d) private practice, and (e) religious experience [41]. The CRS-15 uses a probabilistic model that projects the intensity of religious belief to a spectrum of absent/marginal belief (1.0 to 2.0), partial belief (2.1 to 3.9), or significant belief (4.0 to 5.0) [41]. Psychometric adequacy is provided through several studies. One example using samples across three European countries shows good internal consistency and theoretical alignment with the construct being measured [42]. In a study with 4396 participants, the CRS also demonstrated a high correlation with another measure of Christianity (r = 0.92) and a single factor structure [43].

The Big Five Inventory—Second Edition (BFI-2)

The 60-item Big Five Inventory Second Edition (BFI-2) evaluates five personality domains (i.e., openness, conscientiousness, agreeableness, extraversion, neuroticism) [44]. Adequate psychometric properties (e.g., domain mean α = 0.87/0.87; domain mean retest = 0.80; facet mean α = .76/.77; facet mean retest =.73) were demonstrated on an English-language version of the BFI-2 during an initial evaluation [44]. A subsequent evaluation of the BFI-2 found similar component loadings and acceptable full-scale coefficient alphas for personality domains (0.84 - 0.88) [45].

Spiritual Health and Life-Orientation Measure—Second Edition (SHALOM-2)

The Spiritual Health and Life-Orientation Measure (SHALOM) includes 20 items across four domains of spiritual health/well-being: (a) personal domain, (b) communal domain, (c) environmental domain, and (d) transcendental domain [46]. Input from a large sample (N = 850) of culturally and religiously diverse secondary students was solicited to provide appropriate language and clarity. Confirmatory factor analyses reported adequate reliability and validity, and subsequent evaluations have demonstrated a three-factor model [46], [47]. Further investigations demonstrated high internal consistency (α = 0.94) and discriminant validity across all groups (i.e., atheists, “spiritual, not religious,” and religious) [48].

Analytic plan

All analyses were conducted in Python v. 3.10 [49]. Data manipulation, descriptive statistics, and correlations were computed in Pandas 1.5.2 [50]. Plots were programmed in Seaborn [51]. Machine learning algorithms were completed with sciikit-learn [52]; neural networks were run with fastai [53]. For missing scale data, the K nearest neighbor transformation was applied [54]; only one observation in the SHALOM-2 was imputed this way. One case was removed for having unreported data on biological sex.

Sum scores were computed for all measures (DOCS, CRS-15, subscales on the BFI-2 and the SHALOM-2). A prior publication with these data included data visualizations, correlation matrices, and alpha coefficients as a measure of internal reliability [55]. After cleaning the data and viewing descriptive statistics, features were selected from the dataset. This was done through a lasso regression.

Feature selection and prediction models

Next, we used 5-fold cross-validation to randomly split the training and validation sets into five folds [56]. Cross-validation allows the models to train on the dataset and leave a subset of our data unseen during training for testing purposes. We selected five folds because it gave us the most representative sub-sample size for each group, or fold, with the dataset we collected. We then computed the average RMSE (root mean squared error) across all five folds for four models: a linear regression, decision tree, random forest, and neural network. Sample size considerations

Two broad goals of data analysis are to predict outcomes or to elucidate causal relationships [57]. Machine and deep learning models are chosen for predictive performance, not to estimate effects in a population [56], [58], [59]. Rather than using traditional p-values and power, these models use different methods for model specification, such as rigorous use of training/test sets with cross-validation (described below), adjusting hyperparameters, and comparing model performance (e.g., maximizing accuracy, minimizing error, optimizing loss) [56]. That said, the sample in the present study (N = 230) was comparable to those of other investigations utilizing machine learning methods in OCD research (e.g., N = 37 to N = 215 [25], [60], [61]) increasing our confidence that the results would be meaningful and interpretable.

This happens through a function that includes a tuning parameter that optimizes variable selection. To optimize this tuning parameter, we iterated over a possible range from 0.1 to 10, yielding 99 candidates with 495 possible fits [56]. Neural network

The neural network was trained from scratch with a random data split: 80 % of cases were included in the training sample and 20 % were in the validation sample. All values were normalized, and the rectified linear unit (ReLU) was chosen for the activation functions [59], [63]. We iterated through values to choose an optimal learning rate of 0.17 [64].

The neural network is a complex architecture that includes several layers that process input data (embedding layers, linear layers, dropout layers and batch normalization). Backpropagation was used to update the model parameters in these layers [64]. The architecture is designed to work with tabular data that includes categorical variables (e.g., sex, geographic location, and religion). The total number of parameters indicates the capacity of the model, and these parameters were trained to make predictions based on the input data. Specific layers were added for managing overfitting and working with categorical data. For example, the dropout layer helps prevent overfitting by randomly setting a fraction of input units to 0 at each update during training. Additionally, embedding layers are used to transform categorical variables into continuous representations that capture the relationships between categories to improve the learning capability of parameters (see Thomas, 2018 for an accessible introduction to embeddings) [65]. Discussion

The aims of this study were twofold: first, determine which personality, religiosity, and spirituality features were the most relevant predictors for OCD severity, then identify the most optimal model for prediction. Features of the dataset were selected through lasso regression and then applied to four different models with increasing complexity to account for the nonlinear and heterogeneous nature of OCD. Results indicated that while the linear regression had the best model fit, the neural network best captured the complex relationships between the input and output variables with a RMSE acceptably close to the simple linear model (10.99 vs 11.31). This aligned closely with our hypothesis that predicting OCD severity—known for its complex, multidimensional relationships—is better suited toward models built for nonlinear data. This was the first study in OCD research to account for personality, religiosity, and spirituality simultaneously, and also the first in OCD research to demonstrate the utility of a neural network with categorical embeddings on tabular data.

Feature selection yielded important results. Principally, item-level features were more important than sum scores, even for the personality scale. This result differs from the more common, non-machine learning analyses in the OCD literature in which variables are often included as total scores, based on the premise that they reveal more about underlying psychological constructs than individual items [66], [67], [68]. However, machine learning analyses have found better predictive power from overlapping item-level scores [33]. For example, in a sample of 8700 participants, one study found that item-level models accounted for 30 % more variance than Big 5 models [69]. This may explain why individual items were more important than sum scores on the CRS-15 and the SHALOM-2: Machine learning algorithms may be more capable of capturing the nuanced relationships between items and OCD severity than traditional analyses. For instance, CRS Item 7 (“How important is it for you to be connected to a religious community?”) was broadly important across all 5 folds in the random forest, perhaps because it taps into a willingness to be open with a community of people, which relates to OCD severity by indirectly measuring avoidance or stigma [70]. Similarly, since BFI Item 54 (“Tends to feel depressed, blue”) was present in each fold, this may indicate that for this sample of OCD participants, the item is capturing variance due to depressive symptomatology. This would be in line with Rees and colleagues, who found that personality was best associated with OCD symptom severity when depression was considered as a mediating factor [15].

Overall, the only demographics found to have predictive power were age, sex, location, and religion. Consistent with a review of sex differences in OCD, researchers found several complex relationships between sex and OCD, including that males tended to report more taboo thoughts, and females displayed higher levels of anxiety and depression [17]. Previous studies of OC severity have considered age at onset of symptoms, to differing results. Jakubovski and colleagues found age at onset to significantly interact with improvement of symptoms after first-line OCD treatments, whereas determined that age at onset was not significantly predictive of OC severity, although they were focused specifically on pediatric OCD [66], [71]. It is possible that both current age and age at onset of symptoms are significantly associated with OC symptom severity. Religion as predictive of OC severity aligns with previous research showing that devotees of some religions demonstrate greater fears of punishment and sin from God than others, which can potentially lead to religious obsessions in those with OCD [72].

Of the four models, the linear regression performed best in terms of RMSE—though not much better than the random forest or the neural network (Table 4). This was inconsistent with one treatment study in OCD that found a neural network performed better than a standard logistic regression predicting treatment response [73]. Still, linear regressions are overly simplistic; by definition, the output of a linear regression is a linear combination of its input variables [74]. Such a model is unlikely to represent the nonlinear and complex relationships among personality, religiosity, demographic factors and OCD severity [71], [75]. Attempting to account for these nonlinearities, the decision tree did not outperform the linear regression; nevertheless, a family of decision trees (the random forest) showed more promise. Overall, results for the more powerful random forest aligned with the broader machine learning literature in OCD, which demonstrates multiple applications for prediction [29], [33], [73].

Improving upon the random forest, our study uniquely contributed an analysis with a neural network. Unlike the architecture used in prior work [73], ours was programmed to manage categorical data through multiple embedding layers [53]. Results indicated close—though not better—RMSE to that of the linear model. However, performance may in fact be conceptually better because, as with most psychiatric disorders, OCD is multidimensional [76] and heterogeneous [77]. It interacts with context—socioeconomic status, race, ethnicity, and culture—that play pivotal roles in shaping and maintaining psychological and biological processes [78]. Thus, the neural network was theoretically and empirically stronger than the linear model; it included every interaction between every variable, accounted for all nonlinear relationships, and properly treated large categorical variables as continuous. For these reasons, the neural network was the best model for predicting OCD, reinforcing our understanding that predicting OCD severity works best with models that account for nonlinear relationships.

Implications and limitations

These results have several implications. First, religiosity and personality factors are relevant variables for predicting OCD severity, as shown by item-level findings, and should be considered during assessment. Second, we recommend considering machine and deep learning when research questions involve prediction. Our study contributes to a growing literature suggesting that hypotheses focused on prediction (rather than on a particular parameter) benefit from algorithmic decision making, increasing replicability, generalizability, and model performance [79], [80]. Third, researchers may wish to reconsider “black box” criticisms, or the inability for machine learning models to provide details about features or predictions. The present analysis showed that machine learning can reveal much about a dataset, including relevant features, overall importance, model comparisons and prediction plots. While these analyses cannot show how manipulating an independent variable contributes to a change in a dependent variable (i.e., causal modeling), accurate and generalizable prediction should be considered a different culture of statistical analysis suited to its own kind of procedures [57].

Further, clinicians and researchers should take individual items more seriously in analyses and in practice. For example, Askland and colleagues found several features of individual predictive importance on the YBOCS, including the YBOCS checklist, that often do not contribute to overall severity [29]. Reviewing the variance contributions of individual items may reveal important considerations in the dataset that could lead to new and fascinating research questions. We are hopeful that more sophisticated modeling techniques will provide a more nuanced understanding of the features that contribute to OCD severity.

In clinical settings, the models developed in this study have practical implications, particularly for improving OCD treatment strategies. By assessing for personality, religiosity, and spirituality, clinicians can incorporate these factors into personalized treatment approaches. For example, incorporating individual item-level features—like the importance of one’s connection to a religious community—may offer a better understanding of a client’s condition, reveal more environmental contributors of stress, and provide more exposure ideas than overall scale scores. Furthermore, machine learning models, especially neural networks, can allow clinicians to consider the importance of these variables simultaneously and to aid in clinical decision-making. By embracing machine learning in clinical contexts, clinicians can enhance their diagnostic precision, improve treatment responsiveness, and better understand the underlying complexities of OCD.

Despite these exciting applications, limitations should be noted. Predictive accuracy may have been limited by the sample size. While our study is generally comparable to others and we ran cross-validation procedures, more data points are preferred for improved model accuracy and generalizability [59]. Relatedly, the sample itself was collected through MTurk and included a largely Catholic, White, English-speaking population. Race was not found to be predictive in our lasso regression; however, Williams and co-authors determined racial discrimination to be associated with both obsessions and compulsions [20]. Their work indicates that everyday lived experiences should also be examined in greater detail. It is possible that while race as a characteristic might not be predictive of OC severity, experiences connected to race, including consequences of bias and discrimination, could be predictive. For future studies, the demographics that make up a person cannot be left out of the complex story surrounding obsessive-compulsive symptomology, and neither should the experiences that stem from those characteristics.

Another limitation of our study was our age cutoff of 45-years-old. Although we were guided by the literature regarding an age cut-off in this study, machine and deep learning techniques allow for a deep exploration of demographic variables and their relationship to symptomatology [81], [82], [83]. As such, there may be value in including a wider age group in future work. An additional limitation is that gender identity was not considered as a variable in our data collection or analysis. Gender identity is distinct from biological sex assigned at birth, though they often interact with each other throughout a person’s life [84]. Nevertheless, research in this area often conflates constructs involving gender and sex, highlighting the need for future studies to include both variables [17]. Thus, it is strongly recommended that future studies include gender identity and biological sex in predictive studies of obsessive-compulsive symptoms. Finally, these data were collected in April 2020 during the beginning of the COVID-19 pandemic. Because OCD symptoms were exacerbated in clinical and non-clinical populations during this time, our sample likely included higher than average levels of OC symptoms, depression, and comorbid conditions [85]. Future research should endeavor to externally validate the model with new, post-pandemic data to verify our findings.
