Demographic, socioconomic, and health correlates of sleep quality and sleep duration among community-dwelling older adults in India
Authors: T. Muhammad, Manacy Pai, Khushboo Ahire, Madhurima Sharma
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRecent research has revealed that today’s older adults report more sleep problems than their predecessors, a trend compounded by expanding social stratification. As such, this study examined the demographic, socioeconomic, and health correlates of sleep quality and sleep duration among community-dwelling older adults in India.MethodsThe current study sample draws on data from 7118 respondents aged 50 years and over participating in the World Health Organization’s Study on global AGEing and adult health (WHO-SAGE) wave-2 dataset. Sleep quality (good, moderate, and poor) and sleep duration (in hours and minutes) were self-reported. Adjusted multivariable logistic regression models were employed to examine the associations between sleep quality and sleep duration and several demographic, socioeconomic, and health indicators.ResultsA total of 12.84% and 36.1% of older adults reported long (> 8 h) and short (< 7 h) sleep, respectively. Older adults with primary education had lower odds of poor sleep [aOR: 0.85, CI: 0.73–0.99] than peers with no formal education. The odds of poor sleep were lower among those in higher wealth quintiles than those in the poorest quintile. Older adults with higher education had higher odds of short sleep [aOR: 1.36, CI: 1.06–1.74], and those with primary education had lower odds of long sleep [aOR: 0.70, CI: 0.54–0.91] than those without formal education (base category: age-appropriate sleep, i.e., 7–8 h). Older adults who were widowed had lower odds of both short [aOR: 0.82, CI: 0.68–0.98] and long sleep [aOR:0.74, CI: 0.58–0.95] compared to those who were currently married. Older individuals with adequate nutritional intake reported lower odds of short [aOR:0.59, CI: 0.49–0.72] and higher odds of long sleep [aOR:1.52, CI: 1.20–1.93] relative to their counterparts. Older adults who reported chronic conditions and body pain had higher odds of poor sleep and short sleep than their counterparts.ConclusionsWe identified significant associations between several unmodifiable factors, including age, education, and marital status, and modifiable factors such as dietary intake, body pain, and pre-existing chronic ailments, and sleep quality and sleep duration. Our findings can assist health care providers and practitioners in developing a more holistic and empathic approach to care. Moreover, that several demographic, socioeconomic, and health-related factors are consequential for older adults’ sleep health suggests that early detection through screening programs and community-based interventions is vital to improving sleep among older Indians who are most susceptible to sleep problems.

Background

The proportion of older Indians reached approximately 140 million in 2021, projected to rise to 319 million by 2050, accounting for nearly 20% of the overall population [1, 2]. As this segment of the population grows in India, so will the healthcare challenges, chronic conditions and the prevalence of sleep problems [3–6].

Sufficient good-quality sleep is crucial to human health, happiness, and healthy aging [7, 8]. Short and long sleep duration, typically defined as < 6 or > 9 h of sleep in 24 h, respectively [9], and sleep quality, which typically refers to the subjective and objective characteristics of sleep that determine its overall potency are associated with multiple outcomes of physical, mental, and cognitive health [10, 11]. Unfortunately, epidemiological research has found that sleep troubles are typical among older adults [4, 12]. Over a third of older adults sleep for ≤ 5 or ≥ 10 h, while almost half struggle to fall asleep, stay asleep, or are sleepy during the day [13, 14]. Insufficient and excessive sleep and poor sleep quality are associated with numerous cardiometabolic conditions, including obesity, diabetes, high blood pressure, stroke, and coronary heart disease [10, 15]. They are also linked to increased risks of immunity and hormone dysregulation [16, 17], falls [18], disability [19], depression [20], and cognitive disorders, including memory loss [21], cognitive impairment, and dementia [22, 23].

Relative to their male peers, women may be more susceptible to the adverse effect of aging on sleep. Older women also are more prone to reporting sleep issues [24, 25], and this is especially so among older women who live alone [26]. Marital status is also linked with sleep disturbances [27]. Less-educated older adults are likelier to experience poor sleep quality and shorter sleep duration [28, 29]. Given that higher socioeconomic status (SES) often entails greater income, stable employment, occupational prestige, access to health care, and better nutrition and leisure opportunities, it is associated with sufficient sleep and higher quality of it [30, 31]. Compared to older adults in urban settings, rural older dwellers are more prone to sleep disturbances. Aside from demographic traits, higher body mass index (BMI) and obesity also are linked with inconsistent sleep and poor sleep quality [32, 33] and short sleep duration [34], supporting the idea that chronic sleep deprivation plays a role in the pathophysiology of obesity [35]. Reduced physical activity, which often contributes to weight gain, also has been associated with poor sleep duration [36]. Similarly, studies have shown an association between nutritional deficiencies and moderate to severe insomnia [37]. Nearly one-third of the world’s aging population is at risk of malnutrition, which can occur for various causes, such as old age, apathy, comorbidities, mental distress, decreased appetite, and nutrient intake [37, 38].

Studies on sociodemographic characteristics, behavioral health factors, and sleep quality and sleep duration among older adults are relatively rare in India. Those that exist are limited to smaller samples of older Indians in one or two small regions of the country. As such, this study employed a large-scale sample of older adults from six major states in India, representing different regions of the country to examine [1] the prevalence of sleep quality and duration among older adults; and [2] the association between demographic, socioeconomic, and health-related characteristics of older Indians and their sleep health (Fig. 1). Given that recent research shows today’s older adults report more sleep problems than previous generations, a trend exacerbated by growing social stratification, this study is particularly crucial in a developing country like India [39].

Methods

Data

Data come from the World Health Organization’s Study on global AGEing and adult health (WHO-SAGE), India wave-2, which was conducted in 2015 in six selected states of India, representing different country regions: Assam (Northeast), Karnataka (South), Maharashtra (West), Rajasthan (North), Uttar Pradesh (Central) and West Bengal (East), covering a broadly illustrative aggregate sample of 9,116 respondents aged 18 years and above (1,998 age 18–49 years and 7,118 age 50 and above). This study considered 7,118 older adults aged 50 years and above.

SAGE wave-2 India was a follow-up study of SAGE wave-1 and covered the same states with the same primary sampling units (PSU) and the sample households which were covered in the WHO-World Health Survey (WHS), 2003. From all the states in India, a systematic random sample selection procedure was followed to select the states in WHS. Two-stage sampling in rural areas was used where the villages were the PSUs and households as secondary stage unit (SSU). Three-stage sampling in urban areas with the selection of wards, census enumeration blocks, and households in a specific order was followed. The number of households selected was in proportion to the respective state population and was distributed in urban and rural population. More detailed Information about weights and survey design is available at https://apps.who.int/healthinfo/systems/surveydata/index.php/catalog/117.

Measurements

Outcome

Outcome variables in this study were sleep quality and sleep duration. Sleep quality was assessed using the question, “Please rate the quality of your sleep last night. Was it very good, good, moderate, poor or very poor?” These five categories were consolidated into three categories good (very good or good), moderate and poor (poor or very poor). Sleep duration was assessed using the question, “How many hours did you sleep last night?” The responses in the format of minutes were converted into hours and the duration was classified as per the total number of hours the respondent slept in the last night. Less than 7 h was classified as short duration sleep, 7–8 h was considered as age-appropriate sleep and 9 and more hours was considered as long duration sleep in this study.

Covariates

Health-related factors included nutritional intake, assessed by the total number of fruit and vegetable servings per day (recoded as no and yes for 5 or more times a day), physical activity (vigorous, moderate, light and no activity) and body mass index (measured based on height and weight, and classified as per WHO criteria; less than 18.5 kg/m2 as underweight, 18.5–24.9 kg/m2 as normal, 25.0–29.9 kg/m2 as overweight, and ≥ 30.0 kg/m2 as obese).

Self-rated health was assessed using a single overall self-rated general health question used in SAGE: “In general, how would you rate your health today?” with a five-point response scale from very good to very bad. Respondents who rated their health as bad or very bad were classified into having a poor self-rated health in this study. Chronic conditions in this study included having been diagnosed with any of the following conditions; hypertension, diabetes, stroke, arthritis, angina, asthma and chronic lung disease. Respondents were asked, “Have you ever been diagnosed with the condition?” for each health condition. Body pain was assessed using the question “Overall in the last 30 days, how much of bodily aches or pains did you have?” The responses were none, mild, moderate, severe, extreme/ cannot do. The categories of severe and extreme were combined due to the small sample in the extreme category. Fall-related injury was assessed using the questions, “In the last 12 months, have you had any other event where you suffered from bodily injury?” and “What was the cause of this injury?” Those who reported injury due to a fall were classified as yes and otherwise no.

Other explanatory variables in this study included age (grouped as 50–59, 60–69, 70–79, 80 + years), sex (male and female), educational level (no formal education, less than primary, primary, secondary and higher), current marital status (married, widowed and others which include never married/ separated/ divorced) and household wealth index. The household wealth index, available in the dataset, was computed based on a detailed list of items of durable goods, housing characteristics such as type of floors, walls and cooking stove, and household amenities such as access to improved water, sanitation and cooking fuel. A principal component analysis was used to create the wealth index with quintiles [40]. Lowest represents the quintile with the poorest households and highest represents the quintile with the richest households. Religious groups included Hindus, Muslims and others which include Christianity, Budhism, Jainism, Judaism, Sikhism, No/none and others. Social groups included scheduled castes and scheduled tribes (both are socioeconomically most disadvantaged), other backward classes and others which primarily included those in the upper castes. Place of residence was classified as urban and rural and states included Assam, Karnataka, Maharashtra, Rajasthan, Uttar Pradesh and West Bengal.

Statistical analysis

We report the descriptive statistics of the study sample. Further, we examine the bivariate associations among the explanatory variables and sleep outcomes using cross-tabulations and Chi-Square test of statistical significance. We employed adjusted ordered logistic regression to examine the factors associated with sleep quality (categories ordered as good, moderate, and poor), and adjusted multinomial logistic regression to assess factors associated with short and long sleep duration with the base category as age-appropriate sleep duration.

Survey weights were applied to account for the complex survey design and to provide the estimates at population level. Regression diagnostics were carried out and found no violation of basis assumptions of regression. We report the results in the form of weighted percentages, p-values and adjusted odd ratio (aOR) with 95% confidence interval (CI). All the analyses were carried out in Stata version 16 [41].

Results

Sample characteristics

Table 1 presents the sociodemographic and behavioral health profile of older adults in this study. About 5% of the participants were aged 80 years and above and 52.39% were women. More than 40% of the participants had no formal education with only 15.31% having secured higher education. A proportion of 23.48% older adults were widowed. More than half of older adults identified as to Hindus and about 6.32% of the participants belonged to Scheduled Caste category. Only 19.65% of older adults reported consuming five or more servings of fruits and vegetables per day. A proportion of 44.69% older adults were physically inactive and 14.02% were overweight. Furthermore, 18.11% older adults had poor self-rated health; 4.41%, 5% and 11.75% had three or more number of chronic health conditions, fall-related injury, and severe body pain issues, respectively. 71.52% of the sample lived in rural areas of the country.

Prevalence of sleep quality and duration among older adults

Table 2 presents the prevalence of good, moderate, and poor-quality sleep among older adults by their background characteristics. Prevalence of good, moderate, and poor-quality sleep is estimated 70.5%, 25.3% and 4.2%, respectively. Overall, significant differences were observed in the proportions of older adults with varying sleep quality across socio-demographic groups, including age, sex, education, marital status, wealth quintile, religion, place of residence and states. Moreover, significant differences were found across health-related covariates such as fruit and vegetable intake, body mass index, physical activty, self-rated health, chronic conditions, body pain, and fall-related injuries.

Multivariable ordered logistic regression estimates of sleep quality by background variables among older adults

The odds of having poor sleep was significantly lower among older persons with sufficient intake of fruits and vegetables [aOR:0.78, CI:0.67–0.92] compared to the odds among those without such nutrient conusmption. Body mass index was not associated with sleep quality. Those who engaged in light physical activity had lower odds of poor sleep [aOR:0.77, CI:0.62–0.96] compared to those who engaged in vigorous physical activity. Older individuals who rated their health status as poor had 2.02 times higher odds of poor sleep [aOR: 2.02, CI: 1.68–2.44] compared to their counterparts. Older adults who reported single chronic condition had 1.23 times higher odds of poor sleep [aOR:1.23, CI: 1.06–1.43] relative to those without any chronic disease. Similarly, those who reported mild [aOR:1.43, CI:1.21–1.69], moderate [aOR:2.34, CI:1.93–2.83], or severe/extreme body pain [aOR:2.68, CI:2.09–3.42] had higher odds of poor sleep relative to those without body pain.

Multinomial logistic regression analysis of sleep duration among older adults

Older adults who reported single chronic condition [aOR:1.21, CI: 1.03–1.42] or two chronic conditions [aOR:1.34, CI: 1.07–1.68] had higher odds of short sleep relative to those without any chronic disease. Older adults who reported severe to extreme body pain had higher odds of short sleep [aOR: 1.74, CI: 1.34–2.27] compared to their counterparts with no body pain.

Multinomial logistic regression estimates of short and long sleep duration versus age-appropriate sleep duration by background variables among older adults

Older adults who had upto primary level education had lower odds of long sleep [aOR: 0.70, CI: 0.54–0.91] compared to those without formal education (base category: age-appropriate sleep, i.e., 7–8 h). Older adults who were widowed had lower odds of long sleep [aOR:0.74, CI: 0.58–0.95] in comparison to those who were currently married. Compared to Hindus, older adults identifying as “Others” had higher odds of reporting short sleep [aOR: 1.75, CI: 1.21–2.54]. Besides, older individuals with adequate fruit and vegetable intake reported higher odds of long sleep [aOR:1.52, CI: 1.20–1.93] compared to their counterparts without sufficient servings of fruits and vegetables. Notably, body mass index and physical activity were not associated with sleep duration. Finally, older adults who reported mild [aOR:1.30, CI:1.01–1.68], moderate [aOR:1.99, CI:1.48–2.68], or severe/extreme [aOR:2.68, CI:1.83–3.92] body pain had higher odds of long sleep compared to their counterparts with no body pain.

Discussion

This study examined the associations between demographic, socioeconomic, and health-related factors and sleep quality and sleep duration in community-dwelling older adults in India. Confirming prior reports [12, 42], we found reduced sleep quality in older adults as their age advanced. The proportion of older adults reporting good sleep quality reduced in higher age groups, while those experiencing moderate to poor sleep quality increased. This finding aligns with previous research suggesting that sleep quality declines with age due to a combination of factors, including increased prevalence of medical conditions, medication side effects, and life style changes [43–45].

We observed that older men reported better sleep quality than older women, which aligns with Yang et al. (2022), who found that women reported more sleep problems than men [46]. Estrogen deficiency, heightened sensitivity to stressors (e.g., the passing of family and friends, caregiving), fewer social and economic resources, reduced leisure-related physical activity, and poorer self-rated health may all contribute to increased sleep disturbances in older women, particularly in India [47–52]. We found a significant association between marital status and sleep quality. Widowed older adults report poorer sleep quality than their married counterparts, possibly due to the loss of a confidant and caretaker [53]. Interestingly, relative to their married peers, sleep quality was noticeably higher among never-married, divorced or separated older Indians. Future studies may explore the association between marital transitions, marital histories, and sleep quality. While marriages generally provide material resources and social support, poor-quality marriages may deplete resources and create conflict, adversely affecting sleep [54].

With respect to sleep duration, 12.84% reported long sleep duration (> 8 h), and 36.1% reported short sleep duration (< 7 h). Notably, age-appropriate sleep duration (7–8 h) was observed in approximately one-tenth to two-fifths of older adults as they aged. This pattern suggests that while sleep duration may change with age, a significant proportion of older adults maintain an age-appropriate sleep duration. This finding departs from some previous studies that have reported a general trend of reduced sleep duration with advancing age [55, 56]. The maintenance of age-appropriate sleep duration among a substantial proportion of older adults in this study may be attributed to cultural factors, social support systems, or adaptive behaviors [57–59] that help mitigate age-related changes in sleep duration.

Interestingly, while older men reported better sleep quality than older women, a higher proportion of men reported shorter sleep durations than women. A direction for future inquiry would be to assess gender differences in the interactive effect of sleep duration and quality on the health of older Indians. One recent study [60], for instance, found that individuals with lengthy sleep duration who reported good sleep quality had better self-rated health, indicating that good sleep quality may shield long sleepers from bad health. In terms of health factors, we found that short sleep duration was more prevalent among individuals with poor self-rated health, chronic conditions, and severe body pain. These associations are well-documented in the literature, emphasizing the bidirectional relationship between poor health and inadequate sleep [61, 62].

Demographic, socioeconomic, and health factors associated with sleep quality

Consistent with prior research [63, 64], our multivariable analysis indicates that older adults with a primary level of education had significantly lower odds of poor sleep quality than those with no formal education. Lower education levels are often associated with physically strenuous occupations, poor work conditions, financial inadequacies, lack of quality health care, and mental distress [65–68], all of which all can compromise sleep quality. Additionally, lower literacy, associated with lower health knowledge, correlates with harmful health behaviors [68–71], which could adversely impact sleep quality. SES was significantly determinant related to sleep quality, with those in higher wealth quintiles exhibiting lower odds of poor sleep quality.

Higher SES older adults are better situated to avoid harmful health behaviors and leverage their social, economic, and personal clout to protect themselves from everyday stressors, thereby positively affecting sleep quality [72]. This SES-related finding matches those observed in prior studies like Patel et al. (2010), who found poverty linked with poor sleep quality [73] and Xue et al., (2022) who discussed how social and environmental circumstances associated with lower SES, like social exclusion, overcrowded living conditions, noise, and air pollution can directly affect sleep quality [74]. We also found that a greater proportion of Muslim older adults reported poor to moderate sleep quality than their Hindu peers. This discrepancy may be due to social and economic disparities, which can lead to increased everyday stress and compromised quality of sleep [73, 75, 76]. Experiences of discrimination or social stigma and marginalization could also contribute to poorer sleep quality among Muslim older adults, as chronic stress from discrimination can negatively affect mental health [77] and sleep [78].

Health-related factors such as poor self-rated health, chronic conditions, and severe body pain were strongly associated with poor sleep quality. Older adults who rated their health as poor were more than twice as likely to report poor sleep quality, supported by studies demonstrating the negative impact of poor health on sleep [44, 61]. Additionally, the odds of poor sleep quality were lower among older adults with sufficient fruit and vegetable intake. This suggests that a diet rich in fruits and vegetables, sleep-promoting nutrients and antioxidants, may contribute to better sleep quality, as highlighted in previous research [79]. This is important considering the high rates of food deprivation among older Indians due to social and economic disadvantages, physical morbidity, functional dependence, social isolation, and unreliable transportation [80, 81].

Interestingly, older Indians engaged in light physical activity reported significantly greater sleep quality was than those involved in moderate, vigorous, or no physical activity. A recent study observed that habitual physical activity levels, rather than fitness, were associated with sleep quality in older adults [82]. Moreover, several studies have found that low-intensity physical activity not only improves balance, lower limb strength, functional abilities, while reducing fall risk and frequency [83–86], but is also associated with increased satisfaction and active engagement [87]. The benefits of low-intensity physical activity may translate into less physical stress and better sleep quality.

Demographic, socioeconomic, and health factors associated with sleep duration

Higher educational attainment was associated with higher odds of short sleep duration. This diverges from some previous studies that reported better sleep outcomes among more educated individuals, potentially due to differences in study populations and methodologies [62, 88]. Given that education is linked with greater social, economic, and health resources [66, 67, 89], older adults without formal education may face more everyday stressors due to physically and psychologically harsher conditions, necessitating longer sleep as a coping mechanism for stress and fatigue. Thus, extended sleep hours may be the body’s way of coping with and compensating for higher stress levels. Future studies on sleep health among older Indians should explore the nexus between education and sleep duration over time. Examining whether sleep duration patterns differ by education level or if educational disparities in sleep duration vary with age would provide further insight. While this question has been explored in American middle-aged and older adults [90], such research is lacking in the Indian context.

Older Indians who were not married had lower odds of both short and long sleep duration compared to their married counterparts. This finding contrasts with some studies that have found marriage to be protective of sleep health, suggesting that the social support provided by a spouse can improve sleep quality and duration [91]. The discrepancy may be due to cultural differences in the role of marriage and social support in India. Comparable to the sleep quality disadvantage among older Muslims, we find that relative to those belonging to Hindu or other religious groups, older adults belonging to Muslim religious group had higher odds of short sleep duration. Future research should examine potential mechanisms connecting religious affiliation and sleep quality versus sleep duration. For instance, the higher odds of shorter sleep duration among Muslims compared to Hindus may be explained by daily regimen and prayer times in Islam, particularly early morning prayers. The combination of religious practices and sociocultural factors can lead to habitual shorter sleep durations [92–94]. These factors may not necessarily explain poor sleep quality among older Muslim Indians.

Adequate fruit and vegetable intake was associated with lower odds of short sleep duration and higher odds of long sleep duration. Given that insufficient sleep and poor sleep quality both can exacerbate psychological distress, potentially leading to the consumption of more health harmful and fewer health-promoting foods, future research replicating our study should consider the possibility of a bidirectional association between fruits and vegetable intake and sleep health [95, 96]. Health status also played a critical role in sleep duration. Older adults with poor self-rated health, chronic conditions, and severe body pain had higher odds of short sleep duration. These associations are well-supported by existing literature, emphasizing the detrimental effects of poor health on sleep [61, 62, 97].

Strength and limitations

Our study uniquely examines both the quality and quantity of sleep along with their correlates, and includes a large sample of older Indians, significantly contributing to the relatively underexplored sleep literature in India. The study’s strengths include the use of a representative sample, comprehensive analysis employing both ordinal and multinomial logistic regression, and a detailed examination of a wide range of sociodemographic, socioeconomic, and health-related factors. Additionally, by focusing on older Indians, our study provides a springboard for future studies to consider social, cultural, and religious influences on sleep patterns.

That said, our findings must be construed within the context of several important limitations. First, the cross-sectional nature of our study makes it impossible to form any causal or sequencing related claims between variables of interest. Future work replicating this study should consider the possibility of reverse causation between sleep and health and sleep and SES. Second, our data did not contain information on sleep apnea, insomnia, narcolepsy, or any such specific sleep disorders. Third, information on medication usage for sleep problems, including sleeping pills or non-pharmacologics, also was also unavailable. Fourth, given that insomnia-related symptoms tend to recur, the unavailability of data on sleep hassles endured during earlier life stages, including childhood, presents an important limitation. Fifth, sleep quality and quantity were based on subjective ratings rather than objective measures, like actigraphy. Sixth, the appropriateness of Western-based definition of sleep duration in the Indian population must be examined further. Lastly, it is worth pointing out that the data we use are a decade old. The associations between demographic, socioeconomic, and health-related factors and sleep quality and duration, however, are likely stable over time, rooted in the enduring sociodemographic patterns and persistent social inequities [98]. Our study, as such, provides baseline insights into sleep patterns of older Indians, essential for understanding current states and future trends, and the upcoming second wave of SAGE data will allow future research to replicate our work and evaluate changes.

Conclusions

That several demographic, socioeconomic, and health-related factors are consequential for older adults’ sleep health suggests that early detection through screening programs and community-based interventions is vital to improving sleep among older Indians susceptible to sleep problems. Understanding the relevance of unmodifiable factors such as age, education, marital status, and pre-existing chronic ailments can guide health care providers and practitioners develop a more inclusive and empathic approach to care. In-service training can be used to educate practitioners on the importance of recognizing these influences when assessing sleep health, enabling them to customize care plans to address the specific needs of their older patients. This includes giving specific advice on sleep hygiene, suggesting coping strategies for stress from life changes, and offering guidance on managing chronic conditions to improve sleep quality. Moreover, while social-structural problems, such as socioeconomic deprivation are challenging to address, sleep disturbances related to later-life transitions, such as widowhood can be mitigated through social support interventions, and those arising from injuries and chronic pain can be managed through pain management. Additionally, promoting modifiable factors like healthy eating, physical activity, and maintaining a healthy BMI, though not directly linked to sleep quality in our study, remains beneficial for overall health and well-being.
