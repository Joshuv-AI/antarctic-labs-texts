Burnout, working alliance, and ward atmosphere: A multisite study of mental health professionals and patients with schizophrenia
Authors: Miriam D’Addazio, Laura Poddighe, Renzo Bianchi, Stefano Calza, Martina Carnevale, Fabrizio Starace, Manuel Zamparini, Giovanni de Girolamo, the DiAPASon Consortium
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study examined the association between burnout, psychological distress, ward atmosphere, and working alliance (WA) among mental health workers treating patients with schizophrenia spectrum disorder (SSD). Data were collected from 345 patients with SSD and 151 mental health workers across 98 residential facilities (RFs) as part of the DiAPAson project from October 2020 to October 2021. Participants were excluded from the study if they exhibited patient‐operator matching errors, dropped out, or had many missing Maslach Burnout Inventory (MBI) scores. The final sample included 282 patients and 155 healthcare workers. Burnout was assessed using the MBI. Psychological distress was evaluated with the 12‐item version of the General Health Questionnaire. WA and ward atmosphere were evaluated with the WA Inventory and the Ward Atmosphere Scale, respectively, in both staff and patients. Sociodemographic and clinical data was also collected and analysed. Burnout was associated with a less supportive ward atmosphere, a weaker WA, and higher staff distress. Severe psychiatric symptoms evaluated with Brief Psychiatric Rating Scale in patients were also linked to staff burnout levels. Discrepancies in the perceptions of the ward atmosphere and the WA were observed between staff and patients, with patients reporting better perceptions in both domains. Our findings highlight the complex dynamics of well‐being within psychiatric care settings, emphasizing the importance of role clarity, professional autonomy, and a positive ward atmosphere in mitigating burnout. Interventions focused on such factors may help support mental health professionals involved in SSD patient care. INTRODUCTION

Recent years have witnessed a growing interest towards burnout among healthcare professionals working in different health settings (Elit et al., 2004; Ripamonti et al., 2007). The concept of burnout was initially introduced by the American psychologist Freudenberg (1974) and later defined as a psychological syndrome by Maslach and Jackson (1981). According to Maslach and Jackson (1981), burnout encompasses three dimensions: Emotional Exhaustion (EE), reflecting feelings of emotional depletion and physical fatigue; Depersonalization (DP), characterized by detachment and indifference towards work (negative attitudes towards patients); and reduced Personal Accomplishment (PA), reflecting a negative self‐evaluation of one's competence and professional contribution.

Mental health professionals commonly report experiencing EE, DP, and reduced PA (O'Connor et al., 2018). Age is also correlated to burnout, with increasing age associated to a higher risk of depersonalization, but also to a greater sense of PA.

Factors contributing to burnout encompass a wide range of physical, psychological, and behavioural attributes of healthcare workers, including depression, anxiety, sleep disturbances, and maladaptive coping strategies, alongside characteristics such as ambition and competitiveness (Arif, et al., 2021; Peterson, et al., 2008; Ripamonti & Clerici, 2018). Job‐related aspects such as tenure, workload, job satisfaction, and the nature of tasks have also been linked to burnout and heightened psychological distress (Dugani, et al., 2018; Ripamonti & Clerici, 2018).

Patient‐related factors, including the severity of clinical conditions and perceived aggressiveness, have been identified as exacerbating professionals' vulnerability to burnout (Bressi, et al., 2009; Fothergill et al., 2004). Specifically, nurses dealing with patients with severe mental disorders experience higher stress levels compared to those working with less severe cases (Edwards et al., 2001). Both positive and negative symptoms of Schizophrenia Spectrum Disorder (SSD) have been associated with burnout among mental health workers (Kokurcan et al., 2015).

Moreover, organizational factors, including the quality of healthcare management and interpersonal relationships among staff, significantly impact the psychological well‐being of caregivers. The concept of ‘ward atmosphere’ encompasses the dynamics within clinical environments, impacting both patients and staff. A study by Caldwell et al. (2006) identified a negative correlation between EE and the received support, autonomy, and organization within hospital wards. Work‐related factors, particularly workload and workplace relationships, were identified as significant determinants of burnout, as shown in a recent review conducted by Soares et al. (2023). Conversely, role clarity, professional autonomy, fair treatment, and access to clinical supervision were found to be protective factors against burnout. Finally, staff in ‘ordinary’ community mental health teams might be more susceptible to burnout compared to those in specialised teams, like assertive outreach or crisis intervention teams.

This backdrop of research underscores the intricate relationship between psychological distress, patient symptom severity, and ward atmosphere with burnout levels, potentially affecting treatment outcomes, including the Working Alliance (WA) (Busetto et al., 2019). WA refers to the collaborative and relational bond between service users and providers, crucial for successful treatment outcomes (Bordin, 1979). Despite its significance, the link between professional burnout and WA in the treatment of severe mental disorders remains underexplored.

Several studies have highlighted differences in how staff and psychiatric patients perceive WA, assessed using the WA Inventory (WAI), and ward atmosphere, evaluated through the Ward Atmosphere Scale (WAS). Regarding the WA, a notable discrepancy exists between how patients and staff view caring behaviours and their significance, with patients often valuing certain aspects of care more than staff (von Essen & Sjodeèn, 1995). Recent research by Fusar‐Poli et al. (2024) indicates that in Residential Facilities (RFs), patients' perceptions of care are influenced by factors like education, severity of psychopathology, and psychosocial functioning. Prince (2007) found that patients with schizophrenia and a longer history of hospitalisation tend to have greater illness awareness, but a weaker WA with staff. Generally, patients report slightly higher levels of WA compared to staff, suggesting a more positive perception of the therapeutic relationship (Krohner, et al., 2023; Murphy, et al., 2018).

Differences also emerge in how staff and patients with SSD perceive the ward atmosphere, with varying views on aspects like involvement, support, autonomy, staff control, anger and aggression, order, and organization. Staff often rate the ward atmosphere more positively on certain subscales than patients, who are particularly affected by issues related to autonomy and staff control (Main et al., 1991; Rossberg & Friis, 2004; Schjødt et al., 2003; Soares, et al., 2023). The ward atmosphere also varies depending on the setting: patients in RFs generally have a more favourable view compared to those staying in standard hospital wards (Burti, Andreone, & Mazzi, 2004; Nicholls et al., 2015; Osborn, et al., 2010).

Our study aimed to explore the associations between burnout, ward atmosphere, staff well‐being, and the severity of patients' clinical state. We notably examined how these factors, along with burnout, affect the WA and investigated the differences in perception of the ward atmosphere and WA between staff and patients. To our knowledge, this is the first study that inquiries into the correlations between these different domains among both professionals and inpatients with SSD. Although burnout, ward atmosphere, and patient clinical severity have been extensively studied individually, their interactions in patients with severe mental disorders are not well understood. This study explores these interrelationships and examines how these factors, along with burnout, influence the therapeutic environment and ward atmosphere from both staff and patient perspectives.

METHOD

The data for this study was collected as part of the DiAPAson study (de Girolamo, et al., 2020). From October 2020 to October 2021, 345 patients with SSD were recruited from 98 different RFs as part of the DiAPAson project; 168 healthcare workers caring for these patients were asked to collaborate, including psychiatrists, psychologists, vocational therapists and registered nurses or healthcare assistants. Patients had to meet specific inclusion criteria, including a DSM‐5 diagnosis of SSD (APA, 2013), an age range of 18–60 years old, proficiency in Italian, and current treatment in a RF. Patients who could not provide informed consent, who displayed severe cognitive deficits (i.e., a Mini‐Mental State Examination corrected score lower than 24), who had received a recent diagnosis (within the last 6 months) of substance use disorder according to DSM‐5 criteria (APA, 2013), a history of clinically significant head injury, of cerebrovascular/neurological disease were excluded from the study. Additionally, staff exhibiting patient‐operator matching errors, patients who dropped out, or staff with many missing scores of the Maslach Burnout Inventory (MBI) were excluded from the current analysis. The finale sample comprised 282 patients and 155 healthcare workers (see Figure S1 in Supplementary Material). In the participating RFs, the facility chiefs compiled an alphabetical list of patients who were present on a designated index day. Using this list, residential patients were consecutively invited to participate in the study until the recruitment target was met. Detailed information about the study was provided to the participants, who had the opportunity to ask questions. Assessment tools included structured interviews and both interviewer‐administered and self‐report questionnaires, targeting both patients and healthcare workers. Research Assistants assisted the patients in completing self‐reported questionnaires. The overall assessment, along with demographic data, included measures of psychiatric symptoms (with a particular focus on negative symptoms), psychosocial functioning, and time perception (de Girolamo, et al., 2020). All instruments used were validated and tested for reliability in Italian. All participants provided informed consent before participating in the study. The study received approval from the Local Ethical Committees (see specific section below).

Sociodemographic and clinical variables

Sociodemographic data for both staff and patients (e.g. age, gender, years of school, marital, and working status) and clinical data regarding patients' condition (e.g., length of illness, overall duration of hospitalizations, total number of hospitalizations in the last year, length of time spent in the RF), were obtained from the clinical records.

Assessment of the severity of symptomatology

The presence and severity of psychopathology were assessed using the 24‐item Brief Psychiatric Rating Scale (BPRS) (Morosini & Casacchia, 1995; Overall & Gorham, 1962). The BPRS, consisting of 24 items, was rated on a 7‐point scale ranging from 1 (not present) to 7 (extremely severe). The Brief Negative Symptom Scale (BNSS) (Morosini & Casacchia, 1995; Strauss, et al., 2012), a 13‐item instrument, was used to assess the severity of negative symptoms, including blunted affect, alogia, asociality, anhedonia, and avolition. BNSS scores range from 0 (not present) to 6 (severe deficit). Higher total scores on both scales indicate greater symptom severity.

Assessment of burnout

The MBI was employed to assess burnout symptoms among healthcare workers, rating its three subscales: EE, DP, and PA (Maslach & Jackson, 1981). The MBI consists of 22 items, each rated on a 0–6 frequency scale. Burnout was treated as a continuous variable by summing scores across the three subscales. For descriptive purposes, MBI scores were categorised into high, medium, or low levels based on the tertiles of their distribution.

Assessment of working alliance

WA was assessed using the WA Inventory (WAI) (Horvath & Greenberg, 1989). Specifically, each patient completed the WAI short form (Italian version) for patients (WAI‐P) (Lingiardi, 2002) and directed the WAI ratings towards a specific staff member with whom they had a ‘one‐to‐one’ therapeutic relationship. Similarly, each staff member completed the WAI short form (Italian version) for staff (WAI‐T) (Lingiardi, 2002) for up to three different patients under their care. To mitigate potential ‘social desirability bias’, both treating staff and patients were informed that their responses would remain confidential and only accessible to researchers. For the current analyses, the WAI total ratings were used, with items rated on a Likert scale ranging from 0 (‘never’) to 7 (‘always’). The total score on the WAI can range between 12 and 84, with higher scores indicating a stronger WA.

To address the potential issue of ‘staff/patient specificity’, where staff members completed multiple versions of the WAI‐T corresponding to different patients without differentiating between them, a quality check was implemented. This check involved excluding WAI‐T forms from three staff members who consistently assigned identical scores to every item across different patients.

Assessment of ward atmosphere

The ‘Ward Atmosphere Scale’ (WAS) (Moos & Houts, 1968) was used to assess the ward atmosphere perceived by patients (WAS‐P) and healthcare staff (WAS‐T). This tool comprises 100 dichotomous (Yes/No) statements concerning various aspects of the ward. The higher‐order dimensions of the WAS are relational, personal growth, and system maintenance, which are associated with 10 corresponding subscales. These subscales include involvement, support, spontaneity, autonomy, practical orientation, personal problems orientation, anger and aggression, order and organization, programme clarity, and staff control. Each item is scored as 0 or 1 (Y/N), representing its association with one of the three subscales. The subscale scores can range from 0 to 10, determined by summing the scores of the individual items within the subscale; their averages are then calculated (Burti et al., 1990). Widely utilised in clinical settings and programme evaluation, the WAS scale enables the comparison of patients' and staff ratings about the ward atmosphere (Moos & Houts, 1968). Higher WAS scores indicate a more favourable judgement of the ward atmosphere.

Regarding the inclusion criteria, participants with missing values to at least 6 out of 10 items for WAS and 7 out of 12 items for WAI were excluded from the analyses. Imputation was employed to address missing values for participants whose WAS and WAI scores were below 6 out of 10 and 7 out of 12, respectively. The imputation was performed using random forest algorithms to generate imputed values for missing data (Stekhoven & Bühlmann, 2011).

Assessment of staff psychological distress

Staff distress was assessed with the General Health Questionnaire (GHQ‐12), a self‐report questionnaire designed to measure general psychological distress. The GHQ‐12 consists of 12 items that evaluate symptoms commonly associated with anxiety, depression, sleep disturbances, and social dysfunction. Each item is rated on a 4‐point Likert scale, ranging from ‘much better than usual’ to ‘much worse than usual’. A cut‐off score of 2/3 of each item and a total score higher than 14 is typically considered indicative of psychological distress (Bellantuono et al., 1987). Originally developed by Goldberg and Hillier (1979) as a shortened version of the original 60‐item GHQ, the GHQ‐12 has been validated in various languages and cultures, including Italian (Bellantuono et al., 1987; Picardi et al., 2001). The GHQ‐12 has shown favourable psychometric properties, including high internal consistency, good test–retest reliability, and strong construct validity.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were described using counts and percentages, while quantitative variables were summarised using mean and standard deviations, as well as median and ranges. Independent samples t‐tests and Chi‐square tests were employed to compare sociodemographic variables between patients and staff. Given that each staff member is connected to a different number of patients, WAS and WAI scores were averaged within subject. MBI was modelled, after transformation on a logarithmic scale, using a linear model considering WAI, WAS, BPRS, BNSS, and GHQ‐12 as independent predictors. For the sake of the interpretability of coefficients estimates, the effects of WAI, WAS, BPRS, BNSS, and GHQ‐12 are expressed in a 10‐unit increase scale.

To investigate the relationship between the WAI and WAS evaluated by staff and all matched patients, linear mixed models were employed, accounting for patient/staff clustering (where a staff member and all his/her patients represent a cluster). Specifically, WAS was modelled adopting a single random effect accounting for staff member cluster, while WAI model included a within‐staff member random effect for one‐to‐one staff‐patient WAI scoring (where every staff member scored each associated patient and vice versa).

All analyses were performed using R (v 4.4.0; R Core Team, 2024). All tests assumed a two‐sided 5% significance level.

Table 2 presents the ratings for the WAS and WAI provided by both patients and staff, along with the GHQ‐12 ratings from staff, and the BPRS and BNSS values rated by the treating clinicians. Patients exhibited considerably higher ratings on the WAS (495.9, SD = 31.6) compared to staff (426.8, SD = 26.0), indicating that they experience the ward atmosphere more positively. The correlation coefficient between patients and staff's ratings was negative, albeit very small in size (−0.01691854, 95% CI: −0.177 to 0.144), suggesting that the perceptions of the ward atmosphere by patients and staff are largely independent of each other or that each group focuses on different aspects of the atmosphere. Meanwhile, the patients displayed a substantial level of psychopathology, as indicated by their average BPRS rating of 50.9 (SD = 16.2) and BNSS rating of 25.9 (SD = 16.7).

DISCUSSION

This study explored the connections between mental health professionals working with patients with SSD and various factors, including burnout, psychological distress, ward atmosphere, clinical characteristics of the patients, and WA. We hypothesised and confirmed that higher levels of staff burnout correlate significantly with increased psychological distress, a perception of an unsupportive ward atmosphere, and a weaker WA, though the latter association was not statistically significant. Additionally, managing patients with more severe psychopathology emerged as a significant factor influencing staff burnout levels, aligning with existing research (Ripamonti & Clerici, 2018; Soares, et al., 2023). These findings indicate that protective factors such as role clarity, professional autonomy, supportive ward atmospheres, and access to clinical supervision can mitigate burnout risks. Finally, the study found perceptual differences between staff and patients concerning ward atmosphere and WA, with patients generally perceiving a stronger WA and a more positive ward atmosphere.

Burnout and working alliance

A key finding of our study is the impact of burnout on WA. We observed that higher levels of burnout among staff correlate with a weaker WA as perceived by them, highlighting the influence of caregivers' well‐being on the therapeutic relationship. This aligns with existing research (Rodríguez‐Nogueira, et al., 2024), suggesting that lower levels of burnout are crucial for enhancing WA. Factors such as evidence‐based practices, increased self‐confidence, and reduced perceptions of depersonalisation are linked to lower burnout among staff (Rodríguez‐Nogueira, et al., 2024). Furthermore, according to the existing literature (Krohner, S., et al., 2023; Murphy, Laptook, A., & Bender, J., 2018), our results revealed a discrepancy in WA perceptions between staff and patients, with patients generally perceiving a stronger WA. Patients might view positive interactions as a significant indicator of support and collaboration, especially if they feel isolated or have experienced poor relationships in the past. This can lead them to rate WA more positively, as they might place a high value on any attention and care they receive. Patients' expectations about treatment can also affect how they perceive their interactions with clinicians. If their expectations are met or exceeded, they may rate WA highly. In contrast, staff, who might have a more objective or realistic view of the therapeutic process, could perceive these interactions differently. Moreover, patients and staff may focus on different aspects of their interactions when evaluating their relationship. Finally, treating staff are typically more aware of the complexities involved in treatment and might feel that an ideal WA has not been achieved if certain therapeutic goals are unmet. The relationship between burnout and WA is crucial, given that a robust WA is central to effective outcomes in mental health treatment. Several studies underscore the importance of a strong therapeutic relationship not only to improve patient outcomes, but also to enhance patient integration and reduce staff exhaustion (Kellett, et al., 2020; Marchi, et al., 2023).

Burnout and ward atmosphere

Our study contributes to the understanding of how ward atmosphere influences burnout among staff. We found a negative correlation between supportive, autonomous, and well‐organised ward environments and staff lower levels of burnout, corroborating previous findings (Caldwell et al., 2006). This suggests that the quality of the ward atmosphere plays a pivotal role in the psychological well‐being of mental health professionals. Again, we observed that patients were on average perceiving a better ward atmosphere than staff. Consistent with previous research (Burti, Andreone, & Mazzi, 2004; Nicholls et al., 2015; Osborn, et al., 2010), our findings indicate that patients in non‐hospital RFs characterised by a home‐like environment and close human contact, help, support, have a more favourable perception of the environment in which they live compared to patients treated in standard hospital wards. The national PROGRES study, involving 1370 patients across 15 Italian regions, showed that RFs represent a dual process: while some restrictive environmental characteristics may exist, there are concerted efforts to integrate patients into the local community in order to allow certain degrees of personal autonomy and freedom. This setting facilitates social interactions that foster meaningful interpersonal relationships, essential for effective rehabilitation (Picardi, de Girolamo, et al., 2006; Picardi, Rucci, et al., 2006; PROGRES Group, et al., 2005). The notably positive atmosphere in most Italian RFs (Picardi, de Girolamo, et al., 2006; Picardi, Rucci, et al., 2006) may account for our observations of patients perceiving the ward atmosphere more positively than staff, contrasting with findings from earlier studies.

Burnout and psychological distress

Our findings are consistent with the literature that highlights a clear association between levels of burnout and psychological distress among health workers. Specifically, we observed that those with higher levels of burnout exhibited higher mean scores on the GHQ‐12, indicative of greater psychological distress. This observation aligns with literature indicating that professionals experiencing higher levels of burnout report significantly higher GHQ scores compared to their counterparts with low burnout levels (Malakouti et al., 2011). Additionally, previous findings emphasise the critical role of staff's mental health status in predicting burnout (Jaber & Al‐Zoubi, 2012), further underlining the importance of considering psychological distress as a key factor in understanding and addressing burnout among mental health professionals.

Burnout and patients' clinical severity

Severe psychiatric symptoms, assessed using the BPRS, significantly impact staff burnout levels, unlike the negative symptoms typical of SSD, such as apathy, avolition, and alogia, assessed with the BNSS, which do not show a similar correlation. The heightened burnout among staff may stem from the intensive care demands of patients with severe symptoms, underscoring the need for increased support and resources for caregivers (Kokurcan et al., 2015). Previous studies also indicate that while negative symptoms do not correlate with higher burnout levels, since patients are detached, less demanding, and passively collaborative, positive symptoms often have a more pronounced impact as they may require more intensive clinical care and supervision and may trigger disturbed behaviour (Kate et al., 2013).

Implications for clinical practice

The study findings have significant implications for clinical practice within mental health services, emphasising the need for targeted interventions to address staff burnout and improve the overall work environment.

Firstly, our study underscores the critical need for interventions aimed at reducing staff burnout by addressing stress and workload. Implementing staff well‐being programmes, such as stress management workshops, mindfulness training, clinical supervision and providing access to mental health services for staff when needed, is essential to reduce staff stress levels. Recent evidence suggests that combining these interventions with organizational strategies—like establishing ‘well‐being’ centres, ensuring adequate staffing, clarifying roles, and fostering peer support networks—can significantly improve healthcare workers' morale, reduce job stress, and decrease turnover intentions. Additionally, both individual interventions (for instance, mindfulness practices and meditation) and organizational changes (like workload reduction) have been shown to effectively reduce burnout, perceived stress, anxiety, and depression among healthcare professionals (Blake et al., 2024; Cohen et al., 2023; Nielsen et al., 2017). It is recommended for mental health professionals to adopt self‐care practices into their daily routine to manage stress and prevent burnout, such as encouraging appropriate use of ICT (Khanchel‐Lakhoua & Kadri, 2024). To address workload issues, it is also crucial to improve working conditions and alleviate the pressures that contribute to burnout levels by ensuring adequate staffing levels, role clarity, professional autonomy and ensuring that appropriate resources are available (Kellett, et al., 2020; Livni et al., 2012).

Fostering a positive workplace atmosphere is also a crucial factor in promoting both staff well‐being and patient care (Soares, et al., 2023). Encouraging collaboration among staff members, promoting positive communication, and creating a sense of community within the service can significantly enhance the overall work environment. These measures contribute not only to improved morale among healthcare providers but also to the quality of care delivered to patients, suggesting that organizational interventions focused on these areas might be highly beneficial.

Finally, our study highlights the importance of strengthening the therapeutic alliance between staff and patients, particularly those with SSD. Training in building effective relationships and communication skills is essential to improve the quality of care and support provided to patients. Recent research also shows the effectiveness of tailored communication skills training and emotional regulation strategies in strengthening the WA between healthcare professionals and patients. These approaches not only help mitigate burnout but also improve patient interactions and care outcomes, particularly in high‐stress environments (Adnan et al., 2022).

Future research directions

Future research could investigate the mechanisms underlying the relationship between burnout and WA, with a particular focus on potential mediators such as empathy and communication skills. Additionally, it is important to explore the effectiveness of specific interventions designed to address burnout among mental health professionals treating patients with SSD, as well as to examine the long‐term impact of burnout on both the well‐being of mental health workers and the quality of patient care. Longitudinal studies would be valuable for understanding the temporal dynamics of burnout and its impact on the therapeutic relationship over time. Furthermore, research on the role of specific communication styles and techniques in promoting a strong WA between mental health professionals and patients with SSD is needed to develop evidence‐based strategies that effectively support mental health professionals.

CONCLUSION

The association between burnout and both ward atmosphere and WA underscores the intricate interaction among individual, relational, and organizational factors within psychiatric care settings that affect both patients and staff. Furthermore, the discovery of differing perceptions between staff and patients with SSD regarding WA and ward atmosphere introduces an innovative aspect to the study. This emphasises the benefits of situating patients in alternative settings to traditional hospital wards, such as RFs, which can improve patients' perception of care. By addressing these factors, healthcare organizations can offer better support to mental health professionals, ultimately enhancing the quality of care for patients with SSD.
