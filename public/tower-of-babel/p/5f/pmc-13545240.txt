Mobile real-time detection transformer for computationally constrained devices
Authors: Arjun Prashanth, Andhavarapu Balu, Ishan Jain, Sowmith Reddy, Bhargavi R.
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The recent state-of-the-art model, Real Time Detection Transformer (RT-DETR), has surpassed YOLOv8 in terms of both accuracy and speed. We present a model based on the RT-DETR architecture, with a lightweight backbone, to achieve compactness and high speed. Our model, Mobile RT-DETR (MRTDETR), utilises a series of MobileNetv3 blocks. Specifically, the layers 7, 13, and 15 of the MobileNetv3 backbone were used as inputs to the encoder. Separable self attention was also explored, but not retained. MRTDETR achieves 44.6% mAP with a train batch size of 4 on NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3050 OEM GPU and 43.1% mAP with a train batch size of 64 on NVIDIA H100 PCIe GPUs, hence surpassing existing implementations of FasterRCNN, MobileFormer, and MobileViT. With 15.23 GFLOPs, MRTDETR comes close to the performance of the state-of-the-art YOLOv8 and YOLOv9 detectors of similar size while having 46.7% and 42.3% less FLOPs correspondingly. The model has been tested with Raspberry Pi 5, alongside other models (including quantised models), to profile edge related metrics. It has an average inference time of 1,967.4 milliseconds with a standard deviation of 71.9 milliseconds, takes 435.86 MB of VmRSS memory and 1,350.27 MB of VmSize memory, and has an ONNX file size of 43.1 MB. The MRTDETR model outperforms all other models in optimality, with respect to the trade-off between accuracy and FLOPs. By supplementing model complexity metrics and performance metrics, with real-world profiling results, a more relevant evaluation of the proposed MRTDETR model is provided.

Introduction

Processing data closer to where it originates is known as edge computing1. Edge devices are the hardware units that perform this computation. In the field of computer vision, edge devices are typically located near the camera. These devices are deployed in large numbers, as each physical locality is assigned one edge computing device. Each device is dedicated to processing tasks specific to its locality. Due to these deployment characteristics, edge computing devices are often constrained in computational resources such as storage, memory, processing capacity, temperature regulation, and power. These unique limitations make the analysis and experimental profiling of resource utilization in edge devices even more important than in general-purpose or server-side computing.

Shifting computation to the edge reduces latency1. In the field of computer vision, the reduction in latency is critical to applications such as object detection and image classification, which are used in mission-critical systems such as autonomous vehicles, surgical devices, security cameras, obstacle detection for the visually-impaired, rescue drones, and authentication systems123456.

Object detection has traditionally been dominated by CNN models. However, a recent model called Real Time Detection Transformer (RT-DETR)7 has proven its superiority over YOLOv8, The architecture of this model introduces a novel Cross Scale Feature Fusion module and Attention based Intra scale Feature Interaction (AIFI) module, which reduces computational cost by 3 5% with an increase in average precision.

Despite its impressive performance, RT-DETR is computationally heavy, with even the smallest model, RT-DETR-R18, having 20 million parameters and 60 GFLOPs. Similarly, other small models like YOLOv8 achieve high accuracy but are also computationally expensive, with the small model comprising 11.2 million parameters and 28.6 GFLOPs.

GPUs (Graphical Processing Units) are often unavailable in edge computing devices. Hence, models must be designed to run efficiently on CPUs as well. However, there is no such limitation during the training phase, since models trained on any device (such as a server) can be deployed on any other device (such as an edge computing device). Trained models can be exported and imported across different devices using file formats such as.pt,.pth, or.onnx. In particular, standards like ONNX provide a common open framework that enables training and deploying models across different deep learning platforms (e.g., PyTorch, TensorFlow). This allows the edge device to maintain a single runtime environment capable of handling models trained in any framework8.

This raises the question of whether the architecture used in RT-DETR can be combined with MobileNet and other smaller CNN models to create an efficient object detection model without having to sacrifice much on accuracy. This paper explores replacing the backbone of RT-DETR with MobileNetv39 and conducting experiments to determine the best model configuration.

Related work

Light-weight convolutional neural networks (CNNs)

SqueezeNext10, the successor of SqueezeNet achieves better top-5 classification as compared to MobileNetv1 with 1.3 times lesser parameters by utilizing aggressive channel reduction strategy, skip connections and separable 3 x 3 convolutions instead of depth-wise separable convolutions as in the case of mobilenet.

MobileNets9,11,12 use a series of combination of depth-wise separable convolution and point-wise convolutional blocks to efficiently encode image features.

MobileNetv3 adds novel improvements to the architecture of MobileNetv2 with squeeze and excitation blocks to improve the quality of representations produced by the network by explicitly modeling the inter-dependencies between the channels of its convolutional features leading to a robust architecture. By applying optimizations and redesigning the computationally expensive layers of MobileNetv2, it was able to reduce latency without sacrificing on accuracy.

MobileNetV3 takes advantage of algorithmic approaches to discover the most optimal neural network architecture, departing from the manual design process used in earlier versions of MobileNet. It incorporates two specific techniques, namely MnasNet13 and NetAdapt14, to accomplish this. Initially, it employs MnasNet to search for a coarse architecture. MnasNet uses reinforcement learning to select the best configuration from a set of discrete choices. Subsequently, the model refines the architecture using NetAdapt, a complementary technique that gradually trims under-utilized activation channels.

Traditional convolutions connect each output channel to every input channel, resulting in high computational complexity. MicroNet15 presents micro-factorized convolutions to handle low FLOPs by lowering node connectivity to enlarge network width. The increased network width allows for more diverse feature extraction, potentially improving the model’s representation power and overall accuracy. To strengthen the connections between the groups created by micro-factorization, MicroNet introduces a novel non-linearity function called Dynamic Shift-Max (DY-Shift-Max).

DY-Shift-Max is implemented as a hyper-function that consists of a sequence of operations, including average pooling, two fully connected layers, and a sigmoid layer. This design is reminiscent of the Squeeze-and-Excitation mechanism used in MobileNetV3. By incorporating DY-Shift-Max, MicroNet aims to enhance the connectivity and information flow within the network, further improving its performance.

These models primarily focus on image classification tasks using the ImageNet dataset. However, they can also be combined with object detection heads such as Single Shot MultiBox Detector (SSD)16 and SSDLite to extend their utility to object detection tasks. SSD is a widely used object detection architecture that combines the efficiency of single-shot detection with the accuracy of multi-scale feature maps. SSDLite, on the other hand, is a lightweight variant of SSD designed for resource-constrained environments. By integrating models with SSD and SSDLite, it is possible to perform efficient and accurate object detection, enabling applications in areas such as autonomous driving, surveillance, and robotics.

Real-time object detectors

YOLO models are the de-facto real-time object detectors. The YOLO models dominate in this field with the YOLOv8 small model achieving 44.9% AP with 11.2 million parameters.

A recent model, continuing the YOLO legacy is YOLOv917, which stands out by achieving superior results compared to state-of-the-art models that rely on pre-training on large datasets. One of the key innovations is the introduction of a new concept of programmable gradient information (PGI). It combines PGI with a lightweight network architecture called Generalized Efficient Layer Aggregation Network (GELAN) to surpass the performance of existing models on the COCO dataset.

The PGI framework comprises three main components: the main branch, auxiliary reversible branches, and multi-level auxiliary information. During the inference phase, only the main branch is utilized. However, during training, the auxiliary branches are employed to assist the main branch in overcoming challenges associated with deep neural networks.

One of the issues addressed by the auxiliary branches is the information bottleneck that arises as neural networks deepen. This bottleneck can hinder the propagation of reliable gradients, which are crucial for accurate learning. By incorporating auxiliary reversible branches, YOLOv9 mitigates this problem by allowing the network to access and utilize gradient information from multiple stages of the architecture.

Another challenge tackled by the auxiliary branches is error accumulation caused by deep supervision. Deep supervision involves introducing intermediate supervision signals at various stages of the network to guide the learning process. However, as the network becomes deeper, the accumulation of errors from different stages can lead to suboptimal performance. The auxiliary branches in YOLOv9 help alleviate this issue by providing additional supervision signals that aid in error correction and prevent the accumulation of errors.

The impact of the novel concepts introduced in YOLOv9 is clearly evident in the impressive performance of its smaller variant, YOLOv9-S. Despite its compact size, YOLOv9-S achieves an outstanding Average Precision (AP) score of 46.8% on the COCO val2017 dataset, while operating at a computational cost of only 26.4 GFLOPs.

The drawback with these models is that all the YOLO models use handcrafted component - non-maximum suppression (NMS), Specifically, it is applied after the model predicts the locations, sizes and class probabilities of various objects present in an input image, and generates multiple bounding boxes for each object. Since some of these bounding boxes might overlap, NMS is used to remove the ones with lower confidence scores and retain only the best one. These components can cause performance degradation and the hyper-parameters of NMS have notable influence on the accuracy and speed of these models.

Transformer based object detectors

DETR(DEtection TRansformer)18 proposed by Facebook research group was the first end-to-end object detection model which utilizes the transformer architecture. DETR eliminates the need for NMS and instead, employs bipartite matching to directly predict the one-to-one object set. This strategy simplifies the detection pipeline and mitigates the performance bottleneck caused by NMS. To mitigate the drawbacks of DETR, namely slow training convergence and hard-to-optimize queries, many DETR variants have been proposed, such as Deformable-DETR19, DAB-DETR20, DN-DETR21, Group-DETR22, DINO23 and RT-DETR7. RT-DETR achieves state-of-the-art results and beats yolov8 in terms of accuracy and speed. Although these models improve the performance of DETR architecture, the smallest model in the DETR family has 44 million parameters with 100 GFLOPs, and the RT-DETR model RT-DETR-R18 with the Resnet-18 backbone has 20 million parameters with 60 GFLOPs. All of these models use the ResNet family of models pre-trained on ImageNet dataset as their backbone, which are computationally expensive.

Another model is the MobileFormer24, primarily proposed for Image classification on ImageNet, utilizes the RetinaNet framework and DETR framework for object detection. In the RetinaNet framework, MobileFormer significantly outperforms MobileNetV3, ShuffleNetV2 by 8.3+ AP with similar computational complexity. As compared to ResNet, it achieves higher AP (38%) with significantly less FLOPs in the backbone. The end-to-end MobileFormer detectors, utilizing the DETR architecture, converge in 300 epochs (as compared to 500 by DETR), gains 1.1 AP (43.1) over DETR with 48% less FLOPs and 64% less parameters. MobileFormer achieves these results by parallelizing the model interaction between its backbone (MobileNetv3) and the transformer with a two-way bridge in between. This creates 4 modules - Mobile (the MobileNet module), Former (the transformer module, the output of this module is used to generate parameters for dynamic ReLU in Mobile sub block), Mobile → Former module (A bridge directed from MobileNet module to the Transformer module. It fuses local features into global features), and Mobile ← Former module (This module bridges from global to local features). Furthermore, the Mobile sub-block modifies the inverted bottleneck blocks in MobileNet with dynamic ReLU25 which improves the model’s learning.

The above mentioned models, being light-weight do not have much precision, while the yolo models are precise but compute-intensive. So we explore the need for a light weight model which does not compromise much on accuracy and is able to perform on resource constrained devices.

Proposed mobile real time detection transformer

Fig. 1Proposed architecture of Mobile RT-DETR. The backbone used is MobileNetv3 Large. The 7th, 13th and 15th layers of MobileNetv3 were used to generate the sequence input for the encoder.

Proposed architecture of Mobile RT-DETR. The backbone used is MobileNetv3 Large. The 7th, 13th and 15th layers of MobileNetv3 were used to generate the sequence input for the encoder.

To adapt the RT-DETR (Real-Time Detection Transformer) architecture for mobile applications, we modify the architecture to use MobileNetv3 large as the backbone, which was pre-trained on the ImageNet dataset. For experimentation, the smaller version, MobileNetv3 small, is also used as a backbone.

This choice was motivated by the efficiency of MobileNet architectures, which are designed for mobile and embedded vision applications11. The backbone extracts high-level features from the input image, which are flattened before being inputted to the transformer blocks. The proposed model utilizes the output tensors of a series of MobileNetv3 blocks, specifically the final three stages (C3, C4, C5), to extract local features. This extraction process leverages the effectiveness and efficiency of depth-wise and pointwise convolutions9. In the implementation as shown in Fig. 1, the layers 7, 13, and 15 of the MobileNetv3 backbone were specifically used as inputs to the encoder using an intermediate layer extraction mechanism. This was done to match the specific downsampling strides (8, 16 and 32) expected by the encoder stage, allowing the transformer to work well with both small and larger targets. This is a behavior retained from the original RT-DETR architecture.

The output from the chosen three layers is flattened, concatenated and fed as a sequence to the next stage. Then the hybrid encoder efficiently converts multi-scale features27 into a sequence of image features using intra-scale feature interaction (AIFI) and cross-scale feature-fusion module (CCFM).

A major challenge in this integration was avoiding the information bottleneck28between the mobilenetv3 and the hybrid encoder. The original ResNet outputs feature maps with high channel dimensions (512, 1024, and 2048 channels). In contrast, mobilenetv3 produces feature maps significantly shallower output tensors (40, 112, 160 channels) compared to the heavier resnet original9. Feeding this tensor, with its shallower channel depth, would result in worse performance by the encoder in representing the image and by the self-attention mechanism in making meaningful connections.

To mitigate this, the channel projection layer, which originally used 1x1 depthwise convolutions to reduce features to a 256-dimensional tensor for the transformer, now acts as an expander29. It projects the low-dimensional, highly distilled local features into a higher-dimensional tensor.

Unlike MobileFormer, which relies on a computationally complex two-way cross attention bridge between the backbone and the transformer, our model uses a sequential setup, which was preserved from RT-DETR. This avoids cross-attention overhead and allows the model to achieve more computational efficiency. To investigate the effectiveness of Linear Self Attention26, we modify the architecture of the encoder layers by introducing an intermediate linear layer. This layer converts the query Q, key K, and value V inputs of the traditional MHA transformer layer into a single matrix X. It achieves this by first concatenating Q, K, and V along the first dimension and then using a simple 3 × 1 weight matrix to learn X. This matrix X is then fed to the Linear Attention Module, adapted from MobileViTv2. Since we do not use a Vision Transformer (ViT), our input is of shape (B, k, d), where B is the batch size, d is the dimension of the tokens or embeddings, and k is the number of such tokens. Consequently, we modified the linear attention implementation by replacing the convolutional layers in the original implementation with linear layers.

Experimental setup

Training setup

Dataset: We use the Microsoft COCO 2017 dataset. COCO train2017 for training and COCO val2017 validation. The standard COCO Average Precision (AP) metric was used with a single scale image as input.

Implementation Details: MobileNetv3 was used as the backbone which was pre-trained on ImageNet30 dataset from the Pytorch torchvision library31. The AIFI consists of 1 transformer layer and the fusion block in CSF (cross-scale fusion) consists of 3 RepBlocks. In the Intersection-over-Union (IoU) query selector, we set queries to 300 encoder features to initialize object queries in the decoder. The training strategy for the decoder is similar to RTDETR’s7, with learning rates of the backbone setting follows the DETR strategy, except for the Multi Step LR schedule reconfigured based on the training checkpoints. To potentially reduce computational complexity, the Transformer layer in the encoder of RT-DETR was replaced with Separable Attention, as discussed. This modification reduces the computational complexity of the transformer layer from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O(N^{2})$$\end{document} to O(N). Subsequently, the effects of this new transformer layer on average precision were studied.

Setup1: Training was conducted on NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3050 OEM GPU with 8192 MiB memory capacity and 120 W power capacity. Adam optimizer was used for training. The train batch size used was 4, and a learning rate of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$10^{-5}$$\end{document} was used for the backbone, as it was pretrained so that the learning for it is very slow. A learning rate of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$10^{-4}$$\end{document} was chosen for the encoder and decoder layers with weight decay of 0.0001. The learning rate was controlled using a MultiStepLR scheduler, where the learning rate was reduced to half at epoch 50. The model was trained for 300 epochs on the COCO dataset, using mixed-precision training (AMP). An Exponential Moving Average (EMA) with a decay value of 0.9999 and a warmup period of 2000 iterations was applied to smooth the model weights throughout training.

Setup2: Training was conducted on NVIDIA H100 PCIe GPUs with 81559 MiB memory capacity and, 350 W power capacity per GPU. The system was equipped with dual H100 GPUs running CUDA version 12.2 with driver version 535.274.02. Adam optmizer was used for training. The train batch size is 64 (32 per GPU), and the learning rate was increased to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$10^{-4}$$\end{document} for the backbone and to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$10^{-3}$$\end{document} for the encoder and decoder layers, due to the increased batch size. Using the learning scheduler, the learning rate is reduced by half at 50th, 100th and 150th epoch and then every 25 epochs. The model was trained for total 330 epochs.

Edge profiling setup

Setup:

The edge computing device used for conducting the experiment is Raspberry Pi 5, which is with a 2.4 GHz quad-core 64-bit Arm Cortex-A76 CPU and 8 GB SDRAM32. In our experimental setup, the Raspberry Pi 5 was powered by standard mobile phone chargers connected to it via the USB-C port, and plugged into a wall socket. A 32GB SD card was used for storage of the operating system, the software and the files. The operating system installed is Ubuntu Server 24.04.03 LTS.

No cooling systems were used at all. The experiments were conducted in an indoor environment, with light to moderate air conditioning (except for the quantised models, which were tested without air conditioning), without a fan directly near the Raspberry Pi 5. Experiments were conducted in arbitrary parts of the day and year. Sometimes, there was indirect sunlight.

Methodology:

To profile the computational performance of the models in the experimental setup, inferencing was done on the Raspberry Pi using each model. Instead of using real images, which would require interactions with the secondary memory (SD card), we have used a single image throughout the experiment, so that only the primary memory (RAM) is used. This increases the accuracy of the profiling experiment.

The profiling software itself is a fork (derivative) of the profiling tool available in the Ultralytics framework33, and is explained by Algorithm 1. The fork enables the software to take readings of the memory usage and CPU temperature, along with its existing ability to read inference times. Power readings were not taken in our experiment. The fork is also modified to be able to save the measured readings at the end of the experiment, as CSV files. These CSV files are then used to make interpretations and generate visualisations. Interpretation and visualisation processes do not happen while the model is actively inferencing, in order to reduce the overhead of the profiler.

The profiler takes in models in the form of ONNX files, to use a standard runtime environment for all models. The RT-DETR checkpoints were downloaded as.pth files from their respective GitHub repositories and then converted to ONNX files using the ONNX export support mentioned in the repository. The models YOLOv5s, YOLOv5n, YOLOv5su, YOLOv5nu, YOLOv5mu, YOLOv8s, YOLOv8n, and YOLOv8m were loaded from the Ultralytics Python package33. For the YOLOv7 model, the checkpoint was downloaded from the official GitHub repository34. Checkpoints for YOLOv9t, YOLOv9s, and YOLOv9m models were obtained from the Ultralytics webpage. All aforementioned models were exported to ONNX using the Ultralytics Python package. In the case of YOLOv7-tiny, the model was prepared by following instructions in the official GitHub repository34, based on the Jupyter notebook, and subsequently exported to ONNX using the export script included. The YOLOX series: YOLOX-s, YOLOX-tiny, and YOLOX-nano were directly obtained in ONNX formats from their GitHub repository35. Faster R-CNN ONNX models were sourced from the GitHub repository36 maintained by the open-source community of ONNX. The MobileViT-V2 1.75 pre-trained model was fetched from its respective GitHub repository, and the ONNX export script was generated using PyTorch’s built-in function to export models as ONNX. Quantised versions of the models were generated using the ONNX Runtime quantisation tool, which applies post-training static quantisation to the original ONNX files8.

A compartive analysis is performed between non-quantised and quantised versions of the models, to decide whether full analysis of the quantised models is necessary.

The profiler uses ONNX Runtime as the runtime environment (not to be confused with the ONNX file format itself). The ONNX Runtime’s graph optimisation flag is set to use the maximum possible graph optimisations, for the models being deployed in the experiment.

Once the ONNX file is loaded by the profiler, it reads the expected input image dimensions. The profiler then creates a garbage image, having the same dimensions, but random pixel values. This garbage image is the only image that is used throughout the profiling, as we do not concern ourselves with the actual results being generated.

Importantly, the profiler used in this experiment does not perform any of the pre-processing or post-processing required by the models. This is based on the assumption that such processing can be optimally implemented for a specific application and model, through the hardware design of the peripherals in an embedded system, such as the image processor within the camera providing the input. In particular, Non-Maximal Suppression (NMS) is known to be a post-processing step that demands a large amount of computational resources7. One of the main advantages proposed by the RT-DETR architecture is that these models do not require NMS, whereas YOLO models do. Nevertheless, NMS is not considered in this experiment.

First, 30 warm-up inferences of the image are performed by the model to calculate the approximate average inference time. Based on this value, the number of inferences required for the actual experiment is found. The number of inferences is chosen such that the total runtime is at least 300 seconds when inferencing at the estimated average inference speed. At least 100 inferences will be executed, regardless of the approximate average inference time. Enforcing a minimum total runtime of 300 seconds ensures sufficient data for analyzing memory and temperature trends and their effects on inference times. The profiler does not permanently record any readings from the warm-up phase. All readings in the main phase, are used for calculations and visualizations.

Before starting the experiment for each model, the Raspberry Pi is kept powered off at the experimental site for at least 10 minutes to allow adequate cooling after the previous run. The power supply may or may not have been physically disconnected during this period. After the cooldown, the Raspberry Pi is powered on, and the experiment is initiated through a terminal shell connected via SSH over a mobile hotspot. Throughout the experiment, only the hotspot host mobile phone and a single laptop with one terminal session are connected to the Raspberry Pi. No additional processes are initiated on the operating system of the Raspberry Pi, except those necessary to trigger the experiment for the specified model.

Algorithm 1Profile ONNX Model.

Profile ONNX Model.

The profiling happens as a loop with as many iterations as calculated after the warmup phase. Each iteration of the loop infers the image exactly once. The inference time is calculated as the difference between the system time before and after the line of inference. Memory and temperature readings are taken in every iteration of the loop, but the time taken to execute them, are not counted within the inference time. Each iteration of the loop corresponds to one row of the output CSV, and carries a timestamp of the system time before at the beginning of the loop. Two kinds of memory readings are taken: VmRSS (Virtual memory Resident Set Size) and VmSize (Virtual memory Set Size). VmRSS reports the amount of physical memory currently used by this process alone. VmSize reports the total amount of virtual memory allocated to the process, including the amount of memory occupied by shared libraries that the process depends on. VmRSS would normally be what we are concerned with in a general purpose computer, but in edge computing systems, since we will not be using any other processes and have constrained memory availability, looking at VmSize can be more appropriate too.

In all the models, the memory readings were nearly unchanging with fluctuations only in the order of kilobytes. Therefore, the memory usage values are reported as a table, rounded off to the nearest mebibyte (MiB).

Temperature is measured by reading the /sys/class/thermal/thermal_zone0/temp virtual file, provided by Linux. There is only one thermal zone in the Raspberry Pi.

Before calculating the reported average inference time and standard deviation, iterative sigma clipping is applied on the inference times. The sigma value was 2 and the limit for the number of iterations was 5. These were the default values and process applied in the ultralytics framework. All other unspecified values were the default from the framework from the commit with the hash 88438a86f79c1df8bc3627d0913ca4ca4fd3f956. The visualisation process used the original data without clipping. Some models were not included in certain graphs, if they had extreme outlier values that distorted the graph scale.

To understand how variable the experimental setup is, the profiler was run multiple times for MRTDETR and plotted that way in the graphs. The second run was randomly chosen (by coin toss) to be the one that is used for comparison with other models in the text.

The findings from the ablation study are tabulated in Table 4.

Backbone: Changing the backbone to Mobilenetv3 small reduced the FLOPs by 8.07% with 4% points decrease in AP.

Encoder: We experimented with the encoder layers in the model, changing from 2 layer encoder to 1 layer encoder. The result obtained was, the FLOPs increased by 14.28% but with 4% points decrease in AP. Utilizing Separable Self Attention in the encoder we found that FLOPs decreased by 0.72% and 3.2% points decrease in AP. So we conclude that there is not much gained by using Separable Self Attention, which can be accounted by noticing that transformer layers do not contribute much to the FLOPs as they do in the Vision Transformer model proposed in MobileViTv2.

Decoder: Keeping only a single decoder layer in the model, reduced the AP by 5% points. Increasing the number of decoder layers from 3 to 4 in fact caused a very minute reduction in AP with 20% increase in FLOPs.

Reducing the number of top queries from 300 to 100, reduced the AP by 0.4% points, with only 5.59% decrease in FLOPs. However, this model converged to around 40% AP after 40 epochs.

Experimenting with a model having only 1 encoder layer, 1 decoder layer and MobileNetv3 Large backbone we found that the AP did not rise above 17.1%. This underscores the importance of the encoder and decoder layers.

Inference time

The inference time series for each model is plotted in Fig. 4. All models exhibit some deviation between consecutive inferences, but there are no overall changes as time goes on, except for a slight upward slope. The upward slope can be attributed to the increase in temperature as more and more inferences are run. The average inference time and standard deviation are calculated as described in the Edge Profiling Setup section (4.2), and are reported in Table 6 along with their coefficients of variation. The proposed model reports an average inference time of 1,967.4 milliseconds and a standard deviation of 71.9 milliseconds, on the experimental setup with the Raspberry Pi.

Coefficient of variation

The coefficients of variation of the inference times of the models is plotted in Fig. 5. Although Table 6 shows that the standard deviations of the inference times are higher for models that have higher reported average standard deviation, the coefficients of variation seem to be lower for models with higher reported average standard deviation. In other words, the stability of the inference times are lower for the faster models.

Inference time and mAP

The tradeoffs between precision and inference time are visualised in Fig. 6. The MRTDETR-H100’s 43.1% mAP and MRTDETR-RTX’s 44.6% mAP are less than RT-DETR r18’s 46.5% and RT-DETR r50’s 53.1%, but the MRTDETR has an average inference time of 1,967.4 milliseconds, compared to the 2,496.8 milliseconds by RT-DETR r18 and the much slower 10,444.7 milliseconds by RT-DETR r50, on the Raspberry Pi.

Memory

Within the set of YOLO models and RT-DETR models together, we notice the trend that the models that have higher mAP also have higher memory usage (and higher inference time), as represented in Fig. 6. However, the Faster R-CNN models and the Mobile ViT model do not follow this trend and use even more memory. Mobile RT-DETR occupies 1,350.27 MBs of VmSize memory, which is similar to the 1,383.72 MBs occupied by RT-DETR r18, and the 1,340.16 MBs occupied by YOLO v8 Small. When checking VmRSS, Mobile RT-DETR occupies 435.86 MBs. A similar trend is observed in the ONNX file size of the models. Mobile RT-DETR’s file size is 43.1 MBs, which is a 44% reduction from the 76.8 MBs of RT-DETR r18’s file, and nearly the same as the 42.8 MBs of the YOLO v8 Small’s file.

Throttling impact

Figure 8 qualitatively shows the impact of the throttling, at high temperature, by the Raspberry Pi, on the inference times. There is a visible increase in inference speeds, at the throttling temperature. This can not be attributed to random variations, as the inference times only became higher and not lower. However, as the warmup phase of 30 iterations took different durations for different models, the models started the recorded phase of the experiment at different temperatures. Therefore, it is hard to quantitatively show how severely the models were affected by the throttling, when being compared.

Future scope

Future work will focus on improving the model’s accuracy and reducing model size and complexity by exploring the use of different backbones and using transformer based models such as MobileViT, MobileFormer in the backbone which are much faster than MobileNetv3. The performance of the model can be further improved by training on the Objects 36541 dataset and then fine-tuning to COCO.

Higher mAP was achieved with a smaller batch size, which is a problem because we need a larger batch size to fully utilise better GPUs and have faster training. Due to the training taking several weeks for each attempt, it was not possible to experiment with different hyperparameters or variations of architecture, to improve the mAP further. Future work can focus on faster convergence, faster training times and bigger batch sizes, while maintaining or improving the mAP.

The proposed model can be further optimised for edge devices by using compression techniques such as quantisation, pruning, low-rank decomposition and sparsity.

Edge profiling needs to account for power usage. This can ideally be done using an external power meter, given a stable power supply. More controlled physical setup is needed to measure the impact of temperature. Experiments should also be done with (and without) cooling systems equipped (for the CPU). To be able to quantify how much each model’s inference time is affected by thermal throttling, the software architecture should be modified to remove the “warmup” phase, which takes different amounts of time for different models in the current software architecture due to the static number of iterations.

More edge devices, such as Jetson Nano and ESP32, can be experimented, to understand the performance of the models with different hardware features. More frameworks can be explored, such as Pytorch’s ExecuTorch.

A normalisation method should be created for the profiling results from models that have different input image sizes. MobileViT has an input image size of 320 x 320, while all of the other models tested on the edge devices have 640 x 640. Our model also takes input image size of 640 x 640. The end to end detectors, compared using FLOPs, have 800 x 1333 input size.

Models need to be profiled along with their required and typical setups of pre-processing and post-processing steps. YOLO models in particular need a Non Maximal Suppression (NMS) post-processor, which RT-DETR models do not need. NMS is known to be a very heavy step7. The performance of the RT-DETR models is expected to be even better comparatively, when accounting for YOLO with NMS, unless Application Specific Integrated Circuits (ASICs) have been made for the camera and display modules.

Frameworks have to be improved so that the state-of-the-art (SOTA) models from the research field can be distributed for immediate deployment, and to be tested on a standardised benchmarking platform. This has to be in a way that bundles a model along with its pre-processing and post-processing requirements, beyond just core model weights.

Future work shall explore direct comparisons with compressed variants of the SOTA models compared, with compression techniques beyond simple quantisation. Current literature shows the compression for YOLO remains an active research area42, rather than an established practice. The compressed models presented in existing papers, are not made publicly available, or they lack the metrics needed for direct comparison. The compressed models also require either retraining or fine-tuning after compression, as the optimas are expected to change.

Edge computing setups are dedicated for a specific task, at both the hardware and software levels. Therefore, they are a very stable environment to repeat experiments. Hence, we recommend profiling on edge devices with real world metrics such as inference times, if feasible in budget and computational constraints, even if it isn’t the intended deployment device.

Conclusion

Mobile RT-DETR was proposed, based on the previous RT-DETR architecture, which focuses on achieving compactness and high speed. The proposed model outperforms FasterRCNN and MobileFormer based models and achieves comparable results to DETR-based models, while having a reduced number of FLOPs and parameters. The effectiveness of Mobile RT-DETR was demonstrated by its mean Average Precision (mAP) of 44.6% on the COCO val2017 dataset, achieving comparable results to YOLOv8 small while having 46.7% less FLOPs.

The model has been tested against other state of the art models in an experimental setup with Raspberry Pi 5 as the edge device, to profile its performance in edge related metrics like inference time, temperature and memory usage. It has an average inference time of 1,967.4 milliseconds with a standard deviation of 71.9 milliseconds, takes 435.86 MB of VmRSS memory and 1,350.27 MB of VmSize memory, and has an ONNX file size of 43.1 MB.

The model outperforms all other models in optimality, when considering the trade-offs between accuracy and FLOPs. When tested with edge profiling, the model also demonstrated good results in the trade-offs between accuracy, inference time and memory usage, although not as optimal as shown purely by FLOPs. While recognising that the lack of implementing Non-Maximal Suppression (NMS) while inferencing YOLO models may have affected the edge profiling results unfavourably for Mobile RT-DETR, the experiment has highlighted the need for real world edge profiling beyond heuristics, such as FLOPs.
