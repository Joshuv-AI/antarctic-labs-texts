GmMADS17, a MADS-box transcription factor, promotes flowering in soybean
Authors: Wei Liu, Yubin Wang, Ran Xu, Lifeng Zhang, Xiao Wang, Wei Li, Caijie Wang, Xiaoyan Zhang, Haiying Dai, Yanwei Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSoybean is an important crop used for oil, grain and feed. Due to its strong photoperiod sensitivity, flowering is a critical developmental process regulated by multiple genes that determines reproductive success and yield of soybean. Therefore, identifying regulatory genes controlling flowering is of great importance for developing widely adapted and high-yielding soybean varieties.ResultsIn this study, we identified a soybean MADS-box transcription factor gene GmMADS17 through transcriptome analysis of two extreme flowering phenotype families derived from a recombinant inbred line (RIL) population of Qihuang 34 and Jidou 17. Background

Soybean is an important crop widely used for oil and protein extraction, as well as biodiesel production. It exhibits a high sensitivity to photoperiod [1–4]. This characteristic is directly reflected in its ecological adaptability traits such as flowering time and maturity period. Flowering is a critical developmental process that determines reproductive success and ultimately influences crop yield [5, 6]. Therefore, identifying key regulatory genes controlling flowering is of great importance for developing widely adapted and high-yielding soybean varieties.

Transcription factors function as important regulators to control the initiation of flowering [7–9]. As one of the largest and most important families in flowering plants, MADS-box transcription factors play pivotal roles throughout plant development, especially in the floral transition and floral organ identity [10, 11]. Therefore, they are promising targets for the genetic improvement of crops [12].

The AGAMOUS-LIKE 6 (AGL6) genes encode an ancient subfamily of MIKC-type MADS-box transcription factors [13, 14]. Phylogenetically, AGL6 members are closely clustered with SEPALLATA (SEP) members, and functional analyses in petunia, maize, and rice have proposed AGL6 as additional putative E-class genes [15]. In Arabidopsis, this subfamily contains two members, AGL13 and AGL6. AGL13 has been shown to regulate pollen and ovule development by forming higher-order complexes with AGAMOUS (AG) and APETALA3 (AP3) [16], whereas AGL6 functions in both floral organ development and flowering time regulation. Overexpression of AGL6 in Arabidopsis causes precocious flowering and terminal flower formation [17, 18]. Furthermore, an AGL6–ELF3–FT circuit has been identified, in which AGL6 promotes flowering by directly suppressing ELF3, a repressor of FT [19]. The role of AGL6 in flowering time regulation is further supported by functional studies in other species. For instance, overexpression of the wheat AGL6 gene promotes flowering and directly regulates AP3 expression, thereby affecting both flowering time and stamen development [20]. In cotton, virus-induced gene silencing (VIGS) of AGL6 significantly delays flowering [21]. Similarly, ectopic expression of AGL6 homologs from Prunus mume in Arabidopsis promotes flowering and alters floral organ structure [22], whereas overexpression of IiAGL6 from Isatis indigotica leads to late flowering in Arabidopsis, and its silencing in woad affects stamen development [23]. These findings highlight the conserved and functionally diversified roles of AGL6 homologs in regulating flowering across plant species.

In soybean, several MADS-box genes including GmNMHC5 [24], GmAGL1 [25], SOC1 [26], FUL-like MADS-box genes (designated GmMDEs) [27], GmAP1s [28], GmAP3 [29], and GmFULc [30] have been functionally characterized in soybean for their roles in flowering time regulation. Among them, four AP1 homologs which were mainly expressed in the shoot apex were reported to be involved in controlling flowering time and plant height [28]. Furthermore, GmAP1c physically interacted with two SOC1 proteins, SOC1a and SOC1b, and that these SOC1s upregulated the expression of AP1c, promoting flowering [31]. Although a number of MADS-box genes have been identified in soybean, the function studies of AGL6 subfamily genes are limited.

In this study, GmMADS17, a soybean AGL6 subfamily MADS-box gene was identified from transcriptome profiling of two extreme flowering phenotype families derived from a recombinant inbred line (RIL) population of Qihuang 34 and Jidou 17. Functional validation demonstrated that GmMADS17 promoted flowering in soybean. Yeast two-hybrid (Y2H), bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) and Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays suggested interactions between GmMADS17 and 3 GmAP1 proteins (GmAP1a, c and d) in soybean. Furthermore, GmMADS17 might have undergone strong selection during soybean domestication. This study provides a theoretical foundation for further refining the flowering regulation pathway in soybean and offers valuable information for the improvement of regional adaptation in soybean.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

The recombinant inbred line (RIL) population derived from Qihuang 34 (cultivated by our laboratory team; National Authorized Soybean Variety No. 2013009) and Jidou 17 (provided by Hebei Academy of Agriculture and Forestry Sciences; National Authorized Soybean Variety No. 2013010) was maintained in our laboratory. Williams 82 was obtained from Weimi Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Nicotiana benthamiana was obtained from Boyuan Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Arabidopsis thaliana (Columbia-0 ecotype) was maintained in our laboratory. Soybean plants were cultivated in a growth chamber under conditions of 26 °C and 70% relative humidity. Different photoperiods (12 h light/12 h dark or 16 h light/8 h dark) were applied for expression pattern analysis and phenotype identification. Arabidopsis thaliana and tobacco were cultivated in a growth chamber under conditions of 23 °C and 70% relative humidity with a 16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod.

Transcriptome sequencing

The two extreme flowering phenotype lines (QJ355 and QJ534) from the recombinant inbred line (RIL) population of Qihuang 34 and Jidou 17 were cultivated in a growth chamber under short-day conditions (12 h light/12 h dark). 16 days after emergence, shoot apices were sampled 3 h after light exposure and subjected to transcriptome analysis. Three biological replicates were performed for each material. A total amount of 1 μg RNA per sample was used as input material for the RNA sample preparations. Sequencing libraries were constructed using the NEBNext Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina following the manufacturer's protocol and sequenced on the Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform with 150-bp paired-end reads. Raw reads were processed to remove adapters and low-quality reads. Clean reads were aligned to the soybean reference genome (Glycine max Wm82.a2.v1) using HISAT2. Gene expression levels were quantified as FPKM (Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads). Differential expression analysis between QJ355 and QJ534 was performed using DESeq2, with an absolute log2FC (log2 fold change) > 1.0 and FDR (false discovery rate) < 0.05 as the threshold for significance. The raw sequencing data have been deposited in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive under accession number PRJNA1337803.

Construction of phylogenetic tree and comparison of amino acid sequences

Phylogenetic tree was constructed using MEGA 11.0 by the neighbor-joining method. Bootstrap analysis was carried out with 1000 replications. The tree file was edited using ITOL v5 (https://itol.embl.de/). Clustal W was used to compare the amino acid sequences of the homologous proteins of AGL6 subfamily.

Agrobacterium-mediated transformation of Arabidopsis and flowering time measurement

The CDS of GmMADS17 was cloned into the pC3300s vector by the primers listed in Table S1, generating the construct pC3300s-GmMADS17. This construct was subsequently introduced into Arabidopsis thaliana (Columbia-0 ecotype) via Agrobacterium tumefaciens-mediated transformation using the floral dip technique [32]. Transgenic seeds were selected on 1/2 MS medium supplemented with Basta. The positive transformants were verified by PCR. T3 generation plants were grown under long-day condition (16 h light/8 h dark). The number of days and the rosette leaf number were recorded at the time of bolting.

qRT-PCR analysis

The soybean cultivar Williams 82 was grown in a growth chamber under short day (SD, 12 h light/12 h dark) and long day (LD, 16 h light/8 h dark) conditions to analyze the expression pattern of GmMADS17. For the expression of GmMADS17 in different organs, the roots, shoots, leaves, and shoot apices were sampled at 9 or 19 days after emergence (DAE). The flowers and young pods were sampled at 40 DAE, respectively. For diurnal expression analysis of GmMADS17 over a 24-h period, unifoliate leaves and the shoot apices were sampled every 3 h under short day and long day condition. To investigate the expression of GmMADS17 during soybean development, unifoliate leaves and the shoot apices were sampled at 4, 7, 10, 13, 16, 19, 22, 25, 28, 31 DAE at 3 h after dawn. For analyzing the expression levels of flowering-related genes in the shoot apex of wild-type (WT), GmMADS17 overexpression and gmmads17 mutant, the shoot apices were sampled at 16 DAE at 3 h after dawn.

Total RNA was extracted from all samples using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, USA). Quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed on three biological replicates (each consisting of pooled tissues from three plants), with three technical replicates per biological replicate, to assess the expression levels of target genes. The reactions were conducted on a LightCycler® 480 Instrument II (Roche, Mannheim, Germany) using SYBR® Premix Ex Taq™ (Takara, Japan). Relative gene expression was calculated using the comparative 2−ΔΔCT method [33]. The reference genes GmActin (Glyma.18G290800) and AtActin2 (AT3G18780) were used for normalization in soybean and transgenic Arabidopsis studies, respectively. All primer sequences are provided in Table S1. Data are presented as mean ± SD, and Student’s t-tests were performed to assess the significance of differences. Significance levels are indicated as *P < 0.05 and **P < 0.01.

Subcellular localization

To construct the GmMADS17-GFP fusion plasmid, the coding sequence (CDS) without the termination codon of GmMADS17 was ligated into the pAN580 vector. The constructed pAN580-GmMADS17 plasmid and the empty pAN580 vector (control) were co-transformed with the nuclear marker plasmid OsGhd7-mCherry into Arabidopsis thaliana protoplasts respectively [34]. After transfection, the protoplasts were cultured under low-light conditions for 8–10 h. Subcellular localization was subsequently observed using confocal laser scanning microscopy.

Generation of GmMADS17-overexpressing transgenic plants and gene-edited mutant plants of soybean

For generation GmMADS17-overexpressing transgenic plants, the CDS of GmMADS17 was cloned into the pC3300s vector. The resulting recombinant plasmid was then introduced into soybean cultivar Williams 82 via Agrobacterium-mediated transformation [35]. Putative transgenic plants were initially screened by dabbing leaves with 160 mg/L glufosinate solution and subsequently confirmed by PCR analysis.

For generation the gene-edited mutant plants, the target site of GmMADS17 was designed by using CRISPR-P online tool (http://cbi.hzau.edu.cn/CRISPR2/) [36]. Oligonucleotide corresponding to the single‐guide RNA (sgRNA) was synthesized, annealed, and inserted into the CRISPR/Cas9 vector. The resulting recombinant plasmid was then introduced into soybean cultivar Williams 82. To identify mutations at the GmMADS17 target site, the genomic region spanning the target site was amplified by PCR using specific primers listed in Table S1.

T3 generation of GmMADS17 overexpressing plants, GmMADS17 gene-edited plants, and wild-type plants were grown in growth chambers under both short day (SD) and long day (LD) conditions. The number of days from emergence to the R1 stage (beginning to bloom: one open flower at any node on the main stem) or R8 stage (full maturity: 95% of the pods had reached their mature pod color) [37] was recorded. Data are presented as mean ± SD, and Student’s t-tests were performed to assess the significance of differences.

The yeast two-hybrid assay

To investigate the interactions between GmAP1 proteins and GmMADS17, a yeast two-hybrid assay was performed. The coding sequences of GmAP1 genes (GmAP1a, GmAP1b, GmAP1c and GmAP1d) were cloned into the pGADT7 (AD) vector, GmMADS17 was cloned into the pGBKT7 (BD) vector. Various combinations were co-transformed into the yeast strain AH109. Transformants were selected on SD/-Trp/-Leu (SD-T-L) medium. For interaction testing, the colonies were selected on SD/-Trp/-Leu/-His/-Ade (SD-T-L–H-A) medium supplemented with X-α-Gal. Plates were incubated in the dark at 30 °C, and colony growth and blue color development were recorded.

The bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assay

A BIFC assay was performed to confirm the interactions between GmAP1s and GmMADS17. The coding sequences of GmAP1s were fused to the N-terminal fragment of yellow fluorescent protein (YN), and GmMADS17 was fused to the C-terminal fragment (YC). The constructed plasmids were co-transfected in pairwise combinations into tobacco (Nicotiana benthamiana) leaves. The transfections with each GmAP1-YN construct and empty YC vector, and combinations of empty YN vector with GmMADS17-YC or empty YC vector served as negative controls. The transformed leaves were observed and photographed after 36–48 h of growth.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assay

For Co-IP assay, the vectors of pBWA(V)H2S-FLAG-GmMADS17-GFP, and pBWA(V)H2S-HA-GmAP1s-MYC were constructed. Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101 harboring pBWA(V)H2S-FLAG-GmMADS17-GFP and pBWA(V)H2S-HA-GmAP1s-MYC were transformed into tobacco leaves. After 48 h of agroinfiltration, total proteins were extracted. Protein A/G agarose beads were pre-incubated with Anti-FLAG antibody (MBL M185-3L) for 1 h at room temperature, then washed to remove unbound antibodies. The total protein lysates were added to the antibody-coupled beads and incubated overnight at 4 °C. After five washes with PBS, the immunoprecipitated complexes were eluted by boiling with SDS sample buffer. The input and IP samples were analyzed by immunoblotting using anti-FLAG and anti-HA (Abcam ab9110) antibodies.

Haplotype analysis of GmMADS17 in the soybean population

To evaluate the genetic diversity of GmMADS17, we analyzed the nucleotide variation across a 100-kb genomic region (Chr10: 49,400,000–49,500,000) based on Zhonghuang 13 reference genome utilizing whole-genome sequencing data obtained from the SoyOmics database [38]. Haplotype profiling was performed based on SNPs located within the coding sequence of GmMADS17, which were extracted from a panel comprising 92 wild soybeans, 1040 landraces and 1735 improved cultivars in SoyOmics database. The SNP dataset was subjected to quality control, retaining only those with a missing rate < 10% and a minor allele frequency (MAF) > 5%. Furthermore, to examine the relationship between haplotypes and beginning bloom date, the raw phenotypic values in SoyOmics database were adjusted using the Best Linear Unbiased Prediction (BLUP) method [38].

Results

Identification of GmMADS17 as a putative flowering‑promoting gene in soybean

By analyzing the trait of flowering time, segregation was observed in the F8 generation of a recombinant inbred line (RIL) population from a cross between Qihuang 34 and Jidou 17. Notably, two lines, QJ355 and QJ534, displayed significant differences in flowering time under short-day (SD) conditions (Fig. 1A, B). To identify the MADS box genes associated with flowering time, a transcriptome sequencing was conducted on the shoot apex of these two lines under SD. 1172 differentially expressed genes (|log2FC|> 1) were identified (Table S2). Among them, three MIKC-type MADS-box genes (Glyma.06G324400, Glyma.10G240900, and Glyma.16G200700) were significantly down-regulated in the late-flowering line QJ534, with log2FC values of −2.77, −1.55, and −1.29, respectively. Although Glyma.06G324400 showed the greatest change in expression among the three genes, its average FPKM in the early-flowering line was considerably lower than those of the other two genes (Table S2, Fig. 1D). We therefore selected Glyma.10G240900 (log2FC = −1.55) for study. Consistent with its annotation as “MADS-box transcription factor 17” in both NCBI (National Center for Biotechnology Information, XP_003536491) and Uniprot (https://www.uniprot.org/) database, we designated Glyma.10G240900 as GmMADS17 for subsequent studies.

To further assess whether GmMADS17 is functionally related to flowering time, the nucleotide polymorphisms in the GmMADS17 coding sequence were analyzed in 2363 soybean accessions stored in the SoyOmics database (https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/soyomics/index). Two haplotypes (Hap1 and Hap2) were identified in the coding sequence of GmMADS17. A T/C single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) at position 49,470,628 on chromosome 10 distinguishes Hap1 (T) from Hap2 (C), resulting in a non-synonymous substitution from tyrosine (Hap1) to cysteine (Hap2) in the K-domain of GmMADS17 (Fig. 1E, Fig. S1). The two parents of the RIL population mentioned above, Qihuang 34 and Jidou 17, carry the same haplotype (Hap2) at this locus, confirming that this SNP does not segregate within the RIL population. Nevertheless, in the natural population, analysis of beginning bloom date and pod maturity date from 1512 and 1493 accessions in the SoyOmics database revealed that plants carrying Hap2 exhibited earlier flowering and maturation compared to those with Hap1 (Fig. 1F).

We next conducted functional validation by ectopically expressing GmMADS17 in Arabidopsis. The transgenic plants flowered and matured earlier than the wild type plants (Fig.S2). qRT-PCR analysis displayed that the expression of several flowering-time regulation genes (AP1, AP2, AP3, AG, FUL, FD, PI and SVP) was significantly changed in the shoot apex of the transgenic plants (Fig. S3). These results demonstrate that ectopic expression of GmMADS17 promotes flowering and maturation in Arabidopsis.

Taken together, the differential expression of GmMADS17 between early and late flowering RILs, its haplotype association with flowering time in natural populations, and its functional validation in Arabidopsis collectively support GmMADS17 as a candidate gene regulating flowering time in soybean. Therefore, GmMADS17 was selected for further in-depth investigation.

Phylogenetic analysis

To elucidate the phylogenetic classification of GmMADS17, a phylogenetic tree was constructed using the amino acid sequences of MIKCC-type MADS-box proteins from soybean [39], Arabidopsis thaliana, and common bean (Phaseolus vulgaris) [40]. Our results showed that GmMADS17 belonged to the AGL6 subfamily, which is closely related to the SEP subfamily (Fig. 2A), consistent with previous findings [39]. Furthermore, amino acid sequence alignment revealed that the MADS-box and K domains, particularly the MADS-box domain, are highly conserved among GmMADS17 and other AGL6-like proteins (Fig. 2B) .

Expression pattern of GmMADS17 in leaves and shoot apex under different photoperiod

The diurnal expression patterns of GmMADS17 in leaves and shoot apices over a 24-h period were analyzed (Fig. 3B and C). In leaves and shoot apices, GmMADS17 expression was consistently higher under SD conditions compared to LD conditions. This difference was particularly striking in shoot apex, where GmMADS17 transcript level under SD far exceeded those under LD.

We then monitored GmMADS17 expression patterns in the leaves and shoot apex during development of soybean plants under SD and LD. As shown in Fig. 3D and E, GmMADS17 expression was lower under LD than under SD at most of the time point in both leaves and shoot apex. Notably, in the shoot apex under SD, GmMADS17 expression was relatively low during the early stages of plant growth but increased sharply 13 days after emergence. This high expression level persisted until the first blooming stage, suggesting a potential role of GmMADS17 in flowering time regulation (Fig. 3D and E).

The confocal microscopy analysis suggested that the GmMADS17-GFP fusion protein localized exclusively to the nucleus (Fig. 4).

GmMADS17 promotes flowering of soybean

To explore the function of GmMADS17, 3 overexpression lines (Line 3, Line 7, and Line 13) were created (Fig. S4). The wild-type soybean plants and GmMADS17 overexpressing plants were grown in SD (12 h light/12 h dark) and LD (16 h light/8 h dark) for flowering time statistics. The results showed that under SD conditions, the wild-type plants flowered at 25.5 DAE, while the 3 overexpressing plants flowered at 20.0, 20.5, and 21.3 DAE, respectively (Fig. 5A and D). Under LD conditions, the flowering time of overexpressed plants Line 3, Line 7, and Line 13 was 38.5, 39.4 and 42 DAE, earlier than that of the wild type (46.1 DAE) (Fig. 5B and F). Furthermore, the overexpression lines matured significantly earlier than wild-type plants, with no significant differences in plant height or node number (Fig. 5C, E and G; Fig. S5).

Additionally, CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing targets were designed for the second exon of GmMADS17 to generate mutant lines. Two homozygous mutants (Gmmads17-1 and Gmmads17-2) were obtained, each carrying a frameshift mutation caused by a 1-bp or 2-bp deletion (Fig. S6A-B), resulting in premature termination of translation and truncated proteins (Fig. S6C). Furthermore, the expression of GmMADS17 in the Gmmads17-1 and Gmmads17-2 was significantly lower than that in wild type plants (Fig. S7). Under SD condition, the Gmmads17-1 and Gmmads17-2 mutants exhibited delayed flowering compared to the wild type (Fig. 6A and B). The flowering delay was more pronounced under LD than under SD conditions, with mutants flowering significantly later by 2.9 and 3.0 days, respectively (Fig. 6D and E). The maturation of Gmmads17-1 and Gmmads17-2 mutants was also delayed compared to WT (Fig. 6C and F). No significant differences in plant height or node number were observed between mutants and wild-type plants (Fig. S8). Taken together, these results demonstrate that GmMADS17 acts as a positive regulator of flowering.

GmMADS17 can interact with GmAP1

Early yeast two-hybrid assays revealed that AGL6 interacts with multiple MADS-box proteins in Arabidopsis, including AP1 [41]. Given the important role of AP1 in floral meristem identity and flowering regulation, we focused on the GmMADS17-GmAP1 interaction and examined whether this regulatory module is conserved in soybean. We first performed yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) assays to test the interaction between GmMADS17 and GmAP1 homologs. Y2H assays showed that GmMADS17 interacted with all four GmAP1 proteins (GmAP1a, GmAP1b, GmAP1c, and GmAP1d) in yeast, as indicated by the growth of transformed colonies on selective medium containing X-α-Gal (Fig. 8A). However, BiFC assays revealed that GmMADS17-cYFP only interacted with GmAP1a-nYFP, GmAP1c-nYFP, and GmAP1d-nYFP (Fig. 8B; Fig. S9). Co-IP assays further confirmed the interactions of GmMADS17 with GmAP1a, GmAP1c, and GmAP1d (Fig. 8C).

GmMADS17 was selected during soybean domestication

Using the SoyOmics database (https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/soyomics/index) [38], we analyzed the nucleotide diversity of a 100-kb genomic region (Chr10:49,400,000–49,500,000; reference genome Zhonghuang 13) on chromosome 10, which harbors GmMADS17. The Wright’s fixation index (Fst) for GmMADS17 gene between wild soybean (Glycine soja) and landraces was 0.42, and between wild soybean and improved cultivars was 0.45. While the Fst between Landraces and Improved cultivars was only 0.008 (Fig. 9A). These results suggest significant genetic differentiation between wild and cultivated soybeans but minimal genetic divergence within cultivated soybeans. The reduced genetic diversity between wild soybeans and landraces was 0.68, and that between wild soybeans and improved cultivars was 0.70. While the reduced genetic diversity between landraces and improved cultivars was only 0.08 (Fig. 9B). This indicates that during domestication from wild soybeans to landraces and improved cultivars, the genetic diversity in the GmMADS17 region significantly decreased.

Next, we examined the distribution of the two haplotypes (Fig. 1E) in the wild soybeans, landraces and improved cultivars (Fig. 9C). In the wild soybean, the percentage of Hap1 and Hap2 were 61.96% and 38.04% respectively. However, in the landrace and improved cultivars, the percentage of Hap2 frequencies rose dramatically to 93.08% and 96.25%, respectively (Fig. 9C).

By analyzing GmMADS17 allele distribution across different ecological regions in China (Fig. 9D), we found that among wild soybean populations, the frequency of Hap2 increased with latitude: the early-flowering associated genotype Hap2 accounted for 20% of individuals in the Southern Region (SR), 28.57% in the North and Huang-Huai Regions (NR/HR), and 45.83% in the Northeastern Region (NE). With the domestication, both landrace and improved soybean accessions showed a significantly higher frequency of Hap2 compared to wild soybeans across all three regions, establishing it as the dominant haplotype. Especially in the NE region, 97.86% of the landraces accessions and 99.37% of the improved cultivars carried the Hap2 allele.

Discussion

MADS-box transcription factors are critical regulators of plant growth and development. Although members of the AGL6 subfamily have been reported to participate in flowering control in plants such as Arabidopsis, wheat and cotton [17, 20, 21], their functional roles in soybean remain unexplored. In this study, we identified a AGL6 subfamily gene, GmMADS17 through transcriptome profiling of two extreme-flowering recombinant inbred lines. 3 evidences supported its potential role in flowering regulation: GmMADS17 was significantly down-regulated in the late-flowering RIL line; a non-synonymous SNP in its coding sequences (CDS) defined two haplotypes, with Hap2 associated with early flowering and maturation in natural populations; and ectopic expression of GmMADS17 in Arabidopsis promoted early flowering (Fig. 1). Notably, the two parental lines, Qihuang 34 and Jidou 17, carry the same haplotype at the coding SNP locus, indicating that this polymorphism does not segregate within the RIL population. Therefore, the flowering time difference between QJ355 and QJ534 likely reflects regulatory variants in the promoter or other cis-acting regions of GmMADS17, which in turn may contribute to the flowering time variation. Future studies, such as promoter sequence analysis and promoter activity assays, will be required to identify the causal regulatory variants underlying this expression variation.

To elucidate the biological function of GmMADS17, we generated GmMADS17-overexpressing (OE) transgenic lines and CRISPR/Cas9-mediated mutants. The OE lines exhibited a significantly earlier flowering time compared to the wild-type (WT) controls (Fig. 5). Conversely, the Gmmads17 mutants displayed a late-flowering phenotype (Fig. 6). These results demonstrate that GmMADS17 acts as a positive regulator of flowering time in soybean. Recently, Wang et al. conducted genome-wide association analyses (GWAS) of reproductive period length (RPL) in soybean [42]. We noticed that GmMADS17 was in the region of the QTL and might be a promising candidate gene. However, its function hadn’t been verified. In this study, the early-flowering GmMADS17-overexpressing plants also displayed earlier maturation (Fig. 5). Our reverse genetic functional characterization and the forward genetic mapping [42] solidly confirm GmMADS17 as a regulator in flowering and maturation time in soybean.

Expression analysis revealed that the transcript level of GmMADS17 in the shoot apex gradually increased and peaked at the flowering stage, suggesting its functional importance in shoot apex. Furthermore, the expression of GmMADS17 in shoot apex was induced under short-day (SD) conditions compared to long-day (LD) conditions (Fig. 3C and E). This photoperiod-responsive expression pattern is similar to that of the floral promoter GmFT2a in leaves [43, 44], but is opposite to that of the floral repressor GmFT1a [45]. We observed that GmMADS17 expression was significantly upregulated in GmFT2a-OE lines but downregulated in GmFT1a-OE lines [45] (Fig. S10). This result along with the expression of GmMADS17 in shoot apex indicated a potential association between GmMADS17 and FT homologs.

MADS-box proteins generally interact with themselves or other proteins to form homodimer or heterodimer for performing their functions [46]. In Arabidopsis, AGL6 interacts with multiple MADS-box proteins, for example AP1, SEP1 and SEP3, as revealed by yeast two-hybrid assays [41]. Similarly, IiAGL6 in Isatis indigotica was reported to interact with IiAP1, IiAG, IiSHP2, IiSTK, IiSEP1, IiSEP2, IiFUL and other MADS-box proteins [23]. In soybean, GmAP1 homologs have been most extensively characterized among MADS-box family members and are known to play essential roles in flowering regulation [28, 31, 47]. Here, we confirmed that GmMADS17 interacts with GmAP1a, GmAP1c, and GmAP1d, suggesting a potential conserved interaction between AGL6 and AP1 homologs in plants. Although Y2H assays suggested an interaction between GmMADS17 and GmAP1b, this was not confirmed by BiFC. The discrepancy may be due to the intrinsic differences between the two systems. Y2H can occasionally produce false positives due to protein overexpression in a heterologous yeast environment, whereas BiFC is performed in living plant cells and is more physiologically relevant. Additionally, differences in expression levels or subcellular localization between GmAP1b and GmAP1a/c/d in plant cells may also affect the accessibility of the interaction. By measuring the expression of flowering-related genes, several genes which were influenced by GmAP1s (GmAG、GmFUL1a, GmFUL1b, GmFUL2b, GmFDc and GmPI) [28] were also upregulated in the GmMADS17 overexpressing plant, but down regulated in the Gmmads17 mutant plants (Fig. 7). We speculate that these genes may represent potential downstream targets of GmMADS17 and GmAP1s complex.

Our study also revealed that the expression levels of GmAP1 homologs (GmAP1a, b, c and d) were significantly changed in both overexpression and mutation of GmMADS17 plants (Fig. 7). Based on this finding and the observed protein interactions between GmMADS17 and GmAP1a, GmAP1c, and GmAP1d, it is possible that GmMADS17 not only physically interacts with these GmAP1 homologs but also modulates their expression, either directly or indirectly (Fig S12). For GmAP1b, although it does not physically interact with GmMADS17, its expression was still affected in GmMADS17 overexpression and mutant lines (Fig. 7). We speculate that GmMADS17 might influence the expression of GmAP1b through the formation of protein complexes with other partners. However, the precise mechanisms underlying these transcriptional regulations require further investigation.

The domestication of cultivated soybean from its wild progenitor in China approximately 6,000–9,000 years ago was involved in strong selection for key agronomic traits [48–50]. Early flowering and reduced photoperiod sensitivity are key traits that distinguish domesticated crops from their wild progenitors [51], and flowering phenology has been recognized as a core domestication trait in soybean [52]. In this study, the population genetic analyses revealed a strong selective sweep at the GmMADS17 locus, characterized by high genetic differentiation (Fst) between wild and cultivated accessions and a pronounced reduction in nucleotide diversity in the cultivated germplasm (Fig. 9A and B).

We identified two haplotypes (Hap1 and Hap2) in the coding region of GmMADS17, with Hap2 being significantly associated with earlier flowering and maturation (Fig. 1E and F). Hap1 was found at a moderately higher frequency than Hap2 in wild soybeans. However, Hap2 was nearly fixed in landraces and cultivars, indicating its strong selection during domestication. Furthermore, in the wild soybeans, the frequency of Hap2 gradually increased from low to high latitudes, indicating that GmMADS17-Hap2 might have progressively adapted to the shorter growing seasons in high-latitude areas as a result of the natural selection. In contrast, among cultivated soybeans, Hap2 was fixed at a uniformly high frequency (exceeding 88% in all ecological regions), regardless of their geographic origin (Fig. 9D). This demonstrates that strong artificial selection for early flowering and maturity during domestication overrode the pre-existing pattern of natural adaptation. The near-complete fixation of this early-flowering associated haplotype indicates that early flowering was a key target of selection during soybean domestication. These findings suggest that the selection of GmMADS17-Hap2 might contributed to the broad ecological adaptation of cultivated soybean, thereby facilitating its expansion across diverse latitudinal ranges.

Conclusions

Our study identified a MADS-box gene GmMADS17 as a flowering promoter in soybean and revealed its interaction with three GmAP1 proteins (GmAP1a, GmAP1c and GmAP1d). Furthermore, GmMADS17 may had undergone strong selection during soybean domestication. These findings provide both mechanistic insights into flowering regulation and valuable genetic targets for molecular breeding aimed at enhancing soybean adaptation.
