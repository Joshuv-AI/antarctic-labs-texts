NEAT1 modulates the TIRR/53BP1 complex to maintain genome integrity
Authors: Susan Kilgas, Aleem Syed, Patrick Toolan-Kerr, Michelle L. Swift, Shrabasti Roychoudhury, Aniruddha Sarkar, Sarah Wilkins, Mikayla Quigley, Anna R. Poetsch, Maria Victoria Botuyan, Gaofeng Cui, Georges Mer, Jernej Ule, Pascal Drané, Dipanjan Chowdhury
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Tudor Interacting Repair Regulator (TIRR) is an RNA-binding protein (RBP) that interacts directly with 53BP1, restricting its access to DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs) and its association with p53. We utilized iCLIP to identify RNAs that directly bind to TIRR within cells, identifying the long non-coding RNA NEAT1 as the primary RNA partner. The high affinity of TIRR for NEAT1 is due to prevalent G-rich motifs in the short isoform (NEAT1_1) region of NEAT1. This interaction destabilizes the TIRR/53BP1 complex, promoting 53BP1’s function. NEAT1_1 is enriched during the G1 phase of the cell cycle, thereby ensuring that TIRR-dependent inhibition of 53BP1’s function is cell cycle-dependent. TDP-43, an RBP that is implicated in neurodegenerative diseases, modulates the TIRR/53BP1 complex by promoting the production of the NEAT1 short isoform, NEAT1_1. Together, we infer that NEAT1_1, and factors regulating NEAT1_1, may impact 53BP1-dependent DNA repair processes, with implications for a spectrum of diseases.

Introduction

The p53-binding protein 1 (53BP1) is pivotal in regulating the balance between competing DNA double-strand break (DSB) repair pathways to maintain genomic stability1,2. 53BP1 has been shown to regulate the choice between non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) and homologous recombination (HR) DSB repair pathways by counteracting the function of BRCA1 in the HR pathway3,4. The balance between the 53BP1-BRCA1 axis modulates DSB repair pathway choice in dividing cells and dramatically impacts PARP inhibitor sensitivity5,6. We and others showed that Tudor Interacting Repair Regulator (TIRR) directly interacts with the Tudor domain of 53BP17–9 to prevent its interaction with the histone H4 dimethylated at lysine K20 (H4K20me2)10,11, thereby regulating the access of 53BP1 to DSB sites. Aside from its role in DSB repair, 53BP1 also interacts with and influences the gene transactivation function of p5312,13 through its tandem Tudor and tandem BRCT domains14–16. We demonstrated that the depletion of endogenous TIRR results in an ectopic increase in p53’s gene transactivation capability, and this effect is reliant on 53BP117. Furthermore, in mice, loss of TIRR prevents the generation of p53 proficient tumors18. Overall, TIRR regulates both the DNA damage-dependent and -independent functions of 53BP1.

TIRR is an RNA-binding protein (RBP) that binds RNA with high affinity19–21. Previously, we identified a loop region within TIRR that binds 53BP17 and has overlapping binding sites with RNAs7,20. This indicated that RNAs could compete with 53BP1 for TIRR binding. RNAs regulate various aspects of cellular functions, including maintenance of genome integrity22,23, and noncoding RNAs have been implicated in 53BP1 recruitment to DNA DSBs24–26. We, therefore, hypothesized that riboregulation of TIRR by specific RNAs can modulate the TIRR/53BP1 complex with potential downstream consequences on 53BP1’s function in DNA end resection and DSB repair pathway choice. To test this hypothesis, we proceeded to identify direct RNA-binding sites of TIRR in U2OS cells using individual-nucleotide resolution crosslinking and immunoprecipitation (iCLIP)27. We identified the G-rich sequence motifs enriched at the TIRR-bound RNA sites, and showed that the lncRNA NEAT1 short isoform region, NEAT1_1, contains many such motifs and thereby binds TIRR with high affinity. NEAT1 binding destabilizes the TIRR/53BP1 complex, thereby affecting both the DSB-dependent and -independent functions of 53BP1. NEAT1_1 is mostly present in G1 phase of the cell cycle28, and we showed that this ensures the cell cycle-dependency of TIRR/53BP1 complex dissociation, and consequently 53BP1’s availability in G1 for DSB repair29,30. Finally, we showed that the RNA splicing factor, transactive response DNA-binding protein 43 kDa (TDP-43), implicated in neurodegenerative diseases and known to promote NEAT1_1 production31,32, is hypostatic with NEAT1_1 with regards to 53BP1 function. TDP-43 loss phenocopies the impact of NEAT1_1 loss on 53BP1-dependent DSB repair functions as well as p53 transactivation. Together, we show that the TDP-43-NEAT1_1 axis regulates the TIRR/53BP1 complex for the maintenance of genome integrity.

Results

The LncRNAs NEAT1 and MALAT1 are the main RNA targets of TIRR

To better understand how RNAs can impact the function of TIRR, we used iCLIP to identify the specific RNA targets of TIRR at a global scale. The complexes formed by TIRR and its RNA targets were pulled-down from UV-crosslinked U2OS cells stably transfected with a Flag-tagged version of TIRR (+TIRR) or an empty vector (+EV) and separated by SDS-PAGE (Fig. 1a). The RNA components of this complex (Fig. 1a, left panel), causing a mobility shift of TIRR toward higher molecular weight (Fig. 1a, right panel), were isolated and processed for high-throughput sequencing. TIRR bound to RNAs from different genomic regions, including coding genes (57.2%), long noncoding RNA (lncRNA) genes (23.2%), or intergenic regions (18.9%). The majority of the iCLIP crosslink sites mapped to introns from coding genes (44.6%) or lncRNAs (16.5%), while exons contributed only 3.9% of the iCLIP reads (Fig. 1b). This indicated that a large proportion of TIRR-RNA interactions occurred within the proximity of the chromatin. A large majority of the TIRR/53BP1 complex is in the nuclear soluble fraction. Therefore, the RNA binding TIRR in the context of chromatin was unlikely to be modulating the TIRR/53BP1 complex. Gene length comparison between TIRR-iCLIP enriched genes and non-enriched genes showed that TIRR has an overall binding preference for longer transcripts (Supplementary Fig. 1a). Additionally, GO-term analysis of TIRR-bound RNAs showed strong enrichment for terms related to Polymerase II (Pol II) RNA transcription and chromatin remodeling, which is in line with previous literature outlining that many nuclear RBPs regulate chromatin structure and transcription (Supplementary Fig. 1b)20. By comparing TIRR iCLIP data to RNA-seq data from the same cells, we identified NEAT1 and MALAT1 as the most enriched TIRR interactors (Fig. 1c). We selected these two lncRNAs as likely candidates to modulate the formation of the TIRR/53BP1 complex since they are abundant, nuclear, ubiquitously expressed, conserved across mammalian species, and have been shown to be critical for multiple cellular processes, including protein-protein interactions33–36. NEAT1 and MALAT1 are generally found in paraspeckles and nuclear speckles, respectively – two nuclear bodies containing RNAs, RBPs, and splicing factors33,37–41. However, these lncRNAs can also reside in the soluble nucleoplasm where the TIRR/53BP1 complex forms, as exemplified by the short ∼3.7 kb isoform of NEAT1_142. The iCLIP data indicated that TIRR binding was much more enriched in the region of the NEAT1 gene corresponding to the short NEAT1_1 isoform, as compared to the remaining gene that is specific for the long NEAT1_2 isoform, whereas MALAT1 binding peaks were more spread out across the gene (Fig. 1d; Supplementary Fig. 1c). To confirm that TIRR indeed bound to the NEAT1_1 region and not the paraspeckle-specific long ∼22.7 kb NEAT1_2 region, we used the crosslink-IP steps from the iCLIP protocol followed by qPCR, with primers targeting either the 5’ portion of NEAT1 (common to both isoforms), or the 3’ portion specific to NEAT1_2. In U2OS cells, TIRR bound more than 30% of the total NEAT1 population, yet only 1.4% of the NEAT1_2 population at a similar level to the HPRT1 (0.6%) negative control (Fig. 1e), supporting the idea that TIRR preferably binds the soluble nucleoplasmic NEAT1_1, rather than the paraspeckle component NEAT1_2. This observation was also validated in mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs) (Supplementary Fig. 1d). NEAT1 dissociates the TIRR/53BP1 complex in vivo and in vitro

To study whether NEAT1 and/or MALAT1 regulate the TIRR/53BP1 complex stability, we set up a Proximity Ligation Assay (PLA) to investigate the TIRR/53BP1 interaction in an endogenous context. In the absence of a commercially available antibody to detect the native protein, we developed a specific nanobody against TIRR (Supplementary Fig. 2a–d). Using X-ray crystallography for 3D structure determination for a list of 10 potential candidates, we selected one nanobody recognizing a motif outside the TIRR-53BP1 interface to ensure it detects TIRR complexed to 53BP1 in PLA (Supplementary Fig. 2d and Supplementary Table 1). The specificity of this nanobody and its ability to detect the TIRR/53BP1 complex was confirmed using immunofluorescence (IF) assays (Supplementary Fig. 2b, c) and PLA (Supplementary Fig. 2e, f), respectively. Using CRISPR interference (CRISPRi), we found that NEAT1-depleted cells showed a significant increase in the number of TIRR/53BP1 PLA foci when compared to control (CT) cells (Fig. 1f, g). A similar result was observed in Neat1−/− MEFs (Supplementary Fig. 2g–i) and using three different antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) to knockdown NEAT1 in U2OS cells (Supplementary Fig. 2j–l), thus suggesting that loss of NEAT1 promotes the formation of the TIRR/53BP1 complex. The increase in PLA signal was not due to a change in 53BP1 or TIRR protein expression as shown by Western blot (Fig. 1h). In contrast to NEAT1, loss of MALAT1 did not significantly affect the stability of the TIRR/53BP1 complex (Fig. 1f), underscoring a specific role of NEAT1 in the regulation of the formation of the complex.

Because NEAT1 has been shown to impact various cellular processes including transcription36, we investigated whether NEAT1 modulates TIRR/53BP1 complex stability via its direct interaction with TIRR. To understand whether specific sequence properties of NEAT1_1 explain its efficient binding to TIRR, we performed analysis of sequence motifs that are enriched in vicinity of TIRR crosslink sites across the transcriptome using the PEKA software43. Of the top-ranking 10 motifs (Supplementary Fig. 3a), 8 were G-rich and were broadly enriched around crosslink sites, while the two motifs enriched directly at crosslink sites were more U-rich but contained Gs, which is expected based on the common U-bias at crosslink sites43. To test whether the G-rich sequences were responsible for TIRR-NEAT1_1 interaction, we selected two 42 nucleotide (nt)-long fragments from NEAT1_1 for further in vitro binding studies, such that one fragment was G-rich and derived from the region within a TIRR-binding peak in the iCLIP data (Positive-RNA, Fig. 2e and Supplementary Table 2), and the other was located outside an iCLIP peak and lacked G-rich motifs (Negative-RNA, Fig. 2e and Supplementary Table 2). Using fluorescence polarization (FP) assay, we showed that recombinant TIRR binds the Positive-RNA with a 350 nM binding constant (95% CI: 290–425 nM) (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Fig. 3b, c), which is about 2–3-fold tighter than the affinity previously reported for TIRR-53BP1 Tudor domain9,10. We then assessed the capacity of either unlabeled Positive-RNA or Negative-RNA to disrupt the fluorescein amidite (FAM)-NEAT1-RNA/TIRR complex in an FP-based competition assay. The G-rich Positive-RNA fragment could disrupt FAM-NEAT1-RNA/TIRR complex as measured by decrease in the fluorescence polarizability of the FAM-NEAT1-RNA with the IC50 of 740 nM (95% CI: 630-980 nM), but no change was seen with increasing doses of Negative-RNA (Fig. 2g). We used the same method to also test if 53BP1 could compete with FAM-NEAT1-RNA for binding to TIRR by titrating the 53BP1-Tudor domain into the FAM-NEAT1-RNA/TIRR complex. We observed a dose-dependent decrease in the fluorescence polarization of FAM-NEAT1-RNA due to disruption of the FAM-NEAT1-RNA/TIRR complex with the IC50 of 2.3 μm (95% CI: 1.7–2.8 μm) (Fig. 2h). Unlike WT 53BP1, we found that the previously characterized F1553R mutant7 that does not interact with TIRR did not disrupt the FAM-NEAT1-RNA/TIRR complex, further supporting the observations that 53BP1 and NEAT1 compete for TIRR binding (Supplementary Fig. 3d–f).

In summary, our iCLIP data clearly demonstrated that TIRR had a specific affinity for G-rich sequences, which were abundant in the NEAT1_1 isoform. Our in vitro experiments further supported this observation, revealing that even a single 42 nt G-rich fragment of NEAT1_1 bound to TIRR with greater affinity compared to 53BP1. Therefore, the G-rich binding preference of TIRR provided a clear explanation for why NEAT1_1 was highly effective in outcompeting 53BP1 for binding to TIRR, both in in vitro assays and in vivo.

TIRR RBD mutants interfere with NEAT1 binding to TIRR

Next, we sought to identify specific TIRR RBD mutants that interfere with NEAT1-dependent TIRR/53BP1 complex dissociation and consequently 53BP1’s recruitment to DNA damage sites. We first selected putative RNA interacting surface residues that could either interact with the phosphate backbone (e.g., Lys, Arg) or the nucleobases (e.g., Phe, Tyr) and are not participating in any intra/inter-molecular interactions from the crystal structure of the TIRR-dimer. We grouped the selected residues into five different sets to mutate simultaneously based on their proximity on the TIRR surface to assess their downstream impact (Fig. 2i). Next, we performed structural prediction for a TIRR-RNA binding model using a recently developed machine learning-based RoseTTAFold-NA44 combined with molecular dynamic (MD) simulation. For the structure prediction, we selected a 13-nt long GGGAUGAGGGUGA from the Positive-RNA sequence to test the complex formation with TIRR. The resulting TIRR-RNA model shows that the residues from Set 1 and Set 5 fall in close proximity to the predicted RNA-binding region in TIRR (Fig. 2j, k). To test our prediction, these residues were mutated to alanine in groups (see Fig. 2i) and introduced into TIRR KO RPE-1 cells stably overexpressing NEAT1_1 to monitor the TIRR/53BP1 complex formation. As NEAT1_1 OE dissociates the TIRR/53BP1 complex, we anticipated RBD mutants that do not bind NEAT1_1 to have significantly more TIRR/53BP1 complex formation, compared to TIRR WT or mutants that have retained the ability to bind NEAT1_1. We found that expression of Set 1 (F41, R43, R55, F56, K151) and Set 5 (R55, F56, F41) mutants enhanced the TIRR/53BP1 complex relative to TIRR WT or other sets of mutants (Fig. 2l–n). The R55, F56, and F41 mutations are shared between Set 1 and 5, suggesting that these specific residues could be necessary for NEAT1_1 binding. We confirmed these findings by CLIP-PCR overexpressing either WT TIRR or the 5 sets of RBD mutants in TIRR KO RPE-1 cells (Fig. 2o, p). Whereas WT TIRR bound to around 10% of the total NEAT1 population, binding to NEAT1 was lost in Set 1 and Set 5 mutants. Mutant Sets 2–4 showed decreased binding efficiency compared to WT, whilst retaining binding relative to Set 1 and Set 5 mutants. To further support our findings, we monitored the recruitment of 53BP1 to DSBs under the same conditions (Supplementary Fig. 3g–j). Notably, Set 1 and Set 5 mutants were deficient in the 53BP1 DSB recruitment compared to WT-TIRR, further supporting our observations that NEAT1 is unable to efficiently dissociate mutant TIRR from 53BP1. Note that these mutants did not affect the number of γH2AX foci (Supplementary Fig. 3g, h). Overall, we identified three point mutations in the predicted RNA-binding region of TIRR that interfere with NEAT1-mediated TIRR/53BP1 complex dissociation and impair the recruitment of 53BP1 to DSBs.

NEAT1 impacts 53BP1-dependent p53 activation and PARPi sensitivity

Since loss of TIRR increases 53BP1-dependent gene transactivation property of p5317, we also investigated whether NEAT1 impacts the relationship between 53BP1 and p53. Like TIRR ablation, NEAT1_1 OE caused hyper-activation of a set of p53 target genes in response to the MDM2-inhibitor Nutlin-3 in RPE-1 cells (Fig. 3e). Importantly, this hyper-activation was 53BP1-dependent, as the phenotype was reverted by knocking-down 53BP1 (Fig. 3e, f). Furthermore, a modest but significant decrease in p53 target gene expression was observed in NEAT1 CRISPRi U2OS cells (Supplementary Fig. 4c, d). Similarly to Nutlin-3, IR increased CDKN1A target gene activation in NEAT1_1 OE and TIRR KO RPE-1 cells (Supplementary Fig. 4e, f). However, in contrast to Nutlin-3, IR did not induce the expression of the pro-apoptotic genes BAX and PUMA in the conditions tested (Supplementary Fig. 4e), which we reasoned could be a stress-specific effect on p53 transactivation activity46–49. Collectively, these data indicated that NEAT1_1 indirectly modulates 53BP1-dependent p53 transactivation by interfering with TIRR.

p53 induces cellular senescence and loss of TIRR results in higher levels of senescence due to an aberrant 53BP1-mediated p53-transactivation17. We found that similarly to TIRR KO cells, NEAT1_1 OE caused an increase in senescence-associated genes (ANKRD1, EDN1, and IL-6) and β-galactosidase staining after IR (Fig. 3g and Supplementary Fig. 4g), thus reflecting the impact of NEAT1_1 on 53BP1-dependent p53 activity. Taken together, our results suggested that the cellular concentration of NEAT1 impacts the DNA repair-dependent and -independent functions of 53BP1 by interfering with TIRR.

Next, we studied whether increased 53BP1 recruitment in S phase affected DNA end resection and DSB repair pathway choice. First, we monitored single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) formation with the bromodeoxyuridine (BrdU) assay under native conditions50,51, and observed a decrease in S phase ssDNA foci in NEAT1_1 OE cells (Fig. 5c, d). These results were further validated by the reduced recruitment of the ssDNA-binding protein RPA to DSBs (Fig. 5e, f). Downstream of end resection is RAD51 loading onto ssDNA, which commits the cell to HR-mediated DSB repair52,53. Consistent with our observations on ssDNA formation, NEAT1_1 OE in S phase led to a decrease in the number of RAD51 foci, indicating an HR-repair defect in these cells (Fig. 5g, h).

Next, we monitored whether TDP-43 loss phenocopies NEAT1 loss on DNA end resection. We depleted TDP-43 in either NEAT1 WT or CRISPRi U2OS cells and monitored RPA recruitment to DSBs (Fig. 5i, j). Both TDP-43 and NEAT1 depletion significantly increased RPA recruitment to DSBs, and TDP-43 depletion in NEAT1 CRISPRi cells did not cause an additional increase in the number of RPA foci, further supporting the idea of a hypostatic relationship between TDP-43 and NEAT1. Next, we used the AsiSI endonuclease fused to estrogen receptor (ER-AsiSI) in NEAT1- and TDP-43-depleted cells, and quantified ssDNA generated from a single DSB site (Fig. 5k)54. Upon knockdown of NEAT1 (Fig. 5l, m) or TDP-43 (Fig. 5n and Supplementary Fig. 5m), we observed a significant increase in ssDNA resection track length, which varied between TDP-43-depleted and NEAT1-depleted cells. We propose that these differences were due to the modest KD of NEAT1 in these experiments (Fig. 5m). Overall, our results support a model whereby NEAT1-mediated 53BP1 recruitment to DSBs has downstream consequences on DNA end resection and HR, and that loss of TDP-43 phenocopies NEAT1 loss in promoting ssDNA and end resection.

It has previously been shown that NEAT1_1 is predominantly expressed in G0/G1 phase of the cell cycle28, and we asked whether it impacts the dynamics of the TIRR/53BP1 complex during the cell cycle. We first performed a co-IP from extracts of U2OS cells that were synchronized in different phases of the cell cycle and expressing Flag-TIRR (Fig. 6d), showing that more 53BP1 remains trapped with TIRR following IR in cells synchronized in S phase compared to G1 phase (Fig. 6d). This is consistent with the literature showing that 53BP1 recruitment to DSBs is more efficient in G1 relative to S29,30,55. In line with this, we used quantitative image‐based cytometry (QIBC), a high‐throughput microscopy approach,30,55–58 in TIRR WT and KO conditions and showed that in WT conditions, the number of nuclear 53BP1 foci gradually declined as the cells progressed through S phase as previously observed (Supplementary Fig. 6b–d)30,55. In comparison, 53BP1 foci formation was more prominent in TIRR KO cells and remained constant throughout the cell cycle, showing that TIRR limits 53BP1 access to DNA lesions, especially in the S phase of the cell cycle (Supplementary Fig. 6b–d).

To study the role of NEAT1 in this process, we used dual-probe RNA-FISH approach and demonstrated that NEAT1_2 expression remained unchanged during the cell cycle while NEAT1_1 was high in G1, decreasing significantly after S phase entry (Fig. 6e, f). We confirmed that the paraspeckle-associated NEAT1_2 co-localized with the paraspeckle component NONO throughout the cell cycle28,39,41,59, whereas there was significantly less co-localization between NONO and NEAT1_1, with co-localization being the lowest in G1, then gradually increasing as cells enter S phase (Supplementary Fig. 6e). This is in line with previous findings showing that the NEAT1_1 short isoform is mostly expressed in G1 phase where it resides in the nucleoplasm away from paraspeckles28,60. As cells enter S phase, more NEAT1 is processed into the long NEAT1_2 isoform, thereby leading to an increase in co-localization pattern with NONO (Supplementary Fig 6e).

The dynamics of the TIRR/53BP1 complex formation during the cell cycle was next studied by combining QIBC and PLA. There was a detectable increase in the amount of the TIRR/53BP1 complex in S phase when compared to G1 phase (Fig. 6g, h and Supplementary Fig. 6f, g). As anticipated by the inverse correlation between the amount of TIRR/53BP1 and NEAT1, depleting NEAT1 abolished the cell cycle-dependent dynamic of the TIRR/53BP1 complex, and the amount of this complex remained elevated throughout the cell cycle (Fig. 6g, h). Finally, we wanted to understand whether loss of NEAT1 would lead to cell cycle changes in the context of DNA damage, which could influence the TIRR/53BP1 complex formation. We observed no significant changes in the cell cycle distribution between WT and NEAT1 CRISPRi U2OS cells (Supplementary Fig. 6h, i), demonstrating that NEAT1 deficiency itself is not causing cell cycle changes that influence the TIRR/53BP1 complex formation. While it is well established that exogenous TIRR inhibits 53BP1’s access to DSBs10, our results here suggest that in a physiological context this inhibition occurs in S phase when 53BP1 has been shown to be kept away from DNA damage sites to avoid interference with HR (Fig. 6i)1,30,61.

Discussion

We employed iCLIP in conjunction with high-throughput sequencing to comprehensively identify TIRR-RNA binding sites, which identified NEAT1 lncRNA as the primary binding partner. We subsequently found that NEAT1 plays a pivotal role in destabilizing the TIRR/53BP1 complex both in vivo and in vitro. We found that NEAT1 decreases TIRR/53BP1 complex formation pre- and post-damage, thus enhancing 53BP1 availability. NEAT1 loss increased TIRR/53BP1 complex formation both pre- and post-damage, which decreased the availability of 53BP1, and thereby the damage-induced formation of 53BP1 foci. Thus, although NEAT1 did not directly impact the dissociation of the complex following IR-induced damage, it achieved its impact indirectly by controlling the overall formation of TIRR/53BP1 complex formation pre- and post-damage.

It was recently shown that hairpin-like RNA structures lead to the dissociation of the TIRR/53BP1 complex in a Pol II and DNA damage-dependent manner62. However, the observed binding affinity of such hairpin RNAs with TIRR was weak, raising the question of how specific RNAs could act as TIRR regulators. Additionally, the frequency and origin of hairpin RNAs generated in response to DSBs remain a contentious issue63. Here, we demonstrated that TIRR has a strong preference for binding G-rich motifs across the transcriptome, and such motifs are very common in NEAT1_1, explaining the high specificity of TIRR for this lncRNA. Moreover, in vitro analyses showed that a G-rich portion of NEAT1_1 has a higher affinity to TIRR than 53BP1, explaining how NEAT1_1 can regulate the TIRR/53BP1 complex. We identified point mutations within the predicted RBD of TIRR that interfered with NEAT1_1 mediated TIRR/53BP1 complex dissociation and had functional consequences on 53BP1 localization. Therefore, we inferred that RNA can act with high specificity to regulate TIRR in a DNA damage-independent, but cell cycle-dependent, manner with downstream functional consequences on 53BP1’s activity.

TIRR binds to NEAT1_1, which is mostly expressed in G1 phase and degraded by the RNA exosome complex during S phase28,64. This aligns with the dynamics of 53BP1 recruitment to DSBs that gradually declines in S phase55. Here we demonstrate that NEAT1_1 is responsible for destabilizing the TIRR/53BP1 complex more specifically in G1. We present a model depicting the implication of these new findings for the physiological role of TIRR in the regulation of 53BP1 activity (Fig. 6i). We propose that TIRR participates in limiting the access of 53BP1 to DSBs primarily in S phase, as the negative effect of NEAT1_1 on stability of the TIRR/53BP1 complex is de facto restricted to G1. We included BRCA1 into our model as it is known to negatively regulate NEAT165, and BRCA1 expression typically occurs in S phase. Considering the antagonistic relationship between BRCA1 and 53BP1, the interplay between NEAT1_1, BRCA1 and the TIRR/53BP1 complex will be worth further investigation.

As 53BP1’s recruitment to DSBs has downstream consequences on DNA end resection and repair pathway choice66, we studied how NEAT1-mediated regulation of the TIRR/53BP1 complex affects these processes. Several lncRNAs have been shown to function in the maintenance of genome stability, including affecting DSB repair pathway choice between HR and NHEJ67–69. Here, we showed that NEAT1_1 OE in S phase cells causes aberrant accumulation of 53BP1 on DSBs, leading to defects in ssDNA formation and DNA end resection, as well as downstream defects in RAD51 accumulation, indicative of impaired HR52,53. Conversely, we showed that loss of NEAT1 increased end resection, which we propose is due to increased formation of the TIRR/53BP1 complex, leading to decreased 53BP1 recruitment to DSBs. Overall, we demonstrate how NEAT1 not only affects the dynamics of TIRR/53BP1 complex formation but has downstream consequences on DNA end resection and DSB repair pathway choice.

The transcription and maturation of NEAT1 are both dynamically and tightly regulated by specific stress signals and various splicing factors that intricately influence the expression of NEAT1 isoforms across the cell cycle32,34,70–72. For example, TDP-43 promotes NEAT1_1 by enhancing its 3’ end processing, HNRNPK promotes NEAT1_2 by repressing NEAT1_1 3’ end processing71, and RBM10 governs NEAT1 alternative splicing to downregulate NEAT1_2 production70. Additionally, p53 and BRCA1 both modulate NEAT1 expression65,73. To establish the relevance of such regulatory circuits to the NEAT1-TIRR/53BP1 axis, we opted to explore the consequences of depleting TDP-43. This depletion leads to a reduction in NEAT1_1 production and an increase in the stability of the TIRR/53BP1 complex. This observation holds particular relevance for neurodegenerative diseases characterized by TDP-43 aggregation and its expulsion from cell nuclei, a phenomenon associated with heightened production of the longer NEAT1_2 isoform74. Interestingly, TDP-43 was previously found to be recruited to sites of DNA damage and to promote NHEJ DNA repair through an unknown mechanism: activity that was disrupted by TDP-43 mutants associated with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS)75. Moreover, 53BP1 has been found mislocalized in ALS patient samples76. Here, we showed that loss of TDP-43, similarly to NEAT1, promotes ssDNA generation and DNA end resection. These results are in line with other previous studies on TDP-43’s function in NHEJ75 counteracting long-range resection, and we speculate this mechanism could be through the NEAT1-TIRR/53BP1 regulatory axis. Furthermore, it was shown that expression of NEAT1_1 can ameliorate the toxicity of TDP-43 proteinopathy31, which strengthens our observations on the hypostatic relationship between TDP-43 and NEAT1. Finally, studies have implicated a role for TDP-43 in p53-dependent processes, although the underlying mechanism has remained unclear77–79. Here, we showed that in addition to the DSB repair functions of 53BP1, TDP-43 similarly phenocopies NEAT1_1 in the regulation of p53 transactivation and cellular senescence. Importantly, NEAT1 is a p53 target gene, and p53 has been shown to regulate NEAT1 expression with downstream consequences on p53 activation itself and consequently on tumorigenesis73,80–83. For example, p53 induces NEAT1 expression to promote the formation of paraspeckles, which lessens replication stress-associated DNA damage and p53 activation, demonstrating an autoregulatory negative feedback loop between p53 and NEAT1 that consequently modulates cancer formation in mice80. Finally, while we’ve shown the relevance of the NEAT1_1 short isoform in regulating the TIRR/53BP1 complex using methods like TDP-43 overexpression and depletion, RNA FISH, and NEAT1_1 overexpression, exclusively deleting NEAT1_1 short isoform in cells is a technical challenge which is beyond the scope of this study. Thus, we cannot formally exclude the possibility that both NEAT1 isoforms may mediate the observed effects on the TIRR/53BP1 complex. Taken together, we propose that the various factors governing NEAT1 expression and maturation likely influence the formation of the TIRR/53BP1 complex, with broad implications for the DNA repair process and genome stability in general.

Methods

Cell culture

RPE-1 and U2OS cell lines were grown in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) (Thermo Fisher) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Thermo Fisher). MEF Neat1−/− cells were a generous gift of Dr. Kannanganattu V. Prasanth (University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign). COV362 and MEF cells were cultured in high glucose DMEM (Thermo Fisher) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Thermo Fisher).

To generate the cells overexpressing NEAT1_1, HEK293T cells were transfected with viral packaging plasmids and full-length NEAT1_1 (kind gift of Dr. Kannanganattu V. Prasanth) cloned into the pOZ vector. Retroviral particles were collected after 48 h and used to infect U2OS or RPE-1 cells. NEAT1_1 expressing cells were selected using magnetic CD25-Dyna beads (Thermo Fisher). All cell lines were routinely tested for mycoplasma. All the cell lines and constructs used in this study are included in Supplementary Data 2.

CRISPR-Cas9 assays

CRISPR interference (CRISPRi) was used to knock-down the expression of NEAT1 and MALAT1. Specific guides (Supplementary Data 3) targeting the promoter region of NEAT1 and MALAT1 were chosen using CRISPick (https://portals.broadinstitute.org/gppx/crispick/public). The guides were synthetized (IDT) and cloned into the pLC040 vector (kind gift of Dr. Kaimeng Huang, Dana-Farber Cancer Institute). HEK293T cells were next transfected with the pLC040 constructs and lentivirus packaging plasmids. Cells were infected and selected using 1 μg/mL of puromycin for 3 days.

To knock-in U2OS cells, 80,000 cells were plated in 24 wells in 500 μL. The next day, 5 μL of the crRNA and tracrRNA (100 μM) (IDT) was mixed in a PCR tube for a final crRNA:tracrRNA duplex concentration of 100 pmol/μL. The duplex was annealed using a thermocycler (95 °C for 5 min, 95 °C to 78 °C with –2 °C/second ramp rate, 78 °C for 10 min, 78 °C to 25 °C with –0.1 °C/second ramp rate, 25 °C for 5 min). The cells were next transfected using CRISPRMAX kit (Thermo Fisher). Briefly, 25 μL Optimem, 1250 ng (7.5 pmol) Truecut Cas9 v2.0 (1 μg/μL) (Thermo Fisher) diluted in Optimem (Gibco), 240 ng (7.5 pmol) of the mix crRNA:tracrRNA, 100 pmol of a single-stranded oligodeoxynucleotide (SSODN), and 2.5 μL Cas9 PLUS Reagent were added in a 1.5 mL tube. In a second tube, 1.5 μL Lipofectamine CRISPRMAX reagent was diluted in 25 μL Optimem (Gibco), and preincubated for 1 minute at room temperature. The diluted Lipofectamine CRISPRMAX Reagent was next mixed by pipetting with the gRNA/Opti-MEM I solution and incubated for 10 min at room temperature. 50 μL of the transfection complex were finally added to the plated cells (500 μL) containing 20 μM HDR enhancer (IDT). The next day the medium was replaced, and the efficiency of the transfection was checked at 550 nm. The following day, cells were trypsinized and plated onto a 15 cm cell culture dish. Clones were screened by immunofluorescence using Flag antibody (see Supplementary Data 2).

Immunoprecipitation and western blotting

Cells were collected, washed, and lysed for 30 min in a buffer containing 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.65), 250 mM NaCl, 0.5% NP-40, 5 mM EDTA, 5% glycerol and protease inhibitors (Roche). 30 μg of lysates measured by Bradford assay were loaded into precast gels (Nu-PAGE Invitrogen, 4%–12%). For immunoprecipitation, 500 μg of extract was incubated on a roller for 16 h at 4 °C with anti-Flag-agarose (Sigma). Resins were washed five times with TGN buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.65), 150 mM NaCl, 3 mM MgCl2, 10% glycerol, 0.01% NP-40) before elution in 0.1 M glycine (pH 2.9). Eluted proteins were analysed by immunoblotting.

Gene overexpression and knockdown

For each transfection, 1-2 μg of plasmid DNA was incubated at RT with Lipofectamine 2000 transfection reagent (Thermo Fisher #11668019) in 0.2 mL Opti-MEM (Fisher Scientific) for 15 min at a 1:3 plasmid-to-Lipofectamine ratio and added to cells cultured in a 6-well plate. Media was changed 6 h after transfection and transfection efficiency was assessed after 24-48 h of incubation.

For siRNA-mediated KD, the siRNA sequences for control and indicated genes were transfected into cells using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Thermo Fisher) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. KD efficiency was assessed 48 h after transfection. For ASO-mediated KD, 10 nM of each ASO was incubated in 4 μL Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Thermo Fisher) in 0.2 mL Opti-MEM (Fisher Scientific) and added to cells cultured in a 6-well plate. Lipofectamine RNAiMAX was incubated in Opti-MEM for 5 min before adding to each ASO in OptiMEM for an additional 10 min. KD efficiency was assessed 48 h after transfection. All siRNA and ASO sequences used in the study are listed in the Supplementary Data 3.

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from cells using RNeasy mini kit (QIAGEN) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. 1–5 μg of total RNA was used to generate cDNA using SuperScript III First-strand synthesis system (Invitrogen). For qPCR, relevant primers were mixed with 2X SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems). All qPCR primers used in the study are listed in Supplementary Data 3. For p53 pathway genes (CKDN1A (p21), BAX, PUMA, MDM2), RPE-1 and U2OS cells of indicated genotype were plated and treated with 4 μM Nutlin-3 (Santa Cruz) over 4 h the following day. Relative gene expression was calculated according to the ΔCt method.

RNA-seq differential gene expression analyses

Kallisto84 (version 0.44.0) was used to pseudoalign paired-end sequencing reads to the transcriptome and produce estimated expression abundance for each transcript. GENCODE85 (release 29) was used as the reference transcriptome annotation. Next, the tximport R package86 (version 1.10) was used to aggregate the transcript-level abundance results from all samples and produce the gene-level estimated expression counts. These estimated counts were corrected for a potential bias associated with changes in average transcript length across samples. Genes were filtered out using a low threshold of counts per million (CPM). Differential gene expression analysis was performed using the edgeR package87 and the DESeq2 package88. The estimated log-fold-changes of the two algorithms were confirmed to be highly similar. Multiple testing correction to control the false discovery rate (FDR) was performed using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure on the p values.

iCLIP library preparation

U2OS cells stably transfected with a Flag version of TIRR, or an empty vector (EV), were washed once with 1X PBS, UVC-crosslinked (254 nm) at 150 mJ/cm2 on an ice-filled tray with Stratalinker 2400, then pelleted and lysed in RIPA buffer (Thermo Fisher). For gamma-irradiation, cells were irradiated at 5 Gy with an X-ray source. Two replicates were prepared for each condition. 0.4 Units of RNaseI and 4 Units Turbo DNase were added per 1 mL of cell lysate at 1 mg/mL protein concentration for RNA fragmentation. Antibodies against Flag coupled to magnetic Protein G beads were used to isolate Protein-RNA complexes. Following IP and washes, beads were incubated at 37 °C for 40 min for 3’end dephosphorylation using PNK (NEB M0201L), FastAP alkaline phosphatase (ThermoFisher, EF0654), 0.5 μL RNasin, and 30 μL nuclease-free water. Beads were then washed, and adapter ligation was performed for 75 min at 25 °C in the following reaction using T4 RNA ligase I (M0437M NEB) and of 2.5 μL of pre-adenylated adapter (1 μM). The pre-adenylated infra-red labeled IRL3 adapter had the following sequence: /5rApp/AG ATC GGA AGA GCG GTT CAG AAA AAA AAA AAA /iAzideN/AA AAA AAA AAA A/3Bio/. The excess adapter was removed by incubating with RecJ endonuclease (NEB M0264S) at 30 °C for 1 hour, followed by 30 min at 37 °C. RNA-protein complexes were eluted in 1X NuPAGE loading buffer at 70 °C for 1 minute before being loaded on a NuPAGE 4-12% BisTris SDS-PAGE for 65 min at 180 V with MOPS buffer, and transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane for 2 h at room temperature at 30 V with 1X NuPAGE Transfer buffer containing 10% methanol. The membrane was then visualized at the LI-COR Odyssey-Clx. Protein-RNA complexes were then removed by cutting portions of the membrane at an appropriate size, and digested with proteinase K (Roche; catalog no.: 03115828001) in SDS buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.4, 100 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 0.2% SDS) for 1 hour at 50 °C. RNA was then purified using Phenol:Chloroform:Isoamyl Alcohol 25:24:1 (Sigma, P3803). Pelleted RNA was resuspended in water and reverse transcribed with barcoded RT primers using the Superscript IV (SSIV) Kit. cDNA cleanup was performed using Agencourt AMPure XP beads, then circularized with CircLigase II (Epicentre) for 2 h at 60 °C. After PCR amplification, libraries were size-selected with Agencourt AMPure XP beads. Libraries were sequenced as single-end 100 bp reads on Illumina HiSeq 4000. Please also refer to ref. 89.

Analysis of iCLIP data

The sequencing libraries were processed following the standardized iCLIP analysis through the Flow web server https://app.flow.bio/). The datasets used for this study can be found at https://app.flow.bio/projects/466643576018801094. The +/− IR RNA-seq datasets were processed using the same iCLIP analysis pipeline on the Flow server. For all samples, the GRCh38 genome build and GENCODE v39 annotation were used. The scatter plot of iCLIP crosslink counts against RNA-seq counts was produced using the summary gene file produced by the Flow analysis pipeline, which contains crosslink counts at the whole gene level. Counts were normalized by TPM (transcripts per million). The final plot was produced using the ggplot2 package in R Studio. To determine the iCLIP-enriched transcripts, the average TPM count in the TIRR iCLIP libraries was divided by the TPM of the appropriate RNA-seq input control. Genes were then selected if the CLIP:input ratio was ≥ 1.5. Downstream analysis to produce the RNA maps was done using the Clipplotr tool90 using crosslink sites occurring within peaks, which were identified using Clippy91 following the default settings. For the NEAT1 RNA map, a custom GTF annotation was used containing only the long (NEAT1-202) and short NEAT1 (NEAT1-201) isoforms. PEKA software was used to identify enriched motifs, produce the motif heatmaps and plot PEKA motif scores, with default settings43 (https://github.com/ulelab/peka). GO analysis was carried out in R using the topGO Bioconductor package, with the GO.db, biomaRt and Rgraphviz packages. Genes enriched >1.5 times in the TIRR iCLIP dataset over the RNA-seq input were used as the candidate list for GO analysis. The comparative gene universe consisted of all genes identified in the TIRR iCLIP dataset. Enrichment was determined using the weighted Fisher exact test option, with a cut-off of p = 0.001.

Immunofluorescence microscopy

Cells were grown on 12 mm glass coverslips, permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 10 min at room temperature, fixed in 4% formaldehyde in PBS for 10-15 min at room temperature, washed twice with 1X PBS, and blocked for 1 hour at room temperature in PBS containing 5% BSA. Incubation with primary and secondary (Alexa Fluor, Molecular Probes) antibodies was done in PBS containing 2.5% BSA for overnight at 4 °C in a humidified chamber or 1 hour at room temperature, respectively. Coverslips were mounted using a mounting solution with DAPI (Fisher Scientific). When staining for 53BP1 (NB; NBP131330) and γH2AX (Cell Signaling; 9718S) foci, soluble proteins were pre-extracted with 25 mM HEPES (pH 7.4), 50 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 3 mM MgCl2, 300 mM sucrose and 0.5% Triton X-100 for 3-5 min on ice before processing for immunofluorescence. Images were acquired with Zeiss Axio Observer microscope equipped with an apotome at 40x or 63x magnification. Primary antibodies are listed in Supplementary Data 2.

BrdU foci detection without denaturation

Cells cultured on 12 mm glass coverslips were incubated with 10 μM BrdU for 24 h to visualize single-stranded DNA. Cells were irradiated (Gammacell® 40 Exactor; Cesium-137 source) in the last two hours of the treatment, recovered, and treated with 10 μM EdU 20 min prior to fixation to mark S phase. Cells were pre-extracted as above, fixed, and the number of BrdU foci imaged using a Zeiss Axio Observer microscope equipped with an apotome at 60x magnification.

Proximity Ligation Assay

Cells were grown on 12 mm glass coverslips, fixed in 4% formaldehyde in PBS for 15 min at room temperature and blocked/permeabilized for 1 h at room temperature in PBS containing 0.3% Triton X-100, 1% BSA, and 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). Incubation with primary antibodies (Supplementary Data 2) was done in PBS containing 1% BSA and 0.1% Triton X-100 overnight at 4 °C in a humidified chamber. TIRR nanobody (this study) was used at 1:250 concertation for PLA. The steps of blocking, primary antibody incubation, probe incubation, ligation, and amplification were followed according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Sigma Aldrich).

RNA-FISH/IF

The NEAT1 RNA probes recognizing the long isoform and both isoforms were obtained from LGC Biosearch Technologies. Cells were grown on 12 mm glass coverslips, fixed in 4% formaldehyde in PBS for 15 min at room temperature, washed twice with 1X PBS and permeabilized with 70% ethanol overnight at 4 °C. Samples were processed using the Stellaris RNA FISH protocol (LGC Biosearch Technologies) according to manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, coverslips were washed with Wash Buffer A at room temperature for 5 min, and probe hybridization was carried out in the Hybridization Buffer containing appropriate probes and primary antibodies for at least 4 h at 37 °C away from light. Samples were then incubated in Wash Buffer A containing the appropriate secondary antibody for 30 min at 37 °C. Samples were washed once with Wash Buffer B for 5 min at room temperature and mounted on microscopy slides using mounting solution with DAPI. Images were acquired with Zeiss Axio Observer microscope equipped with an apotome at 63x magnification. Cells were analyzed using Fiji software. Nuclei were segmented using DAPI to identify areas of interest. Then, co-localizing foci were determined using the JaCoP tool and measuring the Manders’ overlap coefficient. Non-co-localizing foci were determined by subtracting the Manders’ overlap coefficient from 1 and converting it to percentages.

PI staining and FACS

Cells were synchronized in G1 by thymidine-RO-3306 treatment. Briefly, cells were seeded in 2 mM thymidine (Sigma Aldrich), cultured for 24 h, washed with warm 1X PBS, and fresh media was added. Cells were released for 5.5 h, after which 72 μM RO-3306 (Fisher Scientific) was added to the cells over 12 h. RO-3306 was washed off with PBS and cells were released in fresh media for 2-4 h before collecting G1 cells. Cells were synchronized in S phase by the double-thymidine method. Briefly, cells were plated in 2 mM thymidine over 16-18 h, then washed with warm 1X PBS, and fresh media was added. Cells were released over 8 h, after which cells were incubated in 2 mM thymidine once more over 16-18 h. Cells were released and collected over 0-8 h after release as indicated in appropriate figures. Synchronized U2OS cells were trypsinized, collected, resuspended in 100% ethanol, and fixed overnight at 4 °C. Cells were washed with 1X PBS and incubated with Propidium Iodide (PI) solution (Fisher Scientific) for 30 min at 37 °C away from light to measure DNA content. Cell cycle profiles were recorded by Flow Cytometry and analyzed by ModFit software.

Senescence assay

RPE-1 control, NEAT1_1 OE, and TIRR KO cells were plated at a low confluency (∼300,000 cells) in a 6-well plate. The following day, cells were irradiated wit 5 Gy IR and allowed to grow for up to 10 days. Cells were washed twice with 1X PBS, fixed, and stained with β-galactosidase according to the manufacturer’s instructions (CST). Images were taken with a brightfield Zeiss Primovert inverted microscope at 20x magnification.

Cell Titer-Glo cell viability assay

250-500 cells per well were plated in a 96-well plate. The following day, cells were treated with increasing concentrations of Olaparib (as indicated in the corresponding figures) and cultured in the drug for 7–8 days. The CellTiter-Glo luminescent cell viability assay kit (Promega) was used to measure cell viability. The plates were scanned with a luminescence microplate reader (CLARIOstar). The surviving fraction of drug-treated cells was normalized to the untreated control. Experiments were performed in technical triplicates over three biological repeats. Survival and significance were determined by GraphPad Prism 10 software.

ER-AsiSI resection assay

The percentage of resection adjacent to a specific DSB was measured as previously described54. The primer pairs for DSB are across BsrG1 and BamHI restriction sites. ER-AsiSI U2OS cells were treated with 4-OHT for 4 h to allow for the nuclear translocation of AsiSI and induction of DSBs. Cells were harvested and genomic DNA was digested with BsrGI or HindIII enzymes or mock digested overnight at 37 °C. Digested samples were used as a template for qPCR. For each sample, a ΔCt was calculated by subtraction the Ct value of the mock-digested sample from the Ct value of the digested sample. Primers used are listed in the Supplementary Data 3.

Protein expression and purification for in vitro FP polarization

C-terminal 6XHis-tagged TIRR10 was expressed in chemically competent RosettaTM cells (Novagen) by inducing protein expression overnight with 1 mM IPTG at 16 °C. The cell pellet was resuspended in lysis buffer (50 mM Tris (pH 8.0), 500 mM NaCl, 5% Glycerol, 1 mM TCEP (pH 7.5), 10 mM imidazole (pH 7.5)), supplemented with protease inhibitor tablets), and cells were lysed by sonication. All subsequent protein purification steps were performed at room temperature unless mentioned otherwise. Clarified lysate was incubated with TALON® (Takara Bio) resin for 1 hour on a slowly rotating shaker. After washing with the lysis buffer, TALON® resin-bound protein was eluted with elution buffer (50 mM Tris (pH 8.0), 500 mM NaCl, 5% Glycerol, 1 mM TCEP (pH 7.5), and 250 mM imidazole (pH 7.5). Protein fractions were concentrated at 15 °C using Amicon® concentrators (10 kDa molecular weight cut-off, Millipore Sigma) and loaded onto a pre-equilibrated Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 gel-filtration column connected to an Akta (GE) at 4 °C for further polishing using SEC buffer (50 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 500 mM NaCl, 1 mM TCEP (pH 7.5)). The sample was immediately harvested after gel-filtration and concentrated using Amicon™-Ultra-15 concentrators (10 kDa molecular weight cut-off, MilliporeSigma) at 15 °C. Tudor domain of 53BP1 was purified using the Ni-NTA affinity chromatography7. His6-tagged Tudor domain of 53BP1-F1553R was expressed and purified as TIRR except the purification was performed at 4 °C. For both 53BP1 Tudor domains (WT and F1553R), the final purification step was performed on a pre-equilibrated Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 gel-filtration column at 4 °C for further polishing using SEC buffer (20 mM Tris (pH 7.5), 500 mM NaCl, 1 mM TCEP (pH 7.5). Protein quality was verified by running a protein gel and Coomassie Brilliant Blue G-250 staining (Supplementary Fig. 3b–d), and protein amounts with corresponding extinction coefficients were quantified by Nanodrop (Thermo Scientific). Purified proteins were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and kept at -80 °C until further use.

Fluorescence polarization assay

FP assay was used to measure the TIRR and NEAT1-RNA binding in vitro. An FP probe of NEAT1-RNA fragment (42-neucleotides (nts) long sequence based on the iCLIP data) with a 5’-FAM label (FAM-NEAT1-RNA) was ordered from Integrate DNA Technology (IDT) with RNAase-free high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) purification. Additionally, an unlabeled NEAT1-RNA fragment of the same sequence (Positive-RNA) and an equally long NEAT1 fragment from the region with no observed iCLIP peaks (Negative-RNA) were made (see sequence details in the Supplementary Table 2). FP experiments were performed at room temperature in the FP buffer (20 mM Tris (pH 8.0), 100 mM KCl, 1 mM TCEP (pH 7.5), 0.05% Tween-20 and 1 mM EDTA (pH 8.0)) by mixing equal volumes of FP probe and TIRR in the FP buffer. The final probe concentration was 20 nM in the FP experiments, where TIRR concentration varied from 1.2-10000 nM with a final tube containing only probe molecule with buffer. FP was measured from a 20 μL sample in a black 384-well plate (Corning®, #3820) on an Envison plate reader (PerkinElmer). FP data from three independent measurements were exported and binding constants were estimated by fitting the data to a 4-parameter logarithmic curve in PRISM (GraphPad). For competition FP experiments, we selected the concentration of TIRR such that about 80-90% of the FP probe was bound to TIRR. Final probe concentration was 20 nM and TIRR concentration was 1 μm in the competition FP experiments, where either Positive/Negative-RNA (from 3-25000 nM) or 53BP1-Tudor domain WT or F1553R mutant (from 6-50000 nM) were titrated. FP was measured and analyzed as TIRR and RNA binding above.

Nanobodies generation

The identification of anti-TIRR VHH antibodies (nanobodies) and their validation by Phage ELISA was performed by Hybrigenics Services SAS (Evry, France) from a VHH library of 3.109 clones (Supplementary Fig. 2a). Briefly, 3 rounds of Phage Display selection were carried out using biotinylated TIRR. A total of 90 VHHs were picked and analyzed further. Thirty-two of these VHHs were positive in non-adsorbed phage ELISA corresponding to 24 different clones. Biotinylated Nudt16 was included to control the specificity of the selected VHH. Ten nanobodies were retained, but only clone #4 was used in this study. The cloning of the cDNA #4 into pFuse vector and the generation of a Fc-VHH antibody was performed by Hybrigenics Services SAS (Evry, France).

Protein purification for TIRR nanobody crystallization

Human TIRR (residues 6-211) was cloned into a pBB75 vector while nanobody #4 (Nb4) was cloned into a pET-based vector, pHISPP, conferring an N-terminal His6-tag to the protein that can be cleaved by rhinovirus 3 C (HRV3C) protease. TIRR and Nb4 were co-expressed in BL21(DE3) E. coli cells grown at 37 °C in LB media to an OD600 of ~0.6 and induced with 0.5 mM isopropyl-β-d-thiogalactoside at 15 °C for ~16-20 h. Harvested cells were resuspended in resuspension buffer (50 mM sodium phosphate (NaPi), 300 mM NaCl, 5 mM imidazole, pH 7.5) and lysed using a microfluidizer (Avestin Emulsiflex C5). Lysed cells were next centrifuged at 48,384 x g (Beckman Coulter JA 25.5 rotor) for 45 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was loaded onto a Ni2+-nitrilotriacetic acid (NTA) agarose column (Qiagen) pre-equilibrated with bind buffer (50 mM NaPi, 500 mM NaCl, 5 mM imidazole, pH 7.5). After extensively washing the Ni+2-NTA resin with wash buffer (bind buffer but with 20 mM imidazole), the TIRR/Nb4 complex was eluted using elution buffer (bind buffer but with 250 mM imidazole). The His6-tag was cleaved overnight with HRV3C at 4 °C. The sample was concentrated and further purified by size-exclusion chromatography using a HiLoad 16/60 Superdex 200 column (Cytiva) and running buffer (10 mM HEPES, 50 mM NaCl, 2 mM TCEP, pH 7.5). Fractions containing the TIRR/Nb4 complex were pooled and concentrated to 20-40 mg/mL.

Structure determination

Crystals of the complex were produced by the hanging-drop vapor diffusion method carried out at 22°C, and by mixing 2 μL of the protein and 2 μL of the reservoir solution made up of 8% Tacsimate, pH 5, and 20% PEG 3350. Crystals were cryoprotected with 30% (v/v) xylitol and X-ray diffraction data were collected at the Advanced Photon Source on beamline 24-ID-E. Crystallographic data were indexed, integrated and scaled with HKL200092, and the structure was solved by molecular replacement using PHENIX PHASER93,94 with the crystal structure of TIRR (PDB 6D0L)95 and the AlphaFold96 predicted structure of Nb4 as search models. There is one copy of the complex molecule in the asymmetric unit and the space group is P 41 21 2. Structure refinement was performed through several iterations of PHENIX REFINE97 and COOT.98 Structure validation was done with MolProbity and through the PDB validation server. Molecular graphic representations were generated using PyMOL (The PyMOL Molecular Graphics System, Versions 2.3.2 Schrodinger, LLC). The PDB accession number for the nanobody structure is 8U3S.

Model building and molecular dynamics simulations

The initial TIRR-RNA complex was predicted using RoseTTAFold2NA44 with one TIRR and one RNA molecule as input. The TIRR homodimer bound to RNA was then assembled in PyMOL99 by replacing monomeric TIRR in the RoseTTAFold2NA model with the crystal structure of TIRR homodimer (PDB entry 6CO1)7. Steric clashes in the TIRR-RNA heterotrimer model were resolved by manually adjusting the RNA conformation in PyMOL prior to carrying out molecular dynamics (MD) simulations using AMBER18100. For the MD simulations, the TIRR-RNA complex was hydrated with TIP3P water molecules to a minimum thickness of 12 Å and neutralized with sodium ions. The simulation setup followed a standard procedure101, including 3000 steps of unrestrained energy minimization, Langevin temperature equilibration with a collision frequency of 2.0 ps−1 over 100,000 steps, and density equilibration at 1 atm over 500,000 steps under a weak restraint of 2 kcal mol−1 Å−2 applied to the solute. An additional 10 ns re-equilibration was performed before the final 500 ns of unrestrained production simulation. The trajectories were collected in the isothermal–isobaric ensemble (300 K, 1 atm) using the ff14SB force field102. The simulations using AMBER18100 were conducted with a 2-fs time step and a 10-Å non-bonded cutoff. Covalent bonds involving hydrogen atoms were restrained using SHAKE103. The AMBER18 trajectories were converted to the.xtc format using ParmEd104 for RMSF calculation using GROMACS (version 2021.4)105. Visualization of the trajectories was performed using VMD (version 1.9.4a8)106.

QIBC

Asynchronously growing cells were seeded onto 12 mm glass coverslips (Fisher Scientific) and cultured until 70%–90% confluent. Cells were fixed with 4% formaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature, washed twice with 1X PBS, and blocked/permeabilized for 1 hour in 1X PBS containing 0.3% Triton X-100, 1% BSA, and 10% fetal bovine serum. Incubation with primary and secondary antibodies (Alexa Fluor, Molecular Probes) was done in PBS containing 1% BSA and 0.1% Triton X-100 for overnight at 4 °C or 1 hour at room temperature, respectively. Cells were washed 3 times with 1X PBS and mounted using mounting solution containing DAPI (Fisher Scientific). For EdU staining, cells were pulsed with 10 μM EdU 20 min prior to fixation, and “click” chemistry was performed using the manufacturer’s instructions (Fisher Scientific). Images were acquired with a Zeiss Axio Observer microscope equipped with an apotome at 20x magnification. DAPI and EdU intensities and total foci counts/nuclear intensities for markers of interests were quantified on Fiji/Image J and analyzed in Excel. The data was then imported to R programming language, and a cell cycle profile was generated107. The data for each phase of the cell cycle was then retrieved from the code and exported. These data were plotted and statistically analyzed using GraphPad Prism. For PLA coupled with QIBC, the PLA protocol was followed as above. Primary antibodies are listed in Supplementary Data 2.

Statistics and reproducibility

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 software unless otherwise specified. The type of statistical test used is indicated in the figure legends. Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) or mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). For comparisons of 2 groups, a t-test or an equivalent nonparametric test was performed. For comparisons of more than 2 groups, one-way ANOVA or an equivalent nonparametric test was performed. All data are representative of 3 independent experiments unless otherwise stated. A p value of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
