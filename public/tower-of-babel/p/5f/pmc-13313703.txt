Global epidemiological trends of moderate/severe TBI from 1990 to 2021: A study based on Global Burden of Disease study 2021
Authors: Chaobo Zheng, Hu Sun, Jiangchun Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Traumatic brain injury is a major cause of death and disability globally, especially moderate/severe traumatic brain injury (MSTBI), which has a significant impact on public health. The aim of this study is to systematically analyze the epidemiological characteristics of MSTBI worldwide from 1990 to 2021. The study measures the burden of MSTBI by gender at global, regional, and national levels based on the results of the Global Burden of Disease study 2021. Using clinically recorded data, it estimates the proportion of each type of injury that requires medical care and is ultimately identified as MSTBI. We applied Global Burden of Disease disability weights to calculate YLDs. It uses Bayesian meta-regression tools for epidemiological modeling, combined with the socio-demographic index (SDI), a composite indicator composed of per capita income, education, and fertility rate, to stratify countries and regions at different levels of development. In 2021, there were 11.8 million new cases of MSTBI worldwide (95% UI, 10.12–13.78 million), with an age-standardized incidence rate of 144.08 cases per 100,000 population (95% UI, 123.64–168.14). In the same year, MSTBI led to 4.42 million YLDs (95% UI, 3.11–5.98 million), corresponding to age-standardized rates of 52.17 per 100,000 population (95% UI, 36.64–70.52). From 1990 to 2021, the estimated annual percentage change of the incidence of MSTBI dropped by 0.5% (95% UI, −0.56 to −0.43), and the estimated annual percentage change of YLDs decreased by 0.49% (95% UI, −0.54 to −0.45). In 2021, high-middle SDI region had the highest age-standardized incidence (171.69 per 100,000 population, 95% UI, 147.16–199.97), while the high SDI region showed the lowest (93.28 per 100,000 population, 95% UI, 77.46–113.95). Similar regional disparities were evident for prevalence and YLDs. Males consistently exhibited higher incidence, prevalence, and YLD rates than females across most age groups and SDI quintiles. This study offers a scientific basis for the prevention and control strategies of MSTBI worldwide and emphasizes the urgency of strengthening preventive measures and enhancing medical conditions, especially in low- and middle-income countries.

1. Introduction

Traumatic Brain Injury (TBI) represents a major global cause of mortality and chronic disability, with moderate/severe traumatic brain injuries (MSTBI) constituting the most substantial public health challenge.[1,2] In recent years, the incidence of head injury has been on the rise with global population growth, increasing urbanization, and increased traffic flow.[3–5] Given that most of the injuries that cause MSTBI are preventable, calculating the extent to which different causes of injury contribute to MSTBI can help understand the possible impact of injury prevention programs. Most epidemiological studies have difficulty in comprehensively measuring the incidence of the overall consequences of MSTBI, leading studies to focus only on the incidence of injury causes, such as falls, road injuries, and interpersonal violence.[6,7] Currently, few comprehensive epidemiological evaluations cover all sources of injury. Several studies that specifically focused on univariate epidemiological statistics for MSTBI have identified considerable burdens but are often limited by the singularity of data sources and do not allow for modeling strategies for estimating the burden in sparse areas.[8,9]

The Global Burden of Disease (GBD) database, an extensive repository curated by international health research organizations, is designed to evaluate and analyze the health impacts of diseases, injuries, and risk factors on both global and regional scales. This initiative is spearheaded by the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation at the University of Washington and incorporates contributions from over 11,000 researchers across more than 160 countries and territories.[1,10,11] Within the GBD study framework, the global burden of TBI[12] and mild TBI[13] has been published, but the 2021 data for MSTBI has not been well evaluated. Recent GBD-based analyses[12] estimated 20.8 million incident cases and 37.9 million prevalent cases of TBI in 2021, with substantial years lived with disability (YLDs). While age-standardized incidence rate has decreased over the past 3 decades, absolute counts have risen due to demographic change. A sizable proportion of cases and disability are attributable to moderate-to-severe TBI, reflecting injuries that require inpatient care and are associated with higher mortality and long-term sequelae. These contemporary data underscore the need to quantify trends and disability specifically for moderate-to-severe TBI to inform prevention, acute care, and long-term services. To address this research gap, we conducted a systematic investigation of the epidemiological characteristics and trends of MSTBI globally from 1990 to 2021, aiming to identify data sources that could be utilized to evaluate MSTBI.

This study significantly enhances the comprehensive understanding of the global epidemiological characteristics of moderate/severe TBI. It provides a robust scientific foundation for public health policymakers and facilitates the advancement of prevention and intervention measures for head injuries on a global scale. Furthermore, it holds substantial guiding significance for the rational allocation of medical resources and the optimization of clinical treatment strategies. Ultimately, this research serves as a comprehensive reference for the prevention and control of head trauma worldwide.

2. Research methods

2.1. Overview and case definition

This study employs a systematic epidemiological analysis of 204 countries and 21 regions to thoroughly assess the global epidemiological characteristics of MSTBI from 1990 to 2021. The research design is grounded in the GBD framework, which systematically examines trends in the incidence, prevalence, and YLDs associated with MSTBI. TBI is defined by an external force applied to the head, causing injury to the brain manifested by different levels of clinical severity. In the GBD 2021 study, TBI is modeled both as a cause of injury and as a nature of injury resulting from various external causes (road injuries, falls, violence). The severity classification (mild, moderate, or severe) is derived from health care utilization data and diagnostic coding (ICD-9 and ICD-10). Specifically, moderate/severe TBI corresponds to inpatient cases that require hospitalization, based on the GBD injury severity hierarchy, whereas mild cases are typically managed in outpatient or emergency settings. The ICD-10 codes for TBI are F07.2, F07.8, F07.81, F07.89, F07.9, S06, S07, T90.2, and T90.5. Among these, S06.1 designates minor TBI, while the others designate moderate or severe TBI. The ICD-9 codes for TBI are 850 (mild), 852 and 907 (moderate or severe). These codes were identified by physician review.[14] This is achieved through the integration of multi-source data using standardized analytical methods, disaggregated by age, gender, year, and region. The study’s logical framework comprises 4 principal steps: data collection, data collation, analysis, and interpretation of results, thereby ensuring the research’s systematic and scientific rigor. Detailed descriptions of these methods, data, and standards are available in other GBD 2021 reports.[15,16]

2.2. Sample collection and selection

Within the GBD research framework, MSTBI is not regarded as a cause of death. Instead, the causes of injury that lead to MSTBI (falls, trauma) are statistically recorded. In this study, we evaluated the incidence, prevalence, and YLDs of MSTBI, but did not account for cause-specific mortality. Assessment of Injury Characteristics: Utilizing clinical record data that encode both the cause and nature of injuries, the following methodologies were employed to evaluate the correspondence between these 2 factors. Initially, for each cause of injury, the proportional distribution of various injury characteristics was calculated. In instances where a single cause of injury resulted in multiple injuries, a mixed-effects regression model was applied. This model incorporated variables such as age, gender, noninjury status covariates, and both national and individual-level random effects to predict the degree of comprehensive disability for individuals. In the GBD 2021 modeling framework, non-status injury covariates refer to covariates used to improve model estimation of injury burden that are not themselves measures of injury occurrence or outcome. These covariates include demographic, socioeconomic, environmental, and health-system indicators that influence injury risk or reporting, such as gross domestic product (GDP) per capita, education level, health service accessibility, and alcohol or road traffic exposure metrics. They are incorporated in the DisMod-MR 2.1 model to enhance predictive accuracy, especially for locations or years with sparse data. The nature of the impairment associated with the highest degree of disability was then identified as the predominant classification. This approach effectively addresses the issue of “multiple injury class-single individual” allocation, which has been a challenge due to data sparsity in previous GBD studies. Subsequently, Dirichlet regression was employed to model the proportions of each cause-property combination of injury, ensuring that the sum of the proportions of all injury characteristics under the same cause equaled one. The final output is a normalized cause-property combination-specific incidence estimate.

2.3. Incidence, prevalence, and derivation of YLDs

Initially, the study modeled short-term (duration ≤1 year) and long-term (>1 year) disability separately, beginning from the initial occurrence of specific cause-nature combinations. It was observed that the proportion of long-term disability increases with age, necessitating adjustments in incidence estimates to account for dynamic population characteristics such as age, gender, and region. For instance, the proportion of long-term disability in cases of MSTBI is notably higher in older populations compared to adolescents. Subsequently, prevalence was calculated by integrating morbidity with standardized mortality, utilizing the differential equation solver available in the DisMod-MR 2.1 tool. This model harmonizes epidemiological data, including the course of illness and comorbidities, with excess mortality data, such as the increased mortality rate observed in patients with severe head injuries compared to the general population, to produce age-gender-region-specific prevalence estimates. Short-term prevalence is determinedbased on the average course of illness, whereas long-term prevalence is considered permanent and is incorporated into the comorbidity adjustment.[6,17,18] Finally, YLDs are calculated using the formula YLDs = prevalence × disability weight. The disability weight, which ranges from 0 to 1, is established based on lay descriptions of health conditions; for instance, the disability weight for stroke is 0.8, while that for mild anxiety is 0.1. It is necessary to adjust for the impact of excess mortality on YLDs within the long-term disabled population. This approach aligns the outcomes with the standards set by the GBD study.

2.4. Data analysis methods

Countries were stratified into quintiles according to their 2021 socio-demographic index (SDI) values, a composite indicator of development that encompasses per capita income, educational attainment, and total fertility rate. Additionally, we conducted a separate analysis to identify the most prevalent causes of MSTBI, categorized by the initial cause of impairment leading to disability. Furthermore, we assessed the global proportion of MSTBI. The analysis was conducted using R (version 4.4.1) and the Joinpoint Regression Program (version 5.0.2.1).

2.5. Future projections

To estimate the future burden of MSTBI, we applied a Bayesian age-period-cohort (BAPC) model using GBD 1990 to 2021 data on age-standardized incidence, prevalence, and YLDs rates. The BAPC model combines Bayesian inference with Poisson regression to account for temporal dynamics and uncertainty across age, period, and cohort effects. Posterior simulations were performed to project global MSTBI burden from 2022 to 2036, generating estimates of the age-standardized rates of incidence, prevalence, and YLDs with corresponding 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs).

2.6. Statement of reliability and validity

To ensure the reliability and validity of the research methodology, the following measures were implemented by GBD 2021 estimate: Data Quality Control: The data were rigorously screened and verified using the quality control standards of the GBD database to ensure accuracy and completeness. Model Validation: The stability and reliability of the Bayesian meta-regression models were assessed through cross-validation and sensitivity analysis. Expert Consultation: Experts in neuroscience, epidemiology, and public health were consulted throughout the research process to ensure the scientific rigor and rationality of the research design and analytical methods. and verification of results: The study’s findings were compared with existing epidemiological studies to validate their credibility. These measures collectively ensured the scientific rigor and reliability of the data collection and analysis processes, thereby providing a robust foundation for the epidemiological study of moderate/severe TBI globally.

To enhance the credibility of estimates derived from the GBD 2021 dataset, we conducted an external validation analysis using an independent data source. Mortality data for MSTBI in the United States from 1999 to 2020 were extracted from the CDC WONDER multiple-cause-of-death database, which compiles death certificate information from the National Vital Statistics System. Age-standardized mortality rates (per 100,000 population) were calculated using the U.S. 2000 standard population and compared with corresponding GBD 2021 estimates for moderate/severe TBI. Temporal trends between the 2 datasets were assessed descriptively to evaluate concordance in both magnitude and direction of change.

This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology reporting standards.

2.7. Ethical Statement

This study used de-identified publicly available data, did not involve individual privacy information, and generally did not require ethical approval or informed consent.

3. Results

3.1. Global burden of MSTBI

In 2021, the global number of incident, prevalent, and YLDs cases of MSTBI reached 11.8 million (95% UI, 10.1–13.8 million), 29.1 million (95% UI, 27.5–30.7 million), and 4.4 million (95% UI, 3.1–6.0 million), respectively, corresponding to age-standardized rates of 144.08 (95% UI, 123.64–168.14), 342.05 (95% UI, 324.13–361.28), and 52.17 (95% UI, 36.64–70.52) per 100,000 population. From 1990 to 2021, the age-standardized incidence, prevalence, and YLDs rates showed decrease trend, with estimated annual percentage change (EAPCs) of −0.50% (95% CI, −0.56% to −0.43%), −0.48% (95% CI, −0.52% to −0.43%), and −0.49% (95% CI, −0.54% to −0.45%), respectively (Table 1).

3.2. Region burden of MSTBI

Marked geographical heterogeneity was observed across the 5 SDI quintiles and 21 GBD regions (Table 1 and Figure 1). In 2021, high-middle SDI region had the highest age-standardized incidence (171.69 per 100,000 population, 95% UI, 147.16–199.97), while high SDI region showed the lowest (93.28 per 100,000 population, 95% UI, 77.46–113.95). Similar regional disparities were evident for prevalence and YLDs. Spatial patterns of EAPCs revealed that high-middle SDI region had fastest decline for incidence (−0.85%, 95% CI, −0.95% to −0.75%), prevalence (−0.87%, 95% CI, −0.96% to −0.78%), and YLDs (−0.87%, 95% CI, −0.96% to −0.79%), while low SDI regions experienced increase prevalence (0.07%, 95% CI, 0.02% to 0.13%) and YLDs (0.06%, 95% CI, 0–0.12%) rate during the study period.

(B) Age-standardized prevalence rate (per 100,000 population). (C) Age-standardized years lived with disability (YLDs) rate (per 100,000 population).

Among GBD 21 regions, East Asia had largest incident (3013,136, 95% UI, 2524,200–3593,406), prevalent (8139,291, 95% UI, 7680,509–8646,241), and YLDs (1242,754, 95% UI, 871,668–1694,970) cases of MSTBI, while Eastern Europe showed highest age-standardized incidence (291.18 per 100,000 population, 95% UI, 254.82–330.23), prevalence (722.47 per 100,000 population, 95% UI, 678.89–768.45), and YLDs (110.45 per 100,000 population, 95% UI, 76.97–151.25) rates. In addition, high-income Asia Pacific showed fastest decline for incidence (EAPC, −1.68%, 95% CI, −1.78% to −1.57), prevalence (EAPC, −1.59%, 95% CI, −1.70% to −1.47%), and YLDs (EAPC, −1.60%, 95% CI, −1.71% to −1.49%) rates (Table 1 and Figure 1).

3.4. Gender and age differences in MSTBI

Age-specific curves demonstrated that MSTBI male incidence cases peaked at 30–34 years, while female peaked at older groups. For both sexes, the incidence rate increased with age, peaking in the 85+ year age group (Fig. 3A). Prevalence and YLDs showed similar patterns, with the peak number of cases occurring at ages 50 to 54 years in males and 65 to 69 years in females. The peak rate was observed around age 65 years in males and around age 80 years in females (Figs. 3B and 3C). Males consistently exhibited higher incidence, prevalence, and YLDs rates than females across most age groups and SDI quintiles, although both sexes showed similar temporal trends (Figs. 3 and 4).

3.5. Association between SDI and the burden of MSTBI

Across 1990–2021, age-standardized incidence (R = −0.19, P < .001), prevalence (R = −0.13, P < .001), and YLDs (R = −0.13, P < .001) rates of MSTBI were inversely associated with SDI, with higher burdens observed in low- or middle-SDI regions. At the national level (204 countries and territories), 2021 age-standardized rates of MSTBI showed a negative correlation with SDI (incidence R = −0.10, P = .137; prevalence R = −0.07, P = .338; YLDs R = −0.06, P = .357) (Fig. 5).

3.6. Predicting the future trend of MSTBI burden

Using BAPC modeling, the age-standardized incidence rate of MSTBI is projected to peak in 2025 (146.23 per 100,000 population; 95% UI, 110.81–181.64) and then decline, reaching 143.36 per 100,000 population (95% UI,–70.39–357.11) by 2036. In contrast, the age-standardized prevalence rate and YLDs rate are expected to increase during 2022–2036, reaching 355.78 per 100,000 population (95% UI, 226.00–485.56) and 53.63 per 100,000 population (95% UI, 33.81–73.44), respectively, in 2036 (Table 2 and Figure 6).

4. Discussion

MSTBI has consistently been a significant disease impacting the social health burden index, primarily due to its high rates of disability and mortality.[19] The findings of this study indicate that as SDI increases, both incidence and prevalence generally decline, suggesting that socioeconomic development is associated with a reduction in new and existing cases of MSTBI. Furthermore, the incidence, prevalence, and YLDs are generally higher in males compared to females, suggesting that males are at a greater risk of adverse outcomes. Although earlier studies have described a trimodal pattern of TBI incidence,[3,20] our findings show that the absolute number of MSTBI cases peaks among middle-aged men. This difference likely reflects demographic structure and occupational exposure, as men in this age group constitute the largest proportion of the global labor force and are frequently involved in high-risk occupations such as construction, transport, and manufacturing, leading to increased exposure to road traffic injuries and falls.[21] Additionally, the rate of YLDs significantly decreases with High and High-middle SDI, indicating that regions with better socioeconomic conditions experience less disability and health loss due to the disease. The regions of northern sub-Saharan Africa, particularly the central and eastern Sahara, showed consistently higher burdens across all indicators, with elevated incidence, prevalence, and YLDs rates. In contrast, the high-income Asia-Pacific and North American regions exhibited lower burdens, characterized by comparatively reduced rates of incidence, prevalence, and YLDs. This study offers a new perspective analysis of the associations between incidence, prevalence, and YLDs of MSTBI with SDIs across different regions, age groups, and genders, highlighting notable disparities in the global health burden.

These findings indicate that enhancing the socio-economic conditions in regions with low SDI is of considerable importance. This can be achieved by augmenting the allocation of medical resources and refining public health policies to mitigate the burden of MSTBI. For instance, the enhancement of local medical services can be facilitated through the development of medical and health infrastructure in areas with low SDI.[22–24] Additionally, increasing awareness and understanding of public health policies can elevate residents’ health consciousness and self-care capabilities.[25,26] Concurrently, governmental policies can be introduced to incentivize and guide private investment in the medical and health sectors, thereby addressing the issue of inadequate medical resources.[27] Furthermore, the international community ought to enhance its assistance and support for regions with low SDI scores, collaborating to advance global health outcomes. The implementation of these strategies is anticipated to progressively diminish health disparities across various regions and enhance the overall health status of the global population.[28] The elevated incidence and prevalence of health issues in sub-Saharan Africa may be attributed to the socio-economic conditions, availability of medical resources, and public health policies prevalent in the region.[29]

Across settings, higher SDI is associated with infrastructure and policy environments that curb both the occurrence and severity of head injuries. First, comprehensive road-safety measures,[30] including WHO-recommended best-practice laws and enforcement for speed, drink-driving, motorcycle helmets, seat-belts, and child restraints, are more frequently implemented and enforced in high-SDI contexts and are linked to lower head-injury burden. Helmet and restraint use substantially reduce the odds of head injury and death, thereby lowering the share of injuries that meet moderate/severe thresholds.[31] Second, high-SDI settings tend to have stricter occupational safety standards and lower exposure to hazardous work, reducing severe work-related head trauma. Third, mature trauma systems (prehospital care, designated trauma centers, standardized protocols)[32] mitigate secondary brain injury and shorten disability duration, decreasing progression to long-term moderate/severe disability and, consequently, prevalence of MSTBI. Together, these structural and health-system factors plausibly explain the observed negative correlation between SDI and both incidence and prevalence of MSTBI. Notably, high-middle SDI regions showed the steepest declines in the age-standardized rates of MSTBI. This pattern likely reflects a confluence of policy and systems gains achieved during periods of rapid socioeconomic development.

The findings of this study align with existing literature and underscore the significant influence of socioeconomic factors on health outcomes.[33] Our estimates are consistent with previous GBD-based and regional epidemiological studies,[1,34] which similarly reported high burdens of TBI in high-middle SDI regions and a steady global decline in age-standardized incidence, prevalence, and YLDs rates since the 1990s. For instance, research conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic has demonstrated disparities in disease burden across various socioeconomic groups, thereby emphasizing the necessity of addressing health equity.[25,26,35] Furthermore, investigations into the regional disparities in the allocation of primary medical and health resources in China indicate that the unequal distribution of medical resources is a critical contributor to health disparities.[36]

This study offers comprehensive data on the global burden of MSTBI, emphasizing the influence of socioeconomic conditions on MSTBI prevalence. The findings serve as a critical foundation for developing public health policies and allocating resources, particularly in resource-constrained settings. Optimizing the distribution of medical resources to mitigate the MSTBI burden remains a crucial challenge. We also observed a noteworthy phenomenon: in traditionally low-SDI settings such as Yemen and Somalia, the incidence of MSTBI remains high, yet both prevalence and YLDs have declined: an apparent divergence from prior findings.[37,38] The paradoxical pattern observed in Somalia and Yemen, with high incidence but declining prevalence and YLDs, likely reflects the combined effects of armed conflict, fragile health infrastructure, and high case fatality. Persistent exposure to conflict- and transport-related injuries sustains high incident rates, yet limited trauma and rehabilitation capacity leads to shorter survival and underestimation of long-term disability. In the GBD framework, prevalence and YLDs are modeled from disability duration. Thus, high early mortality, displacement, and incomplete follow-up can yield lower prevalence and YLDs even when incidence remains elevated. Previous WHO and regional reports from conflict-affected settings support this explanation, underscoring how disrupted health systems constrain injury care and recovery trajectories in such regions.[39–42]

However, the study has several limitations. Firstly, it relies on existing data sources, which may be incomplete or inaccurate. For instance, incorporating disease-specific mortality data would substantially enhance our understanding of the observed declines in prevalence and YLDs within low-SDI regions. Secondly, while the study encompasses multiple regions, it may not fully represent the unique characteristics of all areas, particularly those with limited data availability. Lastly, the study primarily examines the impact of the SDI on MSTBI status, neglecting other potential influencing factors such as cultural, environmental, and policy-related aspects.

Future research should extend beyond the scope of the present analysis to strengthen the global evidence base on TBI. Priorities include improving data completeness and diagnostic accuracy in low- and middle-income countries through enhanced hospital reporting and population-based surveillance systems; developing standardized criteria for injury severity classification to improve comparability across datasets; and integrating longitudinal cohort studies with GBD modeling to better capture long-term functional outcomes and rehabilitation needs. Moreover, advancing predictive modeling and policy evaluation frameworks could help anticipate future burden and assess the effectiveness of prevention strategies. These collective efforts will enable a more precise and equitable understanding of the global trajectory of moderate/severe TBI.

5. Conclusion

Utilizing the GBD 2021 database, this study conducted a systematic analysis of the incidence, prevalence, and YLDs associated with MSTBI worldwide from 1990–2021. The findings indicated a general decline in the global incidence and prevalence of moderate/severe TBI, although YLDs experienced a slight increase in regions with low SDI. Across all metrics, males exhibited higher rates than females, yet both males and females demonstrated a decreasing trend. These results highlight the critical need to enhance MSTBI prevention and treatment strategies on a global scale, particularly in low-income regions. The implications for global public health policymakers and healthcare professionals are significant, suggesting the potential benefit of implementing gender-specific preventive measures to reduce the incidence of MSTBI among men. Nonetheless, this study’s reliance on GBD data may introduce biases related to data collection and reporting. Future research should aim to refine the analysis by incorporating a broader range of influencing factors to more comprehensively assess the disease burden of MSTBI and to develop effective prevention and control strategies.
