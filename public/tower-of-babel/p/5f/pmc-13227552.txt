Post-Traumatic Contusion of the Longus Colli Muscle: An underdiagnosed entity
Authors: Aliyah Baksh, Charif Sidani, Kevin J Abrams, Márcio Luís Duarte, Leonardo Furtado Freitas
Published: 2026

A 72-year-old woman with hypertension, coronary artery disease, hyperlipidemia, and left bundle branch block presented to a tertiary care hospital in Miami, USA, in 2025 after a syncopal episode triggered by a Valsalva maneuver during defecation, resulting in blunt cervical trauma due to a hyperextension mechanism. Following the event, she reported significant posterior neck pain and right shoulder pain. Physical examination revealed right periorbital and facial lacerations. She was hemodynamically stable, normothermic, and denied chest pain, dyspnea, or palpitations. Laboratory evaluation was unremarkable. Orthostatic hypotension was documented, supporting a diagnosis of vasovagal syncope.

Non-contrast computed tomography (CT) of the head demonstrated right frontal, periorbital and malar soft-tissue swelling without skull fracture, intracranial haemorrhage or orbital injury. Non-contrast CT of the cervical spine was initially interpreted as normal, with no evidence of acute fracture or traumatic malalignment [Fig. 1]. Despite CT clearance of the cervical spine, the patient continued to experience significant neck pain, prompting further evaluation with magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Cervical spine MRI revealed fusiform enlargement and marked T2 hyperintensity of the right longus colli muscle with associated edema extending into the retropharyngeal/danger spaces [Fig. 2]. No vertebral fracture, ligamentous disruption, spinal cord injury or abnormal enhancement was identified. These findings were consistent with acute post-traumatic contusion of the longus colli muscle. The patient was managed conservatively and discharged home without neurological deficits, with expected clinical improvement under symptomatic treatment. Patient consent for publication has been obtained.

1. Comment

The longus colli muscle is the principal deep cervical flexor, extending from the atlas to the upper thoracic spine and plays a key role in cervical stabilisation and flexion. Owing to its deep prevertebral location, isolated injury to this muscle is uncommon and typically occurs only in the context of significant biomechanical forces, most often cervical hyperextension associated with falls, motor vehicle collisions or seizure-related trauma. In the present case, the proposed mechanism was contusive or stretch-related injury secondary to hyperextension during a syncopal fall.

CT is frequently the first-line imaging modality in the evaluation of cervical trauma and is highly sensitive for osseous injury; however, it has limited sensitivity for isolated deep muscular lesions. Consequently, longus colli muscle contusion may remain occult on CT, particularly when prevertebral soft-tissue changes are subtle. MRI, especially fluid-sensitive sequences, provides superior soft-tissue contrast and characteristically demonstrates muscle enlargement and oedema without associated osseous or ligamentous injury, as observed in this patient.1

The differential diagnosis includes calcific tendinitis of the longus colli muscle, typically involving the upper cervical spine (C1–C2) and associated with focal calcifications on CT; infectious processes such as osteomyelitis or diskitis, usually accompanied by systemic symptoms and laboratory abnormalities; ligamentous injury; neoplastic processes; retropharyngeal abscess; and soft-tissue haematoma. In rare cases, involvement of the adjacent cervical sympathetic chain may result in Horner syndrome.2

Recognition of post-traumatic longus colli muscle injury is essential to avoid misdiagnosis and unnecessary investigations. In patients with persistent post-traumatic neck pain and a negative CT examination, careful MRI assessment of the deep cervical flexor musculature is warranted, as isolated longus colli injury may represent the sole imaging correlate of symptoms and influence management.
