Cardiorespiratory fitness, muscular strength, balance and tumor biomarker dynamics following a multimodal exercise-nutrition intervention in a multimorbid elderly colorectal cancer survivor with liver metastasis: a case report
Authors: Manuel Vicente Garnacho-Castaño, Héctor Garnacho-Castaño, Eulogio Pleguezuelos, Lorena Molina‑Raya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOlder survivors of metastatic colorectal cancer frequently present with multimorbidity, functional impairment, and limited evidence-based guidance for non-pharmacological treatment. This case report describes a complex geriatric colorectal cancer survivor with a history of liver metastasis and significant comorbidity who underwent a supervised multimodal exercise-nutrition intervention.Case DescriptionA 78-year-old colorectal cancer survivor with previous hepatic metastasis completed an 8-week supervised concurrent exercise and nutritional intervention. The intervention program primarily combined aerobic, resistance, and balance training with a Mediterranean- and Nordic-style diet emphasizing anti-inflammatory and antioxidant foods. Outcomes included anthropometry, cardiorespiratory performance via a 6-minute walk test with gas analysis, lower-limb strength (sit-to-stand test), postural balance on a force platform, and tumor biomarkers [carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), carbohydrate antigen 19-9 (CA 19-9)]. Functional outcomes demonstrated marked improvements: walking distance during the six-minute walk test increased by 52%, oxygen uptake during the 6MWT by 50%, and lower-limb strength by 33%. Balance assessments revealed significant reductions in sway area (−35.6% eyes open; −51.8% eyes closed), indicating improved postural stability. These physiological adaptations were accompanied by modest reductions in body weight (−4.3%) and resting heart rate (−10.8%). Following the intervention, serum CEA and CA 19-9 levels decreased by 48.8% and 31.2%, respectively, with CEA reaching the normal range.ConclusionsThis case illustrates the feasibility and clinical relevance of a supervised, multimodal exercise-nutrition intervention in a multimorbid, elderly survivor of metastatic colorectal cancer. The pronounced improvements in cardiorespiratory fitness, muscular strength, and balance represent the most robust findings, while observed tumor biomarker dynamics should be interpreted cautiously. Although causality cannot be inferred, these observations highlight the potential role of personalized lifestyle-based rehabilitation strategies within survivorship care and support further investigation in longitudinal and controlled studies.Keywords: Case report, oncology, multimorbidity, quality of life, peak oxygen uptake

Highlight box

Key findings

• An 8-week supervised concurrent exercise and nutritional intervention in a 78-year-old multimorbid colorectal cancer survivor with previous liver metastasis resulted in substantial improvements in cardiorespiratory fitness, functional capacity, lower-limb strength, and postural control.

• Balance metrics demonstrated marked reductions in sway magnitude and improved stability.

• Tumor biomarkers decreased following the intervention, although causal interpretation must remain cautious.

What is known and what is new?

• Exercise oncology research shows that structured physical training improves fatigue, physical function, and quality of life in cancer survivors. Nutritional optimization further enhances metabolic health and recovery. However, evidence in older survivors of metastatic colorectal cancer, particularly those with residual neurological impairment and severe baseline functional limitations, remains extremely limited.

• To our knowledge, this case report is the first to document integrated functional, cardiorespiratory, balance, and biochemical dynamics in an older survivor with a history of liver metastasis, paraparesis, and multimorbidity following a multimodal lifestyle intervention.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• The findings highlight the feasibility and potential value of personalized exercise-nutrition rehabilitation even in complex, elderly oncology survivors with major functional deficits.

• Clinics should consider implementing multidimensional rehabilitation approaches that integrate aerobic, resistance, and balance training with evidence-informed nutritional guidance.

• Larger controlled studies are needed to clarify which patient profiles derive the greatest benefit and to determine whether biomarker changes reflect true biological modification or natural clinical variability.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most diagnosed cancer worldwide, and approximately 20–30% of patients present with metastatic disease at the time of diagnosis, most commonly in the liver (1,2). Advances in surgical techniques and systemic chemotherapy have substantially improved survival in metastatic CRC (mCRC), leading to a growing population of long-term survivors, many of whom are older adults with age-related functional decline and multimorbidity (2).

Following CRC treatment, many survivors—particularly older adults—experience persistent impairments in physical function, aerobic capacity, and quality of life (3). Post-treatment sequelae such as muscle wasting, neuropathy, and fatigue are frequently exacerbated by age-related physiological decline, contributing to frailty, sarcopenia, and, in advanced cases, cachexia (4-6). These limitations challenge survivorship care and are often insufficiently addressed by standard follow-up protocols (7).

In the management of mCRC, carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) and carbohydrate antigen 19-9 (CA 19-9) are well-established serum biomarkers used for monitoring response to therapy, detecting recurrence, and providing prognostic information (8,9). Elevated and rising levels of these markers post-treatment are strongly associated with disease progression and poorer outcomes (10,11). In older patients, however, isolated elevations in these biomarkers may create a clinical dilemma, as aggressive oncological treatments are frequently inappropriate due to frailty or comorbidities. This highlights the need for supportive strategies that enhance functional status and physiological resilience, potentially influencing the clinical context in which biomarker changes are interpreted (6,8-11).

Beyond conventional oncological treatments, attention has shifted to modifiable lifestyle factors, particularly exercise and nutrition, as adjunctive strategies in cancer survivorship. Exercise interventions have consistently demonstrated benefits in cardiorespiratory fitness, muscle strength, and quality of life, with reductions in cancer-related fatigue, including CRC (12,13). These benefits are especially relevant in elderly survivors, in whom aging and cancer treatments jointly contribute to sarcopenia, neuromuscular impairment, balance deficits, and reduced aerobic capacity, compromising independence and resilience (14-16).

Similarly, nutritional interventions, particularly those based on Mediterranean- and Nordic-style diets, are hypothesized to promote a metabolic and anti-inflammatory milieu less favorable to cancer progression by reducing levels of growth factors, oxidative stress, and pro-inflammatory cytokines (17,18). These nutritional strategies may further counteract chronic low-grade inflammation and immunosenescence (19,20), thereby supporting functional adaptations and overall physiological resilience during CRC survivorship.

Although the positive effects of exercise and nutrition are well established, evidence regarding integrated exercise-nutrition interventions in geriatric survivors of mCRC with comorbidity, neurological impairment and rising tumor biomarkers such as CEA and CA 19-9 remains very limited. Older survivors of mCRC are frequently excluded from randomized controlled trials due to advanced age, metastatic disease, and comorbid conditions, resulting in a lack of high-quality evidence to guide non-pharmacological management in this population (8,21,22).

Current clinical guidelines from organizations such as American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) and European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO) primarily focus on surveillance strategies and systemic treatment decisions, offering limited practical guidance on lifestyle-based or rehabilitative approaches for clinically stable older patients with functional decline and complex comorbidities (8,9). Therefore, detailed case reports are essential to illustrate the feasibility and potential clinical relevance of multimodal lifestyle interventions in this underrepresented population.

Accordingly, this case report describes the cardiorespiratory, muscular strength, balance and tumor biomarker changes observed in an elderly survivor of mCRC with comorbidities following a supervised multimodal intervention combining concurrent exercise and targeted nutritional guidance. We present this article in accordance with the CARE reporting checklist (available at https://acr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/acr-2025-322/rc).

Case presentation

Patient description

The patient was a 78-year-old male diagnosed in October 2020 with stage IV (pT3, pN1a, pM1) sigmoid colon adenocarcinoma and a well-differentiated intestinal-type liver metastasis in segment V. Treatment consisted of a Hartmann’s procedure at the University Central Hospital of Asturias (HUCA), followed by hepatic metastasectomy at the Hospital of Jarrio. He completed eight cycles of adjuvant capecitabine chemotherapy (2,000 mg every 12 hours) by July 2021, with no further oncological treatment required (stage 1: oncological diagnosis and treatment; Figure 1).

After completion of oncological treatment, the patient entered routine clinical surveillance. Between November 2021 and April 2022, he participated in a hospital-based rehabilitation program. Subsequently, from July to October 2022, a supervised exercise program was implemented in response to a progressive rise in tumor biomarkers (CEA and CA 19-9); however, no formal functional, strength, balance, or cardiorespiratory assessments were performed during this phase (stage 2: surveillance and initial supportive interventions; Figure 1). Following a period of clinical stability, tumor biomarkers increased again in January 2024, prompting a new supervised 8-week integrative exercise-nutrition intervention from late March to June 2024. This latter intervention constitutes the focus of the present case report, during which all physiological and functional evaluations were systematically conducted (stage 3: multimodal exercise-nutrition intervention with comprehensive assessment; Figure 1).

The patient had a long-standing neurological condition dating back to October 2014, consisting of progressive paraparesis [American Spinal Injury Association (ASIA) D] secondary to a spinal arteriovenous fistula treated endovascularly. Neurological sequelae included sphincter dysfunction with neurogenic bladder requiring intermittent catheterization, sensory loss predominantly affecting the right lower limb, impaired gait requiring the use of a cane, and marked balance deficits. Despite these impairments, the patient remained independently ambulatory in daily life, although with reduced stability and lower-limb strength.

Additional medical history included acute pancreatitis that subsequently resulted in type II diabetes mellitus of more than 25 years’ duration and arterial hypertension (>5 years).

Oncological imaging performed during follow-up—including a positron emission tomography-computed tomography scan in May 2023 and a contrast-enhanced thoracoabdominal computed tomography scan on June 20, 2024—showed no radiological evidence of disease recurrence or progression. Consequently, no oncological treatment was initiated, and the exercise-nutrition intervention was implemented as a supportive, non-pharmacological strategy in a clinically stable patient. No imaging was performed at the specific time of the intervention.

Baseline nutritional assessment, based on a dietary record, revealed low intake of fruits, vegetables, and fish, occasional alcohol consumption with meals, and a predominance of red and processed meat intake. The patient also reported a markedly sedentary lifestyle for several months prior to the intervention, coinciding with winter and progressive functional decline.

At baseline, the patient was medically stable and functionally independent but presented with lower-limb weakness and balance deficits, corresponding to an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status of 2–3. The patient was not recruited through the authors’ institutional affiliation. The intervention was conducted in a private, non-institutional context, as the patient was under the direct care of the corresponding author outside his primary workplace. The corresponding author temporarily interrupted his professional activity to personally supervise the intervention due to the patient’s clinical condition and geographical location. All procedures performed in this study were in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and/or national research committee(s) and with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for the publication of this case report. A copy of the written consent is available for review by the editorial office of this journal.

Exercise program

The concurrent training program lasted 8 weeks and was prescribed using the Borg Rating of Perceived Exertion. The use of this method for exercise prescription has been previously described (23) (Table 1). Aerobic training and resistance exercise sessions were performed on alternate days, with a weekly frequency of 2–3 sessions each. Aerobic training consisted of supervised walking on athletics track and, on other occasions, walking on sand or in shallow seawater to provide destabilizing stimuli. Resistance training included multi-joint exercises for the upper and lower body using elastic bands. Balance training was integrated into resistance sessions and comprised bipodal and unipodal stance tasks, elastic band perturbations, and additional instability-based exercises performed on sand or in water.

Nutritional intervention

The nutritional intervention was based on Mediterranean and Nordic dietary principles, emphasizing foods with anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties associated with improved outcomes in CRC survivors (18). The dietary strategy ensured adequate intake of protein (>1.4 g·kg−1·day−1), omega-3 fatty acids, fiber, and polyphenols (Table 2).

Data collection and outcome measures

Cardiorespiratory fitness

Cardiorespiratory fitness was assessed using a 6-minute walk test (6MWT) on a standardized athletics track. Respiratory gas exchange was measured breath-by-breath with a portable metabolic system (Metamax, CORTEX Medical, Leipzig, Germany), while total walking distance was recorded as an index of functional capacity. Heart rate was continuously monitored using a Polar receiver integrated with the metabolic system (Polar Electro OY, Kempele, Finland).

Muscular strength assessment

Lower-limb muscular strength and endurance were assessed using the 30-second sit-to-stand test. The patient performed repeated sit-to-stand cycles from a standard chair (seat height: 43 cm) without armrests, with arms crossed over the chest, completing as many repetitions as possible within 30 seconds. The total number of repetitions was recorded. This test is a validated and reliable measure of functional lower-limb strength in older cancer survivors (24).

Balance assessment

Static balance was assessed using a force platform system (MuscleLab, Ergotest Technology, Langesund, Norway). Trials were performed under eyes-open and eyes-closed conditions, with the patient standing barefoot in a comfortable bipodal stance, arms relaxed alongside the body, for 30 seconds per condition. Force platform measures of postural stability are widely used in oncological populations and are sensitive to treatment-related deficits (25).

Tumor biomarkers

Serum concentrations of CEA and CA 19-9 were assessed as tumor-related biomarkers commonly used in the clinical follow-up of CRC survivors before and after the 8-week multimodal intervention. Reference ranges were <5 ng·mL−1 for CEA and <34 U·mL−1 for CA 19-9.

Results

The intervention was well tolerated, with no adverse events, falls, or hypoglycemic episodes reported. The patient experienced mild delayed-onset muscle soreness during the initial sessions, which resolved spontaneously and did not limit participation. Of the 24 planned sessions, 21 were completed, corresponding to an adherence rate of 87.5%, supporting the feasibility of the intervention in this multimorbid older patient.

Anthropometric, cardiorespiratory fitness, and tumor biomarker data before and after the intervention are presented in Table 3. Body weight and body mass index decreased by 4.3%. Systolic blood pressure showed a slight reduction (−2.1%), while diastolic blood pressure increased (+17.2%), remaining within normal physiological limits.

Patient perspective

“I agreed to participate in this exercise and nutrition program because I wanted to improve my physical condition after my cancer treatments and the episodes of biomarker elevation. Although I had limitations from my previous neurological injury, I gradually noticed improvements in my endurance, walking ability, and balance.

“The program was demanding at times, but it helped me feel stronger and more confident in daily activities. I appreciated the structured supervision and the fact that the exercises were adapted to my abilities. Overall, this experience contributed positively to my recovery and quality of life.”

Discussion

This case report describes the feasibility and functional impact of a supervised, multimodal exercise-nutrition intervention in a complex oncogeriatric patient: a long-term survivor of mCRC with significant neurological and metabolic comorbidity. The most robust and clinically meaningful findings are the substantial improvements in functional capacity, neuromuscular performance, and postural control, accompanied by favorable but exploratory changes in tumor biomarker concentrations.

A central finding was the marked improvement in cardiorespiratory and functional performance. Walking distance during the 6MWT increased by 51.9%, exceeding the minimal clinically important difference reported in oncology populations and underscoring the clinical relevance of this change (26). Although the achieved distance remained below that typically observed in younger cohorts—reflecting the influence of advanced age, prior oncological treatment, and neurological impairment—the magnitude of improvement highlights the responsiveness of functional capacity to structured exercise even in complex survivors (27,28).

VO26MWT increased substantially (from 66% to 105% of predicted), indicating improved aerobic efficiency. Although the 6MWT is not intended to estimate peak VO2 and predictive equations may misrepresent maximal aerobic capacity in cancer survivors (29), the use of portable gas analysis enabled direct physiological assessment during a standardized functional task. Accordingly, these improvements should be interpreted as clinically meaningful enhancements in cardiorespiratory function rather than laboratory-defined maximal capacity. In this context, concurrent exercise emerges as an effective strategy to restore functional performance in CRC survivors, aligning with umbrella-level evidence showing that higher cardiorespiratory fitness is associated with lower cancer incidence and mortality, including colorectal malignancies, without implying individual-level causality (28).

Improvements in lower-limb strength constituted another key clinical outcome. The 33% increase in sit-to-stand performance reflects a meaningful gain in functional strength, particularly relevant in cancer survivors, in whom muscle loss may occur at accelerated rates (30). Resistance exercise is the most effective modality to counteract impaired mobility and muscle weakness, supporting independence and tolerance to daily activities. Evidence suggests that strength-related benefits may be sustained over time in older cancer survivors, underscoring their importance within long-term survivorship care (31).

Balance and postural control improvements represent a further clinically significant finding. The intervention incorporated targeted neuromuscular and balance training in a patient with long-standing paraparesis and high baseline fall risk. Improvements in postural stability suggest enhanced proprioceptive control and neuromotor coordination. Such adaptations are highly relevant in oncology, as postural instability and sensory deficits are common consequences of cancer treatments and neurological comorbidity, contributing to increased fall risk. Previous randomized evidence in patients with mCRC has shown that multimodal exercise counteracts the progress of chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy and improves balance and strength (32), and the current findings align with this evidence.

Changes in CEA and CA 19-9 were observed following the intervention but warrant cautious interpretation. Tumor markers are subject to substantial intra-individual biological variability, and longitudinal interpretation should consider the Reference Change Value, influenced by analytical methodology and population characteristics (33). Accordingly, the reduction in CA 19-9 may fall within expected biological fluctuation, and the decrease in CEA cannot be interpreted as evidence of tumor response in the absence of corroborating clinical or radiological findings.

Notably, in this patient, elevations in tumor biomarkers were followed on two separate occasions by supervised lifestyle interventions, after which biomarker concentrations declined. While this repeated temporal pattern does not establish causality and may reflect biological variability (33), it represents an observation of interest warranting further investigation in longitudinal and controlled studies. Importantly, no radiological imaging was performed at the time of the intervention, and available imaging before and after the intervention demonstrated no evidence of disease recurrence or progression. Therefore, any association between the intervention and biomarker changes must be regarded as temporal rather than causal.

Nutritional counseling was integrated as a supportive component of the intervention, aimed at enhancing exercise-induced adaptations and overall metabolic health. The dietary approach emphasized a whole-food, Mediterranean- and Nordic-style pattern rich in polyphenols, omega-3 fatty acids, fiber, and antioxidant micronutrients, which have been associated with improved overall survival in long-term CRC survivors (18). Although scientific evidence suggests that several of these dietary components may be associated with lower plasma concentrations of inflammatory markers and potentially influence pathways related to cancer progression and recurrence (34), no tumor-specific effects can be inferred from this case. Rather, the nutritional intervention likely contributed to adherence, functional recovery, and physiological resilience within a multimodal lifestyle framework (35).

The primary limitation of this report is its single-case design, which precludes generalization and causal inference. Nevertheless, case reports remain valuable in underrepresented populations, such as oncogeriatric patients with neurological comorbidities, by illustrating feasibility, safety, and clinically relevant outcomes often not captured in randomized trials.

Conclusions

This case report demonstrates that a supervised, multimodal exercise-nutrition intervention is feasible and safe in a multimorbid, elderly survivor of mCRC with neurological impairment. The most robust findings are the substantial improvements in cardiorespiratory fitness, muscle strength, and balance, representing clinically meaningful outcomes in this high-risk population. Although changes in tumor biomarkers were observed, they should be interpreted cautiously within the context of biological variability and without implying causality. Overall, these findings support the relevance of personalized lifestyle-based rehabilitation strategies within comprehensive survivorship care for complex oncogeriatric patients.
