Chromosome-level genome assembly of two cultivated Jujubes
Authors: Tianjun Wei, Hui Li, Xueshuang Huang, Peng Yang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractJujube (Ziziphus jujuba Mill.) is a valuable tree species with economic, edible, medicinal, and ecological conservation benefits. In this study, we used PacBio HiFi sequencing and Hi-C technology to assemble chromosome-level genomes of two cultivated Jujubes, namely ‘Lingwuchangzao’ and ‘Shiguang’. The genome sizes of ‘Lingwuchangzao’ and ‘Shiguang’ were 385.66 Mb and 394.12 Mb, respectively, with contig N50 sizes of 30.62 Mb and 4.30 Mb. These genomes contained 31,082 and 31,015 protein-coding genes, with repeat element contents of 42.11% and 42.33%, respectively. Phylogenetic analysis revealed that ‘Lingwuchangzao’ was sister to ‘Shiguang’ and followed by ‘Dongzao’. Additionally, comparative analysis of gene families among ‘Lingwuchangzao’, ‘Shiguang’, ‘Dongzao’, ‘Junzao’, and ‘Suanzao’ identified 15,988 (57.98%) core gene families, 11,191 (40.59%) disposable gene families, and 394 (1.43%) private gene families. Overall, the assembly of the genomes of these two cultivated Jujube varieties provides valuable genetic information for Jujube evolution, functional genomics research, and molecular breeding.Subject terms: Plant evolution, Comparative genomics

Background & Summary

Jujube (Ziziphus jujuba Mill.), commonly known as Chinese Jujube, is an economically important tree species within the Rhamnaceae family, originating from China1. Jujube fruits are rich in nutrients and contain various medicinal ingredients, such as sugars, organic acids, vitamins, amino acids, minerals, cyclic nucleotides, triterpenoids, flavonoids, and alkaloids2,3. They are considered valuable for both medicinal and dietary purposes. Jujube trees exhibit strong adaptability to environmental stresses, particularly drought and salinity, making them important for economic development and ecological restoration in arid regions4. Cultivated Jujube varieties have been developed from the wild sour Jujube (Z. jujuba Mill. var. spinosa) through selective breeding5. There are currently numerous cultivated Jujube varieties, each with distinct taste, resistance, nutritional content, and medicinal value6. Understanding the genomic characteristics of Jujube can offer insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying these traits. Recently, Jujube genome research has made breakthrough progress, and high-quality genomes of the fresh-eating cultivar ‘Dongzao’7,8, dry-eating cultivar ‘Junzao’9, and wild sour Jujube ‘Suanzao’ have been assembled10. However, limited information is available regarding the genomic information and genetic diversity of other Jujube varieties. Therefore, sequencing and assembling the genomes of additional Jujube varieties can bridge these knowledge gaps and provide valuable genetic information for enhancing Jujube varieties through molecular breeding.

In this study, we collected two cultivated Jujube varieties (‘Lingwuchangzao’ and ‘Shiguang’) that are widely cultivated in Ningxia Hui Autonomous Region of China. ‘Lingwuchangzao’ is well-known for its large fruit size, distinctive taste, and high nutritional value (Fig. 1a), while ‘Shiguang’ is a rare and prized variety appreciated for its large fruit, high yield, sweet and sour flavor, and vibrant color (Fig. 1c). We successfully assembled high-quality chromosome-level genomes for both varieties using PacBio high-fidelity (HiFi) long-reads and high-throughput chromosome conformation capture sequencing (Hi-C) data (Fig. 1b,d). The genome sizes of ‘Lingwuchangzao’ and ‘Shiguang’ were 385.66 Mb and 394.12 Mb, respectively, with contig N50 values of 30.62 Mb and 4.30 Mb, and scaffold N50 values of 31.33 Mb and 30.75 Mb. Repeat element annotation revealed that 42.11% (162.38 Mb) and 42.33% (166.80 Mb) of the genome sequences consisted of repeat elements, with LTRs accounting for 20.79% and 20.82%, respectively. We also annotated 31,082 and 31,015 protein-coding genes, 1,434 and 766 rRNAs, 1,095 and 1,211 tRNAs, 55 and 75 miRNAs in the ‘Lingwuchangzao’ and ‘Shiguang’ genomes, respectively. The BUSCO completeness assessment indicated that the genome assemblies of ‘Lingwuchangzao’ and ‘Shiguang’ were 99.01% and 98.64% complete, respectively, demonstrating high genome completeness. These datasets serve as a valuable genomic resource for further functional genomics research and molecular breeding of Jujube.

Methods

Sample collection and sequencing

Fresh young leaves from two cultivated Jujube varieties (‘Lingwuchangzao’ and ‘Shiguang’) were collected in Zhongning County, Ningxia Hui Autonomous Region, China for genome sequencing. Genomic DNA was extracted from the leaves using the cetyltrimethylammonium bromide method. Libraries with an insert size of 350 bp were constructed following the manufacturer’s instructions. For genome size assessment, the libraries were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq. 6000 platform, generating a total of 52.14 Gb (135×) and 62.53 Gb (71×) paired-end reads (PE150) for ‘Lingwuchangzao’ and ‘Shiguang’, respectively (Table 1).

For PacBio HiFi sequencing, approximately 10 µg of genomic DNA was sheared into 15 kb fragments using Megaruptor 2. These fragments were concentrated using 0.45× AMPure PB beads and SMRTbell libraries were constructed with the SMRTbell Express Template Prep kit 2.0. Each library was sequenced on PacBio Sequel II platform, and HiFi reads were generated using the CCS software (https://ccs.how/) with parameters (–minLength 50–maxLength 50000–minPasses 1–minPredictedAccuracy 0.99 -j 6). A total of 18.35 Gb (48×) and 16.58 Gb (19×) HiFi reads, with N50 sizes of 14,162 bp and 19,791 bp, were obtained for ‘Lingwuchangzao’ and ‘Shiguang’, respectively (Table 1).

For Hi-C sequencing, fresh leaf tissues were fixed in 2% formaldehyde and then digested using the HindIII restriction endonuclease. Hi-C fragment libraries were prepared following the Proximo Hi-C protocol and subsequently sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq. 6000 platform using the paired-end module. A total of 53.38 Gb (138×) and 50.55 Gb (58×) Hi-C data were acquired for ‘Lingwuchangzao’ and ‘Shiguang’, respectively (Table 1).

For genome annotation, transcriptome sequencing was performed on leaves and fruits of ‘Lingwuchangzao’ and ‘Shiguang’, respectively. RNA libraries were constructed using the TruSeq RNA Sample Prep Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and sequenced on the Illumina Novaseq. 6000 platform with PE150. A total of 12.33 Gb and 12.61 Gb RNA-seq reads were obtained for ‘Lingwuchangzao’ and ‘Shiguang’, respectively (Table 1).

Genome survey

The Illumina short-reads were initially filtered using Fastp11 v0.19.3. Jellyfish12 v2.1.4 was used to calculated the frequency distribution with parameters (-h 1000000000). Genomescope13 v2.00 software was utilized to estimate the genome characteristics with parameters (-k 21 -p 2 or 4 -m 1000000000). The evaluation results showed that ‘Lingwuchangzao’ was diploid, while ‘Shiguang’ was autotetraploid, and the haploid genome sizes were approximately 347.77 Mb and 322.83 Mb, with heterozygosity rates of 2.0% and 2.8%, respectively (Fig. 2).

Chromosome-level genome assembly

De novo assembly of PacBio HiFi reads was performed using Hifiasm14 v0.16 with parameters (−l = 2, -n = 4). The assembled draft genomes of ‘Lingwuchangzao’ and ‘Shiguang’ were 392.70 Mb and 876.05 Mb, respectively, with contig N50 sizes of 30.62 Mb and 4.30 Mb (Table 2). The contig cutoff values for ‘Lingwuchangzao’ and ‘Shiguang’ were 17.37 kb and 23.46 kb, respectively, while the mean and standard deviation of contig length for ‘Lingwuchangzao’ (5.37 ± 11.33) were substantially larger than those of ‘Shiguang’ (2.37 ± 3.50), resulting in a higher contig N50 value. Subsequently, the Hi-C sequencing data were aligned to the assembled draft genome using BWA15 v0.7.10. The scaffolds were clustered onto chromosomes using LACHESIS16 with the following parameters: CLUSTER_MIN_RE_SITES = 33; CLUSTER_MAX_LINK_DENSITY = 2; ORDER_MIN_N_RES_IN_TRUNK = 32; ORDER_MIN_N_RES_IN_SHREDS = 31. The resulting chromosome-level genome for ‘Lingwuchangzao’ was 385.66 Mb, with 98.16% of sequences anchored to 12 pseudochromosomes and a scaffold N50 size of 31.33 Mb (Table 3). Due to ‘Shiguang’ genome being an autotetraploid and the low sequencing data depth, a diploid genome was not achieved at the chromosome-level. Therefore, redundant sequences were removed the diploid draft genome using purge_dups v1.2.5, and then the haploid draft genome of ‘Shiguang’ was constructed based on the Hi-C data using the CGAP software developed by Biomarker Technologies. The resulting haploid draft genome of ‘Shiguang’ was 394.12 Mb, and anchoring to the chromosome-level was accomplished using Hi-C data, with 98.01% of sequences anchored to 12 pseudochromosomes and scaffold N50 sizes of 30.75 Mb (Table 3).

Genome annotation

Repeat elements were identified using a combination of homology-based and de novo-based methods. RepeatModeler17 v2.0.1, RepeatScout18 v1.0.6, and RECON19 v1.0.8 were used to construct a de novo repeat library. LTRharvest20 v1.5.9, LTR_finder21 v2.8, and LTR_retriever22 v2.9.0 were employed to identify intact LTR-RTs. A non-redundant species-specific repeat element library was created by merging the de novo library with the established Dfam v3.5 database (https://dfam.org/home). RepeatMasker23 v4.12 was used to identify and categorize the final repeat elements. In the ‘Lingwuchangzao’ and ‘Shiguang’ genomes, 162.38 Mb (42.11%) and 166.80 Mb (42.33%) of repeat elements were annotated, with LTRs accounting for 20.79% and 20.82%, respectively (Table 4). The most prevalent transposable elements were Copia (8.65% and 8.96%) and Gypsy (12.14% and 11.86%) in ‘Lingwuchangzao’ and ‘Shiguang’ genomes.

For protein-coding gene annotation, a combination of homology-based, de novo, and transcriptome-based predictions was employed. GeMoMa24 v1.7 was used for homologous gene prediction based on protein sequences of ‘Dongzao’, ‘Suanzao’, Arabidopsis thaliana, Malus domestica, and Prunus persica. Augustus25 v3.1.0 and SNAP26 v2006-07-28 were used for de novo gene prediction. RNA-seq data were aligned to the genome using Hisat27 v2.1.0 and assembled by Stringtie28 v 2.1.4. GeneMarkS-T29 v5.1 was used to predict genes based on the assembled transcripts. The gene models from these different approaches were merged into a non-redundant gene set using EVM30 v1.1.1. For non-coding RNAs, tRNAscan-SE31 v1.3.1 was used to identify tRNAs, Barrnap v0.9 (https://github.com/tseemann/barrnap) was used to detect rRNAs, and Infernal32 v1.1 was used to identify miRNAs and snRNAs against the Rfam33 v14.5 database. Finally, a total of 31,082 and 31,015 protein-coding genes with an average length of 3,375.98 bp and 3,353.53 bp were predicted in the ‘Lingwuchangzao’ and ‘Shiguang’ genomes, respectively (Table 4). Additionally, a total of 2,725 and 2,270 non-coding genes, including 1,434 and 766 rRNAs, 1,095 and 1,211 tRNAs, 55 and 75 miRNAs, and 141 and 218 other ncRNAs, were identified in the ‘Lingwuchangzao’ and ‘Shiguang’ genomes, respectively.

Functional annotation of protein-coding genes was performed using DIAMOND34 v0.9.29.130 (with an E-value cutoff of 1e−5) against five protein sequence databases: NR (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/refseq/about/nonredundantproteins), eggNOG (http://eggnog-mapper.embl.de/), InterPro (http://www.ebi.ac.uk/interpro/), TrEMBL (https://www.uniprot.org/statistics/TrEMBL), and Swiss-Prot (https://www.sib.swiss/swiss-prot). The predicted genes were aligned with the eggNOG v5.0 database to obtain Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEEG) annotations. The protein domains were annotated by searching against the InterPro v73.0 and Pfam v27.0 (http://pfam-legacy.xfam.org/) databases using InterProScan35 v5.34 and HMMER36 v3.1, respectively. Finally, a total of 29,979 (96.45%) and 30,017 (96.78%) genes were functionally annotated in at least one of the above databases for ‘Lingwuchangzao’ and ‘Shiguang’, respectively (Table 4).

Comparative genomics

To determine the evolutionary relationship among various Jujube varieties, protein sequences from ‘Lingwuchangzao’, ‘Shiguang’, ‘Dongzao’, and ‘Suanzao’ were used for gene family analysis using OrthoFinder37 v2.5.4 with default parameters. A total of 12,803 single copy genes were identified and aligned using MUSCLE38 v3.8.31. The phylogenetic tree was constructed using RAxML39 v8.2.12 with “PROTGAMMAJTT” model and visualized using FigTree v1.4.4 (http://tree.bio.ed.ac.uk/software/Figtree/). Syntenic blocks were identified using JCVI v0.84 (https://github.com/tanghaibao/jcvi). The Ks values of paralogous gene pairs were used to predict WGD events and calculated using wgd40 v1.1.1. Here, phylogenetic tree revealed that ‘Lingwuchangzao’ was sister to ‘Shiguang’ and followed by ‘Dongzao’, indicating a closer relationship among these cultivars, consistent with previous studies based on short-read genome-wide SNP data9 (Fig. 3a). Intergenomic collinearity analysis showed a high degree of relatedness among the different Jujube varieties (Fig. 3b). All four species exhibited a common peak around a Ks value of 1.6 and a smaller peak around a Ks value of 0.1, suggesting recent species-specific duplication occurs in Jujubes, consistent with previous reports (Fig. 3c).

Comparative analysis of gene families

To identify core and variable genes in different Jujube genomes, we used OrthoFinder37 v2.5.4 with the diamond option to cluster the protein-coding genes. Following a methodology similar to previous pan-genome analyses in Brassica rapa, the gene clusters were classified as core genes (present in five genomes), dispensable genes (present in two to four genomes), and private genes (present in only one genome). ClusterProfiler41 v3.14.0 was used to perform KEGG enrichment analysis on the core and private genes.

Overall, the gene family clustering analysis of ‘Lingwuchangzao’, ‘Shiguang’, ‘Dongzao’, ‘Junzao’, and ‘Suanzao’ revealed that the number of gene families in the pan-genome increased with the genome, while the core gene families decreased progressively (Fig. 4a). Among the 27,573 gene families identified in the five Jujube genomes, 15,988 (57.98%) were core gene families, 11,191 (40.59%) were dispensable gene families, and 394 (1.43%) were private gene families (Fig. 4b). Furthermore, comparison of gene lengths, CDS lengths, and exon numbers across different gene family types showed significant differences, with core gene families exhibiting higher values than the dispensable and private gene families, indicating higher conservation (Fig. 4c). The core genes were enriched in the KEGG database for α-linolenic acid metabolism, zeatin biosynthesis, proteasome, and flavone and flavonol biosynthesis (Fig. 4d). Conversely, the private genes showed enrichment in KEGG pathways associated with photosynthesis, carbon fixation in photosynthetic organisms, fatty acid biosynthesis, and diterpenoid biosynthesis (Fig. 4e).

The raw data of RNA-seq reads, Illumina reads, Hi-C reads, and PacBio HiFi reads from ‘Shiguang’ were submitted to the NCBI SRA database with accession numbers SRR2672295146, SRR2672295247, SRR2672295348, SRR2672295449, under BioProject accession number PRJNA1035946. Technical Validation

Completeness assessment was performed using BUSCO53 v.5.2.2 (embryophyta odb10) and CEGMA54 v2.5. The BUSCO completeness for the genome of ‘Lingwuchangzao’ was determined to be 99.01% [S: 97.27%, D: 1.73%, F: 0.25%, M: 0.74%], while for ‘Shiguang’ it was 98.64% [S: 83.40%, D: 15.24%, F: 0.12%, M: 1.24%]. The CEGMA completeness of the genome assemblies for ‘Lingwuchangzao’ and ‘Shiguang’ were 98.47% and 96.29%, respectively. Illumina short-reads and PacBio long-reads were mapped to the genome using BWA15 v0.7.10 and Minimap255 v2.14-r883, and the mapping rates were calculated. The mapping rates for ‘Lingwuchangzao’ were 99.29% for short-reads and 99.68% for long-reads, while for ‘Shiguang’ they were 99.42% for short-reads and 99.88% for long-reads. The depth distribution map of the HiFi long reads covering the genome showed that the average coverage depth of ‘Lingwuchangzao’ and ‘Shiguang’ was 47× and 41×, respectively, and followed a Poisson distribution, indicating high genome assembly completeness and sequencing uniformity. (Fig. 5). The Hi-C heatmap displayed strong interaction signals along the diagonal of the chromosomes, indicating accurate chromosome assembly (Fig. 6). Additionally, the BUSCO completeness for the genome annotations of ‘Lingwuchangzao’ and ‘Shiguang’ were 98.51% [S: 96.84%, D: 1.67%, F: 1.18%, M: 0.31%] and 94.80% [S: 86.74%, D: 8.05%, F: 1.67%, M: 3.53%] with the embryophyta odb10 database, respectively. Overall, the quality control metrics demonstrate that the genome assemblies of ‘Lingwuchangzao’ and ‘Shiguang’ are highly complete and reliable.
