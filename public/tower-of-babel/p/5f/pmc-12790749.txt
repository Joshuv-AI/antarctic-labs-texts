Drivers and implications of lineage-specific cancer-related mutations in human pluripotent and adult stem cells
Authors: Jonathan Jung, Nissim Benvenisty
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryHuman pluripotent stem cells (PSCs) are known to harbor mutations in tumor-associated genes, and here we aim to examine the status of adult stem cells (ASCs). We thus identify cancer-related mutations in 18% of about 600 mesenchymal stem cell samples, and in 41% of about 200 neural stem cell (NSC) samples. We show a lineage-specific profile of cancer-related genes, demonstrating that TP53 is a central mutated gene in human PSCs but not in mesenchymal or NSCs. We suggest that the lineage-specificity of tumor-associated genes correlates with their expression levels and with tumor-specific mutations in patients. We also show the consequences of mutations in oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes on the transcriptome of each specific stem cell lineage. We therefore propose a categorization of these mutated samples for further appreciation of their severity and emphasize the importance of genetic screening in pluripotent and ASC lines.

Introduction

Human pluripotent stem cells (PSCs) are commonly used in developmental research, disease modeling, and regenerative medicine due to their ability to differentiate into all cell types and seemingly endless proliferative capabilities (Ben-David et al., 2012; Avior et al., 2016; Kirkeby et al., 2025). Likewise, tissue-derived adult stem cells (ASCs) play a major role in biomedical research, and some of them can readily propagate in culture due to their self-renewal capacity. Specifically, mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) isolated from a broad range of sources (bone marrow, adipose tissue, cord blood, and more) can differentiate into muscle, bone, cartilage, and adipocytes, and are used in multiple clinical trials (Kimbrel and Lanza, 2020; Rauch and Mandrup, 2021). Neural stem cells (NSCs), which are mainly isolated from fetal central nervous system and differentiate into neuronal and glial cells, are central in understanding brain development and disease, and are also used in clinical trials (De Filippis and Binda, 2012; Fernandez-Muñoz et al., 2021). The self-renewal capacity of PSCs and ASCs implies that they are constantly engaged in DNA replication, increasing their susceptibility to DNA damage and genomic defect accumulation (Branzei and Foiani, 2008). The genomic defects can range from large to small chromosomal aberrations. Indeed, large chromosomal aberrations were documented in human PSCs (Halliwell et al., 2020; Andrews et al., 2022) and adult MSCs and NSCs (Ben-David et al., 2011); however, the type of these aberrations is lineage-specific (Ben-David et al., 2011).

In addition to chromosomal aberrations, human PSCs have been shown to harbor cancer-related mutations. These were first documented in the TP53 gene in multiple lines of either human embryonic stem cells (ESCs) or induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) (Merkle et al., 2017). Later, additional cancer-related genes were identified to be mutated in hPSCs by analyzing RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) (“RNA2CM” tool) (Avior et al., 2021; Lezmi and Benvenisty, 2021) or DNA-seq (genome sequencing) (Rouhani et al., 2022) data. Recently, a large-scale analysis of over 140 human PSC lines revealed cancer-related mutations in about 20% of the samples of pluripotent cells and their differentiated derivatives, and suggested the effects of such mutations on cellular differentiation (Lezmi et al., 2024). Point mutations have been studied in a few lines of MSCs (De Becker et al., 2022; Kim et al., 2017), but analysis of the extent of DNA mutations in ASCs is still lacking. Nevertheless, point mutations exist in vivo (Abyzov et al., 2017; Vijg and Dong, 2020; Fasching et al., 2021), and are often obscured in the body through mechanisms such as mosaicism and immune surveillance (Vijg and Dong, 2020; Corthay, 2014). However, the in vitro culture of stem cells alters this situation, allowing specific mutations to persist and expand. Factors such as prolonged culturing periods and medium conditions may introduce new mutagenic pressures (Kuijk et al., 2020). Here, performing a large-scale analysis, we identify cancer-related mutations in ASCs, either MSCs or NSCs.

We demonstrate lineage-specific patterning and suggest that these genes’ expression levels and involvement in human tumors may underline the lineage specificity. We also show the consequences of such mutations, activating oncogenes and selectively repressing tumor suppressor genes, on different pathways in PSCs and ASCs, in some cases, evident of potential tumorigenicity phenotypes.

Results

Cancer-related mutations in human adult stem cells

To inspect cancer-related mutations in different stem cell types, we analyzed over 1,400 RNA-seq samples, of which 606 are from human ESCs, 603 from MSCs, and 198 from NSCs (Figure 1A; Table S1). The cell identity was validated by principal-component analysis (PCA), uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP), and t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) presentations. Analysis of gene expression profiles with and without batch effect correction have shown defined clusters of the three cell types (Figure S1A). Analysis of cell-specific markers further confirmed cell identity, and random inclusion of male and female samples in all lineages (Figure S1B). Additional gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) for each lineage compared to the others revealed that the top-ranking pathways (of the 100 enriched genes) were highly suggestive of the correct annotation. We next searched for mutations in over 500 tier 1 cancer genes, where the base substitution is the same as in at least 10 patients in the Catalogue of Somatic Mutations in Cancer (COSMIC) database (https://cancer.sanger.ac.uk/cosmic) (Tate et al., 2019). We thus identified a similar percentage of samples containing at least one cancer-related mutation in MSCs and ESCs (18% and 20%, respectively, Figure 1A), and a higher mutation rate in NSC samples (41%, Figure 1A). Breakdown of the data according to sources of derivation revealed that mutations appeared in MSCs regardless of whether they originated from bone marrow, adipose tissue, cord blood, or other sources. Similarly, mutations were detected in NSCs whether derived from fetal forebrain, hindbrain, spinal cord, or other sources (Figure 1B). In addition, at least one mutated sample appears in 28% of ESC studies (38/134), 51% of MSC studies (37/73), and in 63% of NSC studies (12/19; Figure 1C). The relatively high percentage of ASC studies with mutations may be attributed to the fact that many of them include multiple cell lines from different derivations in the same study.

Lineage-specific mutation profiles and in vitro acquisition

Next, we analyzed the specific cancer-related genes emerging in each lineage (Table S2). Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes/Proteins (STRING) analysis (Szklarczyk et al., 2023) of these genes demonstrated most of them to be interconnected, with TP53 serving as the central connector hub in ESCs (Figure 1D). Clustering mutation events by gene revealed distinct patterns across the different lineages (Figure 2A). Notably, TP53 is the predominant gene in the ESC mutation distribution (67% of mutations), consistent with previous findings (Lezmi et al., 2024). In contrast, MSCs and NSCs exhibit a wider variety of mutations, with a more evenly distributed contribution from multiple genes (Figure 2A). TP53 was only the fifth mutated gene in MSCs and NSCs, appearing in seven of the mutated samples in MSCs and in six of the mutated samples in NSCs. These patterns suggest the existence of lineage specificity in cancer-related mutations. Since many of the lines of MSCs and NSCs are uniquely analyzed in single studies, assessing which mutations are acquired in vitro and which originated from the donor tissue is difficult. There are, however, some cases where the same cell line was used in at least two studies, and a mutation appears only in one of the two. In these cases, we could suggest that the mutation was acquired in culture, as seen not only for ESCs but also for MSCs and NSCs (Figure 2B). Correlation of mutation rate with gene expression levels and in vivo tumors

We next examined the expression levels of the specific genes found to be mutated in our analysis. Interestingly, we found the top contributing genes to mutational landscape in each lineage to be highly expressed in that specific lineage when compared to the other two cell types (Figures 3A and S2A). TP53 and FGFR3 are the top contributors in ESCs and NSCs, respectively, and NOTCH2 and MET are top contributors in MSCs (Figure 3A). When compared between lineages, we observed a positive correlation between the genes’ contribution to the mutated landscape and the genes’ expression level mainly in the comparison of the MSCs to either ESCs or NSCs (Figure 3B). We speculate that this correlation suggests that highly expressed genes have also increased functional relevance. There is a notably weaker correlation between ESCs and NSCs, which may be due to their transcriptional similarity, as NSCs arise early during development. This close developmental relationship may limit transcriptional divergence and, in turn, reduce the dynamic range needed to detect a stronger correlation. An example can be observed in the Notch pathway, where NOTCH1 and NOTCH2 are homologous genes (Fan et al., 2004). In MSCs, NOTCH1 is expressed at lower levels than in ESCs and NSCs, while NOTCH2 is found to be highly expressed specifically in MSCs (Figures 3A and S2). Accordingly, NOTCH2 is the top mutated gene in MSCs, while NOTCH1 mutations appear in ESCs and NSCs but not in MSCs. Though this “expression-mutation tendency” correlation exists, it is not very strong and is clearly not the only contributor to the frequency of mutation events, suggesting that additional driving forces should be considered. Following this notion, we revisited the COSMIC database (Tate et al., 2019) to find if the TP53 gene had any lineage-specific correlation with in vivo tumors. Interestingly, when assessing tumors derived from the various lineages, we saw that in teratomas and teratocarcinomas, which tend to be derived from pluripotent cells, the most abundant mutation is in the TP53 gene (Figure 3C). In contrast, in tumors related to the other lineages, TP53 ranks much lower (Figure 3C). Notably, the disproportionate contribution of TP53 to the mutational landscape of ESCs favors analysis of the source of lineage specificity, while MSCs and NSCs do not exhibit such a dominant pattern, and therefore, a similar correlation analysis is less relevant. Following this observation, we suggest that different cell types harbor distinct cancer-related mutations, and that factors beyond the culturing period contribute to the predominance of TP53 mutations in ESCs. These factors could affect other genes as well.

Consequences of mutations in oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes

The mutated genes we found in our analysis are classified by the COSMIC database (Tate et al., 2019) as either oncogenes, tumor suppressor genes, or both (Figure 4A top). When focusing on each lineage separately, we still see that all classifications contribute to each lineage (Figure 4A middle). In oncogenes, a single aberrant allele can underlie potential transformation, shown by an allelic frequency of 0.5, presenting a homogeneous population of heterozygous mutations (Figure 4A bottom). However, in tumor suppressor genes, there may be a need to induce mutations in both alleles, unless a dominant negative feature is involved (Willis et al., 2004). As such, tumor suppressor genes (and those classified as both tumor suppressor genes and oncogenes) can also be found in higher allelic frequencies, up to 1.0, suggesting a loss-of-heterozygosity (LOH) event in some samples (Figure 4A bottom). By applying e-SNP-karyotyping (Weissbein et al., 2016), we confirmed monoallelic expression of chromosome 17p in ESCs, where TP53 is located, further supporting our assumption of LOH (Figure S3). To analyze the consequences of cancer-related mutations in human ESCs, MSCs, and NSCs, we performed a differential expression analysis to unveil possible aberrant phenotypes common to all mutated samples. By initial PCA visualization, we did not identify clear expression profiles of the mutated samples compared to all other samples (even without batch effect correction). This is likely due to the lineage-specific expression patterns, shadowing the more subtle molecular effects promoted by each mutated gene (Figure S4A). Hence, the samples were first segregated by lineage to nullify the clear distinct expression profiles between them. We then isolated the differentially expressed genes in each group by comparing the mutated to unmutated samples, followed by a GSEA (Figure 4B). The analysis identified pathways that were significantly depleted in the mutated samples. Each lineage shows distinct pathways that characterize the mutated vs. unmutated samples. For example, in ESC samples’ analysis, the top depleted pathways are “response to transforming growth factor β (TGF-β) signaling” and “signal transduction by p53.” The latter can be explained by the abundance of the TP53 mutated samples, conferring a downregulation of p53 signaling. Response to TGF-β may have appeared due to its tumor suppressive nature (Massagué, 2008). In MSCs, the most depleted process is aerobic respiration, which may be due to the important role of mitochondria in these cells (Ren et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2022). The second depleted pathway was “response to TGF-β signaling.” However, in MSCs, TGF-β may potentially play a bigger role in differentiation mechanisms, as this annotation appears mostly in the MSC samples with mutated oncogenes, which impede developmental and differentiation processes, expanded further in the following text. NSCs’ depleted pathways consist of “cell adhesion” and “neuropeptide signaling.” Both may suggest a strayed phenotype from the typical characteristics of the cells (Morante-Redolat and Porlan, 2019; Zaben and Gray, 2013).

Subdivision of genes based on their role in cancer was also performed, separating oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes. Interestingly, the samples mutated in oncogenes showed general differentiation and developmental capacity depletion (Figure 4B). The pathways depleted were tube and epithelium development in ESCs, differentiation through TGF-β and circulatory system development in MSCs, and neural precursor proliferation and olfactory lobe development in NSCs. The tumor suppressor gene mutated samples presented depletion of general cellular processes (Figure 4B). Notably, these processes varied between the lineages as well. By identifying the genes contributing to the top depleted pathways in each lineage, we refined our data for the ability to identify intricate nuances between the different samples. By density visualization of samples of PCA of the genes contributing to the lineage-depleted pathways, we managed to observe clear segregation of unmutated, oncogene, tumor suppressor gene, and TP53 mutated samples (Figure 4C). Once more, distinct cancer-related categories appear differently in the various stem cell types, providing lineage-specific patterns. We can see that in the neural lineage, both oncogene and tumor suppressor gene mutated samples diverge from the unmutated samples to a similar extent (Figure 4C, bottom). In the pluripotent state, we observe divergence of all mutated samples, although in different trends (Figure 4C, top). However, in the mesenchymal lineage, only the samples mutated in oncogenes deviate from the unmutated cluster, suggesting that they have a stronger aberrant phenotype than the samples mutated in tumor suppressor genes (Figure 4C, middle). This observation does not include samples with TP53 mutations, which show an even greater aberrant phenotype in the mesenchymal lineage. To further explore the functional consequences of TP53 mutations, we performed GSEA comparing TP53-mutated and wild-type (WT) samples from the same lineage. Proceeding with the analysis for ESCs (as they had the most TP53-mutated samples) showed significant depletion of pathways related to “positive regulation of programmed cell death” and “apoptotic process” (Figure S4B). These pathways were also significantly depleted in samples with TP53 mutations in MSCs and NSCs, suggesting a global functional effect of TP53 mutations in human stem cells (Figure S4B).

Discussion

Here, we performed extensive analyses and identified cancer-related mutations in PSCs, MSCs, and NSCs. Similar to ESCs, in MSCs, about 20% of the samples harbor a cancer-related mutation. This is a relatively high percentage of mutations for an ASC lineage. Moreover, in NSCs, a staggering 40% of the samples had at least one cancer-related mutation (Figure 1A). This high rate may result from the origin of these samples, which was predominantly fetal tissue from abortions. The isolation of NSCs in this developmental period of organogenesis, specifically fetal brain development, is known for high rates of mutagenesis (Bae et al., 2017; Derks and Van Boxtel, 2023). This, coupled with difficulties in isolation and maintenance of NSCs in culture, may have led to the increased selective advantage of cells with cancer-related mutations. Indeed, it is challenging to conclude whether the mutations documented in ASCs come from the donor tissue or are acquired in culture. However, due to selected cases where the same cell line has shown normal and mutated genotypes, we conclude that at least some of the mutations identified in ASCs were acquired during culturing (Figure 2B). The allelic frequencies of the mutations observed fit the mutated gene’s role in cancer, whether oncogenes or tumor suppressor genes, with rare documentation of LOH in tumor suppressor genes (Figure S3).

Notably, we have discovered that the genes involved in the cancer-related mutations are lineage-specific. This is most evident with TP53, which is prevalent in PSCs, but not in ASCs (Figure 2A). TP53 is indeed the most mutated gene in human tumors (Levine, 2020), but is not the most mutated in every type of tumor (Figure 3C). Another intriguing feature in our analysis is the positive correlation between lineage-specific expression of the cancer-related genes and their rate of mutation per the respective lineage (Figure 3B). The differential expression analysis, followed by GSEA, offered a broader view of the pathways that appear depleted in mutated samples within each lineage, with additional patterns emerging when considering their roles in cancer (Figure 4). Both of these observations derived from the samples’ expression may suggest that some genes and pathways are more prominent in specific stem cells as compared to others; thus, mutations in them might be associated with lineage-specific selective pressures. Even depleted general cellular processes were still lineage-specific, possibly reflecting the differing hierarchy of these general pathways in tumorigenic transformation per lineage. Nonetheless, TP53-mutated samples across all lineages showed consistent depletion of pathways related to programmed cell death and apoptosis (Figure S4B), suggesting a conserved functional impact of TP53 mutations across different stem cells.

The gravest safety concern in stem cell therapy is the development of tumors. Indeed, transplantation of cells derived from autologous PSCs into a diabetic patient has already resulted in a malignant immature teratoma (Han et al., 2022). Induction of tumors has also been documented after transplantation of allogeneic NSCs into the central nervous system, which is an immune-privileged site (Amariglio et al., 2009; Berkowitz et al., 2016; Baloh et al., 2022). In one case, a child with ataxia telangiectasia transplanted with NSCs was diagnosed with multifocal donor-derived brain tumors, four years after transplantation (Amariglio et al., 2009). In another case, a 66-year-old patient, after a stroke, was transplanted with neural fetal stem cells and developed glioproliferative lesions in the spinal cord (Berkowitz et al., 2016). In a third case, transplantation of neural progenitor cells secreting glial cell line-derived neurotrophic factor in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) patients resulted in the discovery of benign neuromas in the injection sites at postmortem (Baloh et al., 2022). Notably, all reported cases of tumor formation following ASC transplantation are of neural lineage. This may be partially explained by differences in the immune environments into which these cells are introduced. NSCs are usually transplanted into the brain, an immune-privileged organ, while MSCs are typically delivered into more immunocompetent tissues, where allogenic transplanted tumorigenic cells may be more effectively cleared. Taken together, our data suggest that cancer-related mutations are prevalent in both PSCs and ASCs and should be inspected before their use in cell therapy, as their impact may vary depending on lineage and transplantation environment.

Furthermore, we propose a heuristic framework to divide the cancer-related mutations identified in stem cells into different categories based on the expected severity of the type of mutation (Table 1). This classification is intended as a guiding model, based on our interpretation of the literature, primarily from Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM) (https://omim.org). Starting from the lowest severity—heterozygous mutations in tumor suppressor genes with no evidence of potential tumorigenicity. No known disease phenotype exists for the individuals carrying a germ-line heterozygote mutation; for example, individuals with a heterozygote mutation in the ERCC5 gene do not have increased susceptibility to develop tumors. In these cases, which harbor 4.6% of the samples, we are not sure they harbor a higher risk for tumorigenicity. The next level of severity is of heterozygous mutations in tumor suppressor genes, where individuals carrying a heterozygote mutation have a higher risk of tumors. An example is the APC gene, where a single mutated allele increases susceptibility to adenomatous polyposis and colorectal cancer. About 54.1% of the samples have such a mutation. In this category, dominant negative mutations are a special class with a higher risk. The following level of severity is of oncogenes, where, by definition, a single mutation is needed to turn the proto-oncogene into a cancerous oncogene. An example is HRAS, where point mutations can result in constitutive pathway activation. About 25.7% of the samples had a mutation in an oncogene. We suggest that even a higher risk exists in cells where biallelic hits occurred in a tumor suppressor gene, such as LOH. An example found in this study is of LOH in TP53. About 7.9% of the samples had such an event. The highest severity involves samples found with mutations in multiple cancer-related genes. For example, a sample of mutated TP53 and NF1 may reflect cooperative effects or advanced selection during culture. About 8.6% of the samples had mutations in multiple genes. While we cannot assign definitive tumorigenic risk levels to these categories without functional validation, this classification provides a useful framework for interpreting mutation severity in stem cells. Our data suggest that cancer-related mutations are prevalent in PSCs and ASCs, and more information should be collected to characterize them and their consequences. Limitations of the study

This study demonstrates cancer-related mutations in MSCs and NSCs, with lineage specificity when compared with ESCs. However, it has several limitations that should be considered. First, the stringent filtration criteria and coverage thresholds for mutation identification, although yielding high-confidence results for known cancer-related mutations, may lead to overlooked additional mutations. Second, the cell passaging number, which may correlate with the abundance of mutations, was unavailable for many samples, preventing us from fully assessing the effects of passaging on our findings. Moreover, although we could detect mutations that were acquired in vitro across all three stem cell lineages, our analysis was limited by the available information on each cell line upon its generation, and during its propagation in culture. This limitation is particularly relevant for MSCs and NSCs, where this lack of information prevents definitive conclusions about whether the mutations reflect pre-existing somatic mosaicism or appeared upon culture adaptation. Furthermore, while we have identified many recurrently mutated genes, functional analysis was somewhat limited by sample size, and the functionality of a single gene was only possible for TP53.

Methods

RNA-seq data collection and processing

Bulk RNA-seq datasets of embryonic, mesenchymal, and NSCs were collected from the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sra). Mutation identification was performed using established pipelines and tools (RNA2CM [Lezmi and Benvenisty, 2021], Trimmomatic [Bolger et al., 2014], and STAR [Dobin et al., 2013]). The detailed pipeline and thresholds are provided in the supplemental information.

Analysis of gene expression

The gene expression analysis was performed using R packages: EdgeR (Robinson et al., 2010), limma (Ritchie et al., 2015), fgsea (http://bioconductor.org/packages/fgsea), and dimensionality reduction was applied via three separate methods (PCA [Venables and Ripley, 2002], UMAP [McInnes et al., 2018], and t-SNE [Van Der Maaten and Hinton, 2008]). Loss-of-heterozygosity was examined using an adapted e-SNP-Karyotyping approach (Weissbein et al., 2016). Detailed protocols and statistical methods are provided in the supplemental information.

Datasets used

All RNA-seq data are available in NCBI’s Sequence Read Archive, referenced through accession codes provided in Table S1.
