Anti leucine-rich glioma-inactivated 1 (LGI1) antibody-positive limbic encephalitis with evolving basal ganglia T1WI hyperintensity: A case report
Authors: Ryoko Noha, Akira Yogi, Yukihiro Namihira, Mitsuyoshi Kanazawa, Yukari Tomori, Nanae Tsuchiya, Gyo Iida, Akihiro Nishie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Anti-leucine-rich glioma-inactivated 1 (LGI1) antibody-associated autoimmune encephalitis (anti-LGI1-AE) is a neuronal, surface antibody-mediated type of encephalitis characterized by medial temporal lobe hypertrophy and hyperintensity on T2-weighted images (WI). T1-weighted images (T1WI) can reveal basal ganglia hyperintensity, particularly when patients have faciobrachial dystonic seizures (FBDS). This signal change on T1WI is generally considered to correlate with FBDS. Here, we describe a female patient with FBDS and basal ganglia T1WI hyperintensity in whom imaging abnormalities did not reflect FBDS severity. Although a single observation cannot establish causality, this dissociation raises the possibility that factors other than FBDS activity contribute to altered T1WI signals and that such findings might not always reflect disease activity. Clinicians should be aware that such T1WI changes do not need to parallel FBDS to avoid overinterpreting them as clinical course markers. Further studies are required to clarify these underlying mechanisms.

Introduction

Anti-leucine-rich glioma-inactivated 1 (LGI1) antibody-associated autoimmune encephalitis (AE) (anti-LGI1-AE) is a type of neuronal surface antibody-mediated AE with an estimated incidence of 0.4 per million person-years [1]. Most patients have unilateral or bilateral hypertrophy of the medial temporal lobes, and this is accompanied by elevated signal intensity on T2-weighted images (T2WI) and fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) sequences [2]. Notably, T1-weighted imaging (T1WI) can identify unilateral or bilateral basal ganglia hyperintensity which is a radiological feature considered to be characteristic of anti-LGI1-AE in some patients [3].

For example, T1WI hyperintensity in the basal ganglia became obscured as clinical symptoms of anti-LGI1-AE improved in a patient who presented with faciobrachial dystonic seizures (FBDS) [3]. However, T1WI hyperintensity reappeared when symptoms reappeared in a different location. This suggested a correlation between FBDS disease activity and T1WI hyperintensity. Here, we describe a patient with unusual anti-LGI1-AE who presented with FBDS and basal ganglia T1WI hyperintensity that was characterized by more complicated clinical and signal changes than those previously reported.

Case report

Clinical course and treatment

A 75-year-old female with no history of infection or toxin exposure developed generalized tonic–clonic seizures. Episodes of paroxysmal involuntary twitching that lasted for a few seconds several times a day started in the right side of her face and sequentially propagated to the ipsilateral and the contralateral upper limb at 20 days after initial symptom onset. She had been evaluated, but the cause remained unclear. Levetiracetam partly controlled transient seizures, but her consciousness became impaired and memory disturbances developed. She was admitted to our hospital 54 days after the initial onset of symptoms. A neurological examination revealed brief episodes of right facial and upper arm spasms accompanied by impaired consciousness. Peripheral blood test results revealed hyponatremia (serum sodium: 114 mEq/L). Tests for antinuclear, antithyroid, antiaquaporin 4, and antimyelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein antibodies were negative. Cell counts, protein concentrations, and opening pressure were normal in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF). Tests for herpes simplex and varicella zoster viral DNA and CSF bacterial cultures were negative. Whole-body computed tomography (CT) and endoscopic assessment revealed no malignancy. Serum tumor markers including arginine vasopressin, carcinoembryonic antigen, carbohydrate antigen 19-9, and soluble interleukin-2 receptor were all within normal limits. Electroencephalography revealed no evidence of focal epileptiform activity.

She met the Graus criteria for autoimmune encephalitis (AE) [4] so that was started on intravenous methylprednisolone (1000 mg/d for 3 days) from 79 days after the initial symptom onset. At this time, an indirect immunofluorescence assay detected anti-LGI1 antibody, which confirmed a diagnosis of anti-LGI1-AE. The patient was subsequently treated with oral prednisolone (50 mg/d; approximately 1 mg/kg/d) that was tapered to 10 mg every 2 weeks. Her consciousness, neurological symptoms, and seizure frequency improved within 9 days of treatment. Intravenous immunoglobulin therapy was administered because hyponatremia persisted. Evolution of imaging findings

The patient was assessed by head CT which did not find any abnormalities at 2 days after initial onset. However, head MRI at 27, 54, 58, 66, and 89 days after onset revealed the following. Bilateral medial temporal and frontal lobe hyperintensity with hypertrophy were identified on T2WI and FLAIR at 27 days after initial symptom onset (corresponding to 7 days after FBDS onset, and 52 days before treatment). These findings were consistent with limbic encephalitis (Fig. 2). Follow-up T2WI at 54 days after initial symptom onset (corresponding to 34 days after FBDS onset and 27 days before treatment), revealed that the hyperintensity had slightly improved. In contrast, T1WI initially identified hyperintensity in the left striatum that slightly diminished at 58 days after initial symptom onset (corresponding to 38 days after FBDS onset and 21 days before treatment) (Fig. 3). Thereafter, new T1WI hyperintensity appeared at the bilateral lenticular nuclei 66 days after initial symptom onset (corresponding to 46 days after FBDS onset and 13 days before treatment). Despite clinical improvement and reduced seizure activity, MRI identified progressive bilateral basal ganglia T1WI hyperintensities without new neurological disorders at follow-up (Fig. 4). Head CT performed at 13, 52, 53, 54, 61, and 81 days after symptom onset revealed no calcification or hemorrhage in basal ganglia. There was no evidence of hemosiderin deposition on T2*-weighted imaging (T2*WI) or restricted diffusion on diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) on each MR examination. The patient had no evidence of hepatic dysfunction and peripheral blood tests did not find fluctuating blood glucose levels. She did not receive long-term central venous nutrition or toxin exposure. Discussion

We encountered a patient with anti-LGI1-AE presenting with FBDS and T1WI hyperintensity in the basal ganglia. Based on the MRI findings of T1WI hyperintensity in the basal ganglia, we considered differential diagnoses of anti-LGI1-AE, subacute hemorrhage, acquired hepatocerebral degeneration, hyperglycemia-related basal ganglia injury, and changes associated with long-term central venous nutrition. However, head CT and T2*WI revealed no abnormal findings. Therefore, we considered that a subacute hemorrhage was unlikely. She also had no evidence of hepatic dysfunction on peripheral blood tests, fluctuations in blood glucose levels, or deteriorating oxygenation. In addition, she did not receive long-term central venous nutrition or toxin exposure. Therefore, we excluded hepatocerebral degeneration, hyperglycemia-related basal ganglia injury, and changes associated with long-term central venous nutrition and finally concluded that anti-LGI1-AE caused the hyperintensity of T1WI in the basal ganglia. The hyperintensity in basal ganglia on T1WI was notably indistinct and it reappeared more prominently before treatment and intensified after therapy.

Autoimmune encephalitis comprises a spectrum of disorders characterized by immune-mediated brain tissue inflammation that targets neuronal antigens [1]. Anti-LGI1-AE is a neuronal surface antibody-mediated subtype with an incidence of 0.4 cases per million person-years [1]. Anti-LGI1-AE typically affects middle-aged men and are sometimes associated with thymoma or lung cancer [5]. Up to 90% of patients with anti-LGI1-AE experience seizures, and 36%-44% develop FBDS characterized by frequent, transient, unilateral dystonia-like movements of the face, arms, and occasionally legs that recur for a few seconds several times a day [[5], [6], [7]]. Among anti-LGI1-AE patients, FBDS is considered to be the most specific type of seizure [8]. Other common features include cognitive impairment in 70%-100% of patients, insomnia in 52%-66%, and hyponatremia in 65%-77% [5,6]. Hyponatremia might result from inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion, potentially related to concurrent LGI1 expression in the hypothalamus and kidneys [9]. Electroencephalography typically shows no specific findings for anti-LGI1-AE, although focal or diffuse slowing and occasional interictal epileptiform discharges might occur in the temporal or other brain regions [10]. The mainstay treatment for anti-LGI1-AE is immunotherapy. First-line therapies include high-dose intravenous methylprednisolone, intravenous immunoglobulin, and plasma exchange. Second-line therapies include rituximab, mycophenolate mofetil, cyclophosphamide, and azathioprine, but the disease relapses in 14%-16% of patients [6,11].

Up to 50% of patients with anti-LGI1-AE have no abnormal findings on brain MRI [12]. Hyperintensity and enlargement of the unilateral or bilateral mesial temporal lobes can be confirmed on T2WI/ FLAIR imaging in 63%-78% of patients with anti-LGI1-AE [2]. Restricted diffusivity on DWI, hemosiderin deposition on T2*WI, and contrast enhancement on contrast-enhanced T1WI are rare [1,13]. Abnormalities have been found not only in the temporal lobes, including the hippocampus, parahippocampal gyrus, and amygdala, but also in the basal ganglia.

Basal ganglia involvement can manifest as T1WI hyperintensity, although the underlying histopathological mechanism remains unclear. The proposed contributors include paramagnetic free radical manganese superoxide dismutase in injured mitochondria, iron deposition, swollen astrocytes (gemistocytes), lipid-laden macrophages, and protein denaturation [3]. Ischemia or hypoglycemia secondary to hypometabolism might cause free radicals, macrophages, and related factors [14,15]. In contrast, a stereotactic biopsy of a T1WI hyperintense lesion in the basal ganglia, has revealed mild, nonspecific, and astrocytic gliosis, but no evidence of inflammation [16]. However, further histological analyses are required to elucidate the underlying mechanisms.

Unilateral basal ganglia T1WI hyperintensity might be a characteristic feature of anti-LGI1-AE with FBDS. A review of 48 patients with anti-LGI1-AE by 2 neuroradiologists identified abnormal signal changes on T1WI in 11 of 26 patients with, but not in those without FBDS, supporting the value of this finding as a biomarker for FBDS [3]. Hyperintensity in the basal ganglia has been determined in 19 patients with anti-LGI1-AE using T1WI [3,14,[16], [17], [18], [19], [20], [21]]. Furthermore, the confirmed evolution of T1WI hyperintensity on basal ganglia was associated anti-LGI1-AE relapse in 2 patients [3,17]. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report to describe the complex evolution of T1WI hyperintensity in the basal ganglia. An initial MRI assessment of our patient did not find signal changes on T1WI. During disease progression, right-sided FBDS developed along with new left striatal T1WI hyperintensities. Despite persistent symptoms, the hyperintensity on T1WI of the left striatum became obscure. Treatment improved the symptoms, but bilateral basal ganglia T1WI hyperintensity reappeared and later intensified, even while the clinical improvement continued. This complex shift in basal ganglia T1WI hyperintensity suggested that T1WI changes in the basal ganglia are not consistently correlated with disease activity, treatment response, or recurrence. The fluctuating basal ganglia T1WI hyperintensity found herein was difficult to attribute solely to disease onset or activity, suggesting that additional pathophysiological factors may be involved.

Conclusion

Here, we describe a patient with anti-LGI1-AE characterized by dynamically shifting T1WI hyperintensity. Although a single observation cannot establish causality, the dissociation raises the possibility that additional, yet unidentified factors other than FBDS activity contribute to the development and evolution of altered T1WI signals and that such findings might not always reflect disease activity. Clinicians should be aware that such T1WI changes need not parallel FBDS. This would avoid overinterpreting them as clinical course markers that could lead to inappropriate treatment decisions. Further case studies and systematic investigations are warranted to identify the pathogenic mechanisms involved in altered T1WI hyperintensity.
