O-GlcNAcylation: the metabolic-immune translator in tumor evolution and treatment
Authors: Jinwen Zhao, Jincai Lv, Huanhuan Wang, Zhuangzhuang Zheng, Ying Xin, Xin Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
O-GlcNAcylation is a dynamic post-translational modification of serine/threonine residues in proteins that modulates cellular activities by influencing the function of protein substrates, including their cellular localization, stability, and protein–protein interactions. The hexosamine biosynthesis pathway (HBP) utilizes various metabolites to synthesize uridine diphosphate N-acetylglucosamine, which serves as the substrate for O-GlcNAcylation. Metabolic reprogramming of tumors modulates HBP flux, thereby influencing O-GlcNAcylation levels. O-GlcNAcylation plays a pivotal role in regulating immune signaling pathways and their targets. Consequently, O-GlcNAcylation may act as a central node linking metabolic reprogramming and immune evasion. In this review, we summarize the current progress in understanding the role of O-GlcNAcylation in immunometabolism and explore how O-GlcNAcylation serves as a metabolic–immune translator that regulates tumor progression and therapeutic responses. Furthermore, we discuss the potential of O-GlcNAcylation as a therapeutic target, providing a promising new approach for cancer treatment.

Background

The initiation and progression of cancer are driven not only by dysregulated oncogenic signaling pathways, but also by metabolic reprogramming and dynamic crosstalk within the tumor microenvironment (TME), which collectively sustain proliferation, immune evasion, and metastatic dissemination (Altea-Manzano et al. 2025; Freitas-Cortez et al. 2025). Growing evidence has demonstrated that aberrant protein expression profiles and dysregulated post-translational modifications (PTMs), including phosphorylation, ubiquitination, and O-GlcNAcylation, significantly influence tumor aggressiveness and therapeutic resistance (Liu et al. 2025b; Lin et al. 2024). Consequently, elucidating the interplay between oncogenic metabolic reprogramming and immune evasion mechanisms will provide a rational framework for developing molecularly targeted therapies.

As a hallmark of malignancy, metabolic reprogramming encompasses dynamic adaptations in nutrient acquisition, bioenergetic flux, and redox homeostasis (Heiden and DeBerardinis 2017). These metabolic alterations provide survival advantages under hypoxic and nutrient–deprived conditions and contribute to therapeutic resistance (Zhao et al. 2023). Tumor-derived metabolites reshape the immune landscape (Sung et al. 2026). For example, lactate accumulation acidifies the microenvironment and impairs cytotoxic T-cell function, whereas kynurenine and adenosine directly suppress effector immune responses (Xiong et al. 2024; Chen et al. 2024b; Allard et al. 2024). Hence, understanding the role of tumor metabolic status in tumor evolution and exploring the interplay between metabolism and the immune response are essential for improving cancer treatment.

The field of tumor immunology, particularly the study of immune checkpoints such as the PD-1/PD-L1 and CTLA-4 axes, has significantly transformed the landscape of oncology (Kennedy and Salama 2020). Tumors evade immune surveillance by establishing an immunosuppressive TME, characterized by enhanced PD-L1 expression, T-cell exhaustion, and activation of the complement system (Chen et al. 2026; Shah et al. 2026; Pitt et al. 2016). Interestingly, Interestingly, the tumor immune microenvironment actively reprograms tumor metabolism (Zhang et al. 2024c; Chen et al. 2022). This metabolic–immune crosstalk establishes a feedback loop that perpetuates immune evasion, positioning metabolic reprogramming as both an intrinsic driver of tumor progression and a microenvironmental architect of immunosuppression. Understanding these interconnections provides novel opportunities to combine metabolic inhibitors with immunotherapies, potentially overcoming resistance mechanisms arising from metabolic plasticity.

O-GlcNAcylation is a dynamic post-translational modification catalyzed by O-GlcNAc transferase (OGT) and reversed by O-GlcNAcase (OGA), which modulates protein function through the attachment of β-linked N-acetylglucosamine to serine/threonine residues (Ma et al. 2021). The hexosamine biosynthesis pathway (HBP) integrates metabolic fluxes of glucose, amino acids, fatty acids, and nucleotides to produce UDP-GlcNAc, the substrate for O-GlcNAcylation, thereby serving as the metabolic hub for this modification (Chiaradonna et al. 2018). Tumor metabolic reprogramming, including Warburg effect-induced alterations of glucose flux or glutamine dependency, as well as changes in metabolite concentrations within the TME, may directly influence the HBP activity, consequently affecting the dynamics of O-GlcNAc cycling (Moloughney et al. 2018; Su et al. 2023). Therefore, O-GlcNAcylation functions as a nutrient sensor that responds to metabolic fluctuations in the tumor and TME via the HBP. These metabolic oscillations have a direct impact on anti-tumor immunity via O-GlcNAcylation (Weiss et al. 2021). Although extensive research has been conducted on metabolic reprogramming, O-GlcNAcylation, and tumor immune evasion, the integrative interactions among these processes remain largely unexplored.

This review consolidates current knowledge of O-GlcNAcylation, metabolic reprogramming, and tumor immune evasion as interconnected mechanisms, highlighting the role of O-GlcNAcylation as a pivotal mediator at the metabolic–immune interface (Fig. 1). Crosstalk between metabolic reprogramming and O-GlcNAcylation

Metabolic reprogramming orchestrates the HBP

HBP converts glucose, glutamine, acetyl-CoA, and UTP into UDP-GlcNAc, which serves as the donor substrate for O-GlcNAcylation (Hanover et al. 2012). Moreover, OGT has a low Michaelis constant (Km) for UDP-GlcNAc, making O-GlcNAcylation especially sensitive to small changes in UDP-GlcNAc levels, thereby HBP flux directly translates nutrient availability into protein modification (Haltiwanger et al. 1992). Consequently, HBP serves as a central nutrient-sensing hub that links metabolism to O-GlcNAc signaling (Lam et al. 2021). HBP utilizes approximately 2–5% of glucose and branches from glycolysis through a two-step enzymatic cascade. First, glucose is sequentially phosphorylated by hexokinase and isomerized to fructose-6-phosphate. Subsequently, the rate-limiting enzyme, glutamine:fructose-6-phosphate amidotransferase (GFAT), redirects the glycolytic flux by transferring an amido group from glutamine to fructose-6-phosphate to yield glucosamine-6-phosphate and glutamate. This pivotal step couples glucose and glutamine metabolism to initiate UDP-GlcNAc synthesis, thereby linking nutrient sensing to protein O-GlcNAcylation. Following this, glucosamine-6-phosphate is N-acetylated by glucosamine-phosphate N-acetyltransferase (GNPNAT), which utilizes acetyl-CoA to produce N-acetylglucosamine-6-phosphate (GlcNAc-6P). Subsequently, phosphoglucomutase 3 (PGM3/AGM1) catalyzes the isomerization of GlcNAc-6P into GlcNAc-1-phosphate (GlcNAc-1P), thereby preparing it for subsequent enzymatic reactions. Finally, UDP-N-acetylglucosamine pyrophosphorylase (UAP1/AGX1) facilitates the combination of GlcNAc-1P with UTP to synthesize UDP-GlcNAc (Akella et al. 2019) . recisely because the HBP functions as a central integrator of multiple substances, it is more susceptible to metabolic disturbances and perturbations. This susceptibility positions O-GlcNAcylation as a nutrient sensor that mediates the translation of metabolic reprogramming into malignant phenotypes, oncogenic signaling pathways, and therapeutic resistance. In addition, aberrant expression of the HBP enzymes has been identified as a hallmark of diverse cancer types (Singh et al. 2019). High GFAT expression correlates with poor prognosis in patients with lung cancer, leiomyosarcoma, pancreatic, and breast cancers (Kohara et al. 2024; Kim et al. 2020a; Wang et al. 2021a; Tolwani et al. 2021). Furthermore, GFAT plays a key role in drug resistance, making it pharmacological inhibition a promising target for translational research (Chong et al. 2022; Loison et al. 2024). Therefore, determining the relationship between metabolism and the HBP activity can help further elucidate the significance of O-GlcNAcylation.

Nutrient flux primarily regulates the HBP activity. Metabolic disorders, including diabetes and hyperglycemia, reprogram the nutrient flux within the TME. Elevated glucose levels increase the HBP activity by enhancing substrate availability, thereby promoting tumor progression (Zhou et al. 2023). Conversely, under glucose-restricted conditions, downregulation of PCK1 leads to the accumulation of oxaloacetate, which facilitates de novo uridine triphosphate synthesis to support UDP-GlcNAc production. Simultaneously, suppression of the AMPK-GFAT1 axis results in increased GFAT1 activity, thereby augmenting the HBP flux and O-GlcNAcylation levels. Notably, O-GlcNAcylation is often associated with tumor progression (Xiang et al. 2021). Similarly, mTORC2 operates as a nutrient-sensing hub that aligns glutamine and glucose availability with HBP activity by transcriptionally upregulating GFAT1. This coordination reprograms HBP flux to optimize the utilization of limited glutamine resources, thereby supporting cellular proliferation (Moloughney et al. 2016). Interestingly, nutrient deprivation, specifically of glucose and glutamine, induces the HBP activation through stress-adaptive molecular reprogramming. This counterintuitive activation requires specific molecules and the accumulation of intermediate metabolites that serve as metabolic primers for HBP (Moloughney et al. 2018; Xiang et al. 2021; Moloughney et al. 2016; Gonzalez-Rellan et al. 2021). The abnormal proliferation of cancer cells leads to excessive consumption of glucose and glutamine within the TME, resulting in self-imposed metabolic stress (Mayers and Heiden 2015). To overcome these nutrient limitations, cancer cells strategically utilize the HBP as a central metabolic regulator to facilitate dynamic O-GlcNAcylation, thereby establishing a metabolic buffering system. This metabolic plasticity not only supports the autonomous proliferation of cancer cells but also, through the crosstalk within the TME, serves as a crucial mechanism underlying tumor progression (Cui et al. 2025; Li et al. 2022). Another factor affecting metabolic reprogramming and the HBP is the correlation between metabolites and HBP enzymes. Metabolites such as hyaluronan, oxaloacetate, and α-ketoglutarate promote the HBP by upregulating the GFAT expression (Xiang et al. 2021; Moloughney et al. 2016; Werlen et al. 2022; Zhang et al. 2025; Chanmee et al. 2016). O-GlcNAcylated key enzymes in metabolism pathways

O-GlcNAcylation coordinates cancer metabolism

O-GlcNAcylation functions as a pivotal regulatory switch that influences protein expression, cellular signaling pathways, and gene activity. Elevated levels of O-GlcNAcylation are frequently correlated with tumor progression, immune evasion, and therapeutic resistance (Gou et al. 2025; Liu et al. 2025a; Wang et al. 2024b). Metabolic reprogramming affects O-GlcNAcylation through the HBP, while, O-GlcNAcylation, in turn exerts regulatory control over fundamental metabolic processes, signaling effectors, epigenetic modifications, and stress adaptation, collectively contributing to tumor progression and therapeutic resistance.

O-GlcNAcylation calibrates metabolic flux via key enzymes

Recent studies have increasingly focused on the interplay between PKM2 O-GlcNAcylation and other PTMs, such as acetylation and phosphorylation (Singh et al. 2019; Wang et al. 2022a; Zhou et al. 2024). PKM2 has been found to undergoes O-GlcNAcylation at Thr-405/Ser-406 and Ser-362/Thr-365, and other PTMs also regulate its activity, localization, and oligomeric state. Whether synergistic or antagonistic effects exist among O‑GlcNAc modifications at different sites remains unclear. Moreover, determining how the combination of distinct PTMs influence overall protein function constitutes a major knowledge gap. Variations in cell models, stimulation conditions, and detection methods across different studies further limit the generalizability of some conclusions, which require further validation. The O-GlcNAcylation-driven subcellular relocalization of metabolic enzymes represents a critical mechanism in tumor metabolic reprogramming. O-GlcNAcylation has been shown to activate PGK1 while concurrently promoting its translocation to the mitochondria, thereby coordinating oxidative phosphorylation and glycolysis (Nie et al. 2020). Beyond oncology, O-GlcNAcylation of HK1 in neuronal systems similarly facilitates its mitochondrial translocation (Wang et al. 2024a). Accumulating evidence underscores O-GlcNAcylation as a central regulator of glucose metabolism and tumor development including HK2, PFK1, GAPDH, PFKFB3, PCK1, HIF-1α, SP1, TXNIP and PYGL (Yi et al. 2012; Lei et al. 2020; Lim et al. 2015; Gao et al. 2013; Park et al. 2008; Hu et al. 2019; Liu et al. 2021a; Chen et al. 2021; Hong and Hagen 2015; Panpan et al. 2024; Ferrer et al. 2014). Moreover, nearly all glycolytic enzymes have been identified as OGT substrates (Yi et al. 2012). In the TCA cycle, O-GlcNAcylation plays a role in reprogramming metabolic flux toward glycolysis while diminishing mitochondrial respiration (Kim et al. 2022; Meng et al. 2024). However, current evidence is largely correlative, and direct site-specific evidence demonstrating how O-GlcNAcylation modulates TCA cycle enzyme activity or stability in tumors remains limited. Consequently, further research is warranted to elucidate the functions of O-GlcNAcylation in specific tissues and sites, as well as to determine whether targeting O-GlcNAcylation can reverse metabolic reprogramming and improve therapeutic responses in glycolysis-dependent tumors.

High O-GlcNAcylation levels are linked to fatty acid synthesis and metabolism and are associated with poor prognosis. ATP-citrate lyase (ACLY), a critical rate-limiting enzyme in de novo fatty acid synthesis, is involved in glucose metabolism, fatty acid synthesis, and cholesterol biosynthesis via O-GlcNAcylation. Specifically, O-GlcNAcylation at ACLY at Ser979 detects glucose availability and directs acetyl-CoA toward fatty acid biosynthesis, thereby fueling cell proliferation through lipogenic reprogramming (Liu et al. 2024). O-GlcNAcylated CD36 and HIPK also function as nutrient sensors that exacerbate malignant phenotypes in mice fed a high-glucose/high-fat diet (Jiang et al. 2019; Wong et al. 2020). Recent studies have identified a bidirectional regulatory relationship between O-GlcNAcylation and lipid metabolism. ACSL4 enhances GLUT1-dependent O-GlcNAcylation, which upregulates ACSL4 expression and facilitates liver cancer progression (Wang et al. 2020). Another mechanism involves reciprocal regulation between enzymes such as FASN and OGT, which may represent a feedback regulation driven by metabolites (Raab et al. 2021; Drury et al. 2024). Similarly, nucleotide metabolism is coordinated by O-GlcNAcylation, which governs both de novo DNA biosynthesis and ATP generation through substrate allocation and enzymatic flux control (Gwynne et al. 2022). O-GlcNAcylation of PRPS1, a key enzyme involved in the de novo nucleotide synthesis pathway, counteracts AMPK-mediated phosphorylation, thereby enhancing PRPS1 activity, which confers resistance to chemoradiotherapy (Chen et al. 2023). As a sentinel of metabolic stress, O-GlcNAcylation dynamically recalibrates carbon flux across glycolysis, lipogenesis, and nucleotide biosynthesis (Fig. 3). This PTM functions as a rheostat, enabling cancer cells to consistently prioritize metabolic pathways conducive to cancer progression irrespective of nutrient availability. O-GlcNAcylation interacts with classical molecules and signaling pathways

Beyond its direct modulation of metabolic enzymes, O-GlcNAcylation rewires key signaling nodes and canonical oncoproteins to drive cancer metabolic reprogramming and malignant progression. Rather than independently regulating scattered signaling cascades, this dynamic PTM orchestrates a coordinated hierarchical signaling network, with the O-GlcNAcylation/PI3K/AKT/mTOR axis serving as the dominant regulatory backbone, whereas the AMPK energy-sensing and p53 tumor suppressor pathways function as auxiliary branches that synergize or antagonize the core axis (Fig. 4). The PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling cascade is a central driver of cancer cell proliferation, survival, and therapeutic resistance. O-GlcNAcylation mediates multilayered crosstalk with this axis, sustaining its persistent activation and constituting a core mechanistic basis for malignant tumor progression and clinical treatment failure. At the upstream regulatory level, O-GlcNAcylation indirectly triggers the PI3K/AKT cascade by modulating epigenetic and transcriptional effectors. For example, O-GlcNAcylation of FTO promotes its proteasomal degradation, which relieves FTO-mediated m6A demethylation of SOX4 mRNA and subsequently stabilizes SOX4 expression. The accumulated SOX4 subsequently activates PI3K-AKT signaling, driving the progression of acute myeloid leukemia through integrated epigenetic and metabolic reprogramming (Gou et al. 2025). At the post-translational regulatory level, O-GlcNAcylation directly modifies core components of the mTORC1 complex and AKT kinase to maintain the PI3K/AKT/mTOR axis in an activated state. mTORC1, a central regulator of cell growth and metabolism, relies on raptor for subcellular localization and substrate recognition. O-GlcNAcylation of Raptor at threonine 700 blocks the inhibitory AMPK-mediated serine 792 phosphorylation of Raptor, ultimately inducing robust mTORC1 activation (Xu et al. 2023b). Consistent with these findings, numerous clinical and preclinical studies have confirmed that O-GlcNAcylation enhances AKT phosphorylation and protein stability, thereby sustaining pathway activation and supporting tumor proliferation in multiple malignancies (Kang et al. 2008; Wu et al. 2023; Dai et al. 2020; Kanchanangkul et al. 2025). Notably, minor contradictory findings regarding AKT activation have been attributed to site-specific modifications. O-GlcNAcylation exerts dual regulatory effects on AKT activity in a site-dependent manner, with Thr430/Thr479 modifications promoting AKT activation and Thr305/Thr312 modifications inhibiting AKT function. In contrast, O-GlcNAcylation at Thr308 competes with phosphorylation to fine-tune AKT activity (Heath et al. 2014; Hu et al. 2016; Wang et al. 2012). This site-specific crosstalk explains the context-dependent activation of the core axis and provides a precise target for clinical intervention. Furthermore, O-GlcNAcylation and the PI3K/AKT/mTOR axis form a bidirectional positive feedback loop that amplifies oncogenic signaling and metabolic adaptation. The activated PI3K-AKT-mTOR cascade directly modulates the activity of O-GlcNAc cycling enzymes, elevating global cellular O-GlcNAcylation levels, which further strengthen the activation of the core oncogenic axis (Moloughney et al. 2018; Wu et al. 2023; Very et al. 2018). This self-amplifying loop is a key clinical feature of aggressive tumors that sustain persistent malignant proliferation and resistance to chemotherapy and targeted drugs.

As a critical auxiliary antagonistic branch of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR axis, the AMPK energy-sensing pathway coordinates with O-GlcNAcylation to balance tumor metabolic homeostasis and malignant growth. AMPK and O-GlcNAcylation compete for modification of overlapping substrate sites and mutually regulate each other’s activities, hence forming a metabolic stress regulatory module that opposes the core oncogenic axis. Under nutrient-deficient conditions, activated AMPK acts as a tumor suppressor by phosphorylating downstream substrates to inhibit cell proliferation and induce growth arrest. For instance, AMPK-mediated fumarase phosphorylation triggers cell growth suppression, whereas O-GlcNAcylation at the same substrate site counteracts this inhibitory effect to sustain cancer progression (Wang et al. 2017b). Even under typical nutrient conditions, O-GlcNAcylation competitively occupies AMPK modification sites to promote anabolic metabolism, enabling tumor cells to escape AMPK-mediated growth suppression and maintain high proliferative capacity (Chen et al. 2023). Clinically, this antagonistic regulatory module provides actionable therapeutic targets. O-GlcNAcylation-mediated AMPK inactivation reduces autophagy and accelerates tumor proliferation in breast, bladder, and cervical cancers. Preclinical studies have verified that metformin and quercetin can reverse tumor progression and improve therapeutic efficacy by targeting O-GlcNAcylation to restore AMPK activity, highlighting the clinical translational significance of this auxiliary pathway (Sodi et al. 2017; Ali et al. 2018; Kim et al. 2019; Jin et al. 2020). In addition, AMPK-mediated phosphorylation of OGT and GFAT alters global O-GlcNAcylation levels, which are closely associated with clinical cisplatin resistance, therefore providing a new mechanism for overcoming chemotherapeutic resistance (Xiang et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2024b).

The p53 tumor suppressor pathway serves as a negative regulatory branch of the O-GlcNAcylation-centered network, mediating context-dependent tumor suppression and metabolic reprogramming to counterbalance the oncogenic core axis. O-GlcNAcylation fine-tunes p53 stability and transcriptional activity in a site- and context-dependent manner. O-GlcNAc modification of p53 at Ser149 inhibits MDM2-mediated ubiquitination and degradation, stabilizing p53 to activate downstream target genes such as TIGAR, thereby inhibiting glycolysis and inducing tumor cell apoptosis (De Queiroz et al. 2016; Yang et al. 2006; Shtraizent et al. 2017). However, in specific tissue and nutrient contexts, abnormal O-GlcNAcylation can reduce p53 stability and activity, hence promoting tumor progression (Su et al. 2023; Luanpitpong et al. 2017). This dual regulatory effect is determined by baseline p53 expression and site specific modification, revealing the precise and context-dependent regulatory mechanism of O-GlcNAcylation in tumor suppressor pathway and supplementing the dual role of this PTM in tumor regulation.

O-GlcNAcylation and metabolic stress

Cancer metabolic phenotypes are predominantly influenced by the activation of oncogenic signaling pathways and stressors within the TME. Metabolic stressors, including hypoxia, nutrient deprivation, and therapeutic interventions, induce adaptive reprogramming to ensure cellular survival (Fig. 5). The plasticity of O-GlcNAcylation in response to metabolic stress constitutes a cornerstone of tumor survival strategies (Liu et al. 2021b). Under conditions of nutrient scarcity, including glucose or glutamine deficiency, cancer cells leverage the HBP and O-GlcNAcylation to optimize energy distribution. Although the previous sections have elaborated on the sensitivity of the HBP to nutrient changes, they also emphasize that temporary increases in O-GlcNAcylation during acute starvation promote pro-survival signaling and inhibit AMPK-mediated catabolic processes through interactions with energy sensors (Xiang et al. 2021; Wang et al. 2022b; Zhao et al. 2024; Lv et al. 2024). Beyond glucose and glutamine limitation, arginine deficiency, which has been explored as an anti-tumor strategy, decreases total O-GlcNAcylation while increasing eIF2α O-GlcNAcylation through an unknown mechanism. Hypoxia engages O-GlcNAcylation as a co-driver of adaptive malignancy through mechanistic coupling mediated by HIF-1α. HIF-1α transcriptionally upregulates glucose uptake (e.g., GLUT1) and OGT expression, while regulatory O-GlcNAcylation stabilizes HIF-1α by blocking its hydroxylation and subsequent proteasomal degradation (Ferrer et al. 2014; Rao et al. 2015). This reciprocal reinforcement establishes a feedback loop in which the HIF-1α-OGT axis increases global O-GlcNAcylation levels, subsequently modifying glycolytic enzymes and chromatin remodelers (Yi et al. 2012; Yan et al. 2022). Hypoxia-inducible factors function as central regulators of tumor adaptation to hypoxia, orchestrating survival mechanisms through compensatory angiogenesis, extracellular matrix remodeling, glycolytic metabolism reprogramming, and immunosuppression (Infantino et al. 2021). Therefore, targeting both O-GlcNAcylation and HIF-1α signaling pathways may represent a promising therapeutic strategy.

Therapeutic interventions may inadvertently enhance O-GlcNAc signaling, as tumors deploy this PTM as a "stress shield.” O-GlcNAcylation promotes tumor cell survival by regulating cyclin transcription and the translation of essential proteins through the modification of TOP2A and the translational chaperone calreticulin, thereby causing chemoresistance against platinum-based agents (e.g., cisplatin) and anthracyclines (e.g., doxorubicin) (Liu et al. 2023; Hu et al. 2021; Yan et al. 2024). Additionally, decreased O-GlcNAcylation of SNAP-23 promotes exosome complex assembly, facilitating cisplatin efflux and exacerbating drug resistance (Qian et al. 2021). Chemotherapeutic agents may further exacerbate resistance by directly activating O-GlcNAcylation-related enzymes, such as OGT and GFAT, through AMPK or transcription factor signaling, ultimately altering the dynamics of substrate protein O-GlcNAcylation (Zhang et al. 2024b; Wang et al. 2021b; Huang et al. 2021). Emerging evidence suggests that combining low-dose conventional chemotherapy with O-GlcNAcylation inhibitors not only enhances therapeutic efficacy but also mitigates off-target toxicity in healthy tissues (Loison et al. 2024). Collectively, advances in novel biomarkers and combination therapies targeting O-GlcNAcylation represent a promising strategy for addressing therapeutic resistance (Makwana et al. 2020; Hua et al. 2023). Kinase inhibitors, such as CDK4/6 inhibitors, can induce compensatory O-GlcNAcylation of transcription factors, including RUNX1 and MITF, thereby sustaining cell cycle progression through AKT signaling pathway and nuclear translocation of importin α/β (Zhou et al. 2025; Zhang et al. 2024a). Radiotherapy increases O-GlcNAcylation of melanophilin (MLPH), inhibiting its ubiquitination and degradation, causing radiation resistance through NF-κB activation that enhances anti-apoptotic transcription (Xu et al. 2023a). Notably, O-GlcNAcylation intersects with immunomodulation in therapy-resistant tumor niches. O-GlcNAcylation of upstream molecules of PD-L1 can promote PD-L1 transcription or inhibit its degradation in tumor cells. Conversely, OGT-driven β-catenin stabilization is elevated by O-GlcNAcylation in T cells, thereby enhancing anti-tumor efficacy. Targeting O-GlcNAcylation cycling sensitizes tumors refractory to conventional therapies by dismantling this stress-adaptive architecture, a strategy that has demonstrated synergistic effects with immune checkpoint blockade in preclinical models.

O-GlcNAcylation as a master regulator of immune evasion

Anti-tumor immunity primarily depends on two interdependent mechanisms: immune checkpoint molecules expressed by tumor cells and the functional potency of the immune microenvironment. This dynamic landscape comprises tumor cells and heterogeneous non-malignant cellular populations, including T lymphocytes, macrophages, and regulatory T cells (Tregs), which engage in reciprocal communication with tumor cells to promote tumorigenesis and metastatic progression. Increasing evidence indicates that O-GlcNAcylation is a pivotal regulator that coordinates these immune-evasive networks, with nutrient-sensitive glycosylation directly influencing both cellular and systemic immune surveillance. The following section systematically explores how tumor-intrinsic O-GlcNAcylation collaborates with stromal reprogramming to establish an immunologically privileged niche, thereby highlighting targetable metabolic checkpoints for therapeutic interventions.

O-GlcNAcylation and tumor cells

O-GlcNAcylation in tumor cells has been extensively investigated. Previous studies primarily linked aberrant O-GlcNAcylation in cancer cells to the regulation of core oncogenic processes, including cell proliferation, cell cycle progression, and metastatic dissemination. Nevertheless, mounting evidence has established that the TME is a pivotal driver of tumor progression and therapeutic resistance, thereby shifting the research focus toward the regulatory functions of O-GlcNAcylation in mediating bidirectional crosstalk between cancer cells and the TME. Emerging studies suggest that hyper-O-GlcNAcylation attenuates T cell–dependent tumor suppression (Zhu et al. 2023). The primary mechanism by which O-GlcNAcylation promotes immune evasion involves the stabilization of immune checkpoint proteins, such as PD-L1. In colorectal cancer, O-GlcNAc modification of ENO1 at Ser249 disrupts its interaction with PD-L1, thereby blocking proteasomal degradation of PD-L1 (Zhu et al. 2024). Similarly, in renal cell carcinoma (RCC), HK3-mediated O-GlcNAcylation of EP300 enhances PD-L1 expression through both transcriptional and post-translational regulatory mechanisms. Additionally, IL-10 secreted by M2 macrophages modulates HK3 and PD-L1 expression, potentially establishing a positive feedback loop between M2 macrophages and RCC cells (Zhang et al. 2024d). The cGAS-IFN-γ axis serves as a crucial anti-tumor immune signaling pathway, whereas OGT contributes to immune evasion through HCF-1-dependent stabilization of genomic integrity. This strategic interaction effectively dampens the immunogenic potential of cytosolic DNA sensing by suppressing cGAS-STING pathway activation, thereby establishing an OGT-dependent barrier against anti-tumor immune responses (Chen et al. 2024a). Additionally, the presentation of peptide antigens to T cells by human leukocyte antigen (HLA) complexes is a critical component of immune surveillance. Studies investigating O-GlcNAcylation of HLAs are still in their nascent stages. Although emerging evidence has shown that the HLA-B*07 allele preferentially presents O-GlcNAcylated HLA-associated peptides, the precise effect of such post-translationally modified HLA molecules on tumor immunosurveillance remains unclear (Mukherjee et al. 2021). Moreover, O-GlcNAcylation also modulates immune responses to stimuli such as tumor necrosis factor-related apoptosis-inducing ligand (TRAIL) and transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β). Various immune cells recognize specific antigens on the surface of tumor cells and produce TRAIL following stimulation by IFN-γ and TNF-α. TRAIL subsequently induces apoptosis through binding to TRAIL-R1/DR4 and TRAIL-R2/DR5 (MacFarlane et al. 1997). However, tumor cells may exploit O-GlcNAcylation to circumvent TRAIL-induced apoptosis. Interestingly, DR4 and DR5 exhibit strikingly opposite levels of O-GlcNAcylation during TRAIL-induced apoptosis. Reduced O-GlcNAcylation of DR4 diminishes the formation of the death-inducing signaling complex and aggregation of DR4 within lipid rafts, thereby attenuating TRAIL signaling (Lee et al. 2019). In contrast, O-GlcNAcylated DR5 promotes oligomerization, thereby enhancing TRAIL sensitivity (Yang et al. 2020). This variation may stem from receptor-specific glycosylation motifs located within the intracellular domains of DR4 and DR5, which likely possess distinct O-GlcNAcylation consensus sequences. These motifs may selectively recruit OGT or OGA, leading to different modification levels. TGF-β ligands are secreted by nearly all cell types. Autocrine or paracrine derived TGF- β enhances O-GlcNAcylation in cancer cells by increasing GFAT stability (Liu et al. 2022). O-GlcNAcylation of SMAD4 in the TGF-β signaling pathway further amplifies the efficacy of TGF-β (Kim et al. 2020b). Overall, O-GlcNAcylation serves as a master regulator of immune evasion in tumor cells by stabilizing immune checkpoints, modulating interactions within the TME, and adapting to immune stimuli such as TRAIL and TGF-β. It shapes immune responses by attenuating T-cell suppression, influencing death receptor signaling, and amplifying TGF-β efficacy, thereby facilitating immune evasion.

O-GlcNAcylation and TME components

The TME is a complex ecosystem composed of diverse cell types, including cancer cells, immune cells, cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), and endothelial cells, together with extracellular matrix components and secreted factors. These constituents engage in intricate interactions that are critical for tumor progression and therapeutic responses (Harris et al. 2024). Recent studies have highlighted a crucial link between the TME and O-GlcNAcylation (Fig. 7). Changes in the nutritional status of the TME affect not only tumor cells but also the functions of TME components. Moreover, O-GlcNAcylation profiles in immune cells are critical in modulating the effectiveness of anti-tumor immunity (Feinberg et al. 2022); Qiu et al. 2024a). The cellular constituents of the TME encompass both immunologically active populations, including T cells and natural killer (NK) cells, and immunosuppressive elements, such as Tregs and M2 macrophages. O-GlcNAcylation orchestrates immune evasion by amplifying immunosuppressive circuits and disrupting effector immune responses through the selective modification of critical signaling nodes within these components. Shi et al. demonstrated that M2 macrophages exhibit a high capacity for glucose uptake, fueling the HBP for Cathepsin B O-GlcNAcylation, which promotes cancer metastasis and chemoresistance (Shi et al. 2022). Similarly, elevated glucose concentrations within the TME augment O-GlcNAcylation in macrophages, steering their polarization toward the M2 phenotype and consequently facilitating immune evasion (Rodrigues et al. 2020). Accordingly, pharmacological inhibition of O-GlcNAcylation through direct inhibitors, such as OSMI-1, or indirect approaches targeting Hedgehog signaling represents a promising immunometabolic strategy. These interventions suppress M2 macrophage infiltration/functional activity while reprogramming them toward the tumor-suppressive M1 phenotype, thereby enhancing anti-tumor immunity through these dual mechanisms (Qiu et al. 2024b; Hinshaw et al. 2021). As a pivotal component of immune evasion, Tregs enforce their immunosuppressive function through a specific metabolic reprogramming axis that constrains CD8+ T cell cytotoxicity, with O-GlcNAcylation governing this immunometabolic interaction. Using integrated mouse tumor models and human clinical datasets, Sharma et al. demonstrated coordinated upregulation of GLUT3 and OGT, which was positively associated with Treg infiltration density and poor clinical prognosis. Mechanistically, the authors identified the NF-κB subunit c-Rel as a key substrate for GLUT3-dependent O-GlcNAcylation (Sharma et al. 2024). Furthermore, emerging evidence suggests that O-GlcNAcylation dynamically regulates the functional specilization and terminal differentiation pathways of immunocompetent T cells. Qiu et al. suggested that OGT-mediated O-GlcNAcylation of β-catenin acts as a molecular rheostat that preserves T cell stemness and counteracts exhaustion through stabilization of the Wnt/β-catenin-TCF1 axis. Interestingly, D-mannose supplementation reprograms T-cell metabolism by redirecting glucose flux toward the TCA cycle and the HBP, thereby fueling UDP-GlcNAc synthesis and amplifying protein O-GlcNAcylation. This metabolic-epigenetic interaction not only improves mitochondrial fitness and oxidative phosphorylation but also remodels chromatin accessibility at stemness-associated by modulating the H3K4me3/H3K27me3 balance. Such reprogramming decouples T cell proliferation from terminal differentiation, yielding long-lived "stem-like" T cells with enhanced persistence and tumor control (Qiu et al. 2024). In contrast to conventional cytokine-driven approaches, targeting O-GlcNAcylation through metabolic adjuvants such as D-mannose offers a paradigm-shifting strategy for rejuvenating exhausted T cells.

Bidirectional crosstalk: metabolic-immune feedback loops

The TME functions through a dynamic interplay between metabolism and immune responses, within which O-GlcNAcylation functions as a molecular link that translates nutritional cues into immunomodulatory signals. Our previous analyses have revealed that O-GlcNAc-modified metabolic molecules establish nutrient-dependent immune landscapes through two interconnected pathways.

Metabolic priming of immune evasion

Cancer cells leverage O-GlcNAcylation-driven metabolic reprogramming to construct immunosuppressive niches. For example, the modification of ENO1 at Thr19 and Ser249 promotes glycolytic upregulation and stabilizes PD-L1 or targets upstream transcription factors such as EP300 and c-Myc to augment PD-L1 expression, thereby directly inhibiting cytotoxic T cell activation (Zhu et al. 2024; Zhang et al. 2024d; Shang et al. 2021). In addition, altered metabolic flux in immune cells impairs anti-tumor immunity through mechanisms including mannose metabolism in T cells, GLUT3-dependent glucose flux in Tregs, and diabetes-associated TAMs (Qiu et al. 2024; Rodrigues et al. 2020; Sharma et al. 2024). Notably, elevated lactate production by O-GlcNAcylated tumors reduces the pH of the TME, significantly impairing IL-2-mediated proliferation while reducing mTORC1 pathway and c-Myc expression levels in cytolytic T-lymphocytes (Zhou et al. 2024; Vuillefroy et al. 2024).

Immune-driven metabolic adaptation

Conversely, immune pressure reshapes tumor metabolism through O-GlcNAc-mediated stress responses. IFN-γ produced by activated T cells induce upregulation of MTHFD2, thereby enhancing folate cycle to sustain sufficient UDP-GlcNAc production. This further stabilizes c-Myc and increases PD-L1 transcription (Shang et al. 2021). Meanwhile, TAM-derived IL-10 promotes tumor progression by enhancing HBP flux, which elevates O-GlcNAcylation levels and translates immune signals into tumor metabolic adaptations (Zhang et al. 2024d). Collectively, these processes create a self-reinforcing feedback loop in which immune evasion strategies metabolically prime tumors for aggressive progression.

Future perspectives

The complex dual role of O-GlcNAcylation as both a metabolic regulator and an immune modulator is central to tumor progression. This review highlights accumulating evidence indicating that O-GlcNAc-modified proteins orchestrate metabolic-immune feedback loops through nutrient-driven post-translational modifications, thereby facilitating immune evasion and tumor progression.

Therapeutic approaches targeting O-GlcNAcylation: current landscape

Several small-molecule inhibitors have been developed to modulate O-GlcNAc cycling. OGT inhibitors include OSMI‑1 (IC₅₀ ~ 2.7 µM, cell‑permeable, widely used in preclinical studies), OSMI‑2, OSMI‑3, and OSMI‑4 (later generations with improved cellular activity) (Rahimizadeh et al. 2026). OGA inhibitors include Thiamet G (potent and selective, Kᵢ = 20 nM), PUGNAc (poor selectivity, cross-reacts with lysosomal hexosaminidases), and more selective compounds such as GlcNAcstatin and ASN-120290 (ASN51, which has advanced into early clinical trials for neurodegenerative diseases) (Permanne et al. 2022). Notably, no OGT or OGA inhibitors have yet advanced to clinical trials for cancer treatment, highlighting the major gap between preclinical expectation and clinical translation.

However, several key challenges hinder their clinical translation. First, OGT is essential for normal cellular function in multiple tissues (e.g., the brain, pancreas, and immune system) and systemic inhibition may therefore result in significant adverse effects (Ye et al. 2023). Second, achieving selectivity remains challenging, as many OGA inhibitors exhibit cross‑reactivity with lysosomal β‑hexosaminidases, raising concerns regarding lysosomal storage pathology (Dorfmueller et al. 2010). Third, the large number of O-GlcNAcylated substrates (> 1,000 proteins) complicates mechanistic interpretation, as observed phenotypes may arise from the modification of multiple downstream targets rather than a specific oncogenic driver. Finally, suboptimal pharmacokinetic properties (e.g., poor oral bioavailability) of the current tool compounds limit their clinical development.

Translational evidence and combination strategies

Despite these challenges, accumulating preclinical data support combination strategies as a promising therapeutic treatment. In particular, combining OGT inhibitors with immune checkpoint blockade is particularly promising; because OGlcNAcylation stabilizes PD-L1, whereas OGT inhibitors reduce PD-L1 expression, thereby synergizing with anti-PD ‑L1 antibodies to enhance CD8 + T cell infiltration and drive tumor regression in murine models (Zhu et al. 2023). Similarly, combination with conventional chemotherapy (e.g., OSMI‑1 plus cisplatin or doxorubicin) can redirect senescent cancer cells toward apoptosis and overcome chemoresistance (Feng et al. 2025; Lee and Kwon 2020). Another strategy available is combination with radiotherapy. Inhibition of O-GlcNAcylation sensitizes glioblastoma cells to radiation by blocking MLPH‑mediated anti‑apoptotic signaling (Xu et al. 2023). Collectively, these preclinical findings provide a strong rationale for future clinical trials evaluating OGT/OGA modulators in combination with existing therapies.

To maximize therapeutic efficacy, biomarker-driven patient selection is essential. Potential approaches include: (i) pan-O-GlcNAc staining of tumor biopsies to identify O-GlcNAc-dependent tumors; (ii) quantitative measurement of OGT/OGA expression using immunohistochemistry or RNA sequencing, as elevated OGT expression is correlated with poor prognosis and may predict sensitivity to OGT inhibition; (iii) metabolomic profiling of UDP-GlcNAc and hexosamine pathway intermediates in plasma or tumor tissue to assess pathway activity; and (iv) exploratory clinical studies (e.g., NCT03146374, NCT04406478) have already demonstrated the feasibility of measuring blood O-GlcNAcylation levels, providing a foundation for future companion diagnostics (Kielbasa et al. 2024). Integrating these biomarkers into early-phase trials will enable enrichment of patient populations that are most likely to respond to the therapies.

Controversies, limitations, and knowledge gaps

Despite growing interest in O-GlcNAcylation as a therapeutic target, the field continues to face several controversies and unresolved questions. A major source of conflicting results, exemplified by AKT signaling, in which O-GlcNAcylation has been reported to both enhance and inhibit activity, stems from the lack of site-specific analytical tools. Another major limitation of current experimental models is the oversimplification of global O-GlcNAc manipulation. Most studies rely on global OGT knockdown or small-molecule inhibitors that alter total O-GlcNAcylation levels across thousands of proteins without distinguishing the functional consequences of individual modification sites. Consequently, this “all‑or‑none” approach cannot determine whether observed phenotypes arise from specific residues or represent cumulative and often opposing, effects of multiple modification sites. Furthermore, the field lacks robust site-specific antibodies because of the low immunogenicity and small size of the O-GlcNAc moiety, which severely hampers mechanistic investigation. Addressing these limitations will require the development of protein-‑ and site-specific O-GlcNAc modulation tools and transition from global perturbation strategies toward precise dissection of individual O-GlcNAc events.

Future directions and roadmap for clinical translation

We propose the following priorities for the advancement of O-GlcNAcylation-targeted therapies. First, next-generation OGT inhibitors with improved selectivity, oral bioavailability, and tissue-specific delivery should be developed to mitigate on-target toxicity. Second, combination regimens, particularly OGT inhibitors and immune checkpoint blockade, should be prioritized in well-designed Phase I/II trials for patients with hyper-O-GlcNAcylated tumors. Third, biomarker-guided patient stratification using pre-treatment O-GlcNAc levels or OGT expression should be incorporated to enrich for potential responders. Finally, novel modalities, such as chemical-induced proximity methods for protein-specific O-GlcNAc modulation should be explored to avoid global perturbation of the O-GlcNAc proteome. Addressing these challenges will be crucial for translating promising preclinical evidence into clinically meaningful benefits for patients with cancer. Emerging tools and methodologies will be essential to overcome these barriers.
